Bonjour ! Premiers contacts

First Encounters · Speak and Write French: Foundations of Practical Communication

Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Recognize the accent marks used in this chapter, and begin producing a few French sounds — including the vowel in salut and the French r — in familiar greeting expressions.
  2. Greet someone and say goodbye using level-appropriate expressions.
  3. Introduce yourself using je m'appelle..., and ask someone else's name.
  4. Choose between tu and vous in a small set of controlled everyday situations.
  5. Ask and answer a small set of controlled questions using only formulaic language.
  6. Recognize a small set of fixed classroom expressions, including simple requests for repetition and clarification.

Communication Scenario

Léa is starting a beginner French class on a Tuesday evening. As students arrive, the teacher greets each one at the door, and the students greet each other before the lesson begins. Nobody in the room has met before, so the entire first evening is built from the same small handful of expressions, said again and again, until they start to feel natural. This chapter follows that first evening, greeting by greeting, name by name.

  1. You will meet the same expressions in several short exchanges, so that repetition, not memorization, builds your first working French.
  2. Every new word introduced in this chapter keeps its correct accent, since French spelling includes accents from the very beginning.

Early Exposure — Numbers, not assessed in this chapter

You may notice the numbers 0–10 (zéro, un, deux, trois, quatre, cinq, six, sept, huit, neuf, dix) appear from time to time in classroom contexts, such as when counting off partners for an activity. They are included here only for early, receptive exposure. Numbers become productive vocabulary later in the course, where they connect to prices, ages, and schedules, so nothing on this page or in the chapter tasks requires you to produce or spell them from memory yet.

Pronunciation: Accent Marks and First Sounds

French spelling uses the 26-letter Latin alphabet, plus a small set of accents and one ligature that you will meet gradually across this volume: the acute accent (é), the grave accent (à, è, ù), the circumflex (â, ê, î, ô, û), the diaeresis (ë, ï, ü), the cedilla (ç), and the ligature œ. These marks are a required part of French spelling. Depending on the word, they may affect pronunciation, distinguish one word from another, or preserve an established spelling convention.

Pronunciation Focus — Accents and a few first sounds

Say each vocabulary word above aloud, paying attention to any accent mark. Not every accent changes a vowel's sound. The acute accent in é normally represents one clear vowel sound, close to the vowel in the English word "say" but without the extra glide. An unaccented e is different: depending on the word and its position, it can represent several different sounds, or it can be silent altogether, so it does not have one single pronunciation to compare é against. The grave accent in à, by contrast, does not change how the vowel sounds at all; here it mainly distinguishes the word à ("to, at") in writing from the unrelated word a ("has"). You will meet both functions of French accents again: some mark pronunciation, and some simply distinguish words or preserve spelling.

A few specific sounds are worth practicing carefully from this chapter's vocabulary:

  • The vowel in salut: round your lips as if to say "oo," but try to say "ee" instead. This vowel does not exist in English, so expect it to feel unfamiliar at first.
  • The French r, as in merci and au revoir: it is produced further back in the throat than an English "r," closer to a soft gargling sound. Listen carefully and imitate rather than substituting an English "r."
  • Elision in s'il (as in s'il vous plaît): the vowel of si drops and is replaced in writing by an apostrophe before il. Say the two words as one smooth unit: seel, not see-il.
  • Liaison in vous appelez-vous: the normally silent -s at the end of vous is pronounced as a "z" sound before the following vowel, linking the words together: vooz-apuh-lay-voo.

Beyond these specific points, say each French vowel as a single, steady sound, and try not to glide from one vowel quality into another the way English vowels such as the "ay" in "day" often do. Never drop an accent mark when writing, even informally in this course — accents are required spelling, not optional styling.

Language Focus: Greeting and Introducing Yourself

Purpose: greet someone appropriately, introduce yourself with je m'appelle..., ask a name, and choose between tu and vous.

1.1 The formula Je m'appelle...

Language Focus

Je m'appelle... is a reflexive expression that literally means "I call myself...". At this stage, treat it as a fixed formula for stating your name. You do not yet need to conjugate the underlying verb s'appeler for other subjects; that fuller system develops gradually in a later chapter.

To ask someone's name, French uses two common formulas depending on the relationship: comment tu t'appelles ? with someone you address as tu, and comment vous appelez-vous ? with someone you address as vous. Both are treated as fixed units for now.

  • Je m'appelle Léa. My name is Léa.
  • Comment tu t'appelles ? — Je m'appelle Marc. What's your name? — My name is Marc.

1.2 Tu and vous

Language Focus

French has two words for "you": tu and vous. This distinction is not simply informal versus formal, and it does not depend only on age. Vous has two different jobs: it is the safe way to address one unfamiliar adult, and it is also the only way to address more than one person, no matter how well you know them. Tu is common among peers once the group norm is clear — for example, once classmates have started using it with each other — but a shared age or student status does not automatically make tu correct. Norms vary across Francophone communities and situations, so this chapter presents only a safe, common starting pattern: use vous when addressing a teacher, a stranger, or more than one person, and use tu once a peer relationship has clearly established it, as modeled in the dialogue below. You will refine this choice across the whole course as you meet more contexts.

  • Bonjour, comment vous appelez-vous ? Hello, what is your name? (to a teacher or stranger)
  • Salut, comment tu t'appelles ? Hi, what's your name? (to a classmate)

1.3 Classroom survival language

Formulaic Classroom French — Not for grammatical analysis yet
Comment ? / Pouvez-vous répéter, s'il vous plaît ?
Learn these two expressions whole, exactly as given. Pouvez-vous répéter, s'il vous plaît ? contains a conjugated verb and an infinitive, but you are not expected to break the sentence apart or build new sentences on the same pattern yet — that grammar comes later in the course. For now, simply use the whole expression to signal that you did not understand something.
Comment ? Pouvez-vous répéter, s'il vous plaît ?
What? Can you repeat, please?

You will meet être in Chapter 3, where introductions expand to talking about origin, nationality, and language.

Model Dialogue: Bonjour, enchanté !

Context: The teacher greets a new student at the door on the first evening of class.

La prof : Bonsoir ! Comment vous appelez-vous ? Good evening! What is your name?
Marc : Bonsoir ! Je m'appelle Marc. Good evening! My name is Marc.
La prof : Enchantée, Marc. Entrez, s'il vous plaît. Nice to meet you, Marc. Come in, please. (Entrez is receptive teacher language here — recognize it, but it is not part of your productive vocabulary yet.)

Marc sits down next to another student, who turns to greet him informally.

Léa : Salut ! Comment tu t'appelles ? Hi! What's your name?
Marc : Salut ! Je m'appelle Marc. Et toi ? Hi! My name is Marc. And you?
Léa : Je m'appelle Léa. Enchantée ! My name is Léa. Nice to meet you!

Notice that Marc greets the teacher with bonsoir and vous, and greets Léa with salut and tu, choosing his greeting and form of address according to context and relationship, not a single fixed rule. Et toi ? is a fixed formula for returning a question informally; Entrez is receptive teacher language, included so you recognize it, not because you are expected to produce it yet.

Speaking Practice

Pratique contrôlée — Controlled practice: greet and respond

Practice saying each greeting and courtesy word from the vocabulary table aloud, three times each, paying attention to accents and vowel sounds.

Tâche communicative — Communicative task: meet a classmate

With a partner, greet each other informally using tu, ask and answer each other's names, and say goodbye. Then repeat the exchange as if meeting a teacher, using vous and a more neutral greeting such as bonjour or bonsoir.

Pratique individuelle — Solo alternative

Record yourself greeting an imaginary teacher (using vous) and an imaginary classmate (using tu), introducing yourself in both exchanges. Listen back and check your pronunciation of any accented vowels against the vocabulary table.

Culture and Communication

Culture in Context — Choosing tu or vous

The choice between tu and vous depends on relationship, age, institutional context, and region, not on a single universal rule. Many French speakers also greet in person with a light physical gesture such as a handshake or a cheek kiss (la bise), and the details of this custom vary by region, generation, and relationship. This textbook focuses on the words themselves; nonverbal greeting customs are best learned directly from the community you are engaging with.

Regional Variation — French beyond France

French is spoken as a first, official, or widely used language in France, Belgium, Switzerland, Quebec and other French-speaking communities in Canada, many Caribbean nations, much of North, West, and Central Africa, the Indian Ocean, the Pacific, and Francophone communities elsewhere. This course uses contemporary, broadly understood French based primarily on educated metropolitan usage in France as its production model, and it will introduce labeled comparisons with other varieties as the volume progresses. Speaking French does not by itself indicate a person's nationality, ethnicity, or political views.

Common Mistakes

Common Mistake — Dropping accent marks

Error: Writing a bientot instead of à bientôt, treating accents as optional.

Correction: Always include the acute, grave, circumflex, diaeresis, or cedilla shown in the vocabulary table. Depending on the word, these marks may affect pronunciation, distinguish one word from another, or preserve an established spelling convention — but they are never optional.

Common Mistake — Mixing tu and vous with the same person

Error: Saying comment tu t'appelles ? to a teacher, then switching to vous a moment later with the same person.

Correction: Choose one form of address for a given person and relationship, and keep it consistent within an interaction unless the relationship itself changes.

Common Mistake — Writing the wrong form of a fixed expression

Error: Writing enchanté regardless of who is speaking.

Correction: This chapter teaches enchanté and enchantée as two fixed spellings of the same fixed expression: a speaker who identifies as a man typically writes enchanté, and a speaker who identifies as a woman typically writes enchantée. In ordinary speech the two sound alike. For now, simply reproduce the correct written form of this one expression for yourself; the broader system behind it is introduced later in the course.

Looking Ahead

Ce que vous savez déjà faire — What you can do now

You can greet someone informally or neutrally by time of day, introduce yourself with je m'appelle..., ask someone else's name, choose between tu and vous in simple situations, and use a small set of courtesy expressions, all while preserving French accents.
