Felix Mitchell

Overview

Felix Wayne Mitchell Jr. (1954–1986) was an Oakland drug trafficker who led a structured heroin-distribution enterprise commonly called the 69 Mob or simply the Mob. Federal appellate records describe the organization as beginning heroin sales in San Antonio Village in 1976 and operating through a hierarchy of street sellers, collectors, lookouts, and supervisors.

Mitchell became one of the best-known figures associated with Oakland's late-1970s and early-1980s heroin economy. His organization competed violently with rival groups, including Mickey Moore's Family. Mitchell was federally prosecuted, convicted in 1985, and sentenced to life imprisonment. He was killed at the federal penitentiary in Leavenworth, Kansas, in August 1986 while his direct appeal was still pending.

Rise of the Mob

The Ninth Circuit's later opinion in United States v. Patterson states that the enterprise began dealing heroin in San Antonio Village in early 1976. The court described a hierarchy of approximately twenty to twenty-five members. Street sellers distributed heroin, collectors moved proceeds upward, and lookouts used signals and walkie-talkies to warn of police activity.

According to the appellate record, the organization could generate as much as $15,000 per day during periods of high activity. Mitchell stood at the top of the hierarchy and directed the enterprise's distribution activities.

Rivalries and Violence

Mitchell's organization operated in a competitive Oakland heroin market. Federal records and contemporary reporting describe violent conflicts with rival organizations. One major rival was The Family, associated with Milton “Mickey” Moore. Later reporting also identifies Funktown USA as another important Oakland drug organization of the period.

The 1987 appellate opinion connected the Mob's efforts to protect sales territory with a series of killings and shootings. Individual responsibility for specific acts should be treated according to the relevant charging record, trial record, or judgment rather than inferred solely from organizational membership.

Federal Prosecution

A federal grand jury returned an indictment in February 1983 against Mitchell and multiple co-defendants. The litigation produced a mistrial, extensive pretrial proceedings, a retrial, and later appeals. Mitchell was convicted in 1985 on federal drug-related charges, including operation of a continuing criminal enterprise, and received a life sentence.

Mitchell died before his direct appeal was completed. Under the law then applied to a defendant who died while a direct criminal appeal remained pending, his conviction was ordered vacated posthumously. The vacatur was procedural and did not constitute a factual finding that the underlying conduct had not occurred.

Death and Funeral

Mitchell was stabbed at the federal penitentiary in Leavenworth on August 21, 1986. His funeral in Oakland attracted national attention. Contemporary reporting described more than 1,000 people lining the route as his casket was carried in a horse-drawn carriage through East Oakland.

The spectacle generated substantial public debate. Later that year, thousands participated in an Oakland anti-drug march that organizers framed in part as a response to the attention surrounding Mitchell's funeral.

Historical Significance

Mitchell is historically significant because court records preserve an unusually detailed description of how a large, neighborhood-based heroin enterprise was organized in Oakland before the crack-cocaine era. His career also illustrates the interaction among street distribution, territorial violence, federal narcotics enforcement, public housing geography, and the creation of local criminal celebrity.
