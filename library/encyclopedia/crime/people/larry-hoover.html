Larry Hoover

Overview

Larry Hoover became the longtime chairman of the Gangster Disciples, one of Chicago's largest and most structured street organizations. Federal appellate decisions document Hoover's continued leadership from the Illinois prison system and his role in a large cocaine, heroin, and crack-distribution enterprise.

Origins

Hoover emerged from Chicago's South Side gang environment during the 1960s. Federal Justice Department summaries describe the later Gangster Disciples as developing from an alliance between the organization associated with Hoover and the Black Disciples associated with David Barksdale. The precise dates and names used during this early consolidation period vary among historical sources.

Leadership from Prison

Hoover was imprisoned for murder in 1973 but continued to exercise authority within the Gangster Disciples. Federal court records describe a hierarchy headed by a chairman and board members, followed by governors and additional supervisory and street-level ranks.

Drug-Distribution Structure

The Gangster Disciples specialized in street-level sales of cocaine, crack cocaine, heroin, and other drugs. The organization controlled territory, assigned sellers to locations, collected regular payments from members, and used disciplinary mechanisms to enforce rules and protect its markets.

1995 Federal Indictments

Federal prosecutors returned major indictments against Hoover and numerous high-ranking Gangster Disciples in 1995. The government alleged a decades-long narcotics conspiracy and charged senior leaders with operating a continuing criminal enterprise.

1997 Conviction

Hoover was convicted in federal court on May 9, 1997. He and several other senior figures received life sentences. In 2001, the Seventh Circuit affirmed Hoover's convictions and sentence.

Historical Significance

Hoover's case provides one of the most detailed judicial records of a highly structured Chicago gang operating a large drug-distribution system while senior leadership was incarcerated. It also demonstrates how prison communication, hierarchy, territorial control, and street-level retailing could be integrated into one criminal enterprise.
