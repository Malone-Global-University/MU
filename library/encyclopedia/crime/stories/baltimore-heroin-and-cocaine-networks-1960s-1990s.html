Baltimore Heroin and Cocaine Networks, 1960s–1990s

Overview

Baltimore's narcotics history from the 1960s through the early 1990s was shaped by a persistent heroin economy that later intersected with expanding cocaine markets. The strongest documentary record does not support a single continuous “Baltimore organization.” Instead, the city contained successive and overlapping networks with different leaders, territories, suppliers, and organizational structures.

The Long Heroin Era

Federal court records state that Melvin “Little Melvin” Williams had dealt in heroin and cocaine since 1967. He became one of the city's most prominent traffickers and was especially associated with West Baltimore. His reputation reflects the durability of heroin as a central commodity in Baltimore's illicit economy.

Melvin Williams and West Baltimore

By the early 1980s, investigators described Williams as the head of a substantial organization. A coordinated 1984 investigation involving federal tax agents, the DEA, ATF, and Baltimore police examined both narcotics activity and the financial structure surrounding his assets and businesses.

The Meredith–Ricks–King Organization

A separate major heroin organization was led by Clarence Meredith, Thomas “Joe Dancer” Ricks, and Maurice “Peanut” King. Federal appellate opinions describe a network using street-distribution lieutenants, controlled corners, a stash house, recurring supply meetings, family participants, and centralized collection of drug proceeds.

Law-Enforcement Penetration

The Meredith organization was penetrated after James “Scotty” Wiggins was arrested with hundreds of bags of heroin in 1985 and agreed to cooperate. Investigators used him to record conversations and a group meeting, leading to arrests on October 30, 1985. The case demonstrates the importance of insiders and electronic evidence in dismantling hierarchical drug organizations.

Cocaine Enters the Network

Heroin remained important, but cocaine became increasingly significant during the 1980s. Williams' 1985 federal prosecution arose from an attempted four-kilogram cocaine purchase, while other organizations were developing mixed cocaine-and-heroin distribution systems.

The Canty Organization

Tommy Lee Canty's organization represents this later phase especially clearly. The Fourth Circuit described Canty as buying cocaine and heroin from suppliers in New York, Miami, and Los Angeles and distributing the drugs through a multilayered Baltimore network.

National Supply Connections

The Canty case establishes a direct documentary link between Baltimore and three other major trafficking centers. These relationships make Baltimore a natural bridge in the Crime Encyclopedia between the Northeast Corridor, South Florida, and Southern California.

Changing Investigative Methods

The enforcement response also changed over time. Earlier cases relied heavily on informants, surveillance, and individual narcotics transactions. By the late 1980s, investigators increasingly combined local and federal resources, undercover infiltration, wiretaps, financial investigations, and large conspiracy indictments.

Continuity Without a Single Organization

The key historical continuity lies in the market infrastructure, not necessarily in organizational succession. New organizations inherited access to customers, neighborhood territories, street sellers, transportation routes, and supplier relationships even when their leadership had no formal connection to the groups that came before them.

Assessment

Baltimore adds a distinct dimension to the national Crime Encyclopedia. Unlike cities whose modern drug histories are narrated primarily through crack cocaine, Baltimore's story requires heroin to remain central. The city also provides exceptionally strong federal appellate records documenting how several organizations actually distributed narcotics and how investigators dismantled them.
