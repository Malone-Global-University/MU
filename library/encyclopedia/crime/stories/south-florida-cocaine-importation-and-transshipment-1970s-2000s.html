South Florida Cocaine Importation and Transshipment, 1970s–2000s

Overview

From the 1970s into the twenty-first century, South Florida functioned as one of the principal U.S. gateways for cocaine arriving from South America through the Caribbean. Its role changed over time, but the region remained important as an arrival zone, wholesale market, redistribution center, and financial hub.

Geographic Advantage

Miami's proximity to the Bahamas and Caribbean, combined with major seaports, airports, highways, international banking, and a large import-export economy, gave traffickers numerous ways to move drugs and money.

1970s Maritime Smuggling

DEA histories document elaborate maritime smuggling organizations operating from South Florida during the 1970s. Boats, coastal stash houses, encrypted or radio communications, and financial laundering were already important before cocaine became the dominant commodity.

Colombian Cocaine Expansion

As Colombian cocaine organizations expanded, Miami became a key U.S. entry point. Traffickers moved increasingly large shipments through private aircraft, maritime vessels, commercial cargo, and Caribbean intermediaries.

Caribbean Intermediaries

Federal assessments later described Colombian organizations working with Bahamian, Cuban American, Dominican, Haitian, Jamaican, Puerto Rican, and other Caribbean groups to move cocaine into Florida.

Wholesale Redistribution

South Florida was not merely the endpoint of smuggling. Multikilogram and multihundred-kilogram loads were redistributed to states throughout the eastern United States. In later years, federal assessments continued to identify South Florida as a national distribution center.

Port and Container Traffic

Commercial shipping offered another route. Federal cases documented cocaine hidden in containers entering the Port of Miami and then removed for transport to stash houses and interstate distribution networks.

Medellín Cartel Infrastructure

Later prosecutions documented Medellín Cartel representatives maintaining properties and businesses in South Florida to support importation, distribution, enforcement, and movement of drug proceeds back toward Colombia.

Money Laundering

Billions of dollars in narcotics proceeds created a parallel financial problem. Cash movement, currency exchanges, offshore accounts, real estate, banks, and international business channels became central targets of federal investigations.

Changing Enforcement

The federal response evolved from individual smuggling cases toward coordinated task forces and multi-agency investigations. The South Florida Drug Task Force and later OCDETF and HIDTA structures reflected this institutional shift.

Assessment

South Florida's importance lies in its network position. It connected South American producers and Caribbean transit routes to domestic wholesalers, regional cities, interstate highways, financial systems, and law-enforcement jurisdictions across the United States.
