Overview

The Colombo crime family is one of New York City’s Five Families of the American Mafia, or La Cosa Nostra (LCN). Its earlier leadership was associated with Joseph Profaci; it acquired its present name from Joseph Colombo, who became boss in 1963. The organization is a criminal enterprise rather than a biological family, ethnic association, or representative body for Italian Americans. [1][2][5]

Its history is distinguished by unusually visible and destructive leadership disputes: the Profaci–Gallo conflict around the beginning of the 1960s, the violence following Colombo’s shooting in 1971, and the Persico–Orena war of 1991–1993. These episodes involved rival crews and leaders within one criminal enterprise, not armed conflict between all Italian-American communities. Subsequent federal racketeering prosecutions documented murders, threats, fraud, gambling, loansharking, and attempts to exploit unions and employee benefit plans. [1][4][7][9][14]

This encyclopedia profile traces the organization’s development from the pre-1931 Mafia through documented proceedings in 2026. It assigns specific evidentiary status to allegations, guilty pleas, jury verdicts, sentencing findings, and secondary reports. Neither a press report nor an indictment should be confused with proof of every individual’s culpability or a complete picture of a clandestine organization.

Terms such as boss, acting boss, and street boss have been used in different cases to describe varied combinations of nominal rank, practical control, and delegated authority. A reference to someone as boss at one date does not establish unbroken power at another. Criminal affiliation also must not be inferred from surname, ancestry, kinship, religion, occupation, or presence in the same neighborhood. [5][6][14][18]

Joseph Profaci and the Early Organization

Joseph Profaci was an influential early New York Mafia leader whose organization preceded the use of the Colombo name. The conventional founding period is the late 1920s; the definitive 1931 arrangement of the Five Families reflected the reshaping of older criminal networks in the aftermath of the Castellammarese War. Profaci’s long tenure ended with his death in 1962, not with a voluntary transfer of command. His olive-oil business contributed to the sobriquet “Olive Oil King,” but it is important to distinguish a legitimate commercial activity from specific crimes attributed to organized-crime associates. [1][2]

Profaci also maintained ties with Joseph Bonanno’s organization. Connections among bosses included alliances, family ties, arbitration, and competition; a connection did not make all families branches of a single national corporation. The State of New Jersey Commission of Investigation, surveying these events retrospectively, described Profaci’s New Jersey property and his working relationship with Bonanno as part of the early family’s regional reach. [1]

Long tenure can create an appearance of stability while leaving grievances within the organization unresolved. Tensions over earnings, distribution of proceeds, and leaders’ demands contributed to dissident action against Profaci at the beginning of the 1960s. Those conflicts exposed the difficulty of sustaining a coercive organization through nominal obedience alone.

The Five Families and the Commission

The Colombo organization belongs to the New York Five Families alongside the Bonanno, Gambino, Genovese, and Lucchese organizations. Following the 1931 Mafia realignment, an inter-family institution called the Commission became a forum for disputes and questions affecting multiple groups. It was neither a public institution nor a legal trade association: it represented a mechanism by which illicit enterprises sought to prevent disputes from undermining their operations. [1][2][5]

The Commission’s authority was not always effective, particularly when senior leaders disagreed about succession, the division of illicit income, or violence. The effort to suppress inter-family conflict did not eliminate wars inside a single family. The Profaci–Gallo and Persico–Orena struggles illustrate how internal power contests could generate harm despite the existence of an ostensibly centralized mechanism for arbitration.

Histories of the Mafia sometimes describe Commission decisions as though they were a complete and universally obeyed constitutional system. The public documentary record supports a more constrained interpretation: rulings, alliances, and enforcement depended on available power, knowledge, and the willingness of separate organizations to comply.

The Profaci–Gallo Conflict

In the early 1960s, a faction associated with brothers Joseph (“Crazy Joe”) Gallo, Albert Gallo, and their associates challenged Profaci’s leadership. Historical accounts identify conflicts over leadership, money, and treatment of subordinate crews. The crisis became an armed and coercive internal struggle rather than a simple dispute about organizational procedure. [1][2][4]

The conflict is often called the first Colombo war, though the organization was still known by Profaci’s name when it began. Using the retrospective label can help readers connect the events, but the article should preserve the chronology and not suggest Joseph Colombo was already the boss. Profaci died in 1962 amid these tensions, and the leadership succession did not immediately settle all dissident grievances. [1][2]

Accounts sometimes attach precise death tolls or comprehensive claims of responsibility to the conflict. Since reporting from the era is uneven and competing factions had reasons to distort their behavior, a cautious chronology should separate documented deaths, participants’ narratives, and retrospective interpretations. The broader historical significance is clear: challenges from organized crews weakened the family and created opportunities for intervention by outside Mafia leaders.

Joseph Magliocco and the 1963 Succession

After Profaci died, his brother-in-law Joseph Magliocco briefly succeeded him. Magliocco’s relationship with Bonanno became entangled in a struggle involving the Mafia Commission. The State of New Jersey’s 2004 investigative history recounts a plot against prominent leaders, including Carlo Gambino and Thomas Lucchese, and describes Joseph Colombo’s decision to reveal the scheme to Commission figures. The episode established the basis for Colombo’s promotion. [1][2]

Magliocco’s tenure lasted only into 1963. Colombo’s rise is commonly dated to that year, and the organization thereafter acquired the Colombo name. This succession demonstrated that a senior position could be decided partly by alliances outside an individual family rather than simply by inheritance within the incumbent boss’s household.

The sequence is sometimes reduced to the claim that Colombo “won” control through one act of informing on another boss. A more meaningful institutional interpretation considers the instability created by Profaci’s death, dissatisfaction within subordinate crews, disputes among Commission members, and the strategic interests of other families. Colombo’s elevation did not extinguish all previously existing internal divisions.

Joseph Colombo’s Leadership and Public Profile

Joseph Colombo became the family’s namesake and a conspicuously public Mafia leader. His leadership differed in presentation from the low-profile posture often associated with other senior figures: during the later 1960s and early 1970s, he appeared at public events and made organized public appeals about discriminatory portrayals of Italian Americans. [2][3]

Those efforts must be interpreted carefully. Discrimination against Italian Americans and abusive ethnic stereotyping can be analyzed on their own merits; neither their existence nor the legitimacy of civil-rights claims establishes that Colombo’s criminal organization represented the Italian-American population. Likewise, public participation in a civil-rights event is not evidence that an attendee knowingly endorsed organized crime.

Colombo’s visibility also had organizational consequences. Public media appearances drew attention to his identity and associates at a moment when law enforcement was expanding its scrutiny of La Cosa Nostra. The contrast between a public advocacy campaign and a concealed criminal enterprise is a central subject for historical study, but assigning a single secret motive to every participant in the campaign would exceed available evidence.

Italian-American Civil Rights League

The Italian-American Civil Rights League emerged in 1970 and became associated with Joseph Colombo’s campaigns against use of Mafia-related terminology and what its organizers characterized as discrimination. Large rallies, television appearances, and cultural publicity made the League an unusually visible part of Mafia-era New York history. A U.S. House committee subsequently discussed the League when reviewing the attack on Colombo in 1971. [3]

The League was an identifiable public organization, whereas the Colombo family was a criminal network operating through secrecy and coercion. It is methodologically unsound to describe every League organizer, donor, entertainer, or attendee as a Mafia participant. Public advocacy can be exploited for reputation management while also attracting individuals with independent and lawful reasons to join.

The same distinction applies to media and entertainment. The League’s interaction with productions about organized crime helped make Mafia terminology and Italian-American representation publicly controversial. The encyclopedia should recognize the cultural debate without adopting the assertion that discussing criminal organizations necessarily defames an entire population.

June 28, 1971: Shooting of Joseph Colombo

On June 28, 1971, Joseph Colombo was shot during a League rally at Columbus Circle in Manhattan. The attacker, identified as Jerome Johnson, was killed in the immediate aftermath. Colombo survived the initial shooting with catastrophic injuries and remained severely incapacitated until his death in 1978. This was an attack in a public gathering, exposing bystanders and witnesses to deadly violence. [1][3]

The identity of the immediate assailant does not establish who, if anyone, directed or financed the attack. Congressional examinations and subsequent histories have discussed possible links to organized-crime factions, but the ultimate responsibility has been contested. The entry therefore does not repeat as proven claims that any specific Mafia leader secretly ordered the shooting. [3]

Colombo’s incapacity transformed the question of leadership. Formal naming traditions can obscure the fact that a badly wounded boss was no longer exercising the same practical authority. Other figures assumed greater responsibility, competing interests persisted, and the family’s internal alignment remained unstable into the 1970s.

Joseph Gallo’s Killing and the Early 1970s

On April 7, 1972, Joseph “Crazy Joe” Gallo was shot and killed at Umberto’s Clam House in Manhattan’s Little Italy. The killing became a national news story because of Gallo’s notoriety, the restaurant setting, and renewed concern over Mafia violence after Colombo’s shooting the previous year. Contemporary reporting documents the attack and its immediate circumstances; later accounts disagree about the complete chain of responsibility. [4]

Gallo’s death is commonly linked in historical narratives to the residual Profaci-era disputes and the deteriorating relations among Colombo factions. Linkage by context, however, is not the same as proof about the individual who issued an order. Accounts that firmly assign Gallo’s murder to Colombo, Persico, or a particular crew without identifying their underlying evidence should be treated as hypotheses or attributed allegations, not unquestioned historical verdicts.

The events of 1971–1972 also show why “Mafia wars” are not merely disputes among offenders. Their consequences fell on workers, restaurant patrons, families, witnesses, surrounding neighborhoods, and public institutions compelled to respond to serious violence. An encyclopedia account should not romanticize the events simply because they became famous in crime reporting.

Carmine Persico and Long-Term Control

Carmine “Junior” Persico, often nicknamed “the Snake,” emerged as the family’s dominant leader in the 1970s. His tenure became notable for its extraordinary duration and for repeated periods in which incarceration separated nominal leadership from day-to-day criminal decision-making. In court records and later Department of Justice summaries, Persico is consistently identified as the official Colombo boss during key prosecutions. [2][7][10][17]

Persico’s authority depended on intermediaries, including underbosses, acting bosses, relatives, and loyal captains. Such delegation can preserve an organization while also inviting challenges from officials who control crews or revenue. The persistence of a formal title should not be confused with continuous personal management of all rackets or the actions of all associates.

After his convictions in the 1980s, Persico served a lengthy federal sentence. He died in custody in 2019. The subsequent identification of other leaders in public records should be read against this long period of nominal rule from prison and the resulting succession disputes; it does not imply a simple, uncontested handover of authority. [2][14][15]

1980s Racketeering and the Commission Prosecution

Federal use of the Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act (RICO) made it possible to prosecute conduct carried out through a continuing criminal enterprise rather than treating every predicate offense in isolation. During the 1980s, federal investigators targeted the Mafia Commission and prominent family officials, including Carmine Persico. The Commission case produced major convictions and imposed exceptional prison sentences on senior organized-crime figures. [2][5][17]

The larger significance of the prosecution was institutional. Proof of patterns of racketeering, decision-making relationships, extortion, and agreement among leaders could be assembled into a criminal-enterprise case. It challenged a system that had relied on compartmentalizing the work of bosses, captains, intermediaries, and associates to avoid accountability.

RICO convictions did not mean that every member was arrested or that the Colombo family instantly disappeared. Leadership vacancies, imprisoned bosses who retained influence, and replacement crews could leave criminal networks active. The later 1990s war illustrates the limits of any claim that one landmark trial permanently resolved the family’s governance problem.

Victor Orena and the Road to the 1991 War

During Carmine Persico’s imprisonment, Victor “Little Vic” Orena emerged as an acting leader. Orena and leaders aligned with Persico disagreed over authority, legitimacy, and succession. The split involved persons who had served within the same family; their rivalry exposed how a long-term imprisoned boss and an acting administration could develop incompatible bases of power. [7][9][12]

By 1991 the dispute escalated into violent confrontation. This is often designated the third Colombo war, with earlier conflicts counted as the Profaci–Gallo fighting and the violence of the early 1970s. The numbering is a retrospective convention, not a statute, and individual historians differ over where one period of conflict ended and the next began.

Federal court records established extensive conflict between a Persico-aligned faction and an Orena-aligned faction. A Department of Justice Inspector General review later discussed verdicts involving Orena and the role of informant Gregory Scarpa. A balanced account must distinguish the convicted conduct, evidence presented in trials, and claims later challenged over confidential-source handling. [12]

The Persico–Orena War, 1991–1993

The 1991–1993 Colombo war generated murders, assassination attempts, retaliatory attacks, witness intimidation, and major federal investigations. Public proceedings describe participants aligned with either the imprisoned Persico or the competing acting administration associated with Orena. The dispute harmed victims both inside and outside the organization and drew substantial investigative resources into New York neighborhoods. [7][9][12][20]

In a May 2012 jury verdict, former acting boss Thomas Gioeli and soldier Dino Saracino were convicted of racketeering conspiracy. The verdict found specific murder conspiracies and other racketeering predicates established, while also rejecting certain separate murder-related charges. These distinctions matter: jurors did not accept every prosecution theory simply because they returned racketeering convictions. [7]

Later sentences, including 224 months for Gioeli and 50 years for Saracino, reflected the severity of crimes found in their proceedings. The government also presented other evidence at sentencing, where the burden of proof for relevant conduct differs from the beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard for a jury conviction. Readers should therefore not treat every sentencing statement as a distinct jury verdict. [8][9]

Deaths, Innocent Victims, and Evidentiary Complexity

The 1990s war was not an abstract contest between organizational charts. Federal indictments and trial proceedings addressed killings of numerous individuals, including John Minerva, who was killed in March 1992, and cases involving bystanders who were present when intended targets were attacked. Other prosecutions concerned the death of Joseph Scopo, who had been associated with the Orena faction. Different defendants faced different charges, and the results of those cases should not be combined into one universal finding. [7][20][21]

The 2012 Gioeli–Saracino verdict is instructive because the jury found some predicate conduct proven and returned not-guilty findings for several murder-in-aid-of-racketeering counts. Criminal-history accounts that describe every originally charged murder as a conviction would misstate the trial. Sentencing judges can make additional findings under different standards, but those must be identified as such. [7][8]

Accounts of these conflicts also require attention to the families of victims, business owners, ordinary residents, and witnesses. They suffered threats, violence, and loss even though they were not necessarily participants in the organization. An ethical historical record must not use unverified rumors to assign criminal identities to people who happened to be near a violent incident.

Gregory Scarpa and Informant Controversies

Gregory “Greg” Scarpa Sr. was a Colombo family figure who also supplied information to the FBI. The relationship between Scarpa and his FBI handler R. Lindley DeVecchio became the subject of a substantial Department of Justice Inspector General review. The review documented concerns about source handling, the management of information, and the implications for prosecutions tied to Colombo family violence. [12][22]

Informants are indispensable in some secret-organization investigations, but their access does not guarantee honesty. A participant may supply genuine information while having incentives to conceal his own wrongdoing, undermine rivals, or influence investigative priorities. Investigators must cross-check such accounts against records, physical evidence, independent witnesses, and a coherent chain of custody.

The controversy should not be turned into a sweeping assertion that all Colombo convictions were invalid or that every allegation about the FBI was proved. The appropriate approach is to cite the Inspector General’s specific findings, distinguish its conclusions from speculation, and consult the procedural history of each criminal case rather than judging all defendants as a single category. [12]

William Cutolo, Alphonse Persico, and the 1999 Murder

William “Wild Bill” Cutolo, an important Colombo figure, was murdered in May 1999. Federal prosecutors subsequently established at trial that Alphonse “Allie Boy” Persico, Carmine Persico’s son, and John “Jackie” DeRoss organized the murder. In December 2007, a jury convicted both men of murder in aid of racketeering and witness tampering. [10]

In February 2009, both men received life sentences. The Justice Department explained the killing in terms of leadership rivalry and retaliation following the 1990s war. Unlike unresolved theories surrounding earlier Mafia attacks, this episode has a clear adjudicated basis in the cited federal convictions and sentencing documents. [11]

The case demonstrates that the consequences of factional fighting did not end simply because the most intense 1991–1993 exchanges subsided. Suspicion of competitors, retaliation, and maintenance of coercive authority remained relevant to prosecutions years later. It also illustrates the need to keep Alphonse Persico distinct from his father Carmine and their relative Theodore Persico Jr..

Criminal Enterprises and Revenue

Colombo-related criminal cases reveal several recurring revenue systems: illegal gambling, extortionate loans, labor racketeering, fraud, and the extraction of proceeds through threats and intermediary businesses. Court records do not establish that each crew operated in every field at all times. Rather, the organization’s continuity lay partly in its ability to exploit changing opportunities while maintaining connections among members and associates. [6][13][14][15]

Illegal gambling can produce revenue through wagers, loans to gamblers, and collection of unpaid balances. Loansharking becomes especially damaging when the organization uses threats of violence or retaliation to impose oppressive terms or collect debts. Fraud schemes can involve falsified qualifications, corrupt intermediaries, or misrepresentation in contracts. The affected individuals may include legitimate business owners and borrowers under coercion.

It would be misleading to calculate the entire family's annual income simply by adding money figures mentioned in different indictments. Some amounts describe attempted diversion rather than proceeds actually received; others reflect overlapping defendants, different periods, or counts subject to acquittal or settlement. The source and procedural status of every financial estimate must be preserved.

Labor Organizations and Employee Benefit Plans

Labor racketeering is among the most thoroughly documented modern Colombo criminal activities. A 2011 national organized-crime case alleged extensive activity involving a cement-and-concrete-workers union. Later proceedings concerning a Queens-based labor union described a long-running effort to force a union official to surrender salary and steer contracts from an affiliated health benefit fund to vendors associated with the Mafia. [6][14][15][16]

These schemes rely on a distinction between a lawful institution and individuals attempting to exploit it. Union members and beneficiaries are not automatically wrongdoers; in the 2021–2024 case, prosecutors described union officials as victims of sustained coercion, while particular vendors or brokers pleaded guilty to their own fraud offenses. The harm included threats against the officer’s family and attempts to extract resources intended to support worker health care. [14][15]

Labor infiltration can distort both worker representation and market competition. Funds designated for insurance benefits may be diverted toward illegitimate intermediaries; contractors may face pressure to award business without a fair process. The economic consequences often spread beyond the original victim to employees and their dependents, employers, and the integrity of collective bargaining.

Geographic Reach and Community Context

The Colombo organization has been associated historically with parts of Brooklyn, Queens, Staten Island, and other locations in the greater New York area. The group’s historical footprint cannot be reduced to one headquarters street or one neighborhood. Members and associates have appeared in criminal cases involving New Jersey, Long Island, and enterprises crossing local jurisdictional boundaries. [1][13][14]

Territory in organized-crime history refers to areas where individuals maintained connections, carried on business, or asserted influence, not exclusive political sovereignty over all residents and enterprises. The existence of a Mafia crew in a neighborhood cannot establish that residents, merchants, unions, or ethnic associations there are controlled by crime figures.

Because many investigations are confidential, the absence of a public prosecution in a jurisdiction does not prove the absence of crime, while one indictment does not establish permanent control of that place. Geography should be linked to specific dated operations, addresses in judicial records, and the limits of each investigation.

Hierarchy, Crews, and the Limits of Command

The standard American Mafia model includes a boss, underboss, consigliere, caporegimes or captains, inducted soldiers, and non-inducted associates. Captains usually supervise crews, but real operating arrangements vary across time and criminal family. A prominent figure may exercise authority through a proxy, a committee, or a temporary acting title when imprisoned or under investigation. [5][6][14]

In the Colombo case, the distinction between formal and practical leadership is fundamental. Profaci, Magliocco, Colombo, Persico, Orena, Alphonse Persico, Andrew Russo, and later figures entered the historical record under different circumstances. Some titles reflect adjudicated facts or admissions; others appear only in the government's charging description or media reporting.

Neither the presence of a title nor membership in a crew means that a person personally directed every crime associated with that group. Organizational responsibility should be established through orders, knowledge, benefit, financing, participation, and corroboration. General diagrams help orient readers but cannot substitute for evidence about a particular offense.

Federal Investigations and the 2000s

The 2000s saw substantial federal activity against Colombo defendants, including violent-crime cases spanning offenses from the 1990s. The 2007 Cutolo conviction and a 2008 superseding indictment concerning other cold cases documented how investigators used witnesses and reconstructed criminal events that had remained contested for years. An indictment, however, did not settle every homicide it identified. [10][20]

Theodore Persico Jr. separately pleaded guilty in 2005 to a racketeering offense, with the Justice Department describing extortion-related predicate conduct. This earlier conviction is relevant to his later public profile but should not be merged with his distinct participation in the 2021 family-administration case. [17]

In the broader historical sequence, these prosecutions disrupted particular leadership relationships yet left a continuing pattern of investigations. Such outcomes resist the familiar claim that organized crime can be eliminated permanently by a single conviction. New prosecutions instead require renewed proof of newly alleged conduct.

The 2011 Multi-Family Federal Takedown

On January 20, 2011, the Justice Department announced a major coordinated prosecution charging 91 leaders, members, and associates across multiple La Cosa Nostra families and federal districts. The charges included a wide range of racketeering acts, from violence to corruption and extortion. The Colombo-related indictment described a large group of defendants, including several people identified by prosecutors as serving in the family’s leadership. [6]

The release described Andrew Russo as a street boss, Benjamin Castellazzo as acting underboss, and Richard Fusco as consigliere at that time. Those descriptions are useful historical evidence of the government's understanding in 2011; they cannot be copied unchanged into a 2026 organization chart. [6]

The case also alleged violence associated with the 1990s internal war and the influence of specific labor organizations. These allegations belong to their procedural stage unless the later result of each defendant's case is traced. For the encyclopedia, a mass indictment is a starting point for case histories, not a substitute for individual adjudication.

The 2019 Staten Island Indictments

In October 2019, federal prosecutors announced three indictments charging 20 defendants, including persons described as Colombo members and associates, with offenses involving racketeering, extortion, loansharking, stalking, and attempted sports bribery. The cases centered in part on Staten Island and alleged activity beginning in 2014. Joseph Amato was identified in the release as an alleged Colombo captain. [13]

The procedural qualification is material. The charging announcement records what prosecutors alleged and why the cases drew federal attention; it does not itself establish that each defendant participated in every offense or that every specific count resulted in conviction. Unchecked publication of a charging narrative as a settled verdict would misrepresent the defendants’ legal status.

At an institutional level, the 2019 cases demonstrate that activity attributed to the Colombo name was not limited to the earlier high-profile leadership wars. The alleged enterprises involved money, coercion, and the use of identifiable criminal relationships in ordinary metropolitan settings.

2021 Indictment of the Colombo Administration

On September 14, 2021, the U.S. Attorney’s Office for the Eastern District of New York announced a 19-count indictment against 14 defendants, including Andrew “Mush” Russo, Benjamin “Benji” Castellazzo, Ralph DiMatteo, and several alleged captains. The government described a sustained effort to coerce a senior official at a Queens-based union and interfere with vendor selection for a health-care benefit fund. [14]

According to the indictment, a union official had been pressured for years to surrender a portion of his salary under threat of violence. The government also alleged that senior figures sought to extract more than $10,000 per month from the benefit plan by steering contracts through associated providers and intermediaries. The financial target was an alleged objective, not proof that the full amount was successfully diverted each month. [14]

Other allegations concerned extortionate loans, money laundering, false workplace-safety training certifications, and drug-trafficking activities involving particular defendants. The case also included a Bonanno-affiliated participant; that cross-family connection does not mean the Bonanno and Colombo organizations were legally or structurally identical. The indictment expressly stated that defendants were presumed innocent unless and until proven guilty. [14]

2023 Guilty Pleas and Leadership Disruption

By July 14, 2023, federal prosecutors reported guilty pleas from all 14 defendants charged in connection with the 2021–2022 Colombo-related proceedings. These included Castellazzo on money-laundering conspiracy, DiMatteo on racketeering, and captains Theodore Persico Jr., Richard Ferrara, and Vincent Ricciardo on racketeering offenses. Other defendants pleaded guilty to fraud or racketeering according to their respective roles. [15]

The announcement also recorded that Andrew Russo had died during the pendency of the case. His death is not a criminal conviction. A prosecution involving a deceased defendant must be distinguished from the proceedings against the other persons who entered pleas and received sentences. [15]

Prosecutors described the guilty pleas as a severe blow to the family’s administration. That evaluation is appropriately attributed to the U.S. Attorney's Office, not adopted as proof that the organization ceased to exist or that its remaining leadership arrangements were immediately known. Disruption and institutional extinction are different historical claims.

2024 Ricciardo Sentencing and Worker Harm

On February 28, 2024, Colombo captain Vincent “Vinny Unions” Ricciardo received a sentence of 51 months in prison, along with $350,000 forfeiture and $280,890 restitution. The sentence followed a July 2023 guilty plea to racketeering involving union extortion and other schemes. This is an adjudicated result, not merely a prosecutor's allegation. [16]

The criminal scheme harmed a union official and endangered resources linked to health benefits. The court-related account described pressure on contracts, financial extraction, loansharking, and the involvement of several defendants. Distinguishing forfeiture from restitution matters: the former addresses the surrender of proceeds or property under criminal-law rules, while the latter is designed to compensate specified victims for adjudicated losses.

Unlike popular Mafia stories that focus chiefly on leaders and assassinations, this proceeding reveals the effects of organized crime on routine institutions. Workers depend on union representation, retirement and health arrangements, and impartial vendor procurement. Criminal capture converts those legal protections into potential sources of exploitation.

2025–2026 Theodore Persico Jr. Proceedings

Later public reporting described Theodore “Skinny Teddy” Persico Jr., a nephew of Carmine Persico, as a possible or actual successor to senior Colombo leadership. In January 2026, courtroom reporting attributed to a federal prosecutor the assertion that Persico Jr. had taken control of the Colombo family. At the same appearance, Persico admitted violating supervised-release restrictions by associating with individuals he was prohibited from meeting. The fact of admitted meetings and the separate government assertion about being boss should not be conflated. [18]

A June 2026 legal-news synthesis reported that a federal judge imposed a nine-month sentence on February 11, 2026 for those supervised-release violations. Because that figure is taken from a secondary report rather than a judgment reproduced here, the sentence is reported with source attribution; the court docket remains the appropriate final authority for any correction or subsequent adjustment. [19]

These proceedings establish that federal authorities continued to scrutinize a senior Colombo-associated figure after the 2021 investigation. They do not independently provide a complete, current administration list, a verified family membership count, or proof that every encounter involved a new criminal conspiracy. Leadership claims are time-sensitive and should be updated only against better-documented public records.

Present-Day Status and Limitations of Public Records

As of the editorial cutoff of November 9, 2026, public records establish the continuing legal and investigative importance of the Colombo family name. The 2023 guilty pleas, 2024 sentencing, and 2026 supervised-release reporting are discrete, verifiable events. They should not be rearranged into an invented continuous history of every private meeting or every current crew. [15][16][18][19]

Neither reliable current membership totals nor a complete estimate of annual criminal receipts can be responsibly derived from published indictments. Definitions of member, soldier, associate, and facilitator differ, while confidential-source intelligence may remain sealed. Claims that the Colombos are precisely the “smallest,” “weakest,” or “most violent” family in 2026 generally lack an accepted current measurement that would make such rankings meaningful.

The appropriate editorial practice is to maintain a dated history of each identified leader and to use formulations such as “prosecutors identified,” “a federal jury found,” or “reporting described.” This approach permits substantive historical coverage without presenting rumor, unresolved allegations, or uncertain personal status as fact.

Principal Historical Figures

Joseph Profaci (1897–1962)
Early leader of the crime family that later became the Colombo organization. His long tenure ended at death, amid internal tensions. [1][2]
Joseph Magliocco (1898–1963)
Profaci's brother-in-law and short-serving successor whose association with Joseph Bonanno became central to the 1963 leadership change. [1][2]
Joseph Colombo (1923–1978)
Namesake and boss from 1963; prominent in the Italian-American Civil Rights League; shot in 1971 and died in 1978. [1][3]
Joseph “Crazy Joe” Gallo (1929–1972)
Dissident leader associated with the early 1960s challenges to Profaci; killed in Manhattan in 1972. [4]
Albert “Kid Blast” Gallo
Figure in the dissident Gallo faction, whose roles require differentiation from those of his brother Joseph.
Carmine “the Snake” Persico (1933–2019)
Long-term boss associated with the 1980s Commission prosecution and the Persico faction of the 1990s war. [2][7][12]
Victor “Little Vic” Orena
Acting leader and key figure in the rivalry with the Persico faction during the 1991–1993 Colombo war. [12]
Gregory Scarpa Sr. (1928–1994)
Colombo criminal participant and FBI informant; the handling of his information drew later Inspector General scrutiny. [12][22]
William “Wild Bill” Cutolo (1949–1999)
Senior Colombo figure murdered in 1999; the case resulted in later federal murder convictions and life sentences for two defendants. [10][11]
Alphonse “Allie Boy” Persico
Carmine Persico’s son, convicted with John DeRoss in the Cutolo killing and sentenced to life imprisonment. [10][11]
John “Jackie” DeRoss
Convicted alongside Alphonse Persico in the Cutolo murder and witness-tampering case. [10][11]
Thomas “Tommy Shots” Gioeli
Former acting boss convicted of racketeering conspiracy in 2012; sentenced in 2014. [7][8]
Dino Saracino
Colombo soldier convicted in the 2012 trial and sentenced to 50 years in 2014. [7][9]
Andrew “Mush” Russo (died 2022)
Senior figure described as street boss in 2011 and boss in the 2021 prosecution; died before resolution of charges against him. [6][14][15]
Benjamin “Benji” Castellazzo
Identified as underboss in the 2021 case; pleaded guilty to money-laundering conspiracy. [14][15]
Ralph DiMatteo
Identified as consigliere in the 2021 case; pleaded guilty to racketeering. [14][15]
Vincent “Vinny Unions” Ricciardo
Captain sentenced in 2024 after his racketeering plea involving extortion of a labor union official. [16]
Theodore “Skinny Teddy” Persico Jr.
Nephew of Carmine Persico; pleaded guilty in the 2021-related case, later admitted supervised-release violations, and was identified by prosecutors in 2026 reporting as a leading family figure. [15][17][18][19]

Name caution: Alphonse Persico, Carmine Persico, Theodore Persico Jr., and other persons with the same surname are not interchangeable. A historical or legal claim about one cannot automatically be extended to another.

Key Terminology

La Cosa Nostra (LCN)
U.S. federal terminology for the traditional Italian-American Mafia network.
Crime family
Organized criminal enterprise, not every biological relative of an offender.
Boss / acting boss
Senior leader or temporary administrator; the distinction matters when an imprisoned nominal boss is represented by another figure.
Underboss
Senior subordinate leader or second-in-command.
Consigliere
Senior adviser or mediator within a Mafia family.
Caporegime (capo)
Captain who supervises an operating crew.
Soldier / made member
Inducted participant in a family’s internal membership system.
Associate
Connected individual who is not necessarily formally inducted.
Commission
Historical coordinating body among Mafia organizations.
RICO
Federal law permitting prosecution of enterprise-associated patterns of racketeering activity.
Omertà
Traditionally invoked code of secrecy and resistance to cooperation.
Indictment
Formal accusation, not a finding of guilt.
Guilty plea
A defendant’s admission in judicial proceedings, subject to the particular offense and court record.
Sentencing finding
Court determination for sentencing purposes, not necessarily a separate conviction by a jury.

Research Method and Evidentiary Standards

This article prioritizes court judgments, verdict summaries, official prosecution records, and government historical investigations over unattributed recollections. Historical overview sources are used for continuity and context, while statements about a recent defendant’s conduct are tied to that person’s case. Every source citation is associated with a numbered entry in the register below.

Procedural distinctions: an investigation or indictment identifies suspicion and charges; a guilty plea establishes the admitted offense; a jury conviction establishes the offenses of conviction; a sentence states the penalty and may discuss other findings under differing burdens. Acquittals and dismissals must not be omitted when the same defendant faced unsuccessful charges. The 2012 Gioeli–Saracino trial is a particularly important example of mixed verdicts. [7]

Limitations: publicly accessible records do not resolve every historical murder, interpersonal relationship, jurisdictional disagreement, or disputed leadership appointment. This reference does not reproduce original court transcripts in full, test all secondary-source genealogies, or claim access to confidential law-enforcement intelligence. Information is current only through the stated editorial cutoff.
