1. Historical Overview

Alphonse Gabriel “Al” Capone led the most publicly visible phase of Chicago’s Prohibition-era criminal syndicate. The organization inherited gambling, vice and protection networks connected to James Colosimo and Johnny Torrio, then expanded through illegal alcohol distribution and influence over municipal institutions. Capone’s direct leadership is usually dated from Torrio’s withdrawal in 1925 until the federal proceedings that removed Capone from operations in 1931–32.

Capone became a national symbol of organized crime, but public fame is not a substitute for a criminal judgment. The 1929 massacre and numerous gangland murders were attributed to his circle without a homicide conviction against him. His definitive federal conviction consisted of three felony income-tax-evasion counts and two misdemeanor failures to file tax returns, affirmed on appeal in 1932.[1][2][5][6][10]

2. Name, Birth, and Family Background

Capone was born January 17, 1899, in Brooklyn, New York, to a family of Italian immigrants. Scholarly biography identifies his parents as Gabriele Capone, who worked as a barber, and Teresa Raiola. He entered adolescence during the growth of New York’s densely populated immigrant districts, where neighborhood affiliations, wage labor, saloons and street gangs overlapped.

The familiar surname, birth date and place are well supported in scholarly and museum reference works. This page has not independently compared a certified birth entry, parish material, every census appearance and all subsequent surname variants. His family’s ethnicity explains part of his social environment but does not explain or excuse his later choices or the experiences of other Italian American migrants.[5][9][10]

3. Brooklyn Childhood and Schooling

Raised in working-class Brooklyn, Capone attended school for only part of his childhood and entered jobs and local street life as a young teenager. The FBI summary states that he left school after sixth grade, while other biographies describe expulsion or departure around age fourteen. Working-class neighborhood life offered both regular employment and recruitment channels into street violence and small criminal enterprises.

Exact dates for school departure, early jobs and juvenile gang status depend primarily on later biography rather than a surviving school register in this edition. Chronology should not turn a retrospective anecdote into an adjudicated record. This period matters chiefly because it established the contacts through which Capone later joined Torrio’s network.[5][9][10]

4. Five Points and Early Criminal Associations

Later accounts connect Capone to New York gang leaders including Johnny Torrio and Frankie Yale, along with the wider Five Points and James Street environments. These networks did not function as a single contemporary corporate hierarchy: street crews, gambling businesses and neighborhood political intermediaries formed changing relationships.

Some FBI retrospectives associate future New York figures such as Lucky Luciano with this milieu. Such association is not proof that all later Mafia leaders belonged to the same named gang at the same time. This profile emphasizes the specific link to Torrio, which became critical to Capone’s Chicago career.[5][9][10]

5. Coney Island Work and Facial Scars

Capone worked around New York nightspots and Coney Island hospitality businesses as a young man. Biographers recount that his facial scars came from a knife attack during a dispute at a venue where he worked, conventionally dated around 1917. The scars contributed to the nickname “Scarface,” although accounts report that he disliked the label.

Details of the altercation, the assailant’s exact name and the words allegedly exchanged vary among later narratives. The injuries and public nickname are well established; a verbatim reconstruction of the argument is not. “Scarface” should be treated as media shorthand rather than a description that carries a legal accusation.[9][10]

6. Marriage, Mae Capone, and Their Son

In 1918 Capone married Mae Coughlin, and they raised their son, Albert Francis “Sonny” Capone. Family life and criminal management coexisted across his years in New York, Chicago, Cicero and Florida. His household later remained important while he faced imprisonment and debilitating illness.

Popular biographies routinely romanticize a private family figure to offset the violence of public business. Such contrast is inadequate evidence of either private tenderness or private cruelty in specific incidents. No complete family correspondence, marriage file or household financial ledger was collated for this edition.[9][10][15]

7. From New York to Chicago

Capone followed Johnny Torrio into the orbit of Chicago’s James Colosimo organization at approximately the beginning of Prohibition. The FBI dates his Chicago association to about 1920. Historical accounts place his duties within the city’s vice trade, gambling, security and illicit brewing distribution, rather than identifying a single unchanging office.

Common timelines variously cite 1919 and 1920 for his arrival, reflecting different definitions of a preliminary visit, permanent relocation or entry into Torrio’s operation. This entry uses “about 1920” absent a fully collated itinerary. What is clear is that his Chicago prominence developed rapidly after Colosimo’s death.[5][10][11]

8. James Colosimo and the Antecedent Syndicate

Before Prohibition, James “Big Jim” Colosimo’s Chicago enterprise operated amid First Ward gambling, prostitution and protection rackets. Capone arrived in a system that already had business contacts and relationships with political actors. Colosimo was murdered on May 11, 1920, and Torrio became the principal organizer of its expanding criminal operations.

Claims that Capone personally arranged Colosimo’s killing, or that Torrio’s precise decision can be reconstructed from later gangster stories, are not established in a homicide judgment. The transition should be analyzed as an organizational succession following an unsolved killing, not an officially recorded succession vote.[5][10][11]

9. Torrio’s Operational Apprenticeship

Torrio used alliances, territorial negotiation and bootlegging supply arrangements to reorganize the enterprise during the early 1920s. Capone handled increasingly important work under him, including local enforcement and relations with operators around saloons, gambling and breweries. The connection was a leadership relationship rather than a simple mentor-and-pupil legend.

Later popular accounts exaggerate the neatness of a single master plan. Changes in liquor law, neighboring gangs, corrupt police protection and market demand all shaped events. Capone’s emergence was possible because these institutions existed, and not simply because Torrio identified a talented subordinate.[5][11][12][21]

10. The Economics of National Prohibition

The Eighteenth Amendment and Volstead Act prohibited commercial beverage-alcohol production and distribution within a legal framework that faced substantial public resistance. Chicago’s beer supply chains, illicit distilleries, transport routes, gambling and entertainment venues generated revenue and opportunities for coercion. Capone’s organization grew by integrating operations and enforcing market access.

Revenue figures frequently described as $100 million annually are estimates based on retrospectives, not a complete audited statement of Capone-owned businesses. The supply network involved separate owners, allied operators and independent contractors. The economic scale matters, but precision greater than the evidence permits should not be advertised.[6][10][12][21]

11. Beer, Distribution, and Territorial Arrangements

Illegal beer production required materials, working plants, storage, trucking, customers and protection from raids or rivals. Torrio-era agreements sought to allocate profitable territories, and Capone continued to use negotiated arrangements alongside violence. The North Side and South Side organizations fought over the same commercial advantages.

“Territory” could mean a cluster of saloons, access to particular distributors, protection relations or neighborhoods, not a colored map with universally respected boundaries. Successive alliances broke down when market conditions shifted. A static notion of a single Chicago monopoly misrepresents the changing network.[11][12][21]

12. The Four Deuces and South Side Bases

Chicago histories connect Torrio and Capone to the Four Deuces at 2222 South Wabash Avenue and other commercial addresses linked to their early operations. These venues offered spaces for social meetings, businesses and interactions with local intermediaries. The city’s geography mattered because entertainment corridors and transport connections determined opportunities.

A named address is not proof that every reputed criminal meeting or violent act occurred in that building. This edition does not supply an exhaustive property-deed or permit registry. Students should cross-check contemporary directory listings and photographs when mapping the changing Chicago network.[10][11][12]

13. City Hall, Police, and Political Protection

Capone’s organization depended upon unequal enforcement and contacts with officials willing to provide information, access or protection in return for payoffs. Chicago municipal reform, mayoral politics and local ward organization repeatedly reshaped the feasibility of rackets. Government corruption was not merely background; it influenced which businesses could operate openly.

Public accusations of influence are not evidence that every named official was personally bribed. Historical work must distinguish identified witnesses, indictments, successful prosecutions and the broader reputation of Chicago political machinery. The harm includes compromised administration and unequal protection for ordinary residents and businesses.[5][11][12][13]

14. William Dever and Enforcement Pressure

After reform mayor William Dever entered office in 1923, city enforcement made some Chicago operations less secure. The Torrio–Capone group increasingly concentrated parts of its organization in Cicero, a suburb outside the city. The location offered gambling venues, accommodations and political opportunities that a large syndicate could try to influence.

The shift did not mean the disappearance of Chicago activity: associates, transport routes and revenue flowed across municipal borders. Different contemporary accounts date phases of the move differently. The key distinction is between an administrative headquarters and the wider geography of a decentralized criminal enterprise.[11][12][13]

15. Cicero and the 1924 Election

During Cicero’s April 1924 local election, violence and intimidation involving Capone-aligned gunmen were reported around polling places and political competition. Capone’s brother Frank was shot and killed amid clashes involving law-enforcement personnel. The episode illustrates how organized-crime coercion extended into electoral administration and civil rights.

Individual responsibility for every attack should not be inferred from the family relationship or group label. Local-historical reporting provides the broad political and violent setting, but complete police and coroner files have not been assembled here. The electoral episode should not be reduced to a glamorous anecdote about gangland control.[12][13]

16. The Hawthorne Hotel and Public Headquarters

Capone became associated with the Hawthorne Hotel in Cicero, which served as a well-known base during the 1920s. Its public visibility created both organizational convenience and vulnerability: employees, visitors, rival gunmen and reporters could track who came and went. The hotel became emblematic of the difference between open notoriety and legal accountability.

Accounts of reinforced shutters and protective arrangements appear in local documentaries. Those measures help explain responses to assassination threats, but they should not be interpreted as a complete security blueprint or evidence about every meeting held on the premises.[11][12][13]

17. Torrio’s 1925 Shooting and Retirement

Johnny Torrio survived an attack in January 1925, and his departure from active Chicago leadership created the opening for Capone to take control. The FBI and local historians identify 1925 as the core succession year. Capone then became the recognizable leader of the organization that contemporary observers associated with Cicero and Chicago bootlegging.

“Retirement” did not necessarily extinguish Torrio’s relationships or influence. Conversely, his later contacts do not erase Capone’s operational control. The specific degree of continuing Torrio direction varies by account; this page characterizes the transfer as practical leadership rather than legal title.[5][10][11]

18. Leadership Style and Delegated Authority

Capone’s operation mixed personal notoriety with delegated work: Jake Guzik is often associated with financial and payoff administration, while figures such as Frank Nitti, Louis Campagna and Jack McGurn appear in accounts of enforcement and business control. Such delegation enabled influence across businesses and neighborhoods during the middle and late 1920s.

The form of a mature Mafia rank system should not be applied mechanically to the 1925 Outfit. Contemporary roles could be fluid, and later biographies describe different chains of command. This collection therefore gives each individual a separate entry instead of collapsing the entire organization into Capone’s personal conduct.[5][10][13]

19. Frank Nitti, Jake Guzik, and Financial Management

Capone relied on people who managed collections, cash distribution and relationships with other operators. Jake “Greasy Thumb” Guzik became a prominent associate associated with money and political arrangements, while Frank Nitti developed a position that proved important after Capone’s imprisonment.

Claims about exact percentages, employment contracts and payments must be tested against financial records rather than repeated as operational facts. The absence of a clean payroll record is itself revealing: illicit activity often used cash, intermediaries and nominally legitimate entities.[5][10][13]

20. North Side Conflict and Dean O’Banion

The confrontation with Chicago’s North Side Gang grew from competition over beer distribution, territories and disputed agreements during Torrio’s and Capone’s era. Dean O’Banion was killed in November 1924, escalating rivalry among the city’s principal groups. The killing occurred before Capone’s recognized succession as head of Torrio’s organization.

The murder is frequently attributed to participants aligned with the southern organization. That attribution is historically significant but is not equivalent to a murder conviction against Capone. A serious history identifies the contested perpetrator question rather than making later gangland memoirs function as a jury verdict.[11][16]

21. Hymie Weiss, George Moran, and Escalating War

After O’Banion’s death, Hymie Weiss and later George “Bugs” Moran became central figures in North Side opposition to Capone’s group. Negotiations, attempted killings and retaliatory attacks moved the conflict beyond isolated business disputes. Chicago’s public reputation increasingly centered on gunfire, police failures and national coverage.

Associations changed over time; “North Side Gang” was not a uniform population with fixed membership. Retrospective murder lists can include competing suspect theories. Each violent event requires its own evidence and legal-status assessment rather than guilt assigned automatically to a syndicate leader.[10][11][16]

22. The McSwiggin Killing and Public Scrutiny

In 1926, Chicago prosecutor William McSwiggin was killed alongside other men during a gangland shooting. The episode drew intense public and official attention to Capone’s circle because observers suspected connections between organized crime and law enforcement. It formed part of a broader period in which alleged links to public authority deepened distrust.

Accounts describe the prosecutor’s death as unintended within an attack aimed at others, but that motive remains a reconstruction. Capone was not convicted of murdering McSwiggin. The distinction between an event’s occurrence, police suspicion, witness evidence and court outcome is essential.[10][11][13]

23. The 1926 Hawthorne Hotel Attack

In September 1926, gunmen affiliated in historical accounts with the North Side group attacked the Hawthorne Hotel in Cicero. Local historical reporting describes extensive automatic-weapons fire and Capone’s survival. The shooting showed how rivals could threaten a highly visible base without solving the underlying territorial conflicts.

Specific bullet totals such as “one thousand rounds” occur in retrospective research and should not be treated as a personally audited ballistic inventory in this biography. More consequential is the documented escalation: public places became stages for violence that endangered people beyond the principal criminal factions.[11][13]

24. The Murder of Hymie Weiss

Hymie Weiss was shot dead in October 1926 outside the Holy Name Cathedral area, and the killing altered leadership in the North Side organization. Contemporary investigations and later histories linked the murder to the continuing war between the rival groups. The violence preceded the 1929 massacre by more than two years.

Retrospective accounts may assert who approved or executed the shooting, but no murder conviction of Capone is demonstrated here. The correct scholarly language separates the murdered victim and date from the disputed identification of the organizers.[11][16]

25. Machine Guns and a New Public Image of Violence

By the later 1920s automatic weapons, cars and fast-moving attackers had become part of the public image of Chicago gang warfare. Changes in mobility and weaponry transformed isolated confrontations into mass-casualty events, sometimes in crowded commercial districts. Capone’s reputation grew partly through images and accounts of such attacks.

Media emphasis on technology risks removing victims, civilians and enforcement institutions from the analysis. Firearms and vehicles enabled violence but did not make it inevitable: profit disputes, corrupt protection and decisions by individual participants still shaped attacks.[11][13][17]

26. Publicity, Press Access, and “Scarface”

Capone was unusually accessible to reporters for a prominent criminal figure and cultivated an image of confidence, wealth and concern for Chicagoans. Newspaper photographs, interviews and attention to expensive clothing made him a celebrity in ways that both fed and complicated public efforts to prosecute him.

The label “Scarface” came from his facial injuries and was widely used in headlines. Public performance is not a transparent autobiography: statements made to journalists could be self-serving. The distinction between personal image and independently established action should guide any interpretation of his public remarks.[9][10][11]

27. Estimates of Wealth and Business Reach

Capone’s reputed enormous wealth derived from claims about illegal beer, liquor distribution, gambling, prostitution and protection income. Published estimates often describe yearly syndicate revenues in the tens or hundreds of millions of 1920s dollars. They reveal perceived scale but not a reconciled corporate balance sheet.

Syndicate revenue differs from individual income, cash receipts differ from net profits, and a tax indictment may allege figures that a jury did not accept as a full amount. This profile does not convert headline estimates into precise earnings, net worth or present-value comparisons.[6][10][21]

28. The 1927 Tax-Law Context

United States federal income tax applied to income from illegal activity, a legal principle that became crucial to organized-crime prosecutions. The 1927 Supreme Court ruling United States v. Sullivan is an important background decision establishing that gains from illicit liquor transactions could not simply be excluded from federal tax rules.

This legal development did not automatically convict Capone. Government investigators still had to establish income, wilful conduct and offense-specific elements with admissible evidence. The 1931 verdict and 1932 appeal should be studied alongside tax doctrine rather than replaced by a simplistic story about clever bookkeeping.[1][6][7]

29. Palm Island and the Florida Residence

Capone acquired a residence on Palm Island near Miami Beach, Florida, in the late 1920s, establishing a conspicuous second home while authorities in Florida criticized his presence. His travel between Chicago and Florida mattered in several proceedings, including the timing of a 1929 federal subpoena and his presence away from Chicago during the massacre.

Ownership and occupancy are different from direct knowledge of every distant operation. His Florida location in February 1929 is not proof of innocence in an alleged conspiracy; it is relevant to the question of personal participation at the crime scene. Nor is his residence by itself proof of a particular amount of taxable income.[5][10][15]

30. February 14, 1929: Seven Murdered

On February 14, 1929, seven men associated with the North Side group were shot dead in a Chicago garage after attackers used the appearance of a police operation to control the victims. The event, known as the St. Valentine’s Day Massacre, became the defining symbol of Prohibition-era gang violence and heightened demands for federal and local action.

Investigators and historians have frequently linked the operation to Capone’s organization, but Capone was in Florida and was never convicted of ordering or carrying out the murders. The distinction is not a denial of credible suspicion; it is an accurate statement of the legal record and the limits of historical proof.[5][10][11][17]

31. Witness Accounts, Suspects, and the Massacre Record

Accounts of the massacre describe men disguised as police, forced lineup of victims and heavy gunfire. The weapon evidence, the failed investigation and identification of potential participants have generated considerable historical argument. The garage scene and deaths are documented; the full planning chain has not been established by a murder conviction against Capone.

It is inappropriate to present a single later narrative as direct testimony from all participants. Police theory, forensic findings, press reporting and retrospective books should appear as separate evidence types. No alleged victim should be erased by describing the event merely as a strategic victory over a rival syndicate.[10][11][17]

32. Capone’s Location During the Massacre

Historical records place Capone in the Miami area around the time of the February 1929 killings, and he was questioned in an unrelated investigation. That location became central to debates about whether he directed the attack through intermediaries or simply benefited from the weakening of an opposing group.

An alibi to personal presence does not resolve allegations of a remote order, and a suspected remote order is not proven because Capone led a rival organization. This profile neither exonerates nor convicts him in the unsolved homicide case; it reports the documentary distinction.[5][10][15][17]

33. The 1929 Federal Grand-Jury Subpoena

In February and March 1929 federal authorities sought Capone’s appearance before a Chicago grand jury. He requested postponement on health grounds, submitting a medical affidavit, while agents documented travel and activities inconsistent with his claimed inability to appear. The inquiry produced a contempt-of-court proceeding.

The federal Bureau’s early involvement was connected to this court matter, not a general FBI murder investigation into all Chicago gang warfare. Its jurisdiction at that period was narrower than modern federal organized-crime jurisdiction. The case provides a useful example of how investigators used a concrete federal offense.[5][6]

34. Philadelphia Concealed-Weapons Arrest

In May 1929 Philadelphia police arrested Capone and a bodyguard on concealed-weapon charges. Capone received a one-year sentence in Pennsylvania, serving approximately nine months before release in March 1930. That criminal punishment is distinct from the later Chicago tax conviction and from the outstanding 1929 federal contempt issue.

Popular biographies sometimes combine this prison time with the federal tax sentence or suggest the Pennsylvania weapons arrest ended his syndicate leadership permanently. It did neither. The legal record should track separate sovereign jurisdictions, sentencing dates and actual time served.[5][10][15]

35. The 1930 Public-Enemy Designation

The Chicago Crime Commission labeled Capone “Public Enemy No. 1” in 1930, reflecting civic attention to organized crime and growing dissatisfaction with conventional enforcement. The phrase became a major press identifier and helped transform a Chicago criminal figure into a nationwide example of government incapacity and public fascination.

This designation was not a conviction and should not be confused with a later FBI Most Wanted classification. The Crime Commission was a private civic organization rather than a sentencing tribunal. Its choice reveals the politics of crime control but does not itself prove each criminal allegation directed at Capone.[10][22]

36. The Soup Kitchen and Selective Philanthropy

During the Great Depression, a soup kitchen at 935 South State Street was publicized as financed by Capone. The Library of Congress catalogs a photograph dated November 18, 1930 with that identification. The episode became part of his carefully managed public reputation amid deprivation and high unemployment.

The Library notes that older catalog captions may be unverified, and the photograph alone does not establish how much Capone funded, for how long, or why. A food line can help people materially without cancelling exploitation, bribery or violence. This section treats the record as social history, not redemption narrative.[14][10][13]

37. Treasury’s Investigative Strategy

While the public focused on shootings and illegal liquor, federal Treasury investigators traced income, expenditure and financial records. Frank J. Wilson and other agents worked under federal tax-investigation leadership, piecing together gambling receipts, witnesses and expenditure evidence that could establish taxable earnings despite a cash-intensive business structure.

Treasury investigators, Bureau agents and Prohibition enforcement personnel had distinct legal powers. Later film accounts often merge them. The 1933 Wilson report and the appellate court’s count-by-count analysis provide stronger evidence for the tax conviction than a story attributing the whole result to one famous lawman.[1][3][4][6][7]

38. Eliot Ness and the Untouchables: A Different Case

Eliot Ness and his federal team investigated illicit liquor operations associated with the Capone network, contributing to enforcement pressure. Their work matters to the history of Prohibition but was not the evidentiary foundation of the five tax counts decided by the 1931 jury.

The dramatized notion that Ness personally jailed Capone for tax evasion obscures the roles of Treasury special agents, witnesses, prosecutors and the court. Source criticism here requires separating Prohibition investigations, court subpoenas and income-tax proof, even where the same criminal organization was involved.[5][6][7][10]

39. Income Tax Indictments in 1931

Federal prosecutors brought two consolidated criminal tax indictments against Capone in 1931. The Seventh Circuit later explained that one indictment contained a single felony count and the other contained 22 counts. These allegations addressed tax evasion over specified years and failures to file returns, rather than presenting a general racketeering or murder prosecution.

Indictment counts are accusations. The existence of 23 charged counts does not mean the jury convicted Capone of 23 offenses. The decision identified exactly five guilty findings within the second indictment and acquittal on the remaining counts, including the separate one-count indictment.[1][2][4]

40. The Withdrawn June 1931 Guilty Plea

Capone initially offered a guilty plea in June 1931 in the expectation of a more favorable sentencing outcome. Federal Judge James H. Wilkerson made clear that the court was not bound by the arrangement discussed by the parties. Capone then changed his plea, requiring the government to proceed to a contested trial.

A prosecutor’s recommended disposition did not become a binding two-and-a-half-year sentence. The plea reversal is important because later retellings sometimes describe a deal that already existed as a completed court judgment. Plea discussions and the final October conviction must be kept separate.[5][6][22]

41. Preparing for Trial: Witnesses and Records

The tax prosecution reconstructed earnings without relying on a complete conventional accounting system. Investigators used business contacts, gambling records and Capone’s expenditures to challenge denials of taxable income. Evidence of wealth was relevant only insofar as the government connected it to required returns and statutory charges.

Tax investigators’ allegations about gross income are not synonymous with final assessed tax, and trial exhibits cannot be reduced to a single headline number. An exhaustive financial reconstruction would require comparison of original tax computations, trial exhibits and underlying businesses across multiple years.[1][3][4][7]

42. Jury Tampering Concerns and Judicial Safeguards

Federal Judicial Center and National Archives accounts describe information that prospective jurors may have been targeted for improper influence. Judge Wilkerson responded by substituting a different jury pool immediately before trial. The episode is frequently cited as evidence of the challenges surrounding a nationally publicized prosecution of a notorious defendant.

The historic suspicion of attempted jury tampering should not become a claim that Capone was separately convicted of bribing the jury in this proceeding. Court safeguards and adverse allegations have different procedural significance. This biography uses institutional summaries rather than inventing a verbatim sidebar transcript.[4][6][22]

43. The October 1931 Tax Trial

The federal trial took place in Chicago under Judge James H. Wilkerson, with prosecutors presenting their evidence to a jury in October 1931. It was one of the best-known criminal tax cases of the Prohibition era, demonstrating that prosecutors could pursue legally provable revenue offenses where other charges had failed to produce a lasting conviction.

Courtroom drama is secondary to the legal questions: what tax was due, whether income was concealed, whether required returns were omitted and whether the conduct was willful under the applicable statutes. The 1932 appellate opinion, not retrospective film dialogue, is the principal case-level guide for this edition.[1][2][6]

44. October 17, 1931: Verdict on Five Counts

The jury convicted Capone on five counts: three felonies of willfully attempting to evade income taxes for 1925, 1926 and 1927, and two misdemeanors for failing to file returns for 1928 and 1929. The Seventh Circuit identified the precise convicted counts as 1, 5, 9, 13 and 18 of the second indictment.

The Treasury’s retrospective official report and an October 18 contemporary newspaper account both place the verdict on October 17. Some FBI and later Archives summaries use October 18, apparently confusing the date of reporting and the event. This entry foregrounds October 17 and records the competing summary chronology in the evidence register.[1][2][3][4][18][19]

45. Acquittals and the Limits of the Verdict

Capone was acquitted on the remaining counts of the 22-count indictment and on the separate one-count indictment. That detail matters: the government obtained a major conviction, but the jury did not adopt every charge the prosecution had filed. In later memory the five guilty counts are sometimes inaccurately summarized as blanket convictions for racketeering.

The court’s published appellate opinion is decisive for count identification. The jury was not asked in this tax case to decide whether Capone ordered the 1929 massacre or committed dozens of unrelated killings. Accurate legal biography should never inflate the breadth of a verdict to support claims beyond the charged and decided offenses.[1][2]

46. October 24, 1931: Eleven-Year Sentence

On October 24, 1931, Judge Wilkerson imposed an aggregate sentence commonly described as eleven years, including a combination of felony terms and one-year misdemeanor terms. Capone also received a $50,000 fine and costs, with tax liabilities and interest addressed separately. Contemporary reporting and Wilson’s 1933 Treasury report support the October 24 date.

Some widely circulated institutional summaries instead place sentencing in November 1931. This profile identifies the discrepancy instead of silently choosing a date: the contemporaneous October 24 newspaper and Wilson report are more specific on event chronology. The original sentence should not be confused with years ultimately served after credits and custody adjustments.[1][3][4][5][7][20]

47. Money: Fine, Back Taxes, and Court Costs

The sentence included a $50,000 fine. Agency summaries cite approximately $215,000 in back taxes plus interest and additional court costs. These are separate financial categories, and their totals need not match numbers describing gross income, individual tax years or the estimated revenue of the entire Capone organization.

Federal records and retrospectives show different approximations for costs and assessed liabilities. This edition does not combine all figures into a single exact dollar amount without an original payment and assessment ledger. The $50,000 penal fine is more firmly established than every later cost estimate.[3][4][5][7]

48. The Seventh Circuit Appeal of 1932

Capone appealed, arguing defects in the tax indictments and related procedural questions. On February 27, 1932, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit affirmed the judgment in Capone v. United States, 56 F.2d 927. The opinion is a valuable primary source because it explains statutes, tax years, charged counts and the limited conviction.

The appeal did not convert the tax prosecution into a homicide finding. It addressed legal sufficiency and procedural objections arising from the income-tax case. Students should compare the opinion to the National Archives verdict rather than relying solely on short narrative encyclopedias.[1][2][3]

49. Atlanta Federal Penitentiary, 1932–1934

After his appeal failed, Capone entered the federal penitentiary at Atlanta in May 1932. He was no longer able to direct the Chicago organization openly as before, and other senior figures became more important in ongoing operations. The incarceration marked an institutional turning point regardless of whether individual associates continued to communicate with him.

Imprisonment does not provide a simple legal proof of every successor’s rank. Accounts of preferential conditions and informal influence vary, and prison anecdotes require custody records. The basic transfer and incarceration dates are more secure than stories about everyday privileges.[3][5][8][10]

50. Transfer to Alcatraz, August 1934

In August 1934 Capone was transferred to the new federal penitentiary at Alcatraz, where he was known as inmate 85. The prison’s strict regime sharply constrained his public life. National Park Service research describes work assignments, discipline and the deterioration of his health over the following years.

The prison’s fame often overwhelms the chronology of the actual case. Alcatraz was a custodial phase of a sentence imposed by a Chicago tax court, not the jurisdiction that convicted him. Photographs and souvenir narratives should not be treated as substitutes for medical and transfer records.[5][8][10]

51. Daily Prison Life and Limits of Privilege

Accounts of Capone’s Alcatraz years include ordinary work details, disciplinary episodes, and attempts to negotiate accommodations within a tightly controlled institution. The contrast with his prior wealth and visibility became a staple of public reporting. His ability to command the Chicago criminal world was substantially reduced by confinement.

One prison memoir cannot establish every episode, and public accounts of fights have different levels of documentation. The National Park Service resource study offers a research-based overview but does not authorize invented personal dialogue or a complete psychological portrait.[8][10]

52. Health Decline and Medical Care

During imprisonment Capone suffered serious neurological deterioration associated with late syphilis. Medical assessments and prison histories describe paresis and the impact on cognition, function and independence. His health made a return to the leadership position he had occupied during the 1920s increasingly implausible.

This is a documented historical medical course, not an invitation to speculate about character, intelligence or retrospective symptom-by-symptom diagnosis. Sources vary in technical detail, and patient privacy and records limitations warrant restraint. Causal explanations should be tied to institutional historical accounts rather than sensational claims.[5][8][10][15]

53. Terminal Island and Release in 1939

Capone was transferred away from Alcatraz in 1939, with National Park Service accounts identifying custody at Terminal Island and subsequent release processing. The FBI’s public case history gives November 16, 1939 as his release date after approximately seven years and six months served on the federal term and related confinement.

Public sources sometimes state different durations because they count only Alcatraz, only the tax sentence, or separate state and federal custody episodes. The release date and the original eleven-year sentence answer different questions. This profile uses the FBI date while identifying any subsidiary transfer and calculation differences.[5][8][10]

54. Treatment and Return to Florida

Following release Capone underwent medical care in the Baltimore area and moved back to the family home in Florida. By then the public role that had made him a defining Prohibition-era figure had ended. His remaining years were shaped primarily by illness and family circumstances.

Claims that he secretly resumed control of the Chicago Outfit after 1939 lack the same judicial documentation as his 1925–31 leadership. The organization itself continued, but continuity of the criminal network is not evidence of Capone’s continued personal operational command.[5][8][10][15]

55. Death on January 25, 1947

Capone died on January 25, 1947, at his Palm Island home in Florida, aged 48. He had been removed from public criminal leadership for more than fifteen years. His death ended a comparatively short life with an outsized place in U.S. criminal and cultural history.

Press accounts differ in the emphasis they place on the immediate final medical event and longer illness. The date, place and age are firmly supported by multiple institutional reference works; detailed bedside reconstruction is unnecessary unless an original death record and medical file are compared.[5][9][10][15]

56. Succession: Frank Nitti, Paul Ricca, and Tony Accardo

After Capone’s prosecution and imprisonment, figures including Frank Nitti, Paul Ricca and Tony Accardo shaped the continued organization. Later histories of the Chicago Outfit should distinguish formal public leadership, behind-the-scenes influence and the changing hierarchy during different decades.

The Outfit did not cease to exist when Capone entered prison, and its later leadership should not be projected backward into a seamless command chart. Separate MGU biographies will examine the evidence for each successor rather than treating popular “boss lists” as judicially certified appointments.[5][10][11]

57. The St. Valentine’s Day Massacre in Historical Memory

The 1929 murders remain closely associated with Capone in film, museum interpretation and civic memory. Such historical association helps explain the federal government’s renewed determination to pursue strong charges, but it does not establish that the massacre formed part of his convicted offenses.

When describing the killings, the correct method is to report the seven deaths, the suspicious circumstances, the contemporary investigative failures, and the attributed theories of responsibility. This preserves the central human loss while avoiding a fabricated murder verdict.[5][10][17]

58. Popular Culture: Scarface and The Untouchables

Capone’s image strongly influenced American gangster films, newspaper archetypes and later dramatizations such as Scarface and The Untouchables. These portrayals often collapse years of criminal activity and merge investigators, allies and rivals into story-friendly characters. Their cultural reach is an important historical phenomenon in its own right.

A film scene is not an archival source for a historical event. The tax investigators’ work, the chronology of Torrio’s retirement, and the prosecution’s count-by-count result are more reliably reconstructed from judicial and institutional records. Film belongs in reception history, not the evidence category for a criminal conviction.[6][9][10]

59. Organized Crime, Urban Government, and Public Harm

Capone’s career offers a case study in how illicit markets, bribery, urban administration, political coercion and weapons violence can reinforce one another. The consequences included murdered victims, threatened businesses, weakened public institutions and distrust of law enforcement. Prohibition created opportunity but did not absolve operators of their individual decisions.

This interpretation avoids treating Capone as either a criminal genius to celebrate or a singular cause of Chicago corruption. Other syndicates, public officials, municipal law, consumer demand and economic inequality were part of the system. Historical analysis can be rigorous without glamorizing coercion.[6][11][12][21]

60. Court Findings Versus Alleged Murders

The federal tax convictions are established by the 1931 verdict and 1932 appellate ruling. The 1929 Pennsylvania weapons conviction and 1931 contempt proceeding are separate legal events. Conversely, widely circulated claims that Capone ordered particular murders are historical and prosecutorial allegations unless supported by a specific adjudication.

Conviction on one criminal offense is not evidence of conviction on another. Readers should be able to identify what a jury decided, what an agency suspected, what a historian inferred and what remains unknown. The evidence register below applies that classification to the major claims in the biography.[1][2][5][10][17]

61. The Problem of Conflicting Official Dates

Two federal retrospective summaries commonly repeat October 18 as verdict date or November 24 as the sentencing date. But the 1933 Treasury Intelligence Unit account places verdict on October 17 and sentence on October 24, a sequence also corroborated by contemporary Associated Press reporting published on October 18 and October 24.

The appellate opinion proves the count disposition and appeal date independent of the one-day discrepancy. This entry uses the primary-and-contemporary chronology, discloses the inconsistent later institutional summaries, and declines to invent a reconciliation where the underlying editorial error cannot be demonstrated directly.[1][2][3][4][5][19][20]

62. Historical Legacy and MGU Collection Placement

Capone is the third biography in this Chicago Outfit collection, following James Colosimo and Johnny Torrio. His leadership phase explains the organization’s expansion, greater national prominence, and the eventual shift from violent rivalries to a landmark federal tax prosecution. Later profiles of financial administrators, enforcers and successors trace the organization beyond his imprisonment.

Institutional continuity should not erase differences among the people involved. This biography is an evidence-based reference entry rather than a narrative that makes every major Chicago crime Capone’s personal act. It privileges verifiable milestones and provides source windows for further archival research.[1][2][5][10][11]

Documentary Research Windows

These are editorial documentary research windows, not quotations invented from archival manuscripts or purported complete trial transcripts. Each points to a checkable record and explains its evidentiary limit.

The five tax verdict counts: read the 1932 appellate opinion

Read the opening of Capone v. United States. Build a chart of tax years 1925, 1926, 1927, 1928 and 1929 and distinguish felony evasion from misdemeanor failure to file. Note counts 1, 5, 9, 13 and 18, the two indictments, and the acquittal on other counts; do not present the case as a general racketeering trial.[1][2]

Document type: Sourced editorial analysis, not a purported primary-source quotation.

The National Archives verdict and trial-case identifier

Examine the National Archives at Chicago description of RG 21 criminal cases 22852 and 23232 and verdict identifier 628966. Explain the difference between an original verdict, a descriptive catalog record and a later agency narrative; consult the digitized verdict for direct archival collation.[2][4]

Document type: Sourced editorial analysis, not a purported primary-source quotation.

Frank Wilson and the chronological discrepancies

Compare Wilson’s December 21, 1933 Treasury report with the FBI overview and contemporary October 1931 newspapers. Create separate columns for event date and publication date; explain October 17/18 verdict and October 24/November 24 sentencing discrepancies without inventing a causal source error.[3][4][5][19][20]

Document type: Sourced editorial analysis, not a purported primary-source quotation.

Chicago and Cicero: the spatial history of the enterprise

Use WTTW accounts to place Chicago vice districts, Cicero’s Hawthorne Hotel and the political border between city and suburb. Explain why police jurisdiction, mayoral priorities and business logistics mattered; treat geographic shifts as connected regional networks rather than total moves.[11][12][13]

Document type: Sourced editorial analysis, not a purported primary-source quotation.

St. Valentine’s Day: murder event versus legal attribution

Reconstruct February 14, 1929, listing seven murder victims, police-disguise reports and North Side rivalry from museum sources. Record separately Capone’s Florida location, persistent suspicion of his group and absence of a Capone murder conviction.[5][10][11][17]

Document type: Sourced editorial analysis, not a purported primary-source quotation.

Two investigative strategies: Prohibition and tax

Compare the federal Bureau’s limited 1929 subpoena inquiry, Treasury’s tax inquiry, and Eliot Ness’s Prohibition team. Identify investigative jurisdiction and what each stream actually proved. Do not use modern FBI jurisdiction as a template for the 1920s.[1][3][5][6][7]

Document type: Sourced editorial analysis, not a purported primary-source quotation.

Capone’s 1930 soup kitchen photograph

Consult the Library of Congress catalog for the November 18, 1930 kitchen image at 935 South State Street. Assess what a captioned historical photo can establish, what caption-card warnings imply, and why charity financing is not proof of specific motives or total spending.[14]

Document type: Sourced editorial analysis, not a purported primary-source quotation.

Alcatraz and the 1939 release: custodial sources

Use the NPS Alcatraz Historic Resource Study and FBI biography to chart Atlanta, Alcatraz, Terminal Island and release. Distinguish the eleven-year sentence, different measures of time served and late medical history. Do not fabricate daily prison transcripts or private medical records.[5][8]

Research and Publication Limitations

This first edition does not claim complete original-file collation of Capone’s civil and family records, all municipal police homicide files, Chicago and Cicero property and business ledgers, every trial exhibit, the full 1933 Treasury report’s facsimile pages, the individual penitentiary and medical casefiles, or Florida death certification. Source links serve as a reproducible research register, not a certification of exhaustive archival review.

Authorities disagree on some exact dates: Wilson’s official posttrial report and contemporary newspapers place the guilty verdict October 17, 1931 and sentence October 24, while some federal retrospective overviews give October 18 or November 24. The 1932 appellate opinion conclusively establishes which counts resulted in conviction but does not adjudicate the St. Valentine’s Day murders.

Portrait rights and licensing have not been independently cleared, so archival images are referenced as research sources but not embedded. Structural testing and a local Chromium rendering test do not verify live MGU stylesheet delivery, server deployment, or university-level editorial approval.
