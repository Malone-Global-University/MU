Historical biography · Section 01

1. Historical Overview

Louis “Little New York” Campagna was a Brooklyn-born figure associated with the Chicago criminal organization conventionally called the Outfit. His career spanned Prohibition-era enforcement, gambling and labor-related rackets, the consolidation of Chicago’s underworld after Al Capone’s imprisonment, and a major federal prosecution involving Hollywood’s film business. The strongest individual documentary anchors are a 1936 police-compiled membership list, a 1947 FBI personal-history memorandum, a 1944 federal appellate judgment, and his own sworn Senate testimony in 1950.

Campagna should not be reduced to the familiar image of a bodyguard or presented as an unquestioned single “boss” of the entire Outfit. The Chicago Crime Commission describes him as a high-ranking associate, while the Second Circuit used language suggesting leadership of the particular film-extortion enterprise. A later FBI intelligence note passing through several intermediaries described him as the man in Chicago before 1955, but its distance from the alleged source prevents turning that impression into a definitive organizational succession. These sources establish influence with different degrees of certainty, not an uncontested formal title.

His December 1943 federal conviction and ten-year sentence were sustained in 1944; later court victories concerned the legality of revoking his parole, not an acquittal on the extortion charge. He died in Florida in May 1955. The biography therefore places the verifiable judicial history alongside the oral lore that made him an enduring figure in Chicago’s crime history. Historical biography · Section 02

2. Names, Aliases, and Conflicting Dates

The Senate witness stated that his name was Louis Campagna. The federal appellate reporter captions the same defendant Louis Compagna; FBI files and prison cards alternate the spelling. “Little New York” and “Lefty Louie” were contemporary nicknames. A 1947 FBI memorandum lists additional variants, some difficult to read in the photographed record. These variants are useful for searching archives but should not be treated as proof of separate individuals.

A substantive dating conflict requires explicit disclosure. The 1947 FBI memorandum gives September 23, 1900, Brooklyn, while common online biographies give March 31, 1900. Both place his birth in 1900; without a certified birth registration examined here, this page identifies the dated FBI claim rather than harmonizing two incompatible birthdays. Death records and contemporaneous reporting favor May 30, 1955; some later web listings use May 31, apparently conflating the next day’s publication date with the death itself. [1] [5] [11] [13] [14] [15] [17]

Historical biography · Section 03

3. Brooklyn Family Background

The FBI’s September 1947 personal-history memorandum identifies his parents as Louis and Marie Campagna, born in Italy, and says the couple entered the United States in 1899 and settled in Brooklyn. The document presents an administrative biography compiled decades later. It is a better identifiable record than a free-standing anecdote, but the underlying ship manifest, marriage certificate and Brooklyn birth registration were not independently inspected for this edition.

Brooklyn matters here because the migration from New York to Chicago was integral to the personnel and reputation of organized crime in the 1920s. The Chicago Outfit was not built by a single family or by a single transfer of personnel. Men passed through immigrant neighborhoods, saloons, amusement businesses, and city-to-city associations in which lawful work and illegal protection markets could overlap. [1] [3] [4]

Historical biography · Section 04

4. Teenage Travel and Early Chicago Work

According to the 1947 FBI account, Campagna left home at about fifteen with a theatrical troupe, traveled through Texas and California, and eventually arrived in Chicago. The memorandum says he worked as a bartender and waiter during his early Chicago years. Those statements complicate simplified versions of his career in which he merely followed Capone directly from Brooklyn into a fully formed criminal hierarchy.

A theatrical troupe could expose a young man to travel, lodging houses and the urban entertainment economy; it would not, by itself, prove criminal activity. Likewise, occupations reported on a federal personal-history form should be read as claims about work, not a complete audit of income or affiliations. Corroborating theater payrolls, boarding-house directories and early employment files remain desirable archival targets. [1] [15]

Historical biography · Section 05

5. The 1919 Bank-Robbery Conviction

The FBI’s 1947 parole summary records that Campagna admitted a 1919 bank-robbery conviction and an indeterminate prison term described as one to fourteen years. The Chicago Crime Commission places his confinement at the Pontiac Reformatory in Illinois. This is not merely a circulating rumor: a contemporary federal administrative file refers to an earlier criminal record, although the original state court judgment and reformatory dossier were not opened for this chapter.

The institutional chronology commonly reported is parole in April 1924, a return after a parole violation, and final release in November 1924. Those more granular dates derive here from the Crime Commission’s retrospective and should be treated as provisional until cross-checked against Illinois correctional records. They nonetheless situate his emergence in Chicago’s Capone period after earlier imprisonment rather than in an uninterrupted rise from adolescence. [1] [3] [15]

Historical biography · Section 06

6. New York Street-Gang Associations and Their Limits

Retrospective histories associate Campagna with the Five Points Gang environment that also figures in Al Capone’s early life. The FBI’s history of Capone independently describes Capone’s street-gang origins and links with Johnny Torrio; it does not directly prove that Campagna held a particular rank in the same New York organization. The separate biographies should therefore not be collapsed into one unverified membership roster.

A named street-gang association can explain how reputation and introductions traveled across cities, yet the surviving sources often fuse the realities of early-twentieth-century neighborhood groups with the later concept of a formally inducted Mafia family. Campagna’s biography uses “associate” and “operative” where these fit the evidence, reserving technical titles for records that actually document them. [3] [4] [15]

Historical biography · Section 07

7. Johnny Torrio, Al Capone, and Chicago’s Transition

Johnny Torrio’s move through Colosimo’s Chicago vice organization and the subsequent ascent of Capone are well established in the FBI’s institutional chronology. After Torrio was wounded in 1925, Capone assumed leadership. Campagna’s move into the Capone circle is generally dated to the middle 1920s; Crime Commission retrospective accounts describe a summons from Capone following release from Pontiac.

The distinction between chronology and personal command is important. That Capone headed a powerful Chicago organization does not establish that every named associate acted directly on his orders at every moment. Campagna’s reported duty as a bodyguard reflects longstanding historical identification; the particular episodes and personal conversations often attached to that role rest on weaker later reporting. [3] [4] [15]

Historical biography · Section 08

8. Bodyguard Reputation and Personal Protection

The Chicago Crime Commission describes Campagna as Capone’s bodyguard, sometimes sleeping close to his principal’s hotel-room entrance. Such accounts convey his reputation as a trusted protective operative at a time when intergang assassination was a serious threat. They also risk converting a repeated anecdote into an exact daily security assignment. The available court cases do not adjudicate when, how often, or under what employment arrangement he performed guard duty.

Personal protection in Prohibition Chicago carried political as well as physical meaning. The men able to accompany or shield a leader could filter introductions, transmit threats and earn trust. It is therefore plausible that an individual first known for proximity to Capone later gained authority across rackets; the specific route and sequence remain partially reconstructed from retrospective sources. [2] [3] [4]

Historical biography · Section 09

9. The Prohibition Conflict and Public Violence

Prohibition reshaped Chicago’s criminal markets by placing liquor distribution alongside the older vice and gambling businesses. The FBI’s Capone history describes the organization’s bootlegging operations, neighborhood competition and the notorious escalation that culminated in the St. Valentine’s Day massacre. Campagna was situated within the Capone milieu, but the institutional narrative is not proof he personally participated in a particular killing.

Violent reputations are especially vulnerable to retrospective inflation. A newspaper’s description of an individual as a gunman can record public perception; an arrest or detective suspicion can show investigative attention; a conviction for a named crime establishes an adjudicated result. This biography keeps those categories separate so that collective gang violence is not silently reassigned to an individual without evidence. [1] [2] [3] [4]

Historical biography · Section 10

10. 1927 Joe Aiello Encounter: Reported Incident

The Chicago Crime Commission recounts a 1927 episode involving Joe Aiello, an adversary within Chicago’s criminal disputes. In that retrospective, Campagna and companions arrived at a police station while Aiello was confined; police reportedly disarmed and arrested Campagna, and officers claimed to have heard threats exchanged in nearby cells. This is a secondary retelling of a police episode, not an authenticated transcript or final court ruling.

Aiello was murdered on October 23, 1930. The historical fact of his death does not establish Campagna as the shooter or planner. The Crime Commission itself notes the absence of a prosecution in that killing. A responsible reading uses the station episode to illuminate hostility and intimidation while treating authorship of the later homicide as unresolved within the materials examined. [3] [15]

Historical biography · Section 11

11. The 1936 Capone-Gang List

A July 1936 FBI memorandum preserving information supplied by Cook County coroner personnel includes “Louis (‘Little New York’) Campagna” on its list headed “Capone Gang (South Side).” Its particular value is chronological: it establishes that law-enforcement-linked observers grouped him with the organization by the mid-1930s and used the same nickname that appears elsewhere.

A police list is not an organizational membership certificate. It mixes individuals of different functions and may reflect intelligence gathered from investigators rather than the organization’s own records. Its inclusion of Johnny Torrio, Frank Nitti, Jake Guzik and Tony Accardo demonstrates how broadly the category was drawn. It should support an association claim, not exact internal rank or unbroken control over every business. [2] [15]

Historical biography · Section 12

12. After Capone’s Conviction

Al Capone was convicted of federal tax evasion in October 1931, was sentenced the following month, and never resumed public leadership of Chicago after his imprisonment. This transition intensified questions about who held individual rackets, managed political contacts, or exercised central authority. The FBI’s 1947 Campagna biography records an underworld rumor that Capone left interests in the hands of Campagna, Frank Nitti, Paul DeLucia and others.

That wording makes a difference. A contemporary administrative memorandum preserves a claim that circulated; it does not certify the rumor as an internal transfer deed. The more defensible conclusion is that Campagna remained influential in an organization undergoing successive leadership arrangements. Nitti, Ricca, Accardo and other figures appear in various official and retrospective descriptions, sometimes with competing labels for “boss.” [1] [3] [4] [15]

Historical biography · Section 13

13. From Enforcement to Business-Related Racketeering

Descriptions of Campagna in the 1930s increasingly emphasize gambling profits, protection collections and labor-related coercion. This was not simply a shift from “violence” to “business” but a change in the way illicit revenue could be collected. Control of access to a trade, venue or job could generate regular payments without requiring a spectacular public attack each time.

The judicial record from the Hollywood case later provides a concrete example: labor-strike threats, manipulated union offices and demands imposed on exhibitors and producers. The case concerns a particular proven conspiracy, not a blanket finding that every business investment or employment relationship Campagna ever had was fraudulent. A number of claimed Chicago labor cases remain better documented in local historical retrospectives than in judgments reviewed here. [3] [5] [16]

Historical biography · Section 14

14. Cicero Gambling Interests and Income Claims

The Crime Commission states that Campagna invested around $1,500 in two illegal gambling establishments in Cicero in 1934 and later drew substantial annual returns. A figure of $75,000 per year circulates in secondary accounts. Without original financial books or a tax finding tied to those precise establishments, the numbers should be treated as historical claims rather than independently reconciled financial statements.

At the 1950 Senate hearing Campagna supplied financial records and discussed property holdings. The official hearing is a better source for what he claimed to own or earn at that moment, but even sworn testimony has to be compared with other records before accepting a cash-flow figure as accurate. The gap between declared income and researchers’ estimates is itself part of the organized-crime financial story. [3] [11] [15]

Historical biography · Section 15

15. Chicago Labor Contacts and Local Claims

A Chicago Crime Commission historical sketch associates Campagna with attempts to dominate the bartenders’ union during the 1930s and reports injunction proceedings around 1940. It also attributes a large withdrawal from a retail-clerks union treasury to Campagna and associates in 1943. These are consequential assertions, but the original injunction file, accounting records and adjudications for the claimed union-fund loss were not located in the sources reviewed.

This page therefore reports the allegations with attribution rather than treating them as additional convictions. The historical significance of the claims is clear: unions represented bargaining power, dues income and strategic leverage over employers, making them attractive to criminals seeking to transform a threat of disruption into a recurring payment stream. Demonstrating that mechanism generally is not the same as demonstrating personal guilt in each Chicago case. [3] [5] [16]

Historical biography · Section 16

16. Film Industry Labor Structure and Vulnerability

The Hollywood case centered on the international union serving stagehands and motion-picture machine operators, often referred to by the abbreviation IATSE. That organization could affect both production and exhibition. A threatened stoppage was economically potent because studios and theater owners depended on specialized workers and reliable schedules. Federal prosecutors argued that certain union officials and outside conspirators used those institutional powers coercively.

The legitimate function of collective bargaining must be distinguished from the extortion scheme found by the jury. Strikes, wage demands and union representation are not inherently criminal. In this case the Second Circuit evaluated testimony describing threats used to obtain payments for private conspirators rather than bona fide bargaining benefits for workers. [5] [16]

Historical biography · Section 17

17. George Browne, William Bioff, and the Network

The appellate opinion described how George E. Browne became international union president and how Willie Bioff and Nick Circella received representative positions. Bioff and Browne later gave evidence for the government. The testimony linked Chicago participants, a California intermediary, union appointments and payment demands in a network extending beyond Chicago city limits.

That network is an example of how crime organizations could capture or exploit a legitimate institution without every employee or member participating in the illegal scheme. For a serious history of labor racketeering, identifying the culpable officers matters as much as identifying victims. The conviction should never become a characterization of all union members or of collective bargaining as a whole. [5] [16]

Historical biography · Section 18

18. John Roselli and the California Connection

John Roselli appears in the 1944 appellate ruling as a person presented to Bioff as the group’s California representative. This helps explain the geographic reach of the conspiracy: Chicago participants could leverage union offices and a Hollywood intermediary to communicate with companies based on the West Coast. The court’s account also identifies particular links to New Jersey and Chicago personnel.

The sources do not justify a claim that Roselli simply answered to Campagna in every professional or criminal activity. The court establishes involvement in the charged enterprise. Later accounts that describe permanent chains of command must be evaluated independently and should not be backdated automatically to the late 1930s. [5] [16]

Historical biography · Section 19

19. The 1935–1940 Conspiracy

The Second Circuit described a conspiracy to extort from motion-picture producers and exhibitors during 1935–1940. Its narrative of the testimony included annual payment expectations imposed on major and smaller companies, with strike threats serving as leverage. The opinion mentioned more than $600,000 collected from producers plus nearly $500,000 from exhibitors in the account it considered sufficient to support the jury’s verdict.

Those figures relate to the charged scheme as described in trial testimony and the appellate decision; they do not measure Campagna’s personal take-home share. They should also not be confused with a wholly separate alleged $900,000 retail-clerks matter sometimes mentioned in later Chicago biographies. Precision about the victim class, period and legal record is the only way to keep multiple similar-sounding rackets distinct. [5] [3] [16]

Historical biography · Section 20

20. Campagna’s Particular Role in the Trial Record

The appellate court said that Bioff’s testimony directly connected Campagna to the enterprise and characterized him as apparently its chief. In context, “chief” described the venture before the court. That is powerful evidence of a central role in the extortion conspiracy, not a judicial declaration that he was the unchallenged head of all Chicago organized crime.

This language offers a firmer basis for assessing influence than colorful stories of meetings, hotel security or whispered succession. It also carries an evidentiary boundary: the court was reviewing sufficiency of evidence for convictions and examining possible trial errors, not commissioning a complete corporate chart of an illicit organization. [5] [15] [16]

Historical biography · Section 21

21. March 1943 Federal Indictment

The Second Circuit places the indictment on March 18, 1943. The defendants included Campagna under the appellate spelling Compagna, Paul DeLucia (Ricca), Charles Gioe, Phillip D’Andrea, John Roselli, Frank Maritote and Louis Kaufman. Frank Nitti appears in accounts of the broader investigation and died in March 1943; the conviction discussed in the appellate opinion applies to the seven who appealed.

The indictment occurred after the five-year period of conspiracy alleged in the published opinion. An indictment initiates a prosecution and is not itself a finding of guilt. The government had to prove the charged conspiracy at trial, and the later jury verdict plus appellate affirmance provide the legal outcome on which the page relies. [5] [6] [16]

Historical biography · Section 22

22. Trial, Witnesses, and Defense Challenges

The 1944 appeal describes a case woven chiefly from two accomplice witnesses, Bioff and Browne. As cooperating participants, their credibility was a central issue. The appellate panel reviewed a substantial set of errors asserted by the defendants and evaluated whether a reasonable jury could rely on the prosecution’s narrative. The opinion therefore allows readers to distinguish what the witnesses said from what the judges independently investigated.

Cooperator testimony is not automatically false or automatically true. Juries may credit it, and courts may affirm convictions based on it under applicable evidentiary standards. But a historian using the opinion should write “the court described trial testimony” or “the jury convicted” rather than inventing a direct confession from Campagna or treating every allegation recited in the appeal as an independently corroborated fact. [5] [16]

Historical biography · Section 23

23. December 1943 Verdict and Sentence

An FBI memorandum on Campagna gives the jury verdict as December 22, 1943, and the sentence as December 31, 1943. Another memorandum in the same released file gives December 23 for a co-defendant, creating a limited one-day conflict over the verdict date. The December 31 sentence is corroborated by subsequent judicial findings and prison index cards.

Campagna received a sentence of ten years in federal prison and a $10,000 fine. Some retrospectives loosely describe the date or the offense as generic “extortion”; for the legal ledger the more accurate description is the specific federal conspiracy prosecuted under the anti-racketeering law then in force. The conviction was not later vacated by the parole cases. [1] [5] [7] [15] [16]

Historical biography · Section 24

24. December 1944 Appeal and April 1945 Review Denial

On December 20, 1944, the Second Circuit affirmed the convictions in United States v. Compagna, 146 F.2d 524. The opinion by Judge Learned Hand is the most direct published judicial source on Campagna’s participation in the film-industry conspiracy. The Supreme Court denied certiorari on April 2, 1945.

A denied petition for certiorari does not amount to a separate Supreme Court affirmation of all factual findings. The distinction matters for legal accuracy: the substantive reasoning belongs to the appellate court, while the Supreme Court’s denial leaves the judgment in place without providing an independent opinion on its merits. [5] [6]

Historical biography · Section 25

25. Atlanta and Leavenworth Incarceration

Prison records and federal memoranda place Campagna in the federal penitentiary at Atlanta in 1944 and document his transfer to Leavenworth, Kansas, in July 1945. A parole-board summary states that his prison adjustment initially caused concern, while later conduct was described as acceptable. The records also describe medical, family and employment planning associated with the approaching release.

Institutional transfers can be reconstructed more securely from inmate index cards and Bureau of Prisons correspondence than from stories claiming that one person personally ordered a transfer. The historic file does not establish that every lobbying effort around Campagna’s case was corrupt; the reasons and decision-makers for transfer and release have to be considered separately. [1] [7] [15] [16]

Historical biography · Section 26

26. August 13, 1947: Parole

Campagna and several co-defendants left Leavenworth on parole on August 13, 1947. The FBI’s parole summary identifies July 7 as his eligibility date, while congressional critics emphasized the short interval between eligibility and actual release. The 1951 district court also reconstructs the transport from Kansas to Chicago and identifies the presence of counsel Eugene Bernstein.

Parole was conditional liberty, not pardon, expiration of sentence, or erasure of conviction. It placed Campagna under supervision, with legal consequences if the conditions were violated. The terms of his original punishment and the later litigation remained active components of his public record after he physically returned to the Chicago area. [1] [7] [10] [12] [16]

Historical biography · Section 27

27. Why the Paroles Became a Public Controversy

Members of Congress questioned why a group of men convicted of extorting the motion-picture industry secured parole quickly and how tax claims and outside contacts had been handled. Representative Clare Hoffman described hearings held in Chicago and Washington during 1947 and criticized arrangements connected to the release. These are significant acts of oversight, but a congressional accusation is not, without more, proof that a particular official took a bribe.

The controversy expanded through FBI interviews, grand-jury inquiries, press scrutiny and litigation. A 1947 FBI report documented a congressman’s account of rumors about payments, in part derived from a newspaper reporter. The language and provenance must remain attached to the claims; repeating them as adjudicated corruption would overstate the record. [1] [12] [15] [16]

Historical biography · Section 28

28. The Separate Tax-Settlement Question

Congressional debate examined federal tax assessments against Campagna and settlement terms arranged while he was incarcerated. Hoffman described a large original claim and substantially smaller compromise. Subsequent Senate questioning of attorney Eugene Bernstein referred to a stipulated tax deficiency. The amounts differ across assessments, compromises and hearing descriptions because they refer to different procedural stages or sources.

A settlement with the Treasury does not itself prove criminal influence. Nor does a disputed tax balance directly measure the sums taken from film companies or the profits from gambling. For researchers, it is essential to record the legal status of each amount—asserted deficiency, negotiated payment, or verified conviction fine—before comparing them. [11] [12] [15]

Historical biography · Section 29

29. Retaking on July 23, 1948

Parole-violator warrants dated July 21, 1948, preceded Campagna’s retaking two days later. The district court in Atlanta examined whether adequate grounds existed for issuing the warrants. The government’s action followed controversy over the parole decision, and the litigation addressed what evidence and procedure were needed to restrict liberty again.

This new custody was not a fresh conviction for Hollywood extortion and not an adjudicated later offense. The question was whether an administrative board could revoke conditional freedom on the record it had. Confusing the two proceedings would erase an important difference between the underlying sentence and enforcement of parole conditions. [7] [8] [9] [10] [16]

Historical biography · Section 30

30. The 1948 District Court Decision

In Compagna v. Hiatt, 82 F. Supp. 295, the Northern District of Georgia examined the revocation warrants and concluded that they had been issued without adequate factual information of a parole breach. The court’s ruling favored Campagna and fellow prisoner Charles Gioe, and the prison index indicates a court-ordered discharge in December 1948.

The opinion expressly did not undo the extortion conviction. Its concern was administrative arbitrariness and the right not to be jailed again without lawful basis. As subsequent appellate proceedings changed the posture of the case, a biography that mentions only the 1948 decision would leave the litigation sequence incomplete. [7] [16]

Historical biography · Section 31

31. The Fifth Circuit and a 1950 Split Court

In November 1949 the Fifth Circuit reversed the district court, finding that the parolees had been informed of charges and allowed an administrative appearance, while leaving room for renewed arguments that the revocations were legally void. Judge Waller’s dissent drew a sharper line against revocation based on publicity, politics or unsubstantiated assertions.

The Supreme Court then affirmed the appellate judgment by an equally divided Court in November 1950, with Justice Tom C. Clark not participating. Such a disposition resolves the case at that stage but does not create a majority merits opinion. The courts were still addressing parole legality, not relitigating the evidence supporting the 1943 guilty verdict. [8] [9] [16]

Historical biography · Section 32

32. The 1951 Ruling Restoring Parole Status

After further proceedings, Judge Underwood ruled in September 1951 that the parole revocations were unsupported by substantial evidence or any evidence of violations and were legally null. He ordered Campagna and Gioe discharged as reinstated parolees. His wording is important: the men were restored to conditional supervision rather than released without any continuing obligation.

This outcome is a rare circumstance in which an underworld biography intersects directly with administrative-law safeguards. The result shows that even prisoners with serious criminal convictions retain legally enforceable protections governing how the state restricts liberty. It does not convert the underlying criminal enterprise into lawful conduct. [10] [16]

Historical biography · Section 33

33. 1950 Senate Testimony: Identity and Records

On September 9, 1950, Campagna appeared before the Senate Special Committee to Investigate Organized Crime in Interstate Commerce, commonly called the Kefauver Committee. His testimony appears in Part 5 of the Illinois hearings, printed at approximately pages 53–74. He identified himself by his birth name, produced ledgers and tax returns, and answered questions about imprisonment and parole.

The transcript should be read as an interrogation and record-production proceeding, not as an authoritative biography assembled by disinterested investigators. Some answers were terse, some questions framed claims by counsel, and the hearings were intended to expose organized-crime structures. Accurate presentation requires separating the committee’s questions, the witness’s answers, and independently verified documentation. [11] [15]

Historical biography · Section 34

34. Berwyn Home and Residence Claims

In his Senate testimony Campagna said that he had lived in his Berwyn, Illinois, home since approximately 1928 or 1929. The 1947 parole plan also listed Berwyn as his family residence. This links his long-standing place of residence to the suburban west-of-Chicago geography associated with the Outfit’s Cicero business world.

Residence evidence should not be inflated into proof that every operation in Cicero or Berwyn passed through his household. Property, housing and family records are valuable because they establish durable relationships, geographic proximity and economic capacity. They do not, without further documents, reveal the legal and beneficial ownership of every business attributed to an individual. [1] [11]

Historical biography · Section 35

35. Fowler, Indiana: The 800-Acre Farm

Campagna told the Senate committee that he had purchased an approximately 800-acre farm near Fowler, Indiana, in 1942, giving a purchase price of roughly $100,000. The 1947 parole file also referred to agricultural employment connected to his properties. This is a particularly well-attested element of his later life because the record includes both a prior administrative plan and his own committee testimony.

A large farm may represent investment, family livelihood, a credible lawful occupation or a location for receiving associates. None of those possibilities can be assumed without independent proof of how it was financed or used. This profile documents the property rather than automatically asserting that it was a money-laundering operation. [1] [11] [13]

Historical biography · Section 36

36. Berrien Springs, Michigan: The Second Estate

Campagna also identified an estate in the Berrien Springs area of Michigan. Posthumous local reporting described extensive grounds, controlled entry and buildings adapted for visitors. The property appeared again in the press in 1957, when it was placed on the market. Contemporary photographs and an estate history can illuminate his lifestyle and local reputation but were published after the period of his direct operations.

An accessible 1957 Berrien County newspaper retrospective supplies a local angle often missing from nationally focused organized-crime accounts. It should be distinguished from land deeds, appraisal files and property-tax returns. Those would be the appropriate next sources for testing acreage, ownership chains and valuations. [1] [11] [17] [18]

Historical biography · Section 37

37. Economic Presentation and the Problem of Cash

The coexistence of bank-robbery history, gambling associations, an anti-racketeering conviction, land holdings and tax settlements creates a complex financial portrait. Public sources report substantial sums at different points, but no single comprehensive statement of Campagna’s assets, liabilities and clandestine distributions has been established from the documents consulted.

Tax liabilities and cash contributions linked to a settlement should not be summed with alleged film-company collections as if they were independent streams of personal wealth. Some figures describe gross coercive receipts; others describe disputed tax obligations, purchase prices, or secondary accounts of gambling revenue. This distinction prevents double counting and unsupported estimates of his net worth. [3] [5] [10] [11] [12]

Historical biography · Section 38

38. Relationships with Ricca, Gioe, and D’Andrea

Paul Ricca, Charles Gioe and Phillip D’Andrea appear with Campagna in the film-industry prosecution, imprisonment and 1947 parole controversy. Their shared legal history establishes concrete cooperation or co-defendant status in one enterprise. It does not produce a comprehensive map of friendship, rivalry, seniority and criminal assignments over subsequent decades.

The public record often calls Ricca a successor of Capone or Nitti, and later accounts associate Tony Accardo with the organization’s postwar direction. Campagna’s significance is best expressed as a durable senior participant in a shifting coalition. The strongest judicial description of his leadership remains specific to the film-extortion enterprise, not a declaration of universal authority. [1] [5] [7] [11] [15]

Historical biography · Section 39

39. The Accardo Question and the Parole Rumors

Postwar accounts attribute considerable influence to Anthony Accardo, including allegations that he worked to obtain the prisoners’ release. The FBI recorded rumors of improper payments; Senate testimony by U.S. Attorney Otto Kerner discussed Accardo’s visits to Leavenworth and the use of an alias in a visitors’ register. Those records demonstrate scrutiny of particular conduct, not a judicial finding that Accardo purchased Campagna’s parole.

The distinction protects the historical analysis from two opposite errors: pretending there was no controversy and announcing a corruption scheme as proven when the cited documents principally record questions, testimony and accusations. For Campagna’s biography the definitive outcome is his 1947 parole, 1948 retaking, and eventual judicial restoration to conditional status. [1] [10] [11] [12] [15] [16]

Historical biography · Section 40

40. Murray Humphreys and the Limits of Acquaintance

Campagna was asked about Murray Humphreys at the 1950 hearings and gave a rough estimate of how long he had known him. Accounts of the post-Capone organization treat Humphreys as a labor and business intermediary. An acknowledged acquaintance is meaningful background, but it is not automatically proof of joint responsibility for any one extortion or union case.

The same caution applies to named associates appearing in law-enforcement lists, funeral reporting or Senate questioning. Investigators deliberately used relationships as possible leads, but historians should identify the source of each claimed link and avoid turning proximity into certainty of criminal collaboration. [2] [11] [15]

Historical biography · Section 41

41. Did Campagna Lead the Entire Outfit?

This question produces different answers depending on the source and definition of leadership. The Chicago Crime Commission retrospectively describes a senior figure. The 1944 appellate court calls Campagna apparently the chief of the movie-extortion venture. A 1959 FBI memorandum relays a multi-step report that he was “the man” in Chicago before 1955. It would be misleading to combine those statements into a documented tenure as sole boss with precise start and end dates.

A clandestine organization might maintain operational, financial and political leadership simultaneously. A formal title can matter less than control of particular businesses or personal authority within a coalition. Without contemporaneous internal documentation and careful corroboration, his category should remain senior operative or influential leader rather than a definitive place in a rigid succession chart. [3] [5] [15]

Historical biography · Section 42

42. The 1959 FBI Intelligence Claim

A Chicago FBI memorandum dictated in July 1959 repeated a claim that Campagna had been the dominant Chicago figure before his death, after which Ricca assumed that position. The archived guide traces the statement through an unnamed source, a reporter and another reporter before it reached the agent. The chain shows why an official memorandum can preserve hearsay even while serving as an authentic federal document.

The appropriate use of this item is to document that an intelligence narrative circulated in 1959. It cannot by itself reverse the more complex 1950 public testimony, appellate evidence and Crime Commission assessments. A future history of Outfit succession should compare this note with numerous independent contemporary observations before stating a single chain of command. [15] [16]

Historical biography · Section 43

43. Public Image, Violence, and Retrospective Myth

The “Little New York” nickname, photographs with other Outfit figures, and stories of police-station confrontations helped create a public image of danger and proximity to Capone. Contemporary or near-contemporary press sometimes referred to him as a gunman or extortionist. Those descriptors mix convictions, investigators’ views and reputation in ways that become difficult to separate after decades of retelling.

This page treats the early bank conviction, the 1943 conspiracy verdict and the 1944 affirmance as identifiable legal outcomes. It treats accusations of particular unsolved murders, specific informal threats and undocumented rackets as claims requiring separate proof. The method is designed to make the figure intelligible without endorsing the dramatic parts of the legend merely because they are widely repeated. [1] [3] [5] [13] [14]

Historical biography · Section 44

44. Last Years and Public Visibility

Following his postwar litigation and Senate examination, Campagna was increasingly described in the press through his farms and Berwyn home. Some secondary histories portray him as retired from day-to-day criminal operations; other sources imply continued influence. Neither the printed hearing nor the appellate cases offer a complete day-by-day account of his activities in the early 1950s.

The word “retired” in a newspaper obituary may indicate diminished visibility, less active responsibility, or simply a narrative convention. It should not be taken as an administrative retirement filing. His continuing name recognition among journalists, investigators and former associates demonstrates that historical reputation can persist even when verifiable operational detail becomes thin. [11] [13] [14] [15]

Historical biography · Section 45

45. May 30, 1955: Death in Florida

Near-contemporaneous reports and later accounts place Campagna’s death on May 30, 1955, during a fishing trip in waters near Miami. Accounts describe his collapse after catching a large fish and identify a heart attack. The June 13, 1955, TIME obituary described him as 54, consistent with a September 1900 birthdate at the time of death.

The often reproduced May 31 date is not adopted as settled fact because contemporary May 31 newspaper coverage described an event from the preceding day, while multiple historical reports specify May 30. Even so, this edition has not personally examined a certified Florida death certificate; the page records the strongest available period chronology with that limitation. The broader circumstance—a fatal medical emergency on a Florida fishing outing—is well attested. [3] [13] [14] [17] [18]

Historical biography · Section 46

46. Berwyn Funeral and Mount Carmel Burial

Accounts describe a heavily attended wake in Berwyn, reflecting Campagna’s long social connections in Chicago’s underworld. Allan May’s historical narrative and the TIME obituary name prominent Chicago figures among the mourners. Later references place his burial at Mount Carmel Cemetery in the Chicago suburbs, also associated with Al Capone.

A funeral attendance list documents presence and commemoration; it cannot be transformed into evidence that every person there was an accomplice or subordinate. Some details about a refused church ceremony are repeated in retrospectives but have not been reconciled against parish records or funeral-home files for this edition. Those claims should remain attributed. [3] [13] [14]

Historical biography · Section 47

47. What the Case Reveals About National Crime Networks

The film-industry prosecution demonstrates that the Chicago organization’s reach was not limited to breweries, Cicero gambling rooms or local violence. It depended on intermediaries able to influence specialized institutions and convert the threat of disruption into money. Chicago figures, union executives and California and New Jersey participants appeared in one federal case precisely because the alleged enterprise crossed local jurisdictions.

The legal story also reveals the expanding institutional response: federal anti-racketeering prosecution, prisons in two states, parole administration, congressional scrutiny, FBI investigation and habeas review. Campagna’s significance lies as much in that traceable national system as in his individual criminal notoriety. [5] [7] [10] [11] [12] [16]

Historical biography · Section 48

48. Historical Significance and Final Assessment

Campagna’s most defensible historical profile is that of an early Capone associate who became a consequential participant in the Chicago organization’s gambling and labor-facing enterprises, was convicted in the Hollywood extortion conspiracy, and remained important enough to be central to postwar parole controversy and Senate investigation. The records place him in a network of influential men rather than in a neatly documented single-person dynasty.

His career illustrates three recurring historical problems. Crime organizations changed from volatile street violence to institutional coercion without abandoning the possibility of force; national industries created opportunities for cross-regional rackets; and public narratives about leadership frequently outran the direct documentation. A reliable biography draws those conclusions while keeping the 1943 conviction, 1947 parole, 1951 ruling, and unresolved birth-date or hierarchy questions separate. [1] [2] [3] [5] [10] [11] [15] [16]

Documentary Research Windows

Editorial methodology: These windows are investigative reading prompts based on located records, not facsimile-collated source excerpts or invented quotations. Follow the linked editions and identify the legal character of each record before making claims.

Research Window 1: FBI 1947 personal history and parole memorandum

Locate the Campagna portion of the Roselli file, dated September 16, 1947. Record the precise name variants, Brooklyn birth statement, occupations, 1919 offense, December 1943 conviction and August 1947 parole. This is an agency digest, not a certified birth record or a judicial finding on rumored later conduct.

Document guides: [1] [15]

Research Window 2: July 1936 police-compiled Capone-gang list

Read the memorandum’s heading and list membership. Identify who assembled it and the purpose of the classification; distinguish a police grouping from an internal Outfit hierarchy. Compare names appearing in the user’s adjacent biographies.

Document guides: [2]

Research Window 3: 1944 appellate opinion on the film-industry scheme

Read the court’s account of what the jury could find from Bioff and Browne. Trace the Chicago and California personnel and the court’s specific statement about Campagna’s role. Avoid treating every accusation as if independently reproduced from the trial transcript.

Document guides: [5] [16]

Research Window 4: 1947 and 1948 custody chronology

Compare the Parole Board summary, Atlanta prison index-card stamps, and the 1948 district-court findings. Plot the transfer to Leavenworth, August 13 parole, July 23 recapture and December 1948 ruling. Distinguish sentencing, transfer, parole, revocation and discharge.

Document guides: [1] [7] [16]

Research Window 5: Congressional inquiry into parole and taxes

Read the November 1947 floor statement alongside the FBI memorandum recording Congressman Busbey’s allegations. Tag which statements are from Congress, which are press-derived claims, and which are independently established procedural facts.

Document guides: [1] [12]

Research Window 6: The 1949–1951 federal habeas decisions

Follow the 1949 appellate reversal, the equally divided Supreme Court result in 1950, and the 1951 district-court ruling. Explain why the final remedy restored conditional parole and did not set aside the motion-picture conspiracy conviction.

Research Window 7: Kefauver Committee testimony, September 9, 1950

Research and Publication Limitations

The present research checked federal court-opinion summaries and links, the FBI’s 1947 Campagna memorandum (including examination of a scanned page), the 1936 FBI-hosted gang list, the Chicago Crime Commission retrospective, the public Senate hearing inventory, and period reporting. It did not collate a certified Brooklyn birth registration, 1919 Illinois trial and prison file, the entire 1943 trial transcript, all archived FBI material, every parole-board exhibit, property deeds, exact tax settlement documentation or a Florida death certificate. Some secondary claims therefore remain explicitly attributed.

Death-date conflict is flagged rather than concealed. “Boss” and “chief” refer to source-specific descriptions and are not equated with a formally established place in the Outfit command hierarchy. The page excludes any historic portrait without independently confirmed reuse rights. Local semantic structure and links were programmatically reviewed; deployment on a live site, retrieval of MGU’s remote shared components and full visual parity with the production CSS must be verified at publication.
