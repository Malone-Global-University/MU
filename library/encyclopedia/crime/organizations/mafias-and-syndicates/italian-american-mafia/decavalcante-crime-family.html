Overview

The DeCavalcante crime family is a historically documented New Jersey–based organization of the Italian-American Mafia, or La Cosa Nostra (LCN). It is distinguished from the five principal New York City Mafia families by its core in Elizabeth and Union County, New Jersey, with documented activities extending at different times into other New Jersey counties, Staten Island, parts of Connecticut, and Florida. New Jersey's State Commission of Investigation has called it the state's indigenous or “home-grown” Mafia family. [1][2][17][18]

Its present name derives from Simone Rizzo “Sam the Plumber” DeCavalcante, who succeeded Nicholas Delmore in the 1960s and led the organization until its later transfer of authority to Giovanni “John” Riggi. Yet investigators traced the family's organizational roots to earlier twentieth-century networks; the surname should not be mistaken for the name of a biological clan or proof that DeCavalcante personally founded every predecessor crew. [1][2]

The organization's most documented historical activities include illegal gambling, loansharking, coercion in construction and labor relations, securities-related fraud, stolen-property schemes and violence. Federal investigations produced major indictments in 1999–2000 and a large body of cooperating-witness and appellate evidence. A later 2015–2017 federal investigation established further criminal conduct by named members and associates. These records establish particular enterprises, crimes and legal outcomes; they do not reveal the full membership or revenues of a secret organization in November 2026. [1][6][7][8][9][10][11][12]

An important legal qualification runs throughout this entry: the 1990 Riggi trial and 2003 Vitabile–Abramo–Schifilliti trial cannot be summarized solely by their original verdicts, because appellate courts subsequently reversed or vacated the judgments in question. This article reports what each court decided and identifies the limits of available later case-disposition research. [5][8]

The terms family, boss and member are contextual designations used by investigators, witnesses and courts. A senior title in one dated complaint cannot responsibly be treated as a permanent office. The 2004 commission estimate of approximately 40 members and at least 50 associates applies to that report's historical assessment, not to a 2026 membership census. [1][9]

Early New Jersey Origins and the Delmore Organization

The DeCavalcante organization developed from overlapping Italian-American criminal circles in northern and central New Jersey during the first decades of the twentieth century. A substantial part of its early recorded circle was associated with Ribera, Sicily, and Elizabeth's Peterstown neighborhood. These migration and neighborhood ties supplied social relationships among particular offenders; neither the migrants of Ribera nor the city's Italian-American residents collectively constituted a criminal organization. [1]

The state investigative record identifies Nicholas “Nick” Delmore as an earlier leader. Some popular retrospective leadership charts assign a succession of midcentury bosses before Delmore; such lists differ in their dates and in distinguishing New Jersey groups that may have operated independently. The 1980s federal and state descriptions provide firmer evidence for Delmore, DeCavalcante, Riggi and later administrations than for a single precisely dated chain reaching back to 1910. [1][2]

The environment included gambling, black-market supply, construction contracting and potential opportunities to coerce work arrangements. Geographic importance stemmed partly from New Jersey's transport corridors linking ports, industrial towns, suburban construction, and metropolitan New York. The movement of people and commercial goods made organized crime possible but did not render ordinary businesses or workers culpable. [1][19]

Nicholas Delmore and the Transition of the 1960s

Nicholas Delmore appears in government accounts as the central predecessor to Simone DeCavalcante. A 1969 Union County grand-jury presentment discussed Delmore and his successor's roles in construction-related “labor consulting” arrangements in the state's expanding garden-apartment sector. That document records investigative findings concerning contracting and potential labor pressure, and must be interpreted as a grand-jury presentment, not as a conviction on every assertion it contains. [19]

The New Jersey State Commission of Investigation described Delmore's death in 1964 as the turning point after which Sam DeCavalcante assumed control. This is a well-grounded historical transition even though descriptions of precisely when DeCavalcante began acting on Delmore's behalf vary. [1][2]

Delmore's importance extends beyond chronology. The continuity between a senior figure's commercial interests and his successor's networks illustrates how a Mafia group might outlive its namesake: members, debts, relationships, crews and claimed influence in particular industries were not dissolved merely because a leader died. [1][19]

Simone “Sam the Plumber” DeCavalcante

Simone Rizzo DeCavalcante, often called “Sam the Plumber”, became the family's namesake leader in the 1960s. Investigators described his ostensible business and political connections, suburban lifestyle and cultivation of legitimate social standing, while associating him with illegal gambling and racketeering. The contrast matters: public civic participation or business ownership, by itself, is not evidence of criminal wrongdoing; the evidentiary question is the particular conduct proved by records. [1][2][3][4]

The family was not a detached branch of any New York family. DeCavalcante's associates dealt with New York–based Mafia figures and sometimes participated in multi-family transactions, but the state investigation treated the New Jersey organization as a separately identifiable family with its own administration. Regional autonomy and practical dependence could coexist. [1][18]

The “model underworld boss” characterization found in earlier investigative writing reflected the government's assessment of his public demeanor, criminal role and comparatively discreet social presentation; it is not a scholarly endorsement of his activities. Extortion and unlawful gambling inflicted financial and institutional harm irrespective of whether a leader cultivated an accommodating public image. [1][2][3]

Surveillance, Public Transcripts and the 1969 Investigation

The FBI's monitoring of Sam DeCavalcante and his associates produced electronic-surveillance material that became unusually prominent in 1969 when logs were made available in court proceedings. Contemporaneous coverage described the release of extensive conversations obtained from places including business premises associated with DeCavalcante. The publicity exposed the contrast between secrecy claims and ordinary conversations about criminal relationships and disputes. [4]

Wiretaps and clandestine recordings can supply direct evidence of what speakers said, but the meaning of an excerpt depends on the identity of the speaker, the completeness of the transcript, the dates and the legal basis for admissibility. A colorful quotation in a news article is not a substitute for full recordings or a final judicial finding. [4][5]

The surveillance also revealed how investigators approached a continuing enterprise: instead of merely arresting individual gambling operators, they sought evidence of networks connecting financiers, collectors, labor intermediaries and suspected leaders. That strategy later became central to large RICO cases in New Jersey and New York. [3][6][7]

Federal Gambling Guilty Pleas and 1971 Sentencing

A notably firm legal milestone appears in the U.S. Attorney General's 1971 annual report. It records that Samuel Rizzo DeCavalcante and 45 co-conspirators pleaded guilty to federal interstate gambling violations, following the playing of intercepted conversations. The report states that DeCavalcante received five years' imprisonment and a $10,000 fine. These are recorded guilty pleas and a sentence, not merely an accusation by investigators. [3]

The case illustrates the importance of distinguishing among the different parts of criminal litigation: identification as a suspected Mafia leader; charging instruments; court rulings on evidence; a guilty plea; and sentence. Numbers of accused persons in press accounts of the period are sometimes different from the plea total in the official annual report. This entry uses the report's own count for the outcome it describes. [3][4]

DeCavalcante later lived in Florida and was described by state investigators as withdrawing from day-to-day leadership. Descriptions of a precise retirement date vary by how they define titular boss, acting management and final transfer. The commission's 2004 report places the transition to Riggi in 1982. [1][2]

John Riggi and the Post-DeCavalcante Administration

Giovanni “John” Riggi, a longtime figure in Elizabeth's union and organized-crime milieu, became the central authority identified in the 1980s state investigation. The New Jersey commission states that Riggi was named boss in 1982, while the 1989 report describes him as operating the family during Sam DeCavalcante's extended semi-retirement. These descriptions address an evolving transfer of practical control, not simply the passage of a corporate title. [1][2]

Riggi's significance derived in part from relationships in Laborers' Local 394 and associated construction circles. The state commission treated the union and contracting sector as a key means by which organized-crime members could obtain income, influence staffing and exploit relationships with firms. Neither membership in the union nor having employed a contractor implicated an individual in crimes; the question was which officials and contractors participated in specified coercive or corrupt arrangements. [1][2][5]

The federal criminal record became more complex in 1989–1993. Riggi was charged in a case concerning construction companies and labor-union conduct; his 1990 verdict was partially adverse, then reversed on appeal. Later records concern subsequent criminal pleas and sentencing. This article therefore avoids the misleading shorthand that Riggi was permanently convicted of every count in the 1989 indictment. [5][8][13]

Labor Racketeering, Local 394 and Construction Industries

Construction and labor racketeering is among the clearest historically documented aspects of DeCavalcante activity. The 2004 commission described testimony about the influence exercised by members and associates in Laborers' Local 394 and Local 1030, including alleged no-show jobs, hiring-hall preferences, union positions and payments from employers seeking to avoid union requirements. Cooperating-witness accounts must be read as testimonial evidence with the usual incentives and limitations. [1]

A typical abuse pattern involved the manipulation of employment or labor peace: a business might face demands for payments, a crew could obtain jobs without ordinary work, or an employer might receive improper labor treatment in exchange for money. The harm falls on honest workers, union members, contractors and competing bidders. The state commission noted that the Laborers' International Union of North America undertook internal reform measures in the mid-1990s to reduce corrupt influence. [1]

The relationship with larger construction markets crossed into New York City. Federal labor investigators alleged in 1999–2000 that DeCavalcante-connected participants worked through other Mafia relationships to gain contracting opportunities in painting and construction, sometimes using nonunion labor in violation of contractual arrangements. These assertions are prosecution theories in the cited indictment reports and should not be elevated to a judicial holding without the applicable disposition. [6][7]

An additional historical grand-jury presentment addressed activity in garden-apartment construction during the early 1960s. The relevant long-term lesson is how criminal racketeers tried to convert legal economic institutions—bidding, union representation and contractor selection—into instruments of coercive private gain. [19]

Illegal Gambling and Extortionate Lending

Historical federal and state accounts describe DeCavalcante involvement in illegal bookmaking, numbers gambling, loan transactions coupled with intimidation, and collections from unlawful gambling enterprises. Sam DeCavalcante's interstate-gambling plea provides a concrete early case; later government materials describe additional alleged gambling and loansharking conduct within the family hierarchy. [1][3][6][7]

These revenue streams did not operate as one centralized company. Captains or associates might handle local books and collections; other members might claim a share of proceeds or enforce debts. “Family-wide criminal activity” in an investigative report does not prove that each person was involved in every gambling ring, every loan or all related violence. [1][8]

For purposes of criminal-law analysis, extortionate collection differs from the mere existence of a private loan. The specific threats, violence, unlawful pressure and statutory elements must be identified. A victim may suffer loss even where a criminal case does not identify an entire organization's role. [5][7]

Securities Fraud and Other Financial Schemes

By the late twentieth century, investigators associated elements of the family with securities fraud, stock manipulation and investment-related deception. The 2004 New Jersey report described testimony about Philip Abramo's role in marketing dubious securities through telephone sales and in creating apparent market demand for stocks that later lost value. The source is a government synthesis of testimony and investigations, not a basis to treat every untried assertion about an investor or intermediary as proved. [1]

Securities-fraud allegations also appeared in the broad 2000 federal charges and in the record underlying the 2003 trial. The appellate decision later recounted the government's allegations, trial evidence and verdicts; however, the 2008 vacatur means the original convictions of the three appellants cannot be cited as surviving final judgments. [7][8]

The shift to financial fraud demonstrates adaptability. Criminal associations could exploit ordinary capital-market infrastructure, professional sales channels or public confidence rather than relying exclusively on neighborhood gambling and physical coercion. Victims included people who purchased securities on false premises and legitimate businesses whose markets were distorted. [1][7][8]

New Jersey Territory and Out-of-State Relationships

The family's principal historic center was Elizabeth, particularly Peterstown, with activity described in Union, Middlesex, Monmouth, Ocean and Essex counties. The 1989 commission report also identified operations involving Staten Island, New York, and a Waterbury, Connecticut, connection. A later state report identified activity or membership links beyond New Jersey, including New York, Connecticut and Florida. Such place names are historic intelligence findings, not descriptions of local populations. [1][2]

The geography helps explain the group's positioning among Mafia families. New Jersey also hosted activities of New York's Five Families and the Philadelphia crime family. The DeCavalcante organization was therefore distinct but operated in overlapping industries and sometimes relied on inter-family relationships for contracts or conflict resolution. [1][2][6][18]

A local crew's presence in Staten Island did not transform the New Jersey family into a New York Five Families organization. Likewise, a New York family figure cooperating with a DeCavalcante participant does not by itself show that one family owned, ruled or absorbed the other. Claims of operational jurisdiction require dates and specific evidence. [1][6]

Leadership Structure, Crews and Decision-Making

The Second Circuit's 2008 opinion described the family as led by a boss and supported by an underboss and consigliere, with captains supervising crews of soldiers and associates. An administration referred to the upper ranks and, depending on circumstances, other leaders involved in organization-wide decisions. The court's summary is a dated reconstruction of trial evidence, not a complete map of all modern activity. [8]

  • Boss / acting boss: Claimed or recognized senior authority, with practical power potentially shifting during imprisonment, internal disputes or physical absence.
  • Underboss: Senior manager who could supervise activities or communicate decisions from the boss.
  • Consigliere: Counselor and senior participant in dispute resolution or administration.
  • Captain / caporegime: Leader or organizer of a crew or an income-generating criminal network.
  • Soldier / made member: A formally inducted Mafia member, distinguished by status from associates.
  • Associate: Person linked to one or more members in a business, criminal scheme or social network without equivalent formal initiation.
  • Ruling panel: A group empowered to perform leadership functions in particular intervals; not automatically proof of equal power or permanent membership.

The state commission described ruling panels during the later Riggi period. In 1997–1999, it identified Vincent Palermo, Girolamo Palermo and Charles Majuri as a leadership trio acting amid the disruption caused by Riggi's incarceration. These are time-bound descriptions, not evidence that any of the same figures held those exact posts in 2026. [1][8][16]

The 1989 Indictment, 1990 Trial and Appellate Reversal

In October 1989, federal charges targeted John Riggi, his sons, Salvatore Timpani and another individual in a case involving alleged extortion, labor conduct and construction businesses. A New Jersey commission report later recorded the 1990 convictions of Riggi and Timpani. That historical report is important, but it does not represent the final appellate status. [2][5]

The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit explained that Riggi was acquitted of the charged RICO counts, including the theory that he was the DeCavalcante acting boss for purposes of those charges. The jury convicted him of certain Hobbs Act and Taft–Hartley Act offenses; Timpani was convicted of a Hobbs Act count. The appellate court reversed their convictions and ordered a new trial because the trial judge improperly restricted recross-examination after redirect testimony, violating the defendants' Sixth Amendment rights. The opinion was decided in December 1991, with rehearing denied in February 1992. [5]

Other later sources report that Riggi subsequently entered guilty pleas and received sentences in other proceedings; those outcomes must be distinguished from treating the overturned 1990 judgment as still operative. The records used here are sufficient to say that the 1990 convictions were reversed; they are not a substitute for a full docket-by-docket history of every post-remand count. [5][13]

John D’Amato and the Early-1990s Succession Crisis

State investigators reported that after Riggi's imprisonment, his underboss John D'Amato asserted acting leadership. The commission described D'Amato's killing and the subsequent Giacomo “Jake” Amari administration, which ended when Amari died in 1997. The period displayed both the danger of fragmented command and the limitations of treating a reputed criminal organization as a succession chart without violent discontinuities. [1]

The 2008 Second Circuit opinion reconstructed evidence in the trials concerning the killings of D'Amato and other figures. It discussed testimony that internal opponents accused D'Amato of usurping authority, diverting criminal money and violating their expectations about sexual conduct. These were reported motives in criminal testimony and investigative accounts, not a moral judgment endorsed by the encyclopedia or a conclusion that sexual orientation constitutes criminal wrongdoing. [1][8]

A central legal caution applies: the witnesses' accounts may illuminate why prosecutors believed murders occurred, but convictions of certain defendants in the 2003 trial were later vacated. When assigning responsibility to a named individual, later findings, admissions or surviving judgments must be identified. [8]

Louis LaRasso and Internal Violence

Louis LaRasso was identified in government reporting as a long-standing senior figure in the organization. According to evidence summarized by the Second Circuit, he disappeared in November 1991, and the case record included testimony concerning his murder in a struggle involving senior DeCavalcante figures. The 2004 commission likewise situated the case within the family's internal power conflict. [1][8]

The court's account identifies trial evidence, reported meetings and testimony from cooperators. Such evidence is historically important, especially in an organization deliberately organized around secrecy. But the appellate history is equally important: the later vacatur prevents a reference entry from simply stating that every conviction from the 2003 case remains final. [8]

The violence illustrates how ordinary associates and family members could become victims of a criminal apparatus based on perceived threats to rank or income. When the historical record is incomplete, the encyclopedia avoids assigning an unproven personal role in a murder beyond what the record supports. [1][8]

Relations with New York Families and the Commission

DeCavalcante figures maintained interactions with other Mafia organizations, notably participants associated with Gambino, Genovese and Lucchese networks. The state commission cited both individual relationships and cooperative commercial activities, while federal labor investigators described an alleged connection with Lucchese-associated construction influence. These relationships varied by time and enterprise and do not establish permanent subordination to a single New York boss. [1][6][18]

The New Jersey report also related a purported post-D'Amato understanding in which New York-based Mafia leaders sought to limit future DeCavalcante initiations among New York residents. This is a state investigative account of criminal leadership practices, not a public legal rule or an independently certified treaty. The relevance is the wider Mafia's influence on a smaller family's membership policy. [1]

A smaller organization could be recognized as part of the national LCN network while having fewer geographic markets and less bargaining power than larger groups. It is historically inaccurate to collapse that relationship into the notion that every DeCavalcante crew was merely an arm of the Gambinos—or that the New Jersey family possessed independent command over all nearby criminal activity. [1][2][18]

December 1999 Racketeering Indictments

On December 2, 1999, federal prosecutors announced a series of indictments involving members or associates of several Italian-American Mafia organizations. The Department of Labor inspector general's report identified 39 defendants across three cases, including alleged DeCavalcante participants, and described theories involving murder, extortion, loansharking, gambling, theft, robbery, intimidation and corrupt construction relationships. The number 39 describes the broader cases, not an assertion that every defendant belonged to the DeCavalcante family. [6]

The federal account alleged that members connected to the DeCavalcante group sought advantage in New York City's painting and construction work by coordinating with Lucchese-linked influences and employing contractors whose arrangements allegedly violated collective bargaining agreements. These were serious charging allegations, distinct from findings made against particular defendants after trial or plea. [6]

The cases were significant because investigators connected numerous individuals, crews and financial schemes in a larger enterprise narrative. The later cooperation of members and associates exposed networks that earlier law enforcement largely knew through surveillance and informant reports. The extent of a cooperator's corroboration must still be evaluated claim by claim. [1][6][8]

October 2000 Leadership Indictment

An additional October 19, 2000 federal indictment targeted the DeCavalcante hierarchy in a sweeping RICO prosecution. The Department of Labor's semiannual report stated that Giovanni Riggi and multiple reputed acting bosses and senior officers were named in an indictment containing 63 counts. Alleged offenses included construction extortion, murder and murder conspiracy, loansharking, illegal bookmaking and securities fraud. [7]

The breadth of the charging document reflected a key strategic shift: rather than treat labor corruption, stock fraud and violent enforcement as unrelated, prosecutors attempted to show they were connected through a continuing organization. It also demonstrated the risks of leaders using multiple businesses and crews to diversify illicit income. [1][7]

However, the indictment was not the trial verdict. Every defendant's subsequent plea, trial, dismissal and appeal requires separate treatment. The appellate decision discussed below concerns three defendants from the later trial, not an automatic disposition of the entire 2000 charging package. [7][8]

Vincent Palermo, Anthony Rotondo and Cooperating Witnesses

Vincent “Vinnie Ocean” Palermo emerged as a senior figure in the late 1990s, described by the state commission as part of a ruling group with Girolamo Palermo and Charles Majuri. Following the large federal investigation, Vincent Palermo became a government cooperator, as did Anthony Rotondo and Anthony Capo, according to the 2004 New Jersey report. The public record of their testimony materially expanded what prosecutors could allege about the group's internal operations. [1]

Cooperation challenged the practice commonly described as omertà—the expectation that members would not disclose criminal activity to authorities. It also created evidentiary tensions: former participants sometimes testified in exchange for sentencing consideration or other benefits. Their statements required testing through cross-examination, independent records and judicial instructions. [1][8]

The case therefore illustrates both how a criminal enterprise can be investigated from within and why a serious scholarly history should distinguish cooperator testimony from a final fact finding against a specific defendant. Reliance on untested or inadmissible statements was consequential in the later appeals. [8]

The 2003 Vitabile, Abramo and Schifilliti Trial

In 2003, a Manhattan federal jury convicted Stefano Vitabile, a longtime DeCavalcante consigliere, and captains Philip Abramo and Giuseppe “Pino” Schifilliti on multiple counts arising from a prosecution that encompassed murder, racketeering and construction-related offenses. The government presented testimony from former members and evidence of historical violence. These were actual jury verdicts at the time, not merely accusations. [8]

The trial record also included acquittals on particular counts and findings that certain predicate acts had not been proved. Treating a complex multi-count verdict as a finding that every allegation was true would misstate the legal history. [8]

The district court entered judgments in 2006, with life sentences. But the subsequent 2008 Second Circuit ruling vacated the three appellants' convictions because of the use of non-testifying co-conspirators' guilty-plea statements in a manner inconsistent with the defendants' Sixth Amendment right to confront witnesses. The resulting legal history is not “convicted in 2003 and remained finally convicted.” [8]

2008 Second Circuit Vacatur and Evidentiary Limits

The case reported at 541 F.3d 94 (2d Cir. 2008) is a core source for understanding the DeCavalcante prosecutions. The court held that admitting eight plea allocutions of co-conspirators who did not testify violated standards established under Crawford v. Washington; it vacated the judgments of Vitabile, Abramo and Schifilliti and remanded for further proceedings. [8]

Vacatur is not equivalent to an acquittal. It removes the identified judgments and sends the case back for lawful resolution. It also is not equivalent to a final surviving conviction, which is why the original life sentences should not be quoted as final without discussing the appeal and researching any subsequent court action. [8]

The evidentiary lesson is especially important in organized-crime cases. Plea statements by persons who are unavailable for cross-examination can be prejudicial when used to establish events or conspiracy elements against other defendants. No encyclopedia article should use a historical DOJ press release describing the initial verdict while omitting the subsequent appellate correction. [8]

Riggi’s Later Cases and Death in 2015

Riggi continued to figure in later federal proceedings after the early-1990s labor prosecution. The 2008 appellate opinion describes a 1993 guilty plea involving conspiracy to murder Gaetano “Corky” Vastola, while contemporaneous reporting of Riggi's death describes his later admission concerning the 1989 murder of Fred Weiss. These are distinct proceedings from the reversed 1990 trial convictions and should never be conflated. [8][13]

John Riggi died in August 2015, aged 90, according to Associated Press reporting. His death marks the end of the career of a man state and federal investigators had identified for decades as a central New Jersey crime-family figure. It does not, by itself, establish an immediate or undisputed replacement. [13]

Reports about his philanthropy or popularity in parts of Elizabeth and Linden supply social context but cannot negate criminal admissions or establish guilt for unrelated persons. Public reputation and judicial responsibility are distinct questions. [13]

The 2015 New Jersey Undercover Investigation

On March 12, 2015, the U.S. Attorney for the District of New Jersey announced the arrests of ten people described as DeCavalcante members and associates, charging conduct linked to a proposed murder, drug distribution, an intended prostitution enterprise and other alleged offenses. Named defendants included Charles Stango, Frank Nigro, Paul Colella and Anthony Stango. The charging announcement gave the government's view at that point, rather than a final verdict for every named defendant. [9]

Prosecutors alleged that Charles Stango, a reputed captain, sought senior approval for the murder of a rival and communicated with individuals he believed could carry out the attack. Other defendants were accused of cocaine transactions and developing a prostitution-related operation. The 2015 case demonstrates renewed federal visibility into criminal networks after the major prosecutions of 1999–2003, but it does not disclose the family’s full membership or a verified current boss. [9]

A careful retrospective must continue from charges to dispositions: Anthony Stango subsequently pleaded guilty and was sentenced in 2016, and Charles Stango pleaded guilty to his separate murder-plot offense and was sentenced in 2017. The cases must not all be described simply as the defendants being “convicted of murder.” The intended victim in Charles Stango's plot was not killed. [10][11][12]

Anthony Stango’s 2016 Sentence

On January 5, 2016, federal prosecutors announced that Anthony Stango, described as an associate of the DeCavalcante family, had been sentenced to 72 months' imprisonment. He had pleaded guilty in a case involving cocaine distribution, use of an interstate facility in connection with an unlawful prostitution business, and possession of a firearm by a convicted felon. The sentence followed an adjudicated plea, rather than relying solely on the original complaint. [10]

The Justice Department's sentencing account stated that Stango admitted selling about 1,915 grams of cocaine during eight transactions with an undercover officer between late 2014 and early 2015. It also described his efforts to plan a prostitution enterprise and the firearm offense. Those facts establish specific conduct by one defendant, not a precise revenue estimate for the broader organization. [10]

The case illustrates the persistence of illicit commercial activity in a weakened criminal network while underscoring a limit on attribution: the fact that a defendant was identified as an associate does not prove the existence of a single centralized command over each offense. [9][10]

Charles Stango’s Guilty Plea and 2017 Sentence

On December 7, 2016, Charles Stango admitted using a telephone—an interstate facility—with the intent to arrange the murder of a rival. He also admitted violating the terms of his supervised release. According to the Justice Department, two men with whom he discussed the killing were in fact undercover FBI agents, and the purported victim was not harmed. This is a conviction arising from a plot and communications, not a conviction for a completed homicide. [11]

On March 28, 2017, a federal judge sentenced Stango to 120 months in prison and three years of supervised release. The later sentencing document resolves the outcome more accurately than quoting the 2015 charges or the 2016 plea announcement alone. [12]

The case demonstrates that DeCavalcante-associated violence was not merely a matter of twentieth-century history. Yet the verified legal disposition remains personal to the convicted defendant, and the records do not support turning the case into a claim that every individual named in the original operation was found guilty of the same conspiracy. [9][11][12]

Media Representations and The Sopranos

The New Jersey Mafia has had a cultural afterlife through the television drama The Sopranos. Journalistic accounts and statements attributed to the series' creator have identified the DeCavalcante group as one of several inspirations for fictional portrayals of New Jersey organized crime. The show is not a documentary about the family or an authorized record of the organization's internal decisions. [13]

A resemblance between a television character and a historical gangster must not be converted into a factual claim without independent support. The program combines invented plots, dramatic compression and motifs drawn from multiple organized-crime histories. This encyclopedia treats cinema and television primarily as evidence of public representation, not as evidence that a crime occurred. [13]

The family's established historical relevance instead rests on official investigations, judicial opinions, pleas, convictions and credible reporting of the periods described above. [1][3][5][8][12]

Present-Day Status and the 2026 Evidence Boundary

The publicly documented 2015–2017 cases establish that persons identified by federal prosecutors as DeCavalcante members and associates committed or were charged with particular crimes decades after the organization's most prominent midcentury phase. Those proceedings support a cautious statement of continued activity during the period actually covered by the case records. [9][10][11][12]

In 2026, New Jersey's State Commission of Investigation held a public hearing on the changing face of organized crime. Coverage of the hearing identified La Cosa Nostra groups, including the Elizabeth-based DeCavalcante family, among organizations discussed in the state's wider crime landscape, while emphasizing that gangs, traffickers and outlaw motorcycle clubs also play substantial roles. This is a contemporary institutional discussion, not a published, verified DeCavalcante organizational roster. [14][15]

Some online lists provide exact 2025–2026 boss names, membership totals and purported succession dates. This article does not adopt those claims without an attributable court document, dated investigative record or independently corroborated reporting. The 2004 membership estimate and the 2015 indictment's leadership allegations cannot simply be rolled forward into 2026. [1][9][14][15][16]

Accordingly, the entry regards the group as a historically documented Mafia family with later prosecutions and ongoing public mention, while making no definitive claim about its current boss, precise numerical strength or yearly criminal revenue as of November 13, 2026.

Historical Chronology

Key Historical Figures

The following persons appear in the cited historical record. An entry may identify an investigative designation, association or historical role and does not imply that the person was convicted of every offense mentioned elsewhere in the article. [1][2][5][8][9][13]

  • Nicholas Delmore: Predecessor leader in official state accounts; died in 1964. [1][19]
  • Simone “Sam the Plumber” DeCavalcante: Namesake boss; 1971 government report documents federal gambling guilty plea and sentence. [1][3]
  • Giovanni “John” Riggi: State-identified successor; associated with construction and labor investigations; died in 2015. The initial 1990 convictions were reversed on appeal. [1][5][13]
  • Louis LaRasso: Senior member whose 1991 disappearance and killing became part of later federal testimony. [1][8]
  • John D’Amato: Acting leadership figure reported murdered after internal conflict in the early 1990s. [1][8]
  • Giacomo “Jake” Amari: Reported acting leader between the D’Amato crisis and his own death in 1997. [1]
  • Vincent “Vinnie Ocean” Palermo: Acting-panel figure who later cooperated with the government. [1]
  • Girolamo “Jimmy” Palermo: Senior member on the late-1990s reported leadership panel; unrelated to Vincent Palermo according to the commission. [1]
  • Charles Majuri: Identified in government records as a senior member or member of a ruling panel, with 2004 New Jersey gaming exclusion documentation. Those sources do not prove a 2026 leadership title. [1][16]
  • Stefano Vitabile: Longtime consigliere; 2003 federal conviction vacated by the Second Circuit in 2008. [1][8]
  • Philip Abramo: Captain associated with securities-fraud and violence investigations; 2003 federal conviction vacated in 2008. [1][8]
  • Giuseppe “Pino” Schifilliti: Captain in federal trial records; 2003 federal conviction vacated in 2008. [1][8]
  • Anthony Rotondo and Anthony Capo: Former DeCavalcante figures who testified as government cooperators according to state reporting. [1]
  • Charles Stango: Defendant who pleaded guilty to use of an interstate facility with intent to commit murder; sentenced to ten years in 2017. [11][12]
  • Anthony Stango: Described by prosecutors as an associate; sentenced in 2016 for admitted drug, prostitution-related and firearm crimes. [10]

Terminology

  • La Cosa Nostra (LCN): Law-enforcement term for the American Mafia's regional criminal families, not a label for Italian-American communities. [18]
  • Crime family: A Mafia organization; not necessarily composed of relatives.
  • Boss / acting boss: Leader or temporary replacement, depending on source and period.
  • Underboss: Senior member subordinate to a boss.
  • Consigliere: Senior counselor associated with internal administration and disputes.
  • Caporegime / capo: Captain responsible for a crew.
  • Soldier / made member: A person treated by the organization as formally inducted.
  • Associate: Person working with members without established formal induction.
  • Ruling panel: Temporary or collective governing group reported during leadership disruption.
  • Labor racketeering: Criminal exploitation of employment, contracting, union processes or labor payments.
  • Loansharking: Lending or collection involving unlawful or extortionate terms and conduct; precise legal elements depend on the charge.
  • RICO: Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act; statutory framework used to address qualifying enterprise-related criminal patterns.
  • Vacated conviction: Judgment set aside by a court; neither a surviving final conviction nor necessarily an acquittal.
  • Plea allocution: A defendant's in-court admission forming part of a guilty plea, with separate evidentiary limits when used against others.

Research and Editorial Standards

Outcome-first legal reporting. This entry distinguishes a charge, allegation, plea, jury verdict, sentence, appellate reversal, vacatur and acquittal. The 1990 Riggi verdict and 2003 Vitabile–Abramo–Schifilliti verdict are especially important examples of why a headline can become outdated after appeal. [5][8]

Source hierarchy. Judicial opinions are primary for holdings and procedural history; Justice Department announcements are primary for the particular charging, plea or sentencing announcements they report. State commission reports supply carefully dated investigative syntheses, and journalistic reports supply contextual history. Neither testimony nor an investigative statement is automatically proof beyond a reasonable doubt against every person it discusses. [1][3][5][8][9][12]

Time-specific intelligence. A government report from 1989 or 2004 should never be copied directly into a present-day leadership list. Official records may conflict with later judicial outcomes, and confidential investigative data may be unavailable. This entry deliberately omits precise contemporary member counts, alleged annual income and unsupported claims of a 2026 successor. [1][2][14][15][16]

Community distinction. Individuals involved in organized crime are not representative of immigrants, Sicilians, Italian Americans, union members, construction workers or residents of Elizabeth. The harms from racketeering can include the destruction of honest competition, the theft of wages and investments, and violence affecting families and bystanders. [1][5][19]

Editorial cutoff. Information was reviewed through November 13, 2026. The current case status of an individual or any developments after that date should be checked against relevant dockets and subsequent government releases. Selected References

Sources are numbered as cited above. Court opinions provide procedural and appellate holdings; public prosecutor announcements distinguish charges from dispositions; state investigative reports summarize dated intelligence. Sources do not uniformly prove every historical attribution. References are historical evidence, not continuous monitoring of a clandestine organization's present state.

  1. New Jersey State Commission of Investigation — The Changing Face of Organized Crime in New Jersey (May 2004), pp. 91–96 and 121–124. Principal state investigation for organizational origin, structure, early-2000s figures, 1999–2000 prosecutions, labor and construction racketeering, and dated membership estimates; investigatory assessment rather than adjudication of every statement.
  2. New Jersey State Commission of Investigation — 1989 Organized Crime Report, DeCavalcante Family section (transcription). Dated state investigative account of John Riggi, regional activity, union influence and the 1989 indictment; contemporaneous assertions must be read alongside later appellate decisions.
  3. U.S. Department of Justice — Attorney General Annual Report, 1971. Official account of Sam DeCavalcante and other defendants pleading guilty to federal interstate gambling offenses, with DeCavalcante receiving a five-year sentence.
  4. TIME — Crime: Taping the Mafia (June 20, 1969). Contemporaneous journalism on the public release of FBI surveillance logs and the investigation of Sam DeCavalcante.
  5. United States v. Riggi, 951 F.2d 1368 (3d Cir. 1991; rehearing denied 1992). Authoritative appellate account of the 1990 Riggi–Timpani trial, RICO acquittals, the convictions on other counts, and reversal with remand over restricted recross-examination.
  6. U.S. Department of Labor, Office of Inspector General — Semiannual Report, October 1999–March 2000. Government report of the December 1999 multi-family racketeering indictments and allegations about DeCavalcante-connected construction and painting businesses.
  7. U.S. Department of Labor, Office of Inspector General — Semiannual Report, October 2000–March 2001. Official report of the October 2000 indictment targeting alleged DeCavalcante leadership and a broad claimed pattern of racketeering.
  8. United States v. Riggi (Mannarino), 541 F.3d 94 (2d Cir. 2008). The Second Circuit vacated convictions of Stefano Vitabile, Philip Abramo and Giuseppe Schifilliti due to Confrontation Clause violations and remanded; recounts 2003 verdicts and trial evidence.
  9. U.S. Attorney, District of New Jersey — Ten Members and Associates Arrested (March 12, 2015). Initial charges involving Charles Stango, Frank Nigro, Paul Colella and others; allegations not interchangeable with later guilty pleas.
  10. U.S. Attorney, District of New Jersey — Anthony Stango Sentenced to Six Years (January 5, 2016). Defendant’s guilty plea and 72-month sentence for cocaine distribution, interstate prostitution-related offense and felon firearm possession.
  11. U.S. Attorney, District of New Jersey — Charles Stango Admits Using Interstate Facility to Commit Murder (December 7, 2016). Specific guilty plea in a murder-plot case in which the alleged intended victim was not killed.
  12. U.S. Attorney, District of New Jersey — Charles Stango Sentenced to Ten Years (March 28, 2017). Verified sentencing outcome: 120 months of imprisonment and three years of supervised release.
  13. Associated Press — Convicted Leader of Noted New Jersey Crime Family Dies at 90 (August 5, 2015). Contemporaneous reporting of John Riggi’s death and a 2003 admission relating to the 1989 Fred Weiss murder; also provides cultural context.
  14. New Jersey State Commission of Investigation — The Changing Face of Organized Crime, Northern New Jersey Public Hearing (2026). Official record that state investigators revisited organized-crime conditions in a 2026 hearing; not a finding identifying the current DeCavalcante hierarchy.
  15. New Jersey Monitor — Officers Warn Organized Crime Remains a Strong Presence in New Jersey (May 20, 2026). Independent hearing coverage naming DeCavalcante among historically based groups still discussed by investigators, while noting other organizations now more prevalent.
  16. New Jersey Division of Gaming Enforcement — Charles N. Majuri Exclusion Order (2004). Official casino exclusion describing a named individual’s status in 2002–2004 investigations; not evidence of a particular leadership role in 2026.
  17. Federal Bureau of Investigation — Newark Field Office History. Institutional background on anti-Mafia investigations in New Jersey, including the DeCavalcante family among multiple locally active LCN groups.
  18. Federal Bureau of Investigation — La Cosa Nostra. National institutional overview of the Italian-American Mafia and the distinction between the New York Five Families and other regional groups.
  19. Congressional Record — Union County Grand Jury Presentment on Sam DeCavalcante and Garden-Apartment Construction (December 1969). Primary-source reproduction of a county grand-jury presentment on reported construction and labor consulting activities; presentment not an adjudicated verdict.
