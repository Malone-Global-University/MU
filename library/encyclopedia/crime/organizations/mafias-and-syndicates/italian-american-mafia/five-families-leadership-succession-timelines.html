How to Read the Timelines

The Five Families — Bonanno, Colombo, Gambino, Genovese, and Lucchese — took their modern form after the 1931 reorganization of New York’s Italian-American Mafia. The organizations predated the modern names. This reference traces recognized bosses and predecessor leaders, then separately records acting, front, street, and rival leadership. The distinction is necessary because several figures held a title while incarcerated, and other figures exercised operational authority in their absence.[1][13]

Boss / recognized boss
The figure ordinarily treated as the senior leader for the period. “Reported boss” means a public-source identification that has not been independently confirmed as a present-day appointment.
Acting boss
A delegated leader exercising management when the nominal boss is imprisoned, ill, absent, or shielding himself from scrutiny.
Street boss / front boss
A leader running operations or serving as a visible representative; a front boss may obscure a concealed senior leader.
Rival boss / contested period
A period when competing factions asserted authority or another family’s leaders disputed an appointment.
Approximate dates and asterisks
c. means circa; * means the 2026 identification is a dated public-source assessment, not verification of uninterrupted or current private leadership.

Editorial cutoff: November 10, 2026. Dates before 1931 refer to predecessor networks. Overlapping years are intentional. A boss’s imprisonment did not necessarily end his nominal tenure.

1. Gambino Crime Family

Earlier leadership sequence: D’Aquila → Mineo → Scalice → Mangano → Anastasia → Gambino

The Gambino succession includes two of the most consequential murders in New York Mafia history: the killing of Albert Anastasia in 1957 and the murder of Paul Castellano in December 1985. The post-Gotti period also requires a distinction between a recognized title and leadership exercised on the street.

Earlier leadership sequence: Morello → Terranova → Masseria → Luciano → Costello → Genovese

The comparison between Philip Lombardo, Vincent Gigante, Frank Tieri, and Anthony Salerno demonstrates why nominal and visible authority must be separated.

3. Lucchese Crime Family

Earlier leadership sequence: Reina → Pinzolo → Gagliano → Lucchese → Tramunti → Corallo → Amuso

The organization evolved from Gaetano Reina’s group and became the Gagliano family in 1930. The pivotal postwar transitions were Lucchese’s death in 1967, the rise of the Corallo administration in the early 1970s, and Amuso’s accession as the federal Commission prosecution removed senior leadership.

4. Bonanno Crime Family

Earlier leadership sequence: DiGaetano → Schirò → Maranzano → Bonanno → contested 1960s → Rastelli → Massino

The Bonanno organization presents two unusually important distinctions: Joseph Bonanno continued to contest his removal after other Mafia leaders withdrew recognition in 1964, and Carmine Galante became an influential de facto leader without universal recognition as formal boss in the 1970s.

5. Colombo Crime Family

Earlier leadership sequence: Profaci → Magliocco → Colombo → Persico → Russo → post-2022 transition

The Colombo family experienced repeated violent succession struggles. The shooting of Joseph Colombo in 1971 separated the nominal title from effective authority. Carmine Persico’s imprisonment led to acting leadership and the 1991–1993 Persico–Orena war, while recent succession reporting must be dated and attributed.

Major Turning Points Across the Five Families

1931
Masseria and Maranzano are killed; the modern five-family system and Commission arrangement take shape under Luciano and other leaders.
1951–1957
Mangano disappears, Anastasia is killed, Carlo Gambino succeeds, and Costello relinquishes the future Genovese family to Vito Genovese.
1963–1968
Colombo replaces Magliocco; a rival leadership conflict disrupts Joseph Bonanno’s long tenure.
1971–1976
Joseph Colombo is incapacitated; the Bonanno leadership passes through Evola and Rastelli; Paul Castellano succeeds Carlo Gambino.
1985–1987
Castellano is murdered; John Gotti rises. The Mafia Commission prosecution removes or imprisons multiple senior leaders.
1991–1993
The Colombo family experiences a violent Persico–Orena succession war; Gotti and Amuso enter long-term imprisonment.
2002–2005
John Gotti dies, Joseph Massino becomes a government cooperator, and Vincent Gigante dies in federal custody.
2019–2026
Carmine Persico and Andrew Russo die in separate years; Frank Cali is murdered; later reports identify a new Colombo succession without a fully verified administration roster.

Correction note: Some standard succession summaries disagree about the Corallo, Gagliano, and late Colombo transfer dates. The narrative above uses approximate periods where the evidence is not definitive; the source register documents the editorial basis.

Research Method and Evidence Standards

Priority of evidence. Court findings, sworn testimony tested in proceedings, contemporaneous investigative documents, and government press materials are preferred for specific legal and dated claims. Serious historical research supplies context for earlier eras; specialist crime reporting supplements recent undisclosed leadership information but must be attributed and treated as provisional.

Titles are not interchangeable. An “acting boss” may be a true operational leader without replacing the official boss. Conversely, a front boss may appear to lead while another official holds concealed authority. Rival claimants can appear in overlapping date ranges. Imprisonment, death, assassination, retirement, deportation, and formal succession are distinguished rather than collapsed into a single event.

Editorial limitations. This chronology does not claim access to closed intelligence files, exhaustive original archival records, or an internal roll of each organization. Apparent precision in many published boss lists exceeds their source evidence, particularly before 1931 and in the current decade. The asterisked 2026 designations must be revisited as new court records or corroborated reporting become available.

Community distinction. Mafia membership is a specific, evidentiary question. The organizations described here do not represent Italian-American residents, immigrants, businesses, or cultural communities as a whole.
