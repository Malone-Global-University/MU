Overview

The Patriarca crime family, often called the New England Mafia or New England La Cosa Nostra (NELCN), is a historically documented Italian-American organized-crime family associated principally with Providence, Rhode Island, and Boston, Massachusetts. The familiar name comes from Raymond L. S. Patriarca, whose long tenure in the mid- and late twentieth century made him a dominant figure in regional organized crime. The organization nevertheless preceded his leadership and survived his death in 1984. [1][2][23]

The history of the family is not simply a succession of colorful underworld personalities. Its record includes illegal gambling, extortionate loans, violent enforcement, corruption, and the use of business and social connections to sustain unlawful operations. It also includes unusually informative federal prosecutions: the 1986 convictions of Gennaro Angiulo and others, the electronically recorded 1989 Mafia induction ceremony, the 1990–1992 proceedings against the family’s administration, and early-twenty-first-century racketeering prosecutions. [3][4][9][10]

This article distinguishes historical leadership, descriptions made by investigators, and judicially established criminal conduct. It does not presume that every person named in a prosecution committed every alleged crime, nor does it infer a present-day boss or membership count from decades-old records. Its editorial information cutoff is November 13, 2026.

The designation “family” is a term of Mafia hierarchy, not a claim that the group is one biological family. Its members were never representative of Italian-American residents, immigrant communities, the restaurant trade, labor unions, or Providence and Boston neighborhoods as a whole. [2][23]

Court opinions use Patriarca Family both as the name of a historically identifiable enterprise and as the subject of specific prosecutions. That dual use makes evidence attribution essential: a conviction in a RICO case establishes the specified offenses for the convicted defendant, not a universal finding about all persons mentioned in the court record. [3][4]

Early New England Mafia Networks

Before the Patriarca era, ethnic and neighborhood criminal enterprises operated across New England amid expanding markets for illegal betting, extortion, and later bootlegging. Organized groups emerged through alliances and violent disputes rather than through a single publicly documented act of incorporation. Historical accounts identify early leaders and factions in both Boston and Providence, but the boundaries between an early gang, an aligned crew, and the later formally recognized La Cosa Nostra family cannot always be reconstructed with precision. [1][23]

Prohibition (1920–1933) enlarged the opportunities for illegal alcohol distribution. It did not invent organized crime or establish exclusive territorial boundaries. In practice, gambling and other rackets survived the return of legal alcohol, while relationships forged in the earlier period helped connect criminal groups to transport, entertainment, commercial property, and other business settings.

A careful chronology begins with predecessor networks, not the inaccurate claim that Raymond Patriarca founded all Mafia activity in the region. The durable regional family label became associated with him only after the preexisting organization changed leadership. [1]

Phillip Buccola and the Pre-Patriarca Leadership

Historical overviews commonly identify Phillip Buccola as a senior predecessor to Raymond Patriarca in the New England organization. The shift between the Buccola and Patriarca periods is usually placed in the early-to-mid-1950s. The Mob Museum’s historical account states that Patriarca was exercising daily authority from 1952 while Buccola’s retirement and departure were completed by 1954. These details concern a gradual succession, not necessarily a formally documented transfer on a single day. [1]

Leadership of regional operations was shaped by alliances in Boston and Providence and by recognition from established Mafia figures elsewhere. No successor’s authority should be understood as an unrestricted corporate right to command every criminal actor in New England. Rival groups, independent offenders, and occasionally hostile associates continued to operate in the same area.

For editorial consistency, this entry treats the often-cited 1952–1954 span as a transition and distinguishes Patriarca’s effective influence from the end of Buccola’s own involvement. [1]

Raymond L. S. Patriarca: Consolidation and Authority

Raymond Loreda Salvatore Patriarca (1908–1984) became the organization’s namesake and the central figure in its mid-century history. A prominent regional underworld figure, he exercised authority from Providence while maintaining relationships with criminal operations in the Boston area. His headquarters were closely associated with a legitimate cigarette-vending business in Providence’s Federal Hill district, a specific meeting and business location that should not be generalized to the surrounding community. [1]

Accounts differ on whether 1952 or 1954 should mark the beginning of his rule. The first is associated with effective daily control in one major historical account, while later institutional retrospectives sometimes use the completion of the transfer. His death on July 11, 1984 is a definite endpoint for his personal leadership. [1][2]

Patriarca’s prominence included inter-family relationships beyond New England. Such relationships could assist arbitration or business coordination, but they were not proof of a national command structure or of direct participation in every racket conducted by loosely associated local operators.

Providence and Boston: Two Interlocking Centers

The New England family’s history is best understood through a two-center organization. Providence was closely associated with Patriarca’s executive authority, while a substantial Boston contingent organized gambling and other rackets through its own senior members and crews. The relationship varied over time: at some points Boston leaders accepted central authority, while at others they resisted directions or disputes arose over the proceeds of criminal activity. [2][4][5]

The Boston division is especially well documented because electronic surveillance captured conversations at its operational headquarters. The record shows both organizational affiliation and the limits of top-down control. For example, judicial discussion of the later Raymond Patriarca Jr. period recounts Boston figures attempting to withhold proceeds from Providence, illustrating that titles did not guarantee obedience. [5]

The terms “Boston Mafia” and “Providence Mafia” can be useful geographic shorthand, but historians should not automatically treat them as two wholly separate La Cosa Nostra families in every period. The correct description depends on the organization, date, and evidence at issue. [2][4]

Gennaro Angiulo and the Boston Administration

Gennaro “Jerry” Angiulo was a central figure in the family’s Boston operations and served as a senior officer under Raymond Patriarca. Court records identify other Angiulo family members and Boston figures, including Donato Angiulo, Francesco Angiulo, and Ilario Zannino, in cases arising from surveillance and prosecutions of the city’s organized-crime network. [2][3]

Angiulo’s position illustrates the significance of a strong regional subordinate: senior personnel could supervise local operations, authorize meetings, and exercise control over gambling and loansharking while remaining within a larger organization associated with a Providence-based boss. Criminal authority was transmitted through relationships and enforcement, not merely a standardized written chain of command.

The federal case ultimately resulted in 1986 convictions for Gennaro, Donato, and Francesco Angiulo and a separate 1987 conviction for Zannino. These adjudicated outcomes make the Angiulo period one of the better-established portions of the New England crime family’s record. [3]

Criminal Enterprises and Revenue Systems

The Patriarca family’s documented income sources changed over time. They included illegal gambling and bookmaking, loansharking and extortionate collections, protection payments demanded from businesses, and activities carried out through criminal partnerships. Violence and the threat of violence provided leverage in some operations. The availability of a criminal market did not mean every crew worked in it or that every scheme was centrally designed. [3][9][10][12]

Bookmaking and unauthorized gaming created recurring payment and collection systems. Loansharking could place borrowers under continuing coercive pressure; a borrower might remain in debt despite repeated payments. Extortion reached beyond gambling customers when legitimate enterprises were forced to pay to avoid threatened disruption, loss, or violence. [9][12]

Particular individuals were investigated or convicted in specific drug-trafficking cases, including the 2015–2016 Louis DiNunzio prosecution. That does not support a blanket claim that the family operated every narcotics transaction attributed to criminal actors in New England. [13]

Businesses, Labor, Corruption, and Victims

Organized-crime involvement with an otherwise lawful business can take many forms: an illicit partner, a hidden share of proceeds, placement of illegal machines, forced payments, or coercion affecting employment and commercial access. A legitimate establishment does not become a Mafia enterprise simply because an offender visits it, works with a customer, or demands money from its owner.

The New England family’s prosecution history includes extortion connected to gambling machines and Rhode Island adult entertainment businesses. The 2012 Manocchio and DiNunzio outcomes show business owners paying money over long periods under an extortionate system. The 2016 Spagnolo–Quintina case concerned efforts to protect revenue from video-poker machines. These are concrete examples of racketeering affecting commerce, not evidence of general control over every business in an industry. [9][10][12]

The cost to victims includes lost business income, pressure to remain silent, impaired competition, threats to personal safety, and institutional distrust. Treatment of the rackets as an abstract revenue portfolio should not obscure those consequences.

Geography, Regional Crews, and Neighboring Organizations

The family was historically based in Rhode Island and Massachusetts, with court accounts and prosecutions identifying contacts or members connected to Connecticut and other nearby states. These geographic associations describe documented individuals and activities; they do not characterize a city, neighborhood, or its immigrant populations as criminal. [2][4][19]

A New England regional label should not be taken to encompass every criminal enterprise in New England. Springfield, for instance, has had documented associations with a New York-based Mafia family in particular periods. Organized-crime territories were negotiated, contested, or informally understood; they were not equivalent to state administrative boundaries.

For research purposes, distinguish home base, crew activity, associate contact, and formal leadership authority. An individual traveling between Boston and Providence does not establish that one city’s crew commanded every operation in the other.

Hierarchy, Induction, and Delegated Authority

Like other American La Cosa Nostra groups, the Patriarca organization was described in court as a hierarchy with a boss, senior officers, captains, initiated members, and associates. The particular makeup changed amid incarceration, internal conflict, and federal cases. Court records help identify specific officers in the early 1980s but cannot be used as a timeless organizational chart. [2][4][23]

An inducted member (“made member”) was distinguished from a noninducted associate, who might work with members but not have the same internal status. A caporegime led a crew; the boss or an acting leader might rely on senior intermediaries. In disputes, nominal ranks could coexist with factions that accepted different sources of authority.

The 1989 recorded induction ceremony provided unusual primary evidence of initiation as a real event rather than a film convention. But the ceremony also occurred amid a violent contest for control, making it important to examine who approved it and which factions recognized the new members. [4][5][19]

Relations with Other Mafia Families and Independent Gangs

The family maintained contacts with broader American La Cosa Nostra organizations, including New York families and participants operating across state lines. Federal judicial records refer to New York Mafia figures consulted during the disputes of the late 1980s. Such consultation does not make the New England family a permanently subordinated branch of a particular New York organization. [4][5]

Boston’s Winter Hill Gang and its leaders James “Whitey” Bulger and Stephen Flemmi occupied a separate organizational identity from the Patriarca family. Court records discuss both conflict and cooperation among the networks, including an alleged association-in-fact racketeering enterprise spanning participants with different affiliations. The family and the Winter Hill Gang should never be collapsed into one group merely because some members collaborated. [6][7]

The relationships also became consequential in a major FBI informant scandal. Findings about misconduct by agents or independent gang leaders require separate attribution from the underlying racketeering convictions of Patriarca-family defendants. [6][18]

Electronic Surveillance and the Boston Case

During the early 1980s, investigators obtained court-authorized electronic surveillance targeting Boston-area organized crime. Recordings of conversations and meetings created evidence of operations, rank, proceeds, and command relationships that could be tested in criminal court. The use of recordings was especially consequential because Mafia secrecy had often made it difficult to prove more than individual transactions. [3][6]

The 1994 United States v. Angiulo decision records the convictions and sentences stemming from these investigations. It also documents the appellate review of those convictions, a reminder that the evidentiary record had been subjected to adversarial proceedings rather than standing only as an intelligence assessment. [3]

Surveillance should not be represented as infallible. The significance of any intercepted statement depends on the speakers, context, chain of custody, authorization, and later judicial findings. The separate federal informant controversy shows that even investigations that produced valid convictions could involve serious wrongdoing elsewhere in their handling. [6][18]

1986–1987 Angiulo and Zannino Convictions

In 1986, Gennaro Angiulo, Donato Angiulo, and Francesco Angiulo were convicted on federal racketeering-related charges. A later federal opinion specifies their original prison sentences: 45 years for Gennaro, 20 years for Donato, and 25 years for Francesco. Separately tried Ilario Zannino was convicted in 1987 of loansharking and gambling offenses and received a 30-year sentence. [3]

The court reported that the First Circuit upheld the convictions in United States v. Angiulo and United States v. Zannino. The 1994 proceedings concerned later sentence-reduction motions; an account that mentions those motions should not misrepresent them as blanket reversals of the original convictions. [3]

The prosecutions significantly weakened the Boston hierarchy identified with the Angiulo period. They did not end illegal gambling in the region, and they should not be equated with permanent dissolution of the entire New England family. Subsequent court records show continuing organizational disputes after these defendants were imprisoned. [4][5]

1984 Succession: Raymond Patriarca Jr.

Raymond L. S. Patriarca died on July 11, 1984. Court records identify his son, Raymond J. Patriarca (“Junior”), as succeeding him in leadership. The transfer is well documented, but it did not mean that all officers accepted the son’s authority equally or that the younger Patriarca exercised his father’s degree of personal influence. [2][4]

The younger Patriarca had to negotiate the interests of competing Boston and Providence personnel. Judicial findings describe a growing gap between formal designation and effective command, including Boston members who resisted sharing proceeds. The circumstances created incentives for rival factions to challenge his legitimacy. [4][5]

The family’s later crisis illustrates why historical leadership lists should include descriptions such as formal boss, acting boss, rival claimant, and practical controller when supported, rather than placing a single uninterrupted date span beside each name.

1989 Power Struggle: Grasso, Salemme, and the Boston Faction

By 1989, sharp rivalries involved the younger Patriarca’s Providence-centered leadership and members of a Boston faction. Court accounts discuss William Grasso, the family underboss, and Francis “Cadillac Frank” Salemme as important figures during the dispute. In June 1989, Grasso was killed, and Salemme was shot but survived. These were distinct acts within an unfolding succession struggle. [4][5]

Judicial reconstruction connects the conflict with Joseph Russo, Vincent Ferrara, Robert Carrozza, and others in the Boston faction. A court’s description of a criminal faction is more specific than the claim that “Boston fought Providence” as if whole cities were participants. Only the identified individuals and networks are relevant. [4][5]

This breakdown helped prompt internal negotiations, new inductions, and federal surveillance. It demonstrates an important structural feature: a nominal boss could remain formally recognized while other members conducted operations, made threats, or pursued alliances outside his effective direction.

October 29, 1989: Recorded Mafia Induction Ceremony

On October 29, 1989, the FBI secretly recorded an induction ceremony for new members of the Patriarca organization at a residence in the Boston area. It was a landmark source because the recordings captured a secret ritual described in investigations and later presented in judicial proceedings. Court opinions address the legal authorization for the surveillance and its admissibility. [4][5][19]

The evidentiary value lies in the recorded event, participants, and subsequent review in court. Details of oaths and induction practices became evidence in enterprise prosecutions; an encyclopedia need not reproduce the ceremonial language or portray it as a uniform procedure followed by every American Mafia group at all times.

The ceremony had a specific political context: the younger Patriarca had accepted certain Boston-backed inductions while facing pressure from the faction. Consequently the recording is relevant to both the organization’s membership rules and the breakdown of central leadership. It cannot, by itself, establish who remained in command decades later. [4][5]

1989–1990 Federal Indictments and Enterprise Charges

Federal prosecutors issued indictments against multiple New England Mafia figures in November 1989, followed by major superseding charges unsealed in March 1990. Contemporary reporting described a broad prosecution spanning murder, extortion, kidnapping and racketeering allegations, as well as evidence obtained from the recorded induction. The broad accusation was not itself a finding of guilt for all named defendants. [4][19]

The use of RICO allowed federal prosecutors to present an allegedly continuing organization rather than prosecuting isolated gambling acts. Related charges included the federal Travel Act, which can concern interstate conduct facilitating unlawful activities. Later trials, guilty pleas, sentencing findings, and appellate decisions established outcomes for different defendants on different terms. [4][21]

A contemporaneous political statement that an indictment had destroyed the group was a prediction made at the enforcement announcement. Subsequent prosecutions in the 2000s and 2010s demonstrate why the claim cannot be adopted as a final historical conclusion. [9][10][19]

Nicholas Bianco and the 1991 Racketeering Verdicts

Nicholas “Nicky” Bianco became a prominent figure amid the displacement of the younger Patriarca’s leadership. Contemporary federal trial reporting described him as the reputed leader at the time of the 1991 prosecution. The exact relationship between internal acceptance, investigators’ identification, and court-adjudicated conduct should not be collapsed into an undifferentiated “boss” title. [20]

On August 8, 1991, a Hartford jury convicted Bianco and seven other defendants of at least one racketeering offense. Contemporary reporting also described convictions connected to the murder of William Grasso among certain defendants. It is accurate to say those defendants were convicted of the reported offenses, but not to assert that every person accused of involvement was convicted of the same killing. [20]

The verdicts came during a larger period of cooperation, court testimony, and evidence from intercepted meetings. They were important enforcement results but did not demonstrate that the secret family ceased every form of activity thereafter.

Raymond Patriarca Jr.’s 1991 Plea and 1992 Sentence

In December 1991, Raymond J. Patriarca entered a guilty plea on federal racketeering and Travel Act charges. The detailed sentencing opinion distinguishes the charges to which he pleaded from other violent acts that prosecutors sought to attribute to him at sentencing. This distinction is essential to accurate legal history. [4][21]

On June 17, 1992, Judge Mark L. Wolf imposed 97 months in federal custody, a $50,000 fine, and three years of supervised release, with additional court-ordered costs. The court rejected the argument that Patriarca should automatically be sentenced for every crime committed by other people in the organization during his period of leadership. [4]

The First Circuit later reviewed the sentencing dispute. Thus an account of Junior’s prosecution should neither sanitize his admitted racketeering offenses nor treat the government’s full set of disputed allegations as crimes that had been individually proved against him. [4][21]

Vincent Ferrara, Joseph Russo, and Plea-Based Outcomes

Boston figures including Vincent Ferrara and Joseph Russo were central to the late-1980s litigation and negotiated resolutions. The court discussed multiple interlocking plea agreements and sentence calculations, as well as the different ways the defendants represented their organizational relationships. These case-specific facts should not be flattened into a list of identical convictions. [4]

Ferrara appeared prominently in evidence of internal Boston operations and the recording of the initiation ceremony. Russo was identified in judicial discussion of challenges to Junior’s leadership and in later court proceedings involving earlier crimes. Judicial records must be read with care to separate a defendant’s plea, the government’s allegations about other conduct, and independent evidence concerning co-defendants. [4][5][6]

The fact that a former senior participant later became an investigator’s source or a witness does not eliminate credibility questions. Cooperators may have incentives to minimize their own role, explain events selectively, or obtain concessions; court corroboration and cross-examination remain central to the historical assessment.

Francis “Cadillac Frank” Salemme and Early-1990s Leadership

Francis P. “Cadillac Frank” Salemme emerged as a major family leader after the 1989 conflicts. Federal prosecutors later identified him as the organization’s boss in the early 1990s, and records of the 1993 killing of Steven DiSarro establish how connections between criminal leadership and a Boston nightclub became a matter of federal investigation. [16][17]

Salemme’s history must be treated in layers: a violent succession context, the 1995 racketeering prosecution, later cooperation and obstruction proceedings, and the separate 2018 conviction for the earlier murder. An indictment, a cooperation arrangement, and a later verdict have different evidentiary significance; none should be substituted for another. [6][16][17]

In judicial accounts, Salemme’s connection to the Winter Hill network also brought FBI informant handling and law-enforcement corruption into the historical record. This overlap was an association among criminals and agents in specific circumstances, not proof that the Patriarca organization and Winter Hill Gang were the same enterprise. [6][7][18]

The Boston FBI Informant Scandal and Evidentiary Integrity

Federal court proceedings revealed grave failures in the handling of FBI informants James “Whitey” Bulger and Stephen Flemmi, whose links to criminal activity and law-enforcement sources became central to litigation involving organized crime in Boston. Judicial findings in United States v. Salemme describe misconduct, undisclosed arrangements, and failures that impaired the fair administration of justice. [6][7]

Those findings require careful separation from the Patriarca family’s own operations. Bulger and Flemmi were associated with the Winter Hill Gang, while particular arrangements with Patriarca-family figures were studied in the litigation. Their status as informants and the unlawful acts of certain public officers neither erase valid convictions against other defendants nor excuse official misconduct. [6][18]

For a scholarly encyclopedia, the lesson is institutional as well as criminal: criminal witnesses, source-handling records, court orders, and law-enforcement assertions must be compared rather than accepted uncritically. The government’s ability to investigate one enterprise cannot justify shielding another participant from legal accountability.

Luigi Manocchio and the Post-1990s Organization

Luigi “Louie” Manocchio was identified by federal prosecutors as a longtime New England La Cosa Nostra leader. The Justice Department’s 2012 account states that he held leadership roles in an extortion conspiracy operating over many years. Later official records are more reliable for his admitted conduct than unqualified online succession charts. [8][9]

The federal prosecution placed particular emphasis on coercive payments from Rhode Island adult entertainment establishments. The government described a protection-payment operation extending from 1995 through 2009; the existence of such a scheme contradicts claims that the family’s capacity for criminal activity ended conclusively in 1990. [9]

Manocchio’s period should not be presented as a single uninterrupted and fully visible tenure. Federal documents name him as former boss and underboss, and describe later acting leadership by Anthony DiNunzio. The precise transitions between nominal and functioning leaders should be attributed to the records and their dates. [9][10]

2011 Multi-District La Cosa Nostra Indictments

In January 2011, the Justice Department coordinated a multi-district initiative against alleged La Cosa Nostra defendants; additional charges involving New England defendants were announced in a March 1, 2011 superseding indictment. The nationwide announcement identified 127 charged individuals across multiple districts, including 91 described as alleged LCN members and associates. Those nationwide totals cannot be treated as a count of Patriarca-family members. [8]

In the Rhode Island component, the government charged Manocchio and others in allegations involving extortion and racketeering. As of the indictment date those statements were accusations, not adjudications. Later guilty pleas and sentences provide the basis for the settled outcome of specific defendants. [8][9][10]

The case illustrates the importance of grouping charges by defendant and court: a press release concerning national coordination can contain multiple families, separate enterprises, and charges with very different subsequent results.

2012 Manocchio Guilty-Plea Sentence

On May 11, 2012, federal authorities announced that Luigi Manocchio had been sentenced to 66 months in prison for participation and leadership in a racketeering and extortion conspiracy. The Justice Department reported that the enterprise demanded and received approximately $800,000 to $1.5 million in protection payments from several Rhode Island adult entertainment businesses during 1995–2009. Those figures describe the particular extortion scheme in the charging and plea record, not the annual revenue of the entire crime family. [9]

The same announcement reported a 37-month sentence for Raymond “Scarface” Jenkins in a separate admitted extortion conspiracy involving threats against an individual and his wife. It would be inaccurate to attribute the same victims, amounts, or counts to both defendants without regard to their separate admissions. [9]

These dispositions are concrete examples of continued racketeering activity decades after the prosecutions of the early 1990s. They also demonstrate how extortion can operate for long periods against legitimate businesses while generating repeated proceeds for criminal intermediaries.

Anthony DiNunzio: Acting Leadership and 2012 Sentencing

Anthony L. DiNunzio of East Boston was described in federal proceedings as an acting leader of New England La Cosa Nostra following Manocchio. He pleaded guilty on September 13, 2012 to conspiracy to participate in a racketeering enterprise under RICO. On November 14, 2012, he was sentenced to 78 months in federal prison and three years of supervised release. [10]

The government reported his admitted participation in collecting protection payments, including monthly proceeds in the $2,000–$6,000 range from Rhode Island adult entertainment businesses. The federal account also described his efforts to obstruct or interfere with administration of justice by influencing or delaying testimony. These details belong to his adjudicated case and should not be generalized to all associates. [10]

The same DOJ release identified other defendants sentenced in the enterprise, including Edward Lato and Alfred Scivola. A leadership snapshot from 2012 is not evidence that DiNunzio or any other named individual held the same authority throughout later years. [10]

Operation Excalibur and 2014 Federal Cases

In 2013–2014, federal investigators pursued an operation described as Operation Excalibur, examining alleged illegal gambling, extortion and drug activity among particular New England Mafia-associated defendants. The investigations led to distinct cases rather than a judicial declaration about every member of the broader family. [11][13]

A 2014 indictment identified Anthony “Spucky” Spagnolo as an alleged acting boss and named Pryce “Stretch” Quintina in an extortion case involving video-poker machine operations. The government’s attribution of rank was a statement in the indictment, not a conviction for holding a leadership title. [11]

Later guilty pleas and sentences in the extortion prosecution offer a firmer basis for describing the underlying criminal conduct. Contemporary analysis must retain both stages rather than treating the 2014 announcement as the final legal disposition. [11][12]

Spagnolo and Quintina: 2015 Guilty Pleas, 2016 Sentences

Anthony Spagnolo and Pryce Quintina pleaded guilty in December 2015 to conspiracy to interfere with interstate commerce by extortion. On May 2, 2016, the District of Massachusetts announced that Spagnolo was sentenced to 20 months and Quintina to 15 months in federal prison. [12]

The prosecution concerned payments used to maintain the placement of video-poker equipment in social clubs, including conflict when an establishment sought to replace existing machines. The account demonstrates a commercial coercion scheme rather than merely the presence of a gambling device. [11][12]

The outcome establishes their admitted extortion conspiracy. It does not automatically prove every allegation in the indictment concerning Mafia rank, nor does it justify declaring either person to be the family’s leader at the article’s 2026 cutoff.

Louis DiNunzio and the 2015–2016 Drug Case

A separate federal prosecution involved Louis L. DiNunzio, identified by prosecutors as the son of former New England family leader Anthony DiNunzio. In September 2015, Louis pleaded guilty to conspiracy to distribute marijuana; on January 19, 2016, he was sentenced to 18 months in prison and ordered to forfeit $10,000. [13]

The Justice Department described the particular investigation as involving shipments of large quantities of marijuana during 2013–2014. The case should be understood as an offense-specific conviction of a particular person, not as proof that every New England Mafia group was centrally responsible for that drug market. [13]

Naming a family relationship has explanatory value only when it clarifies the evidence. Kinship itself does not establish formal induction, a leadership role, or criminal liability; the plea is the relevant adjudicated event.

Steven DiSarro: 1993 Murder and Recovery of Remains

Steven A. DiSarro managed a South Boston nightclub known as The Channel, in which Francis Salemme and his son had financial interests. Prosecutors later established that Salemme feared DiSarro might cooperate with federal investigators. DiSarro was murdered on May 10, 1993; his body remained missing for more than two decades. [16][17]

In March 2016, investigators recovered DiSarro’s remains behind a former mill in Providence, Rhode Island. The recovery and subsequent proceedings connected the 1993 homicide to the 2018 trial of Salemme and Paul Weadick. The murder’s location, concealment, and eventual discovery illustrate the interconnections between criminal actors operating in Massachusetts and Rhode Island. [14][15][16]

This was a killing of an individual facing pressure around a federal investigation, not an episode to be reduced to a colorful gangland anecdote. DiSarro’s family suffered decades without a complete account, and the case became a major test of witness protection and accountability.

Robert DeLuca: Cooperation, False Statements, and Sentence

Robert P. DeLuca, a former New England La Cosa Nostra captain, entered a cooperation arrangement after his 2011 racketeering arrest. In November 2016, he pleaded guilty to one count of obstruction of justice and two counts of making false statements because he had lied about the DiSarro disappearance and other Mafia-related murders. This was not merely an accusation of imperfect memory; the Department of Justice reported a guilty plea to specified federal offenses. [14]

On August 28, 2018, DeLuca was sentenced to 66 months in federal prison and three years of supervised release. Prosecutors stated that his falsehoods had delayed recovery of DiSarro’s remains until other information became available in 2016. [15]

The case shows why even an apparently insider witness must be corroborated. A cooperation agreement is not a guarantee of truthful disclosure, and omission of damaging facts can both obstruct justice and distort the historical record.

2018 Salemme and Weadick Verdicts and Life Sentences

On June 22, 2018, a federal jury in Boston convicted former New England Mafia boss Francis P. Salemme and former associate Paul M. Weadick of murdering a federal witness, Steven DiSarro. The prosecution established that the killing had been designed to prevent DiSarro from cooperating with investigators examining Salemme’s activities. [16]

On September 13, 2018, both men received life sentences. The Justice Department’s sentencing announcement described the killing at a residence in Sharon, Massachusetts, and subsequent concealment of the body in Rhode Island. It was an adjudicated murder case, not an unresolved murder allegation. [17]

Salemme died in federal custody on December 13, 2022. His death concluded his personal legal and biographical chronology; it did not independently establish the existence, dissolution, or leadership of any contemporary crime family. [22]

Witness Testimony, RICO, and Court Findings

The principal federal cases against New England organized crime relied on a combination of wiretaps, insider testimony, enterprise allegations, forensic investigation, and witness cooperation. RICO enabled prosecutors to connect specified illegal activities to a continuing criminal enterprise. The Travel Act appeared in the earlier Patriarca proceedings because interstate acts can play a role in racketeering schemes. [3][4][21]

Each legal stage must remain separate. Indictment: charge. Guilty plea: admission to the agreed offense. Verdict: jury or judge’s adjudication on particular counts. Sentencing: punishment and factual findings subject to legal limits. Appeal: possible affirmance, reversal, or change of the judgment. Confusing the stages can wrongly attribute guilt or conceal later exoneration.

This is especially important for claims about murders said to have been ordered by a leader. In Raymond J. Patriarca’s sentencing opinion, the court did not automatically impose responsibility for every crime allegedly committed by members under his nominal leadership. [4]

Later Decline and the Limits of a 2026 Organizational Assessment

By the late 2010s, federal cases documented a considerably different New England criminal landscape from the one associated with Raymond Patriarca and Gennaro Angiulo. Earlier prosecutions removed or imprisoned major figures, investigations exposed organizational procedures, and cooperating witnesses supplied evidence of both rackets and internal disputes. The surviving public record, however, is episodic rather than a complete census of clandestine criminal activity. [3][10][12][17]

This reference therefore does not declare an undisputed 2026 boss, membership total, annual revenue, or regional hierarchy. A person’s alleged rank in a 2014 indictment, an interview, or later crime commentary is not sufficient evidence of a present-day appointment. The most recent cases analyzed substantively here reached adjudicated results during 2016–2018, with Salemme’s death documented in 2022. The absence of a later case in this selected register does not prove inactivity. [12][17][22]

The responsible distinction is between a historically established criminal organization, the particular activities established in dated court records, and unconfirmed contemporary claims. A future update should be made when verifiable court decisions or reliable primary reporting change this evidentiary picture.

Terminology and Legal Distinctions

La Cosa Nostra (LCN)
Law-enforcement and historical term for networks collectively called the American Mafia, including regional criminal families.
Patriarca family / New England LCN
Names for the New England organized-crime organization; not interchangeable with every gang in New England.
Boss / acting boss
Recognized leader or person performing comparable functions for a period; identification may be disputed or source-dependent.
Underboss
Senior officer below a boss; responsibilities and actual authority differ over time.
Consigliere
Senior adviser associated with mediation or executive decisions.
Caporegime / capo / captain
Member who directs a crew or manages local operations.
Soldier / inducted or “made” member
Person formally admitted as a member under an organization’s internal rules.
Associate
Person cooperating with or working for members without confirmed formal induction.
Protection payment
Payment extracted by threat, coercion, or other unlawful pressure; not an ordinary voluntary business fee.
Bookmaking
Taking or operating wagers, which may be unlawful depending on facts, jurisdiction and licensing.
Loansharking
Unlawful lending or extortionate collection; the legal offense depends on statutory elements.
RICO
Federal Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act; criminal and civil provisions concern specified patterns of racketeering and enterprises.
Travel Act
Federal statute addressing certain interstate or foreign-commerce activity facilitating unlawful activities.
Cooperator
Defendant or witness who provides information or testimony in an investigation; credibility must still be examined.
Indictment
Formal accusation returned by a grand jury, not a finding of guilt.
Acquittal / reversal
Distinct outcomes: acquittal is a not-guilty judgment on a tried charge; appellate reversal changes a lower court result according to the reviewing decision.

Research and Editorial Standards

Source hierarchy. This article prioritizes court judgments and official Justice Department statements for charges, pleas, verdicts, sentences, and documented investigations. Contemporary reporting and reputable historical synthesis supplement earlier organizational history. Court records may also present disputed testimony or allegations; their findings must be distinguished from quoted claims. [2][3][4][6][9][10]

Procedural precision. A statement that a defendant was charged in 1990 or 2014 must not be written as a statement that the defendant was convicted. When a plea or verdict is available, it is identified by offense and date. An alleged leadership position is described as an allegation unless supported by a stronger adjudicative record. [11][12][19][20]

Institutional misconduct. Findings concerning FBI source handling and corruption are not suppressed to preserve a simple “law versus Mafia” narrative. They are documented alongside criminal convictions, without treating official misconduct as exoneration of every offender or treating independent gangs as identical to the Patriarca family. [6][7][18]

People and communities. The family was an organized-crime enterprise, not a representative body for Italian Americans, Boston’s North End, Providence’s Federal Hill, immigrants, labor organizations, or legitimate businesses. Where specific commercial venues were victimized or used by offenders, responsibility belongs to documented participants.

Editorial cutoff and limitations. This is a synthetic institutional history through November 13, 2026; it is not a full docket-by-docket or original-archive edition. Links identify sources for further verification. No precise current hierarchy, membership total, or annual revenue is claimed without a suitable public primary record.
