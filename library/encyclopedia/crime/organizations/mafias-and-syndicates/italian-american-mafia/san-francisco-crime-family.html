Overview

The San Francisco crime family, also known as the Lanza crime family, was a historically documented northern California organization of the Italian-American Mafia, or La Cosa Nostra (LCN). Its organizational history is principally associated with Francesco “Frank” Lanza, Anthony Lima, Michael Abati (also rendered Abate), and James “Jimmy” Lanza. Unlike the better-known New York families, it was small, geographically concentrated, and repeatedly described by government investigators as having limited evident control of major criminal markets. [1][2][3][6]

The San Francisco organization grew in a changing environment of waterfront commerce, North Beach businesses, Prohibition, illegal gambling, factional violence, and reciprocal connections with organized-crime groups in other cities. The family was distinct from the neighboring San Jose organization identified under Joseph Cerrito and from the Los Angeles family. An individual’s contacts with outsiders do not make the groups a single chain of command. [1][2][14]

This entry provides an evidence-qualified overview through November 13, 2026. It records contemporaneous FBI findings, state reports, historical scholarship and judicial outcomes, while distinguishing surveillance and informant claims from convictions. It does not assign a verified current boss or treat later San Francisco crimes, Italian-American businesses or the surrounding community as proof that the historic family continued operating.

“Family” designates an organized-crime grouping; it does not describe all of San Francisco’s Italian-American residents, all people sharing the Lanza surname, or every independent operator in the Bay Area. Modern estimates should not be derived from a 1967 FBI roster or an undated online hierarchy. [2][3][13]

The Historical Record: Intelligence, Trials, and Retrospective Accounts

San Francisco’s Mafia history is often told through lists of bosses, nicknames and alleged rackets. Those compilations can obscure what each source actually proves. The 1967 FBI report identifies a current boss and underboss through named informant channels; the 1970 FBI inspection reports a change in leadership that is absent from many simplified histories. Neither file is a public criminal judgment against every person named. [2][3]

The 1978 California Organized Crime Control Commission published a retrospective succession table, while later state reports assessed whether such families were still operational. These accounts are not always reconcilable. The historical problem is not resolved by selecting the longest or most familiar sequence and silently disregarding contemporaneous contradictions. [1][4][5]

Christina Ann-Marie DiEdoardo’s Lanza’s Mob is an important specialized published history of the organization; its publisher describes the book’s extended treatment of North Beach business life and the family’s eventual dissolution. The public bibliographic record, however, is not a substitute for collating each page, archival citation or underlying interview. [6]

North Beach, the Waterfront, and Local Commercial Life

North Beach and San Francisco’s waterfront were important environments for the family’s early history. The city’s Italian-American districts included family restaurants, fish distribution, food importation, shipping-related trades, clubs and many other legitimate businesses. Proximity to such trade networks created ordinary opportunities for social acquaintance but cannot be treated as proof that commercial participants belonged to a criminal conspiracy. [6][11]

Prohibition created illegal alcohol revenues in cities throughout the United States. In San Francisco, the waterfront and its transport networks offered potential channels for smuggling, distribution and illicit exchange, while lawful operations continued alongside them. The organization did not own or create Fisherman’s Wharf; any claim about the “founding” or criminal domination of an entire district should be rejected unless supported by specific documentary evidence. [1][6]

James Lanza’s obituary records his publicly known connections to restaurant, wine and specialty-food businesses. Such civic and commercial facts are biographically relevant, but an obituary neither validates Mafia status nor establishes that every enterprise associated with a relative was used for criminal purposes. [11]

Before Consolidation: Independent Operators and Prohibition

Accounts of the 1920s describe several competing bootleggers and criminal actors in San Francisco rather than one stable citywide Mafia command. Illegal alcohol sales, gambling and other vice businesses could overlap, but investigators and historians must distinguish the existence of a racket from a demonstrated hierarchy controlling it. [1][6]

When historians use an early-1930s establishment date for the family, they usually mean the period when the Lanza grouping emerged more clearly from this fragmented underworld. A precise legal incorporation or formal charter did not exist. Early “boss” designations are therefore partly retrospective labels rather than contemporaneous entries in an official registry. [1][6][7]

The Violent Conflicts of 1928–1932

Early histories connect San Francisco’s underworld to a sequence of killings and retaliation during and after the late-Prohibition years. The names sometimes associated with this turbulent period include Jerry Feri, Alfredo Scariso, and Luigi Malvese. The individual events and their proposed links to later leadership are better understood as parts of a retrospective reconstruction than as a single court-adjudicated war commanded by Francesco Lanza. [6][7]

The murder of a criminal rival does not establish the person who ordered it absent a conviction, corroborated records or other persuasive evidence. The encyclopedia therefore avoids converting every unresolved homicide from these years into an asserted “family hit.” It is also essential to recognize that contemporary press treatment of Italian immigrant neighborhoods sometimes amplified ethnic stereotypes. [6]

Francesco “Frank” Lanza and the Family’s Early Leadership

Francesco Lanza is generally identified in later official and specialist summaries as the early leader or namesake of the organization. The California commission’s 1978 leadership table lists his tenure as beginning on an undetermined date and ending in 1937. The absence of a fixed beginning in the government table is significant: it does not support a falsely exact 1928 or 1932 inauguration date. [1][6]

His position in later Mafia succession narratives should be separated from the broader legitimate Lanza family history. Family relationships can help explain patterns of trust and migration, but inherited surnames did not automatically determine initiation, criminal liability or legitimate business ownership. Specialist genealogical research provides useful context without resolving every organizational claim. [15]

Anthony Lima and the Post-1937 Administration

After Francesco Lanza’s death, the California commission named Anthony Lima as his successor. Its retrospective table spans approximately 1937–1958; specialist research, relying on a different reconstruction of the underlying records, places an earlier transfer of practical authority to Michael Abati around 1953. These different dates should be read as evidence of a contested transition, not as a typographical error to be erased. [1][7]

Lima became important to the later historical record partly because accounts derived from FBI material identify him as a confidential source. A participant’s retrospective account may disclose otherwise inaccessible relationships but may also reflect self-protection, selective recollection and the incentives accompanying contact with agents. [7]

Late-1960s and 1970 documents further complicate any claim that Lima never exercised authority again after the 1950s. Those developments are addressed separately below rather than folded into a fictitious uninterrupted leadership line. [3]

The 1947 Nick DeJohn Murder and Failed Prosecution

In May 1947, Nick DeJohn (also rendered De John), a Chicago-associated criminal living in northern California, was murdered. His body was discovered in the trunk of an automobile in San Francisco. The case attracted sustained attention because of its alleged links to Midwest and West Coast criminal figures. [8]

A later San Francisco Chronicle retrospective explains that three men were tried but the charges were dismissed after District Attorney Edmund “Pat” Brown stated that a crucial prosecution witness was unreliable. The episode is a documented homicide and failed prosecution; it is not a valid basis for asserting that the defendants were convicted of DeJohn’s murder or for attributing a murder order to Lima or Abati as an established fact. [8]

The case is central to the organization’s historiography precisely because official criminal proceedings can fail to produce a conclusive account. Newspaper descriptions of Mafia involvement, informant reports and judicial findings must remain distinct categories of evidence.

The Midcentury Organization and Federal Scrutiny

The 1950s brought wider federal and state attention to interstate organized crime, including congressional inquiries and enforcement against bookmaking, extortion and racketeering. New York and Chicago received far more publicity, but investigators also examined California’s regional groups and the movement of known figures between states. [1][13]

San Francisco cannot be reduced to an “untouched” Mafia refuge. Investigative attention, commercial pressure and the limited public court record all affected the organization. At the same time, the lack of a major prosecution comparable to later New York RICO cases does not itself prove an extensive undetected criminal empire. Both extremes exceed available evidence. [1][2][3][6]

Michael Abati and the 1950s–1961 Transition

Michael Abati—rendered Michael Abate in the 1978 California commission’s leadership table—appears as the third major leader between Anthony Lima and James Lanza. The state table assigns 1958–1961; specialist research instead places Abati’s effective assumption of leadership around 1953. [1][7]

Accounts identify immigration-enforcement problems and deportation as major factors in the transition to James Lanza. It is safer to describe the early 1960s as a succession period than to infer a precise internal election. A 1967 FBI informant described Abati as having earlier bestowed a captain title on Vincenzo Infusino; the value and effectiveness of that title were themselves disputed. [2][7]

James Lanza, Joseph Cerrito, and the 1957 Apalachin Gathering

The November 14, 1957 Apalachin gathering in upstate New York exposed an interstate network of Mafia leaders and produced a major publicity and investigative watershed. California’s 1978 commission reported that James Lanza, identified with San Francisco, and Joseph Cerrito, identified with San Jose, were in the region and lodged in a Scranton-area hotel around the meeting. [1]

The commission distinguished Lanza and Cerrito from people arrested at the gathering: according to its account, both avoided apprehension. Consequently, a statement that Lanza was “convicted in the Apalachin case” would be wrong. The event is meaningful for tracing interstate relationships, not for imputing liability for every participant’s actions. [1]

California’s account names other figures who traveled in the same wider circle, including Los Angeles representatives and Russell Bufalino. Travel overlap and hotel arrangements show contact opportunities but do not establish that the San Francisco group was subordinate to a particular national family. [1]

James “Jimmy” Lanza and the 1961 Succession

James Joseph Lanza, son of the earlier family leader, is conventionally identified as the San Francisco boss from approximately 1961. The 1978 California report dates the transfer to 1961, and a September 1967 FBI document independently reported that informants named Lanza as boss. This combination supports his central historical role, even though his effective title later became contested. [1][2]

Unlike many nationally notorious mob leaders, Lanza maintained a substantial public-facing life in commerce. His 2006 obituary records his food, wine, wholesale and restaurant associations as well as his family survivors. None of that establishes the culpability of his relatives; likewise, press descriptions of an underworld leader do not constitute criminal convictions. [11]

A longstanding label such as “boss, 1961–2006” is best understood as a retrospective shorthand for Lanza’s role and continuing influence, not as evidence that he personally directed a unified racketeering operation every day for 45 years. The contemporaneous 1970 reports complicate that tidy chronology. [3][7]

The FBI’s September 1967 Organizational Findings

The released September 12, 1967 FBI report is a particularly important contemporaneous source because it separates informant assertions by date and source. It records an informant’s January 1967 assessment of three distinct West Coast LCN families: San Francisco, San Jose, and Los Angeles. For San Francisco, the source named James Lanza as boss and Gaspare “Bill” Sciortino as underboss. [2]

The same record placed Joseph Cerrito at the head of the San Jose family, with Emanuel Figlia identified as a senior figure there. The document therefore directly contradicts the notion that all Bay Area Mafia participants belonged to one San Francisco command. FBI informants can be mistaken about a title; nevertheless, the FBI was explicitly investigating distinct organizations. [2]

The report also names Vincenzo Infusino as a purported San Francisco captain whose designation may have been honorary. In this case, a paper hierarchy and working authority did not necessarily coincide. This is a reminder not to turn a period diagram into a universally accepted chain of command. [2]

Hierarchy, Crews, and the Limits of a Small Family

American LCN is commonly described with a boss, underboss, consigliere, captains, inducted members and associates. Those labels are useful in comparative history, but the small San Francisco organization did not always display every position at every moment. A 1967 informant said that one supposed captain, Infusino, had no members directly reporting to him and that members instead dealt with Sciortino. [2][13]

Authority could operate through personal ties, seniority, access to other families and localized income opportunities rather than a large bureaucracy. An associate might know a member or participate in particular misconduct without being initiated, knowing all leaders or committing all the enterprise’s offenses. [2][7]

The presence of a consigliere or captain in an intelligence roster is evidence of how the investigators or their sources understood a relationship at that moment. It should not be treated as an adjudicated or present-day title absent independent corroboration. [2][3]

Vincenzo Infusino and the Problem of Honorary Titles

The 1967 FBI report includes an unusually informative caution concerning Vincenzo Infusino of Modesto. An informant described him as having been appointed a capo de decina under Michael Abati, but asserted the position was largely honorary because Infusino had resources and influence that might benefit the organization. The source said he lacked an identifiable subordinate crew. [2]

This provides a case study in the difference between ceremonial standing and operational supervision. It would be misleading to list Infusino as commanding a ten-man crew simply because the Italian-derived title translates loosely as a captain of ten. The FBI report itself raises the limitation. [2]

Gaspare Sciortino and Practical Administration

Gaspare “Bill” Sciortino was identified in the 1967 FBI document as the San Francisco underboss under James Lanza. The same document attributes to an informant the opinion that other members reported to Sciortino rather than to the nominal captain Infusino. [2]

In the 1970 inspection material, Sciortino continued to appear as underboss while the top leadership was described as having shifted back toward Anthony Lima. These dated observations show why investigators should separate a stable operational intermediary from potentially fluid boss labels. [3]

The 1970 Leadership Dispute: Lanza, Lima, and Commission Recognition

One of the most consequential discrepancies in San Francisco Mafia history appears in October 1970 FBI inspection material. Agents reported that James Lanza had stepped aside and Anthony Lima had resumed the position of boss. An accompanying inspection comment said that the alleged transfer had not yet received Commission approval. [3]

This finding is difficult to square with simplified succession charts giving James Lanza uninterrupted control until 2006. The records can be partially reconciled if the 1970 change was brief, symbolic, disputed or reversed, but the precise duration and effect of the reported transition cannot be established here. The public archival extract supplies a contemporary claim, not a complete sequence of subsequent confirmations and reversals. [1][3][7]

The encyclopedia therefore retains both the conventional periodization and the dated FBI alternative. It avoids representing either as an unquestionably continuous chain of authority throughout the 1970s. [3]

Informants, Surveillance, and Source Reliability

FBI material records extensive efforts to develop top-echelon informants within northern California Mafia families and associated illicit markets. In 1970, the San Francisco Division described the role of confidential sources in reporting leadership and membership changes. The records were made as operational intelligence: they contain assessments, source descriptions, targets and leads, not a sequence of court judgments. [2][3]

That distinction is especially important because some reports attributed information to participants with their own interests. A claimed promotion may reflect the informant’s understanding of an internal conversation, while a recorded conviction would require a separate case record. A good organizational history uses these sources without converting all intelligence into established criminal acts. [2][3]

Released FBI records themselves must be read with attention to redactions, later archival production, mixed document dates, and whether information originated in direct observation or in an informant’s account. Citation to an archive PDF identifies the document, not the truth of each individual claim within it.

Membership Estimates and the Difference Between Count and Control

The 1970 FBI inspection stated that eight San Francisco family members and nineteen San Jose family members resided within the San Francisco Division’s territory. It also identified additional San Francisco members living in other FBI jurisdictions or abroad. Such counts refer to the agency’s known or believed individuals and geographic accounting rules in a particular year; they are not the present-day size of either organization. [3]

Crucially, the same inspection said its top-level informants had not established that either family exercised centralized control over the region’s gambling, prostitution, narcotics or other illicit activities. This is stronger evidence against broad assertions of pervasive territorial monopoly than the mere appearance of gambling in contemporary crime statistics. [3]

Specialist histories sometimes estimate peak numbers at roughly a dozen or two members. The sources use different boundaries and dates; precision beyond the underlying records is inappropriate. [7]

San Jose and the Joseph Cerrito Family: A Separate Organization

Despite proximity, San Jose was identified as an independent LCN family in the released 1967 and 1970 FBI material. Its historical leadership included Joseph Cerrito, and sources named Emanuel Figlia among its senior members. Shared contacts and overlapping commercial networks did not turn San Jose into a subordinate chapter of San Francisco. [2][3]

The distinction matters for several often-confused cases, including the later Marino investigation and violence involving San Jose-associated figures. Those events should not automatically be counted as direct San Francisco-family crimes. The Mob Museum’s historical study of the San Jose organization likewise treats it as a separate subject. [14]

When creating family comparison tables, report San Francisco and San Jose separately, and identify a documented alliance, shared participant or temporary cooperation only when an individual record supports the specific relationship. [1][2][3]

Los Angeles, Chicago, and Other Interstate Mafia Contacts

San Francisco’s alleged Mafia leaders were not isolated from national organized-crime circles. The 1957 Apalachin-related California account places James Lanza in the company of several other state and regional figures. FBI records later demonstrate contacts and intelligence exchanges about the families in San Jose and Los Angeles. [1][2]

Published specialist research describes connections with other figures active in the Bay Area, including Jimmy Fratianno, who had relationships with multiple Mafia groups. Those personal contacts should not be confused with a formal transfer of all local authority to Los Angeles or Chicago. A person’s affiliations and movements can change over time, and one participant may be relevant to more than one organizational history. [7]

Comparative articles should preserve the autonomy of the individual regional groups while explaining why intercity travel, gambling interests, and personal introductions made them part of a larger American Mafia milieu. [1][13]

Las Vegas and the Problem of Overgeneralized Casino Claims

Numerous American Mafia organizations maintained relationships with Nevada gambling and entertainment businesses in the mid-twentieth century. Specialist accounts have also connected some San Francisco figures to individuals active in Las Vegas. But interstate acquaintance is not proof that the Lanza family held a permanent ownership share in a particular casino, directed a nationally integrated skim, or enjoyed the same prosecutorial record as the Chicago–Kansas City operations. [1][7]

Any future claim of San Francisco participation in a specific casino-skimming scheme should identify its licensed property, dates, persons, federal case, and disposition. In the absence of such particulars, the relationship belongs in a section on reported connections, not an assertion of adjudicated enterprise revenue. This approach also prevents the stories of different West Coast families from being merged into one legend.

Illegal Markets and Revenue: What the Sources Establish

Accounts of the family’s history commonly mention bootlegging, illegal gambling, bookmaking, extortionate lending, and commercial coercion. These are plausible and sometimes specifically reported activities of individual criminals and associates. But the 1970 FBI inspection specifically questioned whether San Francisco’s surviving LCN organization controlled the main criminal markets across the city. [3][6][7]

  • Prohibition-era alcohol: historically relevant to early criminal networks, without implying continuous control after repeal.
  • Bookmaking and unlawful gambling: reported in the local underworld, but independently operating bookmakers must be distinguished from family-directed enterprises.
  • Extortion and coercive lending: should be described defendant by defendant where the record includes admissions or convictions.
  • Narcotics activity: California had illegal drug markets, but the mere existence of such markets is not proof they were dominated by the Lanza hierarchy.
  • Legitimate businesses: documented family members’ commercial interests do not establish criminal use of every associated business.

No reliable evidence identified for this article establishes an annual family revenue figure, complete historical market share, or present-day criminal portfolio. [3][5]

Legitimate Businesses, Professional Ties, and Liability

The public-facing lives of some figures named in Mafia reporting included restaurants, food distribution, wine, and other commercial work. James Lanza’s obituary supplies examples from his own career. Such activities may have provided income or social standing independent of any criminal allegations. Their existence should not be flattened into the assumption that an entire industry was an organized-crime front. [11]

Conversely, a lawful company may be exploited by a participant in a criminal enterprise. The relevant distinction is evidentiary: financial records, court findings, guilty pleas or reliable official reports tying a particular transaction to unlawful conduct provide a stronger basis for analysis than a business address or surname. The ethical approach protects both historical accuracy and innocent workers, customers, relatives and trade competitors.

Political Access Claims and the Need for Independent Evidence

San Francisco Mafia histories sometimes repeat assertions that local politicians were beholden to the Lanza organization. The mere fact that a lawyer, official or prominent businessperson met with, represented or knew an alleged racketeer does not establish criminal conspiracy, bribery, or institutional capture. Historical reputation and criminal liability are not interchangeable. [9][10]

These concerns are particularly visible in accusations surrounding San Francisco Mayor Joseph L. Alioto. The charges made by a magazine produced a prolonged civil libel case, not a criminal conviction of Alioto for Mafia membership or corruption. The record requires a dedicated treatment rather than a one-line insinuation in a list of alleged criminal allies. [9][10][18]

The 1969 LOOK Allegations and the Alioto Libel Litigation

In September 1969, LOOK magazine published an article linking Mayor Joseph Alioto to purported organized-crime relationships. Alioto sued the publisher for libel. The federal appellate record shows repeated trials, disputes about whether statements were false and defamatory, and litigation over the constitutional requirement of actual malice for a public official. [9][10]

The Ninth Circuit’s 1980 decision affirmed the judgment in Alioto’s favor. The ultimately reported damages were $350,000. Accordingly, an encyclopedic article cannot responsibly use the original 1969 accusations as established proof of a criminal alliance, ignore the litigation, or suggest that the case produced a criminal finding against the mayor. [9][18]

A civil libel ruling does not establish that no organized-crime figures ever attempted to influence San Francisco politics. It establishes a specific legal outcome concerning statements made in that publication. Both the scope and limitations of the judgment must be respected. [9][10]

California’s 1978 Organized-Crime Commission Assessment

The May 1978 First Report of California’s Organized Crime Control Commission placed the San Francisco group alongside the Los Angeles and San Jose organizations in a historical leadership table. Its San Francisco sequence lists Francesco Lanza (ending 1937), Anthony Lima (1937–1958), Michael Abate (1958–1961), and James Lanza (from 1961 to the report date). [1]

That sequence is useful as a dated state view of the family’s history, but it should not be read as a judicially authenticated record of every internal promotion. It is especially important that the 1978 table does not discuss the 1970 FBI intelligence claiming a return of authority to Lima. [1][3]

The commission’s narrative also documents that federal and state officials were concerned with criminals migrating or making contact across California and the wider United States. Those general findings cannot automatically be attributed to the much smaller San Francisco family. [1]

The 1986 State Report and the Family’s Apparent Dormancy

California’s 1986 organized-crime annual report, issued in 1987, described the Southern California organization as the only functioning traditional organized-crime family based in California under its investigative classification. The statement is an important indicator that state officials did not regard San Francisco and San Jose as comparably functioning organizations at that time. [4]

The wording does not mean that every historically affiliated member had died, that no independent crimes occurred in northern California, or that an exact day of legal dissolution can be assigned. “Functioning” is a law-enforcement analytical category, and a once-recognized family might retain a few aging members or social relationships without commanding an effective enterprise. [4][14]

The 1980s and 1990s: Aging Membership and Fragmented Activity

Specialist research depicts the San Francisco family as shrinking with age and limited recruitment after the high-visibility decades of the 1950s–1970s. Names occasionally appear in secondary accounts of later Bay Area gambling or narcotics cases. But an individual’s asserted family membership does not prove that the historical Lanza organization coordinated each case. [7]

Rather than describing an unbroken criminal government over San Francisco through the dot-com boom, the better-supported position is that whatever continued of the older hierarchy became difficult to distinguish from personal networks and independent operations. State reporting during the 1980s increasingly pointed investigators toward other forms of organized crime and away from a robust northern California LCN structure. [4][5]

California’s 2002–2003 Assessments of Traditional Organized Crime

The 2002 California organized-crime report described several different categories of criminal enterprise affecting the state and observed the activity of East Coast organized-crime groups. It should not be turned into a statement that a surviving San Francisco family controlled those operations. [12]

The 2003 California Department of Justice report emphasized diminishing remnants of traditional West Coast organized crime and described the Southern California family, rather than San Francisco, as the principal small locally based organization under discussion. The same report warned that incoming or cooperating members from other families could still act in California. [5]

This comparison captures a key principle: the decline of an indigenous local LCN family is not the disappearance of all organized crime from its city. Criminal enterprises outside the historic family must be researched on their own terms. [5][12]

James Lanza’s Death in February 2006

James J. Lanza died on February 14, 2006, according to his published obituary in the San Francisco Chronicle. His obituary describes a long life associated with San Francisco and nearby communities, including restaurant and specialty-food ventures. That date is well supported; claims about the precise moment the crime family itself ceased to function are not. [11]

Some leadership lists treat 2006 as the organization’s termination date because Lanza was its best-known surviving figure. Yet official state reporting had already described the northern California families as nonfunctioning or dormant in prior decades. It is therefore better to distinguish death of a named historical leader from institutional disappearance, which need not occur simultaneously. [4][5][11]

Status as of November 2026: No Verified Continuing Command

The sources reviewed for this entry do not independently establish a functioning San Francisco La Cosa Nostra administration as of November 13, 2026. The strongest public state assessments of the organization’s late history already described northern California’s old families as weakened or dormant decades earlier. No basis has been established for naming an undisputed 2026 boss, underboss, crew count or annual revenue. [4][5][12]

That conclusion must be expressed carefully. Lack of public evidence for a continuing organizational hierarchy is not proof that no one with historical Mafia connections remains alive or that no other organized-crime activity occurs in the Bay Area. Nor is a retrospective newspaper article or online roster sufficient to demonstrate a current command structure. [13][16]

Key Historical Figures

Francesco “Frank” Lanza
Early namesake and leader; California commission places the end of his leadership in 1937. [1]
Anthony Lima
Retrospectively listed as a successor to Francesco Lanza; tied to later FBI informant accounts and the disputed 1970 leadership change. [1][3][7]
Michael Abati / Abate
Midcentury leader identified between Lima and James Lanza; start date differs between state history and specialist reconstruction. [1][7]
James Joseph “Jimmy” Lanza (1902–2006)
Identified by 1967 FBI informants as boss; his reported long tenure conflicts with a 1970 FBI leadership notice. Death date documented by his obituary. [2][3][11]
Gaspare Orlando “Bill” Sciortino
Reported underboss who appears in dated 1967 and 1970 FBI intelligence; one source indicated members reported to him directly. [2][3]
Vincenzo Infusino
Modesto resident identified as a captain; FBI informant described his title as nominal and his lack of a crew. [2]
Joseph Cerrito
Leader of the separate San Jose Mafia family; reported in California’s Apalachin and leadership accounts. [1][2]
Emanuel Figlia
San Jose figure named by FBI informants, not a San Francisco administration officer. [2]
Nick DeJohn
Chicago-connected criminal killed in San Francisco in 1947; prosecution of suspects did not culminate in murder convictions in the cited retrospective. [8]
Jimmy Fratianno
West Coast Mafia figure with multiple regional connections; occasional San Francisco contact does not establish his membership in the Lanza family. [7]
Joseph L. Alioto (1916–1998)
San Francisco mayor, subject of 1969 organized-crime allegations that led to his successful civil libel suit; not identified here as a Mafia member. [9][10][18]
Christina Ann-Marie DiEdoardo
Historian and author of the 2016 specialized study Lanza’s Mob; included as a research contributor, not as a criminal organization member. [6]

Being named in a historical figure directory records the individual’s relevance to the subject, not guilt, conviction, induction or current affiliation.

Terminology

La Cosa Nostra (LCN)
Term used by federal investigators for the American Mafia’s network of regional organizations.
Family
Organized-crime group with a historically recognized internal structure; distinct from literal kinship and from local Italian-American communities.
Boss
Person understood to exercise primary organizational authority; the title is not evidence that authority was uninterrupted or exclusive.
Acting boss
A person exercising, claiming or temporarily receiving top-level authority during an absence or succession dispute.
Underboss
Senior coordinator beneath the boss; responsibilities varied by group and period.
Consigliere
Adviser or senior officer recognized in some LCN organizational descriptions.
Caporegime / capo de decina
Captain or crew leader; an honorary designation may not imply subordinates.
Inducted / made member
Person accepted as a recognized member under Mafia organizational norms, as distinct from an outside associate.
Associate
A person with connections to members; no automatic criminal guilt or knowledge of the whole enterprise follows.
Apalachin meeting
1957 gathering of Mafia-connected figures in New York state that attracted extensive police attention.
Commission
Inter-family coordination body described in organized-crime intelligence and participant histories; recognition of specific appointments varied.
Top-echelon informant
Confidential person whom the FBI assessed as having high-level intelligence access; information must still be corroborated.
Intelligence assessment
Law-enforcement information or inference; not itself a criminal conviction.
Acquittal / dismissal
Different legal outcomes: an acquittal follows adjudication of a charge, while dismissal ends a charge without the conviction alleged.
Libel
Civil claim concerning publication of defamatory falsehoods, distinct from a criminal racketeering charge.
RICO
Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act; legal framework for specified enterprise-linked crime patterns.

Research and Editorial Standards

Primary sources and provenance. The most important documents for this article are the September 1967 FBI San Francisco report, the October 1970 inspection materials and the state reports of 1978, 1986 and 2003. The 1967 and 1970 documents were released in federal archival collections and should be read as dated intelligence, not as judicial findings. [1][2][3][4][5]

Unresolved contradictions. The 1978 succession table gives Lima–Abati transition dates different from some specialist accounts, and the 1970 inspection reports a Lima return absent from standard lists that carry Lanza to 2006. These tensions remain explicit instead of being “fixed” through unsupported synthesis. [1][3][7]

Legal outcomes. Nick DeJohn’s killing did not result in the defendants’ murder convictions as claimed in some popular summaries; the contemporary witness issue and dismissal must be retained. Alioto’s libel judgment requires that the LOOK allegations not be repeated as proven fact. No confidential-source statement should be converted into a guilty verdict. [8][9][10]

Geographic and community safeguards. San Jose and Los Angeles were distinct organizations. The historical Mafia family is not an ethnic label and does not represent San Francisco’s Italian-American population, residents of North Beach, all waterfront workers, unrelated small businesses, or descendants of an identified figure. [1][2][13]

Contemporary limitations. Publicly accessible records reviewed for this edition do not establish a 2026 operating hierarchy. Modern status, organization size and revenue are left unquantified, and no individual is designated current boss without a dated, strong evidentiary basis. The source set is selective rather than an exhaustive original-file collation; more archival research could resolve some early disagreements.

The register distinguishes contemporaneous government intelligence, judicial decisions, government retrospective reports, specialist historical interpretation, press research, and biographical notices. A report of a claimed organizational role is not proof of a crime; original charges and appellate outcomes receive priority over rumor.
