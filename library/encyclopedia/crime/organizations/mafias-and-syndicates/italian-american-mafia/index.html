What were the Five Families?

The term Five Families refers specifically to New York's five principal Italian-American Mafia organizations. They operated as part of La Cosa Nostra (LCN), a broader U.S. network of crime families with shared traditions and, at various times, formal mechanisms for coordinating inter-family affairs. The FBI and U.S. Department of Justice identify the five New York families as Bonanno, Colombo, Gambino, Genovese, and Lucchese.[1][2]

“Family” is an organizational term rather than a description of an exclusively biological kinship group. Each organization included initiated members, often called made men or soldiers, and noninitiated associates. The families were neither interchangeable nor continuously united: their histories include cooperation, competition, leadership disputes, factional wars, and shifting alliances.

Their most consequential periods of influence were concentrated in the twentieth century, especially in and around New York City. They acquired revenue through illegal gambling, extortion, loansharking, narcotics trafficking, theft, fraud, and the corruption or coercion of parts of legitimate commerce and organized labor. Their operations had extensive victims, including business owners, workers, borrowers, drug users, and communities affected by violence and corruption.[1][3]

Scope of this entry. This page addresses the American Mafia and the Five Families of New York. They should not be conflated with Italy's distinct organized-crime groups, including Sicilian Cosa Nostra, the Camorra, and the ’Ndrangheta. Nor were the Five Families the only Mafia organizations in the United States.

Bonanno Crime Family

Earlier name: Maranzano family · Namesake: Joseph Bonanno

The Bonanno organization emerged from the circle associated with Salvatore Maranzano. Following Maranzano's killing in 1931, Joseph Bonanno assumed leadership and built one of the most durable individual tenures in the history of the Five Families. The organization developed extensive operations in New York and connections elsewhere in North America. Historical criminal activity included illegal gambling, extortion, loansharking, and narcotics trafficking.

Bonanno's standing eroded during the 1960s amid leadership struggles and serious conflicts with other Mafia figures. The subsequent decades saw recurring changes in authority and federal scrutiny. The family became central to the FBI's Donnie Brasco investigation, in which undercover agent Joseph Pistone infiltrated Mafia circles in the late 1970s and early 1980s. Later, longtime boss Joseph Massino's decision to cooperate with federal authorities marked a major breach in the leadership's tradition of secrecy.

The organization's continued presence in federal case records is evident in racketeering indictments and prosecutions. Its history should be separated into the early Bonanno leadership period, subsequent internal realignments, the Pistone investigation, and the era of leadership-level government cooperation.[1][5]

Colombo Crime Family

Earlier name: Profaci family · Namesake: Joseph Colombo

Originally associated with Joseph Profaci, the Colombo family became known for repeated internal conflict. Competition between Profaci's leadership and the Gallo faction contributed to violence in the early 1960s. After further leadership changes, Joseph Colombo rose to prominence and the organization acquired the name by which it is now generally identified.

Colombo publicly organized the Italian-American Civil Rights League, attracting attention beyond the usual covert setting of Mafia affairs. He was shot at a league rally in 1971 and remained incapacitated until his death in 1978. Later disputes, particularly those surrounding rival leadership factions in the late twentieth century, imposed substantial losses and instability on the organization. Carmine Persico was a central figure in much of the family's later history.

Colombo-related federal cases have documented the attempt to control economic activities through coercion. A 2021 indictment alleged a long-running scheme involving a Queens labor union and its benefit fund. In 2023, the Justice Department announced guilty pleas by fourteen defendants, including senior family figures and others involved in labor-racketeering and related offenses.[6][7]

Gambino Crime Family

Earlier name: Mangano family · Namesake: Carlo Gambino

The Gambino family evolved from the organization associated with Vincent Mangano. Albert Anastasia rose to leadership and was killed in 1957, clearing the way for Carlo Gambino, whose tenure lasted until 1976. The organization acquired interests across New York's criminal economy, including gambling, extortion, loansharking, corruption involving commercial activity, and racketeering affecting transportation and construction.

After Gambino's death, Paul Castellano became boss. His 1985 murder preceded John Gotti's rise to power and the family's unusually conspicuous public profile. Gotti's 1992 federal conviction, following cooperation by former underboss Salvatore Gravano, became a defining episode in the government's challenge to Mafia leadership. The organization's history also demonstrates how a family could survive major prosecutions through changing personnel and continuing crews.

In February 2026, federal prosecutors announced the guilty plea of Gambino member James LaForte, the last of ten defendants charged in a 2023 case involving racketeering-related offenses. Such documented proceedings support the conclusion that parts of the network have remained criminally active, without establishing a reliable current membership total or public roster of leadership.[8]

Genovese Crime Family

Earlier name: Luciano family · Namesake: Vito Genovese

The Genovese family descends from the organization headed by Charles “Lucky” Luciano after the 1931 reorganization. Luciano played a crucial role in replacing the competing claims to supreme Mafia authority with an inter-family Commission. After his 1936 conviction, Frank Costello came to exercise leadership. Vito Genovese assumed control in 1957, and the family eventually became identified with his name.

Later leadership included Vincent “Chin” Gigante, whose elaborate public behavior and use of intermediaries became subjects of extensive attention during federal investigations. Historic criminal enterprises included illegal gambling, extortion, loansharking, corruption in labor and commercial settings, and other forms of racketeering. The organization was frequently described as influential within the New York Mafia, but claims about relative family “rankings” require a defined time period and a credible measurement.

Joseph Valachi, a Genovese-family member who became a government witness, gave nationally significant Senate testimony in 1963 concerning organized-crime structure and practices. Federal prosecutors' accounts of later Genovese cases describe administration committees, acting leaders, and participation in inter-family dealings, underlining the need to distinguish formal titles from the exercise of practical authority.[1][9]

Lucchese Crime Family

Earlier name: Gagliano family · Namesake: Tommy Lucchese

The Lucchese family developed from the group headed by Tommy Gagliano after the 1931 settlement. Tommy Lucchese subsequently served as boss and lent the organization its lasting name. During the twentieth century, the family was connected to organized-crime activity affecting transport, garment and other labor-intensive industries, as well as illegal gambling, loansharking, extortion, and theft.

The family has been extensively discussed in accounts of cargo theft and racketeering near New York airports. The 1978 Lufthansa robbery at John F. Kennedy International Airport became especially prominent in popular culture, although the wider criminal network associated with the theft crossed organizational boundaries; it should not be described as an exclusively Lucchese undertaking. Later leadership disputes and prosecutions involving figures such as Victor Amuso and Anthony Casso form another important part of the organization's history.

The July 2025 sentencing of Lucchese captain Anthony Villani, following a conviction concerning illegal online gambling, money laundering, and racketeering, illustrates the evolution of longstanding rackets into newer forms. Federal prosecutors said the gambling business involved had produced at least $35 million in illicit profits.[10]

Classification and Sources

“Crime family” denotes a historically documented organized-crime grouping, not a biological family or an assertion of present-day criminal involvement. New England is counted as one Patriarca family spanning Boston and Providence. Las Vegas, Miami, and Atlantic City are not listed as independent families; they were important territories for various families. Rochester and Springfield require period-specific classification, and the status of some very small historical groups is disputed.

Origins, Prohibition, and the 1931 Reorganization

Italian-American criminal networks developed during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries within a larger landscape of urban gangs, bootlegging operations, gambling enterprises, and protection rackets. Prohibition (1920–1933) supplied a lucrative illegal alcohol market and helped some groups expand their financial and political influence. These organizations were never representative of Italian immigrants or Italian-American communities as a whole.

During the conflict conventionally called the Castellammarese War, rivals connected with Joseph Masseria and Salvatore Maranzano competed for leadership in New York's Italian-American underworld. Masseria was killed in April 1931; Maranzano was murdered that September. The subsequent rearrangement produced the recognizable five-family system. Charles “Lucky” Luciano emerged as a leading figure and helped establish the Commission, an inter-family body intended to reduce destructive disputes and coordinate certain rules and decisions.[1][4]

This was an institutional turning point, not the sudden creation of organized crime. The five organizations drew upon earlier groups, relationships, neighborhood operations, and criminal markets. Similarly, the names by which the families are best known today reflect leaders whose prominence sometimes came decades after the 1931 settlement.

The Commission did not function as a transparent national government or an omnipotent executive. Evidence from prosecutions describes meetings among leaders to address issues affecting several families, including membership rules and disputes; the precise membership and practical authority of the body changed over time.[2]

Organizational Hierarchy and the Commission

Federal indictments and FBI accounts identify a broadly similar command hierarchy across the families. The familiar arrangement is a general model; a particular organization might use acting officers, leadership committees, or informal intermediaries, and the nominal officeholder was not always the person exercising day-to-day power.[2][3]

  • Boss — Principal leader, or acting leader, responsible for overall direction and senior decisions.
  • Underboss — Senior subordinate who helps supervise crews and may exercise delegated authority.
  • Consigliere — Senior counselor or adviser; often involved in internal consultation and disputes.
  • Caporegime / Capo — Captain responsible for a crew of initiated members and affiliated associates.
  • Soldier / Made Member — Inducted member who participates in the organization's internal obligations and criminal activity.
  • Associate — Noninitiated participant who may collaborate with a crew; not equivalent to an inducted member.

The Commission

Established after the 1931 reorganization, the Commission linked the leadership of major American Mafia organizations in selected collective matters. Rather than operating every street-level crime, it addressed disputes and rules that affected more than one family. The precise roster, functioning, and authority evolved; its membership was not permanently limited to five New York leaders. The FBI describes its creation under Luciano as a major step in organizational coordination, while federal case documents recount inter-family meetings and governance.[1][2]

Members of different families also cooperated directly in particular schemes without needing to establish a universal chain of command. Conversely, formal organizational boundaries did not eliminate conflict over markets, personal disputes, or succession.

Criminal Enterprises and Institutional Infiltration

The Five Families' historical activities cannot be reduced to a single business model. Individual crews specialized in different offenses and drew revenue from opportunities available in their territory. The FBI and federal court records identify recurring patterns that included the following.[1][3]

  • Illegal gambling: betting operations, gambling clubs, bookmaking, and, in later cases, unlicensed online wagering.
  • Extortion and loansharking: threatening violence or economic harm to obtain payments or collect unlawful debts.
  • Labor racketeering: coercion, corruption, or manipulation involving unions, employers, benefit funds, and contracting decisions.
  • Theft, fraud, and fencing: cargo theft, deception, unlawful resale networks, and schemes connected to legitimate business activity.
  • Narcotics trafficking: participation by certain leaders, crews, and associates in domestic and international illegal drug markets.
  • Violence and obstruction: murder, assault, intimidation, witness tampering, and threats used to enforce criminal relationships or impede investigations.
  • Money laundering and corruption: concealing proceeds of unlawful activity and exploiting access to commercial or political institutions.

It is important to distinguish organizational patterns from the conduct of a particular defendant. Evidence that a family had a history of a given racket does not demonstrate that every inducted member, crew, or successive leader participated in it.

Selected Historical Chronology

  1. 1920–1933:

    Prohibition creates a large illegal alcohol market and accelerates the growth of some criminal enterprises.

  2. 1931:

    The Castellammarese conflict ends in a sequence of killings; New York's five-family arrangement solidifies and Luciano helps establish the Commission.[1]

  3. 1936:

    Lucky Luciano is convicted in New York; his removal from direct leadership helps reshape authority in the future Genovese family.[1]

  4. 1957:

    Albert Anastasia is killed; Carlo Gambino ascends within the family later known by his name. A Mafia gathering at Apalachin, New York, exposes national inter-family coordination.

  5. 1963:

    Joseph Valachi testifies before the U.S. Senate about the Mafia's practices and structure.

  6. 1970:

    The Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act (RICO) becomes federal law, providing prosecutors a major framework for pursuing enterprises and patterns of racketeering.

  7. 1985–1986:

    The federal Mafia Commission prosecution challenges the leadership of major New York Mafia families through racketeering charges and convictions.

  8. 1992:

    John Gotti is convicted in federal court, illustrating the significance of cooperating witnesses and sustained investigations.

  9. 2004–2005:

    Bonanno leader Joseph Massino is convicted and begins cooperating with federal prosecutors, marking a major leadership-level break in secrecy.

  10. 2011:

    Federal officials announce sweeping indictments against 127 defendants in multiple districts, including members and associates of all five New York families and other Mafia groups.[3]

  11. 2021–2026:

    Further prosecutions involving Colombo, Lucchese, and Gambino members demonstrate continuing racketeering cases even as the Mafia's earlier national influence has diminished.[7][8][10]

Investigations, Prosecutions, and Declining Influence

American law-enforcement agencies gradually developed more effective ways to prosecute hierarchical criminal enterprises. These included electronic surveillance, undercover work, cooperating witnesses, federal witness protection, financial investigations, and RICO prosecutions targeting a group's pattern of criminal activity rather than treating each offense as entirely independent.

The 1980s Mafia Commission case and subsequent convictions damaged senior leadership; internal defectors supplied information that could be corroborated against surveillance and financial records. The broad January 2011 federal enforcement operation charged 127 defendants across New York, New Jersey, and Rhode Island, with the Justice Department identifying members and associates from all five New York families among those targeted.[3]

The persistence of arrests and guilty pleas shows that Mafia-related offenses did not end with the celebrated trials of the twentieth century. In 2023, the Justice Department reported guilty pleas by fourteen defendants in Colombo-connected cases. In 2025, Lucchese captain Anthony Villani was sentenced in a case involving a large illegal online gambling operation. In 2026, James LaForte pleaded guilty in a Gambino racketeering case. These are dated examples of adjudicated conduct rather than proof of a fixed overall membership level, command structure, or comparable present-day strength for each family.[7][8][10]

Present-day status. The FBI continues to describe American La Cosa Nostra as an organized-crime concern, but available public records do not support definitive, comprehensive claims about each family's current boss, active membership count, revenue, or nationwide ranking. This encyclopedia does not present unverified leadership rosters as established fact.[1]

Definitions and Important Distinctions

American Mafia / La Cosa Nostra (LCN)

A U.S.-based network of distinct organized-crime families historically shaped by Italian-American Mafia traditions and criminal practices.

Five Families

The five principal New York-based LCN families: Bonanno, Colombo, Gambino, Genovese, and Lucchese. The term does not include every American Mafia group.

Commission

An inter-family Mafia body historically used to discuss major disputes, rules, and matters spanning more than one organization.

Caporegime / Capo

A crew leader or captain within a Mafia family.

Made member / Soldier

An initiated participant recognized as a formal member under a family's internal customs.

Associate

A noninitiated participant who works with members or crews; status and degree of involvement vary.

Racketeering

Criminal conduct falling under laws addressing specified offenses and, in the RICO context, patterns of unlawful activity connected to an enterprise.

Italian organized crime versus Italian-American identity

Membership in a criminal organization is an individual and evidentiary question. The existence of Mafia groups does not imply collective criminality among Italian Americans or Italian immigrants.
