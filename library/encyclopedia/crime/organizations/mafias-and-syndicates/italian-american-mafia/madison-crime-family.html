Overview

The Madison crime family, sometimes described as the Caputo family, was a small, historically reported organization of the Italian-American Mafia or La Cosa Nostra (LCN), centered in Madison, Wisconsin. During the middle of the twentieth century, federal investigators described Carlo Peter Caputo as its leader and Joseph Aiello as its underboss. Contemporary intelligence, later reproduced in congressional materials and specialist research, treated Madison as distinct enough from Milwaukee to appear as a separately named regional family. This label records an investigative classification; it does not prove that every person identified as a member committed a particular crime. [1][2][6][7]

The organization's history is unusually difficult to reconstruct. Its core personnel were comparatively few, many were elderly by the 1960s, and the accessible record contains far more intelligence about personal relationships and purported rank than documented collective racketeering convictions. A secondary description once suggested a two-man family, yet archival-source reconstructions identify more people, with membership lists that vary by date and by informant. Neither figure should be mistaken for a definitive continuously employed criminal workforce. [6][7][8][9][13]

Madison's violent 1920s bootlegging history provides important local background but is not interchangeable with the later Caputo organization. The Greenbush neighborhood had residents, workers, voluntary societies, shops and families whose histories were overwhelmingly unrelated to organized crime. The present article therefore treats Prohibition killings, midcentury FBI intelligence, and individual federal legal problems as distinct evidentiary subjects. [4][5][6]

This first-edition profile is researched through November 13, 2026. It does not declare a living boss, a continuing command structure, or an exact dissolution date without defensible public records.

Federal and secondary labels such as boss and soldier require an explicit date and source. The words indicate reported organizational status rather than a judicial verdict about all conduct. A family in the Mafia sense is not a biological family; a namesake's descendants and the wider Madison Italian-American community do not inherit criminal responsibility. [1][2][3][7]

Madison Before the Caputo Era

Madison was Wisconsin's state capital and a university city, not an industrial metropolis on the scale of Chicago or Milwaukee. The urban geography that later investigators associated with the small LCN network included downtown property and the Greenbush area. Greenbush developed as a neighborhood of immigrant households, local shops, restaurants, trades, and social institutions. Its history must be told independently of the actions of a comparatively small circle of alleged or documented offenders. [4][5][8]

The local 1920s Rum War centered on rival bootlegging groups, with contests around Regent and Milton streets. Historic Madison's reconstruction documents a series of shootings around the former intersection of South Murray Street and Desmond Court. That account is valuable for local crime history, but there is no sufficient foundation for identifying all those earlier participants as members of a later, formal Caputo family. The distinction avoids retroactively projecting an FBI-era organizational chart onto events several decades before it was compiled. [4][5]

The redevelopment of Greenbush in the 1960s also transformed the physical setting. Surviving historic preservation material, local memoirs and neighborhood studies allow researchers to distinguish vanished streets from the later downtown commercial properties discussed in biographies of Caputo. Treating an entire demolished neighborhood as a Mafia enclave would erase its much broader social history. [4][12]

Prohibition, the Rum War, and the Limits of Continuity

National Prohibition began in 1920 and created lucrative illegal alcohol markets. Madison's local producers, sellers and competitors included participants in the Greenbush violence. Historical preservation research records the August 1922 killing of James D'Amico, the February 1923 killing of Carl Justo, and other shootings in the district, some of which remained unsolved. These events illustrate law-enforcement and community exposure to violence, not an uninterrupted list of Caputo-directed operations. [4][5]

The historical question is whether individual people, family ties, markets or local practices carried forward into later LCN affiliations. Such continuities cannot be presumed simply because later members lived or conducted business in the same city. No organization is proven to have controlled all bootlegging in Madison merely because a government chart later recognized a Madison Mafia group. [4][6][7]

The term crime family in this article is accordingly used principally for the identifiable mid-twentieth-century group, while the Prohibition era remains documented as a predecessor environment with unresolved links.

Early Sicilian-American Networks and Uncertain Founders

Later specialist reconstructions have identified Benedetto DiSalvo as a possible early power in Madison's organized-crime milieu. These proposals rest on historical records and intelligence-derived inferences rather than a single definitive government succession certificate. They should not be elevated to an unqualified roster of first bosses. [7][8]

Madison and Milwaukee shared numerous commercial and kinship contacts, including people whose family origins lay in Palermo province. Historical relationships may explain why investigators assigned certain persons to a Madison group, but ethnic origin, a Sicilian surname, or membership in a mutual-aid society are not evidence of Mafia induction. Indeed, local voluntary associations served ordinary immigrant needs independently of any illicit activity. [7][8]

The most securely reported leadership sequence begins not with a universally agreed founding date but with the FBI-era designation of Carlo Caputo as boss and Joseph Aiello as underboss by the 1960s. Any earlier years assigned to Caputo's formal rule are approximate unless matched to dated documentary evidence. [6][7][8]

Carlo Peter Caputo: Migration and Establishment in Madison

Carlo Peter Caputo was born in Sicily in 1903 and migrated to the United States as a young man. Jay C. Ambler's historical account places his arrival in 1919 and describes a subsequent period in Chicago before his establishment in Madison, generally dated to around 1940. Those details derive from secondary research and should be checked against immigration, census and property records before being treated as a complete personal chronology. [6]

Caputo became known as a businessman and real-estate owner, with reported interests in restaurants, taverns and other downtown properties. Descriptions of his commercial success are not proof that any particular acquisition was funded by illegal gambling or extortion. A state historic-property listing identifies the Caputo-Milsted Building at 101–105 State Street, independently confirming the existence of a Caputo-named architectural landmark without supplying criminal findings. [6][12]

Historical narratives identify Caputo as a reserved figure whose business reputation coexisted with federal suspicion. When those narratives conflict, this article prioritizes dated agency references and treats undocumented personal judgments as secondary interpretation. There is no need to turn legitimate real-estate entrepreneurship into a presumed laundering mechanism. [6][7][12]

Joseph Aiello: Underboss and Businessman

Joseph Aiello, born in 1901, is identified in reproduced FBI-source accounts as Caputo's underboss. He had earlier lived in Chicago before establishing himself in Madison. The name is common enough in Mafia history to require a precise identifier: the Madison businessman is not to be conflated with unrelated Chicago-era figures named Joseph Aiello. [7]

Aiello operated grocery and food-related businesses. A specialist study drawing upon local records reports that he and Caputo jointly purchased property in 1943 and that Aiello established a pizza business in Madison the following year. These transactions are commercial facts in the researchers' reconstruction, not prima facie evidence that pizza businesses were criminal enterprises. [7]

A February 1970 FBI confidential-source report, as reproduced in the specialist biography, specifically stated that Aiello was operating a grocery business and was not known to be involved in gambling at that point. That unusual evidence is crucial: a law-enforcement description of someone as underboss can coexist with an absence of verified ongoing racketeering activity. Aiello died in Madison on November 7, 1970. [7]

The Small 1960s Organizational Structure

Researchers working from 1964–1965 FBI confidential-source reports describe the Madison group as having a boss and underboss but no identified captains and no settled consigliere. In this picture, a few inducted members were connected more through personal relationships and intercity introductions than through large territorial crews. This is a dated intelligence interpretation, not a permanent organizational constitution. [7][8]

A 1965 source, reproduced by LCN Bios, reported eight people while expressing uncertainty about the eighth name; a later reconstruction of the period identifies nine and discusses a possible tenth. These are not identical measurements. Some names came from informant claims, others from later research, and the composition changed through illness, death and uncertain transfers. The least misleading statement is that the organization was small, probably under a dozen people in the mid-1960s, with a disputed exact roster. [7][8][9]

The common description of a “two-man family” is therefore oversimplified. It may reflect the two best-known officers rather than the complete list of purported inducted members. Conversely, a roster of nine or ten attributed names cannot be assumed to have generated nine or ten active criminal income streams. [6][7]

Reported Members and Identity Problems

Research based on FBI-era accounts and local records names Carlo Caputo and Joseph Aiello as leaders, while attributing member or possible-member status to people including Sam Paul Buscemi, Filippi “Phil” Candela, James/Vincenzo Caruso, Cosimo DiSalvo, Matthew Pellitteri, James “Little Jimmy” Schiavo and Vincent Troia. The list reproduces historical attribution, not separate criminal convictions for the named individuals. [7][8][9]

Caruso illustrates a particular documentary trap: the names James, Joseph and Vincenzo appear in adjacent reports and later reconstructions, with varying degrees of certainty about who was meant. The existence of a grocery operator or a civil registration is not independently enough to merge two similarly named people. Where archival writers themselves note an ambiguity, MGU should retain it rather than present exact identity as settled. [8]

Likewise, reports varied over whether Candela belonged to the Madison group or another family, and whether a younger relative was inducted at a specified date. The role field in an encyclopedic profile should read “identified by a particular FBI informant” or “possible member in later reconstruction” when no stronger evidence exists. [7][8]

Legitimate Businesses and the Uneven Evidence for Rackets

Madison research describes groceries, pizza businesses, cheese-related commerce and real estate in the personal lives of men who appeared in LCN intelligence. These subjects require the same separation of lawful economic activity and proven criminal conduct used in all MGU organization articles. A business's proximity to a person identified in an informant report does not establish fraudulent ownership, sham invoices, illicit financing or extortion. [6][7][8][12]

In contrast with cases involving Chicago, Milwaukee or Cleveland, the open sources reviewed here do not provide a comprehensive federal racketeering prosecution establishing a Madison-controlled gambling, narcotics or labor enterprise. General FBI descriptions of common LCN rackets give background only; they are not Madison-specific counts of crime. The FBI’s own history explains how specialized investigations developed nationally, rather than demonstrating Madison prosecutions. [14] Indeed, specialists reproducing the era's FBI memoranda emphasize that little or no contemporary illicit activity was documented for most of Madison's supposed members. [3][7][8]

That conclusion is narrower than claiming no crimes ever occurred. Public intelligence may be incomplete, and membership reporting can be inaccurate. But historically responsible publication does not fill archival gaps with stereotyped allegations simply to make the small Madison group resemble larger, better-prosecuted families.

Caputo’s Reported Income-Tax Prosecution

Jay C. Ambler reports that federal authorities prosecuted Carlo Caputo in the early 1960s over the accuracy of his reported income. The account states that Caputo received a short custodial term and probation, and describes a substantial disparity between declared and investigated income. These specifics are important historical leads, but in the materials reviewed for this article the underlying indictment, sentencing judgment and any appellate disposition were not independently obtained. [6]

Accordingly, the publication does not use the tax case as a substitute for a conviction that Caputo headed a Mafia conspiracy or committed other uncharged acts. A tax judgment concerns its adjudicated offense; criminal-enterprise allegations demand separate proof. Before specifying statutory counts, precise dollars or a final prison term in an individual biography, an editor should obtain the corresponding federal docket or a reliable contemporaneous court report. [6]

This case also explains why commercial wealth and law-enforcement interest must not be reduced to one claim. Caputo's documented property activity and his alleged income-tax misconduct belong to different evidentiary categories. [6][12]

Chicago’s Wider Regional Influence

A particularly important primary historical source appears in the U.S. House Select Committee on Assassinations' published 1978 testimony of organized-crime investigator Ralph Salerno. Salerno quoted an FBI memorandum dated August 21, 1964, attributed to senior FBI official Courtney Evans, describing Chicago's importance in major policy decisions affecting several Midwestern Mafia groups. Madison was named alongside Milwaukee, Springfield, Rockford, Kansas City and St. Louis. [1]

This testimony supports a careful institutional conclusion: federal intelligence viewed the small Madison organization as part of a regional network influenced by the Chicago family. It does not prove that Chicago collected every Madison dollar, selected every member or directly ordered every action throughout the century. Salerno was reproducing a 1964 intelligence assessment in a 1978 legislative inquiry, not reporting a trial verdict concerning all six communities. [1]

The records help explain the distinctive scale problem: a nominally separate Madison family could possess its own boss and members while remaining dependent on a larger Chicago network for significant inter-family issues. That hybrid status is central to understanding why sources disagree on whether Madison was independent, subordinate or later absorbed. [1][7][9]

Milwaukee: Cooperation, Disputes, and Reclassification

The better-documented neighboring organization was Milwaukee's crime family, closely associated in the postwar decades with Frank Balistrieri. FBI-source accounts reproduced by later researchers describe personal contact between Madison figures and Milwaukee members, including Joseph Aiello, James Schiavo and Nick Fucarino. These relationships included visits, family ceremonies and requests about access to Chicago personnel. Attendance at a wedding or funeral is not itself evidence of criminal participation. [7][9]

One 1965 account describes disagreements after Caputo reportedly advised a Madison associate not to attend a Rockford funeral because of FBI surveillance. Another, from 1969, places Caputo in communications concerning a proposed approach to Chicago leaders by dissatisfied Milwaukee members. Both statements are informant reports; their value lies in reconstructing reported perceptions and networks, not in certifying every conversation or motive. [9]

Later FBI-derived materials reportedly classified Caputo as a Milwaukee captain in January 1976. If accurate, that designation suggests restructuring after the small Madison organization's decline. However, other sources continued to describe a separate Madison family later in the 1970s. Rather than combining these into a single unsupported succession, the article keeps each classification tied to its dated source. [7][10][11]

Rockford and Other Midwestern Contacts

Madison's small circle also interacted with people from Rockford, Illinois, then described by law enforcement as having a separate Mafia family. A 1965 wedding involving the Schiavo and Fucarino families appears in historical FBI-source reconstructions as an occasion that brought together attendees associated with Madison, Milwaukee and Rockford. The event demonstrates social and personal contact, not a formal public summit at which all participants conducted racketeering business. [7][9]

Rockford-based research also cites a spring 1973 source assessment putting the Madison roster at approximately seven people. This differs from mid-1960s figures, as would be expected in a small and aging organization, but both figures are intelligence-derived snapshots rather than audited membership registers. The most defensible analytical point is the small scale and declining vitality of the group. [13]

Chicago, Milwaukee and Rockford should thus be mapped as related but institutionally distinct historical networks. Individual associations alone cannot establish that every Chicago or Milwaukee offense was a Madison activity.

Induction, Rank, and Intelligence Terminology

The FBI and contemporary witnesses used hierarchical Mafia vocabulary that included boss, underboss, capo and member. The public record does not show a comprehensive filmed or adjudicated Madison initiation ceremony. Specialist accounts of individual induction must therefore be attributed to their sources and dated rather than stated as a fact for every named person. [3][7][8]

Several factors make Madison especially vulnerable to classification error: personnel lived across different Wisconsin cities; family membership could allegedly transfer; an elderly member might no longer conduct criminal business; and confidential sources differed in access or reliability. A particular name could appear under Madison in one decade and Milwaukee in another without establishing exactly when the underlying organizational relationship changed. [7][10]

This encyclopedia distinguishes organizational status from criminal liability. Being identified as an inducted member is not the same thing as proof beyond a reasonable doubt of a specific theft, extortion, gambling or drug offense. Such liability belongs to particular convictions or admissions.

Joseph Aiello’s 1970 Death and the Succession Problem

Joseph Aiello's death on November 7, 1970 removed the person most often described as Madison's underboss. The funeral's connections to Milwaukee were noted in later research, but social attendance cannot establish the appointment of a new lieutenant. [7]

A March 22, 1971 FBI confidential-source report, as reproduced by the Joseph Aiello biographical research, continued to refer to Carlo Caputo as Madison boss while noting that the informant did not know who, if anyone, had replaced Aiello. The report also made statements about Caputo taking over a food-related business. These are precise dated intelligence propositions, not independent findings about ownership or an uninterrupted institutional succession. [7]

The vacancy matters analytically. A small family that loses its second-ranking figure may remain a nominal entity while losing much of its administrative activity. Later lists that continue to show a boss are not by themselves proof of continuing rackets, recruitment or meetings.

The Reported Early-1970s Dissolution

One specialist reconstruction, based on records it attributes to FBI-era materials, states that surviving Madison members voted to dissolve the separate family around 1973 and that some were transferred to other Midwestern groups. A related January 1976 classification reportedly placed Caputo as a Milwaukee captain. Taken together, these reports suggest a plausible reorganization of an aging, low-activity group. [7]

But another specialist account cites a spring 1973 roster of seven members, and later reports used Madison's family label. Those variations warn against a definitive proclamation that every office and allegiance ended on a particular day. A vote to dissolve, even if accurately described, might not immediately erase older terminology from agency files or personal relationships among former members. [10][11][13]

For MGU purposes, “reported dissolution or reclassification in the early-to-mid 1970s” is preferable to an exact shutdown date. The historical sequence remains open to more complete review of the underlying FBI files and any contemporaneous recorded transfers.

Why 1979 Reports Do Not Settle the Question

An annotated collection of Milwaukee FBI file notes attributes to an October 31, 1979 informant a statement that Caputo still led the Madison family. The same modern compiler describes the family as essentially dormant, with most members dead or retired. This illustrates the key issue: a reported title can persist after the economic and personnel basis for a separate organization has weakened. [11]

The Schiavo-centered historical research likewise reports conflicting government classifications: Caputo appeared as a Milwaukee official in some accounts, while individual former Madison figures appeared under Madison or Milwaukee at other dates. The inconsistencies are not resolved merely by choosing the document that uses the most familiar label. [10]

Thus the article does not assert that Madison continued as a full-scale independent crime family through 1979; equally, it does not declare that all references after 1973 were factually impossible. Both are stronger claims than the reviewed evidence supports.

The 1980s: Caputo, Memory, and Declining Visibility

Later local reporting described Caputo as a longstanding Madison real-estate figure who disputed the Mafia designation applied to him. His public business identity continued to attract attention after FBI-source accounts had already raised questions about the separate group's viability. His denial should be reported alongside official intelligence, rather than ignored or treated as proof by itself that all official suspicions were erroneous. [6][12]

A government-era organized-crime map reproduced in congressional material continued to label a Madison-area group under “Carlos Caputo”, reflecting the terminology of the compilation. The variant first name and broad “area” label should not be mistaken for an additional leader, a new criminal case or current evidence of a functioning organization. Such charts are valuable snapshots of official perceptions, not timeless directories. [2]

Accessible public sources do not show a sustained 1980s family administration conducting a scale of operations comparable to the Balistrieri family in Milwaukee. Where activity is not established, the responsible phrasing is that the reviewed record does not confirm it—not a claim of total knowledge about all hidden activity.

Carlo Caputo’s Death and the End of the Historical Leadership Era

Carlo Peter Caputo died in November 1993, at approximately ninety years of age, according to the historical sources reviewed. Some later summaries give different exact days; without a verified contemporaneous death certificate or obituary in this source set, the month and year are the safer publication level. [6]

The death of the most consistently named leader is often used retrospectively as the moment the Madison family became “extinct.” That phrasing oversimplifies the evidence. Specialist research points to substantial attrition and possible structural dissolution years earlier; later intelligence sometimes continued using the old family name. Caputo's death is therefore a firm biographical endpoint, not proof that a continuously operating criminal organization survived intact until 1993. [6][7][10][11]

No reliable public record reviewed for this article establishes a post-1993 successor who headed an independent Madison LCN family.

Community History and the Danger of False Attribution

Greenbush's neighborhood history includes the toll of the 1920s Rum War, urban renewal, cultural change and the ordinary labor of immigrant families. Its people should not be retrospectively converted into criminal suspects because a few local men appeared in FBI organized-crime files. The distinction is especially important because some alleged members were known primarily as shopkeepers, food vendors, tradespeople or property owners. [4][7][8][12]

Historical investigation can also have reputational effects on descendants and firms. A commercial establishment may be documented as an address where an FBI source said a person worked, but that fact does not prove the business was used for laundering, gambling or extortion. Where specific charges are absent, the article says so.

For educators, Madison illustrates both the harm of proven Prohibition violence and the limits of later labels applied through confidential intelligence. The two subjects belong together in a local history chapter but must not be collapsed into one organizational indictment.

Federal Case Record: What Is and Is Not Established

Unlike several other MGU family profiles, this article does not present a long succession of Madison-specific RICO judgments. That reflects the limits of the public records retrieved, not the assumption that authorities never investigated the city. The clearest individually described federal matter in secondary history is Carlo Caputo's reported income-tax prosecution. No original docket from that proceeding has been verified here. [6]

In contrast, the 1964 memo quoted in congressional testimony and the period law-enforcement map are primary evidence of the government's organizational characterization. They are not criminal verdicts. Similarly, FBI informant statements reported in modern research are evidence of what sources told investigators and may contain mistakes, memory problems or uncorroborated assertions. [1][2][7][9]

Future additions should not supply a generic list of gambling, narcotics and murder charges borrowed from other Mafia families. A Madison criminal-enterprise table should be expanded only when a case, suspect, statutory count, disposition and judicial or archival source have actually been identified.

Madison Compared with Milwaukee and Chicago

The Chicago Outfit was a much larger organization with documented metropolitan and national operations. Milwaukee had a clearer postwar leadership succession and substantial federal prosecutions in the 1980s. Madison, by comparison, appears in the accessible record as a small regional group whose membership and actual volume of illicit business are hard to establish. [1][3][6][7]

Different documentation does not, by itself, establish an exact quantitative ranking. Prosecutors had different investigative priorities, courts heard different cases, and informant access varied. A lower number of recorded convictions cannot be converted directly into a numerical measure of less criminal revenue or weaker internal discipline.

The Madison profile's distinctive value is to show how American LCN included nominally separate, socially intertwined micro-organizations that may have grown dormant without a famous prosecution or spectacular leadership war. [1][7][10][11]

Present-Day Status and Evidentiary Limits

As of November 13, 2026, the sources reviewed do not establish an independently functioning Madison Mafia family with a verified current boss, recruited membership, treasury or active territorial hierarchy. Most historical accounts characterize the separate group as long defunct, absorbed or inactive; however, precise dates and mechanics of the transition remain disputed. [6][7][10][11]

That judgment applies only to the historical organization under study. It does not claim that modern Madison has no organized crime of other kinds, nor does it use a change of official family label to prove that every former participant permanently ceased wrongdoing.

Exact current membership, revenue and territory figures are omitted. Assertions drawn from popular Mafia lists must be rechecked against the dated record before any future revision; a name retained on a decades-old intelligence chart does not establish an operational 2026 post.

Terminology and Evidence Categories

La Cosa Nostra (LCN)
Term used by the FBI for the American Mafia and its regional organizations. [3]
Madison family / Caputo family
Names applied to the small Madison LCN organization in intelligence and secondary history, not a genealogical designation.
Boss
Reported family leader. An intelligence label is not a judicial verdict proving a specific crime.
Underboss
Second-ranking officer where an identifiable administration exists; Aiello is so described in the 1960s source record. [7]
Consigliere
Senior advisory post in conventional LCN structures; a post need not be filled in a small group.
Caporegime / captain
Supervisor or recognized officer of a crew; Caputo's alleged 1976 Milwaukee reclassification is a dated source assertion. [7]
Made member / soldier
A person recognized as an inducted LCN member according to the organization’s private rules; status in public histories can be unverified.
Associate
Someone linked to a member or scheme who is not necessarily inducted; social or commercial contact alone is insufficient to establish affiliation.
Confidential informant (CI)
A person providing information to investigators; accounts may be reliable, partially correct or mistaken and should be independently assessed.
Intelligence chart
Analytical diagram showing investigators’ understanding at a particular time; not a criminal judgment.
Racket
Profit-seeking criminal scheme, such as extortion or illegal betting, when supported by evidence for the specific operation.
Tax conviction
Criminal judgment concerning a particular tax offense; it does not alone prove membership or leadership of an organized-crime group.
Indictment
Formal accusation; not proof of guilt.
Historical dissolution
Decline or reported termination of a particular organizational structure; it need not coincide with the death of a namesake decades later.

Research and Editorial Standards

Contemporary government evidence. The 1978 House committee testimony and a 1980 congressional hearing exhibit demonstrate how investigators and Congress represented a Madison family, but neither is a Madison criminal verdict. Salerno's account dates a referenced FBI memorandum to 1964; that time specificity prevents its being read as a statement about modern command. [1][2]

Specialist archival work. The LCN Bios and related research projects incorporate transcribed and summarized FBI reports and local records, sometimes with direct attributed source passages. They are useful but not a substitute for a complete independently inspected set of original Bureau files. Conflicts in named ranks, identity and family transfers are retained explicitly. [7][8][9][10][11][13]

Legal accuracy. The Caputo tax case is attributed to its reported secondary source without unsupported courtroom details. This article does not upgrade a reported intelligence designation to a RICO conviction, nor infer convictions for every person on an FBI roster. No crime is attributed to any modern business or descendant merely because of a similar name. [6][12]

Organizational uncertainty. The early-1970s dissolution claim, 1976 Milwaukee-captain classification and 1979 Madison-boss description are preserved as separate propositions. They may reflect administrative change, old terminology, informant error or different access to the same network; the available sources do not conclusively choose one explanation. [7][10][11][13]

Community distinction. Historic Madison's account of the Greenbush Rum War supplies local context, not proof that the neighborhood's immigrant communities or later redevelopment sites constituted a criminal enterprise. The article must not equate Sicilian ancestry with criminality. [4][5]

Cutoff and scope. The article is current to November 13, 2026. No complete microfilmed FBI case-file audit, original tax-court docket collation, or current intelligence investigation was conducted. Missing documentation is disclosed rather than filled with generalized Mafia assertions.

Selected References and Source Register

The source register distinguishes contemporary legislative records and FBI institutional accounts from secondary historical writing and specialist reconstructions of confidential-source documents. A source citation indicates what supports the adjacent statement; it does not make every informant allegation a proven fact.

  1. U.S. House Select Committee on Assassinations — Testimony of Ralph Salerno, September 28, 1978, pp. 425–428. Printed congressional testimony reproducing a 1964 FBI memorandum about Chicago influence over several Midwestern families, including Madison; intelligence characterization, not an adjudication.
  2. U.S. Senate Committee on the Judiciary — Bankruptcy Fraud Oversight, 1979–1980 hearings (GPO 1980), organized-crime family map. Congressional hearing exhibit identifying a Madison-area family under the name Carlos Caputo. This illustrates period law-enforcement classification, not a finding about each member or crime.
  3. Federal Bureau of Investigation — La Cosa Nostra. Institutional introduction to American La Cosa Nostra, its regional-family structure and typical organized-crime activities; not a Madison-specific prosecution record.
  4. Historic Madison, Inc. — Death Corner: South Murray Street and Desmond Court, Mark Gajewski. Local historical reconstruction of 1920s Greenbush bootlegging violence and later urban renewal; does not assign those earlier killings to the postwar Caputo family.
  5. Gavin Schmitt — Murder Capital: Madison Wisconsin—The Mafia Under Siege (2018). Book-length research on Prohibition-era Madison and its early underworld; distinct from documentation of the later FBI-identified Caputo group.
  6. Jay C. Ambler — 26 Mafia Cities: Madison, Wisconsin (AmericanMafia.com). Secondary portrait of Carlo Peter Caputo, income-tax proceedings, business holdings, Joseph Aiello and late-1980s family status; certain dates and the two-man formulation are contested.
  7. LCN Bios — Joseph Aiello (Madison), February 8, 2024. Independent specialist transcription and reconstruction drawing upon FBI confidential-source memoranda, local newspapers and genealogical records; reproduces varying 1965 counts and a 1971 succession report.
  8. LCN Bios — Vincenzo Caruso (Madison), March 2023. Specialist archival biography with reported 1964 FBI source classification and records of legitimate commercial life; records uncertainty over some person-name identifications.
  9. LCN Bios — Nick Fucarino (Milwaukee), July 2026. Reproduces attributed 1965 and 1969 FBI-source information on Madison, Milwaukee, Rockford and Chicago relationships; confidential-source allegations require caution.
  10. Silver Screen Wiseguys — Madison Soldier “Little Jimmy” Schiavo. Specialist compilation of FBI-file-derived references, including conflicting late-1970s and early-1980s classifications, 1985 lists and posthumous inclusion.
  11. Milwaukee Mafia Podcast — Balistrieri Years III: 1973–1979, FBI File Notes. Secondary annotated chronology attributing a 1979 statement about Carlo Caputo to an FBI informant, while describing the Madison network as largely dormant.
  12. Wisconsin Historical Society — 101–105 State Street / Caputo-Milsted Building. State historic-preservation property record; corroborates a Caputo-named commercial property without proving any offense.
  13. LCN Bios — Rockford family research collection. Specialist reconstruction of FBI-source reports referencing seven Madison members in spring 1973 and separate regional leadership networks.
  14. Federal Bureau of Investigation — Crime and Corruption Across America, 1972–1988. National historical context on federal investigatory methods and the limits of older organized-crime cases, not independent Madison-specific proof.

Publication note: This scholarly first-edition textual profile provides a qualified historical synthesis. It does not claim exhaustive primary FBI-file collation, judicial verification of every historical member, or a confirmed 2026 Madison family hierarchy.
