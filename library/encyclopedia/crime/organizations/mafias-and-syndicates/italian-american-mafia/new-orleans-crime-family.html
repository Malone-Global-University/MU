Overview

The New Orleans crime family, also called the Marcello crime family in accounts of its later history, was a regional Italian-American Mafia organization centered in New Orleans, Louisiana, and its Gulf Coast connections. It grew from several nineteenth-century criminal factions associated with waterfront, commercial and vice markets, and eventually became closely identified with Carlos “the Little Man” Marcello, whose influence extended from the late 1940s into the 1980s. The label La Cosa Nostra is useful for the organization’s modern Mafia context, but should not be imposed uncritically on every loose Sicilian-associated group operating in the city in the 1860s or 1870s. [4][5][21]

Three eras define the documentary record. The 1890–1891 Hennessy proceedings exposed criminal accusations entangled with virulent anti-Italian prejudice and culminated in the lynching of eleven prisoners. The Marcello era brought congressional scrutiny, immigration litigation, gambling investigations and separate federal criminal cases. The 1990s video-poker investigation produced guilty pleas and appellate judgments concerning Anthony Carollo, Frank Gagliano and businesses linked to New York organized-crime figures. [1][3][14][15][18]

This profile distinguishes historical organization from allegation, ethnic stereotyping, and later legend. It follows public records available through November 13, 2026; it makes no unsupported claim that a particular boss, membership tally or operating budget is verifiable today. The crimes and documented associations of individuals do not define New Orleans’s much larger Italian-American or Sicilian-American communities.

Sources, Historical Continuity, and Evidentiary Caution

New Orleans possesses two very different bodies of relevant evidence. The first consists of nineteenth-century police files, court records, newspaper accounts and later scholarly reconstructions. The second consists of federal hearings, court judgments, immigration decisions, recorded conversations and financial or licensing investigations of the twentieth century. They cannot simply be collapsed into a single continuous roster of “bosses” without accounting for decades of incomplete evidence. [1][2][3][7][14][18]

Early descriptions of a citywide Mafia sometimes arose from political sensationalism or anti-immigrant campaigns. The murder of Police Chief David Hennessy was real, and rival commercial networks were involved in bitter disputes, but an accusation in 1890 was not an adjudicated finding against the people killed by a crowd in 1891. The surviving trial records and modern scholarship make this distinction indispensable. [1][2][3]

For the later period, appellate opinions distinguish the facts the government alleged, evidence the jury accepted, guilty pleas and convictions ultimately vacated. Especially significant are United States v. Marcello (1989) and United States v. Salvatore (1997). This article gives those judgments priority over generalized “Mafia history” summaries when reporting legal results. [14][18]

Sicilian Immigration, the Port, and Commercial Competition

New Orleans had established international commercial links, a major Mississippi River port and a growing Sicilian migrant population during the nineteenth century. Migrants worked in agricultural supply, fruit importing, docks, retail commerce and other occupations. These lawful industries gave New Orleans a distinctive economic geography compared with the later New York Five Families. Many people built legitimate businesses and civic institutions, while specific criminal actors competed for control of profitable markets. [2][3]

Port labor and shipping arrangements were often contested. Contracts to unload produce and access to transportation networks created opportunities for commercial competition, intimidation and corruption. The same docks and markets also generated stereotypes of immigrants as a homogeneous underworld. Scholarship on the 1891 lynchings stresses that economic conflict and anti-Italian politics helped shape the public response to violent crime. [2][3]

A rigorous organization article therefore does not transform the history of Sicilian immigration into a story in which crime explains the community. The organization arose within a large commercial and social world; the wrongdoing of identifiable participants was not representative of it.

Nineteenth-Century Factions and the Matranga–Provenzano Conflict

Later histories commonly associate the origin of the New Orleans Mafia with the brothers Charles and Antonio Matranga, whose group competed with a network connected to the Provenzano brothers. Traditional accounts date the formation of the Matranga organization to the late nineteenth century, sometimes offering around 1870 as a starting point. This is a historical convention rather than a demonstrated date when an inducted, nationwide-recognized La Cosa Nostra family was formally created. [3][4]

The groups became associated with business and labor conflicts around fruit importing and port facilities. Allegations of intimidation and violent retaliation circulated before Hennessy’s murder. The commercial rivalry supplied a setting for police intervention and accusations, but historians disagree about the exact motives and responsibility for particular killings. Court files, contemporaneous statements and ethnic politics are all necessary to evaluate the claims. [1][2][3]

The distinction between factions and a unified family matters: rival Sicilian-linked groups in the 1880s do not necessarily correspond to the later hierarchy led by Carolla and Marcello. Later retrospective charts often smooth over these ruptures.

October 1890: The Murder of Police Chief David Hennessy

New Orleans Police Chief David C. Hennessy was shot on the night of October 15, 1890 and died the following day. Police suspected men associated with the Matranga–Provenzano dispute. The city archives record that nineteen people were arrested and that nine defendants eventually went to trial beginning in February 1891. This was a politically explosive murder investigation, not a case that concluded with convictions of the men later killed by the mob. [1]

Contemporary press coverage frequently presented the accused as representatives of a terrifying, secret “Mafia,” a framing with substantial anti-Italian and anti-Sicilian content. The public campaign linked a specific criminal charge to a much larger immigrant population. The archival record and later scholarship document both the severity of the homicide and the danger of substituting collective suspicion for proof. [2][3]

At trial, six defendants were acquitted and the jury deadlocked as to three, according to the New Orleans Public Library’s archival summary. The absence of convictions did not settle every historical question surrounding the assassination; it did mean the prosecution failed to secure the verdicts that public agitators demanded. [1]

March 1891: The Lynching of Eleven Italian Prisoners

On March 14, 1891, a large crowd invaded the Orleans Parish jail and killed eleven prisoners of Italian descent after the Hennessy proceedings. The victims included people acquitted or whose trials had not produced guilty verdicts, as well as others in custody. The event is a major episode of racialized and ethnic collective violence in United States history. It was neither a legal sentence nor proof that any victim was guilty of the police chief’s murder. [1][2][3]

Scholars emphasize how political and business elites helped mobilize violence and how newspapers amplified fears of an allegedly omnipresent Sicilian conspiracy. Three of the victims were Italian citizens, making the killings a diplomatic matter between the United States and Italy. The murders and the ensuing international controversy are central to understanding how anti-Italian prejudice shaped early popular views of the American Mafia. [2][3]

The modern distinction is nonnegotiable: historical existence of organized criminal networks does not justify extrajudicial killing, collective guilt or ethnic defamation. An encyclopedia should identify the 1891 victims as victims of mob violence, not casually list them as convicted gangsters.

The Matranga Organization after 1891

Charles Matranga survived the Hennessy-era crisis and became a familiar name in historical narratives of the city’s criminal underworld. Subsequent accounts describe a group with influence over gambling, vice and certain commercial relationships, but the precise boundaries between Matranga loyalists, former adversaries and later Mafia members are not equally visible in the surviving public record. [4]

The Matranga story also illustrates why the word “boss” requires context. A man who headed a faction in an urban labor conflict could have substantial local influence without the standardized administration familiar from later post-1931 Mafia accounts. It is methodologically unsafe to assign every nineteenth-century operator a clean twentieth-century job title.

For the period between the lynchings and Prohibition, the article treats the existence of underworld continuity as historically plausible but does not claim a complete list of acting bosses or captains. The absence of full records is a reason for precision, not for filling gaps with invented succession dates. [2][4]

Prohibition and the Emergence of Sam Carolla

National Prohibition, in force from 1920 to 1933, changed illegal alcohol markets across the country. In New Orleans, the opportunity to sell, transport and protect unlicensed liquor overlapped with established vice and gambling networks. Criminal operators could earn revenue from new smuggling channels while continuing older forms of illicit business. The era was one of market transformation, not the automatic birth of a Mafia organization from nothing. [4][21]

Silvestro “Silver Dollar Sam” Carolla is conventionally identified as successor to the Matranga-led organization, frequently dated to the early 1920s. Retrospective histories commonly state that Carolla rose to control important New Orleans rackets. Exact succession years, factional boundaries and the degree of Commission recognition should be understood as historical reconstructions rather than court-adjudicated facts. [4]

Carolla’s period linked local gambling and bootlegging opportunities with connections to other American organized-crime markets. The later Marcello regime built upon these relationships but operated in a substantially changed legal environment after the repeal of Prohibition.

Carolla, Federal Pressure, and the 1947 Deportation

Carolla appeared in accounts of narcotics offenses, federal scrutiny and long-running immigration difficulties. The Mob Museum’s historical profile records that authorities ultimately deported Carolla to Italy in 1947, removing the leader who had dominated the city’s underworld in the interwar era. This is a useful marker of regime change, even though informal influence and surviving personal ties may have outlasted the official removal. [4]

Leadership did not pass through a public election with a contemporaneous published roster. Later histories present Carlos Marcello as the major beneficiary of Carolla’s removal, commonly dating his rise to leadership to 1947. Those accounts identify a succession widely accepted in Mafia history, but they do not warrant precise day-by-day assertions about when every faction recognized him. [4]

Carolla’s departure also anticipated a striking feature of Marcello’s own career: immigration law became one of the federal government’s principal tools for challenging a prominent organized-crime leader long before modern enterprise prosecutions matured.

Carlos Marcello: From Local Criminal Cases to Leadership

Carlos Marcello, born in 1910 in Tunis to a Sicilian family, came to Louisiana as a child. His birth name appears in sources as Calogero Minacore or a similar variant. The details of transliteration and his later family name differ in biographical reports, so the official courts’ description of his Tunisian birth is preferred over later obituaries that call him Italian-born. [4][8][22]

Marcello had a documented 1938 conviction under the Marihuana Tax Act, significant both to his criminal history and to his subsequent deportability. Immigration judges and appellate courts later revisited that conviction repeatedly. Biographical accounts also discuss earlier robbery-related proceedings, but historical association or arrest should not be converted into a conviction without the relevant disposition. [4][8]

By the late 1940s Marcello had emerged as a leading figure in the New Orleans underworld. His reputation rested partly on relationships, business investments and access to gambling markets; the precise division between direct management and influence over independent operators remains difficult to quantify. [4][23]

Jefferson Parish, Gambling, and Business Investments

Postwar gambling markets in and around Jefferson Parish became a prominent feature of accounts of Marcello’s organization. Illegal casinos, slot machines, bookmaking and associated business arrangements attracted both customers and enforcement scrutiny. The concentration of such activities in neighboring parishes demonstrates why a “New Orleans” Mafia profile must consider the metropolitan and suburban economy rather than only the French Quarter. [4][22][23]

Marcello also maintained interests in land, real estate and ordinary businesses. The existence of a lawful enterprise owned by a suspected gangster does not mean every employee or transaction was illegal; what matters is whether particular conduct constituted fraud, bribery, money laundering or another offense. Contemporary press accounts of the Marcello portfolio are valuable evidence of public reputation, but their aggregate revenue estimates frequently relied on contested intelligence or advocacy calculations. [23]

The commercial pattern was an overlap between illicit operations, respectable investment and personal networks. This overlap complicated investigations because some financial relationships were ordinary commerce, some were criminal schemes, and others became matters of rumor rather than adjudication.

The 1951 Kefauver Hearings and Marcello

The Senate’s Special Committee to Investigate Organized Crime in Interstate Commerce, popularly known as the Kefauver Committee, investigated gambling, political protection and syndicate activity in multiple American cities in 1950–1951. New Orleans became a key part of the inquiry into regional operations and interstate organized crime. [6]

A government report records that Carlos Marcello appeared under subpoena in New Orleans on January 25, 1951 and documents subsequent congressional contempt proceedings. The committee’s demands for business and income records underscore its interest in possible links between licit enterprises and illegal revenue. The inquiry was important nationally, but a Senate investigation is not itself a verdict establishing every accusation reported during testimony. [7]

The hearings widened the public profile of Marcello and related figures. They also helped shape federal approaches that increasingly treated organized crime as a network of finance, influence and corruption rather than solely as isolated violent offenses. [5][6]

National Mafia Relationships and Local Autonomy

Marcello maintained relationships with figures in other American Mafia cities. Historical accounts place him in the network of bosses and associates that included Frank Costello, Santo Trafficante Jr. and other regional operators. Those relationships facilitated gambling, introductions, financing or negotiated participation in markets but did not automatically make the New Orleans family a subsidiary of another city’s organization. [4][23]

Later video-poker litigation establishes a particularly concrete cross-regional connection. Prosecutors and federal courts described joint dealings among participants linked to the New Orleans-based Marcello organization and the Gambino family of New York, with allegations also involving individuals associated with the Genovese family. This is better evidence of a particular criminal collaboration than a generalized claim that the national Mafia controlled every Louisiana casino. [16][18]

Formal national Commission membership, jurisdiction and enforcement powers are more difficult to establish with precision for all periods. This article avoids treating common meeting attendance, social ties or a gangster’s asserted prestige as proof of undisputed institutional authority.

1955 Supreme Court Deportation Decision

Marcello fought the government over immigration status for decades. In Marcello v. Bonds, 349 U.S. 302 (1955), the Supreme Court considered his deportability under the 1952 Immigration and Nationality Act. The Court’s opinion specifically discussed the 1938 narcotics conviction and the statutory grounds for removal. The decision confirmed the government’s authority to proceed under the applicable immigration framework. [8]

The ruling does not itself prove that Marcello committed every illegal act attributed to the New Orleans Mafia, nor does it resolve all subsequent challenges to how his removal was carried out. It establishes a particular immigration-law issue and explains why the federal government maintained pressure even while other criminal investigations encountered evidentiary difficulties. [8][9]

Separating the 1955 Supreme Court result from the later 1961 physical deportation keeps the chronology accurate. A final administrative or judicial decision may precede actual enforcement by several years.

April 1961 Deportation to Guatemala and Return

On April 4, 1961, federal authorities physically deported Marcello to Guatemala. The D.C. Circuit later summarized his legal challenge, including his allegation that the government had used an improper procedure and had not honored asserted notice requirements. Those claims were matters raised in litigation; their appearance in the opinion does not itself prove each assertion. [9]

Marcello subsequently returned to the United States without lawful readmission, according to a later Fifth Circuit immigration decision. His return triggered another set of deportation proceedings and prolonged appeals. The Guatemala episode became part of the public mythology of Marcello’s defiance of federal authority, but the more useful archival account concerns exact immigration decisions, dates, procedures and statutory grounds. [9][10]

The deportation also became a point of political conflict in later discussions of the Kennedy administration. Marcello’s hostility toward Attorney General Robert F. Kennedy is documented in congressional inquiries, but animosity does not establish participation in the assassination of President Kennedy, addressed separately below. [19]

Historical Criminal Enterprises and Income Systems

The organization’s economic activity changed with the period. Retrospective histories and judicial cases identify multiple streams of illicit income, but they do not support a single uninterrupted accounting statement for “the New Orleans Mafia” from the nineteenth century to the 1990s. [4][11][18][23]

  • Gambling and bookmaking: Illegal casino activity, sports wagering, slot-machine operations and protection of betting markets in the metropolitan region.
  • Bootlegging and vice: Prohibition-era alcohol distribution and other unlawful entertainment markets attributed to individual factions and historical periods.
  • Commercial influence: Alleged interference with distribution, transport and market access, particularly in the city’s port-linked economy.
  • Corrupt contracting: Schemes involving bribes and public or union-related insurance business, central to the BRILAB case.
  • Legalized-gaming infiltration: The 1990s licensing and video-poker fraud case involving companies presented as legitimate operators.
  • Criminal intimidation: Threats and violence documented in the history of competing networks, evaluated by individual offense and evidence.

These categories are historical and case-specific. They should not be taken to imply that every named associate participated in every scheme, or that every Italian-owned business or Louisiana gambling operator had organized-crime ties.

Labor, Public Institutions, and Political Corruption Allegations

The city’s docks, transportation and service economy created opportunities for corrupt individuals to pressure employers, labor representatives or licensing authorities. But the economic role of unions and dockworkers was overwhelmingly legitimate; alleged misconduct by a few does not transform an entire workforce into a criminal organization. [2][3][23]

The later BRILAB investigation turned specifically on a proposed system for placing insurance contracts through bribes and shared commissions. Its relevance to political corruption lies in the commercial access investigators said was being sold. The original prosecution described certain public and labor markets as targets, rather than establishing that all local officials or union organizations acted together as one Mafia enterprise. [11][14]

Public corruption cases deserve special analytical attention because ordinary taxpayers, workers and consumers may bear hidden costs. However, charges against one official cannot be generalized to an entire administration, and acquittals, vacaturs and separate convictions must remain visible.

Federal Surveillance and Enterprise-Oriented Investigation

The FBI’s New Orleans field-office history describes a gradual expansion from individual criminal cases and intelligence collection toward enterprise investigations supported by modern federal racketeering statutes. Surveillance, cooperating witnesses, electronic recordings and financial inquiry became increasingly important as prosecutors sought to prove coordinated activity. [5]

The shift did not produce inevitable convictions. Electronic evidence still required lawful authorization, reliable context and proof of each charged element. Courts also had to determine whether the law actually covered the kind of alleged bribery or fraud presented by prosecutors. The BRILAB litigation eventually demonstrated that an initial conviction could be overturned after changes in controlling Supreme Court doctrine. [11][14]

For the historian, this means two things: the investigation may yield rich evidence of interactions and proposed arrangements, while the specific crime of conviction depends on the legal standard in effect after all appeals.

The Kennedy Assassination: Allegation and Documented Limit

Marcello has been repeatedly named in theories concerning the 1963 assassination of President John F. Kennedy. The U.S. House Select Committee on Assassinations considered Mafia-related hypotheses and stated that Marcello had potential motive, means and opportunity. Crucially, the committee also said it could not establish direct evidence of his complicity. It expressly warned that suspicious associations are not equivalent to proof of conspiracy. [19]

Later publicity has sometimes presented alleged recorded admissions by Marcello as definitive. The Assassination Records Review Board reported in 1998 that it reviewed BRILAB surveillance transcripts and did not locate the specific conversations alleged to contain such confessions. It did identify other assassination-related discussions and arranged for relevant transcripts to be made available. The board’s narrower finding must not be represented as either proof of a confession or proof that all questions are resolved. [20]

Accordingly, this article does not present Marcello as a convicted, judicially established or demonstrably proven participant in the JFK assassination. Readers should consult the official congressional and archival documents rather than treating dramatizations, hearsay or repeated internet claims as findings.

1979–1980: Operation BRILAB

Federal agents conducted an undercover investigation known as BRILAB—a contraction of bribery and labor—focused on attempts to obtain insurance contracts through corruption. Agents posed as representatives of a fictitious California insurance consultancy and worked with cooperating participant Joseph Hauser. The scheme investigated concerned valuable government and labor-related contracts and alleged sharing of insurance commissions. [5][11][14]

The district court’s published opinion explains the June 1980 indictment and its charging theories: RICO conspiracy and substantive RICO, mail and wire fraud, and interstate travel in aid of racketeering. Defendants included Carlos Marcello, former Louisiana administration official Charles E. Roemer II, lobbyist I. Irving Davidson, attorney Vincent Marinello and, in a superseding indictment, Aubrey Young. Their later outcomes differed. [11]

The operation supplied an unusually detailed public picture of political access, offers of payment and the risk that government contracts could be diverted by unlawful arrangements. Nonetheless, the indictment’s narrative must be tracked to the actual counts on which a jury convicted or acquitted and to later appellate relief.

1981–1983: BRILAB Verdict and Initial Appeal

On August 3, 1981, a jury convicted Marcello and Roemer of RICO conspiracy. They were acquitted on the substantive counts tried, while Davidson and Marinello were acquitted; a judge had previously ordered an acquittal for Young when the government failed to establish its case against him. These are materially different procedural outcomes. [11][12][14]

Marcello was sentenced in January 1982 to seven years on the BRILAB count, while Roemer received a shorter prison term. In 1983 the Fifth Circuit affirmed the defendants’ convictions. At that moment the federal judgments remained in force, and many later biographies still quote that result without considering what happened next. [12][13][14]

The convictions’ eventual fate changed the legal status of this episode. A historically accurate profile cannot stop at the 1983 affirmance and characterize the BRILAB judgment as a surviving conviction in 2026.

Separate Los Angeles Federal Judge-Bribery Convictions

Marcello faced a separate prosecution in California alleging an effort to bribe and improperly influence a federal judge. In the resulting case, Marcello, Samuel Orlando Sciortino and Phillip Rizzuto went to trial on related but distinct charges. The jury convicted Marcello on all three counts; Sciortino and Rizzuto had different verdicts. The Ninth Circuit affirmed the judgments in 1984. [15]

This matter is not the BRILAB insurance-contract case. It concerned an attempted corrupt approach to a federal judicial officer, and the appellate opinion explains the evidence and different counts of conviction. References that simply say “Marcello’s 1980s convictions were overturned” conflate separate cases and produce a false legal history. [15]

The distinction between a conviction that was vacated and another judgment that survived appellate review is central to accurately explaining both federal enforcement and Marcello’s late career.

1989: BRILAB Convictions Vacated

In United States v. Marcello, 876 F.2d 1147 (5th Cir. 1989), the court reviewed Marcello’s and Roemer’s surviving BRILAB RICO-conspiracy convictions in light of the Supreme Court’s McNally decision, which constrained the government’s use of an intangible-rights theory under federal mail-fraud law. The Fifth Circuit reversed the district court and vacated the challenged convictions. [14]

The court reviewed what the prosecution had submitted to the jury and why the theory of mail fraud as a predicate no longer supported the judgment as charged. The fact that a conviction was originally affirmed in 1983 does not negate the 1989 vacatur. A legally correct chronology records both the original result and the later controlling disposition. [13][14]

This decision did not vacate Marcello’s separate California judge-bribery convictions affirmed by the Ninth Circuit. The two procedural histories must be reported independently. Nor does a vacatur prove that every action alleged during the undercover investigation did not occur: it decides the legal validity of the conviction under the law and jury instructions at issue. [14][15]

Marcello’s Later Years and Death in 1993

Marcello’s authority weakened amid federal prosecutions, imprisonment, health problems and changing criminal markets. Contemporary obituaries describe a man whose national notoriety had outlasted the conditions that originally enabled his rise. His long period of prominence should not be read as proof of continuing operational control over the New Orleans underworld after his health deteriorated. [4][22]

Carlos Marcello died on March 2, 1993. His death marked the close of the organization’s best-known leadership period, but it did not mean no associated individuals or criminal schemes remained. Subsequent federal investigations into Louisiana’s legalized gambling industry disclosed networks that prosecutors and courts characterized as the continuing Marcello crime family. [4][18][22]

Reports conflict over whether his brother Joseph acted as an interim head and over the exact start of Anthony Carollo’s leadership. This entry uses dated court documents to describe what was alleged or established in the 1990s, rather than inventing a seamless succession calendar.

Anthony Carollo and the 1990s Administration

Anthony S. Carollo was identified as head of the New Orleans-based organized-crime family in federal accounts of the 1990s video-poker case. The Fifth Circuit’s United States v. Salvatore opinion discusses the testimony of a cooperating participant who dealt with Carollo and other people identified with the Marcello family. It also refers to Frank J. Gagliano Sr., Joseph Marcello Jr., Joseph Gagliano and Sebastian Salvatore in the network. [18]

The court record does not establish that each person had identical formal rank or responsibility. In particular, relatives, associates, intermediary business owners and alleged Mafia officers may all participate in a transaction without having the same organizational status. Public accounts identify Frank Gagliano Sr. as an underboss figure, but the most defensible narrative associates that description with the dated prosecution and its sources. [16][18]

This is the last era for which this article has substantial public judicial evidence of a functioning New Orleans Mafia-linked criminal network. Claims of an uninterrupted administration beyond it require fresh records, not a copied genealogical list of bosses.

Louisiana’s Video-Poker Law and Licensing Opportunity

In 1991, Louisiana legalized certain video draw-poker devices subject to a state licensing framework. That law created a regulated market for manufacturers, distributors, route operators and businesses hosting machines. It also created incentives for prohibited participants to conceal their involvement through intermediaries. The Fifth Circuit later explained the regulatory environment in the Salvatore appeal. [18]

The prosecution centered in part on Worldwide Gaming of Louisiana and Louisiana Route Operators. Testimony described the businesses as fronts with license applications that concealed organized-crime involvement. Such a case differs from traditional illegal casino raids because the machines could be legal while the ownership, licensing or financial arrangements were fraudulent. [18]

The larger lesson concerns regulatory capture: a criminal organization may seek income not by prohibiting lawful commerce, but by inserting itself into a newly legalized and tightly licensed market. The distinction between the licensed gaming industry and individual defendants’ fraud matters for both legal analysis and reputational fairness.

1994: Federal Video-Poker Indictments and Arrests

In May 1994, a federal investigation involving the FBI and Louisiana State Police led to charges against seventeen men associated by prosecutors with the New Orleans, Gambino and Genovese organized-crime networks. Authorities alleged that individuals used gaming businesses to divert money and profit from a lawful video-poker market while concealing criminal ties. Initial arrest reporting documents the charging theory, not convictions of everyone named. [16]

The indictment concerned the use of multiple companies, including Worldwide Gaming, and alleged losses associated with the business relationship with Bally Gaming. The participants were from Louisiana and other states, showing how a regional commercial opportunity could attract cross-family arrangements. The later appellate record provides more precise evidence about how the companies functioned. [16][18]

The broader organized-crime claim should not be stretched to imply that all Louisiana casinos, gaming regulators or manufacturers were complicit. In the government’s account Bally was a victim of the alleged scheme, and the charged actors were a defined group rather than the entire industry. [16]

1995: Guilty Pleas and Organizational Consequences

In September 1995, defendants in the video-poker case entered guilty pleas, including prominent individuals linked to the New Orleans organization and associates of New York families. Contemporary Associated Press reporting describes a series of pleas by people accused of conspiring to infiltrate the industry. The later appellate opinion also records that most co-defendants pleaded guilty before the remaining trial. [17][18]

Prosecution reporting identified Anthony Carollo and Frank Gagliano Sr. as senior New Orleans Mafia figures. Their pleas make it inaccurate to describe the entire 1994 matter as allegations that never advanced to adjudication. At the same time, the exact charges and outcomes varied among defendants, and the record does not justify assuming that all original indictment counts resulted in convictions. [17][18]

One anti-crime advocate characterized the case at the time as a decisive blow to the New Orleans organization. That was a contemporary assessment of its impact, not an independently measurable declaration that the family had ceased to exist on a particular day. [17]

1997: United States v. Salvatore and the Trial Record

The Fifth Circuit’s April 1997 opinion, United States v. Salvatore, 110 F.3d 1131, affirmed the convictions of Sebastian Salvatore, Anthony Tusa Jr. and Victor Tusa Sr. for conduct connected to the licensed video-poker scheme. The court examined testimony, financial arrangements, licensing deception and the defendants’ relationships to companies operating in the business. [18]

The opinion describes cooperating witness Christopher Tanfield and corporate intermediaries used in the scheme, as well as evidence gathered from conversations recorded at a deli. Courts distinguished actual agreement and knowledge from mere association with other defendants. The resulting appellate judgment gives a stronger foundation for the organizational narrative than popular anecdotes about a supposed Mafia takeover of all gambling on the Gulf Coast. [18]

Care is needed in reporting the decision: the three named appellants were convicted and their convictions affirmed, whereas numerous co-defendants had already resolved their cases by pleas. These are separate legal paths, not one collective verdict. [18]

Decline, Market Change, and the Limits of a “Last Boss” Narrative

The cumulative effect of prosecution, leadership aging and exposure of clandestine business relationships substantially weakened the New Orleans family’s documented public profile. The video-poker case demonstrates that associated networks persisted after Carlos Marcello died, but it does not establish continuous command, crew strength or control of Gulf Coast illicit markets in later decades. [16][17][18]

Lists sometimes give Anthony Carollo a precise start and end date or identify an additional successor after him. Such lists often cite one another rather than dated primary proceedings. The evidentiary threshold for a current “boss” is higher than a historical mention in a 1995 prosecution; the position may have changed or ceased to have practical organizational meaning.

The decline of this particular crime family should not be confused with the disappearance of organized crime from Louisiana. Other networks, different markets and independent offenders can continue to operate in the same region without being members of a functioning Marcello family.

Geography: New Orleans, Jefferson Parish, and the Gulf Coast

The family’s historical base was the New Orleans metropolitan area, with important connections in neighboring Jefferson Parish. Economic links through the port, the Mississippi River, roads and commercial travel made the area a regional hub. During the Marcello era, wider ties extended toward neighboring Gulf Coast markets and other U.S. crime groups. [4][22][23]

Those connections must be defined by the transaction or relationship concerned. An individual from Dallas, Florida, Mississippi or New York working with a New Orleans associate does not by itself establish that the entire city or state was a permanent branch of one family. Equally, the fact that a family’s leader was influential does not prove absolute command of every gambler, nightclub or corrupt official in the region.

Later federal video-poker proceedings establish cross-state business relationships. Geographic reach in that prosecution was partly a function of license applications, distribution agreements and travel by individual defendants rather than an all-purpose territorial government of organized crime. [16][18]

Organizational Structure and Decision-Making

Modern American La Cosa Nostra generally has distinguished a boss, sometimes an underboss and consigliere, followed by captains, inducted members and noninducted associates. But published details about the New Orleans family’s complete ranks are much thinner than for major New York families. A standard schematic is an analytical analogy, not a verified 1990s organizational chart. [18][21]

The later New Orleans case shows an arrangement involving alleged senior family figures, trusted local associates, outside Mafia figures and business intermediaries. It does not imply that each defendant belonged to a formal captain-led crew, much less that every licensed business operator was inducted. Participants could cooperate for a particular commercial purpose while retaining separate affiliations. [18]

Successions are similarly complex. Matranga, Carolla, Marcello and Carollo can be placed in broad historical sequence, but precise offices, temporary administrators and overlapping authority require date-specific documentation. Family surnames are not evidence of biological inheritance of organizational power.

Present-Day Status and Evidentiary Limits

By the editorial cutoff of November 13, 2026, the strongest publicly verified organizational case discussed here remained the 1994–1997 video-poker investigation, supplemented by earlier court and Senate records. Those materials firmly establish important historical activity, but they do not supply a verified list of present-day members, an undisputed incumbent boss, recent family income or proof that the organization continues as a fully functioning Mafia administration. [16][18]

Conversely, absence of a recently publicized Mafia indictment is not a court judgment that every surviving associate has retired or that all organized-crime activity in the region is extinct. Public proceedings offer only a partial view of clandestine networks, and the meaning of “defunct” varies between historians and law-enforcement agencies.

The responsible editorial conclusion is historically documented family; contemporary hierarchy unverified. Future updates should be based on new indictments, verdicts, judgments, records or verifiable investigative reporting, and should never infer guilt from family relationship or geographical proximity.

Terminology

La Cosa Nostra (LCN)
Historical label for organized American Mafia families; the structure and documentary clarity differ by city and period. [21]
Crime family
Underworld organizational category; it does not refer to all people sharing an ethnic identity or surname.
Boss / acting boss
Individual exercising top authority or temporarily substituting for it; titles must be supported by dated records.
Underboss / consigliere
Senior deputy and adviser roles in common Mafia descriptions; local use may vary.
Caporegime / soldier / associate
Crew leader, inducted member, or noninducted participant; distinctions relevant to evidence and attribution.
Bootlegging
Illegal production, transport or sale of alcohol, particularly during Prohibition.
Bookmaking
Organization of wagers, often on sporting events, outside applicable licensing or tax requirements.
RICO
Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act, allowing certain prosecutions based on an enterprise and a specified pattern of predicate offenses.
Predicate offense
An underlying specified crime used in an enterprise/racketeering charge; whether a charge survives depends on governing law and proof.
BRILAB
FBI undercover bribery-labor investigation into corrupt placement of insurance business, 1979–1980. [5][11]
Vacatur
Court order nullifying a judgment; the 1989 ruling vacated BRILAB convictions. [14]
Affirmance
Appellate court decision leaving a challenged judgment in effect; the separate 1984 Ninth Circuit judge-bribery decision affirmed the relevant convictions. [15]
Acquittal
Judicial or jury determination that the prosecution did not prove the charged count, not a historical organizational rank.
Video-poker route operator
Licensed or purportedly licensed participant managing locations and revenue flows of regulated gaming machines, central to the 1990s case. [18]
Commercial front
Business through which ownership, financing or control is allegedly concealed; the specific element of fraud must be proved.
Anti-Italian lynching
Extrajudicial murder driven partly by ethnic prejudice; the 1891 victims were not criminally convicted of Hennessy’s murder. [1][3]

Research and Editorial Standards

Primary sources and hierarchy. The New Orleans Public Library archives, federal appellate opinions, government report catalogs and the FBI’s institutional account establish different kinds of facts. Courts are authoritative on the procedural consequences of cases; an FBI institutional history is its own perspective on investigations; historical synthesis supplies context, not a substitute for trial outcomes. [1][5][7][14][15][18]

Correct handling of the Hennessy episode. There were no convictions of the eleven prisoners lynched in March 1891. The article separates genuine nineteenth-century factional violence from unfounded collective accusations and the subsequent mass killing of Italian prisoners. It does not use the mob’s violence as proof of criminal guilt. [1][2][3]

Correct handling of Marcello litigation. The BRILAB RICO-conspiracy verdict was initially affirmed in 1983 but vacated in 1989. Separate California convictions for attempted judicial corruption were affirmed in 1984. Neither case’s legal outcome can be reduced to a generic statement that all Marcello convictions survived or all were reversed. [13][14][15]

Assassination claims. The House committee considered Marcello a person of investigative interest but found no direct evidence of complicity; the records board did not corroborate the specific alleged BRILAB confessions. The profile does not substitute conspiracy theories for criminal proof. [19][20]

Late organizational status. Guilty pleas and appellate judgments in 1995–1997 document criminal activity and reported family officers at that time, not a current boss or an exact date of extinction. No unsupported membership or revenue figures are supplied. [17][18]
