Overview

The Bonanno crime family is one of the five principal New York City organizations of the Italian-American Mafia, also called La Cosa Nostra (LCN). Its name commemorates Joseph Bonanno, who assumed leadership in 1931 after the death of Salvatore Maranzano. The organization, however, developed from criminal associations that preceded both Bonanno and the formal five-family settlement of that year. It is not a biological family or a representative institution of Italian-American communities. [1][2][22]

For much of the twentieth century, Bonanno leaders and crews participated in illegal gambling, loansharking, extortion, narcotics markets, corruption, labor racketeering, and other crimes. The family's history is also unusually well documented through a series of federal investigations: the FBI's undercover Donnie Brasco operation, the trials of boss Joseph Massino and other members, and later prosecutions concerning illegal gambling, extortionate loans, and interference with lawful institutions. [3][4][14]

This article follows the organization's history from the Castellammarese War through publicly documented proceedings reported by November 9, 2026. It distinguishes court findings and guilty pleas from allegations; it does not claim that public reporting establishes the complete current membership, financial scale, or commanding officer of a secret enterprise.

Federal racketeering cases characterize the organization as an enterprise whose members operated as a continuing unit. This is a legal and investigatory description, not an assertion that every participant was responsible for every offense or that every person identified by prosecutors was convicted. [10][12]

Origins and the Maranzano Organization

During the opening decades of the twentieth century, Italian-American organized crime in New York consisted of overlapping neighborhood groups, bootleggers, extortionists, gambling operators, and criminal associations that frequently competed with one another. The national prohibition of alcoholic beverages between 1920 and 1933 substantially expanded illegal alcohol opportunities, but Mafia enterprises were not created by Prohibition alone. Migration from Sicily and southern Italy supplied some personal and regional ties within such networks; ethnic origins do not justify attributing organized crime to the much larger immigrant communities from which individual offenders emerged.

The nucleus of the later Bonanno organization was connected with migrants from Castellammare del Golfo, Sicily, and with Salvatore Maranzano. In the so-called Castellammarese War, factions aligned with Maranzano and Joseph Masseria struggled over the leadership and economic organization of New York's Italian-American underworld. Masseria was killed in April 1931; Maranzano briefly emerged as the dominant figure before his own murder in September. [1][2][22]

Maranzano's removal was followed by a reconfiguration of New York's principal Mafia groups. Charles “Lucky” Luciano became associated with the creation of the Commission, through which representatives of several criminal families could address disputes and other common interests without permanently uniting their operations. Within this settlement, Joseph Bonanno took over the organization that had been led by Maranzano. [1][2]

The conventional 1931 founding date thus marks a reorganization and succession, not the birth of a crime syndicate from nothing. For historical precision, the encyclopedia should distinguish the pre-1931 faction from the named Bonanno family that developed after Maranzano's death.

Joseph Bonanno’s Leadership, 1931–1960s

Joseph Bonanno became head of the former Maranzano organization while still in his twenties. The very long tenure traditionally associated with him—beginning in 1931 and ending in the turmoil of the 1960s—gave the group its enduring name. He cultivated relationships across the wider American Mafia and maintained ties that extended beyond New York, including the western United States and parts of Canada. These were networks of people and commercial relationships rather than evidence of an undivided national command. [2][22]

The business of the crime family changed as illegal markets changed. Bootlegging diminished after Prohibition ended, while gambling, extortionate lending, and control of commercial opportunities remained available sources of income. Narcotics trafficking became important to some members and associates, although the family did not operate as one single trafficking company under uninterrupted centralized management.

Bonanno cultivated an image of traditional leadership and later offered his own retrospective account in A Man of Honor (1983). His memoir is a valuable statement of how he represented himself but is not an independent verification of disputed Mafia operations. Scholars and courts must compare his narrative against surveillance records, testimony, contemporary reporting, and prosecutions.

A succession question eventually became central. Bonanno's son Salvatore “Bill” Bonanno was prominent in the family orbit, but leadership of a Mafia “family” was not automatically inherited in the manner of a family business. Competing captains and other bosses had their own interests, which became especially important during the conflicts of the 1960s. [2]

The 1960s Commission Crisis and Bonanno War

Joseph Bonanno's relationship with other senior Mafia leaders deteriorated amid disputes over influence, appointments, and the Commission. Historical accounts describe allegations that Bonanno and Joseph Magliocco, a leader in the former Profaci organization, were involved in a proposed plot against rival bosses. The circumstances of these allegations have been reconstructed largely through participant accounts and later reporting; they should not be treated as a transparently documented boardroom decision. [2][22]

In 1964, Bonanno disappeared during an acute leadership crisis. Accounts differ over aspects of the episode: Bonanno maintained he had been kidnapped, while critics raised questions about his period out of public view. His extended absence became part of a struggle over recognition of the family's leadership. The internal conflict, often called the “Banana War” or “Bonanno War,” involved a faction associated with Bonanno and his son and a rival faction recognized by other Mafia leaders. [2]

Gaspar DiGregorio became one of the chief figures aligned against the Bonanno faction. The resulting conflict underscored the limitations of the Commission: it could influence the recognition of a boss but could not simply eliminate rival loyalties or prevent prolonged violence. Bonanno withdrew from active New York leadership and relocated his life to Arizona; 1968 is widely used as the end of his rule, although the effective loss of power began earlier. [2][22]

These events make two distinctions essential. First, the title “boss” does not always capture who actually gave orders during a disputed succession. Second, the absence of the historical namesake did not bring about the dissolution of the family, which continued through new administrations and factions.

Leadership Changes, Rastelli, and Galante

After the Bonanno War, the organization's leadership moved through several hands, including Paul Sciacca and Natale Evola, before Philip “Rusty” Rastelli became a leading boss figure during the 1970s. The dates associated with these transitions vary among accounts, particularly where a nominal boss was imprisoned or competing members exercised authority outside prison. A rigorous leadership history should distinguish formal selection, acting administration, and actual power. [2][7]

Carmine Galante, an earlier Bonanno underboss and major figure in narcotics-related organized crime history, was another central power broker in the 1970s. Following his release from prison, he pursued influence over the family and its criminal opportunities. Historical descriptions sometimes call him boss even when Rastelli is recorded as holding the title. The competing descriptions reflect the importance of separating an individual's position, ambition, and practical control. [2][18]

The disputes were not merely personal feuds. Whoever controlled crews and recognized which criminals could operate in particular markets influenced access to gambling income, high-interest loans, narcotics networks, and protection rackets. When a leader was incarcerated, intermediaries could relay instructions, while other figures might act autonomously or challenge orders.

Carmine Galante and the 1979 Killing

On July 12, 1979, Carmine Galante and two other men were shot dead at Joe and Mary's Italian-American Restaurant in the Bushwick section of Brooklyn. Contemporary reporting identified the other men killed as Leonardo Coppola and Giuseppe Turano, the restaurant's owner. This was a public triple homicide, not a single isolated “mob execution” without additional victims. [18]

Later histories often link Galante's murder to conflicts over narcotics activity and to opposition among senior Mafia figures. Some accounts present approval by the Commission as settled; attributing a particular murder order to a collective decision requires careful differentiation among evidence at trial, participant testimony, prosecution theory, and retrospective reconstruction. The central, directly documentable fact is the killing itself and the long-running leadership struggle surrounding it. [2][18]

Galante's death weakened the faction associated with him and reinforced the authority of competing figures. It also illustrates the extent to which the family’s leadership disputes could produce direct violence in public commercial settings, placing bystanders, employees, and unrelated businesses at risk.

The 1981 Three-Captains Murders

In May 1981, three senior Bonanno captains—Alphonse “Sonny Red” Indelicato, Philip “Philly Lucky” Giaccone, and Dominick “Big Trin” Trinchera—were killed in an episode central to later federal prosecutions. Their murders are commonly understood in the context of conflict over Philip Rastelli's leadership and competing crews. Subsequent cooperating witnesses and trials helped reconstruct events that Mafia silence initially concealed. [2][4]

Federal prosecutors later made evidence of historical murders part of racketeering cases against senior Bonanno figures. The significance is methodological as well as biographical: acts committed years earlier could be prosecuted and described in detail when surviving participants cooperated, physical evidence was recovered, and the government's account could be tested in court.

These killings should not be confused with the separate murder of captain Dominick Napolitano later in 1981. Napolitano's death followed exposure of the FBI undercover operation and became another important element of later criminal cases. [2][3]

Donnie Brasco: FBI Undercover Penetration

The FBI's Donnie Brasco operation is among the most consequential undercover investigations in American organized-crime history. Special Agent Joseph D. Pistone entered Mafia circles in 1976 under an assumed identity and gradually developed relationships with people connected to the Bonanno family. Rather than simply observing from outside, he gained prolonged access to members' conversations, habits, relationships, and criminal business practices. [3]

Pistone's undercover contacts included Benjamin “Lefty” Ruggiero and captain Dominick “Sonny Black” Napolitano. His work showed how an outsider could acquire trust within a closed criminal network through repeated interaction, reciprocal services, and introductions. This was a long-term investigation that demanded unusual attention to personal security and evidence preservation.

After the FBI ended the operation in 1981, Bonanno participants learned that “Donnie Brasco” was a federal agent. The revelation was deeply damaging to Mafia claims about internal screening and secrecy. The FBI says the investigation ultimately contributed to more than 100 federal convictions; this is a program-wide FBI result, not an assertion that all those convictions were Bonanno family convictions. [3]

Napolitano was killed later that year. His death became one of the murders connected with later Bonanno prosecutions. Undercover investigation, witness cooperation, and electronic surveillance all changed the ability of federal investigators to document enterprise hierarchy rather than relying solely on street arrests. [3][4]

The 1997 film Donnie Brasco is a dramatization based on these events. Its scenes and dialogue are not substitutes for FBI and court records.

Criminal Businesses and Revenue Systems

The Bonanno family historically drew revenue from a portfolio of unlawful markets rather than a single continuous enterprise. Specific criminal schemes depended upon crews, geographic territory, opportunities, and the period in question. The following categories appear in federal accounts of the family and its members: [4][7][9][12]

  • Illegal gambling: Bookmaking, gambling parlors, games operated outside lawful licensing, and collections from people who hosted or managed games.
  • Loansharking and extortionate collection: High-interest loans coupled with threats, coercion, or unlawful collection tactics; repayment frequently failed to reduce principal when interest alone was collected.
  • Labor racketeering: Corruption or coercion involving some union officials, job allocation, industry contracting, or benefit arrangements; lawful labor organizations are not automatically Mafia entities.
  • Narcotics trafficking: Drug distribution and associated financing by documented members or associates in particular periods, including historical international links.
  • Fraud and financial misconduct: Schemes involving fraudulent transactions, business concealment, and laundering of criminal revenue.
  • Violence and intimidation: Threats, assaults, murders, and retaliation used to enforce disputed debts, protect criminal earnings, or challenge rivals.

These are documented areas of activity, not proof that every crew participated in each category. They also have different evidentiary foundations: a guilty plea for extortion proves particular conduct; an indictment listing drug trafficking describes an accusation pending adjudication unless subsequent disposition is established.

Labor Racketeering and Legitimate Institutions

Labor racketeering did not require an entire union to be a criminal organization. The relevant mechanisms involved gaining leverage over particular officials, employers, hiring arrangements, wages, benefits, contracts, or transportation networks. Such leverage could yield payments, protect monopolistic arrangements, or pressure businesses to comply with criminal demands.

In the late 1980s, federal authorities brought a civil RICO action concerning the Bonanno family and allegations connected with Teamsters Local 814, which represented employees in moving, storage, and related services. Such proceedings are useful for understanding proposed links between criminal enterprises and legitimate labor institutions, but the scope of allegations and the legal disposition must be distinguished carefully. The existence of corrupt participants did not make every member of a union complicit.

The labor connection also appears in cases involving transportation and distribution operations. Federal prosecutions of Bonanno members have included violence and workplace-associated offenses. In a July 2006 case, prosecutors announced convictions of Baldassare Amato, Stephen LoCurto, and Anthony Basile on racketeering conspiracy charges encompassing murders, including the killing of a newspaper delivery superintendent. The court outcome is stronger evidence for those defendants and offenses than broad journalistic claims about permanent control of an entire industry. [6]

From an institutional perspective, the victim pool can include unionized employees whose representation is undermined, honest employers facing pressure, legitimate competitors excluded from markets, and public pension or benefit systems placed at risk.

Geographic Reach and Canadian Relationships

The Bonanno family's organizational base lay in the New York metropolitan area. Historically important locations included several New York City boroughs and nearby communities on Long Island, where illegal gambling and loansharking cases continued into the twenty-first century. Geographic reference to these areas does not characterize their residents or local businesses generally as associated with crime. [1][12][15]

Contemporary and retrospective accounts also describe connections with criminals and syndicates in Canada, especially Montreal, as well as relationships in other U.S. states. These links need precise treatment: the Rizzuto and Cotroni networks, for example, had their own participants, histories, and changing relationships with American families. It is historically misleading to presume that every Canadian Mafia operation was a local branch continuously and directly controlled by New York. [2]

Similarly, evidence of international movement of money or contraband is not evidence that a single Bonanno administration commanded every stage of a transnational network. Many schemes involved brokers, associates, independent participants, and temporary alliances. A serious geographic profile should identify the particular operation, dates, documents, defendants, and jurisdiction before asserting organizational control.

Family Hierarchy and Decision-Making

Federal descriptions of the Bonanno enterprise show a boss at the top of the organization, generally assisted by an underboss and a consigliere. Beneath that administration stood caporegimes (captains) responsible for crews made up of inducted members or soldiers and noninducted associates. A person may be called an acting boss or acting captain when substituting for an imprisoned or unavailable official. [10][12]

  1. Boss / acting boss: Top-ranking family authority or a temporary substitute, depending on the period and evidence.
  2. Underboss: Senior officer who may coordinate captains and relay administrative decisions.
  3. Consigliere: A senior counselor, often described in prosecution documents as involved in disputes and executive business.
  4. Captain / capo: Supervisor of a crew that conducts or coordinates criminal operations.
  5. Soldier / made member: An initiated member recognized within the organization under its rules.
  6. Associate: A person who works with family members but does not have the same formal status as an inducted member.

Crews could generate income and convey a portion upward, often described as kicking up. Recognition of an individual as an associate did not mean the person had access to the family's highest-level deliberations, knew every offense, or was legally responsible for every member's conduct.

The Commission was an inter-family coordinating body rather than a permanent replacement for individual family administrations. A family could lose recognition or effective representation during a crisis, as the Bonanno organization did amid the upheavals of the 1960s. Reports of Commission decisions should be dated and evidenced rather than presented as universally valid for every era. [1][2]

Joseph Massino and the 1990s

Joseph Massino emerged as a central Bonanno leader during the late twentieth century and is generally identified as boss beginning in 1991, after Rastelli's death. His administration attempted to repair an organization damaged by internal warfare, federal prosecutions, and the exposure of informants. Accounts of this era describe an emphasis on secrecy, discipline, and limiting exposure to investigators. Whether every claimed operational innovation was distinctive to Massino cannot be established solely through retrospective narratives. [2][17]

Massino's importance is demonstrated by the outcome of the federal investigation into his racketeering activities. On July 30, 2004, a federal jury convicted him on eleven counts, including offenses involving multiple murders and racketeering. Government annual reporting identified his conviction as a major organized-crime enforcement success. [5][17]

He later agreed to cooperate with the government. The FBI describes this as an unprecedented development in the New York Mafia hierarchy, identifying him as the highest-ranking mobster to become an FBI cooperator. His assistance contributed to further investigations of family leadership and earlier crimes. The shift had particular significance because the system relied upon members' willingness to conceal wrongdoing and resist cooperation. [17][21]

Massino's conviction and cooperation are a reminder that organizational restoration can be temporary. The same network of captains, intermediaries, and trusted confidants used to supervise operations can also supply the documentary and testimonial record that investigators need once participants decide to cooperate.

Federal Prosecutions and Cooperating Witnesses

Between the early 2000s and the following decade, federal authorities brought extensive Bonanno cases involving murder, racketeering, extortion, illegal gambling, drug-related allegations, and other offenses. In February 2007, the Eastern District of New York said its investigation had produced prosecutions of more than 100 Bonanno members and associates, and stated that several bosses or acting bosses had been convicted. A separate October 2009 release referred to more than 125 prosecutions in that district since the investigation began. These numbers describe prosecutions during identified periods, not the family's total membership or the number of persons imprisoned at any one time. [4][7]

The volume of cooperating testimony changed the kind of evidence available. Members could explain initiation, rank, orders, transactions, murders, collections, and disputes to investigators and juries. Cooperation, however, creates incentives that courts must scrutinize: witnesses may hope to reduce their own sentences, avoid other penalties, or negotiate benefits. Recorded conversations, corroborating documents, financial trails, physical evidence, and cross-examination remain essential safeguards.

The federal prosecutions also employed the Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act (RICO), a statute that permits prosecution of certain patterns of specified crimes connected to an enterprise. Enterprise cases can explain the organization of illegal activity more effectively than disconnected prosecutions of individual acts, but a RICO indictment remains an allegation until the relevant count is proved or admitted. [4][6][7]

Particular cases should therefore be analyzed by defendant, charges, result, and appeal status, not merely by a cumulative count of dramatic headlines.

Leadership Disruption after 2004

After Massino was prosecuted, other Bonanno officers performed acting leadership functions. Justice Department records identify Anthony “Tony Green” Urso as having led the organization from early 2003 to early 2004 and Vincent “Vinny Gorgeous” Basciano as having assumed leadership afterward. The department reported convictions involving both men, distinguishing them from mere charges. [4][6]

A June 2005 Justice Department announcement included charges against Basciano relating to murder conspiracy and solicitation of the murder of a federal law-enforcement official. As a charging announcement, that document is evidence of the government's allegations at the time; any statement about guilt in a specific count requires a later disposition. [20]

Michael “The Nose” Mancuso later appeared in federal proceedings described as an acting boss. In August 2008, the Justice Department announced that Mancuso and three Bonanno soldiers had pleaded guilty to murder-conspiracy-related charges, including Mancuso's acknowledged role in the 2004 murder of Randolph Pizzolo. The release documents the plea, not a claim that he remained boss in every subsequent year. [8]

The distinction between a formal boss, an acting boss, a prison-based leader, and a ruling panel is crucial in this era. A snapshot of ranks in a 2007 or 2009 case does not establish a current organizational chart decades later.

Cases and Dispositions in the 2010s

Later cases document continuing family-associated activities and the importance of following charges through to the legal outcome. In August 2018, acting captain Ronald “Ronnie G” Giallanzo was sentenced to fourteen years of imprisonment for racketeering conspiracy involving extortionate lending and collection. The court also ordered forfeiture and sale of property associated with the proceeds. This is an adjudicated result tied to a specific defendant and conduct. [9]

In a separate January 2018 indictment, federal prosecutors identified Joseph Cammarano Jr. as an acting boss and John Zancocchio as a consigliere at the time described in their case. An indictment is not a conviction, and those accusations should not be used to create a permanent or unqualified leadership roster. [10]

In fact, Cammarano and Zancocchio were acquitted of the charges tried against them. A subsequent federal court decision in another defendant's case expressly records their acquittals. Any encyclopedia profile that reports the 2018 charges without the acquittals would materially misstate the procedural history. [11]

A separate December 2018 sentencing involved Frank “Frankie Boy” Salerno, described by prosecutors as a Bonanno soldier, who received a sixty-month sentence for racketeering conspiracy. The conduct described in that superseding indictment included drug trafficking, loansharking, gambling, and obstruction-related predicate acts. [19]

2022 Gambling and Corruption Investigation

On August 16, 2022, the Eastern District of New York announced indictments against members and associates of the Bonanno and Genovese families and a police detective concerning illegal gambling, racketeering, money laundering, and related alleged conduct. The government identified Anthony Pipitone, Vito Pipitone, and Agostino Gabriele as Bonanno participants in its charging narrative. [12]

Investigators alleged that illegal gambling parlors operated behind or within otherwise ordinary establishments in Queens and Long Island. They also described a business relationship between the two crime-family networks and a system of distributing gambling proceeds. The alleged corruption of a public official served two purported purposes: protecting certain unlawful operations and applying pressure to competitors.

At the indictment stage, those descriptions were allegations requiring proof. The police-corruption component subsequently reached a jury verdict in 2025, addressed separately below. The case also demonstrates why geographically specific crime reporting must not confuse a commercial storefront used by criminals with the criminal liability of every customer, employee, or neighboring business. [12][15]

2024–2025 John Ragano Proceedings

John Ragano, also called “Bazoo,” was identified by federal prosecutors as a Bonanno member in a criminal case involving extortionate collection of credit. A federal jury convicted him on October 11, 2024 of extortionate collection. The same jury acquitted him of other charged counts including conspiracy, witness harassment, and witness tampering. Both sides of that outcome are important to accurate legal reporting. [13]

The evidence described by prosecutors concerned a $150,000 loan extended in 2021 with interest payments of approximately $1,800 per week. The government said Ragano continued demanding payment despite arrest, supervision, and a prior guilty plea on related charges. Trial evidence included recordings made by the victim; in one confrontation, prosecutors said Ragano compelled him to remove his clothes while attempting to determine whether he was recording the encounter. [13][14]

On March 19, 2025, Ragano received a 37-month prison sentence for the convicted offense. The Justice Department stated that sentence would follow an earlier 57-month term for related conspiracy conduct. This sequence illustrates the difference between a charge, conviction, sentence, and the legal treatment of additional offending while already under court supervision. [14]

It also provides concrete evidence of the human consequences of loansharking: financial extraction, fear, humiliation, and interference with an ordinary person's ability to report coercion.

2025 Hector Rosario Verdict

On March 5, 2025, a federal jury convicted former Nassau County police detective Hector Rosario of making false statements to FBI agents concerning his work for the Bonanno family. Prosecutors reported that he had accepted money to protect illegal gambling locations, had sought to disrupt competing locations, and had warned a Bonanno associate about investigative scrutiny. He was acquitted of obstruction of justice, and the distinction should be preserved whenever the case is summarized. [15]

The case is particularly significant because it describes an alleged form of criminal protection purchased from an office entrusted with enforcing the law. Investigators described activities over multiple years and identified both Bonanno-associated establishments and rival Genovese gambling businesses. The jury's verdict specifically established the false-statements offense; the prosecutor's account of other conduct should be attributed appropriately to the evidence presented and the government's description.

From a public-administration standpoint, police corruption can weaken community trust, distort lawful competition, obstruct investigations, and expose innocent witnesses. It should not be taken to mean that all officers within the police department participated or knew of the misconduct. [15]

2025 Illegal-Poker Indictment

On October 23, 2025, federal prosecutors announced a seven-count indictment against 31 defendants involving alleged technology-assisted cheating in illegal high-stakes poker games. The prosecution described involvement by members and associates of the Bonanno, Gambino, and Genovese organizations. The alleged scheme involved organizers, cheating devices, professional-athlete recruitment, money movement, and violent pressure on participants. The case was national in its alleged reach, while certain games were centered in New York. [16]

According to prosecutors, one game on Lexington Avenue in Manhattan was regarded as being under Bonanno influence; defendants identified in the release as linked to the family included Ernest Aiello, Julius Ziliani, and Thomas Gelardo. The government alleged that Mafia-connected defendants received portions of the proceeds and were involved in coercion related to gambling debts. These are allegations in the 2025 indictment, not independently adjudicated guilt for every named person. [16]

This investigation is a modern example of how traditional organized-crime relationships can overlap with technologies and financial arrangements not associated with the familiar imagery of the twentieth-century mob. A sound encyclopedia entry should distinguish unlawful poker operations, the specific allegations of rigging, the accused participants, and any later court dispositions rather than amalgamating them into one proven conspiracy.

Present-Day Status and Evidentiary Limits

Publicly reported federal investigations through November 9, 2026 demonstrate the continuing use of the Bonanno name in proceedings involving organized criminal activity. Convictions and sentences in the 2024–2025 Ragano case and the 2025 Rosario false-statements case are concrete events. The October 2025 poker case, by contrast, was announced as an indictment, and its accusations cannot be treated as adjudicated findings merely because defendants were arrested or described as members. [14][15][16]

The organization is widely understood to have substantially less national influence than the American Mafia exercised around the middle of the twentieth century. But accurate present-day quantification is difficult: undercover and confidential intelligence is not public, arrests are episodic, different sources use “member” and “associate” differently, and geographic visibility can be uneven. There is no responsible basis here for a precise current membership total, family annual revenue, or comparative ranking among all five families.

For the same reason, this entry does not declare an undisputed current boss. Persons identified as acting leaders in dated indictments, or in later secondary reporting, should not automatically be described as currently directing the organization. Publicly verified leadership claims require a stated date, source, degree of confidence, and a clear indication of whether they represent a law-enforcement allegation, a judicial finding, or an independent historical assessment.

Terminology

La Cosa Nostra (LCN)
Term commonly used by U.S. law-enforcement agencies for the American Mafia, including multiple regional crime families.
Five Families
The Bonanno, Colombo, Gambino, Genovese, and Lucchese organizations of New York City.
Boss / acting boss
Head of a family or a member temporarily exercising comparable authority, subject to factual verification.
Underboss
Senior member of an administration below the boss.
Consigliere
Senior adviser or counselor; duties vary by organization and period.
Caporegime / capo / captain
Member who manages a crew and may transmit payments, directions, and information to senior leaders.
Soldier / made member
An inducted member; separate from an associate who has not been inducted.
Associate
A person connected to a crew or member; the relationship does not automatically prove knowledge of each offense.
Racket
An illegal income-generating operation, such as extortion or unlawful gambling.
Loansharking
Extortionate lending or collection; lending at high interest alone must be distinguished from the specific crime established by applicable law.
Omertà
A code associated with secrecy and refusal to cooperate with outside authorities; actual conduct has varied.
Commission
An inter-family governing and dispute-resolution institution described in Mafia history and prosecution materials.
RICO
Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act; U.S. federal law governing particular patterns of racketeering activity and enterprises.

Research and Editorial Standards

Standard of proof. Allegations in complaints and indictments are identified as allegations. An indictment is not a guilty verdict. A conviction or plea is reported by offense and defendant; an acquittal is reported where relevant. An arrest is not treated as proof of participation in a crime family. The presumption of innocence applies to unresolved charges.

Source hierarchy. Federal court records and official reports establish procedural events most directly. FBI histories describe the Bureau's documented perspective but are institutional accounts, not neutral proof of every disputed historical interpretation. Historical journalism and documentaries help contextualize older periods; reminiscences by organized-crime participants may be self-serving. [1][2][3][11]

Leadership caution. Historical bosses are not presented as an uninterrupted legal succession with exact start and end dates when the evidence shows conflicting factions, incarceration, and acting roles. Present-day “boss” lists and estimated membership totals circulate in secondary sources; this article omits figures that cannot be independently established.

Community and identity. A Mafia organization is a criminal enterprise, not a proxy for Italians, Italian Americans, Sicilians, immigrants, business owners, unionized workers, or residents of communities in which members operated. Guilt is individual and must be evidenced by conduct.

Editorial cutoff. The source-backed discussion covers developments available as of November 9, 2026. Earlier charges may have subsequent developments not identified in this reference; readers should consult the linked primary records and court dockets before relying on a current procedural claim.
