Overview

The Dead Rabbits are one of the best-known names attached to the street violence of New York City’s nineteenth-century Five Points district. They are closely associated in journalism and subsequent popular history with the disturbances of July 4–5, 1857, usually called the Dead Rabbits–Bowery Boys Riot. The riot and the use of the name in the period press are well documented. Far less certain is that a single, enduring and self-identified criminal organization operated under that title, with continuous membership, a designated chief and a stable command structure. [1][2][3]

The principal source problem is unusual: people publicly identified as members of the so-called Dead Rabbits disputed that any club of that name existed. Historian Tyler Anbinder, examining contemporary accounts and the politics of the Sixth Ward, places the Roche Guard (also written Roach Guard), Mulberry Boys and followers of local political figure Matthew Brennan at the center of the 1857 fighting. He argues that “Dead Rabbits” probably became an adversarial nickname adopted by the press, rather than a reliable organizational name. Other histories continue to use Dead Rabbits as a convenient label for the fighting faction. The two usages must not be treated as identical claims. [1][4]

This article profiles a historically reported gang name and the neighborhood coalition to which it was applied. It covers the documented riot, social clubs, ward political machines, volunteer fire networks, policing, public disorder and later legend without assigning every offense committed in Five Points to a single organization. Irish immigrant communities were neither uniform political blocs nor collectively responsible for gang activity. [1][5][6]

Editorial finding: the July 1857 street fighting is established; a “Dead Rabbits” label existed; the Roche Guard’s existence is better supported than a separate, unified “Dead Rabbits” command. The sensationalized rabbit-banner origin story, exhaustive member counts and long-duration dates require explicit qualification. [1][4][7]

Political clubs, fire companies, saloon patrons, neighborhood combatants and criminal offenders could overlap, but their membership categories cannot automatically be collapsed into one gang roster. Five Points: Geography and Social Life

Five Points lay in lower Manhattan’s Sixth Ward around the meeting of streets near the old Collect Pond district. During the first half of the nineteenth century it became home to many working-class households and successive immigrant populations, including large numbers of Irish arrivals. Its crowded tenements and informal public spaces were famous to visitors and reporters. Streets, shops, churches, saloons and firehouses served as practical places for work, politics, recreation and conflict. The district’s notoriety must be analyzed together with ordinary housing and wage conditions. [1][5][8]

The zone most closely associated with the July 1857 combatants included Mulberry, Baxter, Mott, Bayard and Elizabeth streets, with the Bowery as an important contested boundary. These streets should not be turned into a neatly mapped “Dead Rabbits territory”: different political factions and social clubs occupied adjoining blocks, and participants moved throughout the Sixth Ward. A street intersection in a riot report locates an incident, not a permanent criminal headquarters. [1][2]

Archaeologist Rebecca Yamin’s analysis of excavated material from Five Points provides an essential corrective to lurid portrayals. Household goods, work remains, food and children’s objects document families trying to maintain domestic life under difficult circumstances. Such findings do not erase street violence, but demonstrate why depictions of every resident as a criminal “gangster” are historically untenable. [5]

The district’s reputation was also produced by journalists addressing middle-class readers. Poverty and danger became commercial storytelling devices, while labor, religious practice, mutual aid and the ambitions of migrants received less attention. An encyclopedia should retain the distinction between conditions the evidence documents and a journalistic stereotype applied to an entire neighborhood. [1][5]

What Did “Dead Rabbits” Mean?

Nineteenth-century accounts employed “Dead Rabbits” as a recognizable term for the Five Points side of the fighting. Precisely what it named is disputed. A club title, a separate secessionist gang, a neighborhood insult, a collective nickname for riot participants and a newspaper shorthand are different historical possibilities. The continuing visibility of the name does not resolve which explanation is correct. [1][3]

Anbinder describes testimony from Metropolitan officer Thomas Harvey that thieves in Five Points were colloquially called the “dead Rabbit party.” His reconstruction suggests that political or street adversaries may have applied the phrase to their opponents, after which newspapers circulated it. The same research records emphatic repudiations of the label by local people. This possibility explains how a name could become famous without being used as an organization’s own formal title. [1]

Another traditional explanation involves a dead rabbit tossed into a club meeting after a dispute, followed by a splinter group adopting the name. That colorful tale occurs in later historical retellings and is discussed by Anbinder as a story circulated in the nineteenth-century reporting. It should be described as an origin legend, not as verified foundational history. No authenticated surviving charter or membership ledger establishes such a founding ceremony. [1][10]

Nor does the name automatically indicate a literal rabbit carried in every conflict as a flag emblem. Illustrated depictions, competing meanings of slang, and stories of animal standards must be individually dated and attributed before they can be offered as evidence of a consistent gang insignia. [1][9]

The Roche / Roach Guard

The Roche Guard, often rendered Roach Guard in contemporary reporting, has a more identifiable place in the neighborhood record than a purported self-named Dead Rabbits club. Anbinder describes it as a political and social association formed in the early 1850s to honor or support saloonkeeper Walter Roche, an Irish immigrant from County Carlow, who operated a saloon at 19 Mulberry Street. This was a network shaped by the associational and electoral life of the Sixth Ward, not a proven syndicate with a nationwide criminal franchise. [1]

Such clubs could gather young working men through taverns, target companies, ward politics and relationships with volunteer fire companies. They could mobilize for parades and elections, and their members or supporters could also become involved in violent street confrontations. The existence of a social or political club does not establish that every member committed theft, nor does participation in a riot by some associates prove a uniform institutional criminal purpose. [1][7]

Roche’s contacts overlapped with those of Matthew Brennan, a major Sixth Ward political figure. Anbinder records that after Brennan became a police justice in 1854, Roche was put in charge of Brennan’s Monroe Hall saloon. Their association helps explain the political alignment of the Mulberry Street faction in 1857; it should not be converted into a claim that Brennan was the “boss” of a formal Dead Rabbits crime family. [1]

Contemporaneous disagreement even affected memorial badges. According to Anbinder, a newspaper first reported badges of a “Dead Rabbit Club” at a funeral, then corrected the wording the following day to identify the Roach Guard. The correction is a revealing example of how a press label could overrun the precise self-description of a real association. [1]

Mulberry Boys, Roche Guard, and Alleged Secession

The designation Mulberry Boys described men associated with the lower Mulberry Street area, the neighborhood base of one side of the 1857 conflict. Police and reporters used different names for overlapping crowds. Anbinder’s account treats the Mulberry Boys and Roche Guard as key actors; it does not demonstrate that every individual labeled a Dead Rabbit belonged to a separate third organization. [1][4]

Some newspaper writers proposed that the Dead Rabbits were an offshoot of the Roche Guard and that a prior internal quarrel explained the change of name. Others wrote as if Dead Rabbits, Roach Guards and Five Pointers were interchangeable. These claims are evidence about how contemporaries described the street fighters; without corroborating club records, they are not a reliable membership genealogy. [1][3]

Accordingly, this entry uses “Roche Guard” for the attested association when the evidence warrants it, “Mulberry Boys” for neighborhood-based participants, and “Dead Rabbits” when discussing the name employed in newspaper accounts or later history. The three expressions are preserved rather than merged into a false organizational chart. [1][4]

Sixth Ward Factions and Ward Politics

Competition among Democratic factions in the mid-1850s provides an indispensable explanation for the violence. Mayor Fernando Wood appealed to many immigrant voters and battled rivals within his own party. Local leaders used ward patronage, salaried positions, election inspectors, policemen and the influence of saloons and clubs to maintain support. The result was not a simple division of Irish immigrants on one side and native-born Americans on the other. [1][6]

Anbinder identifies the July 1857 conflict primarily with supporters of Matthew Brennan on one side and those of Patrick Mathews and James Kerrigan on the other. Political loyalties and territory could pull groups into temporary alliances even when their positions on a citywide controversy did not perfectly match. Men sometimes labeled Bowery Boys were also part of the Irish Catholic political world; the popular portrayal of universally nativist Bowery Boys against uniformly Irish Dead Rabbits is misleading for this particular conflict. [1][4]

The power of neighborhood leaders was reinforced by practical institutions. A ward-aligned fire company, tavern or target-shooting club could assemble men quickly and establish a visible street presence. Clashes at such boundaries were therefore contests about which leaders could mobilize supporters, defend patrons and influence public offices as much as conflicts between criminal business partners. [1][7]

Local politics does not excuse attacks, gunfire or injuries. It explains why the fighting escalated and why a narrowly criminal-gang account omits actors who mattered to the episode, including elected officials, firefighters, policemen, bystanders and residents. [1][2]

The Municipal–Metropolitan Police Crisis

The New York State legislature enacted the Metropolitan Police Act of 1857, transferring control of policing from city authorities to a state-appointed board. Wood resisted dissolving the older Municipal Police force. The competition between the two departments produced confusion over arrests, patrol authority and the legitimacy of the new officers. Their confrontation on the steps of City Hall in June 1857 has become known as the Police Riot. [1][6]

In Five Points, the replacement force was widely suspected of political and anti-immigrant prejudice. The new arrangements threatened the employment and influence of locally connected policemen. A separate liquor licensing law added pressure on tavern keepers and their social networks, while talk of attacks on Catholic institutions amplified anxiety. These fears do not establish that every rumor was true; they help reconstruct the perceptions driving local conduct. [1]

A court ruling in early July compelled the dissolution of the Municipal force. With the new system about to exercise authority in the Sixth Ward, confrontations intensified. The July street battles occurred at a moment when institutional legitimacy, neighborhood employment and political patronage were all being contested. [1][6]

The police conflict is a causal context, not proof that the Dead Rabbits were simply an auxiliary police resistance movement. Different participants acted from differing motives: political protest, fear, retaliation, street rivalry, protection of local allies or the momentum of crowd violence. [1][4]

July 4, 1857: Morning Confrontations

The Fourth of July disturbances began with assaults on the new police and confrontations near the Bowery. Anbinder reconstructs an early attack on Metropolitan officer Abraham Florentine Jr., who sought shelter at 40 Bowery, a saloon associated with Patrick Mathews and his supporters. Another officer took refuge at a nearby refreshment establishment at 36 Bowery. Crowds attacked windows and occupants before Bowery-aligned men intervened. These episodes demonstrate the overlap between anti-police feeling and a clash along a neighborhood boundary. [1][2]

Accounts place fighting and intimidation in streets including Chatham, Mulberry, Bayard and the Bowery. The rapid sequence and conflicting affiliations make it inappropriate to narrate every disturbance as an order issued by a single “Dead Rabbits” commander. The same individuals could be identified by a street, a club, a political allegiance or a hostile journalist’s chosen collective name. [1][4]

Contemporary reporting often characterized the events as a fight between Dead Rabbits and Bowery Boys, a shorthand that readers recognized. A more precise reconstruction identifies attacks on police, the saloons where officers took refuge, and a succession of neighborhood responses. It was a developing urban riot before it became a familiar gang-war legend. [1][2][3]

Bayard Street Barricades and Street Fighting

By late afternoon on July 4, fighting escalated on Bayard Street. Anbinder describes Metropolitan officers entering from the White Street station and being driven back by stones and other objects dropped from roofs and windows. Bowery-aligned men then came westward in support of the patrolmen. The police attempted to withdraw while opposing groups advanced and retreated across the adjoining blocks. [1]

Combatants improvised weapons from construction debris and household materials. Bricks from a work site became especially important; carts and wagons were assembled into makeshift barricades. Later in the day firearms added to the danger. The scene was not confined to two rows of neatly distinguished “gang members”: residents supplied missiles, spectators gathered and some individuals joined the action to defend their houses or friends. [1][2]

Anbinder identifies the competing camps as followers of Brennan on one side and those of Mathews and Kerrigan on the other. The tactical geography explains the street movements: the Bowery side attempted to hold ground, while the lower Mulberry faction fought to protect or regain nearby streets. Popular illustrations and later accounts made this confrontation emblematic of Five Points history, but they cannot substitute for an actual chain of command. [1][9]

The events of the weekend were reported in multiple wards; the Bayard Street fighting is the most closely associated with the “Dead Rabbits” label, not necessarily the entire geographic extent of public disorder recorded on July 4–5. [1][2]

Women, Children, Spectators, and Civilians

Women and children appear in contemporary accounts and in illustrated representations of the fighting. Anbinder recounts residents collecting stones and passing them to people on rooftops or at windows. These details show that a neighborhood disturbance did not remain a matter of two male fraternities. They do not establish that the women depicted belonged to the Roche Guard or a formally enrolled Dead Rabbits gang. [1][9]

Crowds gathered to see the conflict, often putting bystanders at risk. Richard Henry Dana Jr.’s diary, as discussed by Anbinder, describes the confusion and injuries visible to an observer. A boy watching from a window was struck by a bullet. The presence of spectators is a reminder that an estimated crowd size cannot be translated into an organization’s membership count. [1]

Families searched for the injured after the fighting; pharmacy and hospital accounts reveal anxiety extending well beyond the combatants. The costs of riot violence included deaths, wounds, bereavement, damaged property, lost wages and the consequences of arrests. An institutional history must document civilian experience rather than treat the event simply as exciting folklore. [1][2]

Deaths, Injuries, and Disputed Totals

The casualties were severe, but published totals differ. A widely circulated contemporary New York Times headline identified six dead and more than one hundred wounded, while other modern summaries give eight deaths. Anbinder’s reconstruction concludes that twelve New Yorkers died, with thirty-seven wounded admitted to New York Hospital and additional people likely treated elsewhere. These are not interchangeable figures drawn from an agreed final casualty register. [1][2][3][11]

Differences may reflect evolving reports, the precise duration of violence counted, injuries followed by later deaths, and the scope of surrounding ward disturbances. This article does not choose one number as an uncontested final toll. For a rigorous reconstruction, each named fatality would require reconciliation with death certificates, inquest testimony, hospital returns and dated press corrections. [1][2]

Several accounts indicate that the Mulberry Street side bore an especially heavy burden of fatal injuries. The uneven losses have implications for interpreting prosecution and later descriptions of who prevailed in the fighting. Combatant fatalities must not be conflated with a claim about the size or effectiveness of a single enduring gang. [1]

How the Fighting Ended

At the Bayard Street front, fighting subsided after intervention and persuasion efforts by local figures. Anbinder recounts a reported intervention by a former policeman named Shangles, who convinced participants that military forces were approaching. Other claimants credited Sixth Ward political and police intermediaries with obtaining peace. These competing descriptions are part of the contemporary record, not grounds for identifying one sole decisive peacemaker. [1][9]

Troops were mobilized to restore public order during the wider holiday disturbances, and subsequent accounts often present militia intervention as the reason the riots ended. The record is more complex: some major fighting stopped following local negotiations, while unrest and the broader mobilization extended beyond a single Bayard Street confrontation. The start and end of the entire citywide disturbance should not be reduced to one cinematic instant. [1][2][11]

The immediate aftermath involved the removal of injured people, criminal proceedings and public debate about the city’s capacity to police itself. Within weeks the label “Dead Rabbits” had become a durable public explanation of the incident, even as the people allegedly assigned that title objected to it. [1]

Arrests, Trials, and Selective Prosecution

Anbinder’s study traces prosecutions of men arrested during the confrontation with Metropolitan officers. Several defendants said they had been passersby or observers rather than attackers. According to the reconstruction, six men were convicted; a sixteen-year-old received a suspended sentence, while the other five received six-month terms. These results should be read as findings in particular riot cases, not judicial proof that a criminal syndicate called the Dead Rabbits existed. [1]

The defendants lacked substantial legal resources. Their proceedings became an argument about unequal access to justice and the possible political alignment of police and prosecutors. Anbinder notes that Bowery-aligned participants who inflicted serious injuries were not prosecuted in the same way, contributing to Irish-American complaints about selective enforcement. [1]

No comprehensive racketeering indictment, nineteenth-century corporation register or formal criminal-organization judgment established a modern-style Dead Rabbits enterprise. The record is composed of individual episodes, eyewitness statements, local club references and later interpretations. Assigning a uniform institutional legal status to everyone on one side of the riot would overstate the evidence. [1][4]

Membership, Leadership, and Organizational Structure

Accounts that describe a president, secretary and other officers for the Dead Rabbits often conflate nineteenth-century political-club practice with the existence of a separate gang. A club might have formal offices and meeting rituals, while the crowds that fought under a hostile label could be temporary alliances. The first does not prove that the second had a standing, centralized command structure. [1][7]

Later summaries sometimes assign numerical memberships in the low hundreds or identify a narrow age range. Those claims should be handled as published estimates rather than verified enrollment figures. Numbers quoted for combatants, political demonstrators, athletic-club members, fire companies and neighborhood onlookers describe different populations. Neither “Dead Rabbits” nor “Roach Guard” can be assigned a full modern personnel roster on available evidence. [1][7]

There is no sufficiently grounded list of captains, treasurers, lieutenants or territorial divisions to produce a mafia-style hierarchy chart. For this entry the preferred explanatory model is a ward-centered social and political network capable of street mobilization, with an important question remaining whether “Dead Rabbits” was a separate institutional name at all. [1][4]

Documented Violence and Criminal-Activity Claims

The July 1857 records support reporting of street assaults, riots, property damage, armed confrontations and obstruction or attacks on police officers. Participants hurled bricks and objects from buildings, broke windows, erected barricades and used guns. Individual culpability depends on what witnesses and judicial records established; violence by a crowd should not be assigned to every named or alleged club member. [1][2]

Broader popular histories attribute theft, pickpocketing, burglary and intimidation to the Dead Rabbits. These offenses certainly existed in the Five Points environment, but the attribution is especially contested because the name itself was disputed. The Horbelt protest specifically objected to portraying Roche Guard members as thieves and pickpockets. An encyclopedia should distinguish verified neighborhood crime from verified organizational crime. [1][7]

Nothing in the reviewed sources establishes that the Dead Rabbits operated a stable large-scale revenue system comparable to later Prohibition syndicates, a modern protection racket or the twentieth-century Irish-American Mafia. Violence related to patronage or territorial influence is analytically important, but is not automatically a commercial racketeering enterprise. [1][4]

Volunteer Fire Companies, Target Clubs, and Saloons

Before New York developed a fully professional fire service, volunteer companies were important civic institutions and neighborhood centers of loyalty. Their members could take pride in a company’s record, stage parades and build political ties. Rival companies sometimes fought, making the boundary between civic association and street faction difficult to reconstruct without details about a particular incident. [1][7]

Anbinder links political figures in the riot to fire company networks. Kerrigan was associated with Hose Company No. 14, while Walter Roche and Marcus Horbelt had connections with Brennan Hose Company No. 60. These connections illuminate the social infrastructure of riot mobilization; they are not evidence that each firefighter was a professional criminal or member of a “Dead Rabbits” crime family. [1]

Saloons likewise served several roles: commercial businesses, meeting places, information exchanges and ward political headquarters. The recurring mention of 19 Mulberry Street for Roche and 40 Bowery for the Mathews faction is historically useful. Each venue should be described in relation to the people documented there, not as proof of a citywide crime empire. [1][2]

Irish Immigration, Nativism, and Intraethnic Conflict

Irish immigration and anti-Irish sentiment formed an important backdrop to the 1850s. Religious prejudice, the Know Nothing movement and arguments over liquor licensing made immigrant voters suspicious of state-driven changes in municipal authority. Those pressures mattered, especially when Metropolitan officers began to replace local police. [1][6]

Yet the direct alignment in the 1857 riot should not be flattened into a straightforward ethnic war. Anbinder points out that Mathews was born in Ireland and Kerrigan later supported Irish nationalist campaigns. The immediate rivalry was heavily shaped by intra-ward political struggles involving men of overlapping ethnic or religious backgrounds. Ethnic prejudice remained present in the environment, but the factions cannot be reliably classified as exclusively “Irish” versus exclusively “native.” [1][4]

Some modern presentations make this riot a simple conflict between Catholic Irish laborers and hostile, native-born Bowery Boys. That interpretation captures part of the broader climate but does not account adequately for the identities and allegiances of principal 1857 leaders. This entry prefers a layered explanation: anti-immigrant fears, local patronage, disputed police authority and neighborhood territory all interacted. [1][2][11]

Equally, the activities of a few combative clubs cannot be generalized to Irish Americans as a whole. Most residents of Five Points were working, raising families and seeking economic opportunities, even when surrounded by strained housing and antagonistic political institutions. [1][5]

Relations with the Bowery Boys and Other Five Points Groups

“Bowery Boys” was not a timeless organization with one fixed command and membership list. It originally named a recognizable urban social style and later appeared in reporting on different political or street-fighting factions. For the specific 1857 conflict, Anbinder connects the label to the Mathews and Kerrigan camps and men living along Elizabeth Street and the Bowery. [1]

Their quarrel with Mulberry Street supporters involved disputed ground and influence within the Sixth Ward. These men lived close enough to cross one another’s paths frequently; the contested boundary mattered because it connected political representation, saloon networks and street prestige. In some phases of the fighting the Bowery men helped police; in others, their primary concern was defending their side of the district. [1][4]

Later lists of nineteenth-century gangs sometimes connect the Dead Rabbits to the Forty Thieves, Kerryonians, Plug Uglies or other named groups as allies, rivals or descendants. Broad coexistence in the urban gang landscape is established, but specific alliances and chains of succession require dated evidence. No command relationship with the older Forty Thieves is demonstrated merely because both appear in histories of Five Points. [1][10]

The Dead Rabbits are therefore most securely compared with the Roche Guard, Mulberry Boys, Atlantic Guards and Bowery-linked factions active in the same recorded episode. Unverified genealogies are excluded. [1][2]

Contemporary Newspapers and Illustrated Representations

The press played two roles: it recorded incidents and helped construct the identity under which those incidents would be remembered. Newspapers including the New York Times, Tribune, Herald and Morning Express described violent scenes, participants and competing versions of events. Their accounts preserve valuable details, but the terms chosen by reporters often reflected the claims of one side or the conventions of sensational crime reporting. [1][2][3]

The Library of Congress holds a wood engraving published in Frank Leslie’s Illustrated Newspaper in July 1857 portraying several scenes from the Sixth Ward riot, including the contrasting types “Dead Rabbit” and “Bowery Boy.” It is excellent evidence of the imagery sold to readers in 1857. It is not a photographic identification of a verified gang member or a membership document. [9]

Newspaper stories also supplied the enduring episode of a dead rabbit thrown into a political meeting, then repeated it until it appeared to be institutional history. Anbinder’s examination of correction notices and participant letters illustrates how important it is to read competing reports rather than lift a single dramatic description from the press. [1]

In this encyclopedia, period illustrations are treated as contemporary representations; modern reconstructions as secondary interpretation; and primary statements of witnesses as situated testimony, potentially shaped by interests or fear of prosecution. [1][9]

The Horbelt and Molony Denials

One of the strongest reasons for caution is the response from people who were publicly associated with the supposedly notorious group. Marcus Horbelt, a young shoemaker from Mulberry Street, protested the press accounts of the Roche Guard. He denied the existence of a “Dead Rabbits” club and rejected the accusation that the association’s members were thieves. Harry Molony also denied that such a club belonged to the Sixth Ward. [1]

Their statements are not conclusive proof that no member of the fighting coalition ever used the expression in any setting. They are, however, direct evidence against accepting newspaper terminology as a clear self-designation. A careful reference article must preserve disagreement within the record rather than resolve it by repeating a name that readers already know. [1]

These letters also reflect a serious contemporary reputational concern. To call a workingman a member of a gang of thieves could damage his prospects and associate a legitimate social club with criminality. The question was not merely semantic; it had consequences for men attempting to distinguish political participation from actual offenses. [1]

From Riot Nickname to Urban Legend

Once “Dead Rabbits” entered headlines, it proved a memorable label. Anbinder notes that newspapers and city residents continued using it long after the 1857 confrontation for unruly people or disturbances. The term could operate as a generic insult or a literary symbol detached from any original organization. [1]

Herbert Asbury’s The Gangs of New York (1928) and subsequent popular versions helped transform fragmentary neighborhood histories into stories about tightly defined criminal tribes, named leaders and recurring wars. Such accounts have enormous cultural influence, but their highly polished organization charts and symbols should not be imported uncritically into an academic reference page. [10]

Modern public history, film and tourism have further cemented the romantic conflict between “Dead Rabbits” and “Bowery Boys.” These works are legitimate subjects in the history of memory, but fictional characters and dramatized events cannot establish an actual nineteenth-century founding date, territory, secret ritual or succession structure. [1][4][10]

The most defensible legacy is thus twofold: a real and deadly riot in a divided city, and a powerful name whose actual organizational referent was disputed by some of the people contemporaries claimed it described. [1][4]

Terminology and Historical Distinctions

Dead Rabbits
A name used in 1857 press and later histories for one side of the Five Points violence; its status as an actual separate club is disputed.
Roche (Roach) Guard
Named early-1850s Sixth Ward political/social association associated with Walter Roche.
Mulberry Boys
A neighborhood-based designation for men from or associated with Mulberry Street; not necessarily a separate corporation.
Bowery Boys
Period term with changing social and political meanings; in the 1857 riot often tied to the Mathews–Kerrigan side.
Atlantic Guard / Atlantic Guards
A Bowery-aligned political or target-company identity appearing in accounts of the 1857 events.
Target company
Organization associated with military-style drilling, target practice, outings and local social/political life.
Volunteer fire company
Neighborhood fire-fighting institution that could also provide powerful associations and mobilizing networks.
Municipal Police
Older city-controlled New York police organization dissolved in 1857.
Metropolitan Police
State-controlled replacement police system at the center of the 1857 authority crisis.
Nativism
Political hostility to immigrants or perceived outsiders; important background, but not an exclusive or complete account of the 1857 fighting.

Research Method and Remaining Gaps

Contemporaneous versus retrospective evidence. The primary anchors are 1857 newspapers and engravings, political and legal descriptions from the period, and firsthand statements as analyzed by Tyler Anbinder. The image in the Library of Congress is a contemporaneous publication artifact, not a photograph. The full original newspaper page set and every criminal court docket have not been independently facsimile-collated for this edition. [1][2][9]

Contradictory casualty figures. The literature uses six, eight and twelve deaths depending on source and scope. A definitive register would require named decedents, inquests, date-of-death checks and differentiation between the Fourth of July Bayard Street fighting and other incidents. [1][2][11]

Membership and guilt. The reference does not infer criminal involvement from Irish birth, residence in Five Points, club association, the use of a nickname or appearance in a popular illustration. It does not assign those charged in particular riot cases to an unproven durable organization absent specific membership evidence. [1][5]

Historiographical dispute. Anbinder’s source-critical interpretation is given substantial weight, but the entry also explains the broader “Dead Rabbits versus Bowery Boys” narrative still used by museums and general histories. Where historians differ, the text states the difference rather than reporting a settled scholarly consensus that does not exist. [1][2][4][11]

Editorial cutoff. This article reflects materials reviewed through November 13, 2026. Future improvements would include page-specific collation of the New York Times, Tribune and Herald on July 4–10, 1857; full transcription of Horbelt’s letter; and a person-by-person reconciliation of casualties and prosecutions. Selected References and Source Register

References are organized for source transparency. Some documents reproduce or quote newspapers that have not been independently collated at original-page level. Statements about sources are limited to what was reviewed; conflicts between retrospective accounts and period testimony remain visible.

  1. Tyler Anbinder — Five Points: The Nineteenth-Century New York City Neighborhood (Free Press, 2001), “The Old Sixth Reminded One of a House in Mourning,” pp. 277–292, hosted excerpt. Principal archival and interpretive basis for the political factions, 1857 chronology, direct denials of the “Dead Rabbits” name, casualties, arrests and contested popular narrative.
  2. United States Department of the Interior / National Register documentation — The Bowery Historic District, Section 8 (historical statement). Historic-preservation study reproducing period newspaper material on the 1857 disturbances and identifying Bowery locations. Its simplified opposing-gang characterization is assessed alongside Anbinder.
  3. Alexandra Gomer — “Bowery Boys and Dead Rabbits,” Seton Hall University, History of New York City project (December 13, 2016). Student digital-history essay summarizing July 1857 press coverage and linking an 1857 New York Times account; useful for locating quotations, not independent primary archival verification.
  4. Lehigh University — Reel American History, “1857: The Bowery Boys Riot.” Educational historical interpretation highlighting uncertainty about a separate Dead Rabbits gang and the utility of “Mulberry Boys” as a more specific term.
  5. Rebecca Yamin — “New York’s Mythic Slum,” Archaeology 50, no. 2 (March/April 1997). Archaeological rebuttal to the image of Five Points as wholly criminal; evidence of varied immigrant households and everyday labor.
  6. Joseph A. Williams — “In 1857, NYC Police Didn’t Keep the Peace—They Caused a Riot,” HISTORY (2018; updated 2025). Background account of the June 1857 Municipal–Metropolitan Police confrontation and its political context; distinct from the July riots.
  7. Hugh O’Rourke — “Irish Collective Violence and the New York Catholic Church,” New York Irish History 22 (2008). Secondary study of nineteenth-century Irish political and street-fighting institutions. Its estimates of Dead Rabbits membership and structure are not treated as a verified roster.
  8. Tyler Anbinder — Five Points, publisher edition and bibliographic record. Publisher record for the larger research monograph; the hosted chapter excerpt above is the source used for page-specific interpretation.
  9. Library of Congress, Prints and Photographs Division — “Four scenes from the riot in the Sixth Ward …,” Frank Leslie’s Illustrated Newspaper (July 1857), catalog no. 98510936. Contemporaneous wood engraving and editorial labels. Demonstrates 1857 visual framing, not positive identification of alleged members.
  10. Herbert Asbury — The Gangs of New York (1928), edition listing. Influential retrospective gang history; invoked as a primary artifact of later popular mythmaking, not a substitute for 1857 evidence.
  11. Ancestry — “Dead Rabbits Riot,” historical event overview. Accessible general retelling that reports eight deaths and foregrounds the two-gang narrative; contrasts with Anbinder’s source-critical reconstruction.
  12. New York Times — 1857 Fourth of July riot coverage, archive URL preserved in the Seton Hall project. Contemporary source as cited and excerpted by later works; archive scan could not be retrieved directly during this preparation, so its text is attributed through Anbinder and secondary archival citations.
  13. New York Tribune, July 10, 1857 — “Dead Rabbit Demonstration” issue catalog entry. Period newspaper item catalog record demonstrating use of the Dead Rabbit label; listing does not reproduce the full article text and is not used to infer its detailed contents.
