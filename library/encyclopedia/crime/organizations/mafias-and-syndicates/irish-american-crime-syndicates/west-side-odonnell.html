Overview

The West Side O’Donnell Gang was a Chicago-area Prohibition criminal network linked principally to William “Klondike” O’Donnell, Myles (also spelled Miles) O’Donnell, and Bernard O’Donnell. It operated in the West Side and adjacent Cicero beer markets during the 1920s and remained a recognizable name in police and press accounts into the early 1930s. The organization participated in the illicit manufacture, transportation, distribution and contest for customers of alcoholic beverages; it was one of several independent or loosely aligned groups competing and cooperating around the larger Torrio–Capone syndicate. The term gang is historically attested, but it does not establish a permanent formal hierarchy. [1] [2] [3]

Three documentary anchors are particularly useful. First, sociologist John Landesco identified the “Klondike O’Donnell gang” as a distinct criminal grouping in the Illinois Crime Survey of 1929, while stressing the weakness of criminal-record systems. Second, contemporary newspapers placed O’Donnell associates James J. Doherty and Thomas “Red” Duffy among those killed with prosecutor William H. McSwiggin on April 27, 1926. Third, reporting in March 1927 tied Klondike to an attempted removal of bonded whiskey from a government-supervised warehouse, and February 1930 reporting still described O’Donnell associates as an active police target. [1] [4] [5] [6] [7]

The West Side group should not be confused with the South Side O’Donnells led by Edward “Spike” O’Donnell. Histories of the Chicago Beer Wars sometimes place both families into the same narrative, creating mistaken brother lists, murders and alliances. The West Side network also did not become the Chicago Outfit by direct organizational succession. This article separates documented events, reported police assessments, retrospective interpretations, and unresolved criminal allegations. Editorial cutoff: November 13, 2026. [2] [3] [8] [12]

“West Side” distinguishes a sphere of activity, not a legal jurisdiction or a fixed polygon of controlled streets. “Irish-American” is used as an editorial historical category and should never be read as assigning criminality to the broader Irish population of Chicago. [1] [2]

The Two O’Donnell Organizations

The name O’Donnell occurs in accounts of two different Chicago beer-running gangs. The West Side enterprise is identified with Klondike, Myles and Bernard; the separate South Side organization is generally associated with Edward “Spike” O’Donnell and his brothers. Crime historian Allan May’s account of the South Side Beer Wars explicitly warns that the families were not related. The distinction is essential to any correct chronology of Chicago Prohibition violence. [8] [9]

South Side accounts of Spike’s struggle with Joe Saltis and Frank McErlane cannot be transferred to Klondike as if he were their chief. Conversely, the McSwiggin case, the Morand warehouse incident, and the February 1930 West Side roundup relate to Klondike’s circle, not to an imaginary joint O’Donnell family syndicate. The two names may appear side-by-side in a survey of Chicago gangs precisely because they were separate groupings operating at the same time. [1] [5] [8]

A 1934 retrospective newspaper list further complicates family details by identifying Klondike as a brother of Myles and “Steve,” whereas other sources emphasize Bernard. Since newspaper names can be abbreviated or confused, this encyclopedia lists only relationships directly supported by the individual cited items and does not invent an exhaustive family tree. “Same surname” is not proof of common ancestry, criminal alliance, or shared territory. [11] [9]

Chicago and Cicero During Prohibition

National Prohibition sharply altered the economics of alcohol in American cities. Commercial breweries, wholesalers, transportation contractors, saloons, and private drinking establishments could continue to operate legally only within a restrictive regulatory environment. Demand for unlawful beer and liquor nevertheless persisted, giving suppliers a powerful incentive to preserve delivery routes and customer lists. Competition over those routes generated new opportunities for extortion, intimidation, bribery and retaliatory violence. These wider conditions provide context for the West Side O’Donnells without demonstrating that they controlled every speakeasy in an entire district. [1] [13]

Chicago’s West Side and the municipality of Cicero were connected by streets, commerce and political interests, yet had different police and governing institutions. A bootlegger’s influence could cross city borders even when police recordkeeping did not. Landesco emphasized that suburban arrests were not systematically captured in Chicago’s central identification files. The operational geography of the O’Donnell group therefore should be reconstructed using individual saloon addresses, transportation routes and case records, not only a generalized label on a citywide map. [1] [2]

The West Side liquor trade also overlapped other criminal businesses: gambling, political patronage, protection payments and transport contracting. Overlap is not identity. Documentary evidence tying a particular O’Donnell associate to a liquor market is stronger than a broad assertion that the gang ran all Chicago West Side rackets or belonged under a single citywide chain of command. [1] [12]

Origins: From Informal Association to Beer Enterprise

Popular histories portray a group of brothers developing local influence as drinking establishments sought steady supplies of illegal beer. What can be established more securely is that the O’Donnell names were prominent in Chicago police, press and sociological accounts by the mid-1920s. No authenticated organizational charter, founding meeting, fixed recruitment roll or date of first beer delivery has been located for this MGU entry. [1] [2] [12]

It is plausible that relationships formed through neighborhood acquaintances, transport work and saloon clientele. Yet one cannot infer a precise youth-gang origin or a linear progression from petty theft to bootlegging merely because other Chicago gangs followed that pattern. The evidence used here is strongest for a mature Prohibition enterprise, not for an invented childhood biography of its leaders. [1] [14]

The gang’s public name itself emerged through labels applied by police, newspapers and later researchers. Such an organization could be recognizable and commercially coordinated without the formal ranks associated with the later Mafia. The appropriate reconstruction is a network of leading brothers, recurring associates, supply relationships and sometimes shifting partners, whose exact membership and decision rules remain incompletely documented. [1] [2]

Myles “Miles” O’Donnell

Myles O’Donnell, whose name is also printed Miles in some period documents, was a leading West Side figure. The April 1926 news coverage placed him near the network surrounding Doherty and Duffy; police sought him and other O’Donnells for questioning after the McSwiggin shooting. One early dispatch speculated that he might also have been injured or killed, an inference shown to be premature by his later appearance in public reports. [4] [5] [7]

Landesco’s 1929 review found little in the Chicago Identification Bureau for Miles beyond a 1926 fine for carrying a gun. This is a statement about the contents of one recordkeeping system, not an acquittal of every rumor, proof of innocence, or evidence that his group was trivial. Landesco’s central analytical point was that influential bootleggers often did not accumulate complete records in the bureau. [1]

The February 13, 1930 press account identified Myles and Bernard among men detained in a West Side roundup. Later popular biographies give Myles a March 1933 death date and circumstances involving a shooting and pneumonia, but a contemporary death record was not independently collated for this edition. That sequence should remain attributed, not treated as a verified forensic finding. [7] [15]

William “Klondike” O’Donnell

William “Klondike” O’Donnell was treated by Landesco as the chief of the West Side O’Donnell group, an unusually strong period source for a leadership designation. The nickname appears with variant spellings, including Klondyke. Neither the nickname’s origin nor a complete life chronology is firmly established by the materials inspected, so the article avoids speculative stories that he necessarily earned it in Alaska or was identical with another historical figure using the surname. [1] [10]

The principal documented criminal episode connected with Klondike is the Morand Brothers bonded-whiskey warehouse case of 1927. Landesco reported a federal warehouse-burglary charge in the police identification record and specifically warned that the file did not fully show whether prison time had been served. A later 1934 retrospective claimed Leavenworth imprisonment, but this edition does not substitute the later summary for the complete federal docket or sentence record. [1] [6] [11]

Klondike remained prominent in the press even after his group’s 1926 losses. The 1934 newspaper retrospective associated him with labor racketeering; the assertion is recorded as a published allegation. It does not establish a proven leadership succession after Myles, a verified union presidency, or participation in every later crime attributed to the Chicago West Side underworld. [11] [15]

Bernard O’Donnell and the Brother Question

Bernard O’Donnell appears in contemporary-era reporting as an O’Donnell brother associated with the West Side liquor faction. The April 1926 press named him among people investigators wished to locate after the McSwiggin triple killing. Landesco separately recorded that Bernard was indicted in relation to the Tancl killing and lacked an Identification Bureau entry. An indictment is not a conviction, and the cited survey does not by itself resolve Bernard’s outcome in that case. [1] [5]

The 1930 police roundup account again names Bernard and Myles, which helps demonstrate that the O’Donnell label was still in active law-enforcement circulation years after the 1926 gun attack. But being detained for questioning during a mass roundup does not establish membership in a permanent leadership committee, guilt of the offenses under investigation, or that every other person in the room answered to Bernard. [7]

Later summaries sometimes add additional brothers or describe Bernard as a brewery owner. Those details may warrant future archival work, but the encyclopedia does not publish an exact ownership share, corporate entity name, or dates of control without corroborating business or court records. The distinctions matter because surname-based family reconstruction has been a recurrent source of error in these two O’Donnell gang histories. [1] [8] [11]

Leadership, Associates and Organizational Structure

The organization was recognizable enough for Landesco to include it in comparative analytical charts. That is evidence of a real contemporaneously identified group, but his survey also complained that police and court files were incomplete and that association charts frequently relied on newspaper and detective reports. Accordingly, the existence of a Klondike O’Donnell gang does not justify claiming a verified boss–underboss–captain hierarchy, prescribed membership oath, fixed initiation protocol or exact roster. [1]

At a practical level, the working network appears to have joined leadership, saloon customers, drivers, gunmen and personal associates whose responsibilities could overlap. Doherty and Duffy are among the best documented named associates because they were identified by their contemporaries and later accounts in connection with the 1926 shooting. The 1930 roundup linked the brothers to figures including “Three-Fingered Jack” White and George “Red” Barker. These reported associations require event-specific treatment; they do not prove that all men belonged to one stable subordinate chain. [3] [7]

The historical FBI file includes a list labeled “O’Donnell Gang (West Side),” naming Klondike, Miles, Bernard and other associates. It is useful for reconstructing how authorities compiled suspected relationships. It is not, by itself, a court-tested list of guilty persons or definitive proof that every name worked for the gang simultaneously. [10]

Territory: West Side, Cicero and Beyond

The group’s nickname associates it with the West Side of Chicago, yet the major 1926 killing took place in Cicero, and the associated saloon business crossed political boundaries. Contemporary sources place key activity around West Roosevelt Road, and later press accounts identify meeting spaces on West Madison Street. These are individual historical sites and routes, not proof of exclusive ownership of every intervening neighborhood. [4] [7] [14]

Landesco’s analysis concluded that members attributed to the O’Donnell group had residences recorded across the north, south and west sides, rather than being confined to a compact residential block. This distinguishes an adult beer enterprise from certain youth gangs in the same survey whose participants lived close together. A geographic footprint of markets and associates may be broader than the home addresses of any leaders. [1]

The article therefore uses West Side as an established operational and archival label. It does not publish an unsupported street-by-street turf map, claim uninterrupted authority from Chicago’s Loop to the western suburbs, or treat Cicero’s entire government as an O’Donnell-controlled institution. [1] [12]

Economic Model: Illicit Beer Distribution

Beer running during Prohibition required an interlocking sequence of supply, storage, movement and payment. A supplier had to secure beer or liquor, reach outlets willing to sell it, schedule transport while avoiding seizure, collect money, and manage unpaid debts or territorial conflicts. Illegal market power could be enforced through violence, but the process also relied on business relationships and mutual reliance among customers and intermediaries. [1] [13]

The O’Donnells’ documented saloon associations and their presence in a regional beer war place them within this industry. Yet the sources consulted do not yield a reliable profit-and-loss statement, list of breweries owned by the brothers, per-bar sales quota, or the number of trucks working for the group at a given date. Later narratives sometimes describe such gangs as millionaire enterprises, an estimate that requires account records rather than repetition. [1] [7]

The 1927 bonded-liquor episode further shows that whiskey, not only beer, could be a target of illicit acquisition. It would be incorrect to assume that every participant in that warehouse operation was also a permanent beer distributor or that an attempted theft was completed merely because police described a substantial quantity of bonded liquor on site. [6] [11]

Saloons, Customers and the Harry Madigan Connection

The saloon of Harry Madigan became a major fixed point in the written history of the West Side conflict because the April 1926 gun attack took place nearby. Contemporary reporting and Landesco connected Madigan’s establishment with competing supplies of illicit beer and political entanglements. The saloon’s location and clientele matter more for this history than dramatic claims that it served as a formal O’Donnell headquarters. [4] [1]

Landesco’s later summary stated that Madigan had recorded charges connected to election activity, some subsequently stricken from the record; it considered the possibility that his place was used for political meetings. The survey stopped short of proving that Madigan himself directed the triple killing or operated a particular gang hierarchy. Arrest descriptions, booking records and criminal convictions must be kept separate in any source register. [1]

Accounts vary in the exact street number given for the saloon vicinity. The encyclopedia treats West Roosevelt Road in Cicero as the meaningful historical location without pretending to have verified a surviving building parcel or precise modern map pin. The attack’s relation to beer-customer disputes is significant, but the identity of whoever ordered it remains unresolved in the surviving syntheses reviewed here. [4] [14]

Competition and Coercion in the Beer Market

The economic incentive behind much of the Chicago Beer Wars was the right to supply retail customers. A bar that changed suppliers might deprive a rival gang of recurring revenue. Threats, shootings and hijackings could therefore serve as intimidation intended to make competitor delivery routes dangerous. That explanation is a historically grounded model, not automatic evidence assigning every unsolved Chicago killing to a particular supplier. [1] [13]

The West Side O’Donnells are best understood as one participant in this contested market. The same decade saw activity by Torrio and Capone, the North Side group around O’Banion and successors, the Valley Gang, and independent South Side organizations. Alliances and practical arrangements evolved; they should not be described as permanent corporate subsidiaries unless contemporary evidence shows sustained command. [1] [8] [12]

Some later crime histories describe an early accommodation with Torrio–Capone and a subsequent dispute over business or loyalty. The 1926 reporting clearly depicts the West Side O’Donnells as a rival faction at that point, but this does not establish an exact switch date or that the whole gang formally enrolled in Moran’s organization. The article distinguishes economic cooperation from a formal merger. [5] [9] [12]

Relations With Johnny Torrio

Johnny Torrio’s consolidation of bootlegging markets is a major feature of Chicago’s organized-crime history. Period crime research and later histories describe agreements in which independent suppliers accepted territorial or business arrangements while retaining their own operations. The O’Donnell network appears in those accounts as one of several parties whose interests could overlap or collide with Torrio’s. [1] [9]

It is important not to treat a historical “agreement” as equivalent to a notarized national commission of crime chiefs. Prohibition-era suppliers could exchange beer, agree to restrict direct competition, or temporarily desist from attacks without producing a stable political constitution. A reputed Torrio–O’Donnell understanding therefore requires qualification about date, precise terms and which individual leaders consented. [1] [12]

Once the Cicero beer trade became a focus of the 1926 crisis, any earlier accommodation was no guarantee of safety. The implication is a changing market rather than a simple story in which one permanent alliance became another at an exact documented ceremony. [4] [5]

Rivalry With Capone-Associated Interests

The 1926 McSwiggin affair placed the West Side O’Donnells inside the public conflict between the independent beer runners and Al Capone’s Cicero-based interests. Newspaper accounts described police searching for both O’Donnell figures and Capone, while journalists advanced several competing theories about who intended to kill whom. These searches are evidence of investigative attention, not proof that Capone personally ordered the shooting. [4] [5]

Later crime histories often present the killing as retaliation by Capone gunmen after O’Donnell associates interfered with his beer business. The hypothesis is plausible in the context of the market dispute, yet Northwestern’s historical homicide account treats the crime as an important illustration of failed justice rather than a solved prosecution with an adjudicated perpetrator. The precise identity and order-giving responsibility must remain attributed rather than declared established. [2] [12] [14]

Relations also should not be read as uniformly hostile throughout the decade. Press accounts and retrospective histories describe earlier cooperation, changing rivals and practical coexistence. The group’s presence in an unstable distribution system is better documented than a permanent binary division between two single-minded “armies.” [1] [9]

Relations With the North Side Gang

The North Side Gang under Dean O’Banion and later Hymie Weiss, Vincent Drucci and George “Bugs” Moran was a separate, multiethnic Chicago organization. The West Side O’Donnells occupied a different sphere of activity even though both came into conflict with Capone-associated supply interests at different times. Later gangster summaries sometimes suggest the O’Donnells shifted toward the Moran camp, but a verified membership merger has not been demonstrated. [1] [9]

Shared hostility to a competitor can generate temporary alliances, exchanges of information, or nonaggression arrangements. It does not prove common leadership, identical finances or that the West Side members obeyed a North Side boss. Landesco cataloged the O’Donnell and O’Banion groups separately and analyzed their weak police records together for methodological reasons, not because they constituted a single organization. [1]

This distinction matters for the MGU directory: readers should be able to move between separate group profiles without the cross-reference implying organizational descent. A period comparison is historically appropriate; a fabricated hierarchy is not. [1] [2]

The Separate South Side O’Donnells

Edward “Spike” O’Donnell and the South Side group are not identified as the West Side leadership. Their principal Beer War disputes involved South Side suppliers and rivals such as Joe Saltis and Frank McErlane. The brothers associated with Spike are generally given as Steven, Walter and Thomas, while the West Side references name William “Klondike,” Myles and Bernard. [8] [9]

The two groups are often juxtaposed because the surname is memorable and because both were active in the same Chicago criminal economy. That coincidence can encourage errors: a murder of a South Side associate may be placed in the West Side gang’s chronology, or an assault involving a West Side companion may be attributed to Spike’s men. Each incident must be assigned only where case-specific evidence supports that attribution. [8] [1]

The source notes for this page retain separate identity even when broad works combine both groups in a chapter on Irish-American crime. The difference is one of family, geography, leadership and specific events—not simply alternative spellings of a single organization. [8] [12]

The Eddie Tancl Case and Its Evidentiary Limits

The Eddie Tancl killing is often inserted into stories of West Side gang escalation before McSwiggin’s death. Landesco’s 1929 survey gives a concrete, narrower fact: Bernard O’Donnell was indicted in relation to the Tancl killing, even though he had no Chicago Identification Bureau entry. That is meaningful evidence of the judicial system’s attention to a named West Side figure. [1]

The survey excerpt does not alone establish exactly who fired each fatal shot, the chronology of every death at the scene, the motive, or the disposition of every charge. Later narratives reconstruct a crowded saloon confrontation involving Myles and Doherty; such accounts should be checked against original inquest testimony and trial dockets before a public encyclopedia presents them as direct observations. [1] [15]

The appropriate historical classification is therefore documented indictment, unresolved result within the records reviewed. The case may have contributed to later feuding and police suspicion, but those causal links cannot be treated as established simply because a later author connected the incidents. [1] [12]

The Crisis Before April 27, 1926

By spring 1926, Chicago-area police and rival gang figures were already reacting to increasingly violent confrontations in the illicit beer economy. The O’Donnells’ influence in West Side and Cicero distribution was a matter of public comment; Capone’s interests had their own commercial and political concerns. Police attention to changing saloon suppliers forms the backdrop to the murders, but surviving news articles often turned conjectures about motives into dramatic certainty. [4] [5] [14]

William H. McSwiggin was a young Cook County assistant state’s attorney known for obtaining capital verdicts. His social relationship with men connected to the West Side liquor trade became a central public controversy after his death. Familiarity between a prosecutor and criminal suspects raised obvious questions, but it does not itself prove he accepted bribes or had a lawful investigatory purpose in visiting every speakeasy. [2] [14]

Accounts of the hours before the attack differ in small details. This article adopts the secure core—three identified victims killed in or around Cicero on April 27—and explicitly avoids supplying a fully scripted route or single definitive motive for the fatal automobile ambush. [4] [5]

April 27, 1926: The McSwiggin Triple Killing

On the evening of April 27, 1926, gunfire from an automobile struck a group near a Cicero saloon. Three men died: William H. McSwiggin, James J. Doherty, and Thomas “Red” Duffy. News reports describe the use of a rapid-firing weapon and a chaotic aftermath in which bodies were moved before being recovered. The killing was among the most notorious episodes of Chicago’s Prohibition Beer Wars. [4] [5] [14]

The victims had different institutional positions. McSwiggin was a prosecutor. Doherty and Duffy were tied by contemporary reporting to the O’Donnell liquor network; Duffy was also described in some accounts as a political worker or beer distributor. The men should not all be categorized as defendants or formal O’Donnell gang members merely because they died at the same scene. McSwiggin’s presence is a documented event and a historical problem, not proof of a conspiracy by the victim. [4] [14]

The episode generated intensive police searches, public outrage and multiple investigative theories. Its prominence in later histories sometimes hides the principal limitation: the record reviewed here does not establish a conviction identifying the gunmen and the person who ordered the killing. MGU therefore preserves the date, victims and investigation while qualifying the motive and responsibility. [2] [4] [12]

James Doherty, Thomas Duffy and the Prosecutor

James J. Doherty is consistently associated in contemporary coverage with West Side beer running and with O’Donnell acquaintances. A prosecutor who had formerly brought a criminal case against Doherty later appeared in his company on the night the two were killed. The relationship has attracted speculative commentary; the fact of their presence together should not be embellished into a proof of paid protection. [4] [14]

Thomas “Red” Duffy appears as the third victim. Contemporary descriptions vary between beer operator, barber and local political figure, reflecting the way lawful occupations and illicit networks could overlap in a district’s social world. There is no basis here to claim that Duffy held a permanent formal rank in the O’Donnell organization, even though his connection to the liquor trade is supported. [4] [5]

William H. McSwiggin, as a serving assistant state’s attorney, was a public official and a homicide victim, not a gang commander. His death resulted in unusually intense scrutiny of the prosecutor’s office, Chicago policing and Cicero’s affairs. The distinctions between these three men are essential to explaining the incident without turning everyone at the scene into a criminal syndicate member. [2] [14]

The McSwiggin Investigations and Grand Juries

The triple murder prompted police roundups, subpoenas and repeated grand-jury inquiries. Public authorities pursued questions about gang rivalry, the possible targeting of a prosecutor, local political protection and why law-enforcement officials had failed to stop gun violence. The extensive official attention is documented in Landesco’s chapter on the assassination and in Northwestern University’s historical homicide materials. [2] [4]

Contemporary newspapers reported that police wanted to locate O’Donnell brothers and Capone for questioning. These references should not be rewritten as murder convictions. Nor did the number of investigators or grand juries guarantee a clear outcome. A central lesson of the McSwiggin case is that a highly publicized murder may leave a thin judicial record of final responsibility. [2] [5]

The case also illustrates how publicity can distort retrospect. Early police theories are evidence of what officials suspected in April 1926; a later historical writer’s acceptance of one theory does not retroactively transform it into a jury verdict. The article maintains that evidentiary distinction throughout its chronology and person register. [2] [14]

The 1926 Federal Prohibition-Conspiracy Indictments

A federal grand jury in 1926 returned a large indictment involving Cicero officials, gangsters and saloon interests accused of conspiring to violate the Prohibition laws. Contemporary Associated Press reporting named Myles and William “Klondike” O’Donnell alongside Al Capone and figures within Cicero government. This was a liquor-conspiracy case, not a conviction for the McSwiggin murders. [3] [2]

The indictment’s breadth demonstrates that federal investigators considered criminal liquor distribution and official tolerance to be interdependent. It also cautions against interpreting proximity among defendants as a business merger. A federal indictment may name competing operators in one alleged pattern of illegality, even where those people were hostile to one another in the marketplace. [3]

The specific verdicts, pleas and sentencing dispositions of each named O’Donnell defendant were not independently reconstructed from the complete court docket for this page. For that reason, the encyclopedia reports the act of indictment and the broad alleged conspiracy, not guilt on every count or a presumed sentence. [3] [1]

March 1927: Morand Brothers Whiskey Warehouse

In March 1927, newspapers reported a dramatic attempted removal of bonded whiskey from the Morand Brothers warehouse in Chicago. Accounts described a long hose extending from government-controlled stocks toward another premises and a confrontation involving police or federal officers. Among those reported captured were William “Klondike” O’Donnell and James “Fur” Sammons, together with men named Barry and Davis in press reports. [6]

The case is especially useful because it concerns specific people, a specific kind of property and a dated enforcement action. It demonstrates that the economic opportunities facing bootleggers extended beyond local beer deliveries to high-value illicit diversion of stored liquor. Some accounts quoted striking estimates of whiskey value, but such figures are contemporary official or journalistic claims, not independently audited valuations of what the participants actually obtained. [6] [1]

Landesco’s 1929 study later referred to a federal charge against Klondike in relation to the Morand warehouse and noted gaps in the police bureau’s record of his incarceration. A 1934 retrospective said he had served time at Leavenworth. The encyclopedia separates arrest, charge and later prison assertion, leaving full sentencing details for future federal docket verification. [1] [11]

What Police Records Can — and Cannot — Prove

Landesco’s study is particularly valuable because it did not equate a short arrest sheet with a minor criminal career. He found that the Chicago Identification Bureau frequently lacked records of suburban arrests, federal proceedings, dropped cases, and people shielded by political influence. Even the most conspicuous Prohibition figures might have surprisingly little recorded against their names. [1]

For Myles, Landesco located a 1926 firearm fine; for Klondike, a federal Morand warehouse entry and an earlier larceny acquittal; and for Bernard, he discussed a Tancl indictment despite an empty identification entry. These are narrow statements about particular archival systems. They should not be used to infer that the accused were either guilty of every story circulating in the press or innocent of every uncharged act. [1]

The same survey explains its own method: researchers collected a large card file of names from newspaper reports, police bulletins, probation records and field interviews, then constructed association charts. That design makes it a major near-contemporary research work while still requiring readers to distinguish journalists’ attributions from case-disposition evidence. [1]

The 1929 Sociological Gang Assessment

The Illinois Crime Survey gives the O’Donnell gang a place among several important Chicago criminal networks, alongside the Capone, Valley, O’Banion, Saltis–McErlane and others. Landesco treated the Klondike O’Donnell gang as distinct enough to include in analytical comparisons of criminal records and addresses. This is stronger evidence of contemporary identity than an unreferenced later internet roster. [1]

However, the survey merged some comparative charts across the Saltis–McErlane, O’Banion and Klondike O’Donnell groups because the records for any one group were too few to support separate numerical conclusions. That means ratios and average ages in the pooled chart cannot legitimately be assigned to the West Side O’Donnells alone. Similarly, conclusions about the O’Banion group’s burglary profile should not be mechanically transferred to Klondike’s circle. [1]

Landesco also observed that the O’Donnell associates had varied recorded residential origins across Chicago. This finding complicates the picture of a strictly neighborhood-based youth gang and supports treatment as an adult commercial criminal network whose supply relationships spanned more than a single compact block. [1]

February 1930: The West Madison Street Police Roundup

Contemporary press reports of February 13, 1930 describe a police raid involving men identified by investigators as the O’Donnell gang. The meeting took place in or near a second-floor restaurant on West Madison Street during a broader Chicago anti-gang roundup. Reporters identified Myles and Bernard O’Donnell among the detained men, along with William “Three-Fingered Jack” White, George “Red” Barker, Mike Quirk, Edward Simek, and John O’Connor. Contemporary reporting also mentioned Frank Lacey. [7]

The incident matters because it is a dated public reference to the continuing West Side gang label years after the killings of Doherty and Duffy. It also shows that the network’s contacts reached figures associated with later labor or other criminal disputes. The precise configuration at that table was not a corporate board of directors in a legal sense, whatever figurative terminology newspapers used. [7]

Detention, presentation at a police identification show-up and potential referral to a grand jury are distinct from conviction. The source does not establish that all those present were permanently enrolled under the O’Donnells, had committed any common specific crime, or received a uniform judgment. The article does not convert a mass-raid report into a verified roster of guilty gang members. [7]

The Labor-Racketeering Question

The collapse of Prohibition revenue gave some criminal enterprises incentives to look for other income sources. In Chicago, labor relations, transport contractors, coercive service businesses and union disputes became subjects of law-enforcement interest. A June 1934 Chicago Tribune retrospective stated that William “Klondike” O’Donnell’s more recent activities were said to concern labor racketeering. [11]

The February 1930 press had already placed Jack White and George Barker in company identified with the O’Donnell group. Those men were associated in crime reporting with labor violence and rackets, but association at one meeting is not proof of a joint syndicate’s control over a named union or freight business. A reliable labor-racket history would need court files, union proceedings, named victims and dates for each separate enterprise. [7] [11]

This article therefore records a later reported connection, not an unquestioned conversion of the 1920s West Side beer gang into one continuous post-Repeal labor syndicate. No exact transition day, formal successor president or continuous roster has been demonstrated. [11] [1]

Decline, Repeal and the Early 1930s

The most directly documented phase of the West Side O’Donnells is the mid-1920s beer trade, followed by late-1920s enforcement and a 1930 police reference. Nationwide repeal of Prohibition in December 1933 eliminated the specific federal ban that had sustained much of the illegal alcohol distribution market, although taxation, licensing and other liquor violations continued. That economic transformation affected criminal groups unevenly and did not instantaneously dissolve every gang or relationship. [11] [13]

Later popular biographies give Myles O’Donnell a March 10, 1933 death date, but the immediate underlying death certificate and newspaper report were not verified here. The date is recorded as a later biographical claim, not used as an unquestioned marker of corporate succession. Klondike remained named in a 1934 newspaper retrospective, yet surviving references do not establish a neat final transfer of assets or a precisely dated dissolution meeting. [11] [15]

The defensible conclusion is that the O’Donnell beer-running operation lost its defining market with Prohibition’s end and later criminal associations took different forms. No verified active contemporary gang, family enterprise or institutional successor is documented under the West Side O’Donnell name. [11] [1]

Ethnic Identity and Neighborhood Context

The West Side organization is grouped within Irish-American Crime Syndicates because major named figures were identified as Irish-American and contemporary journalists sometimes characterized their beer faction in ethnic terms. The label describes particular men and a historical formation, not a trait of Chicago’s Irish population. Most residents of Irish descent held ordinary occupations and were not members of the criminal enterprise. [5] [12]

Landesco’s group analysis also warns against simple ethnic uniformity. Gang membership and residence changed as men cooperated across neighborhood lines; the West Side group’s own list of associates, as compiled by researchers and investigators, does not amount to an independently checked exclusive Irish roster. To describe every person at a meeting or every person who delivered beer as ethnically Irish would exceed the records. [1] [10]

A recurring failure of older gang narratives is to present complicated conflicts as races or nationalities fighting as if the communities themselves were armies. This article instead traces named networks and dated events while treating ethnicity as a contextual variable that does not explain every commercial dispute. [1] [5]

Politics, Police and Corruption Allegations

The 1926 indictment of Cicero officials and alleged Prohibition conspirators reflects federal concern that illegal liquor distribution could be facilitated by public officials. The McSwiggin killing made those relationships especially visible because a serving prosecutor was killed while socializing with men associated with illicit beer. These circumstances generated charges of corruption and competing accusations in the press. [2] [3]

The record does not authorize a conclusion that all public officials involved with Cicero affairs were bought by the West Side O’Donnells. Nor does it establish that McSwiggin himself was knowingly part of their enterprise. Persons may have political, social or professional contacts in the same environment without having the same legal obligations or participating in the same crimes. [2] [14]

Landesco regarded incomplete police records, frequent jurisdictional boundaries and weak case dispositions as central to the historical problem. His study supplies structural context, not a judicial finding of corruption against every individual named in its pages. [1]

Firearms, Killings and Attribution

The West Side network was associated with a dangerous era of rapid-fire weapons, retaliatory shootings, attempted protection of liquor routes and intense police attention. The McSwiggin triple killing and Tancl case both appear in the group’s historical record, but with different levels of proof: the triple homicide victims and date are well reported, while specific culpability is disputed; Bernard’s Tancl indictment is mentioned by Landesco, but conviction and precise role are not settled by that citation. [1] [4]

A sober crime history distinguishes victim, witness, person sought, suspect, indicted defendant, and convicted offender. These terms cannot be swapped simply because they appear near one another in a lurid newspaper narrative. The later notion of a formal execution squad under Klondike is not established by the documentation examined here. [1] [2]

The organization’s significance comes not from a credible tally of murders “owned” by the gang, but from its traceable place in Chicago’s violent struggle over the Prohibition market and the way that struggle frustrated investigators. [2] [13]

Historical Significance and Limits of the Story

The West Side O’Donnells illustrate how illegal markets could sustain recognizable adult criminal networks without the bureaucratic structure often attributed to later Mafia families. They had named leaders, recurring associates, saloon relationships, reported coercive activities, and a place in a contemporary sociological survey. They also exemplify the problems of reconstructing criminal organizations whose most important relations were informal and whose official records were incomplete. [1] [3]

The 1926 McSwiggin case was a public turning point because a prosecutor died alongside men linked to a criminal liquor faction. It exposed the intersection of personal familiarity, criminal commerce, political rumor and weak investigative outcomes. The 1927 warehouse case then supplies another specific window onto the economics of liquor diversion, while the 1930 police raid demonstrates continued identification of the O’Donnell network. [2] [6] [7]

The history should not be reduced to a moral story of “Irish gangs” versus “Italian gangs.” Chicago’s criminal markets involved cross-ethnic cooperation, unstable rivalries, diverse neighborhood populations and multiple legal jurisdictions. The MGU collection gains value from treating each gang as a separately documented organization rather than a branch of one all-encompassing Chicago Irish Mafia. [1] [12]

Terminology and Historical Usage

West Side O’Donnell Gang

Historical name for the Chicago Prohibition group associated with Klondike, Myles and Bernard O’Donnell.

South Side O’Donnells

Separate organization led by Edward “Spike” O’Donnell; not a West Side branch.

Beer running

Illegal transport or distribution of beer under Prohibition restrictions.

Bootlegging

Unlawful sale, production or movement of alcohol, particularly during national Prohibition.

Cicero

Cook County municipality west of Chicago; important in the 1926 McSwiggin investigation.

Beer Wars

Shorthand for a succession of conflicts over Chicago-area illegal beer markets, not a single centrally commanded war.

Bonded whiskey

Spirits held subject to government warehousing and regulatory requirements; relevant to the 1927 Morand case.

Indictment

Formal accusation charging an offense; not a conviction or verdict of guilt.

Identification Bureau

Chicago police recordkeeping institution whose files Landesco studied and criticized for incompleteness.

Grand jury

Body empowered to investigate and assess charges; proceedings do not necessarily produce a trial or conviction.

Racket

Coercive or illegal enterprise; term must be connected to specific conduct rather than applied to any business.

Historical source attribution

Explicitly identifying the difference between a police assertion, contemporary news, later recollection and legal judgment.

Research and Editorial Standards

Source hierarchy. This entry emphasizes John Landesco’s near-contemporary sociological survey, dated 1926–1934 newspapers and Northwestern University’s historical homicide resource. Later books and biographies are used for general interpretive context. The survey’s individual chapters are directly available and were reviewed for its limitations on gang charts and police-record completeness. Modern books cited through publisher or catalog summaries are not represented as having been independently collated page-by-page. [1] [2] [16]

Murder attribution. The McSwiggin murders are not presented as solved by a conviction, and the article does not manufacture a definitive perpetrator from police suspicions. Bernard’s Tancl indictment is an allegation rather than a verdict. People who were killed are not assigned criminal guilt because of the company they kept. [2] [4]

Identity and continuity. The South Side O’Donnells are a separate organization. Names from police association lists and roundups are not transformed into a verified permanent roster. The early-1930s endpoint and reported Myles death are not given unsupported exact conclusions. No inference of formal succession to later Chicago Outfit organizations is made. [1] [8] [10]

Further research. Priority tasks are to retrieve complete Cook County case records for the Tancl killing; federal dockets for the 1926 conspiracy indictment and 1927 Morand warehouse case; the original 1926 McSwiggin inquests and grand-jury materials; the 1930 detention dispositions; and independent vital records for Myles and Klondike.
