Overview

The Danny Greene network was a Cleveland-based criminal coalition that emerged around Daniel J. “Danny” Greene and challenged the authority of the established Cleveland Mafia during the 1970s. Its nucleus was an informal circle associated with Greene and sometimes described as the Celtic Club; its wider alliances included John Nardi, a Teamsters-connected figure with his own organized-crime relationships. In 1984 the Sixth Circuit described Greene as the leader of a rival criminal organization that had come to dominate criminal activity in parts of west Cleveland. This judicial characterization is the strongest starting point for distinguishing Greene’s network from folklore. [1][2][3]

Greene’s trajectory ran from the Cleveland waterfront and International Longshoremen’s Association Local 1317 through his efforts to organize private garbage haulers, then into gambling, extortionate lending, and conflict over territorial and criminal income. A 1984 Department of Justice statement to the U.S. Senate described a competing coalition that made inroads into Cleveland rackets, with Nardi as an important partner. The network’s participants and alliances changed over time; the evidence does not support treating every person associated with Greene as an inducted member of a permanent Irish Mafia “family.” [1][3][4][5]

The best-known events were the 1975 death of Alexander “Shondor” Birns, the 1975 bombing of Greene’s home and office, the May 17, 1977 murder of Nardi, and the October 6, 1977 assassination of Greene after a dental appointment in suburban Lyndhurst. Later testimony and prosecutions exposed a murder conspiracy involving Cleveland Mafia figures. State murder cases, federal RICO cases, and subsequent drug prosecutions must be separated because they involved different charges, evidence, defendants, and legal outcomes. [2][3][6][7][8]

This organization profile is a historical study of criminal actors, the damage caused by violence and corruption, and the limits of the surviving public record. The editorial cutoff is November 13, 2026; it does not assign present-day leadership to a coalition whose namesake was killed in 1977.

Classification caution. Greene’s Irish ethnic identity and his associates’ imagery do not identify Irish-American communities as criminal. Likewise, Nardi’s Mafia contacts do not make the Greene coalition a subsidiary of an Italian-American family; the contemporary court record describes a rival to Cleveland’s Mafia. [2][3][9]

Naming: Celtic Club, Greene Crew, and Network

“Danny Greene network” is the encyclopedia’s editorial subject label. Local journalism and later recollections call a close group around Greene the Celtic Club, a name associated with his conspicuous Irish symbolism and his Collinwood base. Greene himself referred publicly to the Celtic Club after John Nardi’s killing, according to a retrospective compilation of broadcast and newspaper reporting. That supports a contemporary-used label, but not a certified legal title or a comprehensive membership roll. [8][9]

The label “Irish mob” can be useful as an informal historical shorthand, yet it blurs important distinctions: some members were Greene’s longstanding personal friends, others were business or criminal associates, and Nardi operated with a separate base of influence. The group was not a statutory corporation, a social club encompassing its whole neighborhood, or a branch of the Cleveland Italian-American Mafia. [2][3][9]

In this article, core associates refers to individuals repeatedly identified with Greene’s circle; allies identifies separately situated figures whose interests overlapped; and adversaries refers to those documented as opposing the coalition. The terminology avoids implying that every named person held a verified rank or was convicted of a specific offense.

Evidence: Courts, Senate Testimony, and Local Archives

The most reliable account of the 1977 Greene killing’s adjudicated criminal conspiracy is the Sixth Circuit decision in United States v. Licavoli (1984). The opinion describes the rival organization, investigation of Greene’s movements, the car bombing, the federal RICO convictions, and the distinct state-court results. Its account reflects the evidence viewed under the standard applicable on appeal, not a comprehensive biography of each participant. [2]

A second anchor is Deputy Assistant Attorney General John C. Keeney’s January 31, 1984 statement to the Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations. It explicitly characterizes Greene as a challenger to Cleveland’s established family, describes the Nardi alliance, lists prosecutions arising from both men’s murders, and traces later narcotics activity involving people connected to the old network. It is an official government perspective, not a judicial verdict for every allegation in the statement. [3]

For Greene’s employment, union controversies, neighborhood activities, and personal circumstances, the 1968 appellate decision, Case Western Reserve University’s Encyclopedia of Cleveland History, the Cleveland Memory Project, Cleveland Historical, and contemporary reporting are useful comparators. Their level of proof differs: a dated archival photograph verifies a scene, while an attribution about who committed an unsolved murder remains an attribution. [1][4][5][6][7][8]

Cleveland’s Criminal Landscape Before Greene

Greene emerged in a city with established illegal gambling, protection rackets, corrupt business relationships, and a Cleveland Mafia organization that had operated through successive leaders. Organized-crime enterprises competed over illicit income, but not every racket was directly operated by one family. The gambling and money-lending economy also involved independent brokers and figures outside formal Mafia membership. [3][10][11]

Under John Scalish, a long-serving Cleveland Mafia leader, the family had maintained influence for decades. Greene and his allies developed as the boundaries between independent operators, tolerated subcontractors, and open rivals were changing. The departure or imprisonment of an intermediary could create disputes over who could collect from particular gambling businesses. [3][10]

Cleveland’s neighborhoods should not be depicted as uniformly “mob controlled.” Much of the city’s port, refuse collection, industrial labor, and retail economy remained ordinary and lawful, while specific criminal participants exploited opportunities for coercion within these sectors.

Danny Greene’s Background and Collinwood Years

Daniel J. Greene grew up in Collinwood, on Cleveland’s east side, and entered employment connected with Lake Erie shipping. Local historical accounts describe early military service, work on the docks, and a strong public identification with Irish heritage. Sources differ on his precise November 1929 birth date; this profile avoids selecting one uncritically when the exact day is unnecessary to its institutional history. [1][4][5]

Greene’s Collinwood associations later provided a social world in which friendship, neighborhood reputation, and mutual protection could be translated into criminal relationships. The neighborhood was also home to many residents with no connection to his crimes, and the term “Collinwood crew” cannot be used as evidence against them. [5][8]

His later public persona—green clothing, Irish emblems, forceful interviews, and dramatic stories of surviving assassination attempts—became a central part of the legend. A historically responsible analysis separates the verified workplace and court chronology from anecdotes that Greene or his adversaries may have cultivated. [8][9]

International Longshoremen’s Association Local 1317

Greene rose within Local 1317 of the International Longshoremen’s Association (ILA) after arriving on Cleveland’s waterfront in 1957. Congressional proceedings in 1964 reproduced contemporaneous newspaper investigations into hiring practices, union payments, and workers’ complaints. Leadership of a legitimate union was an institutional position; it did not make all longshoremen participants in Greene’s later criminal network. [1][12]

In the union setting, control of job referrals and workplace access could offer leverage over workers and employers. The documentary record includes allegations that employees were pressured to surrender earnings or provide services and a federal case involving union money and reporting. The crucial distinction is between complaints investigated by journalists, counts prosecuted in court, and the disposition of those counts. [4][12]

For the Crime Encyclopedia, this period illustrates how an individual could convert legitimate administrative access into coercive influence. It also demonstrates why labor-racketeering cases must be described without assuming that the union’s rank-and-file, its parent institution, or the Cleveland shipping trade shared criminal culpability.

The 1968 Federal Reversal: United States v. Greene

In United States v. Greene, 400 F.2d 847 (6th Cir. 1968), the appellate court reviewed a conviction on five counts arising under the Labor-Management Reporting and Disclosure Act and related conspiracy provisions. The indictment included union-funds embezzlement and false-financial-report allegations. Greene’s codefendant had been acquitted. [4]

The Sixth Circuit held that the government’s extended cross-examination about Greene’s expensive living was improperly prejudicial and could not be cured by belated instructions to disregard it. It reversed the judgment and remanded for a new trial. This was not an appellate finding that no theft had occurred; it was a decision that the original convictions could not stand because of the unfairly admitted evidence. [4]

Local historical summaries subsequently record a 1970 guilty plea involving union-law violations and a $10,000 fine. That later resolution must not be confused with the overturned 1966 jury judgment. Unless the precise 1970 plea transcript is available, this article avoids inventing its detailed count-by-count wording or sentence conditions. [1][5]

Private Refuse Collection and the Solid Waste Guild

After losing his union position, Greene organized activity among Cleveland’s private trash haulers through what local history calls the Cleveland Trade Solid Waste Guild. Cleveland Historical reports a June 1969 charter and describes attempted consolidation of companies that remained legally separate businesses. Accounts include allegations that operators feared exclusion from routes or pressure on commercial customers if they refused to participate. [5]

An association of legitimate haulers could, in principle, represent business interests; unlawful coercion arises when access to routes, customers, pricing, or safety becomes dependent on threats or intimidation. The problem is not the business of waste collection but the use of force and fear to direct it. Greene’s reputation grew amid reported confrontations involving individual haulers. [1][5]

One prominent dispute involved Michael Frato, who opposed Greene’s efforts and later became a direct adversary. The sequence leading to Frato’s death must be separated from the separate deaths and allegations attached to the refuse-hauling conflict. [1][7]

Arthur Sneperger and the Limits of Bombing Allegations

Local accounts report that Arthur “Snep” Sneperger, associated with Greene, died when an explosive device detonated during the refuse-hauling conflict. Some later tellings accuse Greene of having deliberately engineered the death, while other accounts describe suspicions rather than an adjudicated offense. The Case Western Reserve University encyclopedia attributes that theory to police suspicion. [1][5]

No homicide conviction against Greene for Sneperger’s death is established by the sources consulted here. Therefore, the article treats the death as documented and the alleged motive or responsibility as unresolved. This distinction matters because retrospective crime writing frequently turns unsolved incidents into confident chronological assertions.

The incident also marks the human and public-safety costs of explosive violence. The danger extended well beyond criminal rivals: industrial employees, drivers, residents, and visitors could be injured by devices placed around ordinary streets or businesses.

Michael Frato’s Death and Greene’s Acquittal

On November 26, 1971, Greene and Michael Frato confronted each other during a violent encounter. Historical summaries report that Frato approached with a firearm and that Greene shot and killed him. Greene faced prosecution but was acquitted on a self-defense theory. The record supports reporting the death and the acquittal; it does not support describing Greene as convicted of murdering Frato. [1][6][7]

The confrontation compounded Greene’s notoriety. It also increased the difficulty of separating real violence from self-promotional claims that he could survive attacks and dominate opponents through personal fearlessness. An acquittal resolves the particular criminal charge as tried; it is not a global judicial endorsement of Greene’s other activities. [7][8]

Media depictions often collapse this episode into a simple succession of “hits.” The more accurate sequence is a criminally violent encounter followed by a legal determination in which the defendant was acquitted. The difference is a basic requirement of the encyclopedia’s editorial standards.

Alexander “Shondor” Birns: Patron, Competitor, and Rival

Alexander “Shondor” Birns was a powerful independent Cleveland racketeer associated with gambling, numbers, loans, and violence. He was not simply a formal Cleveland Mafia boss. Greene’s relationship with Birns is widely portrayed as moving from cooperation to confrontation as Greene gained independent opportunities in illicit gambling. Case Western Reserve University records Birns’s death on March 29, 1975. [1][10][11]

Local histories recount a disputed $75,000 loan intended for a gambling-related venture, with controversy over whether a courier delivered the funds. The narrative helps explain the reported escalation, but precise financial terms and responsibility should not be asserted as adjudicated facts merely because several later authors repeat them. [10][11]

This episode illustrates how criminal markets could be governed through personal relationships rather than enforceable written contracts. If an arrangement broke down, participants could resort to coercion and retaliation, exposing nearby communities to the consequences.

March 29, 1975: Birns Killed in a Car Bombing

On March 29, 1975, Birns died when a bomb destroyed his vehicle after he left a west-side Cleveland establishment. Police Museum and local historical accounts document the incident, which was widely interpreted at the time as an escalation in the Greene–Birns dispute. [10][11][13]

Greene was widely suspected of involvement, but the historical and museum sources consulted do not establish that Greene was convicted or even charged in Birns’s murder. One museum history states that no one was arrested for the killing. The responsible formulation therefore is “suspected in contemporary or retrospective accounts,” not “convicted killer.” [10][11][13]

The public terror of such a bombing is not diminished by uncertainty over the perpetrator. Its location in a commercial setting placed noncombatants at risk and added to Cleveland’s perception of a city whose underworld conflicts could erupt in public.

The Celtic Club and Greene’s Close Circle

Greene’s close associates are frequently discussed under the Celtic Club label. Retrospective local journalism identifies Kevin McTaggart, Keith Ritson, Brian O’Donnell, and Danny Greene Jr. in his circle. The relationships varied, and source descriptions such as “lieutenant,” “enforcer,” or “club member” are not standardized legal classifications. [8][9]

Greene’s symbolic Irish identity, neighborhood gatherings, and use of an informal meeting place supplied solidarity and public recognition. Neither ethnicity nor attendance at a social club establishes participation in illegal activity. Specific allegations require named acts, court records, or other evidence; the encyclopedia avoids an uncorroborated complete member list. [8][9]

The group’s social cohesion was one resource among several. Access to gambling operators, relationships with labor-connected brokers, and coercive reputation helped explain why a relatively small network could challenge a larger, older crime family for economic space. This remains a qualitative assessment, not a reliable membership estimate.

Organizational Form: A Coalition, Not a Mafia Family

The court’s 1984 description of Greene as leading a rival criminal organization should be read functionally. His associates cooperated in a continuing set of illegal activities, but the published opinion does not supply a formal chain of command modeled on a La Cosa Nostra boss, underboss, consigliere, captains, and soldiers. [2]

Personal authority appears to have been important: Greene’s reputation and direct relationships attracted participants, while individual criminal associates retained outside contacts. Unlike a formal Mafia induction system, there is no verified administrative register showing who was “made,” who paid fixed dues, or who received official geographic franchises from Greene. [2][3][8]

John Nardi deserves separate classification. The Senate statement calls him a Cleveland Teamster official with Mafia ties who aligned with Greene. Describing Nardi merely as a subordinate employee would erase the independent relationships that made the alliance consequential. [3]

John Nardi: Labor Position and Independent Influence

John Nardi had connections to the Teamsters and Cleveland’s organized-crime world before he became closely allied with Greene. Federal descriptions emphasize his labor position and Mafia ties; local histories portray him as a significant rival to the leadership associated with James Licavoli. The evidence does not require us to call Nardi either a full subordinate of Greene or the head of the same Celtic Club. [3][8][9]

The alliance combined complementary assets: Greene had an increasingly visible network and a reputation for resisting rivals; Nardi brought experience in criminal and labor-related circles. Their partnership escalated conflict with the established organization, particularly once leadership positions and access to illegal markets were disputed. [3][8]

Nardi’s death in May 1977 weakened this alliance and increased the danger to Greene. It is historically misleading to tell the last five months of the story as only a private quarrel between Greene and Licavoli; Nardi’s independent trajectory helps explain the struggle’s political structure within the criminal world.

Relationship to the Cleveland Crime Family

The Cleveland crime family was a separate La Cosa Nostra enterprise with a recognized history predating Greene’s rise. A 1984 Justice Department Senate statement says that Greene’s coalition gradually undercut the family in several illicit markets. The federal appellate opinion calls Greene’s group a rival to Licavoli’s organization. This is affirmative evidence that the two should remain distinct encyclopedia subjects. [2][3]

Their relationship was not uniformly hostile from the first day Greene entered criminal enterprises. Earlier personal accommodations, mutual contacts, and dealings through intermediaries are reported in local histories. But association with Mafia figures at one moment is not the same as initiation, central control, or enduring subordination. [8][9]

For cross-referencing, the companion Cleveland Crime Family article should carry the Mayfield Road and Licavoli administration; this page treats the Greene network’s formation, alliance with Nardi, fate, and surviving former associates.

The June 1975 Bombing of Greene’s Waterloo Road Property

In June 1975, an explosive device destroyed Greene’s home and office on Waterloo Road in Collinwood. Case Western Reserve University and Cleveland Historical document that Greene survived the attack. The incident became a major element of his contemporary reputation for narrowly escaping violence. [1][5][6]

It would be improper to assign a specific bomber or commanding conspirator solely from the fact that an attack occurred during a feud. The available summaries establish the event and the survival, while details of culpability require separate evidentiary sourcing. [1][5]

The bombing also exposed the dangers of attacks on residences and commercial premises. Other occupants, neighbors, emergency responders, and people in adjacent buildings had no role in the disputed rackets but could have suffered the consequences.

Criminal Markets: Gambling, Credit, and Coercion

The Justice Department’s 1984 statement identifies gambling, narcotics, and loansharking among the markets in which Greene’s coalition challenged Cleveland’s traditional organization. This is an official retrospective assessment of the coalition’s influence, not a claim that every participant personally traded drugs or operated every scheme. [3]

Gambling enterprises, particularly numbers and bookmaking, generate ongoing cash flows and territorial disputes. Extortionate lending can make a borrower dependent on unlawful collection practices. In labor and commercial markets, coercion can function through threats to employees, customers, competitors, and contractors rather than open theft of goods. [1][3][11]

A mafia-versus-gang narrative focused only on assassinations neglects these underlying economic incentives. The violence was frequently about who could command streams of illegal earnings, demand payments, or exclude competing operators. Available documents do not establish a reliable annual revenue total for the Greene network, and one should not be invented.

Collinwood, West Cleveland, and Greater Cleveland

Greene’s upbringing and symbolic base were connected with Collinwood on the east side. His trade-union period involved the lakefront and industrial docks, while his refuse-hauling activities affected companies across the city. The Sixth Circuit identifies the criminal organization’s territorial reach in West Cleveland during the period of open competition. These locations refer to different phases and functions; none implies that every local business was involved. [1][2][5]

Major violent incidents occurred in multiple jurisdictions: the Waterloo Road bombing in Cleveland, Birns’s killing on the west side, Nardi’s assassination near a Teamsters building, and Greene’s death at a Lyndhurst medical-office parking area. The geography demonstrates how conflicts migrated from illicit commercial relationships into civic streets, work sites, and ordinary professional spaces. [5][6][8][10]

The court record also describes criminal rivalries and payments involving the wider northern Ohio region. Those contacts do not prove that Greene administered a unified regional Mafia family stretching to all connected towns. [2]

Bombings and the Problem of Attribution

The mid-1970s Cleveland conflict became notorious for bombings and attempted killings. Contemporary journalists and later writers often aggregate all explosions into a “bomb city” narrative, but aggregate totals vary according to the period, geography, and whether an incident was confirmed as criminal. This article avoids assigning an unsupported citywide count to Greene’s own crew. [5][8][9]

For an individual attack, reliable analysis asks who was harmed, when and where the device detonated, whether there was a charge, and whether a court found the alleged perpetrator responsible. An allegation based on criminal rumor is not comparable to the detailed evidence reviewed in United States v. Licavoli. [2][8]

The resulting pattern affected more than targets. Ordinary residents and business owners lived with the risk of vehicles or buildings being booby-trapped; police and fire personnel had to respond; and witnesses faced intimidation. Public-safety consequences are part of the organizational history, not peripheral detail.

1976: John Scalish Dies and the Leadership Balance Changes

John Scalish died in May 1976, after decades as a leader of the Cleveland Mafia. His death changed an already contentious environment for criminal influence. Contemporary and retrospective accounts emphasize the vulnerability of competing leadership claims and the rise of James “Jack White” Licavoli within the traditional family. [8][9][14]

Greene and Nardi were among the external challengers to the Mafia administration. The court record is clearer about the murder conspiracy than about every conversation surrounding the disputed transition; it does not justify a cinematic scene-by-scene reconstruction of a boardroom-style succession struggle. [2][8]

This period is an example of how an established criminal organization can face pressures from both internal leadership changes and an outside competitor with an independent income base. The distinction between these two dynamics is essential to the article’s structure.

Spring 1976: The Licavoli Murder Conspiracy

The Sixth Circuit’s 1984 opinion states that in the spring of 1976, Licavoli decided Greene should be killed and arranged for other members of the Cleveland crime organization to contact Raymond Ferritto. The federal prosecution relied on Ferritto’s testimony about meetings, attempted attacks, surveillance, and promised compensation. These facts were assessed within the appeal from the defendants’ racketeering convictions. [2]

The opinion describes efforts to identify Greene’s whereabouts and plans that did not proceed because of the risk of harming others or difficulty reaching him. It also documents a broader attempt to target people connected with his organization. The focus here is the conspiracy’s existence and evidentiary support, not the technical methods of producing or using a weapon. [2]

A crucial legal point: the men charged in the federal RICO case were adversaries of Greene, not members of Greene’s organization. The case is therefore a major source about the Greene network even though the convicted enterprise in that proceeding was the rival Cleveland Mafia.

May 17, 1977: John Nardi Assassinated

On May 17, 1977, John Nardi was killed by a car bomb near a Teamsters office in Cleveland. Contemporary recollections and local reporting treat the killing as a central escalation in the struggle between Greene and the Cleveland Mafia. The 1984 Justice Department testimony groups the Nardi and Greene murders within the campaign that prompted coordinated prosecutions. [3][8]

Because the murders were separate events and criminal cases may have attached different defendants to different conduct, the reader should not infer from the Greene RICO conviction that every defendant was also convicted in a state murder trial for Nardi’s death. The Senate statement speaks more broadly of cases arising from or related to the two murders. [2][3]

Nardi’s death eliminated a powerful ally. Greene continued to speak publicly after the killing, including about the Celtic Club, but public bravado is not evidence that the network retained stable numbers or an effective long-term succession plan. [8]

The Final Months: Threats, Fear, and Public Persona

After Nardi’s assassination, threats against Greene became a matter of widely reported public concern. Local accounts portray Greene as openly challenging his enemies and remaining unusually visible despite repeated attempts on his life. Those statements reveal a public persona but do not settle internal strategic planning or the reliability of every alleged attempted attack. [1][8][9]

His notoriety may have helped sustain loyalty and intimidate rivals, but it also created a recognizably identifiable target. Greene’s criminal activity, the threats against him, and the public interest in his survival all contributed to a story that later entertainment would dramatize. [6][8]

The practical record is narrower than the legend: by October 1977, evidence presented in court showed that people within the rival Cleveland organization were planning his murder, while Greene continued to carry on ordinary personal activities. [2]

October 6, 1977: Greene Murdered in Lyndhurst

On October 6, 1977, Greene visited a dentist at a professional office complex in Lyndhurst, Ohio. A bomb concealed in a nearby vehicle exploded as he returned to his own automobile, killing him. This event is established by the 1984 Sixth Circuit opinion, the Cleveland Memory Project, and Cleveland Historical’s use of archival photographs. [2][6][7]

The court’s description connects the killing with the conspiracy organized under Licavoli and its participating associates. The crime was not merely a fabled underworld “duel”: it involved planning by multiple people, the use of an explosive device in an ordinary office parking lot, and an actual homicide. [2]

The precise scene, including the dentist’s visit and the location, matters because it demonstrates how organized-crime violence reached spaces used by patients, workers, and other uninvolved people. This article does not reproduce technical details that would make the device or method of attack a practical blueprint.

The Investigation and Raymond Ferritto’s Evidence

The murder investigation developed into a major attack on Cleveland organized crime. Raymond Ferritto became a critical government witness, furnishing information about the plot and relationships among the men involved. His testimony was central to the account later considered by the Sixth Circuit. His status as a cooperating participant also required the court and juries to assess incentives and corroboration. [2][7]

Contemporary reporting in December 1977 described indictments involving Licavoli and other accused participants. An indictment is a formal charge, not proof of guilt; later proceedings produced different results in state and federal courts. [15]

The combined investigation exposed the weakness of closed criminal organizations once associates cooperated, witnesses testified, and investigators linked events over time. But criminal investigations should not be credited with proving unrelated rumors merely because they produced significant convictions.

State Murder Trials and Distinct Defendants

The Sixth Circuit noted that the six defendants in the subsequent federal appeal had also been tried in state court on charges related to Greene’s murder. It recorded state murder convictions for Pasquale Cisternino, Ronald Carabbia, and Kenneth Ciarcia. This does not mean the state convicted all six defendants of murder. [2]

The Cleveland Memory Project provides a complementary account identifying acquittals of Licavoli and Angelo Lonardo in the earlier state proceedings and highlighting separate outcomes for other defendants. Its retrospective summary reinforces why the federal appellate decision should not be generalized into an inaccurate state murder-conviction roster. [7]

In an encyclopedia chronology, the label “acquitted” must remain visible even when a defendant was later convicted of another offense in a different court. The state cases and later federal RICO convictions were not identical charges, and their outcomes are not interchangeable.

The 1982 Federal RICO Verdicts

On July 8, 1982, a federal jury convicted James Licavoli, Anthony Liberatore, John Calandra, Pasquale Cisternino, Ronald Carabbia, and Kenneth Ciarcia of racketeering-related offenses arising from the criminal enterprise’s conduct connected with Greene’s murder. Contemporary reporting documented sentencing later that month, while the final appellate opinion describes the racketeering-conspiracy convictions at issue. [2][16]

Under the federal Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act (RICO), an enterprise’s affairs may be prosecuted through a pattern of specified illegal acts. The federal conviction therefore addressed participation in the rival Mafia enterprise’s racketeering operation; Greene, already dead, was not the convicted RICO defendant. [2]

The opinion describes additional criminal conduct associated with FBI-information theft and a bribery case. Those charges had their own proof and outcomes, and the court addressed specific legal arguments instead of issuing a generalized endorsement of every past allegation about Cleveland organized crime. [2]

January 9, 1984: Sixth Circuit Affirms

On January 9, 1984, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit issued its opinion in United States v. Licavoli, 725 F.2d 1040. It affirmed the judgments of conviction of the six RICO appellants and upheld the challenged order denying Liberatore a new trial on a separate bribery conviction. [2]

The court considered whether conspiracies to commit murder could qualify as predicate racketeering activity, along with other questions about the required pattern and proof. Its legal analysis is the core adjudicated record for the federal case and should be cited instead of relying on newspaper shorthand such as “every mob boss was found guilty of Greene’s murder.” [2]

The judgment marked a major federal success against the Cleveland Mafia. It does not establish that Greene’s own coalition was a formally inducted Mafia family, that all its members were known, or that it continued as an independent organization after his death.

The FBI Information-Leak and Bribery Episode

One unusual feature of the Licavoli opinion concerns confidential FBI material obtained by a Cleveland office employee, Geraldine Rabinowitz, through contacts connected to a car dealership. The record describes how documents were passed to people within the criminal enterprise and how money was provided in a transaction examined in the bribery proceedings. [2]

The episode is distinct from the plot to kill Greene, even though it appeared in the same broader prosecution narrative. It illustrates the institutional stakes of leaks: confidential investigative material can expose law-enforcement interests, reduce investigative effectiveness, or intimidate potential witnesses. [2]

It should not be conflated with later stories about Greene’s own possible contacts with federal agents. Being named in an FBI document, being an investigative source, and bribing someone to steal federal records are legally and historically different categories.

After Greene: Fragmentation and New Criminal Alliances

Greene’s death was the destruction of a leader-centered coalition, not necessarily the immediate disappearance of every associate or criminal interest it had touched. The 1984 Justice Department Senate statement described how James Cappola, identified as a former lieutenant of Greene, collaborated with Carmen Zagaria and others in a later narcotics enterprise. [3]

The same statement explains that by 1979 parts of the new drug ring operated with protection from Angelo Lonardo, then underboss of the Cleveland Mafia. One victim it identifies is Keith Ritson, a former Greene loyalist later killed amid the narcotics enterprise’s violence. These developments show crossover among surviving relationships, but they do not demonstrate that Greene’s Celtic Club remained intact under a successor. [3]

The Justice Department reported that investigators disrupted the later organization in 1982. The distinction between the original Greene coalition and post-1977 drug businesses is especially important: common people and relationships can persist while the organization, command structure, and offenses change. [3]

Federal Enforcement and the Cleveland Crime Family’s Decline

The Greene investigation helped federal and local authorities assemble evidence against senior Cleveland Mafia figures. The 1984 Justice Department statement described multiple prosecutions flowing from the Greene–Nardi killings and larger racketeering investigations, while later accounts link these cases to reduced Mafia authority in northeastern Ohio. [3][7][16]

An official investigative “success” claim should be treated as a dated institutional assessment. It does not prove the total disappearance of organized crime, the end of every local gambling operation, or an immediate permanent transformation of every criminal market. [3]

A central historical lesson is institutional: use of enterprise charges, cooperating witnesses, and corroborated surveillance could document leadership and patterns of conduct that had previously appeared only in scattered street cases. Greene’s network is thus relevant even though the decisive convictions targeted his adversaries.

Claims About FBI Contacts and Confidential Sources

Popular accounts sometimes characterize Greene as a police or FBI informant. Whether, when, and in what capacity any individual gave information requires identifying a dated original file, report, or sworn testimony; receiving investigators’ attention does not establish formal confidential-source status. [7][9][17]

The known, citable Licavoli opinion establishes details of the rival Mafia murder prosecution, but it does not by itself prove every later claim about Greene’s arrangement with the FBI. The article therefore does not treat a claimed protected-informant relationship as a settled explanatory key for his survival or eventual death. [2]

A similar caution applies to FBI files about a “Daniel J. Greene” whose identity must be independently established from the file’s biographical details. A researcher should not merge namesakes, surveillance references, and actual source-payment records without document-level checking. [17]

Victims and Community Impact

The Greene–Mafia conflict included named homicide victims—among them Birns, Frato, Nardi, and Greene—but the ramifications reached much further. People who ran ordinary businesses, worked on docks, drove refuse trucks, used medical offices, or lived near targeted properties could be harmed, frightened, or drawn into investigations. [1][5][6][10]

Workplace coercion undermined honest union representation; gambling protection and debt collection harmed people financially; bombings diverted public-safety resources and degraded trust. The narrative should not romanticize these effects as simply a glamorous struggle over which criminal controlled Cleveland. [3][12]

Because many victims are less prominent in historical accounts than notorious offenders, the encyclopedia retains the institutional consequences even when a reliable name-by-name victim roster cannot be reconstructed from the available published sources.

Books, Films, and the “Irishman” Legend

Greene’s life has been recounted in published crime histories and dramatized in Kill the Irishman (2011), whose catalog entry identifies actor Ray Stevenson as Greene and Vincent D’Onofrio as Nardi. A feature film is a narrative adaptation; it is not trial testimony, a substitute for FBI archives, or a definitive source for exact conversations. [18]

What makes the case attractive to storytellers is a combination of elements: a labor leader turned racketeer, a flamboyant public image, failed assassinations, an alliance with a Mafia dissenter, and a deadly confrontation with an established crime family. Those elements are well supported in outline, but dramatized dialogue, compressed chronology, and character motivations may depart from the record. [1][2][8][9]

This entry prioritizes events that can be located in court decisions, Senate testimony, archival exhibits, and dated local records. A reader may consult dramatizations for cultural reception, but not use them as primary evidence of who committed an uncharged murder.

Historical End Point and Present-Day Status

The historic Greene coalition lost its principal figure in October 1977, after Nardi’s death earlier that year. Some former associates participated in later illegal activity, according to the 1984 Senate statement, but the sources do not establish a continuing, centrally administered “Danny Greene Network” with a known present-day boss, membership census, or annual income. [3]

The organization is therefore classified as historical. This is not a claim that criminal activity ended in Cleveland in 1977 or 1982; rather, it reflects the absence of verified continuity between the specific Greene-era coalition and any present-day organization. [3][7]

Where later groups reused criminal contacts or reputational mythology, they should be studied in their own time and with their own evidence. The encyclopedia will not infer institutional survival merely because a name appears in a documentary, reunion interview, or local recollection.

Terminology

Criminal coalition
An association of people with overlapping illegal interests; a term that does not presume permanent rank or a fixed constitution.
Celtic Club
Reported designation for Greene’s close Cleveland circle; not proof of a complete membership list.
La Cosa Nostra (LCN)
Formal Italian-American Mafia organization; the Greene network was not itself an LCN family.
Cleveland crime family
The separate Cleveland LCN organization led by Scalish and, later, Licavoli during the events studied here.
Independent racketeer
Criminal operator not necessarily an inducted member or subordinate of a particular Mafia family.
Numbers racket
Illegal lottery or policy gambling market common in historical American organized crime.
Bookmaking
Taking or arranging wagers outside legal authorization.
Loansharking
Extortionate or illegal lending and collection practices; not every high-interest loan is the same legal offense.
Labor racketeering
Criminal exploitation or coercion relating to labor unions, employment, or labor markets.
Crew
Small set of associates coordinating conduct; not necessarily a formal Mafia administrative unit.
RICO
Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act, governing certain enterprise-connected patterns of criminal conduct.
Predicate act
A statutory offense relied on as part of a pattern of racketeering activity.
Cooperating witness
Participant or observer who provides evidence to authorities, often with incentives that require scrutiny.
Indictment
Formal criminal accusation, not a verdict of guilt.
Acquittal
Verdict or judgment that a specific criminal charge was not proved as required.
Reversal
Appellate action setting aside a lower-court judgment, which may or may not entail a new trial.

Research and Editorial Standards

Legal precision. This profile distinguishes the 1968 reversal of Greene’s union judgment, his later reported plea, the Frato acquittal, unresolved attribution regarding Birns, the differing state murder-trial results, and the 1982–1984 federal RICO convictions. It does not equate a charge with a guilty verdict or treat an acquittal in one case as resolving separate allegations. [1][2][4][7][10]

Organizational precision. Greene’s close Celtic Club associates, Nardi’s independently based alliance, and the Cleveland Mafia are not merged into one hierarchy. A person’s ethnicity, surname, neighborhood, or participation in ordinary labor organizations is not evidence of criminal membership. [2][3][9]

Evidence hierarchy. Appellate opinions carry greatest authority for the outcomes appealed; government statements and testimony indicate dated investigative conclusions; regional historical institutions document local events; period journalism records contemporary reporting and sometimes unresolved allegations; feature films reconstruct a narrative. No source alone provides an exhaustive, continuously verified gang membership register. [1][2][3][4][5][8][18]

Chronological limits. The article focuses on roughly 1957–1984 and considers later cultural representations without treating the network as currently active. The editorial cutoff is November 13, 2026. The sources used do not constitute an exhaustive collation of every local trial transcript or sealed investigative file.
