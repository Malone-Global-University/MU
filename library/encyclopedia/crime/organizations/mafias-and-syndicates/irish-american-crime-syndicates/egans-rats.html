Overview

The Egan’s Rats were a St. Louis criminal organization that developed from neighborhood street groups and political-enforcement networks around the city’s near North Side, especially the historically Irish-American district called Kerry Patch. The gang became associated with Thomas “Snake” Kinney, Thomas “Tom” Egan, William “Willie” Egan and William P. “Dint” or “Dinty” Colbeck. Its history spans the turn of the twentieth century through the early Prohibition era, when rivalry with Edward “Jelly Roll” Hogan’s network and a major federal mail-robbery prosecution undermined its leadership. [5] [7] [8]

Unlike an organization known chiefly from popular legend, Egan’s Rats left a substantial record in period newspapers, judicial proceedings and later historical research. A particularly important anchor is Colbeck v. United States, 10 F.2d 401 (7th Cir. 1925), which affirmed nine convictions and twenty-five-year sentences in the 1923 Staunton, Illinois mail-robbery case. The court’s opinion itself does not use the nickname “Egan’s Rats”; that group identification appears in contemporary coverage and a National Archives catalog description. This distinction prevents an archival label from being misreported as an express jury finding of gang membership. [1] [2] [3] [4]

The Rats combined three partly overlapping functions: neighborhood-based criminal cooperation, political intimidation and patronage, and proceeds from robbery and the liquor economy. These activities evolved across several leadership generations. A later claim that the group possessed hundreds of members should not be confused with a verified active criminal roster: electoral workers, associates, street fighters and participants in particular crimes may have been counted differently. [5] [6] [7]

This encyclopedia entry separates judicial findings, archival descriptions, contemporary press reports, participant testimony and later narrative histories. It gives special attention to the people harmed by gang violence, the limits of claims about individual responsibility, and the distinction between the collapse of the Rats as an identifiable command structure and the later careers of men associated with them. Editorial research cutoff: November 13, 2026. [1] [2] [5]

The gang’s boundaries are historically more fluid than the borders of a registered corporation or a later Mafia family. An associate may have been attached to a saloon, political club, particular robbery crew or faction without answering to a single centralized chain of command in every period. The leadership labels below distinguish established political office and criminal convictions from broader judgments made in period journalism and retrospective accounts. [2] [5] [7]

Kerry Patch and the Irish North Side

Kerry Patch was a working-class Irish-American district on and near the northern riverfront of St. Louis. Residents made lives around labor, parish networks, modest housing, saloons and wards in which party loyalties had immediate practical significance. The area provided the social setting from which many figures later connected with Egan’s Rats emerged. It was neither uniformly criminal nor a neighborhood controlled in every respect by the gang. [5] [6] [7]

Commercial shipping and rail corridors created legitimate work as well as opportunities for theft. Neighborhood familiarity also made it possible for men to organize around a social circle or drinking establishment before taking part in more enduring rackets. Such conditions help explain the formation of the Ashley Street grouping; they do not show that Irish ethnicity caused criminal behavior or that ordinary residents endorsed violent activity. [5] [7]

Geographical labels also changed as wards were redrawn and the urban fabric evolved. Accounts may describe the same general milieu as Kerry Patch, the river wards, the near North Side or the St. Louis Place vicinity. Exact addresses require independent period checking rather than treating a modern street map as an unchanged picture of the 1890s. [5] [8]

Ashley Street Gang: The Early Organizational Base

The organization’s antecedent is usually identified as the Ashley Street Gang. Local historical reporting links Tom Egan to this group before “Egan’s Rats” became the better-known collective name. The transition did not occur at a documented public founding ceremony; it was a gradual change in public identification as street associates accumulated political and economic influence. [5] [7]

Early activity described in the literature includes theft, burglary, assaults and strong-arm intervention in local disputes. These were the forms of crime often associated with urban street bands in the late nineteenth century, but the degree to which every offense attributed to the Ashley Street name involved later Rat leadership remains uncertain. Distinguishing a predecessor network from a legally defined continuing enterprise is an important editorial safeguard. [5] [7]

The transition also marks the difference between gang membership and patronage. A political faction might call on men from an informal group for an election, while a robbery crew might overlap with only part of that larger circle. A name could therefore refer to a core of repeat participants and to a looser set of dependents. Neither interpretation licenses an unsupported numerical roster. [5] [7]

Thomas “Snake” Kinney: Politics and Street Power

Thomas E. “Snake” Kinney was an early figure in the same neighborhood network as Tom Egan. Historical narratives portray him as adept at translating local street influence into electoral and political power. The Missouri Secretary of State’s historical legislator index independently confirms a Thomas E. Kinney in the state legislature during the 1904–1910 period, grounding the political-office portion of his biography more securely than the anecdotes about how votes were delivered. [5] [6] [7]

Kinney’s political career is central to interpreting the Rats: power did not flow only from illegal goods. A ward politician could help constituents find work and navigate city offices while simultaneously tolerating or employing unlawful election-time intimidation. Reports of coercion require source-specific attribution; his actual service in public office does not by itself prove the contents of each sensational newspaper accusation. [5] [6]

Later writers sometimes describe Kinney as a conventional “boss” with a disciplined criminal hierarchy. A better formulation is that Kinney occupied a pivotal position in a fluid political-criminal network, while Tom Egan became increasingly prominent in its criminal and neighborhood operations. Kinney’s commonly reported death in 1912 is not an organizational dissolution date: Egan’s associates remained active after his passing. [5] [7] [8]

Political Clubs, Ward Elections and Patronage

Early twentieth-century St. Louis ward politics created recurring opportunities for intimidation and rewards. Electoral contests took place in a setting where personal connections, saloons, neighborhood clubs and sometimes violence could influence turnouts and disputes at polling places. Historical studies describe Egan-associated men as partisan enforcers, with some entering nominally respectable political positions while continuing to move in underworld circles. [5] [7]

A political machine was not identical to a criminal gang. Its supporters included legitimate voters, employers, reformers and officeholders whose conduct cannot be inferred from proximity to a Rat-affiliated official. Conversely, officials who used armed supporters crossed an important institutional boundary: public authority was made vulnerable to private coercion. This is why the Rats belong to both the histories of organized crime and municipal government. [5] [6]

The Rat network’s electoral usefulness may have sheltered criminal activity, but individual bribery or corruption allegations must be traced to named actors and proceedings. The 1904 election and the 1909 courthouse violence are useful case studies because they show the contact between personal loyalty, officeholding, policing and the threat of retaliation. [5] [7]

Thomas “Tom” Egan and Consolidation of the Name

Tom Egan is the namesake most closely associated with the gang’s expansion. Unlike Kinney, whose office gave him a visible political identity, Egan appears in accounts as a street leader and saloon-centered organizer. The two roles overlapped, however; this was not a strict division between government and an entirely detached criminal market. [5] [7]

Local reporting presents Egan as influential in coordinating men who could commit violence for pay, engage in theft or function as enforcers during electoral disputes. Such accounts make him historically significant even where a case-by-case ledger of alleged crimes cannot be recovered. The contemporary habit of describing his whole sphere as an army should be treated as an estimate of reach, not a verified system of ranks and command. [5] [7]

Egan died in 1919 according to later accounts drawing on contemporary obituary coverage. His death left an organization already changing under the pressures of illegal-liquor markets and individual rivalries. The major succession should therefore be described as Tom Egan to his younger brother Willie Egan and then, after Willie’s murder, to Colbeck, without suggesting each transition was constitutionally or unanimously accepted. [7] [8]

Saloons as Social and Organizational Infrastructure

Saloons supplied meeting spaces, news, credit contacts and points of access to neighborhood political networks. In the Rats’ case, a named drinking establishment could become associated with an influential figure and appear repeatedly in press descriptions of meetings or shootings. This mattered because access to a room, a telephone, trusted employees and local patrons can make a gang recognizable even when its membership is fluid. [5] [7] [8]

Later narratives make Willie Egan’s Franklin Avenue saloon especially prominent, both as a social base and as the scene near which he was killed. Some accounts supply exact street numbers and reconstructions of gunmen’s movements, but those specifics are not all corroborated at the same level. The editorial priority is the documented public shooting and ensuing feud, not a dramatic floor plan reconstructed from later memories. [7] [8]

A saloon used by gangsters did not make all customers members. Nor did proprietorship or employment in a neighborhood establishment establish guilt for nearby crimes. The distinction is important when reporting the wider Irish-American social history of St. Louis. [5] [8]

Theft, Robbery and Local Strong-Arm Activity

The Rats were described as participating in thefts, railroad- or freight-related crimes, intimidation and hired violence. The types of offenses changed over time, with larger robbery operations becoming especially significant in the 1920s. This development was not a sudden transformation from an innocent club into a perfectly organized syndicate; it reflected expanding opportunities and the growing sophistication of particular criminal crews. [5] [7]

Accounts of gang-directed assaults against commercial rivals or private parties should be separated from adjudicated acts. Newspapers often presented any sudden attack in the gang’s neighborhood as a Rat enterprise. Contemporary attribution is evidence of reputation and police suspicion; a conviction and an appellate ruling are stronger evidence of the particular defendant’s culpability. [1] [5] [7]

The 1923 Staunton mail-robbery case is consequently treated in detail later in this article. It is a rare instance where a specific operation, defendants, trial and appellate outcome can be reconstructed from federal records, rather than inferred from a total dollar value in a popular history. [1] [2] [4]

Strikebreaking and Business-Related Violence

Regional histories describe Egan-associated men as available for strikebreaking and other paid intervention in labor and commercial disputes. The same enterprise could supply private coercion to different clients without maintaining a permanent contract with any one employer. Such practices blurred lines between a street-gang identity, hired muscle and an early form of labor-related racketeering. [5] [7]

These reports should not be generalized to all businesses or unions in St. Louis. A retrospective claim that men were hired by a named brewery, for example, is a lead for checking payrolls, injunctions, assault cases and contemporary press reports; it is not proof that every employee, owner or labor activist knew of or endorsed illegal conduct. [5] [7]

The useful historical lesson is institutional: a criminal group can extract income from the threat of violence even when there is no conventional product to sell. That economic logic helps explain why political protection could be valuable independently of alcohol or theft receipts. [5] [7]

Sam Young and the Violence of 1909

Samuel “Sam” Young, identified in local historical accounts as a constable and associate of Tom Egan, was killed during a confrontation involving Fred “Yellow Kid” Mohrle in April 1909. St. Louis Magazine’s reconstruction, drawing on period newspaper coverage, records competing versions of what happened at Mohrle’s location: witnesses disagreed about who threatened whom before shots were fired. [5]

Young’s death was not merely a private quarrel. It became part of an escalating sequence in which alleged factional loyalty, official authority and the fear of retaliation intersected. The identity of Mohrle as an alleged former Rat and suspected political defector is a claim made in the historical reconstruction; it should not be taken as a verified motive for every participant. [5]

This case is notable for the quality of the reported contemporary trail. Local journalists described the episode, subsequent legal proceedings and the murder that followed in the courthouse. Yet even abundant reporting can include incompatible accounts, especially when sources are frightened or directly involved. [5]

The Red Kane Courthouse Killing

On June 7, 1909, Thomas “Red” Kane shot Fred Mohrle in the St. Louis Four Courts Building. The killing was explicitly connected in contemporary reporting with the earlier death of Sam Young. A 1910 obituary quoted by a later local-history investigation identified Kane as a member of Egan’s Rats and stated that he had been sentenced for Mohrle’s killing. [5]

The case illustrates the vulnerability of courts to organized private violence. Kane reportedly gained access to the building in connection with a deputy constable position, a circumstance that made the courthouse shooting especially alarming. A judicial venue designed to resolve conflicts became the site of retaliation against an accused man before the criminal process ran its course. [5]

Kane died in December 1910. His killing of Mohrle is an established historical incident; explanations about who specifically ordered it vary in evidentiary strength. Statements by Mohrle’s relatives or other contemporaries are crucial evidence about fear and suspicion, but they should be attributed rather than converted into a court finding about an untried commander. [5]

Gang Reputation, Media and Headcounts

The reputation of Egan’s Rats was built partly in newspaper stories of election intimidation, bloody feuds and spectacular theft. Public fear itself could increase the effectiveness of threats: people who believe an organization will retaliate may comply without a new assault. This is a mechanism of coercive influence, not direct evidence that the gang controlled every resident or political office. [5] [7]

Modern historical summaries circulate estimates ranging from more than one hundred to several hundred “members.” Daniel Waugh’s publisher description, for example, refers to approximately four hundred. Such numbers may reflect total associates, occasional political supporters or different years rather than a single census of active criminals. No contemporaneous authenticated roster supporting a precise total has been reviewed for this entry. [5] [7]

This article therefore uses qualitative descriptions—an extensive organization with a core leadership and a wider circle—rather than a definitive headcount. A St. Louis gang’s notoriety should be measured by demonstrable historical effects, not by repeating the largest published number as a fact. [5] [7]

Changing Factions in the 1910s

The 1910s brought gradual change after the political rise of Kinney and the wider recognition of Tom Egan. Loyal relationships, rivalries and disputes among younger men complicated the idea of an unbroken, obedient hierarchy. Local reconstructions describe internal violence before national Prohibition began, reinforcing the point that a common gang name did not eliminate conflicts among its members. [7] [8]

Other accounts discuss an alleged 1916 quarrel involving “Skippy” Rohan and a shooting at Egan’s saloon. Such incidents help explain the instability of the organization, but the participants’ motives and precise assignments of responsibility require direct police and courtroom documentation for firm conclusions. [8]

The early Rats also belonged to a pre-Prohibition world in which the legal alcohol trade, commercial patronage and neighborhood politics created opportunities for coercive private services. It would be anachronistic to classify every early offense as a Prohibition liquor crime. [5] [7]

Tom Egan’s Death and Willie’s Succession

Thomas Egan’s death in 1919 marked the end of one leadership generation. Historical accounts place his younger brother William “Willie” Egan at the head of the organization in the ensuing years, with Colbeck prominent among trusted associates. Willie inherited an established reputation, but younger men and independent partners increasingly challenged the stability of old arrangements. [7] [8]

Kinney had already died several years earlier, so the political foundations that Tom Egan had relied upon were changing. In addition, the approaching national alcohol ban promised new illicit profits while creating disputes over shipments, distribution and enforcement. Retrospective stories frequently treat this sequence as an inevitable transition from politics to bootlegging; the actual organization remained involved in several types of criminal activity. [5] [7]

Willie’s importance lies partly in the contrast between inherited standing and the emerging autonomy of aggressive robbery crews. His eventual killing made that tension visible to the entire city. A stable succession model is not assumed simply because historians can name a person who was widely described as a leader. [7] [8]

Prohibition and the Liquor Economy

Federal Prohibition began in January 1920, multiplying potential returns from the manufacture, movement and sale of illegal alcohol. St. Louis’s position near the Mississippi River and the Illinois border also connected criminal entrepreneurs to different jurisdictions, transportation routes and retail markets. Histories of Egan’s Rats place the gang in this bootlegging economy alongside its established gambling and robbery interests. [7] [8]

Liquor revenue did not necessarily flow into one centralized bank. An operator could own a supply source, arrange transport, protect a distribution point or use violence against a rival shipment without sharing all profits equally. Reporting the network as one consolidated enterprise risks exaggerating the financial integration of individuals who alternated between cooperation and conflict. [7] [8]

The record supports a broad involvement in illegal liquor but not a certified annual revenue statement. Later anecdotes about extraordinarily large proceeds should be read against contemporaneous price data, accounting evidence and prosecutions before being converted into exact earnings. [5] [7]

Max Greenberg and the Egan–Hogan Break

Max Greenberg is central to the customary explanation of the feud between the Rats and Edward “Jelly Roll” Hogan’s rival group. Regional narratives describe a dispute over a liquor shipment, a shooting episode in 1921 and Greenberg’s shift toward the Hogan side. The accounts vary in some details and are not equivalent to a judicial finding against Willie Egan for an attempted murder. [7] [8] [9]

Personal betrayal carried special importance within a gang culture organized around trust in dangerous undertakings. Moving from one group to a rival could transfer knowledge of associates, supply routes and vulnerable places. It might also furnish an incentive for revenge and provide the newspaper a ready explanation for subsequent unexplained shootings. [7] [9]

The Greenberg episode should therefore be treated as a major reported cause of the Egan–Hogan conflict, not as proof that every later killing arose from one simple quarrel. Decisions about responsibility for specific attacks remained matters for investigators and courts. [7] [8]

October 31, 1921: The Killing of Willie Egan

On October 31, 1921, Willie Egan was shot outside or near his saloon on Franklin Avenue in St. Louis and died of his wounds. Contemporary accounts summarized by regional historian Ken Zimmerman identify the date, the saloon setting and a subsequent police investigation in which no homicide conviction resolved who fired the fatal shots. [8]

Later narratives have named particular Hogan associates as shooters and placed the attack within the dispute involving Max Greenberg. Such accusations mattered to the feud because they could motivate retaliation, whether or not they were proved. The historically responsible formulation is that Egan was murdered and the rival organization was suspected or blamed by participants—not that a named individual was judicially found guilty of the killing. [7] [8] [9]

The death also demonstrates the limits of rank. A figure described as a leader could be vulnerable in a familiar public setting; the organization’s reputation did not prevent attack. Colbeck subsequently became the most visible leader in accounts of the surviving Rats. [7] [8]

William “Dint” Colbeck: The Post-Egan Leader

William P. “Dint” or “Dinty” Colbeck emerged as the leading Rat after Willie Egan’s death. An archivist’s description of the Staunton case later identified him as leader of Egan’s Rats, while contemporary press accounts associated him with the command of a powerful robbery-and-bootlegging network. The court’s own opinion, however, did not make a separate explicit finding that he held the post of gang boss. [1] [2] [3]

Colbeck was a veteran of the First World War according to the National Archives description, and his public profile also touched ward politics. These experiences gave him different types of social capital. They do not establish that an army-derived hierarchy existed inside the Rats, or that the military service of any other codefendant can be inferred from Colbeck’s biography. [2] [3]

His federal prosecution is unusually well documented. The official records allow a firm account of the robbery indictment, trial and appeal, and prison identification materials survive. They are less informative about a full citywide criminal command structure, so the formal role assigned to Colbeck in retrospective narrative remains properly distinguished from the conviction itself. [1] [2] [3]

Edward “Jelly Roll” Hogan and the Rival Organization

Edward “Jelly Roll” Hogan led the network generally described as the Rats’ principal rival. The Hogan side combined its own political contacts and criminal associates, and contemporary accounts describe a battle over influence during the years after Willie Egan’s death. The two organizations were not branches of a single Irish-American Mafia, and neither should be assumed to have controlled every illegal activity in St. Louis. [7] [9]

The existence of a rival with overlapping ethnic, political and economic roots is revealing. Shared background did not prevent violent competition; loyalties shifted as individual operators sought protection or opportunity. Conflict could involve a disputed killing, liquor distribution or the desire to remove a competitor rather than one stable frontier between two neighborhoods. [7] [9]

The labels “Hogan Gang” and “Egan’s Rats” help identify major sides in the feud, but wartime alliances and freelance gunmen were not always perfectly aligned with those labels. A person mentioned in a newspaper as a Hogan associate should not automatically be placed in a permanent gang roster. [2] [7]

The Egan–Hogan War, 1921–1923

The Egan–Hogan feud brought shootings, vehicle attacks and retaliation to public St. Louis streets. Accounts place its most intense phase after Willie Egan’s Halloween 1921 death and through 1923. Local historians cite incidents in which spectators, passengers and other people outside the central dispute were placed in danger, showing how private criminal contests could become a broader urban security problem. [7] [9]

The conflict was not necessarily fought as a continuous series of operations ordered by two commanders. Different groups of associates may have acted for personal motives, anticipated orders or reprisals. A single casualty total would require systematic reconciliation of homicide files, contemporary reports, victim identities and charging outcomes. This article does not adopt an unverified exact death count. [7] [9]

Retaliation also had strategic costs: the more visible the violence, the more pressure authorities and citizens placed on police and prosecutors. Those circumstances help explain why the 1924 federal sentences had consequences beyond one robbery—yet the gang war alone did not generate the federal convictions. [4] [7] [9]

Negotiations, Accommodation and the End of Open Warfare

Secondary accounts describe efforts to bring the Egan–Hogan conflict to an end in 1923. Some refer to a negotiated settlement or informal peace. Such reports are relevant to understanding the decline of highly visible street fighting, but the existence of a fully documented written treaty, its supposed signatories and its enforcement terms have not been independently established for this article. [7] [9]

A fall in visible attacks may mean several things: mutual exhaustion, police pressure, new revenue opportunities, displacement of individual participants, or a temporary balance of power. It need not mean the underlying organizations were dissolved. Colbeck’s faction remained active enough to face a major federal trial based on a separate robbery from May 1923. [1] [4] [7]

The history is better understood as overlapping transitions: the end or moderation of the most notorious feud, the continued development of robbery operations, and the later criminal convictions that sharply weakened surviving leadership. [1] [7]

Robbery Crews and the Shift to Higher-Value Targets

Robbery became increasingly important in the remembered career of Egan’s Rats. Retrospective histories describe bank, payroll and transport-related robberies and claim substantial aggregate proceeds. Those figures vary and frequently combine multiple people or events without attaching each sum to a judicially proven Rat operation. [7]

A large heist required cooperation among planners, transport personnel, armed offenders and people able to dispose of proceeds. Yet the existence of a robbery crew does not by itself prove that every person contributed to a durable organization or that proceeds were distributed under a permanent headquarters. In court, participation must be proved individually according to the applicable legal standard. [1] [2]

The Staunton case provides a comparatively precise example. Prosecutors charged both participants at the scene and others involved in preparing the crime. The appellate court discussed how advance assistance could support conviction even where the defendant was not physically present at the moment of the robbery. [1]

May 26, 1923: The Staunton Mail Robbery

On May 26, 1923, armed men robbed custodians of United States mail at Staunton, Macoupin County, Illinois. The record involves a pouch containing registered mail, including substantial remittances associated with the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis. The exact amounts in particular registered packages belong to the indictment, which is a charging instrument rather than proof that all alleged proceeds were recovered or spent. [2] [4]

The federal proceeding is important because it provides identifiable victims who had lawful custody of the mails, a specific date and location, and defendants charged under federal criminal statutes. A regional historical museum summarizes the case as a nine-person conviction involving the kidnapping or detention of mail custodians; the court opinion, however, focuses on the charged mail robbery and associated statutory counts rather than a separate definitive kidnapping conviction. [1] [4]

National Archives case-file descriptions call the men Egan’s Rats and identify Colbeck as a leader. That statement is an archivist’s summary, not text appearing in the indictment’s operative counts or the Seventh Circuit’s opinion. The evidence supports the affiliation in its appropriate source category without misrepresenting what the judges expressly held. [1] [2] [3]

September 1924 Federal Indictment

The indictment was returned on September 3, 1924. The federal appellate opinion identifies four counts: robbery of persons lawfully in charge of the mails; robbery placing custodians’ lives in jeopardy through deadly weapons; receipt or concealment of stolen mail; and knowing possession of stolen mail. Those statutory distinctions matter when explaining why some defendants were convicted on fewer counts than others. [1]

The associated archive notes identify fourteen people named in the indictment, while the eventual appellate decision concerns nine men whose convictions were before the court. An indictment list is therefore not equivalent to a list of people all convicted, and a defendant named in the proceeding should not automatically be described as having been sentenced without checking the disposition. [1] [2]

The federal jurisdiction arose from crimes involving the United States mails. It did not require a court to recognize a citywide criminal “family” with formal ranks, nor did the court need to prove an organization called Egan’s Rats as a standalone entity. This is a legal and historical distinction essential to an accurate encyclopedia article. [1] [2]

The 1924 Trial and Sentences

Trial opened on November 10, 1924. The appellate opinion records that eight of the eventual appellants were convicted on all four counts, while Gustav B. Dietmeyer was convicted on the two robbery counts. Each of the nine defendants whose convictions were reviewed received a twenty-five-year prison sentence. A museum biography of Judge Louis FitzHenry independently discusses the sentencing and its prominence in the region. [1] [4]

The case illustrates the unusually serious federal consequences that awaited robbery crews once criminal conduct crossed into protected postal operations. Punishment did not depend on the romantic public history of the Rats. It depended on the charges, evidence admitted at trial, verdicts and applicable sentencing rules. [1]

Popular accounts sometimes say that “the entire gang” was sent away. That is imprecise. Nine appealed sentences are legally documented; they cannot be equated with every person ever described as a Rat or with all later violent figures in St. Louis. [1] [2] [4]

Raymond Renard: Cooperation and Credibility

Raymond (“Ray”) Renard appears in the federal appellate opinion as a government witness who admitted participation in the Staunton offense and had already been convicted of another crime. The defense challenged his credibility by presenting witnesses who said his reputation for truthfulness was poor. The appellate court nevertheless concluded that the challenged treatment of credibility evidence had not substantially prejudiced the convicted defendants. [1] [10]

Renard’s testimony illustrates a basic evidentiary problem in organized-crime history. Participants may know facts unavailable to outsiders, but they may also have strong motives to minimize their own role, shift blame or gain favorable treatment. A verdict following contested testimony is not the same as a historical license to adopt every separate story the witness later told about unrelated acts. [1] [10]

The scholar Frederic Thrasher referred to a 1925 series by Renard, then a former gang participant, as a source on the Rats’ conflict. The series is important for reconstructing how participants described their organization, but its claims require comparison with police reports, newspapers and court files. This article does not convert all of Renard’s narrated murders into adjudicated facts. [1] [7] [10]

December 8, 1925: Appellate Affirmance

The United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit decided Colbeck v. United States, 10 F.2d 401, on December 8, 1925. The court affirmed the nine convictions and sentences. Rehearing was denied on January 13, 1926. The opinion is the most important judicial document available for this organizational profile. [1]

The court addressed objections to the indictment, alibi instructions, restrictions on a proposed question about Renard’s credibility and the reasonable-doubt instruction. It explained that advance assistance could constitute participation in the robbery even where an offender was absent from the scene. This reasoning is a matter of legal record and should not be replaced by a compressed narrative that all nine were physically present in Staunton. [1]

The judgment affirmed a defined federal case, not a comprehensive prosecution of every suspected Egan’s Rats offense. It established criminal liability for the counts proved against the defendants while leaving unresolved the identity of several homicide perpetrators and the formal structure of the gang as a whole. [1]

Leavenworth, Prison Records and the Collapse of Leadership

Prison records provide a second unusually concrete layer of evidence. The archival compilation identifies Leavenworth identification photographs for several convicted defendants, including Colbeck, and an Alcatraz notebook page for Frank Epplesheimer describing his codefendants. The photographs are valuable identification records; they do not independently prove every nickname, earlier offense or gang relationship attached to a person by later writers. [2] [3]

Removing key planners and associates through long sentences dramatically reduced the operational capacity of Colbeck’s group. Local histories describe the 1924 convictions as a turning point or breakup of the Egan organization. That formulation is reasonable when applied to the leadership network, but less so if read as proof that every subordinate instantly stopped committing crimes. [4] [7]

Later activity by individual former members may belong to the history of other criminal groups. The distinction between personnel migration and the continuing existence of Egan’s Rats as one commanded institution prevents an artificial organizational lifespan stretching into unrelated decades. [2] [7]

Former Associates and Out-of-Town Careers

Narrative histories report that some men once associated with the Rats moved into other Midwestern underworld environments, including illegal-liquor, robbery or gambling groups. Those personal movements are plausible in an era of extensive interstate travel and criminal cooperation, but they should not be described as formally authorized branch operations of a surviving St. Louis headquarters without documentary support. [5] [7]

Some later stories connect former Egan associates to notorious crimes in Detroit and Chicago, including the St. Valentine’s Day Massacre. An overlap of names and remembered criminal affiliations may warrant biographical cross-reference; it does not itself establish a direct Rat order, a gang-wide conspiracy or a verified successor structure. [5] [7]

MGU will treat individual claims as a separate research question. Where there is an independently documented case involving a person, that case can be linked after the relevant biography or organization profile has been published. No speculative membership chain is generated from geography alone. [2] [5]

Colbeck’s 1943 Death and the Limits of Continuity

A later federal Senate committee staff exhibit, identified in the archival compilation, states that William P. Colbeck was killed in St. Louis on February 17, 1943, and describes the homicide as unsolved. Contemporary or retrospective journalism likewise reports that Colbeck was shot in an automobile. The Senate exhibit is a historical government source, but the compilation notes that it does not supply an underlying death file or homicide investigation. [2] [3] [11]

Colbeck’s violent death long after the mail-robbery convictions should not be used to claim that Egan’s Rats remained a fully operational syndicate through 1943. He was a former gang leader with a subsequent individual career and reputation. Both facts can be true without an unbroken institutional chain. [2] [3]

Stories that he was killed because he attempted to reclaim control of the local underworld are retrospective interpretations and are not presented as a proven criminal motive. The source-supported conclusion is narrower: former leader Colbeck was killed, and no conviction establishing responsibility is identified in the records reviewed. [3] [11]

Leadership, Membership and Internal Organization

The Rat network appears to have possessed recognized leaders, recurring associates and a shifting periphery. Its organizational form was not that of the later Italian-American Mafia’s formalized boss–underboss–caporegime hierarchy. Political clubs, neighborhood ties and robbery crews helped create overlapping systems of loyalty. A modern organizational chart that assigns everyone to one permanent rank would be largely speculative. [5] [7]

Tom Egan, Kinney, Willie Egan and Colbeck occupy important positions in narrative and documentary histories, but the strength of the leadership evidence varies. Kinney’s elected office is independently certified; Willie Egan’s death is historically attested; Colbeck’s conviction is an adjudicated fact; exact orders in particular uncharged homicides are often not. [1] [5] [6] [8]

Caution also applies to headcount. Publisher descriptions and local accounts refer to hundreds of participants, but the source base reviewed here does not provide a complete contemporaneous roster with dates of activity and membership criteria. The organization is best described as substantial and politically connected, with the exact size unresolved. [5] [7]

Political Machine versus Criminal Syndicate

The history of the Rats warns against equating a party machine with a gang, or separating them so sharply that corrupt dependence becomes invisible. A political club might perform ordinary community functions while some participants resorted to intimidation; a criminal associate could hold public office without every voter supporting his conduct. The group’s historical importance lies in the particular overlap, not in collective guilt. [5] [6] [7]

One form of exchange gave violent associates access to information, patronage and symbolic legitimacy. Another gave political factions the threat of force in contested environments. Commercial clients could then seek similar coercive services. These relationships made reputation valuable but also encouraged factional violence and resistance from victims and reformers. [5] [7]

The real-world institutional risk is the privatization of public enforcement: officeholding and police proximity become resources in a private contest. Investigating that relationship requires records of appointments, elections, prosecutions and commercial disputes rather than assigning omnipotent control to one reputed boss. [5] [6]

Economic Model and Revenue Uncertainty

The Rats’ revenue cannot be reduced to one product. The group’s historical career encompassed robbery and theft, income from violent services, and participation in the illicit liquor market. Political influence itself could be valuable without appearing as ordinary cash receipts. These streams likely varied across decades and leaders, so a single “annual revenue” figure would be an invented precision. [5] [7]

One large robbery could dwarf a year of petty theft for a subset of participants. Yet an advertised haul is not equivalent to net distributable income. Intermediaries, failed recoveries, costs, betrayals and prosecutions all changed what offenders could retain. Reporting aggregate sensational figures without accounting records would overstate what is known. [1] [2] [7]

The most defensible quantitative details concern the defined federal case: dates, conviction counts and twenty-five-year sentences. Even the specific registered-mail sums alleged in the indictment are charge details rather than an audited profit statement for the organization. [1] [2]

Irish-American Identity without Collective Attribution

Egan’s Rats emerged from an Irish-American social milieu and belongs in the MGU Irish-American Crime Syndicates collection for historical discoverability. That categorization does not mean every member had the same ancestry or that the group represented Irish-Americans as a whole. Neighborhood solidarity, family ties and party affiliation are distinct from criminal agreement. [5] [7]

Kerry Patch also contained workers, families and institutions whose histories should not be swallowed by a story of gang violence. The gang’s political coercion and predatory activity damaged people within its own social setting as well as others. Describing those harms accurately is part of preventing romanticized criminal folklore from substituting for community history. [5]

The label is editorial rather than a legally recognized ethnicity-based syndicate jurisdiction. It implies no single national Irish Mafia, and it establishes no command relationship with unrelated New York, Boston, Chicago or Philadelphia groups covered in the same directory. [2] [7]

Victims, Public Order and Civic Consequences

The violence surrounding Egan’s Rats affected more than the men directly engaged in gang rivalries. Public shootings endangered passersby, intimidation could interfere with voting, and violence around trials undermined confidence that legal disputes would be settled by law. The Young–Mohrle–Kane sequence starkly demonstrates how criminal conflict could intrude on government institutions. [5] [9]

The Staunton robbery targeted persons entrusted with federal mail, making the offense an attack on a public service as well as on property. The broader enforcement response shows how federal jurisdiction sometimes reached conspiracies that local politics and police resources struggled to contain. [1] [4]

Public accounts often memorialize gangsters’ aliases while leaving custodians, threatened residents and witnesses in the background. The purpose of this article is therefore analytical rather than celebratory: it places offenders’ actions in their historical context while retaining the difference between accusation, conviction and hearsay. [1] [5]

Retrospective Histories and the Problem of Legend

Daniel Waugh’s book Egan’s Rats (2007) is the best-known modern narrative devoted to the group, and local journalism has drawn extensively on his research. These works supply important leads, particularly for criminal culture, neighborhood politics and the changing rivalry with the Hogans. The descriptions consulted for this edition are not a complete page-by-page collation of the published book. [5] [7]

The popular gang-history genre favors clear leaders, dramatic betrayals, multi-decade continuity and exact body counts. Those narrative choices can be illuminating or misleading. A court opinion may establish a robbery conviction yet say nothing about the alleged killer in another shooting; a newspaper report can document the existence of an accusation yet not establish its truth. [1] [2] [7]

For publication, this entry gives priority to direct legal documents for legally adjudicated outcomes, official records for officeholding, and attributed contemporary or scholarly reporting for the larger criminal history. It does not borrow entire passages or reproduce colorful descriptions without qualification. [1] [5] [6]

Decline and Historical Significance

The term “Egan’s Rats” continued to be used in retrospective histories long after the most important federal trial. The 1924 sentences and 1925 affirmance gravely weakened the Colbeck organization; no demonstrated uninterrupted central leadership from then through the mid-twentieth century is adopted here. The fate of former associates belongs in their individual biographies or the histories of later organizations. [1] [2] [4]

The group’s historical significance is threefold. First, it illustrates how nineteenth-century neighborhood street associations could become organized criminal networks. Second, it demonstrates a particularly visible combination of ward politics, patronage and violent private coercion. Third, the Staunton case provides a detailed record of federal prosecution that makes it possible to distinguish legend from adjudication. [1] [5] [7]

For the MGU Crime Encyclopedia, the Rats offer a valuable comparison with the political gangs of early New York, the Chicago Prohibition networks, and later Boston and New York racketeering groups. Such comparison is analytical, not evidence of one inherited organizational chain. [5] [7]

Terminology and Historical Concepts

Kerry Patch

Historic Irish-American neighborhood area of St. Louis associated with the gang’s social origins.

Ashley Street Gang

Predecessor street association linked to the later Egan’s Rats name.

Political machine

Network of electoral organization, personal patronage and officeholding; its lawful activity must be distinguished from illicit enforcement.

Ward politics

Local electoral contests and municipal representation at the city-district level.

Saloon politics

Political organizing, informal commerce and patronage centered around drinking establishments.

Strikebreaking

Employment of personnel against a labor stoppage; illegal assault or intimidation requires separate evidence.

Prohibition

The national constitutional and federal statutory regime restricting alcohol manufacture, sale and transportation beginning in 1920.

Bootlegging

Illegal production, transport or sale of alcoholic beverages, particularly under Prohibition.

Mail robbery

Federal offense involving taking mail from lawful custodians; central to the 1923 Staunton case.

Indictment

Formal criminal charging document; not a finding of guilt.

Conviction

Judicial determination of guilt following proceedings such as a jury verdict or plea.

Appellate affirmance

Appeals court decision leaving an appealed judgment in place.

Accomplice testimony

Testimony of a person involved in the offense, potentially valuable but requiring scrutiny for incentives and credibility.

Organizational continuity

Demonstrated survival of a particular coordinated group over time, not merely the later activity of its former members.

Retrospective attribution

Claim made after the event, assessed separately from surviving period records.

Evidence hierarchy

Distinction between judicial findings, official descriptions, contemporary reports and later narrative interpretations.

Research and Editorial Standards

Source priority. The 1925 Seventh Circuit opinion is used for statutory charges, verdict scope, trial dates, credibility rulings and affirmance. The archival compilation is used to identify National Archives case files, prison photographs and contemporary newspapers. A catalog description is not presented as the text of an indictment, and an indictment is not presented as a conviction. [1] [2] [3]

Limits on source access. The court opinion was reviewed in accessible full-text form. The National Archives material was evaluated through its detailed public finding aids and records compilation rather than a full independent page-by-page inspection of every original case file, transcript, photograph and press clipping. Daniel Waugh’s published monograph was identified through publisher and library records; a complete page-specific collation of the book was not conducted. [1] [2] [7]

Names and numbers. Variants including Dint/Dinty, Epplesheimer/Eppelsheimer, and Daugherty/Dougherty are not silently normalized into assertions of separate persons. Disputed headcounts, total proceeds, exact casualty totals and alleged assignments of individual murders are qualified rather than supplied as false precision. [1] [2] [7]

Community context. Irish-American identity is an editorial classification grounded in the gang’s historical origins, not a generalization about St. Louis residents. Political party affiliations, family relationships and proximity to defendants do not imply individual criminal guilt. [5] [6]

Publication scope. This is a source-attributed historical encyclopedia synthesis, not an exhaustive judicial reconstruction or facsimile-collated archival edition. Further archival priorities include the complete 1924 trial transcript, original 1909 courtroom coverage, death records for Willie Egan and Colbeck, and municipal election and constable appointment registers. [1] [2] [5]
