Overview

The Hogan Gang was a St. Louis criminal-political network associated with Edward J. “Jelly Roll” Hogan Jr., active most visibly during the era of national alcohol prohibition. Its best-documented public confrontation was with Egan’s Rats, the rival network associated with William “Willie” Egan and later William “Dint” Colbeck. Violence between the organizations affected streets, homes, businesses, lawyers, gang participants and bystanders during the early 1920s. A March 7, 1923 wire-service report names the followers of Hogan and Egan’s Rats as opposing factions and records police frustration over the killings. [1] [2] [5]

The Hogan organization cannot be described adequately as only a bootlegging company or only a street gang. The historical record places its principal figure within electoral politics, reported liquor enforcement and commercial activity, and an underworld network involved in violent competition. Missouri’s official historical legislator index independently establishes Hogan’s election to the House and Senate. It does not, by itself, establish the legality or criminality of every transaction attributed to his circle. [3] [4] [5]

The most important surviving contemporary summary of the feud is W. F. Sullivan’s March 1923 reporting; a second is Frederic M. Thrasher’s discussion of the “Jellyrolls” in The Gang (1927). Thrasher relied heavily on Raymond Renard’s 1925 serialized account, and his printed text itself contains an unusual correction from a St. Louis respondent questioning the authorities’ role in the alleged peace agreement. Both items are historical evidence, but neither is a substitute for complete trial files or independently established responsibility for particular murders. [1] [2]

This entry distinguishes a named rival gang from an assumed corporation with stable headquarters, officers and branches. It avoids treating retrospective crime stories as conviction records, treats the Irish-American heading as a historical cataloging choice rather than a claim of exclusive ancestry, and differentiates the organization’s early-1920s history from Hogan’s much longer public career. Research cutoff: November 13, 2026. [3] [5] [6]

St. Louis: Neighborhoods, Politics, and the Liquor Economy

Early twentieth-century St. Louis combined industrial employment, riverfront movement, breweries, saloons, ward politics and ethnically varied working-class districts. Legal labor and political organization existed alongside illegal gambling, theft and violence. A historical account of criminal groups must not imply that a district’s Irish, Italian, Jewish or other residents generally participated in those organizations. [5] [8]

Competition for customers, deliveries, retail outlets and police tolerance intensified when prohibition restricted legal alcohol sales. Informal relationships could join saloon keepers, commercial intermediaries, people holding public positions and armed associates without creating a nationwide Mafia-style command structure. Hogan’s alleged beverage-inspector role made conflict-of-interest questions particularly important, but a claim of illicit protection must be distinguished from evidence that he held a public job. [5] [6]

Descriptions of the Hogan outfit refer to streets around Cass Avenue and Jefferson Avenue; a retrospective account gives the family home as 3035 Cass Avenue. These are historically useful location leads, not proof that every building was owned or permanently staffed by a formal criminal headquarters. The exact addresses and property uses would warrant follow-up in city directories, land records and contemporaneous police files. [5] [6]

Formation and Early Development

The dedicated collection gives the Hogan Gang a broad conventional span of the 1910s–1920s. A wire reporter in March 1923 described antagonism extending back “ten or more years,” evidence that the public story reached into an earlier era. That statement is a witness to how the dispute was described at the time, not a certified 1913 date of corporate formation or a proof of unbroken armed warfare every year. [1]

Later local histories place Hogan among ward political and saloon figures before the liquor ban. They describe a grouping that expanded from local influence into a recognizable rival organization. Sources do not provide a founding charter, a complete set of early associates or an audited accounting of gang proceeds. The appropriate category is a historically named network with a reported leader and recurring participants. [5] [6]

A plausible evolutionary pattern includes overlapping political workers, drinking-establishment personnel and criminal associates whose relationships grew more durable once liquor profits increased. That is an interpretation consistent with the setting, not an established gang constitution. The historical article therefore avoids unsupported claims about “captains,” regular dues, membership rituals and a fixed number of soldiers. [1] [2] [5]

Edward J. “Jelly Roll” Hogan Jr.: Personal and Public Identity

Edward John Hogan Jr. was born in St. Louis in 1885 and died in 1963, according to the Political Graveyard’s compiled political biography. Sources commonly associate him with saloon-related work, Democratic politics and union activity. His nickname appears both in contemporary news and in later histories, but it should not be treated as an official office or title. [1] [4] [5]

Hogan’s public and criminal associations create a historiographical difficulty: he was a real elected official, and newspapers called a rival organization his “followers.” Neither fact necessarily proves that all political supporters committed crimes or that every criminal linked to his surname was personally directed in every act. The entry gives Hogan a central place in the network while reserving judicial conclusions for legally established proceedings. [1] [3] [4]

The Missouri Secretary of State’s historical index names “Hogan, Edward J., Jr.” in the House for elections 1916 and 1934–1940, and in the Senate under 1944–1956 election entries. Other biographical sources list service years somewhat differently, partly because election-year entries and terms of service are not identical. Rather than flattening those formats into a misleading continuous career, the article identifies the underlying index convention. [3] [4]

Missouri House and Senate: An Independently Verified Career

The strongest official record available for this profile is the Missouri Secretary of State’s Historical Missouri State Legislators index. It provides two House groupings and Senate election periods for Hogan; the public record is much more secure than many anecdotal claims concerning his underworld affairs. The official index establishes public office, not criminal intent, illicit payments, or personal involvement in the specific Egan–Hogan shootings. [3]

A separate political-biography compilation identifies his work as an electrician and business representative for a bottlers’ union. Those occupations help explain his connection to the beverage economy, but no inference of a criminal labor monopoly follows from union membership or labor representation alone. The union, its members and ordinary beverage workers should not be portrayed as a criminal enterprise on that basis. [4]

Hogan’s later career also cautions against a common narrative shortcut: the fact that he remained a public figure after Prohibition does not establish that a wartime street-gang organization survived into the postwar Senate. The directory’s organization profile and any separate biographical entry serve different purposes. [3] [4] [5]

Beverage Inspection and the Problem of Conflicted Authority

Local historical accounts report that Hogan served as a deputy inspector in Missouri’s State Beverage Department around 1920. The appointment appears in later St. Louis historical writing and in summaries of the gang. Because the underlying appointment order, payroll and inspection docket have not been directly collated here, its exact start date and scope are reported as attributed history, not as independently reconstructed administrative fact. [5] [6]

If a person connected to illicit liquor sales also exercises regulatory authority, the potential for influence and compromised enforcement is evident. Potential conflict, however, is not a criminal conviction. The encyclopedia therefore differentiates public employment, allegations of selective enforcement, and proof of a particular bribe. It does not invent a precise payoff rate or claim that every brewer, inspector or saloon was controlled by Hogan. [5] [6]

For future research, Missouri departmental rosters, state audits, legislative proceedings and contemporary newspaper notices could test the details of the inspector story. This matters because colorful local history often merges official career facts with scandal narratives that require a different evidentiary standard. [3] [5]

Prohibition Bootlegging and Distribution

The national prohibition of alcoholic beverages created lucrative illegal supply and distribution channels. Hogan’s organization is regularly described as handling beer and liquor in and around St. Louis. Reports associate the group with saloon-based commerce and attempts to secure or protect retail distribution. These descriptions support a general bootlegging classification without establishing a complete inventory of suppliers, customers, market share or annual gross proceeds. [5] [6] [7]

Illegal liquor networks could depend on the same transportation systems used by legitimate businesses—roads, rail, warehousing and small retail outlets. A gang’s competitive advantage could come from supply access, credit arrangements, intimidation or political influence. The record reviewed for this article does not show Hogan owning every stage of production or commanding all of the city’s alcoholic beverage trade. [1] [5]

Secondary authors sometimes characterize the group as enormously wealthy after the 1924 convictions crippled the Rats. The extent of such gains is not supported here by tax files, financial statements or a linked series of judicial forfeitures. A better formulation is that the reduction of Egan competition may have strengthened the surviving network, while its exact profits and territorial gains remain unverified. [5] [6] [7]

Organizational Structure and Associates

The contemporary reporter’s term “followers of Hogan” and Thrasher’s “Jellyrolls” suggest a publicly recognized circle rather than a later Mafia family with fixed commissions. The names do not establish an official hierarchy of boss, underboss, consigliere and soldiers. The group likely encompassed associates with different functions, some involved in commercial operations and others named in shootings or legal controversies. [1] [2]

Retrospective registers name James Hogan, Luke Kennedy, John Doyle, Abe Goldfeder, Patrick Scanlon, John Connell, Humbert Costello, Charles Mercurio and other figures. These names should be interpreted individually. A person may be reported as an associate by a particular author without having a documented membership card, command appointment or conviction for joining a conspiracy. [5] [6] [7]

The presence of both Irish-surnamed and Italian- or Jewish-associated individuals demonstrates the inadequacy of defining membership by ethnic ancestry alone. This collection groups the organization within Irish-American crime history because of its political leadership and historical categorization. It does not exclude other participants or treat their ethnic identities as causes of crime. [5] [7]

Ward Politics, Patronage and Alleged Intimidation

The meeting of machine politics and criminal enforcement is a central theme of St. Louis gangster histories. Writers describe Hogan’s organization as useful to electoral campaigns or party patrons, much as rival Egan associates moved between local politics and street activity. Such reports are relevant to understanding the group’s influence but require care: holding office or supporting a political candidate does not prove vote fraud or organized violence. [5] [6] [8]

Specific claims about repeat voting, intimidation of opponents and paid political protection should be tied to identifiable election complaints, newspaper allegations or judicial findings. The materials reviewed here substantiate Hogan’s political career and the public identification of his criminal associates, but they do not contain a complete set of adjudications for each alleged election offense. [1] [3] [4]

The political dimension also complicates simple claims that police could end the rivalry by arresting a few known gang figures. Where suspected gang members enjoyed personal access to officials and local constituencies, law enforcement operated within a contested environment. This does not imply that every officer was corrupt or that public institutions uniformly supported either faction. [1] [2] [5]

Irish-American Association and Multiethnic Membership

Both Hogan’s organization and Egan’s Rats are commonly placed in histories of Irish-American crime in St. Louis. The label reflects networks and leadership emerging around Irish-associated neighborhoods and political structures. It is not an assertion that Irish ethnicity determined participation or that the groups represented Irish residents at large. [5] [6]

Local writers describe a range of surnames and backgrounds among Hogan associates. Such diversity is consistent with a larger city in which neighborhood loyalties, commercial relationships and shared criminal opportunities crossed ethnic lines. The specific ancestry of an individual named in a retrospective list should not be guessed from surname alone. [5] [7]

The encyclopedia treats “Irish-American Crime Syndicates” as an editorial navigation category. Individual organizations can be historically comparable without having a common national charter or any documented command relationship with criminal groups in New York, Chicago or Boston. [5] [7]

Egan’s Rats: The Principal Rival

Egan’s Rats emerged from St. Louis neighborhood and political networks connected with the Kinney and Egan figures. By the early 1920s the group was associated with illegal liquor, robbery and armed enforcement, as well as William “Willie” Egan’s leadership and William “Dint” Colbeck’s later command. Its rivalry with the Hogans was locally conspicuous enough to become a national wire-service story. [1] [2] [8]

These were distinct organizations, notwithstanding overlapping neighborhoods and the opportunity for defections or negotiated truces. A member of one faction could later cooperate with another, and a public nickname might cover more than one level of association. None of this means the organizations were formal subsidiaries of a unified Irish Mafia or that one belonged to the other. [1] [2] [5]

After Egan was killed on October 31, 1921, accounts describe Colbeck intensifying retaliation against people blamed for the murder. The word “blamed” matters: a faction’s identification of adversaries is evidence of the feud’s logic, not in itself a criminal verdict against the named individuals. [1] [6]

Max Greenberg and the Reported Defection

Max Greenberg appears repeatedly in the history of the feud. Later accounts describe a dispute over a liquor shipment, an attempt to kill him, and a subsequent alignment with Hogan’s faction. The March 1923 contemporary wire report confirms at least the core narrative elements that Greenberg had survived an earlier attack and was tied by family relationship to attorney Jacob Mackler. [1] [5] [6]

The more dramatic details—that one failed delivery precipitated the war, that Greenberg paid a fixed amount to each killer, and that he personally planned Willie Egan’s death—derive principally from retrospective or participant-based narratives. No independent judgment establishing that entire chain has been identified for this entry. The article thus characterizes the payments and plot attribution as reported claims, not settled judicial facts. [5] [6]

A defection narrative is important because it shows how rival criminal networks were not rigid ethnic armies with impermeable boundaries. Commercial trust, personal survival and grievances might outweigh previous loyalties. That general interpretation does not require assigning Greenberg a formal office in the Hogan organization. [1] [7]

October 31, 1921: The Killing of Willie Egan

On October 31, 1921, William “Willie” Egan was fatally shot outside his saloon near Fourteenth Street and Franklin Avenue. The killing became the symbolic beginning of a more intense period of warfare between Egan’s Rats and Hogan-associated men. Local historical accounts and the surviving 1923 contemporary report place the murder at the center of the dispute. [1] [5] [6]

Retellings report that Colbeck identified James Hogan, John Doyle and Luke Kennedy as the perpetrators. Some also claim that a large payment was offered to make the killing happen. Such accounts describe an accusation within the underworld; the specific criminal responsibility was not established by the materials consulted here through a final verdict against all three men. [1] [6] [7]

Willie Egan’s death did not prove that all Hogan associates were involved or that Hogan personally ordered the murder. The article records the leader’s death, the attribution made by adversaries and the violent sequence that followed, while keeping the categories of event, allegation and judgment distinct. [1] [5]

December 1921: Police Headquarters Ambush

Historical accounts describe a December 30, 1921 attack on Hogan-associated figures near St. Louis police headquarters. Among those reported present were James Hogan, Luke Kennedy, Abe Goldfeder and attorney Jacob Mackler. Several versions describe Kennedy being wounded and Mackler narrowly escaping injury during the shooting. [5] [6]

The location and victims are important because the event, if accurately reported, illustrates how attackers could target a rival even immediately after the group had left an official law-enforcement location. Nevertheless, the detailed scene is reconstructed from later authors and should not be transformed into a finding that particular named Rats were convicted for firing the weapons. [1] [5]

This incident is best understood as part of a cycle of retaliatory violence. It threatened lawyers and associates as well as people accused of ordering earlier murders, blurring the line between direct combatants and others close to a faction. [1] [2] [6]

John Doyle and the Limits of Retaliatory Attribution

John Doyle is named in several accounts as a Hogan-associated individual blamed by Egan’s Rats for the October 1921 killing. Later summaries report that he was killed by police in January 1922 during a pursuit. This event should be documented as an encounter with officers, not casually classified as an Egan Gang execution. [5] [6]

Being accused by a rival criminal group and then dying in a separate incident are distinct evidentiary questions. The former may reflect a genuine investigative lead or an attempt to justify retaliation. The latter requires contemporaneous police and coroner records to settle the circumstances and legal status of the use of force. [1] [5]

The case illustrates a persistent problem in organized-crime histories: once a man is named in a celebrated feud, later summaries may convert every subsequent event in his life into the work of the rival organization. This article rejects that shortcut. [1] [2]

Luke Kennedy and the 1922 Killing

Luke Kennedy is another recurrent name in the narrative of Willie Egan’s death and the ensuing feud. Local accounts depict him as wounded in the late-1921 ambush and fatally shot in spring 1922. The dates and circumstances appear with some variation among accounts; the specific crime-scene and death records have not been independently reconciled for this edition. [5] [6]

Thrasher’s 1927 treatment describes a prominent “Jellyrolls” member who had been wounded and then killed after continuing to antagonize the Rats. This near-contemporary sociological account is consistent with the broad Kennedy storyline, but it does not name the person in the text examined. Identifying Thrasher’s unnamed figure as Kennedy requires a separate cross-source inference and is not presented as his direct testimony. [2] [5]

The historian should also resist counting a named killing as a conviction of Colbeck or any specified suspect without consulting court outcomes. The feud is demonstrable as an organized pattern even when responsibility for individual acts remains unresolved. [1] [2]

Jacob H. Mackler: The Lawyer’s Murder

The most securely detailed case in the accessible contemporary press is the killing of attorney Jacob H. Mackler. In a St. Louis dispatch dated March 7, 1923, W. F. Sullivan described Mackler’s earlier shooting while driving through a business district, with gunmen firing from another automobile. The account reports that Mackler was killed while two passengers escaped, and that police regarded the crime as connected to the Egan–Hogan feud. [1]

The same report described Mackler as the cousin of Max Greenberg and linked his earlier legal work to a defendant charged in connection with Willie Egan’s murder. Those details make his death historically important beyond a simple “gang member killed” tally. A defense attorney had a legally distinct role from an armed participant, and affiliation by representation does not prove criminal membership. [1]

The press story said investigators had difficulty obtaining cooperation and noted the absence of convictions in the murders then being discussed. This is primary evidence of what was known or believed in March 1923; it does not make the reporter an eyewitness to the killing or establish the shooters’ identities. Mackler’s death should remain a murder with disputed perpetrator attribution. [1] [2]

Attacks on Hogan-Associated Property

Accounts of the feud describe gunfire directed at Hogan-associated addresses on Cass Avenue, including a house identified as his family residence. Other reports describe shots fired at a plumbing establishment associated with rival William “Dint” Colbeck. These incidents indicate the targeting of homes and businesses, where occupants and neighboring residents could be endangered irrespective of their role in a criminal dispute. [2] [5] [6]

Retrospective sources differ in how many times a particular house was attacked and which exact faction initiated a particular episode. A numerical count of bullets, cars or attackers should not be repeated as established fact without the original police report or dated press account that generated it. [5] [6]

The violence reached beyond direct underworld participants. If a family dwelling was attacked, residents and household members had an entirely different position from the gang leaders whose names appeared in headlines. The article keeps those categories separate. [1] [2]

September 1922: Goldfeder, Gordon and Street Violence

A St. Louis County historical article describes a September 2, 1922 shooting in which Egan-associated men pursued individuals named Abe Goldfeder and Max Gordon on or near Locust Street. In that retelling Gordon sustained a serious eye injury. This is a specific case suitable for further newspaper tracing, but the local retrospective account is not itself a criminal judgment. [5]

The incident matters in showing how gang conflict could spill into ordinary traffic lanes and commercial streets. Multiple moving vehicles and witnesses create evidentiary problems: rapid events may be described differently by participants, police and residents, and dramatic later narratives can standardize details that were originally uncertain. [1] [5]

Although the Egan and Hogan labels provide a framework for the episode, the roles of each person still require individual verification. The article neither creates an unproven criminal record for the injured man nor attributes every shot to a particular named assailant. [1] [5]

Truce Attempts and Monsignor Timothy Dempsey

Public revulsion at the feud encouraged mediation efforts. The March 7, 1923 newspaper dispatch reported that a prior truce had been attributed to Father Timothy Dempsey of St. Patrick’s Church and that recent violence indicated the difficulty of maintaining it. Local history describes Dempsey, public officials and the press as participants in a broader effort to halt the warfare. [1] [5]

These accounts should not be combined into a fictional single meeting that permanently ended all violence. There were cease-fire reports, renewed killings and a later written public-pledge episode. The sequence demonstrates that negotiation among leaders did not necessarily discipline all associates or stop retaliation by individuals acting without authorization. [1] [2] [5]

The churchman’s reported intervention reflects community efforts to protect life and public order, not approval of either group. The historical focus belongs on the failures of coercion and police protection as well as the effort to secure a cessation of shootings. [1] [5]

The St. Louis Star Pledges and a 1926 Historical Correction

Thrasher’s The Gang (1927) describes an agreement signed by leaders of the Rats and Jellyrolls. Crucially, his own published discussion includes a correction dated July 19, 1926, from a St. Louis informant disputing how the original story credited police. The informant said representatives of the St. Louis Star obtained written pledges from the Egan and Hogan leaders, not the police acting alone. [2]

That printed disagreement makes the episode unusually revealing. A contemporary or near-contemporary source openly disclosed competing understandings of how the feud was restrained. It would be historically improper to use only the main text while suppressing the qualification in the footnote. [2]

Later writers place public letters from Hogan and Colbeck in 1923. The underlying newspaper issue and signatures have not been independently facsimile-collated for this article. Accordingly the article records the reported written assurances and the contemporary dispute about who secured them, without reproducing imagined text or claiming the pledges had the legal effect of enforceable treaties. [2] [5] [7]

Bystanders, Innocent Victims and Collective Harm

The Egan–Hogan conflict was not confined to people who chose to carry weapons. Thrasher described a young boy injured in a shooting and another episode in which a person near the intended target was killed. The March 1923 press account depicts gunfire around busy city streets. Such narratives illustrate the wider human cost of attempts to punish adversaries through public violence. [1] [2]

Statistics for the death toll vary in later popular histories, with some giving totals in the dozens. The sources reviewed here do not provide a single reconciled list of all victims, dates, death certificates, and legal attributions. Therefore the article does not adopt one precise total as a verified accounting of gang homicides. [5] [6]

Each injury and killing deserves individual treatment when records permit. Family members, pedestrians, lawyers and children should not be reduced to background details in a story of rival bosses. A source-critical encyclopedia can document the structure of conflict without celebrating the people who orchestrated it. [1] [2]

Police, Journalism and the Evidentiary Problem

The March 1923 Brownwood Bulletin printing of a St. Louis wire dispatch reported police frustration with unwilling witnesses and the difficulty of securing murder convictions. In Thrasher’s later text, newspaper accounts and the testimony of a former Rat are among the ingredients of the narrative. Those layers are not independent corroboration simply because they appear in separate publications. [1] [2]

The role of the St. Louis Star in obtaining public pledges shows newspapers acting as participants in the effort to end violence, not merely neutral observers. Such involvement could produce useful records and also shape how journalists later described success or failure. The distinction is a methodological issue, not a reason to discard all period reporting. [2]

Police sources, defendants, informants, political allies and press reporters had differing incentives. A gang attribution should be tested against dates, suspect identification, witness testimony and final court dispositions wherever possible. Alleged crimes should not be converted into convictions by repetition across decades of secondary writing. [1] [2] [5]

Ray Renard and Participant-Based Accounts

Raymond “Ray” Renard’s 1925 serialized account of Egan’s Rats is identified by Thrasher as a principal source for his description of the feud. Renard’s relationship with the Egan group could provide access to information unavailable to outsiders; it could equally produce self-justifying statements, misplaced blame or dramatized recollections. [2] [8]

Thrasher himself summarized Renard and other sources in a compact narrative rather than printing a trial transcript. His summary was published only a few years after the principal fighting, making it valuable for the evolving public memory of the conflict. Nevertheless an interview or serialized confession cannot stand in for evidence formally tested in a particular homicide case. [2]

The appropriate research strategy is triangulation: compare participant claims with dated newspapers, the public pledges, municipal and court records, and the chronology of each named victim. This entry identifies the serial’s significance without treating every colorful detail as a verified fact. [1] [2]

Renewed Violence and the End of the Major Feud

Although the 1923 public-pledge effort is a turning point in most accounts, several versions describe continued incidents afterward. The historical description should therefore avoid a single instantaneous “end of the war” that ignores holdouts, attempted shootings and the possibility of violence outside the control of either nominal leader. [2] [5] [6]

Thrasher’s narrative itself reports continued shooting after an agreement, while the appended St. Louis informant emphasizes the efficacy of newspaper-obtained pledges. Those statements are in tension. Rather than pick one as an unquestioned verdict, the article recognizes that the exact stopping point depends on whether one counts organized retaliatory campaigns, individual attempted assaults or later unrelated crimes. [2]

By the mid-1920s, the scale of the Egan network changed dramatically, in part because of federal convictions in a separate mail-robbery case. That legal shift was distinct from the peace letters and should not be confused with a court order ending the Hogan feud. [2] [9]

1924–1925: The Egan’s Rats Federal Prosecutions

The Egan network was seriously disrupted following the federal prosecution arising from the 1923 Staunton, Illinois mail robbery. In Colbeck v. United States, 10 F.2d 401 (7th Cir. 1925), the appellate court affirmed the convictions of nine defendants. The opinion is an important primary legal source concerning the rival group, but it does not adjudicate every allegation against Hogan’s outfit. [9]

Retrospective narratives interpret the long sentences imposed on Colbeck and others as opening a period of reduced competition and expanded opportunity for Hogan-associated enterprises. That is a plausible business and criminal-history interpretation, but exact territorial transfers and profits are not proved simply by the court judgment. [5] [6] [9]

The collapse of one leadership circle did not require formal absorption of all its associates by Hogan. Other St. Louis criminals continued to operate under separate names and interests, and the city’s underworld cannot be represented as a two-firm monopoly resolved in one federal case. [5] [8] [9]

The Organization after the Egan War

Later writers claim that the Hogan outfit survived and enjoyed a stronger position after the decline of Egan’s Rats. The sources are persuasive that Hogan himself survived the feud and continued in public life. They are less precise about how many criminal associates remained under coordinated leadership, what areas they actually controlled and whether the label “Hogan Gang” retained the same meaning throughout the rest of Prohibition. [3] [4] [5]

A historical network can dissolve without a single arrest, indictment or death. Associates can become independent, make new partnerships, retire from illegal activity, or appear in different records under different labels. The absence of a public dissolution document is common and should not be filled with an invented ceremony or transfer of command. [5] [7]

The distinction between the person and the group is especially important here because Hogan lived and held office long after 1933. Extending the gang’s operating years to his death would confuse a career biography with a documented continuing criminal organization. [3] [4]

Hogan’s Later Public Career, 1930s–1963

Missouri’s historical legislative listing records Hogan returning to House elections in the 1930s and subsequently appearing in Senate election entries. The Political Graveyard provides additional biographical particulars and reports that he died in St. Louis on August 11, 1963. These are facts about the public figure, not evidence that the criminal organization persisted into the 1960s. [3] [4]

A St. Louis County local history article describes a property associated with Hogan near Eureka and an estate constructed in the 1930s. It is an interesting illustration of his later life and social position, but the original deed, construction date and financing have not been examined here. The property cannot be labeled proceeds of a specific illegal enterprise without a corroborating financial record. [5]

The biography offers a broader lesson in historical interpretation: elected office, community reputation, reported criminal association and eventual death are separate chapters. A research institution should examine how they intersect without allowing a sensational biography to supply every missing fact in the gang’s organizational history. [3] [4] [5]

Relations with Other St. Louis Criminal Networks

St. Louis contained multiple criminal factions during Prohibition, including Italian-American groups and other bootlegging or robbery networks. Secondary authors suggest Hogan’s circle maintained working relationships with some non-Irish participants and perhaps with Italian-associated enterprises. Such claims require specification of the people, transactions and dates involved. [5] [7]

The fact of a temporary business relationship does not establish that Hogan was a made member of La Cosa Nostra, or that the organization operated as a formally subordinate Mafia family. Even when groups shared markets, individual deals could coexist with independent leadership and frequent disputes. [7]

This article does not identify a direct succession from the Hogan Gang to the later St. Louis Mafia family, or to the Chicago Outfit, absent documentary evidence. It uses cross-references to situate these groups in the same regional crime history while preserving the integrity of their separate organizational timelines. [5] [7]

Not the Danny Hogan Organization of St. Paul

The Hogan Gang of St. Louis must not be confused with the organization associated with Daniel “Danny” Hogan of St. Paul, Minnesota. The shared surname and Irish-American cataloging category do not create a family relationship, command link, branch structure or merger. [5]

For the MGU directory, the dedicated St. Louis slug is hogan-gang.html; the St. Paul subject is separately indexed as the Danny Hogan organization. Cross-references should label them as distinct regional histories. [5]

Likewise, the name “Hogan” in a historical press account might refer to Edward, James or another person. Full identification requires the given name, date, location and context rather than casual surname matching. [1] [4]

Terminology

Hogan outfit / Jellyrolls

Terms used for the persons or network identified with Edward “Jelly Roll” Hogan; not proof of a fixed membership roster.

Egan’s Rats

A rival St. Louis criminal-political organization with a separately documented leadership history.

Kerry Patch

Historical Irish-American-associated St. Louis working-class neighborhood; not a synonym for the Hogan organization.

Prohibition

The national constitutional and statutory restrictions on intoxicating liquor in force between 1920 and repeal in 1933.

Bootlegging

Illegal manufacture, transport or distribution of alcohol during Prohibition.

Ward politics

Local political activity centered on electoral wards, party relationships, offices and public patronage.

Political patronage

Distribution of jobs, opportunities or favors through political relationships; not inherently proof of a crime.

Gang attribution

Description assigning a person or act to an organization; must be assessed independently.

Retrospective account

Interpretation made long after the event, possibly incorporating repeated hearsay.

Primary news report

A newspaper account close to the date; strong for what was being reported, but not automatically correct on all allegations.

Participant testimony

Account from someone said to have taken part in events; may contain special knowledge and motivated omissions.

Judicial finding

Issue actually established by a court’s judgment; distinct from charges, rumors and press allegations.

Truce / public pledge

Agreement or assurance to stop hostilities; actual compliance and legal force must be separately established.

Organizational continuity

Evidence that a particular commanded network persists over time; the survival of its leader alone is insufficient.

Irish-American crime syndicates

MGU editorial category for distinct organizations, not a single national Irish Mafia or statement about Irish communities generally.

Research and Editorial Method

Primary and near-contemporary material. Sullivan’s March 1923 dispatch provides a period identification of the rivals and substantial details of Mackler’s murder. Thrasher’s 1927 text provides a near-contemporary synthesis and explicitly reports his dependence on Renard’s later recollections. These materials support a historical organization profile but do not replace the homicide dockets, coroner records and complete newspaper runs that would be necessary for case-level reconstruction. [1] [2]

Legal specificity. Colbeck v. United States is used for the federal convictions of Egan-associated defendants, not as a judgment declaring Hogan guilty of their crimes or as a judicial verdict settling the feud. No murder charge is upgraded to a conviction without a final disposition. Likewise Missouri’s legislative index is not a criminal-history record. [3] [9]

Political chronology. Sources give slightly different service ranges for Hogan’s legislature and Senate career. Where dates differ, this article prioritizes the official state election-year index and identifies it as such rather than treating that list as exact continuous term dates. The existence of a later public career is not proof of continuing gang leadership. [3] [4]

Terminology and identity. Historic “gang” labels may include loosely associated criminals, political supporters and accused persons with different actual roles. This article does not infer ethnic identity from a surname or declare Irish immigrant communities responsible for these crimes. A surname coincidence with the St. Paul Danny Hogan organization is not evidence of a relationship. [5] [7]

Research limitations. The University of Chicago scan is cited for Thrasher’s searchable text, but no exhaustive facsimile-to-text verification was completed for every sentence. The purported St. Louis Star cease-fire letters and relevant homicide files were not independently collated. These limitations are disclosed instead of concealed behind a spurious documentary claim. [1] [2] [5]

Archival Research Priorities

The highest-value next step is to locate the original St. Louis Star articles containing the antiwar pledges, including signatures, exact dates and the surrounding editorial campaign. That will clarify the apparent tension between the main narrative and the corrective footnote in Thrasher’s account. [2]

A second priority is to retrieve local criminal and coroner records for Willie Egan, Jacob Mackler, John Doyle, Luke Kennedy and people harmed in shootings around Cass and Locust avenues. Those sources could distinguish documented perpetrators, unresolved investigations and later legend while allowing a victim-centered chronology. [1] [5]

A third priority is Missouri legislative and employment records concerning Hogan’s reported beverage-inspector appointment, as well as early beverage-industry and union records. These could test the scope of his public authority and commercial influence without presuming that every workplace relationship was corrupt. [3] [4] [5]

Historical Significance

The Hogan Gang illustrates how American urban criminal organizations could intersect with electoral authority, regulatory access and illegal markets without necessarily adopting the formal structure later associated with Mafia families. The organization’s recognition in contemporary reporting confirms that the name was more than a twentieth-century invention, but does not make every retrospective detail a proven fact. [1] [3] [5]

The Egan–Hogan feud also shows the inadequacy of treating gang history as a sequence of celebrated rival leaders. Lawyers, children, bystanders, residents, police and clergy were drawn into or affected by the conflict. Thrasher’s unusual footnote about the press role in stopping the violence reminds readers that historical accounts are constructed and sometimes openly contested. [1] [2]

Finally, Hogan’s long public career distinguishes his biography from that of the gang. The organization was most visible in the early Prohibition conflicts, whereas his official political service lasted much later. Maintaining that chronological distinction is one of the most important editorial safeguards for any institutional encyclopedia. [3] [4]
