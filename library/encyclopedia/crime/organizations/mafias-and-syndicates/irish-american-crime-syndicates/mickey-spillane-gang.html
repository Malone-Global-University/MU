Overview

The Mickey Spillane Gang—also described here as the Mickey Spillane organization—was an Irish-American-led neighborhood racketeering network based in Hell’s Kitchen on Manhattan’s West Side. It operated in the world of gambling, loansharking, local intimidation, labor access and waterfront commerce before losing its position to the criminal group later identified as the Westies. The name is a useful encyclopedia classification; it is not evidence that the participants registered a formal association or consistently used “Mickey Spillane Gang” as an internal title. [1] [2] [3]

Michael “Mickey” Spillane was the prominent local criminal figure whom the younger James “Jimmy” Coonan challenged beginning in the 1960s. The federal appellate opinion in United States v. Coonan explicitly identifies Spillane as the predominant leader when Coonan and associates began contesting control. That ruling is especially valuable because it anchors the rival organizations in a litigated record, while leaving unresolved many biographical details supplied by later popular histories. [1]

The immediate end of Spillane’s power came through escalating violence in the mid-1970s: the fatal shooting of associate Eddie Cummiskey in August 1976, the loss of racketeering leverage and Spillane’s own killing in May 1977. Yet the death of a leader, the collapse of his circle and the emergence of Coonan’s gang were overlapping developments, not evidence of a clean corporate-style transfer of ownership. This profile distinguishes the Spillane-era record from the better-documented late-1970s and 1980s Westies prosecutions. Editorial cutoff: November 13, 2026. [1] [2] [5]

The summary below separates historical catalog labels from court-established information. No verified contemporary membership roll, uniform hierarchy, consolidated revenue ledger or exact creation date has been produced for this particular organization. The directory’s broad 1950s–1970s period should be understood as a research range; the strongest sources specifically identify Spillane’s dominance by the 1960s. [1] [2] [3]

What the Name “Mickey Spillane Gang” Means

The expression is chiefly a historical shorthand for associates and rackets under Spillane’s influence. Police, newspaper and later true-crime writing might use crew, Irish mob or gang interchangeably; these terms should not be mistaken for a single chartered entity with fixed officers or a reliably numbered membership. [1] [3] [7]

An encyclopedia entry can identify Spillane as a criminal leader while remaining precise about the unit of analysis. Some individuals who worked in gambling or the ports were independent operators, workers acting under pressure, or intermediaries with other criminal interests. Names that appear in the same recollection or police investigation are not automatically confirmed members with duties assigned by Spillane. [2] [5]

The entry is also distinct from the novelist Mickey Spillane. The coincidence of names often creates search-result confusion; the person studied here is Michael “Mickey” Spillane, the Hell’s Kitchen racketeer, not the writer of detective fiction. [3] [7]

Hell’s Kitchen and the West Side Setting

Hell’s Kitchen developed as a densely populated working-class neighborhood west of Midtown Manhattan, with tenements, small merchants, saloons, social clubs, entertainment venues and access to the Hudson River waterfront. These conditions gave local groups repeated opportunities to demand payments, oversee informal gambling or intimidate people whose businesses relied on daily neighborhood relationships. [3] [8]

The docks were an economic system, not simply a backdrop to criminal stories. Movement of freight, hiring of crews, truck access, seasonal work and disputes over wages created points at which coercion could distort employment and contract decisions. Control over a street corner and influence over a hiring practice were different forms of power; neither established legal ownership of a pier or union. [2] [8]

Neighborhood poverty or Irish family origins cannot be treated as explanations that make crime inevitable. Families worked, founded institutions, organized labor, ran stores and pursued ordinary lives alongside the smaller number who engaged in predatory activity. A professional history must avoid substituting an ethnic slum stereotype for the actual mechanisms of racketeering. [3] [8]

Long-Term Context: Earlier West Side Gangs

The West Side had older traditions of street gangs and semi-organized local criminal associations, including the Gopher Gang of the early twentieth century. Those predecessors help explain the reputation of the neighborhood, but the existence of earlier gangs does not establish that their personnel or leadership continued without interruption into Spillane’s era. [3] [7]

Prohibition, waterfront employment and postwar changes in commercial activity shaped successive generations of New York organized crime. Racketeering structures could be rebuilt after arrests, demographic change or shifting labor markets, using familiar activities without preserving a single institutional lineage. For this reason, the Gophers, Spillane’s group and the Westies belong in related but distinct MGU entries. [1] [3]

Popular depictions sometimes speak of the “Irish mob” controlling Hell’s Kitchen from the nineteenth century into the 1980s. The label describes recurring neighborhood social and criminal networks, not a verified continuous command. The Westies appellate record offers evidence of direct 1960s rivalry; it does not provide documentation of an unbroken franchise dating to Owney Madden. [1] [3]

Michael “Mickey” Spillane: The Leading Figure

Michael Spillane emerged as a powerful West Side racket figure before Coonan established his own rival circle. Later journalism characterizes him as a bookmaker and loanshark who cultivated neighborhood relationships, and the federal appellate court identifies him as the prevailing leader against whom the Westies formed. Beyond those broad points, much of his childhood, early business career and private life rests on retrospective accounts rather than readily available judicial findings. [1] [2] [7]

Descriptions of Spillane as an “old-fashioned” or “gentleman” gangster express a contrast made by later writers between his public manner and the brutality of the Coonan era. They are not a finding that he was nonviolent, benevolent, or innocent of coercion. A reputation for gifts or local favors can coexist with threats and exploitation; anecdotes about generosity require the same scrutiny as sensational murder stories. [7] [10]

His neighborhood influence was partly personal. A local operator could use relationships with gambling customers, lenders, bar owners and contractors to reduce disputes, facilitate payments or deter competitors. Such influence differed from the hierarchical rank, initiation rules and formal criminal “family” arrangements commonly described for La Cosa Nostra. [1] [3]

When and How Did Spillane Become Prominent?

The collection directory places the organization broadly in the 1950s–1970s. The most secure published judicial landmark is that Spillane was already the predominant Hell’s Kitchen racketeer when Coonan challenged him in the mid-1960s. The precise year Spillane first assumed leadership, the identity of an immediate predecessor and the mechanism of his ascent remain less well established in sources examined here. [1] [2]

It is therefore more defensible to write “prominent by the 1960s” than to assign him an unsupported foundation ceremony or a succession beginning on a particular day in 1955. Organized crime influence often expanded through partial or negotiated control of markets, not a single takeover. Different neighborhoods, piers and gambling operations could have different intermediaries. [1] [3]

The source gap also cautions against exact membership totals. An individual called a lieutenant in a later recollection may have served in changing capacities, moved between factions or maintained his own operations; this page uses such titles as descriptions of influence rather than evidence of a complete chain of command. [3] [7]

Territory: Streets, Commercial Venues, and the Waterfront

Hell’s Kitchen, west of Eighth Avenue and close to the Hudson, was the symbolic and commercial center of Spillane’s power. Later reporting mentions activity around bars, gambling premises, entertainment properties and waterfront businesses. The geographical claim is one of criminal influence, not legally bounded territorial sovereignty or control over every enterprise in Midtown Manhattan. [2] [3]

Adjacent districts and waterfront facilities mattered because customers, workers and cargo did not remain inside neighborhood lines. A crew that could pressure a handful of business operators might affect a broader chain of trade. But an asserted connection to a given dock or venue should be traced to a witness, investigation or surviving commercial record rather than inferred from proximity. [2] [8]

Accounts of Spillane’s later residence in Queens also illustrate the distinction between home address and operating territory. The fatal shooting in Woodside was outside Hell’s Kitchen, but does not establish that his organization had relocated its primary criminal markets to Queens. [5] [7]

Bookmaking and Illegal Gambling

Bookmaking was among the businesses later identified with Spillane’s leadership. Wagering businesses used regular bettors and intermediaries, and their profits depended on the collection of debts and predictable access to premises. A local power able to demand payments from operators could profit without personally taking every bet. [2] [7]

The economic significance of gambling also helps explain why violent competition for neighborhood control mattered. A change in who could operate or protect a bookmaking network could alter recurring cash flows. Yet no verified Spillane-era ledger has been presented here, and the article publishes no fabricated daily “take,” exact betting volume or percentage distribution. [2] [3]

Some broader Westies cases established gambling-related crimes in the later enterprise, but those convictions cannot be backdated to the Spillane organization. The Coonan appeal itself lists a gambling count of which Kevin Kelly was acquitted; it illustrates why a precise case outcome matters even when a group has a broader reputation for gambling. [1]

Loansharking, Credit, and Coercive Collection

Illegal high-interest lending was another source of criminal influence in the neighborhood. Borrowers could include gamblers, small proprietors or people excluded from conventional credit markets. The coercive power lay not merely in charging interest but in threatening harm when repayment was disputed. [2] [3]

Later Westies accounts describe lenders and their relationships with outside financiers. Those details illuminate the market Spillane’s competitors sought but should not be taken to mean every later lender, borrower or loan book had once been under his command. In particular, the relationship of Coonan to Charles “Ruby” Stein belongs to the emergent Westies and does not prove Stein was Spillane’s subordinate. [1] [2]

No comprehensive Spillane loan book has been verified for this entry. Reported loansharking is therefore treated as a well-supported broad enterprise category, not as a basis for inventing exact customers, balances, victim identities or a forensic financial statement. [1] [2] [3]

Labor Racketeering and Employment Gatekeeping

A central feature of the West Side underworld was the opportunity to exploit labor relationships: influencing hiring lists, arranging unauthorized fees, threatening strikebreakers, intimidating vendors or using access to a building site as leverage. The Mob Museum specifically places union influence at the Madison Square Garden, New York Coliseum and dock facilities within the Spillane-era milieu. [2]

That institutional claim does not mean that every union member or local union officer was corrupt. The relevant distinction is between legitimate collective bargaining and the actions of individuals who used fear, kickbacks or coercion to capture private income. Workers could be victims of racketeering as readily as participants. [2] [8]

The exact union locals, contracts and periods covered by Spillane’s personal influence are not reconstructed from their original files here. For a mature archival edition, those issues should be checked against labor records, court testimony and contemporaneous reporting before numerical loss estimates or individual corruption findings are published. [1] [2]

Madison Square Garden, the Coliseum, and Entertainment Work

New York’s entertainment facilities generated large pools of temporary employment, deliveries and service contracts. The Mob Museum reports that the Spillane-era organization influenced unions at Madison Square Garden and the New York Coliseum. The value of such influence arose from hiring access and the ability to disrupt or facilitate business activity. [2]

A general association between a criminal crew and entertainment-industry labor should not be read as ownership of either venue or a legal franchise granting exclusive control. Venue management, legitimate unions and employees operated within wider municipal and commercial systems. MGU uses reported labor influence rather than “Spillane owned the Garden.” [2] [8]

Specific claims about a particular contract or an alleged payoff require identification of the year, witness and adjudicative status. The absence of those details from the general histories consulted is a research limitation, not grounds to invent a line-item case history. [2] [6]

West Side Piers and Maritime Commerce

The Manhattan piers created opportunities for cargo theft, gambling, loan collections and influence over hiring and trucking. Later federal proceedings found that the Westies exploited the West Side piers; the same location also appears in secondary accounts of Spillane’s earlier influence. Continuity in economic opportunity does not establish continuity in legal or criminal enterprise membership. [1] [2]

Waterfront racketeering could involve a series of people who controlled only fragments of a cargo or labor system. That complexity matters in reconstructing attribution. A pier-related criminal investigation dating from the 1980s may be relevant to the broader history of the district but cannot by itself establish an offense in the 1960s attributed to Spillane. [1]

In particular, the Westies’ later penetration of the Intrepid Museum on Pier 86, as described by the Mob Museum, belongs to the post-Spillane chronology. It is not counted as a Spillane Gang criminal enterprise, even though it occupied the same waterfront geography. [2]

Protection Demands and Local Intimidation

The possibility of intimidation made organized-crime reputations economically useful. A business owner who feared violence might pay for “protection” that actually meant not being harmed by the demanding party or its associates. In historically specific cases, the sources must distinguish genuine security, voluntary business arrangements and extortion. [1] [3] [8]

Spillane’s criminal power is often presented as more restrained than that of his successors, but the fundamental business model of neighborhood control could still be coercive. The claim that later Westies were especially brutal is not a reason to sanitize the earlier organization or assume its victims freely consented to payments. [2] [3]

Some popular stories treat every West Side bar or social club as a gang property. This encyclopedia does not make that leap. Owners and workers could have been intimidated, unrelated, partially cooperating or engaged in independent legal and illegal transactions. [3] [6]

Leadership Structure and the Limits of the Roster

The strongest defensible organizational statement is that Spillane exercised broad influence and had trusted associates. It is not possible from the sources reviewed to draw a complete, date-specific chart with an underboss, captains, soldiers and initiations like those associated with Italian-American Mafia families. [1] [2] [3]

Later writers regularly identify Edward “Eddie” Cummiskey as an important enforcer or associate connected with Spillane. They also discuss figures such as Thomas Devaney and Thomas Kapatos in narratives of mid-1970s violence. Their precise responsibilities, allegiances and date-by-date relationships require separate assessment, particularly because alliances could shift during the feud. [2] [3] [7]

Coonan and Featherstone should not be listed as Spillane deputies by default: the appellate record describes Coonan’s group as a rival to the existing Spillane leadership. The fact that some actors knew one another or used the same local saloons is not proof of a common membership register. [1]

Irish-American Identity Without an Ethnic Stereotype

Spillane’s organization is conventionally categorized as Irish-American because of its leadership and Hell’s Kitchen social associations. The broader neighborhood was home to many communities and included workers who did not share the ancestry of any criminal leader. Ethnicity was neither the legal basis of the rackets nor reliable evidence of individual criminal involvement. [3] [8]

A person could cooperate with Spillane in business, oppose him, be victimized by him or simply reside nearby. Converting any of these relationships into “Irish mob membership” would reproduce the category errors that responsible gang history is intended to correct. [1] [6]

The phrase Irish Mafia is sometimes used by journalists for disparate Irish-American crime groups. It should not imply that a national organization existed with uniform membership ceremonies or centralized authority. The index is an editorial umbrella for separate documented cases. [1] [3]

Jimmy Coonan and the Beginning of a Rival Organization

The U.S. Court of Appeals states that Coonan and confederates began challenging Spillane in the mid-1960s, and that this activity formed the nucleus of the Westies. This provides a secure chronological distinction: the rival organization arose while Spillane was powerful, not after his death. [1]

The earliest personal causes, shooting stories and precise membership shifts are narrated differently in secondary sources. One should resist turning every alleged personal insult into a definitive cause of the conflict. The reliable economic explanation is that overlapping rackets and neighborhood influence made rival claimants incompatible. [1] [3] [7]

Coonan eventually gained the upper hand. His later success did not mean his organization was a legal successor or that all Spillane associates joined him. Rivalries can destroy one network while leaving people active independently or under new terms; a court’s broad succession narrative is not a verified roster-to-roster merger. [1] [5]

The Escalation of Violence in the Mid-1970s

By the mid-1970s, competition in Hell’s Kitchen had become lethal. Later accounts link several killings of men regarded as Spillane’s associates to the struggle involving Coonan’s group and Italian-American Mafia interests. The evidence varies by incident: a death may be securely dated while the responsible gunman or person ordering it remains contested. [2] [3] [7]

This is why the article does not publish a single “war casualty count” as though a verified comprehensive list existed. Later retrospectives sometimes combine men attached to several factions, people killed for unrelated reasons and murders whose motive remains uncertain. Each homicide would require a homicide file, conviction record or thoroughly sourced reconstruction. [3] [6]

Violence also changed the terms of business. A leader who lost trusted associates could lose access to debt collections, gambling outlets or protection relationships even without being removed by formal vote or negotiation. [1] [2]

Eddie Cummiskey and the August 1976 Killing

Edward “Eddie” Cummiskey, sometimes called “Eddie the Butcher,” appears prominently in later accounts of the gang wars. The Mob Museum reports that he was killed at the Sunbrite Bar on August 20, 1976 and characterizes him as important muscle for Spillane. That dated report is a stronger anchor than undifferentiated lists of alleged victims. [2]

Cummiskey also figures in stories about the emerging Westies, where his relationships with other men and his reputation were complex. The fact that a single person appears in accounts of both factions should caution against constructing a permanent, exclusive affiliation from a nickname or one anecdote. [1] [3] [10]

The source consulted does not provide an independently reviewed trial judgment identifying the shooter, ordering party and exact motive for this killing. Accordingly, the article records the death and its significance for Spillane’s declining leverage without presenting an unverified murder conviction. [2]

The Genovese Crime Family: Overlapping Interests

The West Side waterfront and convention-industry labor markets attracted the attention of major Italian-American Mafia organizations. Later accounts commonly describe conflict between Spillane’s circle and Genovese-linked figures over access to labor and construction business. Such accounts are relevant to the environment of the conflict, but individual agreements and killings must be traced to specific evidence. [2] [9]

A direct statement that the Genovese family “controlled every contract” or that a named Genovese leader ordered a specific Spillane-era shooting is not warranted merely from the fact of competition. The Coonan opinion establishes later relationships of the Westies with the Gambino family; it is not a judicial ruling about every earlier Genovese–Spillane encounter. [1] [2]

In short, the Mafia organizations were external forces and possible partners or rivals in particular markets—not parent firms of one unified Irish-American national syndicate. The editorial label “Irish mob” must not flatten different criminal associations into one. [1] [3]

The Convention-Center Story and Its Chronology

Popular accounts often make a planned Midtown convention center a central reason for the Irish–Italian racketeering war. There was real incentive to influence large construction projects, but the construction histories must be distinguished by time. The Jacob K. Javits Convention Center opened in the 1980s, after Spillane’s death; the later Westies’ construction and labor interests should not be retroactively attributed wholesale to his gang. [2] [9]

The 2012 Publishers Weekly feature describes the underworld rivalry and a later arrangement surrounding the convention-center opportunity. That is a retrospective narrative of the Westies–Mafia relationship, not proof of a particular Spillane-era contract awarded, lost or controlled by the Irish crew. [9]

MGU therefore treats arguments about the prospective site as historical interpretations of commercial stakes, while avoiding precise construction-revenue claims, rigged-bid totals or dates not grounded in project and court records. [1] [2]

Loss of Influence Before Spillane’s Death

Spillane’s decline was underway before May 1977. The Mob Museum connects Cummiskey’s August 1976 murder with reduced Spillane influence and Coonan’s growing control of rackets. That explanation is consistent with the appellate court’s broader finding that Coonan successfully displaced the earlier leader. [1] [2]

Retreat need not imply an orderly retirement from organized crime. An individual might retain friends or residual income while losing the capacity to defend businesses, command feared associates or negotiate from strength. The surviving sources do not provide a precise liquidation date for Spillane’s gambling or lending interests. [1] [3]

Accounts also describe Spillane’s move to Queens. A new residence is not proof of voluntary abdication, and it does not transform the Irish-American operation into a Queens-based enterprise. The disputed homicide and succession claims are treated separately below. [5] [7]

The Killing of Mickey Spillane, May 13, 1977

Michael “Mickey” Spillane was shot to death in Woodside, Queens, on May 13, 1977. The homicide became a turning point in accounts of the Hell’s Kitchen racketeering struggle. The setting—outside the neighborhood he had influenced—also underscored that physical relocation did not necessarily remove a racketeer from conflict. [2] [5] [7]

Later writers commonly associate the killing with the Coonan–Gambino alliance and, in some accounts, Roy DeMeo or his associates. The Mob Museum attributes a DeMeo role to the account in T. J. English’s book; that is an attributed historical account, not a cited conviction for Spillane’s murder. Other secondary narratives assign different roles to the Westies and Mafia-affiliated gunmen. [2] [3]

The history of criminal prosecution is a separate question from historians’ assessment of motive. The source set consulted does not supply a final judgment conclusively establishing who shot Spillane or directed the murder. Accordingly, this article uses “killed” for the fact and “attributed” or “alleged” for the identity of perpetrators, preserving the distinction for future archival review. [2] [5] [7]

Mickey Featherstone: Not Mickey Spillane

Two prominent names in this history begin with “Mickey.” Michael “Mickey” Spillane was the older Hell’s Kitchen racketeer killed in 1977. Francis “Mickey” Featherstone became a principal figure in the later Westies led by James Coonan. They were different people, and conflating them produces incorrect biographies, timelines and trial attributions. [1] [3]

The 1991 appellate opinion describes Featherstone as Coonan’s right-hand man by the mid-1970s and as a significant source of later trial evidence. That evidence concerns the later Westies enterprise. It should not be used to represent Featherstone as the undisputed chief lieutenant of Spillane. [1]

Some accounts note investigative suspicion or proceedings concerning Spillane’s death, but this entry does not convert such statements into a murder conviction. The distinction between arrest, acquittal, witness testimony and court-established facts is central to the collection’s editorial standard. [5] [7]

Roy DeMeo and the Gambino Connection

The Gambino crime family became an important external ally of Coonan’s Westies. The appellate court describes a late-1970s arrangement under which the Westies could use Gambino financing and reputation in exchange for a share of proceeds. This is a finding about the successor network, not evidence that Spillane had operated as a Gambino subordinate. [1]

The Mob Museum reports that Coonan developed ties with Gambino figure Roy DeMeo, and summarizes English’s claim that DeMeo killed Spillane. It is important to retain the author-attributed nature of that claim instead of writing it as a settled result of a murder prosecution. [2] [3]

The story illustrates a substantive structural change: Coonan’s group built a mafia-backed position while Spillane’s earlier local dominance was collapsing. Their arrangements should be dated to their own period, not made part of a mythical stable hierarchy from the 1950s to the 1980s. [1] [2]

From the Spillane Organization to the Westies

A major virtue of the Coonan appellate opinion is that it distinguishes the two phases in plain terms: the Westies were formed amid a challenge to Spillane, and Coonan eventually displaced him. It does not say that every Spillane associate became a Westie or that the successor group was merely a new label for the same membership. [1]

The later Westies became known for loansharking, narcotics distribution, extortion and violence. The court evidence includes crimes committed after Spillane died, notably the 1977 killing of financier Charles “Ruby” Stein and a 1978 killing involving Richard Tassiello. These later offenses belong to the Westies article and should not be counted as Spillane Gang operations. [1]

Continuity is nonetheless historically meaningful. The neighborhoods, customer bases, union access points and criminal opportunities overlapped. The economic succession was substantial even if legal and organizational continuity cannot be assumed. The best editorial designation is rival predecessor and subsequent displacement, not a merger of two named gangs. [1] [2] [3]

What Happened to Spillane’s Associates?

A leader’s murder does not determine the fate of every associate. Some individuals could leave the rackets; others might work independently, remain in local businesses, become informants, be imprisoned or cooperate with competing organizations. The sources reviewed do not provide a fully verified roster from which individual outcomes could be reconstructed. [1] [3]

The institutionally significant outcome was loss of the cohesive Spillane-era position, not the disappearance of all crime from Hell’s Kitchen. The later Westies maintained activity into the 1980s, demonstrating that a local criminal market could persist under different leadership and organizational practices. [1] [2]

For this reason, MGU does not publish an exact successor rank for unnamed Spillane lieutenants, an invented “final meeting,” or a list claiming that all later Westies formerly answered directly to him. [1] [3]

Police Investigations, Witnesses, and Evidentiary Gaps

The available federal appellate record was created after Spillane’s death and centers on prosecutions of the Westies. That record reliably shows how the later criminal enterprise was described and what later juries found; it is not a complete Spillane-era case docket. The important distinction is between primary judicial findings about the successor and retrospective context concerning its predecessor. [1]

Journalists reporting on the 1970s violence faced changing witness stories, fear of retaliation and competing police theories. Police statements can explain what investigators believed at a given time, but allegations must not be silently turned into convictions. Conversely, an acquittal of a particular individual cannot be written as proof that no criminal organization existed. [5] [6] [7]

A fuller legal reconstruction would require homicide dockets, state indictments, trial transcripts, archived contemporary police and newspaper files, and labor-racketeering prosecutions for the exact years in question. These materials were not exhaustively collated for this encyclopedia edition. [1] [5]

The Later Westies Trials: Historical Context, Not Spillane Convictions

The Westies became the subject of extensive organized-crime prosecutions during the 1980s. The appellate opinion in United States v. Coonan concerned the convictions of Kevin Kelly and examines evidence about the Westies enterprise. It states that Kelly was convicted of racketeering, attempted murder, extortion-related and drug-conspiracy offenses, while he was acquitted of a gambling-business count. [1]

This is useful background for the criminal world that replaced Spillane’s, but Spillane died in 1977 and was not a defendant in these later proceedings. Named Westies defendants, their offenses and penalties should not be transferred to a fictitious Spillane-era indictment. [1]

The court also discusses the later relationship with the Gambino family. That material helps explain why local rackets could survive a violent change in leadership, but it does not establish that the older group had the same business terms or relative independence. [1] [2]

Economic Comparison: Two Generations of Racketeering

Both the Spillane-era network and Coonan’s Westies used neighborhood economic power, but the evidentiary records differ sharply. For Spillane, secondary histories and later legal context establish broad activities. For the Westies, trial testimony and judicial appellate analysis support more detailed statements about enterprise structure and particular crimes. [1] [2] [3]

The changing commercial landscape also matters. The dock economy, entertainment work and large construction opportunities created new or evolving rents to seize. A local operator’s earnings could depend less on the number of “soldiers” than on leverage over a small number of economically important gates. [1] [8]

No credible audited revenue, membership or profit comparison between the two groups is available from these sources. This article therefore avoids numeric claims such as “Spillane employed 100 men,” “received a fixed cut of every project” or “controlled all of New York’s ports.” [2] [3]

The “Gentleman Gangster” Reputation and Its Limits

Later accounts sometimes portray Spillane as a less flamboyantly violent, more paternal neighborhood boss than the notorious Westies who followed him. Such contrasts can reveal changing public images, but they risk romanticizing predatory enterprise. Gifts to local people, if documented, did not eliminate the harms of illegal loan collection, intimidation or coercive workplace influence. [7] [10]

Journalistic accounts of organized crime frequently give a compelling face to complicated institutions by focusing on one leader’s temperament. The historical task is to reconstruct commercial relationships, incentives, witnesses and outcomes rather than repeat memorable nicknames as moral verdicts. [3] [8]

The same qualification applies in reverse: the later Westies’ violent notoriety does not prove that every Spillane-era criminal act was mild or that every reported killing happened on his instructions. Reputation, allegation and adjudicated conduct are different categories. [1] [3]

Historical Significance

The Spillane organization is significant as a bridge between older Hell’s Kitchen neighborhood racketeering and the later Westies enterprise. It demonstrates how local gambling, lending, labor connections and territorial reputation could yield durable influence without the formal structure associated with an Italian-American Mafia family. [1] [2] [3]

Its decline also shows that criminal authority is contingent. Rivals could target associates, attract alternative sponsors, command different types of coercion and gradually displace an older network. The 1960s rivalry and 1976–1977 violence make Spillane’s story more than a footnote to the Westies: they show what the younger organization was formed to defeat. [1] [2]

A careful separate page prevents the subsequent Westies prosecutions from overwriting the earlier history. It keeps the two organizations connected as neighboring historical subjects while respecting different memberships, dates, documentary records and criminal responsibility. [1] [3]

Terminology

Hell’s Kitchen

Historic Midtown West Manhattan neighborhood with extensive ties to waterfront commerce and entertainment labor.

West Side piers

Hudson River terminals and associated docks, warehouses, transport and labor arrangements.

Bookmaking

Taking and accounting for wagers, often outside authorized gambling channels in the period described.

Loansharking

Extending illicit or extortionate credit, commonly associated with threats in debt collection.

Labor racketeering

Criminal exploitation of union or employment processes through extortion, corruption or coercion.

Protection racket

Demands for payment backed by threats of violence or disruption rather than genuinely voluntary protection services.

Crew / organization

Descriptive terms for an association; do not by themselves prove formal membership ranks or a corporate structure.

Westies

Coonan-led Hell’s Kitchen organized-crime enterprise that challenged and displaced Spillane’s earlier network.

La Cosa Nostra

Italian-American Mafia organizations with separate criminal histories; not synonymous with every Irish-led group.

Appellate opinion

Written court ruling reviewing issues from a prior proceeding; here, the 1991 Coonan-related appeal.

Retrospective history

An account written after events, possibly incorporating interviews and earlier reporting; must be attributed and assessed.

Attribution

A statement that a crime or order was linked to a person; source-specific and not automatically a conviction.

Research and Editorial Standards

Hierarchy of sources. This edition gives priority to the federal appellate opinion for the general relationship of Spillane and Coonan and for later court findings about the Westies. The Mob Museum and contemporaneous-to-later journalism supply historical context and carefully attributed chronology. The book by T. J. English is an important interview-based synthesis, but author and publisher descriptions are not substitutes for a homicide transcript or direct archival comparison. [1] [2] [3] [4]

Criminal attribution. An allegation, arrest, source’s interpretation, trial verdict and appellate summary are different evidentiary objects. For Spillane’s killing, this article records the established death and later accounts assigning responsibility, but does not claim an unverified conviction. The same caution applies to other reported 1976 murders and their suspected sponsors. [2] [5] [7]

Separating enterprises. The Westies are the rival group that ultimately displaced Spillane, not merely an alternative title for Spillane’s crew. Conduct established in the Westies’ later RICO cases is not assigned retroactively to the earlier group. The Gopher Gang is a still earlier separate historical subject. [1] [3]

Research limitations. This article does not reproduce a fully collated Spillane-era membership list, full transcripts of 1970s homicide cases, court-certified individual union racketeering files or comprehensive labor records. Precise membership, revenue and perpetrator numbers remain outside verified scope. The research and editorial cutoff is November 13, 2026. [1] [2] [5]

Selected References and Source Register

The register identifies sources used in the narrative and their limitations. A later court’s summary of earlier events is not itself a contemporary homicide finding. Book descriptions, historical reportage and investigators’ statements are explicitly distinguished from judgments and verdicts.

  1. United States v. Coonan, 938 F.2d 1553 (2d Cir. 1991), opinion on Kevin Kelly’s appeal. Federal appellate opinion reviewing the record in the Westies racketeering prosecution. Its historical introduction establishes Spillane as the existing Hell’s Kitchen leader whom Coonan challenged; findings concerning the later Westies must not automatically be attributed to Spillane.
  2. The Mob Museum — “In 1980s New York, the Mob had its hands in everything — even a museum” (May 22, 2024). Institutional historical synthesis describing Spillane-era bookmaking, loansharking, labor influence, the August 20, 1976 killing of Eddie Cummiskey and the later Gambino–Westies relationship. Retrospective, not a court verdict about Spillane’s killing.
  3. T. J. English — The Westies: Inside the Hell’s Kitchen Irish Mob (G. P. Putnam’s Sons, 1990; later editions). Author’s bibliographic summary of his interview-based historical reporting and the Coonan/Featherstone organization. Consulted as a secondary treatment of the transition, not a facsimile of 1970s police records.
  4. Macmillan — The Westies: Inside New York’s Irish Mob, publisher’s bibliographic page. Publisher description and bibliographic information for the later edition of English’s work. Describes the later Westies; this publisher summary is not independent verification of individual crimes.
  5. United Press International — “The alleged boss of the Westies, an Irish gang…” (November 20, 1986). Contemporaneous reporting on accusations against Coonan and the investigators’ account of the Hell’s Kitchen succession. A 1986 indictment and prosecutors’ remarks are allegations, not a finding that all charged conduct was proved.
  6. Michael Daly and John Hamill — “The Ghosts of Hell’s Kitchen,” New York magazine (April 12, 1982). Bibliographically verified 1982 feature from the neighborhood’s immediate post-Spillane period. The complete article has not been line-by-line collated for this entry; used to identify a significant contemporary retrospective, not as a source of unverified quotations.
  7. James Traub — “The Lords of Hell’s Kitchen,” The New York Times Magazine (April 5, 1987). Frequently cited investigative historical account of the earlier Spillane era and the later Westies. The original full text was not independently reproduced here; descriptions attributed to Traub are not treated as court findings.
  8. PBS / One-on-One — T. J. English interview, “T.J. English explores organized crime in the U.S & Cuba” (October 29, 2026). Author’s recent public discussion of the Hell’s Kitchen waterfront and labor-racketeering context, useful for interpretation rather than independent proof of each Spillane-era incident.
  9. Publishers Weekly — “Always a Market: BEA 2012” (2012). Modern publishing feature summarizing the traditional account of Spillane, Coonan and the Gambino relationship; commercial retrospective rather than primary evidence.
  10. The Washington Post — Rory Quirk, “The Guts & Gore of an Irish Gang” (March 29, 1990). Contemporary review of English’s book, valuable for evaluating the sensationalism of later storytelling and the difficulty of cleanly separating predecessor and successor operations.
