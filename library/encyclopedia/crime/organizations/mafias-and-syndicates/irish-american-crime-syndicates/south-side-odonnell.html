Overview

The South Side O’Donnell Gang was an independent, Irish-American-associated bootlegging and street-violence network operating primarily in southern and southwestern Chicago during the Prohibition era. Its best-known figure was Edward J. “Spike” O’Donnell. Historical narratives identify several of his brothers and other associates as participants in attempts to gain beer-distribution customers and defend their business against established competitors. The gang entered Chicago’s organized-crime record most vividly through the 1923 South Side Beer War, the recurring struggles with the Joe Saltis–Frank McErlane network and the 1925 attack on Spike at 63rd Street and Western Avenue. [1] [2] [3]

The South Side gang was not the West Side O’Donnell Gang led by William “Klondike” O’Donnell and his relatives Myles and Bernard. The same surname, simultaneous activity in Chicago and overlapping references to Capone have repeatedly encouraged confusion. A Chicago Tribune retrospective published in 1934 identified Spike as unrelated to Klondike, and later research likewise differentiates the two enterprises. Neither group should be presented as a subdivision of the other or as an automatic branch of the Chicago Outfit. [1] [4] [5]

This entry gives priority to dated journalism and historical research, then distinguishes those materials from colorful retrospective gang biographies. The most important late development is that, by 1930, newspapers described the former enemy McErlane as an ally of Spike’s organization; renewed South Side killings in 1932 further complicate any claim that the gang had permanently disappeared after 1925. The article describes a shifting criminal network rather than a timeless, centrally governed syndicate. Editorial cutoff: November 13, 2026. [6] [7] [8] [9]

“South Side” was a descriptive criminal-market label, not a rigorously surveyed boundary. It did not designate all Irish residents, all liquor outlets south of the Loop, or one organization possessing every local weapon or vehicle. A network with overlapping family ties, paid muscle and drinking-room customers is not identical to a twentieth-century Mafia family with a verified leadership ladder. [1] [10]

South Side versus West Side O’Donnells

The distinction is foundational. Contemporary newspaper accounts differentiate the Spike O’Donnell faction from the Myles and Klondike O’Donnell faction. A report circulated internationally in late 1926 independently listed “Spike” O’Donnell among South Side camps, while locating Myles and Klondike on the West Side. The 1934 Chicago Tribune expressly described Spike as unrelated to Klondike. This is more direct evidence than a modern gang chart grouping the surname into a single clan. [4] [5]

The West Side group is principally relevant to the April 1926 McSwiggin triple killing, federal Cicero investigations, the Morand warehouse incident and the 1930 roundup of Myles and Bernard associates. Those events cannot simply be transferred to Spike’s history. Conversely, the 1923 confrontation with Jacob Geis, the September 1925 attempt to shoot Spike and the 1932 violence around his South Side associates belong in this entry. Some sources name O’Donnell siblings with different lists and spellings, and persons with the same surname must not be merged without identification records. [2] [4] [5] [11]

Cross-referencing the two articles is essential, but the relationship must be labeled contemporaneous, similarly named, separate Chicago gang organizations, not proven kinship, merger or command succession. The verified conflict map is complex enough without introducing an invented trans-city O’Donnell boss. [1] [4]

Prohibition Chicago and the Southern Beer Markets

National Prohibition, imposed in 1920, pushed much alcohol production and distribution outside the lawful licensed market. Chicago’s existing brewing infrastructure, streetcar and railway routes, industrial workplaces, densely patronized saloons and nearby suburbs made beer a valuable illicit commodity. Those same conditions generated incentives for private territorial restrictions: an operator able to compel a bar to purchase from one supplier could stabilize sales, raise prices and squeeze rival delivery organizations. [10] [12]

The southern districts were not an unbroken criminal province. The Saltis operation, McErlane associates, Ralph Sheldon’s network and the Torrio–Capone interest each had relationships with different suppliers, saloons and neighborhoods. The O’Donnells entered a market where supply deals could be enforced by threats or violence. Such enforcement was neither a normal commercial contract nor evidence that all businesses participated voluntarily. [1] [2]

Markets must be distinguished from residences. A dispute at a particular intersection does not prove ownership of an entire ward; a brewery or truck located in another city does not itself establish a permanently administered territory there. The best reconstruction follows individual dated incidents and the larger supply network without converting retrospective sketches into exact modern district maps. [2] [10]

Background of Edward “Spike” O’Donnell

Edward J. O’Donnell—known as “Spike”—became the best-known public face of the South Side organization. Later criminal histories place him among men active in pre-Prohibition political rough-and-tumble and property crime, with a documented reputation that preceded his beer enterprise. The best-known summary describes a conviction connected with the Stockmen’s Trust & Savings Bank robbery and a 1923 release or pardon by Governor Len Small. These assertions are useful biographical leads but should be verified against the original judgment, pardon papers and correctional docket before a separate full criminal biography treats every detail as independently authenticated. [2] [13]

The organization was not a one-man undertaking. Family ties appear repeatedly in records, yet there is no verified dated charter listing a formal “president,” “vice president,” cashier and fixed number of soldiers. Spike’s prominence as the name under which the gang appeared in headlines does not mean every gunman or delivery driver operated under his personal orders in every incident. The press often used his surname as shorthand for multiple loosely cooperating men. [1] [2] [14]

Brothers, Kinship, and the Problem of a Fixed Roster

Allan May’s historical narrative identifies Steven, Tommy and Walter O’Donnell alongside Edward “Spike” O’Donnell in the early beer-war organization. The account also names Jerry O’Connor, George Bucher and George Meeghan among armed participants in a September 1923 confrontation. Such named associations are stronger than anonymous claims of hundreds of “soldiers,” although each alleged criminal action still depends on the reliability of the underlying reporting. [2]

Later materials introduce additional relatives, including Charles O’Donnell, reported as Spike’s brother and described as a victim of the 1932 fighting. The historical category brother must be tied to the particular report that uses it; families may include older and younger siblings not named in the initial 1923 roster, and newspapers sometimes misidentify relationships. This article does not convert a few period mentions into a complete genealogical chart. [8] [9]

The gang’s ethnic characterization derives mainly from widely reported Irish-American kinship and neighborhood networks. It provides no basis for treating the broader Chicago Irish community as a criminal constituency, or for inferring the ancestry of every named associate. [14]

The Beer Enterprise: Product, Customers, and Coercion

The early struggle centered on distribution, not solely on a romantic notion of “turf.” Breweries and illicit wholesalers depended on customers who could repeatedly take deliveries, pay invoices and tolerate enforcement pressure. Allan May’s reconstruction describes the O’Donnells offering beer to retailers and then employing intimidation when proprietors refused to switch. A separate 1936 Chicago Tribune account likewise discusses coercive solicitation of South Side bars, while requiring care because a retrospective narrative is not direct sworn testimony. [2] [15]

The business model carried substantial financial vulnerabilities. A supplier who needed to discount beer to win customers faced low margins; if competitors could respond by discounting their own product, the new entrant might turn to threats. Violence then damaged the very retail relationships that made the business viable. These factors explain why several nominally competing groups could temporarily cooperate to resist a disruptive new supplier. [2] [10]

No audited O’Donnell balance sheet, brewery ownership ledger or comprehensive daily delivery record has been established for this article. Precise profits, membership salaries, weekly case volume, number of controlled speakeasies and branch-office structures should remain unquantified rather than filled with figures borrowed from accounts of Capone or other Chicago gangs. [2] [12]

Saltis, McErlane, Sheldon, and Torrio: Distinct Interests

Joe “Polack Joe” Saltis and Frank McErlane are generally associated with one of the major independent South Side bootlegging interests. Ralph Sheldon’s organization represented a separate set of customers and relationships. Meanwhile Johnny Torrio and the emerging Capone network supported a broader scheme of production and distribution arrangements across Chicago and adjacent municipalities. The O’Donnells challenged this environment as competitors seeking access to established markets. [1] [2]

Popular summaries sometimes portray Torrio as a literal appointing authority who handed a map of all Chicago to subordinate bosses. The market was not so tidy: alliances shifted, customers could switch suppliers, factions fought within larger combinations, and the police and courts responded unevenly. Treating the O’Donnell conflict as a contest with multiple commercial and armed factions is more accurate than naming Torrio the direct commander of every anti-O’Donnell action. [2] [10]

The presence of a Chicago Outfit affiliate in a fight does not establish that the Saltis gang or O’Donnell gang formally belonged to the Outfit. Distinguish rivalry, occasional alignment, market pressure and command relationship; they are not interchangeable historical assertions. [1] [12]

1923: The South Side Beer War Begins

The best-developed retrospective chronology marks September 7, 1923 as a decisive outbreak. On that evening, men identified with the O’Donnell party allegedly pressured saloonkeeper Jacob Geis, who refused to abandon an existing beer supplier. Violence against Geis and a bartender preceded retaliation against O’Donnell associates. The incident became a compact illustration of how customer solicitation could escalate into armed warfare. [2]

This date and the precise composition of the attacking party are transmitted through later crime histories that cite older press reporting. The surviving contemporary record should be checked case by case before assigning every blow or gunshot to a named defendant. More secure is the general historical setting: a dispute over supply relationships, named individuals, and several killings that followed amid intense and contested Chicago newspaper coverage. [2] [10]

The term Beer War was not a single declared war with a clear legal start date and signed termination document. It describes successive confrontations across several South and West Side business groups from 1923 onward; later writers may use the label differently for a local clash and a multi-year Chicago-wide sequence. [1] [10]

The Jacob Geis Saloon Assault

Jacob Geis was a Chicago saloonkeeper whose refusal to switch suppliers placed him in the path of the O’Donnell expansion. Allan May’s reconstruction says an O’Donnell party confronted him in front of customers, beat him severely and injured bartender Nicholas Gorysko when he intervened. The attack is significant because the alleged target was a retailer rather than a rival gangster: the abuse of ordinary business operators was central to the economics of coercive distribution. [2]

Descriptions differ in the weapon used during the assault, which May expressly acknowledges. A careful encyclopedia entry should preserve the reported injuries and business motive while avoiding a falsely exact reconstruction of each movement inside the bar. Nor should the assault be made into evidence of an O’Donnell-owned police department, a written distribution contract or a universal citywide protection tax. [2]

Geis and Gorysko also illustrate the costs imposed on people whose workplaces were caught between criminal suppliers. Their injuries were not simply a stage in a war of charismatic gang chiefs. They matter as individual victims of an intimidation system. [2]

Klepka’s Saloon and the September Retaliation

Later historical research places the O’Donnell party at Joseph Klepka’s saloon near 53rd and South Lincoln Street after the attacks on saloon customers. There a force associated with Ralph Sheldon and Daniel McFall reportedly confronted O’Donnell associates. The encounter left Jerry O’Connor dead, according to the account, and became the start of further retaliatory killing. The claimed address is a historical locator in the narrative, not proof that the gang formally owned or permanently headquartered in the building. [2]

Accounts of exactly who fired, which weapon caused particular injuries and which participants were legally responsible are more fragile. May reports later investigative disputes and dismissals. The article must therefore describe the event as a reported gang confrontation and avoid turning a period accusation into a conviction unless the corresponding court file supports it. [2]

The wider meaning is organizational: men from different supplier groups could assemble rapidly at an informal drinking establishment, and a single violent encounter could reorganize alliances around revenge or deterrence. Such mobilization differs from a verified Mafia-style administrative chain of command. [2] [10]

Deaths and Prosecutions Following the 1923 Fighting

Histories of the 1923 confrontation also describe the deaths of O’Donnell-linked men George Meeghan and George Bucher, and the shooting of another survivor whose testimony later complicated the case. These are important names because they appear in accounts attempting to reconstruct the war, not merely in anonymous aggregate death totals. The available material does not justify assigning each death to one convicted assailant. [2]

According to Allan May, prosecutors investigated McFall and other rivals in connection with the killings, but charges were dropped or not pursued to lasting convictions. That procedural outcome is not an exoneration of every suspect on the historical evidence; equally, an indictment or press rumor is not a judicial finding of guilt. The correct register distinguishes people killed, people named in allegations and persons proven responsible. [2] [16]

Any final casualty count must define its time frame and the groups counted. The O’Donnell–Saltis confrontations overlapped the broader Sheldon–Saltis and Torrio–Capone fighting; adding all South Side deaths to the O’Donnell gang would greatly overstate its provable responsibility. [11] [16]

1924: Brewing Capacity and Enforcement Claims

A detailed collector’s chronology describes Spike’s alleged investment in a brewery at Fort Atkinson, Wisconsin, followed by a September 1924 enforcement raid involving beer trucks and men connected to the operation. This is a plausible and useful research lead for how a Chicago distributor might have sought supplies outside municipal boundaries, but the present entry has not independently collated the original Wisconsin property records, federal search warrants, inventories or final court dispositions. [13]

The allegation should not be inflated into the claim that O’Donnell owned all breweries in Wisconsin, or that every truck found at one plant belonged personally to him. Manufacturing, financing, hauling, warehousing and selling were related but distinct business functions. They could involve partners whose precise ownership shares are not known. [10] [13]

The episode also cautions against equating a police raid with conviction. The number of people detained, goods seized and proceedings that followed are separate questions, requiring original court or Prohibition Bureau evidence to answer definitively. [13]

September 25, 1925: The Attack at 63rd and Western

The most famous near-fatal event in Spike O’Donnell’s career occurred on September 25, 1925, near 63rd Street and Western Avenue. Accounts place him outside a drugstore, talking with a police officer, when gunmen in an automobile opened fire. Spike reportedly dropped to the pavement and survived. Later narratives associate Frank McErlane with the attack; the attribution needs to remain a historical allegation unless corroborated by a decisive judgment. [2] [13] [17]

The episode is regularly called the first gangster use of a Thompson submachine gun in Chicago, and sometimes, more extravagantly, the first such attack anywhere in American history. Because “first” depends on a comprehensive record of earlier unreported or differently documented incidents, this article uses the more careful formulation: one of the earliest widely publicized submachine-gun attacks in Chicago’s beer wars. The exact weapon identification and the ultimate shooter attribution should be treated separately. [2] [17]

The site belongs to the geography of a particular ambush. It does not establish a single fixed territorial border there, and the survival of a leader tells us little about the total strength of his enterprise at that moment. [2]

Machine Guns, Newspaper Spectacle, and Attribution

The growth of automatic weapons in Chicago became a centerpiece of the city’s public reputation during the 1920s. Writers could use the dramatic appearance of a gun to imply that any named criminal chief possessed all the modern technology used in a district. Yet accounts of automatic-fire attacks regularly pass from police suspicion to tabloid assertion to retrospective certainty without a surviving ballistic comparison or a criminal conviction. [10] [16]

For the September 1925 ambush, the strongest defensible summary is that Spike was the intended victim and survived, that the incident became notorious as an early automatic-weapons attack, and that McErlane was later identified as a suspected gunman. The exact composition of the vehicle’s occupants and responsibility for firing the weapon are more uncertain. The encyclopedia refuses to substitute a familiar legend for a proven forensic history. [2] [13] [17]

The effect of such attacks also extended beyond gang personnel. Residents, pedestrians, storekeepers and police had to navigate public streets that could become firing ranges. An organization profile is incomplete unless it recognizes civilians as the people whose safety was exposed by the struggle for illicit markets. [10]

Late 1925: Continued Ambushes and Territorial Pressure

Retrospective chronologies describe further attempts on Spike’s life later in 1925, including an encounter near a railroad crossing in the southwest suburbs involving Spike, brother Tommy and another companion. Accounts typically describe O’Donnell as surviving while one companion was wounded. The precisely reported intersection and timing vary across popular retellings and should be checked against the dated press reports before being used as a reconstructed combat diagram. [13] [17]

These incidents should not be treated as proof that a single uninterrupted two-sided war continued without changes in alliances. The Saltis–McErlane and Sheldon groups also fought one another, while Torrio’s criminal organization underwent leadership changes. Control of customers, resentment over previous attacks and fear of retaliation could produce overlapping, temporary alliances. [2] [10]

Unverified casualty totals, specially chosen nicknames for every shooter and fictionalized dialogue are omitted. The important historical conclusion is that the 1925 attack did not eliminate Spike as a public actor, and that conflicts in his vicinity continued. [2] [13]

Al Capone and Johnny Torrio: Competition without Simple Subordination

Johnny Torrio’s and Al Capone’s enterprises sought to influence markets in which the O’Donnells competed. Later biographies frequently describe Capone-aligned suppliers as backing the Saltis and Sheldon groups, but the level of operational direction differed across cases. A gang that opposed a Capone-supported beer seller was not necessarily fighting a single unified Capone command in each street encounter. [1] [2] [15]

The year 1925 was also a transition period for the Torrio–Capone leadership. It is misleading to portray Chicago as a perfectly settled monopoly with all independent gangs formally enrolled or all rivals permanently outlawed by decree. The persistence of O’Donnell violence, disputes among nominal allies and later cooperation among former enemies suggest a more contingent underworld market. [2] [10]

This article neither gives Spike a rank in the Chicago Outfit nor treats him as an Outfit capo. It also avoids the opposite unsupported simplification that he directed all Irish-American resistance to Capone. Independent groups might share ethnicity, customers or adversaries without belonging to a single political or criminal institution. [4] [12]

The Beer Wars as a Changing Network of Factions

The so-called Beer Wars were a series of violent commercial struggles, not one war with stable opposing flags. The Saltis–McErlane network, Ralph Sheldon, Torrio–Capone interests and Spike O’Donnell’s group negotiated from positions that changed when members were arrested, killed, injured or defected. An individual might remain a recognizable bootlegger while changing partners and supply routes repeatedly. [2] [10] [16]

This framework helps explain why a person named as a defendant or suspected gunman in one episode could later become an ally of someone previously attacked. It does not absolve the parties of violence; it prevents a false reading of twenty years of Chicago newspapers as a tournament between two permanent families. The archival approach traces persons and events over time, rather than projecting the end state backward. [6] [16]

A date range such as “1923–1932” for the O’Donnell group identifies recurring visibility of a name in gang reporting. It does not certify one continuous legally incorporated institution or prove that every contemporary South Side homicide belonged to its campaign. [8] [9]

1926: Investigation, Survival, and Public Notoriety

By 1926, reports continued to identify Spike as an identifiable South Side gang figure. He appeared amid police questioning, repeated murder scares and continued concern over gang competition. Retrospective treatments place several assassination attempts in this period; their exact counts differ depending on whether unsuccessful threats, mistaken identity and actual shootings are included. The encyclopedia does not publish a universal “number of times shot at” as an independently verified statistic. [13] [17]

The wider Chicago violence also included the April 1926 McSwiggin triple killing, a distinct incident centered on prosecutor William H. McSwiggin and associates of the West Side O’Donnells. Identical surnames make this a frequent chronological trap. It must not be moved into Spike’s personal biography or described as retaliation for the South Side Geis incident without independent evidence. [4] [5]

Contemporary newspapers offer valuable snapshots of official knowledge but also reveal a culture of speculative arrests and fragmented interagency investigations. “Questioned by detectives” should never be changed to “convicted,” and a police theory of responsibility must remain identified as such. [10] [16]

1927: Continuing Chicago Gang Conflict

The south-side beer markets remained unstable in 1927, even when an individual O’Donnell brother was not the named defendant in every headline. Saltis, McErlane and Sheldon-related cases continued as authorities tried to reconstruct killings from testimony that was sometimes withdrawn or unavailable. This surrounding litigation matters because the rival organizations did not disappear merely because Spike temporarily receded from headlines. [2]

In the wider McErlane record, the 1927 proceedings over attorney Thaddeus S. Fancher’s killing resulted in an acquittal. The case was separate from the attempted shooting of Spike. Conflating McErlane’s known acquittal in one homicide case with formal exoneration or conviction for every suspected O’Donnell attack would distort the record. [2] [18]

The phase also illustrates the limits of arrest lists: a suspect’s presence in multiple investigations does not establish a specific role in each crime. Accurate organization histories preserve verdicts, case scope and dates rather than collapsing all allegations into a generic murder total. [16]

1928: Gunfire near Stephen O’Donnell’s Garage

An Associated Press report of October 10, 1928 described gunmen using machine guns against the garage of Stephen O’Donnell, identified as Spike’s brother. The report placed Spike’s organization in an ongoing South Side Beer War and described the attack as a multiple-automobile raid during the preceding night. Unlike a mere late gang legend, it is a dated near-contemporary description of a still-recognizable O’Donnell faction. [19]

Later narratives supply additional details about the address near South Wood Street, the number of vehicles and a confrontation involving Percy O’Donnell. Those particulars should be cross-checked before a publication presents a second-by-second attack reconstruction. The essential verified point is that the gang’s public identity persisted in 1928 and a close relative’s premises became a target. [13] [19]

The garage incident also shows a changing pattern of vulnerability: a fixed place associated with a family member could attract violence even when business transactions occurred elsewhere. The social costs extended to people sharing streets and residences near the target. [19]

1930: Frank McErlane Reported as a New Ally

The most important reversal in the gang’s later organizational history concerns Frank McErlane. A February 1930 Associated Press report described him as a recent ally of Spike O’Donnell and his gang, despite the earlier Saltis–McErlane conflict with Spike. The report connected the change of associates with a possible explanation for an attempt on McErlane’s life. This is a contemporary police assessment reported by the press, not a signed alliance treaty. [6]

Modern historical research also acknowledges uncertainty over the timing and depth of the split between McErlane and Saltis. Binder and Eghigian describe McErlane as probably a co-leader with Spike around 1930–1931 rather than presenting a fixed command ledger. The difference matters: alliances could be temporary, individual and transactional. It is safer to say that credible sources identify a reversal and possible cooperation than that McErlane automatically inherited the O’Donnell gang. [2] [16]

The report is particularly valuable because it directly contradicts an overly tidy history ending with the O’Donnells’ supposedly permanent defeat in 1925. Survival and a change of personnel make a different later phase historically visible. [6] [16]

1931: Attacks on Homes and Shifting South Side Alignments

Several later compilations report gunfire directed at Spike’s residence in 1931 during renewed tension with other South Side beer interests. They also connect some violence with the McGeoghegan–Quinlan circle. The existence and character of individual attacks should be verified against the original police and newspaper records before every allegation is attached to a precise perpetrator. [13] [17]

The episode, if reconstructed from contemporary notices, illustrates that the location of conflict had shifted from saloon counters and streets to private homes. Even if a rival intended to kill a gang figure, spouses, children and neighbors could be endangered by armed attacks on residences. This article does not invent the emotions or conduct of family members, nor does it classify them as gang associates merely by kinship. [13]

By this time the O’Donnell label was better understood as a network of relationships and mutual defenses than as a fixed 1923 roster. The precise role of McErlane after his reported change of allegiance remains an open historical question. [6] [16]

1932: The Death of Charles O’Donnell

A newspaper report published on April 2, 1932 stated that Charles O’Donnell, identified as Spike’s brother, had died in a Chicago hospital from machine-gun wounds after an ambush on March 6. The report attributed a possible link to a renewed South Side Beer War as a police assessment. It supports the important fact of a reported death and a contemporaneous relationship identification; it does not establish the shooter’s identity through a criminal conviction. [8]

The name Charles is not included in every short four-brother retrospective list of the early 1923 group. That discrepancy should be noted rather than concealed. Families and gangs were not coterminous, and a later-reported relative need not have been a founding participant in the beer enterprise. [2] [8]

The killing also shows that the gang story did not simply stop at the famous 1925 machine-gun ambush or at the 1928 garage attack. By the early 1930s, persons described as O’Donnell relatives or associates were still involved in the pattern of lethal reprisal. [8] [9]

July 1932: The Del Rio Roadhouse Killings

On July 7, 1932, an Associated Press report reproduced by the Brownsville Herald described two gunmen entering the Del Rio roadhouse and killing McGee and Larkin, men whom the report identified as connected with Spike O’Donnell’s circle. A waiter was reportedly seriously wounded. The account placed the attack after Charles O’Donnell’s death and other recent killings in a renewed pattern of South Side retaliation. [9]

The reporting is particularly useful because it names victims and explains that police were investigating possible revenge motives, but did not identify proven shooters. A press description of men as “henchmen” should be recorded as the newspaper’s label, not an independently verified payroll or formal office. The reported waiter’s injury also reminds the reader that restaurant workers could be caught in gang disputes. [9]

The aftermath remains unsettled in the materials reviewed for this edition. This page does not allocate the murders conclusively to the Saltis faction, the McGeoghegan–Quinlan circle or any individual without a reliable case disposition. [9] [16]

Spike’s 1932 Los Angeles Visit

Newspapers covered Spike’s presence in Los Angeles during the 1932 Summer Olympics. A later research article by J. H. Graham reproduces dated Los Angeles reporting in which Spike described himself as a visitor interested in the games, distanced himself from bootlegging there and commented on the approaching political debate over Prohibition repeal. Those reported statements are evidence of what he said publicly, not independent proof that his earlier criminal associations had ended. [17]

The travel is relevant to separating personal mobility from organizational expansion. A known Chicago gang figure visiting California did not thereby establish a Los Angeles O’Donnell branch, an office, a merger with western rackets or control of a local labor union. Historical claims about franchise-like national gang expansion require documentary support well beyond a newsworthy trip. [17]

The visit also shows how gang figures performed public identities before the press: a person suspected by police of past violence could present himself as an ordinary tourist or legitimate businessman. Rather than adopt either posture uncritically, the article records the juxtaposition and its evidentiary limits. [17]

1933: Repeal and the End of the Beer-War Business Model

The repeal of national Prohibition in December 1933 sharply changed the market in which the South Side O’Donnells had achieved notoriety. Legalized and regulated beverage production and distribution weakened the profit advantage that arose from criminalized beer supply, although illicit liquor sales did not vanish overnight. A 1934 Chicago Tribune retrospective described the old bootlegging economy as largely broken and identified Spike as a former prominent South Side beer figure. [5]

A change in the law does not provide a precise organizational dissolution date. Men who previously profited from bootlegging could move into trucking, gambling, other offenses, lawful trades or retirement; each path requires individual evidence. It is misleading to claim a clean corporate succession from the South Side O’Donnells to the Chicago Outfit or any later Irish-American criminal organization merely because both survived in some form after 1933. [5] [12]

The better conclusion is functional: the particular prohibition-era beer monopoly disputes that gave the group its central historical role had lost their legal and commercial basis. That is distinct from proving what every former associate did afterward. [5] [10]

1934: Spike, Los Angeles, and the Public Record

In April 1934, Los Angeles police detained Spike, and contemporary newspaper coverage later summarized by J. H. Graham recorded a vagrancy proceeding and his return toward Chicago. The account portrays him as claiming to operate a trucking business. These statements and actions are part of his individual later biography, not evidence that the 1923 gang had established an organized West Coast distribution system. [17]

The same year, the Chicago Tribune distinguished Edward J. “Spike” O’Donnell from unrelated William “Klondike” O’Donnell and associated Spike with years in South Side beer dealing. This near-period summary is an important external check on both name confusion and later attempts to project a single O’Donnell family syndicate across Chicago. [5]

None of these reports supplies a final gang dissolution decree. The article therefore places the decline of the old beer enterprise in the 1930s while leaving the exact last date of coordinated criminal activity unresolved. [5] [17]

Spike’s Later Life and the Limits of Organizational Continuity

Later biographical accounts report that Spike survived into the 1960s and died in 1962. A historical figure’s longevity must not be substituted for the operating life of the enterprise with which he was once associated. Even if a former gang leader remained recognizable to the press for decades, the organization may have dissolved, changed partners or become irrelevant long before his death. [17]

A personal chronology would require death records, residence files, later arrests and documented business relationships. This organization profile does not infer that Spike directed 1920s-style armed beer enforcement through the 1940s and 1950s, or that all later associates remained under his command. Where the evidence is thin, the statement of nonverification is a positive scholarly finding. [16] [17]

Likewise, no direct institutional link is established here between the South Side O’Donnells and later New York Irish-American groups, including the Westies. Historical ethnic similarity or a comparable nickname is not evidence of lineage. [5]

Organizational Structure and Membership

The organization appears in historical accounts as a network clustered around the O’Donnell brothers, their close armed associates, saloon operators and beer-delivery relationships. This suggests a practical structure based on personal loyalty and access to customers rather than a documented formal charter. The contemporaneous label “O’Donnell gang” should not be read as evidence that the group used Mafia ranks such as boss, underboss, consiglieri or caporegime. [1] [2]

Identifying a reliable membership total is difficult. Arrest reports may include people who were drinking with suspected gunmen, individual criminal associates may change allegiance, and a family member may not be an active participant. A roster built from every person mentioned in police reporting would therefore overcount, while an early four-brother list would omit some later named associates. Both errors can be avoided by listing individuals with their evidence status. [2] [8] [16]

No complete payroll, initiation rules, organizational treasurer, official governing committee or uniform territorial hierarchy was located for this edition. The absence of such documentation does not disprove temporary coordination; it limits the precision with which the organization can be described. [10] [16]

Revenue and Capital: What the Evidence Actually Shows

The plausible revenue streams were illicit beer supply, delivery margins and coercive control of saloon purchasing arrangements. The economic incentive was straightforward: recurring beer customers could generate significant cash flow, while control of entry points and trucks protected a wholesaler from rivals. Later narratives add brewery investments and distant supply connections, but those allegations need separate property, tax and Prohibition-agent confirmation. [2] [13]

No reliable organization-wide financial statement was found. It would be inappropriate to publish a dollar estimate of the O’Donnell network’s wealth based on Capone’s profits, the annual gross sales of Chicago beer or the purchase price of a particular brewery. Such numbers measure different organizations or markets. For the O’Donnells, the proper financial record is currently descriptive rather than numerical. [2] [10]

Similarly, an associate’s luxurious residence, car or travel arrangements do not prove that those assets were purchased with a particular gang’s proceeds. A comprehensive economic reconstruction would need ownership records, seized ledgers and tax proceedings tied to named participants. [12] [13]

Policing, Arrests, and Evidentiary Limits

Police records from Prohibition Chicago document real shootings and investigations but also reveal the difficulties of sustained prosecution. Witnesses feared retaliation, suspects used aliases, violence crossed police jurisdictions and newspapers often described persons as “gangsters” before any criminal trial. The modern scholarly study by Binder and Eghigian demonstrates why the published category gangland killing cannot automatically be translated into an individual organization’s proven murder count. [10] [16]

When this article states that a person was arrested, questioned, suspected, charged, convicted or acquitted, those terms refer to different stages and outcomes. A police belief reported in 1930 about McErlane’s affiliations is not a judicial finding; a later historian’s statement about a 1923 murder does not supply a verdict that may never have occurred. [2] [6]

The highest-value research next steps are direct interrogation of period police reports, Cook County court dockets, contemporaneous Chicago daily newspapers, federal Prohibition Bureau files and individual death certificates. These would refine which named persons were defendants, survivors, bystanders or victims. [10] [16]

Victims, Bystanders, and Community Consequences

Popular Chicago gangster narratives concentrate on the survival of famous gunmen and the ingenuity of their ambushes. That perspective can obscure saloonkeepers forced to change suppliers, bartenders injured during intimidation, drivers exposed to armed hijackings and residents placed in danger when gunfire crossed public streets. The Geis and Gorysko assault, 63rd-and-Western shooting and 1932 Del Rio roadhouse incident demonstrate different ways outsiders could be harmed. [2] [9]

The social costs were not confined to the immediate moments of violence. Recurring threats could alter business decisions, discourage testimony and strengthen dependence on illicit “protection.” These are analytical inferences from the pattern of coercive enforcement, not individually measured losses or claims that a whole neighborhood acquiesced. [10] [16]

A careful crime encyclopedia should not romanticize the technology of violence or convert identifiable injury into entertainment. Specific responsibility still must be established by source quality, but the broader harms of armed control over retail markets remain historically central. [2] [16]

Ethnicity, Neighborhood, and Editorial Classification

The South Side O’Donnells are included in an Irish-American Crime Syndicates collection because of the historically reported background of their principal family leadership and the gang’s established place in accounts of Chicago’s Irish-associated criminal networks. That is an editorial classification, not evidence of exclusive recruitment or a mandate from Irish communities. [1] [2]

Chicago’s southern neighborhoods included workers of many ethnic backgrounds, political loyalties and occupations. A criminal faction’s identity cannot be projected onto every household sharing a surname, parish or address. Nor should the crime index imply that the organization was a chapter of a national Irish Mafia. The same caution applies to oversimplifying Italian-American competitors into one permanent monolith. [10] [12]

The source record demonstrates shifting business alliances and rivalries across ethnic lines. Those contacts are best described as historically specific relationships among persons and suppliers, rather than as inevitable ethnic warfare. [16]

Gang Lore, Fiction, and the First-Machine-Gun Story

The South Side O’Donnell story entered popular history partly because it combines an identifiable leader, family relationships, extraordinary survival, competing beer suppliers and the emergence of automatic weapons. Such ingredients encourage a familiar literary form: a solitary “gang king” battles an all-powerful Capone machine and escapes repeated certain death. Dramatic scenes can retain genuine historical kernels while obscuring the evidentiary status of dialogue, motive and shooter attribution. [2] [13] [17]

The best-known examples are the formulaic claim that Spike escaped a fixed number of attacks and the supposed “first use” of the Thompson submachine gun. Both require definitional care: attempted attacks include threats that may never have become shootings, and claims of technological priority must compare dated earlier incidents. A narrower, defensible account is more informative than a sensational exact total. [2] [16]

Historical motion pictures and later dramatizations can be mentioned in media history, but they cannot validate an assault, homicide or membership list. An encyclopedia source register should identify retrospective books and reenactments as such. [2]

Geographic Register and Mapping Cautions

The best-known places in this story include the stockyards-adjacent South Side beer markets; the 53rd Street saloon location in the 1923 narrative; 63rd Street and Western Avenue for the September 1925 attack; and locations associated with Stephen O’Donnell’s garage and later 1932 gunfire. These are incident sites, not modern property boundaries legally owned by the gang. [2] [13] [19]

Historical street addresses can change, and newspaper references to “South Side” often include adjacent southwestern city districts and suburbs. MGU should not publish a street-by-street controlled-territory map without collating contemporary city directories, police district maps and dated source reports. A simple atlas depicting major incidents and separating confirmed addresses from retrospective locators would be appropriate for a future illustrated edition. [10] [12]

A violence map also must avoid showing an inferred route between attacks as if it were a verified path taken by gunmen. The available records describe places where events were reported, not enough continuous evidence to reconstruct criminal travel itineraries. [16]

Terminology

Bootlegging

Illicit manufacture, transport, wholesale supply or retail sale of alcoholic beverages under prohibition law.

Beer War

Press and retrospective label for overlapping violent disputes among rival beer suppliers in Chicago during Prohibition.

South Side O’Donnells

Historically reported organization associated with Edward “Spike” O’Donnell and family-linked associates.

West Side O’Donnells

Separate O’Donnell organization centered on Klondike and Myles; not a branch of Spike’s group.

Speakeasy

An establishment where alcohol was sold or consumed in violation of Prohibition regulations.

Protection racket

Coercive pressure, threats or violence used to demand commercial compliance or payment.

Territory

Contested sphere of customers or influence, not necessarily a formally surveyed boundary.

Thompson submachine gun

Automatic firearm often called a “Tommy gun”; early gangland-use claims require separate historical verification.

Retrospective source

An account written after the event, potentially informed by prior reports but subject to retelling and mythmaking.

Contemporaneous report

A document published close to the event; important for chronology but not identical to a legal finding.

Acquittal

A formal outcome in a specified criminal case; not proof concerning unrelated incidents.

Association

A historically reported personal or business relationship, less precise than a verified rank or sworn membership list.

Prohibition repeal

The 1933 legal change ending national constitutional prohibition of intoxicating liquor.

Gangland homicide

A classification used in police or crime-commission research; not automatically a murder proven against a particular gang.

Research and Editorial Standards

Source hierarchy. Dated newspaper accounts and original court or police records rank highest for the timing and procedural status of a particular incident. A verified article in a contemporary paper shows what was published, not automatically that all allegations were true. Scholarly work by Landesco and by Binder and Eghigian provides broader interpretive context; anecdote-heavy gang histories and curated timelines remain clearly attributed. The long 1929 Illinois Crime Survey was identified through its contents and institutional research guide rather than exhaustively facsimile-collated for each episode. [10] [12] [16]

Individual responsibility. No individual is declared the convicted shooter in an attack merely because later popular histories named him. The 1927 McErlane acquittal must not be generalized to all other incidents. Police arrests, released suspects, dismissed charges and acquittals are different outcomes. [2] [18]

Organizational identity. Two unrelated O’Donnell groups are kept separate, as are the Saltis, Sheldon, Torrio–Capone, Valley, North Side and Touhy enterprises. “Ally in 1930” does not prove “co-founder in 1923.” Likewise a late visit or continued personal life does not prove the earlier gang persisted as an unchanged institution. [4] [5] [6]

Ethnicity and place. An Irish-associated criminal surname cannot represent Irish Chicago or imply collective ethnic misconduct. Violence targets individual people and businesses; a reported address is not proof of complete territorial ownership. [10] [12]

Editorial scope. This is a historically sourced encyclopedia synthesis through November 13, 2026. Uncollated original court files, exact property ownership, gang payroll, and exhaustive full-city gunshot chronology are flagged as limitations rather than hidden. A primary-source follow-up would prioritize Geis/Gorysko court materials, the 1928 garage report, the 1932 death records and the legal status of named suspects. [2] [8] [9] [19]
