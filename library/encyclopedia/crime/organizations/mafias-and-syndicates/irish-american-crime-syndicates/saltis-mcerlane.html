Overview

The Saltis–McErlane Gang was a major bootlegging and armed-enforcement network in Chicago’s South Side liquor economy during United States Prohibition. Named for Joseph “Polack Joe” Saltis, a beer distributor and gang leader, and Frank McErlane, an exceptionally violent associate frequently treated as a co-leader, the group supplied illegal beer to saloons and speakeasies in and around the stockyards district. It confronted the South Side O’Donnell brothers over customers in 1923 and later fought men tied to the Sheldon–Stanton interest. Its history was entangled with, but not reducible to, the better-known Torrio–Capone organization. [1] [2] [3]

The hyphenated label is a useful retrospective designation. Researchers do not agree that there was a corporation-like two-man partnership possessing a single unchanging territory, command ladder, and membership roster from 1920 through 1932. Binder and Eghigian deliberately call the earlier entity the Saltis gang, explain McErlane’s co-leading role, and reserve McErlane gang for a later group active after his departure. This article follows those changes rather than collapsing several organizations into one. [3] [4]

Among the strongest near-period evidence are John Landesco’s 1929 Illinois Crime Survey, a 1927 TIME report identifying two slain Saltis associates, and the published Illinois Supreme Court decision People v. Saltis confirming a concealed-weapon conviction. Each source proves a different category of fact: contemporary characterization, a reported death, or a court judgment. The violence attributed to Saltis and McErlane often exceeds what can be reconstructed as a specific criminal conviction. Editorial cutoff: November 13, 2026. [1] [5] [6] [7]

The name joins a businessman-like liquor supplier and a gunman whose notoriety has sometimes eclipsed the structure of the business. It does not establish that every homicide attributed to McErlane was ordered by Saltis, that every Saltis customer was a member, or that the gang was an incorporated enterprise or formal Mafia family. [3] [4]

Names, Spellings, and Organizational Labels

Period newspapers variously rendered the principals’ names and nicknames. Saltis appears as “Joe,” “Joseph,” and “Polack Joe”; his ethnicized nickname belongs to the historical record but is not a neutral contemporary description of his ancestry. McErlane’s name appears with inconsistent spellings in some digitized news copy. Likewise “Sheldon,” “Stanton,” and “South Side O’Donnell” refer to factions whose membership and alliances changed. [2] [4] [8]

The MGU article title follows the familiar catalog form Saltis–McErlane while identifying alternative organizational conventions in the text. A contemporary newspaper can establish that a name was then used, but it cannot by itself prove a membership rule. The term gang here means a repeatedly reported criminal business and enforcement association—not necessarily a legally defined entity with enduring bylaws. [1] [3]

Prohibition and Chicago’s Beer Economy

The Volstead Act and national Prohibition began in 1920, creating a powerful market for beer sold through illicit retail channels. Saloon proprietors, licensed businesses that ceased lawful liquor service, clandestine drinking rooms, breweries, freight routes and distribution vehicles all became economically important. A distributor’s greatest strategic asset could be a reliable base of establishments willing—or compelled—to buy from him and not a competing brewer. [1] [6]

The coercive features were central. A beer outlet could be pressed to alter suppliers with threats, assault, or demonstrations of force. Distribution conflicts became local armed contests because control of a few dozen customers could change recurring cash receipts. Scholars caution, however, that the traditional picture of Chicago carved into fixed permanent fiefdoms exaggerates the precision of surviving information about criminal markets. [1] [3]

These transactions were embedded in ordinary neighborhoods. Drivers, bar workers, bystanders, patrons and their families bore risks that rarely appear in theatrical crime accounts. An encyclopedia needs to explain the underlying commercial incentives without presenting intimidation or murder as a legitimate competitive technique. [3]

Back of the Yards and the South Side Market

The Saltis–McErlane group is most strongly associated with beer outlets in the south and southwest of Chicago, including communities adjacent to the Union Stock Yards. The phrase Back of the Yards refers to a distinct residential-industrial neighborhood; it should not be used as a synonym for the entire South Side. Railways, factories, working-class housing and many retail outlets produced overlapping opportunities for licensed trade, clandestine trade and illicit alcohol delivery. [1] [2]

Allan May reconstructs a Saltis-controlled market separate from the Torrio-sponsored suppliers that operated elsewhere in the metropolitan area. Some later summaries go further by assigning Saltis exclusive and exact borders. The former proposition is a defensible structural interpretation; the latter requires independent delivery records and a period map that has not been assembled for this edition. [2] [3]

A shooting near an intersection establishes the event’s locality, not ownership of every surrounding block. The article uses named intersections chiefly for incidents and avoids inventing a continuous geographic polygon of control. [1]

Joseph “Polack Joe” Saltis: Business Leadership

Joe Saltis emerged in contemporary reporting as a prominent Chicago beer operator with a reputation for supplying drinking establishments and maintaining armed allies. His prominence is not merely a feature of later gangster literature: in March 1927 TIME explicitly identified Saltis as the head of one major South Side bootlegging gang, while the Illinois Supreme Court identified him personally in a 1927 appeal arising from carrying a concealed revolver. [5] [7]

Saltis’s precise immigrant background is variously described in popular biographies; spelling, birthplace and ancestral classifications should be checked against civil and immigration records before being used to define the ethnic composition of his business. The period nickname “Polack Joe” is not a reliable genealogical statement. He may have commanded commercial resources without personally conducting every act of violence attributed to his organization. [6] [7]

Later retellings describe significant brewing resources, employees and retail accounts. This article avoids publishing specific fortunes, brewery ownership percentages and saloon counts absent original business or tax records. Saltis’s role can be established as a recognized participant in the illegal beer economy without attaching an invented balance sheet. [1] [2]

Frank McErlane: Earlier Criminal Career

Frank McErlane entered the public record before his partnership with Saltis. Allan May summarizes a 1913 conviction connected with automobile theft, imprisonment in Pontiac, and later legal trouble in the 1910s. These retrospective facts provide context for his later reputation but should not be turned into a perfect chronology of every charge, sentence and incarceration without consulting individual court and prison registers. [2] [8]

McErlane is routinely described as an extraordinarily dangerous gunman. That reputation appears in both near-period criminological commentary and later true-crime narratives, but epithets are assessments rather than criminal verdicts. The distinction matters because an individual accused of many murders might face dismissals, acquittals or no charges in particular cases. [1] [8]

The available evidence favors viewing McErlane as a heavily armed, operationally influential associate whose status could change when alignments shifted. His criminal career after leaving Saltis belongs partly to a later and different organization, not automatically to a continuous Saltis–McErlane command. [3] [4]

Formation of the Saltis–McErlane Partnership

Later histories connect Saltis’s distribution business with McErlane’s armed protection during the early years of Prohibition. The pairing combined access to beer retail clients with an ability to impose costs on competitors. Yet no partnership agreement, meeting minute, payroll register or founding charter has been produced showing precisely when the two men began cooperating or what share of receipts each received. [2] [3]

The organization is more securely visible through incidents and named associates in the years from 1923 to 1927 than through a dramatic single founding episode. Political influence, neighborhood associations and opportunistic joint ventures could have helped the partners, but neither Saltis’s business access nor McErlane’s violent reputation proves membership in a national hierarchy. [1] [3]

For this reason, the article treats co-leadership as a useful interpretation grounded in later criminal-history research and keeps formal executive roles, permanent staff numbers and revenue splits as unanswered questions. [3] [4]

Ralph Sheldon and the Changing Supplier Network

Ralph Sheldon led a separate criminal interest that competed and at times cooperated with the Saltis organization. Academic researchers identify the Sheldon–Stanton mob as a distinct gang by the middle 1920s and note that its early participants had been part of the broader Saltis environment before separating. This matters for casualty counts: a death attributed to the later Sheldon group does not necessarily belong on a stable Saltis roster. [3] [4]

The rivalry was not linear. In 1923 Sheldon-linked and Saltis-linked men reportedly opposed the O’Donnells’ efforts to force saloon operators to purchase from a new supplier. Later, fighting among the earlier allies intensified. Shared interests on a particular night did not eliminate business rivalry or establish a permanent merger. [2] [8]

The historical task is to specify the date of each relationship: competitive proximity, temporary cooperation, former associate, and violent enemy are different claims requiring different sources. [3]

The South Side O’Donnells: Distinct Rivals

The South Side O’Donnell Gang under Edward “Spike” O’Donnell and associated brothers attempted to enter the existing South Side beer market following Spike’s return to public life in 1923. Accounts portray O’Donnell agents as pressing saloonkeepers to abandon Saltis-connected and Sheldon-connected suppliers. The resulting hostility was chiefly over access to recurring beer accounts, not ethnic loyalty or a citywide political program. [2] [8]

This O’Donnell enterprise must not be confused with the separate West Side O’Donnells under Klondike and Myles O’Donnell. Their shared surname is a recurring trap in Chicago gang indexes. Neither group’s actions should be assigned to the other, and evidence of a temporary alignment against a Saltis rival does not establish a single consolidated O’Donnell family across the city. [2] [9]

The Saltis–McErlane story is thus connected to the South Side O’Donnell profile, but the two entries remain organizationally independent. The 1923 Beer War was an encounter between commercial rivals, not a proven transfer of control from one “family” to another. [2] [3]

September 1923: The South Side Beer War

The retrospective chronology reconstructed by Allan May identifies September 7, 1923 as the outbreak of a consequential sequence of confrontations. An O’Donnell party visited saloonkeeper Jacob Geis, described as a customer of Saltis’s operation, and assaulted him after his refusal to change beer suppliers. Associated violence later that evening led to the killing of Jerry O’Connor and additional retaliatory events. [2] [8]

The specific sequence and alleged participants derive largely from historical reconstructions that assemble earlier press accounts. They should not be taken as an indictment or trial transcript. It is secure that the event is part of the contemporary history of coercive beer competition; determining the exact weapon, every shooter’s identity and criminal guilt in each instance requires separate original files. [1] [2]

The label Beer War also shifts in scope across sources. It may indicate the immediate Saltis–Sheldon–O’Donnell clash or the wider multi-year violence among Chicago brewers and distributors. A date assigned to one local battle should not be used as the official start of every Prohibition confrontation in Chicago. [3]

Jacob Geis, Nicholas Gorysko, and Injured Workers

The Geis incident demonstrates how the beer conflict reached beyond famous gang members. Geis reportedly suffered grave head injuries during the saloon confrontation; bartender Nicholas Gorysko was also injured after intervening. These victims were retail workers caught between distributors and should be identified in any serious organizational account, not reduced to anonymous scenery in a story of competing criminal chiefs. [2] [8]

Some accounts disagree about the weapon used in the assault. That is a useful reminder that detailed retellings may combine divergent newspaper descriptions. The case establishes alleged intimidation of a business customer and the immediate consequences of refusing a supplier; it does not establish that the saloon was owned by Saltis or that a written monopoly contract existed. [2]

A historical article should preserve the distinction between sustaining injuries, being identified as the target of an attack, and proving the guilt of named assailants. The publicized crime may be real even when the evidentiary chain is inadequate for a definitive list of convicted perpetrators. [3]

Klepka’s Saloon and Jerry O’Connor’s Death

May’s reconstruction continues the September 1923 sequence at Joseph Klepka’s saloon, where O’Donnell-linked men reportedly gathered after pressuring other retailers. A party linked to Ralph Sheldon and Daniel McFall confronted the group; Jerry O’Connor died amid the resulting shooting. Retrospectives identify McErlane among persons in the surrounding violence, though accounts of exactly who fired the fatal shot vary. [2] [8]

The episode became part of McErlane’s violent legend, but an investigator’s suspicion is not a judgment of guilt. A saloon visit was not necessarily a formal gang executive session; even recurring hangouts did not create a proved headquarters for each faction in the exchange. [2]

Violence at one establishment could nonetheless prompt rapid retaliation, new temporary alliances and coercive pressure on other customers. The case shows why a liquor-supply dispute could become a sequence of interpersonal shootings far beyond the initial customer complaint. [3]

The 1923 Aftermath and Limits of Murder Attribution

Later accounts identify George Bucher and George Meeghan among O’Donnell-affiliated victims in the aftermath of the 1923 fighting. They also describe arrests, witness difficulties and legal proceedings that did not produce a simple roll of convictions matching the sensational descriptions of the conflict. The correct encyclopedia treatment recognizes the deaths and disputed claims without pronouncing every person mentioned in police theories a convicted killer. [2]

This is especially important for McErlane, whose reputation often supplies an all-purpose explanation for deaths in his wider orbit. Homicide counts assembled by scholars frequently differentiate a gang member killed, an accomplice allegedly involved, and a homicide legally proved against a particular defendant. The distinctions are not technicalities: they determine whether the profile describes documented history or inflated folklore. [3] [4]

No integrated Saltis–McErlane murder ledger has been identified for this edition. The article therefore attributes individual events to their actual level of support rather than producing a fictional account of an official hit department. [1] [3]

Breweries, Trucks, and Customer Accounts

Retrospective research describes Saltis as supplying his own accounts rather than simply purchasing all beer through Torrio’s distribution chain. Such independence gave the group commercial leverage, but the exact arrangement could include contractors, rented premises, outside breweries and wholesalers. Owning a brewery, buying its output, financing it and providing security for deliveries are different activities that many narratives casually collapse. [2]

Illegal beer routes relied on dependable repeat customers, storage, transport and protection from seizure. If a competing gang persuaded an outlet to change suppliers, the loss could be recurring rather than a one-time shipment. That logic explains why coercion focused on individual saloonkeepers and why neighborhood-level alliances shifted quickly. [1] [2]

No reliable year-by-year accounts, audited revenue series or comprehensive truck fleet register is available in the consulted evidence. Numbers applied to Capone’s wider liquor economy must not be presented as the smaller Saltis network’s proven receipts. [3]

Territories, “Satellites,” and the Capone Question

The Saltis–McErlane interest appeared in the same market as suppliers aligned with Johnny Torrio and Al Capone. Some accounts call it the most notable southern territory not directly supplied by Torrio; others highlight Saltis’s periodic peace initiatives and relationships with factions opposed to Capone. These descriptions are compatible with a degree of operational independence and shifting political calculation. [2] [3]

Neither a meeting with Capone nor a temporary effort to end a beer war proves that Saltis became an inducted Chicago Outfit lieutenant. Similarly, fighting an Outfit-associated distributor does not make Saltis a permanent ally of the North Side Gang. Organizational classification requires more than a citywide map with neat colors and permanent alliances. [3] [4]

For MGU, the Saltis organization should be indexed as a separate Prohibition bootlegging network while being cross-referenced to the Outfit, O’Donnell interests, Sheldon–Stanton faction and North Side gang where the relationship is documented. [2] [3]

Firearms and the Machine-Gun Legend

Frank McErlane is widely credited in crime histories with early use of the Thompson submachine gun in Chicago’s beer wars, particularly the September 25, 1925 attack directed at Spike O’Donnell near 63rd Street and Western Avenue. The shooting and its association with the period’s escalation of automatic-fire violence are prominent in accounts of the South Side conflict. Definitive identification of an individual shooter and an absolute first-use claim are separate, more demanding questions. [2] [9]

A “first machine gun” assertion depends on what counts as a gangster attack, what earlier police or newspaper records survive, and whether the weapon was correctly identified. A source-supported formulation is that the event was among the earliest frequently cited Thompson attacks in Chicago gang history. It is not evidence of exclusive ownership of such weapons by the Saltis group. [2]

Binder and Eghigian’s homicide analysis notes that shotguns were also common in killings associated with gang members and that contemporary violence did not consist exclusively of spectacular machine-gun battles. Technological novelty should not displace attention to victims, police evidence and legal consequences. [3]

The September 25, 1925 Attack on Spike O’Donnell

Retrospective accounts place the attempt on Spike O’Donnell near a drugstore at the intersection of 63rd Street and Western Avenue, where gunmen in an automobile fired toward him. He survived. The incident strengthened McErlane’s reputation because later histories repeatedly identify him as the attacker or link him closely to the ambush; a final adjudication of that personal responsibility is not established in the reviewed record. [2] [9]

The event belongs to the Saltis–McErlane organizational story because it arose from competition over South Side distribution routes. It also belongs to the separate O’Donnell entry as an attempt on that group’s leader. Cross-referencing one shooting in two profiles is legitimate; representing both gangs as one organization is not. [2] [3]

Reports about the episode differ in details of the weapon and personal involvement. The article uses the date and general circumstances without converting every colorful account into a courtroom finding. [2]

1926: John “Mitters” Foley and the Sheldon Conflict

As conflict with the Sheldon interest escalated, the death of John “Mitters” Foley became a major legal and public event. Accounts date his fatal shooting to August 6, 1926 and identify Saltis associates as suspects. The underlying fight had included attacks on men linked to McErlane and rival gunmen; the killing should be described in this context without treating every accusation as established. [2] [8] [10]

A crucial legal distinction is that Saltis was reported acquitted in the Foley murder prosecution. Contemporary and near-contemporary writing places the alleged murder charge and the later firearm case in different proceedings. An acquittal cannot be silently converted into a conviction, and the existence of one separate weapons conviction is not proof of homicide guilt. [5] [10] [11]

The rivalry also illustrates the difficulty of static membership classification. Individuals described as Saltis supporters in one period could be opponents in a later conflict; any casualty analysis should locate them in the actual month and faction, not only the gang label attached years afterward. [3] [4]

Hymie Weiss and the Complexity of Alliances

Some gang histories describe Saltis as cultivating relations with elements of the North Side Gang led by Hymie Weiss during the middle 1920s. These ties overlapped the broader Torrio–Capone and O’Banion–Weiss conflict. They help explain why a South Side commercial rivalry could become entangled with shootings elsewhere in the city. [2] [8]

Yet North Side presence at a trial, a lawyer’s personal association or a rumored joint shipment does not prove a durable governing coalition. The Chicago gangs interacted as businesses, enemies and occasional negotiators. Even the word alliance needs to be attached to a period and a specific issue. [3] [4]

The assassination of Weiss in October 1926 belongs principally to the North Side–Capone history. It is contextual here, not a murder this article assigns to the Saltis–McErlane organization. [3]

1926 Attempts at a Citywide Truce

Chicago press accounts described peace conferences and apparent arrangements to restrain gang violence in 1926. The parties were trying to reduce losses, preserve illicit revenue and prevent police crackdowns. Such talks could involve intermediaries whose loyalties did not match modern organizational charts. The status of any negotiated truce was fragile, and later shootings undermine claims of lasting settlement. [5] [3]

A March 1927 TIME dispatch referred to the collapse of an earlier October peace arrangement after the deaths of Saltis associates. The source documents how national journalism understood the conflict at that moment; it is not an audited account of every signer, condition, or breached term. [5]

The repeated failure of truces shows the limits of portraying an all-powerful centralized Chicago syndicate before the early 1930s. Groups could negotiate jointly, ignore terms selectively and continue to compete within the same industry. [3]

The 1927 Illinois Supreme Court Decision: People v. Saltis

The most unambiguous legal source directly involving the gang’s principal is People v. Saltis, 328 Ill. 494 (April 20, 1927). It records Saltis’s municipal-court conviction for carrying a concealed revolver and an appeal challenging the constitutionality of the state firearms statute. The Illinois Supreme Court rejected the constitutional challenge and affirmed the judgment. [6]

This decision must be cited for what it determines, not as a judicial history of Saltis’s entire beer business. It did not convict him of the Foley murder, validate the police’s catalog of alleged killings, or establish a complete list of employees. The court’s treatment of statutory exemptions and equal protection is legally significant in its own right. [6]

A reader should also distinguish the decision date from later rehearing or enforcement dates in legal databases. The court decision is conventionally identified as April 20, 1927; subsequent litigation and delay in serving a sentence are separate chronology items. [6] [11]

The Concealed-Weapon Sentence and Delayed Incarceration

Contemporaneous accounts and a 1929 local-paper discussion described delays while Saltis contested his concealed-weapon punishment and faced additional liquor-law scrutiny. A retrospectively published Capone biography recounts a sixty-day Bridewell term and a fine, with successive attempts to appeal the conviction. The appellate holding supplies direct confirmation of the conviction; the exact incarceration chronology needs dated jail records for full verification. [6] [10] [11]

The point is not that police never pursued Saltis. Rather, there were identifiable legal actions amid frequent public claims of impunity. Combining an acquittal in one case, a confirmed conviction in another and unresolved charges in a third into a single “found guilty of everything” narrative is methodologically unsound. [6] [11]

The surviving pattern also shows the relationship between sensational enforcement publicity and administrative outcomes. Arrest, charging, bond, conviction, affirmation, delayed commitment and release all belong to different stages of criminal procedure. [6] [11]

March 1927: Frank Koncil and Charles Hrubek

A contemporaneous TIME article dated March 21, 1927 identified Frank Koncil and Charles Hrubek as members of Saltis’s bootlegging organization and reported their deaths in a coordinated automobile attack the previous week. The source’s immediacy makes it valuable for identifying the two deceased men and their reported association at the time. [5]

The report linked the attack to renewed rivalry after an attempted truce. It also illustrated how newspaper crime writing could mix precise named deaths with sweeping estimates of citywide gang profits and lawlessness. The individual deaths are more readily documented than a complete reconstruction of the killers, orders and strategic objectives. [5] [3]

Later studies count the Saltis gang’s killed members within a larger classification of distinct Chicago factions; those scholarly totals should not be inflated by adding every person who died in other organizations’ conflicts. [3] [4]

November 1927: The Killing of Frank Herbert

An Associated Press dispatch carried by Oregon’s Capital Journal on November 22, 1927 described the death of Frank Herbert, brother of Earl “Big” Herbert, who was identified as a bodyguard to Saltis. Another contemporary report, published in the Daily Iowan the next day, instead described Herbert as having been shot by police responding to a saloonkeeper’s complaint. The sources therefore cannot support a definite assertion that rival gangsters killed him. These accounts provide a named victim and an important reported connection to the Saltis milieu. [12]

It does not demonstrate that Frank Herbert was a formally enrolled gang member or identify his killer beyond the police theories reported at the time. The distinction between a bodyguard’s brother and a verified member matters when assembling casualty registers. [12] [3]

Violence aimed at relatives or associates could transmit intimidation even when the person killed was not a principal in the illegal beer business. A responsible account should not erase these differences for the sake of a simplified gang chart. [3] [12]

McErlane and the Thaddeus Fancher Case

McErlane faced an Indiana murder prosecution concerning the 1924 killing of lawyer Thaddeus Fancher. Allan May reports that he was acquitted on November 3, 1927. The case is important because the acquittal is distinct from the public image of McErlane as responsible for an extensive series of killings. [2] [8]

Some retrospective reports identify a witness who died before trial and describe the prosecution’s difficulty proving the charge. Those narratives should not be used to invent a judicial finding that McErlane ordered the witness killed, nor to ignore the verdict. A legal charge, police belief, surviving witness statement and jury decision are different sources of historical knowledge. [2] [8]

The Fancher matter also took place outside Chicago’s ordinary beer-distribution market. It concerns McErlane’s individual criminal history and allegations; unless a record proves joint organizational direction, it should not be classified automatically as a Saltis-issued business killing. [3]

One-Way Rides and the Limits of Gangland Lore

Later accounts frequently credit McErlane with inventing the “one-way ride,” the forced automobile transport of a victim to a killing site, often linking the expression to the death of rival Morris Keane. A February 1933 retrospective newspaper account repeated this characterization. Such descriptions are historically important because they show the reputation attached to him, but “invention” is a much stronger historical claim than participation in or association with one incident. [8] [13]

Binder and Eghigian’s homicide study provides a useful empirical correction: it describes automobile abduction killings as a substantial but minority pattern, not a uniquely McErlane innovation or the standard form of every Chicago gang killing. A crime technique can appear in police records without having a demonstrable single inventor. [3]

The article therefore treats McErlane’s supposed invention as a traditional attribution. It does not supply a precise first date or an unverified confession, nor infer Saltis’s direct order in every incident associated with the method. [3] [8]

Profit, Payroll, and Membership: What Is Known?

The organization’s enduring public image combines a prosperous beer operator and a formidable armed staff. But neither full annual receipts nor a reliable organization-wide headcount has been independently established here. Reported “armies” of gangsters sometimes include temporary enforcers, adjacent allied gangs or saloon customers whose connection to Saltis was commercial rather than membership-based. [1] [3]

A careful financial history would require brewery leases or title records, alcohol seizure inventories, delivery sheets, ledgers, bank deposits, tax cases and testimony from identified employees. A named gunman photographed with Saltis may be an associate without being part of every subsequent transaction. Because the record is incomplete, this profile does not invent company-like departments, salary schedules or profit-sharing percentages. [3] [4]

The distinction is analytic, not apologetic. A criminal business could make substantial money and exert coercive power while leaving only a highly fragmented documentary trail. The correct historical response is to mark the gap, not to fill it with the wealth of the better documented Chicago Outfit. [1] [3]

Policing, Corruption, and Court Records

Landesco’s 1929 crime survey connected Chicago’s organized-crime problem to lucrative illegal alcohol commerce, the weakness of prosecutions and relationships between criminals and public institutions. The report is significant contemporary research but should be read as a broad diagnosis of the city rather than proof that a particular Saltis associate paid a named officer on a particular day. [1]

For the Saltis organization, the published concealed-weapon appeal is a stronger case-specific record than a general assertion of universal immunity. Conversely, arrest notices and newspapers describing unprosecuted homicides are evidence of a troubled investigative environment but not automatically proof of individual official bribery. [6] [11]

Future archival work should locate municipal-court dockets, Cook County criminal records, the Illinois Crime Commission material and police homicide case files to reconcile the dispositions of each named prosecution. The encyclopedia will not substitute a dramatic general narrative for those missing case-specific findings. [1] [3]

Ethnicity, Immigration, and Why the Article Is in This Collection

MGU indexes this group under Irish-American crime syndicates because McErlane’s historical placement is within the Irish-American gangster tradition and because the directory groups several Chicago Prohibition organizations. That editorial location must not be mistaken for a claim that Saltis himself was Irish, that the entire membership shared one ancestry, or that any ethnic community endorsed the gang. [2] [4]

Saltis is described in many sources as an immigrant from Central Europe; his nickname was a period ethnic label of imprecise meaning. Individual ancestry is a biographical question that should be answered with birth and immigration records, not by reasoning backward from a nickname or gang category. [2]

The criminal enterprise is best understood as a mixed personal and economic network. Its members’ illegal actions are not representative of the Chicago communities, laborers or immigrants among whom they operated. [1] [3]

Rivalry versus Temporary Cooperation

The Saltis organization’s many relationships are easy to misstate if a single static diagram is used. The South Side O’Donnells threatened customers in 1923. Sheldon-linked men could help resist that threat, yet Sheldon’s gang later became a major enemy. In a wider city conflict, individual associates were reported alongside North Side figures and the organization sometimes sought accommodation with Capone. [2] [3]

No single account supports turning all these episodes into a permanent bipolar “Capone versus Saltis” war. Rather, several groups negotiated, battled and separated as commercial opportunities and criminal leadership changed. A criminal alliance must be dated and its subject specified; supplier cooperation is not the same thing as merger or induction. [3] [4]

This relational method also prevents errors in cross-referencing: the later McErlane organization did not remain permanently identical to Saltis’s early network, and the South Side and West Side O’Donnell groups were distinct enterprises. [3]

1928–1929: Pressure on Saltis and the Mature Beer Market

By the late 1920s, the Saltis group had survived the first major South Side clashes but faced legal pressure and repeated turnover among violent associates. The concealed-weapon proceedings moved through appeals, and reports of additional Prohibition-law enforcement brought Saltis into the public eye. A 1929 newspaper account described his eventual service of a short sentence and further trouble associated with liquor and gambling equipment. [6] [11] [14]

These episodes should not be treated as proof that the entire beer business ended on the date of a particular arrest. Criminal markets often continued through intermediaries while a leader faced temporary detention; conversely, continued personal notoriety does not prove organizational health. [3]

A separate question is when Saltis’s own business ceased to be a major competitor. The published sources support a gradual erosion through attrition, enforcement, and changing alliances, not a verified one-day transfer of every South Side outlet to another organization. [2] [3]

The Saltis–McErlane Split Around 1930

One of the most important chronological corrections appears in the scholarly work of Binder and Eghigian. Their study identifies a Saltis gang from which McErlane departed by approximately 1930, and a later separate McErlane gang for analytical purposes. Popular gangster histories use the original hyphenated label for both men across their entire careers, but doing so blurs an actual change in association. [3] [4]

Some retrospective accounts attribute the separation to disputes over earnings, patronage or control. The existence of the later distinct faction is better supported than the precise personal conversation or contract that ended the earlier relationship. No document examined here establishes the exact day the split took effect. [2] [3]

Thereafter, a 1930 shooting involving McErlane is not automatically an operation ordered by Saltis. Organizational identity must be reconstructed at the time of the event, especially amid alliances with the O’Donnells and the emergence of other South Side groups. [3] [4]

February 1930: McErlane’s Hospital Shooting

McErlane survived an especially publicized gun attack while hospitalized in Chicago in February 1930. A surviving newspaper listing confirms that the Chicago Daily Tribune front page of February 25 carried a prominent report of the shooting. Later reconstructions describe McErlane returning fire from his sickbed; precise details of the attackers’ identities and original medical observations should be checked against contemporaneous reporting. [8] [15]

This episode illustrates how personal conflict and attempted murder continued even after the Saltis partnership began to unravel. It should not be read as proof that Saltis directed the attackers or that the original business remained unchanged. At the time, McErlane’s own relationships were shifting toward other South Side factions. [3] [8]

The associated story of John “Dingbat” O’Berta and Sam Malaga being found dead after the attack appears in later reconstructions. Police suspicions about responsibility must remain attributed, not represented as a concluded judicial verdict. [8] [13]

O’Berta, Malaga, and Retaliation Claims

John “Dingbat” O’Berta (also written Oberta or O’Berta) appears in reports of Saltis’s circle and in later stories surrounding the 1930 hospital shooting. His association underscores that Chicago bootlegging organizations could overlap electoral ambition, street violence and personal disputes. Sam Malaga is named in retrospective accounts of the same sequence of disappearances and deaths. [2] [8]

Sources vary in the dates, precise sequence and extent to which each incident can be traced to a named perpetrator. An alleged “one-way ride” is not itself a court finding about who ordered a death. The article does not assert that Saltis personally authorized revenge or that every man called an associate had an unchanged rank in the network. [8] [13]

For a future primary-source edition, the appropriate materials are death certificates, coroner inquests, police homicide registers and original press facsimiles for the specific dates—not a generalized list of gangster killings reused across multiple websites. [3]

McErlane and Spike O’Donnell after the Break

By 1930, McErlane was described in press reporting and later scholarly analysis as associated with the very South Side O’Donnell faction he had previously fought. Binder and Eghigian suggest he may have been a co-leader with Spike O’Donnell around 1930–1931 before further fragmentation. This does not require a dramatic secret merger: enemies in illegal markets could become partners when personnel, competition and opportunity changed. [3] [4]

The realignment is a key reason for giving the Saltis–McErlane group an organizational endpoint distinct from McErlane’s death. A biography centered solely on the gunman can run until 1932; the earlier Saltis-led partnership cannot automatically be stretched across his subsequent alliances. [3]

The adjacent South Side O’Donnell and Sheldon–Stanton entries should use dated cross-references rather than contradictory permanent friendship or rivalry labels. [3] [4]

1931: Independent McErlane and Other Offshoots

The 2013 homicide study distinguishes a McErlane gang active from approximately 1931 from his older Saltis association and from the temporary O’Donnell-linked combination. It also discusses the Downs–McGeoghegan–Quinlan (DMQ) group as part of the evolving South Side structure. These classifications are analytic choices designed to prevent later casualties from being assigned to an obsolete organizational configuration. [3] [4]

A name change in a historian’s dataset does not mean that every gunman formally signed a new membership roll on January 1 of that year. It recognizes real fragmentation in patronage and violent networks. For MGU, a potential separate McErlane successor article would require its own dated roster and evidence, not merely a copy of this profile. [3]

The important result is continuity of some individual actors amid discontinuity of the organization. It is entirely possible for the beer business to decline, personal violence to continue, and surviving associates to move into other criminal enterprises after the original Saltis arrangement lost coherence. [3]

October 8, 1932: McErlane’s Death

Contemporaneous reporting confirms that Frank McErlane died of pneumonia at Beardstown, Illinois, on October 8, 1932. The La Grande Evening Observer carried a wire report that day. A later February 1933 press retrospective expanded his final illness into a dramatic gangster obituary and reviewed many alleged violent acts. [13] [16]

The contrast between the same-day death notice and later narrative is methodologically useful. The fact, date and general cause of death are relatively secure; vivid descriptions of hospital behavior, precise numbers of murders and claims that he invented particular killing methods should be attributed to the later reporting rather than presented as verified clinical or judicial evidence. [13] [16]

McErlane’s death did not constitute a documented dissolution vote by the Saltis organization, which had already changed shape. His later career and personal end belong to the history of Chicago violence, but the group named in this article is most strongly documented in the preceding 1920s. [3]

Saltis after the Partnership

As his earlier network lost influence, Saltis became a less central participant in public accounts of Chicago’s criminal market. Retrospective biographies describe his later residence in Wisconsin and an eventual 1947 death, but the full legal and business chronology of those years has not been independently reconstructed from civil records for this article. [2]

The fading of newspaper interest is not equivalent to documentary proof that every associate retired or that all of Saltis’s property passed directly to Capone. Former customers could change suppliers, individual workers could take new jobs, and violent associates could join competing networks. Without an audited succession record, there is no basis for precise corporate-style transfer dates or percentages. [3]

Saltis’s documented court record and historical appearance in the beer wars remain central irrespective of the often poorly sourced stories about his fortune or retirement. [6] [7]

Deaths, Injuries, and Community Consequences

A gang history centered exclusively on bosses neglects the ordinary costs of coercive beer markets. Saloonkeeper Jacob Geis and bartender Nicholas Gorysko were attacked over a supplier conflict; a number of named Saltis associates were killed; and other workers, relatives and bystanders were exposed to armed retaliation. Historical responsibility should be attached to actual injuries, recorded deaths and properly qualified suspects. [2] [5] [12]

Binder and Eghigian examined the Chicago Crime Commission’s 729 recorded “gangland-style” killings in Cook County during 1919–1933 and found that many had no direct connection to organized criminal groups. Their careful coding cautions against automatically turning every body found in a Prohibition neighborhood into a Saltis–McErlane victim or perpetrator. [3]

The study identifies six Saltis-gang members killed in its particular classification. This is a historical dataset figure, not a total of all deaths caused by the organization or a roster of persons whom a court found Saltis to have murdered. [3] [4]

What the Homicide Data Actually Establish

The historical homicide study used Chicago Crime Commission records, police reports and newspapers to revisit a widespread assumption that Prohibition killing consisted almost entirely of warfare between famous gangs. Its findings are more nuanced: many killings classed as gangland-style did not arise from major organized crime, and many homicides did not involve bootlegging at all. [3]

The authors explicitly separate the Saltis gang, Sheldon–Stanton group, South Side O’Donnells, later McErlane organization and other derivatives. Their casualty totals are therefore categories defined for research, not counts that can be multiplied together or assigned to one supposed supergang. The paper also explains that some events may be omitted when bodies were recovered outside Cook County. [3] [4]

A defensible encyclopedia profile should offer the relevant method and its limits. It should never borrow a citywide figure such as 729 and assert that all were Saltis-related murders. [3]

Allegations, Acquittals, and Convictions

Three legal statements must remain separate. First, Saltis was publicly accused in connection with the Foley killing but later accounts report an acquittal. Second, the Illinois Supreme Court directly affirmed his concealed-firearm conviction in 1927. Third, McErlane’s acquittal in the Indiana Fancher case is distinct from the many other crimes attributed to him. None of the outcomes can be substituted for another. [6] [8] [11]

Contemporary journalism may reliably record a death date while falsely assigning responsibility or repeating police speculation. A later historian can correct those errors but may also depend on incomplete microfilm or the same original account recycled through several books. The article’s register identifies which type of source supports each kind of claim. [1] [3]

No fictional confession, guilty plea, definitive shooter list or appellate reversal is supplied. Where the original court file has not been collated, the entry says so. [6]

Historical Significance

The Saltis–McErlane organization is important precisely because it complicates the familiar history of Prohibition Chicago as a binary conflict between Capone and the North Side Gang. South Side beer suppliers maintained their own customer lists, armed associates and rivalries; sometimes they cooperated with a more powerful faction without surrendering their identities. The 1923 Saltis–Sheldon–O’Donnell confrontations reveal an independent theater of conflict with a commercial center of gravity. [1] [2] [3]

It also demonstrates the dangers of biography-driven gang history. McErlane’s sensational reputation for machine-gun attacks and “one-way rides” can obscure Saltis’s business leadership, the changing positions of Sheldon and the O’Donnells, and the later division of McErlane’s affiliations. A network history produces a more accurate picture than a succession of lurid personal anecdotes. [3] [8]

The best historical baseline combines near-contemporary crime surveys, dated reporting, a verified appellate decision and the later scholarly analysis of homicide data. Where those sources disagree, the article records the limits rather than forcing a false degree of certainty. [1] [3] [6]

Landesco’s 1929 Gang Analysis: Geography and Evidence

John Landesco’s Illinois Crime Survey provides unusually direct analysis of the named group. In Chapter XXVII, “A Who’s Who of Organized Crime in Chicago,” he reports assembling a criminal identification catalogue from newspapers, police bulletins, records and interviews. He explicitly lists the Saltis–McErlane gang among the groups analyzed and warns that police identification files were too incomplete to sustain confident calculations for every organization. [17]

Landesco notes that the Saltis–McErlane, O’Banion and Klondike O’Donnell gangs had too few police records for separate statistical charts and were analyzed together. The survey characterized Saltis–McErlane associates’ histories as especially marked by murder prosecutions, which is not the same as murder convictions. It located their residential concentration south of the center and west of Halsted Street, consistent with the gang’s liquor distribution area. [17]

The survey also described most individuals in these named gangs as Chicago-born despite Saltis’s own reported immigrant origins. These observations concern selected people represented in the survey, not a census of all associates. They provide valuable near-contemporary checks on simplified narratives, but their authors openly acknowledged gaps in police records and classification. [17]

Northwestern Homicide Case Records

The Northwestern University Homicide in Chicago, 1870–1930 archive includes a coded case record for John “Mitters” Foley, listing his August 6, 1926 death near 65th and Richmond and recording the dispositions. The case summary states that Saltis and Frank Koncil were acquitted November 9, 1926, while proceedings against other named suspects were subsequently stricken. That is firmer evidence than retellings that call the men his convicted killers. [18]

The same archive has a record for the March 11, 1927 double killing of Charles Hrubec (also known as Hayes) and Frank Koncil near West 39th Street, noting that the assailants escaped and were not arrested at the scene. One archival note uses a different street number from the tabular address, an example of why the source should be cited as a case summary rather than treated as a perfectly reconciled reconstruction. [19]

Northwestern warns that its ethnicity coding was sometimes inferred from surnames and is not consistently reliable. Accordingly, this article relies on the archive for dates, case disposition and reported case relationships but does not reproduce its inferred ethnicity as genealogical fact. [18] [19] [21]

Terminology

Beer War

A series of violent disputes over illicit brewery output, deliveries and saloon customers; not a single formally declared war.

Bootlegging

Illegal production, transport, distribution or sale of alcohol under Prohibition.

Beer territory

A claimed commercial market of establishments supplied or intimidated by a particular network; boundaries may shift.

Speakeasy

An establishment serving alcohol illegally during Prohibition.

Strong-arm enforcement

Coercion, threats or violence used to impose a commercial arrangement or discourage rivals.

One-way ride

A term for forced automobile abduction associated in later narratives with gang killings; not proven to have been invented by McErlane.

Thompson submachine gun

A repeating firearm associated with Prohibition gang imagery; precise first-use stories require corroboration.

Gang affiliation

Reported connection to a named group; not necessarily a verified membership roll or legal finding.

Rival supplier

A competitor for recurring beer delivery and saloon accounts, with or without a formal gang.

Acquittal

A criminal trial disposition in which the defendant is not found guilty; distinct from dismissal, conviction or a conclusion about all historical allegations.

Stricken off

A recorded case disposition or removal from an active docket; its meaning requires attention to local procedural usage.

Retrospective attribution

A claim made years later about an earlier event; should be distinguished from eyewitness or judicial evidence.

Organizational succession

A demonstrable transfer or continuation of an actual group, not simply an individual moving among gangs.

Irish-American crime syndicates

MGU research category; does not establish that every member of every included network was Irish-American.

Research and Editorial Standards

Priority of sources. The 1929 Landesco survey and its separately digitized Chapter XXVII, the Illinois Supreme Court opinion, Northwestern homicide cases, and dated near-period press accounts take priority for narrow factual propositions. Later crime historians and a peer-reviewed retrospective homicide study provide synthesis and explicitly marked interpretations. [1] [3] [6] [17] [18]

Limits of primary collation. The public archival material was reviewed in digital textual form where available; this edition has not exhaustively collated all original police files, trial transcripts, certified vital records, and facsimiles of every newspaper. The complete survey volume was not exhaustively reviewed page by page; the separately hosted Chapter XXVII text was consulted for gang-specific analysis. The exact source limitations are recorded rather than concealed. [1] [17]

Legal accuracy. The 1926 Foley acquittal and the 1927 concealed-firearm conviction are different proceedings. McErlane’s Fancher acquittal and the accusations connected with other killings are also separate. Police suspicion, indictment, press report and criminal finding are never synonymous. [6] [8] [18]

Ethnic and organizational restraint. Individuals do not inherit ancestry from a gang label. Landesco’s record selected a non-exhaustive sample of men and warned that the existing identification files were incomplete. The bibliography marks overlapping publication versions by one researcher as a shared evidentiary base, not as independent corroboration. [3] [8] [17] [21]

Future archival work. Priority follow-up includes original 1923 police and inquest files; the October–November 1926 Foley trial transcript; the March 1927 Koncil–Hrubec homicide file; McErlane’s Indiana Fancher criminal case; the February 1930 hospital-shooting report; and post-Prohibition civil records for Saltis. [2] [18] [19]
