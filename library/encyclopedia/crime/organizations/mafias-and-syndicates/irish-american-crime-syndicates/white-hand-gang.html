Overview

The White Hand Gang was a label applied to Irish-associated street and waterfront criminal groups in Brooklyn during the early twentieth century. At different times, police and newspapers also described a Lovett gang, a Lonergan gang, the Red Onions, and other dockside factions. The name refers to a real pattern of criminal association and violent competition, but it should not automatically be read as one corporation-like organization with a single uninterrupted membership or formal succession list. [1] [2] [3]

The most prominent individuals in the familiar account are Dennis “Dinny” Meehan, William “Wild Bill” Lovett, and Richard “Peg Leg” Lonergan. Their deaths—in 1920, 1923, and 1925 respectively—became milestones in waterfront crime reporting. Yet the documentary record did not close with Lonergan’s murder: newspapers described people fighting over Lovett-linked rackets in 1928, 1930, and 1931. The history therefore requires a distinction between a named gang’s best-known leadership era and the later persistence of related waterfront factions. [1] [4] [7] [8] [9]

Contemporary reports and municipal crime-scene records are especially valuable for specific arrests, places, and killings. They are less conclusive about who ordered a murder, whether all suspects belonged to a single gang, or how profits were divided. This article compares surviving newspaper transcriptions and archival materials with later historical writing and labels unresolved matters explicitly. The editorial research cutoff is November 13, 2026. [1] [7] [10] [3]

The Name “White Hand” and Its Limitations

The name is commonly explained as a response to the Black Hand label associated with extortion and threatening letters in Italian immigrant neighborhoods. This explanatory contrast is repeated in modern gang histories. However, “Black Hand” was itself a widely used description of an extortion method and of several groups, not a single nationwide syndicate. Consequently, the names do not prove that two unified, ethnic armies fought one another for decades. [2] [10] [12]

The label “White Hand” was already being used in Brooklyn crime reporting before Prohibition. The surviving notices describe local youths and neighborhood assailants; other reports describe waterfront chiefs and dock rackets years later. A shared name and geography may suggest continuity of personnel or reputation without establishing one constitution, one founding ceremony, or a central committee. [1] [3]

The name also carried ethnic boundary-making. Irish-identified criminals sometimes portrayed Italian rivals as outsiders; later narratives magnified that antagonism. Recording such prejudice is different from adopting it as the encyclopedia’s framework or depicting Irish and Italian communities as collective participants in criminal violence. [10] [3] [12]

Brooklyn Waterfront Geography and Neighborhood Context

South Brooklyn’s docks, warehouses, cartage routes, taverns, and tenement districts formed a dense working landscape. Red Hook and Erie Basin were major settings for shipping-related work, while criminal episodes involving the same wider milieu were reported around Warren, Hoyt, Court, Baltic, Sands, York, Bridge, Jay, and Nassau streets. Contemporary location references indicate multiple interconnected pockets of activity, not proof of a gang monopoly over all those blocks. [1] [3] [8]

Longshoremen, stevedores, freight drivers, and warehouse workers handled goods that moved through the harbor. Loading appointments, control over work crews, the ability to block competitors, and gambling around the docks all created opportunities for coercion. Most waterfront labor was legitimate. Accounts of extortion by particular intermediaries should not be generalized to the work force or its ethnic communities. [1] [8] [13]

Local historian and novelist Eamon Loingsigh describes Red Hook, the Warren Street “Red Onion” milieu, the Jay Street area, and other neighboring groups as a loose collection of waterfront gangs. This is a valuable warning against imagining one White Hand headquarters governing every South Brooklyn group; it is a modern reconstruction, not a surviving membership ledger. [3]

Early Evidence and the Problem of Founding Dates

There is no verified founding charter or unambiguous evidence naming one founder of the White Hand Gang. Local retrospective research places references as early as 1905; a transcribed May 1906 Brooklyn Standard Union item reports that police called Thomas Towey a member of the White Hand gang. Those notices establish a period of use for the label, not the precise date of organizational formation. [2] [3]

A New York Times notice dated May 15, 1910, separately reports arrests of two alleged White Hand members after an attack on John McDonnell and his wife. The paper identifies Henry Engelhardt and John Gibney as suspects. The summary does not establish convictions, leadership rank, or whether their associates were the same individuals later active on the docks. [14]

Some modern accounts say Meehan created the group in response to Italian racketeers. As a historical proposition, that requires evidence tying his early career to founding decisions. The contemporary record demonstrates that he was a recognized Brooklyn gang leader by 1920, but this is not proof he originated the White Hand name in the first decade of the century. [1] [4] [3]

The 1910 Court Street Attack and Joseph Boyle

A report published on October 14, 1910, described the killing of engineer Joseph Boyle near Court and Baltic streets and serious injuries to James Donahue. According to the newspaper, Boyle intervened when men attacked Donahue near a saloon on Court Street. The article attributed the assault to members of the “White Hand Gang” and identified a man known as “Peter the Buck” among the alleged participants. [1] [5]

This report is particularly important because it supplies an early, place-specific gang attribution outside the later Meehan–Lovett–Lonergan leadership mythology. It is still a newspaper account of allegations: the assembled source does not include verdicts for each reported assailant. The death of an intervening bystander also illustrates the human costs of intimidation beyond rival gang members. [1] [5]

No evidence reviewed establishes that every named defendant in this episode later joined a dockside syndicate or that Boyle’s death was part of a war against Frankie Yale. The incident belongs to the gang-name’s earlier documented neighborhood context. [1] [10]

Dockside Revenue, Extortion, and Street Crime

The clearest later economic pattern was the coercive capture of value around the piers. Reports from the late 1920s and 1930 describe men who levied tribute on ship loaders, influenced access to loading jobs, and sought control of gambling among workers. These practices could produce cash without the elaborate hierarchy of a national criminal family. [1] [8] [9]

The contemporary record also describes robbery accusations, cargo-theft indictments, assaults, and shootings. In the 1920 report about Dennis Meehan, police referred to allegations involving a stolen automobile truck filled with shoes and a warehouse silk theft. These were pending indictments and police accusations at the time of his murder, not convictions for every charge. [4]

Later popular descriptions sometimes give the White Handers a monopoly over narcotics, smuggling, or the entire harbor. These claims are not independently demonstrated in the source set used here. This profile prioritizes the rackets for which contemporary accounts identify a mechanism or particular alleged incident. [1] [8] [10]

Dock Labor, Hiring Power, and Coercion

Waterfront power was partly a matter of who could organize or obstruct labor. A person controlling a loading crew, access to a hiring point, or the conditions of an informal gambling game could pressure workers who depended on daily wages. Some leaders were described as dock loaders or stevedore czars by reporters, terms that reflect contemporary power claims rather than audited income figures. [1] [8] [13]

In explaining Meehan’s 1920 killing, police told the press that one possible motive involved his supplying strikebreakers during a United Fruit Company waterfront dispute. That was an investigative theory reported immediately after the murder; it should not be turned into an established labor policy or a finding that a named union ordered the attack. [4]

A 1931 New Yorker investigation portrayed a violent competition among waterfront strongmen. Its broad rhetoric about masculinity and “codes of silence” must be evaluated as contemporary journalism, not accepted as a universal description of dockworkers. Most harbor workers should not be conflated with the racketeers who targeted them. [13]

The Red Onions, Dock Gangs, and Overlapping Identities

Not all Brooklyn waterfront groups called themselves White Handers. Contemporary accounts also mentioned Red Onions and other dock factions, sometimes as rival organizations and sometimes in discussions of the same circle of men. In 1931, for example, police coverage spoke of “the Red Onions and the White Handers” as sources of successive leadership disputes. This phrasing cautions against presenting those labels as automatically interchangeable. [1] [9] [3]

Dennis Meehan is connected to the earlier Red Onion milieu in later local research, and several men served or contested overlapping waterfront interests. A researcher might reasonably speak of a White Hand network or Lovett-era dock factions when discussing these relationships, provided the label is marked as an editorial synthesis rather than an officially defined entity. [3]

This article therefore does not publish a roster in which every Red Onion, labor tough, alleged Italian rival, or murder suspect is enrolled as a White Hand member. Individual attribution depends on its particular source. [1] [9]

Dennis “Dinny” Meehan and the Pre-1920 Leadership

Dennis L. “Dinny” Meehan emerged in contemporaneous reports as a major Brooklyn criminal figure. The New York Times described police calling him a particularly dangerous gang leader after his murder, while noting earlier arrests and an acquittal in a shooting case. That police characterization is evidence of his reputation and the authorities’ assessment, not a full judicial history of every accusation. [4]

The 1920 reporting links him to claims about theft of shoes and silk, and to a dispute over strikebreaking. These details reveal the mixture of property crime, dock work, and personal enmities surrounding his career. They do not establish that he owned the entire Red Hook waterfront or created a formally incorporated White Hand organization. [4]

The popular sequence Meehan → Lovett → Lonergan captures three prominent names but glosses over a more fragmented environment. Contemporary accounts also name Garry Barry and other contenders in successive waterfront power struggles. The page records the familiar sequence as a guide, not a verified complete list of bosses. [1] [8]

Meehan’s Murder on March 31, 1920

Meehan was shot to death in his home on Warren Street on March 31, 1920, as reported the following day. The published account says his wife Sadie Meehan was wounded and that the assailants entered the family apartment while a child and Sadie’s mother were nearby. The presence of family members is part of the historical record and demonstrates that the violence exposed noncombatants to direct danger. [4]

Police suggested several possible motives, including a dispute over a theft prosecution and tensions involving strikebreakers. The immediacy of those theories does not establish the perpetrators’ identities. Later gang histories frequently attribute Meehan’s death to specific rivals or to Lovett himself, but the consulted materials do not supply a dispositive court judgment identifying who ordered or committed the killing. [4] [3]

Because his death is well documented, the uncertainty is not whether Meehan was killed but who was responsible and whether the murder transferred a single, unified command. The latter proposition cannot be resolved simply by naming the next famous gang leader. [1] [4]

William “Wild Bill” Lovett and the Shifting Dock Balance

William J. “Wild Bill” Lovett became a prominent figure among Brooklyn waterfront gunmen after the First World War. Modern biographies describe him as a decorated veteran; the precise military decoration and service record have not been independently checked against Army personnel documentation for this article, and that detail is not used to explain his authority. [10] [3]

A contemporary report from October 31, 1919, says police arrested Lovett and Charles Donnelly after Samuel Angelo was wounded at a Sands Street restaurant. That is an arrest report, not evidence by itself of conviction or an organizational order. By May 1923, press coverage explicitly described Lovett as a leader or alleged leader of a violent Brooklyn gang. [1]

The later depiction of Lovett as an uncontested ruler of every Brooklyn pier is too strong. He had influence and enemies; the press simultaneously described battles among multiple bands and individuals. A criminal leader could be feared in one district while still facing rival claimants within the same waterfront world. [1] [13]

Lovett Shot in January 1923

On January 3, 1923, Lovett was found with three bullet wounds in a room off Front Street and taken to a hospital. The next day’s newspaper account reported that he would not identify his attacker. Police considered the shooting related to gang conflict, and the initial prognosis was grave. He nevertheless survived. [1]

The episode is often retold as proof of an inflexible gang “code.” What the contemporaneous record establishes is more limited: Lovett refused to answer particular questions while wounded. Silence could reflect retaliation fears, personal loyalty, or other motives; it is not evidence of a written oath binding every alleged White Hander. [1] [13]

Multiple Killings and Police Action in 1923

May 1923 saw a series of Brooklyn shootings reported in connection with contending waterfront groups. In a May 2 saloon incident, postal employee Timothy Quilty was killed and his brother James Quilty was injured. Later that month, reporting described the deaths of Frank Healy and another James Martin amid a broader feud. [1]

Lovett and Richard Lonergan appeared in the resulting arrests and questioning. A newspaper account of May 28 reported their detention in a homicide inquiry. Such references document law-enforcement action and competing allegations; they do not entitle a later writer to treat every arrested person as a convicted murderer. [1]

The contemporary witness accounts and police theories in the period disagreed about motives and loyalties. They expose the limits of retrospectively drawing a tidy two-sided White Hand-versus-Italian war from what newspapers often described as quarrels among local dock factions. [1] [3]

The Killing of Wild Bill Lovett, 1923

Lovett was discovered dead on the morning of November 1, 1923, in the rear of a Bridge Street premises used by dock loaders. A New York Times report published November 2 described serious head injuries and bullet wounds. The medical estimate placed the assault several hours before discovery, probably during the early morning. Some retellings therefore date the event to the night of October 31; the contemporaneous discovery date is November 1. [1] [6]

Police investigated people who had been with Lovett and described competing theories about how attackers entered the premises. Later writers sometimes name particular Italian gunmen and detail the exact weapons used. Unless supported by a directly verified case file or judicial finding, these statements remain attributed theories, not an adjudicated account of the killers. [1] [10]

Lovett’s widow Anna Lonergan subsequently appears in newspaper narratives about successive waterfront chiefs. Those relationships are relevant to tracing the political and familial context of the dock gangs, but they do not make her the operator of every gang or an accomplice in her husbands’ deaths. [1] [9]

Richard “Peg Leg” Lonergan and the Mid-1920s

Richard “Peg Leg” Lonergan was identified in 1925 contemporary news as a leader of the White Hand gang. He was connected by family and association to the Lovett circle; reporting also connects him to the broader Red Onion milieu. His nickname and the prominence of his final shooting have made him the most recognizable late White Hand figure after Lovett. [1] [7] [8]

The label last boss appears in popular histories because Lonergan died in 1925. It is misleading if understood to mean no waterfront gang using Lovett’s name or pursuing related rackets survived thereafter. The subsequent McGuire, Donnelly, and Martin stories reveal continuing disputes over dock loading and gambling, even if they do not prove an unchanged White Hand command. [1] [8] [9]

A precise roster of Lonergan’s followers, his authority over specific piers, and his relationship to each contemporary Irish gang remains unverified. The group should not be supplied with fabricated captains, initiation rules, or a territory map more exact than the sources allow. [1] [3]

Frankie Yale, Italian Rivals, and the Limits of a Two-Sided War

The historical narrative commonly pits White Handers against the Italian-American underworld faction associated with Francesco “Frankie Yale” Ioele. Michael Newton’s modern biography of Yale describes an extended contest for the Brooklyn docks. It is strong contextual research for the rival organization, but its publisher’s overview alone does not identify who committed particular killings. [10]

Conflict over hiring access, goods movement, illicit commerce, and neighborhood prestige could bring Irish- and Italian-associated criminals into violent contact. Yet contemporaneous waterfront coverage also includes rivalry within Irish-associated factions. The evidence therefore does not support an uninterrupted, unified ethnic war in which all Irish criminals belonged to the White Hand and all Italian criminals answered to one boss. [1] [9] [10] [3]

The familiar opposition of “White Hand” and “Black Hand” can also obscure the nature of Black Hand activity: historians describe it as a set of extortion practices rather than necessarily a single secret society. The postwar American Mafia and the early Brooklyn White Handers should be examined as different organizational phenomena. [12] [15]

Ethnicity, Nativism, and Community Boundaries

The White Hand label was often presented through Irish-American identity, and some later retellings attribute anti-Italian language or conduct to specific men. The anti-Italian rhetoric in several accounts of the Adonis Club confrontation forms part of the historical allegation, not language the encyclopedia endorses. Where stories about exact words or a planned racial provocation lack stable eyewitness documentation, they should remain qualified. [7] [3]

Both Irish and Italian immigrant neighborhoods contained workers, families, businesses, mutual-aid institutions, and law-abiding residents; a gang faction was not their collective representative. The social setting helps explain why ethnic shorthand appealed to sensational journalists, but it cannot substitute for case-specific evidence. [10] [3] [15]

For this encyclopedia, “Irish-American” is an editorial classification of an associated criminal network. It does not establish the ancestry of every participant or imply that competing groups shared fixed racial or ethnic memberships. [1] [3]

The Adonis Social Club Killings, December 26, 1925

The most thoroughly documented event in the group’s popular history occurred in the early hours of December 26, 1925, at the Adonis Social Club on Twentieth Street in Brooklyn. Richard Lonergan and two other men were killed during a shooting at the club. The New York City Municipal Archives identifies the other victims as Aaron Haines and Neil Ferry in an NYPD homicide photograph caption; contemporary transcripts print variant spellings including Heins, Harms, and Neils “Needles” Ferry. [1] [7] [17]

The Archives gives the location as 154 Twentieth Street, whereas at least one contemporary newspaper transcription gives 152 Twentieth Street. This article follows the municipal-archives location for its main identification and preserves the discrepancy here rather than silently harmonizing it. [1] [7]

Contemporary newspapers described a chaotic holiday-night disturbance, a deserted room, and several people injured. Later tellings add speeches, carefully coordinated lights-out attacks, and dramatic exchanges between Irish and Italian patrons. Those added details may reflect later testimony or narrative embellishment; they are not reproduced here as a reliable minute-by-minute transcript. [7] [16] [17]

Arrests, the Capone Allegation, and Court Outcomes

Alphonse “Al” Capone and several others were detained in connection with the Adonis Club homicides. The New York City Municipal Archives states that Capone was arrested but not ultimately charged in connection with the triple murder. A contemporary January 1, 1926 court report, as transcribed in a Brooklyn local-history collection, says murder charges against nine suspects were dismissed at a preliminary hearing; the men then faced assault allegations arising from a surviving victim. The two accounts differ in how they summarize early procedural stages and should not be flattened into a definitive participant-by-participant charge history without the court docket. [1] [7]

The distinction is essential: arrest, suspicion, arraignment, dismissal, and conviction are different legal statuses. Neither the municipal archive account nor the 1926 transcript establishes that Capone, Yale, or a named associate was convicted of murdering Lonergan and his companions. The familiar assertion that a particular mastermind “ordered” the club massacre is therefore reported as an allegation, not as a proved historical judgment. [1] [7] [10] [16]

Samuel Leibowitz’s representation of Capone became part of later accounts of the case. A 1932 New Yorker retrospective describes the defense and the difficulties encountered by investigators, but it does not transform a failed prosecution into proof of the identities of the shooters. [16]

What Survived after Lonergan?

A conventional summary gives 1925 as the end of the White Hand Gang. That year marks the death of a prominent chief and a major weakening of his circle, but not the disappearance of all the associated rackets. Police and newspapers continued to describe remnants of the Lovett–Lonergan faction and rival dock leaders through the early 1930s. [1] [8] [9]

The danger in identifying “the White Hand” across decades is that the press may have recycled a famous label for changing combinations of men. The continuing competition over loading rights and gambling suggests institutional and economic persistence, but specific surviving lines of authority cannot be reconstructed without a roster, financial documents, or unbroken participant testimony. [1] [13]

This article accordingly treats 1925 as the end of the best-known Meehan–Lovett–Lonergan era, not as a demonstrably final dissolution date for the broader Brooklyn waterfront networks. [1] [8] [9]

Edward McGuire and the November 1928 Dock Killing

A New York Times report dated November 5, 1928, described the shooting of Edward McGuire, portrayed by police as a former Lovett lieutenant, on a pier near Brooklyn Bridge. The newspaper told a vivid story of a leadership contest decided by dice immediately before gunfire. That narrative is a contemporary report based partly on witnesses and police descriptions; the precise sequence remains an account, not a court-established reconstruction. [1] [11]

McGuire’s death is significant because it directly contradicts the simplistic proposition that the Lovett faction had no successor conflict after December 1925. It is also a warning about source transmission: some modern recaps incorrectly place the episode in 1929, even though the cited newspaper story is from November 1928. [1]

McGuire should be identified as a reported contender linked to the Lovett gang, not automatically installed as a verified White Hand boss for a specified tenure. [1]

Charles “Red” Donnelly and Waterfront Tribute in 1930

A contemporaneous Associated Press report printed on January 29, 1930, described the shooting death of Charles “Red” Donnelly at a Brooklyn pier. The report identified him as a former Lovett lieutenant and connected the gang’s revenue to payments extracted from stevedores and the control of local gambling. [8]

Police theory and press phrasing portrayed Donnelly as one of a series of waterfront chiefs killed in succession. Such descriptions are useful for mapping the persistence of criminal competition after Lonergan but are not proof that Donnelly inherited an unaltered 1906-era organization. [1] [8]

The late-1920s and 1930 dock wars belong in a comprehensive White Hand historical entry because they reveal the afterlife of its personnel and rackets. They do not justify adding every later waterfront murder to a single formal gang homicide count. [1] [8]

Matthew “Matty” Martin and the 1931 Succession Struggles

In December 1931, reporting identified Matthew “Matty” Martin as a Brooklyn waterfront gang leader and described a shooting that fatally wounded him. The press also identified him as a later husband of Anna Lonergan, Lovett’s widow. The account ties Martin’s story to the same social and criminal network but depicts immediate circumstances that police did not initially classify as a straightforward continuation of an ethnic gang war. [1] [9]

The December 1931 coverage specifically referred to “Red Onions and the White Handers,” reinforcing the distinction between remembered labels and current factions. Martin died from the wounds, and the aftermath generated renewed investigative attention. His death is a strong terminus for tracing the known linked leadership stories, not evidence that every associated individual disappeared. [1] [9]

The continued use of the Lovett designation in reports extending into 1932 further underscores why an encyclopedia must differentiate the end of one leader’s tenure from the total disappearance of a criminal network. [1]

Police Investigations, Court Records, and Attribution

The historical record includes numerous arrests, grand-jury inquiries, court appearances, and public police theories. They provide named people and dates but often fall short of identifying convicted perpetrators. Police suspicion that a feud caused a particular shooting cannot be equated with a demonstrated criminal conspiracy. [1] [4] [7]

The 1910 Boyle attack, the 1920 Meehan murder, the 1923 Lovett homicide, and the 1925 Adonis Club massacre illustrate different evidentiary problems. The first is a contemporary attribution to a gang; the second and third contain multiple possible motives; the fourth has a documented crime scene and suspects but lacks a proven assignment of responsibility in the sources reviewed. [1] [4] [7]

Contemporary reporters often accepted a person’s silence as proof of an “underworld code.” Such silence may instead have been caused by fear, community distrust of police, or weak witness protection. It is a phenomenon to be studied, not a substitute for admissible identification of an offender. [9] [13]

Organization, Membership, and Succession

The common boss list has explanatory value but should not be mistaken for a complete constitution. Brooklyn’s White Handers were not a documented counterpart of the later Italian-American Mafia’s formal hierarchy of boss, underboss, consiglieri, captains, and inducted soldiers. The evidentiary material suggests adaptable crews, local affiliations, and temporary leadership claims around economic opportunities. [1] [3]

Neither a fixed membership count nor verified admission rituals, dues, rank insignia, or treasury ledgers are established here. The evidence is better for names of particular men associated by police or newspapers, and for recurring sites of crime, than for continuous membership between 1906 and 1932. [1] [2] [3]

The distinction matters when evaluating “succession.” Meehan, Lovett, and Lonergan were influential; McGuire, Donnelly, and Martin were later linked in reports to rival leadership struggles. Those associations do not prove an uninterrupted chain of command or that the White Hand label meant exactly the same thing in 1906, 1925, and 1931. [1] [8] [9]

Historical Significance and the End of the Waterfront Era

The White Hand history illustrates an intermediate stage between neighborhood street violence and more specialized waterfront racketeering. Its clearest recurring economic subjects were access to dock work, protection payments, and gambling. The shifting identity of the gangs challenges models that assume all twentieth-century organized crime operated as a centralized national structure. [1] [8] [13]

The story is also important for the history of Brooklyn’s changing ethnic relationships. Competition with Italian-associated criminals was one component of a larger set of conflicts that included Irish-on-Irish factional violence and personal disputes. The prominent 1925 massacre is a watershed, not a complete explanation of either the preceding gang history or the later transformation of waterfront rackets. [9] [10] [3]

Later decades saw distinct Mafia-linked figures become important in waterfront corruption, but that later development is not proof that the White Handers were absorbed wholesale in a documented merger or that a named Mafia family controlled each early-century episode. Separating these chronologies prevents false institutional continuity. [10] [15]

Italian-American gangster whose circle appears in retrospective accounts of Brooklyn dock competition; no Adonis murder conviction established. [10]

Alphonse “Al” Capone (1899–1947)

Arrested in the aftermath of the Adonis killings but never charged in that triple-murder case, according to the municipal archives. [7] [16]

Edward McGuire (d. 1928)

Reported former Lovett lieutenant killed during a pier leadership dispute in November 1928. [1]

Charles “Red” Donnelly (d. 1930)

Dock racketeer and reported Lovett-era lieutenant killed at a Brooklyn pier in January 1930. [8]

Matthew “Matty” Martin (d. 1931)

Waterfront gang leader linked through marriage and earlier factional associations to the Lovett circle; fatally wounded in 1931. [9]

Joseph Boyle (d. 1910)

Engineer killed while reportedly intervening during an assault attributed to White Hand members near Court Street. [1]

James Donahue

Man injured in the 1910 incident reported alongside Joseph Boyle’s death. [1]

Evidence Assessment and Commonly Repeated Claims

ClaimEditorial assessmentBasis / limitation
“The White Hand existed in early-1900s Brooklyn.”SupportedLocal newspaper notices use the name before Prohibition, including a May 1906 transcription.
“Dennis Meehan founded the whole organization.”UnverifiedHe was a prominent gang leader by 1920; a founding act has not been established.
“Meehan, Lovett, and Lonergan were the only three leaders.”Over-simplifiedContemporary accounts mention other dock chiefs, factions, and claimants.
“White Hand and Red Onions were the same gang.”Not establishedSome reports distinguish the two labels; personnel and reputation may overlap.
“All violence was an Irish-versus-Italian gang war.”Not supportedBrooklyn waterfront reports include internal factional violence, personal disputes, and competing non-ethnic motives.
“Frankie Yale ordered every White Hand murder.”UnverifiedRetrospective attribution is not a substitute for a case-specific judgment.
“Al Capone was convicted in the Adonis shooting.”False in reviewed recordNYC Municipal Archives says Capone was arrested but not charged in the triple homicide.
“All three Adonis victims were killed December 26, 1925.”SupportedContemporary reporting and NYPD municipal-archive photo caption.
“The White Hand ceased to exist completely in 1925.”Too categoricalPress describes Lovett-linked dock factions and leadership disputes into the early 1930s.
“The name survived unchanged as one entity to 1932.”Not establishedA recurrent label does not prove identical membership, charter, or chain of command.

The assessments are about claims as phrased; an unresolved claim is not necessarily disproved. The purpose of the matrix is to keep the evidence threshold visible. [1] [7] [8] [9]

Terminology

White Handers

Period shorthand for men whom press or police associated with the White Hand Gang.

Black Hand

A historic term for extortion activities and threatening communications; not necessarily a single centrally governed organization.

Red Hook

Brooklyn waterfront neighborhood with docks and labor-intensive freight activity.

Erie Basin

Harbor and industrial waterside area associated with Brooklyn dock commerce.

Stevedore / longshoreman

Worker loading or unloading vessels; the occupation itself is legitimate.

Dock loader / boss loader

Person involved in arranging or controlling loading labor; period press sometimes used the terms in reports about coercive waterfront power.

Protection money

Payment extorted through threats or threatened interference with work or business.

Red Onions

Contemporary designation for Brooklyn dock factions, not always treated as identical to the White Handers.

Gang attribution

Claim by a source tying a person or incident to a group; strength depends on evidence and judicial findings.

Arrest / indictment / conviction

Separate procedural stages. An accusation alone is not a finding of guilt.

Prohibition

Federal ban on manufacture and commercial sale of beverage alcohol under the Eighteenth Amendment and implementing statutes, effective in 1920.

Historic continuity

Evidence that the same organization persisted across time; a reused gang name alone is insufficient.

Research and Editorial Standards

Contemporary sources. Newspaper reports from 1906, 1910, 1919, 1920, 1923, 1925, 1928, 1930, and 1931 provide much of the named-person chronology. Many New York Times items are available here through a Brooklyn genealogy collection that transcribes excerpts and can introduce typing errors or omissions. Those transcripts were inspected, but complete original newspaper page facsimiles and court dockets were not systematically collated for this first edition. [1] [2] [3] [4] [8] [9]

Archives and legal outcomes. The New York City Municipal Archives provides a high-value documentary anchor for the December 1925 Adonis shooting, including the NYPD photographic record and a description of Capone’s arrest without subsequent murder charges. The article separates witness accounts, law-enforcement suspicion, charges, and verdicts. [7]

Retrospective narratives. Newton’s biography of Frankie Yale, Loingsigh’s local research, and older crime-writing traditions provide useful interpretations but do not automatically resolve who committed the unsolved killings. Inconsistent spellings (Haines/Heins/Harms; Neil/Neils Ferry) and address details (152/154 Twentieth Street) remain identified where they matter. [7] [10] [3]

Limits of classification. The White Hand is cataloged in the Irish-American Crime Syndicates collection for historical discoverability. It is not described as the operational predecessor, division, or subordinate branch of a twentieth-century Mafia family without case-specific evidence. [10] [3] [12]

Further verification priorities. Direct inspection of original 1905–1910 newspapers, Brooklyn Supreme Court and Kings County case records, homicide files for 1920 and 1923, the 1925 Adonis case docket, and pier employment records would improve the first-edition source register. The page reflects material reviewed through November 13, 2026.

Selected References and Source Register

Numbered references identify the basis of this article. “Contemporaneous” means close in date to the event, not automatically true or complete. Some historical newspaper excerpts were accessed via a later transcriber; the article does not represent those as independently checked original facsimiles.

  1. Brooklyn Genealogy Information — “The White Hand Gang,” curated transcriptions of New York Times and Brooklyn Eagle articles, 1910–1932. Key contemporary-news record for persons, arrests, murders, and later dock feuds. The site is a later transcription collection: original page images and court files were not collated as a complete series.
  2. Brooklyn Genealogy Information — May 1906 Brooklyn Standard Union newspaper transcription. Early use of “White Hand gang” with a reported Brooklyn police attribution; transcription and attribution should be checked against the newspaper facsimile.
  3. Eamon Loingsigh — “Brooklyn’s Irish-American White Hand Gang,” The Wild Geese (July 11, 2013). Local retrospective investigation proposing a loose waterfront-gang origin and questioning over-simplified accounts; not a contemporary membership registry.
  4. New York Times — “Gunman Murdered Asleep with Wife” (April 1, 1920), transcribed in Brooklyn Genealogy Information. Contemporaneous account of Dennis Meehan’s murder, family injuries, pending criminal allegations, and early police theories. Accessed via a secondary transcription.
  5. New York Times — “Gang Beats Man to Death” (October 14, 1910), transcribed in Brooklyn Genealogy Information. Source of the Boyle and Donahue incident and an explicit early White Hand attribution; not itself a judicial verdict.
  6. New York Times — Lovett shooting and killing reports (January 4, May 27–28, and November 2, 1923), transcribed in Brooklyn Genealogy Information. Near-contemporary sequence separating Lovett’s January survival, 1923 police inquiries, and death discovered November 1; original page-by-page collation outstanding.
  7. New York City Department of Records and Information Services — Stuart Marques, “Prohibition” (March 8, 2019), NYPD photograph 7890c. Municipal-archive account identifying the December 26, 1925 Adonis shooting, victims, photo caption, and absence of murder charges against Capone.
  8. Capital Journal (Salem, Oregon) — Associated Press report on Charles “Red” Donnelly’s death (January 29, 1930). A contemporary syndicated account of 1930 waterfront leadership conflict, stevedore tribute, and gambling.
  9. New York Times — “Gang Chief Is Shot in Brooklyn Row” and “Martin, Gang Chief, Dies of His Wounds” (December 15 and 17, 1931), transcribed in Brooklyn Genealogy Information. Late primary-era references to Red Onions, White Handers, Matthew Martin, and Lovett-related waterfront figures; interpret attribution cautiously.
  10. Michael Newton — Iceman of Brooklyn: The Mafia Life of Frankie Yale (McFarland, 2021). Published researched biography of the rival Italian-American criminal milieu. Publisher synopsis and bibliographic contents confirmed; complete text not collated for this article.
  11. New York Times — “Brooklyn Gang Leader Is Slain by Four Rivals Soon After Winning Title by Throw of Dice” (November 5, 1928), transcribed in Brooklyn Genealogy Information. Source for Edward McGuire’s 1928 dock killing; documents that this event should not be misdated to 1929.
  12. Robert M. Lombardo — The Black Hand: Terror by Letter in Chicago (University of Illinois Press, 2009). Academic context for Black Hand as extortion activity rather than one centrally controlled universal organization; Chicago comparison, not proof of Brooklyn affiliations.
  13. Alva Johnston — “Twenty-one Stevedores,” The New Yorker (1931). Period journalism describing Brooklyn waterfront violence and its local dynamics; rhetoric and broad generalizations are treated critically.
  14. New York Times — May 15, 1910 newspaper issue, article summary indexed by NewspaperArchive. Secondary index of an early White Hand assault report; not an independently checked image of full legal proceedings.
  15. United States Postal Inspection Service — “Black Hand Gang,” historical overview. Federal institutional account of Black Hand extortion letters; background to the naming contrast, not evidence of a single Italian syndicate.
  16. The New Yorker — “Let Freedom Ring—II” (June 11, 1932). Retrospective account of Samuel Leibowitz, Capone, and the 1925 Adonis Club investigation; not a court judgment establishing the gunmen.
  17. Houston Post-Dispatch — United Press dispatch on Brooklyn’s Adonis Social Club shootings (December 27, 1925). Contemporary wire account identifying the White Hand name and Lonergan as a leader; the original paper is digitized by the University of North Texas.

Publication note: First-edition source-attributed historical synthesis. The most uncertain items remain the precise foundation and membership of the group, the identity of killers in several murders, the relationship between White Hand and Red Onion factions, and the degree to which the gang name persisted into the 1930s.
