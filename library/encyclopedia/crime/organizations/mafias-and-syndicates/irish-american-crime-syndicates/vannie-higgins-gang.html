Overview

The Vannie Higgins Gang was a Brooklyn-centered Prohibition-era bootlegging and rum-running network associated with Charles “Vannie” Higgins, a waterfront entrepreneur and recurrent figure in New York criminal investigations. Its operating world centered on Bay Ridge, the waters near the Narrows, Long Island approaches and the city’s highly competitive illegal liquor trade. Contemporary newspapers often used “gang” or “mob” for Higgins and associates; this encyclopedia uses the fuller title as a catalog label, not as the proven legal name of an incorporated criminal fraternity. [2] [3] [5] [6]

Higgins’s network should not be conflated with the older neighborhood street gangs of Five Points or Hell’s Kitchen, nor with a single Irish-American Mafia governed by permanent ranks. It was primarily a distribution, maritime logistics and protection enterprise, with associates who could also belong to independent businesses, police circles or rival groups. Much of the membership and leadership detail comes from the names appearing in arrest reports and the recollections of later historians, not from a recovered syndicate roster. [1] [3] [4] [7]

The organization’s most securely dated crises were the March 1929 Owl’s Head Tavern shootings, the violent factional disputes of 1931, the January 1932 federal conspiracy acquittals and Higgins’s fatal attack on June 19, 1932. This article distinguishes event dates, allegations, dismissals and acquittals, and conflicts in later retellings. Research and editorial cutoff: November 13, 2026. [3] [4] [5] [9] [10]

Identity and Classification

The summary that follows is not a census of known members or a guarantee of exclusive operational territory. “Irish-American” is the conventional historical classification of a network headed by Higgins; it must not be applied automatically to everyone involved in its liquor economy. [2] [6] [7]

The Meaning of “Vannie Higgins Gang”

Newspapers referred to “Vannie’s gang,” the “Higgins mob,” “rum runners,” “beer racketeers” or similar descriptions. The exact form of the organization changed over time as arrests, commercial alliances and shootings disrupted individual ties. It is not established that a single name was used continuously by insiders or that participants agreed to written membership rules. [3] [4] [5]

The word syndicate is useful for studying coordinated commercial crime; gang was the contemporary idiom for groups linked to armed disputes. Neither term is a substitute for evidence of particular transactions. A police arrest of someone thought to know Higgins is evidence of an official suspicion or association, not proof that the suspect took orders from Higgins. [1] [3] [4]

Historical summaries also style Higgins “Brooklyn’s last Irish boss.” That epithet captures perceptions of changing influence by the early 1930s, but it does not establish a recognized citywide championship, that all Brooklyn rackets belonged to him, or that organized Irish-American crime disappeared at his death. [5] [6] [7]

National Prohibition and the New York Liquor Economy

The Eighteenth Amendment and National Prohibition Act made the manufacture, sale and transportation of intoxicating beverages unlawful in the ways specified by federal law. The national enforcement regime began in January 1920. Its existence generated substantial illicit demand in a metropolis with dense commercial districts, established harbor facilities and customers willing to pay premiums for imported beverages. [1] [15]

The National Archives describes New York’s offshore Rum Row and the layers needed to bring liquor ashore: foreign or Canadian supply ships, smaller contact boats, coastal landing points, trucks, storage and wholesale distribution. Changes in maritime enforcement in 1924 affected the operating geography. Higgins belonged to this broader market, but this article does not infer that every offshore transaction involved him. [1]

Bootlegging organizations could cooperate on a specific cargo while competing for customers or protection on another. Their financial interdependence made alliances unstable; control of a boat, a garage or a group of drivers did not mean exclusive command over an entire borough. The period’s policing and courtroom records offer only partial windows into such arrangements. [1] [3] [5]

Brooklyn Origins, Family, and Uncertain Birth Date

Higgins was born in Brooklyn in the late 1890s. Older profiles generally give 1897 and describe Bay Ridge as his birthplace. Tom Hunt’s record-based review finds conflicting dates: the 1900 census supports November 1897, while Navy and death-related records suggest 1898. A civil birth certificate has not been verified in the materials consulted; an exact date of birth is therefore not asserted. [2] [6] [7]

Hunt’s family reconstruction places the Higgins household in Gowanus during his early years before a later move to Bay Ridge, where his criminal and liquor-smuggling career developed. Addresses in city directories, census schedules and family records can refine that picture. Saying he grew up entirely in Bay Ridge compresses two distinct phases of his life. [2]

Some accounts expand his name to Charles Van Wyck Higgins and explain “Vannie” as a diminutive of a middle name. The nickname is solidly reported from the late 1920s; the middle-name explanation is less firmly documented and appears prominently in national press only around his 1932 death. The page identifies him principally as Charles “Vannie” Higgins. [3] [6]

Navy Service, Waterfront Work, and Maritime Knowledge

Hunt’s review identifies Higgins as a U.S. Navy serviceman during the First World War, advancing to quartermaster third class and departing the service in 1919. The reconstruction names assignments associated with the Wanderer and a vessel transformed from Saratoga into the hospital ship Mercy. These are biographical research leads based on service records, not evidence of criminal acts during wartime service. [2]

After the war Higgins worked in cargo-handling contexts close to the Brooklyn shoreline. Knowledge of boats, navigation, coastal approaches and commercial logistics would have been commercially useful in a bootlegging market. That causal connection is an interpretation, not proof that naval training itself organized a criminal syndicate. [2] [1]

This distinction matters: military service and dock employment were ordinary occupations for large numbers of New Yorkers. The later unlawful uses made of particular skills should not be projected backward onto every fellow sailor, dockworker or neighborhood acquaintance. [2] [1]

Early Arrests and the Limitations of the Police Record

Historical biographies report assault arrests in the mid-1910s, with probationary dispositions. Other arrests accumulated as Higgins became more prominent in the rum-running era. Newspaper tallies of his arrests vary and are often compressed into the familiar formula that he was repeatedly charged but rarely convicted. [2] [4] [7]

A police arrest is not a conviction, and a dismissal is not evidence that the alleged act must have occurred. This article records the existence and reported outcomes of cases where the historical accounts specify them; it does not convert a long arrest record into a list of proved killings or confirmed gang headquarters. [3] [4] [5]

The practical challenge is source survival. An accused man could appear in a newspaper under a nickname or misspelled surname, while court dockets and police logs might use a legal name. Original docket examination is necessary before presenting precise lifetime counts of arrests, charges, acquittals, convictions or time served. [2] [14]

Bay Ridge, the Narrows, and Brooklyn Operating Geography

Bay Ridge placed Higgins near the Narrows and the approaches to New York Harbor, a strong logistical position for coastal liquor shipments. The area offered streets, garages, shore access and connections to the wider Brooklyn market. The network’s geography reached beyond the neighborhood into Gerritsen Beach, Long Beach and other places referenced in investigations. [2] [3] [4] [5]

An investigator’s reference to a specific private residence, speakeasy or garage is not proof that the premises functioned as the gang’s permanent central office. The sites should instead be assessed according to their documented use: residence, alleged meeting point, vessel access, storage, arrest scene or location of an encounter. [3] [4]

The Brooklyn waterfront was also an ordinary workplace and residential landscape. Residents and workers should not be represented as a collective part of a racketeering network merely because illegal cargo might pass through a nearby pier or landing. [1] [2]

Speedboats, the Cigarette, and the Claim of a Private Fleet

Later histories and period reports associate Higgins with speedboats, trucks, taxicabs and at least one seaplane. The motorboat Cigarette appears prominently in accounts of his fast maritime craft. References to a “fleet” reflect his reputation for coordinating substantial transport capacity; a complete vessel register and title search have not been assembled for this edition. [6] [7]

Smuggling involved movement from offshore suppliers to smaller boats and then to drivers and warehouses. The value of a fast boat lay partly in its ability to approach or evade patrols, but logistics also depended on timetables, cargo transfers, communications, storage and compliant customers. Maritime methods are described here as historical functions, not as operational guidance. [1] [3]

The known vessels and vehicles are not enough to calculate an exact number of employees, an annual tonnage or a personal fortune. Stories about an enormous criminal “empire” should be understood as estimates unless supported by seized-vessel documents, tax filings or contemporaneous accounting. [1] [6] [7]

Seaplanes, Aviation, and the Comstock Prison Visit

Accounts associate Higgins with aerial travel and a seaplane used or available within his maritime world. Public attention to his flying was unusual: the airplane conveyed wealth, technological novelty and access to destinations beyond the borough. Whether each flight attributed to him was personally piloted by Higgins should be checked against pilot and aircraft records. [5] [6] [7]

A well-known anecdote concerns his arrival by aircraft at Great Meadow State Prison in Comstock, New York, where Warden Joseph H. Wilson reportedly received him as a friend. Later stories differ in timing and detail, and Tom Hunt’s 1932 reconstruction identifies the journey close to Higgins’s death. This article records the visit as reported without asserting that the prison was an organized-crime operating base. [5] [6] [7]

The distinction between aerial transportation as a symbol of notoriety and as a documented liquor-smuggling method is important. Evidence that Higgins owned or used aircraft does not by itself document the cargo, destination and purpose of each flight. [1] [5]

Wholesale Supply, Trucking, Taxis, and Customer Networks

A Brooklyn alcohol-distribution business required more than a boat with barrels aboard. Cargo needed to be landed, moved in smaller parcels, stored, collected by wholesalers and delivered to retailers operating outside legal channels. Higgins’s reported trucks and taxicabs were suited to urban movement, but not every vehicle attributed to his enterprise can be linked to a known contraband shipment. [1] [6] [7]

The revenue chain created recurring disputes over money, delivery territory, theft, supplier obligations and police intervention. Some participants could work as chauffeurs, mechanics, boatmen or guards on a short-term basis rather than hold lifelong rank. Accounts of “Higgins men” in a specific incident do not prove continuous paid service over the entire decade. [3] [4] [5]

The article avoids fabricated profitability ratios or annual revenues. Neither the number of boats nor the possession of luxury automobiles is a reliable proxy for net income. Payments to law-enforcement personnel alleged in federal investigations likewise cannot be treated as audited spending figures without case files. [1] [5]

Big Bill Dwyer and the Boundaries of Cooperation

William Vincent “Big Bill” Dwyer was a major West Side liquor smuggler whose activities began earlier in the Prohibition decade. Later writers sometimes call Higgins a Dwyer lieutenant, while other narratives emphasize Higgins’s distinct Brooklyn operations and his connections to Dwyer’s business associates. The relationship is best described as reported cooperation in a changing market, not proven life-long formal subordination. [1] [6] [7]

Dwyer’s criminal conviction in 1926 is well documented in federal legal history and the National Archives. It does not establish that every later liquor venture connected to a former partner was under Dwyer’s direction. Higgins’s operations were sufficiently independent to appear in separate Brooklyn police and federal liquor investigations in the late 1920s and early 1930s. [1] [3] [5]

The companion encyclopedia profile of Dwyer examines offshore procurement, financing and syndicate economics in more depth. Here Dwyer is part of Higgins’s relationship network, not a substitute for tracing the named Bay Ridge participants documented in later records. [1] [6]

Owney Madden, Frank Costello, and Cross-Ethnic Alliances

Higgins was variously linked in newspaper and later historical accounts with Owney Madden and Frank Costello. These contacts reflected the larger Prohibition market’s dependence on overlapping carriers, financiers, brokers and distributors. Neither Irish ancestry nor an Italian-American surname defines the boundaries of participation in a liquor smuggling transaction. [1] [6] [7]

There is a danger of drawing a single organizational chart encompassing everyone named in retrospective gangster histories. Madden was a major West Side figure with his own business interests; Costello’s later significance in American Mafia history should not be projected backward as an official supervisory role over Higgins’s every operation. [1] [6] [7]

This profile distinguishes an association—a source reports contact or shared interests—from a partnership documented through a transaction and from an organizational command relationship established by dependable evidence. The surviving sources for Higgins support the first category far more often than the third. [3] [5]

Jack “Legs” Diamond, Dutch Schultz, and Shifting Rivalries

Contemporary reporting connected Higgins and Jack “Legs” Diamond to the disputes surrounding Brooklyn and Manhattan liquor markets. The names of Dutch Schultz, Anthony “Little Augie Pisano” Carfano and Vincent “Mad Dog” Coll appear in accounts of concurrent conflicts. But these relationships were neither uniformly friendly nor consistent across the years. [3] [4] [8]

A September 1930 TIME report describes rumors of factional warfare, including allegations of periodic clashes involving Diamond and Higgins associates against a faction linked with Pisano. A report describing rumors is evidence of contemporary perceptions, not confirmation of each supposed alliance or shooting. [8]

Secondary narratives sometimes place all four names on a united front against Schultz. That arrangement oversimplifies conflicts and timing; in some reporting Carfano is framed as a rival of Higgins. This article does not publish a permanent anti-Schultz coalition as an established command structure. [3] [4] [7] [8]

The June 1928 Bay Ridge Gunfire and Naming Evidence

Hunt identifies newspaper coverage of an incident on June 3, 1928, in which men in a taxi were fired upon near Fifty-eighth Street and Fourth Avenue, Brooklyn. Reports named figures including Martin Benson, Arthur Sorenson, William Bailey and Higgins, but differed on exactly who was present. The episode reveals how contemporary newspapers began using the nickname “Vannie.” [3]

The shooting should not be confused with the March 1929 Owl’s Head Tavern case. Some later articles date the latter to 1928, creating a misleading impression that two separate events occurred in the same year. The dated 1929 newspapers, including the March 19 New Britain Herald, provide a reliable correction. [3] [7] [9]

What these 1928 accounts establish is a public group of men associated in the press with Higgins and conflict in Bay Ridge. They do not establish the rank of each named person or identify who fired every shot. [3]

Owl’s Head Tavern, March 18–19, 1929

The most important documented armed encounter linked to Higgins’s circle occurred outside the Owl’s Head Tavern at Third Avenue and Sixty-ninth Street in Bay Ridge on the night of March 18, 1929. Accounts describe a tense meeting between Higgins and Thomas “Tommy” Connell, also called Kilduff, against a background of disputes over liquor operations. Off-duty police officer Daniel J. Maloney was present. [3] [9]

As gunfire erupted, Brooklyn Police Sergeant John J. McGarty encountered an armed Maloney, who reportedly turned and fired toward the sergeant. McGarty shot Maloney, who later died. Another man was wounded. The exact sequence was contested in early reports, but the record does not sustain the simplified claim that Higgins personally murdered Maloney. [3] [9]

Higgins and others were arrested and faced accusations. At an early-April hearing, witnesses reportedly described only the two off-duty or responding officers firing weapons; charges against the accused were dismissed for insufficient proof. The case thus illustrates the intersection of alleged bootlegging contacts and compromised policing without establishing Higgins’s criminal liability for the officer’s death. [3]

Police Protection, Favoritism, and Evidentiary Boundaries

The Owl’s Head incident immediately raised questions about whether serving officers were moonlighting as guards for bootleggers. Accounts described Maloney carrying several weapons while off duty, prompting official concern about police involvement in liquor disputes. The questions surrounding one officer do not establish that an entire police department acted collectively on Higgins’s payroll. [3] [9]

In August 1929, a search linked in press accounts to the home of former detective John P. McCarthy yielded firearms but reportedly no liquor. Robert “Whitey” Benson was also arrested on a disorderly-conduct charge. These reports provide specific evidence of policing around Higgins-connected persons, with distinct alleged offenses and dispositions. [3]

Later allegations involving the Long Beach police department would become part of a 1932 federal conspiracy prosecution. That case ended in acquittals, so its assertions about systematic payments must continue to be described as prosecution allegations or witness testimony rather than judicially proved facts. [5]

Baltimore Contacts and the Limits of Interstate Reach

Higgins had friends and reported business contacts in Baltimore, Maryland. After the 1929 Owl’s Head episode, he was briefly arrested in Baltimore following a police inquiry involving a car and unexplained blood found in a garage. The contemporary accounts did not produce proof of a specific liquor offense, and he was released. [3]

Some later biographies tell a dramatic story of Higgins being shot by police during a Baltimore trip. Hunt’s source comparison identifies a much less conclusive contemporary record: the Baltimore officers checked a report of a foot injury, found no such injury and released the man. The later story may have conflated incidents or added details not shown in the located reports. [3] [7]

A visit to another Atlantic port makes interstate contacts plausible, but it does not establish a permanent Higgins branch office or organized command over Maryland rum-running. The page avoids mapping undocumented markets from an isolated detention record. [1] [3]

Leo Steinberg, Federal Inquiry, and the Rum-Ring Story of 1930

In September 1930, newspapers described a federal investigation into a large liquor-smuggling operation associated with Leo Steinberg and other defendants. Reports spoke of hundreds of workers across land and water operations and the discovery of a radio transmitter believed to be used for contact with rum-running fleets. [3]

Steinberg was reportedly known to Higgins, while accounts of his professional and criminal relationships are complicated by conflicting retrospectives. The often-repeated estimate of 250 employees describes the alleged Steinberg conspiracy in period reporting; it must not be reissued as a verified headcount of the Higgins gang. Nor does proximity to the investigation automatically prove Higgins was indicted as one of its directing officers. [3]

Higgins became less publicly visible around the time of these developments. Later writers considered whether he was avoiding investigators, underworld rivals or both; his motive for each absence cannot be reconstructed with certainty. Economic scale, personal ties and an indictment are different types of evidence. [3] [7]

The October 1930 Attack on Legs Diamond

The shooting of Jack “Legs” Diamond in October 1930 became a magnet for stories about an expanding Brooklyn rum war. Contemporary accounts and later books proposed several interpretations involving Higgins, Steinberg, Anthony Carfano and other operators. Those claims form a record of suspected underworld motives, not a settled attribution of criminal responsibility. [3] [8]

Histories of the event have linked it to Leo Steinberg’s disappearance, alleged ransom disputes or conflict between Diamond and Higgins. Some accounts assert that Higgins directed the attack; others posit different motives or actors. This encyclopedia does not report a verified Higgins murder conspiracy based on the competing versions. [3] [7]

The case is important to organizational history because it reveals how liquidity, personal debts, overlapping smuggling routes and retaliatory violence could destabilize relationships. That is a supported structural interpretation; it is not evidence that all stories of kidnapping and ransom in the underworld were true. [3] [8]

Vivian Gordon, Police Investigations, and the Limits of Association

After Vivian Gordon was found murdered in the Bronx in February 1931, newspapers drew attention to her reported acquaintances among New York racketeers and to her possible knowledge of police corruption. Higgins appeared before a grand jury in March as investigators pursued leads. [4]

An acquaintance, name in an address book or appearance as a witness does not demonstrate involvement in a homicide. The sources consulted here do not establish that Higgins planned or ordered Gordon’s killing. This episode is presented as an investigation touching his social circle, not as a proven offense by his organization. [4]

Gordon’s death was a distinct human tragedy and a politically consequential criminal investigation; it should not be reduced to a decorative chapter in Higgins’s biography. Claims about her livelihood or character in contemporary sensational papers require scrutiny for stigma and reporting bias. [4]

The May 21, 1931 Stabbing of Higgins

In the early hours of May 21, 1931, Higgins suffered serious knife wounds during or after an encounter associated in reports with the Blossom Heath Grill in Manhattan. He was delivered to a hospital, given intensive care and later recovered. Some summaries place the incident at a different street address; contemporaneous coverage is not uniform about the exact scene. [4] [6]

Investigators focused on Higgins’s companions and on Robert “Whitey” Benson, previously described as one of his trusted associates. Witnesses reportedly implicated Benson in the attack. Higgins refused to identify the assailant publicly, and the sources examined do not establish a conclusive criminal judgment assigning responsibility. [4]

The stabbing mattered because it indicated serious trouble within a network once described as cohesive. The conclusion that an internal split was underway is plausible and echoed in press reporting; identifying the instigator and complete cause remains more difficult. A personal conflict, rival sales interests and a general gang war are not necessarily the same explanation. [4]

Gerritsen Beach, July 1931, and Arrested Associates

On July 26, 1931, authorities interrupted a liquor-unloading operation at Gerritsen Beach, Brooklyn. Period coverage summarized by Hunt names Salvatore Spitale and former detective John P. McCarthy among those arrested. This is a stronger link to a specific alleged transportation event than a generic claim that Higgins controlled every Brooklyn inlet. [4]

An arrest scene is not a certified chain of command. The articles examined for this edition do not demonstrate that all fourteen reported arrestees were full-time Higgins employees or that a court found each liable for the same wrongdoing. [4]

The episode illustrates the continuing vulnerability of coastal operations to surveillance and police action. It also anchors the participation of named figures more concretely than later broad statements that every underworld celebrity was an allied syndicate chief. [1] [4]

William Price and Edward Flannagan: October 1931 Killings

Two men identified as William A. “Bill” Price and Edward “Skip” Flannagan were found dead in Brooklyn on October 7, 1931. Their bodies had been concealed in a vehicle. Death certificates and press descriptions do not agree in every detail about injuries or the order of violence. [4]

Period reporting variously attributed these deaths to rival factions, including associates of Legs Diamond or Vincent Coll. Such claims are evidence of the underworld theories circulating in 1931; they do not prove that a particular named man ordered or personally carried out the killings. [4]

The victims appear in the historical account because investigators and newspapers placed them near Higgins-related conflicts. Their deaths are not counted here as “confirmed murders committed by the Vannie Higgins Gang,” because the legal or documentary basis for that conclusion is inadequate. [4]

Robert “Whitey” Benson: The Former Associate Killed October 26, 1931

Robert H. “Whitey” Benson was shot dead near Broome Street in Manhattan on October 26, 1931. Earlier records placed him among Higgins’s associates. Police linked the killing in their inquiries to recent tensions and the May stabbing, but other theories circulated as well. [4]

Higgins was sought for questioning and subsequently arrested, but the matter did not culminate in a proved murder conviction against him in the records reviewed. It would be inaccurate to describe Benson’s death simply as an “execution ordered by Higgins.” [4]

Benson’s life also demonstrates the limits of assigning a fixed Irish ethnic identity to the whole network. Hunt’s census-based research reports that Benson’s father was Norwegian-born and worked in maritime trades. An Irish-associated leader could operate alongside people of other backgrounds. [4]

The Fragmentation of the Higgins Circle in 1931

Taken together, the 1931 stabbing, arrests, murders and changing friendships suggest serious fragmentation. Associates once named together were later reported as enemies, victims or suspects. This is a more convincing explanation of the gang’s weakening than the notion that a fixed three-tier hierarchy simply chose a new boss whenever an individual fell. [4]

However, factional “collapse” is an interpretive summary, not a date stamped by a corporate filing or judicial dissolution order. Some boating, supplier and retail relationships could survive while personal loyalties changed. The surviving record does not give a complete map of every participant’s actions after 1931. [3] [4] [5]

Competing criminal interests, alleged political contacts and enforcement pressure all contributed to the overall instability. No particular massacre or homicide should be treated as definitively caused by “the Mafia taking over Brooklyn” unless the underlying proof is supplied and the attribution narrowed. [4] [5] [6]

January 1932 Federal Conspiracy Trial and Acquittals

A major federal case alleged that Higgins and others participated in an illegal liquor conspiracy involving corrupt Long Beach police personnel. Proceedings in Brooklyn in January 1932 included Long Beach Police Chief Morris Grossman among the defendants. Prosecutors alleged that police protected or enabled shipments and presented testimony about payments. [5]

The outcome must be reported accurately: after some defendants were removed from the case for lack of evidence, the jury on January 20, 1932 returned acquittals for the remaining thirteen defendants, including Higgins, according to the contemporary press record reconstructed by Hunt. An accusation of corrupt payments is therefore not an adjudicated conviction. [5]

This is not proof that every reported contact between smugglers and law enforcement was innocent; it is proof that the legal case at hand did not establish guilt against the acquitted defendants. The article distinguishes the prosecution’s theory, courtroom testimony, court-directed dismissals and final jury verdict rather than collapsing them into “Higgins beat the case.” [5] [14]

The May 1932 WhichOne Coast Guard Encounter

On May 3, 1932, Coast Guard vessels pursued the rum-running ship WhichOne and a separate cabin cruiser near the New Jersey shore. The Coast Guard seized the cargo ship with liquor aboard; Higgins was found on the cruiser and investigated for a possible offense. [5]

The distinction between the two vessels was legally decisive. Reports summarized by Hunt say that no liquor was found aboard the cruiser carrying Higgins, and authorities released him and two companions. It would misstate the record to claim that he was convicted or held as a crew member of the seized cargo ship. [5]

Contemporary estimates of the seized cargo’s value varied widely. They cannot be translated into a proven Higgins-owned shipment without evidence of title, financing or a distribution agreement linking him to the liquor. The case nevertheless shows the continuing prominence of seaborne smuggling in his final months. [1] [5]

Higgins and Madden in Aviation Reports, June 1932

Newspaper articles in early June 1932 described Higgins and Owney Madden’s interest in aviation, with reporters playing up the image of established New York racketeers taking to the sky. The reports provide a useful indicator of continued public visibility only days before Higgins’s death. [5]

A later prison-visit story described a flight to Great Meadow State Prison in Washington County, New York, close to the June 18 family event. The facts of a personal aviation trip should not be converted into evidence of a smuggling flight or an agreement with prison officials to operate criminal business. [5] [6]

Where exact aircraft ownership, pilot qualifications and routes would change an interpretation, the proper next sources are civil aviation records, registrations and contemporaneous photographs with provenance, rather than recycled claims about an unlimited fleet of airplanes. [5]

June 19, 1932: The Shooting Outside a Brooklyn Family Event

On the evening of June 18, Higgins attended a children’s dance program with members of his family at a Knights of Columbus clubhouse near Prospect Park West and Union Street. His young daughter Jeanne participated. The family left after midnight, in the early hours of Sunday, June 19. Gunmen opened fire near their vehicle. [5] [10] [11]

Higgins was seriously wounded. His daughter suffered a minor wound to an ear; descriptions disagree on the precise movement of attackers and victims and on the number of shots. Higgins was taken for treatment but died later on June 19. The contemporaneous New York Times issue of June 19 reported the shooting, while its June 20 issue reported his death; these are related but distinct headlines. [5] [10] [11]

The historical record should keep the family context visible rather than recast the child as a gang participant. The attack struck a father attending his daughter’s public performance and endangered other relatives and bystanders. The article does not repeat sensational “last words” as if mechanically recorded fact: reporters attributed different conversations and emphases to him as he lay dying. [5] [10]

Who Killed Higgins? Suspects, Motives, and Unresolved Attribution

Period police investigations and newspaper stories floated multiple motives for Higgins’s murder: rival liquor suppliers, former associates, competing underworld factions and grudges related to earlier killings. Later narratives sometimes assert that an Italian-American Mafia takeover required his elimination. These are competing explanations, not a settled judicial finding. [5] [6]

The claim that a single named gangster personally ordered the shooting requires case-specific evidence. The surviving materials reviewed for this page do not establish a final conviction assigning the attack to one identified organizer. Accounts of how many gunmen participated, which cars they used or precisely how Higgins was hit also differ. [5] [10]

Hunt’s reconstruction notes that contemporary newspapers soon portrayed Anthony Carfano, called “Little Augie Pisano”, as an ascendant Brooklyn racketeer. The observation that another man gained influence after the killing is not proof that he planned or commanded it. Motive and benefit alone cannot identify an assassin. [5] [8]

Family, Burial, and Immediate Public Reaction

Reports after the murder described Higgins’s funeral, the presence of family members and his burial at St. John’s Cemetery in Queens. Local newspapers treated his death as a significant turning point in Brooklyn’s underworld, with imagery of a disappearing Irish criminal leadership. The funeral coverage also continued press habits of blending private grief with public fascination. [5] [6]

Rebecca Rector’s local history account describes considerable attendance, and Tom Hunt’s newspaper research tracks contemporary commentary about successors. Specific funeral headcounts differ among retellings and are not used here as a measure of either gang membership or political strength. [5] [6]

The relatives named in family-history records, including his wife Grace and daughter Jeanne, are not presented as criminal participants. Their relevance is biographical and as victims or witnesses of the violence surrounding the final attack. [2] [5]

The End of the Higgins-Led Network

Higgins’s death in June 1932 removed the central public figure from an already fractured organization. Some individuals who had worked with him continued in liquor, waterfront or other activities, but the sources consulted do not show a formally appointed successor heading an unchanged “Vannie Higgins Gang.” [4] [5]

It is therefore defensible to describe the Higgins-led network as effectively ended with his death, while leaving open whether particular business arrangements continued under new sponsors. The later growth of other Brooklyn racketeers cannot be described as a documented legal acquisition of his entire enterprise. [5] [6]

The repeal of national Prohibition on December 5, 1933 occurred more than a year after Higgins died. It transformed the illicit market but did not itself produce the 1932 shooting. Liquor was subject to new regulatory and tax systems rather than simply vanishing as a commercial business. [12] [15]

Irish-American Classification and Mixed Underworld Networks

Higgins is widely called an Irish-American crime boss. The descriptor is useful for cataloging his place within a history of Irish-associated racketeering in New York, but the ethnicity of a leader does not make every shipmate, policeman, supplier, lender or rival part of an all-Irish entity. [2] [4] [6]

The network’s associated names span varied family backgrounds. Partnerships crossed ethnic lines because liquor supply, access to boats, local political protection and paying customers mattered to enterprise survival. The use of an ethnic category must not imply that Irish immigrants as a group ran the city’s illegal liquor trade. [1] [3] [4]

The more general historical process was one of shifting criminal markets and personal coalitions. A popular account that “Irish gangs were replaced by Italians” conceals simultaneous organizations, collaboration and conflicting forms of evidence. No tidy succession chart for Brooklyn’s entire waterfront is justified by Higgins’s killing. [5] [6] [7]

Violence, Rumor, and the Problem of Assigning Criminal Responsibility

Higgins’s career intersected violent competition, but historical attribution must be event-specific. The Owl’s Head case involved a police officer shot by another officer; the Price and Flannagan murders involved victims linked in reports to changing gang factions; Benson’s killing generated an unsuccessful homicide inquiry into Higgins; and Higgins himself was the victim of a fatal ambush. [3] [4] [5]

A short gangster biography often collapses all such incidents under a single “gang war” label. That shorthand obscures the distinction between suspicion, police theory, witness statement, charge, dismissal, acquittal, civil death record and conviction. The source register here provides pathways for revisiting those individual records. [3] [4] [5] [9]

Neither the mere presence of armed men nor association with a suspected bootlegger establishes that a particular murder was ordered by a criminal organization. The article avoids invented body counts, undisclosed hit lists and confident attributions for unresolved crimes. [4] [5]

Organization, Membership, Financing, and Decision-Making

Higgins’s operation likely relied on a core of recurring associates, transport workers and flexible commercial partners rather than a highly bureaucratic hierarchy. Names supported in particular press records include William Bailey, Robert Benson, John P. McCarthy, Salvatore Spitale and others. The nature and duration of each relationship varied. [3] [4] [5]

No definitive surviving register shows the exact number of members, a standard salary, a fixed territory map, a formal initiation system or a named underboss. A former detective and a seagoing employee might both appear among “Higgins men” yet have fundamentally different roles. Titles imported from later Mafia organizations would misrepresent the evidence. [3] [4]

The network’s commercial logic involved access to supply, buyers, transportation and informal enforcement. But estimating total revenue, bribery expense or profits from newspaper adjectives such as “millionaire” would create false precision. The federal January 1932 case illustrates how substantial allegations can remain legally unproved. [1] [5]

Retrospective Gangland Narratives and Common Errors

Allan May’s 2009 profile and Rebecca Rector’s 2023 regional history article helped preserve interest in Higgins; both draw on episodes from earlier reporting. Tom Hunt’s 2025 multi-part reconstruction uses dated newspaper accounts, certificates and census records to revisit chronology and membership claims. These works should not be treated as interchangeable evidence. [2] [3] [4] [5] [6] [7]

Several errors recur in shortened narratives: placing Owl’s Head in 1928 rather than 1929; describing Daniel Maloney as personally murdered by Higgins rather than shot by a police colleague; treating the alleged Steinberg organization’s 250 workers as Higgins’s verified roster; treating January 1932 corruption allegations as convictions; and naming a definitive killer without clear proof. [3] [4] [5] [7]

The designation “last Irish boss” also demands care. It is a colorful retrospective and contemporary press formulation, not evidence that every Irish-American criminal association in Brooklyn ended in 1932. The appropriate historical unit of study is Higgins’s identifiable commercial circle and its changing relations. [5] [6] [13]

Historical Significance

The Higgins organization illustrates the transformation of New York street racketeering into complex Prohibition supply networks. Unlike the early Five Points gangs, his circle operated in a world of boats, trucks, communications, wholesale orders, police scrutiny and maritime interception. A commercial structure could be substantial without being a unified national crime family. [1] [2] [3]

Its history also shows why apparent gang leadership can be fragile. Rivalries, police pressure and disputes among long-standing associates undercut stability years before the leader’s death. A charismatic or notorious boss was not necessarily able to preserve a coherent organization across multiple criminal marketplaces. [3] [4] [5]

Most important, the record rewards distinction rather than legend: sources can document a 1929 police death and a 1932 acquittal with substantial precision, while leaving underworld killings and internal command unresolved. That is not a weakness in historical reporting; it is the boundary separating evidence-based history from folklore. [3] [4] [5] [9]

Terminology

Terms in this entry are historical and analytical; they should not be interpreted as a claim of shared methods or continuous command between distinct generations of organized crime. [1] [3]

Rum Row

Offshore anchorage area where suppliers waited to transfer illegal liquor to vessels approaching the United States.

Contact boat

Smaller vessel transacting with offshore supply craft or carrying cargo toward coastal landing points.

Rum-running

Unlawful transportation or importation of liquor during national Prohibition; often maritime.

Bootlegging

The unlawful beverage trade generally; not every bootlegger operated a smuggling ship.

Volstead Act

National Prohibition Act of 1919, statutory mechanism for enforcement of the Eighteenth Amendment.

Narrows

Strait connecting Upper and Lower New York Bays between Brooklyn and Staten Island.

Gerritsen Beach

Brooklyn coastal neighborhood named in reported 1931 liquor landing investigation.

Speakeasy

Establishment selling alcoholic beverages unlawfully during Prohibition.

Association

Reported contact or joint appearance; does not alone establish command, shared crimes or fixed membership.

Indictment

Formal criminal charge or accusation, distinct from a conviction.

Acquittal

Judicial or jury disposition finding a defendant not guilty of the charged offense.

Prosecution allegation

An accusation asserted in a case; not necessarily accepted by a judge or jury.

Rum-ring

Contemporary label for a coordinated liquor-smuggling enterprise.

Historical attribution

Assignment of an act or identity by a source, which must be tested against the record and competing claims.

Research and Editorial Standards

Source hierarchy. A court verdict, death record, dated newspaper and federal vessel file have different evidentiary functions. The 2025 Hunt articles provide exceptionally detailed references to some of those records; original 1929–1932 newspapers and court files have not been exhaustively facsimile-collated for this edition. Secondary writers are therefore credited as intermediaries when appropriate. [2] [3] [4] [5] [9] [10]

Case dispositions. The March 1929 charges reportedly were dismissed and the January 1932 federal liquor-conspiracy defendants were acquitted. This entry will not claim that Higgins was convicted of those charges or that every homicide associated with his circle was the act of a proved criminal conspiracy. [3] [5]

Ethnicity and scope. An Irish-associated boss does not prove exclusively Irish membership, and a neighborhood description does not criminalize its residents. The word “gang” is a catalog convenience for a changing network rather than an assertion that an organization incorporated itself under that name. [1] [2] [4]

Outstanding research. Priority archival tasks include original birth documentation, military service records, Coast Guard files for the Cigarette and WhichOne, original criminal dockets for the Owl’s Head and Long Beach cases, and full homicide inquiry files for the May–October 1931 incidents and June 1932 assassination. The editorial cutoff is November 13, 2026. [2] [3] [4] [5] [14]
