Overview

The Mullen Gang, also called the Mullens or Mullins Gang, was a neighborhood-based Irish-American criminal group associated with South Boston, Massachusetts. Its best-documented period was the late 1960s and early 1970s, when men associated with Paul “Paulie” McGonagle and Patrick “Pat” Nee fought the organization of Donald and Kenneth Killeen for local criminal influence. The conflict has become a foundational chapter in the history of James “Whitey” Bulger, but the Mullens were a distinct group before the 1972 accommodation with Bulger and Howard Winter. [1] [5] [9]

A May 23, 2001 federal grand-jury indictment identified a “Mullins Gang” and a rival group led by Donald and Kenneth Killeen. It described a shooting war that severely reduced both groups, followed by the consolidation of surviving South Boston associates and an affiliation with Howard Winter’s Somerville organization. This is firm evidence of the government’s historical organizational theory; an indictment is not, by itself, a judicial finding that every alleged individual crime occurred exactly as charged. [1] [2] [3]

The Mullen name also appears in the later murder record. In 2013 a federal jury found that Bulger participated in the murder of former Mullen member Paul McGonagle and in the murder of Thomas King, who had crossed into the later Bulger enterprise. The same verdict did not find the separate murder of Francis “Buddy” Leonard proved against Bulger. These rulings concern the post-1972 organization and must not be retroactively classified as Mullen Gang operations. [2] [3] [4] [13]

This article separates a loosely organized neighborhood group from the later, more enduring Winter Hill racket; distinguishes the names and spellings used by sources; and marks uncertain dates, headcounts, murder attributions, and conflicting memoir claims. The editorial record reviewed for this entry extends through November 13, 2026. [1] [5] [6] [7]

Neither the name “Mullen” nor the quoted “Mullins” spelling proves separate gangs existed. The variants are retained to help readers recognize federal records and later books. Similarly, a retrospective list of neighborhood associates should not be mistaken for an authenticated membership roll or a fixed chain of command. [1] [5] [6]

The Name: John Joseph Mullen Square

Several local reconstructions say that the gang took its name from John Joseph Mullen Square, a public memorial location around the meeting of O Street and East Second Street in South Boston. The naming tradition emphasizes a place where neighborhood young men met and socialized, not a known founder named Mullen. That distinction is essential: the identity of a memorialized individual should not be converted into a criminal biography, and there is no basis to call the honoree the gang’s leader or patron. [5] [12]

The federal indictment used the variant “Mullins Gang,” a spelling also employed in some newspapers and later popular accounts. Another convention is “the Mullens,” which treats the name as a collective neighborhood label. The precise spelling does not establish a separate organization, nor does a place-based label guarantee that everyone regularly present in the square was a member. [1] [5] [7]

The square’s significance is partly explanatory: it reflects how a recognizable neighborhood group could form through proximity before becoming involved in violent underworld competition. This entry uses Mullen Gang for its title and preserves Mullins when discussing documents that use that form. The claim of an original group active since a particular year in the 1940s or 1950s remains historical background rather than a verified organizational founding date. [1] [5] [12]

South Boston: Geography and Neighborhood Context

The Mullen story is inseparable from the compact geography of South Boston, with its residential streets, commercial corridors, corner businesses, taverns, schools, parishes and waterfront surroundings. Locally familiar blocks made chance encounters and reprisals especially consequential: rival associates could know each other’s families, routes and favored gathering places. This was neither a self-contained city-state nor a geography where a criminal faction owned all commerce. Its residents maintained lives and institutions that extended well beyond the underworld. [5] [6] [8]

The dispute with the Killeens centered on who could exercise influence in local illegal business and who could move openly without being attacked. Former investigators and memoirists describe the Killeens’ gambling network and younger Mullen associates operating within overlapping South Boston social circles. The economic map was therefore less a set of formally partitioned borders than a changing combination of clients, enforcement relationships and personal reputations. [1] [5] [6]

The Irish-American label refers to a historical pattern within these groups. It does not imply that the neighborhood’s residents collectively supported them, that all participants had identical backgrounds, or that the community can be characterized through gang activity. The same caution applies when postwar military service and immigrant connections appear in personal biographies: a veteran’s or immigrant’s status alone says nothing about criminal conduct. [9] [12]

Origins and the Limits of a Founding Date

Some popular gang histories assign the Mullens a history beginning in the 1950s, while participant recollections place younger men in neighborhood associations much earlier than the active gun feud. The reliable documentary core is later. The 2001 federal indictment retrospectively names the group in connection with the battle ending in 1972, and a contemporary local reconstruction identifies Mullen associates and the contested 1969 beginning of the shooting conflict. No single dated founding meeting, constitution or first membership list has been established for this article. [1] [5] [9]

It is plausible for a local street circle to have existed before it had major gambling interests or a stable leadership identity. But a neighborhood gathering place, a school-age clique and an armed racketeering organization are not automatically the same institution across decades. Historians must distinguish continuity of friendships from continuity of criminal management and revenue sharing. That distinction is especially important when youthful associates appear later as witnesses, rival leaders, or victims. [5] [6] [9]

The most defensible periodization is an earlier neighborhood association of uncertain formation, a late-1960s to 1972 rivalry with the Killeens, and a post-truce realignment in which the independent Mullen name became less useful as an organizational description. Modern summaries that give one uninterrupted operational date range should be read as conventions, not detailed findings of fact. [1] [5] [7]

Leadership: Paul McGonagle and Patrick Nee

Paul “Paulie” McGonagle is repeatedly described as a central Mullen figure and its principal leader in accounts of the shooting war. The 2001 indictment later identified him as a former member of the Mullins Gang and an opponent of Bulger in South Boston’s struggle. This does not prove he exercised the powers of a formal “boss” over every associate, but the recognition of his leadership is strong enough to feature prominently in the group’s identity. [1] [5] [8] [9] [14]

Patrick “Pat” Nee appears as a senior participant in local accounts and as a key source for the gang’s perspective. His 2006 memoir, A Criminal & an Irishman, co-written with Richard Farrell and Michael Blythe, places the Mullen conflict within his own life history and subsequent underworld associations. The memoir is an insider narrative of real historical importance, but a participant’s interpretation and assignment of blame are not automatically equivalent to evidence tested in court. [5] [7] [9]

Describing McGonagle and Nee as the principal figures does not require a rigid two-person executive committee. Accounts also name Thomas King, Dennis Roache, Jimmy Lydon, Mickey Dwyer and Francis Leonard in different contexts; not all occupied the same place or remained in the same faction after 1972. Some individuals later cooperated with Bulger, while others were targets of the emerging organization. Their changing affiliations are part of the historical explanation rather than a discrepancy to be erased. [5] [9] [10]

Membership, Associates, and Unverified Headcounts

Popular descriptions have claimed around 60 Mullen members, whereas a local source-critical reconstruction suggests that the men directly engaged in the shooting conflict were far fewer. The figures measure different things, or may simply be unreliable: habitual companions, acquaintances, occasional helpers and armed participants are not interchangeable categories. This encyclopedia does not adopt sixty as an authenticated membership total, nor does it estimate the number of active gunmen from anecdote alone. [5] [6]

Police intelligence terminology can amplify uncertainty because a person “associated with” a gang may have been a friend, a relative, a business contact or an individual suspected of committing crimes with its principal members. Lists published long after the events may also reflect later Winter Hill associations. A name’s appearance in such lists therefore requires attribution: reported as associated is not the same as proven to have joined. [1] [6] [7]

This entry does not manufacture named captains, initiation ceremonies, monthly member dues or territorial districts. A collective of armed neighborhood acquaintances could still be dangerous and consequential without the documentary characteristics of a tightly structured Italian-American Mafia family. [1] [5]

Criminal Activity and Economic Interests

The Mullens are associated in local reporting and Patrick Nee’s retrospective account with burglary, theft, armed robbery, violent enforcement and competition over illegal gambling. The evidence is strongest for the conflict itself, the identities of some principal participants and the subsequent attempt to organize South Boston betting proceeds. There is no verified Mullen-era accounting record from which to compute monthly receipts, membership shares or annual criminal profit. [5] [7] [9]

The Killeens represented an older gambling and loan-collection power center in many accounts. The Mullens’ challenge can be understood as a struggle not only over existing operators but also over the practical entitlement to operate independently. Criminal turf was enforced through threats and violence, yet neither the sources nor common sense justify treating every bookmaker or commercial establishment in South Boston as a Mullen asset. [1] [6]

It is especially easy to import later Winter Hill activities—large-scale extortion structures, drug distribution, firearms trafficking and more developed protection arrangements—into the older Mullen label. The 2001 indictment describes those later enterprises in considerable detail but does not establish that the Mullens before 1972 possessed the entire same apparatus. This profile marks that institutional break throughout. [1] [3]

The Killeen Organization: Principal Rival

The direct opponent was the Killeen Gang, associated with Donald and Kenneth Killeen, along with William “Billy” O’Sullivan and James “Whitey” Bulger. The 2001 federal indictment explicitly identifies the two brothers’ leadership and Bulger’s alignment with their side. The groups differed in reputation and generation in many popular accounts, but it is more accurate to describe two overlapping South Boston criminal networks than two armies with measured membership and a formal war command. [1] [5] [6]

When the rivalry escalated, a fight between familiar local men could lead to intimidation of associates and revenge directed at individuals who were not personally responsible for the original quarrel. Accounts of tavern confrontations, near-miss shootings and mistaken identity became central to the feud’s folklore. The initial motive, however, varies across sources: some emphasize a physical altercation and humiliation; others emphasize the expanding competition for gambling income. Those explanations need not be exclusive, but their relative importance cannot be quantitatively established. [5] [7] [9]

The MGU companion Killeen Gang profile follows that organization’s internal history. This article concentrates on the Mullens’ side, the evidentiary status of alleged attacks and the independent identity of the participants before the truce. [1] [7]

When Did the Mullen–Killeen War Begin?

Published chronologies disagree on the war’s first significant act. Some retrospective summaries place a dramatic Transit Café altercation involving Kenneth Killeen and Mullen associate Mickey Dwyer in 1971. A source-critical reconstruction, drawing on earlier accounts, dates an encounter involving Dwyer and the injury to his nose to July 1969. Another account points to broader personal and criminal tensions already building before then. Without a fully collated contemporary police and newspaper file, a single exact “declaration of war” would be misleading. [5] [6] [7]

The reported altercation is generally said to have involved Kenny Killeen, a shooting or firearm injury, and the biting of part of Dwyer’s nose. Its unusual details made the story memorable, but repeated summaries often disagree about the date and sequence. This article preserves the incident as an important reported catalyst and expressly avoids claiming that the argument alone proves every subsequent killing was directed by one gang against the other. [5] [6]

A careful organizational chronology places the open feud by the late 1960s, with repeated shootings and killings thereafter, and dates its negotiated ending to the period following Donald Killeen’s death in May 1972. This broad window is better supported than exact accounts of who first ordered retaliation. [1] [5] [7]

Donald McGonagle’s 1969 Death and the Mistaken-Identity Account

Donald McGonagle, the brother of Mullen figure Paul McGonagle, was shot dead in November 1969, according to retrospective reporting by the Boston Globe and the local historical reconstruction. Some later television summaries have placed the mistaken-identity killing in 1971. The 1969 dating is therefore used here with attribution, rather than treating inconsistent secondary accounts as though they agreed. [6] [8] [11]

Former Bulger associate Kevin Weeks later stated that Bulger mistakenly shot Donald while intending to kill Paul. That is a serious allegation from an underworld witness and a major explanation for the personal bitterness between the rivals. Donald should not be called a Mullen gunman merely because he was Paul’s brother; retrospective reporting specifically distinguishes him from the gang’s criminal activity. The loss to the family stands independently of the later narrative about motive. [8] [11]

The 2013 federal verdict materials identifying murders proved against Bulger include Paul McGonagle, but do not establish a separate adjudicated finding about Donald McGonagle’s killing. Accordingly, the mistaken-identity explanation is attributed to Weeks and subsequent reporting rather than elevated into a jury finding. This separation keeps the documentary status of the two brothers’ deaths clear. [2] [11] [13]

The March 1971 Killing of Billy O’Sullivan

William “Billy” O’Sullivan was an important figure on the Killeen side, generally described as an enforcer and ally of Bulger. He was shot dead on March 28, 1971, an event repeated in numerous accounts of the feud. His death was a serious blow to the Killeen organization because it removed a man identified with its capacity to use violence and protect gambling interests. [5] [6] [7]

Patrick Nee and other later narrators have attributed direct responsibility for O’Sullivan’s killing to Mullen participants, including Paul McGonagle. A source-critical comparison points out that different versions of the incident conflict on the number and movements of the attackers. This entry records the homicide and its consequences, but avoids presenting a particular retrospective shooter identification as an uncontested criminal conviction. [7] [9]

Later discussion sometimes turns O’Sullivan’s death into proof of a simple tit-for-tat sequence after Donald McGonagle was killed. The killings may have been linked in the minds of participants, yet the exact chains of instruction and retaliation require case-specific evidence. The gang-war label is an interpretive umbrella, not a substitute for forensic findings or individual court records. [6] [7] [11]

Escalation, Threats, and the Limits of a Casualty Count

The early 1970s were marked by attacks, efforts to avoid ambush and intense fear among those directly involved. Historical accounts frequently describe Killeen men taking precautions, Mullen associates attempting retaliatory strikes and bookmakers being pressured to choose sides. The economic conflict created incentives to intimidate rival enforcers; personal grievances added volatility that cannot be reduced to accounting disputes. [5] [6] [7]

Published estimates of total deaths vary sharply. One historical reconstruction quotes a period police statement identifying Donald Killeen as roughly the seventh or eighth victim in a several-year feud, while the same researcher challenges the way those deaths were counted. An article may quote that police figure as a reported assessment; it must not turn it into a verified ledger of Mullen-versus-Killeen killings. Each named casualty and evidentiary link must be checked independently. [7]

This article therefore does not adopt popular suggestions of a decade-long all-out campaign or assign every unsolved South Boston murder to one of the two groups. A smaller set of lethal incidents is well documented, but it is not an exhaustive list of all threats, nonfatal assaults and shootings that witnesses experienced. [1] [6]

May 13, 1972: Murder of Donald Killeen

The assassination of Donald “Donnie” Killeen outside his home in Framingham, Massachusetts, on May 13, 1972, decisively changed the South Boston balance of power. The homicide is repeatedly identified in independent accounts and serves as the clearest chronological breakpoint between the shooting war and the negotiations that followed. Donald had been a central figure in the older South Boston gambling operation. [1] [7]

Later memoir-based versions accuse named Mullen participants, often James “Jimmy” Mantville and Thomas “Tommy” King, of carrying out the shooting. The details are contested. A retrospective researcher compared Nee’s narrative with period police reporting and found significant disagreement over the hour and circumstances of the attack. That conflict demonstrates why an attractive cinematic version of a murder cannot be treated as an official investigative finding. [7] [9]

For purposes of this encyclopedia, Killeen’s death and its organizational consequences are documented; the identity and exact actions of the shooters are treated as attributed claims unless supported by independently verified judicial records. The killing does not make every individual on the Mullen side legally responsible, and it does not prove an order came from a formal “Mullen high command.” [1] [7]

Kenneth Killeen’s Withdrawal and Later 1972 Pressure

Accounts of the months after Donald’s murder describe surviving Killeen associates facing continued pressure. A local historical review cites October 5, 1972 Boston Globe reporting that Kenneth Killeen, brother of the murdered bookmaker, had signaled a desire to leave the gambling business. The same reconstruction reports a shotgun attack at or near Kenneth’s South Boston residence in September. These details are secondary presentation of period coverage rather than independently collated original police reports in this edition. [7]

Kenneth’s reported withdrawal matters because gang transitions are often simplified to a single moment when a leader is killed. In practice, remaining participants decide whether to contest old clients, abandon territory or negotiate working relationships. Fear, financial incentives and outside mediation can all shape those decisions. The available record supports a transition away from open conflict; it does not establish a single formal treaty that every former associate signed. [1] [7]

The Killeen organization’s weakening also altered Bulger’s position. Bulger was no longer merely a subordinate enforcer of an intact brother-led operation. But this did not automatically make him the universal commander of every independent South Boston offender. His consolidation of influence was a later, contested process. [1] [3]

The Truce: Howie Winter, Bulger, and the Mullen Delegation

By late 1972, the opposing sides had strong reasons to end their conflict. The 2001 federal indictment says Bulger, aligned with the Killeens, asked Howard “Howie” Winter to help end the fighting. Winter was the leader of a Somerville criminal organization with a regional reputation. A local reconstruction, drawing on Nee’s memoir and historical reporting, places a negotiation at Chandler’s Restaurant in Boston’s South End, with Bulger representing former Killeen interests and Pat Nee and Thomas King linked to the Mullen delegation. [1] [7]

Accounts differ about who first requested the meeting and what role various North End Mafia intermediaries played. Some versions emphasize Winter’s initiative, others cite Gerry Angiulo or other intermediaries. The major result—the groups’ accommodation and reorganization—is better attested than the precise order of telephone calls, security assurances or concessions offered behind closed doors. This entry describes the Chandler’s meeting as a reconstructed event rather than reproducing an unverified transcript. [1] [7] [9]

The settlement concerned criminal business and personal survival. It did not confer legal legitimacy on gambling or loansharking, nor did it turn the former rivals into equal shareholders of an enduring corporation. Individual loyalties and past grievances continued to matter after the ceasefire, helping to explain some later killings. [3] [7]

From Mullen and Killeen to Winter Hill

The government’s 2001 indictment presents the post-1972 formation as the consolidation of remnants of the two South Boston groups under Bulger, followed by their association with Winter’s Somerville group. The combined enterprise became known colloquially as the Winter Hill Gang. This is the central institutional connection between the Mullens and Boston’s better-known racketeering network. [1]

The description should not be misread as saying the old Mullen Gang simply changed its sign, retained the same membership and inherited all Winter Hill activities. Howard Winter, John Martorano, Stephen Flemmi and other figures belonged to wider criminal relationships that had their own earlier histories. The federal appellate court in United States v. Bulger referred to a 1972 meeting and later activities of a separate racketeering conspiracy, helping to establish the continuity of the case’s theory without dissolving the earlier organizational distinctions. [1] [3]

The Mullen group’s historical identity consequently has a meaningful end around the 1972 settlement. Individual former members and associates continued living and acting in the criminal world, but their subsequent acts should be attributed to the people and organizations actually involved at the later date. That distinction is particularly important for the McGonagle and King killings. [1] [2] [4]

Paul McGonagle after the Truce

The transition did not resolve all personal conflicts. Accounts describe Paul McGonagle remaining deeply hostile to Bulger, especially after the killing of his brother Donald. The 2001 federal indictment characterized Paul as a former member of the Mullins Gang and an earlier opponent in the contest for South Boston. His personal situation after the ceasefire therefore illustrates a gulf between a negotiated organizational accommodation and reconciliation among former adversaries. [1] [8] [9]

McGonagle disappeared in November 1974. The federal indictment later alleged that Bulger and others killed him and buried his remains near Tenean Beach in Dorchester. The available documentary record does not justify an elaborate reconstruction of every conversation or movement before he was killed; witness accounts of planning, location and motive are separate evidentiary questions. His family lived with uncertainty over his disappearance before the recovery of his remains. [1] [9]

In 2013 the Bulger jury found the McGonagle murder proved as a racketeering act. It is therefore appropriate to characterize Bulger’s adjudicated responsibility for that murder as established by the verdict, while distinguishing it from allegations about the earlier Donald McGonagle homicide. Paul’s death was post-truce violence associated with Bulger’s later enterprise, not a documented internal punishment ordered by an independently operating Mullen Gang. [2] [4] [13]

Thomas “Tommy” King: From Mullen Associate to 1975 Victim

Thomas “Tommy” King is repeatedly identified in historical accounts as a Mullen participant during the feud and a later associate of the reorganized South Boston network. His trajectory is a clear example of why a name cannot be fixed to a single organizational roster for every year of a person’s life. By the mid-1970s, his position and perceived loyalty were being judged in the setting of Bulger’s growing power. [1] [9] [10]

The May 2001 indictment alleged that King was murdered in November 1975 and buried near the Neponset River in Quincy. The indictment classified him at that stage as a member of the Bulger Group regarded as a threat, rather than as an active officer of the old independent Mullens. Some local accounts put his disappearance at the end of November; the charging document uses a date “on or about” November 5. This article uses the broader November 1975 time frame in its chronology and flags the discrepancy. [1] [10]

The 2013 jury found both conspiracy to murder King and the murder itself proved against Bulger. That verdict is a stronger attribution than a general memoir claim. King’s killing belongs to the documented violent consolidation of the later organization, not to an ongoing, independent Mullen–Killeen war that had formally resumed in 1975. [2] [3] [13]

Francis “Buddy” Leonard: A Separate Verdict Distinction

Francis “Buddy” Leonard is named among Mullen-associated figures in retrospective group listings. The 2001 federal indictment later alleged that his November 1975 killing was intended to distract attention from Thomas King’s disappearance. This allegation concerns the post-1972 Bulger Group and does not establish an execution carried out under the old Mullen organization. [1] [5]

An important judicial qualification is often lost in compressed histories: the 2013 jury did not find the racketeering act alleging Bulger’s responsibility for Leonard’s murder proved. The same jury found the King and Paul McGonagle murders proved. The correct treatment is to record Leonard’s reported death and the government’s specific allegation while preserving the actual jury’s distinction. A verdict of “not proved” is not proof that the death never occurred or that no one was responsible; it is a conclusion about the prosecution’s charged racketeering act. [2] [13]

This difference is not editorial hairsplitting. It is the dividing line between what a government prosecutor alleged and what a jury found. The same standard applies to other killings discussed in memoirs and criminal biographies: attribution depends on the source and legal status, not on repetition of a familiar narrative. [13]

Patrick Nee’s Subsequent Career and Memoir

Patrick Nee’s later life has often been used as a retrospective lens on the Mullen Gang. His autobiography describes early involvement in neighborhood crime, wartime military service, the conflict with the Killeens and eventual relations with Bulger. The narrative continues beyond the life of the Mullens into Irish republican gunrunning and later criminal convictions. These are important personal chapters but are not proof that the original Mullen Gang conducted every later activity Nee undertook. [9] [12]

The original 2006 edition of A Criminal & an Irishman was published by Steerforth Press with Richard Farrell and Michael Blythe as collaborators. Publisher and library records confirm the book’s publication history and give useful context for its perspective. The book is not a court judgment; participants may seek to defend decisions, allocate blame or omit facts that would incriminate themselves or associates. The article therefore uses the memoir as a named, qualified source. [9] [12]

Nee’s role in negotiating a truce is a recurring theme in later narratives. The historical convergence on an accommodation is stronger than the precise reconstruction of who invited whom. Readers should also distinguish what Nee claims to remember from what later federal trials specifically established regarding Bulger and other defendants. [1] [7] [9]

Winter Hill, the Patriarca Family, and Local Mediation

The development of the South Boston underworld involved other Boston-area criminal organizations. Some later accounts of the 1972 settlement mention Gerry Angiulo and intermediaries associated with the New England Mafia, while the federal indictment emphasizes Howard Winter. References to the North End network are relevant to understanding the leverage and reputations involved in mediation; they do not, by themselves, prove that the Mullens were a formal subsidiary of the Patriarca family. [1] [7]

The difference between making contact with, accepting mediation by and coming under the permanent control of another organization is substantial. Criminal parties may call on a trusted regional figure to guarantee safe negotiation without conceding all future profits or administrative decisions. The records reviewed for this entry do not justify creating a standing “Mullen–Patriarca alliance treaty” or fixed percentage of betting proceeds. [1] [7]

The post-1972 Winter Hill arrangement provided a broader business and enforcement setting than the old neighborhood feud. Its expansion also exposed South Boston participants to new conflicts, including later disputes with rival non-Mafia operators and encounters with the federal government. Those later matters merit separate coverage under the Winter Hill profile. [1] [3]

Mullen and Killeen Organizations Compared

Comparison helps explain the contest without inventing a winner-takes-all founding myth. Both groups were neighborhood-based criminal associations, but sources commonly portray the Killeens as better established in gambling and loan collection and the Mullens as younger men whose willingness to use force challenged that position. The contrast is useful as a broad interpretation; the exact number of participants, financial resources and territory of each faction remain uncertain. [5] [6] [9]

Attributing Gang-War Violence Responsibly

A responsible chronology separates the occurrence of a killing from the identity of the perpetrator, the alleged motive and the organizational instruction to commit it. The deaths of Donald McGonagle, Billy O’Sullivan and Donald Killeen were pivotal events in accounts of the feud. But contemporaneous criminal case files for those specific killings have not been fully collated here, and retrospective eyewitness or underworld claims are not interchangeable with convictions. [7] [8] [11]

The later federal case offers a different standard of evidence for two former Mullen figures: a jury found the racketeering acts of Paul McGonagle’s and Thomas King’s murders proved against Bulger. It did not find the charged Francis Leonard killing proved. Using one phrase—“Bulger killed them all”—would obscure a central legal distinction. Conversely, treating a non-proved act as evidence that the reported death was fictional would also be wrong. [2] [13]

The same approach is used for less famous names. Being named in an underworld memoir as a “gunman” or appearing in an indictment’s narrative does not establish guilt absent the specific supporting record. This article avoids lengthy graphic embellishment and concentrates on the events’ human cost and organizational consequences. [1] [9] [13]

The 2001 Indictment: What It Does and Does Not Establish

The Third Superseding Indictment in United States v. Bulger, filed May 23, 2001, is the backbone of the official retrospective account. Paragraph four identifies the Mullins–Killeen conflict, describes their losses and links the 1972 mediation to the Winter Hill formation. Later paragraphs address specific murders, including Paul McGonagle and Thomas King. It is a detailed primary legal source for the prosecution’s position. [1]

An indictment is an accusation voted by a grand jury, and its narrative should not be restated as if every allegation were separately established at trial. The important methodological step is to compare claims in the charging paper with later verdict documents and appellate judgments. Some allegations were proved, others were not; some historical background in the indictment was not itself a count that the jury had to decide. [1] [2] [3] [13]

The federal document also makes an organizational distinction easy to miss: it describes a 1972 merger and affiliation rather than claiming that one intact gang simply renamed itself in 1972. For the Mullen article, that point is as important as the names of the principals. [1]

The 2013 Bulger Verdict and the 2016 Appeal

On August 12, 2013, a federal jury found Bulger guilty of racketeering conspiracy and other offenses. The Justice Department announced jury findings connecting him with eleven murders, including Paul McGonagle and Thomas King. A contemporaneous Associated Press count-by-count account separately records that the alleged murder of Francis “Buddy” Leonard was not proved as a racketeering act. The difference between a government press summary and the more granular verdict listing is recognized rather than blurred. [2] [13]

Bulger was sentenced in November 2013 to two consecutive life terms and an additional five years. On March 4, 2016, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the First Circuit affirmed his conviction. The appellate decision summarizes the government’s theory of a 1972 meeting and later consolidated criminal business. Those judicial events are much later than the independent Mullen Gang’s main period, but they provide unusually clear legal confirmation for some of the organization’s historical aftermath. [3] [4]

There is no basis to interpret Bulger’s conviction as a criminal conviction of all earlier Mullen participants or as a judicial determination of each alleged Killeen–Mullen shooting between 1969 and 1972. This article deliberately limits the verdict to the acts actually described in the court record. [2] [3] [13]

Federal Informants and the Danger of Backward Projection

Bulger’s relationship with the FBI is central to the later Winter Hill history. The First Circuit recounts testimony that he became an informant for Agent John Connolly around 1975, and that some law-enforcement officials improperly protected his later criminal operations. These events occurred after the period of the Mullen Gang’s independent rivalry with the Killeens. [3]

A source-critical commentary also discusses FBI agent Dennis Condon’s earlier contacts with Bulger in 1971. The presence of contacts in both periods does not mean that all participants in the Mullen–Killeen feud were protected informants or that the Mullen organization itself had an agreed intelligence relationship with the FBI. The status, scope and nature of particular contacts require careful distinctions among informal reporting, recruited informant status and later corrupt protection. [3] [5] [6]

The Mullen Gang should therefore be studied as a local criminal group involved in violent competition, not as an invented front organization for the bureau. The federal informant scandal is relevant to subsequent investigative outcomes and merits fuller coverage in the Winter Hill and Bulger articles. [3]

South Boston Residents and the Cost of Gang Rivalries

Gang narratives often frame South Boston as a small arena in which the choices of a few criminal leaders determined daily life. That picture is historically incomplete. Residents maintained families, workplaces, schools, community organizations and local businesses while violent actors exploited neighborhood familiarity. Threats and killings imposed fear not only on intended targets but on relatives, neighbors, witnesses and people who encountered violence without choosing to participate. [8] [9]

The mistaken-identity account of Donald McGonagle’s death illustrates this wider human cost. Even where later sources disagree on perpetrator or date, they distinguish Donald from the criminal identity of his brother Paul. Paul’s family then experienced another loss after he disappeared in 1974. A serious encyclopedia account should record these people as victims and relatives, not merely as devices in a succession story about a more famous gangster. [8] [11]

Irish ancestry, military service and residence in South Boston cannot be used as proxies for involvement in crime. The gang was a small and historically bounded subject within a far larger community. This editorial principle also guards against turning every tavern, street gathering or neighborhood square into evidence of a criminal headquarters. [5] [9] [12]

Memoir, Television, and the Making of a “Southie Gang War”

Much of what readers think they know about the Mullens comes from later accounts of Bulger’s rise. Participant memoirs, local newspaper investigations, television profiles and biographies repeatedly retell the Killeen–Mullen feud to explain a larger criminal dynasty. This process made the group more recognizable, but it encouraged compressed timelines and vivid stories with conflicting versions of the same scene. [5] [7] [9]

The Transit Café episode, the mistaken-identity killing, the assassinations and the Chandler’s meeting are all legitimate historical research topics. Their details vary among narrators. A source-critical reconstruction identifies contradictions in the dating of the early tavern feud and the precise hour of Donald Killeen’s death. The article prefers dated documents for the chronology and explicitly labels the rest as retrospective narration. [5] [7]

Works by former gang members can preserve voices that official files omit, but the limits are real: memory changes, participants may conceal their own roles and dramatic dialogue may be impossible to verify. Media accounts also differ on whether “Mullen” or “Mullins” is the proper label. The purpose of this entry is to preserve historically useful stories without laundering them into official fact by repetition. [1] [9] [12]

End of the Independent Mullen Organization

The strongest evidence for the Mullen Gang’s end as a distinct network is the 1972 accommodation that federal prosecutors later described. It followed Donald Killeen’s death and the exhausted rivalry. Survivors of the original factions thereafter interacted within a wider criminal framework. None of the reviewed sources establishes an independently run Mullen command system after the settlement with an uninterrupted leader, fixed roster and separate treasury. [1] [3] [7]

That conclusion does not mean every former Mullen disappeared from crime in 1972. Some became associates of Bulger; others remained estranged or pursued separate activities. The later deaths of Paul McGonagle, Tommy King and Buddy Leonard reveal the insecurity of former relationships even after organizational reconciliation. But the post-1972 violence is not evidence that a coherent Mullen Gang continued prosecuting the same war indefinitely. [1] [2] [13]

The article therefore records the organization’s independent existence as historical, with a practical turning point in 1972. It avoids a fabricated “final meeting,” formal dissolution vote or transfer of an exact number of members and businesses to Winter Hill. [1] [7]

Historical Significance

The Mullen Gang is historically significant primarily because its conflict with the Killeen brothers precipitated the South Boston power reorganization of 1972. The subsequent association with Howie Winter’s group provided the setting from which Bulger emerged as a major Boston racketeer. This is a material organizational connection, but one should not confuse the original neighborhood association with the far larger and later enterprise. [1] [3]

The record is also useful as a study in organized crime’s informal structure. A familiar local circle could escalate from robbery and intimidation to violent competition without developing standardized ranks or documented member rolls. Its ability to challenge an older gambling outfit depended on social relationships, access to weapons and the willingness of individual men to risk reprisals—not merely on territorial labels. [5] [6]

Finally, the gang illustrates the need to compare sources. Federal indictments preserve the prosecution’s organizational theory; verdicts establish specific findings; participant memoirs offer insight into perceptions and rivalries; and local journalists can expose inconsistencies among them. An accurate account is necessarily less cinematic than the recurring legend, but it supports more useful comparative history for the MGU collection. [1] [3] [7] [13]

Terminology and Historical Concepts

Mullen Gang / Mullins Gang

Variant labels for the same South Boston group as discussed in later media and federal records; spelling difference alone does not establish separate gangs.

South Boston / Southie

Boston neighborhood central to the Mullen–Killeen rivalry and later Bulger history.

John Joseph Mullen Square

Memorial public-place name associated in local histories with the gang’s name; not a proven criminal headquarters.

Gang war

Descriptive label for repeated violence and threats between rival criminal associates; not a formal war involving all residents.

Bookmaking

Organized acceptance of wagers, often conducted unlawfully outside authorized gambling channels.

Loansharking

Extortionate or unlawful lending and collection of debts under threats or coercion.

Enforcer

Informal term for someone used to intimidate rivals or collect debts; not necessarily a verified rank or legal office.

Turf

Claimed spheres of criminal influence; not legal land ownership or confirmed control of every street.

Sit-down

A negotiated meeting among criminal representatives intended to settle disputes or allocate business.

Winter Hill Gang

Later Boston-area criminal organization combining Somerville and South Boston relationships; historically distinct from the independent Mullens.

Racketeering act

A charged predicate offense forming part of the government’s broader racketeering case; findings may differ act by act.

Indictment

Formal grand-jury accusation that sets out a prosecutor’s allegations, not a blanket judicial determination of guilt.

Jury finding: proved / not proved

Specific outcome on a racketeering act; must not be collapsed into a general narrative of guilt.

Historical attribution

A named source’s assignment of an event or act to a person or group; requires attention to the source’s basis.

Organizational continuity

Evidence that the same functioning group persisted across time, rather than just some individuals continuing to operate.

Research and Editorial Standards

Primary legal sources. The 2001 federal indictment is cited for the existence of the government’s Mullen–Killeen organizational theory and specific allegations. The 2013 jury findings and 2016 appellate ruling are used for what courts actually decided. Allegations about the identity of the early feud’s shooters are not upgraded to verdicts without case-specific proof. [1] [2] [3] [13]

Retrospective interpretation. Patrick Nee’s memoir and local essays are important for understanding what participants reported. They are also subject to conflicting recollections, self-justification and incomplete archives. Where two sources give different dates, the text either attributes the difference or uses a defensible date range. No quoted dialogue is presented as a stenographic transcript. [5] [6] [7] [9]

Community and persons. The article treats named victims as individuals and avoids representing South Boston’s Irish-American population as collectively responsible for criminal conduct. The account does not automatically assign gang membership to relatives, veterans, witnesses or bystanders. [8] [11]

Limits and revision priorities. Full Boston police homicide files, complete transcripts of every pre-1972 criminal matter, municipal records for the Square, participant memoir pagination and all original 1969–1972 newspaper facsimiles were not exhaustively collated. A future edition should examine those documents before assigning exact membership figures, perpetrator identities and a definitive feud start date. This is an evidence-qualified editorial synthesis through November 13, 2026.
