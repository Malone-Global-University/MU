Overview

The Forty Thieves were an early New York City street group associated with the Five Points district of Lower Manhattan. Newspapers of the late 1820s referred to a gang by that name, and later writers made it a founding chapter in the history of American street gangs. The organization should be understood as a nineteenth-century neighborhood criminal association, not as an Irish-American Mafia family or a predecessor under the control of modern organized-crime syndicates. [1] [2] [3]

The basic existence of a gang name is better supported than many familiar details about the group. A researcher tracing period newspapers identified an April 1829 report naming the Forty Thieves in the Five Points and later 1829 coverage connecting Henry Sutton with the organization. By contrast, the popular picture of an established 1825 headquarters, a formal daily theft quota, a clearly identified founding boss, and a continuous operation into the 1850s largely rests on later retellings rather than demonstrated records from the 1820s. [3] [2] [4]

This entry therefore separates contemporaneous references from retrospective tradition. It also examines how immigrant poverty, exaggerated crime journalism, later popular histories, and the archival reconstruction of everyday Five Points life have shaped the gang’s reputation. The editorial assessment is based on material reviewed through November 13, 2026. [5] [6]

“First gang” is a retrospective label, not a judicial or census classification. New York residents engaged in collective illegal activity before the surviving references to this name; the distinction is between one of the earliest identified named street gangs and a demonstrably first gang of any kind. [3] [4] [7]

The Five Points Neighborhood

The Five Points took its name from the converging streets of a district that emerged as lower Manhattan expanded over and around the filled Collect Pond. The surrounding Sixth Ward included densely occupied housing, workshops, small shops, drinking establishments, lodging rooms, and a growing range of immigrant and Black households. Street geography, rented housing, informal employment, and busy pedestrian traffic all mattered to people who lived and worked there. [5] [6] [8]

Early and mid-nineteenth-century observers regularly described the Five Points as a place of violence, poor sanitation, prostitution, and disorder. Some descriptions reflected conditions residents confronted; others projected class, racial, anti-immigrant, and anti-Catholic fears onto an entire neighborhood. A locality could experience real crimes without all its households being criminal. Evidence of productive households, skilled work, food preparation, and the everyday possessions of residents complicates the later image of an undifferentiated slum. [5] [6] [8]

Archaeological excavations associated with the Foley Square federal courthouse recovered building foundations and extensive household artifacts. Rebecca Yamin and other researchers have shown that these remains document family life, consumption, work, and efforts to maintain ordinary domestic life. They are not evidence that every saloon was a gang headquarters or that the Forty Thieves dominated the district. [6] [8]

Contemporaneous Reports, 1828–1829

The most important distinction in the Forty Thieves record is chronological. An annotated investigation of Herbert Asbury’s account identifies newspaper references to the “Forty Thieves” or “Forties” concentrated in 1828–1829, a much narrower window than the customary multi-decade lifespan found in general summaries. The review reports that Freedom’s Banner of Chester, Vermont, on April 29, 1829, described trouble at New York’s Five Points and attributed much of it to a gang called the Forty Thieves. [3]

The same investigator reports coverage later in 1829 of the arrest of Henry Sutton, identified as belonging to the Forty Thieves and subsequently sent to Sing Sing. This is an important traceable lead: a named person tied to the gang by near-contemporary reports, rather than a personality retroactively inserted into its history. However, the underlying court and prison files have not been independently collated for this encyclopedia article. Sutton’s reported connection is therefore attributed to the researcher’s examination, not elevated into a reconstructed career or formal command position. [3]

Newspaper mentions are valuable evidence that the name circulated, but a journalist’s attribution cannot, by itself, establish membership rolls, exact criminal incidents, regular payment obligations, or the survival of one organization for decades. Nor does a cited newspaper report establish that every offense around the Five Points was committed by the same group. [3] [5]

When Did the Gang Form?

Popular presentations commonly place the gang’s formation in or around 1825–1826. A 2013 history feature, later updated in 2025, describes the group operating in the 1820s and later decades. Herbert Asbury’s account links the gang’s origin to the spread of cheap drinking rooms in the Five Points. These dates are useful for understanding the established narrative, but they are not equivalent to a surviving dated charter, a court record naming founding members, or a contemporary account of an inaugural meeting. [2] [4]

A cautious chronology begins with the late-1820s documentary references. The gang may have existed earlier, and variants of its name could have been used later, but those possibilities must not be converted into precise operational dates without new evidence. The directory’s conventional c. 1820s–1850s classification can remain a broad historical discovery label while the dedicated article makes the shorter verified window explicit. [3] [9]

The difference between first described, first organized, and first documented matters. Informal bands may leave little paper trail until a violent encounter, prosecution, or police complaint makes them visible. Historians should resist turning the earliest located mention into proof that no earlier gang existed. [5] [7]

Rosanna Peers and the Alleged Headquarters

In the best-known story, the Forty Thieves gathered in the back room of a cheap grocery and drinking establishment operated by Rosanna Peers on Centre Street near Anthony Street, later Worth Street. Asbury pictured the location as a meeting place where leaders received reports and assigned members to criminal errands. Its enduring appeal comes from the concreteness of the image: a single room, an identifiable proprietor, and the apparently organized birth of New York street crime. [1] [2]

The evidentiary chain is more complicated. A subsequent investigation traced Peers to Frank Moss’s The American Metropolis (1897), which portrays her grocery as an early source of inexpensive liquor in the neighborhood but does not directly identify it as a Forty Thieves headquarters. Asbury later joined the Peers story to the gang. The investigator found no additional independent reference sufficient to confirm the purported meetings. [3] [10]

The date of the shop itself also varies among retrospective accounts: Asbury’s 1927 magazine article places the first such drinking room considerably earlier than the date assigned to it in versions of his later book. That instability is an additional reason not to report the store’s founding or its role as a documented gang office with unwarranted precision. The site remains a traditional alleged meeting place, not a verified headquarters. [1] [2] [3]

Edward Coleman: Alleged Leader or Later Invention?

Edward Coleman is routinely introduced as the founder or commander of the Forty Thieves. The association stems above all from Herbert Asbury’s The Gangs of New York, which presents Coleman as a Five Points criminal chief. Many later accounts repeat this linkage and identify his execution in 1839 as the end of a notorious gang leader’s career. The fact of his execution, however, is not evidence that he commanded the Forty Thieves. [2] [3] [11]

A source-critical review of 1838–1839 newspaper accounts found no contemporary evidence naming Coleman as a Forty Thieves member or chief. Those accounts identify him as a Black man who had worked as a mat maker and had come to New York from Philadelphia. This conflicts with the popular picture of an Irish gang leader operating continuously from the mid-1820s. The review also finds no independent evidence of a previous gang career. [3] [11]

For MGU purposes, Coleman belongs in the history of how the Forty Thieves were remembered, not in an uncontested leadership succession. A future primary-source discovery might alter that assessment; absent such evidence, assigning him a founding date, administrative role, territory, or detailed subordinate command system would be speculative. [3]

The 1838 Murder of Ann Coleman

The later gang story frequently uses Coleman’s murder of his wife, Ann Coleman, as proof of the leader’s cruelty. Contemporary accounts instead document a serious act of domestic homicide, without demonstrating an organizational connection. On July 28, 1838, Coleman killed Ann on Broadway with a razor. He was prosecuted and executed on January 12, 1839, at the newly built Tombs jail. [3] [11]

The reconstructed newspaper record describes Ann as a young woman from Haiti who sold hot corn and Coleman as Black. Later popular versions transform the crime into a tale of earnings, sexual jealousy, and gangland power, sometimes changing the manner of death. Those narrative embellishments should not displace the killing of an identifiable woman with her own life and work, nor should an unrelated homicide be counted as an operation or murder ordered by the Forty Thieves. [3] [11]

This article mentions the case because of its outsized influence on the gang’s published biography. It does not list Coleman as a verified member, treat Ann as a gang associate, or infer a criminal conspiracy from their marriage. [3]

Henry Sutton and the Limits of the Membership Record

Henry Sutton is less famous than Coleman but is more relevant to reconstructing the Forty Thieves from contemporaneous records. The 2019 archival commentary identifies Sutton in an 1829 newspaper account of an arrest and reported commitment to Sing Sing. Unlike Coleman’s later designation as a founder, Sutton’s attribution reportedly appeared within the gang’s period of documented public mention. [3]

This does not establish the size of the organization or Sutton’s rank, age, residence, ancestry, accomplices, or other convictions. Those details would require the text of the underlying press notices and relevant jail, trial, or Sing Sing records. If further research establishes that two people were called members in a newspaper, it still would not create a verified command structure or prove that every individual described as a Forty Thief belonged to a single stable roster. [3] [5]

At present, the most defensible register is asymmetrical: Sutton—reported gang associate in contemporary-era newspaper research; Coleman—later alleged leader, association not corroborated. Asserting more would confuse the availability of a famous story with the quality of historical evidence.

Theft, Street Crime, and Economic Activity

Writers associate the Forty Thieves with pickpocketing, small-scale stealing, robbery, street assaults, intimidation, and the resale of stolen goods. These activities were also broadly attributed to the wider early nineteenth-century Five Points underworld. The available sources support a general criminal profile; they do not supply a case-by-case ledger of offenses committed by defendants proven to belong to the same Forty Thieves organization. [1] [4] [7]

Crowded markets, taverns, transit approaches, theaters, street vendors, and commercial thoroughfares offered opportunities for both lawful work and opportunistic theft. Claims that particular businesses served as fences for the gang, or that the group maintained a formal theft-proceeds treasury, require more than generic descriptions of nineteenth-century urban crime. This article does not assign the Forty Thieves control of the waterfront, an organized vice monopoly, labor unions, or municipal offices. [3] [5]

The group’s local historical importance therefore derives less from a proven large-scale criminal economy than from the way a named band could become visible through newspaper attention amid an unstable and rapidly changing urban environment. A street gang and a twentieth-century racketeering syndicate may both be studied under an encyclopedia of criminal organizations without being treated as identical institutional forms.

Did the Forty Thieves Require Theft Quotas?

The claim that members were required to steal a fixed amount of property each day is one of the most repeated characteristics of the Forty Thieves. Modern popular summaries sometimes add that failure meant expulsion. The story conveys a disciplined, almost corporate system of supervision, but the sources reviewed here do not identify a contemporaneous 1820s constitution, named witness, court deposition, or account book establishing a daily quota. [4] [2]

It is possible that members of a band shared proceeds, made demands on junior offenders, or pressured one another to produce stolen goods. It is a further and unproven step to state that this particular group had a fixed, enforceable quota system. To avoid recycling a colorful detail as settled fact, the encyclopedia records daily theft quotas as a traditional but unverified claim. [2] [3]

The same caution applies to stories of formal membership categories, routine assignments distributed at a headquarters, and expulsion rules. Unless those claims can be tied to independent near-contemporary documentation, they are best understood as the later institutionalization of a poorly documented street-gang narrative.

The “Forty Little Thieves” Tradition

Asbury described a younger group called the Forty Little Thieves, consisting of children who imitated older offenders and allegedly supplied stolen property. That description later developed into the idea of a formal junior division or apprenticeship system for the adult gang. His 1927 magazine account includes the younger group, but the material examined for this article does not independently confirm an official subordinate branch. [1] [2]

Child street work was nonetheless a real feature of nineteenth-century New York. Children sold goods, carried messages, performed casual jobs, and occupied street spaces that also exposed some to theft, violence, arrest, and exploitation. Historians studying archaeological and social evidence of working-class childhood emphasize the varied lives of children rather than classifying all unaccompanied or impoverished young people as criminals. [5] [12]

The encyclopedia therefore identifies the “Forty Little Thieves” as a name reported in a retrospective source. It does not portray every begging child or informal youth companion of a Five Points adult as an inducted or coerced member of the Forty Thieves. Nor does it assert verified command by older gang leaders over identifiable named children. [1] [12]

Organization and Membership: What Can Be Established?

Calling a set of named offenders a gang does not itself prove that it had a boss, underboss, captains, uniform ranks, official admission process, or central treasury. Asbury asserted that the Forty Thieves were exceptional for acknowledged leadership, but the named leaders in his narrative have not been established through contemporary evidence. [1] [2] [3]

An early street association might have operated through acquaintanceship, neighborhood proximity, shared drinking spaces, collective violence, and temporary cooperation rather than a stable bureaucracy. The term gang could also be applied by residents or journalists to groups whose membership was inconsistent. For the Forty Thieves, the size, exact membership criteria, meeting frequency, and internal discipline remain open historical questions. [3] [5] [9]

No reliable membership total is adopted here. The word Forty is part of a label—not a verified headcount—and the name’s appearance in the familiar story of Ali Baba creates an additional danger of interpreting a literary number as a historical roster. [9]

Irish Association and the Multicultural District

The Forty Thieves are regularly characterized as an Irish immigrant or Irish-American gang in histories of New York street crime. Their inclusion in the MGU Irish-American Crime Syndicates directory follows that historical classification. It does not mean that the encyclopedia has established Irish ancestry for every alleged participant or that the organization represented an ethnic community. [4] [7]

The Five Points was not exclusively Irish. Residents included Black New Yorkers as well as successive populations of Irish, German, Jewish, Italian, Chinese, and other migrants. Its social history cannot be reduced to one ethnicity, nor can crime rates be inferred from ethnic composition. Contemporary prejudices shaped what reporters chose to notice and how they described it. [5] [6] [8]

The Coleman controversy is an instructive warning. An individual later installed as the “Irish” gang’s founder was in fact described as Black in period records. The correct conclusion is not to substitute another invented ethnic narrative, but to distinguish the group’s commonly accepted association from the much narrower set of personal identities that can actually be verified. [3] [11]

Other Five Points Gangs and Alleged Rivalries

The Forty Thieves are often grouped with the Kerryonians, Chichesters, Roach Guards, Shirt Tails, Dead Rabbits, and other names appearing in histories of early New York street gangs. Some of these associations belong to later developments in the same urban environment. Overlap of location does not mean direct organizational succession or confirmed combat between the named groups. [1] [7]

The celebrated confrontations involving the Dead Rabbits and Bowery Boys took place in the 1850s, well after the narrow 1828–1829 cluster of located Forty Thieves references. Without evidence documenting uninterrupted Forty Thieves activity, those mid-century riots cannot be classified as the group’s battles. The same restriction applies to statements that the Forty Thieves allied with, founded, absorbed, or gave rise to another gang. [5] [7]

These organizations can be linked in a historical catalog as related Five Points or New York gang history without creating a fictional command genealogy. A relationship marked “same neighborhood or historiographical context” is materially different from a proven alliance, merger, or rivalry.

Police, Municipal Politics, and the Problem of Attribution

Nineteenth-century New York experienced intense contests over municipal government, policing, elections, saloon regulation, urban poverty, and the authority of neighborhood political networks. Accounts of the Five Points frequently place street violence next to those wider political developments. However, general proximity to political machines or election-time disorder is not proof that the Forty Thieves were a formally retained political enforcement unit. [5] [7]

This distinction is especially important because “Forty Thieves” was also used as a political epithet in American history, including for the notoriously corrupt New York City Board of Aldermen of the early 1850s. The municipal nickname does not establish that the aldermen were the same people as the 1820s Five Points gang, or that either body directed the other. [13]

Official police histories and modern law-enforcement surveys sometimes compress early-gang history into a simple beginning around 1820. Such retrospectives illustrate how the story entered criminological convention, but they do not settle the leadership, exact founding date, or lifespan of the particular New York group. [7]

Disappearance from the Record and Claims of Dissolution

Popular accounts frequently state that the Forty Thieves disappeared around 1850 or remained active until the 1850s. A broad date range appears in the collection directory because the group is customarily placed within the early-to-mid-nineteenth-century phase of New York crime history. Yet the archival survey discussed above emphasizes the narrow range of gang-specific newspaper mentions it found in 1828–1829. [3] [4]

The disappearance of a name from available newspapers could represent dissolution, a change of name, reduced news attention, individual arrests, dispersal, or simply an incomplete research record. It does not establish a particular last meeting, merger, defeat, or transfer of territory to a successor gang. This article therefore does not publish a firm dissolution year or claim a verified succession by the Whyos, Dead Rabbits, or any other organization. [3] [5]

A more precise conclusion is that the Forty Thieves are historically attested in the late 1820s but that an organizational history continuous through the Civil War period has not been demonstrated from the sources consulted.

Herbert Asbury and the Construction of a Gang Legend

Herbert Asbury’s The Gangs of New York (1928), preceded by magazine writing in 1927, played a decisive role in fixing the Forty Thieves in the public imagination. Asbury wrote memorable narrative history built around vivid criminals, dramatic violence, and colorful places. The material is valuable for studying how New Yorkers in the early twentieth century remembered the old city, but it should not be treated as a verbatim reconstruction of 1820s institutions. [1] [2] [5]

Historian Tyler Anbinder has challenged the tendency of earlier popular histories to reproduce sensational nineteenth-century stories without adequate verification. The archaeological findings described by Rebecca Yamin reinforce that criticism from another direction: the physical traces of Five Points life reveal families, trades, and material aspirations that lurid depictions often ignored. [5] [6]

Asbury’s Peers-and-Coleman account illustrates the danger of combining facts separated by decades. A nineteenth-century description of a liquor-selling grocery, the real later murder committed by Edward Coleman, and newspaper mentions of a gang called the Forty Thieves can be assembled into a coherent tale. The narrative’s coherence, however, is not independent evidence that the three elements were linked in the way alleged. [1] [2] [3] [10]

The goal is not to dismiss the gang as fictional: late-1820s newspaper references are significant. It is to locate which elements have period support and where later authors supplied characters, offices, procedures, and chronology not yet verified by the records.

Other Organizations Called “Forty Thieves”

The expression Forty Thieves was not unique to Manhattan. It drew on the widely known tale of Ali Baba and the Forty Thieves and became a reusable label for groups suspected of collective theft, sometimes in Britain and sometimes as a metaphor for political corruption. Thus an identical name in a different city or later decade is insufficient proof of migration, branch offices, or shared leadership. [9] [13]

Shaw, Maxwell-Stewart, and Harman’s peer-reviewed work examines British accounts of alleged “Forty Thieves,” including juvenile theft networks and the problem of interpreting identification marks. Their research cautions against assuming a single coherent British organization from a recurring name. This is a comparative warning, not direct proof of how the New York group operated. [9]

London’s later Forty Elephants, a predominantly female shoplifting network, are a separate subject and should not be presented as an overseas affiliate or successor of the Five Points group. Museum and modern academic accounts discuss the later London network on its own terms. [14] [15]

Historical Significance

The Forty Thieves matter to the history of American organized wrongdoing because they show how an identifiable name could circulate around early nineteenth-century collective street crime before the later consolidation of professional rackets, bootlegging enterprises, and nationally connected criminal networks. The gang’s fame also illustrates the historical tendency to treat groups in poor neighborhoods as more centralized, enduring, and ethnically representative than the surviving evidence supports. [3] [5] [7]

A careful account is both narrower and more useful than the canonical legend. It documents references to a late-1820s Five Points gang, recognizes the context of informal crime and street disorder, and marks as unresolved its leader, exact roster, headquarters, revenue procedures, relationships, and end date. That approach provides a stronger comparative baseline for future MGU articles on the Dead Rabbits, Whyos, Gopher Gang, and much later Irish-American racketeering syndicates.

People and Names in the Historical Record

Henry Sutton
Reported by a 2019 researcher as a named man associated with the Forty Thieves in 1829 newspaper coverage. The reported arrest and incarceration should be verified against court and prison records before building a full criminal biography. [3]
Edward Coleman (d. 1839)

Terminology

Five Points
A historical Lower Manhattan neighborhood at a convergence of streets; an urban district rather than an individual criminal organization.
Sixth Ward
Municipal ward containing the Five Points area during the period described.
Street gang
A potentially loose association involved in street-level collective wrongdoing, conflict, or intimidation; not automatically a formal syndicate.
The “Forties”
A shortened label reported in archival discussion of the 1820s Forty Thieves.
Retrospective account
A description produced substantially after the events it narrates; useful but requiring attention to intervening retellings.
Contemporaneous source
A record created close to the relevant event; generally stronger for dating a claim, though not immune from error or prejudice.
Gang attribution
A source’s assignment of an act or person to a named group; its evidentiary strength depends on the underlying basis.
Historic continuity
Demonstrated persistence of a particular organization over time; identical names or similar activities alone do not establish it.
Five Points mythology
The accumulated popular representation of the district as a uniformly criminal environment, sometimes at odds with archival and archaeological evidence.
Irish-American crime syndicates
MGU editorial category encompassing distinct groups; not an implication that a single national Irish Mafia governed them.

Research and Editorial Standards

Source hierarchy. Dated primary newspapers, court dockets, prison records, and household documentation are the strongest sources for specific people and incidents. The 1829 newspaper evidence cited here was located and summarized by a modern researcher; the original newspaper images and underlying Sutton court record were not independently facsimile-collated for this edition. Asbury and the general-interest summaries are treated as retrospective narratives rather than definitive membership lists. [1] [2] [3] [4]

Leadership and membership. The article will not describe Edward Coleman as the proven founder or leader; identify every early Five Points offender as an associate; convert “Forty” into a membership count; or assert precise quotas, meetings, rules, rivalries, or a dissolution date without records supporting them. [3]

Geography, ethnicity, and community. Labels attached to gangs do not characterize the Irish, Black, immigrant, or working-class communities of the district. The violence and poverty described in certain period accounts were real historical problems, but archaeological and demographic findings demonstrate that the neighborhood was also a place of productive ordinary life. [5] [6] [8]

No false institutional continuity. This early street gang had no demonstrated connection of command with twentieth-century Irish-American organized-crime syndicates, the Italian-American Mafia, the political “Forty Thieves” aldermen, or later British organizations sharing the name. [9] [13] [14]

Editorial scope. This is a source-attributed historical encyclopedia synthesis, not a criminal docket reconstruction or an exhaustive facsimile edition. Its publication cutoff is November 13, 2026. Further research priorities are the 1828–1829 newspaper notices, Henry Sutton’s incarceration record, and independent period documentation for the asserted Peers headquarters.
