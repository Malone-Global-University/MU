Overview

The Westies were a New York City Irish-American-led organized-crime enterprise centered on Hell’s Kitchen, Manhattan. Federal appellate courts identify James “Jimmy” Coonan as its principal leader and Francis “Mickey” Featherstone as a leading associate. Its activities included illegal lending, extortion, gambling, narcotics distribution, interference with waterfront labor, fraud and lethal violence. Unlike earlier local gangs whose existence is known chiefly through retrospective folklore, the Westies are documented in lengthy federal criminal proceedings, witness testimony and multiple appellate opinions. [1] [2] [4]

The organization emerged as Coonan and associates challenged the existing Hell’s Kitchen rackets of Michael “Mickey” Spillane beginning in the mid-1960s. By the late 1970s, Coonan’s network had established substantial influence and negotiated an alliance with the Gambino Mafia family. Federal racketeering charges in 1987 and convictions beginning in 1988 dismantled the leadership structure that had operated through the preceding decades. A later criminal prosecution does not prove that a present-day Westies syndicate continues to function. [1] [2] [7]

This article treats the Westies as a distinct enterprise from Spillane’s earlier organization and from the Italian-American Mafia families with which it dealt. It uses judicial records for verdicts and the outline of proven racketeering activity; dates, identities and motives offered in press or later books are attributed to those sources. Research and editorial cutoff: November 13, 2026. [1] [3] [10]

A court finding that an informal group was a RICO enterprise does not imply that it operated like a legally incorporated business or that every associate was formally initiated. “Westies” was also a police and press identifier, and contemporaries sometimes used “Irish gang” or “Coonan’s crew.” A collective label did not have to appear on a membership card to satisfy federal law’s definition of a criminal enterprise. Courts examined sustained association and coordinated activity, not self-branding alone. [1] [11]

Names, Boundaries, and the Meaning of “Irish Mob”

The name “Westies” refers to Manhattan’s West Side and became well known through prosecutors, reporters and later popular histories. The Second Circuit explicitly listed several alternative labels when discussing the charged enterprise. For MGU indexing, the label is useful because it distinguishes a defined 1960s–1980s prosecution record from both the earlier Spillane circle and looser twentieth-century “West Side” street-gang identities. [1] [11]

Irish-American identity was prominent among the leading defendants, but ethnicity was neither a legal element of the RICO offense nor proof of membership. The gang’s victims, customers and partners came from different backgrounds; collaboration with Italian-American Mafia groups was commercial and coercive rather than a replacement of the Westies’ separate identity. The category does not characterize the Irish-American population of Hell’s Kitchen. [1] [10]

The expression “Irish Mafia” is journalistic shorthand. It should not be confused with the formal hierarchy, initiation practices or inter-family Commission arrangements associated with La Cosa Nostra. The evidence shows an association-in-fact sustained by overlapping criminal projects, personal authority and relationships, not a national Irish Mafia franchise. [1] [2]

Hell’s Kitchen: Neighborhood and Local Economy

Hell’s Kitchen developed beside Midtown’s West Side transportation, industrial and entertainment corridors. Hudson River piers, truck deliveries, construction work, bars, hiring arrangements and neighborhood shops brought workers and businesses into repeated contact. These activities created opportunities for extortion and illicit lending without implying that the gang owned every facility or controlled every union worker. [1] [6]

The locality was also a residential community whose households and organizations cannot be reduced to crime statistics. Coercive lenders exploited real residents; demands placed on waterfront operations burdened ordinary workers and honest contractors. Historical analysis must distinguish the relatively small number of racketeers from the much larger working population of the area. [6] [10]

A later federal prosecution defined a criminal enterprise partly through conduct that extended beyond the neighborhood, including cocaine distribution in bars on Manhattan’s Upper East Side and killings or transactions involving New Jersey. Its base remained Hell’s Kitchen, but its criminal network did not follow a precise geographic boundary. [1] [3]

Predecessors: The Gophers and Mickey Spillane’s Organization

The early-twentieth-century Gopher Gang and other historical West Side groups belong to the neighborhood’s longer criminal history. Contemporary statements occasionally implied a continuous “Irish gang” from those days to Coonan, but no unbroken membership, succession or command chain is established by the federal Westies cases. The Gophers were a different generation and an analytically separate organization. [1] [9]

The immediate precursor was the racket network associated with Michael “Mickey” Spillane, a prominent figure by the 1960s. The 1991 appellate opinion describes Coonan and his associates organizing in opposition to Spillane rather than receiving an uncontested appointment as his successors. The leadership transition involved a struggle for revenue sources and coercive reputation. [1] [6]

This distinction matters for encyclopedia cross-references. Spillane-era activities and deaths should be studied in the separate MGU Mickey Spillane Gang profile. The Westies profile focuses on Coonan’s group, its separate federal prosecutions and the acts attributed to that group by the courts. [1] [10]

James Coonan and the Organization’s Rise

James Coonan’s rise began in the mid-1960s as he assembled associates in Hell’s Kitchen and challenged existing underworld authority. The appellate court later accepted trial evidence showing that he prevailed over Spillane and that his organization expanded through the 1970s and early 1980s. This was a change in criminal influence rather than a public change of ownership or a formally recorded election of a “boss.” [1]

Coonan’s power derived from access to credit, trusted enforcers, relationships with other criminal organizations and the capacity to threaten or inflict violence. A centralized decision could reach people outside his immediate location: the record of the Vincent Leone murder indicates that instructions were carried while Coonan was imprisoned, a useful demonstration of influence without requiring every associate to share a single residence or office. [1] [3]

The later federal case also put limits on the heroic mythology of the gangster. Coonan’s leadership exposed associates and civilians to serious harm, created witnesses against the organization and provided prosecutors with evidence of continuing racketeering activity. The 2025 appellate opinion recorded that he was serving a 75-year sentence following his convictions. [1] [4]

Mickey Featherstone: Lieutenant and Witness

Francis “Mickey” Featherstone became a principal associate and, according to the Second Circuit’s account, Coonan’s second-in-command by the middle of the 1970s. He figured in the gang’s violence and lending enterprises, and his eventual cooperation was pivotal to the prosecution. “Second-in-command” summarizes a relationship of influence; it should not be mistaken for a complete, bureaucratic roster of captains. [1] [5]

Featherstone was wrongly convicted in New York state court of the 1985 murder of Michael Holly. Evidence developed after his conviction identified William Bokun as the killer, led to the conviction being overturned in 1986, and helped establish that Featherstone had been set up. The National Registry of Exonerations documents the wrongful conviction and subsequent investigation. This exoneration concerned the Holly homicide; it does not erase his documented participation in other criminal acts. [1] [3] [5]

Marcelle (“Sissy”) Featherstone assisted the investigation that challenged the false conviction. Afterward, Featherstone provided law-enforcement testimony concerning the Westies. His cooperation is not simply a colorful betrayal story: it was a causal turning point in identifying acts, communications and participants that an ordinary neighborhood investigation had struggled to connect. [1] [5]

Structure, Associates, and the RICO Enterprise Finding

The Westies did not require a Mafia-style initiation ceremony to constitute a criminal organization under federal racketeering law. The appellate court held that an association-in-fact enterprise could be proven through an ongoing group functioning as a continuing unit. Former members and associates testified about leadership, common criminal activities, operations and coordinated violence. Evidence of crimes could help prove the existence of the enterprise. [1]

Specific projects could involve different combinations of participants. The same individual might enforce a debt, arrange an illicit business, transport a person or supply cocaine on different occasions. Such overlapping activity made the organization more durable than a single pair of offenders, even though the record does not justify publishing a complete, fixed membership roll. [1] [3]

No universal headcount is adopted. Estimates printed in later books vary and risk conflating regular members, occasional associates, unwilling intermediaries and people merely known in Hell’s Kitchen. This page identifies individuals when the record gives a reason to name them and distinguishes defendants, witnesses, victims and outside criminal figures. [1] [6] [11]

Criminal Markets and Revenue Systems

The appellate record identifies illegal credit, gambling, extortion, narcotics distribution, counterfeiting and labor-related racketeering. These activities shared a method: the ability to enforce debt or exclusion through the gang’s violent reputation. Crime proceeds did not have to come from a single headquarters for members to support one another through financing, enforcement and shared contacts. [1] [2] [4]

Money flowed through multiple projects rather than a publicly reported consolidated balance sheet. A loan might originate with one associate, be collected by another, and be supported by the reputation of a third party. Waterfront influence could produce employment or contract opportunities while narcotics and gambling created independent cash flows. The court record is strong on these mechanisms but is not a verified annual corporate income statement. [1] [3]

A prosecution exhibit’s financial figure, a witness estimate and a contemporary newspaper total are different kinds of evidence. This entry avoids giving the Westies a precise lifetime revenue number or announcing that it controlled all docks and labor unions. Those claims require transaction-specific accounts that are not reconstructed here. [1] [6]

Loansharking: Capital, Collections, and Coercion

The mid-1970s loansharking system described by the appellate court involved Coonan supplying funds to Westies associates who in turn lent at unlawfully high interest. Kevin Kelly began as a collector and enforcer for others before operating his own loansharking activity. Violence reinforced payment demands and made the organization’s name a tool of coercion. [1]

The criminal significance was not simply an expensive loan. A borrower faced potentially serious consequences for missed payments, and enforcing one debt helped create fear among others. In the Kelly appeal, the court summarized testimony of beatings and threats in collection disputes. The case established Kelly’s involvement but did not transform every lender in Hell’s Kitchen into a Westies member. [1]

By the late 1970s, the Gambino family’s participation in financing deepened the Westies’ capacity. The appellate court described an arrangement under which the Westies could use the Gambino reputation and paid a share of specified illegal proceeds. The lending arrangement underscores how separate enterprises could profit from cooperation without merging into a single family. [1] [3]

Illegal Gambling and Neighborhood Venues

Illegal bookmaking and gambling were part of the charged racketeering history and the group’s neighborhood reputation. Informal premises and social ties could give an illicit gambling operator repeat customers and ways to resolve disputes, but the sources do not establish one gang-owned gambling hall or a single universal book of wagers. [1] [2]

The Kelly appellate opinion supplies an important legal qualification. Kelly was acquitted on a count of conducting an illegal gambling business even while other convictions were upheld. An offense category attributed to an enterprise must not be translated into a conviction for every named defendant. [1]

The same principle applies to venue ownership. A bar where an associate sold cocaine or collected money was not necessarily a bar legally or beneficially owned by the gang. Economic influence and property title are different questions. [1] [6]

Cocaine Operations in the 1980s

The federal record identifies four interrelated cocaine-distribution operations between 1984 and 1986. Kevin Kelly participated in distribution connected to bars on Manhattan’s Upper East Side, furnished cocaine and financing to other associates, and helped collect drug debts. This is more specific than the generic statement that the gang was “in drugs.” [1]

The drug cases show both continuity and diversification: associates used the gang’s existing financial and enforcement network to pursue markets outside its traditional Hell’s Kitchen base. Participation varied by person and transaction; the court did not describe a single vertically integrated operation controlling the entire New York cocaine trade. [1] [3]

Later popular narratives often make drug trafficking appear inseparable from every phase of a gang’s history. The dated court evidence here supports important cocaine activity in the mid-1980s, but it does not warrant moving those same operations backward into the 1960s or treating all contemporaneous narcotics activity in Midtown as controlled by the Westies. [1] [10]

West Side Piers and Longshoremen

The Hudson River waterfront provided a particularly valuable point of criminal influence. The Westies pursued profits and leverage connected with port employment and the International Longshoremen’s Association (ILA) Local 1909. The 1991 opinion links the Gambino family, union official Vincent Leone and Westies efforts to oversee illegal business on the West Side piers. [1] [3]

Influence over hiring and opportunities could support both direct extortion and indirect income from arrangements with contractors or individual labor intermediaries. It does not mean that a criminal group controlled all waterfront commerce, nor that every member of ILA Local 1909 was implicated. Honest workers were vulnerable to coercion and distorted hiring practices. [1] [6]

The evidence surrounding Leone is particularly instructive because it connects financial disputes to homicide and organizational command. Such cases allow historians to study a specific corrupt arrangement rather than repeat a broad and unsupported claim that the Westies “ran the entire waterfront.” [1] [3]

The Intrepid Museum and Payroll/Ticket Schemes

The Mob Museum’s institutional history describes how Westies-linked activity affected the Intrepid Sea, Air & Space Museum, which opened on the aircraft carrier at Pier 86 in 1982. Its account describes siphoned ticket receipts and no-show or improperly directed labor positions. The museum’s legitimate historical mission did not make it a criminal enterprise; the documented concern was exploitation of an otherwise legitimate institution. [6]

The Mob Museum relates discovery of irregularities to financial examination amid the Intrepid’s July 1985 bankruptcy proceedings. This is useful evidence of the opportunities created by labor access, cash handling and weak oversight, not proof that a museum board authorized the rackets or that every museum employee was complicit. [6]

Because this particular discussion is a later historical synthesis, the encyclopedia does not convert it into an unqualified finding against every individual named in secondary accounts. Specific dollar losses, personal culpability and criminal convictions should be tied to court or financial records before being asserted as separate adjudicated facts. [6] [1]

Alliance with the Gambino Crime Family

The most consequential external relationship was an alliance with the Gambino crime family during the late 1970s. The federal appellate opinion states that Gambino figures supplied financing for loansharking and permitted the Westies to use the family’s reputation in their rackets. In return, the Westies paid ten percent of proceeds from various illegal activities as described in the court’s trial-evidence account. [1]

That arrangement indicates a transactional alliance between separate groups. It does not prove the Westies were formally inducted as a Gambino crew or that Coonan held a La Cosa Nostra rank. The Westies retained a distinguishable organization whose violent and financial operations were independently prosecuted. [1] [3]

Later books identify important Mafia personalities and intermediaries, including Roy DeMeo and senior Gambino figures. Those details help describe the wider underworld network but require source-specific qualification when attributing personal knowledge, approval of a particular murder or command over a particular Westies member. The judicial findings on the financing and ten-percent arrangement are the firmer basis for this section. [1] [6] [10]

Other Organized-Crime Relationships

The Westies operated inside a city where Italian-American Mafia families and independent criminal entrepreneurs competed or cooperated in lending, gambling and labor rackets. Charles “Ruby” Stein, whose death was discussed at trial, was a loansharking financier connected to this milieu. His dealings with Coonan demonstrate how financial interdependence could develop into serious conflict. [1] [10]

Connections should be represented at the level supported by particular records: a loan, a shared business, a witness’s account of an introduction, or a proven extortion arrangement. A name appearing in several criminal biographies does not prove there was a permanent syndicate-wide alliance, and it cannot be used to invent an inter-family command chart. [1] [3]

Likewise, the fact that a Mafia associate appeared in a waterfront racket does not make a union or employer part of a Mafia family. Distinguishing organizations, intermediaries and victims gives the encyclopedia a more useful institutional map than a single undifferentiated “mob” label. [1] [6]

Violence as a Means of Organizational Control

The Westies employed threats and serious assaults to enforce debts, retaliate against perceived challenges and discourage disobedience. Court evidence described murder, attempted murder and violent loansharking activity. The extreme brutality associated with the gang was not merely a literary flourish: several killings were discussed in detail by federal appellate judges reviewing the trial record. [1] [3]

At the same time, accusations must be assigned to the proper evidentiary category. An indictment enumerating dozens of racketeering acts is a statement of charges; a witness can testify to events not charged against a particular defendant; and a conviction may establish a narrower set of offenses than a press report suggests. This profile does not equate a 1987 indictment count with a proven homicide. [2] [7]

Graphic dismemberment details can be historically relevant because they explain concealment and witness difficulty, but repetition for spectacle adds little. The following case summaries concentrate on victims, evidence and adjudicated outcomes rather than sensational description. [1] [14]

The 1977 Killing of Charles “Ruby” Stein

The 1991 appellate opinion recounts that in May 1977, Coonan, William (“Billie”) Beattie and other Westies members killed Charles “Ruby” Stein, a loansharking financier to whom Coonan owed a large sum. The opinion describes the concealment of Stein’s remains as part of the evidence presented at trial. Stein’s death illustrates the hazards of underworld lending disputes and the use of violence to evade financial obligations. [1]

Stein should not be listed as a confirmed “Westies lieutenant” merely because he extended credit to people in the organization. His role as a financier and his status as a homicide victim are distinct from membership. Evidence of his financial dealings does not supply a complete ledger of the group’s debts or a motive for every related crime. [1] [10]

The 1978 Killing of Richard Tassiello

The same appellate history identifies January 1978 as the period of the killing of Richard Tassiello, a borrower who had fallen behind in payments. The account identifies Coonan, Featherstone and Anton (“Tony”) Lucich as involved and describes the disposal of the body. The case shows how loansharking and lethal violence could be connected by disputes over repayment. [1]

Tassiello was a victim of a homicide described in court evidence, not a supporting character whose death should be used only to underline the gang’s reputation. For publication purposes, the date, participants and loan dispute come from the appellate summary; the underlying trial exhibits and witness transcripts have not been independently reproduced here. [1] [3]

Vincent Leone, ILA Local 1909, and the 1984 Murder

Vincent Leone was an officer of ILA Local 1909 and, according to the appellate court’s account, an associate of the Gambino family who helped oversee illegal waterfront business with the Westies. In late 1983, the gang concluded that he had diverted money from those operations. Coonan, then incarcerated, relayed instructions that Leone be killed; the opinion describes Edna Coonan acting as a channel for those directions. [1]

On February 4, 1984, Leone was killed during a car journey in New Jersey. Federal appellate decisions identify Kevin Kelly and James McElroy in the attack and describe William Bokun’s role afterward. The case is important both for its victim and because it links waterfront financial misconduct, organizational command and an out-of-state killing. [1] [3]

A union affiliation, a criminal association and participation in a particular murder are separate factual propositions. Leone’s position was not evidence of guilt by all union members, and the court’s findings about individual defendants should not be generalized to unnamed workers or officers. [1] [3]

Michael Holly’s 1985 Murder and the 1977 Dispute

The background to Michael Holly’s murder began on March 25, 1977, when John Bokun shot Holly during a confrontation and then fired at an off-duty police officer who intervened. The officer returned fire and fatally wounded John Bokun; Holly survived. The Westies thereafter treated Holly as responsible for Bokun’s death, according to the later federal record. [1]

On April 25, 1985, William Bokun shot and killed Holly on a Manhattan street while wearing a disguise, with Kenneth Shannon driving the getaway vehicle. Appellate evidence links the action to a plot involving Westies members. These dates matter because some summaries collapse the 1977 confrontation, the 1985 murder, and the 1986 wrongful-conviction episode into a single anecdote. [1] [3]

Featherstone was arrested in connection with Holly’s death and initially convicted, despite not being the shooter. Subsequent investigation and cooperation overturned that conviction. The encyclopedia treats Holly as the homicide victim, Bokun as the shooter identified in court, and Featherstone as exonerated of this specific killing. [1] [3] [5]

Witness Cooperation and the Collapse of Secrecy

Featherstone’s 1986 exoneration and subsequent cooperation changed the scale of the case against the Westies. Federal sources credit his and Marcelle Featherstone’s assistance with uncovering evidence of the organization’s crimes. Other former associates, including William Beattie and Anton Lucich, provided testimony concerning the structure and violent actions of the group. [1] [5]

The power of the testimony lay in connecting acts across years: loans, instructions, disputed collections, alleged killings and the relationships among participants. Instead of one isolated homicide or bar fight, the prosecution could argue that separate acts formed part of a continuing enterprise. That is why witness credibility and the legal definition of a RICO organization were major appellate issues. [1] [2]

Cooperating witnesses may have motives to minimize their own conduct or obtain favorable treatment. A sound historical method considers their testimony alongside corroborating evidence and court review, while also recognizing that a jury finding is not an unlimited endorsement of every statement a witness made. [1] [3]

The September 17, 1987 Federal Indictment

On September 17, 1987, a federal indictment in the Southern District of New York charged ten defendants with crimes connected to the alleged Westies racketeering enterprise. A 1988 appellate opinion describes fifteen counts and 32 charged predicate acts, including allegations of murders, attempted murders, kidnappings, loansharking, extortion, narcotics, gambling, mail fraud and counterfeiting. [2]

Those counts were accusations to be tested at trial. The number 32 is the number of alleged racketeering acts in the indictment, not a court finding that 32 crimes occurred as charged or that each defendant was responsible for all of them. As the prosecution proceeded, some allegations were dismissed, altered or subject to different jury verdicts. [2]

The charged RICO enterprise was central to the case: prosecutors sought to prove that coordinated criminal activity extended beyond isolated offenses. The significance of the indictment lies in the consolidation of evidence over a long period, not simply in the shock value of a long list of allegations. [1] [2]

The 1987–1988 Trial and February 1988 Verdicts

The main Westies trial began in autumn 1987 before U.S. District Judge Whitman Knapp. On February 24, 1988, contemporaneous reporting recorded the jury’s guilty verdicts against seven defendants, including James and Edna Coonan. An eighth defendant, John Halo, was acquitted. The case established the existence of the Westies criminal enterprise as a matter tried before the jury. [2] [7]

The defendant list included James McElroy, William Bokun, Richard Ritter, Thomas Collins and Florence Collins. Reporting and later court rulings document differing charges and sentences; it is incorrect to say that all accused persons were convicted of every racketeering act or that the acquitted defendant was found criminally responsible. [3] [7] [13]

The first verdicts also did not conclude every Westies proceeding. Kevin Kelly and Kenneth Shannon had not been tried with the initial eight, and the subsequent Kelly prosecution produced its own jury findings, appeal and sentence. Keeping the separate proceedings visible is essential to an accurate chronology. [1] [12]

Kevin Kelly’s Separate Trial and 1991 Appeal

Kevin Kelly was a fugitive during the first Westies trial. After surrendering, he faced a separate jury trial. The 1991 Second Circuit opinion records convictions for substantive and conspiratorial racketeering, attempted murder and assault in aid of racketeering, unlawful-lending conspiracies, cocaine conspiracy and extortion conspiracy. He was acquitted on a gambling-business charge and received a 50-year prison sentence. [1] [12]

His appeal argued, among other points, that the organization was insufficiently coherent to be a RICO enterprise and challenged the use of evidence concerning Michael Holly’s murder after a state acquittal. The appellate court rejected the enterprise challenge, explained the legal distinction between state and federal prosecutions, and affirmed Kelly’s convictions in the case before it. [1]

The opinion offers more than a procedural outcome: it is one of the clearest accessible judicial reconstructions of the Westies’ rise, lending, narcotics, waterfront relationships and violent disputes. At the same time, its background summarizes trial evidence and cannot be used to infer unrelated crimes or uncharged acts. [1]

William Bokun and the 1995 Sentencing Appeal

William “Billie” Bokun’s record requires its own distinction between offense findings and changing sentence orders. An appellate opinion states that a jury convicted him on February 24, 1988 of racketeering participation and conspiracy and cocaine conspiracy. Initially sentenced to 50 years, he received a reduction to 35 years in 1991 and a later reduction to 25 years, which the court of appeals reversed in 1995. [3]

The 1995 court ordered reinstatement of the 35-year sentence. This sequence is not merely technical: repeating the original 50-year sentence as his unchanged final sentence would misstate the published appellate result. His participation in the Holly and Leone cases is discussed in the same opinion, with testimony and the basis for criminal liability identified separately. [3]

Edna Coonan and Organizational Communications

Edna Coonan appears in the federal case as more than a person related to the principal leader. The 1991 appellate opinion recounts communications transmitted while James Coonan was imprisoned, including instructions regarding Vincent Leone. Her role shows how spouses and close associates could become important intermediaries in organized criminal activity. [1]

The 1990 Southern District of New York order records that Edna was convicted in connection with Westies racketeering and sentenced in May 1988 to 15 years and a fine. That decision addressed a specific post-conviction request; it should not be misrepresented as a fresh trial of all the underlying historical episodes. [13]

Her conduct should be assessed through the convictions and evidence, not by treating marriage as evidence of participation. Spouses of criminals do not automatically become members of a criminal enterprise, and statements about culpability require independently supported individual acts. [1] [13]

Law Enforcement, Jurisdiction, and Evidence

The Westies case involved federal racketeering prosecutions built from evidence of crimes ordinarily investigated at different times and locations. Federal RICO law permitted the prosecution to explain how extortion, drugs, violence and corrupt labor relationships belonged to the activities of an ongoing enterprise. The use of state and federal investigations also created disputes about evidence, prior acquittals and the exact scope of charges. [1] [2]

The 1988 appellate procedural opinion arose from a disagreement about how a jury should be instructed in the ongoing RICO trial. The 1991 Kelly opinion addressed sufficiency of enterprise evidence and double-jeopardy claims. The 1995 Bokun case concerned sentencing authority. These documents are complementary but must not be summarized as if each were a verdict finding every Westies crime. [1] [2] [3]

A criminal organization can be demonstrably real without every popular story about it being accurate. This is why the encyclopedia uses decisions, charged counts, witness accounts and later historical interpretation as separate source categories. [1] [10]

Disruption, Dissolution, and Claims of Continuity

The federal cases removed or imprisoned central figures and seriously disrupted the Westies’ ability to operate as the enterprise described in court. The events of 1986–1988 are the strongest basis for describing the decline of Coonan’s network. Later court proceedings reflect the long legal consequences of those convictions, not necessarily an active organization continuing in the same form. [1] [3] [4]

Some internet summaries offer a single dissolution year, sometimes the late 1980s or early 1990s. The record supports a loss of the prosecuted leadership structure, but not a verified last meeting, formal dissolution decree or proof that every local gambling or lending operation stopped on one date. The phrase “no verified continuing Westies organization” is more precise than assigning a confidently dated ending. [1] [4]

Comparisons to the older Gophers or Spillane network should not be used to imply an unbroken, century-long Irish Mafia. Economic conditions and neighborhood criminal markets may persist after particular groups are dismantled; organizational identity requires evidence of actual continuing associates and command relationships. [1] [9]

Coonan’s 2025 Appellate Ruling

On July 9, 2025, the Second Circuit decided a later appeal brought by James Coonan. The opinion described his Westies convictions, 75-year sentence and attempts to seek release or reduction. It ruled that the sentence-reduction mechanism in 18 U.S.C. § 3582(c)(1), as amended by the First Step Act, was not available for the pre-November 1, 1987 offenses in his case. [4]

This decision is a valuable current legal landmark, but it did not revisit the original verdicts or convict a newly active Westies syndicate. The opinion reported a projected future mandatory-release date as information from the Bureau of Prisons in 2025; a projection is not an assertion here about Coonan’s precise custody location or status on the encyclopedia’s 2026 publication date. [4]

The distinction between a continuing prison sentence and a continuing criminal organization is essential. A group may remain a subject of litigation for decades after its original racketeering network was disrupted. [4]

People Associated with the Historical Record

The following register identifies individuals by the role established or reported in the sources consulted. Being named in a trial caption is not proof of a conviction. A murder victim is not classified as an organizational member unless separate evidence supports that status. [1] [2] [3] [5]

James “Jimmy” Coonan

Community Impact and the Limits of Crime Narratives

Loansharking violence, labor racketeering and coercive control imposed costs on working people. Borrowers faced physical threats, workers could lose honest opportunities, and public-facing institutions could be exploited. The legal record documents specific mechanisms of harm, which are more informative than presenting Hell’s Kitchen as a universally lawless neighborhood. [1] [6]

The presence of an Irish-American-led criminal group is not an explanation for Irish-American community identity, culture or political behavior. Neighborhood residents were victims, witnesses, ordinary business owners and participants in civic life; ethnic solidarity neither makes collective wrongdoing inevitable nor provides a membership criterion. [1] [10]

Gang history should also preserve victims’ names and the uncertainty around allegations. A dramatic account in a popular book may be well researched, but a criminal verdict, exoneration, historical inference and eyewitness recollection are different categories of knowledge. [5] [10] [14]

T. J. English, Reporting, and Westies Mythology

T. J. English’s The Westies, first published in 1990, is central to the organization’s modern historical visibility. The book drew on reporting, interviews and the dramatic federal prosecutions. A contemporary Publishers Weekly review noted that the label “Westies” was associated with reporters and police rather than a formal self-title used by all participants. [10] [11]

Crime writing often emphasizes spectacular violence, hidden bodies and colorful personalities. Those events may have a factual basis, but they can also eclipse the ordinary economics of debt collection, labor access, fear and cash flow. A publication-quality encyclopedia should use the trial record to anchor specific claims and use literary histories chiefly to identify narrative traditions, leads and contexts requiring further verification. [1] [10] [14]

The gang’s notoriety also creates a temptation to assign every Hell’s Kitchen killing to Coonan or to treat Mickey Spillane, the Gophers and the Westies as one long-lived “Irish Mob.” The documented change in leadership and the absence of a century-long membership chain are reasons to keep the articles separate. [1] [9] [11]

Historical Significance

The Westies illustrate how a neighborhood-based, ethnically associated criminal group could conduct enduring racketeering without the formal institutions of a national Mafia family. Its association-in-fact structure was still cohesive enough for the federal government to prosecute it under RICO, and its criminal businesses reached from local loans to piers and out-of-neighborhood drug distribution. [1] [2]

The case is also an important study of criminal alliances: the Gambino relationship gave the Westies access to finance and an underworld reputation, but did not erase organizational distinction. The violence of the gang, and the eventual breakdown of secrecy through witnesses, helped produce the evidence on which federal juries and appellate courts relied. [1] [3] [5]

For historians, the Westies record is a rare opportunity to compare public memory with legal documentation. The criminal enterprise’s existence is not chiefly dependent on colorful gang lore; its victims, legal outcomes, business activities and contested stories can be studied from multiple independent sources. [1] [4] [10]

Terminology

Association-in-fact enterprise

An organized group of individuals working together for a shared purpose; RICO does not require a legally incorporated entity or formal charter.

RICO

Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act; federal law addressing patterns of racketeering associated with an enterprise.

Predicate act

An alleged or proved offense serving as part of a racketeering charge; the existence of an allegation does not establish a verdict.

Loansharking

Unlawful lending and/or collection often involving extortionate terms or threats.

Waterfront racketeering

Criminal exploitation of dockside hiring, labor positions, business access, transportation or union relationships.

Gambino family

Separate Italian-American Mafia organization whose alliance with the Westies was described in court evidence.

Cooperating witness

Person providing evidence to investigators or prosecutors, potentially with incentives that must be considered.

Exoneration

Vacatur or other reversal establishing that a criminal conviction should not stand; scope is specific to the case.

Double jeopardy

Constitutional doctrine restricting multiple prosecutions for the same offense; dual-sovereignty principles were discussed in Kelly’s appeal.

Acquittal

A not-guilty verdict on a charge; not interchangeable with a dismissal or reduction of sentence.

Prosecutorial allegation

Claim made in indictment or advocacy, not itself a finding of guilt.

Historic continuity

Evidence of an organization persisting over time; shared neighborhood or a recurring label does not automatically prove succession.

Research and Editorial Standards

Source hierarchy. Federal appellate opinions establish what appellate courts described, the basis of legal rulings and their treatment of trial evidence. District-court decisions establish their particular orders. Contemporaneous wire-service accounts help date verdicts, statements and reactions; their descriptions of pending charges remain allegations. The National Registry of Exonerations adds a specialized account of Featherstone’s case. Museum interpretation and T. J. English’s narrative supply broader context without automatically superseding the court record. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6] [7]

Individual outcomes. This page distinguishes Coonan’s convictions from Kelly’s separate trial, Bokun’s later sentencing appeal and Halo’s acquittal. It does not describe all ten indicted defendants as having been convicted in the same 1988 trial. Neither a witness’s association with the Westies nor a family relationship is sufficient, alone, to establish criminal participation. [1] [3] [7] [12] [13]

Violent acts. Cases involving Stein, Tassiello, Leone and Holly are grounded in the federal court’s summary of trial evidence. A federal appellate narrative is strong authority for the matters decided but is not a complete reconstruction of each original police file, medical report or transcript. The present edition does not claim independent examination of every underlying exhibit. [1] [3]

Continuity and scale. No exact membership count, annual profit, nationwide franchise, universal ethnic makeup or formally dated dissolution is asserted. The 2025 Coonan appeal is a sentencing decision rather than proof of a present-day Westies crew. Cross-references to the Gophers and Spillane organization describe historical context, not an uninterrupted chain of leadership. [1] [4] [10] [11]

These numbered references are the documentary basis for the entry. Judicial opinions establish the proceedings and evidence they summarize; newspaper allegations and retrospective history are identified as such. Links go to the source records rather than to invented citations.
