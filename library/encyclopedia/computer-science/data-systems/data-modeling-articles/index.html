Collection Overview

The Data Modeling unit is part of the Data Systems division in the Computer Science volume of the MGU Encyclopedia. It extends the existing Data Modeling parent article, whose coverage ranges from domain discovery and entity relationships through relational dependencies, temporal data, dimensional models, document and graph structures, and stewardship. This page is a specialist directory and editorial guide; it does not substitute for that parent or write any of the child articles.

A data model records decisions about what facts mean, how they relate, and which constraints make a data state valid. A diagram alone is not a model, a schema alone may not capture business meaning, and an implementation may satisfy all declared column types while returning incorrect answers to a business question. Sound modeling begins by identifying the domain, the unit of identity, relevant relationships, expected operations, time semantics, and the evidence needed to verify the design. Conceptual, logical and physical models answer different questions and need not have one-to-one correspondence.

The unit spans operational relational systems, analytical fact-and-dimension structures, documents, graphs and published semantic contracts. The objective is not to crown one modeling style as superior. Each approach should instead be examined against the domain’s rules, transaction and query workloads, historical obligations, interoperability requirements and change-control process. Performance decisions are meaningful only after the model preserves the required facts.

Taxonomy and division of labor: this is a Level 4 unit index represented by data-systems/data-modeling-articles/index.html. Its 15 linked titles are planned Level 5 unit subarticles. More granular Level 6 companion articles, and deeper companions, may be developed when warranted, but are not produced here. The four thematic groups below are editorial reading groups rather than extra directories or taxonomy levels.

Source-controlled roster: the parent already prescribes exactly 15 specialist titles and filenames. This index retains their spelling, wording, capitalization and original order, and changes only the presentation of planned destinations to active, extensionless hyperlinks in accordance with the current MGU policy. No additional subjects are added to that Level 5 roster. The longer article scopes, editorial boundaries, comparisons and worked cases are new planning material.

Core learning outcome: a reader should be able to convert a domain statement into a precise model; distinguish conceptual, logical and physical decisions; explain an integrity rule with counterexamples; select relational, dimensional, document or graph structures without confusing their purposes; and describe how a schema will evolve without silently changing the meaning of existing information.

Modeling Layers and Diagrammatic Foundations

These four entries build the translation chain from what stakeholders mean, to formal logical structure, to an implementable system, and to visual communication of the relationship model. Their boundaries prevent a single general modeling article from absorbing the distinct responsibilities of conceptual analysis, logical structure, physical tuning and diagram interpretation. A future author should demonstrate the same small domain across multiple modeling levels rather than introduce unrelated examples that hide the transformation.

1. Conceptual Data Modeling

Subject mandate: Stakeholder vocabulary, business rules, domain scope and technology-independent concepts. Develop the distinction between what an organization needs to know and how any database will store it. Begin with domain interviews, policy documents, observable events and counterexamples. Identify entities, relationships and their business meaning without prematurely deciding column types or storage indexes. Address competing stakeholder terminology, entity identity, ambiguous rules, organizational boundaries, assumptions and exclusions. Show how a conceptual model supplies a common reference for business experts, developers and data stewards, including situations in which two departments use one term differently. Explain iteration, traceability to source requirements, review of cardinality and optionality, and the use of meaningful examples to resolve ambiguity. The intended result is a clear shared semantic picture, not a prematurely optimized database schema.

Editorial boundary: Logical Data Modeling owns detailed attributes and identifiers; Data Dictionaries and Semantic Models owns formal metadata publication. Do not treat a conceptual diagram as a physical design or as proof that all business policies have been discovered.

Required instructional treatment: Model a university domain containing students, courses, course offerings and enrollments; give four stakeholder questions, an initial concept map and one revised relationship after uncovering a waitlist policy.

2. Logical Data Modeling

Subject mandate: Platform-independent attributes, keys, relationships, dependencies and explicit integrity rules. Translate the domain concepts into a logical schema that defines entity attributes, identifier choices, data types at the semantic level, uniqueness, mandatory versus optional values and allowed relationships. Demonstrate how one conceptual relationship may become several logical relations, and how constraints should represent real policy rather than convenient application behavior. Introduce candidate keys, primary and alternate identifiers, natural versus surrogate keys, relationship participation and the effect of nullability. Connect functional dependencies and normal forms to business rules without suggesting that normalization is a purely mechanical substitute for requirements discovery. Make a revision trail showing how an overlooked many-to-many relationship changes the schema. End with a logical schema that is comprehensible without a vendor-specific storage engine.

Editorial boundary: Physical Data Modeling owns indexes, concrete data types and partitions; Keys and Referential Integrity and Database Normalization own their respective deep formal treatments. Logical models should not be described as an automatic guarantee of data quality.

Required instructional treatment: Convert a student–course enrollment conceptual model into relations with candidate keys, required and optional columns, foreign key intent, and one explicit unresolved business assumption.

3. Physical Data Modeling

Subject mandate: Implementation-level types, constraints, access structures, partitioning and deployment trade-offs. Show the transition from the validated logical model to an implementation in a specified database family and version. Discuss type choice, precision, nullability, identity generation, collation, constraints, indexes, partitions, storage layout and common migration consequences. Demonstrate workload-led decisions: point lookup, join, aggregate, insert, and historical update patterns may justify different indexes or partitioning. Explain why performance optimizations must be measured with realistic cardinalities, actual query plans, maintenance costs and write amplification. Examine physical schema differences between row stores, column stores and distributed platforms without suggesting that they all support identical guarantees or syntax. Cover physical model review, deployment sequencing, backfill, rollback and the need to retain business semantics when introducing denormalized projections.

Editorial boundary: Database Indexing owns detailed index algorithms; Data Storage Methods owns storage mechanisms; Data Warehousing owns complete warehouse-platform design. Physical Data Modeling focuses on translating a documented model into justified structures.

Required instructional treatment: Implement a small enrollment schema with appropriate SQL types, keys and two candidate indexes; explain how a new reporting workload changes the physical design without changing the conceptual relationships.

4. Entity–Relationship Diagrams

Subject mandate: Visual notation, cardinality, participation and interpretation of relationship structure. Teach ER diagramming as a precise communication method, not merely box-and-line drawing. Introduce entities, attributes, identifiers, relationship names, unary and many-to-many relationships, weak entities, optionality and minimum/maximum cardinality. Compare Chen and crow’s-foot conventions, noting when symbol meanings differ and why readers must identify the chosen notation. Discuss layout discipline, readable labels, crossing avoidance, hierarchical decomposition and how diagrams can communicate business rules that are otherwise easy to overlook. Work through examples where one-to-one and one-to-many relationships are confused or where a many-to-many relationship requires an associative entity with its own attributes. Explain why a diagram needs supporting definitions and examples to express complicated temporal or conditional constraints.

Editorial boundary: Conceptual and Logical Data Modeling retain their distinct modeling decisions; this article teaches representations and interpretation. Avoid implying that all constraints, especially cross-record policies, fit naturally in a static ER diagram.

Required instructional treatment: Draw and interpret two alternative enrollment ER diagrams; test each with a student taking multiple offerings and an offering taught by multiple instructors, then record the missing rules.

Group checkpoint: Explain how this group contributes to the overall model and name at least one decision that belongs to a neighboring specialist article. Record a counterexample that would invalidate an apparently correct model, then revise the proposed design or its stated assumptions.

Relational Integrity, Dependencies, and Time

These four entries answer whether the modeled facts can remain internally consistent as records are inserted, corrected, deleted and reinterpreted over time. Key constraints, normal forms and dependency theory describe different aspects of validity, while temporal modeling asks which historical state a valid assertion refers to. A model must survive real counterexamples: duplicate identities, missing parents, unintended updates, hidden dependencies and late corrections.

1. Keys and Referential Integrity

Subject mandate: Entity identity, candidate and foreign keys, relationship consistency, and update policies. Explain candidate keys, superkeys, chosen primary keys, alternate keys and surrogate identifiers, using examples that distinguish identity from a mutable name or display code. Introduce composite keys, foreign keys, unique constraints, null handling and the implications of optional relationships. Demonstrate insert, delete and update behavior for restrict/no action, cascading and set-null actions, noting differences in database engines and deferred constraint support. Examine cross-system identifiers, soft deletion, effective-dated keys and the reasons a foreign key cannot enforce every business rule. Make integrity preservation concrete through invalid write attempts and cleanup procedures for legacy data. Stress the need to distinguish application validation from database-enforced guarantees.

Editorial boundary: Database Transactions owns commit, isolation and recovery behavior; Data Model Governance owns policy approvals and key migration control. A globally unique identifier is not proof that a record represents the correct real-world person or object.

Required instructional treatment: Create a student, course offering and enrollment key scheme; test duplicate enrollment, missing parent, parent deletion and historical identifier correction and document which invariant rejects each case.

2. Database Normalization

Subject mandate: Functional-dependency-based decomposition, normal forms, and modification anomalies. Work from a realistic poorly structured relation through first, second and third normal forms, then introduce Boyce–Codd and higher normal forms where dependencies require it. Explain atomic values as a model-relative concept rather than insisting that all strings or arrays must be split mechanically. Show how repeating groups, partial dependencies and transitive dependencies create insertion, update and deletion anomalies. Address lossless joins, dependency preservation, surrogate keys and the trade-offs of decompositions that complicate common transactions. Compare normalization with deliberate denormalization for read performance and analytics, requiring evidence of maintenance responsibility and synchronization costs. Include test data that reveal anomalies rather than relying on diagrams alone.

Editorial boundary: Functional Dependencies owns systematic identification and inference of dependencies; Dimensional Data Modeling owns intentional analytical designs. Normal forms should not be advertised as the only design criteria or as guarantees of semantic correctness.

Required instructional treatment: Normalize an enrollment table carrying course title, student name and instructor office; show one update anomaly before and after decomposition and evaluate whether all original constraints remain enforceable.

3. Functional Dependencies

Subject mandate: Attribute determinants, candidate-key reasoning, closure, and decomposition tests. Give a precise account of a functional dependency X → Y as a constraint on permissible relation instances, not an accidental correlation in a small sample. Introduce trivial and nontrivial dependencies, Armstrong’s inference principles, attribute closure, candidate-key search, minimal covers and the role of domain rules. Demonstrate how a dependency such as course_code → title can break when the institution permits title changes across editions, requiring a different business key or effective-time dimension. Connect dependency analysis to third normal form and BCNF decomposition, distinguishing losslessness from dependency preservation. Use counterexamples to show that discovering apparent uniqueness in test data does not establish an invariant. Encourage explicit documentation of assumptions and verification with domain stakeholders.

Editorial boundary: Database Normalization owns the full normal-form design workflow. Functional dependencies are logical relationships; they are not the same as cross-service consistency guarantees, SQL foreign keys or causal dependencies.

Required instructional treatment: Given a registration relation with an explicit dependency set, calculate attribute closure, identify candidate keys and analyze a proposed split for lossless join and dependency preservation.

4. Temporal Data Modeling

Subject mandate: Valid time, recorded time, corrections, effective dates and historical query semantics. Separate the time when a fact was true in the modeled world from the time when a system knew or recorded it. Teach valid-time, transaction-time and bitemporal patterns using a university tuition rate or employee assignment history. Compare current-state overwrite, effective-dated rows, immutable events and temporal database features, with caveats about engine support. Explain overlapping effective intervals, temporal key constraints, retroactive corrections, late-arriving events, time-zone interpretation and the need to distinguish business “as of” questions from database rollback. Show how to avoid duplicate current records and ambiguous histories with explicit validity rules. Discuss privacy, retention and controlled correction without implying that keeping all historical values is always permissible.

Editorial boundary: Slowly Changing Dimensions owns warehouse-specific historical dimension methods; Database Transactions owns transaction isolation; Data Governance retains retention obligations. Temporal truth and recorded history must not be conflated.

Required instructional treatment: Model a scholarship eligibility decision entered on September 12 but effective September 1; show queries answering “what was valid on September 5?” and “what had we recorded by September 5?”

Group checkpoint: Explain how this group contributes to the overall model and name at least one decision that belongs to a neighboring specialist article. Record a counterexample that would invalidate an apparently correct model, then revise the proposed design or its stated assumptions.

Analytical Models and Historical Reporting

These three entries concern information prepared for analytical questions rather than simply reflecting the operational transaction structure. They begin by declaring the grain of a measured event, then choosing dimensional arrangements, and finally controlling the historical interpretation of changing attributes. The critical failure mode is a plausible-looking total that changes because a join duplicates facts, a measure has the wrong grain, or a dimension was retroactively overwritten.

1. Dimensional Data Modeling

Subject mandate: Fact grain, measures, dimensions, conformed meaning and reporting correctness. Begin with the business process and define the precise grain of a fact table before selecting measures. Contrast transaction, periodic snapshot and accumulating snapshot fact patterns, and establish when a measure is additive, semi-additive or nonadditive across dimensions. Explain surrogate dimensional keys, conformed dimensions, role-playing dimensions, degenerate dimensions, bridge patterns and the risk of fanout when joining incompatible grains. Demonstrate how a change to fact grain can produce apparently plausible but incorrect totals. Compare dimensional designs with operational normalized schemas and show why a report-friendly model can intentionally repeat attributes while still requiring validated meaning, lineage and refresh procedures. Include late-arriving facts and dimensions as essential implementation cases.

Editorial boundary: Data Warehousing owns complete warehouse systems; Star and Snowflake Schemas owns physical pattern comparisons; Slowly Changing Dimensions owns detailed history management. This article owns the conceptual logic of analytic facts and dimensions.

Required instructional treatment: Design an enrollment-count reporting model at the grain of one student registration per course offering; test whether headcount, credits and tuition measures can be summed across term and department.

2. Star and Snowflake Schemas

Subject mandate: Analytical table structures, denormalized dimensions, joins and maintenance trade-offs. Compare a star schema’s central fact with largely denormalized dimensions against a snowflake schema that normalizes selected dimension hierarchies. Explain how join paths, attribute duplication, hierarchies, storage, update complexity and engine-specific optimization shape decisions. Distinguish schema names from guaranteed performance: storage format, distribution, indexing, caching and query optimizer behavior all matter. Address multiple fact tables, shared conformed dimensions, snowflaked product hierarchies and bridge relationships that defeat naive one-to-many assumptions. Provide examples of aggregation errors caused by many-to-many joins and inconsistent dimensional history. Describe how to decide which structure is understandable to business users and testable for data correctness, rather than treating one pattern as universally superior.

Editorial boundary: Dimensional Data Modeling owns choosing fact grain and measures; Physical Data Modeling owns engine-specific physical tuning. This topic compares structural families and their semantic and maintenance consequences.

Required instructional treatment: Render equivalent enrollment reporting schemas as a star and a selectively snowflaked design; compare query joins, department changes, maintenance steps and a total-enrollment test.

3. Slowly Changing Dimensions

Subject mandate: Warehouse attribute history, Type 1/2/3 patterns, effective intervals and restatement. Explain the motivation for maintaining historical dimension attributes: reports must distinguish current values from values valid when events occurred. Compare Type 0, Type 1, Type 2 and Type 3 approaches and note that additional hybrids exist; emphasize that these are modeling patterns rather than a universal implementation standard. For Type 2, cover surrogate keys, natural business keys, effective intervals, current flags, uniqueness, late-arriving corrections and fact linkage to the correct historical row. Discuss retroactive restatements, overlapping rows, missing early dimension values and how policy determines whether historical reports should change. Walk through a department rename versus a student academic-major change, showing why different attributes may require different history treatment.

Editorial boundary: Temporal Data Modeling owns generic valid/recorded-time theory; Dimensional Data Modeling owns broader fact and dimension design. This child focuses on analytic dimension change management and reconciliation.

Required instructional treatment: Build a three-version department dimension with Type 2 effective dates; join enrollments before and after a reorganization and explain when a Type 1 correction would rewrite historical aggregates.

Nonrelational Models, Semantics, and Governance

These four entries extend modeling beyond operational relational rows. Document aggregates and graph relationships embody different access and consistency assumptions; dictionaries and semantic models establish shared meaning; governance determines how those contracts change over time. Nonrelational does not mean without a schema, and a well-documented schema does not automatically imply correct or current data. Each child must make its assumptions explicit.

1. Document Data Modeling

Subject mandate: Nested records, embedded objects, references, schema validation and evolution. Show how to model document aggregates around common retrieval and update boundaries rather than reproducing relational tables as JSON. Explain embedding versus referencing, array cardinality, document identifiers, schema variation, update atomicity, and the cost of duplicated fields. Compare a student record containing current addresses and program memberships with referenced course offerings or enrollments. Discuss the dangers of unbounded arrays, cross-document consistency, index behavior, storage limits and concurrent updates, recognizing that capabilities differ by database product. Include versioned document schemas, migration of old documents, compatibility in readers and the need for representative workload testing. Explain where a relational or graph model may be a better fit.

Editorial boundary: Data Storage Methods and Databases own platform-level operation; the Data Modeling unit owns the structural contract and domain choices. Do not assert identical transaction behavior or schema capabilities for all document databases.

Required instructional treatment: Design two document alternatives for a student’s multiple enrollments and frequent course updates; compare read shapes, update fanout, constraints and migration impact.

2. Graph Data Modeling

Subject mandate: Node/edge identity, relationship properties, traversal semantics and graph constraints. Introduce property graphs and the basic modeling roles of nodes, edges, labels, types and properties. Explain why the same domain may call for graph representation when multi-hop relationship traversal is central, but caution that relational or document systems can also express relationships. Model direction, multiplicity, edge identity, time-varying relationships and required property constraints. Compare an instructor–student mentoring network with a course-enrollment network, noting which queries require path discovery versus ordinary keyed joins. Discuss high-degree nodes, invalid relationships, cycles, traversal depth, duplicates and the impact of changing schema assumptions. Distinguish graph data-modeling choices from graph algorithms and graph-database query languages, both of which have neighboring dedicated treatments.

Editorial boundary: Computational Foundations/Graph Theory owns mathematical graph properties and algorithms; Data Systems databases own execution engines. This child owns graph-shaped business data semantics and integrity.

Required instructional treatment: Model a research collaboration network with people, projects and institutions; write three relationship questions and demonstrate why node identity and edge time validity affect the answers.

3. Data Dictionaries and Semantic Models

Subject mandate: Shared business terms, field definitions, metadata meaning and interpretation contracts. Explain why a schema that validates structurally can still be semantically ambiguous. Develop field definitions, allowed values, business ownership, calculation rules, identifiers, null meanings, canonical measures and controlled vocabularies. Relate data dictionaries and business glossaries to logical schemas, application APIs and analytical semantic layers, avoiding an assumption that these are identical artifacts. Introduce ontology concepts only to the degree useful for expressing relationships among terms. Examine synonyms, conflicting source definitions, multilingual labels, lineage references and stewardship review. Show how a common word such as “active student” may encode different eligibility criteria in admissions, finance and academic reporting; require formula, effective-date and exclusion documentation before labeling metrics as harmonized.

Editorial boundary: Data Catalogs and Metadata Management owns enterprise discovery, lineage infrastructure and catalog operations; this child owns interpretation specifications attached to the data model. Treat metadata publication as evidence of definition, not automatic evidence of truth.

Required instructional treatment: Draft a data dictionary for enrollment_status, active_student, academic_term and credits_attempted with owner, type, allowed values, null semantics, valid dates and calculation rules.

4. Data Model Governance and Schema Evolution

Subject mandate: Model ownership, review controls, change compatibility, release gates and lifecycle decisions. Describe governance of a data model as a reviewable practice for changing meaning and structure across systems over time. Establish owner, steward and implementer roles; version-control artifacts; require change proposals specifying old/new meanings, affected producers and consumers, data migration and rollback. Classify additive, breaking and semantic changes: adding an optional field is not automatically harmless if a consumer assumes absence has a different meaning. Compare backward, forward and full compatibility concepts with concrete caveats for encoding formats and reader behavior. Address phased deployment, backfills, dual reads, data contracts, deprecations, approval evidence, privacy impact and retention obligations. Define acceptance criteria covering sample data, constraints, reporting reconciliation, performance and lineage, including a process for resolving undocumented legacy assumptions.

Editorial boundary: Enterprise Data Governance owns organization-wide policy; Data Engineering owns pipeline deployment; Data Catalogs owns metadata platforms. This child owns model-level stewardship and compatibility through the change lifecycle.

Required instructional treatment: Prepare a change record replacing one course_code with a new institutional identifier; include impact analysis, database constraints, dependent reports, backfill rules, compatibility window, owner approval and rollback test.

Data Modeling Methods and Design Comparison

The methods in this unit are complementary but not interchangeable. A conceptual model may identify the right entities but leave key selection unresolved. A logically sound relational schema may still perform poorly under a workload, and a fast analytical star schema may return incorrect totals if its grain or historical meaning is misunderstood. The table treats each method as an answer to a particular design question, with its main evaluation evidence and characteristic failure mode.

Critical distinctions: a schema is not the whole domain model; a primary key is not necessarily a stable real-world identity; normalization is not the same as minimizing all repetition in analytical systems; valid time is not necessarily the time a row was recorded; document and graph are modeling patterns with concrete platform-dependent guarantees, not categories exempt from constraints.

When alternatives are being compared, require a shared scenario with representative records and operations. State what counts as a correct answer before measuring latency or storage cost. A structure that silently changes the result of the query fails the modeling objective regardless of performance.

Illustrative Data Modeling Scenarios

These constructed cases are intentionally cross-cutting. Each exposes a failure that a model can create while all underlying storage services remain available. They should be expanded by the specialist authors into reproducible examples with small tables, diagrams, keys or queries, and an explicit statement of what “correct” means.

Example 1 — From stakeholder language to stable business entities

A university registrar says “students take courses,” while the scheduling office manages specific course offerings and sections. If enrollment is linked directly to a course catalog entry, the model cannot say which semester, instructor or section was involved. The conceptual model should distinguish student, course, offering and enrollment; the logical model then assigns identities and participation rules. A candidate constraint might require that one student has at most one active enrollment in a particular offering, while permitting enrollment in different offerings of the same course. A worked diagram should demonstrate a student retaking a course in another term without overwriting the first attempt. See Conceptual Data Modeling, Logical Data Modeling and Entity–Relationship Diagrams.

Example 2 — A normalization problem hidden by plausible rows

Suppose a registration table stores student_id, offering_id, course_title and instructor_office on every enrollment row. At first it may seem convenient to generate reports from one table. But if the instructor moves offices, dozens of records can disagree; if no student enrolls, the course title may have no suitable row. Model the actual determinants and separate enrollment facts from course and instructor descriptions as appropriate to the business rules. Test a course-title change and the deletion of a student’s last enrollment before accepting the decomposition. The result requires more than invoking “third normal form” as a slogan; show the surviving functional dependencies and their enforcement. See Functional Dependencies and Database Normalization.

Example 3 — Incorrect duplicate enrollment despite apparently unique IDs

A system uses a newly generated surrogate key for each enrollment row and therefore accepts two rows for the same student and course offering. Every surrogate ID is distinct, but the business rule requiring one active enrollment may still be violated. Decide whether the invariant applies to all records or only active records, specify the natural candidate key and status-related nuances, and choose a database-supported enforcement mechanism. Consider what happens when an enrollment is canceled, reinstated or transferred to another section. Then test a missing parent offering, a duplicate logical enrollment and a deletion attempt. This separates record identity from real domain uniqueness and demonstrates why primary-key generation alone cannot establish integrity. See Keys and Referential Integrity and Logical Data Modeling.

Example 4 — Historical truth versus what the system previously knew

A scholarship office decides on September 12 that a student became eligible on September 1. At the end of September 5 the system had not recorded the decision, but the corrected business truth now says eligibility began earlier. A valid-time model can answer “who was eligible September 5?” under the current corrected understanding; a transaction-time history can answer “what did our system record by September 5?” Those are different and both may matter for an audit. Model effective starts and ends, correction provenance, and the handling of overlapping records. Do not equate a database snapshot with an independently governed bitemporal history. See Temporal Data Modeling and Data Model Governance and Schema Evolution.

Example 5 — A report doubles its enrollment count

An analytical team defines one fact row per student-course enrollment but joins it to a table with two department affiliations for the same student. The resulting join duplicates fact rows and doubles some totals, even though the SQL runs successfully and source counts look plausible. Start by declaring the fact grain and the dimensional relationship cardinalities. Test a report for a student with multiple departments and show which join or bridge rules are required. Then assess which measures remain additive by term, campus and department. A star schema does not protect against this error without explicit modeling and reconciliation. See Dimensional Data Modeling and Star and Snowflake Schemas.

Example 6 — A department is renamed after historical events

Suppose the School of Applied Sciences becomes the School of Computing on October 1. Should a report of last spring’s tuition and enrollment use the old school name or the new one? There is no universally correct choice without a reporting policy. A Type 1 dimension update can restate the historical label, while a Type 2 approach can preserve the department description at the time of each event. Design surrogate keys, effective intervals and rules for late-arriving records, and identify how historical fact rows attach to the correct version. Compare the results of “as originally classified” and “as currently organized” reporting. See Slowly Changing Dimensions, Dimensional Data Modeling and Temporal Data Modeling.

Example 7 — Embedded documents or referenced relationships?

A student portal displays a learner’s contact information and current course enrollments. Embedding each full course offering inside the student document can simplify one read but create many duplicated copies of course schedules, capacities and instructor changes. Referencing the offering avoids some repetition but changes the number of reads and update consistency assumptions. Evaluate read frequency, bounded versus potentially unbounded arrays, identity, multi-document updates and how schema versions will coexist during deployment. Explicitly state which database capabilities the proposal assumes rather than treating all document stores as identical. See Document Data Modeling and Physical Data Modeling.

Example 8 — Modeling research collaborations as a graph

A research office wants to discover possible collaborators who worked together indirectly through shared projects. A property-graph model can represent researchers, projects, institutes and dated participation links, but careless node identity may merge people with similar names. A multi-hop query also needs maximum depth, direction, edge types and treatment of duplicated paths. Define whether two researchers sharing a project constitutes a collaboration, whether archived affiliations count, and how revocations affect the result. Compare the graph representation against a relational join solution on the same small dataset; choose based on task requirements rather than novelty. See Graph Data Modeling and Data Dictionaries and Semantic Models.

Example 9 — A schema change that preserves bytes but changes meaning

The admissions application changes enrollment_status to include “deferred,” while one report assumes every status other than “active” means “withdrawn.” Adding a new allowed value may be technically backward-compatible for some storage readers but semantically breaking for that report. Produce a data dictionary revision, owner approvals, reader impact inventory, a safe migration window and a reconciliation test. Run both the old and new report logic over rows with the new value to exhibit the failure. Maintain a rollback plan that addresses both structure and meaning: rolling back code alone may not restore the old classification of written data. See Data Dictionaries and Semantic Models and Data Model Governance and Schema Evolution.

Scenario-review discipline: record the source requirement, at least one representative and one adversarial record, the intended invariant, the chosen modeling construct, the testable consequence and the owner who can approve the interpretation. Where answers change with time, identify whether the question asks about the historical world, a past record state or a current restatement of past events. These examples are hypothetical and do not represent actual MGU student records or institutional systems.

Model acceptance sequence: (1) confirm business-language definitions; (2) test identity, cardinality and null semantics; (3) demonstrate integrity under create, update and delete operations; (4) validate historically correct answers and reporting grain; (5) choose implementation structures; (6) measure representative workloads; and (7) review compatibility, governance and recovery of the model’s meaning during change. Failure in an earlier stage should not be obscured by strong later-stage benchmark results.

Operational caution: a database implementation, diagramming notation or automated modeling tool may support only a subset of proposed constraints. State whether each invariant is enforced by a database constraint, a transaction protocol, an application service, an external reconciliation job or an institutional process. A review is incomplete if it merely assumes the protection exists.

Suggested Study Pathways

The pathways below sequence the source-prescribed specialist articles by use case rather than changing their production order. A reader may enter through relational design, reporting, temporal data or nonrelational data, but every path should return to identity, semantic correctness and independently testable constraints. Active future links identify planned content and are not a statement of live availability.

Foundations for new readers: begin with the Data Modeling parent overview, then read Conceptual Data Modeling, Logical Data Modeling, Entity–Relationship Diagrams and Physical Data Modeling. Build the vocabulary of domain meaning, structure, diagrams and implementation. Use one enrollment model for all four exercises, recording which decisions are introduced or revised at each level.

Relational schema and integrity engineering: begin with the Data Modeling parent overview, then read Keys and Referential Integrity, Functional Dependencies, Database Normalization and Logical Data Modeling. Focus on candidates for identity, true domain dependencies, lossless decomposition and invalid-state tests before interpreting physical performance.

Temporal correctness and institutional records: begin with the Data Modeling parent overview, then read Temporal Data Modeling, Slowly Changing Dimensions and Data Model Governance and Schema Evolution. Work from valid versus recorded time to historical analytical representations and the approval trail for correcting old facts. Compare both current and as-of questions.

Business intelligence and analytics: begin with the Data Modeling parent overview, then read Dimensional Data Modeling, Star and Snowflake Schemas, Slowly Changing Dimensions and Data Dictionaries and Semantic Models. Define fact grain and measure meaning before building the reporting join path. Verify that the same total is obtained under each supported report slice.

Database platform and performance designers: begin with the Data Modeling parent overview, then read Logical Data Modeling, Physical Data Modeling, Database Normalization and Document Data Modeling. Evaluate alternatives with matched workloads and representative data sizes. Record which correctness requirements are carried from the logical model to the deployed platform.

Nonrelational and relationship-centric systems: begin with the Data Modeling parent overview, then read Document Data Modeling, Graph Data Modeling, Keys and Referential Integrity and Data Dictionaries and Semantic Models. Compare aggregate boundaries and graph paths, with emphasis on identity, duplicated values and schema assumptions. Cross-reference generic data systems only where they supply needed execution details.

Data stewards and governance leads: begin with the Data Modeling parent overview, then read Data Dictionaries and Semantic Models, Conceptual Data Modeling, Data Model Governance and Schema Evolution and Temporal Data Modeling. Document authoritative terms, acceptable changes, historical accountability and who resolves conflicting interpretations across organizational units.

End-to-end capstone project: begin with the Data Modeling parent overview, then read Conceptual Data Modeling, Entity–Relationship Diagrams, Database Normalization, Dimensional Data Modeling and Data Model Governance and Schema Evolution. Deliver an auditable domain model, normalized operational schema, warehouse fact grain, change log and evidence that queries remain correct after a difficult schema revision.

Capstone deliverable: prepare a domain model for a fictional university enrollment service. Include a stakeholder-approved term list, entity–relationship diagram, logical relations and constraints, physical schema proposal, one historically corrected student status, a fact table at explicit grain, a student document example, a graph relationship question and a change-management plan. Evaluate the design using both correct and intentionally invalid records. The result should reveal which assumptions are model-specific and which constraints are actually enforced by the proposed technology.
