Definition, Scope, and Place in the Encyclopedia

A hex dump is a human-readable rendering of a byte sequence, usually arranged as rows that pair a starting offset with hexadecimal byte values and, optionally, a character-oriented side view. Binary inspection uses such representations, together with explicit file-format rules and reproducible tools, to investigate a byte stream without assuming that its filename, extension, or displayed text accurately describes its contents. Forensic reading adds evidence discipline: the analyst distinguishes observations from interpretations, records the transformations used to obtain a view, preserves the original when required, and describes the limits of any conclusion. These techniques support software debugging, protocol analysis, file-format reconstruction, data recovery, security incident response, and digital evidence examination.

The fundamental principle is that a hex viewer displays bytes, not their definitive meaning. The row 53 65 6E 73 6F 72 00 FF could be a mixture of ASCII-compatible characters and control or non-ASCII bytes; it could also be an arbitrary binary payload. A plausible text fragment is a clue, not proof of the record's purpose. The same byte sequence can be interpreted as signed and unsigned integers, a floating-point bit pattern, characters under a specified encoding, or part of a compressed structure. Those interpretations become justifiable only when the relevant format, offset, width, byte order, and context are established.

This Level 5 article belongs under Binary Data and its specialist unit directory. Its prerequisites include Binary, Hexadecimal, and Octal Notation, Endianness and Byte Order, and Binary File Headers and Magic Numbers. It complements Binary Payloads in Media and Executable Formats by concentrating on the inspection workflow, not on rewriting the individual media or executable specifications.

Learning Objectives and Interpretive Discipline

A reader completing this article should be able to read a conventional hex dump without confusing hexadecimal addresses with byte values; calculate precise file offsets and half-open ranges; detect the limits of ASCII side columns; compare signatures with formal format requirements; interpret a declared length using the correct endianness; and conduct a bounded inspection without executing suspicious content. A more advanced reader should be able to reconstruct the header of an illustrative binary record, verify a length and checksum, distinguish damaged data from merely unfamiliar encoding, and prepare a defensible inspection note identifying which findings are verified and which remain hypotheses.

The article asks five recurring questions: What exact bytes were observed? At which coordinate? Under what representation or transformation? According to which specification? With what evidentiary limit? Good analysis answers all five. “At offset 0x18 the four bytes are …” is an observation. “The value is a CRC-32 of the payload” is an interpretation requiring a format rule and computation. “The file has not been maliciously altered” is a much stronger claim that the first two facts do not establish.

Byte observation 0x00–0x03 contain 42 58 4C 47 Reproducible byte read “This is definitely a valid file”
Structural interpretation Those bytes are the declared four-byte magic Known or proposed format contract “Magic means trustworthy”
Arithmetic finding Field at 0x08 encodes length eight in big-endian Width and byte-order declaration “All later fields are valid”
Integrity finding Computed payload CRC matches stored CRC Exact algorithm and coverage range “Authenticity is proven”

Hexadecimal Representation and the Byte Model

A byte normally comprises eight bits and is printed with two hexadecimal digits: 00 for zero, 0F for fifteen, 10 for sixteen, and FF for 255 when treated as unsigned. Every hexadecimal digit represents four bits, called a nibble. A space-separated run such as 41 00 FE 10 represents four bytes, not one number. If a tool prints 4100FE10 as a continuous character string, the analyst must learn whether the output is a sequence of bytes, a big-endian integer, or merely text that happens to look like hexadecimal. Hex is a display convention and does not itself impose a storage byte order.

In a typical dump, each row begins with the address of its first byte, and sixteen byte values follow. Rows often include a character sidebar formed by mapping printable ASCII bytes to characters and substituting a dot for nonprintable values. Other tools group bytes into columns of eight, display a midpoint separator, or show an address relative to a region rather than the entire file. An analyst should record the viewer's conventions before importing offsets into a report.

Hex byte Binary value Unsigned decimal ASCII-oriented display Interpretation warning
00 00000000 0 . May be terminator, padding, or payload
09 00001001 9 . Tab in ASCII; sidebar may not expose it
20 00100000 32 space A blank may be visually indistinguishable from padding
30 00110000 48 0 Digit character, not numeric value zero
41 01000001 65 A Could also be an arbitrary field byte
7E 01111110 126 ~ Last printable US-ASCII graphic character

The Anatomy of a Hex Dump Row

A conventional line may be rendered as 00000010 53 65 6E 73 6F 72 00 FF 9A 11 02 33 45 4E 44 21 |Sensor.....3END!|. The eight digits on the left denote a row offset, not eight data bytes. The hexadecimal values in the middle are sixteen observed bytes, and the right column is a lossy character-oriented annotation. The 9A, 11, and 22 values are no more inherently suspicious than the printable END! suffix: their meaning is determined by the format and their positions.

The example line is illustrative, not the actual contents of the later checksum-based worked record. That distinction matters: never treat an invented display snippet as a captured sample. Real tools may omit the sidebar, pad the final row, insert asterisks for repeated rows, suppress offsets, or number addresses from an arbitrary base. A line in xxd-style output and a line in a debugging program's memory view can look similar even when one measures positions in a file and the other measures virtual addresses in a process.

A row address is the starting address of a group. The target byte offset is the row address plus the byte's zero-based position in that row. Thus, in a sixteen-byte display, byte number three in the row beginning at 0x20 is at 0x22 if “byte number three” counts from one, but 0x23 if it denotes a zero-based index of three. Avoid this language ambiguity by stating row offset + column index, where both numbers use a specified base.

Offsets, Ranges, and Coordinate Systems

A file offset is usually zero-based: the first stored byte begins at 0x00, the seventeenth at 0x10, and the final byte of a 32-byte record at 0x1F. Every investigation should make clear whether an offset is expressed in decimal (16), hexadecimal (0x10), or another base. When only digits appear, the notation is ambiguous unless a header or report convention resolves it. A decimal value of 100 corresponds to hexadecimal 0x64; silently converting one into the other misplaces the observation.

For region arithmetic, use half-open ranges [start, end), which include the starting position and exclude the ending position. The length is end − start; the region [0x10, 0x18) contains exactly eight bytes at addresses 0x10 through 0x17. An inclusive prose expression 0x10–0x17 describes the same eight bytes but requires adding one when calculating its size. Half-open notation is especially useful when checking fields, framing payloads, verifying hashes, or aligning slices from programming languages.

Coordinate Meaning Typical example Pitfall
File offset Position from start of a file 0x3C in a PE file Confused with an in-memory address
Relative offset Position from enclosing record or chunk +0x08 from record start Wrong origin applied
Physical disk offset Location on a storage device or image Sector plus byte displacement Not necessarily contiguous file content
Virtual address Location in a process address space 0x00007… Not directly a file offset
RVA Relative virtual address in an executable image PE data-directory entry Requires section mapping for file read
Logical block address Numbered storage block LBA 1000 Requires block size and mapping
Packet offset Byte within a captured frame or payload TCP payload byte 0x20 Protocol headers may shift the base

For example, to turn sector address LBA 7 into a byte offset in a simple 512-byte-per-sector linear image, multiply 7 × 512 = 3584 (0xE00). This calculation assumes that the image begins at logical block zero, that the block size is 512, and that the intended data has not been transformed by a sparse container, volume abstraction, or logical-file extraction. A forensic image can require additional mapping metadata; multiplying by 512 is not a universal rule.

Choosing an Observation Surface

The same underlying bytes can be presented by a hex editor, a command-line dump, a debugger, a packet analyzer, or a structured parser, but each tool has a different acquisition boundary. A hex editor typically displays an entire on-disk file or region. A debugger might show memory after a loader has mapped an executable, performed relocations, and zero-initialized certain sections. A packet analyzer may show decoded header fields together with raw bytes from a captured frame, often after link-layer truncation or reassembly policies. A structured viewer can normalize a container and hide bytes that it does not regard as significant.

A safe workflow starts with the least transformative representation available for the question. If the question concerns the first four bytes of a file, read those four bytes without invoking a media player or executable loader. If the question concerns a PNG chunk, check the raw boundaries first, then apply a PNG-aware parser in a controlled environment. If the question concerns a deleted file in a disk image, use a read-only or write-blocked acquisition and tools appropriate to the image rather than editing the original medium. Tool output is evidence of what that tool reported; the tool's configuration and parsing choices are part of the observation.

Tool class Strength Limitation Preferred question
Raw hex viewer Shows byte values and offsets Does not assign definitive semantics What bytes are present?
File identification utility Quickly finds likely signatures Heuristic or limited validation Which format should be investigated?
Structured format parser Applies a grammar or standard Bugs or unsupported variants can mislead Do the fields satisfy a declared format?
String extractor Finds character-like runs False positives and encoding blindness Where might readable text appear?
Hashing tool Checks byte equality or changes Cannot establish source authority alone Did these exact byte streams match?
Disk image tool Preserves block-level context Requires geometry and acquisition care Where were bytes on storage?
Debugger/memory viewer Studies loaded program state Memory differs from stored file What bytes exist at this address now?

Safe Scope, Authorization, and Read-Only Practice

Inspect only data and devices that the investigator is authorized to access. This applies equally to enterprise incident response, personal-file repair, educational exercises, and legal evidence. Do not upload confidential artifacts to public converters merely to identify a signature, and do not execute a suspicious binary merely because its header looks familiar. For untrusted material, maintain a non-executing workflow and consider an isolated analysis environment with restricted access and appropriate handling procedures.

Where evidentiary preservation matters, document the source's condition and acquisition method; prefer a verified working copy; use a hardware or software read-only mechanism when appropriate; and note any necessary exceptions. Opening a file in a nominally read-only editor does not guarantee that operating-system access times, preview caches, antivirus records, or sidecar metadata remain unchanged. The goal is to understand and control changes, not to imply that every normal read of a consumer file requires a full forensic laboratory.

NIST Special Publication 800-86 describes integrating forensic techniques into incident response and emphasizes systematic acquisition, integrity verification, and documentation. Its operational emphasis is useful for this article, but an organization's legal requirements, privacy obligations, evidence rules, and incident-response playbooks may impose additional steps. This article does not certify any procedure as universally admissible in court.

A Compact Evidence and Observation Log

A defensible inspection record should say what was examined, how it was acquired, and how the reader reached each result. For routine engineering debugging, a concise log may suffice; for an evidentiary investigation, chain-of-custody requirements can be substantially stricter. Record timestamps with time zones, the relevant byte coordinate system, tool identity/version when consequential, scope of acquisition, hashes when appropriate, and whether subsequent transformations occurred.

Record field Illustrative entry Why it matters
Evidence or sample ID TRAINING-BXLG-001 Distinguishes artifacts without sensitive file names
Source description Instructor-generated 32-byte record Establishes provenance as claimed, not independently proven
Acquisition or generation Created in local Python demonstration Identifies origin of the copy

The investigator must distinguish a cryptographic digest recorded at acquisition from a trusted attestation of a source's origin. Matching SHA-256 digests provide strong evidence of byte equality under ordinary collision assumptions; they do not themselves establish who created the file, when it was created, or whether it was already compromised before hashing. A checksum such as CRC-32 has still narrower guarantees: it is mainly a noncryptographic accidental-error detector defined by a format's contract.

The Worked BXLG Training Record

Consider a deliberately fictional binary record named BXLG, built solely to teach offsets, fields, and observations. It is not a published file format and its four-byte signature is not assigned by any registry. The record has a sixteen-byte header, an eight-byte payload, a four-byte CRC-32 stored in big-endian byte order, and a four-byte trailer. The payload is 53 65 6E 73 6F 72 00 FF: six ASCII letters spelling Sensor, followed by NUL (00) and FF. The final two bytes deliberately frustrate naive “the payload is a text string” claims.

File range Bytes/interpretation Width Declared rule
[0x00,0x04) 42 58 4C 47 (BXLG) 4 Exact fictional signature
[0x04,0x05) 01 1 Version number 1
[0x05,0x06) 03 1 Two illustrative flag bits set
[0x06,0x08) 00 10 2 Header length 16, big-endian
[0x08,0x0C) 00 00 00 08 4 Payload length 8, big-endian
[0x0C,0x10) 00 00 00 00 4 Reserved field required to be zero
[0x10,0x18) 53 65 6E 73 6F 72 00 FF 8 Opaque payload bytes
[0x18,0x1C) Calculated CRC-32, big-endian 4 CRC covers payload only
[0x1C,0x20) 45 4E 44 21 (END!) 4 Exact fictional trailer

Total record length is 16 + 8 + 4 + 4 = 32 bytes. In the half-open convention the last position is 0x20, even though the last included byte is at 0x1F. The CRC bytes in the output below are computed in the accompanying executable example rather than guessed; this prevents a mismatch between the prose and the code. There is no schema rule here claiming that flags 03 identify a specific sensor model; those two low bits are simply reserved as examples of a bit field.

Reading the Training Record by Hand

Start at 0x00, where four bytes match the claimed fictional signature. Offset 0x04 contains version 01; offset 0x05 contains flags 03; [0x06,0x08) contains 00 10, so the header declares sixteen bytes under the record's big-endian contract. [0x08,0x0C) contains 00 00 00 08, so the payload is declared to occupy eight bytes. Verify that the header is actually sixteen bytes and that the file is at least 16 + 8 + 4 + 4 bytes long before using any subsequent field.

At [0x10,0x18), the payload's first six bytes form Sensor under ASCII. However, the byte 00 is not an ordinary printable letter, and FF is not valid as a standalone UTF-8 character. A text sidebar may show Sensor.., but that is a lossy display transformation, not an eight-character text field. The four bytes at [0x18,0x1C) must be interpreted as the recorded CRC-32 only because the invented specification assigns them that meaning. The four bytes 45 4E 44 21 at the end are a trailer marker, not proof the preceding content was fully validated.

Do not assume that because the header contains a length, its numerical value can be trusted. A malicious or damaged input could claim 0xFFFFFFFF payload bytes while the actual file holds only thirty-two bytes. A robust reader checks the declared length against the file size using non-overflowing arithmetic and an explicit application limit before allocating memory or reading the payload. This worked record includes a consistency check and a CRC check; neither should be mistaken for a security-authentication mechanism.

A Repeatable Python Hex-Dump and Decoder Example

The following Python 3 program generates the exact 32-byte training record, prints an offset-aware dump, validates its structure, and demonstrates tamper detection. It uses only the standard library and operates entirely on generated bytes; no untrusted input needs to be downloaded or executed. The logic uses half-open slices and checks total length before attempting to interpret the CRC or trailer.

import hashlib
import struct
import zlib

MAGIC = b"BXLG"
PAYLOAD = b"Sensor\x00\xff"
HEADER = MAGIC + bytes((1, 3)) + struct.pack(">HI", 16, len(PAYLOAD)) + bytes(4)
RECORD = HEADER + PAYLOAD + struct.pack(">I", zlib.crc32(PAYLOAD)) + b"END!"


def hex_rows(data, width=16):
    for offset in range(0, len(data), width):
        row = data[offset:offset + width]
        hex_side = " ".join(f"{b:02X}" for b in row)
        ascii_side = "".join(chr(b) if 32 <= b <= 126 else "." for b in row)
        yield f"{offset:08X}  {hex_side:<47}  |{ascii_side}|"


def check_record(data):
    if len(data) < 24 or data[:4] != MAGIC:
        raise ValueError("bad or truncated header")
    version, flags = data[4], data[5]
    header_len, payload_len = struct.unpack(">HI", data[6:12])
    if version != 1 or flags != 3 or header_len != 16 or data[12:16] != bytes(4):
        raise ValueError("unsupported or malformed header")
    if payload_len > 4096:
        raise ValueError("declared payload exceeds training limit")
    expected_total = 16 + payload_len + 4 + 4
    if len(data) != expected_total:
        raise ValueError("record length mismatch")
    payload = data[16:16 + payload_len]
    stored_crc = struct.unpack(">I", data[16 + payload_len:20 + payload_len])[0]
    if data[20 + payload_len:24 + payload_len] != b"END!":
        raise ValueError("bad trailer")
    if zlib.crc32(payload) != stored_crc:
        raise ValueError("payload CRC mismatch")
    return payload


assert len(HEADER) == 16 and len(RECORD) == 32
assert check_record(RECORD) == PAYLOAD
print("TRAINING RECORD")
print("\n".join(hex_rows(RECORD)))
print("SHA-256:", hashlib.sha256(RECORD).hexdigest())

corrupted = bytearray(RECORD)
corrupted[16] ^= 1  # A payload change without recomputing CRC.
try:
    check_record(bytes(corrupted))
except ValueError as err:
    print("Alteration test:", err)
else:
    raise AssertionError("unexpected acceptance")

try:
    check_record(RECORD[:-1])
except ValueError as err:
    print("Truncation test:", err)
else:
    raise AssertionError("unexpected acceptance")

The program prints two dump rows, each with a zero-based row offset. Its checksum is not a secret or authentication tag; any party who can modify the bytes can recompute CRC-32. The SHA-256 digest is generated for reproducibility, not as an assertion of the record's external origin. The example intentionally rejects additional trailing bytes: an extensible real format would instead need an explicit trailing-field or multi-record policy. The 4,096-byte upper bound is a teaching limit, not a globally safe maximum for binary formats.

Interpreting the Python Output and CRC Field

The first row corresponds exactly to the sixteen-byte header. Its ASCII sidebar includes BXLG and dots or spaces for nonprintable or low-value header bytes. The second row begins at 0x10, contains the eight-byte payload, then four CRC bytes and the END! trailer. Those fields may share a display row even though the specification assigns them distinct roles. Never equate a dump-row boundary with a format-record boundary; the former depends on the viewer's configured width, while the latter depends on data structure.

The CRC is zlib.crc32(PAYLOAD) for this fictional contract, stored as four big-endian bytes using struct.pack(">I", value). Here “CRC-32” refers specifically to the standard CRC-32 variant implemented by the Python function, not an unspecified polynomial family. A record can pass a CRC yet still contain nonsensical or malicious application content; moreover, the example's CRC does not cover the magic, version, flags, lengths, reserved field, or trailer. A successful check therefore establishes only that the selected payload bytes match the recorded accidental-error check under the stated algorithm.

The alteration test flips one bit of the first payload byte without changing the recorded CRC. The decoder rejects the modified record. The truncation test removes one trailer byte and causes a length mismatch. These tests demonstrate two distinct failure classes: an integrity mismatch inside an otherwise structurally sized record and an incomplete record whose declared layout cannot fit. A production reader should expose both as clear error categories rather than silently padding an incomplete byte stream or ignoring an unexpected mismatch.

A Safe Python Inspection Tool for Local Files

For an arbitrary local file that you are authorized to inspect, begin with a bounded preview and a streaming cryptographic digest. This program avoids loading the entire file into memory, never executes file contents, and prints only the first 64 bytes by default. It reports observed bytes rather than guessing a file type. Provide a file path on the command line, and do not run it on a sensitive file if its output would be logged or shared inappropriately.

from pathlib import Path
import argparse
import hashlib


def preview(path, preview_limit=64, chunk_size=1024 * 1024):
    size = path.stat().st_size
    h = hashlib.sha256()
    front = b""
    with path.open("rb") as stream:
        while True:
            block = stream.read(chunk_size)
            if not block:
                break
            if len(front) < preview_limit:
                front += block[:preview_limit - len(front)]
            h.update(block)
    return size, h.hexdigest(), front


def dump(data, width=16):
    for off in range(0, len(data), width):
        line = data[off:off + width]
        hex_text = " ".join(f"{n:02X}" for n in line)
        ascii_text = "".join(chr(n) if 32 <= n < 127 else "." for n in line)
        print(f"{off:08X}  {hex_text:<47}  |{ascii_text}|")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    parser = argparse.ArgumentParser(description="Read-only bounded binary preview")
    parser.add_argument("file", type=Path)
    args = parser.parse_args()
    size, digest, first_bytes = preview(args.file)
    print("File size:", size)
    print("SHA-256:", digest)
    dump(first_bytes)
    if size > len(first_bytes):
        print(f"Preview only: {size - len(first_bytes)} bytes not displayed")

Usage is python inspect_bytes.py "C:\\path\\to\\authorized-sample.bin" when the code is saved in inspect_bytes.py. The preview's offsets start at the file beginning; when analyzing an embedded payload at another base, label its coordinate separately. The hash computation reads the entire accessible byte stream, but the preview prints only the initial part. If a file changes while it is being read, a single hash cannot prove that the read represented a coherent snapshot; make a controlled copy or image when that possibility matters.

A Windows PowerShell 5.1 Hex Preview

Windows PowerShell 5.1 offers Format-Hex for displaying bytes and Get-FileHash for computing digests. Format-Hex -Path is convenient for a small training file, but printing an entire multi-gigabyte image can overwhelm the console or logs. The example below opens a local file for reading, prints at most 64 bytes with explicit offsets, closes the stream, and then calculates a SHA-256 digest over the complete file. It does not modify the file or change execution policy.

$Path = 'C:\MGU\training\authorized-sample.bin'  # Replace with your real file.
$MaxPreview = 64
$stream = [System.IO.File]::Open(
    $Path,
    [System.IO.FileMode]::Open,
    [System.IO.FileAccess]::Read,
    [System.IO.FileShare]::Read
)
try {
    $buffer = New-Object byte[] $MaxPreview
    $count = $stream.Read($buffer, 0, $buffer.Length)
    for ($offset = 0; $offset -lt $count; $offset += 16) {
        $stop = [Math]::Min($offset + 16, $count)
        $hexParts = @()
        $asciiParts = @()
        for ($i = $offset; $i -lt $stop; $i++) {
            $b = [int]$buffer[$i]
            $hexParts += ('{0:X2}' -f $b)
            if ($b -ge 32 -and $b -le 126) {
                $asciiParts += [char]$b
            } else {
                $asciiParts += '.'
            }
        }
        '{0:X8}  {1,-47}  |{2}|' -f $offset, ($hexParts -join ' '), ($asciiParts -join '')
    }
} finally {
    $stream.Dispose()
}
Get-FileHash -LiteralPath $Path -Algorithm SHA256

The hard-coded training path is illustrative and must be replaced. If the file is locked, the chosen file-sharing mode can prevent access; do not bypass authorization or acquisition controls to force a read. Windows PowerShell's code-page handling does not alter the file bytes read by FileStream; the sidebar is deliberately limited to printable US-ASCII and does not claim to decode arbitrary UTF-8 or UTF-16. Unlike a whole-file Format-Hex view, this bounded preview reduces accidental disclosure and excessive terminal output, but it still prints actual content from the selected file.

File Signatures: Recognition Is Not Validation

A file signature is a fixed or patterned byte sequence associated with a format. It may appear at offset zero, at a fixed interior offset, or within a more complex structure. Signatures are useful starting points because applications often preserve them even when filenames change or extensions are missing. But file identification tools frequently return candidate formats: a coincidentally matching prefix, a deliberately crafted polyglot, a truncated file, or a header planted by an attacker may all produce misleading matches. Correct identification requires checking the grammar and constraints that follow the signature.

The PNG format begins with eight bytes 89 50 4E 47 0D 0A 1A 0A; the W3C PNG specification describes a chunk sequence beginning with IHDR and ending with IEND, with per-chunk length fields and CRCs. The gzip format begins with 1F 8B and defines a compression method and flags in the following bytes under RFC 1952. ELF object files begin with 7F 45 4C 46, followed by fields that determine word class and endianness. Windows PE executables ordinarily begin with an MZ DOS-compatible header and locate the PE\0\0 signature via a file offset stored at 0x3C. None of these prefixes establishes that the remainder of the file is safe or structurally coherent.

Format family Recognizable bytes Typical position Necessary next check
PNG 89 50 4E 47 0D 0A 1A 0A Offset zero IHDR chunk bounds, legal fields, CRC and chunk sequence
gzip 1F 8B Start of gzip member Compression method, flags, optional fields, data and trailer
ELF 7F 45 4C 46 Offset zero Class, data encoding, header sizes and table bounds
PE 4D 5A (MZ); later 50 45 00 00 First two bytes; signature at referenced offset Bound and validate e_lfanew and COFF/optional headers
SQLite database SQLite format 3 followed by NUL First sixteen bytes Page size, header constraints and database pages
ZIP Often 50 4B 03 04, but not a universal start Common local-file-header signature Central directory, member offsets, bounds and checks

Treat these examples as known format clues, not a comprehensive identification registry. ZIP-derived formats may contain a preamble or other structures, and an embedded ZIP payload need not begin at file offset zero. SQLite's initial string is useful because it fixes a recognizable sixteen-byte prefix, but database correctness still requires structural checks. Different editions and profiles of specifications may alter what is legal after a common signature.

PNG Header Inspection Without Decoding an Image

A truncated PNG fragment may begin:

00000000  89 50 4E 47 0D 0A 1A 0A 00 00 00 0D 49 48 44 52  |.PNG........IHDR|

The first eight bytes match the PNG signature. The next four bytes, 00 00 00 0D, specify a chunk data length of thirteen bytes in big-endian notation, and 49 48 44 52 spells IHDR. This sixteen-byte prefix does not contain the thirteen-byte IHDR data region or its CRC, and so it cannot prove that the file is a valid PNG image. The complete IHDR chunk consists of the four-byte data length, the four-byte type, thirteen data bytes, and a four-byte CRC, for twenty-five bytes after the eight-byte signature. This calculation follows the W3C PNG specification's defined chunk structure.

A byte analyst should annotate positions before decoding: PNG signature [0x00,0x08); first chunk length [0x08,0x0C); chunk type [0x0C,0x10); IHDR data [0x10,0x1D); and CRC [0x1D,0x21). The next chunk, if any, starts at 0x21. These boundaries depend on the stated length of thirteen bytes. A deliberately malformed file might declare an enormous length, end unexpectedly, or contain a bad checksum; such a file must not be treated as valid merely because the first sixteen bytes look convincing.

Integer Endianness and False Length Interpretations

Multibyte fields cannot be interpreted correctly from their hex representation alone. The training record's length bytes 00 00 00 08 mean eight under a four-byte big-endian unsigned rule, but treating the same four bytes as a little-endian unsigned integer yields 0x08000000, or 134,217,728. A parser that substitutes native machine endianness for a specified wire-format order can thus change a harmless eight-byte request into an impossible allocation or out-of-bounds read.

The reverse problem arises with low-order bytes at the front. The two bytes 10 00 equal sixteen under little-endian unsigned interpretation but 4,096 under big-endian. In a hex dump, there is no inherent “correct direction” to read a compound integer; byte order is assigned by the format or protocol. A careful analyst records the exact observed bytes and the explicit interpretation separately, especially when reverse engineering an unknown layout.

Field bytes Width Big-endian value Little-endian value Why analysts care
00 10 2 16 4,096 Header length or field count
10 00 2 4,096 16 Reversed field meaning
00 00 00 08 4 8 134,217,728 Potential allocation failure
01 00 00 00 4 16,777,216 1 Example version or count
FF FF 2 65,535 65,535 Same result under both byte orders

Single-byte fields have no endianness ambiguity, and symmetric repeated-byte patterns such as FF FF are poor tests for determining byte order. When investigating an unknown protocol, seek known values with asymmetric byte patterns, verify them across multiple examples, and corroborate the hypothesis through the record's length and semantic constraints. One plausible interpretation is not enough to declare a binary schema discovered.

Signedness, Floating-Point Bits, and Typed Views

A dump usually prints each byte as an unsigned hexadecimal value. That is a convenient representation, not a declaration that every logical field is unsigned. The byte FF may represent the unsigned integer 255, the signed two's-complement 8-bit integer −1, a mask of eight set bits, or part of a larger field. The four bytes 00 00 80 3F can be the little-endian IEEE 754 binary32 representation of 1.0, but only if the file contract identifies a 32-bit floating-point field at that location. Interpreting unrelated bytes as a float can produce a plausible-looking value by coincidence.

When a record contains a sequence of numeric samples, confirm the signedness, field width, byte order, scale, and units before publishing values. A sensor field interpreted as “24.5” may require multiplying the raw integer by a declared factor or applying a fixed-point convention. Some formats use special sentinel values, reserved bits, or a valid range narrower than the integer type itself. These constraints cannot be recovered from an ASCII sidebar and frequently cannot be inferred from a single capture.

Hex viewers may also show word-oriented modes that rearrange bytes visually. A four-byte word displayed as 0x12345678 might be stored in memory as 78 56 34 12 on a little-endian system. When exporting evidence or comparing screenshots from different tools, ensure that the display has not grouped and byte-swapped values automatically. A raw byte-order display is preferred for precise file-position claims.

Text-Like Sequences, Encoding, and the ASCII Sidebar

Character sidebars are valuable landmarks, but they introduce several traps. Under a simple ASCII display, values 0x20–0x7E render as printable characters while controls and most high bytes appear as dots. That procedure loses distinctions: a NUL, a tab, a newline, and an invalid UTF-8 byte may all become .. A run of 41 42 43 may look like ABC, but it could be a numeric vector or compressed data coincidentally containing those byte values. It is analytically unsafe to claim human-readable text without an encoding rule or strong contextual evidence.

In UTF-8, a non-ASCII character is encoded using multiple bytes. For example, the euro sign € is encoded E2 82 AC; an ASCII-only sidebar normally displays ..., while a UTF-8 decoder correctly renders one character. UTF-16LE commonly inserts zero bytes between ASCII-range characters (41 00 42 00 for AB), although this observation alone does not prove a string uses UTF-16. Byte-order marks and external metadata may help identify a text interpretation, but not every text file has one, and a BOM-like pattern can appear as ordinary binary content.

Do not run a broad “strings” extraction as though it were lossless. A conventional extractor may apply an ASCII assumption, a minimum length, or limited support for multibyte encodings. Its findings are candidates, not an exhaustive inventory of readable content. Record the extraction encoding, threshold, relevant offsets, and whether character decoding replaced errors or skipped bytes. For exact byte evidence, retain the raw hex values alongside any decoded text.

Bounded Pattern Searches and False Positives

A byte-pattern search is useful for finding candidate magic values, field labels, delimiters, or known constants. For example, searching for the four bytes 50 4B 03 04 may locate a ZIP local-file-header signature at an interior offset. A match proves that those bytes occur at that offset, not that a valid ZIP archive begins there. The probability of accidental matches grows with the amount of searched data and is particularly high for short or common patterns. Multiple corroborating fields are needed to infer a format boundary.

Scanning must also be bounded by available data and respectful of confidentiality. A file carving tool may attempt to identify and extract objects from slack space, a corrupted disk image, or an opaque container; a naive scanner may accept a forged length and jump beyond the intended image. Treat each candidate offset as a hypothesis. Check that the claimed structure fits within the enclosing region, has coherent internal fields, and does not overlap another claimed structure in an impossible way under the format's rules.

The Python example below searches generated bytes for exact byte patterns and reports file-relative positions; it does not decode or execute any matched content.

blob = b"AAAA" + bytes.fromhex("504B0304") + b"BBBB" + bytes.fromhex("504B0304")
needle = bytes.fromhex("504B0304")
positions = []
start = 0
while True:
    hit = blob.find(needle, start)
    if hit == -1:
        break
    positions.append(hit)
    start = hit + 1  # Preserve overlapping matches where relevant.
assert positions == [4, 12]
print("Candidate signature offsets:", [f"0x{x:X}" for x in positions])

Even with a four-byte signature that may be distinctive in many datasets, the two matches in this synthetic buffer do not constitute two validated ZIP archives. A genuine archive inference requires checking its metadata and directory structures. If the material is an actual disk image, the analyst must also track the transformation from physical/image offsets to the logical offset within any recovered file.

Structural Delimiters, Framing, and Misleading End Markers

One of the most common decoding errors is mistaking a recognizable terminator for the actual end of a record. A binary format can delimit records with a fixed size, a length prefix, a sentinel sequence, an escape mechanism, a checksum, or a combination. If the payload is permitted to contain the sentinel sequence, the format must specify escaping or other framing rules. An analyst who assumes every occurrence of 00 00 or a visible “END” string closes a record will misidentify boundaries in legitimate binary content.

In the fictional BXLG record, the END! trailer is required after the header-declared payload and CRC. A marker found inside the payload would not terminate the record. Conversely, a trailer found at the expected offset cannot rescue a corrupted length field or validate a checksum that does not match. The most reliable analysis starts from the documented field layout and verifies every derived boundary independently.

For streams, a partial read is not automatically a truncated file: a network or pipe reader may return fewer bytes than requested even when more bytes will arrive. At end of stream, however, a record whose declared payload is incomplete is truncated. A hex dump of one capture fragment therefore cannot always determine whether the underlying application message was complete. Capture boundaries, transport reassembly, and protocol lengths must be reconciled before asserting that a message was malformed.

Identifying Truncation, Trailing Data, and Embedded Content

A truncated record lacks bytes required by its schema. A record with trailing data contains extra bytes beyond the structure the parser expected. These conditions require different handling. Some file formats permit optional trailing structures, padding, concatenated members, appended indexes, or metadata, while others require an exact end-of-file boundary. Rejecting every nonempty trailer is appropriate only when the particular format contract requires it. Conversely, silently ignoring all trailing bytes can hide polyglot behavior, undeclared application data, or parser disagreements.

For the BXLG training record, exactly 32 bytes are required and no trailing bytes are allowed. A 31-byte file is truncated. A 33-byte file is also malformed under this invented contract, even if the extra byte looks like padding. In gzip, by contrast, RFC 1952 permits concatenated gzip members, so “trailing data” after one member may be another legitimate member. Analysts should consult the actual format standard rather than imposing a universal rule based on one introductory example.

A hex dump may show content of another format embedded within a container. A resource section of an executable, an archive member, an image within a document, or a database BLOB can begin with recognizable magic bytes, but those occurrences have parent-relative coordinates and may be compressed or transformed. A report should state both the offset within the outer artifact and the offset within the extracted child when possible; losing that relationship makes later validation difficult.

Binary Differences: Comparing Two Captures

A hex diff compares byte sequences at corresponding positions, typically highlighting changed ranges. It is particularly effective when the structures are fixed-length and aligned: one changed bit of a counter field becomes immediately visible. However, inserting a single byte near the beginning of a variable-length file shifts all subsequent positions, making naive side-by-side comparisons appear to show extensive changes. A useful comparison first establishes whether byte offsets remain aligned, then optionally uses structural parsing or edit-distance methods to account for insertions and deletions.

When the file has checksums, modification of one byte may cause a second change in the checksum field. Compression can amplify changes: a small change in uncompressed data can alter a large fraction of compressed output even if the visible content changes little. Encryption under a secure mode commonly obscures direct field-level relationships. These properties are reasons to combine byte-level and semantic comparisons, not reasons to abandon exact byte evidence.

Observed difference Possible explanation Additional check
One byte differs at a fixed offset Changed flag, measurement, or bit error Determine field meaning and range
Payload and checksum differ Payload edited, recompressed, or corrupted Recalculate declared check
All later offsets shift by one Inserted/deleted byte or framing change Locate first true divergence
Large changes in compressed region Small source change or different compressor settings Decode under the correct standard
Large changes in encrypted region New nonce/key or changed plaintext Follow the authorized cryptographic protocol
Different header timestamps only Repackaging or metadata update Compare decoded content and provenance

Hash Comparison, Integrity, and Their Limits

A cryptographic hash such as SHA-256 turns arbitrary-length bytes into a fixed-size digest under a specified algorithm. It is well suited to checking whether a working copy matches a recorded acquisition or whether two files are byte-identical. Hashing should use the entire intended byte stream and capture the exact identity of the artifact: a decoded media image, compressed file, extracted archive member, and original container are different byte streams and can yield different digests even when their human-visible content appears similar.

A strong digest match is evidence of matching bytes under ordinary cryptographic assumptions; it is not a digital signature, an ownership record, or a chain of custody by itself. If someone supplies both an altered file and its newly computed digest, a later analyst cannot infer that the pair corresponds to the original source. That connection requires independent provenance controls, trusted records, attested signatures, or other appropriate evidence. Preserve the digest algorithm name and its output in a stable case log and do not rely on file names as identity keys.

A noncryptographic checksum serves a different purpose. The PNG chunk CRC and the fictional training record's CRC are intended to detect accidental corruption under a defined rule. They are not intended to resist a malicious actor capable of editing the data and recomputing the checksum. See the planned specialist article Binary Checksums, CRCs, and Integrity Checks for deeper comparison of algorithms, coverage rules, and assurance claims.

Byte Entropy and the Limits of Visual Pattern Recognition

An experienced analyst may notice repeated values, long zero regions, uniform-looking random bytes, or transitions from readable metadata to opaque payload. These observations can help locate boundaries. Repeated 00 bytes may indicate padding, unused space, a sparse region, or meaningful zero-valued samples. A high-entropy region might contain compressed media, encryption output, random test data, or an intentionally obfuscated payload. Entropy alone does not diagnose encryption, and a lack of visible text is not evidence that data is secure or meaningless.

When statistical measures are used, record the sample window and methodology. A byte-frequency estimate computed from a tiny 32-byte header cannot reliably characterize a megabyte-scale compressed region. Sliding-window entropy is a heuristic that may suggest candidate transitions, especially when metadata and payloads differ, but it does not replace grammar-aware parsing. A simple view of 256 possible byte values also ignores the correlations and symbol structures present in real binary encodings.

A hex analyst should prefer conclusions such as “the region exhibits little visible repetition in the inspected 4 KiB sample” over “this section is encrypted.” Stronger claims require identifying the applicable algorithm or demonstrating behavior under a validated parser. In digital forensics, confidence should rise with independent observations, not with the familiarity of a visual pattern.

Fragmented Files, Sparse Regions, and Deleted Data

Logical files need not occupy contiguous physical sectors. A file system may map a logical file into multiple extents, use sparse regions that read as zeros, compress content, deduplicate blocks, or hold content in memory before persistence. A hex dump of a reconstructed logical file can therefore differ from a direct sequence of physical disk sectors even though both views are correct under their separate coordinate systems. If the question concerns deleted or damaged files, precise extent mapping and acquisition context become especially important.

Deleted data recovery has significant limitations. Storage devices may reuse blocks, solid-state drives may perform TRIM and internal garbage collection, and encrypted volumes may expose ciphertext without keys. A carved sequence found in unallocated space may be incomplete, may overlap unrelated files, or may contain stale bytes from different historical versions. A matched header at one physical offset and a matching footer far away do not, by themselves, establish a continuous original file.

For ordinary encyclopedia teaching, these examples illustrate why file offset and physical offset must not be conflated. For an actual forensic case, work from a verified acquisition and appropriate file-system or storage tools; record how physical regions were mapped into the byte sequence under inspection and identify any speculative reconstruction.

Memory Dumps and Process Images

A memory dump is another byte representation, but its addresses and contents can differ materially from on-disk files. A running process may contain decompressed assets, decrypted application data, initialized global variables, relocated code, heap objects, or stale memory fragments. A debugger's displayed address is normally a process virtual address, and translating that position back into a file offset requires loader and mapping knowledge that may not exist for anonymous allocations.

A memory buffer holding a parsed PNG's decoded pixels is not itself a PNG file, and it should not be expected to begin with the PNG signature. Likewise, the same executable file can occupy different virtual addresses under address-space layout randomization. Differences between a disk file and a memory view may result from normal loading, self-modifying or dynamically generated code, legitimate runtime state, or malicious tampering. A hex screenshot alone cannot distinguish these causes.

Memory investigation also raises privacy and secrecy concerns because credentials, personal data, and cryptographic material may be present. Retain only authorized content, restrict reporting to relevant offsets, and consider redaction when publishing demonstrations. The synthetic BXLG record avoids real credentials and user data precisely so that byte dumps can be shared safely in an instructional context.

Network Payloads and Capture Boundaries

Packet capture tools often show several synchronized views: decoded protocol fields, hexadecimal bytes, and a character sidebar. The offsets in the raw pane may begin with an Ethernet frame, an IP datagram, a TCP segment, or an application-layer payload, depending on the capture format and interface selection. Adding an application-record offset directly to a frame offset without accounting for intervening headers can produce a wrong absolute position.

TCP exposes an ordered byte stream to applications rather than preserving arbitrary application message boundaries. One application record can be split across multiple segments, and several records can appear in one segment. Consequently, a single TCP packet's hex dump might show only a prefix of an application header, with the remainder in later traffic. Packet loss, capture truncation, reassembly options, encrypted transport, and offloading can further complicate what the analyzer displays. State whether the examined bytes are raw capture bytes, reassembled stream bytes, decrypted application bytes, or a transformed extraction.

The same care applies to UDP datagrams and other transports: a datagram has its own length and checksum semantics, but application messages may define additional framing. Analysts should not infer a protocol payload's meaning from its transport location alone. A field could be a length prefix, a counter, a serialized integer, an opaque ciphertext fragment, or application-defined metadata under a higher-level contract.

Parsing Untrusted Files Without Executing Them

“Inspection” does not mean “risk-free.” Complex document viewers, thumbnail generators, media decoders, archive extractors, and binary-analysis tools can contain vulnerabilities. Use maintained tools, restricted privileges, and isolation proportionate to the artifact's trust level. For initial triage, prefer small read-only operations and bounded structural checks before invoking complex decoders. Treat archive member names and declared lengths as untrusted, and avoid following unexpected file paths, symlinks, or embedded URLs during analysis.

A defensive parser should verify field availability before interpreting values, check integer ranges without overflow, enforce resource limits before allocating or decompressing, and reject unsupported versions or impossible combinations. If a parser reads an offset and a length from file data, a robust check first validates offset <= file_size, then length <= file_size - offset in an appropriately wide integer type. The same rule must be applied to nested structures relative to their enclosing bounds. This teaching article does not attempt to cover every parser-security failure mode; the next unit article, Binary Input Validation and Safe Parsing, develops those rules more fully.

Reporting Findings With Calibrated Confidence

A strong technical report presents three layers: raw observation, reproducible interpretation, and inference with limitations. A good finding might read: “In the 32-byte instructional record identified as TRAINING-BXLG-001, offsets 0x08 through 0x0B contain 00 00 00 08. Under the documented big-endian unsigned 32-bit length field, the value is eight. The file contains eight payload bytes at [0x10,0x18), and the program's CRC-32 computation matches the four-byte stored field at [0x18,0x1C).” That formulation does not imply authorship, a legal conclusion, or that the file is secure.

Avoid vague phrases such as “the bytes looked encrypted,” “the file was clean,” or “the hash proves it was not tampered with” when the supporting tests are narrower. Where a suspected format cannot be fully verified, use wording such as “consistent with,” “candidate signature,” or “requires further validation.” Keep speculation out of the observation column, report undecodable ranges explicitly, and state whether the file was complete, reconstructed, or viewed through an intermediate extraction process.

Reports should also be readable by a second analyst. Include byte offsets and lengths in a stated numerical base; cite the relevant specification or schema; preserve small, non-sensitive snippets showing why a conclusion was reached; and describe the tools or code used to reproduce computations. Prefer short, labeled dump excerpts over screenshots with unknown row widths. If the sample is sensitive, preserve evidence privately and publish a redacted or synthetic excerpt that cannot be mistaken for original evidence.

A Complete Inspection Workflow

The following sequence is a practical framework, not a universal legal checklist. First, establish purpose, permission, and source scope; identify whether the goal is debugging, file-format education, incident response, or formal digital evidence. Second, preserve or acquire a suitable working copy and record the identity and byte size of the source, with a cryptographic digest where appropriate. Third, establish the coordinate system and take a bounded raw preview, recording offsets and byte values. Fourth, look for candidate signatures and structural fields without inferring validity from signatures alone.

Fifth, consult a format specification or documented schema; decode version, flags, widths, byte order, offsets, lengths, and checksums according to that contract. Sixth, validate nested boundaries and resource limits before accessing or decoding deeper content. Seventh, corroborate interpretations by computing independent values and comparing multiple samples where available. Eighth, document positive findings, mismatches, unexplained bytes, incomplete reads, and tool limitations. Finally, protect the source and logs under the relevant confidentiality and retention policies, and retain a clear link from conclusions to the raw observations that support them.

Inspect Bounded hex excerpt and field table Unexpected offsets or incomplete region
Interpret Documented schema and decoded values No reliable format contract
Validate Bounds, versions, checksums, relationships Malformed or unsupported structure

Common Analytical Errors and Their Corrections

The most frequent errors can be summarized as mistaken coordinate systems and unwarranted certainty. A reader may count offset labels as bytes; confuse a hexadecimal label 10 with decimal ten; reverse a multibyte integer without consulting its specification; assume every printable run is text; or treat a checksum match as authentication. Other mistakes include accepting a declared size without checking available bytes, overlooking a file's expected internal signature, confusing an extracted resource offset with an original image offset, or assuming that a damaged partial file must be malicious.

Practical corrections follow directly. Count bytes explicitly and use half-open ranges; qualify each numeric base; specify field widths and endianness; preserve raw bytes next to decoded text; identify checksum algorithms and covered ranges; and avoid claims about authorship or intent without separate evidence. When unfamiliar bytes occur, do not fill the gap with an invented story. Describe them as unknown, note their position and size, and identify what additional artifact or documentation would be needed to interpret them.

The discipline is valuable beyond formal digital forensics. An API developer debugging a length-prefix error, a database engineer repairing a corrupt record, a security analyst investigating an attachment, and an archivist identifying an old media file all benefit from the same separation: what the bytes are, what a format says they mean, and what the available evidence permits one to conclude.

Worked Comparison: Healthy, Altered, Truncated, and Overlong Records

Return to the fictional 32-byte BXLG record and consider four variants. The healthy record has 16 header bytes, eight payload bytes, four CRC bytes, and four trailer bytes; its encoded size and checksum agree. The altered record has the same length but one changed payload bit and an unchanged CRC, so it fails the payload-integrity check. The truncated record has only 31 bytes and therefore cannot hold the complete trailer, even though its header still declares an eight-byte payload. The overlong record adds a single byte at the end; under the exact-length contract this is invalid, even though all first 32 bytes remain unchanged.

Variant Observed size Structural result Integrity result Correct conclusion
Original 32 Matches invented layout Payload CRC matches Well-formed under teaching contract
One payload bit flipped 32 Fields still fit CRC mismatch Data altered or corrupt; origin unknown
Trailer byte removed 31 Length mismatch Not sufficient to rescue file Incomplete or malformed
Extra trailing byte 33 Violates exact-length contract Payload CRC may still match Unsupported trailing content

A simplistic analyzer that checks only the BXLG signature would accept all four. One that checks only the total length might accept the altered record. One that checks only the payload CRC might overlook the extra trailing byte. The point is not to claim that the teaching contract is secure, but to demonstrate that several independent structural properties must be checked before asserting conformity. This is the logic of good forensic reading: each test supports only the conclusion it actually measures.

Practice Exercises

  1. A hex dump shows sixteen bytes per row. At row 00000020, the byte in zero-based column five is 7F. What is its absolute offset in hexadecimal and decimal?
  2. A file has 48 bytes total, and a payload occupies [0x14,0x2C). How many bytes are in the payload? How many bytes follow it in the file?
  3. At file offset 0x08, the bytes are 00 00 01 00. Calculate the corresponding unsigned 32-bit big-endian and little-endian values.
  4. A sidebar prints ABC.... Name three different original bytes that could be shown as dots under the ASCII-only rendering rule.
  5. A record begins with the standard PNG eight-byte signature and then stops. What does this prove, and what cannot be concluded?
  6. Explain why a PE header's relative virtual address is not automatically the same as a raw file offset.
  7. Two copies have matching SHA-256 hashes computed at different times. What does this strongly support, and what does it not prove about their origin?
  8. A file has a correctly matching CRC-32, but its first four bytes violate the required magic signature. Should a strict parser accept it? Explain.
  9. A two-byte little-endian unsigned field contains 10 00. State its decimal value and explain why 0x1000 would be a wrong interpretation.
  10. A packet capture begins its displayed byte pane at the Ethernet header, but an application specification gives offsets relative to its message body. What extra steps are required before the positions can be compared?
  11. For the training BXLG record, identify the payload's half-open range, its last included file offset, and the starting offset of the trailer.
  12. The training file is extended by one byte but its original CRC stays valid. Explain why a full structure validator should still report a problem.
  13. A byte scanner finds 50 4B 03 04 at file offset 0x100. What can be reported confidently, and what further evidence is needed to identify a valid ZIP structure?
  14. Under what conditions should a raw hex excerpt be withheld or redacted in a published investigation report?
  15. Explain why a high-entropy region may be compressed, encrypted, random, or simply difficult to interpret; propose an appropriate next verification step.

Selected Exercise Answers and Method

(1) Add the zero-based column index 0x05 to 0x20; the result is 0x25, decimal 37. (2) Payload length is 0x2C − 0x14 = 0x18, or 24 bytes; a 48-byte file ends at exclusive offset 0x30, leaving four trailing bytes. (3) 00 00 01 00 is decimal 256 when big-endian, but 0x00010000 or decimal 65,536 when little-endian. (4) NUL 00, tab 09, and high byte FF each appear as a dot in the example ASCII-only side view. (5) The observed prefix matches PNG's signature; it does not validate IHDR, dimensions, chunk structure, checksum, or image decodability.

(7) Equal SHA-256 values strongly support byte equality under normal cryptographic assumptions; a hash alone does not independently verify source identity or provenance. (9) 10 00 equals sixteen under little-endian interpretation; reading it as big-endian yields 4,096. (11) The payload is [0x10,0x18); its last included byte is at 0x17; the trailer begins at 0x1C after the four-byte CRC. (12) The record schema requires exactly 32 bytes, so an extra byte violates the schema even if the payload CRC remains correct. (13) Report the exact candidate signature and location; verify valid ZIP records, directory information, and bounds before claiming a complete archive.
