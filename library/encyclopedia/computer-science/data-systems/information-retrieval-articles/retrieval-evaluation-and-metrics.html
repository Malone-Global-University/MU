Definition and Scope

Retrieval Evaluation and Metrics is a specialist Level 5 subject within the Information Retrieval subarticle directory and extends the parent Information Retrieval overview. Judgments, qrels, precision, recall, MAP, MRR, nDCG, leakage and experimental design. The governing question is: How can we measure whether ranked results serve an actual information need?

This article emphasizes mechanisms, testable examples, operational tradeoffs, and failure conditions. Search systems should be assessed against the information needs and permissions of their actual users, rather than treating an abstract relevance score as an absolute indicator of truth or quality.

Evaluation Begins with Information Needs

Information-retrieval evaluation asks whether a system helps users find information relevant to their tasks. It requires more than measuring server speed or counting matches. A test collection usually consists of source documents, representative topics or queries, and relevance judgments stating which documents or passages satisfy those needs. Judgments may be binary or graded; the evaluation protocol must say what “relevant” means for each task.

TREC and related shared evaluations established important practices of common collections, relevance assessments and repeatable system comparisons. However, no benchmark automatically represents the priorities of every institutional corpus. A scholarly encyclopedia may need tests for exact title navigation, historical-name disambiguation, authoritative source discovery and multilingual access, not just generic web-click behavior.

Evaluation should distinguish candidate retrieval coverage, ranking quality, latency and cost, freshness, privacy and permissions, and answer faithfulness if generation is added. Treating a single number as a universal quality rating obscures these different responsibilities.

Precision, Recall, and Cutoff Measures

Precision is the fraction of retrieved items that are relevant under the evaluation's label policy; recall is the fraction of all judged relevant items that were retrieved. At a cutoff k, precision@k measures the share of the top k that are relevant. Recall@k measures how many of all relevant items appear within that top k. Precision often matters to readers examining the first few hits, while recall matters to investigators who need broad coverage.

If a corpus contains five relevant documents and the system retrieves ten documents, three of which are relevant, precision is 3/10 = 0.30, and recall is 3/5 = 0.60. These denominators must not be confused. The F1 score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall when both are defined, but it does not capture ranking order among the top results.

Measure Main question Important qualification
Precision@k How much of the displayed top k is relevant? Ignores relevant items below cutoff
Recall@k How many relevant items appear by rank k? Requires an estimate of all relevant items
MRR How early is the first relevant result? Does not reward later relevant results
MAP How well are relevant results distributed across ranks? Depends on binary labels and judgment completeness
nDCG@k How useful is graded relevance near the top? Requires a gain and discount convention

Average Precision, MAP, and Reciprocal Rank

Average precision (AP) averages precision at ranks where relevant documents occur, with a denominator convention based on the total number of relevant documents for the query. Suppose a query has exactly two relevant items appearing at ranks 1 and 3, with both retrieved. Precision at rank 1 is 1/1 = 1, and precision at rank 3 is 2/3 ≈ 0.667. Thus AP ≈ (1 + 0.667)/2 ≈ 0.833. Mean Average Precision (MAP) averages AP across the specified test queries.

Reciprocal rank instead considers only the first relevant result: if the first relevant result appears at rank 3, reciprocal rank is 1/3. Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR) averages this quantity over queries. MRR suits known-answer or navigational tasks where one correct result often suffices, but it can conceal the poor coverage of other relevant sources.

Averages require per-query inspection. A system can achieve a high mean while failing entirely on a small but important subset of language or topic categories. Report distributions or representative failure cases and use paired comparisons when possible.

Discounted Cumulative Gain and Graded Judgments

Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (nDCG) supports graded relevance. One common DCG convention assigns each ranked item gain 2^rel−1 and discounts it by log2(rank+1); the total is then divided by the ideal DCG for that query and cutoff. The exponential gain convention emphasizes highly relevant items, while logarithmic discounting favors earlier ranks. Other valid conventions exist and must be stated.

For example, graded labels 3, 2 and 0 give gains 7, 3 and 0 before discounting. A ranking putting grade 3 first generally earns more DCG than one placing grade 2 first, though exact differences depend on all ranks and the cutoff. nDCG is often useful when documents can be partially useful rather than simply relevant or irrelevant. It is not a substitute for defining what the grades mean.

Unjudged documents complicate calculations. A system that retrieves newly discovered relevant material may be penalized if the evaluation implicitly treats every unjudged item as irrelevant. Pooled judging and careful incomplete-judgment analysis are therefore central in large collections.

Worked Evaluation Scenario

Take five ranked results for a question: A (relevant), B (not relevant), C (relevant), D (not relevant), E (relevant). If the total judged relevant set contains four items, then precision@5 is 3/5 = 0.60 and recall@5 is 3/4 = 0.75. Precision@1 is 1.0; reciprocal rank is 1 because the first relevant result appears immediately. These measures express different aspects of a single list.

Now compare a modified system ranking C, A, E, B, D. Precision@5 and recall@5 are unchanged, but AP and graded rank-sensitive measures can improve because all three retrieved relevant items appear earlier. If A is the only truly authoritative source and the others are minor mentions, binary relevance may be too coarse to reflect the new ordering's actual usefulness. This motivates graded judgments and explicit authority/fidelity assessments where appropriate.

For a retrieval-augmented answer system, also ask whether the generator used the retrieved evidence accurately and provided correct citations. A high passage recall score cannot by itself establish a faithful answer.

Experimental Design and Statistical Reliability

An evaluation should freeze or version the corpus, query set, relevance judgments, analysis configuration and model deployment so a result can be reproduced. Use held-out queries for tuning comparisons. Report paired per-query changes, confidence intervals or appropriate statistical tests when sample size permits. Do not select dozens of parameters on one small benchmark and then present performance on that same benchmark as independent evidence of generalization.

Separate navigational, informational, exploratory, multilingual and rare-entity queries. Check failure cases where no relevant document exists. If a system returns polished but irrelevant answers for such queries, its apparent click engagement may obscure a serious scholarly reliability problem.

Bias, Incomplete Judgments, and Reporting

Relevance is affected by task framing, assessor expertise, language, source accessibility, and the set of documents available for judging. Pooling documents from systems under comparison can leave unseen relevant sources unjudged; click-derived labels are affected by position and interface design. Annotation guidelines, reviewer disagreement records and sample audits make evaluation more defensible.

A responsible report states the exact measure, cutoff, averaging policy, query count, label scheme, baseline and evaluation limitations. It also avoids assigning a single “search accuracy” percentage without specifying whether the number measures recall, precision, relevance ranking or a distinct operational property. Measurement should direct investigation, not replace it.

Review and Investigation Questions

  2. What changes when relevance judgments are graded?
  3. What are the dangers of pooled-incomplete qrels?
  4. Why are per-query error analyses necessary after computing a mean metric?
