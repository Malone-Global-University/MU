The governing question is: What can retrieved evidence support, and what can a language model still get wrong?

This article emphasizes mechanisms, testable examples, operational tradeoffs, and failure conditions. Search systems should be assessed against the information needs and permissions of their actual users, rather than treating an abstract relevance score as an absolute indicator of truth or quality.

Retrieval as Context for Answer Generation

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is a family of systems that brings information from an external collection into a generative model's task context. A retriever finds potentially relevant passages, a context assembly process prepares them, and a generator produces a response conditioned on the question and selected evidence. RAG can improve access to changing, private or specialized information without placing the entire corpus into a model's parameters, but it does not guarantee that the generated response is correct or that the retrieved evidence actually supports every claim.

A central distinction is between retrieval relevance and answer faithfulness. A passage may be topically relevant but insufficient to answer the question; a model may misread a correct passage; or it may add an unsupported conclusion that sounds reasonable. The system must evaluate the stages separately. Treating a fluent answer as proof of accurate retrieval undermines source-grounded institutional work.

Components of a RAG Pipeline

A typical architecture begins with approved source ingestion, parsing, chunking, metadata retention, indexing and access control. A query is analyzed and used to retrieve candidates with lexical, dense or hybrid methods. The system may rerank or deduplicate passages, assemble a bounded context window, label source identifiers, generate an answer, attach citations and apply final validation or uncertainty handling.

Each stage creates failure opportunities. Poor extraction can omit a qualifying sentence; coarse chunking can separate a conclusion from its date; a vector search can select the wrong event; a reranker can bury the correct passage; context truncation can remove the vital exception; and generation can invent or incorrectly combine claims. Engineering must provide traceability from the displayed answer back to the exact source version and passage span.

Stage Intended function Example failure
Source ingestion Preserve authorized, attributable content Old document version indexed
Retrieval Find relevant evidence Semantically similar but wrong source
Reranking Prioritize useful passages Correct passage excluded from candidate set
Context assembly Provide coherent, bounded evidence Contradictory passages silently merged
Generation Synthesize supported response Model adds ungrounded factual claim
Attribution Link claims to evidence Citation points to unrelated paragraph

Chunking, Context Selection, and Provenance

Chunk sizes affect retrieval and interpretation. A short passage may match a precise question but omit the qualifying paragraph immediately below it. A large chunk may contain multiple topics and bury the relevant sentence. Structured chunking around headings or semantic boundaries, sometimes with measured overlap, can improve usability, but a universal optimal length does not exist. The strategy should reflect document genres, model limits and evaluation tasks.

A citation identifier should point to a stable source and ideally a revision and passage location, not merely to a vector index row that may change during rebuilding. Access restrictions and redactions must travel with the source. When a document is withdrawn or a user loses permission, cached context and derived search indexes require a revocation strategy.

RAG architectures sometimes insert summaries rather than original passages to save context space. Summaries are additional derived interpretations and can introduce information loss or mistakes. The system must be able to distinguish source text, machine summaries and model-generated conclusions.

Worked Example: A Question About Institutional Policy

A student asks whether a course may be repeated for credit. The knowledge base includes a current policy specifying conditions, an obsolete policy with different conditions and a departmental exception. A retriever that selects all three without dates or hierarchy may provide contradictory context. A responsible system first filters to approved documents, records the active version, identifies relevant exceptions and, if necessary, reports that the answer depends on department or enrollment date.

An answer like “the course can always be repeated twice” is unsafe if the cited policy only permits repetition under certain criteria. Even if the citation links to the correct policy page, the claim is not supported. Claim-level checking asks what exact sentence or paragraph justifies the assertion; document-level citation alone is insufficient.

An evaluation set should include questions with clear answers, conditional rules, outdated documents, missing records, contradictory sources, access restrictions and unanswerable questions. Test the ability to acknowledge missing evidence rather than rewarding a system for confidently filling every gap.

Security: Permissions and Prompt Injection

Retrieved documents are data, not trusted commands. An attacker may place text in a page instructing the generator to reveal secrets, ignore its rules or cite false material. The system must preserve an instruction hierarchy separating authorized user/system directives from untrusted retrieved content. Quoted policies, web pages and search snippets must never be treated as permission to perform unrelated actions.

Access control needs to operate during retrieval and context assembly, not merely after answer generation. Returning a summary of a restricted document can leak its contents even when the final answer omits the original URL. Redaction should be applied consistently across search results, embeddings, caches, logs and user-facing citations. Tool actions should require their own authorization checks.

Another risk is source poisoning: a malicious or low-quality document may be highly ranked through keyword manipulation or misleading metadata. Provenance, editorial approval and independent comparison can mitigate this, but there is no automatic retrieval score that converts content into trustworthy authority.

Evaluating Retrieval and Answers Separately

Evaluate retrieval with candidate recall and ranked relevance metrics on judged questions. Evaluate answer faithfulness with source-supported claim checks, citation correctness and detection of unsupported additions. Also assess answer completeness, usefulness, calibration of uncertainty, user trust and the behavior when no valid source exists. A system could have perfect passage recall yet still produce false synthesis; it could produce a correct answer by chance while citing inadequate evidence.

When claims depend on multiple passages, check whether the synthesis is actually licensed by the documents together. If sources conflict, answer generation should identify the conflict and its provenance instead of silently choosing the most fluent interpretation. Report performance by topic, language, document age and permission scope.

Production Architecture and Editorial Rules

A production RAG service should version its source corpus, extraction process, embedding model, retrieval index, reranker, prompt/context template and generation model. Provide reproducible trace identifiers so maintainers can diagnose why a response cited a particular passage. Apply source updates and deletions consistently, and consider a simple direct-document answer or abstention when evidence is too weak to justify generation.

For an encyclopedia, the preferred outcome is not maximal automation but reduced effort in locating and understanding authentic sources. The answer should show where its claims came from, make it easy to open the underlying articles, distinguish fact from inference, and avoid presenting an unsupported model synthesis as settled scholarship.
