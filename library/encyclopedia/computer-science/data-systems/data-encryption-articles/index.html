Collection Overview

The Data Encryption unit is part of the Data Systems division of the Computer Science volume of the MGU Encyclopedia. Its established parent, Data Encryption, introduces plaintext and ciphertext, mathematical encryption mechanisms, secret keys, confidentiality, data at rest and in transit, operational exposure and encryption's role in modern systems. This unit creates a specialist reading and production directory beneath that summary rather than replacing it.

Encrypted information remains present, stored and transferable, but authorized processes can recover its plaintext using the appropriate secret or decryption service. This distinction creates two connected design obligations: protect ciphertext against the intended observer and protect the keys, endpoints and mechanisms that can reveal plaintext. A strong cipher alone cannot stop an application from exposing data after decryption, prevent a backup from being lost, guarantee that a ciphertext has not been modified, or determine whether a legitimate user is authorized. A complete protection design must state its threat model and identify every plaintext access boundary.

Scope: This unit concentrates on encryption applied to datasets, files, records, storage media, services, backups and transfers. The broader Cryptography unit owns mathematical primitives and protocol theory. Access Control owns authorization decisions; Network Protocols owns the full transport protocol stack; Data Compression owns size reduction, which is not encryption. Those boundaries are explicit throughout the specialist articles.

Taxonomic placement: This page is the Level 4 Unit index. Its 20 linked specialist subjects are planned Level 5 Unit subarticles; Level 6 or deeper companion articles may later refine those topics without changing this unit's role. The four subject groups on this index are instructional groupings, not additional formal taxonomy levels.

Editorial provenance: The existing parent has a Related Topics section but no prescribed child-article roster. The 20 titles and destination slugs here are a new, proposed editorial seed grounded in its published scope. Links are intentionally active as planned destinations and do not imply that those Level 5 pages are already written, deployed or accessible.

Interpretation discipline: Encryption is not hashing, access control, anonymization, compression or a guarantee of end-to-end secrecy. Ordinary encryption does not intrinsically ensure ciphertext integrity or source authentication; authenticated encryption provides specified integrity/authentication properties under its documented threat model. Keys and application endpoints remain critical, even with hardware-backed key services.

Encryption Foundations and Protection Properties

These five articles establish the concepts behind confidential data transformation, then progressively add algorithm type, state management and authenticated message semantics. They emphasize the difference between a mathematical primitive and a secure deployed construction; they are not a replacement for an advanced cryptography textbook.

1. Encryption Threat Models and Confidentiality

Research and instructional scope: Begin with plaintext, ciphertext, a cipher, a key, encryption and decryption. Build distinct threat models for a lost unpowered device, a stolen backup, passive network observation, malicious storage administrators and an already-compromised application endpoint. Explain the difference between strong cryptographic protection and the operational question of who can reach the decryption mechanism. Identify where plaintext is generated, retained, displayed or logged. State that a secure algorithm alone cannot protect a session after the running endpoint, account or key is compromised. Compare confidentiality with integrity, authenticity, availability and authorization; give examples in which the latter properties require additional controls. Distinguish algorithm secrecy from key secrecy and explain the purpose of publicly scrutinized cryptographic constructions.

Editorial boundary: Cryptography in Security Systems owns full cryptographic theory. This child owns the specific confidentiality requirements of data-system deployments, not every class of security control.

Expected specialist treatment: Construct an asset-flow diagram for a laptop, cloud database and backup archive; mark plaintext boundaries, key custody, adversary access and residual risks.

2. Symmetric Encryption and Block Cipher Services

Research and instructional scope: Describe the symmetric-key model: authorized parties or services possess suitable secret material; the key and algorithm determine reversible transformations. Explain block ciphers as keyed permutations on fixed-sized blocks and show why a block primitive is not by itself a safe file-encryption protocol. Introduce AES as a widely deployed cipher without treating a particular key length as a universal deployment recommendation. Address permitted key sizes, key separation, algorithm profiles, library interfaces and managed access to a secret. Contrast bulk symmetric encryption with public-key cryptography, which is often used to establish or wrap a smaller symmetric key instead of handling an entire large object. Explain why independently encrypting repeating blocks with raw ECB mode leaks structure and why approved modes or schemes must be selected for the entire application.

Editorial boundary: The Instruction Set Architecture and Digital Logic units cover hardware acceleration and circuits. This child treats the data protection service, leaving proof-level cipher mathematics to Cryptography.

Expected specialist treatment: Trace the encryption of three data blocks through an authenticated mode and identify which properties come from the primitive versus the complete construction.

3. Stream Ciphers, Keystreams, and Nonce Safety

Research and instructional scope: Explain stream encryption as combining plaintext with a key-dependent keystream and recovering the bytes by reversing the operation. Distinguish a stream cipher from block-cipher counter modes and from an authenticated encryption scheme that happens to use a stream mechanism. Define nonces and counters as public or nonsecret values that still require protocol-specific uniqueness constraints. Show conceptually why two ciphertexts encrypted under the same key and repeated keystream can reveal relationships between corresponding plaintexts, and why a serious violation may not be repaired by hiding the ciphertext afterward. Introduce approved, maintained library interfaces for algorithms such as ChaCha20 and the importance of authenticated variants, per-key usage limits, restart-safe nonce allocation and distributed writers.

Editorial boundary: Detailed algorithm proofs and primitive selection belong to the Cryptography unit. This child documents encryption-state safety for real storage and transport workloads.

Expected specialist treatment: Compare a restarted writer reusing counter zero with a system assigning unique message identifiers; document how to test nonce-collision prevention across parallel jobs.

4. Authenticated Encryption and Associated Data

Research and instructional scope: Define authenticated encryption with associated data (AEAD): it encrypts designated plaintext and authenticates both ciphertext and associated data, subject to its scheme and threat assumptions. Explain that unencrypted associated data, such as a record identifier or format version, may be bound to the message without being concealed. Differentiate accidental error checks, bare encryption, a MAC, a signature and AEAD; each solves a distinct problem. Discuss tag verification, tampering failures, constant-time library behavior where relevant, and the critical need to avoid acting on unauthenticated plaintext. Use AES-GCM or ChaCha20-Poly1305 as familiar examples but do not claim they are interchangeable in every constrained environment. Make nonce reuse, per-key message limits and complete framing explicit requirements rather than invisible implementation details.

Editorial boundary: Security Systems owns cryptographic protocol design generally; this child owns the practical message and record protection contract for encrypted data handling.

Expected specialist treatment: Design a message with a key ID, version, nonce, authenticated record ID, ciphertext and tag; state which fields need integrity, which need secrecy and what happens on tag failure.

5. Cipher Modes, Initialization Vectors, and Padding

Research and instructional scope: Compare counter, chaining, tweakable storage and authenticated modes at the level of their security properties and common usage contexts. Clarify that an IV or nonce need not be secret but its unpredictability or uniqueness requirements depend on the mode; do not apply one rule mechanically to every construction. Explain padding and message length as format issues, distinguishing padding for block alignment from any claim of application-layer anonymity. Discuss why using a mode without authentication can allow undetected tampering and why ad hoc combinations are inappropriate. Include ciphertext overhead, error propagation, truncated input, record identifiers and mode-specific constraints for repeated writes. Treat AES-XTS as a storage-oriented confidentiality mode, not as a general AEAD replacement for arbitrary files.

Editorial boundary: The File Systems and Storage Hardware units own storage organization; the cryptographic primitives unit owns formal mode definitions. This child focuses on mode-specific decisions when encoding persistent data.

Expected specialist treatment: Build a mode-selection table for disk sectors, a database record and a network message; justify why the same mode is not appropriate for all three.

Key Management and Cryptographic Operations

These five articles address what frequently determines real-world encryption effectiveness: how keys are generated, derived, protected, distributed, rotated, retired and recovered. Each article must define the authoritative party for the key and the consequences when the key becomes unavailable or compromised.

1. Cryptographic Key Generation and Randomness

Research and instructional scope: Describe how data-encryption keys originate, emphasizing generation through a trusted cryptographically secure random number generator or approved key-derivation process. Distinguish a randomly generated key from a user password, a public nonce, a key identifier and a cryptographic salt. Explain why predictable randomness undermines even well-specified algorithms and why copying a fixed seed between virtual machines or development images is dangerous. Include security strength, key length versus entropy, provisioning time, validation of algorithm-specific key parameters, auditability and testing strategies. Address the special risk of generating keys before a device has adequate randomness and of cloning images that include encryption keys. Describe operational responsibility for generated keys, without claiming that randomness tests alone can prove cryptographic suitability.

Editorial boundary: Probability and Statistics for Computing owns mathematical distributions; Cryptography owns generator construction. This child owns the custody and generation requirements of encryption deployments.

Expected specialist treatment: Audit a deployment where ten service replicas share an identical development secret; produce a corrected generation, distribution and rotation plan.

2. Password-Derived Encryption Keys and KDFs

Research and instructional scope: Explain why a human-memorable password should not be treated as an unpredictable random encryption key. Describe password-based key derivation with a unique salt and appropriate work/memory settings, and distinguish stretching a password from deriving multiple context-separated keys from stronger shared material. Discuss the purpose of Argon2id, scrypt and PBKDF2 at the level of documented platform support, calibrated cost, offline-guessing exposure and migration. Separate encryption of user-held files from safe server-side password verification; password storage normally uses password hashing rather than reversible encryption. Explain recovery-key and account-reset implications when the password derives the only decryption key. Include the need for rate limits and enrollment safeguards without suggesting that salts are secret.

Editorial boundary: Authentication Systems and Cryptography own complete password-hashing policies. This child covers the limited case where a password must derive a key to decrypt retained data.

Expected specialist treatment: Compare a portable encrypted archive accessed by a user passphrase with a web login database; specify why the two systems need different secret-storage designs.

3. Key Lifecycle, Rotation, and Cryptoperiods

Research and instructional scope: Map each encryption key to an explicit lifecycle with responsible owner, creation evidence, permitted operations, activation and retirement conditions, and recoverability requirements. Explain cryptoperiod as a policy-governed usage interval influenced by data lifetime, exposure, volume and algorithm constraints rather than a single universal number of days. Distinguish rotating a wrapping key from re-encrypting every data object, and distinguish stopping future encryption under an old key from retaining decryption capability for archived records. Discuss revocation, key inventory, backup copies, deletion evidence, separation of duties and the risks of indefinite access to retired keys. Describe how to preserve lawful and required access while minimizing stale material and ensuring recoverable failures during migrations.

Editorial boundary: Security Risk Management owns enterprise policy; this child defines the lifecycle controls specifically needed to keep encrypted datasets readable and confidential.

Expected specialist treatment: Create a lifecycle runbook for ten years of encrypted backups, with key expiration, restore testing, legal retention, compromise response and eventual erasure.

4. Envelope Encryption and Data Encryption Keys

Research and instructional scope: Explain envelope encryption as using a data-encryption key (DEK) to protect a data object, then protecting that DEK under a key-encryption key or suitable managed wrapping mechanism. Map where wrapped keys, identifiers, metadata, algorithms and access policies live. Show how large object stores can use separate DEKs to limit blast radius and ease rewrapping, but clarify that rewrapping does not change or re-encrypt the underlying data ciphertext. Discuss context binding, authenticated metadata, key versions, restore dependencies, independent tenants, rekeying costs and the risk of storing all key material in one unprotected location. Contrast one shared static application key with per-object encryption, without presenting either as universally optimal for every workload.

Editorial boundary: Cloud Computing owns provider-specific service APIs and Cryptography owns key-wrap algorithm design. This child owns the key hierarchy and encrypted data-object relationship.

Expected specialist treatment: Draw a key hierarchy for 1,000 tenant objects; distinguish rewrap, rotate, revoke and re-encrypt operations and the exact data affected.

5. Hardware-Protected Keys and KMS Boundaries

Research and instructional scope: Describe what changes when keys are generated, stored or used through a hardware security module, trusted hardware facility or cloud key-management service. Explain API-enforced privileges, audit logs, service identity, custody and what a managed service does or does not attest about endpoint security. Distinguish a key that is non-exportable from plaintext that remains accessible to the calling workload; protecting the former does not inherently prevent malicious authorized callers from decrypting data. Introduce high availability, latency, throughput quotas, region/failover constraints, deletion or disablement, disaster recovery and tenant separation. Avoid conflating a hardware-backed key service with a generic secrets manager or assuming every processor’s isolated execution feature provides identical properties.

Editorial boundary: Hardware Systems owns device design and Cloud Computing owns vendor-specific provisioning. This child focuses on operational guarantees and limits of managed encryption-key custody.

Expected specialist treatment: Evaluate an application using a remote key service: show which service principals can unwrap DEKs, where plaintext exists and what an outage means for recovery.

Encryption Across Storage, Database, and Transport Layers

These five articles compare encryption placement across block devices, files, backup archives, structured records, network connections and client-controlled workflows. They ask who can decrypt, when plaintext exists and which metadata remains visible; no layer should be described as independently solving all others.

1. Full-Disk and Volume Encryption

Research and instructional scope: Explain full-disk and volume encryption at the storage block layer, including the role of encrypted sectors, keys, unlock mechanisms and recovery material. Contrast protection against removal or theft of an offline drive with the much weaker value against an attacker already operating a legitimately unlocked device. Discuss boot integrity as a separate protection, preboot access, suspend states, hibernation, virtual disks, swap, removable volumes and secure device disposal. Describe storage-oriented modes as distinct from file-by-file AEAD and note that data integrity and anti-rollback generally require additional mechanisms. Consider who can initiate unlock and whether a recovery escrow procedure changes the assumed confidentiality boundary.

Editorial boundary: Storage Hardware and File Systems own physical media and mount behavior. This child owns encryption coverage, key access and the protected state transitions.

Expected specialist treatment: Draw a timeline for a powered-off encrypted laptop, an unlocked session and a removed SSD; specify what data each threat actor can access.

2. File, Object, and Backup Encryption

Research and instructional scope: Compare per-file encryption, object-level encryption, backup-set encryption and provider-managed storage encryption. Discuss whether file names, object keys, sizes, timestamps, access patterns or directory structures remain visible even when content is encrypted. Explain where encryption occurs, who controls data-encryption keys, and what changes when an object is copied, replicated, versioned or restored from a snapshot. Include per-object authenticated framing, partial reads, chunk nonces, metadata integrity, key retention, ransomware-resilience boundaries, restore testing and cold-storage access. Differentiate encrypted backups from immutable backups: confidentiality does not establish recoverability, integrity, malware resistance or offline availability.

Editorial boundary: File Systems, Cloud Computing and Business Continuity own storage systems and resilience plans. This child owns encryption and decryption obligations throughout the file or backup lifecycle.

Expected specialist treatment: Design an encrypted backup policy with quarterly restore tests; specify who has both ciphertext and decryption capability after primary infrastructure loss.

3. Database and Field-Level Encryption

Research and instructional scope: Separate storage-volume encryption, database transparent data encryption, application-side field encryption and client-side encryption of selected records. Explain which adversaries each layer addresses and why a database administrator or running query process may still see plaintext for transparent encryption. Address query and indexing limitations when data fields are encrypted before reaching the server; mention deterministic encryption only with its pattern-leakage risks and required threat-model review. Include schema evolution, join/search constraints, backup and replication handling, tenant separation, field-level key policies, authentication tags and key recovery. Explain why masking, tokenization and encryption are not synonyms even when combined in a data-protection program.

Editorial boundary: Databases and Data Modeling own query semantics and schema design; this article owns encryption-placement and data-exposure trade-offs for structured records.

Expected specialist treatment: Compare TDE, application field encryption and tokenization for customer records; identify which actor can read each sensitive attribute and which queries remain feasible.

4. Transport Encryption and TLS Data Paths

Research and instructional scope: Describe encryption in transit using TLS as a principal example. Define the endpoint pair and the negotiated protected channel instead of claiming that one encrypted link secures data from original sender to final consumer. Explain server authentication, optional client authentication, certificate or trust-store validation, key agreement, session keys and authenticated record protection at a conceptual level. Show where proxies, load balancers, content delivery networks, gateways and service meshes may terminate or re-establish encryption. Discuss plaintext in application processes, logs and intermediaries, certificate expiration, configuration drift and downgrade risks. State that a successful TLS connection alone does not prove a service is authorized to handle the transferred data.

Editorial boundary: Network Protocols, Network Security and TLS-specific security material own the complete protocol handshake and configuration standards. This unit focuses on data-path exposure boundaries.

Expected specialist treatment: Trace an API request through a browser, reverse proxy, service and database; mark separate TLS sessions, authentication decisions and every plaintext-handling node.

5. End-to-End and Client-Side Encryption Boundaries

Research and instructional scope: Define end-to-end encryption in terms of who possesses decryption capability and where messages become plaintext, rather than as a generic marketing label. Compare server-side encryption at rest with client-side encryption before upload and with systems in which only intended communicating endpoints hold the content keys. Discuss device enrollment, recipient identity, public-key or hybrid establishment at a high level, account recovery, backups, metadata exposure, search and collaboration trade-offs, multi-device synchronization and compromised endpoints. Clarify that end-to-end confidentiality does not automatically hide sender or receiver identity, timing, message lengths or device security events. Include shared-folder access and key revocation challenges as data-lifecycle issues.

Editorial boundary: Communications Security and Cryptography own complete secure-messaging protocols. This child owns how encrypted data custody changes when servers do or do not hold content keys.

Expected specialist treatment: Compare a managed cloud document service and a client-encrypted file vault; map who can decrypt under normal operations, support recovery and compelled administrative access.

Encryption Operations, Migration, and Assurance

These five articles connect cryptographic protection with production implementation, durable formats, migration, benchmarking and incident response. They require declared limits, test evidence, compatibility planning and fault scenarios rather than generic instructions to “turn on encryption.”

1. Data Encryption Architecture and Threat Modeling

Research and instructional scope: Present a design process that starts with data classification, assets, actors, retention and permissible exposure rather than a list of available ciphers. Map ingress, processing, caching, transit, persistence, replication, logs, analytics, backups and deletion. Assign encryption controls to each risk boundary, state where keys reside, and check whether the authenticated application can still disclose plaintext in its own operation. Include defense in depth, separation of duties, incident scenarios, administrative access, availability requirements and lawful recovery. Explain why unauthorized reading, unauthorized modification and accidental loss are separate risks. Treat regulatory requirements as context-dependent and avoid declaring a single encryption configuration universally compliant with all regimes.

Editorial boundary: Enterprise Data Governance owns organizational data policy and Security Architecture owns the overall system security design. This child owns the encryption-specific trust-boundary inventory and decision record.

Expected specialist treatment: Produce an architecture decision record with protected datasets, plaintext touchpoints, key owner, recovery role, attack scenario, chosen layer and unmitigated exposure.

2. Encrypted Record Formats and Metadata

Research and instructional scope: Explain that ciphertext alone rarely makes a durable file format. A decryptor also needs an algorithm or suite identifier, key reference, nonce or tweak, framing lengths, tag and format version, with exact contents determined by the scheme. Distinguish metadata that may remain public from metadata that needs authentication to prevent substitution or cross-object replay. Discuss canonical encodings, associated data, record IDs, truncation handling, incremental reads, ciphertext overhead and the danger of using unauthenticated headers to select sensitive processing paths. Design for future decryption without permitting silent fallback to obsolete or insecure configurations. Avoid using the same IV or nonce across records just because a file is segmented into blocks.

Editorial boundary: Binary Data and Data Serialization own general framing and parsers; this child owns cryptographic format boundaries, integrity binding and compatibility.

Expected specialist treatment: Specify a compact versioned encrypted-record envelope and four negative tests: tag corruption, altered key ID, truncated payload and swapped record context.

3. Algorithm Agility and Cryptographic Migration

Research and instructional scope: Explain algorithm agility as the managed capability to change cryptographic implementations and policies without losing access to protected data or silently weakening security. Distinguish changing the cipher suite, rotating a key, rewrapping a data key and re-encrypting the underlying payload: these are not equivalent operations. Discuss inventories of algorithms and dependencies, unsupported clients, retained ciphertext, version-tagged formats, staged rollout, read-old/write-new transitional design, fallback restrictions and rollback plans. Address classical-to-post-quantum transition planning where relevant but avoid claiming that symmetric data encryption automatically becomes obsolete or that any unspecified post-quantum scheme has universal approval. Require measured compatibility and recovery tests before deleting old decryption material.

Editorial boundary: Cryptography owns the research and formal evaluation of algorithms; this unit owns migration sequencing and data recoverability at scale.

Expected specialist treatment: Plan replacement of an aging encryption container across a million stored objects; model staged re-encryption, format detection, observability and disaster recovery.

4. Encryption Performance, Side Channels, and Information Leakage

Research and instructional scope: Show how encryption affects CPU cycles, memory transfers, key-service calls, concurrency, message sizes, storage layout, startup time and tail latency. Discuss hardware acceleration as an implementation-dependent optimization rather than a substitute for secure construction. Explain residual information exposed by traffic timing, ciphertext lengths, access frequency, deterministic mapping, observable error differences and application logs. Distinguish formal ciphertext indistinguishability goals from full traffic-analysis resistance; a secure AEAD does not hide which object was requested. Introduce timing and cache side channels as risks requiring vetted implementations and controlled interfaces, without promising that constant-time code solves every system-level leak. Describe a benchmark that reports not just raw cryptographic throughput but end-to-end application performance under realistic concurrency.

Editorial boundary: Algorithm Analysis and Microarchitecture own general performance methodology; Security Vulnerabilities owns broad side-channel classes. This child integrates encryption-specific data and metadata leakage.

Expected specialist treatment: Measure a hypothetical file-upload service before and after envelope encryption; report service latency, KMS calls, ciphertext growth and exposure of object names and sizes.

5. Cryptographic Validation, Monitoring, and Key Compromise Response

Research and instructional scope: Define what a defensible encryption-system validation program must check: correct round trips, authenticated rejection of tampering, nonce and key separation, supported algorithms, format version handling, recovery and negative parsing tests. Distinguish known-answer tests from security proof, compliance certification and a real-world adversarial assessment. Explain observability without leaking secret material or full plaintext into traces. Describe compromise response: identify affected keys and ciphertext, stop new encryption under compromised material, preserve lawful evidence, rotate or replace keys, re-encrypt where necessary, evaluate potential plaintext exposure and confirm restores. Emphasize that cryptographic erasure works only when all key copies and access paths are controlled; do not promise retroactive secrecy after a key disclosure.

Editorial boundary: Incident Response and Security Operations own organization-wide response procedures. This child owns the encryption-specific evidence and remediation dependencies.

Expected specialist treatment: Write a tabletop exercise for a compromised cloud wrapping key: identify affected object sets, escalation conditions, key replacements and verification evidence.

Choosing where to encrypt is as important as choosing how. The table compares typical protection boundaries and remaining exposure. Cross-layer reading: Data can legitimately have several encryption layers simultaneously: a client may protect a file, the server may secure its storage, backups may use separate keys and transport links may have their own sessions. These provide different security properties and failure modes; counting “four layers” without identifying the same keys, endpoints and authorized decryption points does not demonstrate confidentiality.

Critical distinctions: AEAD can authenticate defined ciphertext and associated data under its secret-key assumptions; a conventional hash of a public ciphertext does not authenticate the sender. A secure password hash is not intended to be decrypted. Disk encryption is not the same as a protected database field. A keyed access-control decision is not equivalent to ciphertext confidentiality.

Worked Data Encryption Scenarios

These are constructed educational scenarios, not reports of actual MGU systems. Each demonstrates how an apparently adequate cipher configuration can leave meaningful gaps in decryption authority, authentication, availability or system observability.

Worked example 1 — Stolen laptop and unlocked session

A company encrypts a laptop volume and protects its recovery key. When the laptop is powered off and locked, an attacker removing its drive should not obtain ordinary readable file contents without the key. If the same laptop is stolen while an authorized user session is unlocked, the threat changes: a program running with the user’s rights may request plaintext. Review Full-Disk and Volume Encryption, Encryption Threat Models and Confidentiality, and Hardware-Protected Keys and KMS Boundaries. The lesson is to name the protected state and not equate disk encryption with complete device security.

Worked example 2 — Ciphertext substitution between customers

A fictional service encrypts customer statements. The ciphertext is confidential but the identifier of the intended customer is kept in a database row next to it. An attacker swaps encrypted values between two records. An authenticated encrypted-record format can bind the record ID and tenant context as associated data so that an authorized decryptor rejects mismatched context. The AEAD design still needs correct keys and nonce management. Read Authenticated Encryption and Associated Data, Encrypted Record Formats and Metadata, and Database and Field-Level Encryption. This example shows why secrecy alone does not guarantee safe interpretation.

Worked example 3 — Encryption nonce collision across writers

Two independent upload workers share a symmetric key but both restart with nonce counter zero. A scheme requiring unique nonces can suffer serious confidentiality or authentication failures when the same key/nonce pair repeats, even if the nonce itself is public. A corrected implementation partitions nonce spaces, uses appropriate library generation, observes per-key usage limits and tests restart behavior. Study Stream Ciphers, Keystreams, and Nonce Safety, Authenticated Encryption and Associated Data, and Cryptographic Validation, Monitoring, and Key Compromise Response. A larger random-looking key does not cure a broken state-allocation protocol.

Worked example 4 — Envelope encryption at scale

A service protects 50,000 objects using distinct object-level DEKs wrapped by a managed key. An administrator requests rotation of the wrapping key. Rewrapping the encrypted DEKs may avoid rewriting every object payload, but it is not the same as generating new object DEKs and re-encrypting all 50,000 objects. Key service availability and restore custody still matter. Compare Envelope Encryption and Data Encryption Keys, Key Lifecycle, Rotation, and Cryptoperiods, and Hardware-Protected Keys and KMS Boundaries. Record exactly what changed, which ciphertext remains identical and which old keys are retained.

Worked example 5 — Database encryption and administrator access

A database enables transparent data encryption for its storage files, then assumes database administrators cannot read any sensitive customer columns. That conclusion does not follow: the running database service may decrypt pages to execute authorized queries. Application-side field encryption changes the boundary but may complicate equality search, range predicates and indexing. Read Database and Field-Level Encryption, Data Encryption Architecture and Threat Modeling, and End-to-End and Client-Side Encryption Boundaries. The right design begins with the exact attacker and necessary query functions.

Worked example 6 — TLS termination at an application gateway

A browser connects over TLS to a public load balancer, which terminates that session and initiates a separate encrypted connection to a backend. Traffic may be encrypted on both network segments, yet the gateway processes plaintext between the sessions. The gateway becomes part of the data-trust boundary. Examine Transport Encryption and TLS Data Paths, Encryption Threat Models and Confidentiality, and End-to-End and Client-Side Encryption Boundaries. A padlock does not mean that no intermediary ever sees application data.

Worked example 7 — A ten-year encrypted backup

An organization retains encrypted backups for ten years, but rotates a KMS key each year and deletes all prior key versions immediately. The newest production system remains operational while old backups may be undecryptable. A defensible policy coordinates retention, decryption authority, backup of necessary key material, restore tests, key expiration and eventually authorized destruction. Study File, Object, and Backup Encryption, Key Lifecycle, Rotation, and Cryptoperiods, and Cryptographic Validation, Monitoring, and Key Compromise Response. Confidentiality and future recoverability are independent requirements.

Worked example 8 — Algorithm migration with legacy ciphertext

An application decides to replace an aging encryption container. A safe rollout inventories versions and clients, deploys new readers, switches writers, monitors errors, re-encrypts retained objects when required and retains only appropriately controlled legacy decryption ability during the transition. Treating old ciphertext as if it were created with the new algorithm results in data loss or security mistakes. Read Algorithm Agility and Cryptographic Migration, Encrypted Record Formats and Metadata, and Cryptographic Validation, Monitoring, and Key Compromise Response. A migration plan must include forward and backward failure handling.

Worked example 9 — Timing and object-length disclosure

A cloud service applies robust AEAD to each document but leaves object names and lengths visible and exposes which records a user accesses. An observer may still infer workflow patterns without breaking encryption. A further threat appears if secret and attacker-controlled content are compressed together before encryption and the observer can measure ciphertext length. Review Encryption Performance, Side Channels, and Information Leakage, Data Encryption Architecture and Threat Modeling, and Data Compression. Distinguish confidentiality of file contents from privacy of metadata and access patterns.

Learning rule: for each case identify the adversary, protected asset, plaintext exposure points, key owner, relevant security property, failure behavior and a test that would show whether the chosen controls work. A claim such as “AES is enabled” supplies too little evidence to finish that assessment.

Protection Selection and Implementation Framework

A data-encryption proposal should be reviewable as a technical decision record. Start with the specific asset and risk, choose where the data must become and remain opaque, then account for key custody, authenticated integrity, data lifetime, application performance and recovery. Minimum decision record: identify the data owner and controller; enumerate actors who may legitimately decrypt; map encryption and decryption boundaries; select maintained, reviewed schemes and library interfaces; document nonce and key-reuse constraints; specify key rotation and recovery; test corruption rejection; set logging boundaries; record expected performance; and explain which security properties are not supplied by the chosen encryption layer.

Deployment rule: Do not design new homegrown cryptographic primitives or unauthenticated encryption formats when a suitable established authenticated solution is available. Follow the supported scheme's complete instructions for tag length, IV/nonce management, permitted message size and key usage. A cryptographic-library call is one dependency of the design, not a substitute for identifying all plaintext and key-management boundaries.
