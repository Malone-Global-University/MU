Collection Overview

The Digital Logic unit belongs to the Computer Architecture division of the Computer Science volume of the MGU Encyclopedia. It extends the established Digital Logic parent article, which surveys Boolean functions, combinational circuits, arithmetic, state elements, clocking, hardware descriptions, programmable logic, verification, and electrical implementation trade-offs.

Digital logic concerns systems whose intended computation is expressed using discrete states and logic functions. This abstraction enables engineers to reason about truth tables and state transitions, but realizable circuits also operate with finite propagation delay, supply voltages, physical noise margins, and timing constraints. A useful learning progression therefore moves from functional meaning to state and timing, then to implementation and verification. Neither a truth table alone nor a passing simulation is sufficient to describe all properties of a manufactured circuit.

Within the MGU taxonomy, this is the Level 4 unit index. The linked subjects are planned Level 5 unit subarticles; more specialized companion material at Level 6 and beyond can follow only as needed. This deliverable seeds the directory and does not create the Level 5 content.

Editorial provenance: unlike the Bus Systems unit, the existing Digital Logic summary already enumerates a complete list of 15 proposed specialist titles and filenames. This index preserves all 15 verbatim and supplies a deeper educational directory, clear scope assignments, and active intended destinations. The existence of a hyperlink is not a claim that the linked article is written or published.

Boolean Foundations and Combinational Design

These articles establish functional correctness before discussing physical timing. They teach a reliable route from a requirement to a Boolean expression, equivalent gate network, or combinational datapath. Every future child should distinguish the function being computed from the particular hardware structure used to realize it.

1. Boolean Algebra for Digital Circuits

Primary scope: Identities, canonical forms, and equivalence transformations. Explain Boolean expressions as specifications of functions over binary variables. Develop identity, complement, absorption, distributive and De Morgan transformations, the difference between algebraic equivalence and a particular gate realization, and how minterms and maxterms express a complete truth table.

Editorial development boundary: Include truth-table checks and an algebraic derivation showing why two different-looking expressions implement the same function. Clarify that Boolean semantics alone do not account for voltage thresholds, propagation delays, or hazardous switching transitions.

Expected instructional deliverable: A normalized Boolean expression and a proof or complete verification of equivalence.

2. Logic Gates and Gate-Level Design

Primary scope: Primitive gates, universal gate networks, and physical implementation. Treat NOT, AND, OR, XOR, XNOR, NAND, NOR, buffers, and tri-state outputs as logical functions with implementation constraints. Show how a gate-level network is assembled, how a diagram encodes connections, and why NAND and NOR are functionally complete.

Editorial development boundary: Differentiate a logical gate model from CMOS devices and synthesized standard cells; account for fan-out, loading, path depth and input arrival times. Do not reproduce the full CMOS device physics covered in hardware units.

Expected instructional deliverable: Equivalent gate networks with a justified technology or logic-depth trade-off.

3. Truth Tables and Logic Minimization

Primary scope: Karnaugh maps, minterms, maxterms, and optimization. Develop a repeatable procedure from requirements to truth table, canonical form, simplification, and verified circuit. Cover two-to-four-variable Karnaugh maps, prime implicants, essential implicants, and what a don't-care input actually permits.

Editorial development boundary: Distinguish minimizing literal count, gate count, logic depth, power, and technology-specific area. Explain why the smallest Boolean expression need not be the fastest or most robust physical implementation.

Expected instructional deliverable: A documented minimized function with input cases and equivalence verification.

4. Multiplexers, Decoders, and Encoders

Primary scope: Combinational selection and decoding. Define selection, demultiplexing, address decoding, encoding, and priority assignment through their truth tables and gate structures. Compare the role of a multiplexer in choosing data with the role of a decoder in selecting a target.

Editorial development boundary: Include priority-encoder corner cases, invalid inputs, enable pins, output defaults, and the risks of implicit assumptions about mutually exclusive inputs. Connect to memory selection and datapath steering without duplicating the broader Bus Systems unit.

Expected instructional deliverable: A small selector-decoder design with explicit invalid-input behavior.

Arithmetic, Storage, and State-Machine Design

These subjects introduce numeric datapaths and remembered state. Their key transition is from purely combinational mappings of current inputs to circuits whose observable behavior depends on clocked history, reset choices, and state transitions. They should provide explicit cycle-by-cycle examples, not only schematic symbols.

1. Binary Adders and Arithmetic Circuits

Primary scope: Half/full adders, carries, and arithmetic units. Derive sum and carry equations, combine full adders into wider adders, and compare ripple-carry structures with faster carry strategies. Show how fixed-width addition differs from mathematical addition over unbounded integers.

Editorial development boundary: Discuss carry-out versus signed overflow, two's-complement values, propagation cost, arithmetic-unit selection, and interfaces to neighboring architecture and data-representation units. Explicitly distinguish overflow flags from saturation behavior.

Expected instructional deliverable: A four-bit arithmetic example checked for both unsigned carry and signed overflow.

2. Sequential Logic and State Elements

Primary scope: Latches, flip-flops, and registers. Explain why a sequential circuit depends on stored state as well as current inputs. Compare level-sensitive latches, edge-triggered flip-flops, register banks, enables, resets, and feedback mechanisms.

Editorial development boundary: Describe synchronous state updates, reset behavior, asynchronous inputs, setup/hold assumptions, and what remains stable between clock edges. Separate the conceptual state element from physical memory arrays and operating-system storage abstractions.

Expected instructional deliverable: A state table and clock-by-clock trace for a small register or state element.

3. Counters and Register Transfers

Primary scope: Modulo counters, shifting, and controlled state. Build up counters, down counters, modulo-N sequences, load/hold functions, shift registers, and synchronous register transfers. Explain how enables, conditions, and clock edges determine when state changes.

Editorial development boundary: Address wraparound, comparison with terminal values, rollover, reset choices, and the timing of inter-register transfers. Distinguish data movement through a small register datapath from transaction movement over a system bus.

Expected instructional deliverable: A counter or shift-register design with a trace demonstrating rollover and reset.

4. Finite-State Machine Design

Primary scope: State diagrams, encoding, and outputs. Show how behavioral requirements become states, transitions, input guards, outputs, and reset conditions. Compare Moore and Mealy output definitions and discuss binary, one-hot, and other state encodings as hardware design choices.

Editorial development boundary: Include unreachable states, incomplete transition definitions, default or safe recovery paths, and interaction with registered outputs. Coordinate with the existing State Machines and Automata Theory units: this article owns implementation of an electronic controller rather than abstract language recognition.

Expected instructional deliverable: A complete small FSM: transition diagram, transition table, and testable behavior.

Clocking, RTL, and Logic Implementation

These articles address the gap between a correct logical specification and implementable synchronous hardware. They should make clock assumptions, width and signedness choices, timing constraints, and tool-flow boundaries visible. A successful simulation is not equivalent to a physically timed device.

1. Clocking and Timing Analysis

Primary scope: Setup/hold constraints, propagation, and timing closure. Explain combinational path delay, launch and capture clocks, setup and hold windows, clock uncertainty, and how a design's feasible period is bounded by its critical path. Use a concrete timing budget with stated assumptions.

Editorial development boundary: Separate maximum-delay setup analysis from minimum-delay hold analysis, and distinguish latency from throughput. Note that achieving a target simulation result does not demonstrate implementation timing closure.

Expected instructional deliverable: A worked clock-period calculation and separate setup/hold checks.

2. Clock-Domain Crossing and Metastability

Primary scope: Synchronization, handshakes, and asynchronous FIFOs. Explain the risks of sampling signals without a guaranteed timing relationship, how metastability arises, and why synchronizers reduce its probability of propagation without eliminating it.

Editorial development boundary: Compare a two-stage synchronizer for suitable single-bit controls, toggle or request/acknowledge handshakes for events, and asynchronous FIFO techniques for data streams. Explain why independently synchronizing every bit of a multi-bit bus does not ensure a coherent word.

Expected instructional deliverable: A clock-domain crossing plan identifying source and destination assumptions and validation needs.

3. Hardware Description Languages

Primary scope: Synthesizable SystemVerilog and VHDL. Introduce hardware-description languages as languages for concurrent hardware structure and behavior, not just sequential software notation. Distinguish combinational and clocked processes, assignment and event semantics, parameterization, and synthesizable design from testbench-only behavior.

Editorial development boundary: Explain blocking versus nonblocking assignment in common RTL patterns, width and signedness rules, reset styles, and simulation unknowns. Avoid treating HDL compilation or passing a simulation as proof of hardware correctness.

Expected instructional deliverable: A synthesizable small register or combinational block plus an explanation of its intended hardware.

4. Logic Synthesis and Technology Mapping

Primary scope: RTL optimization, netlists, and technology libraries. Trace the transformation from behavioral RTL to an intermediate representation, simplified logic, technology-mapped cells or FPGA resources, and a realizable physical implementation. Explain when equivalence checks protect intended behavior.

Editorial development boundary: Discuss timing constraints, fan-out, resource sharing, register inference, area/power trade-offs and the effects of technology libraries. Keep place-and-route and chip fabrication at a conceptual boundary while making synthesis' limits explicit.

Expected instructional deliverable: A description of how two equivalent RTL expressions can map to different hardware costs.

Programmable Platforms, Verification, and Reliability

These subjects evaluate what happens after an abstract design is targeted to a device and tested against its requirements. They must distinguish an implementation platform, a verification method, and a reliability characteristic, and avoid the unsupported assumption that any single check certifies the entire system.

1. FPGA Digital Logic Design

Primary scope: Lookup tables, flip-flops, routing, and device resources. Explain how configurable logic blocks, lookup tables, registers, embedded memories, arithmetic resources, and programmable interconnect support digital circuits. Describe the design flow from source constraints through synthesis, placement, routing, timing, and device programming.

Editorial development boundary: Distinguish an FPGA implementation from a fixed ASIC and a software program running on a CPU. Include resource utilization, clock routing, timing closure, I/O standards, and integration with memory and I/O subsystems without duplicating motherboard-level hardware.

Expected instructional deliverable: A simple mapped design showing LUT/register usage and timing/resource implications.

2. Digital Circuit Verification

Primary scope: Testbenches, assertions, formal equivalence, and coverage. Present simulation, directed tests, constrained-random stimuli, property assertions, formal model checking, and equivalence verification as distinct assurance mechanisms. Define a specification, reference model, reset assumptions, and acceptance criteria.

Editorial development boundary: Include edge cases such as invalid input combinations, overflow, simultaneous control requests, reset during operation, and unknown simulation values. Clarify that coverage metrics indicate explored conditions, not guaranteed proof of correctness or silicon reliability.

Expected instructional deliverable: A small verification plan with executable claims and failure-reporting expectations.

3. Digital Logic Power and Reliability

Primary scope: Energy, switching, noise, faults, and robust design. Relate switching activity, effective capacitance, clock frequency, supply voltage, and leakage to power and energy. Explain why the qualitative dynamic-power relation depends on implementation assumptions and why total energy also depends on task duration.

Editorial development boundary: Develop risks from glitches, noise margins, clock/reset errors, transient faults, process and temperature variation, and power integrity. Address fault detection, defensive state design, and reliability verification without promising fault elimination or conflating safety and cybersecurity.

Expected instructional deliverable: A power/reliability trade-off study with observable failure modes and proposed tests.

Digital Logic Design and Realization Comparison

Digital logic topics can all be described as circuits, yet their underlying questions are different. The table helps a reader identify whether a problem concerns logical equivalence, remembered state, timing, implementation, or evidence of correct behavior. The same circuit may require several of these viewpoints in sequence.

Design viewpointKey representationMain questionRead first
Boolean specificationTruth table, equation, mintermsWhich output is required for every allowed input?Boolean Algebra for Digital Circuits
Combinational realizationGate network, decoder, muxHow can a function be implemented and simplified?Logic Gates and Gate-Level Design; Truth Tables and Logic Minimization
Arithmetic datapathAdder slices, carry chainHow are finite-width numbers processed correctly?Binary Adders and Arithmetic Circuits
Sequential controlState table, state transition graphHow does behavior depend on earlier inputs and the clock?Sequential Logic and State Elements; Finite-State Machine Design
Timing realizationPath delays, timing constraintsCan signals be captured reliably at the intended rate?Clocking and Timing Analysis
RTL and synthesisHDL source, mapped netlistWhat actual resources will a description infer?Hardware Description Languages; Logic Synthesis and Technology Mapping
Programmable device implementationLUTs, flip-flops, routing and constraintsDoes the design fit and meet timing on the selected platform?FPGA Digital Logic Design
Behavioral assuranceTests, assertions, equivalence propertiesWhich requirements have been checked, and under what assumptions?Digital Circuit Verification
Physical robustnessPower estimates, clock/reset integrity, fault modelsWhat failure modes persist beyond functional correctness?Digital Logic Power and Reliability

Critical distinctions: a combinational circuit has no intended stored state; a sequential circuit does. Latency is the delay for an operation, while throughput measures sustained completion rate. Functional verification checks behavior against a stated property; static timing analysis checks timing under specified constraints and models. No one metric substitutes for the others.

Illustrative Digital Logic Scenarios

Example 1 — Turn a three-input decision into a verified circuit

A fictional controller accepts three Boolean inputs A, B, and C and asserts its output when at least two inputs are high. The required majority function is AB + AC + BC. It is true for input combinations 011, 101, 110, and 111 and false for the four others. Boolean Algebra for Digital Circuits, Truth Tables and Logic Minimization, and Logic Gates and Gate-Level Design should guide the specification, simplification, and gate realization. Verification must check all eight input combinations; selecting a physically fast or low-power realization is a subsequent engineering problem.

Example 2 — Add four-bit values and interpret the result correctly

A four-bit adder computes 0111 + 0001 = 1000. As unsigned values this means 7 + 1 = 8, which fits in four bits. As four-bit two's-complement signed values, it means 7 + 1 is not representable as +8; the same bit pattern is interpreted as −8, indicating signed overflow. No carry-out from the top bit is required for signed overflow. Binary Adders and Arithmetic Circuits establishes the arithmetic and width semantics; Digital Circuit Verification supplies test cases for sign, carry, zero, and overflow conditions.

Example 3 — A request and acknowledgement control sequence

A small device controller must wait for a request, assert a busy signal, and eventually signal completion. The intended contract includes reset state, whether a request arriving while busy is ignored or queued, and whether completion is a level or one-cycle pulse. Finite-State Machine Design provides the transition model, Sequential Logic and State Elements defines the register behavior, and Hardware Description Languages implements the intended clocked machine. A waveform trace should verify reset, back-to-back requests, and completion timing rather than assume a state diagram implies a valid implementation.

Example 4 — A button crosses into a fast system clock

An external control signal changes independently of a 100 MHz logic clock. Sampling it directly can violate setup or hold requirements and cause metastability. A carefully engineered synchronizer can reduce the chance that a metastable event propagates, but mechanical buttons also need debouncing and event interpretation. Clock-Domain Crossing and Metastability addresses synchronization; Clocking and Timing Analysis explains the clock assumptions; Digital Circuit Verification defines pulse and event tests. The proposed treatment must not claim that two synchronizer stages eliminate metastability.

Example 5 — A small FPGA design passes simulation but misses its clock target

A counter and decoder behave correctly in functional simulation, yet post-implementation timing reports show a register-to-register path longer than the permitted period. The appropriate response is to identify the path and its logic depth, fan-out, routing, constraints, and clock assumptions. Compare FPGA Digital Logic Design, Logic Synthesis and Technology Mapping, and Clocking and Timing Analysis before changing RTL. A functional test cannot demonstrate meeting physical timing, and a passing timing report cannot establish correct functionality.

All examples are constructed learning cases. They are not claims about MGU hardware, actual device tests, or certified implementations.

Design and Verification Framework

Use this decision sequence to move from a requirement to a defensible digital hardware specification. Each step identifies an evidence type and a likely failure if the step is skipped.

Question to resolveEvidence or decisionSuggested starting article
Is the function purely a mapping from current inputs to outputs?Truth table and allowed-input assumptionsTruth Tables and Logic Minimization
Does the machine depend on earlier events or transactions?States, transition rules, reset and output timingFinite-State Machine Design
Must a value be interpreted as signed, unsigned, or saturated?Bit width, overflow behavior, representationBinary Adders and Arithmetic Circuits
Which physical circuit realizes the specification?Gate topology, mapping targets, resource constraintsLogic Gates and Gate-Level Design
Where do the clocked values originate and terminate?Clock domains, valid timing assumptions, crossing protocolClock-Domain Crossing and Metastability
Can registers reliably capture values at the target frequency?Setup/hold analysis, worst and best path delaysClocking and Timing Analysis
Is the design description actually synthesizable and correctly sized?RTL review, width and signedness checks, inferred cellsHardware Description Languages
What device resources and constraints are required?Placement, routing, LUT/register use, implementation reportsFPGA Digital Logic Design
Which functions and edge conditions have been tested?Directed tests, assertions, formal properties, measured coverageDigital Circuit Verification
What occurs during reset, power fluctuation, or a transient fault?Fault model, reset strategy, noise margin, recovery testsDigital Logic Power and Reliability

Minimum evidence package for an eventual child article: precise definitions and assumptions; at least one representative circuit, Boolean expression, state model, or RTL example; a traceable worked exercise; identified implementation limitations; and verification questions that do not confuse mathematical proof, behavioral simulation, timing analysis, and physical testing.

Suggested Study Pathways

Foundational logic learners: start with the Digital Logic overview, then Boolean Algebra for Digital Circuits, Logic Gates and Gate-Level Design, Truth Tables and Logic Minimization, and Multiplexers, Decoders, and Encoders. Work through a truth-table-to-circuit example before considering sequential design. The neighboring Binary Logic summary provides complementary mathematical background.

Datapath and processor designers: follow Binary Adders and Arithmetic Circuits, Sequential Logic and State Elements, and Counters and Register Transfers, then compare these components with the broader Microarchitecture and Instruction Set Architecture summaries. A register-transfer operation is a circuit behavior; an instruction is a programmer-visible abstraction.

Controller and embedded-system designers: study Finite-State Machine Design, Clocking and Timing Analysis, Clock-Domain Crossing and Metastability, and Digital Circuit Verification. Use complete traces for reset, input pulses, simultaneous events, and unexpected transitions. Refer to State Machines for general formal models.

RTL, FPGA, and hardware implementation practitioners: read Hardware Description Languages, Logic Synthesis and Technology Mapping, FPGA Digital Logic Design, and Clocking and Timing Analysis. Compare source-level readability with synthesized resource cost and place-and-route behavior; avoid treating a source file as the final hardware configuration.

Verification, power, and reliability teams: integrate Digital Circuit Verification with Digital Logic Power and Reliability, Clock-Domain Crossing and Metastability, and Truth Tables and Logic Minimization. The objective is to uncover gaps between the intended function, the implemented state and timing behavior, and the device's physical operating environment.

Instructional progression: demonstrate logical correctness first, trace the behavior of state under a clock second, apply physical timing and implementation limits third, and evaluate verification evidence fourth. Many advanced design failures occur when a later step is assumed by evidence from an earlier one.
