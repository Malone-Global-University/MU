Scope and the Central Timing Distinction

A bus timing discipline specifies when an initiator presents a request, how a target recognizes it, when transferred information may be sampled, and what counts as completion. A synchronous interface uses a defined clock relationship to recognize transfer events. An asynchronous interface need not use a shared sampling clock and must therefore communicate progress with transitions, acknowledgments, or other timing and completion rules. A handshake is a coordination method, not a synonym for asynchronous signaling: modern synchronous fabrics use handshakes extensively.

These distinctions determine which circuits are legal, how long a slow target can delay an initiator, how performance is measured, and how signals pass between clock domains. A common mistake is to classify a protocol as asynchronous because it contains a READY or ACK signal. In AXI, for example, READY and VALID are sampled together on a clock edge; in a four-phase asynchronous request/acknowledge protocol, participants communicate progress without that common edge. The two arrangements can express similar logical negotiations but require different physical implementations and verification assumptions.

This Level 5 specialist article focuses on transfer coordination, wait states, backpressure, timing closure, and safe clock-domain crossing. Full arbitration policies, protocol transaction ordering, and electrical signal integrity belong to adjacent Bus Systems specialists. Examples are deliberately simplified and identify the assumptions needed for each timing conclusion.

Vocabulary: Clock, Transfer, Beat, Handshake, and Completion

A clock cycle is a time interval between corresponding clock edges. A transfer beat is one protocol-defined accepted unit of address, control, or data; a transaction may span multiple beats and include separate response phases. A handshake event is the condition by which both endpoints agree that a defined unit has been accepted. In a ready/valid channel, that event is the rising edge at which both signals are high. In an asynchronous channel, the event is identified by the protocol's signal transitions and acknowledgment state.

Acceptance is not necessarily end-to-end completion. An accepted write request may still be queued; a read address may be accepted long before the return-data handshake; and a bridge may accept traffic before the downstream target responds. An interface can therefore be busy while completing zero useful payload beats, or can accept one beat per edge while older transactions remain outstanding. A useful timing trace records request assertion, acceptance, data availability, final response, and any ordering or visibility requirements as separate events.

Synchronous Buses and Shared Sampling Edges

In a fully synchronous channel, the source and destination agree which active edges recognize information. The source must present its signal values early enough that they satisfy the destination's setup requirement and hold them long enough after the edge to satisfy the hold requirement. The shared timebase may come from a single distributed clock, related clocks from one source, or a protocol-defined clock-forwarding arrangement; a common nominal frequency alone does not guarantee a usable phase relationship.

The advantage is a precise cycle model: an engineer can count edges, pipeline registers can be placed to meet timing, and accepted transfers can be characterized in beats per second. The disadvantage is that longer combinational paths, clock skew, board delay, and variable-target readiness must be addressed explicitly. A slow destination does not become fast merely because the bus carries a high-frequency clock. Clocked interfaces commonly solve this through wait states, credit limits, registered control, and buffering rather than pretending every device always accepts a transfer.

Setup, Hold, Clock Skew, and Timing Margins

A register-to-register synchronous path must satisfy both a maximum-delay and a minimum-delay constraint. For a simple same-frequency, same-edge path, the setup check is approximately Tclk ≥ Tcq(max) + Tlogic(max) + Tsetup + Tskew_penalty + Tuncertainty, where the skew term depends on how arrival times are defined. The hold check asks whether new data can arrive too soon after the receiving edge; adding pipeline registers without reviewing minimum delays can introduce hold violations even as setup paths shorten.

Physical timing closure therefore requires actual library delays, placement, routing, clock insertion, and uncertainty constraints. Neither a READY signal nor a simulation-only clock period overrides these electrical requirements. The relevant question is not simply whether an RTL process uses posedge clk, but whether every sampled path—including payload, valid, status, and reset release—obeys the interface's timing model under the implemented clock relationship.

Wait States and Variable-Length Synchronous Transfers

A synchronous bus can be designed for fixed-latency service, but many real targets vary in response time. A wait-state protocol lets the requester retain the operation while the target delays acceptance or completion. In a simple peripheral interface, a request may enter an access phase and remain there until the target asserts a ready indicator. Additional wait cycles do not create new transfers: they extend the same pending operation. The address and applicable control fields ordinarily must remain stable while the target is being asked to complete that operation.

Wait states reduce effective bandwidth because transfer opportunities elapse without a completed beat. They also require endpoint state machines to distinguish request presented from request consumed. If the requester increments its address every clock irrespective of ready status, it can skip an access or send incorrect data. If the target repeats a side effect on every wait cycle rather than at the defined completion event, a single register write may accidentally execute several times.

Handshake Protocols as Coordination Contracts

A handshake is a contract that answers four operational questions: who may present information, who may defer it, how acceptance is detected, and when either endpoint can move on. Ready/valid makes availability and acceptance separate signals; request/acknowledge commonly describes a command followed by explicit recognition; credit-based flow control advertises future receiving capacity; and a fixed-timing interface may infer acceptance from the prescribed edge without an individual response signal.

These are not mutually exclusive topologies. A packet-based fabric may use a credit scheme between neighboring routers, ready/valid within a router pipeline, and a completion packet for the original transaction. Likewise, a synchronous processor bus can cross a clock boundary using an asynchronous FIFO while preserving an ordinary synchronous handshake at each external port. The timing protocol should be assessed at each interface boundary, rather than attaching a single label to the entire system.

Ready/Valid: The Acceptance Rule

For a synchronous ready/valid channel, the source asserts VALID when a meaningful payload is available; the destination asserts READY when it can accept a payload. The transfer occurs on an active clock edge for which VALID && READY is true. This rule is checked per edge, not as a continuously lasting high level that transfers arbitrary numbers of items between clocks. A source may present back-to-back payloads on consecutive edges if both sides sustain the handshake.

The source must keep the offered payload and VALID stable while VALID=1 and READY=0, subject to the exact protocol's rules. When a handshake occurs, the source may retire that offered beat and update the next beat for a later edge. The destination may assert READY before it sees VALID; doing so often enables immediate acceptance. The source must not depend on the remote READY being high before asserting its own VALID in an AXI-style channel, because mutual waiting can prevent any handshake.

Backpressure and Stable Payload Requirements

Backpressure occurs when the receiver deasserts READY or otherwise withholds capacity. If the source has a pending beat, it must retain that same beat until acknowledgment. A correct waveform therefore shows repeated cycles of unchanged data during a stall, followed by precisely one acceptance when READY finally asserts. An upstream producer may need its own buffer so it can tolerate backpressure without losing incoming data.

The rule is especially important when a register controls a peripheral with side effects or when a stream comprises uniquely numbered packets. If a producer changes the payload while stalled, the receiver can receive an item different from the one offered. If a producer counts both a presentation and a later ready edge as separate transfers, duplicates appear. Robust designs define the transfer predicate in one place and use it to update counters, pointers, queue occupancy, and externally visible side effects.

Deadlock, Livelock, and Handshake Dependencies

A ready/valid protocol should avoid circular conditions such as “source will assert VALID only after READY” combined with “destination will assert READY only after VALID.” For AXI, the source must establish VALID independently of the destination's READY, and must hold it until acceptance. A destination may choose to wait for VALID before asserting READY; the important restriction is that the two sides cannot both be designed to wait for the other first. Across multiple channels, dependencies can be more complex, because read requests, write data, and responses have different legal ordering constraints.

Deadlock means no participant can make progress even though work remains; livelock means state changes continue without useful completion. Simulation that tests only continuous READY=1 can miss both defects. A sound design exercise introduces arbitrary stalls, simultaneous requests, reset during backpressure, delayed responses, and bursts of saturated queues. A liveness proof always needs assumptions about the downstream endpoint eventually providing service; an indefinitely unready legal target cannot be forced to accept data by protocol logic alone.

Registered Interfaces, Skid Buffers, and Elastic Pipelines

Combinational READY paths can grow very long when many pipeline stages directly propagate downstream backpressure upstream. A register slice cuts a timing path by registering some interface signals; a skid buffer can preserve an item already launched when a newly registered downstream stall takes effect. A correct elastic stage tracks both occupancy and whether its stored data are accepted. Registering only READY, without accounting for one additional beat in flight, can overflow the receiving stage.

Buffering introduces architectural trade-offs. A depth-one stage can be enough for some low-traffic protocols but may insert bubbles if it cannot refill and drain on the same cycle. A properly designed two-entry elastic buffer can absorb a late stall and permit consecutive handshakes under sustained demand. These performance properties are implementation-dependent rather than guarantees implied by the words “pipeline register.” Published AMD AXI register-slice descriptions distinguish modes that preserve back-to-back throughput from modes that trade that capability for simpler register boundaries.

Throughput, Initiation Interval, and Round-Trip Latency

If a W-byte synchronous data channel runs at f clock edges per second and accepts one beat per edge, the ideal one-direction payload rate is W × f bytes per second. More generally, throughput = W × accepted_edges / observed_time; a 200 MHz, 8-byte channel has an ideal 1.6 GB/s payload ceiling, but achieves 0.8 GB/s if exactly half the eligible edges actually accept data. The ratio of accepted to total eligible edges is the handshake utilization.

Latency is a different measure: it may be several cycles from presentation to acknowledgment even when a pipelined interface accepts a new beat every cycle. A full request/acknowledge round trip can constrain an interface that permits only one outstanding item. For long operations, increasing buffering or the number of outstanding transactions may improve throughput without reducing the latency of any individual operation. Peak edge rate should never be represented as an observed system-transfer result without counting handshakes.

AMBA APB: Setup, Access, and PREADY

Arm's AMBA APB is an instructive synchronous, phase-based peripheral interface. A transfer passes through SETUP, where the selected peripheral and relevant address/control fields are presented, and ACCESS, marked by PENABLE. PREADY controls whether ACCESS completes. With PREADY low, the initiator remains in the ACCESS state, preserving the ongoing transfer; on a rising edge with PSEL, PENABLE, and PREADY high, that access completes. The chosen target can then be deselected or a following transfer can enter a new setup phase.

APB's compulsory setup phase means that even a zero-wait-state access generally takes a setup cycle and an access cycle. Its strength is modest peripheral logic complexity, not a promise that every clock can carry a new independent access. The PREADY convention is an example of a clocked handshake, not an asynchronous acknowledgment. Correct monitors observe the completion edge and avoid counting each ACCESS wait cycle as a separate transaction. See the Arm APB specification for exact signal-stability and error-response requirements.

AMBA AXI: Independent Ready/Valid Channels

Arm AXI uses distinct synchronous ready/valid channels for write address (AW), write data (W), write response (B), read address (AR), and read data (R). A transfer on one channel is accepted independently of another channel's handshake, subject to the transaction-level dependency rules specified by the protocol. For example, a write-address handshake does not itself prove that every write-data beat has been accepted or that the target has returned its write response. This separation supports pipelining and overlapping transactions.

An AXI channel must satisfy its own VALID/READY stability rules. Writing an RTL module that consumes AW and W only when both are valid in the same cycle can be incorrect when those events are legitimately decoupled. Similarly, gating a response acceptance on an unrelated request READY may create a circular dependency. Proper design keeps per-channel state, associates responses with requests as required, and distinguishes the last data beat from actual completion signaling.

WISHBONE: Strobe, Cycle, and Termination

The WISHBONE Classic cycle demonstrates a different synchronous handshake vocabulary. A requester asserts cycle and strobe controls to indicate an active bus operation; an addressed target reports termination, commonly through ACK, with optional error or retry variants defined by the applicable revision. A requester must keep its operation valid until the prescribed termination is observed. Certain WISHBONE modes also support pipelined acceptance, with a separate stall concept rather than only classic request termination.

Do not equate an ACK level with an AXI READY level merely because both participate in transfer coordination. Their permitted timing, repetition, payload association, and state transitions differ. A protocol bridge must translate complete semantics—for instance, whether an accepted request is still awaiting final response—not simply rename signals. The engineer should select a specific WISHBONE revision and Classic versus Pipelined rules before relying on a timing diagram.

Asynchronous Buses Without a Shared Sampling Clock

An asynchronous bus does not assume that the endpoints share a clock edge that determines every transfer. A requester may indicate that information is available; the receiving endpoint may acknowledge after a variable delay; and the requester may restore signals or toggle a control to prepare for another operation. The relevant completion event is governed by the signaling protocol and physical stability assumptions, not by a global integer count of cycles. Some systems still use local clocks internally; the interface between them can nevertheless be asynchronous.

Potential benefits include flexible device timing, reduced need for global clock distribution, and compatibility with independently timed participants. Costs include more complicated delay assumptions, transition-detection circuitry, acknowledgment paths, and verification. Asynchronous should not be understood to mean “timing requirements disappear.” Completion detection, pulse widths, wire delays, setup at receiving storage elements, and safe data capture remain essential, even though their contracts differ from those of a common-clock bus.

Four-Phase Request/Acknowledge Handshakes

A common four-phase, return-to-zero handshake proceeds as follows: (1) the source puts stable data on the interface and raises REQ; (2) the receiver captures or accepts the item according to the interface's bundled-data assumptions and raises ACK; (3) the source lowers REQ; and (4) the receiver lowers ACK. Only after the return-to-zero sequence can the same handshake loop begin another operation. The order establishes explicit request and acknowledgment ownership, but the delay between phases need not be an integer number of shared clock cycles.

The data-stability contract matters. A receiver must not sample a transient bundle simply because one bit of REQ changed. In bundled-data implementations, the control transition must be delayed or constrained relative to data-path stabilization. Four-phase signaling may be intuitive to verify but incurs several control transitions per item, which can limit peak throughput compared with pipelined or transition-based alternatives.

Two-Phase Toggle Handshakes

In a two-phase, transition-signaled protocol, successive events can be indicated by toggles rather than a pulse that always rises and returns low. The source toggles REQ to announce a new item; the receiver later changes ACK to the matching phase to indicate acceptance. A later new item is identified by another toggle, regardless of whether that transition is rising or falling. Thus both polarities carry meaning, and an idle-high control value is not automatically an active request.

Toggle protocols can reduce return-to-zero transitions, but they require careful treatment of initialization, reset, duplicate transitions, and phase agreement. If one side resets its phase bit while the other retains state, an old transition can be misinterpreted as a new transaction. Readers should distinguish this event-oriented mechanism from a conventional synchronous READY/VALID channel, in which a continuously high VALID can represent different consecutive beats on successive accepted clock edges.

Bundled Data, Completion Detection, and Delay Assumptions

Some asynchronous designs use bundled-data signaling: ordinary data wires are accompanied by a request signal whose timing implies the data have already stabilized. That implication is valid only if the implementation meets a stated relative-delay requirement. Other asynchronous circuit families employ delay-insensitive or quasi-delay-insensitive encoding and completion detection, often at a cost in wire count or logic. These design families cannot be interchanged by merely replacing a clock with request/acknowledge bits.

In educational diagrams, a receiver is often shown capturing data immediately after REQ rises. A real implementation needs evidence that setup and hold or completion-detection requirements are satisfied under variation in placement, process, voltage, and temperature. A handshake manages when an operation is agreed upon; it is not, by itself, proof that the accompanying analog signal transition has settled or that all individual payload bits represent the same logical item.

Synchronous Handshake Versus Asynchronous Handshake

Both mechanisms coordinate availability and acceptance, but their event models differ. In synchronous ready/valid, simultaneous high levels at a sampling edge constitute a beat; holding both high over two successive edges can accept two beats, assuming the source presents the corresponding sequence. In four-phase asynchronous request/acknowledge, holding both request and acknowledge high does not repeat acceptance: the complete edge sequence must progress before a new operation is represented.

The difference has practical RTL consequences. A counter driven by valid && ready belongs inside the specified clocked process. A four-phase asynchronous endpoint must recognize transitions and return-to-zero states without assuming that two controls high on an arbitrary clock edge form a new transfer. Treating one type as the other can produce duplicate actions, lost packets, or indefinite waiting in a bridge.

Clock-Domain Crossing Is a Separate Design Problem

Clock-domain crossing (CDC) occurs when a signal enters storage driven by a clock that lacks a guaranteed safe sampling relationship with the source clock. Two clocks can both be present and nominally synchronous in frequency yet have an unconstrained phase relationship; conversely, separate clocks produced in a controlled, phase-related way may permit a valid synchronous crossing if the implementation and timing constraints establish that relationship. The safe architecture follows the actual clock relationship, not superficial signal names.

CDC failure is not fundamentally a protocol-arbitration problem. It can manifest as metastability, missed pulses, inconsistent multi-bit samples, or broken reset sequencing. Good architectures place an explicit CDC block between independent domains and keep all source-side and destination-side handshakes local to their respective clocks. CDC verification complements, rather than replaces, simulation of transaction-level correctness.

Metastability and Mean Time Between Failures

When an asynchronous signal changes near a destination register's sampling edge, the register can enter a temporary metastable state before settling to a stable logic value. Digital simulation normally cannot establish the analog resolution behavior of such an event. Synchronizer design reduces the probability that unresolved metastability reaches downstream logic; it does not make the probability mathematically zero. Mean time between failures (MTBF) depends on implementation technology, settling time, destination frequency, and input transition activity.

Adding a properly placed second synchronizer stage commonly improves reliability by giving the first stage more time to settle, but tools must recognize the chain and preserve its intended physical implementation. If several independently synchronized control bits are later decoded together, each bit can still be observed in a different cycle. That is a coherency problem, not merely a single-bit metastability problem. Device-specific design and timing guidance should be consulted for the actual MTBF target.

Two-Flop Synchronizers: Appropriate Uses and Limits

A familiar single-bit CDC technique places two destination-clocked flip-flops in series. The asynchronous control signal enters the first stage; the second stage samples the first stage's output, reducing the chance of propagating a metastable transition. This pattern is appropriate for selected level-like status or control signals when the source holds the value long enough to be observed and the resulting latency is acceptable.

It is not a general replacement for a multi-bit bus protocol. If a 16-bit value changes and each bit has a separate two-flop chain, destination logic may see a mixture of old and new bit values because the bits resolve and arrive on different edges. A narrow pulse may also be missed if it never overlaps a receiving edge. Reliable pulse, event, and bus transfers need additional protocol design—such as toggle signaling, held-data handshakes, or asynchronous FIFOs.

Pulse Transfer, Event Transfer, and Toggle Synchronization

A source-clock pulse that is only one short cycle wide may disappear between two destination sampling edges when the destination clock is slower or phase-unrelated. Possible solutions include stretching a pulse under a proven frequency assumption, converting pulses to toggles, or using a request/acknowledge event synchronizer that holds state until the receiving domain acknowledges it. The correct technique depends on whether multiple events can arrive before one acknowledgment returns.

A toggle scheme can represent individual events without relying on pulse width, but if the source toggles twice before the destination detects the first transition, the two changes can cancel in the sampled state. That is why rate constraints or acknowledgment feedback are necessary. In a fully lossless event stream, an event counter or dual-clock FIFO may be more appropriate than an unacknowledged toggle. Verification must cover minimum spacing between events, back-to-back pulses, reset phase, and unequal clock rates.

Multi-Bit Bus Transfer and Data Coherency

For a multi-bit payload, the goal is not merely to avoid a metastable bit; it is to capture one coherent value. A handshake-based bus synchronizer can make the source hold all payload bits stable until the destination has sampled them under a synchronized request indication and returned an acknowledgment. Such a design makes stability, arrival, and release explicit. The data bundle itself is not necessarily passed through independent synchronizers; instead, control timing protects the sampling interval.

Another approach is a properly designed asynchronous FIFO, which allows sustained item streams while keeping each endpoint's data interface synchronous to its own clock. Vendor CDC primitives can embody these contracts, but the caller must obey their interface restrictions, including when the source may present another item and when the output is declared valid. A generic “synchronizer” label gives too little information to establish coherency or flow-control behavior.

Asynchronous FIFOs and Independent Clock Domains

A dual-clock or asynchronous FIFO is a buffer with write-side control driven by one clock and read-side control driven by another independent clock. The data storage is shared through a designed memory interface while the pointers and fullness indicators cross domains through specialized synchronization logic. The write-side can accept data while the read-side is temporarily stopped, up to the buffer's true capacity; once full, it must apply backpressure or reject writes according to its contract.

An asynchronous FIFO is more than a pair of synchronizers attached to the ends of an ordinary register array. Its correctness depends on pointer encoding, full/empty detection, synchronizer constraints, simultaneous read/write conditions, reset coordination, and latency of status updates. Because the pointer information takes time to cross clock boundaries, newly freed space might not be immediately visible to the writer and newly written data might not be immediately visible to the reader. Throughput must therefore be analyzed together with buffer depth and flag latency.

Gray-Coded Pointers and Full/Empty Detection

Many dual-clock FIFO implementations use Gray-coded read and write pointer representations, in which only one encoded bit changes between adjacent counts. Synchronizing such pointers lowers the risk that several concurrently switching binary bits are sampled as an unrelated value. Local binary pointers may still be used for memory addressing; encoded copies are transported into the opposite domain for comparison. This technique requires proper constraints on relative bit arrival times and carefully defined full/empty logic.

A read domain can declare empty when the synchronized write progress indicates no unread entries, while the write domain can declare full when its next write position would overtake unread data according to the chosen pointer scheme. Common implementations include an extra wrap bit to distinguish identical low-order addresses after different numbers of laps. Designers should use a verified FIFO architecture rather than inventing pointer comparison expressions from an informal diagram; off-by-one errors can silently overwrite data or report nonexistent bytes.

Reset Assertion, Reset Release, and Domain Reinitialization

Reset is a timing and protocol event, not merely a convenient RTL branch. An asynchronous reset can be asserted without a local clock, but releasing it near a sampling edge can violate recovery/removal requirements or let different state elements resume inconsistently. Many designs therefore use asynchronous assertion combined with clock-synchronous deassertion within each domain. An asynchronous FIFO must bring write/read pointers and synchronization pipelines into a mutually consistent startup state before ordinary traffic resumes.

Reset while traffic is pending raises a second issue: what happens to an accepted but not yet completed transfer? A bridge might need to discard an internal request, generate a documented error, or wait until both sides are safely restarted. Merely clearing VALID on one side can strand a target or lose acknowledgment state. Correct designs specify reset scope, which outputs are trustworthy during recovery, the startup latency, and whether software must reinitialize affected devices.

Clock Enables, Clock Gating, and Temporarily Stopped Clocks

A handshake assumes that the relevant endpoint can eventually observe the event that advances it. If a receiver's clock is gated or stopped, an ordinary synchronous READY/VALID handshake cannot complete because there is no receiving edge. A low-power design may first quiesce the interface, park any outstanding work, and then gate the clock; or it may use an always-on control island and a clock-domain bridge to resume safely. A stopped-clock condition is not equivalent to an asynchronous four-phase endpoint that continues to detect transitions.

Wakeup circuits need explicit guarantees about when clock stability is restored, when reset has finished, and when READY may be asserted again. If an upstream source continuously holds VALID and its payload during the pause, it must remain within buffer and watchdog limits. Systems with deadline-sensitive functions should budget wakeup and CDC synchronization delays, not only nominal active-clock transfer latency.

Clock-Domain Bridges in Larger Interconnects

System-on-chip designs often contain a fast processor fabric, a slower peripheral fabric, and independently clocked media, storage, or accelerator domains. A clock-domain bridge terminates one timing contract and reconstructs another on the far side. It may contain request queues, response queues, ID tracking, error propagation, data-width conversion, and registered clock-boundary control. The interface can remain logically the same memory-mapped protocol at both ends while the physical timing is handled by the bridge.

Bridges should be analyzed as stateful endpoints, not just wires. They can accept a request before a slower target executes it, and they can bottleneck throughput despite high nominal frequency on the fast side. Separate read and write paths may have different queue depths, and responses can require their own independent crossing. A full-system timing diagram should label which edge accepted the request at each boundary and where true completion is reported.

Buffering, Width Conversion, and Clock Ratios

Suppose an 8-byte data interface at 200 MHz feeds a 4-byte interface at 100 MHz. Their theoretical one-direction limits are 1.6 GB/s and 0.4 GB/s respectively, before protocol overhead. A bridge can collect, split, or merge beats, but it cannot sustain the fast-side peak into the slow-side link without accumulating unbounded data. A finite FIFO can absorb bursts; thereafter the bridge must exert backpressure. The clock ratio determines how quickly the buffer fills under sustained mismatched rates.

Transfer size, alignment, and byte-valid markers also matter. A split write of one 8-byte source beat may require two 4-byte destination beats, and a partial write may require different byte strobes. If a target returns responses per transaction rather than per converted beat, the bridge must not prematurely acknowledge completion. Bandwidth arithmetic therefore belongs next to protocol ordering and buffer-occupancy accounting.

Control-Path Versus Data-Path Crossing

Some systems separate a low-rate control register interface from a high-rate streaming data interface. A single status level can sometimes cross with a two-stage synchronizer, while high-volume payloads are better served by an asynchronous FIFO. A configuration write that affects several related bits can need an atomic update handshake so that the destination never observes a half-old, half-new parameter set. Reset and start/stop events may require explicit acknowledgments even when the configuration payload changes infrequently.

It is useful to classify every crossing as a level, pulse, event count, multi-bit configuration, request/response exchange, or sustained stream. Each class has a different loss, duplication, latency, and coherency hazard. The classification must then be matched to an implementation with known reset behavior and throughput limits. Treating every crossing as a generic synchronization problem obscures the most important functional requirements.

Performance Limits: Handshake Occupancy and Queueing

Counting transfer-accepted edges is a clearer performance measure than counting asserted READY cycles alone. A receiver can be ready for many cycles with no valid offered payload, yielding zero data throughput. Conversely, a heavily loaded source can hold VALID continuously while READY stalls, yielding a low accepted-edge fraction. A useful counter set includes valid-only cycles, ready-only cycles, accepted beats, average queue occupancy, full/empty cycles, and response latency distribution.

As a first-order capacity check, Little's law connects outstanding work, average latency, and completion rate: N = λ × L. If a nonpipelined transaction takes eight destination cycles from request acceptance to final response, one outstanding transaction can sustain at most roughly one completion every eight such cycles. Multiple outstanding transactions may overlap those delays where the underlying service can pipeline. This is a concurrency observation, not evidence that every implementation scales linearly with queue depth.

Error Responses, Timeouts, and Lost Acknowledgments

A handshake protocol must define how an operation ends when the target cannot serve it. Some interconnects return error completion; others may allow indefinite backpressure unless an external watchdog intervenes. An ACK line that never returns low can block a four-phase loop; a READY line that remains low indefinitely can keep a synchronous payload stalled. Neither condition is automatically a data-integrity failure, but both are availability risks that require system-level policy.

Timeouts should be placed at the appropriate layer. If a legitimate device operation takes a variable amount of time, imposing an arbitrary cycle limit can create false failures. When a timeout is needed, the system should specify whether the abandoned request might still execute downstream, whether retry is safe, and how late responses are handled. Retrying a side-effecting write without knowing whether it committed can duplicate actions; idempotence and transaction identifiers matter as much as the detection of delay.

Electrical Constraints and Signal Integrity

Even a logically correct handshake can fail across long traces, poorly terminated lines, or mismatched clock/data delays. Synchronous high-speed buses must account for propagation time, clock skew, edge rate, reflections, jitter, and receiver margins. Asynchronous bundled-data protocols likewise require data to settle before the controlling acknowledgment relationship permits capture. Handshake signaling does not compensate for crosstalk or insufficient voltage margin.

For board-level interfaces, designers may use source-synchronous strobes, differential signaling, retiming, or link-level training. These mechanisms should not be called “asynchronous” simply because they do not use the CPU's master clock. A transferred clock or recovered clock may define the edge relationship within a link. Physical assumptions are tied to the actual technology and belong alongside the logical protocol's timing statement.

Security and Isolation at Timing Boundaries

Backpressure and shared handshake capacity can reveal contention to other participants. A low-priority requester might infer activity from access latency, or an untrusted endpoint might hold resources occupied by withholding expected progress. Such observations are not always security defects, but they become relevant when traffic from different trust domains shares arbiters, queues, or response paths. Interconnect isolation therefore considers timing channels, resource quotas, timeout handling, and whether malformed participants can block otherwise independent agents.

CDC blocks also have security implications when reset, privilege changes, or access-permission updates cross domains. A delayed enable or stale permission must not authorize work after revocation. The correct strategy is not to claim that a synchronizer enforces authorization; rather, permission checks, control-state handshakes, and recovery sequencing must form a coherent system-level contract. Verification should examine both confidentiality and availability while preserving the hardware's required service semantics.

Verification: Safety Properties and Liveness Assumptions

For a ready/valid channel, a key safety property is that once VALID is asserted without READY, VALID and the payload remain stable until a handshake or a precisely defined reset exception. Another property is that queue occupancy never exceeds physical capacity and never becomes negative. Monitors should count exactly one beat per asserted VALID/READY sampling edge. For APB, PSEL and PENABLE define the active phase, and completion must be recognized only at its proper edge with PREADY asserted.

For four-phase asynchronous handshakes, a protocol checker should require the legal REQ/ACK transition order and prohibit reissuing a request until the return-to-zero sequence is complete. For CDC designs, static analysis and implementation review should identify unsynchronized crossings, multi-bit reconvergence, unsafe reset release, and incorrectly constrained synchronizer paths. Formal or dynamic liveness checks must state assumptions such as the downstream participant eventually acknowledging; otherwise a legally stalled interface cannot be proven to complete.

Measurement, Logic Analyzers, and Diagnostic Practice

When investigating a stalled interface, begin by identifying the actual acceptance predicate. On AXI, probe VALID and READY for the relevant channel, together with its payload and response association. On APB, probe PSEL, PENABLE, PREADY, address, and write/read fields. On an asynchronous request/acknowledge interface, probe transition order and data-stability relationships, remembering that ordinary synchronous trace sampling may miss short transitions.

Next determine whether the root cause is absent offered work, downstream backpressure, circular handshake dependencies, a clock not running, CDC status delay, reset, or physical errors. A trace from one side of a bridge cannot by itself show acceptance on the other side. Record both interface clocks and both endpoint conditions, count accepted items at each boundary, and reconcile them with queued outstanding work. Performance claims should use matching timebases and declare whether they count payload bytes, addresses, or completed transactions.

Design Selection Framework

Choose a timing arrangement by first defining clock relationships, allowable latency, target transfer rate, burstiness, and the cost of buffering. Then classify the crossing's information type: persistent control level, infrequent event, coherent configuration word, or sustained data stream. A single-clock ready/valid channel is often effective for regular on-chip streams; APB is useful where simple peripheral access dominates; asynchronous FIFOs handle persistent traffic between independent clocks; and explicit request/acknowledge transfer can suit infrequent multi-bit control updates.

There is no single fastest handshake architecture independent of implementation. A four-phase asynchronous exchange can be adequate for infrequent controls but wasteful for sustained bulk transfers. A deeply pipelined ready/valid fabric can provide high throughput yet demand buffer resources and careful deadlock avoidance. A large FIFO can hide clock-rate bursts yet increase area, startup latency, and reset complexity. The sound decision balances correctness, timing closure, utilization, and recoverability under explicit workload assumptions.

Worked Example 1 — A Ready/Valid Stall Trace

Assume a synchronous channel where payload A is available starting before edge 1. The source asserts VALID continuously while waiting; the destination initially deasserts READY. Acceptance is evaluated at each rising edge. This constructed trace records the sampled values:

Rising edgeVALIDREADYOffered payloadAccepted?

The source must not present B on edge 2 because A has not been accepted. After the handshake at edge 3, it may present B for edge 4. The two high signals sampled at edge 3 do not authorize a second beat at an arbitrary instant between edges. The receiver's READY at edge 5 alone is not a transfer. This table is a general ready/valid teaching model; reset and channel-specific AXI requirements add further constraints.

Worked Example 2 — Backpressure and Effective Data Rate

A fictional 128-bit channel runs at 250 MHz and can accept one 16-byte beat per rising edge. Its ideal capacity is 16 bytes × 250,000,000 edges/s = 4,000,000,000 bytes/s, or 4.0 GB/s in decimal units. In a measurement window of 10,000 eligible edges, VALID is high on 8,000 edges but only 6,000 edges satisfy both VALID and READY.

Delivered payload is 6,000 × 16 = 96,000 bytes. The window lasts 10,000 / 250,000,000 = 40 microseconds, so measured accepted payload rate is 96,000 / 40 µs = 2.4 GB/s. Handshake utilization is 60%, not 80%; the source's VALID occupancy alone overstates the useful transfer share. The difference may be caused by backpressure or by READY/VALID timing overlap. This is an interface payload calculation, not an end-to-end application benchmark.

Worked Example 3 — APB Setup and Wait States

Assume a peripheral requires a SETUP edge followed by two ACCESS wait edges and then completes on the next ACCESS edge. A simplified sampling trace is:

EdgePSELPENABLEPREADYInterpretation
110Not relevantSETUP

The single access occupies four protocol clock cycles in this illustrative timeline, including one setup and three access cycles. At 100 MHz, four 10-ns periods correspond to 40 ns of occupied bus time in the chosen convention. There is one completed register transaction, not three. Its address, direction, and relevant write data remain stable throughout the delayed ACCESS phase. A zero-wait-state version would instead complete on the first ACCESS edge, after the mandatory SETUP interval.

Worked Example 4 — Full-Duplex Ready/Valid Independence

Consider an AXI read where the address channel accepts a request on edge 5 and the data channel accepts its first read beat on edge 11. The address handshake is ARVALID && ARREADY at edge 5. The return beat is separately recognized by RVALID && RREADY at edge 11. If RVALID becomes high at edge 9 while RREADY is low at edges 9 and 10, the read-data payload must remain available according to the channel stability rules until the edge-11 handshake.

A performance monitor that logs only the request acceptance at edge 5 cannot state the read's data-return latency or completion. The target also cannot interpret a long interval with ARREADY high and ARVALID low as repeated read requests. For burst reads, each data beat has its own R-channel acceptance, and the protocol supplies fields identifying completion of the burst. Channel-specific event counting prevents accidental duplicates and clarifies where congestion occurred.

Worked Example 5 — Four-Phase Asynchronous Request/Acknowledge

Suppose an asynchronous source sends an 8-bit status value. It first holds the value stable and asserts REQ. The destination waits until it can safely capture the value and then asserts ACK. After seeing ACK, the source lowers REQ; after detecting that release, the receiver lowers ACK. Only then can the source begin the next four-phase operation. This sequence can be expressed as:

PhaseREQACKResponsibility
1 — request10Source holds a coherent payload
2 — acknowledge11Destination has accepted the payload
3 — release request01Source recognizes acknowledgment
4 — return to idle00Destination permits next request

Unlike a synchronous handshake, this protocol does not count a new item on every hypothetical clock edge when both controls are high. Its timing depends on physical transition propagation and the bundled-data stability requirement. A design must state when the value can be changed again and how a reset returns both phase controls to agreement.

Worked Example 6 — A Pulse That a Slower Clock Can Miss

A fast producer creates a pulse lasting 5 ns. The independent receiving clock has a 20-ns period. There is no guarantee that one of its sampling edges lies within the 5-ns pulse; a phase arrangement can place the whole pulse between adjacent receiving edges. Routing the pulse through two destination flip-flops does not solve this basic event-width problem: the first flip-flop may never observe it high.

A held request with acknowledgment, a toggle detector with an event-rate guarantee, or a small event FIFO can preserve the notification. The correct method depends on whether multiple events can be outstanding. If another pulse occurs before the previous toggle has been observed, an unacknowledged toggle may change twice and return to its initial state, losing the count. Pulse-stretching is only safe when pulse width and phase relationships are proven for every relevant operating mode.

Worked Example 7 — Multi-Bit Reconvergence Failure

Imagine a 4-bit binary counter changing from 0111 to 1000 in the source domain. All four bits change logically as part of one count update, but they do not traverse independent synchronizers with identical propagation and resolution times. If the destination synchronizes each bit separately, it could briefly observe a mixture such as 0000 or 1111. Neither represents a genuine intermediate source count.

The risk is not eliminated by labeling each synchronizer as metastability-safe. To transfer a coherent configuration word, the source can hold it while a synchronized request crosses and wait for an acknowledgment before changing it. For continuing sequences of count or data values, a dual-clock FIFO may be appropriate. Gray coding can help with pointer synchronization when only one bit is intended to change per step, but it does not magically make arbitrary multi-bit payloads safe across clock domains.

Worked Example 8 — Buffer Fill Under Mismatched Clock Rates

A hypothetical FIFO connects a writer producing one 8-byte item every 5 ns (200 million items/s) to a reader consuming one item every 10 ns (100 million items/s). When both operate continuously, backlog grows at 200 − 100 = 100 million items/s. From empty, an idealized 64-entry FIFO would therefore approach saturation in about 64 / 100 million = 640 ns, ignoring synchronization delays and integer scheduling.

Afterward, the writer must stall or drop items according to the explicit contract; the FIFO cannot continuously absorb the excess. The sustainable payload rate is at most 100 million × 8 bytes = 0.8 GB/s under these assumptions. A deeper FIFO provides a longer transient burst cushion, not a larger downstream service capacity. Real full flags may assert conservatively because cross-domain pointer information is delayed, so the exact usable burst window requires the implementation's flag semantics.

Worked Example 9 — Latency, Outstanding Requests, and Little’s Law

An in-order request bridge has a measured average round-trip time of 400 ns, while the downstream system can sustainably finish one request every 50 ns when its internal pipeline is kept full. In a simplified steady-state model, the target throughput is 20 million requests per second. Little's law then suggests N = λ × L = 20,000,000 × 400 ns = 8 outstanding requests are needed, on average, to sustain that rate.

Allowing eight outstanding requests does not guarantee the target rate: queue allocation, burst grouping, ordering rules, and service variability may demand more headroom. With only one outstanding request allowed, a serialized requester is limited to about 1 / 400 ns = 2.5 million completions/s if it waits for each response before issuing another. The example separates per-operation latency from concurrency, and assumes the target can genuinely overlap requests.

Worked Example 10 — Timing Budget for a Synchronous Interface

A simplified register-to-register path runs at 250 MHz, giving a 4.0-ns period. Suppose clock-to-Q delay is 0.4 ns, maximum logic delay 2.5 ns, setup requirement 0.3 ns, and combined skew/uncertainty allowance 0.5 ns. The required time is 0.4 + 2.5 + 0.3 + 0.5 = 3.7 ns, leaving a nominal setup slack of 0.3 ns in this illustrative model.

If an unregistered backpressure mux adds 0.6 ns to the critical path, the requirement becomes 4.3 ns, violating the 4.0-ns period by 0.3 ns. A registered-ready stage might repair setup timing, but must buffer any item that was already in flight when READY was sampled. This is why physical timing closure and lossless handshake logic must be designed together. The numbers are fictional and omit detailed hold, on-chip variation, and library-specific analyses.

Worked Example 11 — Two-Phase Toggle and Reset Consistency

Suppose a source initializes its request toggle to 0 and a receiver initializes its acknowledgment toggle to 0. The source presents one item by toggling REQ to 1; the receiver accepts the item and toggles ACK to 1. A second item can eventually be represented by toggling REQ back to 0, after the first has been acknowledged. The binary value 0 is not inherently “no request”; the change relative to the agreed phase is what represents an event.

If only the source resets to 0 while the receiver remains in phase 1, the receiver may interpret that forced transition as a second request. A reliable implementation needs a reset and synchronization contract that restores phase agreement and prevents stale data from being accepted as new. This subtlety explains why a two-phase handshake cannot be reduced to a simple high-level request signal without losing its event semantics.

Worked Example 12 — APB-to-AXI Bridge and Acceptance Boundaries

A fictional processor issues an AXI write, and an AXI-to-APB bridge accepts its address and data into a small queue. The peripheral then waits four APB access cycles before asserting PREADY. The AXI side may accept parts of the request earlier than the peripheral actually executes the register write, but the bridge must not announce a successful completion response until its protocol rules allow it and the APB access result has been determined.

Under sustained writes, the bridge's queue fills if the AXI-side request rate exceeds APB service. Backpressure can then propagate to AXI address or data channels, with each channel following its own acceptance contract. This example reveals three distinct timings: upstream acceptance, downstream peripheral completion, and upstream completion response. All three must be traceable to avoid claiming that a posted or buffered operation is already visible at the target.

Engineering Checklist for Reliable Timing Interfaces

A release review should first identify every clock and its relationship to the next domain. For each channel, record the transfer predicate, payload-stability interval, reset behavior, outstanding work limit, and treatment of delayed or failed responses. Separately document the minimum reliable pulse width or use a lossless event protocol, and identify where a multi-bit value crosses as a coherent unit. Backpressure must be tested at the very first and very last beat of a stream as well as during sustained throughput.

Timing closure must use constraints matching the real clock topology, while CDC analysis must confirm synchronizer placement and absence of unsafe multi-bit reconvergence. Verification should cover slow and stopped destinations, source-side bursts, full/empty transitions, independent resets, overlapping requests, and clock-ratio extremes. Finally, measure accepted beats and completed transactions separately, reconcile outstanding counts, and document the boundary between interface timing and actual end-to-end operation completion.
