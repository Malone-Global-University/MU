How owners and authorized delegates determine who may use shared resources—through permission grants, group membership, file-system rules, and application sharing—and where those choices must be bounded by stronger policies.

Article overview

Discretionary access control (DAC) permits a resource owner or authorized delegate to decide who receives specified access. It supports everyday collaboration, but it also transfers responsibility for sensitive sharing decisions to many local actors. This article examines DAC's formal basis, operating-system and application implementations, information-flow limitations, practical safeguards, and institutional governance.

Core theoryOwnership, delegation, the protection matrix, grant and revocation authority.
Practical systemsUnix mode bits and POSIX ACLs; Windows DACLs, inherited rights, and collaboration services.
Assurance and operationsConstrained sharing, effective permissions, information leakage, testing, and lifecycle reviews.

1. Definition and essential character

Discretionary access control (DAC) is a model of authorization in which an object owner, or another principal entrusted with control over the object, has some authority to decide who may use it and what operations they may perform. The discretion is not an absence of rules: a computer still authenticates or identifies the requester, records a grant or restriction, and enforces a decision. What distinguishes DAC is where the authority to grant access originates. Instead of requiring every ordinary sharing decision to be made by a central security authority, the system delegates a bounded share of that decision to owners or designated administrators. The NIST glossary definition explicitly emphasizes this control exercised at the discretion of an object's owner or another authorized party.

A familiar example is a file owner giving a colleague permission to read a report. The owner is not necessarily an operating-system administrator; they exercise authority that the platform already permits them to exercise. The user's access can be represented through permission bits, an access-control list, membership in a group, or an application-specific sharing entry. A request is permitted only when the effective controls authorize the requested action.

Three distinctions frame the subject. Ownership identifies a principal with a specified relationship to a resource; permission identifies an operation on that resource; and grant authority identifies who may change the permission arrangement. These are related but not identical. One user may own a file yet be constrained by an organization-wide rule; another may administer access to a project without owning each individual record; and a recipient may be permitted to read information without being permitted to reshare it.

DAC is pervasive because people must collaborate. It is limited because a user who can grant access can also make mistakes, be deceived, or transfer data outside an intended security boundary. The engineering task is therefore not simply to enable sharing, but to make discretionary sharing comprehensible, narrowly scoped, reversible where possible, and subject to overriding controls.

2. Historical development and practical significance

Early multiuser computing made it necessary to divide shared machines among users. Personal files, working directories, programs, and common libraries could not all be treated as equally open. Operating systems developed ownership identities, user and group permissions, and mechanisms for granting access beyond the creator's own account. In classic Unix-derived environments, the compact owner/group/other permission model became one prominent form. Later systems extended these principles with named-user ACL entries, inheritance, and more expressive rights. Enterprise document services and cloud applications have translated the same concept into sharing dialogs, invite links, collaborative workspaces, and resource-level roles.

The term discretionary must be read in contrast with mandatory control. In a mandatory model, policy restrictions such as classification or sensitivity labels are imposed by an authority that ordinary resource owners cannot freely override. Real systems combine both styles. An operating system may offer ordinary owner-managed file permissions while also applying mandatory integrity policies, sandbox restrictions, or organization-managed rules. A SaaS application may let a document owner invite reviewers while disallowing external sharing through a tenant policy.

DAC remains relevant across desktop operating systems, network file servers, source-code repositories, research workspaces, digital libraries, learning-management systems, and collaborative business software. The same central question repeats: who is entitled to decide that another person may have access? Answering it requires not just a permission list, but an authority model defining ownership, delegation, inheritance, and revocation.

3. Formal model: subjects, objects, operations, and authority

A DAC system can be described using four kinds of information:

  • Subjects: users, groups, processes, or service identities that request operations.
  • Objects: files, directories, documents, records, datasets, folders, queues, or other protected resources.
  • Operations: read, create, edit, execute, delete, share, change permissions, or other domain-specific actions.
  • Authorization state: the owner, applicable access entries, group memberships, inherited rules, and overarching constraints.

A simple protection matrix places subjects in rows and objects in columns; each cell lists permitted operations. This is a conceptual model rather than a prescription for how a system must store permissions.

Subject Draft report Final report Project folder
Document owner Read, edit, share Read, edit Read, create, manage
Editorial colleague Read, edit Read Read
External reviewer Read None None
Archiving service Read Read, archive Read

For a request by subject s to perform operation a on object o, an illustrative decision is:

Permit(s, a, o) = grant_exists(s, a, o) AND all_required_constraints_hold(s, a, o).

The first term represents a DAC permission, possibly inherited from a group or parent container. The second recognizes that an owner-controlled grant is not necessarily sufficient to overcome other organizational, operating-system, or application restrictions. This expression is a teaching abstraction, not the exact algorithm of Windows, Linux, or any particular cloud product.

A crucial governance rule is that permission to use data is not necessarily permission to grant permission. Systems sometimes combine those capabilities, but a well-designed policy makes the distinction explicit.

4. Ownership, stewardship, and the right to change access

Ownership is the center of DAC, yet the meaning of owner depends on the system. In a Unix-style file system, a file has a user owner and group owner; management operations are constrained by the kernel's ownership and privilege rules. In Windows, an object's security descriptor identifies an owner, and owners commonly receive implicit rights relevant to reading and changing discretionary permissions, subject to special controls and context. In an enterprise document application, the apparent “owner” may be a creator, designated custodian, workspace administrator, or organization rather than a unique file-system owner.

A resource owner ordinarily controls some access decisions, not every property of the resource. Their authority may include granting read access, choosing a collaborator group, or removing an invitation. It may exclude altering retention requirements, transferring a regulated record into an unapproved jurisdiction, or exempting the object from audit. The implementation must state which choices are discretionary and which are not.

The distinction between business owner and technical owner is especially important. A department may be accountable for the lawful and appropriate use of a dataset, while a platform team administers its ACL. Neither should silently assume that the ability to perform a technical grant constitutes authorization to share the data for any purpose. Effective DAC governance identifies the decision-maker for each class of grant, how that person is appointed, and how their authority is reviewed.

Ownership transfer requires its own rules: what happens when a creator leaves, changes departments, loses their account, or transfers a project? An organization needs a documented successor or administrator pathway that preserves availability without turning abandoned objects into anonymously shared repositories.

5. Permission vocabulary and operation granularity

Permissions vary by resource type. File systems commonly distinguish reading contents, writing contents, executing programs, and traversing directories. Application APIs may add viewing metadata, editing comments, exporting a dataset, approving an invoice, changing collaborators, and administering settings. These operations should not be collapsed into a vague “access” flag when their consequences differ substantially.

Permission Typical meaning Important qualification
Read Obtain the contents of an object May include export or copying unless separately controlled
Write Modify contents May not imply ability to rename or delete
Execute Run a program or traverse a directory, depending on object type Meaning is platform- and object-specific
Create Add a new object to a container Container permissions usually matter
Delete Remove an object or its directory entry May depend on parent-directory or separate delete rights
Share Change another subject's access Should be separately governable
Own / change ACL Alter security metadata or discretionary access entries High-impact authority, not mere content access
Transfer ownership Assign control to a different owner Often subject to elevated rights or workflow

An entitlement describes the access a principal has through applicable policies and grants. An effective permission is what remains after all the applicable resolution, inheritance, masks, explicit denials, and independent restrictions have been considered. A screenshot of a single allow entry does not prove that access works, and the absence of one entry does not prove denial if another group or inherited entry may authorize it.

Permission granularity is an architectural choice. Coarse “viewer/editor/owner” roles make collaboration easier but can hide operations such as exporting, changing link reachability, or deleting. Fine-grained permissions improve control but increase administrative and usability costs. Design should follow the risks of the actions being controlled.

6. The lifecycle of a discretionary grant

A complete grant is a managed change of authorization state, not a standalone click. Its lifecycle commonly includes the following phases:

  1. Establish an authoritative owner or delegate. Confirm that the grantor is permitted to change access for the exact object.
  2. Identify the recipient. Resolve a person, group, service identity, or invited external party to a trustworthy account or credential.
  3. Select scope and operations. Determine the exact file, folder, record, project, and allowed actions.
  4. Evaluate constraints. Apply external-sharing policy, classification restrictions, conflict-of-interest requirements, and any other overriding conditions.
  5. Persist the grant. Record an ACE, group assignment, resource permission, or application sharing entry.
  6. Enforce use. Check the applicable authorization state whenever access is attempted at a protected boundary.
  7. Review and revoke. Expire or remove grants when the purpose ends, the recipient leaves, or the resource's sensitivity changes.

A well-designed interface makes the consequence of a grant legible: “view only until Friday” is materially different from “can edit and invite anyone.” An implementation should make it difficult to accidentally broaden scope through defaults, hidden group memberships, or permissive inheritance.

Grant records should support investigation: who authorized the sharing change, on whose behalf, for which object, to which identity, under which permission, at what time, and with what expiry or approval? The exact level of event detail varies with sensitivity and organizational policy.

7. ACLs, permissions bits, groups, and capability representations

DAC is a policy model; an ACL is one representation and enforcement mechanism that can implement it. An access-control list associates an object with rules identifying principals and their rights. A traditional Unix mode uses three permission classes—owner, group, and other—with read/write/execute bits. Named-user ACLs can augment these compact modes. A capability-based system instead represents authorization through possession of an appropriate protected authority token or reference; capability systems are not automatically or exclusively DAC, but they can enable discretionary delegation under defined rules.

A central distinction is between identity-oriented grants and transferable authority. An ACL typically asks whether a named user or group is allowed to operate on the object. A shareable capability may allow a holder to access a resource by presenting a valid token; safety then depends on issuance, attenuation, secrecy, revocation, and constraints. Both approaches must address the problem of unintended redistribution.

A group is a practical shortcut that lets owners grant access to a changing set of members. This creates a second control surface: an object owner may grant “project reviewers” read access, while a different administrator decides who belongs to that group. The effective exposure then depends on both the object ACL and group management. The apparent owner-only decision is therefore part of a broader administration chain.

ACLs also do not settle information-flow policy. A user may read an object through a perfectly configured ACL and then send a copy elsewhere. Controlling disclosure after reading requires additional measures beyond object-level DAC, discussed below.

8. Unix permission bits: owner, group, and other

Many Unix and Linux file systems implement a familiar DAC baseline using three sets of permissions: user owner (u), owning group (g), and others (o). Each set may contain read (r), write (w), and execute (x) permissions. A display such as -rw-r----- means that the owning user has read and write permissions, the owning group has read permission, and others have none; the first character denotes the file type and is not itself one of the nine permission bits.

For a regular file, r permits reading the contents, w permits modifying its contents, and x permits execution subject to other system rules. For directories, the meanings differ: r allows listing entry names, w permits modification of the directory's entries when other necessary conditions hold, and x allows searching or traversing the directory. This distinction explains why setting a file readable does not necessarily make its contents reachable through an enclosing directory.

Illustrative examination commands on a test system:

ls -ld project-data
ls -l project-data/
stat project-data/report.txt

These commands are observational. Changing permissions with chmod, adjusting group ownership with chgrp, or changing the owner with chown modifies security state and should be done only on resources for which the operator has authority. In common Unix models, an ordinary file owner may change the mode bits of their file; changing the ownership identity itself is more restricted.

Mode bits are useful for simple delegation patterns but quickly become awkward when a file must be shared with several independently managed persons or teams. Named ACL entries address part of that need.

9. Directory authorization, deletion, and the path to a file

Directory permissions are not interchangeable with file permissions. To open a file by pathname, a requester generally must be allowed to traverse the relevant directories. The object itself must then permit the requested operation. A file with permissive read bits may remain inaccessible to a user who lacks search permission on an ancestor directory.

Conversely, the authority to delete a file under common Unix file-system semantics is primarily determined by rights on its parent directory, not simply by the write bit on the file. A user who can alter directory entries may be able to remove a file entry they cannot modify, subject to sticky-bit protections, mount and filesystem behavior, and other controls. The sticky bit on a shared writable directory can restrict deletion and renaming to qualifying owners or privileged users; this is why shared temporary directories need more thought than “make it writable.”

An illustrative path dependency:

/research/                   traverse required
/research/restricted/        traverse required
/research/restricted/a.csv   read required

In access reviews, checking only the final file's mode is therefore insufficient. Examine parent path traversal, default directory ACLs, group memberships, special bits, mount options, and any other mandatory protection layers. This remains a DAC lesson even when the failure arises from object hierarchy rather than a direct owner decision.

10. POSIX-style extended ACLs and effective masks

On Linux systems supporting POSIX-style ACLs, extended ACL entries can grant permissions to named users and named groups in addition to the standard owner/group/other classes. For example, a document might allow its owner full read/write rights, a project group read access, and one named reviewer read access. The getfacl utility displays these entries; setfacl can alter them where supported and authorized.

# Inspect a non-sensitive test file.
getfacl project-data/report.txt

# Example of a controlled lab change by an authorized owner:
setfacl -m u:reviewer:r-- project-data/report.txt
getfacl project-data/report.txt

An often-missed detail is the ACL mask. In the Linux POSIX ACL model, the mask limits effective permissions for named users, named groups, and the owning group class; the owner user and “other” entries have different treatment. An entry showing user:reviewer:rwx does not guarantee that the reviewer effectively has all three rights if the mask narrows them. Mode-bit changes may also affect the ACL's effective rights. The Linux acl(5) and setfacl(1) manuals describe the detailed evaluation and mask relationships.

A directory may also have a default ACL, which influences newly created children. It is not simply an access grant to the directory itself: the directory's access ACL controls access to the directory, while the default ACL participates in initializing a new child's ACL, subject to creation rules. File-mode creation settings and implementation details still matter. In practice, test newly created files rather than assuming inheritance produces the desired effective permissions.

11. Windows security descriptors, DACLs, and access checks

Windows securable objects commonly have a security descriptor containing an owner security identifier (SID), optional primary group information, a discretionary access-control list (DACL), and a system access-control list (SACL) used for separate purposes such as auditing. DACL entries, called ACEs, can allow or deny specified access rights to users or groups. A SACL is not another spelling of the DACL; audit configuration and access authorization are distinct functions.

Windows access checking evaluates the requested rights against the applicable token identities, privileges, ownership-related rights, and the object's security descriptor. The exact algorithm varies by object type, requested access, and applicable rules. ACE order matters for a DACL: Windows documents canonical ordering practices that place explicit entries and inherited entries in defined relationships, with deny and allow entries organized appropriately. Do not implement Windows ACL resolution by simply saying “a deny always wins” without examining ACE ordering, requested rights, and other applicable access rules.

A particularly important distinction is between a null DACL and an empty DACL. A missing or null DACL can permit broad access, whereas a present DACL containing no ACEs normally permits no access through its ordinary allow entries. The Microsoft DACL documentation makes this distinction explicit. These are different security states, and confusing them can create serious exposure.

Ownership is also nuanced: owners ordinarily have important rights to inspect or alter discretionary security data, but special restrictions such as Owner Rights ACEs and directory-service-specific behavior can change the effective result. An ownership label alone must not be treated as proof that every requested read, write, or administrative action will succeed.

12. Windows inheritance and the effective-access problem

Windows permission inheritance is designed to reduce repetitive policy administration across directory trees and other hierarchies. A parent object can carry inheritable entries that flow into eligible child objects. The child may also have explicitly configured entries, subject to the object's settings and supported inheritance model. Inheritance is convenient for predictable team folders but risky when an inherited grant is broader than the child resource's sensitivity.

A useful diagnostic distinction is declared access versus effective access. A permissions dialog may show several entries granting or denying rights, while actual access depends on identity tokens, nested group memberships, inherited ACEs, object-specific rights, share-level controls, and any additional policy. The Microsoft AccessCheck explanation provides an account of DACL evaluation.

For network-shared files, SMB share permissions and file-system permissions can independently restrict operations. An allow entry in an NTFS DACL does not bypass a more restrictive share rule, and a permissive share rule does not override a restrictive NTFS rule. The common administrative shorthand is that effective access is limited by both layers, although real outcomes require checking the actual context and features in use.

Administrators should favor intentional group-based grants on controlled parent folders over ad hoc changes scattered through thousands of files. Where exceptions are necessary, they should be visible in review reports. A single direct-permission exception can outlive the project that justified it.

13. User-created sharing in cloud storage and collaboration services

Cloud file-sharing and team collaboration tools often present a DAC-like interface: an author or permitted collaborator chooses a recipient, level of access, and sometimes an expiration date. This resembles file-system DAC in that a resource controller can authorize other people. The resemblance should not obscure the application's actual policy model. Many cloud products mix owner discretionary grants, tenant administrator restrictions, group or role entitlements, link tokens, conditional access, and centralized data controls.

A sharing link is not always equivalent to a named-user ACL. Links may be restricted to a named recipient, members of an organization, anyone in possession of a link, or another scope defined by the service. The security implications depend on authentication requirements, whether the link functions like a bearer credential, expiry, download rights, and the ability to audit or revoke it. A resource owner who selects “anyone with the link” may be granting substantially wider practical access than they intended.

A governance design should treat link scope, operations, and lifetime as first-class permission attributes. For sensitive resources, the safer default is a named-user or tightly scoped group grant, with an expiry when the collaboration is temporary. External sharing should require explicit policy approval where necessary, and identity resolution should be tested rather than assumed from a display name or email string.

Cloud storage also introduces copies, synchronization clients, offline caches, version history, search indexes, and exported documents. Revoking a service permission may block future access through the platform but cannot automatically erase a previously downloaded local copy. This is not an argument against DAC; it is a boundary on what any access check can accomplish after information has left the enforcement point.

14. DAC in applications, records, and collaborative workspaces

Application designers often implement DAC without calling it DAC. A user creates a project, invites collaborators, assigns them “viewer” or “editor” status, and later changes those assignments. Behind this workflow are resource ownership rules, sharing records, invitations, access checks, and lifecycle handling.

For multiuser services, object identity and organizational scope are essential. An application may store a permission tuple such as (tenant_id, resource_id, principal_id, operation, grantor_id). Every protected request must then verify that the requesting principal is authenticated, belongs to an allowed scope where required, and is permitted to perform the particular operation on that exact object. The presence of a plausible resource identifier is not itself an authorization grant.

Consider a learning-management system. An instructor might own teaching materials within a course and invite a guest reviewer. That discretionary grant must not automatically expose student grades, institutional financial records, or unrelated courses. The application should separate resource classes and operation sets, rather than equating course material editing with unlimited course administration.

A sound data model distinguishes direct shares, inherited workspace membership, team membership, and grants through a public or restricted link. It should also record ownership transfers, revoked invitations, pending invites, and deleted identities. These are not merely user-interface states: each one can change the meaning of a subsequent authorization check.

15. Default permissions and secure creation

A newly created object needs an initial owner and an initial protection state. The defaults are therefore part of the security architecture. If a newly created folder automatically grants broad access to a large group, later “tightening” its permissions may not undo early exposure, copies, backups, or synchronization. If all new objects are inaccessible to the teams that need them, users may respond by granting overly broad access in frustration.

On Unix-like systems, a process creation mode, a umask, and any relevant default directory ACL can influence new file or directory permissions. A umask filters selected permission bits from the requested creation mode; it is not a complete, universal ACL policy engine. Creating an object within a directory that has default ACLs introduces additional inheritance behavior. Administrators should verify actual results on their chosen filesystem rather than rely solely on a numeric umask description.

On Windows, security descriptors and inheritable ACEs can influence access on new objects. The creation context, owner assignment, and parent-container rules all matter. On applications, default sharing often depends on whether an object is created in a personal space, project workspace, team, tenant, or publicly visible collection.

A defensible default policy answers four questions before an object is created: who owns it; who can read it; who can modify it; and who can change future access? The most sensitive environments may require a separate answer for export or sharing outside the organization.

16. Group membership as indirect discretionary delegation

Group-based sharing is administratively efficient but introduces a delegation chain. Imagine a project owner grants read access to a named group. If a group manager can add new members without the project owner's review, the manager now influences who reads the object even though the file's ACL never changes. In that sense, a resource grant and a group-membership grant combine to produce effective authority.

This can be beneficial: a department gains a clean handoff model when staff join or leave a team. But it can also create hidden access. Nested groups, broad default memberships, stale contractor accounts, and cross-tenant group mappings increase the difficulty of understanding effective access. A group name such as “Finance Team” is not an adequate control unless the membership definition is known and maintained.

Controls should align group-management authority with the sensitivity of the resources to which the group is entitled. An owner should know whether the group is centrally managed, self-managed, or externally administered. Changes to especially powerful group memberships should be logged and, where needed, approved. Periodic reviews should test the actual membership chain rather than checking a superficial list of visible principals on one object.

Dynamic groups introduce another subtlety: a membership rule can change effective access across many resources immediately or after propagation. Rule changes deserve the same governance attention as direct ACL modifications when they expand access materially.

17. Delegation chains, propagation, and the grant-option question

Discretionary access can be as simple as “Alice may read the file” or as complex as “Alice may read and may authorize Bob to read.” Those are different delegations. Systems must define whether the recipient may reshare the resource and, if so, whether their authority may be passed onward.

Some formal DAC models represent grant options explicitly. A subject may possess a permission with or without the power to grant that permission to another subject. A system can also support ownership transfer, shared administration, and bounded delegation. These mechanisms increase flexibility but create propagation paths that are harder to audit and revoke.

A useful analytic technique is to construct a grant graph: each edge records a grantor, recipient, resource or scope, and permitted operations. Where onward delegation is allowed, the graph shows how authority could flow. The risk is not merely the number of recipients but the number of nodes allowed to create additional recipients.

Governance choices include restricting reshare privileges to owners; requiring approval for public sharing; preventing indefinite delegation; capping the set of organizations to which access may be granted; and setting expiration dates. These restrictions are external or additional rules imposed upon discretionary powers. They preserve useful owner choices while reducing uncontrolled spread.

18. DAC, information flow, and the Trojan-horse limitation

A defining limitation of simple DAC is that it primarily controls which subjects may access which objects. It does not necessarily control where information flows after an authorized read. Consider a subject who is permitted to read a confidential file. If that subject can also write to a broadly readable location, the subject—or a malicious program running with their privileges—may copy information from the confidential object to the shared location.

This pattern underlies the classic Trojan-horse problem associated with discretionary controls. An apparently legitimate program operating under an authorized user's identity can misuse the user's read and write privileges. DAC can faithfully enforce both original grants while still allowing a flow the organization considers unacceptable. The failure may therefore be in the policy model's coverage, not in the correctness of a particular ACL check.

Mandatory information-flow controls, compartmentalization, constrained application environments, data-loss prevention, egress restrictions, and workflow-specific approval rules may mitigate such risks. No single control prevents every possible leak. For example, even a strong access restriction does not fully stop an authorized reader from memorizing or photographing displayed information.

This limitation should shape system choice: DAC is well suited to flexible collaboration among trusted participants, but it should not be the only control for data whose classification, legal obligations, or safety consequences demand mandatory non-disclosure boundaries.

19. Why DAC is neither role-based nor mandatory access control

DAC, role-based access control (RBAC), attribute-based access control (ABAC), and mandatory access control (MAC) address overlapping problems from different organizing principles. DAC emphasizes the owner's discretion to grant or manage access. RBAC emphasizes permissions associated with defined roles and the assignment of principals to roles. ABAC evaluates policy using attributes of subjects, objects, actions, and context. MAC constrains decisions according to a policy that ordinary users cannot override.

Model Central basis Typical authority to change access Main strength Principal limitation
DAC Ownership and discretionary grants Owner or authorized delegate Flexible resource-level collaboration Owner errors and uncontrolled propagation
RBAC Assigned roles and role permissions Role/identity administrators, according to policy Standardized job-function access Role proliferation; weak fit for unusual object scopes
ABAC Attributes and policy evaluation Policy administrators and trusted attribute managers Context-sensitive and scalable policy expression Attribute quality and policy complexity
MAC Mandatory labels or centrally imposed rules Authorized security policy authority Enforced boundaries owners cannot simply waive Operational complexity and reduced discretion

These are not mutually exclusive product categories. An application can use RBAC to determine who is allowed to become a document owner, DAC to let the owner invite reviewers, ABAC to restrict sharing to particular organizational contexts, and MAC-like controls to prohibit release of a classified dataset. The decisive analytic question is which actor or policy is allowed to override each decision.

A platform that exposes a “share” button is therefore not necessarily a pure DAC implementation. A resource can be owner-controlled within a carefully constrained system. Likewise, an ACL syntax alone does not reveal whether a policy is discretionary: a centrally maintained ACL that ordinary owners cannot alter may implement a centralized policy instead.

20. Interactions with mandatory restrictions and separate boundaries

A trustworthy DAC deployment makes overriding restrictions explicit. A user may be permitted to share a file with another employee but prohibited from sharing it with an external address. A developer may modify application source files but lack permission to disable endpoint security. A department manager may distribute reports within the department but lack authority to override retention settings or classification rules.

Authorization can be conceptualized as the intersection of several conditions:

Authorized request
  = discretionary grant present
  AND resource/tenant boundary respected
  AND mandatory restrictions satisfied
  AND other applicable policy checks satisfied

Real systems implement these conditions through different services and algorithms, so the expression should not be mistaken for a universal operating-system formula. It is a design checklist: an allow decision from one layer cannot automatically prove that every other required layer permits the action.

Architectural reviews should specify whether discretionary grants can bypass classification controls, whether administrators can invoke emergency access, and which component is responsible for final enforcement. A system that places a mandatory restriction only in the graphical interface, but not in the API or background processing path, has not established a reliable boundary.

21. Revocation, expiration, and residual access

Revocation removes a grant or changes permissions so that subsequent authorization checks should no longer allow the prohibited operation. It is a necessary feature of DAC but often not instantaneous or complete. The effective result can be delayed by group-membership caches, tokens, open file handles, synchronization clients, application authorization caches, or distributed propagation.

An owner may remove an invited reviewer, but the reviewer might still possess a document already downloaded, an email attachment, a printout, or a screenshot. Revocation generally prevents future mediated access through the controlled system; it does not retroactively erase every instance of information legitimately acquired. This difference matters when writing security guarantees and incident-response procedures.

A revocation plan should address: how the owner requests removal; how the system confirms the change; which caches or sessions need invalidation; whether existing handles continue operating; and what evidence demonstrates that the expected enforcement has taken effect. Time-limited grants reduce the number of stale permissions requiring manual discovery, but expiration logic must itself be enforced and tested.

In highly sensitive applications, changing a permission may need to trigger a re-evaluation of active sessions or encryption-key access. Such measures are design-specific; they do not make downloaded plaintext retractable.

22. Access control drift and permission accumulation

Permission drift occurs when the live access state gradually diverges from what business owners intended. A file passes between teams, a temporary reviewer remains in a group, a project moves to a different department, or inherited permissions remain after a folder restructure. The grant may originally have been authorized, yet its continued existence can become inappropriate.

DAC makes drift likely when discretionary authority is distributed across many owners. Each owner may make a reasonable local choice without seeing the aggregate exposure created by overlapping groups and shared workspaces. Over time, privilege creep can produce broad access that no single participant deliberately approved.

Useful countermeasures include periodic access reviews; expiration defaults for external recipients; an orphaned-owner recovery process; alerts for unusually broad grants; workflow controls when moving high-sensitivity resources; and automated identification of accounts that no longer exist. Effective reviews need enough context to judge whether the access is justified—not just an export of raw group names.

Review cadences should match risk. A public reference collection may need little individual grant review, while a research dataset containing sensitive personal information may need tighter controls and frequent exceptions reporting. Metrics are most useful when they reveal risks that lead to specific corrective action.

23. Confused deputies, shared identities, and accountability

DAC usually presumes that the system can distinguish the identity exercising discretion and the identity requesting access. Shared credentials undermine this presumption. When many people use one service account or group login, an access log cannot reliably attribute discretionary grants to a responsible individual. The same problem arises when a process performs access checks using an overly powerful identity and then serves data to less privileged callers without checking their authorization.

The latter is a form of the confused-deputy problem: a component that possesses legitimate authority is induced to exercise it for a caller who should not receive its benefits. The defense is to preserve the caller's identity and requested resource scope through the operation, check authorization at the relevant boundary, and avoid treating an internal service's credentials as evidence of the end user's entitlement.

For owner-managed sharing APIs, the caller must be allowed to manage that particular object. A generic “is logged in” check is insufficient. So is a broad “is a project member” check when the policy reserves permission management to the resource owner or authorized administrators.

Audit records should distinguish the actor who performed the grant from the subject who received it and the resource affected. If an administrator grants access on a user's behalf, the record should reflect both the actual actor and the delegated or approved business purpose.

24. Common failure modes in discretionary control

DAC deployments fail in predictable ways. Reviewing these failure patterns is more useful than memorizing a single ACL command.

Failure pattern Consequence Control response
“Anyone with link” chosen unintentionally Unbounded practical audience Named-recipient defaults, warnings, reviewable link inventory
Overly broad group membership Access expands without an object ACL change Govern membership and inspect nested groups
Inherited permission misread Unexpected access to a child object Evaluate effective rights at actual resource scope
Linux ACL mask overlooked Granted entry does not work as expected Inspect effective rights and mask together
Null DACL confused with empty DACL Excessively broad Windows access Security-descriptor validation and targeted tests
Owner changes without successor Orphaned content or unreviewed sharing Ownership transfer workflow
Share check only in user interface API or background-job bypass Enforce at server-side resource operations
Delayed revocation Removed party retains mediated access temporarily Invalidation strategy and revocation testing
Edit permission conflated with reshare Collaborator can broaden access unexpectedly Separate operation-specific grants
Sensitive data copied to open location Information leaves original protected boundary Layered information-flow and data controls

An effective review maps every failure mode to its detection method, responsible party, and remediation pathway. For example, “too many links” is not operationally meaningful without knowing which scopes are unacceptable and who can disable them. A system is not safe merely because it provides a permissions panel; it is safer when owners can understand the implications of changes and administrators can detect the exceptions that matter.

25. Organizational governance and bounded owner discretion

A policy allowing employees to control their documents should define the boundaries of that control. This includes permitted sharing audiences, protected data categories, restrictions on external recipients, approval requirements, and mandatory safeguards. A sensible governance model avoids two extremes: uncontrolled local sharing and central bureaucracy that prevents routine collaboration.

Organizations can adopt tiered discretion. An internal draft may be shareable within its project team by its owner. A restricted financial dataset may require department approval for any external grant. Highly regulated or classified records may prohibit owner-initiated external sharing altogether. The distinctions should be based on business purpose, sensitivity, and legal or contractual obligations—not just the preferred user interface.

Ownership assignments should be documented for managed repositories. A team or department can remain accountable for a resource even if one person created it. The organization's offboarding and reorganization processes must preserve legitimate continuity without retaining unnecessary access. Exceptional grants should record a justification and an accountable approver.

A DAC program also needs a serviceable user experience. If the approved sharing path is difficult to understand, people may copy files into personal accounts, circulate attachments, or use broad groups to work around restrictions. Security review should therefore include the usability and timeliness of authorized collaboration workflows as well as their restrictive strength.

26. Worked scenario A: a shared research directory

A university research team maintains a project directory on a Linux file server. The principal investigator owns the research workflow; a technical custodian maintains the server; two analysts need edit access to a set of working files; an external reviewer needs temporary read access to one exported report. The team should not make the entire project tree world-readable merely to accommodate the reviewer.

Authorization design. Keep core project files within a controlled group. Give the analysts the rights needed for the working directory. Place the reviewable report in a specifically scoped review folder or grant a named reviewer read permission on the approved object and necessary traversal along the path. Establish an end date and document the grantor. If default ACLs are used for future project files, verify the new-file effective rights against both default ACL behavior and the ACL mask.

Resource Authorized subjects Permitted operations Explicitly excluded
Raw study records Custodian, approved analysts Read/edit as assigned External review access
Working analysis directory Project group Read, create, edit, traverse Other institutional users
Approved summary report Project group; named reviewer Project group edits; reviewer reads Reviewer reshare or edit
ACL administration Authorized owner/custodian Change grants with evidence Ordinary reviewer

Verification. On a test copy, inspect paths with ls -ld, mode bits with stat, and extended ACLs with getfacl. Test ordinary analyst and reviewer identities using controlled access checks, not just a privileged administrator identity. Confirm that the reviewer can read the approved report, cannot read the raw records, and cannot modify the report or broaden its permissions. Confirm that removing the reviewer grant blocks subsequent access; separately document any existing local copies.

The lesson is that a discretionary share can be precise, but only when the path, inherited permissions, named ACL entries, group membership, and permission mask are evaluated together.

27. Worked scenario B: departmental Windows file shares

An academic department has an NTFS-backed network share. Faculty create course materials, teaching assistants edit designated teaching folders, and visiting assessors receive read-only access to selected assessment artifacts. The department wants delegated ownership without permitting faculty to publish internal evaluations to the entire institution.

Design choices. Define departmental ownership and inheritance at controlled folder boundaries. Use managed groups for recurring teaching assistant roles, rather than adding each assistant individually to hundreds of files. For assessors, use a dedicated read-only folder with a documented expiry or a narrow scoped entitlement. Ensure both share-level and NTFS-level settings permit the intended operations and restrict unintended ones.

An administrator can inspect assigned permissions on an authorized test folder with a tool such as Windows icacls, and can use platform-supported effective-access views where appropriate. However, a static ACL listing alone does not fully predict the effect of all groups, privileges, ownership behavior, inheritance, or share restrictions. Use identity-specific tests for important boundaries.

Example failure. A newly created faculty subfolder inherits read access for a broad “All Staff” group. The faculty member sees only the direct assessors' entries and assumes the folder is private. The real control problem is inherited access, not lack of a direct deny entry. The remedy is to correct the hierarchy's permitted audience and inheritance design, then validate effective access for representative identities. Blanket deny ACEs without assessing inheritance and administrative effects may create their own maintenance problems.

Exit test. When an assessor's engagement ends, confirm that the account's group membership or scoped grant has been removed and that new access attempts fail. Document how existing synchronized copies or exported files are handled separately.

28. Worked scenario C: owner-managed sharing in a web application

A project-management application allows project owners to create documents and invite collaborators. It stores each resource with a tenant identifier, an owner identifier, and a set of sharing grants. The security goal is to let owners collaborate without allowing one tenant's users to access or modify another tenant's projects.

An illustrative request to share a document can follow this sequence:

request: actor A grants READ on document D to user B
1. Authenticate A and establish trusted session context.
2. Load D by authoritative resource identifier and tenant.
3. Check that A has SHARE permission on D, not merely READ.
4. Verify B is an eligible recipient under tenant policy.
5. Enforce any classification or external-sharing restriction.
6. Persist the grant with actor, subject, scope, expiry, time.
7. Log the grant event; return a confirmation.
8. On B's next READ request, independently authorize B for D.

The endpoint that changes access is itself a protected operation. A user who can edit content must not automatically be able to call a hidden API to add new collaborators. Nor may the API rely on a client-provided owner identifier without verifying it against trusted resource data. On each read, write, export, delete, or reshare operation, authorization must be checked for the exact resource and operation.

Negative tests matter as much as the successful invitation. Attempt a read with the wrong tenant; request an edit using a read-only collaborator; try to grant access as a collaborator without reshare rights; try to reuse a revoked invitation; and try to read a document by substituting another document's identifier. The expected outcome is denial, no unauthorized state change, and an appropriately scoped audit event. These principles align with OWASP authorization guidance.

29. Assessing and testing a DAC implementation

A DAC test program should confirm grant correctness, denial correctness, change authority, and lifecycle behavior. Testing should be grounded in real subject identities and real resources; only inspecting the rules written into a database or permissions panel can miss bypass paths.

Test family Example Expected evidence
Positive authorization Owner invites a permitted colleague Colleague can perform only granted actions
Negative authorization Uninvited user requests same object Access denied by enforcement boundary
Grant authority Reader attempts to reshare Change rejected unless separately authorized
Inheritance New child created under shared folder Effective rights match inheritance policy
Group membership New team member joins managed group Access changes according to controlled membership
Revocation Owner removes a collaborator Fresh access attempt denied within defined propagation bound
Scope isolation User substitutes another tenant's object ID Access denied; no cross-tenant disclosure
Policy override Owner attempts forbidden external share Organizational restriction takes precedence
Session/cache behavior Access removed while user is active Documented invalidation or bounded residual behavior
Audit integrity Grant and revoke operations performed Actor, object, recipient and changes are attributable

Reviews should account for effective rights rather than just declared ACLs. In Linux, include masks and parent traversal. In Windows, include inherited ACEs, relevant identities, and share permissions. In applications, include object-level scope, server-side enforcement, background jobs, and authorization caches.

Testing is also a policy-validation exercise. A technically correct grant that violates institutional requirements is not an acceptable result. Negative tests should therefore cover both the owner’s discretionary limits and the platform’s mandatory constraints.

30. Operational controls and review metrics

Security operations can manage DAC without centralizing every legitimate sharing decision. Useful controls include reliable ownership inventories, periodic identification of broadly shared objects, reporting on external recipients, automated expiry where supported, and review workflows for sensitive folders or data collections.

A compact measurement program can track the following indicators:

Indicator Definition Operational use
Orphaned-object rate Protected objects with no accountable active owner Trigger ownership reassignment
Public or broad-share count Objects reachable by broad links or groups Review exposure against data classification
Stale grants Grants surviving beyond purpose or approved end date Prioritize revocation
External access concentration Sensitive objects shared outside approved domains Escalate exceptions
Owner review completion In-scope resources reviewed within required cycle Identify review backlog
Grant-authority violations Attempts to modify sharing without permission Investigate policy confusion or misuse
Revocation propagation delay Time from approved removal to effective denial Evaluate operational assurance
Group-membership drift Unauthorized or obsolete group memberships Improve identity governance

Counts are not automatically good or bad: a large library of public teaching resources may legitimately be widely readable. Metrics need a risk denominator, a responsible owner, and a defined response threshold. A zero count of denied requests may indicate good policy adherence, but it may also reveal missing audit coverage or ineffective enforcement.

Review evidence should show not just that a manager clicked “approve,” but that the manager was presented with the resource, actual recipients, permission types, and meaningful justification. Sensitive environments may need reviewer independence from the original grantor.

31. Implementation roadmap and institutional checklist

A staged rollout should begin with the existing resource landscape rather than installing new permission software first. The following sequence is suitable for an institutional access-control modernization program:

  1. Classify resources and data flows. Identify which resources permit local owner discretion and which require mandatory restrictions.
  2. Define ownership. Establish accountable business owners, technical custodians, and valid successors.
  3. Define permission vocabulary. Separate use operations, content editing, sharing, administration, and ownership transfer.
  4. Choose representation. Map the model onto mode bits, ACLs, group grants, or application records as appropriate; document capability-like link behavior where present.
  5. Specify baseline defaults. Decide on ordinary recipient scope, public-sharing limits, inheritance, and creation rules.
  6. Implement change authority. Ensure a grantor can modify only permissions they are authorized to manage.
  7. Enforce at actual resource boundaries. Do not rely on user-interface concealment, client-side checks, or a stale login-time snapshot.
  8. Implement revocation and audit. Define propagation expectations, event fields, group-change controls, and exception reporting.
  9. Test representative identities. Include explicit denials, group and directory inheritance, cross-tenant access, active sessions, and owner transitions.
  10. Operate the lifecycle. Review grants, expire temporary access, remediate drift, and improve the sharing experience.

Publication and implementation note. This article explains engineering principles; the exact command syntax and enforcement semantics should be checked against the target operating system, filesystem, application version, and institutional policy before production changes. A production administrator should always test changes on appropriately scoped, recoverable resources.
