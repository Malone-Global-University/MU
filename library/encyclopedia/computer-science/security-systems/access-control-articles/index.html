Collection Overview

The Access Control unit belongs to the Security Systems division of the Computer Science volume of the MGU Encyclopedia. It expands the existing Access Control overview into a deliberately tiered study of how authorization policy is specified, administered, evaluated, enforced, and checked over time.

Access control is not the same operation as authentication. Authentication supplies evidence about the identity or nature of a requesting subject. Authorization determines whether that subject is permitted to perform a particular action on a particular resource, under the policy and conditions that apply at the moment of use. A successful login does not imply permission to read every record, modify every configuration, approve every transaction, or delegate privileges to another person or program.

The unit follows four layers of analysis. Foundations establish policy and the distinction between discretionary and mandatory authority. Decision models examine roles, attributes, and relationships. Mechanisms address policy engines, least privilege, and the representation of rights. Operations examine permissions, access reviews, and adaptive authorization. The same implementation may combine several layers; the purpose of classification is to expose design choices rather than insist upon one exclusive model.

Production convention: all twelve specialist destinations are active links because they identify intended Level 5 articles. The first two have completed HTML drafts supplied during this production sequence; the remaining ten are planned. Local draft completion does not establish live publication. The live parent overview is independent of this directory and remains the conceptual starting point.

Foundations and Owner Versus System Authority

The first group explains what an authorization decision is and who may establish the rules that govern it. A discretionary permission may be managed by a file or document owner; a mandatory restriction is imposed by a controlling security policy. Both require a correctly placed enforcement point. These distinctions are essential before introducing richer attributes, roles, relationships, or policy engines.

1. Access Control Fundamentals and Policy Enforcement

Establish the conceptual and operational baseline for every other article in this unit. Define subject, object, action, context, decision, entitlement, policy, and enforcement point; distinguish authentication from authorization. Examine policy administration and information points, the policy decision point (PDP), the policy enforcement point (PEP), and the path from request receipt to grant or denial. Treat fail-closed handling, complete mediation, default denial, decision freshness, multitenant isolation, audit evidence, and negative authorization tests as first-class requirements. This article owns the cross-cutting lifecycle rather than the detailed rules of any single access model.

2. Discretionary Access Control

Analyze discretionary access control (DAC) in which an owner or otherwise designated grantor can authorize others to use a resource, within higher-level restrictions. Develop examples from Unix permission bits and POSIX-style ACLs, Windows security descriptors and DACLs, and user-managed sharing in collaboration applications. Explain grants, inheritance, group membership, effective access, privilege accumulation, revocation, and the limits of owner discretion. Include the information-flow and Trojan-horse weaknesses of DAC, which cannot by itself enforce mandatory restrictions on onward disclosure. Keep mandatory labeling and central policy outside this article’s primary scope.

3. Mandatory Access Control

Define mandatory access control (MAC) as enforcement of system-level constraints that a typical resource owner cannot independently override. Examine security labels, subject clearances, object classifications, compartments, dominance relations, and the distinction between access decisions and information-flow controls. Introduce confidentiality-oriented Bell–LaPadula and integrity-oriented Biba as models with explicit assumptions, not universal turnkey implementations. Compare operating-system mandatory controls and policy-enforced isolation with DAC permissions. Cover relabeling authority, trusted processes, policy administration, operational friction, and the risk of oversimplifying real hybrid systems.

Organizational, Attribute, and Relationship Models

The second group organizes authority around stable duties, contextual predicates, and explicit relationships. Real systems rarely operate as pure examples of one model. A university application may use a registrar role, a course-enrollment relationship, and a record classification in the same decision. The editorial goal is to articulate the strengths and boundaries of each model without recasting implementation patterns as incompatible alternatives.

4. Role-Based Access Control

Study how role-based access control (RBAC) connects people or service identities to permissions through organizational or functional roles. Distinguish users, roles, permissions, sessions, and role activation; treat core RBAC, hierarchies, and static or dynamic separation-of-duty constraints accurately. Explain joiner–mover–leaver changes, role explosion, toxic combinations, overbroad groups, delegated role administration, and temporary elevation. Provide examples from university registration, accounting approvals, and enterprise applications. The central question is how stable job functions can structure authorization without assuming that a role alone expresses every resource, tenant, or contextual constraint.

5. Attribute-Based Access Control

Develop attribute-based access control (ABAC) from trusted attributes and policy predicates, using NIST SP 800-162 terminology where appropriate. Examine subject department, object classification, resource owner, requested operation, time, device posture, and contractual context as possible inputs. Distinguish the source and freshness of an attribute from the rule that consumes it; address missing or conflicting attributes and combiners such as deny-overrides. Present policy information, decision, administration, and enforcement responsibilities without conflating a model with any particular vendor platform. Compare ABAC flexibility with testing burden, explainability, governance, and performance costs.

6. Relationship-Based Access Control

Examine relationship-based access control (ReBAC) through explicit facts such as student-in-course, clinician-on-care-team, user-member-of-project, or editor-of-document. Show how an authorization graph can support shared folders, delegated organizations, collaborative SaaS applications, and hierarchical membership. Distinguish direct relationships from transitive derivations, delegation depth, contextual caveats, graph traversal, caching, and tuple or edge revocation. Contrast relationship predicates with role membership and attributes, noting that real applications often combine these forms. Preserve the principle that a graph path must be authorized by a precise policy, not merely exist in the data.

Policy Engines, Privilege Boundaries, and Representations

These articles connect conceptual rules to implementable controls. A policy engine evaluates rules; a resource gateway or local kernel enforces the outcome; least privilege constrains the scope of every grant; ACLs and capabilities provide different means of representing authority. Readers should not confuse a policy language, storage format, or entitlement object with a distinct security objective.

7. Policy-Based Access Control and Policy Engines

Investigate policy-as-code and centralized or distributed policy engines as engineering approaches to authorization. Define policies, rules, obligations, decisions, enforcement adapters, and the governance process for authoring and reviewing them. Explain the distinction between a policy engine’s architecture and the underlying DAC, RBAC, ABAC, or relationship facts it evaluates. Cover deterministic evaluation, versioning, approval workflows, conflict resolution, decision logs, safe fallbacks, and integration tests that ensure the application actually enforces the engine’s answer. Include tradeoffs between in-process and remote decision points and caution against stale permission caches.

8. Least Privilege and Privilege Boundaries

Treat least privilege as a security principle applied across access-control models rather than a competing authorization model. Distinguish required task permissions from broad standing access; examine privileged roles, service identities, operating-system capabilities, temporary elevation, just-in-time privilege, and separation of duties. Explain the boundary between read, modify, approve, administer, impersonate, and delegate rights. Analyze privilege escalation, confused-deputy behavior, transitive trust, and overprivileged automation. Include evidence for measuring high-risk permissions, evaluating the practicality of controls, and maintaining emergency-access procedures without normalizing persistent exceptions.

9. Access-Control Lists and Capability Systems

Compare access-control lists (ACLs), which commonly attach entries to protected resources, with capability-based approaches in which an unforgeable authority-bearing reference permits defined operations. Explain identity lookup, group inheritance, access-check ordering, possession and transfer of capabilities, and why ordinary URLs or bearer tokens are not automatically robust capabilities. Trace how each representation affects authority discovery, delegation, confinement, revocation, auditing, and least privilege. Keep the distinction clear: ACLs and capabilities describe authority representation and enforcement mechanisms, while DAC, MAC, and other models describe different policy semantics.

Entitlements, Assurance, and Adaptive Decisions

The final group addresses the operational consequences of authorization. Permissions must be expressible at useful granularity, discoverable to reviewers, promptly revoked when conditions change, and reevaluated when contextual risk justifies it. Successful governance connects identity data, policy versions, access decisions, incident investigation, and periodic review without treating a dashboard indicator as proof of enforcement.

10. Resource Permissions and Entitlements

Define permissions as allowable operations over a scoped resource and entitlements as administered grants of authority. Examine resource hierarchies, record ownership, tenant identifiers, object-level and field-level checks, group expansion, inherited grants, default scopes, service accounts, and delegated administrative actions. Develop a permissions vocabulary that remains stable as applications change, avoiding overloaded notions of “admin” and accidental conflation of view, export, delete, and grant. Show how effective permissions emerge from multiple grants and constraints, and how to document them in an auditable entitlement inventory. Focus on precise semantics rather than vendor-specific click paths.

11. Authorization Auditing and Access Reviews

Develop evidence-based authorization oversight from identity enrollment to revocation. Differentiate policy audit, entitlement review, decision logging, use monitoring, and independent verification that the enforcement layer follows policy. Explain periodic access certification, review of high-risk grants, service-account ownership, conflicting duties, dormant access, and exceptions with expiry dates. Include event evidence, time synchronization, user and resource identifiers, reviewer accountability, false assurance from nominal approvals, and data-minimization limits in security logging. Establish measurable corrective outcomes without assuming that a completed review alone proves effective least privilege.

12. Contextual and Risk-Adaptive Authorization

Explore authorization decisions that depend on device posture, network context, geographic or temporal restrictions, session risk, step-up authentication, business state, and resource sensitivity. Separate authentication assurance from authorization policy and explain why risk scores alone should not become opaque universal permission rules. Consider signal provenance, uncertainty, spoofing, privacy, decision caching, continuous reevaluation, and graceful failure. Develop scenarios such as temporary high-value payment approval, a compromised device, or a high-risk export operation. Close with usability, accessibility, appeal, and auditability requirements for dynamic decisions.

Access-Control Models and Mechanisms Compared

Authorization design conversations often compare unlike categories: an access-control policy model, a decision service, a representation of authority, and a security principle are related but not equivalent. A mature system can combine elements: a staff role may define baseline privileges; a relationship may restrict access to assigned clients; an attribute policy may deny access from an untrusted device; a policy engine may render the decision; an API endpoint may enforce it; and review evidence may justify the grant. A role label alone is not sufficient to enforce a customer or tenant boundary. Likewise, possessing a signed identity token does not establish permission to access an arbitrary object.

Illustrative Authorization Scenarios

The following cases demonstrate how multiple articles contribute to one defensible authorization design. They are simplified instructional scenarios, not complete deployment specifications.

Scenario 1 — Student records in a university registry

A faculty member needs to view records for a course they teach, but not all student records in the institution. A basic job role can distinguish faculty from students; a course-to-instructor relationship narrows the cohort; object sensitivity and institutional policy limit exports. The registry must enforce these conditions on each protected API or data read, even if the interface hides the unauthorized record. Start with Role-Based Access Control, Relationship-Based Access Control, and Resource Permissions and Entitlements. The central test is whether changing the record identifier or calling the API directly crosses the authorized student boundary.

Scenario 2 — An accounts-payable workflow with conflicting duties

An analyst creates a payment request while a separate authorized manager approves it. An “accounts payable” role alone may be too broad; the policy must prohibit the initiator from approving the same request, apply monetary thresholds, and record approvals. A temporary manager substitute should receive a constrained delegation with an expiry. Read Least Privilege and Privilege Boundaries, Attribute-Based Access Control, and Authorization Auditing and Access Reviews. The decisive question is not whether both users are employees, but whether their identities, roles, transaction relationships, and separation-of-duty constraints jointly authorize the action.

Scenario 3 — Owner-managed research collaboration

A project lead shares a folder with a research assistant for reading and editing but should not transfer unrestricted ownership or expose unrelated projects. DAC sharing permits timely collaboration, while mandatory research-data restrictions, scoped entitlements, and review controls limit leakage. Read Discretionary Access Control, Mandatory Access Control, and Access-Control Lists and Capability Systems. A revocation test should check inherited group grants, existing share links, downstream copies, active sessions, and cached decisions; removal of one visible ACL entry may not close every route to the data.

Scenario 4 — Tenant isolation in a hosted business application

A SaaS administrator has valid credentials and a legitimate organization role. A request crafted with another customer’s object identifier must nevertheless be denied unless an explicit cross-tenant delegation exists. The resource tenant and the subject’s authorized memberships must be checked at the API/data boundary, not only in a user-interface filter. Read Access Control Fundamentals and Policy Enforcement, Resource Permissions and Entitlements, and Policy-Based Access Control and Policy Engines. Required negative tests include unrelated-tenant reads, writes, exports, bulk operations, and stale-session reuse.

Scenario 5 — Context-sensitive elevated access

An infrastructure engineer normally has diagnostic read access. During a production incident, a time-limited permission permits a narrow remediation action after appropriate approval and stronger authentication, with complete logging. Unfamiliar device posture or an unusual access pattern may require denial or verification. Read Contextual and Risk-Adaptive Authorization, Least Privilege and Privilege Boundaries, and Authorization Auditing and Access Reviews. The design must state what happens if context signals are missing, the policy service is unavailable, or elevated access remains active after the incident closes.

Minimum policy specification: enumerate each protected resource and operation; identify the source of applicable rules and subject/resource facts; define explicit permit, deny, and missing-data behavior; document where enforcement occurs; establish revocation behavior; and test both allowed and forbidden requests. A policy architecture that can be bypassed by a direct object request or a second application interface is not an effective control.

Suggested Study Pathways

No single reading order suits every role. The following pathways retain the unit hierarchy while prioritizing the authorizations readers are most likely to design, verify, or maintain.

Foundations and students: Begin with the Access Control parent overview, then Access Control Fundamentals and Policy Enforcement, Discretionary Access Control, Mandatory Access Control, and Role-Based Access Control. Make the distinction among owner choice, central policy, and role-based permission assignment explicit before comparing technical products.

Application and API engineers: Read Resource Permissions and Entitlements, Relationship-Based Access Control, Attribute-Based Access Control, and Policy-Based Access Control and Policy Engines. Apply them to direct API calls, tenant and object identifiers, bulk export routes, and test cases in which an otherwise valid identity requests the wrong resource.

System and cloud administrators: Prioritize Discretionary Access Control, Access-Control Lists and Capability Systems, Least Privilege and Privilege Boundaries, and Authorization Auditing and Access Reviews. Determine how inherited grants, privileged groups, automation identities, break-glass access, and revocation actually behave in deployed systems.

Risk, assurance, and compliance specialists: Follow Access Control Fundamentals and Policy Enforcement, Least Privilege and Privilege Boundaries, Authorization Auditing and Access Reviews, and Contextual and Risk-Adaptive Authorization. Compare written policy with tested effective access and review whether access certifications produce observable corrections.

Security architects and policy-system designers: Work across Mandatory Access Control, Role-Based Access Control, Attribute-Based Access Control, Relationship-Based Access Control, and Policy-Based Access Control and Policy Engines. Focus on combining semantics, policy provenance, enforcement placement, stale decisions, and the resulting failure modes.

Related Encyclopedia Topics

This is a Level 4 unit directory for the Access Control parent article, not a replacement for the subjects linked below. The distinctions prevent the curriculum from collapsing identity verification, privilege administration, software testing, network policy, and privacy into a single oversized authorization topic.

  • Authentication Systems — Provides evidence of identity or credential possession; it does not grant unrestricted access.
  • Identity and Access Management — Operates enrollment, assignment, lifecycle administration, and organization-wide access governance.
  • Security Architecture — Connects authorization to defense-in-depth, trust boundaries, service design, and resilience.
  • Application Security — Applies authorization checks to application operations, APIs, sessions, and object-level requests.
  • Network Security — Protects flows and access at network and remote-service boundaries.
  • Computer Security — Covers operating-system, hardware, endpoint, and platform protections that host enforcement points.
  • Security Operations — Monitors access events and responds to suspicious or prohibited behavior.
  • Security Risk Management — Prioritizes permissions and enforcement according to threats, business impact, and residual risk.
  • Privacy Engineering — Constrains personal data use, collection, sharing, and retention beyond the authorization layer.
  • Cybersecurity — Places authorization in wider governance, resilience, and security-program context.

The parent overview describes the general meaning and motivation of access control. The specialist articles in this directory develop the inner models, mechanisms, operational constraints, and evidence requirements. Articles in adjacent security divisions retain their own deeper treatment rather than being duplicated here.

Standards and Further Reading

These primary resources provide durable vocabulary and implementation guidance for the unit. Standards and recommendations describe particular requirements or methods; they do not replace a threat model or verification of a specific deployed product.

  • NIST SP 800-162 — Guide to Attribute Based Access Control — terminology for ABAC attributes, policies, and architectural decision and enforcement components.
  • NIST SP 800-207 — Zero Trust Architecture — continuous, context-aware decisions and policy enforcement in distributed systems.
  • NIST SP 800-53, Revision 5 — Security and Privacy Controls — Access Control family and associated identity, audit, assessment, and configuration controls.
  • OWASP Authorization Cheat Sheet — default denial, least privilege, per-request checks, and authorization testing in applications.
  • OWASP Authorization Patterns Cheat Sheet — practical enforcement patterns and common integration hazards.

Terminology, product capabilities, and implementation details should be checked against current authoritative documentation when a specific security system is commissioned or deployed.
