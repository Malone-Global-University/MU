Collection Overview

This directory supports the Computational Models parent article in the MGU Computer Science Encyclopedia. The parent explains why abstract models of computation are useful, how they omit implementation details, and how different models can express symbolic, stateful, parallel, probabilistic, and physically motivated computation. That existing overview remains the authoritative summary and is not rewritten by this index.

The seventeen specialist entries below propose focused studies of machine operations, state transitions, interacting processes, alternative computational paradigms, model equivalence, and cost assumptions. The collection is organized around the questions a model answers—what can be computed, how the system evolves, what resources it uses, and which physical assumptions matter—rather than by computing hardware brands or programming languages.

Models frequently overlap in expressive power while differing sharply in convenience or resource costs. A machine model suitable for a computability proof may not faithfully predict the performance of a cache-sensitive implementation; a distributed model may explicitly represent communication delays that a sequential model ignores. These differences motivate separate specialist entries.

How Computational Models Differ

Four dimensions are especially useful when comparing computational models.

DimensionKey questionExamples
State and representationWhat information can the model hold and manipulate?Tapes, registers, words, environments, markings, continuous values
Transition rulesWhat constitutes a valid computational step?Instruction execution, term reduction, token firing, message receipt
Interaction and uncertaintyHow are concurrent events, messages, randomness, or measurement represented?Process calculi, message passing, probabilistic machines, quantum circuits
Resources and assumptionsWhat costs are measured, and what idealizations are permitted?Time steps, bit operations, processors, communication rounds, finite precision

Computational equivalence must be distinguished from equal efficiency: two models may compute the same family of functions under suitable encodings while requiring different amounts of time, space, or communication to simulate a given computation.

Formal and Symbolic Machine Models

These models specify storage, operations, transition rules, and evaluation without relying on a particular physical machine.

  1. Random-Access Machine and Word-RAM Models — Instruction-cost assumptions, addressable words, memory access, and the difference between idealized and bounded word size. (planned)
  2. Register and Counter Machine Models — Small instruction sets, register updates, counter operations, and their computational power. (planned)
  3. Lambda Calculus as a Computational Model — Functions, substitution, reduction, evaluation strategies, and their relation to effective computation. (planned)
  4. Recursive Function Models — Primitive recursive operations, partial recursive functions, and the relationship between function definitions and computability. (planned)
  5. Operational Semantics and Abstract Machines — Machine configurations, small-step transitions, environment and store models, and execution semantics. (planned)

State, Interaction, and Concurrency Models

Models of reactive behavior describe not just input–output calculations but continuing interaction, synchronization, and communication.

  1. Transition Systems and State-Based Computation — State spaces, transition relations, reachable behavior, traces, and invariants. (planned)
  2. Petri Nets and Concurrent Computation — Places, transitions, markings, enabling rules, and the representation of independent events. (planned)
  3. Process Calculi and Communicating Systems — Communication primitives, process composition, equivalence, and interaction protocols. (planned)
  4. Dataflow and Stream Computation Models — Dependency graphs, tokens, streams, firing rules, and incremental or continuous processing. (planned)

Parallel, Distributed, Probabilistic, and Quantum Models

These frameworks alter the execution or information assumptions of computation and require explicit definitions of coordination and cost.

  1. Parallel Random-Access Machine Models — Shared-memory parallel steps, processor counts, and exclusive or concurrent memory-access variants. (planned)
  2. Distributed Message-Passing Models — Nodes, communication channels, synchrony assumptions, failures, and local versus global knowledge. (planned)
  3. Probabilistic Models of Computation — Random choices, probability distributions over executions, bounded-error decisions, and expected resource use. (planned)
  4. Quantum Circuit Models — Qubits, gates, measurements, circuit families, and the relationship between physical assumptions and abstract algorithms. (planned)

Model Comparison, Resource Costs, and Physical Limits

Model selection depends on which operations are permitted, which resources are charged, and which implementation constraints are retained.

  1. Computational Model Equivalence and Simulation — Encodings, simulations, computational equivalence, and the distinction between computability and efficiency. (planned)
  2. Computational Cost Models — Unit-cost versus bit-cost accounting, memory and communication costs, and the limits of cost comparisons across machines. (planned)
  3. Finite-Precision and Real-Number Computation — Finite representations, idealized real arithmetic, numerical models, and their mathematical versus physical assumptions. (planned)
  4. Analog and Hybrid Computational Models — Continuous states, differential-equation models, digital interfaces, and constraints on physical realization. (planned)

Suggested Study Sequence

First, compare sequential formalisms. Read the parent Computational Models overview, then examine random-access machines, register and counter machines, lambda calculus, recursive functions, and abstract machines. Ask what is encoded as a state, what constitutes a step, and what is assumed about memory or representation.

Second, extend from a single calculation to interaction. Study transition systems, Petri nets, process calculi, and dataflow models. The central questions become interleaving, synchronization, nondeterminism, and ongoing behavior.

Third, vary the computation assumptions. Explore parallel memory access, distributed message passing, randomized computation, and quantum circuits, being careful to distinguish a change in model definition from a claim of practical superiority.

Finally, compare models rigorously. Use the equivalence, cost, finite-precision, and analog/hybrid studies to evaluate simulation results and the limitations imposed by resource accounting or physical realization.
