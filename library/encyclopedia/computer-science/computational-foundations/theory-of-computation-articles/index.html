Collection Overview

This directory is a specialist companion to the Theory of Computation article in the Computational Foundations section of the MGU Computer Science Encyclopedia. The parent introduces three principal branches—computability, complexity, and formal languages and automata—and asks what tasks algorithms can solve, which models capture computation, and what resources are required.

The present directory translates that foundation into 18 proposed specialist articles organized in four groups. The list is a new editorial structure derived from the parent's scope; unlike some other MGU parents, the existing Theory of Computation page does not prescribe specific child titles or filenames. These entries are therefore proposed assignments for subsequent article production, not previously published contents.

Questions about computability concern whether a general algorithm exists. Questions about complexity concern resources required by an algorithm solving a computable problem. Formal-language theory provides rigorous examples of expressive power and algorithmic decision questions. These areas are related, but a proof that a problem is undecidable is not the same as evidence that a problem is computationally expensive.

This index is a navigational and editorial resource, not a replacement for the completed parent overview. It is distinct from the adjacent Automata Theory, Complexity Theory, and Computational Models overviews. Each child should emphasize the cross-cutting theoretical claim or proof obligation designated here rather than duplicating those broader entries.

Publication convention: all child hyperlinks are intentionally active planned destinations. A child link is not evidence that the article is currently live. When created, each physical file belongs in computational-foundations/theory-of-computation-articles/<slug>.html; public links omit .html.

Formal Foundations and Effective Computation

These articles turn the broad question “What is computation?” into precise mathematical objects. They distinguish a problem from its encoding, a mathematical function from a concrete program, and an idealized computational procedure from a physical implementation.

  1. Computational Problems and Formal Specifications

    Scope: Problem instances, mathematical input and output domains, decision and function formulations, correctness conditions, and the distinction between an abstract problem and a particular algorithm.

    Central learning task: Translate natural-language questions into well-specified mathematical problems; identify valid inputs and what constitutes a correct answer. Planned specialist article.

  2. Strings, Languages, and Decision Problems

    Scope: Finite alphabets, encoded instances, membership questions, languages as sets of strings, and why yes-or-no problems provide a common basis for theoretical comparisons.

    Central learning task: Show how binary encodings turn many mathematical problems into language-membership problems without treating encodings as the problems themselves. Planned specialist article.

  3. Computable Functions and Algorithmic Procedures

    Scope: Total and partial functions, finite descriptions, algorithmic steps, effective procedures, and how termination affects whether a procedure computes a promised result.

    Central learning task: Distinguish a total computable function from a partial computable one and identify the role of termination for specified domains. Planned specialist article.

  4. Universal Computation and Program Encodings

    Scope: Programs represented as data, universal interpreters, universal machines, encoded machine–input pairs, and the theoretical importance of self-interpretation.

    Central learning task: Explain universal simulation and effective encodings without claiming that every computational model supports the same operations at the same cost. Planned specialist article.

  5. The Church–Turing Thesis and Effective Procedures

    Scope: The thesis relating effectively calculable procedures to Turing computability, equivalent classical formalizations, and the difference between a mathematical thesis and a proven equivalence theorem.

    Central learning task: State the thesis accurately, distinguish it from formal model-equivalence results, and identify its assumptions and limits. Planned specialist article.

Computability, Decidability, and Impossibility Proofs

The question here is whether any algorithm can solve a stated problem on all relevant inputs. The children separate outright undecidability from failure to find an efficient algorithm, and distinguish semidecision procedures from guaranteed terminating decisions.

  1. Decidable and Recognizable Languages

    Scope: Deciders, recognizers, recursive and recursively enumerable languages, co-recognizability, closure consequences, and what nontermination means.

    Central learning task: Contrast guaranteed halting with one-sided recognition, and show how recognizability of both a language and its complement implies decidability. Planned specialist article.

  2. The Halting Problem and Its Variants

    Scope: Formal halting predicates, self-reference, universal-program inputs, restricted versus general instances, and why observing many terminated runs is not a general decision procedure.

    Central learning task: Construct the core contradiction carefully and distinguish general undecidability from solvable restricted cases. Planned specialist article.

  3. Diagonalization and Self-Reference

    Scope: Cantor-style diagonal arguments, machine enumeration, self-application, and constructions that demonstrate the nonexistence of universal decision procedures.

    Central learning task: Identify where self-reference enters a proof and explain why the constructed contradiction addresses an assumed universal algorithm. Planned specialist article.

  4. Mapping Reductions and Undecidability Proofs

    Scope: Computable transformations between decision problems, preservation of yes/no answers, direction of reductions, and the transfer of undecidability from established hard problems.

    Central learning task: Demonstrate the correct reduction direction; do not confuse computable many-one reductions with resource-bounded complexity reductions. Planned specialist article.

  5. Rice's Theorem and Program Semantics

    Scope: Nontrivial extensional properties of program-computed partial functions, semantic versus syntactic properties, and the theorem’s scope and exclusions.

    Central learning task: Distinguish a nontrivial property of computed behavior from a checkable property of source-code syntax. Planned specialist article.

Formal Languages and Recognition Boundaries

Formal languages provide controlled examples of differences in expressive power. This group connects classes of grammars to recognition procedures and proof techniques; the wider treatment of individual automata constructions remains in the separate Automata Theory parent article.

  1. Formal Grammars and the Language Hierarchy

    Scope: Productions, derivations, regular and context-free languages, context-sensitive and recursively enumerable languages, and containment among major language families.

    Central learning task: Explain why grammar restrictions affect language families while avoiding conflation of a grammar with the language it generates. Planned specialist article.

  2. Machine Models and Language Recognition

    Scope: What finite-state, stack-based, linearly bounded, and unrestricted machine resources permit in language recognition; equivalence principles at the class level.

    Central learning task: Compare computational power through accepted languages and resource assumptions rather than hardware labels. Planned specialist article.

  3. Closure and Decision Properties of Language Classes

    Scope: Union, complement, intersection, concatenation, emptiness, membership, equivalence, and which questions are decidable for which classes under stated assumptions.

    Central learning task: Distinguish language-class closure from decision-procedure availability, with examples where one property holds and another fails. Planned specialist article.

  4. Proofs of Nonregularity and Noncontext-Freeness

    Scope: Pumping arguments, closure-based contradictions, witness construction, and why pumping lemmas are necessary properties rather than complete classification algorithms.

    Central learning task: Use the right quantifier order when applying pumping lemmas and avoid inferring membership from passing a pumping condition. Planned specialist article.

Resources, Hardness, and Theoretical Boundaries

A solvable problem may still be difficult for feasible resource budgets. These topics connect the basic computability question to time, space, reductions, and hardness while reserving detailed complexity-class theory for the separate Complexity Theory parent article.

  1. Time and Space as Theoretical Resources

    Scope: Asymptotic step and memory measures, input-length functions, machine invariance principles, and the difference between computability and feasible computation.

    Central learning task: Describe a resource measure explicitly and recognize why model changes often affect costs even when computable functions are unchanged. Planned specialist article.

  2. Complexity Reductions and Completeness

    Scope: Polynomial-time reductions, tractability classes, complete problems, and the reasoning needed to establish both membership and hardness.

    Central learning task: Keep the direction and resource bound of a reduction explicit, distinguishing these reductions from unrestricted undecidability proofs. Planned specialist article.

  3. Intractability and Hardness Assumptions

    Scope: Worst-case versus typical-case instances, conditional hardness, implications for cryptography and optimization, and why unsolved separations require careful language.

    Central learning task: Separate proven lower bounds and conditional beliefs from practical benchmark observations; do not present P versus NP as resolved. Planned specialist article.

  4. Alternative Models and the Limits of Computation

    Scope: Classical models, randomness, quantum computation, interactive and distributed settings, physical assumptions, and disputes about hypercomputation.

    Central learning task: Clarify which extensions change efficiency or interaction and which would claim new computability power under additional physical or mathematical assumptions. Planned specialist article.

Choosing the Right Theoretical Method

Students frequently confuse the kind of conclusion being sought. The following table aligns recurring questions with suitable tools, so that one does not substitute practical evidence for a universal theorem or assume that an algorithm's inefficiency implies noncomputability.

QuestionAppropriate reasoningWhere to begin
What precisely constitutes an input instance?Specify alphabets, encodings, output obligations, and valid domains.Computational Problems and Formal Specifications
Does a procedure halt for all promised inputs?Distinguish total functions, partial procedures, recognizers, and deciders.Computable Functions and Algorithmic Procedures
Can any decider solve the problem?Use diagonalization, an impossibility theorem, or a computable reduction.Mapping Reductions and Undecidability Proofs
Can a program property be universally decided?Determine whether it is semantic, extensional, and nontrivial.Rice's Theorem and Program Semantics
Does a language belong to a restricted family?Use appropriate automata, grammars, closure properties, or nonmembership arguments.Proofs of Nonregularity and Noncontext-Freeness
How much time or memory is required?State the model, input-length function, resource measure, and quantifiers.Time and Space as Theoretical Resources
How is a complexity problem shown hard?Give a bound-preserving reduction from a known hard problem.Complexity Reductions and Completeness
Can a different computation model bypass a limit?Identify whether the claim changes cost, communication, probability, or computability assumptions.Alternative Models and the Limits of Computation

Methodological warning: successful tests and benchmark runs cannot by themselves establish a theorem about all inputs; likewise, a reduction in the wrong direction does not prove the intended hardness or undecidability result. A theoretical proof must make the relevant universality, termination, and resource conditions explicit.

Illustrative Theoretical Questions

Example 1 — Checking whether a program ever stops

Suppose a system is asked to decide, for every possible program and input, whether the program will terminate. That request defines a general decision problem about a program–input pair, not a task of monitoring one observed run. The The Halting Problem and Its Variants specialist topic will explain why there cannot be one algorithm that always answers correctly for every encoded program and input. The proof proceeds by effective self-reference, as developed in Diagonalization and Self-Reference. Restrictions on programming languages or bounded execution may permit particular decidable cases, but those do not yield a universal decider.

Example 2 — Recognizing a language with a stack

A student compares a pattern requiring arbitrary nested delimiters with a pattern recognized by a finite-state machine. The relevant question is not simply which program runs faster, but what memory structure the model has and which language family it recognizes. Machine Models and Language Recognition establishes the formal correspondence; Formal Grammars and the Language Hierarchy situates the corresponding language classes; and Proofs of Nonregularity and Noncontext-Freeness develops appropriate techniques for showing when a restricted class is inadequate.

Example 3 — A solvable problem with severe resource costs

An optimization task may have a brute-force algorithm that eventually finds an answer for each finite input. That establishes a kind of computability but says little about how well the procedure scales. Time and Space as Theoretical Resources defines the resource question. Complexity Reductions and Completeness develops conditional structural evidence of difficulty. Intractability and Hardness Assumptions separates theorems, unproved assumptions, and empirical performance on particular workloads.

Example 4 — A claim of an exotic computational breakthrough

A proposed randomized, quantum, or physically unusual machine should be evaluated on the claim it actually makes. A new way to obtain speedups for certain tasks does not automatically imply an ability to decide an undecidable language. The relevant comparisons belong in Alternative Models and the Limits of Computation and The Church–Turing Thesis and Effective Procedures; the detailed mathematics of quantum algorithms belongs to the separate Quantum Computing article.

These examples are instructional thought experiments, not descriptions of deployed MGU systems. Each illustrates a distinct theoretical question and the precise evidence necessary to address it.

Recommended Study Pathways

Foundational theory: start with Computational Problems and Formal Specifications and Strings, Languages, and Decision Problems. Continue to Computable Functions and Algorithmic Procedures, then Universal Computation and Program Encodings, and finally The Church–Turing Thesis and Effective Procedures. This order establishes a formal account of a problem before asking what a machine can compute.

Undecidability proof practice: read Decidable and Recognizable Languages, The Halting Problem and Its Variants, and Diagonalization and Self-Reference. Follow with Mapping Reductions and Undecidability Proofs and Rice's Theorem and Program Semantics. For each theorem, record the assumed algorithm, permitted reduction, contradiction, and exact conclusion.

Formal-language theory: study Strings, Languages, and Decision Problems, then Formal Grammars and the Language Hierarchy and Machine Models and Language Recognition. Finish with Closure and Decision Properties of Language Classes and Proofs of Nonregularity and Noncontext-Freeness. Consult the existing Automata Theory overview for full model constructions.

Complexity and applied theory: begin with Time and Space as Theoretical Resources, proceed to Complexity Reductions and Completeness and Intractability and Hardness Assumptions, and then compare Alternative Models and the Limits of Computation. Read Complexity Theory for the specialist study of complexity classes and lower-bound techniques.

Proof-checking discipline: whenever a proposed algorithm or theorem is discussed, ask whether inputs are finitely represented, whether termination is required, whether a quantifier ranges over all programs or only a restricted subset, and whether a claimed reduction respects the necessary computation bound. This practice is useful across all four groups.

Related Encyclopedia Topics

The following are existing broader or neighboring MGU Encyclopedia articles, not additional children of this index. Their entries offer fuller treatment of specialized subject domains. Use them where a theoretical subsection requires more detail than a cross-cutting survey can provide.

  • Automata Theory — Finite automata, context-free grammars, language recognition, and decidability in established models.
  • Complexity Theory — Detailed study of resource-bounded complexity classes, reductions, and separations.
  • Computational Models — Alternative formal machine models and their assumptions.
  • Algorithm Analysis — Proving procedural correctness and analyzing resource costs of concrete algorithms.
  • Discrete Mathematics — Sets, relations, logical proof techniques, combinatorics, and mathematical induction.
  • Binary Logic — Boolean operators and symbolic rules at the foundations of digital computation.
  • State Machines — State transitions, reactive behavior, specifications, and model checking.
  • Recursion — Recursive program definitions, reasoning, and termination.
  • Definition of a Computer — Conceptual boundaries of programmable computers and computation.
  • Graph Theory — Graphs, reachability, networks, and structural algorithmic problems.
  • Compilers — Translation and parsing of formal-language descriptions.
  • Algorithms — Practical algorithm design and computational procedures.
  • Cryptography — Security systems and computational hardness assumptions.
  • Quantum Computing — Physical models and algorithms of quantum computation.
  • Distributed Computing — Theoretical and operational implications of distributed execution.

Editorial scope must stay consistent: distinguish classical undecidability from complexity-class hardness, exact theorems from the Church–Turing thesis, algorithmic proof from empirical evidence, formal-language recognition from software parser design, and mathematical models from asserted physical machines.
