Collection Overview

This directory is a specialist companion to the Theory of Computation article in the Computational Foundations section of the MGU Computer Science Encyclopedia. The parent introduces three principal branches—computability, complexity, and formal languages and automata—and asks what tasks algorithms can solve, which models capture computation, and what resources are required.

The present directory translates that foundation into 18 proposed specialist articles organized in four groups. The list is a new editorial structure derived from the parent's scope; unlike some other MGU parents, the existing Theory of Computation page does not prescribe specific child titles or filenames. These entries are therefore proposed assignments for subsequent article production, not previously published contents.

Questions about computability concern whether a general algorithm exists. Questions about complexity concern resources required by an algorithm solving a computable problem. Formal-language theory provides rigorous examples of expressive power and algorithmic decision questions. These areas are related, but a proof that a problem is undecidable is not the same as evidence that a problem is computationally expensive.

This index is a navigational and editorial resource, not a replacement for the completed parent overview. It is distinct from the adjacent Automata Theory, Complexity Theory, and Computational Models overviews. Each child should emphasize the cross-cutting theoretical claim or proof obligation designated here rather than duplicating those broader entries.

Publication convention: all child hyperlinks are intentionally active planned destinations. A child link is not evidence that the article is currently live. When created, each physical file belongs in computational-foundations/theory-of-computation-articles/<slug>.html; public links omit .html.

How to read a theoretical claim: begin by identifying the problem family and input representation, then ask whether a terminating algorithm is required, which machine model is assumed, and whether resources are bounded. A negative result might establish that no algorithm can decide all inputs, that a restricted model cannot express a language, or that a certain cost bound cannot be achieved under a specified assumption. These are different kinds of impossibility and require different arguments.

Formal Foundations and Effective Computation

These articles turn the broad question “What is computation?” into precise mathematical objects. They distinguish a problem from its encoding, a mathematical function from a concrete program, and an idealized computational procedure from a physical implementation.

  1. Computational Problems and Formal Specifications

    Scope: Problem instances, mathematical input and output domains, decision and function formulations, correctness conditions, and the distinction between an abstract problem and a particular algorithm.

    Central learning task: Translate natural-language questions into well-specified mathematical problems; identify valid inputs and what constitutes a correct answer. Planned specialist article.

    Specialist brief: Precise problem statements begin with a domain of admissible instances, a chosen encoding, and an output obligation. For a decision problem, define the yes-instances rather than simply saying that an algorithm should “solve” the task. For a function problem, say what output is required on each valid input and how errors or invalid instances are treated. A proposed algorithm must be assessed against that specification: an implementation can terminate rapidly and still be incorrect because it answers a different question. This entry should introduce promise problems without treating an unstated promise as a universal guarantee.

  2. Strings, Languages, and Decision Problems

    Scope: Finite alphabets, encoded instances, membership questions, languages as sets of strings, and why yes-or-no problems provide a common basis for theoretical comparisons.

    Central learning task: Show how binary encodings turn many mathematical problems into language-membership problems without treating encodings as the problems themselves. Planned specialist article.

    Specialist brief: Finite strings provide a uniform way to represent instances of mathematical and computational questions. A decision problem can be represented as a language over a fixed finite alphabet, provided the encoding and validity conventions are specified. Readers should practice distinguishing a string from the object it encodes, and a language from a grammar or machine that describes it. The article should compare membership, emptiness, and equivalence questions at the level of formal problems, preparing students to understand why different representations can affect efficient solvability without changing the underlying yes-or-no content.

  3. Computable Functions and Algorithmic Procedures

    Scope: Total and partial functions, finite descriptions, algorithmic steps, effective procedures, and how termination affects whether a procedure computes a promised result.

    Central learning task: Distinguish a total computable function from a partial computable one and identify the role of termination for specified domains. Planned specialist article.

    Specialist brief: A total computable function returns an output on every input in its domain, whereas a partial computable function may be undefined because the procedure does not halt. That distinction is fundamental when interpreting program specifications and claims of universal termination. The planned treatment should contrast functional and decision-problem descriptions, illustrate computation by a step-by-step effective procedure, and distinguish finding one correct answer from providing an algorithm that meets its entire obligation. It should explain that a known upper bound on execution time is stronger than mere eventual termination, and that simulation of selected inputs proves neither property universally.

  4. Universal Computation and Program Encodings

    Scope: Programs represented as data, universal interpreters, universal machines, encoded machine–input pairs, and the theoretical importance of self-interpretation.

    Central learning task: Explain universal simulation and effective encodings without claiming that every computational model supports the same operations at the same cost. Planned specialist article.

    Specialist brief: When programs can be encoded as finite data, a single universal machine or interpreter can simulate many separately described computations. This property enables the formal treatment of interpreter execution, self-application, and the halting problem. The article should explain effective pairing of machine descriptions and inputs, the requirement that the universal simulation preserve the relevant outcome, and the difference between simulation overhead and computational expressiveness. Its purpose is not to describe a particular modern operating system or virtual machine, but to establish the abstraction that lets an algorithm receive another algorithm as part of its input.

  5. The Church–Turing Thesis and Effective Procedures

    Scope: The thesis relating effectively calculable procedures to Turing computability, equivalent classical formalizations, and the difference between a mathematical thesis and a proven equivalence theorem.

    Central learning task: State the thesis accurately, distinguish it from formal model-equivalence results, and identify its assumptions and limits. Planned specialist article.

    Specialist brief: The Church–Turing thesis connects the informal notion of an effectively calculable procedure to formal Turing computability. This statement should be distinguished from proven equivalences among formal models such as Turing machines, lambda calculus, and partial recursive functions. A proof of equivalence between mathematical definitions does not by itself prove a thesis involving an informal concept. This article should examine the historical motivation, the role of idealizations, and what follows when computational methods are assumed to be effective and finitely describable. It must not confuse differences in running time, quantum algorithm design, or speculative physical mechanisms with a settled violation of classical computability.

Computability, Decidability, and Impossibility Proofs

The question here is whether any algorithm can solve a stated problem on all relevant inputs. The children separate outright undecidability from failure to find an efficient algorithm, and distinguish semidecision procedures from guaranteed terminating decisions.

  1. Decidable and Recognizable Languages

    Scope: Deciders, recognizers, recursive and recursively enumerable languages, co-recognizability, closure consequences, and what nontermination means.

    Central learning task: Contrast guaranteed halting with one-sided recognition, and show how recognizability of both a language and its complement implies decidability. Planned specialist article.

    Specialist brief: A decider halts on every input and correctly determines membership; a recognizer may run forever on nonmembers while accepting every member. These machines answer different contractual questions even though both may accept the same yes-instances. The specialist study should establish examples of decidable, recognizable, and nonrecognizable languages, and develop the important result that a language is decidable when both it and its complement are recognizable. The argument requires dovetailing the two recognizers so that one eventually accepts; simply running one to completion first would not work when that computation could fail to halt.

  2. The Halting Problem and Its Variants

    Scope: Formal halting predicates, self-reference, universal-program inputs, restricted versus general instances, and why observing many terminated runs is not a general decision procedure.

    Central learning task: Construct the core contradiction carefully and distinguish general undecidability from solvable restricted cases. Planned specialist article.

    Specialist brief: The unrestricted halting problem asks whether an arbitrary encoded program halts on a specified input. A hypothetical total decider can be used to construct a program whose behavior contradicts the prediction made about its own encoding. The article should present that argument carefully and separate it from particular instances that can be decided through inspection, bounded execution, or restrictions on the programming model. It should also distinguish detecting a termination event in one real run from predicting termination for every possible program-input pair. This separation prevents appeals to testing or timeouts as purported solutions of a mathematical impossibility result.

  3. Diagonalization and Self-Reference

    Scope: Cantor-style diagonal arguments, machine enumeration, self-application, and constructions that demonstrate the nonexistence of universal decision procedures.

    Central learning task: Identify where self-reference enters a proof and explain why the constructed contradiction addresses an assumed universal algorithm. Planned specialist article.

    Specialist brief: Diagonal constructions use an assumed complete enumeration or universal procedure to define an object that differs at a strategically chosen position. Self-reference provides the mechanism behind several computability impossibility proofs, but the specific formal construction and its encoding assumptions must be shown. This study should compare Cantor-style diagonal reasoning with machine-based diagonalization, identify the logical quantifiers, and clarify exactly where the contradiction arises. The goal is a reusable proof technique, not the misleading impression that paradoxes or circular prose suffice to disprove the existence of an algorithm.

  4. Mapping Reductions and Undecidability Proofs

    Scope: Computable transformations between decision problems, preservation of yes/no answers, direction of reductions, and the transfer of undecidability from established hard problems.

    Central learning task: Demonstrate the correct reduction direction; do not confuse computable many-one reductions with resource-bounded complexity reductions. Planned specialist article.

    Specialist brief: A computable many-one reduction from A to B turns each instance of A into an instance of B while preserving the yes-or-no answer. To show that B is undecidable, begin with an established undecidable A and establish A reduces to B; reversing the direction generally does not support that conclusion. Readers should learn to construct the transformation, prove it is computable, establish both directions of the answer equivalence, and write out the contradiction obtained from assuming B decidable. The article must separate this computability-level reduction from polynomial-time reductions, whose additional resource bound supports different complexity-theoretic conclusions.

  5. Rice's Theorem and Program Semantics

    Scope: Nontrivial extensional properties of program-computed partial functions, semantic versus syntactic properties, and the theorem’s scope and exclusions.

    Central learning task: Distinguish a nontrivial property of computed behavior from a checkable property of source-code syntax. Planned specialist article.

    Specialist brief: Rice’s theorem addresses nontrivial extensional properties of the partial functions computed by programs under an appropriate effective programming model. For instance, asking whether an arbitrary program computes an everywhere-zero function is a semantic question, unlike checking whether its source text contains a particular token. The article should explain how nontriviality and extensionality enter the theorem and why its conclusion concerns a universal decision procedure. It should warn against the overbroad claim that all program questions are undecidable: syntactic checks, restricted language questions, and some semantic properties under suitable restrictions may be decidable.

Formal Languages and Recognition Boundaries

Formal languages provide controlled examples of differences in expressive power. This group connects classes of grammars to recognition procedures and proof techniques; the wider treatment of individual automata constructions remains in the separate Automata Theory parent article.

  1. Formal Grammars and the Language Hierarchy

    Scope: Productions, derivations, regular and context-free languages, context-sensitive and recursively enumerable languages, and containment among major language families.

    Central learning task: Explain why grammar restrictions affect language families while avoiding conflation of a grammar with the language it generates. Planned specialist article.

    Specialist brief: A grammar uses production rules to describe a set of strings; the restrictions on those rules shape which languages can be generated. The article should contrast regular, context-free, context-sensitive, and recursively enumerable language families, making their containment relationships precise and avoiding the misconception that every grammar uniquely determines one implementation strategy. Worked derivations can show how a context-free grammar generates balanced nesting while ordinary finite-state recognition cannot capture arbitrary depth. This entry remains a comparative survey of formal expressiveness: full parsing algorithms and grammar-engineering techniques belong in the neighboring Automata Theory and compiler subjects.

  2. Machine Models and Language Recognition

    Scope: What finite-state, stack-based, linearly bounded, and unrestricted machine resources permit in language recognition; equivalence principles at the class level.

    Central learning task: Compare computational power through accepted languages and resource assumptions rather than hardware labels. Planned specialist article.

    Specialist brief: Finite automata, pushdown automata, linearly bounded automata, and unrestricted Turing machines provide contrasting recognition resources. The intended treatment should state the kind of memory or control each model possesses and relate the accepted language families to those capacities. It should explain why a stack permits nested structure that pure finite-state control cannot handle and why additional machine resources change the kinds of languages recognized. Theoretical equivalences depend on exact definitions; students should not infer that all models execute with identical efficiency or that a modern hardware processor is itself the formal model used in a proof.

  3. Closure and Decision Properties of Language Classes

    Scope: Union, complement, intersection, concatenation, emptiness, membership, equivalence, and which questions are decidable for which classes under stated assumptions.

    Central learning task: Distinguish language-class closure from decision-procedure availability, with examples where one property holds and another fails. Planned specialist article.

    Specialist brief: Closure means that applying a specified operation to languages in a class yields another language in that class; decidability asks whether an algorithm can answer a specified question about descriptions of those languages. These are logically separate properties. For example, context-free languages are closed under union but not under arbitrary intersection, while some questions about context-free grammars have decision procedures and others do not. The planned article should organize a carefully qualified comparison by language class and operation, with constructions for positive results and counterexamples or reductions for negative ones. It should always state the input representation assumed for any decision procedure.

  4. Proofs of Nonregularity and Noncontext-Freeness

    Scope: Pumping arguments, closure-based contradictions, witness construction, and why pumping lemmas are necessary properties rather than complete classification algorithms.

    Central learning task: Use the right quantifier order when applying pumping lemmas and avoid inferring membership from passing a pumping condition. Planned specialist article.

    Specialist brief: Pumping lemmas give necessary conditions satisfied by languages in the relevant class; violating a pumping property can therefore prove nonmembership, but satisfying one does not prove membership. The article should teach the sequence of quantifiers in the regular-language pumping lemma and how to choose a witness string so that every permissible decomposition fails under an appropriate pumping choice. It should then contrast context-free pumping arguments and closure-based proofs of noncontext-freeness. A central learning result is disciplined proof construction: choose the right theorem, preserve its assumptions, and never infer the converse of a necessary condition.

Resources, Hardness, and Theoretical Boundaries

A solvable problem may still be difficult for feasible resource budgets. These topics connect the basic computability question to time, space, reductions, and hardness while reserving detailed complexity-class theory for the separate Complexity Theory parent article.

  1. Time and Space as Theoretical Resources

    Scope: Asymptotic step and memory measures, input-length functions, machine invariance principles, and the difference between computability and feasible computation.

    Central learning task: Describe a resource measure explicitly and recognize why model changes often affect costs even when computable functions are unchanged. Planned specialist article.

    Specialist brief: Once computability is established, resource analysis asks how the required operations and working storage scale with input length. The model and encoding determine what one step or memory unit costs, so a theoretical upper bound must name those assumptions. This article should distinguish worst-case, average-case, and expected measures, explain asymptotic notation in a model-sensitive setting, and illustrate why a procedure that halts for every input can still be unusable for large instances. The emphasis is the bridge between the existence of an algorithm and its resource requirements, not a replacement for the detailed complexity-class and algorithm-analysis units.

  2. Complexity Reductions and Completeness

    Scope: Polynomial-time reductions, tractability classes, complete problems, and the reasoning needed to establish both membership and hardness.

    Central learning task: Keep the direction and resource bound of a reduction explicit, distinguishing these reductions from unrestricted undecidability proofs. Planned specialist article.

    Specialist brief: Complexity-theoretic reductions preserve problem instances with an explicit computational budget, commonly polynomial time for classical NP-completeness. To claim that B is complete for a class, show both that B belongs to that class and that every problem in the class reduces appropriately to B, normally through transitivity from a known complete problem. This entry should show how to prove the transformation correct and how to avoid the frequent error of reducing a target problem to an already hard problem. It should also distinguish completeness under different reduction notions and avoid implying that NP-completeness proves the nonexistence of fast algorithms absent a class-separation theorem.

  3. Intractability and Hardness Assumptions

    Scope: Worst-case versus typical-case instances, conditional hardness, implications for cryptography and optimization, and why unsolved separations require careful language.

    Central learning task: Separate proven lower bounds and conditional beliefs from practical benchmark observations; do not present P versus NP as resolved. Planned specialist article.

    Specialist brief: Hardness can mean a proven lower bound in a specified model, a class-theoretic completeness result, or a conditional hypothesis used to motivate expectations about practical difficulty. These evidentiary levels are not interchangeable. The article should contrast worst-case and average-case complexity, explain why a brute-force baseline is not itself a lower bound, and illustrate conditional results based on unproved conjectures. It should state that NP-hardness does not automatically provide cryptographic one-wayness, nor does a hard worst-case instance imply typical inputs are difficult. Particular benchmark results should be reported as measurements under a workload rather than as universal theorems.

  4. Alternative Models and the Limits of Computation

    Scope: Classical models, randomness, quantum computation, interactive and distributed settings, physical assumptions, and disputes about hypercomputation.

    Central learning task: Clarify which extensions change efficiency or interaction and which would claim new computability power under additional physical or mathematical assumptions. Planned specialist article.

    Specialist brief: Alternative models may introduce random choices, quantum gates, interaction, parallel agents, communication, continuous quantities, or nonstandard physical assumptions. The first task is to identify whether a proposal changes the set of computable functions, the resource cost of computing them, or merely the interface by which computation is observed. Standard bounded-error probabilistic and quantum computational models do not by themselves establish decision procedures for the classical halting problem. The article should distinguish mathematical idealization from physically supported mechanisms, describe what evidence a claimed hypercomputer would need, and connect readers to specialized quantum and distributed-computing entries for deeper treatment.

Choosing the Right Theoretical Method

Students frequently confuse the kind of conclusion being sought. The following table aligns recurring questions with suitable tools, so that one does not substitute practical evidence for a universal theorem or assume that an algorithm's inefficiency implies noncomputability.

What precisely constitutes an input instance?Specify alphabets, encodings, output obligations, and valid domains.Computational Problems and Formal Specifications

Methodological warning: successful tests and benchmark runs cannot by themselves establish a theorem about all inputs; likewise, a reduction in the wrong direction does not prove the intended hardness or undecidability result. A theoretical proof must make the relevant universality, termination, and resource conditions explicit.

Illustrative Theoretical Questions

Example 1 — Checking whether a program ever stops

Suppose a system is asked to decide, for every possible program and input, whether the program will terminate. That request defines a general decision problem about a program–input pair, not a task of monitoring one observed run. The The Halting Problem and Its Variants specialist topic will explain why there cannot be one algorithm that always answers correctly for every encoded program and input. The proof proceeds by effective self-reference, as developed in Diagonalization and Self-Reference. Restrictions on programming languages or bounded execution may permit particular decidable cases, but those do not yield a universal decider.

Example 2 — Recognizing a language with a stack

A student compares a pattern requiring arbitrary nested delimiters with a pattern recognized by a finite-state machine. The relevant question is not simply which program runs faster, but what memory structure the model has and which language family it recognizes. Machine Models and Language Recognition establishes the formal correspondence; Formal Grammars and the Language Hierarchy situates the corresponding language classes; and Proofs of Nonregularity and Noncontext-Freeness develops appropriate techniques for showing when a restricted class is inadequate.

Example 3 — A solvable problem with severe resource costs

An optimization task may have a brute-force algorithm that eventually finds an answer for each finite input. That establishes a kind of computability but says little about how well the procedure scales. Time and Space as Theoretical Resources defines the resource question. Complexity Reductions and Completeness develops conditional structural evidence of difficulty. Intractability and Hardness Assumptions separates theorems, unproved assumptions, and empirical performance on particular workloads.

Example 4 — A claim of an exotic computational breakthrough

A proposed randomized, quantum, or physically unusual machine should be evaluated on the claim it actually makes. A new way to obtain speedups for certain tasks does not automatically imply an ability to decide an undecidable language. The relevant comparisons belong in Alternative Models and the Limits of Computation and The Church–Turing Thesis and Effective Procedures; the detailed mathematics of quantum algorithms belongs to the separate Quantum Computing article.

Example 5 — A language and its complement are recognizable

Suppose there is a recognizer for membership in a language L and another for membership in its complement. Neither recognizer is required to halt on its rejected inputs. Running the two machines in parallel by alternating simulation steps yields a decider: every string lies in exactly one of the languages, so one of the two machines must eventually accept, and the combined procedure can return the corresponding yes-or-no answer. This argument is a constructive relationship between recognition and decision; it does not mean a single recognizer can be turned into a decider without the complement recognizer. Study Decidable and Recognizable Languages.

Example 6 — Correct direction in an impossibility reduction

A student wants to prove that a hypothetical program-analysis question B is undecidable. They already know that the halting problem H is undecidable. The needed direction is H reduces to B: a computable transformation maps every encoded halting instance into an instance of B and preserves the answer. If B had a decider, this transformation would provide a decider for H, producing a contradiction. Merely showing B reduces to H would not establish B's undecidability. The same direction-of-implication discipline matters in resource-bounded hardness proofs, but the permitted reductions differ. Consult Mapping Reductions and Undecidability Proofs.

Example 7 — Counting is not the same as recognizing a pattern

Consider strings containing the same number of a symbols followed by b symbols, with all a symbols preceding all b symbols. A finite-state device cannot recognize the entire language, because arbitrarily large counts must be matched; a pushdown automaton can use stack depth to enforce the relationship. A proof of nonregularity uses the required pumping-lemma quantifiers or another sound method; drawing an inadequate finite-state diagram is not a proof. The comparison belongs to Machine Models and Language Recognition and Proofs of Nonregularity and Noncontext-Freeness.

These examples are instructional thought experiments, not descriptions of deployed MGU systems. Each illustrates a distinct theoretical question and the precise evidence necessary to address it.

Proof Obligations and Theoretical Evidence Framework

A computational claim is only as precise as its inputs, quantifiers, and model assumptions. The same phrase—“this problem cannot be solved efficiently”—can refer to an unconditional impossibility theorem, an unproved complexity conjecture, an inadequacy of a restricted automaton model, or a measurement from a collection of test cases. This framework helps readers choose the right evidence before interpreting results or selecting a specialist article.

Six checks before accepting a theorem-level conclusion

  1. Instance definition: identify the mathematical question and its finite input encoding, including validity restrictions and promises.
  2. Quantifiers: distinguish all inputs from selected examples; an existence claim from a universal claim; and some algorithm from one fixed algorithm.
  3. Computational model: specify the permitted operations, memory, nondeterminism, randomness, or other formal powers.
  4. Termination and correctness: decide whether recognition, decision, total-function computation, or approximation is being asserted.
  5. Resource and reduction bounds: record the exact reduction direction and any time, space, probability, or communication budget.
  6. Logical status: label a result as proved, conditional on a conjecture, a thesis about effective procedures, or empirical evidence.

This checklist is intended to support reading and proof construction, not to replace the specialist theorems. A formal proof of undecidability establishes a universal boundary for the named model, whereas a benchmark or prototype can establish only a bounded observation. Likewise, the Church–Turing thesis is not proved merely by showing that several formalizations of effective computation have the same extension.

Recommended Study Pathways

Foundational theory: start with Computational Problems and Formal Specifications and Strings, Languages, and Decision Problems. Continue to Computable Functions and Algorithmic Procedures, then Universal Computation and Program Encodings, and finally The Church–Turing Thesis and Effective Procedures. This order establishes a formal account of a problem before asking what a machine can compute.

Undecidability proof practice: read Decidable and Recognizable Languages, The Halting Problem and Its Variants, and Diagonalization and Self-Reference. Follow with Mapping Reductions and Undecidability Proofs and Rice's Theorem and Program Semantics. For each theorem, record the assumed algorithm, permitted reduction, contradiction, and exact conclusion.

Formal-language theory: study Strings, Languages, and Decision Problems, then Formal Grammars and the Language Hierarchy and Machine Models and Language Recognition. Finish with Closure and Decision Properties of Language Classes and Proofs of Nonregularity and Noncontext-Freeness. Consult the existing Automata Theory overview for full model constructions.

Complexity and applied theory: begin with Time and Space as Theoretical Resources, proceed to Complexity Reductions and Completeness and Intractability and Hardness Assumptions, and then compare Alternative Models and the Limits of Computation. Read Complexity Theory for the specialist study of complexity classes and lower-bound techniques.

Proof-checking discipline: whenever a proposed algorithm or theorem is discussed, ask whether inputs are finitely represented, whether termination is required, whether a quantifier ranges over all programs or only a restricted subset, and whether a claimed reduction respects the necessary computation bound. This practice is useful across all four groups.

Suggested practice sequence: write an exact decision-language definition for a small computing question; classify the meaning of its possible nontermination; prove a closure or nonmembership property; then review a reduction with the source and target problems explicitly labeled. For an advanced capstone, take an alleged faster or more powerful model and separate its formal operation set, known results, conjectured advantages, and claims about physical realizability. This sequence emphasizes mathematical habits rather than the number of articles visited.
