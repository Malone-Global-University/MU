Sampling Is a Representation Contract

Sampling converts a physical signal varying over continuous time into observations indexed by discrete time. It is not simply recording many measurements: every sample schedule expresses a claim about the frequencies, timing stability, filtering, and reconstruction relevant to the application. A sensor may record an accurate voltage at each sample instant yet create a misleading record of the underlying waveform if those instants are too widely separated.

A complete analysis specifies the signal class, the desired information, the analog input path, the sampling clock, the acquisition bandwidth, and the processing applied after capture. An ideal mathematical sample is a value of a continuous function at an exact instant. A practical sample comes from a converter and acquisition circuit, whose finite aperture, noise, clock uncertainty, and front-end bandwidth add further errors. The theorem developed here gives a powerful ideal baseline; it does not certify an unspecified instrument.

This article concerns the sampling theorem, aliasing, bandlimiting, reconstruction, and sample-rate selection. Converter implementation, quantization statistics, and clock-jitter circuit specifications have separate specialist articles in this unit. The objective is to understand what the sampling operation preserves, what it makes ambiguous, and how to design a defensible acquisition chain.

Continuous-Time and Discrete-Time Notation

Write a continuous-time signal as x(t), where t is measured in seconds. A uniform sample sequence is x[n] = x(n T_s) for every integer n in the observation interval. The sample interval T_s is the reciprocal of the sampling frequency f_s; for example, a 2,000 samples/second system uses T_s = 0.0005 s between ideal sample instants.

Continuous-time frequency f is measured in cycles per second (Hz), while the sampled sequence has a normalized angular frequency ω = 2π f/f_s radians per sample. Frequencies that are distinct in continuous time may give the same normalized frequency modulo integer multiples of 2π. A sampled sequence therefore knows sample order and sample values, but it does not uniquely disclose every possible continuous signal that could have produced them.

fs = 2_000.0
Ts = 1/fs
times = [n*Ts for n in range(6)]
assert times[1] - times[0] == Ts
print('Sample interval:', Ts, 'seconds')
print('First six instants:', times)

Sample Rate, Nyquist Frequency, and Bandwidth Are Different

The sample rate f_s is the number of uniformly spaced samples acquired each second. The Nyquist frequency is f_N = f_s/2: the boundary of the usual unique positive-frequency interval for a real-valued sampled sequence. A separate phrase, Nyquist rate, is commonly used for twice the highest frequency of a baseband signal under the ideal bandlimited theorem. The two quantities must not be confused.

The signal bandwidth is the spectral range required to describe the desired waveform; in a baseband measurement with significant content from DC through B Hz, B is the highest occupied frequency under the stated model. The analog bandwidth of a front end is the frequency range it actually passes with some specified response. A converter or amplifier may respond to frequencies far above the nominal Nyquist frequency and can therefore admit out-of-band interference unless an appropriate filter attenuates those components.

A Sinusoid Sampled at Uniform Instants

For the real sinusoid x(t)=A cos(2π f_0 t + φ), uniform sampling gives x[n]=A cos(2π(f_0/f_s)n+φ). The phase increment between adjacent samples is Δφ=2π f_0/f_s. This is the essential origin of aliasing: the sampled phase increments are interpreted modulo whole turns, because adding 2π k to the increment changes no samples for integer n.

If f_0 increases by an integer multiple of f_s, the sample values are unchanged for the same initial phase. A real cosine also cannot distinguish positive and negative discrete frequency, since cos(−θ)=cos(θ). For a sine, frequency reflection changes phase or sign, so a statement of identical waveforms must retain the phase convention. These facts are identities, not approximate claims about noisy ADCs.

from math import cos, pi, isclose
fs=1_000.0
phase=0.37
x700=[cos(2*pi*700*n/fs+phase) for n in range(100)]
xminus300=[cos(2*pi*(-300)*n/fs+phase) for n in range(100)]
assert all(isclose(a,b,abs_tol=1e-11) for a,b in zip(x700,xminus300))
print('700 Hz and -300 Hz produce the same real-cosine samples.')

Aliasing as a Many-to-One Mapping

An alias is a continuous-time frequency that yields the same sample sequence as another frequency under the relevant phase and signal model. Sampling by itself identifies classes of frequencies rather than a unique unbounded range. Aliasing is not caused by a faulty converter; it exists in the ideal mathematical operation, and real hardware adds rather than removes uncertainty.

Two possible source signals that agree at all sampled instants are indistinguishable to any subsequent deterministic digital processing of those samples. If an 8 kHz tone has already become an aliased in-band tone at a 10 kHz sample rate, digital filtering can remove that in-band output only by sacrificing desired in-band content as well. The appropriate preventive control is to constrain the analog input frequencies before sampling, or use a carefully designed alternative acquisition architecture.

Frequency folding can also make high-frequency sensor vibrations appear as slow movement, power-line harmonics appear in an unrelated band, and high-frequency switching interference appear to be low-frequency drift. These false components may look numerically clean because they are faithfully calculated from an ambiguous sequence.

Compute the Baseband Alias by Folding

For a real sinusoid of nonnegative frequency f sampled at f_s, choose an integer k such that f − k f_s lies in [−f_s/2,+f_s/2]. Its nonnegative folded frequency is f_alias = |f − k f_s|, with the understanding that the phase and orientation can change under reflection. One convenient robust expression is f_alias = |((f + f_s/2) mod f_s) − f_s/2|. At the Nyquist boundary multiple k choices describe the same absolute frequency.

For f=700 Hz and f_s=1,000 samples/s, select k=1. Then 700−1,000=−300 Hz, so the observer sees a 300 Hz component after the appropriate phase reflection. For f=2,300 Hz, the folded frequency is again 300 Hz because subtracting 2×1,000 gives 300 Hz. The specific physical component cannot be inferred from these samples alone.

def folded_frequency(f,fs):
    if fs<=0 or f<0: raise ValueError('require fs>0 and f>=0')
    return abs(((f+fs/2) % fs)-fs/2)
assert folded_frequency(700,1000)==300
assert folded_frequency(2300,1000)==300
assert folded_frequency(2900,1000)==100
for hz in (100,300,700,1300,2300,2900):
    print(hz, 'Hz ->', folded_frequency(hz,1000), 'Hz')

Why Sampling at Exactly Twice a Tone Frequency Is Fragile

A sinusoid exactly at f_0=f_s/2 has sample values A cos(πn+φ)=A(−1)^n cosφ. Its discrete sequence records only the cosine projection of its original phase. With φ=π/2, a nonzero sine wave is sampled only at zero crossings, yielding an identically zero sequence. The amplitude and phase cannot be uniquely recovered from that record without further assumptions.

This boundary example explains why practical sampling advice uses f_s>2B for a signal strictly confined below B Hz. Mathematical formulations involving boundary spectral values require careful treatment of whether energy is actually present at the boundary and the precise function class. In engineering, filter transition widths, sample clock error, and unknown spectral content make equality a poor design target.

from math import sin,pi
fs=1000
nyquist_tone=[sin(2*pi*(fs/2)*n/fs) for n in range(16)]
assert max(abs(v) for v in nyquist_tone)<1e-13
print('The ideal 500 Hz sine sampled at 1000/s yields only zeros.')

The Bandlimited Sampling Theorem

In the standard ideal baseband setting, suppose a continuous-time signal has no nonzero spectral content outside |f|≤B and that the signal satisfies the mathematical conditions needed for the reconstruction formula. If samples are taken uniformly at a rate f_s>2B, the signal can be reconstructed uniquely in the specified bandlimited class from an infinite, exact sample sequence. The strict inequality provides separation between shifted spectral copies and avoids the problematic ordinary boundary case.

The theorem does not say that every waveform can be faithfully sampled at any arbitrarily high but finite rate; an unbounded spectrum cannot satisfy the finite baseband premise. Nor does it assert that a finite, noisy collection of measurements reconstructs a waveform exactly, or that samples taken by a practical ADC equal perfect point evaluations. Those qualifications are not optional engineering footnotes: they delimit which conclusions follow from the theorem.

The theorem is a uniqueness and reconstruction statement about a restricted signal class. If an input violates the bandlimit premise, several candidate continuous signals can match the same samples. A reconstruction algorithm then returns a bandlimited representative, not proof of what the unfiltered analog input actually contained.

Why a Bandlimited Signal Is Special

A signal with finite spectral support has a constrained time-domain structure. Unlike an arbitrary continuous-time waveform, its values between uniformly spaced samples cannot vary independently in every possible way. When the spectral replicas do not overlap, the samples encode the full restricted spectrum; a suitable interpolation function reconstructs consistent values between observation instants.

The idealized mathematical picture commonly involves signals that extend indefinitely in time or mathematical limiting cases. A waveform that starts and stops abruptly at a finite interval generally has spectral energy extending beyond every finite frequency, even if most of its energy is concentrated in a useful band. Engineers therefore design with attenuation, measurement uncertainty, and tolerable residual aliases rather than claiming that a switch-on transient or finite-duration pulse is literally perfectly bandlimited.

Sampling Viewed as a Periodic Impulse Train

An ideal sampling model multiplies the continuous signal by a weighted impulse train: x_s(t)=x(t) Σ_n δ(t−nT_s). This object is a mathematical distribution, not the ordinary voltage waveform produced by a physical sample-and-hold circuit. The Fourier transform of the impulse train consists of equally spaced impulses at multiples of f_s. Multiplication in time corresponds to convolution in frequency.

Under the usual Fourier-transform convention, the sampled spectrum takes the form X_s(f)=(1/T_s) Σ_k X(f−k f_s). Each shifted copy has the same shape but is centered at an integer multiple of the sample frequency. If adjacent copies do not overlap in the desired band, ideal low-pass filtering can select the central copy (with an appropriate gain). If they do overlap, frequency components add together and a unique separation is generally impossible.

Deriving the No-Overlap Criterion

Suppose X(f) is zero outside [−B,B]. The central spectral copy occupies [−B,B] and the neighboring copy centered at +f_s occupies [f_s−B,f_s+B]. To guarantee a gap between them, require B<f_s−B, or f_s>2B. The negative-frequency side gives the same condition.

This is the spectral explanation of the baseband sampling theorem. It connects the time-domain phase alias formula to a frequency-domain picture: modulo-frequency equivalence appears because the sampler periodically replicates the spectrum. The same derivation also explains why slowing the sample clock without changing analog filtering can cause a previously acceptable capture to become aliased.

def spectral_replicas_disjoint(fs,bandlimit):
    return fs>2*bandlimit
assert spectral_replicas_disjoint(9000,4000)
assert not spectral_replicas_disjoint(8000,4000)
assert not spectral_replicas_disjoint(7000,4000)
print('Strictly separated baseband replicas require fs > 2B.')

Ideal Sinc Reconstruction

Under standard ideal bandlimited assumptions, the continuous signal may be expressed as x(t)=Σ_n x[n] sinc((t−nT_s)/T_s), where sinc(u)=sin(πu)/(πu) for nonzero u and sinc(0)=1. Every sinc interpolant equals one at its own sample instant and zero at the other integer-spaced sample instants. As a result, the sum passes exactly through all ideal samples while forming the uniquely consistent bandlimited waveform in the assumed signal class.

This formula uses an infinite set of samples and ideal filters with infinite support, which cannot be implemented literally with finite latency or finite memory. A finite sinc kernel introduces truncation ripple, and a practical digital resampler uses finite-duration anti-alias filters and carefully specified phase and endpoint behavior. A well-designed approximation can be excellent without being mathematically identical to the infinite ideal.

from math import sin,pi

def sinc(x):
    return 1.0 if abs(x)<1e-13 else sin(pi*x)/(pi*x)
assert abs(sinc(0)-1)<1e-14
assert all(abs(sinc(k))<1e-12 for k in range(-10,11) if k)
print('Sinc kernels interpolate exact uniform sample positions.')

A Finite-Window Reconstruction Demonstration

To see a practical limitation, form a finite sum of sinc terms around a target instant. For a sufficiently low sinusoid, increasing the number of ideal sample terms usually improves the interior estimate. It does not, however, automatically give exact results at every finite truncation boundary; the infinite-series theorem and a finite-duration implementation are different objects.

from math import pi,cos,sin

def sinc(u):
    return 1 if abs(u)<1e-12 else sin(pi*u)/(pi*u)

def truncated_reconstruction(f,fs,t,halfwidth):
    return sum(cos(2*pi*f*n/fs)*sinc(t*fs-n)
               for n in range(-halfwidth,halfwidth+1))
fs=1000.0
exact=cos(2*pi*125*0.00225)
short=truncated_reconstruction(125,fs,.00225,5)
long=truncated_reconstruction(125,fs,.00225,80)
print('Exact:',round(exact,6),'11-term:',round(short,6),
      '161-term:',round(long,6))
assert abs(long-exact)<0.01

Ideal Reconstruction Versus Zero-Order Hold

A zero-order hold (ZOH) outputs the latest sample value until the next update, generating a staircase waveform. Many digital-to-analog output paths use some version of a hold, but a staircase is not the same as ideal sinc interpolation. A hold introduces a frequency-dependent response, including high-frequency attenuation and spectral images, and ordinarily requires an analog reconstruction filter for a smooth desired output.

The normalized magnitude of an ideal rectangular hold response is proportional to |sin(π f T_s)/(π f T_s)|, with phase delay corresponding to the hold interval. It attenuates higher in-band frequencies (passband droop) while leaving images that a reconstruction filter can suppress. Depending on the DAC architecture, pulse shapes, internal oversampling, and reconstruction may differ; do not assume every physical DAC presents a textbook rectangular ZOH to the load.

This distinction prevents an important category error. A sequence of perfect digital samples represents a bandlimited signal under the theorem, but merely joining points or holding the last value is an approximation procedure with its own frequency response.

Sample-and-Hold Acquisition Is Also Not an Ideal Impulse

On the input side, practical converters acquire a voltage over a nonzero duration. Depending on architecture, the sample circuit settles an acquisition capacitor, holds it for conversion, or integrates over a specified aperture. Those physical operations interact with source impedance, amplifier settling, signal bandwidth, and clock timing. The simplest model assumes a point sample after adequate settling; real instruments must justify that approximation.

An input that changes rapidly during the acquisition aperture can be attenuated or distorted by aperture averaging, while insufficient settling after multiplexed channel changes creates a different error. These effects coexist with aliasing but are not aliases in the strict spectral-replica sense. System documentation should identify the effective sample time, analog bandwidth, and aperture behavior separately from converter bits and throughput.

Aliasing and Quantization Are Different Information Losses

Aliasing arises because two or more continuous-time frequency contents can generate the same sampled sequence. Quantization arises because a converter maps a continuous amplitude into a finite set of numeric codes. An ideal 24-bit converter sampling an unfiltered 700 Hz input at 1,000 samples/s cannot infer whether the measured 300 Hz component came from 300 Hz or from 700 Hz solely from those samples. A lower-bit converter with appropriate anti-alias filtering may preserve frequency interpretation more reliably.

The two effects demand different controls. To reduce aliasing, constrain the analog bandwidth or change the sample strategy. To reduce quantization effects, select suitable conversion range, resolution, signal conditioning, or oversampling and decimation under appropriate assumptions. More bits cannot repair information lost through spectral overlap; more samples cannot guarantee amplitude accuracy if the front end clips or has unacceptable reference drift.

Anti-Alias Filtering Must Precede the Ambiguous Sample

A conventional baseband measurement chain places a filter before the ADC to attenuate frequencies above the useful measurement band. This is an analog anti-alias filter, even when an oversampled converter later uses digital filtering. The filter is designed to ensure that any source content capable of folding into the measurement band is sufficiently suppressed before the critical sampling operation.

If an out-of-band component and a desired component already map to the same discrete frequency, no ordinary digital filter operating only on that output can generally determine which part came from which analog frequency. Digital filtering remains valuable for further downsampling and in-band conditioning, but its role is complementary to—not a universal replacement for—the necessary analog control of unwanted spectral content.

The practical engineering problem is never simply “put a filter at exactly half the sample rate.” Filter gain falls gradually; a useful acquisition specification includes passband flatness, passband edge, transition width, required stopband attenuation, relevant out-of-band interference range, and acceptable phase/group-delay behavior.

Passband, Transition Band, and Stopband

A low-pass anti-alias filter allows desired frequencies through its passband, rolls off across a transition region, and attenuates unwanted higher frequencies in its stopband. A realizable filter has finite steepness. To preserve a 0–3 kHz baseband at 8 ksample/s, the Nyquist frequency is 4 kHz; the available 3–4 kHz transition interval is 1 kHz. That does not imply that an arbitrary inexpensive filter will provide adequate attenuation by 4 kHz.

Moreover, stopband requirements can extend far above the Nyquist frequency. A high-frequency component at, for example, 51 kHz also folds into the sampled baseband when sampling at 8 ksample/s (51−6×8=3 kHz), provided the analog input path passes it. The entire frequency range admitted by the sensor and ADC input, not only the narrow strip just above Nyquist, is relevant to a credible interference budget.

fs=8000.0
passband_edge=3000.0
nyquist=fs/2
transition=nyquist-passband_edge
assert transition==1000
# A component at 51 kHz folds to 3 kHz at 8 ksample/s.
assert abs(((51_000+4000)%8000)-4000)==3000
print('Available transition width:',transition,'Hz')

Calculate Required Interference Rejection

Suppose an unwanted analog sinusoid has an amplitude of 1 V at a frequency that aliases into the desired passband, while the maximum permitted spur in that output band is 1 mV. Under a linear, magnitude-only design model, its amplitude must be reduced by at least a factor of 1,000. The corresponding rejection is 20 log10(1,000)=60 dB. This is a minimum at the offending frequency, not a statement that all real filters meet it.

A full specification includes margins for component variation, temperature, aging, gain tolerance, clock-rate range, and unanticipated interference. Multiple out-of-band noise contributions must be considered as a spectrum or noise-power budget; they cannot always be modeled as a single worst-case tone. If spurious energy is specified as a power ratio, 10 log10(P1/P2) is used instead of the voltage-amplitude expression under compatible impedances.

from math import log10
unwanted_peak=1.0
allowed_peak=0.001
rejection_db=20*log10(unwanted_peak/allowed_peak)
assert round(rejection_db,8)==60
print('Minimum attenuation for this illustrative tone:', rejection_db, 'dB')

Why the Filter Corner Is Not a Brick-Wall Boundary

The cutoff frequency named in a filter datasheet is typically associated with a specified attenuation such as −3 dB, not with sudden elimination of all higher-frequency content. A first-order low-pass network has a gradual roll-off, approximately 20 dB per decade at sufficiently high frequency, while higher-order responses can be steeper. The exact result depends on the topology, passband goals, frequency ratio and loading.

Consider an illustrative first-order low-pass magnitude |H(f)|=1/sqrt(1+(f/f_c)^2). With a 3 kHz corner and a 4 kHz input, attenuation is only about −4.44 dB, far from the 60–80 dB needed in many precision acquisitions. Even high-order filters need finite transition room and care with phase, group delay, tolerances, and stability. Sample-rate selection and analog filter complexity are therefore coupled design decisions.

from math import sqrt, log10
fc=3000.0
for f in [1000.0,3000.0,4000.0,12000.0]:
    magnitude=1/sqrt(1+(f/fc)**2)
    print(f,'Hz',round(20*log10(magnitude),2),'dB')
assert -4.5 < 20*log10(1/sqrt(1+(4000/fc)**2)) < -4.3

Sampling Rate Is a System Choice, Not Just Twice a Number

A sample rate must leave adequate space for the actual analog filter to attenuate nuisance frequencies before they fold into the band of interest. If the desired highest frequency is 3 kHz, choosing exactly 6 ksample/s leaves no transition band at all between 3 kHz and the Nyquist boundary. Choosing 8, 12, or 16 ksample/s may allow progressively wider transition regions, although acquisition throughput, data storage, interface performance, and clock quality also matter.

An engineering selection procedure starts by documenting useful bandwidth and maximum allowed spectral error, then characterizing the available front-end transfer function. It calculates a candidate rate from filter passband, rejection, and output-band requirements. It finally checks channel multiplexing, input settling, ADC conversion rate, clock uncertainty, downstream bandwidth, acquisition duration, and any later decimation. Arbitrary rules such as “always sample ten times faster” may be useful rough rules of thumb but are not substitutes for a measurable error budget.

Oversampling Expands Design Options Without Violating the Theorem

Oversampling means taking samples at a rate exceeding the minimum required by the useful signal bandwidth. It can ease the design of analog transition bands and, in suitable noise and quantization models, support digital noise filtering followed by decimation. It does not remove a strong out-of-band analog interferer that has already folded into the signal passband at an earlier stage. Nor does it automatically increase the meaningful accuracy of a sensor whose noise, nonlinearity or reference error dominates.

For example, a recorder might sample a desired 0–5 kHz waveform at 80 ksample/s and digitally low-pass it before creating an output sequence at 20 ksample/s. The initial analog input must still be adequately constrained relative to the 80 ksample/s sampling event. Before dropping to 20 ksample/s, a digital filter must attenuate components that would fold into the final 0–10 kHz output interval. These are two distinct anti-alias requirements at two different sampling stages.

original_rate=80_000
output_rate=20_000
factor=original_rate//output_rate
assert factor==4
print('First Nyquist:',original_rate/2,'Hz')
print('Post-decimation Nyquist:',output_rate/2,'Hz')
print('Decimation factor:',factor)

Decimation: Filtering Before Keeping Fewer Samples

Decimation by integer factor D retains one sample out of each D samples after applying a suitable low-pass filter. The new sample rate becomes f_s/D. If the original sequence contains frequencies above the new Nyquist limit, naively retaining every D-th sample aliases those components into the narrower output band. A digital anti-alias low-pass filter must operate before, or as part of, decimation.

The mathematical operation is sometimes represented as y[n] = (h*x)[Dn], where h is a discrete-time filter and * denotes convolution. Efficient polyphase filters avoid calculating intermediate output samples that would immediately be discarded. The shape and effective cutoff of h are chosen for the final rate, not automatically for the original one.

from math import sin,pi
fs=8000.0
# This 3 kHz tone is valid at 8 ksample/s but not at 2 ksample/s.
samples=[sin(2*pi*3000*n/fs+0.4) for n in range(80)]
naive=samples[::4]
assert len(naive)==20
# A 3 kHz sinusoid folds to 1 kHz after naive 4:1 decimation.
assert abs(((3000+1000)%2000)-1000)==1000
print('Decimation without filtering changes the interpretation.')

A Transparent Finite-Impulse-Response Low-Pass Example

A simple moving average is a finite-impulse-response (FIR) filter with equal weights. It can be useful for smoothing, but its frequency response has a sinc-like shape with sidelobes and is generally not a high-performance anti-alias filter. Demonstrating its frequency response is an opportunity to distinguish “a signal looks smoother” from “the filter meets a required stopband attenuation.”

A more demanding resampling path may require a carefully designed FIR window or equiripple filter, or an IIR filter where its phase and transient behavior are acceptable. Every candidate should be characterized with passband ripple, stopband attenuation, transition width, group delay, processing latency, and arithmetic range. The selection is linked to the signal's correctness budget rather than just its average bandwidth.

from math import sin,pi

def boxcar_gain(f,fs,taps):
    # Complex-exponential amplitude response magnitude.
    return abs(sum(complex(__import__('math').cos(-2*pi*f*k/fs),
                           __import__('math').sin(-2*pi*f*k/fs))
                   for k in range(taps))/taps)
assert abs(boxcar_gain(0,8000,4)-1)<1e-12
assert boxcar_gain(2000,8000,4)<1e-12
assert boxcar_gain(3000,8000,4)>0
print('Moving averages have nulls and nonzero sidelobes.')

Polyphase Resampling and Rational Rate Conversion

A rate change by rational factor L/M can be described as upsampling by L, filtering, and downsampling by M, with appropriate filter normalization. Upsampling inserts conceptual zero-valued intermediate samples; filtering forms useful intermediate values and suppresses spectral images. The final downsampling also requires suppression of frequencies that would alias at the reduced output rate.

A polyphase implementation reorganizes the arithmetic to avoid constructing a large array of inserted zeros. In the SciPy ecosystem, scipy.signal.resample_poly implements polyphase FIR resampling and exposes window, padding, and rate-ratio choices. It is helpful for offline research and production code, but its output still depends on chosen filter coefficients and boundary conditions. A software function cannot retroactively undo aliases already present in its input samples.

For example, converting 48,000 to 44,100 samples/s has the reduced rational ratio 147/160. Conversion quality depends on the transition band, filter design, precision, and handling of file edges; an apparent output length alone cannot establish fidelity.

from math import gcd
in_rate=48000; out_rate=44100
g=gcd(in_rate,out_rate)
up=out_rate//g
down=in_rate//g
assert (up,down)==(147,160)
assert in_rate*up//down==out_rate
print('Rational resampling factor:',up,'/',down)

Upsampling Alone Adds No New Analog Information

Interpolating a sequence from 10 ksample/s to 100 ksample/s can produce a visually smoother digital waveform and may be useful for DAC interfacing or further signal processing. It does not create ten times the original physical measurement information. Every output estimate still depends on the assumptions and frequency content of the original capture. If two analog inputs produced the same 10 ksample/s record, interpolation cannot determine which one occurred.

In particular, a sequence containing a 2 kHz alias from an unseen 8 kHz analog input at 10 ksample/s will interpolate as a 2 kHz discrete-time pattern under the normal low-pass reconstruction. The 8 kHz origin is not restored merely by increasing the output sample count. A higher-rate new acquisition with appropriate analog filtering can collect new information; resampling an old record cannot.

Sample Clock Precision and Jitter

The sampling theorem assumes precisely uniform instants. In practice, sample instants may differ from their nominal schedule by deviations δt_n. For a differentiable signal, the first-order error is x(t_n+δt_n)−x(t_n) ≈ x′(t_n)δt_n. A sinusoid of amplitude A and frequency f has maximum slope magnitude 2πfA, so a given timing deviation grows more consequential as frequency rises.

For a sine wave with appropriate assumptions on small uncorrelated timing error and signal phase, a widely used jitter-limited SNR estimate is SNR_jitter≈−20 log10(2π f σ_t) dB, where σ_t is RMS timing jitter. This is an estimate for a specified signal and noise model, not a universal converter specification. Clock phase noise, aperture uncertainty, synchronization between channels, and deterministic timing spurs can require more detailed models.

The key distinction is that changing the nominal rate does not automatically fix an inaccurate clock or aperture. This unit's Converter Clock Jitter and Timing article treats the clock hardware and measurement details at greater depth.

from math import pi,log10
f=100_000.0
rms_jitter=1e-12
snr=-20*log10(2*pi*f*rms_jitter)
assert 120<snr<130
print('Illustrative jitter-limited SNR:',round(snr,2),'dB')

Finite Observation Records and Spectral Resolution

In a finite measurement of N equally spaced samples at rate f_s, the record duration is approximately N/f_s seconds under the usual sample-record convention. The discrete Fourier transform (DFT) has a nominal bin spacing Δf=f_s/N. Increasing the rate while holding N fixed increases bin spacing and shortens the observation interval; it does not automatically improve the ability to resolve nearby tones.

The available observation length matters because finite records constrain how finely one can distinguish close frequencies in noise. Window shape and estimator type also determine the practical resolution and uncertainty. The DFT grid is a convenient set of analysis frequencies rather than a proof that source signals can occur only at integer-bin frequencies.

fs=10_000
N=1000
seconds=N/fs
bin_spacing=fs/N
assert seconds==0.1
assert bin_spacing==10
print('Record duration:',seconds,'s; DFT bin spacing:',bin_spacing,'Hz')

Frequency Leakage Is Not Aliasing

If a finite record cuts across a sinusoid that does not complete an integer number of cycles in that record, the DFT typically spreads its energy across multiple bins. This spectral leakage is a consequence of observing a finite window and multiplying the infinite signal by that window. It is different from aliasing, which is caused by periodic frequency ambiguity at sampling. A record can be free of analog aliases and still display leakage.

Using a Hann or another tapering window can reduce distant sidelobes but broadens the main lobe and changes amplitude correction. Coherent sampling—choosing record duration so the tone spans an integer number of cycles—can sharply reduce leakage for a stable sinusoid under appropriate conditions. Neither technique reconstructs unknown out-of-band frequencies after sampling. Users should not “fix aliasing” by changing only the FFT window.

from math import sin,pi
N=64; fs=1024; f=170
x=[sin(2*pi*f*n/fs) for n in range(N)]
hann=[0.5-0.5*__import__('math').cos(2*pi*n/(N-1)) for n in range(N)]
assert len(x)==len(hann)==N
assert all(0<=w<=1.0+1e-12 for w in hann)
print('The window changes leakage, not analog aliasing.')

An Explicit Small DFT to Inspect a Sampled Tone

A DFT maps a finite discrete record into discrete-frequency bins. It is computationally feasible to demonstrate on a short record without introducing a large third-party dependency. The code below places a sinusoid exactly on a DFT bin, making its principal energy easy to locate. With a non-bin-centered tone, energy appears across multiple bins, even when the original tone lies below Nyquist.

import cmath
from math import cos,pi
N=32; fs=320.0; frequency=50.0
x=[cos(2*pi*frequency*n/fs) for n in range(N)]
X=[sum(x[n]*cmath.exp(-2j*pi*k*n/N) for n in range(N)) for k in range(N)]
strong=sorted(range(N),key=lambda k:abs(X[k]),reverse=True)[:2]
assert set(strong)=={5,27}
print('Dominant DFT bin indices:',sorted(strong))

FFT Axes, Real-Valued Symmetry, and Negative Frequency

A real-valued signal's DFT has conjugate symmetry: components at positive and negative discrete frequencies are paired. Plotting only positive frequencies up to Nyquist is customary for a one-sided spectrum, but normalization and amplitude factors depend on whether one is displaying amplitude, power, power spectral density or an unscaled transform. Mislabeling a one-sided amplitude spectrum as a two-sided power density can lead to substantial quantitative errors.

The samples x[n] do not physically contain “negative-frequency vibrations” as separate classical objects; complex exponentials provide a convenient mathematical representation. Negative frequencies make the aliasing equations, analytic signals, and bandpass translation coherent. They also prevent common sign mistakes when converting between real cosines and complex-valued I/Q captures.

Complex Sampling and I/Q Signals

In-phase/quadrature (I/Q) systems represent a bandpass waveform by complex samples after mixing and appropriate low-pass filtering. The sampled sequence may then distinguish positive and negative baseband offsets because it retains both quadratures. This differs from a single real-valued channel in which a cosine frequency reflection may have equivalent output values after phase adjustment.

Complex sampling does not repeal aliasing. Digital frequencies remain periodic modulo the sample rate, and front-end filtering, mixer images, local oscillator offsets, quadrature mismatch and clock errors matter. The effective supported bandwidth should be defined by the actual complex sampling rate and the occupied band, not inferred from the physical RF carrier frequency alone.

import cmath
from math import pi
fs=1000
positive=[cmath.exp(2j*pi*100*n/fs) for n in range(10)]
negative=[cmath.exp(-2j*pi*100*n/fs) for n in range(10)]
assert positive[1]!=negative[1]
assert abs(positive[0]-negative[0])<1e-12
print('Complex I/Q samples distinguish frequency orientation.')

Bandpass Sampling and Deliberate Undersampling

For a known narrowband signal centered at a high carrier frequency, engineers can deliberately sample below twice that carrier frequency and allow a whole isolated band to fold into a lower-frequency interval. This is called bandpass sampling or intentional undersampling. Its design is valid only when the original occupied band and all relevant interference are constrained so that the desired folded band does not overlap itself or other folded content.

The simple baseband condition f_s>2f_high is sufficient for straightforward low-pass reconstruction but may be unnecessarily strong for carefully filtered bandpass signals. There are additional bandpass sampling-rate inequalities that depend on the band edges and folding zones. Their existence is not permission to sample an arbitrary 100 MHz waveform at 1 MHz with no RF preselection. Once frequencies fold into the same band, the same irrecoverable ambiguity arises.

When intentional aliasing is used as frequency translation, the design specification should state desired RF band, front-end bandpass filter shape, final alias interval, spectral reversal, local oscillator or clock stability, and images from neighboring bands.

Harmonics and Nonlinear Front Ends

Even when the physical source is approximately a single tone, amplifiers, sensor interfaces, mixers, and ADC input circuits can generate harmonic distortion at integer multiples of the source frequency. For a 1 kHz fundamental sampled at 8 ksample/s, harmonics at 5 kHz or 7 kHz can fold into 3 kHz and 1 kHz, respectively. Thus, out-of-band content may originate inside the measurement chain rather than only from the external environment.

Harmonics generated before the anti-alias filter may be attenuated by that filter; nonlinearities arising after the filter can create new high-frequency components closer to the sampling stage. Real hardware placement matters. A robust design considers sensor behavior, front-end nonlinearity, amplifier headroom, switching interference, and whether the converter's own analog input circuit admits or creates spurious responses.

def fold(f,fs):return abs(((f+fs/2)%fs)-fs/2)
fs=8000
assert fold(5000,fs)==3000
assert fold(7000,fs)==1000
print('5 kHz and 7 kHz harmonics can fold to 3 kHz and 1 kHz.')

Broadband Noise and Noise Folding

Out-of-band noise is usually not concentrated at one frequency. A real acquisition path can contain thermal noise, switching noise, sensor noise, and interference over a wide bandwidth. Sampling folds translated copies of the noise spectrum into the measured baseband. A result can therefore show an elevated in-band noise floor even without a conspicuous aliased tone.

The amount of folded noise depends on the continuous input power spectral density, analog filter response, effective input bandwidth and sampling rate. Integrated noise is a power calculation, not the sum of absolute noise voltages. Narrowing the analog bandwidth before sampling can improve the measurement even when the digitizer's nominal quantization step is much smaller than the source noise.

Some converter architectures use high oversampling ratios and digital decimation filters to reduce quantization noise in the final band, but they still have front-end noise and analog alias-rejection constraints. Specifying “no aliasing visible on a tone test” is not sufficient evidence for a low-noise broadband measurement.

Multichannel Sampling and Simultaneity

If a digitizer multiplexes several analog channels through one converter, each channel may be observed at a different instant. The advertised aggregate conversion throughput may exceed the per-channel sampling rate. For four channels scanned at an aggregate 40 ksample/s, the nominal per-channel rate is 10 ksample/s under an equal allocation, with interchannel skew determined by the scan schedule.

For a rapidly changing physical phenomenon, sequential channel capture can distort inferred phase, correlation or instantaneous power even when each individual channel satisfies its own bandlimit constraint. Truly simultaneous-sampling channels or carefully measured timestamp offsets may be required for vibration phase comparison, power-quality analysis and synchronized sensor fusion. An acquisition plan must document per-channel rate and skew, not just the aggregate figure.

aggregate=40_000; channels=4
per_channel=aggregate/channels
assert per_channel==10_000
print('Per-channel rate under equal round-robin allocation:',per_channel)

Timing, Triggering, and Timebase Drift

Ideal uniform sampling presumes sample times can be associated accurately with elapsed physical time. A clock that runs 100 parts per million fast changes the effective physical sample rate and therefore shifts inferred frequencies if the nominal rate is used in analysis. Trigger uncertainty affects the alignment of records, while dropped samples or asynchronous data transfers create irregular gaps that a naive array index conceals.

A sound measurement record carries its timebase assumptions: actual or calibrated sampling rate, clock reference, trigger method, missing-sample behavior, timestamp provenance, and channel synchronization. If the clock drifts over a long record, a static FFT-axis label may misrepresent the physical frequency. Such effects are related to sample timing but are distinct from the classical frequency folding caused by insufficient bandwidth separation.

Nonuniform Sampling Requires Its Own Model

Uniform sampling gives a particularly simple repetition of the Fourier spectrum and a sinc reconstruction formula. With irregular sample times, the mathematical problem changes: exact time stamps, sampling geometry, function class and reconstruction method become essential. Uniform-sampling rules cannot simply be applied to an irregular stream by dividing the number of observations by elapsed time.

Some specialized nonuniform sampling schemes can recover certain constrained signals, while missing or jittered samples may be handled by interpolation, inverse problems or model-based estimation. Recovery claims depend on stated assumptions and numerical conditioning. For an instrumentation workflow, retaining actual time stamps and sample-loss flags is more defensible than pretending that every record element was acquired exactly one nominal interval apart.

Sampling Versus Event Counting

A digital input channel recording edge events is not necessarily sampling a continuous amplitude waveform. Event counting, frequency counters, time-to-digital converters, and logic analyzers may observe transitions using different measurement rules and timing resolutions. They can be very effective for sparse digital events, but their error and observability conditions are not identical to the bandlimited analog sampling theorem.

For a rotating shaft, periodically sampling position provides one type of record; a tachometer counting pulses during a gate interval provides another. Both may appear to estimate speed, yet they handle acceleration, pulse loss, timing uncertainty, and aliasing differently. Identify the measurement mechanism before applying f_s>2B to a problem that may actually be event counting or asynchronous timestamping.

Sampling for Step Changes and Transient Events

A step transition contains frequency content over an unbounded idealized range, and a short pulse may contain substantial energy far above its repetition rate. Therefore “the pulse occurs only ten times per second” does not establish that sampling it at 20 samples/s preserves its shape, rise time or peak amplitude. The distinction between event repetition and waveform bandwidth is central to transient acquisition.

If the purpose is only to detect whether a threshold crossing occurred, event-triggered hardware or appropriately designed peak capture may be suitable. If the purpose is to measure pulse shape, sample spacing and analog bandwidth must support the rise/fall features of interest. A short pulse can fall between sample instants, producing a misleadingly low peak or no visible event at all. For repeated periodic pulses, asynchronous timing relative to the sample clock can make the apparent amplitude change between records.

Where a transient must be faithfully reconstructed within a defined band, the measuring system should include an analog bandwidth limit and a statement of how that filter shapes edges and overshoot. The finite-bandwidth result is not the discontinuous mathematical ideal; describing the filtering is part of honest measurement.

Rise Time, Sensor Bandwidth, and Sample Rate

A common approximation for a first-order low-pass measurement channel relates its 10–90% rise time to its −3 dB bandwidth by t_r≈0.35/B. This relationship is specific to a first-order-like response and should not be used blindly for an arbitrary sensor, digital filter, oscillatory transfer function or compensated oscilloscope probe. It is useful because it demonstrates that time-domain edge fidelity depends on analog response as well as sample interval.

For an illustrative 10 kHz one-pole channel, the approximate 10–90% rise time is 35 microseconds. A digitizer that samples at 20 ksample/s has a 50-microsecond sample interval and cannot expect to map the edge with many points, even if the channel bandwidth is itself 10 kHz. Capturing transition shapes often requires substantial sampling headroom beyond the minimum bandlimited sinusoid criterion.

analog_bandwidth=10_000.0
rise_time=0.35/analog_bandwidth
sample_interval=1/20_000.0
assert abs(rise_time-35e-6)<1e-12
assert abs(sample_interval-50e-6)<1e-12
print('Approximate one-pole rise time:',rise_time*1e6,'us')

Sine Sweeps and Aliasing Diagnostics

A controlled sine sweep can expose the presence of aliased components. Apply known frequencies above the desired passband, record the sampled output, and predict each folded frequency using the alias formula. If a tone at 12.5 kHz appears at 3.5 kHz when sampling at 8 ksample/s, the frequency location is consistent with aliasing because 12.5−8=4.5 kHz and reflecting about 4 kHz gives 3.5 kHz. The observed magnitude must still be related to the analog filter response and overall gain.

Sweeps should cover the relevant input bandwidth rather than stop immediately above the Nyquist boundary. Some interference bands are admitted through unexpected analog coupling paths, and certain notches can make one test frequency appear harmless while nearby frequencies fold strongly. A defensible characterization measures gain, noise, phase, and sample timing over operating conditions and records the experimental configuration.

fs=8000
f=12500
folded=abs(((f+fs/2)%fs)-fs/2)
assert folded==3500
print('A 12.5 kHz source at 8 ksample/s folds to',folded,'Hz')

Amplitude Accuracy and Frequency Accuracy Are Different

The sampling theorem concerns representation and reconstruction under a bandlimit assumption. It does not guarantee amplitude calibration. A sensor output may be frequency-unambiguous but scaled incorrectly by an amplifier, have converter offset, or be distorted by a filter's passband gain. Conversely, a filter may have excellent low-frequency flatness while failing to reject a strong out-of-band interferer that folds into the same interval.

Amplitude accuracy should be specified in physical units, including gain error, offset, quantization contribution, noise floor, and input range. Frequency accuracy involves clock calibration and observation interval; frequency resolution also depends on estimator and record length. A system that measures frequency very precisely may still have large amplitude error or incorrect source-frequency identification if analog aliases are possible.

Designing for Unknown or Changing Input Spectra

A bandlimit premise can be enforced through a sufficiently characterized analog filter, but a system with unknown ambient interference needs a conservative out-of-band assumption. Industrial sensor inputs, acoustic microphones, and laboratory probes can encounter harmonics, switching spikes, electromagnetic coupling and mechanical resonances outside the intended useful band. A one-time sine test at a convenient frequency does not bound every future operating condition.

Monitoring can include analog overload indicators, range-change logs, clock-status flags, sample-loss counters, and repeated out-of-band injection tests during qualification. Some systems change sampling rates dynamically; their analog and digital filters must be revalidated at each supported rate. The phrase “sampled above Nyquist” should always be followed by the signal bandwidth and front-end rejection assumptions that make it meaningful.

Audio Sampling: Useful Band and Filter Transition

Audio acquisition offers a familiar example. A sample rate of 48 ksample/s gives a Nyquist frequency of 24 kHz. If the desired captured audio is approximately limited to 20 kHz, the available transition region for a conventional baseband analog low-pass is about 4 kHz. Higher internal oversampling in converters can change the implementation and improve rejection behavior, but the record's nominal sampling rate alone does not describe its entire analog filter chain.

The presence of frequencies above 20 kHz is not automatically desirable for an audio application; the design must balance desired content, ultrasonic interference, amplifier nonlinearity, input range and filter artifacts. The device's digital output may have a rate such as 48 ksample/s while its internal modulator or conversion path operates at a different rate. Those rates should not be silently equated.

record_rate=48_000.0
desired_audio_max=20_000.0
nyquist=record_rate/2
assert nyquist==24_000
assert nyquist-desired_audio_max==4_000
print('Transition-room example:',nyquist-desired_audio_max,'Hz')

Vibration Monitoring: Dangerous Slow Aliases

A rotating machine can have high-frequency tooth-mesh, bearing or structural resonance content. If sampled inadequately, a strong high-frequency vibration may be reported as a low-frequency oscillation. That false low-frequency component can mislead fault classification and trend analysis because it looks like a genuine slow mode in the captured sequence.

For a 2.7 kHz vibration sampled at 2 ksample/s, the folded frequency is 700 Hz. If there is also a genuine 700 Hz vibration, those components overlap in the sampled spectrum. Inspecting only the acquired digital record cannot uniquely identify the two sources. A suitable analog filter, an increased acquisition rate, or a separate instrument path with documented bandwidth is required for reliable interpretation.

fsource=2700; fs=2000
f_alias=abs(((fsource+fs/2)%fs)-fs/2)
assert f_alias==700
print('2.7 kHz vibration appears at',f_alias,'Hz at 2 ksample/s.')

Camera Sampling, Moiré, and Spatial Analogues

Aliasing also arises when a continuous spatial pattern is sampled on a pixel grid. Fine repeating lines can appear as broad false stripes or moiré structures when their spatial frequencies exceed the sampling capability of the sensor and optical prefilter. The sample dimension is position rather than time, but the same mathematical principle applies: periodic sampling creates spectral replicas and ambiguous reconstructed patterns.

Motion artifacts in cameras involve additional mechanisms: temporal frame sampling, shutter integration, rolling-shutter line timing and object movement. A wagon-wheel pattern that seems to rotate backward can be a temporal alias. Spatial moiré is a different sampling direction. These examples clarify why increasing bit depth or enhancing displayed sharpness does not automatically resolve patterns that were ambiguous at capture.

Telemetry and Power Measurement

A telemetry channel might acquire an electrical waveform in order to estimate RMS voltage, power factor, harmonics or transient response. The required sampling bandwidth depends on the requested outputs. If only the mean value is needed, a deliberately filtered low-frequency estimate may suffice; if harmonic distortion or waveform crest factor is required, relevant harmonics must be within the passband and not aliased from uncontrolled frequencies.

High-voltage and power-electronics environments may add switching components far above the 50/60 Hz fundamental. Low-pass conditioning should address potential aliasing from those switching frequencies. Phase-sensitive multichannel measurements also need known skew and clock coherence. A legitimate 60 Hz estimate is not evidence that the same record accurately describes every fast switching event in the circuit.

How to Read Sampling Specifications Critically

Datasheets may advertise aggregate sampling throughput, per-channel throughput, maximum analog bandwidth, effective analog bandwidth, input frequency range, or output data rate. These figures answer different questions. Maximum conversion throughput does not specify the anti-alias filter; a nominal Nyquist frequency does not describe the filter's actual rejection; input bandwidth is often wider than the intended alias-free passband. A high-resolution amplitude converter can still be paired with a poor timebase.

A reproducible engineering assessment requests: the analog input transfer function, source-impedance restrictions, acquisition settling and aperture, clock accuracy and jitter, anti-alias response across the front-end's admitted frequency range, channel timing, and output decimation characteristics. An instrument's “alias-free bandwidth” can be less than its Nyquist frequency because the required filter transition needs room.

A Minimal Sampling Design Checklist

  1. State the measurand and required outputs: waveform, spectrum, event count, peak, phase, or average.
  2. Specify highest desired frequency, lowest relevant frequency, dynamic range, and accepted error.
  3. Identify the maximum possible unwanted frequency and power admitted by the analog source and input circuitry.
  4. Select candidate sampling rates and calculate Nyquist frequencies for each.
  5. Design or verify analog anti-alias passband, transition width, and stopband rejection across relevant frequencies.
  6. Check source impedance, settling time, aperture, clock uncertainty and channel timing.
  7. If decimating, specify a separate digital anti-alias filter for each new output rate.
  8. Determine record duration, frequency-bin spacing, windowing and amplitude normalization.
  9. Test known out-of-band tone injections, broadband noise, and worst-case transients.
  10. Record actual configuration, tolerances and limits so another engineer can reproduce the measurement.

A sampling design is complete only after the relevant signal and error conditions are stated. An isolated sample-rate number is not an acceptance criterion. The next specialist article expands the converter-side implementation choices that must support this signal-level plan.

Worked Investigation 1: A 700 Hz Oscillator Measured at 1,000 Samples per Second

Scenario. A laboratory oscillator is set to 700 Hz. The digitizer records 1,000 uniformly spaced samples per second and its software reports a strong 300 Hz component. A researcher suspects that the oscillator has been misconfigured. Analysis. Compute 700−1,000=−300 Hz. The sampled real sinusoid therefore has a 300 Hz alias, with phase reversal expressed by its negative discrete frequency. A 300 Hz physical source with an appropriate phase could yield the same numerical samples.

Decision. The record alone cannot distinguish those two source hypotheses. Increasing the amplitude resolution, using a different FFT window, or interpolating the samples does not fix the ambiguity. Repeat the measurement with a higher acquisition rate and known analog bandwidth, or add a filter that excludes undesired content before sampling. If the oscillator is known a priori to produce only 700 Hz, the observation can be interpreted accordingly, but that interpretation depends on external information—not unique recovery from the digital sequence.

from math import cos,pi,isclose
fs=1000; phi=0.43
source=[cos(2*pi*700*n/fs+phi) for n in range(50)]
alias=[cos(2*pi*(-300)*n/fs+phi) for n in range(50)]
assert all(isclose(a,b,rel_tol=0,abs_tol=1e-11) for a,b in zip(source,alias))
print('Two continuous-time candidates fit all 50 samples.')

Worked Investigation 2: Choosing a Rate for a 3 kHz Measurement Band

Scenario. A sensor's desired useful baseband extends through 3 kHz. An engineer suggests 6 ksample/s because that number is exactly twice 3 kHz. Analysis. At 6 ksample/s the Nyquist frequency is exactly 3 kHz, leaving no filter transition band and exposing the boundary phase ambiguity. At 8 ksample/s the Nyquist frequency is 4 kHz, leaving a 1 kHz transition interval. At 12 ksample/s it is 6 kHz, leaving 3 kHz. A wider transition makes a given rejection target easier to implement, all else equal.

Decision. Select the acquisition rate only after evaluating the required analog filter response and unwanted input spectrum. For an instrument with substantial interference at 10 kHz, check where that interference would fold under each candidate rate and how much the filter attenuates it. Also verify throughput, logging volume, source settling, and clock behavior. This is a design tradeoff rather than a theorem that 8 or 12 ksample/s always suffices.

desired=3000
candidates=(6000,8000,12000)
for rate in candidates:
    print(rate,'samples/s, available transition:',rate/2-desired,'Hz')
assert [rate/2-desired for rate in candidates]==[0,1000,3000]

Worked Investigation 3: Out-of-Band Switching Noise at 51 kHz

Scenario. A sensor is sampled at 8 ksample/s. Its desired range ends at 3 kHz, but the amplifier passes a switching ripple near 51 kHz. The reported spectrum shows a persistent 3 kHz spur. Analysis. An alias computation gives 51−6×8=3 kHz. The high-frequency ripple lies exactly on a frequency that folds into the desired output band. The software has no way to infer whether the 3 kHz energy arose from an actual physical 3 kHz event or from the switching source after folding.

Decision. Characterize the analog path up to at least the relevant interference frequency, not merely to 4 kHz. Apply suitable analog filtering or change the acquisition architecture so the ripple is rejected before sampling. Then repeat the controlled injection test. If the unwanted 51 kHz tone is 0.5 V peak and its allowed alias is 0.5 mV peak, the desired amplitude rejection is at least 60 dB at that frequency before adding margin.

from math import log10
fs=8000; out_of_band=51_000
alias=abs(((out_of_band+fs/2)%fs)-fs/2)
needed=20*log10(0.5/.0005)
assert alias==3000 and abs(needed-60)<1e-12
print(alias,'Hz folded spur;',needed,'dB rejection target')

Worked Investigation 4: Resampling an Audio Archive

Scenario. A digital recording exists at 48 ksample/s. A delivery platform requires 44.1 ksample/s. Analysis. The nominal rational conversion is 147/160. An anti-alias filter must limit energy according to the 44.1 ksample/s destination bandwidth before effective downsampling. A naive “delete every 160th sample” or nearest-neighbor interpolation will not meet the same fidelity objective; a designed polyphase FIR or appropriate FFT-based rate converter is more suitable under its assumptions.

Decision. Use a tested resampler with suitable filter quality and explicit boundary handling, then measure the in-band passband response and out-of-band rejection. Preserve the source's rate and provenance in the conversion log. If the original recording already contains aliases from an inadequate analog front end, the new sample rate will not restore their sources. The conversion improves format compatibility, not original capture validity.

from math import gcd
source=48000; target=44100
g=gcd(source,target)
ratio=(target//g,source//g)
assert ratio==(147,160)
print('Upsample',ratio[0],'then downsample',ratio[1])

Worked Investigation 5: Monitoring a Rotating Machine

Scenario. Vibration diagnostics observe a 700 Hz feature from a machine while a mechanical analysis suggests a resonance at 2.7 kHz. The logger samples at 2 ksample/s. Analysis. Both a physical 700 Hz tone and a 2.7 kHz tone produce an alias at 700 Hz under this rate. Depending on phase and amplitude, they may reinforce, cancel or produce an apparent time-varying envelope. If the recording is the only evidence, simply locating a 700 Hz FFT peak cannot establish which physical mode produced it.

Decision. Use a vibration sensor and analog input path with adequate characterized bandwidth, verify the actual anti-alias filter, and acquire at a rate sufficient for the desired diagnostic frequency span. Compare measurements under different justified rates and filtering configurations. If the feature moves according to the predicted alias map, that is evidence of an aliased source, although other changes in the test setup must be controlled.

Worked Investigation 6: An Apparent Improvement from Extra ADC Bits

Scenario. An 18-bit digitizer and a 12-bit digitizer both sample an unfiltered 5.3 kHz interfering tone at 8 ksample/s. The team assumes the 18-bit device must report the frequency more faithfully. Analysis. Both devices fold 5.3 kHz to 2.7 kHz under ideal uniform sampling, independent of their amplitude-code widths. The 18-bit device may measure the aliased waveform amplitude with finer nominal code spacing, but its extra bits provide no missing source-frequency information.

Decision. Compare the anti-alias filtering and the relevant usable bandwidth of the entire input path, not just the ADC word length. Instrument resolution, noise performance and bandwidth are separate procurement requirements. The appropriate improvement may be a filter redesign, rate increase, or better front-end shielding rather than more bits.

f=5300; fs=8000
alias=abs(((f+fs/2)%fs)-fs/2)
assert alias==2700
print('Aliased frequency is',alias,'Hz regardless of nominal bit depth.')

Worked Investigation 7: Why a Short FFT Gives a Broad Peak

Scenario. An acquisition system records 512 samples at 16 ksample/s and attempts to resolve two tones only 5 Hz apart. The analyst proposes doubling the sampling rate but keeping 512 samples. Analysis. The first record lasts 32 ms and its DFT bin spacing is 31.25 Hz. Doubling the rate to 32 ksample/s halves the duration to 16 ms and widens the nominal bin spacing to 62.5 Hz. More frequent samples over a shorter window have not solved the two-tone resolution problem.

Decision. Choose a longer observation window and a suitable spectral estimator consistent with frequency drift and stationarity, while still meeting the sample-rate and analog anti-alias requirements. A properly normalized FFT, coherent acquisition when feasible, and a specified window can improve the interpretation. Zero padding increases the density of displayed spectral samples but does not increase the independent information from the recorded duration.

n=512
first=16000/n
second=32000/n
assert first==31.25 and second==62.5
print('DFT spacing:',first,'then',second,'Hz')

Worked Investigation 8: Four Sensor Channels and Phase Accuracy

Scenario. Four vibration sensors are connected to a multiplexed ADC with an aggregate conversion rate of 40 ksample/s. An operator labels the output as “40 ksample/s per channel” and uses the sample index to infer simultaneous phase. Analysis. Under equal round-robin scanning, each channel receives 10 ksample/s before accounting for scan overhead and gaps. If scans occur sequentially, channels can have deterministic offsets in acquisition time. The phase offset for a sinusoid at frequency f and channel skew Δt is 2π f Δt radians.

Decision. Read the instrument's conversion and timestamp specifications, document actual channel acquisition instants, and perform skew calibration or use simultaneous sampling. At a 1 kHz test tone, even 25 microseconds of channel skew corresponds to 9 degrees of phase. A higher aggregate throughput alone does not eliminate interchannel skew or enforce synchronous observation.

from math import pi
frequency=1000
skew=25e-6
phase_deg=360*frequency*skew
assert abs(phase_deg-9)<1e-12
print('Illustrative interchannel phase skew:',phase_deg,'degrees')

Integrated Sampling Analysis Workflow

The eight investigations illustrate a common reasoning procedure. First, identify the physical question and the frequency interval that must be distinguished. Second, write down candidate analog input components, including plausible harmonics and broadband noise. Third, apply a frequency-folding calculation using the actual per-channel sampling rate. Fourth, determine whether the analog input path rejects all components capable of producing unacceptable in-band aliases. Fifth, model quantization, timing, finite-window spectral analysis, and sensor calibration separately.

After designing the chain, verify it using controlled inputs. The evaluation should state both what was proved by the mathematical model and what was observed in a laboratory test. The classical theorem establishes exact reconstruction only under strict assumptions; practical accuracy is an engineering claim requiring real filter, clock, converter, and environmental evidence.

Review Answer Key and Explanations

The solutions state the assumptions on which an answer rests. If a question involves a filtered or instrument-specific signal, the answer identifies which extra device evidence would be required before accepting a real measurement.

  1. Sampling is a representation contract. It records x[n]=x(nTs) at known instants Ts=1/fs; it does not uniquely identify every unrestricted continuous waveform.
  2. Quantities are not synonyms. The sample rate is fs samples per second; the Nyquist frequency of real uniform samples is fs/2 Hz.
  3. Modulo folding. 700−1,000=−300 Hz, so the nonnegative alias is 300 Hz; the phase orientation is reversed.
  4. Aliasing arithmetic. 5,500−8,000=−2,500 Hz, so the nonnegative alias is 2,500 Hz.
  5. Boundary case. At the Nyquist boundary, phase can be unrecoverable; practical filters need transition width, and the strict bandlimited theorem commonly assumes fs>2B.
  6. Assumptions matter. A suitably well-behaved continuous signal is bandlimited to below B and sampled exactly and indefinitely with fs>2B, so ideal bandlimited reconstruction is possible.
  7. Spectral interpretation. Sampling periodically replicates the input spectrum at kfs; overlapping replicas mix source frequencies irreversibly.
  8. Interpolation. The normalized sinc kernel sinc((t−nTs)/Ts), summed over all sample instants under the theorem conditions.
  9. Implementation limitation. It truncates an infinite-support reconstruction and introduces endpoint behavior, passband ripple and residual errors.
  10. DAC output. No. It holds values piecewise constant and imposes a rectangular-pulse frequency response and images.
  11. Signal chain. An analog anti-alias filter with enough rejection over all relevant unwanted input frequencies.
  12. Real filter design. Physical filters roll off gradually rather than discontinuously at a cutoff.
  13. Rate selection. Nyquist is 6 kHz; the interval between desired 3 kHz and Nyquist is 3 kHz.
  14. Tone budget. The voltage ratio is 1,000 and 20log10(1,000)=60 dB.
  15. Irreversibility. Not in general; the sampled sequence no longer identifies which analog component created the in-band energy.
  16. Decimation. The new Nyquist frequency is one quarter of the original sample rate divided by two; frequencies above it would otherwise fold into the new baseband.
  17. Resampling. It interpolates the existing sampled information under a chosen model; it does not create missing original analog information.
  18. Clock effects. First-order sample error is approximately x'(t)δt; maximum sinusoid slope grows with both frequency and amplitude.
  19. Finite observation. Duration N/fs=0.2 s and nominal DFT bin spacing fs/N=5 Hz.
  20. Leakage versus aliasing. It changes finite-record spectral leakage after capture; it cannot undo analog overlap.
  21. Specialized design. Using a filtered, known occupied RF band and a lower sample rate that maps it to a nonoverlapping lower frequency interval.
  22. Complex signals. Its two quadratures retain the sign/orientation of baseband frequency offset, although frequencies still repeat modulo fs.
  23. High-frequency alias. It can fold to 3 kHz and contaminate the desired band if the analog input admits it.
  24. Noise budget. Out-of-band spectral power folds into the sampled baseband according to spectral replication, raising the measured in-band noise floor.
  25. Multiplexing. 10 ksample/s per channel under equal allocation, subject to actual device scheduling.
  26. Datasheet interpretation. Its front end may pass frequencies that fold into the sampled output; adequate filter rejection typically leaves a smaller usable range.
  27. Transient capture. A finite-width event may not coincide with any of the chosen discrete instants; the pulse shape is not guaranteed bandlimited.
  28. System verification. A specified source spectrum, input filter response, per-channel fs, clock/timing, amplitude error budget, and controlled validation across relevant interference.
