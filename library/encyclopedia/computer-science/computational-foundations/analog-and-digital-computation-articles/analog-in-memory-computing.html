Analog In-Memory Computing: Definition and Computational Claim

Analog in-memory computing (AIMC) is a class of hardware approaches that use the physical behavior of a memory-containing structure to carry out arithmetic, especially vector–matrix or matrix–vector multiplication. Instead of repeatedly fetching each coefficient from a separate memory hierarchy into digital arithmetic units, an array may store coefficients as conductances or charge states and use applied signals and shared electrical paths to form sums. The result is measured, scaled, digitized when required, and combined with other processing stages.

Its distinctive claim is co-location of data and arithmetic. This is not a claim that an analog machine performs every type of computation efficiently, or that moving a multiply–accumulate operation into an array automatically removes all other movement and conversion. Input values must still reach the array; outputs must be sensed; weights may require costly programming; and a system may need many tiles, converters, buffers and digital operations.

The article evaluates AIMC as a physical and system-level algorithm: it begins with Ohm's and Kirchhoff's laws, derives the ideal arithmetic relation, introduces device and circuit nonidealities, and then accounts for accuracy, energy, throughput, programming and reliability. All numerical examples are teaching models unless a published measurement is expressly identified.

Learning Objectives and Editorial Boundary

After reading this article, the learner should be able to derive the ideal crossbar current equation; convert between dimensionless mathematical weights and physical conductances; represent signed operands; explain where DAC and ADC work occurs; estimate first-order errors from conductance mismatch and quantization; construct a full-system energy and throughput budget; and distinguish a chip demonstration from a deployable application result.

This specialist owns array-embedded arithmetic and its engineering costs. General converter construction belongs to the unit's Analog-to-Digital Converters and Digital-to-Analog Converters articles; comprehensive numerical precision belongs to Fixed-Point and Floating-Point Representation and Quantization Noise and Effective Resolution; physical scaling is treated in its own article; and detailed spiking-neuron circuits are assigned to the later Neuromorphic Analog and Event-Driven Circuits article.

Do not confuse illustrative circuit equations with foundry-ready design. Technology-specific conductance windows, voltage limits, writing pulses, timing constraints, retention and endurance differ among devices. Every real implementation needs measured operating curves, verified interfaces and a workload-specific accuracy standard before a quantitative advantage can be claimed.

Why Data Movement Can Dominate Arithmetic

In a conventional processor, a multiply–accumulate may be inexpensive relative to fetching operands through off-chip or distant on-chip interconnects. The cost depends on locality, memory levels, precision, reuse and the accelerator's design; there is no universal ratio between energy spent moving a byte and energy spent multiplying it. AIMC targets workloads with many repeated uses of the same stored coefficients, often with broad parallel input application.

Suppose the weight matrix of a dense layer is reused over many inference queries. A conventional system tries to keep it in registers, caches or accelerator SRAM; AIMC may instead make the retained physical memory array participate directly in each multiply–accumulate. The array's broad current summation can reduce repeated readout of individual coefficients. The comparison must still include the transfer of input activations to rows and digitized outputs to subsequent layers.

A useful first question is whether weight movement is actually a bottleneck. If a model is small enough to reside in an efficient on-chip digital buffer, or the workload has irregular access and little reuse, the hypothesized advantage can be much smaller. Bottleneck identification, rather than a device label, should determine whether AIMC is investigated.

Computing Near Memory Versus Computing Within Memory

Near-memory computing places conventional arithmetic resources next to storage banks, reducing distance and often offering ordinary digital precision. In-memory computing uses the storage structure itself—or circuitry closely integrated into its access path—as an active participant in operations. AIMC is the analog subset of this latter family: it uses continuous currents, voltages, charges or time quantities in at least part of the computation.

A design may be called 'compute in memory' even when its arithmetic is fully digital. For example, a memory bank can support bitwise logic or bit-serial accumulation without ever summing analog currents. Conversely, a crossbar storing multi-level conductances whose columns sum currents provides a clear analog arithmetic primitive. Classification therefore asks what physical quantity represents operands and how the sum is formed, not just whether the article uses the phrase 'memory wall'.

Real systems are mixed-signal: data outside the array are often digital; signal drives and sensing are analog; local control and higher-level arithmetic may be digital. Calling the complete chip 'analog' would hide important digital overhead and might incorrectly imply that nonlinear operators and memory management are all computed as continuous quantities.

The Matrix–Vector Multiplication Contract

The central mathematical target is y = W x, where W has m output rows and n input columns, x is a vector of length n, and y has length m. Explicitly, y_j = Σ_i W[j,i] x_i. The order of summation, scaling, sign convention and output quantization must be specified before an electronic array is treated as implementing the mathematical contract. A different transposed wiring arrangement simply changes which physical lines are called inputs and outputs.

In neural-network inference, a dense layer may add biases and apply a nonlinear activation afterward: z = W x + b, a = φ(z). A crossbar's native primitive usually produces only the linear accumulation; bias addition, activation, normalization and softmax may be separate hardware operations. Counting them as 'free' analog arithmetic can distort a throughput or energy comparison.

This matters especially for small arrays: the fixed cost of moving data, selecting tile addresses, sensing results and coordinating control may be large relative to the number of actual multiplications. For large layers, multiple tiles or time steps may be needed, and digital reduction of partial sums can dominate certain formats.

# The mathematical contract for a 2 x 3 layer.
W = [[0.5, -1.0, 2.0], [1.5, 0.25, -0.5]]
x = [0.2, 0.4, -0.1]
y = [sum(w*xv for w, xv in zip(row, x)) for row in W]
assert all(abs(a-b) < 1e-12 for a,b in zip(y, [-0.5, 0.45]))

Physical Laws Behind a Conductance Crossbar

For an ideal resistive element, Ohm's law gives I = G V, where conductance G is measured in siemens, applied voltage V in volts and resulting current I in amperes. If several element currents meet at a column held at a defined potential, Kirchhoff's current law gives I_col,j = Σ_i G[i,j] V_i with signs set by the wiring convention. This resembles a dot product because the multiplication occurs through element conductance and voltage, while electrical addition occurs at the shared node.

The resemblance is exact only under the model assumptions: each element is effectively linear during the read operation; row voltages are what the equation assumes; column sensing maintains an appropriate potential; and interconnect parasitics do not materially alter those voltages. A real current is not an abstract floating-point number. It has physical limits, noise, offset, settling behavior, and dependencies on temperature and operating voltage.

An array produces a current for every enabled output column in parallel, but 'parallel' should not be confused with zero latency. Rows must be driven, currents must settle, output interfaces must measure them, and subsequent stages must accept the values.

# Ideal currents (microamps) from conductances in microsiemens.
G = [[4, 1], [2, 3], [1, 5]] # input rows x output columns
V = [0.2, 0.1, 0.3]
I_microamps = [sum(G[i][j]*V[i] for i in range(3)) for j in range(2)]
assert all(abs(a-b)<1e-12 for a,b in zip(I_microamps,[1.3,2.0]))

Crossbar Geometry and Indexing Conventions

A rectangular n-by-m array can place n driven word lines across m sensed bit lines. Each intersection may contain a memory element, a selection element, or a compound structure, depending on technology. Its stored state controls an effective conductance and therefore the contribution of that intersection to an output current. Using G[i,j] for input row i and output column j gives I_j = Σ_i G[i,j] V_i.

The physical array in this convention implements a map proportional to GᵀV; the mathematical weight matrix W is often written with output units as rows. A mapping is correct when it explicitly sets W[j,i] = k G[i,j], or a more elaborate signed encoding. Failing to state the transpose is a common error when translating software tensors into device placements.

Also record whether read voltages are applied to one row at a time, simultaneously to many rows, or encoded in pulse timing. The number of operations, interference between currents and ADC conversion demand all depend on that schedule. An abstract crossbar diagram alone is not a throughput specification.

G = [[1,2,3],[4,5,6]]   # two inputs, three columns
x = [0.25,0.5]
physical = [sum(G[i][j]*x[i] for i in range(2)) for j in range(3)]
software_W = [[1,4],[2,5],[3,6]]
software = [sum(wi*xi for wi,xi in zip(row,x)) for row in software_W]
assert physical == software == [2.25,3.0,3.75]

Worked Example: A Two-Output Crossbar

Consider three driven rows with voltages 0.2, 0.1 and 0.3 V. Suppose the conductances to output A are 4, 2 and 1 µS, while those to output B are 1, 3 and 5 µS. In the ideal grounded-column model, A produces 4×0.2 + 2×0.1 + 1×0.3 = 1.3 µA; B produces 1×0.2 + 3×0.1 + 5×0.3 = 2.0 µA. These are currents, not dimensionless mathematical outputs.

If a transimpedance amplifier converts current to voltage with feedback resistance 100 kΩ, the ideal magnitudes are 0.13 V and 0.20 V; an inverting stage may produce negative signs. Those voltages must remain inside the amplifier and ADC input ranges. If the ADC saturates at 0.15 V in magnitude, B is clipped even though all matrix multiplications are mathematically well defined.

This example does not establish a precision claim. Real measured values depart from it when the conductances vary, the amplifier has offset, supply rails limit swing, or the column potential is not maintained. The physical result should always be compared with the scaled intended output.

I = [1.3e-6,2.0e-6]
feedback_resistance = 100e3
vout_inverting = [-feedback_resistance*i for i in I]
assert all(abs(a-b)<1e-12 for a,b in zip(vout_inverting,[-0.13,-0.2]))

Signed Weights with Differential Conductances

A single passive conductance is nonnegative, whereas neural-network weights may be positive or negative. One common solution uses two conductance elements per weight and interprets the difference: w = k(G⁺ − G⁻). The two contributions can be sensed differentially or read separately and subtracted. With matched offsets the representation can reduce some common-mode errors, though it doubles nominal storage elements and may increase peripheral demand.

Choose a baseline and dynamic range that keep both devices within a programmable interval [G_min,G_max]. For example, with G_base = 5 µS, a normalized signed weight w = −0.4 and scale k = 0.2 per µS, a difference of −2 µS is needed; G⁺=4 µS and G⁻=6 µS satisfy the model. The two devices are not guaranteed to drift or age equally.

Differential coding has limitations: cancellation can create a small desired signal from two relatively large noisy currents, increasing relative uncertainty. It also requires explicit common-mode headroom and usually does not make a two-device pair behave as an ideal signed resistor.

k = 0.2  # normalized weight per microsiemens of difference
G_plus, G_minus = 4.0, 6.0
w = k*(G_plus-G_minus)
assert abs(w-(-0.4))<1e-12

Representing Negative Input Activations

Signed weights are only one part of the encoding problem. The input vector itself may contain negative values. A unipolar row driver cannot express a negative input simply by assigning a negative bit pattern to an ADC. Options include applying differential voltages, splitting inputs into positive and negative phases, adding offsets that are compensated later, or moving a sign operation into the digital readout.

For a two-phase scheme write x = x⁺ − x⁻ with x⁺ = max(x,0) and x⁻ = max(−x,0). Two nonnegative crossbar evaluations yield Gx⁺ and Gx⁻, whose difference recovers Gx in the ideal arithmetic model. This doubles the nominal read passes if no parallel hardware is added. A signed-weight representation can similarly require differential columns or phases.

There is no free signed arithmetic. Every encoding determines device count, pass count, input-driver capability, output range, energy and digital recombination. Quantify these overheads when comparing an analog tile with a digital matrix multiplication kernel.

x = [-0.4,0.7,-0.1]
pos = [max(v,0) for v in x]
neg = [max(-v,0) for v in x]
assert all(abs(p-n-v)<1e-12 for p,n,v in zip(pos,neg,x))

Mapping Mathematical Units to Physical Signals

An AIMC computation needs a declared mapping from mathematical inputs to voltage or pulse duration and from numerical weights to conductance. One simple model is V_i = α x_i and G[i,j] = G_0 + β w[j,i] for limited ranges. The resulting current contains desired products and possibly an offset term αG_0 Σx_i that must be compensated. Differential conductance pairs can remove the nominal baseline term but have their own mismatch.

Scaling cannot be chosen independently of accuracy. High voltages may increase usable output current while driving devices into nonlinear read characteristics, increasing read-disturb stress and power. Low voltages reduce current but can worsen output signal-to-noise and increase susceptibility to sensing offsets. Larger conductances can improve sensed signal while increasing static read dissipation and line drops.

A complete mapping worksheet specifies ranges for every layer or tile, extrema for intermediate sums, calibration coefficients, allowed read energy, and the interpretation of clipped or unrepresentable values. The circuit output must be dimensionally reconciled with the software reference before application-level accuracy is evaluated.

The Output Sense Path and Transimpedance Conversion

Column currents must be converted into usable signals. A transimpedance amplifier can hold a bit line near virtual ground and map current to voltage, V_out ≈ −R_f I_col within its linear bandwidth and output swing. Alternatives include current integrators, charge accumulation, comparators, integrating ADCs and time-domain encoders. Each readout choice changes the array boundary conditions and conversion cost.

Sensing is not necessarily a passive observer. A nonzero column potential changes the element voltage and hence the current; amplifier finite gain and input impedance can break the ideal virtual-ground approximation. Readout load, sampling capacitance and settling can introduce inter-column or history-dependent errors. The output amplifier itself may consume substantial energy, particularly for small arrays or high throughput.

An engineering model should represent sensing gain, offset, bandwidth, noise, maximum current, saturation and conversion latency. Treat an observed digital code as a calibrated result of array plus sense chain, not a pure readout of an exact dot product.

# A simple readout: convert current to voltage, then clip at ADC rails.
Rf = 80_000.0
I = 4e-6
unclipped = -Rf*I
clip = lambda x,lo,hi: min(hi,max(lo,x))
out = clip(unclipped,-0.25,0.25)
assert abs(unclipped+0.32)<1e-12 and out==-0.25

Input DACs, Pulse Width, and Row Drivers

Inputs may arrive as digital activations. A voltage-mode DAC can generate one analog level per row, but high-resolution converters require area, power and settling time. Some implementations encode an activation as a pulse width or a sequence of low-precision pulses; a sense integrator then accumulates charge across time. In such cases the arithmetic is partly in the time integral, and operation count must include pulses and settling intervals.

Pulse-width approaches avoid some static-voltage precision burdens but introduce pulse-width quantization, duty-cycle timing, edge skew, output saturation and possible read-disturb constraints. Serial bit-plane driving can trade area and converter resolution for additional cycles. An architecture described as 'one analog MVM' may in fact require several cycles per input bit group or signed component.

Input drivers must handle the capacitive word-line load, distribute levels across resistive wires and respect device read-voltage limits. The power needed to charge and discharge these lines belongs in the end-to-end energy budget even when a paper focuses on the conductance array itself.

ADC Resolution, Sample Rate, and Multiplexing

An ADC converts the sensed analog result to a discrete code. The required resolution depends on the distribution and dynamic range of column sums, not simply on the nominal bit count of a stored weight. An 8-bit weight and an 8-bit activation can produce a sum requiring more than 8 bits before rounding. Conversely, an inference model may tolerate lower precision at some layers after retraining or calibration.

Converters can be dedicated per column, shared among columns or integrated with sensing structures. Sharing reduces silicon area but requires multiplexing and increases the time before all outputs are available. Dedicated ADCs enable greater parallel readout at potentially significant power and area cost. A single stated array cycle time is therefore insufficient to infer complete tile throughput.

The ADC specification must include full-scale, effective resolution, input bandwidth, differential linearity where relevant, conversion rate and energy per conversion under the intended settings. Digitized outputs may then require digital scaling, bias addition, nonlinear activation and partial-sum accumulation across tiles.

Current-Domain, Charge-Domain, and Time-Domain Arithmetic

The conductance crossbar is the canonical image of AIMC, but it is not the only physical implementation. Current-domain designs exploit conductance-weighted currents and their summation. Charge-domain approaches use capacitors, charge sharing or switched-capacitor accumulation. Time-domain approaches encode values or products in delay, pulse width or event timing. Hybrid circuit blocks can combine these domains within one accelerator.

Different domains expose different error mechanisms. Currents are sensitive to IR drop, conductance nonlinearity, readout offsets and leakage. Capacitive storage has mismatch, parasitic capacitance, incomplete charge transfer and leakage over hold time. Time encodings have jitter, pulse-width dispersion and comparator threshold dependence. None of these choices turns physical arithmetic into exact computation; they redistribute precision, energy and bandwidth costs.

The comparison should be expressed at the boundary of a complete operator, not as an isolated physics advantage. Specify whether the reported 'MAC' is an idealized physical product, a readout sample, a digitally represented sum or a verified model-layer output.

SRAM-Based and Digital Compute-in-Memory Distinctions

Standard SRAM arrays store bistable digital values, but access-line charge sharing or custom peripheral circuits can implement analog multiply–accumulate behaviors involving bitline voltage or current. Other SRAM-CIM designs use bitwise logic and digital accumulators and are better classified as digital compute-in-memory. The storage technology alone does not identify the arithmetic domain.

SRAM avoids the nonvolatile programming challenges of some emerging memories but consumes area for transistor-based cells and generally loses state on power removal. Analog SRAM-CIM can still face capacitor mismatch, bitline nonlinearity, limited precision and peripheral conversion costs. Its repeated-read operation may be efficient for specific quantized inference workloads, but area and retention comparisons with RRAM or PCM must be technology- and node-specific.

A useful taxonomy records four attributes separately: storage medium, arithmetic encoding, accumulation method and digital interface. This avoids the misleading binary contrast that labels all SRAM approaches 'digital' and all resistive-memory approaches 'analog'.

Resistive RAM and Programmable Conductance States

Resistive random-access memory (RRAM) devices can retain different resistance or conductance states after a programming operation. Such states can represent weights in a crossbar. Depending on material system and circuit design, programming can involve set and reset pulses, verify reads and iterative adjustment. Multilevel storage is desirable for dense weights but may be difficult to control consistently across cells and over time.

A programmed state is not an exact floating-point constant. Device-to-device variability, cycle-to-cycle programming spread, read noise, history dependence, retention change and occasional stuck-at faults can alter the realized coefficient. The useful quantity is often the measured effective conductance over a specified read range, with uncertainty, rather than the nominal code written by the controller.

RRAM crossbars can be attractive for compact MVMs; however, technology maturity, read disturb, programming endurance, line parasitics and peripheral area must be included in feasibility studies. A paper demonstrating a matrix operation in a small array does not automatically validate a large neural network or product lifetime.

Phase-Change Memory: Conductance, Drift, and Programming

Phase-change memory (PCM) stores information by changing the physical phase configuration of a material, producing different effective conductances. PCM-based AIMC demonstrations have mapped neural-network weights to such states and used analog read currents to realize matrix operations. IBM's published 2023 multi-core demonstrations provide an important example of system integration rather than a universal performance specification for AIMC.

A particular concern is conductance drift: a cell's effective conductance can change after programming, in addition to noise and programming variability. Hardware-aware training, calibration, reference structures and differential weight mappings can reduce some impacts, but the mitigation may consume additional devices, cycles or digital operations. An effective calibration at one elapsed time or temperature may need revalidation later.

The device's read conditions, write energy, retention requirements and endurance envelope jointly determine suitability. For fixed inference models, infrequent weight updates can help amortize programming cost; workloads with continuous online training may face a different total energy and lifetime balance.

Ferroelectric FETs and Other Emerging Weight Media

Ferroelectric field-effect transistors (FeFETs), electrochemical memory devices, conductive-bridge memories and other emerging elements have been explored for analog or multi-level weight storage. The relevant question is not merely whether a device is nonvolatile, but whether its transfer characteristic supports controllable, sufficiently stable and repeatable multiplication under an intended sensing regime.

A transistor-based device may present an effective channel conductance that depends on stored state and applied bias. Consequently, its current is not always a linear product G×V across the whole dynamic range. Device architecture, access transistor configuration and read-voltage limits can strongly affect which mathematical operations are feasible. State programmability may be asymmetric, nonlinear and stochastic.

Treat each medium as a set of measurable trade-offs: available states, state noise, switching variability, conductance distribution, programming pulses, read energy, drift, retention, failure rates and integration cost. An emerging device's promising cell-level metric should not be substituted for a measured accelerator-level accuracy or efficiency figure.

Array Size, Density, and Tile Organization

A single crossbar's capacity is limited by physical and computational considerations. Larger arrays store more weights and can increase parallel summation, but they can also amplify wire resistance, capacitive loading, readout dynamic range and device variation. Even if each crosspoint is compact, periphery such as drivers, amplifiers, ADCs, write circuitry, calibration memory and control may determine practical tile size.

An accelerator may partition a large weight tensor across several tiles. Columns represent subsets of output features, rows represent input slices, and partial sums from multiple tiles must be recombined. The recombination may occur digitally, by charge accumulation, or in a second analog stage. The resulting communication and summation work belongs in the model even when an array operation appears constant-time on a schematic.

Tile geometry also affects utilization. A fixed 256×256 tile allocated to a 257×20 matrix may require multiple tile regions with many unused cells, depending on mapping. Calculate active crosspoints, output routing, conversion passes and memory occupancy independently; otherwise peak performance numbers may grossly overstate effective application efficiency.

import math
rows,cols = 257,20
tile_rows,tile_cols = 256,256
num_tiles = math.ceil(rows/tile_rows)*math.ceil(cols/tile_cols)
utilization = rows*cols/(num_tiles*tile_rows*tile_cols)
assert num_tiles == 2 and 0 < utilization < 0.05

Sneak Paths, Access Devices, and Cell Selection

In passive crosspoint structures, undesired current paths may pass through cells not intended to contribute to the addressed operation. These sneak paths disturb reads and writes and can cause array leakage and interference. The severity depends on architecture and biasing. Access transistors, nonlinear selectors, multi-terminal devices and carefully chosen read patterns can mitigate leakage or isolate cells, but they consume area or introduce voltage drops and complexity.

An MVM is not the same as a single-cell memory read: many cells may legitimately conduct simultaneously. Designers must distinguish useful column summation from unintended parallel paths caused by imperfect isolation or inactive-line bias. Device selection and grounding affect the difference. Selection devices also change read linearity and the voltage available across the weight element.

A validated circuit model includes each access device's I–V behavior, row/column bias, unselected-line states and sense impedance. The ideal expression I=ΣGV is an approximation only after these surrounding network conditions are justified. In some technologies, full-circuit simulation is essential even for moderate arrays.

Wire Resistance and IR Drop Across Crossbars

Real word lines and bit lines have finite resistance. As current travels along a word line, voltage drops across the interconnect, so a distant device may see less voltage than a near device. Column resistance and sensor input impedance can also alter terminal potentials. The resulting IR-drop error depends on which cells are active, their conductances, the input vector, and position. It is generally not captured by one global gain correction.

A simple first-order bound makes the concern concrete. If a wire segment carries 100 µA through 10 Ω, its voltage drop is 1 mV. Against an intended 100 mV device voltage, that drop alone is 1% of the drive level; multiple segments and data-dependent currents can compound the effect. This is an illustration, not a predicted error for a named crossbar.

Mitigations include smaller tiles, lower average conductance, alternative wiring, hierarchical array structures, improved drivers and hardware-aware mapping. Each may affect area, throughput or signal-to-noise. The correct comparison uses simulated or measured line voltages with representative input patterns rather than treating array size as an abstract free parameter.

current=100e-6
wire_resistance=10.0
drop=current*wire_resistance
relative=drop/0.1
assert abs(drop-0.001)<1e-12 and abs(relative-0.01)<1e-12

Line Capacitance, Settling, and Transient Behavior

Crossbars contain distributed capacitance on long metal lines and device nodes. A change in word-line voltage does not instantaneously establish the ideal steady-state current at every column. Drivers must charge parasitic capacitances, devices may exhibit intrinsic dynamics, and sensing integrators must accumulate sufficient signal. The result measured too early can depend on the previous input pattern.

For a crude lumped approximation, an R C time constant is τ=RC, and a first-order response remaining error declines like e^(−t/τ). Reaching within 0.1% requires about ln(1000)≈6.91 τ under that model. A distributed array need not obey a single-pole response, so this example provides a conceptual lower-level check, not a device-specific settling guarantee.

Clocking faster than valid settling may increase apparent throughput in a simulation that samples ideal currents, while degrading actual inference accuracy. Include word-line setup, current settling, ADC aperture and any reset or discharge interval in the physical operation time.

import math
R=2000.0
C=10e-12
tau=R*C
t99_9=math.log(1000)*tau
assert abs(tau-20e-9)<1e-18
assert 130e-9<t99_9<140e-9

Read-Voltage Nonlinearity and Conductance Dependence

A device can be programmed to a nominal conductance yet behave nonlinearly when voltage changes. Instead of I=GV with constant G, a more realistic approximation may be I(V)=G_0 V + cV² + ... over a limited range. The quadratic term creates a signal-dependent error and may break simple superposition. Different cells can have different coefficients and temperature sensitivities.

There are two separate challenges. Reading a stored weight must preserve a known transfer over an allowed voltage interval; writing a weight must reliably move it to a target state. A device with good read linearity may have nonlinear update pulses. Modeling the update response as if it were the inference transfer curve is a category error.

Characterize at multiple input levels and polarities, not just one read voltage. Compare measured output against the weighted sum predicted by the actual mapped conductances and inputs. If the deviation scales with the input amplitude, a simple additive offset correction will not be enough.

G0 = 20e-6  # S
c = 10e-6   # A / V**2
V=0.2
ideal=G0*V
measured=ideal+c*V*V
assert measured>ideal
assert abs((measured-ideal)/ideal-0.1)<1e-12

Limited Conductance Levels and Weight Quantization

A memory cell may realize only a limited number of sufficiently distinct conductance levels. Even if a programming algorithm assigns many target codes, device noise, drift and overlapping state distributions can reduce effective precision. A common weight-mapping scheme quantizes a trained numerical weight to one of these levels; another uses multiple cells and bit slicing to represent each weight with greater precision.

For L uniformly spaced states spanning [G_min,G_max], the nominal interval is ΔG=(G_max−G_min)/(L−1). The maximum ideal nearest-level error is ΔG/2 for values within the range, assuming all levels are reachable and ordered. That formal bound omits random programming error, hardware drift and state-dependent read nonlinearity.

The engineering requirement is defined by the application, not by a marketing label such as 'multi-bit cell'. Report a measured distribution of read currents, separability across conditions, read-disturb tests and the application error after mapping. Conductance precision and numerical layer precision are related but not identical.

levels=16
Gmin,Gmax = 2.0,17.0  # microsiemens
step=(Gmax-Gmin)/(levels-1)
target=8.4
code=round((target-Gmin)/step)
reconstructed=Gmin+code*step
assert step==1.0 and code==6
assert abs(reconstructed-target)<=step/2

Programming Algorithms and Write-Verify Loops

Weight loading may require multiple pulses per cell. A write–verify controller applies a write pulse, measures the resulting conductance, compares with a target, and repeats or changes pulse size. This resembles iterative control more than storing an exact digital word. Some devices show asymmetric set/reset behavior, stochastic increments or overshoot; overly aggressive writing can damage the device or reduce remaining endurance.

Programming throughput therefore depends on average pulses per weight, degree of array parallelism, verify read time, retry behavior and exceptions for unreachable cells. A matrix with one million weights and ten serial pulses per weight is not instantaneously configured merely because inference later uses parallel analog summation. A full model counts initial programming and periodic refresh separately from each inference call.

A well-defined stopping rule includes an acceptable conductance tolerance and maximum attempts; it must also handle a cell that cannot reach the target. The observed programming distribution should be saved alongside actual weight maps so a hardware-aware software reference can reproduce the implemented computation.

# Toy program-and-verify controller: deterministic demonstration only.
target=7.0
state=2.0
attempts=0
while abs(state-target)>0.25 and attempts<20:
    state += min(1.25,target-state)
    attempts +=1
assert abs(state-target)<=0.25 and attempts==4

Programming-Energy Amortization Over Reuse

An analog layer may consume less energy per read while needing nontrivial energy to program conductance states. If a weight image costs E_program to load and each inference saves ΔE versus a comparable baseline, the basic break-even count is N_break-even = E_program / ΔE, provided ΔE > 0 and no other fixed costs differ. If refresh or calibration is periodic, add those recurring costs before drawing conclusions.

For illustration, suppose programming costs 0.8 J, and the proposed architecture saves 20 µJ per completed inference after all readout and communication overhead. It takes 40,000 inferences to recover that programming energy. If a deployment sees only 1,000 calls between model updates, this architecture loses energy over that interval under the stated assumptions, regardless of its array-only efficiency.

The example does not describe a particular published chip. It shows why an accelerator for fixed, repeatedly reused weights may have a different economic and energy case from an accelerator trained and reprogrammed continuously. Include loading, calibration and idle power in the actual application model.

program_energy_j=0.8
saved_per_query_j=20e-6
break_even=program_energy_j/saved_per_query_j
assert break_even==40000.0

Drift, Retention, and Time-Dependent Weight Error

Some analog weight states evolve after programming. A simplified phenomenological drift law for a conductance may be written G(t)=G(t₀)(t/t₀)^(-ν) for t≥t₀, where ν is a positive drift exponent. This form has been used for phase-change-memory behavior, but it is not a universal law for all storage media, and the exponent may depend on device, state and operating condition.

If the drift exponents differ among cells, a single layer-level scale correction will not restore exact relative weights. Temperature change and read history can further alter effective values. Calibration may periodically measure reference cells, estimate per-column gains or update downstream compensation. Each procedure consumes time, energy and possibly array endurance.

A meaningful retention claim states an interval, temperature, allowable computational error and whether the system performs refresh or recalibration. Cell-level retention of a binary bit does not automatically imply retention of an analog numerical weight accurate enough for a specific neural-network layer.

import math
G0=10.0
nu=0.05
ratio=100.0  # elapsed time ratio
g=G0*ratio**(-nu)
assert 7.0<g<8.1 and g<G0

Endurance, Refresh Policy, and Update Workloads

Endurance concerns the number and severity of write events that a memory device can withstand while meeting a stated specification. It is distinct from read retention and distinct from the numerical error of a single matrix product. An analog programming operation may consist of many pulses, and a training cycle may write the same logical coefficient repeatedly. System lifetime must therefore account for actual device stress, not merely the number of software updates.

A fixed-weight inference engine may program once, verify, and execute many reads. A continuously adapting network may need frequent writes and might be dominated by endurance, programming energy or weight-state mismatch. Hybrid approaches can keep most training digital, map weights to the array only periodically, and use measured conductance states during inference.

Refresh is not free: it may pause computation, require conversion and verification cycles, or generate heat. Choose and validate a refresh policy using measured drift, environment and required accuracy, rather than assuming that 'nonvolatile' implies immutable numerical coefficients.

Device Variation, Defects, and Spatial Structure

Device-to-device variation creates different current responses even for nominally identical programming states. Cycle-to-cycle variation means the same cell may not land at exactly the same state after repeated writes. Some cells may be stuck high or low, while others exhibit excessive noise or nonlinear reads. These errors may be independent, correlated by row or column, or spatially clustered due to fabrication and thermal gradients.

Random independent weight error can sometimes average partially during accumulation, while systematic column gain errors may add coherently. Therefore a single global RMS conductance error is insufficient to predict application accuracy. Calibrate or characterize distributions by tile position, weight level, polarity and temperature, and preserve correlation where it affects system predictions.

Spares, remapping, differential encoding, error-aware placement and model retraining are possible mitigation tools. All trade off hardware, programming time or software effort for a desired yield and correctness margin. The right acceptance metric is a validated computational output under the anticipated physical variability.

Read Noise, Flicker, Random Telegraph Signals, and Offset

Readout currents contain noise and systematic offsets from devices, sense amplifiers, references and power supplies. Thermal noise, low-frequency flicker noise and random-telegraph-like fluctuations have different spectra and time behavior. Averaging may reduce suitable uncorrelated components but not eliminate fixed offsets or slow drift. The conversion circuitry also contributes quantization and input-referred noise.

A useful first-order model for a sensed output is z_j = a_j Σ_i (G_ij+δG_ij)V_i + b_j + η_j(t), with gain a_j, offset b_j, conductance errors δG, and noise process η. This is still simplified: nonlinear, line-dependent and saturation errors can invalidate an additive model. Its virtue is that it separates quantities engineers can measure independently.

When reporting signal-to-noise, define the signal amplitude or dataset and measurement bandwidth. A spectacular peak current with a wide noise bandwidth may be less useful than a smaller, well-filtered signal if the output must be resolved into a low-energy ADC code.

import statistics
samples = [1.02,0.99,1.01,1.00,0.98]
mean=statistics.mean(samples)
spread=statistics.pstdev(samples)
assert abs(mean-1.0)<1e-12
assert spread>0

From Cell Errors to Matrix Output Errors

Let the ideal mathematical multiplication be y=Wx and realized weights W+ΔW, ignoring other errors for the moment. Then the output error is exactly Δy=(ΔW)x. For any compatible induced matrix norm, ||Δy||≤||ΔW||·||x||. This bound is not a promised operating accuracy, but it provides a way to connect cell-level perturbations to matrix-level behavior.

Errors can cancel or reinforce. For a column where every weight is too large by the same fraction, output error is highly correlated with the input. For random errors of mixed signs, an average-case estimate may be smaller, but only with defensible assumptions about independence and distributions. Always identify whether the bound is worst-case or statistical.

A deeper model adds input encoding error Δx, amplifier offsets, read nonlinearity and output quantization. Even with perfect weights, a digital-to-analog input conversion error can enter every product in that row, yielding correlated changes across multiple output columns. Device-level accuracy therefore does not map one-to-one to final inference accuracy.

W = [[1.0,2.0],[-1.0,0.5]]
dW = [[0.01,-0.02],[0.0,0.03]]
x = [0.4,-0.2]
y0=[sum(a*b for a,b in zip(r,x)) for r in W]
y1=[sum((a+da)*b for a,da,b in zip(r,dr,x)) for r,dr in zip(W,dW)]
err=[b-a for a,b in zip(y0,y1)]
pred=[sum(a*b for a,b in zip(r,x)) for r in dW]
assert all(abs(a-b)<1e-12 for a,b in zip(err,pred))

Input Quantization and Accumulation Accuracy

An input DAC rounds a digital activation to a finite set of representable voltages or pulses. If x is replaced by x+Δx, the array ideally returns W(x+Δx), so input rounding contributes WΔx to output error. A single row-voltage error affects every output using that row. A small error in a large input component may be more consequential than a larger error in an inactive input.

Column accumulation can also demand more precision than a single stored weight. With n terms each bounded by magnitude A, the worst-case absolute sum is nA; a readout range sized for typical inputs may clip rare but valid cases. Use workload-derived distributions, explicit worst-case requirements or saturation-aware training, and do not silently mix these criteria.

Digitization introduces another rounding stage. Higher ADC precision reduces ideal quantization interval but usually costs additional energy, time or area; it cannot repair upstream analog clipping or lost information. Precision should be allocated across input, storage, accumulation, and output in that order.

ADC Saturation and Distribution-Aware Readout

An ADC must cover a useful range of column currents or sensed voltages. If the full-scale is too small, large sums saturate; if it is too large, the number of effective codes available for typical small sums may be inadequate. Differential accumulation creates additional common-mode concerns. The optimum full-scale is tied to data and required error tolerance, not only to the largest physically possible current.

Suppose a nominal ADC spans 0 to 1 V with 8 bits. Its ideal nominal step is approximately 1/256 V ≈ 3.906 mV. If typical transformed outputs occupy just 0 to 0.1 V, fewer than 26 ideal code intervals cover that ordinary range. Adjusting gain or using layer-dependent scaling may improve resolution, but outliers can then clip. Calibration and clipping-aware inference tests are needed.

A test set should cover dense activations, sparse activations, high-sum cancellation, extreme positive and negative weights, and layer transitions. Accuracy on a single convenient input distribution is not evidence that the ADC range is well chosen for all deployed workloads.

full_scale=1.0
bits=8
lsb=full_scale/2**bits
ordinary_range=0.1
assert abs(lsb-0.00390625)<1e-12
assert 25<ordinary_range/lsb<26

Quantitative Error Decomposition and Sensitivity

A practical approximate operator can be written \hat y = (W+ΔW)(x+Δx) + e_array + e_sense + e_quant + e_digital. Expanding the first product yields first-order terms ΔW x and W Δx plus the second-order product ΔWΔx. This decomposition helps assign characterization work: programming controls ΔW; row drivers control Δx; circuit simulation targets e_array; ADC and sensing target e_sense and e_quant.

For bounded errors, triangle inequalities produce conservative worst-case estimates. Root-sum-square aggregation can be useful when independent zero-mean contributors are supported by measurements, but it is not a general substitute for worst-case analysis. Shared supply noise, thermal gradients and correlated programming errors may cause multiple terms to move together.

The important deliverable is an error budget with assumptions, not a decorative percentage. Identify the output norm, input distribution, ambient conditions, time since programming, allowed clipping rate and whether the bound must hold per inference, per batch, or on aggregate classification accuracy.

# First-order scalar decomposition plus the second-order cross term.
w,dw=2.0,0.01
x,dx=0.5,-0.02
actual=(w+dw)*(x+dx)
linear=w*x+dw*x+w*dx
cross=dw*dx
assert abs(actual-(linear+cross))<1e-12

Differential Sensing: Common-Mode Rejection and Costs

A differential weight pair ideally subtracts two current paths, suppressing identical common-mode offsets. The suppression is imperfect when sense gains differ, offsets drift separately, or conductance distributions have unequal temperature response. If a desired output is a small difference between large path currents, the ADC input must handle their dynamic range or the analog subtraction circuit must provide enough matching and headroom.

For instance, a desired 1 µA differential result might be formed from 101 µA and 100 µA currents. A 1% mismatch in one current path is about 1 µA, comparable to the desired output. This example explains why differential mapping is not equivalent to arithmetic without noise or calibration.

A full energy model includes both paths' read currents, devices and sensing. A design might choose differential representation for accuracy and sign support even if it raises nominal energy; that can still be an appropriate trade if the software quality requirement cannot be met otherwise.

plus,minus=101.0,100.0  # microamps
signal=plus-minus
error=0.01*plus
assert signal==1.0 and error>signal

Calibration Modes: Offset, Gain, Column, and Cell

Offset calibration measures a reference or zero-input response and subtracts a bias. Gain calibration applies known amplitudes to estimate a column's scale. More elaborate procedures estimate per-cell conductance deviations, input-driver nonlinearity, temperature response or a whole-tile correction matrix. Increasing calibration granularity can reduce residual error while increasing calibration time, memory and data movement.

A system should distinguish calibration of the physical array from calibration of the readout chain. If an output amplifier's gain changes, a stored per-cell correction might be unnecessary; if a particular conductance drifts, a global gain correction might fail. Diagnostic patterns can isolate these sources only if the measurement design is sufficiently informative.

Calibration data have their own integrity requirements: timestamps, temperature, weight-image identifier, device location, validity range, procedure version and an uncertainty estimate. Use independent validation patterns not used for fitting. Fitting every output in a training set perfectly is not proof of correctness on new inputs or after device aging.

Post-Training Quantization and Hardware-Aware Training

One deployment path trains a numerical model digitally, then quantizes and programs the learned weights to analog conductance states. This post-training mapping is simplest when the model tolerates limited precision and physical error. It may require output-scale calibration, bias correction or clipping-range selection without updating all trained weights.

A second path incorporates approximate device behavior during optimization. Hardware-aware training can inject measured conductance noise, model nonlinearity, account for quantization, or expose interconnect effects through differentiable approximations. It may improve accuracy on a particular hardware distribution, but its results depend on the quality and validity of the hardware model. Overfitting a simulator to one wafer or temperature can create fragile deployments.

A third path retrains after mapping using measured effective weights or outputs. It potentially repairs mismatch but adds programming, measurements and software cost. All three methods should be evaluated with a frozen held-out dataset and measurements across device aging and environmental variation—not only with accuracy immediately after calibration.

Bit Slicing and Multi-Cell Weight Encoding

When one physical cell cannot reliably distinguish enough weight levels, a designer can spread one logical weight across multiple cells. In bit slicing, cells or sub-arrays hold separate weighted significance groups, which are read and combined to form a higher-precision result. The combination may be performed with weighted analog summation or digitally after individual ADC conversions. Either implementation can improve representational fidelity while increasing storage, routing, conversion or cycle count.

For example, an eight-bit unsigned number can be partitioned into two four-bit digits q = q_low + 16 q_high. If each digit is stored in a separate low-precision array and its contribution is recovered accurately, the logical eight-bit result can be reconstructed. This does not guarantee eight-bit analog accuracy: each partial result retains device, driver and readout error, and the higher-significance slice can amplify its own error sixteenfold.

Bit slicing should be evaluated with a complete accounting of analog reads, tile count, shift-and-add operations, intermediate precision and energy. A design description saying 'eight-bit weights' without identifying physical state resolution and recombination method is incomplete.

q=173
lo=q%16
hi=q//16
assert (lo,hi)==(13,10)
assert lo+16*hi==q

Partitioning Large Matrices into Tiles

A dense layer whose dimensions exceed a physical array must be tiled. Rows can be divided into input blocks, and columns into output blocks. Each tile computes a partial matrix–vector product. For a given output block, partial results are accumulated across row tiles; for multiple output blocks, results must be routed to the next stage in the model.

If a matrix has n=1,024 inputs and m=512 outputs, a 256×128 tile array would require (1,024/256)×(512/128)=16 fully populated tiles under a simple rectangular mapping. Each output block needs four partial sums per inference. Whether those four parts are accumulated digitally or in analog hardware affects required ADC resolution, bandwidth and interconnect energy.

Mapping has to preserve operator order and quantization semantics. Quantizing each tile's partial sum before adding is not identical to accumulating all analog contributions and quantizing once. The two methods have different rounding and saturation behavior. The design should specify the dataflow, number of passes, tile utilization and partial-sum format.

import math
n_inputs=1024
n_outputs=512
r,c=256,128
row_tiles=math.ceil(n_inputs/r)
col_tiles=math.ceil(n_outputs/c)
assert row_tiles==4 and col_tiles==4 and row_tiles*col_tiles==16

Convolution Mapping and Input Reuse

Convolution can be transformed into repeated vector–matrix products, but that representation can demand substantial input rearrangement. An im2col lowering builds overlapping patches into a matrix and can duplicate activation data. A hardware accelerator may instead stream patches, reuse on-chip input windows or support specialized array dataflows. Each option changes the number of converter activations and input transfers.

A useful utilization metric is the number of useful mathematical multiplies mapped onto a driven crosspoint during the measured interval, not the number of fabricated cells. Small convolution kernels, sparse channels, boundary padding and unusual tensor shapes can underutilize a tile. Bias and activation functions may require digital resources between layers.

Benchmark convolution on actual tensor shapes and batches. Record the cost of creating or streaming patches, weight programming, per-layer ADC/DAC work and tensor layout conversion. Counting only ideal crossbar column sums omits a large part of a deployable convolutional-network pipeline.

Transformers, Attention, and Constant-Weight Versus Dynamic Products

AIMC is naturally suited to some fixed-weight linear projections in attention-based neural networks, such as learned matrices used to produce queries, keys and values. However, the attention score QKᵀ multiplies data-dependent matrices whose elements change with each input. An architecture optimized for slowly programmed, retained weights may not map such dynamic products efficiently if reprogramming is slow or costly.

Softmax, normalization, residual additions, activation functions and sequence-dependent memory traffic may also remain digital. For autoregressive inference, model-cache activity and small batch sizes can affect utilization. Therefore a transformer benchmark should state exactly which operators run in memory, which require external digital compute, and whether weight or activation programming occurs during every request.

This distinction prevents a misleading inference from 'fast matrix–vector multiplication' to 'fast end-to-end language-model inference'. The latter includes nonlinear functions, dynamic state, attention-cache management, communication and usually multiple precision regimes. A valid comparison must match quality, sequence lengths, batches and the actual deployment procedure.

Sparse Inputs, Sparse Weights, and Irregular Operations

Ideal crossbar summation can exploit broad parallelism, but sparsity does not automatically translate to proportionally lower energy. A dense array may drive many lines and include many conducting or leakage paths even when the logical matrix contains zeros. To save energy, the architecture may need input-gating, weight-state coding or selective tile activation. The cost of detecting and routing sparse structure matters.

Irregular workloads, such as graph traversal or pointer-heavy programs, involve data-dependent access patterns and branch decisions. They may be dominated by control and routing rather than dense linear algebra. Mapping them onto an analog crossbar can require repeated reconfiguration or extensive digital orchestration, undermining the proposed data-movement savings.

Distinguish algorithmic sparsity from physical inactive circuitry. Reporting a software sparsity percentage without measured disabled-driver current, leakage, readout activity and tile utilization does not establish a power reduction. Compare against a digital baseline that also supports sparse kernels when relevant.

Multi-Layer Inference and Inter-Tile Communication

A single layer's analog MVM can be fast while a multilayer model is dominated by moving activations among tiles. Analog signals may be digitized between layers for storage, nonlinear processing, routing and error correction, and later converted back to analog voltages. An all-analog inter-layer path may reduce conversions but introduces analog storage, fan-out, buffering, noise growth and precision challenges.

A system architect must specify the interface after each operator: physical current, analog voltage, sampled charge or digital word. Different interfaces have different bandwidths and data lifetimes. A digitized output is easier to buffer and route at scale, but incurs conversion and interconnect energy. Analog forwarding may be efficient for fixed, local dataflows and less suitable for arbitrary dynamic routing.

Published systems demonstrate concrete approaches; for example, IBM's multi-core PCM research addresses integrated inference and inter-tile communication. Its measured results should be read alongside the particular workload, technology, model accuracy and inclusion of peripheral operations, rather than extrapolated to all networks or process nodes.

Application-Level Accuracy Versus Arithmetic Precision

Hardware arithmetic can be approximate while the application still meets its quality requirements. A classifier may tolerate moderate perturbations in many weights, whereas a scientific solver or safety-critical estimator may require tight absolute or relative numerical error. An accuracy metric such as top-1 classification does not replace a numerical comparison of intermediate MVM outputs, and neither alone establishes reliability outside a test distribution.

Define at least two complementary evaluation layers: operator fidelity, which compares array outputs with a high-precision reference under chosen test patterns, and task performance, which measures the effect on the whole model. Record calibration state, temperature, time since programming and any test-time adaptation. A neural-network accuracy score before and after analog mapping is useful only when the evaluation dataset and model implementation are comparable.

For tasks with rare but consequential errors, overall average accuracy can mask tail failures. Test extreme inputs, drift conditions, adversarially sensitive patterns and out-of-range behavior. Approximate arithmetic is not synonymous with unreliable computing; it means the required reliability case must be established at the relevant system boundary.

Composing Layer Errors Through a Network

Suppose a network layer has form h_{k+1}=φ(W_k h_k+b_k). If φ is Lipschitz with constant L_k over the relevant domain, an output perturbation can be bounded in terms of the weight perturbation, input perturbation, bias error and nonlinear amplification. A rough bound includes L_k(||W_k||·||δh_k|| + ||δW_k||·||h_k|| + ||δb_k||) plus higher-order terms.

Across layers, errors may accumulate or be attenuated. Saturating nonlinearities can suppress some perturbations; high-gain or ill-conditioned transformations can amplify others. A good hardware-aware simulation evaluates realistic joint error distributions and measures actual model outcomes rather than assuming every layer's errors add independently in quadrature.

Calibration also changes the mathematical network: inserting layer-specific affine corrections can improve output fidelity but adds arithmetic and possibly communication. Its cost belongs in the end-to-end benchmark, and its parameters must be frozen during unbiased evaluation.

# Elementary sensitivity propagation for two linear layers.
W1,W2=2.0,0.5
x=0.3
delta1=0.01
ideal=W2*(W1*x)
perturbed=W2*((W1+delta1)*x)
assert abs((perturbed-ideal)-(W2*delta1*x))<1e-12

A Full-System Energy Accounting Equation

A credible per-inference estimate should state a boundary such as E_total = E_input_io + E_DAC + E_array_read + E_sense + E_ADC + E_digital + E_interconnect + E_memory + E_control + E_idle_share, with separate amortized terms for programming and calibration. These categories are not necessarily independent if circuits share supplies, but the accounting should avoid omitting them or counting the same energy twice.

For example, an illustrative inference might consume 2 µJ in array reads, 4 µJ in converter and sensing operations, 3 µJ in digital postprocessing, and 1 µJ in transfers and control, for 10 µJ total. Reporting only the 2 µJ array component would overstate the full-system efficiency advantage by a factor of five. This demonstration contains no measured values.

Idle energy depends on inference rate: a powered accelerator doing sporadic work can be dominated by clocking, leakage or standby cost. Document active, standby and transition energy, throughput, duty cycle and amortization interval before making comparisons to GPUs, CPUs or specialized digital inference engines.

components_uj = {'array':2,'conversion_and_sense':4,'digital':3,'transfer_control':1}
total_uj=sum(components_uj.values())
assert total_uj==10 and total_uj/components_uj['array']==5

Power, Energy, Throughput, and Latency Are Distinct Metrics

Power is energy per unit time; energy per inference is the integrated joules needed to produce one valid result; throughput is completed useful work per unit time; and latency is elapsed time from an accepted request to a validated output. A design can reduce energy while increasing latency or increase throughput with many parallel arrays while using more total power.

When a tile processes several column sums simultaneously, counting mathematical operations requires a declared convention, often two operations per multiply–add in reported TOPS figures. Such convention cannot replace actual delivered inference requests, which may contain additional operators, padding, idle tiles or redundant passes. A high-frequency cycle quoted for the array may not include programming, conversion, interconnect or queueing.

Use both component-level and application-level metrics. The former help diagnose bottlenecks; the latter determine whether a user workload benefits. Specify batch size, input distribution, model dimensions, required numerical quality, measurement boundary and whether preprocessing and host transfers are included.

Interpreting TOPS and TOPS per Watt

The common metric TOPS/W represents nominal or delivered tera-operations per second divided by watts, numerically equivalent to tera-operations per joule under the chosen operation-count convention. It depends strongly on what is counted as an operation and which components are included in measured power. A chip's reported TOPS/W may be a valuable experimental result, but it is not directly comparable to every other accelerator unless precision, utilization, operators and measurement boundaries match.

Suppose an array executes one million useful multiply–accumulates per query and each MAC is conventionally counted as two operations. At 100,000 successful queries per second, the stated throughput is 2×10¹¹ operations/s = 0.2 TOPS. If entire-chip power is 10 W, the useful delivered efficiency is 0.02 TOPS/W. A peak figure based on all physical cells toggling every cycle could be much larger but would answer a different question.

Report input/output precision and task quality alongside power. A design that produces more nominal operations but must rerun or repair a significant fraction of results needs a metric tied to accepted outputs.

mac_per_query=1_000_000
queries_per_s=100_000
power_w=10.0
ops_per_s=2*mac_per_query*queries_per_s
tops=ops_per_s/1e12
eff=tops/power_w
assert abs(tops-0.2)<1e-12 and abs(eff-0.02)<1e-12

A Fair Digital Baseline and Measurement Boundary

The baseline should run the same model or mathematical workload to the required quality, with realistic memory behavior and the strongest relevant digital optimizations available for that platform. Comparing an AIMC tile against an unoptimized CPU loop makes little sense if a conventional digital accelerator, quantized kernel or high-reuse SRAM design is the relevant alternative. Equally, treating every digital operation as an off-chip memory fetch unfairly penalizes caching and register reuse.

Match input batch size, accuracy target, output precision, model sparsity, update frequency, thermal envelope and physical power boundary. If AIMC must perform calibration or digital error correction to reach the required model quality, include those costs. If the baseline has model compilation or weight loading costs, include them over the same number of queries.

A transparent benchmark table separates array-only arithmetic, full accelerator chip, host-plus-accelerator, and datacenter system power. The answer to 'which is more efficient?' may change as that boundary expands. This is not an inconsistency; it reveals which components dominate real deployment.

Throughput Scaling and Converter Bottlenecks

An array may generate m column currents at once, but if the sensing system has only r ADC channels, complete digitization may take at least ceil(m/r) conversion groups, ignoring setup and pipeline overlap. More ADCs increase potential throughput and area/power. Higher ADC resolution may slow conversion or consume more energy, depending on architecture. The input drive system can introduce another bottleneck if activations must be serialized by bit plane.

For illustration, m=256 columns read by 16 ADC channels in groups implies 16 groups; if each group takes 200 ns and no overlap is possible, that portion alone consumes at least 3.2 µs. A parallel group scheduler or integrating converter may alter the bound, but the bottleneck cannot be erased by quoting an instantaneous current-summation time.

Performance analysis must consider latency pipeline fill/drain, overlapping of tile operations, data dependencies between layers and controller scheduling. Ideally, publish a timeline showing when arrays drive, settle, sense, convert, transfer and finish the accepted output.

import math
columns=256
adc_channels=16
groups=math.ceil(columns/adc_channels)
readout_time_s=groups*200e-9
assert groups==16 and abs(readout_time_s-3.2e-6)<1e-15

Latency Budget for a Complete Inference

A single completed request can involve input movement, input digital-to-analog conversion, array settling, sensing, ADC conversion, activation processing, routing to the next layer and final readout. Some stages are overlapped or pipelined, but a latency budget must follow actual data dependencies rather than summing a convenient set of peak reciprocals.

For a nonoverlapped toy design, let input loading take 0.6 µs, driving and settling 0.4 µs, sensing/ADC 1.2 µs, digital processing 0.5 µs and output transport 0.3 µs. The end-to-end latency is 3.0 µs, even though the analog accumulation phase occupies only a fraction of that interval. A different pipeline may overlap requests, improving throughput without reducing one request's latency.

Deadline-sensitive use requires a tail latency specification across worst-case input patterns, thermal states, queueing and calibration interruptions. Average latency alone cannot establish real-time correctness. Include the treatment of outlier outputs and any software retries in the agreed service envelope.

stages_us=[0.6,0.4,1.2,0.5,0.3]
latency_us=sum(stages_us)
assert abs(latency_us-3.0)<1e-12

Array Utilization and the Cost of Padding

A rectangular memory tile is most efficient when the mapped tensor uses most of its weighted intersections. If a layer is smaller or irregular, inactive cells may still occupy area, and peripheral circuitry may still need to power whole columns or banks. In some designs, spare capacity can host another layer or multiple batches, but reconfiguration and simultaneous resource conflicts must be analyzed.

Define utilization carefully: weight occupancy, active row/column fraction, operations per sensing event and duty factor are different measures. A tile can have high weight occupancy but low runtime utilization if the software network waits on another operator; it can have low occupancy but good power efficiency if unused blocks are effectively power-gated.

Tiling decisions should consider model shape, quantization, layer scheduling, readout sharing and reuse across batches. Report both theoretical mapping efficiency and measured active energy. An allocation diagram with exact array dimensions makes a performance claim much easier to audit than one aggregate utilization percentage.

The Algorithmic Role of Approximate MVM

The physical crossbar is not an exact general-purpose matrix multiplication unit. Its output is a calibrated estimate of a constrained linear operator; precision and range vary with hardware, workload and time. That is enough to make it useful for certain applications whose algorithms tolerate bounded approximation. It does not establish superiority for arbitrary integer arithmetic, cryptographic primitives, transactional databases or control logic requiring precise sequential state transitions.

For approximate MVM, define a numerical contract: allowed norm-wise error, per-component error, bias, probability of exceeding limits, overflow behavior and conditions of validity. For machine-learning inference also include the task-level metric and acceptable degradation from a digital reference. An output that is 'almost right' must be judged against an explicit application requirement.

Hardware-aware software can compensate for physical imperfections, but its compensation cost and future validity are part of the computational design. The best architecture is selected from the entire pipeline, not by treating a physics-based dot product as though it replaces a complete digital computer.

On-Device Training and Update-Rate Constraints

Training introduces weight updates, gradients and optimizer state. If every gradient step requires analog programming, update latency, nonlinear state changes and endurance may dominate. The physical update rule is not necessarily proportional to the requested arithmetic increment, and positive versus negative updates may have different dynamics. One repeated-use advantage of AIMC—stable stored weights—can be diminished by frequent writes.

Possible strategies include digital gradient computation with occasional remapping, restricted analog updates, hardware-aware compensation, or training only selected layers. Each changes model quality, write energy and operational complexity. A hardware demonstration of inference should not be described as demonstrating full training capability unless the weight-update and backward-pass costs are actually measured.

When evaluating online learning, specify writes per cell, mean and tail update latency, convergence quality, environmental drift, data-retention behavior, and cumulative endurance consumption. These are workload-specific constraints and cannot be inferred merely from a read-MVM throughput number.

Reliability, Yield, Redundancy, and Fault Recovery

Practical arrays need to cope with defective cells, unstable conductance states, peripheral failures and temperature-dependent behavior. Redundancy can remap weights around failing devices or store multiple physical copies of important coefficients. Error correction may operate on digital outputs, measured conductance maps or model parameters. These repairs consume space, write cycles and computation.

Reliability evaluation needs more than average classification accuracy at delivery. Record the rate of outputs outside a numerical tolerance, calibration failures, programming retries, and the proportion of chips that meet the specified workload requirement over time. Spatial fault clustering can defeat approaches that assume independent random failures. Service policies must define when a tile is disabled, recalibrated or replaced.

The required quality depends on the application. A demonstration of approximate keyword spotting has different failure consequences from an embedded medical or industrial control system. Safety-critical use would require independent system-level assurance rather than relying on nominal neural-network accuracy.

Thermal Effects and Operating-Point Management

Temperature changes device conductances, sense-amplifier offsets, access-transistor behavior and interconnect resistance. It also changes power dissipation and may alter retention or programming response. A weight map characterized at a laboratory temperature may therefore produce a biased or noisy output when deployed in a compact device with variable workload heating.

A realistic test varies both ambient temperature and self-heating caused by workload duty cycle. Measure the time needed to reach a new steady state, whether a global calibration remains valid, and how input patterns affect power hotspots. If recalibration is required, its energy and unavailable-computation interval belong in the operating cost.

Thermal design involves a coupled trade-off: a higher read current can strengthen the sensed signal but create more heat and line drop. Lowering active voltage may save power but worsen signal-to-noise or lengthen settling. The preferred operating point is the one that satisfies application error and speed requirements with margin across the specified environment.

Security and Model Confidentiality Considerations

An AIMC accelerator stores valuable model parameters as physical device states and exposes analog interfaces whose behavior may depend on data and stored weights. Threat models can include malicious input patterns, probing of accessible conductance states, fault injection, access to programming interfaces, and side-channel signals associated with current and power use. The severity depends on physical access, architecture and deployment.

A device that stores weights nonvolatilely can preserve sensitive parameters after power loss. Erasure procedures must establish whether resetting a digital pointer actually overwrites physical analog states. Wear-leveling, redundancy and failed programming cells can complicate secure deletion. Standard authentication, signed updates and key management remain necessary for the digital controller.

Do not claim that analog uncertainty automatically provides privacy or cryptographic protection. Evaluate confidentiality, integrity, availability and model-update provenance with the same rigor used for other embedded accelerators, while adding measured analysis of novel physical exposures where relevant.

A Systematic Hardware-Aware Simulation Workflow

A useful software prototype begins with a verified digital reference implementation of y=Wx and the complete application model. Add one nonideality at a time: weight quantization, measured programming spread, input DAC quantization, wire-dependent IR drop, sense gain and offset, ADC clipping, drift, and timing. Maintain a deterministic seed where randomness is simulated, but do not confuse a seeded model with physical reproducibility.

Characterize parameters from measured devices and retain distributions or correlations where the effects require them. For each model version, report operator error over representative and challenging patterns and application accuracy on held-out data. Compare model predictions with hardware measurements and revise the model if it fails outside its calibration subset.

A synthetic noise injection that improves a model's robustness is not direct proof that the real device will meet the same standard. Validation requires independent hardware test cases, environmental coverage, and repeated measurements across programming cycles and elapsed time.

Physical Design and Circuit Verification Workflow

After behavioral simulation, validate the cell and array equations with circuit models that include realistic voltage-dependent current, access devices and parasitic networks. Progress from a single cell to columns, representative subarrays and full array corners. At each scale compare sensed outputs with the mathematical reference, identify the dominant residual, and verify power and timing under intended read patterns.

Peripheral circuits need separate verification: row-driver linearity, DAC settling, amplifier bandwidth and stability, ADC transfer, bias generation, reference noise, thermal behavior and reset sequencing. MVM accuracy can change when a readout circuit that worked at low load is connected to a larger physical array. Include layout parasitics and realistic interconnect loading before claiming a cycle-time target.

Formal digital control checks and firmware integration tests can catch configuration mistakes, address aliasing and invalid sequencing. The final gate is a fully measured operator test, not the independent success of every schematic block. A stitched performance estimate is useful for planning but must be labeled as such.

Benchmark Dataset, Traceability, and Statistical Reporting

A reproducible AIMC study should specify model weights, input tensors or their generative distributions, mapped array layouts, device programming maps, calibration state and environmental conditions. Each reported accuracy figure needs a dataset version and inference procedure. Each energy figure needs a power measurement boundary and workload rate. Precision and batching are essential metadata, not footnotes.

For repeated measurement, present median, spread and tail error as appropriate, plus sample count and uncertainty. Distinguish transient warmup measurements from steady state, and time shortly after programming from time after expected drift. A performance result from one exceptionally successful chip must not silently be treated as a population-level yield claim.

Where benchmark figures depend on a publication, cite the original experiment and do not interpolate unsupported speedups. This article uses published research to illustrate architectural progress and uses labeled teaching examples for calculations that have not been measured on a commercial device.

What Published Demonstrations Establish—and What They Do Not

IBM researchers reported multi-core phase-change-memory analog inference chips in 2023, including a 64-core mixed-signal demonstration and a 34-tile design for complex neural workloads. These papers are important because they address more than isolated conductance measurements: they describe integration, routing, peripherals and application-level accuracy. Their published throughput and energy results, however, are tied to the exact experimental chip, model, workload and measurement boundary.

Research on cross-layer error analysis, hardware-aware training and high-precision AIMC has continued through 2026. Reviews document how line resistance, sensing nonlinearity, memory variability, drift, quantization and correction circuitry interact. These results support disciplined feasibility analysis; they do not justify assuming that a particular array dimension, energy gain or effective precision is available from every device technology.

When comparing studies, check whether they report peak array operations, chip-sustained operations, model accuracy, end-to-end inference, or power including test-platform equipment. Those are distinct scientific claims. A study's success under a chosen benchmark is evidence of possibility, not proof of universal dominance over optimized digital systems.

A Practical Feasibility and Design-Review Checklist

Begin with the application's computational graph and identify the linear operations actually worth moving into memory. Quantify matrix dimensions, batch rates, activation statistics, model refresh schedule, numerical error tolerance, required latency and environmental limits. Choose a matched digital baseline and define the benchmark boundary before optimizing a cell or tile.

Map the operator to physical dimensions and specify conductance/voltage coding, sign handling, tile count, ADC/DAC sharing, partial sums and inter-tile movement. Create measured or validated models for IR drop, nonlinearity, programming variation, drift, quantization and readout noise. Establish full energy and latency budgets including programming, calibration and idle power.

Finally, run held-out inference and stress patterns on actual hardware. Record failures, tail errors, environmental response, yield, updates and repair behavior. If the system misses an accuracy or energy target, determine whether the bottleneck is the array, the conversion chain, programming, communication or application-level routing. No single cell-level figure resolves the entire design decision.

Applied Investigation 1: A Two-Column Crossbar Verification

Scenario. A three-input array has an ideal conductance matrix in microsiemens with rows [(4,1),(2,3),(1,5)]. Inputs are 0.2, 0.1 and 0.3 V. A circuit team reports sensed currents of 1.28 and 2.06 µA, and wants a rigorous comparison with the ideal arithmetic rather than an assertion that the array 'works'.

Analysis. Compute the expected ideal currents: 1.3 and 2.0 µA. The measured residuals are −0.02 and +0.06 µA. They represent about −1.54% and +3% of the corresponding nonzero ideal currents. Those two observations alone cannot distinguish conductance mismatch, amplifier gain, row-voltage error or IR drop. Repeat with zero input, individual activated rows, several amplitudes and independent known weights. This isolates offsets, per-cell deviations and nonlinearity.

Design decision. Accept or reject the array only against a stated tolerance and input family. A ±2% relative-current target fails on the second output in this case. For near-zero expected outputs, relative error becomes unstable and an absolute-current tolerance is more appropriate. Record the exact drive voltage at the cell terminals if possible; otherwise, ideal array currents are not an adequate physical ground truth.

ideal=[1.3,2.0]
observed=[1.28,2.06]
relative=[(m-t)/t for m,t in zip(observed,ideal)]
assert abs(relative[0]+0.02/1.3)<1e-12
assert abs(relative[1]-0.03)<1e-12
assert abs(relative[1])>0.02

Applied Investigation 2: Signed Four-Bit Weight Mapping

Scenario. A small classifier requires both positive and negative weights. Each available memory element can reliably realize 16 conductance levels, and the designer proposes a two-cell differential mapping. The digital reference requires signed values in approximately [−1,1]. The aim is to distinguish logical weight precision from physical cell precision.

Analysis. Set G_min=2 µS, G_max=17 µS, and interpret one unit of normalized weight as a 15 µS difference: w=(G⁺−G⁻)/15. The maximum positive weight can be represented by (17,2), and the maximum negative weight by (2,17). Interior weights must be mapped to available cell levels. If a requested w=0.3 requires 4.5 µS difference, rounding to integer-microsiemens steps yields 4 or 5 µS, corresponding to 0.2667 or 0.3333, before device noise and drift.

Design decision. Evaluate the mapped weight matrix by measured currents and model accuracy, not simply by assigning four bits per cell. Differential storage doubles nominal cell count, and the programmed difference can be noisier than each individual state. Quantify array read energy, comparator or ADC range, calibration frequency and any additional bit slicing necessary to meet the classifier's accuracy requirement.

levels=list(range(2,18))
scale=15.0
mapped_diff=round(0.3*scale)
represented=mapped_diff/scale
assert mapped_diff==4
assert abs(represented-0.3)<0.04
assert (max(levels)-min(levels))/scale==1

Applied Investigation 3: Writing a Model That Rarely Runs

Scenario. A factory installs an AIMC accelerator that must be reprogrammed with a new weight image each morning. The proposed device saves an estimated 5 µJ per successful full inference compared with the optimized digital baseline, while reprogramming and verification together consume 0.5 J per model image. The factory expects only 40,000 useful calls per day.

Analysis. The daily operational energy saving before programming is 40,000×5 µJ = 0.2 J. Subtracting the 0.5 J programming energy leaves a net deficit of 0.3 J for AIMC under this limited model. Break-even requires 0.5/(5×10⁻⁶)=100,000 inferences per new weight image. Calibration, idle power and discarded outputs would worsen the result; a smaller program energy or larger query volume could reverse it.

Design decision. This result does not justify rejecting AIMC on all grounds—it may still offer latency, thermal, packaging or availability benefits—but it disproves an energy advantage for the stated duty cycle. The decision record should explicitly separate one-time programming energy from repeated read energy and should confirm that both devices' full-system power boundaries are comparable.

queries_per_day=40_000
saved_j=5e-6
program_j=0.5
net_energy_saved=queries_per_day*saved_j-program_j
breakeven=program_j/saved_j
assert abs(net_energy_saved+0.3)<1e-12
assert round(breakeven)==100_000

Applied Investigation 4: Converter Sharing Versus Latency

Scenario. A tile has 128 output columns and each ADC conversion group consumes 250 ns, including the specified settling interval. The architecture team is comparing eight shared converters with 32 shared converters. Assume conversion groups cannot overlap and hold all other delays constant for this illustration.

Analysis. Eight converters require ceil(128/8)=16 groups, or 4.0 µs of readout. Thirty-two converters require four groups, or 1.0 µs. The change saves 3.0 µs of readout under these assumptions. It does not imply a fourfold improvement in complete inference: row setup, array settling, activation functions, routing and host transfers remain, and the additional ADCs require silicon area and power.

Design decision. Calculate the end-to-end latency with real overlap and pipeline stages, then measure total energy per accepted inference. Compare dynamic ADC energy, ADC idle power, calibration overhead and thermal conditions. A latency-critical application may justify more converters despite higher instantaneous power; an energy-limited batch workload may prefer the smaller readout bank if throughput targets are still satisfied.

import math
columns=128
t_group=250e-9
slow=math.ceil(columns/8)*t_group
fast=math.ceil(columns/32)*t_group
assert abs(slow-4e-6)<1e-15
assert abs(fast-1e-6)<1e-15

Applied Investigation 5: Drift After a Model Update

Scenario. A system uses PCM-like weight elements whose conductance follows an illustrative law G(t)=G₀(t/t₀)^(−ν) over a specified interval. One weight has G₀=10 µS and ν=0.04. The deployment seeks to know whether a fixed calibration remains appropriate after time increases by a factor of 1,000.

Analysis. The example predicts G/G₀ = 1000^(−0.04) ≈ 0.759, a drop of about 24.1%. This is a deliberately selected modeling example; it is not a measured or universal PCM drift exponent. If every cell shared exactly the same exponent, a global gain correction might recover some scale. Real cells and conductance levels can drift differently, so the relative weight pattern and task accuracy may still change.

Design decision. Capture conductance maps at multiple time points, temperatures and read patterns, and evaluate application accuracy on held-out data. Select calibration intervals based on measured error trajectories and budget their sensing time and energy. Do not interpret a binary nonvolatile retention statement as a guarantee that a multi-level analog weight remains accurate.

ratio=1000
nu=0.04
remaining=ratio**(-nu)
assert 0.75<remaining<0.77
assert abs(1-remaining-0.241)<0.005

Applied Investigation 6: A Tiled Neural Layer

Scenario. A dense layer maps 1,024 inputs to 512 outputs. Available AIMC tiles support 256 driven rows and 128 output columns. The team quotes the speed of a single tile operation as though it were the speed of the full layer.

Analysis. The layer occupies 4×4=16 full tiles. Each of the four output-column groups requires four row-tile partial results. If every tile can operate concurrently, the current-summation stage may be short, but the architecture must still digitize or combine those partial results, route input activations to all sixteen tiles and preserve enough partial-sum precision to avoid saturation and rounding loss. If there are fewer than sixteen physical tiles, weight remapping or multiple passes increase delay.

Design decision. Produce a scheduling trace listing physical tiles, resident weights, input broadcasts, conversions, accumulations and nonlinear activations. Report actual layer latency and energy at the required precision, including bandwidth to the next layer. Counting sixteen tile MVMs as if they were one free operation overstates the computational capacity of the hardware.

import math
input_tiles=math.ceil(1024/256)
output_tiles=math.ceil(512/128)
assert input_tiles==4
assert output_tiles==4
assert input_tiles*output_tiles==16

Applied Investigation 7: Why TOPS per Watt Does Not Settle Procurement

Scenario. Two accelerators report different efficiencies. Accelerator A advertises a high peak array TOPS/W figure but delivers only 20,000 complete inferences per second on a specified model at 8 W whole-chip power. Accelerator B delivers 35,000 accepted inferences per second at 10 W and meets the same application-accuracy requirement.

Analysis. The direct energy-per-completed-inference values are 8/20,000 = 0.4 mJ for A and 10/35,000 ≈ 0.286 mJ for B. Despite higher power, B expends less energy for each accepted completed inference on this particular workload. The published peak array metric might still be valid for A, but it measures a different boundary and potentially different utilization and precision.

Design decision. Compare both devices on identical model versions, batches, input distributions and quality thresholds. Include any host processing, calibration, update cycles and idle intervals that materially affect deployment. An acquisition decision should use throughput, accepted-output energy, latency percentiles, lifetime, operating temperature, software support and reliability together—not the largest isolated TOPS/W number.

power_a,queries_a=8.0,20_000
power_b,queries_b=10.0,35_000
energy_a=power_a/queries_a
energy_b=power_b/queries_b
assert abs(energy_a-0.0004)<1e-12
assert energy_b<energy_a

Applied Investigation 8: Hardware-Aware Model Acceptance

Scenario. A research team reports 98.0% test accuracy digitally, 96.5% immediately after analog programming, and 95.0% after a temperature and drift stress interval. The product requirement is at least 97% measured accuracy on a specified evaluation set after stress. The team proposes improving average cell noise and declaring the design ready.

Analysis. The current device misses the deployment requirement by two percentage points after stress, despite acceptable-looking immediate results. Improving one RMS noise term may not solve drift, IR drop or distribution shift. The team must localize error sources, test whether column calibration or retraining recovers the gap, and measure the energy and latency costs of those repairs. It should avoid testing only on the calibration set.

Design decision. Freeze the dataset and acceptance threshold, validate a revised architecture across devices and operating conditions, and retain an audit trail of model weights, mapping, programming history, compensation logic and measured outcome. The numerical threshold here is illustrative; the method is general: task-level acceptance follows application requirements, not enthusiasm about a new device primitive.

required=0.97
digital=0.98
initial=0.965
stressed=0.95
assert digital>=required
assert initial<required and stressed<required
assert abs(required-stressed-0.02)<1e-12

Synthesis: What an AIMC Design Must Prove

AIMC changes how some arithmetic is physically implemented, but it does not remove the need for quantitative engineering. The ideal crossbar product derives from basic circuit laws; practical fidelity depends on resistive and capacitive parasitics, device programming, time variation, conversion interfaces and numerical mapping. Some errors are calibratable; others depend strongly on input pattern and cannot be corrected by one constant gain.

To claim a workload-level benefit, the system must satisfy a quality contract at the full application output and an energy or time contract at a fully stated measurement boundary. Weight programming, refresh, calibration, ADC/DAC use, movement among tiles, digital nonlinear stages, standby power and failed or repeated operations belong in the calculation. A comparison against a deliberately weak digital baseline is insufficient.

The appropriate conclusion is conditional: properly designed analog in-memory engines can be valuable for high-reuse, approximate linear algebra, as demonstrated in research systems, but their relevance is determined by workload characteristics and measured end-to-end performance. They are not a blanket replacement for exact, general-purpose digital computation.

Review Questions and Answer Key

The following questions test physical interpretation, mathematical modeling, system accounting and editorial distinctions. Answers appear directly beneath each question for independent study.

  1. What defines analog in-memory computing, rather than simply placing logic near memory?

    Answer: The storage structure participates physically in continuous-valued arithmetic; near-memory digital units can remain ordinary digital computation.

  2. Why is a fast crossbar MVM not sufficient to claim fast whole-model inference?

    Answer: Programming, conversion, sensing, nonlinear operators, tiling, communication and control can dominate complete-model time.

  3. State the ideal column-current equation for an input-driven conductance array.

    Answer: I_j = sum_i G[i,j] V_i under suitable linearity, line-voltage and sensing assumptions.

  4. Which physical laws underlie the usual resistive MVM explanation?

    Answer: Ohm's law for each element and Kirchhoff's current law for each sensed column.

  5. What mathematical transpose often arises between physical array indexing and W x?

    Answer: Rows indexed by inputs and columns by outputs implement a map proportional to G-transpose times x.

  6. How can two conductance elements encode one signed weight?

    Answer: Use a scaled difference G-plus minus G-minus, with extra cells and differential sensing or subtraction.

  7. Why may negative activations require two analog passes?

    Answer: A unipolar driver can encode x as positive-minus-negative components, whose results are subsequently subtracted.

  8. What must a voltage-to-mathematical-weight scaling worksheet declare?

    Answer: Input voltage range, conductance mapping, sum range, gain, limits, error allowance and any offset compensation.

  9. How does a finite-transimpedance-amplifier output range affect results?

    Answer: Large sensed currents clip at the amplifier or ADC limits, causing unrecoverable output error.

  10. Why do DACs and ADCs matter to analog-array energy?

    Answer: They drive and digitize signals, consume time/area/energy, and introduce their own quantization and noise.

  11. How is analog SRAM-CIM different from fully digital SRAM-CIM?

    Answer: Analog SRAM-CIM accumulates current/charge/voltage; digital CIM uses discrete logic and digital accumulation.

  12. What distinguishes phase-change-memory drift from a digital bit-retention specification?

    Answer: Analog conductance may gradually change even while a binary stored state remains distinguishable.

  13. What is IR drop and why does its error depend on data?

    Answer: Interconnect resistance changes local drive/sense voltages; current and voltage drops depend on active cells and inputs.

  14. What causes sneak-path currents?

    Answer: Unintended conduction paths through unselected devices or weakly isolated structures.

  15. What additional cost does a write-verify algorithm incur?

    Answer: Repeated programming pulses, verify reads, control, energy, latency and endurance stress.

  16. How is weight-update energy amortized across inference requests?

    Answer: Spread initial programming and any recurring refresh costs across the number of accepted inferences reusing that weight image.

  17. What is the first-order matrix output perturbation from a weight error?

    Answer: Delta y = Delta W times x, before including input, sensing and quantization errors.

  18. Why is a root-sum-square error budget sometimes invalid?

    Answer: It requires suitable independence or uncorrelated zero-mean assumptions; systematic and correlated terms may add coherently.

  19. How does bit slicing increase logical precision?

    Answer: Multiple lower-precision physical states encode digits that are weighted and recombined, at additional hardware or cycle cost.

  20. Why does tile count depend on both matrix dimensions?

    Answer: Input rows and output columns are partitioned independently; their required tile counts multiply.

  21. Why are dynamic attention products harder for fixed-weight AIMC?

    Answer: Query–key matrices change with input and may require frequent effective weight programming or different dataflow.

  22. Does application classification accuracy establish numerical operator fidelity?

    Answer: No; test both intermediate arithmetic errors and end-to-end task quality under specified distributions.

  23. What does a fair digital baseline require?

    Answer: Matched workload, model quality, batch, precision, thermal conditions and an equally complete energy/time measurement boundary.

  24. How can ADC sharing limit effective MVM throughput?

    Answer: Fewer ADC channels require multiple conversion groups to digitize all column outputs.

  25. Why is peak TOPS/W not equivalent to energy per accepted inference?

    Answer: Peak counts and power boundaries can exclude idle, routing, conversion, incomplete utilization and application operations.

  26. What must a calibration record contain?

    Answer: Weight-image and tile IDs, measured coefficients, procedure, environmental and time conditions, validity range and uncertainty.

  27. How should accelerator acceptance be established after drift or temperature stress?

    Answer: Apply an independent prescribed task-level threshold with measured error and availability across the operating envelope.

  28. State the defensible high-level conclusion about AIMC superiority.

    Answer: AIMC can benefit selected approximate, repeated linear-algebra workloads; no universal superiority over optimized digital computing follows.
