The Converter Clock Is a Measurement Reference

An analog-to-digital converter (ADC) does not merely map an input voltage to a number. It associates that number with a moment in time. A digital-to-analog converter (DAC) similarly produces or updates a physical output at an instant established by its clock and internal signal path. Consequently, timing uncertainty is a direct source of measurement and signal-generation error, distinct from limited bit depth. Two converters with identical nominal resolutions can produce substantially different spectral fidelity if their clocking systems differ.

This specialist article treats the entire timing chain: source oscillator, clock synthesizer, distribution network, input driver, converter aperture, digital interfaces, and downstream timestamps. Clock frequency, accuracy, phase, jitter, skew, and latency are not interchangeable metrics. A 100 MHz clock can be extremely frequency-accurate yet have substantial cycle-to-cycle phase noise; two clean clocks may be offset from one another; and a stable link delay may be long without adding random sample-time noise. Engineering success begins by declaring which property the application actually needs.

The neighboring articles on sampling theory, ADCs, DACs, and quantization noise address complementary physical and mathematical boundaries. This article owns the temporal accuracy of the combined conversion system.

Learning Objectives and Coverage Boundaries

After completing the article, readers should be able to: (1) define an ideal sample-time sequence and distinguish fixed delay from random uncertainty; (2) derive the voltage error induced by a small time displacement; (3) compute the jitter-only signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) for a sine input and invert that expression to set a clock budget; (4) relate specified single-sideband phase noise to an integrated root-mean-square (RMS) time error; (5) identify independent and correlated clock disturbances; (6) classify ADC and DAC timing requirements; and (7) plan multichannel synchronization and acceptance measurements.

The focus is analytical, not a catalogue of specific oscillators or a complete design manual for serial transceivers. Numerical exercises describe idealized models unless explicitly labeled as measurements; a manufacturer's clock phase-noise specification must be checked over the integration offsets required by the intended application. Likewise, a converter's aperture-jitter number should not be added to a published total jitter number if that number already incorporates the converter itself. Every worked result states its assumptions and units.

Timing Path from Oscillator to Reported Sample

A representative acquisition path is frequency reference → clock-generation PLL or divider → fanout and board routing → optional isolation or gating → ADC sampling switch → converter pipeline or digital filter → transport interface → capture buffer → timestamp or analysis. At any handoff, distinguish a fixed propagation delay from variation in that delay. Fixed delays are often calibratable; random changes require a noise budget or a revised architecture. A repeatable transport delay is not the same as the instant when the analog signal was physically sampled.

In a waveform generator, the corresponding path is digital sample scheduling, clock distribution, DAC register or modulator, output stage, and reconstruction filter. A phase-coherent multi-DAC design must keep the time origin and sample alignment meaningful across channels. A clock supervisor that reports valid frequency and lock state supplies useful health information, but those two facts alone do not prove low phase noise or sample-to-sample coherence.

A Mathematical Model of Sample Time

Let the ideal sampling instants be t[n] = n T_s, where T_s = 1/f_s. One useful timing model is t_actual[n] = n T_s + tau_0 + n alpha T_s + e[n]. Here tau_0 is an initial phase/time offset, alpha is a small fractional frequency error, and e[n] represents timing fluctuations relative to that trend under a defined observation procedure. A clock-frequency error causes a growing time displacement; random jitter need not. For a narrow observation interval, part of the drift may look like a slowly changing offset rather than noise.

The experimenter must specify the reference clock, observation duration, detrending method, and bandwidth before reporting RMS jitter. If the same physical reference drives both a signal generator and the ADC, common-mode fluctuations can cancel in a measured relative phase. That is not evidence that either source is noise-free against an independent time standard.

fs = 100_000.0
period = 1 / fs
initial_skew = 8e-9
frequency_error_ppm = 2.0
alpha = frequency_error_ppm * 1e-6
n = 200_000
actual = n * period * (1 + alpha) + initial_skew
ideal = n * period
print('sample period (us):', round(period * 1e6, 3))
print('elapsed offset (us):', round((actual-ideal) * 1e6, 3))
assert abs((actual-ideal) - (4e-6 + initial_skew)) < 1e-10

Clock Frequency Error and Long-Term Drift

Frequency accuracy is often stated in parts per million (ppm), whereas jitter is generally reported in seconds RMS or as a phase-noise spectrum. If a free-running clock runs p ppm faster than its reference, its approximate accumulated time difference after duration t is p * 10^-6 * t seconds, assuming constant fractional frequency error. For example, a persistent 5 ppm error accumulates about 5 milliseconds over 1,000 seconds. Whether this matters depends on the application: a short spectral capture may tolerate it, but timestamp matching across widely separated instruments may not.

Real oscillators also drift with temperature, aging, supply variation, and mechanical stress. Frequency stability over different averaging intervals is sometimes characterized using Allan deviation; it should not be substituted blindly for integrated phase jitter over MHz-wide offset bands. When two autonomous converters must stay aligned for minutes, discipline or calibration of their clock frequencies is as important as their short-term sampling noise.

Fixed Phase Offset and Channel Skew

A constant time offset between nominally simultaneous channels is skew, even if both clocks are individually very quiet. A fixed delta_t between two sinusoid measurements at analog frequency f_in corresponds to a phase difference delta_phi = 2*pi*f_in*delta_t radians, or 360*f_in*delta_t degrees. At 10 MHz, 100 picoseconds of skew is 0.36 degrees. This is a systematic phase error and will not average away by repeatedly taking samples.

Skew may arise in clock-distribution branches, isolators, trace lengths, converter aperture delays, and pipeline alignment. A deskew calibration can remove a reproducible difference under controlled conditions, but temperature-dependent and channel-dependent changes require margin. If a multichannel timing specification gives only absolute delay but no matching or uncertainty, the specification is incomplete for coherent phase measurements.

Period Jitter, Cycle-to-Cycle Jitter, and Time-Interval Error

A clock edge sequence supports several different observables. Time-interval error (TIE) compares each observed edge to an agreed ideal timing grid. Period jitter measures changes in consecutive-edge separation from the nominal period, using a specified statistical procedure. Cycle-to-cycle jitter typically describes the difference between adjacent period lengths. These statistics emphasize different temporal frequencies and should not be compared as if they had identical RMS values. For a slowly drifting oscillator, TIE can grow while local period variation remains small.

For an edge timing error sequence e[n], period deviations are approximately e[n] - e[n-1]; differences between adjacent periods are e[n] - 2e[n-1] + e[n-2]. Both difference operators suppress some low-frequency drift. Report the mathematical definition alongside every jitter number, especially when comparing oscilloscope measurements against a phase-noise-derived integrated RMS value.

errors_ps = [0, 4, 9, 5, 2, 7, 3]
period_errors_ps = [b-a for a,b in zip(errors_ps, errors_ps[1:])]
cycle_to_cycle_ps = [b-a for a,b in zip(period_errors_ps, period_errors_ps[1:])]
print('period deviations (ps):', period_errors_ps)
print('cycle-to-cycle deviations (ps):', cycle_to_cycle_ps)
assert len(period_errors_ps) == 6
assert len(cycle_to_cycle_ps) == 5

RMS Jitter, Peak-to-Peak Jitter, and Probability of Extremes

RMS jitter represents the standard deviation of a defined random timing fluctuation, after any stated treatment of mean and trend. An RMS specification is not a worst-case bound. A Gaussian model allows predictions about extreme timing excursions, but physical jitter can include deterministic spurs, heavy tails, or correlated wander that violate that model. Peak-to-peak jitter depends on observation interval, sample count, measurement bandwidth, and acquisition conditions; it should therefore be interpreted as a procedure-dependent result rather than a universal constant.

For low-probability failures, an engineering team must specify the tolerated event probability and the tails of the actual timing distribution. A promise such as “less than 10 ps RMS” does not prove that a 100 ps deadline is never violated. Conversely, adding arbitrary fixed multiples of RMS values without a stated distribution, confidence level, or correlation model can create an unjustified acceptance criterion. Digital setup-and-hold deadlines and analog converter SNR are related to timing but have different statistical consequences.

Random, Deterministic, and Bounded Timing Disturbances

Random jitter includes oscillator thermal noise and other stochastic fluctuations. Deterministic timing deviations may result from periodic power-supply coupling, reference spurs, duty-cycle distortion, data-dependent crosstalk, and periodically loaded clock logic. A spur generates correlated phase modulation and often produces discrete sidebands rather than a smooth broadband noise floor. A perfectly repeatable delay, by contrast, is static skew or latency rather than fluctuating jitter.

The classification matters for remediation. A noisy oscillator calls for cleaner clock generation or better filtering; a supply-coupled spur calls for isolation, layout, or power-network correction; a constant interchannel phase offset calls for deskew. Total observed variation can include both stochastic and deterministic components, but an RMS root-sum-square treatment is not universally valid when terms are correlated or when the metric is a bounded peak displacement rather than variance.

Aperture Delay Is Not Aperture Jitter

The aperture delay of an ADC is the nominal timing difference between its external clock reference event and the effective analog sampling instant, under the manufacturer's reference convention. Aperture jitter is the variation of that effective instant from one conversion to another. A device may have a stable aperture delay of several nanoseconds while contributing only a fraction of a picosecond of timing uncertainty. The delay matters to channel alignment; the uncertainty matters to time-dependent conversion noise. Some architectures have an acquisition window rather than an ideal impulse sample, so the datasheet's precise definition is essential.

The sampling clock's external jitter and the converter's internal aperture uncertainty together determine the effective sample-time uncertainty at the analog input. The manufacturer's quoted internal jitter may already be inferred from an SNR measurement; be careful not to count the same noise twice. Consult the converter timing diagrams for the required clock edge, setup conditions, and aperture-delay reference point.

Deriving the Voltage Error from Timing Error

For a continuously differentiable input v(t), a sample taken at t + delta_t instead of t has error delta_v = v(t + delta_t) - v(t). A first-order Taylor expansion gives delta_v ≈ (dv/dt)(t) * delta_t when the timing displacement is small enough for higher-order terms to be negligible. Input slope is therefore the immediate physical bridge between clock uncertainty and ADC voltage error. At a zero crossing of a sine wave, slope magnitude is maximal; at a peak, first-order slope is zero.

For arbitrary waveforms, this local derivative method is more fundamental than a single formula expressed only in terms of “signal frequency.” Step edges, high-frequency interference, and multitone inputs can have large local slopes. Noise introduced by jitter is generally signal-dependent and may be correlated with the signal and clock. In a practical spectrum, the underlying phase-noise spectrum controls how the error power is distributed among frequency offsets.

The Sinusoidal Jitter-Limited SNR Formula

Let v(t) = A*sin(2*pi*f_in*t). Its derivative is 2*pi*f_in*A*cos(2*pi*f_in*t). If small, zero-mean jitter has RMS sigma_t, is statistically independent of the signal, and the sample phases adequately cover the sine cycle, the RMS first-order error is approximately 2*pi*f_in*A*sigma_t/sqrt(2). Since the sine itself has RMS A/sqrt(2), their ratio is 2*pi*f_in*sigma_t. The resulting jitter-only limit is SNR_j = -20*log10(2*pi*f_in*sigma_t) dB.

This model presumes a sine input, small timing deviations, and an appropriate effective jitter bandwidth. It is not the total SNR of a real ADC; quantization, thermal noise, harmonic distortion, and input-chain errors are additional mechanisms. Most importantly, f_in is the analog input frequency, not automatically the sample clock frequency. For an undersampled RF input, the original high analog input frequency governs the slope even if its aliased digital frequency is low.

import math

def jitter_snr_db(f_input_hz, rms_jitter_s):
    if f_input_hz <= 0 or rms_jitter_s <= 0:
        raise ValueError('positive input frequency and rms jitter required')
    return -20*math.log10(2*math.pi*f_input_hz*rms_jitter_s)

print('SNR at 20 MHz / 500 fs:', round(jitter_snr_db(20e6, 500e-15),2))
print('SNR at 200 MHz / 500 fs:', round(jitter_snr_db(200e6, 500e-15),2))
assert abs(jitter_snr_db(20e6, 500e-15)-84.04) < .03

What the Standard SNR Formula Does Not Predict

The formula does not, by itself, predict harmonics, discrete clock-spur sidebands, adjacent-channel interference, missing codes, or a worst-case individual conversion error. A large periodic phase modulation can put conspicuous discrete energy at frequency offsets from a tone; modeling it as white Gaussian jitter loses that spectral structure. Likewise, a low-frequency drift may affect coherent phase over a long interval rather than raising the short-record noise floor in the same way as broadband jitter.

When comparing a prediction with an FFT-based converter measurement, use a matched phase-noise integration range, consistent input conditions, and a clear definition of whether the reported quantity is SNR (excluding harmonics) or SINAD (including distortion). Different spectral windows, bin widths, aliases of out-of-band clock noise, and noncoherent stimulus generation can dominate the observed difference. An ideal formula is a necessary budget screening tool, not an acceptance test by itself.

Inverting an SNR Requirement into a Jitter Budget

Given a targeted jitter-only SNR and a maximum relevant sine input frequency, solve for sigma_t ≤ 10^(-SNR_target/20)/(2*pi*f_in). This budget is conservative only with respect to the modeled sine and the stated total timing noise; it does not allocate any other ADC noise or system margin. For example, reaching a jitter-only limit of 80 dB with an input at 100 MHz requires no more than about 159 femtoseconds RMS effective timing uncertainty. A requirement of 80 dB total converter SNR would normally demand a better jitter-only limit, so clock noise contributes only part of the total noise power.

A good design starts from the analog spectrum and required dynamic range, budgets the acceptable clock contribution, then works backward through the oscillator, PLL, fanout network, isolation elements, and converter aperture. Each term must use compatible RMS and offset-band definitions.

import math

def max_rms_jitter(f_input_hz, snr_jitter_db):
    return 10**(-snr_jitter_db/20)/(2*math.pi*f_input_hz)

budget_fs = max_rms_jitter(100e6, 80)*1e15
print('80 dB at 100 MHz -> femtoseconds:',round(budget_fs,2))
assert abs(budget_fs-159.154943) < .001

Sampling Rate Versus Analog Input Frequency

Changing sample rate does not automatically relax the jitter requirement for an unchanged analog input signal. For an ideal fixed f_in, the first-order error from an absolute time shift depends on f_in and timing RMS. Increasing f_s can change aliasing, filter requirements, and the measurement's effective integration bandwidth, but it is not a substitute for identifying which clock-error frequencies reach the signal band. For an oversampled data-converter system, digital filtering may reject some noise outside the final bandwidth, including parts of the jitter-induced spectrum depending on correlations and modulation details.

A designer sometimes inserts the sample clock's frequency into the sine-jitter SNR formula simply because the clock determines time spacing. That is an analytical error. A converter sampling a 10 MHz signal at 125 MSPS and a converter sampling the same 10 MHz signal at 250 MSPS can have the same first-order jitter-only SNR if their effective timing jitter is equal; their other noise and filtering properties can differ substantially.

Frequency-Dependent Sensitivity: A Worked Table

With fixed 300 fs RMS jitter, the idealized jitter-only SNR changes by about 20 dB each time the input frequency increases tenfold. This is why a converter may measure very well with a 1 MHz laboratory tone but fall short for RF input tones hundreds of MHz away from baseband. Use a frequency-sweep table before purchasing a supposedly 'low-jitter' clock. The table is a predictive model; it is not a substitute for the converter's actual frequency-dependent internal noise and analog bandwidth.

The example preserves consistent units: 1e-12 seconds is one picosecond and 1e-15 seconds is one femtosecond. Confusing the two changes the predicted SNR by 60 dB.

import math
jitter = 300e-15
for f in [1e6, 10e6, 100e6, 500e6]:
    snr = -20*math.log10(2*math.pi*f*jitter)
    print(f'{f/1e6:>5.0f} MHz: {snr:6.2f} dB')
assert -20*math.log10(2*math.pi*100e6*jitter) > 74

Converting the Timing Limit to Equivalent Effective Bits

For an ideal full-scale sinusoidal quantizer, a frequently used approximation is SNR_ideal ≈ 6.02*N + 1.76 dB for N bits, assuming a suitable quantization-noise model. Accordingly, (SNR_j - 1.76)/6.02 gives an equivalent bit-count ceiling imposed by timing noise alone under matching full-scale sine conventions. This is not automatically the ENOB of the installed ADC, because measured ENOB generally derives from SINAD and includes distortion and additional noise.

For a 500 fs RMS clock at a 100 MHz analog input, the jitter-only SNR is roughly 70 dB, comparable to about 11.3 ideal bits by this calculation. A nominal 16-bit output field does not undo the missing dynamic precision. Equally, a 12-bit converter does not require a 10 fs clock if its quantization and front-end noise are already much larger than the jitter component; budget the actual system rather than maximizing one isolated specification.

Combining Independent Jitter Sources Correctly

If independent, zero-mean timing errors have RMS values sigma_1, sigma_2, ..., their variances add and the composite RMS is sqrt(sum(sigma_i^2)). This root-sum-square (RSS) model is useful for an oscillator, PLL additive jitter, distribution buffer, and internal aperture uncertainty only if the terms are defined on compatible bands and do not duplicate one another. Do not linearly add unrelated RMS numbers as though they were guaranteed peak excursions, and do not RSS fixed channel skew into a statistical jitter limit.

Consider 180 fs at the oscillator, 140 fs from an independent distribution stage, and 250 fs of converter aperture jitter. The combined value is approximately 338 fs RMS. Eliminating the 140 fs contribution helps, but cannot push the total below the 250 fs converter floor plus remaining terms.

import math
parts_fs = [180, 140, 250]
total_fs = math.sqrt(sum(x*x for x in parts_fs))
print('total rms (fs):',round(total_fs,2))
assert abs(total_fs-338.3785) < .01

Correlated Noise, Shared References, and Double Counting

When two timing errors are correlated, the variance of their sum includes covariance: Var(X+Y) = Var(X)+Var(Y)+2*Cov(X,Y). A shared oscillator may introduce common timing error that partly cancels in a relative channel-phase measurement while remaining fully present against an external time reference. Conversely, correlated interference may reinforce rather than average down. Measuring different points in the same chain and adding their reported absolute jitter numbers can double-count common noise.

The right budgeting practice is to mark measurement points, transfer functions, and references. An oscillator's total phase noise already contains source contributions; a PLL output's integrated jitter includes transformed reference noise plus internally added noise. A post-fanout measurement may include both. If the numerical analysis models component additions, use additive jitter data referred to the appropriate measurement point, or build a spectral model of each noise source through the chain.

Adding Independent Noise Powers to Total SNR

Noise powers, not decibel values, add. If an ADC's non-jitter mechanisms alone yield SNR SNR_other and the timing mechanism independently yields SNR_j, their combined result under the corresponding model is SNR_total = -10*log10(10^(-SNR_other/10) + 10^(-SNR_j/10)). This is a noise-only combination, not a general distortion or SINAD equation. If one contribution is far below the other, reducing it further yields little benefit; if they are equal, the combined SNR is about 3.01 dB worse than either limit.

The formula makes an engineering tradeoff visible: improving the jitter-only SNR from 90 dB to 100 dB barely changes a system whose other noise sources already limit it to 75 dB. An extra-expensive oscillator may therefore be unnecessary, despite having an attractive standalone specification.

import math

def combine_snr_db(*limits_db):
    return -10*math.log10(sum(10**(-x/10) for x in limits_db))

print('75 dB non-jitter + 90 dB jitter:',round(combine_snr_db(75,90),3))
assert abs(combine_snr_db(80,80) - (80-10*math.log10(2)))<1e-10

High-Slope Pulses and Multitone Inputs

For nonsinusoidal signals, a useful local error model is sigma_v(t) ≈ |dv/dt| * sigma_t, assuming jitter is small and uncorrelated with the instantaneous signal. A nearly flat sensor signal experiences little jitter-induced voltage error; a very fast edge experiences more. A narrow pulse can have a slope large enough that a picosecond variation affects its apparent amplitude or threshold-crossing time even if its repetition frequency is moderate. In a multitone or modulated RF signal, the derivative reflects all contributing components, not merely the displayed center frequency.

If the signal and clock share correlated phase fluctuations, the generic independent-noise derivation may overestimate or underestimate the observed error. A physically accurate test should vary analog input frequency, slope or modulation while holding the rest of the chain controlled. This determines whether clock timing, input-driver linearity, thermal noise, or digital processing dominates the measured performance.

Undersampling and RF Clock Sensitivity

An ADC can intentionally sample an RF band above half its sample rate when analog bandpass filtering and sampling-zone planning prevent ambiguity. The sampled tone may appear at a much lower numerical alias frequency, but aperture-jitter sensitivity still follows the original analog input frequency because the sampling switch sees the high-frequency physical slope. For example, a 490 MHz tone digitized at 200 MSPS may alias to 90 MHz; the time-error voltage does not behave as if its analog source had only 90 MHz slope.

Clock quality, input-frequency planning, and anti-alias filtering must therefore be coordinated. A low alias frequency is not a low-jitter entitlement. This distinction is particularly important when comparing RF-sampling ADCs to low-bandwidth precision ADCs; the latter can use a much looser clock under an equivalent sine-SNR target if their analog frequencies and measurement bandwidths are genuinely smaller.

Phase Noise as a Frequency-Domain Description

A clock is often specified with single-sideband phase noise L(f_offset), given in dBc/Hz at an offset from the carrier frequency. This quantity expresses the distribution of phase-fluctuation power around the nominal oscillator line, relative to the carrier, within a 1 Hz-equivalent bandwidth and under the usual small-noise convention. A value of -140 dBc/Hz at 1 MHz offset is not itself an RMS jitter specification: the total integrated disturbance depends on the spectral values over a specified range of offsets.

A phase-noise plot may have close-in 1/f-like regions, a flatter noise floor, and narrow discrete spurs. Two clocks can cross each other in such a graph: one is better near the carrier but worse far away. Which one produces lower application-relevant time jitter depends on the integration limits and on the converter or receiver's noise-transfer characteristics. Any comparison must state the clock carrier, offset band, and treatment of spurs.

From Phase Fluctuation to Time Displacement

For a clock with nominal frequency f_c, a small instantaneous phase deviation phi(t) in radians is equivalent to a timing displacement delta_t(t) = phi(t)/(2*pi*f_c), after adopting a consistent sign and reference convention. Thus an RMS phase fluctuation of sigma_phi radians corresponds to sigma_t = sigma_phi/(2*pi*f_c) seconds for the same measurement bandwidth. Changing the clock carrier changes the radians-to-seconds conversion even if the quoted phase variance were held fixed.

That relation should not be mistaken for a claim that making a clock faster inevitably reduces its output jitter: real multipliers, dividers, and PLLs transform phase noise. To assess a proposed frequency change, translate the actual output phase-noise spectrum rather than applying only the conversion factor to unchanged phase-noise data. State whether you are evaluating absolute phase, relative phase between channels, or a period-error statistic.

Integrating Single-Sideband Phase Noise

Under the standard small-phase-noise single-sideband convention, sigma_phi^2 ≈ 2 * integral(10^(L(f)/10) df) over selected positive frequency offsets, and sigma_t = sigma_phi/(2*pi*f_c). The factor of two accounts for both sidebands when L is the one-sideband density. L(f) must first be converted from dBc/Hz to a linear spectral ratio; integrating the decibel numbers themselves is meaningless. The result is the RMS noise in the stated band, not a universal intrinsic jitter constant.

The example uses a deliberately flat -140 dBc/Hz profile between 10 kHz and 10 MHz on a 100 MHz carrier; it is a teaching model, not a real oscillator specification. Because actual phase-noise plots vary with offset, integrate appropriately sampled spectral points using interpolation justified in the linear power domain. Do not extrapolate a product's unmeasured low- or high-offset noise floor.

import math
carrier = 100e6
low, high = 10e3, 10e6
L_db_per_hz = -140.0
linear_ssb = 10**(L_db_per_hz/10)
phase_variance = 2 * linear_ssb * (high-low)
time_rms_s = math.sqrt(phase_variance)/(2*math.pi*carrier)
print('flat-noise-model rms jitter (ps):',round(time_rms_s*1e12,3))
assert 0 < time_rms_s < 1e-12

Why Integration Limits Change the Result

Manufacturers may quote RMS phase jitter from 12 kHz to 20 MHz, 100 Hz to 10 MHz, or another range selected for a particular application. A clock with a lower quoted RMS may simply have been integrated over a narrower band. In an FFT converter test, close-in noise inside the fundamental's measurement bin may be counted differently from noise distributed across other bins; the sample count, capture duration, and chosen exclusion bins matter. Out-of-band clock noise can also fold into a converter's measured band through the sampling mechanism.

For high-speed converter selection, the relevant band should reflect the analog input, the sampling process, and the desired output analysis. For long-term synchronized instruments, very low offset frequencies may be important even if a short spectral capture does not resolve them. Document all bands explicitly and avoid carrying a single RMS jitter number into every product without reconsidering the measurement and filter transfer functions.

Close-In Wander, Wideband Noise, and Spurs

Close-in phase noise typically represents slower phase excursions or oscillator instability; wideband phase noise produces faster fluctuations. Narrow spurs can arise from synthesizer references, fractional-divider patterns, supply switching, and data-correlated interference. A deterministic spur may contribute an integrated phase variance but also create a distinct spectral tone that violates spurious-free dynamic range (SFDR) well before a broadband RMS limit looks problematic. Conversely, a system interested only in slow relative phase drift may care strongly about close-in components and little about distant offsets.

A useful acceptance table therefore reports at least three things when relevant: integrated RMS jitter for a named offset range, the phase-noise curve or representative points, and the largest deterministic spur at specified offsets. The same clock may pass the first metric and fail the third. Treating every narrow spur as white random noise conceals the failure mode and sends debugging toward the wrong component.

Evaluating a Piecewise Phase-Noise Spectrum

A more realistic calculation uses measurements at discrete offset frequencies. Integrate 2*10^(L/10) over frequency, where L is single-sideband noise density in dBc/Hz. The following trapezoidal approximation operates on the linear density at each measured offset. It does not automatically account for unrecorded narrow spurs, instrument floor corrections, or values beyond the stated endpoints. For a sloping oscillator spectrum with widely spaced points, log-log interpolation or finer measurements may be more accurate than this elementary linear-frequency trapezoid.

This is a valuable audit example because it makes the input data and the integration domain explicit. If two clock vendors report jitter numbers over different offset ranges, re-integrating comparable curves over a common range is more defensible than comparing their headline numbers.

import math
# Measured offset Hz, one-sideband phase-noise density dBc/Hz
points = [(1e3,-110),(1e4,-125),(1e5,-137),(1e6,-145),(1e7,-150)]
phase_var = 0.0
for (fa,la),(fb,lb) in zip(points,points[1:]):
    phase_var += (fb-fa)*(10**(la/10)+10**(lb/10))
carrier = 200e6
jitter_fs = math.sqrt(phase_var)/(2*math.pi*carrier)*1e15
print('illustrative integrated jitter (fs):', round(jitter_fs,1))
assert jitter_fs > 0

The Oscillator and Reference-Clock Selection

Crystal oscillators, temperature-compensated crystal oscillators, oven-controlled crystal oscillators, and synthesizer modules occupy different cost, frequency, environmental stability, and close-in noise envelopes. The best choice depends on whether the application needs absolute frequency discipline, low short-term jitter, rapid tuning, low power, or operation through a specified temperature range. There is no single ranked list that optimizes all these metrics simultaneously. A very stable reference disciplined over seconds can still require an exceptionally clean high-frequency clock output for a wideband ADC.

When comparing candidates, obtain a phase-noise curve at the final required clock frequency or a validated noise model through the synthesizer. Check environmental and supply conditions and the manufacturer-recommended output load. A voltage-controlled oscillator's phase noise, a complete clock-module's output jitter, and an ADC's total aperture jitter must not be silently treated as interchangeable properties.

PLL Clock Generation and Noise Transfer

A phase-locked loop (PLL) uses a phase comparator, loop filter, controllable oscillator, and often dividers to relate an output clock to a reference. Within the relevant loop bandwidth, reference noise and PLL additive mechanisms can be transferred toward the output; outside it, the controlled oscillator's own noise may dominate. These are general tendencies, not universal curves: actual loop transfer functions, reference scaling, and synthesizer architecture govern the result. A PLL used as a jitter cleaner must be evaluated with its actual input-noise spectrum and output requirements.

A clean oscillator feeding an unnecessarily noisy wideband PLL can degrade the clock; a carefully designed PLL may attenuate noisy reference components while supplying a required frequency. The loop can also introduce peaking and spurs. The correct design question is not merely whether the PLL is 'locked,' but what spectrum reaches the sampling instant after all transfer functions are included.

PLL Loop Bandwidth and Jitter Tradeoffs

Narrow loop bandwidth may suppress high-offset input reference noise while allowing more free-running VCO behavior; wide bandwidth may follow the reference more closely and suppress certain VCO deviations. Neither extreme always minimizes integrated output jitter. A practical selection balances oscillator phase noise, reference noise, bandwidth, spurs, lock time, stability margins, and the offsets that materially affect the measurement. A 'cleaned' clock can have lower RMS jitter in one band yet worse close-in drift or a higher discrete spur.

Loop analysis should use the device's specified noise-transfer curves or a validated PLL simulation, not an arbitrary claim that a 100 kHz bandwidth removes every disturbance above 100 kHz. A real loop has finite roll-off and potential peaking. When temperature changes or supply regulators modulate the oscillator, transfer characteristics and spurious outputs should be tested under those conditions rather than only at the bench's nominal temperature.

Integer and Fractional Synthesis Spurs

An integer-N or fractional-N synthesizer may generate discrete spurious components at reference-related offsets, fractional patterns, or supply-coupling frequencies. The spectral placement of these tones matters because sampling-clock phase modulation can translate into input-dependent ADC sidebands. A low integrated RMS jitter number can coexist with one large discrete spur that invalidates a receiver's adjacent-channel requirement. Fractional synthesis provides useful frequency flexibility but requires care in choosing reference frequencies, output plans, and loop settings.

When assessing a clock plan, note spur locations relative to the signal of interest and protected bands. Repeating a synthetic phase-modulation measurement at different input frequencies can help separate clock-correlated sidebands from converter intrinsic distortion. A mitigation may involve selecting another PLL divider or reference plan, correcting a supply disturbance, or isolating a data bus—not merely purchasing a lower-noise reference oscillator.

Clock Multiplication and Division: Avoid Naive Scaling

An ideal mathematical divider changes carrier frequency and transforms phase fluctuations in a known way, but real divider circuitry adds noise. An ideal frequency multiplier scales phase excursions as well as frequency, while its output timing behavior must be considered in seconds against the resulting noise spectrum. In particular, the clock phase-noise numbers in dBc/Hz do not remain unchanged under an arbitrary synthesizer transformation. A fractional sampling clock produced by a complex PLL requires the manufacturer's output data or a full source-to-output noise budget.

A designer sometimes concludes that dividing a clock by two halves its RMS jitter, or that doubling a clock frequency halves timing jitter. Neither shortcut is generally justified without specifying which noise sources are common, how phase is transformed, and how the integration bands map. Compute at the actual ADC clock pin, and distinguish frequency conversion from an improved physical clock.

Input Clock Slew Rate and Threshold Sensitivity

Clock noise is not only oscillator phase noise. A noisy logic threshold on a slowly rising edge causes time variation because delta_t ≈ delta_v / (dv_clock/dt) for sufficiently small threshold-voltage disturbance delta_v. A large differential edge slope at the receiving clock input can reduce sensitivity to a given receiver threshold noise. This provides a physical reason for clock-input amplitude and transition-time requirements in converter datasheets; the fastest possible edge is not automatically desirable if it creates additional EMI or crosstalk.

For example, 1 mV RMS threshold uncertainty across a local 0.5 V/ns edge slope corresponds to about 2 ps RMS time variation under the simple model. This is enormously larger than a 100 fs oscillator budget. Actual receivers have different differential amplitudes, bandwidths, hysteresis, and noise statistics, so the calculation is a screening estimate. Specify the receiving pin's amplitude and common-mode conditions, not merely the driver's advertised output slew rate.

Estimating Threshold-Crossing Jitter

The threshold-slope model lets an engineer identify a potentially dominant interface impairment. It is valid for small perturbations around a locally monotonic crossing; it is not an exhaustive receiver noise model. If noise and slope vary over time, use a worst-region or statistically conditioned calculation. When the derived jitter dwarfs the desired budget, improving the oscillator alone is unlikely to solve the problem.

The numerical example uses 0.8 millivolts RMS disturbance and 1 volt per nanosecond transition slope. The resulting time uncertainty is 0.8 ps, or 800 fs, before accounting for other stages.

noise_v = 0.8e-3
slew_v_per_s = 1.0e9
uncertainty_s = noise_v/slew_v_per_s
print('threshold jitter (fs):',round(uncertainty_s*1e15,1))
assert abs(uncertainty_s-0.8e-12)<1e-18

Differential Clocks and Common-Mode Rejection

Differential clock inputs such as LVDS or appropriately specified sine and CML interfaces can reduce sensitivity to some common-mode disturbances and provide controlled signaling at high rates. They still require suitable common-mode voltages, input swing, termination, and device-specific electrical compliance. A differential pair does not cancel disturbances that couple asymmetrically or appear as differential noise. Phase imbalance and unequal routing also create timing errors, while poorly implemented AC coupling can distort low-frequency or startup behavior.

Use a clock-distribution architecture that matches the converter's input pin structure rather than assuming any fast differential output is interchangeable. Many manufacturers recommend particular transformer, balun, or differential-driver arrangements for sinusoidal source clocks. Check how clock amplitude changes the receiving input's slew rate and noise performance. Separate electrical interface compliance from actual phase-noise acceptance; both must hold.

Clock Termination, Reflection, and Transmission Lines

At fast edge rates, a board trace behaves as a transmission line rather than a negligible wire. Incorrect source or load termination can produce reflections, ringing, double crossings, and threshold modulation. These symptoms may create deterministic timing deviations correlated with other switching activity or even spurious clock events. Clock specifications measured at a driver test point may be irrelevant if the ADC input sees a poor eye or unacceptable common-mode voltage.

Trace geometry, return paths, connector transitions, and fanout stubs should be modeled or measured using the intended input load. Differential termination belongs near the appropriate receiving interface as specified by the topology. A designer should inspect the waveform at the converter pin or an equivalent calibrated measurement point and distinguish analog ringing from the statistical phase noise of the source clock.

Duty Cycle, Clock Pulse Width, and Edge Polarity

A nominal 50% duty cycle is not guaranteed at the receiver after buffers, AC coupling, unequal rise and fall behavior, and routing. Some ADCs sample on one specified edge and require the other edge to remain within pulse-width or duty-cycle limits; others use both edges or derive internal phases. Excess duty-cycle distortion can violate timing requirements and generate deterministic spurs. The relevant time-domain measurements are not just frequency and RMS edge jitter, but minimum high/low times, rising and falling edge separation, and edge polarity.

If a clock is multiplied or divided on chip, its duty cycle may also affect internal phase generation. Do not infer a device's full clocking contract solely from a typical block diagram. Compare data-sheet minimum and maximum timing constraints at the applicable voltage, temperature, and frequency range, including any additional requirements for synchronous triggers or sampling-edge alignment.

Fanout Buffers, Additive Jitter, and Distribution Networks

A multi-converter board commonly distributes one reference through a fanout buffer or clock-tree network. Each output adds a propagation delay and a potentially different amount of additive jitter. The fanout system must satisfy edge amplitude, slew, cross-channel skew, and synchronization control in addition to low noise. Separate output branches can still share common reference phase fluctuations; their differential skew noise may be much smaller than their absolute phase noise, or vice versa if the outputs have noisy independent circuitry.

Select a buffer with specified additive jitter over an appropriate offset band and sufficiently low differential channel skew, then account for trace and device delays. Where a serializer, isolated interface, or FPGA path relays convert-start signals, its jitter contribution belongs to the sampling-clock path, even if its digital link appears functionally correct. Validate the completed clock tree under the same simultaneous switching conditions present in normal operation.

Delay Mismatch from Trace Length

Physical route differences contribute deterministic skew. A first-order estimate uses the propagation velocity of the board medium, often on the order of 0.5–0.7 times light speed depending on geometry and effective dielectric constant. At an illustrative 160 mm/ns, a 16 mm extra trace produces approximately 100 ps delay. This is a large phase shift for a high-frequency signal; it is not directly an RMS random-jitter source.

Delay matching is important, but matching copper lengths does not necessarily match complete channel phase. Package delays, fanout branches, on-chip aperture delays, connectors, isolation, and filter group delays may be different. Measure or calibrate residual skew after the hardware is assembled.

velocity_mm_per_ns = 160.0
extra_length_mm = 16.0
skew_ps = extra_length_mm/velocity_mm_per_ns*1000
phase_degrees = 360*10e6*(skew_ps*1e-12)
print('skew ps:',skew_ps,'phase degrees at 10 MHz:',phase_degrees)
assert abs(skew_ps-100)<1e-12
assert abs(phase_degrees-.36)<1e-12

Power-Supply Coupling, Ground Return, and Clock Crosstalk

Switching regulators, digital output buses, FPGA activity, and reference-distribution drivers can inject periodic or broadband noise into clock sources. Even if the oscillator is excellent at its own pins, an inadequately isolated PLL or clock buffer can experience supply modulation and produce additional output phase noise or spurious sidebands. Poor current-return geometry can couple signals across channels, turning a deterministic digital activity pattern into converter interference.

A practical diagnosis repeats measurements while varying digital-bus activity, switching-regulator load, fanout output enables, and cable configuration. If the measured converter spur follows a digital activity frequency, examine coupling and clock distribution before blaming quantization. Decoupling, power isolation, differential routing, ground-return planning, and clock source placement are system design tools, not substitutes for measured verification. Temperature sweeps should confirm that an apparent fix survives realistic operation.

Converter Internal Aperture and Front-End Input Path

The external clock event triggers an internal switching and acquisition process. Internal switch-drive buffers, sampling-network impedance, package parasitics, and converter architecture establish an effective aperture delay and uncertainty. Internal jitter can be intrinsic to the device, while an external clock source provides additional timing variation. Acquisition settling is another independent problem: a sample held at the correct time can still have the wrong voltage if the input source cannot charge the sampling capacitance sufficiently fast.

Do not use the jitter SNR formula to excuse a high-impedance driver with inadequate settling, or use extra settling time to infer a lower clock-jitter floor. In a multiplexed SAR system, channel switching changes the source impedance and acquisition transients; in a high-speed RF ADC, the sampling switch may see broadband analog content and clock-related feedthrough. These constraints should appear as separate lines in the system error budget.

Throughput, Conversion Time, and Output Latency

Sampling throughput describes how many analog measurements can be accepted per second. Conversion time describes a particular device's internal conversion process. Latency is the delay before the corresponding digital result becomes available, which may include pipeline stages, digital filtering, transport, and buffering. These are not identical. A pipelined ADC can output one sample each clock cycle after startup while retaining several cycles of data latency. A delta-sigma ADC can run a fast internal modulator while producing filtered output words at a slower, configured output data rate.

For closed-loop control and event reconstruction, data age matters: a correct value delayed unpredictably may be operationally unacceptable. For offline spectral analysis, constant latency can be relatively harmless if timestamps identify the actual sampling instants. Always specify whether the output timestamp refers to the sample aperture, the first valid data word, the serialized transport boundary, or the end of a digital filter.

Pipelined Converter Latency Versus Clock Jitter

In a pipeline converter, successive stages process different samples concurrently. Once the pipeline is full, its sustained sampling rate can be high despite a multicycle sample-to-output delay. That delay is usually fixed or deterministically related to configuration, while aperture jitter is sample-to-sample uncertainty. The analog sampling event occurs before many downstream pipeline operations; a stable internal digital clock that advances pipeline data correctly does not automatically repair the initial sampling-time error.

When synchronizing pipeline converters, align actual analog sample instants and compensate the deterministic processing latencies. Matching digital arrival cycles without checking analog aperture delay can leave interchannel phase errors. The designer should inspect manufacturer latency definitions carefully, especially when a subsequent JESD-style link adds additional framing and elastic-buffer behavior.

Sigma-Delta Converter Filter Group Delay

A sigma-delta ADC oversamples internally and applies a decimating digital filter to generate output words. The filter has a frequency-dependent phase characteristic and an associated group delay; a common approximately linear-phase configuration can still have a significant fixed time delay. The first output after startup or changing filter settings may require settling before it represents steady-state filtered information. Different filter modes trade output data rate, noise rejection, settling behavior, and latency.

Do not compare the output data-ready signal directly to the instant a physical input changed without accounting for the digital filter response. A high-resolution channel may be unsuitable for a tight real-time controller despite excellent low-frequency noise figures. Conversely, a slow filter delay is not evidence of poor sample-clock phase noise. These are separate engineering choices, and both should be reflected in acquisition metadata and acceptance tests.

DAC Clock Jitter and Output Spectral Purity

A DAC associates each output code transition or reconstructed waveform event with a timing reference. Jitter can modulate an output waveform's phase or transition instants, causing noise and sidebands after the reconstruction filter. The precise error depends on DAC architecture, zero-order-hold effects, pulse shape, analog reconstruction, and the measurement bandwidth. The simple sine jitter-SNR formula is a useful first-order screening relationship in some continuous-wave contexts, but it should not be applied blindly to every DAC as a universal broadband noise formula.

A DAC output stage may also be limited by code-dependent switching glitches, nonlinearity, reference noise, reconstruction-filter response, and load-induced settling. A clock's frequency-domain phase-noise profile often matters more than a single integrated RMS number when generating a clean RF carrier. The application may need tight phase coherence among multiple outputs even when each isolated channel's SNR is acceptable.

DAC Reconstruction Filtering and Timing Errors

A conventional sample-and-hold DAC produces a piecewise waveform that must be filtered or otherwise processed to obtain the intended analog spectrum. The hold response, analog reconstruction filter, and sample timing interact. Clock-induced sidebands can fall inside the passband and remain after filtering, whereas images and high-frequency switching components may be attenuated. A filter cannot restore timing information that has already been converted into in-band phase modulation of the signal.

In multichannel waveform generation, the reconstruction filters can have unequal group delays. A device may launch digital codes simultaneously yet produce phase-shifted analog outputs because of dissimilar filter chains. System alignment therefore requires a chain-level measurement including DAC update timing, analog output stages, filters, connectors, and the measurement reference—not merely matching register-write timestamps.

Simultaneous Sampling and Relative Phase Accuracy

Multichannel acquisitions may need more than clean independent clocks. Power analysis, beamforming, impedance spectroscopy, and vibration measurements often require known relative sample times. If voltage and current are sampled with relative skew delta_t, their measured phase relation at frequency f shifts by 2*pi*f*delta_t radians. A small phase bias can turn into a consequential error in power-factor or array-response calculations, even if each channel's amplitude SNR is high.

A shared timing reference reduces long-term relative drift, but it does not guarantee equal effective aperture delays at each ADC. Fanout skew, isolation, local clock buffers, input-path group delay, and sample-capture logic must be included in calibration. Whenever channels are nominally simultaneous, the acceptance test should explicitly measure relative timing over temperature and under expected digital switching loads.

Computing Phase Error from Channel Skew

The phase corresponding to a fixed skew is easy to compute once a frequency is specified. The same 250 ps mismatch is negligible for some slow industrial sensors but creates a 9-degree phase error at 100 MHz. This fixed phase error should be calibrated or bounded; it is not eliminated by averaging. If relative skew fluctuates randomly, its contribution to phase variance can be computed separately.

Here the conversion of seconds to degrees is explicit and reusable. In a wideband application the phase error is frequency-dependent, so a single constant degree correction may not align all frequencies unless a true time-shift operation is applied.

def phase_error_degrees(f_hz, skew_seconds):
    return 360*f_hz*skew_seconds

assert abs(phase_error_degrees(100e6,250e-12)-9.0)<1e-12
print('1 MHz:',phase_error_degrees(1e6,250e-12),'degrees')
print('100 MHz:',phase_error_degrees(100e6,250e-12),'degrees')

Time-Interleaved ADCs and Interchannel Timing Mismatch

A time-interleaved ADC combines samples from multiple converter channels operating at staggered phases. Ideally, K channels acquire at offsets separated by T_s/K relative to a common aggregate sampling grid. Channel-dependent offset, gain, and timing mismatch produce different error signatures. A timing-skew mismatch creates signal-slope-dependent errors and spurious spectral components whose magnitude generally increases with analog input frequency. High aggregate rate does not automatically imply that the individual channel timing grid is accurate.

Calibration can estimate relative skew with appropriate test signals and correct some resulting error digitally. But the calibration model must distinguish stable skew from random aperture jitter; a digital fractional-delay correction can remove a repeatable offset but cannot perfectly reconstruct individual sample times perturbed by unpredictable sub-sample jitter. Interleaving performance must be tested across input frequency, temperature, channel combination, and relevant spurious-frequency bands.

A Basic Interleaving Skew Error Model

For a nominal sample at t_n, channel k actually sampling at t_n + delta_k incurs first-order error v'(t_n)*delta_k. If the channel assignment cycles periodically, the error pattern is cyclostationary, and periodic spectral artifacts can appear near interleaving-related aliases. Gain and DC offset mismatches produce their own spectral signatures, so diagnosing every spur as jitter is premature. A swept-frequency input helps expose the characteristic growth of timing-skew errors with signal slope.

An engineering workflow first measures per-channel DC offset and gain, then timing skew with a tone and sufficient spectral resolution, and finally residual random aperture jitter. Correction coefficients should be validated with different amplitudes, frequencies, and temperatures to avoid merely fitting one calibration tone. The number of interleaved channels changes the spectral pattern, so a two-channel illustration should not be generalized to all interleavers without modeling their periodic schedule.

Skew Calibration with a Reference Sine

If two measurement channels receive the same sinusoid, the phase difference of their fitted complex sinusoidal coefficients estimates a relative time offset of approximately delta_t = delta_phi/(2*pi*f) after consistent phase-unwrapping and polarity choices. At a single frequency, this estimate is ambiguous modulo the signal period; using known coarse delay constraints or multiple frequencies resolves ambiguity. Amplitude mismatch, filter group delay, spectral leakage, and correlated source/clock noise may bias the estimate.

Calibration should use a stimulus and cable arrangement that represent the intended signal paths. A short-term calibration may not track thermal changes in board traces, analog filters, or converter aperture delays. Repeat across a relevant temperature range and signal band. Realtime systems may maintain per-channel deskew coefficients with periodic validation; archival systems should record the coefficients and reference used for each acquisition.

Synchronous and Asynchronous Clock Domains

Two digital blocks can share an underlying reference yet operate with different internal phases or divisors. Their data-transfer boundaries may require explicit timing analysis. When blocks are truly asynchronous, transferring a control signal without synchronization risks metastability and missed events. This is a digital clock-domain crossing failure, not the same as analog sample-time jitter at an ADC aperture. Both problems can exist on one converter board, but their design methods differ.

A synchronizer reduces the probability that a metastable control bit reaches downstream logic; it does not guarantee a clean timestamp for an analog signal and does not safely move an unconstrained multibit word by independently synchronizing each bit. Asynchronous FIFOs and handshake protocols can transfer coherent data between domains when implemented and constrained correctly. The timing architecture should identify every boundary where the analog sample instant, digital data-valid edge, and destination clock are related or independent.

Metastability Is Not Analog Aperture Jitter

Metastability occurs when a bistable element samples a changing digital signal close to its setup/hold aperture, so its resolution to a stable logic value takes an uncertain time. Its probability is modeled through device-dependent parameters and clock/data event rates. Analog aperture jitter, in contrast, describes variation in the instant an ADC samples a continuous signal. One influences digital state correctness; the other can turn signal slope into voltage error. A clean conversion clock does not prove that data-valid pulses cross a processor clock domain safely.

A multi-flop synchronizer is appropriate for some slow single-bit status controls; event pulses may need stretching or a toggle handshake, and wide sample buses need a coherent transfer mechanism. The throughput and latency of this transfer must be budgeted separately from converter aperture and filter latency. Both domains should have independent verification procedures: analog SNR/phase measurements and digital CDC structural/functional checks.

Timestamp Error, Sample Skew, and Data Age

A software timestamp may describe when the CPU or FPGA received a result rather than when the signal was sampled. USB, Ethernet, DMA buffering, interrupt servicing, and queue scheduling can add variable delay. Such transport jitter is not automatically aperture jitter, but it can ruin event alignment if timestamps are assigned at receipt instead of the converter's sample counter or hardware trigger. An acquisition design should anchor timestamps to a stable hardware clock domain and record sample indices, trigger epoch, and any deterministic latency calibration.

When comparing two remote systems, add uncertainty from clock synchronization, sample-clock frequency offset, local timestamp capture, transport path, and analog aperture. A low-noise local oscillator is necessary for some measurements but does not alone establish the absolute time of an event in a global time scale. A network time synchronization protocol can discipline clocks at its intended accuracy while still leaving fast sample jitter and interchannel skew to the converter design.

Estimating Timestamp Drift between Independent Devices

Two free-running devices whose timebases differ by 3 ppm drift apart by approximately 3 milliseconds over 1,000 seconds. This is not an RMS sampling aperture-jitter estimate, and the amount can change if either oscillator's frequency wanders. The example demonstrates a requirement for common references or periodic timebase calibration when cross-instrument phase or timestamp alignment is operationally important.

The model assumes constant fractional frequency difference and ignores the starting time offset. For real synchronization, record the error-versus-time trajectory, including discontinuities from resets or clock reconfiguration, instead of reporting only a final drift number.

relative_ppm = 3.0
elapsed_seconds = 1000.0
drift_ms = relative_ppm*1e-6*elapsed_seconds*1e3
print('relative drift (ms):',drift_ms)
assert abs(drift_ms-3.0)<1e-12

Trigger Fanout and Synchronous Conversion Starts

Many multi-ADC systems distribute a shared convert-start pulse or a common low-jitter sample clock. The two approaches are not equivalent: a convert-start event may traverse separate asynchronous paths, while a shared periodic sampling clock can align repeated apertures if each converter's relevant edges and internal delays are calibrated. Trigger generators must meet pulse-width, input threshold, propagation, and timing-margin requirements. A nominal simultaneous software command generally cannot replace a hardware timing reference in precision phase measurements.

The design should document which clock edges are authoritative, whether triggers must be synchronous to a device clock, and how each device reports the relationship between its first valid output sample and the trigger event. Test the simultaneous sampling outcome with a common analog stimulus. If an isolation barrier carries the trigger, its propagation-delay variation and clock-induced jitter can enter the system alignment budget.

JESD204B Deterministic Latency and SYSREF

High-speed converters may transport samples over a framed serial interface such as JESD204B. Its deterministic link latency is defined over particular digital transport boundaries, not automatically from the analog aperture all the way to the final DAC output. JESD204B subclass 1 can use a common SYSREF timing reference to align local multiframe clock boundaries; compatible devices and link implementations must meet SYSREF setup/hold, phase, and distribution conditions. Subclass 0 does not inherently provide the same deterministic-latency guarantee.

A multi-converter system can have deterministically aligned link frames yet retain unequal analog aperture delays, converter filter latencies, or cable group delays. Alignment requires accounting for every stage. Periodic SYSREF distribution may itself couple into the analog spectrum, and repeated realignment events can be disruptive if improperly configured. Link training, reset behavior, deterministic-latency repeatability, and analog-sample verification all belong in the commissioning procedure.

JESD204B Subclasses and Timing Responsibilities

A design using subclass 1 coordinates device-clock and SYSREF timing; subclass 2 uses a different synchronization approach involving the SYNC mechanism, and subclass 0 leaves deterministic-latency management to other system-level strategies. The exact interface revision, supported subclass, synchronization sequence, and setup/hold constraints must be read from the chosen converter and FPGA implementation. Avoid applying a JESD204B detail automatically to JESD204C or to a different proprietary serial interface without verifying the protocol specification.

Deterministic link latency means repeatability of the defined digital link delay under specified startup/configuration conditions. It does not mean zero delay, and it does not inherently guarantee that every analog channel acquired the same physical instant. Proper system verification correlates a common analog event with recovered digital sample indices after link initialization, power cycling, and any intended recovery modes.

Coherent Acquisition, FFT Bins, and Spectral Leakage

Spectral testing often assumes an input sine and sampling clock whose frequencies produce a near-integer number of cycles in the captured record. Coherent sampling avoids leakage of the strong fundamental into neighboring FFT bins under an idealized noiseless model. If the sine frequency is not coherent with the record, an appropriate window and careful spectral integration are needed. A leaking fundamental can make a clean clock appear noisy; a poorly chosen fundamental exclusion band can hide genuine close-in phase noise.

A shared reference between stimulus generator and ADC can improve coherent captures but creates correlation that must be documented. The record length determines frequency-bin spacing, which in turn determines the ability to resolve close-in phase-noise components. Instrument source purity, converter aperture noise, input-driver noise, and clock phase noise can all shape the observed spectrum. A single pretty FFT is therefore not a full clock qualification.

Planning a Coherent Sampling Test

For N samples at f_s, a coherent tone of integer cycle count k has f_in = k*f_s/N, assuming the signal generator and clock remain phase-coherent over the capture. The bin spacing is f_s/N. Choosing k coprime with N can help the sample phases cover the unit circle rather than repeating a small subset, although additional constraints determine suitability for a real ADC linearity test. This calculation says nothing about jitter floor unless the analog source and clock purity are also controlled.

The example chooses an odd integer cycle count for a power-of-two FFT length and reports the tone frequency. In practice, the analog input filtering and converter's input band must also support that tone.

import math
fs = 125e6
N = 16384
cycles = 1237
fin = fs*cycles/N
assert math.gcd(cycles,N)==1
assert abs(fin/(fs/N)-cycles)<1e-12
print('bin spacing (Hz):',fs/N,'coherent tone (MHz):',round(fin/1e6,5))

Measuring Phase Noise and Instrument Noise Floor

A dedicated phase-noise analyzer or appropriately designed cross-correlation setup can estimate an oscillator's phase-noise spectrum. At very low noise levels, instrument residual noise and calibration uncertainty can dominate. The analyzer's phase-noise floor, carrier power, connection scheme, and chosen offset range must be recorded. Cross-correlation can suppress certain uncorrelated measurement-system noise contributions, but it is not a magic correction for spurs or errors common to both measurement channels.

Before integrating an observed curve, distinguish continuous phase-noise density from discrete spurs, account for instrument calibration, and state whether noise-floor subtraction was performed. A scope-derived edge-jitter RMS and an analyzer-derived integrated jitter can differ because they use different time/offset bandwidths, references, and estimators. The correct interpretation is a measurement-model comparison, not an assumption that one instrument is defective.

Oscilloscope Jitter Measurement and Probe Loading

An oscilloscope can measure edge TIE, period variation, and sometimes eye-diagram jitter metrics, subject to its timebase, bandwidth, trigger scheme, probe, and voltage noise. Probe capacitance and poor grounding can alter the very edge under test. If an analog threshold is inferred from a finite-slew signal, vertical instrument noise becomes apparent horizontal jitter through the slope relation. Measurements taken relative to an unstable trigger may conceal or exaggerate common jitter components.

For reliable work, specify whether the instrument uses an external reference, recovered clock, or its own timebase, and whether results are detrended. Record sample count, acquisition duration, threshold level, and confidence treatment. A measured 1 ps RMS output is not automatically a 1 ps RMS additive jitter contribution of a buffer; the source and instrument may contribute to that observation. Characterization of additive jitter requires a suitable subtraction or correlation method with stated assumptions.

Using ADC SNR Versus Input Frequency to Detect Jitter

A useful experiment measures converter SNR for a sequence of analog sine frequencies while holding amplitude, clocking configuration, and other conditions as constant as practical. If timing noise dominates, the simple model predicts about 20 dB degradation per decade of analog input frequency. Deviations can reveal a fixed noise floor, frequency-dependent input-chain distortion, clock spurs, or changes in analog filter behavior. A measurement at one input frequency cannot reliably separate all these mechanisms.

To estimate effective jitter from an apparent high-frequency SNR limit, one may invert the sine model under an explicit assumption that jitter dominates and other noise sources are negligible or separately accounted for. The result is a model-derived estimate, not direct proof of the oscillator's phase-noise integral. Distinguish datasheet SNR excluding harmonics from SINAD and use the same stimulus conditions for comparisons.

Model-Based Jitter Estimate from an SNR Limit

Suppose a 100 MHz input-tone test yields an SNR of 72 dB that has been independently established as jitter-dominated. Under the idealized sine relation, the corresponding effective jitter is approximately 10^(-72/20)/(2*pi*100e6) seconds, around 400 fs RMS. If other significant noise remains, this direct inversion overestimates jitter: the complete measured noise power cannot all be assigned to the clock.

The code deliberately labels the result an equivalent total-noise timing estimate. It is a diagnostic upper limit on the jitter contribution under independent nonnegative noise powers, not an oscillator part-number specification.

import math
measured_snr = 72.0
analog_freq = 100e6
apparent_jitter_fs = 10**(-measured_snr/20)/(2*math.pi*analog_freq)*1e15
print('equivalent all-noise jitter estimate (fs):',round(apparent_jitter_fs,1))
assert 300 < apparent_jitter_fs < 500

Measurement Clock, Stimulus Clock, and Correlation

A measurement system often uses a signal generator whose own phase noise is comparable to or larger than the ADC clock noise. If the generator and the sample clock are independent, phase fluctuations from both sources can contribute to the measured spectrum. If they derive from a common reference, some correlated components can cancel depending on their signal paths and multiplication ratios. A shared reference can therefore improve test repeatability while potentially masking particular absolute phase-noise effects.

An acceptance plan must state the reference architecture and stimulus purity, not merely identify the converter under test. When a measured SNR is unexpectedly poor, repeat with a known lower-noise stimulus or alternate clock, and compare contributions at the same analog frequency. When it is unexpectedly excellent, investigate whether common-clock phase correlation has made the test less representative of real independent-signal operation.

Temperature, Supply, Aging, and Field Conditions

Clock performance changes with supply quality, ambient temperature, oscillator warmup, board strain, and component aging. A clock device characterized at room temperature with a quiet laboratory regulator may exhibit different phase noise or spurious output in a compact field instrument near a switching power supply. Relative channel skew also changes with components' temperature coefficients and unequal paths. A single laboratory measurement does not automatically guarantee deployment behavior.

A representative validation matrix varies clock frequency, analog input frequency, temperature, supply tolerance, fanout load, and digital activity. Record post-warmup stabilization requirements. Separate temporary drift in phase or frequency from RMS short-term jitter measured over a selected band. For highly coordinated instruments, verify whether a lost reference or PLL unlock causes a silent change in sample period and whether the system exposes that state in its metadata or diagnostics.

Building a Clock and Timing Error Budget

Begin with the application-level quantity: allowable in-band noise, relative phase error, absolute timestamp uncertainty, event deadline, or deterministic trigger-to-output latency. Translate each into a timing requirement using its own model. A sinewave SNR budget yields an RMS effective aperture noise limit; a phase-sensitive array yields a skew and drift limit; a control loop yields a latency and jitter distribution requirement. These separate limits should not be collapsed into one unexplained 'timing accuracy' number.

Next divide the total clock budget among the oscillator, synthesis, fanout, isolation, and internal converter aperture. Use RSS only for justified independent random terms with compatible frequency bands. Keep deterministic delay and spurs as distinct entries. Finally, validate at the relevant analog input frequency and in the assembled configuration, with measurements that include the actual data path and synchronization mechanism.

A Feasibility Budget for a Proposed Clock Chain

An RF ADC system targets 78 dB jitter-only SNR at an analog input of 80 MHz. The clock chain is expected to contribute 160 fs RMS at the reference distribution output, 110 fs added independently by a buffer, and 200 fs internal aperture jitter. Compute the full RSS estimate and compare it to the maximum allowed by the sine formula. This demonstrates why setting a source-oscillator requirement independently of the converter's internal floor can produce a physically impossible procurement specification.

The analysis assumes all three terms are independent, cover matching effective bands, and that no additional correlated spur dominates. The pass/fail result applies to this idealized jitter-only condition; actual total converter SNR must be budgeted separately.

import math
fin = 80e6
required_snr = 78
max_allowed_fs = 1e15*10**(-required_snr/20)/(2*math.pi*fin)
actual_fs = math.sqrt(160**2+110**2+200**2)
print('allowed fs:',round(max_allowed_fs,1),'predicted fs:',round(actual_fs,1))
assert actual_fs > max_allowed_fs

A Reproducible Design and Verification Workflow

First, state the analog signals and their frequency or slope range. Second, define required SNR, relative phase, timestamp precision, latency, or spurious limits with actual units. Third, compute the corresponding jitter or skew budget under explicit assumptions. Fourth, map every clock and data path from the reference to the analog aperture and to the reported sample identifier. Fifth, obtain phase-noise and additive-jitter data on compatible bands. Sixth, budget independent terms and correlated/spurious mechanisms separately. Seventh, verify electrical clock input conditions, PCB return paths, synchronization triggers, and converter datasheet timing. Eighth, repeat relevant tests across input frequencies, power/temperature corners, activity patterns, and reset sequences.

Store an evidence package: part numbers and firmware configuration, oscillator plan, PLL settings, integration limits, measured jitter/phase-noise spectra, bench conditions, raw captures, accepted skew corrections, and signoff criteria. Without those records, a future engineer may unknowingly compare unlike jitter specifications or invalidate a previously calibrated phase relationship.

Common Analytical Errors and Their Corrections

The most consequential errors are: inserting sample-clock frequency where the formula requires analog input frequency; calling a fixed aperture delay 'jitter'; adding RMS terms from mismatched bands; treating phase noise in dBc/Hz as a directly summable voltage or time; attributing all converter noise to the clock; assuming synchronous data links automatically align analog apertures; assuming a valid PLL lock signal proves low phase noise; and using receive timestamps as though they mark the physical sample instant. Each error can survive ordinary functionality testing while undermining quantitative results.

Correct them with explicit variables and references. Write the timing path and time origin on every diagram, label measurements with bandwidth and statistical definition, and preserve the distinction between clock-source quality, distribution-added uncertainty, device aperture, deterministic latency, and digital transport. A physical instrument's accuracy is a property of its full system and measurement procedure, not a single appealing specification copied from a component datasheet.

A Compact Reusable Timing Calculator

This self-contained calculator computes four basic quantities: jitter-only sine SNR, permitted RMS jitter for a target SNR, phase offset due to fixed skew, and the RSS of independent random timing terms. The use of seconds internally reduces scale mistakes; input wrappers can be added for femtoseconds, picoseconds, and nanoseconds. It intentionally does not model a phase-noise spectrum or non-Gaussian tails. Engineers should compare these simple screening results against frequency-domain and hardware evidence.

The assertions provide dimensional and algebraic checks. They do not certify that any component or system meets a product requirement.

from math import pi, log10, sqrt

def snr_from_jitter(f_hz, jitter_seconds):
    return -20*log10(2*pi*f_hz*jitter_seconds)

def jitter_from_snr(f_hz, snr_db):
    return 10**(-snr_db/20)/(2*pi*f_hz)

def phase_from_skew(f_hz, skew_s):
    return 360*f_hz*skew_s

def rss(*values_seconds):
    return sqrt(sum(v*v for v in values_seconds))

jitter = 300e-15
assert abs(jitter_from_snr(50e6,snr_from_jitter(50e6,jitter))-jitter) < 1e-25
assert abs(phase_from_skew(10e6,1e-9)-3.6)<1e-12
assert abs(rss(3e-12,4e-12)-5e-12)<1e-25
print('screening model checks passed')

Applied Investigation 1: RF Receiver Clock Feasibility

Scenario. A direct-RF ADC digitizes a 400 MHz sine. The engineering requirement is at least 72 dB jitter-only SNR. A proposed 500 fs clock distribution and 200 fs internal aperture contribution are specified as independent RMS values over comparable effective bands. Determine whether the proposed chain meets the requirement, and identify the relevant frequency. The ADC's 250 MSPS rate is deliberately less than twice the input frequency, so a planned RF undersampling architecture would be required and alias-filter checks remain separate.

Analysis. Combine the two timing terms by RSS, then insert the 400 MHz analog frequency into the sine relation. The resulting clock limit is far below 72 dB; an architect cannot rescue it merely by quoting the 100 MHz aliased frequency. Estimate the maximum allowable composite RMS, evaluate whether the converter alone exhausts that allowance, and recommend a revised device or budget. No source filter, front-end SFDR, or actual noise figure has been supplied, so the verdict is limited to jitter-only feasibility.

from math import pi, log10, sqrt
f_analog = 400e6
jitter_fs = sqrt(500**2 + 200**2)
snr = -20*log10(2*pi*f_analog*jitter_fs*1e-15)
allowed_fs = 1e15*10**(-72/20)/(2*pi*f_analog)
print('effective jitter (fs):',round(jitter_fs,1))
print('jitter-only SNR (dB):',round(snr,2))
print('required jitter (fs):',round(allowed_fs,1))
assert snr < 72 and allowed_fs < 200

Applied Investigation 2: Multichannel Power-Phase Calibration

Scenario. Two current and voltage measurement channels observe a 60 Hz waveform, but one analog sampling path is 2 microseconds later than the other. The phase-based power calculation is sensitive to their relative time. Calculate the resulting electrical phase offset and determine whether averaging acquisitions fixes the error. Then explain how the same delay behaves for a 10 kHz harmonic used in an advanced power-quality analysis.

Analysis. The skew creates a repeatable phase displacement of 360*f*delta_t degrees. At 60 Hz the displacement may look small, but it is systematic. At 10 kHz the same skew is much more consequential. Calibrate relative aperture delay with a common stimulus and record any analog-filter group-delay differences. Do not identify the skew as RMS jitter or add it to the clock phase-noise budget. Verify the applied deskew against both fundamental and harmonic components.

lag = 2e-6
for frequency in (60,10_000):
    print(frequency,'Hz:', round(360*frequency*lag,4),'degrees')
assert abs(360*60*lag-.0432)<1e-12
assert abs(360*10_000*lag-7.2)<1e-12

Applied Investigation 3: A Phase-Noise Datasheet Comparison

Scenario. Vendor A advertises 180 fs RMS integrated from 12 kHz to 20 MHz; Vendor B advertises 130 fs integrated from 100 kHz to 10 MHz. Is B necessarily the better converter clock? No: the bands omit different portions of the spectrum. Create a comparison request that includes phase-noise curves, spurs, output frequency, supply condition, and a common integration band selected for your ADC. If only the summary numbers are available, no rigorous ranking can be established.

Analysis. Assume as a pedagogical special case that both sources have a flat phase-noise density. An RMS value then scales with the square root of integrated bandwidth. Show how the ratio between offset bandwidths changes that value under the model. Explicitly note that a real source's close-in noise often is not flat, so this calculation illustrates noncomparability rather than producing a valid vendor normalization.

from math import sqrt
band_a = 20e6-12e3
band_b = 10e6-100e3

Applied Investigation 4: Sampling-Clock Driver Slew Limitation

Scenario. A designer installs an expensive 90 fs oscillator, but the ADC receives a 0.2 V/ns clock edge amid 0.4 mV RMS receiver threshold uncertainty. Estimate the threshold-jitter contribution and compare it with the oscillator's standalone noise, assuming independence. A poor clock input stage can dominate system noise even when the source is outstanding.

Analysis. The slope method gives 2 ps RMS from the threshold mechanism alone—more than twenty times the oscillator noise in this model. The RSS total is barely different from 2 ps. The rational design change is to validate the clock input amplitude, receiver noise, termination, differential interface, and source-to-pin path before seeking an even cleaner oscillator. Because real threshold noise can be correlated with other sources, bench validation is still needed.

from math import sqrt
threshold_noise=0.4e-3
clock_slope=0.2e9
threshold_jitter_fs=threshold_noise/clock_slope*1e15
overall_fs=sqrt(90**2+threshold_jitter_fs**2)
print('threshold rms (fs):',threshold_jitter_fs,'combined (fs):',round(overall_fs))
assert abs(threshold_jitter_fs-2000)<1e-9

Applied Investigation 5: Pipeline Data Arrival Is Not Sample Alignment

Scenario. Two ADC channels both emit their first valid sample 12 device clocks after activation, yet a common injected pulse appears shifted by one sample in the combined data. A project team proposes treating the link's reported equal latency as proof of aligned sampling. Explain why this inference is incorrect, identify the relevant internal and external paths, and propose a diagnostic procedure.

Analysis. Equal digital output latency alone does not prove equal physical aperture times. The converters may have different sampling clock phases, aperture delays, trigger processing, pipeline configurations, alignment registers, transport buffering, or analog input filter group delays. Apply one controlled event simultaneously to both analog input pins, record each device's sample counter or frame alignment, and compare fitted event times. Verify after reset and reconnection. Separate a fixed one-sample offset (possibly correctable in digital alignment) from random residual timing uncertainty, which is not fixed by moving a buffer pointer. Record the phase of the actual sample clocks at the converter inputs, not only at the reference-source fanout.

Applied Investigation 6: Real-Time Motor Control and Data Age

Scenario. A motor controller receives 40 kSPS current samples from a sigma-delta path. The sample value is highly repeatable, but the filter and transport add a known 0.8 ms effective delay; occasional software scheduling adds another 0.4 ms. The controller is expected to meet a 1.0 ms sense-to-actuate deadline. Assess the failure and identify whether a lower-noise sampling oscillator would solve it.

Analysis. Even before accounting for control computation and actuation, the worst stated end-to-end delay is 1.2 ms, beyond the 1.0 ms deadline. The problem is principally latency and scheduling, not jitter-induced ADC voltage noise. A control-compatible lower-latency filter, hardware-triggered capture and actuation, and deterministic scheduling may be required. Validate a latency distribution and tail criterion under actual load rather than relying on mean latency. If the application also requires low phase error in a waveform, that becomes an additional independent clocking requirement, not a substitute for the deadline test.

Applied Investigation 7: Clock-Correlated Converter Spur

Scenario. An RF ADC shows a large narrow spur 500 kHz away from the test tone even though its integrated clock RMS specification appears excellent. The board uses a fractional clock synthesizer and a switch-mode supply. Suggest experiments that distinguish reference-related spurs, regulator coupling, analog input harmonics, and measurement-source contamination.

Analysis. Inspect the clock phase-noise plot at the spur's offset and search for a corresponding reference or supply-switching component. Shift the input tone while holding clock frequency fixed and observe whether the spur follows the tone as a sideband; vary synthesizer reference and loop settings within permitted ranges; change supply switching conditions; and replace the test source or clock temporarily with independent known-clean alternatives. A strong deterministic phase modulation can violate SFDR even if its contribution to broad integrated RMS is relatively small. Report spur power and offsets separately from random-jitter RMS, and do not claim the source solely from one spectrum.

Applied Investigation 8: Multi-Converter Acceptance Budget

Scenario. A six-channel acquisition platform must deliver at least 78 dB jitter-only SNR for a 50 MHz input and channel skew below 150 ps after calibration. The clock has 180 fs RMS distribution jitter, internal converter aperture adds 170 fs, and local clock drivers add 130 fs, assumed independent and in comparable bands. The worst residual calibrated channel-skew measurement is 120 ps. Test both requirements separately and specify additional acceptance tests.

Analysis. RSS the clock terms and calculate the sine timing limit; independently compare 120 ps with the 150 ps skew tolerance. Summary of Equations, Units, and Assumptions

Relationship Expression Required interpretation
Ideal sample period T_s = 1/f_s Stable nominal sample rate
Constant frequency error delta_t ≈ (ppm*10^-6)*elapsed_time Constant relative frequency offset
Fixed skew to phase delta_phi_deg = 360*f_in*delta_t Sine frequency and fixed delay known
Slope-induced error delta_v ≈ (dv/dt)*delta_t Small timing displacement, differentiable input
Jitter-only sine SNR -20*log10(2*pi*f_in*sigma_t) Small, appropriate RMS time noise, independent sine model
Jitter for target SNR 10^(-SNR/20)/(2*pi*f_in) Same sine-jitter assumptions
Independent jitter combination sigma_total = sqrt(sum(sigma_i^2)) Independent zero-mean terms, matched bands
Correlated jitter combination Var(sum X_i) = sum Var(X_i)+2*sum Cov(X_i,X_j) Covariance explicitly included
Phase to time sigma_t = sigma_phi/(2*pi*f_clock) Same reference and integration bandwidth
Integrated phase-noise jitter sigma_phi^2 ≈ 2*integral(10^(L(f)/10)df) Small-phase-noise SSB convention and stated offsets
Total independent noise limit SNR_total = -10*log10(sum(10^(-SNR_i/10))) Independent additive noise powers, not generic SINAD

The frequency f_in in voltage-error and sine-SNR formulas refers to the analog signal, whereas f_clock in phase-to-time conversion refers to the clock carrier. That distinction is a core analytical requirement. Never mix seconds, picoseconds, and femtoseconds, and never report an integrated jitter figure without its offset range.

Supplementary Demonstration: Jitter-to-Voltage Monte Carlo

A small simulation illustrates how clock timing noise becomes signal-dependent voltage error. Draw independent zero-mean Gaussian timing offsets for a sine input and compare actual samples at perturbed instants against ideal samples. The simulation standard deviation approaches the first-order RMS error for sufficiently small jitter and broad sampling phases. It is a model of random uncorrelated jitter, not a simulation of a real oscillator's colored phase noise or deterministic spurs.

The random seed makes the illustration reproducible. Increasing sample count improves the Monte Carlo estimate but does not validate a component specification. The purpose is to connect the derivative formula with an explicit time-domain error, not to replace calibrated spectral measurements.

import math, random, statistics
rng = random.Random(407)
f = 20e6
jitter = 2e-12
N = 50_000
errors = []
for _ in range(N):
    phase = rng.random()*2*math.pi
    dt = rng.gauss(0,jitter)
    actual = math.sin(phase+2*math.pi*f*dt)
    ideal = math.sin(phase)
    errors.append(actual-ideal)
measured = math.sqrt(statistics.fmean(x*x for x in errors))
predicted = (2*math.pi*f*jitter)/math.sqrt(2)
print('measured/predicted error:',round(measured/predicted,3))
assert abs(measured/predicted-1)<.04

Supplementary Demonstration: A Periodic Clock Spur

A deterministic sinusoidal sampling-time modulation is not equivalent to independent random jitter. For a tone sampled at an effective clock phase that varies sinusoidally with another frequency, the output contains structured modulation rather than a purely flat noise floor. This simplified example computes a first-order phase-modulation index beta = 2*pi*f_input*t_peak and the small-modulation approximation of each first-order sideband relative to its carrier, 20*log10(beta/2) dBc. It applies when beta << 1, with a sinusoidal timing modulation and no other defects.

A 2 ps peak periodic timing disturbance at a 100 MHz analog input yields a modulation index of about 0.00126 rad. Its sidebands can be visible in a sensitive spectrum even though the modulation's RMS time displacement is small. A spur-aware SFDR test is therefore necessary alongside broad integrated jitter calculations.

import math
f_in = 100e6
peak_time_mod = 2e-12
beta = 2*math.pi*f_in*peak_time_mod
sideband_dbc = 20*math.log10(beta/2)
print('modulation index:',round(beta,6),'sideband (dBc):',round(sideband_dbc,2))
assert beta < 0.01 and sideband_dbc < -60

Supplementary Demonstration: Noise Budget Sensitivity

Suppose a converter's internal aperture jitter is fixed at 240 fs RMS while the distribution clock may be improved from 300 fs to 100 fs. Compute the total jitter and equivalent jitter-only sine SNR at a 150 MHz analog input in both cases. Reducing a clock contribution improves performance, but gains eventually flatten against the converter floor. This helps teams decide whether to invest in clock hardware or consider a different ADC.

The example holds all other factors constant and assumes independence. It is a decision illustration, not a substitute for verifying a candidate component's noise bandwidth and phase-noise spectral shape.

import math
f=150e6
aperture_fs=240
for external_fs in [300,200,100,0]:

    snr=-20*math.log10(2*math.pi*f*effective*1e-15)
    print(f'external {external_fs:>3} fs: total {effective:.1f} fs, SNR {snr:.2f} dB')
assert math.hypot(0,aperture_fs)==aperture_fs

Worked Review Answers

  1. How do ideal sample time, clock frequency, and sample rate relate? At fixed nominal sample rate f_s, ideal instants are n/f_s. Frequency accuracy concerns the rate over time, while phase offset and jitter describe when individual edges occur relative to a chosen time reference. A correct nominal count does not prove exact relative timing.
  2. Why is fixed aperture delay not the same as aperture jitter? Aperture delay is the nominal difference from an external reference edge to an effective sample instant; jitter is variation around an appropriate timing trend. Fixed delay may be calibrated in a phase-sensitive array. Random timing variation appears as signal-slope-dependent voltage error.
  3. Derive the first-order voltage error caused by a small time displacement. Taylor-expand v(t+delta_t) about t: v(t+delta_t)-v(t) is approximately v prime(t)*delta_t. The approximation requires small displacements and a sufficiently smooth input over the excursion. It predicts greatest error where absolute signal slope is largest.
  4. Which frequency enters the conventional jitter-only sine SNR formula? The physical analog sine input frequency f_in, not automatically the ADC sample rate and not the low digital alias frequency. The sampling switch encounters the slope of the actual input waveform.
  5. State the assumptions behind the sine jitter-only SNR expression. The input is sinusoidal; timing perturbations are small, suitable RMS noise is independent of sample phase and signal, and relevant phases are represented. The result describes only jitter-induced voltage noise, not the total converter SNR or SINAD.
  6. Find the approximate jitter ceiling for 80 dB at 100 MHz. Invert the relation: sigma_t ≤ 10^(-80/20)/(2pi100e6) = approximately 159 fs RMS. A design requiring 80 dB overall noise performance needs additional margin because other noise mechanisms consume part of the budget.
  7. What is the difference between sampling jitter and channel skew? Jitter is time variation relative to an appropriate clock model. Skew is a relative time offset between two paths, often largely deterministic. Clock trees can have tiny RMS jitter yet unequal channel delays.
  8. How does 100 ps skew affect a 10 MHz sine phase? Use 360fdelta_t: 360(10 million)(100 picoseconds) = 0.36 degree. It is a fixed phase displacement if the skew is constant, not a noise floor that averages away.
  9. When may independent clock jitters be combined by root-sum-square? The terms must be zero-mean or suitably centered, uncorrelated or independent, non-overlapping and defined on compatible integration bands. Do not RSS deterministic skew or duplicate total jitter measurements from the same chain.
  10. How does correlation change the variance budget? The variance of a sum includes twice the covariance between each pair of terms. Shared references can cause relative cancellation, while correlated interference can reinforce errors. A source-to-measurement transfer model is needed.
  11. Why cannot two vendor RMS phase-jitter numbers be ranked without their bands? The integrals may cover different oscillator offset frequencies. A lower RMS over a narrow band can coexist with worse omitted close-in noise or wideband noise. Compare spectra and integrate over common application-relevant limits.
  12. What does dBc/Hz mean in a clock phase-noise plot? It is a relative single-sideband noise-power spectral density at a specified carrier offset, under a measurement convention. It is not a direct RMS time unit. Convert to linear density and integrate the relevant band to obtain phase variance.
  13. Outline the small-angle phase-noise-to-time calculation. Integrate roughly twice the one-sideband linear density over positive offsets to obtain phase variance in radians squared. Take the square root, then divide by 2pif_clock for RMS timing jitter. Specify conventions, bands, and spur treatment.
  14. Why are PLL lock and low jitter different guarantees? A PLL may be frequency- or phase-locked yet exhibit substantial output reference noise, VCO noise, loop peaking, or spurs. Lock detection does not measure the integrated spectral purity at the converter pins.
  15. How can a slow clock edge make a good oscillator seem bad? Voltage noise at a receiver threshold produces crossing-time variation approximately voltage noise divided by local slew rate. An attenuated, ringing, or poorly terminated edge can add more time variation than the oscillator itself.
  16. What is the difference between throughput and latency in a pipelined ADC? Throughput counts accepted or delivered samples per unit time once the pipeline is running; latency is the delay from the relevant analog sample event to its corresponding output. Multiple pipeline stages can preserve high throughput but delay each datum.
  17. Why does sigma-delta filter delay matter in a feedback controller? The filtered word can represent an earlier input interval. A large or variable sample-to-result delay reduces control timeliness even when amplitude resolution is excellent. Clock phase noise is a separate requirement.
  18. Does a shared sample clock guarantee phase-coherent analog inputs? No. Distribution skew, different apertures, analog input filter group delays, and local trigger paths can produce interchannel phase differences. Verify a common physical stimulus and calibrate relative signal paths.
  19. What produces timing mismatch artifacts in an interleaved ADC? Per-channel sampling offsets create periodic slope-dependent errors, potentially producing spurious tones. Offset and gain mismatches can also generate spurs, so frequency-dependent testing and separate calibration are required.
  20. Why is a synchronization pulse not interchangeable with a clean sample clock? A trigger defines an event or phase epoch but may traverse variable paths or fail digital setup/hold timing. A sampling clock governs every aperture. Systems may require both, with specified relationship and skew.
  21. What does JESD204B deterministic latency actually guarantee? With a supported subclass and compliant synchronization, it controls repeatability of defined digital framing/link delay boundaries. It does not automatically include ADC front-end aperture, DAC analog output latency, or other group delays.
  22. How does SYSREF relate to subclass 1 JESD204B alignment? SYSREF provides a common reference for local multiframe clock alignment when properly distributed and captured against device clocks. Its phase, setup/hold margin, and interference coupling matter. Actual analog channel alignment still needs verification.
  23. Why might receive-time CPU timestamps misrepresent sample time? Transport, DMA, buffering, interrupt scheduling, and software queue delays occur after sampling. Timestamp hardware sample indices or a known aperture reference, not just packet arrival, when phase or event timing is important.
  24. How does an FFT record affect observable phase noise? Record length sets bin width; fundamental leakage, excluded bins, windows, and reference correlation determine which phase-noise offsets can be distinguished. Use a matching observation model before comparing integrated phase jitter with an FFT SNR.
  25. Why should a narrow spur be reported separately from RMS jitter? A phase-modulation spur can produce a concentrated spectral sideband and violate SFDR even if its broadband integrated variance is small. Spectral placement and power matter; treating it as white Gaussian jitter hides the mechanism.
  26. What should a clock acceptance report include? At minimum it should include analog and clock frequencies, phase-noise offset band, jitter statistic, source clock and signal-generator reference, temperature, power condition, relevant spurs, converter configuration, timing origin, data path, and raw measured evidence.
  27. Why might reducing an external clock from 200 fs to 50 fs show little improvement? An internal converter aperture floor, thermal noise, quantization noise, or distortion may dominate. Combining the independent timing terms by RSS and the noise powers separately reveals diminishing returns.
  28. How should designers decide whether to invest in phase noise, skew, or latency? Start from the actual application failure: dynamic SNR/SFDR points toward effective clock noise and spurs; multichannel phase bias toward skew; absolute synchronization toward timebase accuracy; real-time control toward end-to-end latency and timing tails. Verify each dimension independently.
