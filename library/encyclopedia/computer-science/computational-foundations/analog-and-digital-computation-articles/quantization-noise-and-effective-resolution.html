Quantization as a Physical Information Boundary

Quantization replaces a continuously varying physical quantity with one of a finite set of representable values. In analog-to-digital conversion, an input voltage is assigned a code; in digital-to-analog conversion, a code selects an output level; in digital signal processing, a mathematical result is rounded to a finite word length. Every case introduces a difference between an ideal continuum and a realizable representation. The effect is sometimes a benign, bounded rounding discrepancy and sometimes an audible tone, visible contour, unstable control behavior or loss of a small measurement. The engineering challenge is to predict which interpretation applies to the signal and operating regime at hand.

This specialist article examines quantizer transfer characteristics, error statistics, signal-to-quantization-noise ratio, signal-to-noise-and-distortion ratio, effective number of bits, dither, oversampling and noise shaping. It expressly separates a mathematical model from a device's measured behavior. Nominal resolution is a code-count specification; dynamic effective resolution is a performance measurement; absolute accuracy also depends on calibration, reference stability and front-end errors. These quantities can move independently. The preceding ADC and DAC articles own circuit architectures; the present article owns representation error and the measurement logic needed to judge its significance.

Learning Objectives and Analytical Scope

At the end of the article, readers should be able to specify a quantizer by range, code count and decision thresholds; derive ideal RMS quantization noise under stated assumptions; predict noise power in a reduced bandwidth; distinguish harmonic distortion from broadband noise; and interpret ENOB in the context of a prescribed test tone, amplitude and bandwidth. They should also know when these ideal calculations fail because the quantization error is deterministic or correlated with a slowly varying input. More bits can create smaller steps without guaranteeing the converter has equally small thermal noise, offset, drift or spurious distortion.

  • Derive code width, rounding bounds and quantization-error examples under an explicit endpoint convention.
  • Prove the conditional q²/12 variance result and the approximate 6.02N + 1.76 dB formula for an ideal full-scale sine.
  • Interpret SNR, SINAD, THD, SFDR, ENOB and noise-free resolution without conflating their definitions.
  • Design simple dither and oversampling experiments while recognizing their assumptions and trade-offs.
  • Estimate when independent noise sources add in power and when coherent spurs demand separate analysis.
  • Write a reproducible measurement plan that retains input amplitude, frequency, bandwidth, sample count, filter settings and uncertainty.

End-to-End Quantization and Measurement Chain

A converter performance result belongs to an entire signal path. An analog source is conditioned and band-limited, sampled with finite timing precision, mapped into discrete codes, and often filtered or resampled before software displays a result. A reference voltage and the source's own noise set the scale of the codebook. The converter's analog front end can distort before quantization; downstream fixed-point calculations can quantize again. Thus an observer measuring a fluctuating digital output is not automatically seeing only quantization noise. The word “resolution” should always be accompanied by what is resolved, under which bandwidth and under which source conditions.

Physical signal → source impedance / conditioning → anti-alias filter
               ↓ source noise, gain error and distortion
Sampling aperture → finite code quantizer → digital filter / decimator
        ↓ jitter          ↓ thresholds             ↓ rounding
Reference / clock / supply noise → output record → spectral or DC analysis
                                     ↓
                     stated SNR, SINAD, ENOB or noise-free result

The chain is not necessarily linear in its error behavior: saturation clips, threshold nonuniformity adds harmonics, and a nonlinear analog stage can intermodulate tones. An analytically useful report first asks where error entered, whether it is random or deterministic, and whether the chosen metric captures it. For example, a dynamic SINAD test may reveal third-harmonic distortion that a near-DC repeatability test cannot; the DC test may reveal drift that the short dynamic record understates.

Code Width, Full-Scale Span and Endpoint Convention

Let FSR denote the specified input span, Vmax − Vmin, and let N be the nominal number of code bits. For a common 2^N-bin model, the nominal step width is q = FSR/2^N. An ideal rounding quantizer chooses the nearest reconstruction level within a legal range. Depending on whether one represents bin centers or transition-centered code values, endpoint output and offset conventions differ. Datasheets may also use an endpoint-inclusive model with FSR/(2^N − 1) spacing for certain DAC transfer definitions. Mixing those conventions can create a one-LSB disagreement even when all arithmetic is otherwise correct.

For a 12-bit converter covering a 4.096 V input span, q = 1 mV in the 2^N convention. A voltage separation of 0.3 mV does not guarantee two distinct codes; either side of a decision threshold can map to the same output, and a tiny shift across a threshold can change a code. Nominal step width is not equal to maximum absolute system error or minimum reliably detectable sensor change. Avoid describing q as an instrument's intrinsic accuracy without specifying noise, nonlinearity, reference and calibration.

def lsb_step(full_scale_span, bits):
    if full_scale_span <= 0 or bits < 1:
        raise ValueError('positive span and bit count required')
    return full_scale_span / (2 ** bits)
assert abs(lsb_step(4.096, 12) - 0.001) < 1e-15
assert abs(lsb_step(5.0, 10) - 5.0/1024) < 1e-15
print('12-bit step:', lsb_step(4.096,12), 'V')

Ideal Rounding Quantizer and Decision Thresholds

A midpoint-rounding quantizer can be written Q(x) = q·round(x/q) for values away from range boundaries, provided the precise tie-breaking rule is stated. Its error e(x) = Q(x) − x ordinarily lies between −q/2 and +q/2; the bound does not apply when the requested value exceeds representable range and clips to an endpoint. An offset may shift both the reconstruction levels and the thresholds without changing their spacing. Choosing floor rather than rounding to nearest changes the normal error interval to approximately (−q,0], under the matching positive step convention.

A quantizer is not merely a “random error generator.” For any fixed input and fixed rounding policy, its output is determined. A constant analog input that falls between levels can produce a fixed bias, not random noise. Repeating the same conversion with no noise or dither supplies no additional information about the sub-LSB position. The statistical noise model introduced later is an approximation justified by the variability and relation of the input to the thresholds; it is not part of the deterministic definition of quantization.

def quantize_nearest(x, step):
    return step * math.floor(x/step + 0.5)
step = 0.25
assert quantize_nearest(1.11,step) == 1.0
assert quantize_nearest(1.14,step) == 1.25
assert abs(quantize_nearest(1.11,step)-1.11) <= step/2
print('Rounding errors:', round(quantize_nearest(1.11,step)-1.11,3),
      round(quantize_nearest(1.14,step)-1.14,3))

Truncation, Rounding Modes and Bias

Different arithmetic units and converters may round to nearest with a specified tie rule, truncate toward zero, floor toward negative infinity, or use stochastic rounding. The direction matters in feedback systems and cumulative processing. A uniformly distributed fractional part combined with round-to-nearest can have zero mean error; the same fractional distribution combined with a floor rule can have approximately −q/2 mean error when the quantized result is defined as output minus input. But an actual signal's fractions need not be uniformly distributed. A fixed sequence of products may repeatedly round in the same direction and accumulate an appreciable offset.

A code or floating-point value without its rounding policy is an incomplete numerical specification. Consider an accumulator updated once every millisecond: an error of 0.01 unit on every operation becomes ten units after one thousand operations if it persists coherently; independent zero-mean errors instead have RMS scaling nearer the square root of the operation count. The distinction is about correlation, not simply word length. Saturating, wrapping and exception-generating overflow policies must also be recorded because they are not equivalent to ordinary rounding.

q=0.1
samples=[0.17]*100
floor_error=sum(q*math.floor(x/q)-x for x in samples)
nearest_error=sum(q*math.floor(x/q+0.5)-x for x in samples)
assert floor_error < 0 and nearest_error > 0
assert nearest_error > floor_error
print('100 repeated errors:', round(floor_error,3))

Overload, Clipping and Granular Error

The half-step rounding-error bound holds only for signals that remain within the defined nonoverload quantizer region. When a large positive input is forced to the maximum legal code, the mismatch can be many steps, and the output is no longer approximately the original waveform plus small independent noise. Hard clipping creates flat waveform peaks and strong harmonics. An instrument displaying a spur-rich spectrum after overload is not measuring the same phenomenon as the small granular error of an ideal in-range quantizer.

A useful quantization model therefore identifies two domains: granular error among supported code levels, and overload error outside them. It should state the maximum permitted input, not just the number of codes. For a large unexpected transient, preventing overload through input gain staging or wider span may be more important than adding nominal bits. Conversely, an extremely wide span wastes fine-code resolution for a low-level sensor unless the input signal is amplified appropriately. Gain staging, protection and signal bandwidth must be considered together.

Quantization Error as a Deterministic Waveform

Take a sine wave of amplitude smaller than one code step and sample it repeatedly. The quantized result may remain at one code for most of the cycle, occasionally switch near the peaks, or become a repetitive pattern synchronized with the input. Subtracting the original sine produces a periodic sawtooth-like residual rather than independent white noise. A short-period digital oscillator can yield prominent discrete spectral lines. For constant DC near a threshold, the output may alternate between adjacent codes if the system has analog noise; without noise, it may never alternate at all.

This behavior motivates an important analytical habit: inspect the sequence and its spectrum before assuming the error is white. The usual uniform error density is useful for budgets and for rich, decorrelated signals, but it may overstate the random component of an idle channel and understate a disturbing tone that happens to land at a sensitive frequency. “Quantization noise” is a standard umbrella term, yet its physical manifestations depend on signal, quantizer, dither and measurement bandwidth.

q=1.0
x=[0.4*math.sin(2*math.pi*k/32) for k in range(32)]
y=[q*math.floor(v/q+0.5) for v in x]
assert set(y)=={0.0}  # sine never reaches the +/-0.5 thresholds
error=[yy-xx for yy,xx in zip(y,x)]
assert max(abs(t) for t in error) < 0.5
print('Distinct quantized output codes:', sorted(set(y)))

Conditions for the Additive White Quantization-Noise Approximation

The classical simple model writes Q(x) = x + e and treats e as approximately independent of x, uniformly distributed across an interval of width q, and spectrally white over the stated Nyquist band. These assumptions can be reasonable when the input is active, spans many quantization levels and has enough variability that threshold phase is effectively mixed. A small deterministic sine sampled coherently, a constant DC input, an overloaded waveform or a short repeating integer sequence may violate them conspicuously. A sample clock that is carefully phase-locked to a repetitive input can make error correlations more visible, not less.

A rigorous engineering calculation therefore says “under the uniform, uncorrelated error model” before writing an RMS noise formula. It does not claim the converter emits physical thermal noise of that magnitude. Dither can be introduced to break undesirable correlations, and filtering may integrate a specified fraction of broadband noise power. If the residual is tonal or shaped in frequency, a different spectral calculation is needed. The purpose of the ideal model is to create a useful baseline and expose deviations, not to replace actual device characterization.

Deriving the Mean and Variance of Uniform Quantization Error

Under a symmetric uniform error assumption, e is distributed over [−q/2,+q/2]. Its expected value is zero: E[e] = (1/q)∫e de = 0 over the symmetric interval. Its mean-square value is E[e²] = (1/q)∫e² de = q²/12. Hence the standard deviation and RMS error are σ_q = q/√12. The calculation is exact for the stipulated uniform model, not a universal theorem about all deterministic signals passing through a quantizer. The result applies directly to noise-power budgets expressed in squared volts or squared engineering units.

For the 12-bit, 4.096 V example, q = 1 mV and σ_q ≈ 0.288675 mV. That RMS figure is a statistical expectation under the distribution model; it is neither the maximum per-sample in-range error (0.5 mV) nor an accuracy guarantee for an actual 12-bit ADC. Distinguishing maximum magnitude, bias, standard deviation and spectral distribution prevents many design errors.

q=0.001
rms=q/math.sqrt(12)
assert abs(rms-0.0002886751345948129)<1e-15
print('Uniform quantization-error RMS:',round(rms*1000,6),'mV')

Why the Variance Formula Is Not a General Independence Proof

A uniform histogram of residual error does not by itself establish that errors are uncorrelated over time or independent of the signal. Consider a periodic deterministic input whose error visits the full quantization interval in a highly ordered cycle. Its error histogram may approximate a uniform distribution while its spectrum still contains concentrated lines. In that case q²/12 may accurately describe mean-square error across a full cycle while an assumed flat spectral density gives the wrong in-band distribution.

For noise-folding and oversampling claims, the spectral and temporal relationships matter at least as much as the scalar RMS value. If an input sequence repeats exactly every R samples, the quantization error can have a discrete spectrum at multiples of the repetition rate. Averaging may cancel some spectral components or reinforce another depending on phase and filter shape. Inspecting autocorrelation and the measured spectrum is therefore a better diagnostic than reporting only an aggregate variance.

Full-Scale Sine and Ideal Signal Power

A standard dynamic benchmark drives a converter with a full-scale sinusoid that fits inside its nominal input range without overload. If the full-scale peak-to-peak span is FSR = 2^N q, the sinusoid amplitude is approximately A = FSR/2 = 2^(N−1)q. Its mean-square AC signal is A²/2 and its RMS amplitude is A/√2. The noise model's mean-square error is q²/12. Comparing signal power with error power leads to the familiar ideal sinusoidal quantization SNR. The approximation treats the endpoint occupancy and noise decorrelation in the conventional way.

This test deliberately defines its input amplitude. A smaller sine has lower signal power while the ideal broadband q²/12 noise baseline remains approximately fixed, reducing observed SNR. Therefore a quoted high-SNR number must say whether it is referenced to actual sine RMS level, a full-scale hypothetical sine, or a device-defined dBFS calibration. “Resolution” estimates derived from the measurement inherit those conventions.

Deriving the 6.02N + 1.76 dB Rule

Divide the ideal full-scale sine power by uniform quantization-noise power: (A²/2)/(q²/12) = 6(A/q)². With A = 2^(N−1)q, the ratio is 6·2^(2N−2) = (3/2)·2^(2N). In decibels, SQNR_dB = 10 log10(3/2) + 20N log10 2 ≈ 1.7609 + 6.0206N dB. This derivation relies on a full-scale sine, conventional quantizer transfer definition and an uncorrelated uniform error model; it is not a universal formula for an arbitrary input waveform, ADC architecture or measurement bandwidth.

An ideal 12-bit converter has an approximate full-Nyquist-band SQNR of 74.01 dB. The slope of approximately 6.02 dB per additional bit follows because halving step width reduces mean-square quantization error by a factor of four. An instrument whose actual SINAD is 68 dB is not mysteriously “missing bits”; thermal noise, distortion, nonlinearity and the test configuration may all contribute beyond ideal quantization. The proper next step is to identify the error contributors, not only compute a number of equivalent bits.

def ideal_sine_sqnr_db(bits):
    return 20*bits*math.log10(2)+10*math.log10(1.5)
assert abs(ideal_sine_sqnr_db(12)-74.008)<0.01
assert abs(ideal_sine_sqnr_db(16)-98.09)<0.02
print('Ideal 12-bit sine SQNR: %.3f dB'%ideal_sine_sqnr_db(12))

SNR Variation with Input Amplitude

The 6.02N + 1.76 dB estimate assumes a near-full-scale sine. If its amplitude decreases by 12 dB without changing quantizer step or the applicable random-noise floor, its signal-to-noise ratio decreases by approximately 12 dB. A laboratory's −1 dBFS test tone therefore has a different ideal reference than a hypothetical 0 dBFS sine. For ENOB estimation, this is one reason amplitude normalization must be documented; otherwise two converters tested at different levels may appear to have different “effective bits” merely because one was driven more softly.

At very low amplitude, the uncorrelated-error assumption may itself fail, creating idle tones, threshold sticking or input-dependent harmonics. One cannot extrapolate a straight 6 dB-per-bit line down indefinitely without examining the actual transfer behavior. Low-level converter applications often benefit from an explicitly designed dither, appropriate analog gain and DC noise characterization in addition to a sine-wave spectrum. Distinguish sensitivity to weak signals from maximum undistorted large-signal range.

n=12
ideal=ideal_sine_sqnr_db(n)
for level in [0,-3,-12,-24]:
    print(level, 'dB relative sine -> model SNR',round(ideal+level,2),'dB')
assert abs((ideal-12)-62.008)<0.01

Quantization Step Compared with Instrument Accuracy

A 16-bit display may suggest an extremely small unit change, but the displayed code is not an absolute voltmeter certificate. Offset is a fixed or drifting displacement of the transfer curve; gain error changes its slope; INL and DNL describe deviations from the ideal conversion geometry; reference uncertainty scales the physical size of codes. A system can have low short-term output noise and excellent repeatability while measuring every voltage 1% high because its gain was never calibrated. Conversely, a calibrated 12-bit instrument can measure a stable aggregate value more accurately than its individual instantaneous code spacing might suggest when suitable averaging, noise and calibration are present.

This separation is fundamental: resolution describes fineness of representation; precision describes repeatability; accuracy describes closeness to an appropriate reference; and dynamic ENOB summarizes noise-plus-distortion performance under a specified sine test. They are related yet noninterchangeable. A datasheet statement such as “24-bit delta-sigma converter” says what codes exist, not how many meaningful bits emerge at the intended output data rate and temperature.

Uniform Noise Spectral Density and Nyquist Band

In the ideal white quantization model, the total variance q²/12 is spread approximately uniformly over the one-sided baseband from 0 through fs/2, where fs is the sample rate. The one-sided noise power spectral density is thus (q²/12)/(fs/2) = q²/(6fs), in squared signal units per hertz. In practice, a discrete-time sequence's normalized spectrum uses different factor-of-two conventions for one-sided and two-sided definitions; do not compare numbers without checking whether the zero and Nyquist bins are treated specially.

The integral of this ideal density over a chosen baseband B no greater than fs/2 is (q²/12)·(2B/fs). This predicts how much uncorrelated quantization noise remains after an ideal low-pass filter with passband B. It does not imply an analog signal contains only frequencies below B, nor does it grant noise reduction when the error is a coherent tone inside that band. Measurement windows, equivalent-noise bandwidth and actual digital-filter response must be included in a nonideal analysis.

q=1e-3; fs=100_000; B=5_000
p_full=q*q/12
p_band=p_full*(2*B/fs)
assert abs(p_band/p_full-0.1)<1e-15
print('In-band ideal RMS, mV:',round(math.sqrt(p_band)*1000,6))

SINAD, SNR, THD and SFDR Are Different Metrics

SNR compares the desired signal with included noise while normally excluding identified harmonic distortion components according to the test convention. SINAD compares the fundamental signal with the aggregate of noise and harmonic distortion, again subject to explicitly stated band and exclusions such as DC. Total harmonic distortion (THD) describes harmonics relative to the fundamental and is often quoted as power or voltage ratio in decibels. Spurious-free dynamic range (SFDR) compares the desired fundamental with the largest single undesired spectral component. Consequently two converters with identical SINAD can have very different SFDR and different susceptibility to a nearby spur.

A low-noise converter with a severe second harmonic may exhibit high SNR yet poorer SINAD. A converter with broad noise but no strong individual spur may exhibit the reverse ordering of perceived suitability for a narrowband radio channel. Never combine values reported over different bandwidths or input frequencies into an informal ranking. Noise power adds across nonoverlapping bins, whereas a dBc spur refers to the amplitude or power of one component relative to a tone.

Working with Power Ratios in Decibels

Dynamic converter specifications are power ratios. If signal RMS amplitude is S and the unwanted aggregate RMS amplitude is E at the same impedance, SINAD_dB = 20 log10(S/E). Equivalently, if signal and unwanted mean-square values are Ps and Pe, SINAD_dB = 10 log10(Ps/Pe). The formulas are numerically identical when amplitudes represent the square roots of compatible power measures. A common error is to use 20 log10 on powers or 10 log10 on voltage amplitudes and thereby misstate the result by a factor of two in decibels.

Suppose a measured full-scale sine has RMS 1 V and the total included noise-plus-distortion RMS is 0.0005 V. SINAD is 20 log10(2000) ≈ 66.02 dB. If the measured denominator is actually 1 mV, SINAD falls to 60 dB. These calculations depend on compatible measurement filters: a filtered 1 mV figure cannot be used indiscriminately with a wideband fundamental measured in a different path.

def ratio_db(signal_rms, error_rms):
    if signal_rms<=0 or error_rms<=0: raise ValueError('positive rms required')
    return 20*math.log10(signal_rms/error_rms)
assert abs(ratio_db(1,0.0005)-66.02059991327963)<1e-9
print('SINAD example: %.2f dB'%ratio_db(1,0.0005))

Defining Effective Number of Bits from SINAD

Under the usual full-scale-sine convention, a hypothetical ideal N-bit quantizer has approximately SINAD = 6.02N + 1.76 dB when quantization is its only meaningful impairment. Equating a real converter's measured SINAD with that hypothetical ideal value yields ENOB ≈ (SINAD − 1.76)/6.02. The quantity is an equivalent dynamic performance description, not evidence that the ADC's output register literally contains fewer bits. Measurement bandwidth, signal frequency, amplitude, front-end chain and full-scale normalization must accompany the number.

An ADC with 16 nominal bits and a measured 74 dB SINAD has roughly (74 − 1.76)/6.02 = 12.0 ENOB under this convention. It can still emit 65,536 nominal codes; their instantaneous fluctuations and nonlinear errors mean the captured sine has noise-plus-distortion roughly equivalent to an ideal 12-bit converter at the specified test conditions. A second device may have higher ENOB at 1 kHz but lower ENOB at 100 kHz because input bandwidth and distortion increase with frequency.

def enob_from_sinad(sinad_db):return (sinad_db-1.76091259)/(20*math.log10(2))
assert abs(enob_from_sinad(74)-11.998)<0.01
assert abs(enob_from_sinad(98.09)-16)<0.01
print('ENOB at 74 dB SINAD:',round(enob_from_sinad(74),3))

Amplitude Normalization and ENOB Comparability

The simple ENOB equation presumes an appropriately defined near-full-scale sine. If a real test uses an input amplitude below the reference full-scale sine, measured SINAD decreases even if the unwanted RMS error stays fixed. Some measurement methodologies report an input-amplitude-corrected ENOB: they first refer the measured noise and distortion to a stated full-scale signal convention. Other methodologies simply calculate ENOB from observed SINAD. Both may be useful if accurately labeled, but the results should not be mixed as if they were identical.

Suppose two reports quote a converter at −0.5 dBFS and −6 dBFS respectively. The uncorrected dynamic ENOB cannot establish which device has a better intrinsic noise floor unless the difference in input signal power, distortion versus level and the chosen bandwidth are considered. Correcting solely for sine amplitude is justified only when the test model supports the correction; strong level-dependent distortion defeats a naive one-number adjustment. For procurement and academic comparison, retain the original measured SINAD along with the ENOB calculation.

Example: An Ideal Converter Versus a Realistic SINAD Budget

Consider a nominal 14-bit ADC, whose ideal full-Nyquist SQNR is about 86.04 dB. Suppose the measured error budget includes signal-independent thermal noise and a harmonic component that together yield a 72 dB SINAD at the test frequency. The corresponding ENOB is about 11.67 bits, not fourteen. Simply retuning the digital display to show more decimal places will not recover the missing dynamic performance. The next investigative step is to measure whether the limiting contributor is a broadband noise floor, one or more strong harmonics, clock jitter or a poor analog driver.

It is valuable to keep an absolute power budget, not just log-domain adjectives. Independent zero-mean errors may be combined by summing variances, whereas a coherent harmonic has a specific spectral location. For many routine dynamic test summaries, total noise-plus-distortion power can be summed after excluding the desired fundamental and conventions such as DC. This produces a convenient one-number SINAD, but the component breakdown is indispensable for selecting a remedy.

ideal_14=ideal_sine_sqnr_db(14)
real_enob=enob_from_sinad(72)
assert abs(ideal_14-86.049)<0.01
assert 11.6<real_enob<11.8
print('Ideal 14-bit SQNR %.2f dB; measured ENOB %.2f bits'%(ideal_14,real_enob))

Dynamic ENOB Versus Near-DC Effective Resolution

The term “effective resolution” also appears in precision measurement datasheets to describe near-DC repeatability or noise after output filtering, often not a sine-wave SINAD test. For a converter measuring an essentially constant level, one might report RMS noise, peak-to-peak noise in a specified observation window, effective bits based on an RMS noise estimate, or noise-free bits based on a peak-to-peak excursion. These metrics are distinct from dynamic ENOB derived from SINAD. They cannot be substituted without stating how the measurement was made and how the converter full-scale range enters the calculation.

A precision delta-sigma ADC may advertise nominal 24-bit output words, a lower noise-free resolution at a particular data rate, and still another dynamic ENOB under a sine test. None is inherently contradictory. Conversion speed, digital-filter selection, mains rejection, reference noise and drift affect the results. Near-DC noise-free resolution will often be lower than an RMS-based effective resolution because peak-to-peak measures are more demanding and sample-count-dependent.

RMS Noise, Peak-to-Peak Noise and Noise-Free Codes

RMS noise captures the square root of the average squared deviation from a mean, while peak-to-peak noise reports the span between observed extrema. A stationary Gaussian-like noise process has no mathematically fixed peak-to-peak value over unlimited observation time; the observed span generally increases as more samples are collected. Therefore informal rules such as “peak-to-peak is always 6.6 times RMS” depend on a chosen confidence convention and observation interval. A claimed noise-free bit count must identify those assumptions, including any digital averaging and bandwidth restriction.

A straightforward convention for an instrument with input span FSR and measured RMS noise σ is log2(FSR/σ) effective noise-limited divisions, while a peak-to-peak-based count can be written log2(FSR/noise_pp). Different vendors may choose other normalizations and rounding. A noise-derived number greater than nominal bit count should not be interpreted as new physical ADC code states. When comparing devices, prefer direct noise in microvolts RMS, sample rate and bandwidth in addition to any bit-style summary.

fsr=5.0
rms_noise=20e-6
noise_pp=130e-6
rms_bits=math.log2(fsr/rms_noise)
noise_free_bits=math.log2(fsr/noise_pp)
assert rms_bits>noise_free_bits
print('RMS-based: %.2f, observed-pp-based: %.2f bits'%(rms_bits,noise_free_bits))

Total Harmonic Distortion and Nonlinearity

An ideal uniform quantizer is nonlinear at its decision thresholds, and real converters add nonuniform code transitions, capacitor or resistor mismatch, input buffer distortion and reference interaction. A sinusoidal stimulus can therefore produce harmonics at integer multiples of its input frequency. Under a specified sampling rate, those harmonics may fold into the Nyquist band and appear at nonobvious frequencies. Harmonic power should be computed from the original tone and its aliases with the correct bin accounting, not estimated from generic “noise level.” A strong harmonic may dominate SINAD even if broadband noise is remarkably small.

Integral nonlinearity (INL) describes deviation from a chosen ideal straight line; differential nonlinearity (DNL) describes code-step irregularity relative to nominal q. Both are static transfer-characteristic metrics and neither alone predicts every dynamic error. Smooth frequency-dependent analog distortion can reduce SINAD without implying large DC INL; threshold discontinuities can also produce low-level spurs. Report both static and dynamic tests when a design must measure small DC offsets and preserve a high-fidelity waveform.

Spurious-Free Dynamic Range and Spectral Failure

SFDR focuses on the strongest unwanted spectral component, typically relative to the desired fundamental, and is often expressed in dBc. Suppose converter A has many small noise bins totaling −70 dBc but no individual spur larger than −100 dBc; converter B has less aggregate noise but one spur at −75 dBc. For a narrowband receiver that places that spur exactly in a protected channel, B might be worse even if its aggregate SINAD is higher. The metric is specifically useful when discrete interferers, not integrated broadband noise, set the performance limit.

An FFT may show supply-coupled lines, periodic code-pattern artifacts, clock feedthrough, distortion harmonics, or interference from another subsystem. An undesired line is not automatically quantization error, and improving nominal converter resolution may not move it. Diagnostics require the frequency to be traced against input tone, sample clock, reference circuits and board-level activity. Observe how each spur changes when the input level, frequency or reference source changes.

Quantization Error Autocorrelation and Idle Tones

Autocorrelation describes how a sequence resembles a time-shifted copy of itself. Ideal white quantization error has negligible nonzero-lag autocorrelation under its model; a deterministic repeating error often has strong peaks at the sequence period. In a low-level sine test or with a nearly static DC input, the residual can exhibit limit cycles and idle tones even when its total RMS energy is near an ideal q/√12 prediction. The perceptual or system impact depends on where those tones land: a faint narrowband line may be more disruptive than a larger amount of broadband noise in some instruments.

A simple diagnosis subtracts the ideal test signal from the coded and appropriately scaled output, plots the residual, computes a spectrum and checks autocorrelation over several suspected repetition periods. Sample synchronization affects the result; changing the input phase slightly can move code-transition patterns. Before labeling a peak an ADC fault, test with calibrated source, cable, termination and clock conditions. Dither may turn a concentrated tone into a benign broadband floor, trading an increase in measured wideband RMS for a reduction in deterministic artifacts.

q=0.25
x=[0.37*math.sin(2*math.pi*7*k/128) for k in range(128)]
e=[quantize_nearest(v,q)-v for v in x]
lag=128//7
mean=sum(e)/len(e)
autocorr=lambda k:sum((e[j]-mean)*(e[j+k]-mean) for j in range(len(e)-k))/(len(e)-k)
assert autocorr(0)>0
print('Residual RMS:',round(math.sqrt(sum(z*z for z in e)/len(e)),5))

Dither: Why Adding Noise Can Improve Fidelity

Dither is a deliberately introduced signal, often random, that alters the relation between an input value and a quantizer's thresholds. Without dither, a sub-LSB DC change can remain invisible until a decision boundary is crossed; a repetitive signal can generate correlated harmonic residuals. With appropriately chosen dither, repeated quantized measurements may change codes in proportion to a sub-step offset. Averaging then reveals the mean more faithfully. The price is added output variability and, for non-subtractive approaches, generally increased total noise power. Dither does not improve the underlying absolute scale calibration or correct out-of-range clipping.

The aim is not indiscriminately to “improve SNR” by injecting noise. One may accept more broadband noise to suppress perceptually objectionable tones or obtain an unbiased low-level estimate. Audio word-length reduction, precision low-frequency measurements, and dithering of quantizer thresholds have related but not identical implementations. Dither amplitude, probability distribution, whether it is added before or after filtering, and whether it is removed after quantization are all important to the outcome.

Rectangular Dither and Mean Unbiasing

Consider a unit-step nearest-rounding quantizer and a fixed in-range input x between two code levels. Add independently sampled noise d uniformly distributed between −q/2 and +q/2 before rounding. Over many trials the probability of rounding to the upper level changes linearly with the fractional position of x; as a result, the expected quantized output equals x under ideal interior conditions. This establishes a specific kind of statistical linearization. It does not mean every individual code is accurate or that output error is independent of x, and endpoints must be treated separately.

For x = 0.2q and q = 1, the quantized outcome is 1 with probability 0.2 and 0 with probability 0.8, yielding expected output 0.2. Without dither the outcome is always 0 for nearest rounding, creating −0.2 bias. A deterministic midpoint-coverage or numerical probability calculation can verify the expectation without relying on a lucky random seed. In practical electronics, the injected noise must cover thresholds despite analog front-end noise, response bandwidth and nonuniform transitions.

q=1.0
x=0.2
n=10000
# Midpoint integration over an exactly uniform rectangular dither.
noise=[q*((j+0.5)/n-0.5) for j in range(n)]
mean_output=sum(quantize_nearest(x+d,q) for d in noise)/n
assert abs(mean_output-x)<1e-12
print('Dithered estimate mean:',mean_output)

Triangular Dither and Non-Subtractive Quantization

A triangular probability density function (TPDF) dither can be formed by summing two independent rectangular noises, each uniformly distributed across a one-step-wide interval. When designed for a compatible uniform quantizer and applied non-subtractively, it is commonly used to remove signal-dependent bias and stabilize low-order error statistics. The total output error includes the dither itself because the reconstruction side does not know and remove the exact injected value. Under a conventional ideal model its mean-square error is higher than undithered uniform quantization; for the common two-rectangular-step TPDF choice, total RMS error is q/2 rather than q/√12. This result must not be generalized to every possible dither level or distribution.

Dither is often appropriate at the final word-length reduction in digital audio because low-level tonal distortion may be more objectionable than the slight broadband floor increase. In an extremely low-power sensor, the noise cost could outweigh the benefit if the application needs only a threshold crossing. Dither selection is therefore an objective-specific design step. Test both mean estimation and the frequency distribution of residual error, and specify whether you describe total output noise or only the internal quantization component.

q=1.0
uniform_dither_var=q*q/12
triangular_dither_var=2*uniform_dither_var
modeled_total_var=triangular_dither_var+q*q/12
assert abs(modeled_total_var-q*q/4)<1e-12
print('Illustrative ideal non-subtractive TPDF total RMS:',math.sqrt(modeled_total_var))

Subtractive Dither and Independent Residuals

Subtractive dithering adds a known pseudorandom or random dither signal before quantization and subtracts the identical signal in the reconstruction domain. Under the standard nonoverload conditions with an appropriate uniform dither distribution and independent randomization, the residual can become exactly uniform over one quantization step and independent of the signal. Unlike non-subtractive dithering, the added random sequence is removed from the decoded value; the residual then has variance q²/12 under the matching ideal construction. This is a mathematical design with synchronization requirements, not an operation every off-the-shelf ADC supports.

If the reconstruction side misses one dither sample, uses the wrong seed, rescales the sequence differently, or saturates before subtraction, the guarantee fails. Subtractive dither is useful in theoretical treatments and some controlled digital workflows, but standard sensor sampling seldom provides a return path in which the exact analog dither can be perfectly removed. In those systems, non-subtractive threshold randomization and subsequent averaging are more practical, while requiring careful noise-budget accounting.

q=1.0
x=0.23
n=20000
# Deterministic midpoint integration validates the subtractive property.
d=[(j+0.5)/n-0.5 for j in range(n)]
errors=[quantize_nearest(x+v,q)-v-x for v in d]
assert abs(sum(errors)/n)<1e-12
variance=sum(v*v for v in errors)/n
assert abs(variance-q*q/12)<0.0001
print('Subtractive dither error RMS:',round(math.sqrt(variance),5))

Dither Implementation Failure Modes

Dither must be introduced before the quantization operation it is intended to linearize. Adding noise to an already quantized recording cannot recover a sub-LSB change that never affected the ADC output. The randomization should reach the quantizer with sufficient amplitude and bandwidth, but must not cause frequent overload. A digital generator whose amplitude is rounded away before the intended operation may likewise provide no useful randomness. In devices whose front ends dominate the errors, dither cannot repair offset, gain drift, aperture jitter, or transfer-curve nonlinearity without additional strategies.

The correct sequence is to identify the relevant quantizer, specify the desired statistical property, pick a suitable distribution, calculate its total added noise power, and verify the resulting mean and spectrum under the actual input range. Do not assert that one LSB of added noise always creates one extra meaningful bit. For a DC sensor, a naturally occurring sub-LSB random input may already provide adequate threshold crossing; adding more noise can be counterproductive. Evaluate noise-free resolution and measurement latency after averaging rather than relying on a graph of a single code trace.

Oversampling Ratio and In-Band Noise

For a low-pass signal band extending from DC to B hertz, and a sample rate fs, the baseband oversampling ratio is OSR = fs/(2B). This is a common convention; sigma-delta systems may specify OSR relative to an internal modulator frequency and an output rate, so the particular reference frequencies must be stated. If ideal white quantization noise occupies the full Nyquist region, an ideal low-pass filter retaining only B hertz captures 1/OSR of its total power. Thus in-band noise power is reduced by OSR and the potential in-band SNR improvement is 10log10(OSR) dB.

This is processing gain, not an increase in the number of physical code values emitted by the raw converter. The digital filter must reject out-of-band content before decimation, and the signal itself must be limited to the desired band. If correlated quantization spurs fall within B, the simple white-noise fraction formula is invalid. The result also assumes the sample rate is increased while the useful signal bandwidth remains fixed; increasing both proportionally does not produce the same gain.

def processing_gain_db(osr):
    if osr<1: raise ValueError('OSR must be at least 1')
    return 10*math.log10(osr)
assert abs(processing_gain_db(16)-12.041199826559248)<1e-9
print('OSR 16 processing gain:',round(processing_gain_db(16),2),'dB')

Fourfold Oversampling and the One-Bit Rule

Under a white uncorrelated error model and ideal bandlimiting, a fourfold oversampling ratio improves in-band SNR by 10log10(4) ≈ 6.02 dB. Because one ideal bit corresponds to about 6.02 dB of SQNR, this is often called one extra effective in-band bit. To gain two bits requires approximately sixteenfold oversampling; three bits require approximately sixty-fourfold oversampling. Equivalently, Δbits ≈ 0.5 log2(OSR). These figures apply to the white-noise-limited component and should be treated as theoretical opportunities, not guaranteed improvements in overall SINAD.

A converter whose output is limited by a distortion spur, input jitter, reference drift or 1/f noise might gain far less than the ideal rule predicts. Doubling sample rate can also increase power, output data rate and downstream computational demands. Fourfold oversampling without an actual noise-bearing threshold variation may merely duplicate deterministic codes; no new information is necessarily recovered. A robust design uses both a noise model and a measured filtered output spectrum to validate any claimed resolution increase.

def ideal_bits_from_osr(osr): return 0.5*math.log2(osr)
for osr in [1,4,16,64]:
    print(osr,'x:',ideal_bits_from_osr(osr),'equivalent additional bits')
assert ideal_bits_from_osr(16)==2.0

Digital Filtering, Decimation and Noise Bandwidth

After acquiring oversampled codes, a digital low-pass filter removes energy outside the retained band, and a decimator reduces sample rate. The order is crucial: decimating first aliases out-of-band noise and tones into the new Nyquist region, where a later low-pass filter cannot uniquely undo them. A finite impulse response filter's effective noise bandwidth depends on the sum of squared filter coefficients and its actual frequency response, not simply the advertised −3 dB corner. Real stopbands are finite, and images from decimation need suppression according to the application's error budget.

For independent equal-variance input noise with coefficient vector h, filtered output noise variance is σ²·Σh_i². An M-point moving average has coefficients 1/M and variance σ²/M for white uncorrelated samples, improving RMS noise by √M. If samples are correlated, the full covariance terms matter; the naive 1/M prediction can fail badly. A moving average also introduces group delay and a non-flat passband, making it inappropriate for every waveform-reconstruction task.

def moving_average(xs, length):
    return [sum(xs[i-length+1:i+1])/length for i in range(length-1,len(xs))]
coeff=[1/8]*8
noise_power_gain=sum(t*t for t in coeff)
assert abs(noise_power_gain-1/8)<1e-15
print('White-noise power gain for 8-point moving average:',noise_power_gain)

Averaging Identical Codes Cannot Recover Hidden Detail

Suppose an ideal 8-bit ADC repeatedly observes a constant voltage with no source noise or dither. It emits the same code on every conversion. An average of a thousand identical codes is that same code; the fractional position within the original quantization interval remains unknown. The textbook 1/√M reduction in RMS uncertainty assumes independent random fluctuations. The acquisition chain must actually supply enough threshold variability for averaging to carry sub-LSB information, and the random variation must be appropriately characterized. This may come from naturally present analog noise or intentionally introduced dither.

Once threshold transitions occur, a stable mean can often be estimated more precisely with more samples, although lower noise still does not remove systematic offset or gain error. Averages must be interpreted with their acquisition bandwidth and observation time. In control systems, an extremely long average might suppress noise but make the controller respond too slowly to genuine changes. In event detection, occasional faults could be hidden by averaging. Filtering is a cost-and-accuracy decision rather than a universal improvement.

x=0.23;q=1.0
repeated=[quantize_nearest(x,q)]*1000
assert sum(repeated)/len(repeated)==0
assert abs(x-sum(repeated)/len(repeated))==0.23
print('Averaging an unchanged code still returns',sum(repeated)/len(repeated))

Oversampling and the Analog Front-End Budget

The ideal gain from oversampling assumes a fixed desired signal bandwidth, noise spread over a wider acquisition band and sufficient dynamic range to avoid saturation. The analog anti-alias filter still must limit content capable of folding at the physical sampling rate, even when software performs later decimation. A high-frequency interferer passing through a nonlinear front end can mix into the desired band before conversion; a digital low-pass filter cannot always remove the resulting intermodulation products. Increased sample clock rate also changes jitter tolerance and the data-transfer burden.

Evaluate the entire chain as source → anti-alias filter → sampling → quantizer → digital filter → decimation. Record sample rate, passband, transition bandwidth, stopband attenuation, filter latency, processor throughput and input power. A design requesting a 100 Hz measurement passband may reasonably sample at several kilohertz to help noise filtering, but it must still show whether the measured uncertainty is set by quantization, sensor noise, thermal drift, 50/60 Hz interference or reference instability. Oversampling addresses only some of these terms.

Noise Shaping: Moving Error Power Across Frequency

Noise shaping changes the spectral distribution of quantization error so that less error falls in a desired band, usually at the expense of more at higher frequencies. A simple first-order idealized error transfer function is NTF(z)=1−z^(−1); on the unit circle its magnitude is |1−exp(−jω)|=2|sin(ω/2)|, which tends toward zero near DC. An analysis that assumes the internal quantizer contributes approximately uncorrelated error then predicts high-pass-shaped error at the output. Such a model is useful for understanding delta-sigma modulation, but actual loop stability, overload and quantizer nonlinearity complicate exact behavior.

A digital low-pass filter can remove the high-frequency noise raised by the modulator if the physical and digital signal chain preserve the intended band. The converter is not creating free precision; it trades oversampled operating rate, noise spectral distribution and often latency for improved performance where the signal resides. If the out-of-band shaped noise drives another nonlinear stage, additional filtering and headroom may be required. The analysis must include the total integrated output bandwidth, not merely the low-frequency notch in a theoretical transfer function.

def ntf1_gain(omega):return abs(1-complex(math.cos(omega),-math.sin(omega)))
assert ntf1_gain(0.0) < 1e-12
assert abs(ntf1_gain(math.pi)-2) < 1e-12
print('First-order NTF gain at omega=pi/2:',round(ntf1_gain(math.pi/2),6))

Theoretical First-Order Shaping Gain

For an ideal low-frequency first-order noise-shaping model with flat internal quantization-error spectrum, the in-band noise power declines approximately as the cube of OSR for sufficiently large OSR. A widely used asymptotic expression is P_in ≈ (q²/12)·π²/(3·OSR³), using a particular normalization of OSR and the first-order error transfer function. This is steeper than the 1/OSR behavior of unshaped white noise. In the ideal asymptotic region, doubling OSR improves in-band noise by roughly 9 dB, versus roughly 3 dB for unshaped white noise.

It is important to state this as an idealized in-band power result, not a guarantee for any named 1-bit or multibit converter. Practical delta-sigma modulators introduce thermal noise, finite amplifier gain, nonlinearities and possible idle tones, and higher-order modulators may have stability constraints. Filter design and decimation are essential. The effective resolution at a given output data rate must be measured or taken from device specifications rather than extrapolated indefinitely from the asymptotic formula.

def shaped_power_fraction_first_order(osr):
    return math.pi**2/(3*osr**3)
ratio=shaped_power_fraction_first_order(64)/shaped_power_fraction_first_order(32)
assert abs(ratio-1/8)<1e-15
print('Doubling OSR first-order ideal shaped noise change:',round(10*math.log10(ratio),3),'dB')

Higher-Order Shaping and Stability Boundaries

For idealized Lth-order shaping whose low-frequency NTF behaves approximately as (1−z^(−1))^L, the in-band error power has an OSR exponent of 2L+1 under the familiar white internal-error model. This suggests diminishing in-band noise with increasing order and sampling rate, but the feedback loop may become unstable, quantizer overload may violate linearized assumptions, and out-of-band noise can become very large. High-order modulator design therefore requires state-space stability analysis, signal-range restrictions and nonlinear simulation rather than simply maximizing L.

Noise-shaped conversion is attractive for high-resolution low-bandwidth measurements and digital audio, but converter topology, reference design, decimation filter and data rate determine the real result. For wideband measurement, a SAR or pipeline architecture may offer a better latency and power balance. A high advertised bit count from a sigma-delta ADC should always be paired with passband, effective output data rate, latency and noise specifications. Do not compare a low-rate filtered output directly with a high-speed raw converter without normalizing the measurement context.

Quantizer Nonlinearity, Missing Codes and Error Models

Real code widths are not always uniform. Differential nonlinearity (DNL) measures each transition interval relative to an ideal LSB and can reveal excessively wide or narrow codes; severe errors can cause missing codes. Integral nonlinearity (INL) evaluates the cumulative departure of transition locations from an ideal line under a stated endpoint or best-fit convention. A white quantization-noise approximation does not account for code-transition irregularity in a general, source-independent way. The physical source's path through specific thresholds determines the resulting distortion and bias.

Dither can sometimes reduce the signal dependence of such artifacts after averaging, but it is not a replacement for calibration or a guaranteed repair for a missing-code region. Digital correction tables can improve systematic INL if the transition structure is sufficiently repeatable and characterized. Thermal drift, hysteresis and reference changes limit the persistence of calibration. To understand converter performance, compare a histogram or code-density test with a dynamic sine spectrum; one measures transition geometry while the other measures aggregate waveform fidelity.

Histogram and Code-Density Testing

A code-density test feeds a known smooth distribution—often a carefully generated sine wave or ramp—through a converter and counts how many samples fall into each code. Under ideal assumptions, counts are related to code transition widths and can be used to estimate DNL. A coherent short recording or an inaccurately shaped stimulus can yield misleading code populations. The stimulus must traverse thresholds with sufficient statistical coverage, and the analysis must correct for the chosen stimulus distribution. A sine-wave test naturally spends more time near the peaks than the center; uniform expected counts would be the wrong reference.

A histogram is useful for identifying missing codes, major-carry artifacts, and noisy transitions. It does not directly quantify input-referred RMS noise versus frequency, and a static ramp may miss frequency-dependent distortion. For complete characterization, supplement it with stable DC records, frequency-swept sine tests, carefully controlled clock conditions and a measurement of the reference source. Keep raw histograms and expected distributions with the analysis to support reproduction.

Spectral Measurements and Coherent Sampling

An FFT-based converter test estimates power in the fundamental, harmonic bins, noise bins and other spectral lines. Coherent sampling selects a record that contains an integer number of cycles of the input tone so the ideal tone falls into a discrete Fourier bin without leakage under a rectangular window. For M samples and coherent integer cycle count k, fin = k·fs/M. Choosing k relatively prime to M often helps distribute sampled phases and exercise more code transitions, although practical fixture and input conditions still matter. Quantizer errors may remain correlated with the stimulus, so coherent sampling does not by itself make quantization noise white.

Noncoherent acquisition requires a suitable window and correct accounting for coherent gain and equivalent-noise bandwidth. Otherwise energy spread from a strong fundamental can masquerade as a raised noise floor or a spur. The test should exclude the correct bins around the fundamental and relevant harmonics, then integrate the remaining error over a clearly defined band. A plotted dBFS/bin spectrum is not a calibrated dBFS/Hz density unless bin width and window bandwidth are incorporated.

fs=48000; M=1024; k=37
fin=k*fs/M
assert math.gcd(k,M)==1
assert abs(fin-1734.375)<1e-12
print('Coherent signal frequency:',fin,'Hz')

Frequency-Domain ENOB Measurement Procedure

Begin with a source whose noise and harmonics are substantially below the converter impairment level, and apply an appropriately filtered, near-full-scale sine. Record a documented number of output samples at a stable sample clock and known input frequency. Compute a correctly normalized spectrum, identify the fundamental and harmonics (including aliases), and integrate noise-plus-distortion over the specified bandwidth while applying the prescribed DC exclusion and window corrections. From the ratio of signal power to included error power, calculate SINAD and then ENOB under the selected full-scale convention.

A scientifically useful test repeats the procedure across input level, frequency, temperature, data rate and reference conditions. Every reported ENOB point should retain the associated SINAD and test parameters. For example, “12.1 ENOB at 10 kHz input, −1 dBFS, 1 MS/s, 100 kHz analysis bandwidth, Hann window with correction, 65,536 samples” is substantially more informative than “12.1 effective bits.” The source and test fixture may be part of the measurement limit, especially for very high-resolution devices.

  1. Define span, gain, input band, full-scale tone convention and target environment.
  2. Choose and verify a low-distortion stimulus and clock; establish anti-alias filtering.
  3. Capture steady-state raw output with exact sample count and sample rate.
  4. Normalize spectrum and account for coherence or window equivalent-noise bandwidth.
  5. Separate fundamental, harmonic and remaining noise energy under a written rule.
  6. Calculate SINAD and ENOB; repeat and document uncertainty, calibration and conditions.

Harmonic Aliasing and Correct Bin Classification

For a sinusoidal input frequency fin, the second harmonic occurs at 2fin, the third at 3fin, and so forth before sampling. Harmonics above half the sample rate fold into the observed Nyquist band: the observed frequency can be calculated by reducing each harmonic modulo fs and reflecting frequencies above fs/2. It is a mistake to exclude only integer multiples that appear directly below Nyquist. A third harmonic at 180 kHz in a 200 kS/s sample stream appears at 20 kHz, not at an out-of-band location that can be ignored.

In SINAD computation, harmonic energy must be classified under the chosen measurement definition even when aliased. Its origin is nonlinearity, not random broadband quantization noise. When different harmonics collide into the same FFT bin, source-phase and measurement limitations may affect decomposition, and controlled frequency sweeps help isolate contributors. A particularly revealing test shifts input frequency while holding amplitude: harmonic spurs move predictably relative to the fundamental while many fixed clock and power-supply spurs do not.

def folded_frequency(harmonic_frequency, sample_rate):
    f=harmonic_frequency%sample_rate
    return min(f,sample_rate-f)
assert folded_frequency(180000,200000)==20000
assert folded_frequency(220000,200000)==20000
print('180 kHz harmonic sampled at 200 kS/s ->',folded_frequency(180000,200000),'Hz')

FFT Windowing and Equivalent Noise Bandwidth

A finite record is a multiplication of the continuous measured sequence by a time window. In frequency space this produces convolution with the window's spectrum, so a non-bin-centered strong tone leaks energy across bins. Windowing reduces sidelobes but changes amplitude response and widens the effective noise bandwidth. For a window w[n] of M points, the equivalent noise bandwidth in FFT bins is ENBW_bins = M·Σw[n]²/(Σw[n])². A rectangular window has ENBW 1 bin; a long-record Hann window has approximately 1.5 bins. This distinction matters when converting a plotted spectrum into a spectral density or integrating noise.

Different windows optimize different goals. A flat-top window can provide excellent amplitude accuracy for off-bin tones at the expense of a wider main lobe; a Hann window reduces leakage efficiently for broad spectral assessment; coherent sampling may support a rectangular window for precise tone bin isolation. The intended quantity determines the window, not habit. Always preserve whether the display uses amplitude RMS, power per bin, one-sided PSD or a density integrated over physical hertz.

M=1024
rect=[1.0]*M
hann=[0.5*(1-math.cos(2*math.pi*i/(M-1))) for i in range(M)]
calc_enbw=lambda w: len(w)*sum(v*v for v in w)/(sum(w)**2)
assert calc_enbw(rect)==1
assert abs(calc_enbw(hann)-1.5015)<0.005
print('Hann ENBW in bins:',round(calc_enbw(hann),4))

Input-Referred Noise and Sensor Units

Converter noise stated in volts must be translated into the physical units of the sensor and its gain chain. Suppose a pressure sensor produces 2 mV per kilopascal, and the input-referred RMS noise of the entire acquisition chain is 8 µV. The equivalent measurement noise is 0.004 kPa RMS, before considering sensor calibration and any deterministic disturbance. If a 100-fold analog gain is present, the converter's own input-referred noise must be divided by that gain when referring it back to the sensor, while noise that originated before the gain is not reduced by the gain. Confusing the reference plane can underestimate the true measurement uncertainty by orders of magnitude.

A defensible budget draws the blocks and labels each error contribution at one consistent point—sensor input, ADC pin, or final displayed engineering unit. Temperature-dependent sensitivity and nonlinear transfer conversion may make the propagation coefficient depend on operating point. Input-referred quantization noise is often small compared with a transducer's own noise, in which case selecting another nominal bit may not improve field performance. A useful low-level measurement report includes sensitivity, total bandwidth and a calibration uncertainty as well as RMS noise.

sensitivity_v_per_kpa=0.002
input_referred_rms_v=8e-6
noise_kpa=input_referred_rms_v/sensitivity_v_per_kpa
assert abs(noise_kpa-0.004)<1e-12
print('Input-referred pressure noise:',noise_kpa,'kPa RMS')

Combining Independent Noise Contributions

For independent zero-mean contributions with RMS values σ_1, σ_2, and so forth, the total variance is the sum of variances: σ_total² = Σσ_i². Therefore σ_total = sqrt(Σσ_i²), not the arithmetic sum of RMS noise values. For correlated sources, cross-covariance terms appear; a shared reference fluctuation may move two channels together and does not qualify as independent noise. Deterministic biases such as gain error should not simply be inserted into the same independent-noise sum as though they were zero-mean random processes.

Consider three independent RMS components at the ADC input: quantization 0.29 mV, amplifier noise 0.50 mV, and reference-related broadband noise 0.10 mV. Their combined RMS is approximately 0.587 mV. Reducing the quantization component to nearly zero would still leave approximately 0.510 mV; therefore several additional nominal bits may yield modest total improvement unless the analog stage changes too. An error budget should identify its dominant contributors and quantify potential improvements before selecting hardware.

components_mv=[0.29,0.5,0.1]
total_mv=math.sqrt(sum(v*v for v in components_mv))
assert abs(total_mv-0.58745)<0.002
print('Combined independent RMS:',round(total_mv,4),'mV')

Noise Spectral Density and Bandwidth Scaling

Broadband analog noise is often specified in nV/√Hz and integrated through a filter with equivalent noise bandwidth. If a voltage-noise spectral density e_n is flat across an effective bandwidth B, its output RMS value is approximately e_n√B. A reference voltage noise density of 10 nV/√Hz gives about 1 µV RMS over 10 kHz under that simplified assumption. If the density instead rises at low frequency because of flicker noise, this square-root rule with a single constant density is not accurate. Each contributor should be integrated using its actual spectrum and transfer function.

Noise density in V/√Hz, power spectral density in V²/Hz, and RMS voltage integrated over a specific band are related yet different quantities. An ADC specification of 10 µV RMS can correspond to very different bandwidths across device configurations. To use oversampling productively, one must know whether the limiting noise is broad and white, shaped outside the desired band, correlated at low frequency, or concentrated at supply-related frequencies. The optimum filter may need deliberate notches rather than a broad low-pass characteristic.

density=10e-9; bandwidth=10_000
rms=density*math.sqrt(bandwidth)
assert abs(rms-1e-6)<1e-15
print('Flat input noise density integrated over 10 kHz:',rms,'V RMS')

Flicker Noise, Drift and Long Observation Intervals

Low-frequency noise, often referred to as 1/f or flicker noise over some operating range, does not obey the same integration and averaging rules as independent white samples. Increasing observation time can expose temperature drift, reference aging, correlated thermal changes and slow power-supply variation. A one-second repeatability test may look excellent while an hour-long log of the same nominal input shows systematic movement larger than many LSBs. A device's dynamic ENOB at kilohertz frequencies says little about its long-term DC stability unless the underlying error mechanisms are independently characterized.

A useful validation suite therefore includes short-term RMS noise, observed peak-to-peak noise over a stated record, Allan deviation or other time-scale diagnostics when appropriate, and temperature-cycle checks. Precision sensor systems can require careful thermal design and autozero or chopper techniques to manage low-frequency offset behavior. Quantization theory is necessary for their design but seldom the only limiting factor. Distinguish random repeatability from drift and calibration accuracy throughout the report.

Aperture Jitter as a Resolution Limiter

An input sampled at the wrong instant incurs an amplitude error proportional to its time derivative. For a sine of frequency fin under small, independent RMS sampling-time error σ_t, a common approximate clock-jitter SNR ceiling is SNR_jitter ≈ −20 log10(2π fin σ_t). The frequency appears explicitly: a clock with adequate precision for a 1 kHz sensor may limit a 50 MHz RF input. This error is not ordinary q/√12 quantization noise and is not eliminated simply by using a converter with more bits. Jitter from clock generation and aperture uncertainty must be characterized using consistent RMS and independence assumptions.

For fin = 10 MHz and σ_t = 1 ps, the idealized jitter-limited SNR is about 84 dB. At 100 MHz with the same timing uncertainty it is about 64 dB. The result is a first-order sensitivity approximation for sine sampling, not a stand-alone test of any complete ADC. Phase noise distribution, signal slope, aperture characteristics and frequency-dependent analog distortion also affect actual results. The sibling Converter Clock Jitter and Timing develops the clock-system architecture; this article includes jitter to prevent false attribution to quantization.

def jitter_snr_db(signal_hz, jitter_rms_seconds):
    return -20*math.log10(2*math.pi*signal_hz*jitter_rms_seconds)
assert abs(jitter_snr_db(10e6,1e-12)-84.04)<0.02
assert abs(jitter_snr_db(100e6,1e-12)-64.04)<0.02
print('10 MHz, 1 ps ideal jitter limit:',round(jitter_snr_db(10e6,1e-12),2),'dB')

Distortion, Thermal Noise and Jitter as a SINAD Floor

When distinct impairment powers can be measured on a consistent reference basis, an approximate overall SINAD can be estimated by summing their normalized powers. An 80 dB thermal-noise SNR and a 75 dB distortion-limited ratio are not jointly equivalent to 155 dB. Their reciprocals as linear power ratios must be added. For example, 1/SINAD_linear ≈ 10^(−80/10)+10^(−75/10), yielding roughly 73.8 dB. The result is dominated by the worse limitation. Quantization, jitter, supply coupling and distortion should be treated as separate contributions until the combination step.

This power sum is an approximation when cross terms and correlations can be neglected, and it assumes the metrics share the same fundamental amplitude and integration band. A deterministic harmonic and broadband noise can be summed in power over distinct spectral bins, but phase-coherent same-bin signals can interfere constructively or destructively. A useful error budget keeps the assumptions beside the numbers and reports both overall SINAD and the largest removable contributor. It avoids promising gains that another fixed floor would immediately prevent.

def combined_error_ratio_db(*limits_db):
    return -10*math.log10(sum(10**(-v/10) for v in limits_db))
combined=combined_error_ratio_db(80,75)
assert 73.7<combined<74.0
print('80 and 75 dB independent power limits combine to',round(combined,2),'dB')

Quantization of Digital Coefficients and Intermediate Products

Quantization is not confined to ADCs. In a finite-precision filter, coefficient rounding changes pole and zero locations; multiplying a sample by a coefficient creates an intermediate result that may require rounding again; and an accumulator that is too narrow may overflow. A small coefficient change can have a disproportionate effect near a marginally stable recursive pole. It is therefore common to retain wider internal products, round only at designated boundaries and verify the resulting frequency response. Whether arithmetic is fixed-point or floating-point changes the numerical error model.

For a simple first-order filter y[n] = a·y[n−1] + (1−a)·x[n], quantizing a from 0.999 to 1.000 in a coarse format radically alters the decay time even though the absolute coefficient error is only 0.001. At a=1 the input term vanishes; the state can freeze rather than track new input. This example illustrates why converter bit count cannot predict the behavior of the complete digital chain. Revisit the sibling Fixed-Point and Floating-Point Representation for representational format details.

a_true=0.999
a_coarse=round(a_true,2)
assert a_coarse==1.0
true_response=a_true**1000
coarse_response=a_coarse**1000
assert coarse_response==1.0 and true_response<0.4
print('1000-step state decay:',round(true_response,4),'versus',coarse_response)

Rounding After DSP Operations and Feedback Loops

In feedforward signal processing, unbiased small rounding errors often appear as additional output noise under a suitable model. In a feedback loop, the same quantizer can create limit cycles because the rounded state influences future operations. An IIR filter driven by zero input may continue oscillating among a few values instead of decaying all the way to mathematical zero. A digital controller with a deadband and limited output granularity can produce periodic actuator motion near the setpoint. Such behavior is often deterministic, input-dependent and poorly described by an additive white noise assumption.

Useful countermeasures include wider internal accumulators, rounding policy changes, deliberately chosen deadbands, state reset and stability analysis. Dither can break certain cycles, but might also increase actuator chatter or violate power and safety constraints. The right optimization depends on whether the final user sees an audio waveform, a DC temperature estimate, a motor position or a numerical scientific calculation. Always simulate or test the complete closed loop with the actual code width and timing.

Perceptual and Application-Specific Resolution

Quantization quality is objective-dependent. In digital audio, the distribution of noise across hearing-sensitive frequencies and the presence of low-level harmonic artifacts can matter more than a single integrated RMS figure. In high-dynamic-range imaging, coarse steps may produce visible banding in smooth gradients, while added dither can trade structured bands for a fine-grained texture. In precision metrology, low-frequency drift and uncertainty of calibration standards often dominate perceptual-style spectral shaping. A system designer must identify the output decision the quantizer supports before choosing its quality metric.

An actuator setpoint may require monotonic output to guarantee safe direction of motion, while a sensor used in a threshold alarm may require a specified probability of correct detection rather than a nominal bit depth. An FFT-based radio receiver may be limited by an individual spur near the desired channel. These are not objections to ENOB; they are reasons to use ENOB as one component of a richer performance and acceptance framework. A good test report explicitly links specifications to the intended application.

Numerical Resolution, Display Digits and False Precision

A digital instrument may display 0.000001 units even if its repeatable resolution is only 0.001 units and its calibration uncertainty is 0.01 units. The display is a formatting choice; it does not establish meaningful input information. It can still be useful to retain internal digits for averaging or unit conversion, provided the measurement record includes uncertainty and avoids giving a false impression of absolute accuracy. Similarly, storing an 8-bit sensor sample in a 32-bit integer does not create new sensor information, although wider arithmetic can prevent further loss during subsequent processing.

A sound reporting convention separates raw code, engineering-unit conversion, measurement noise, uncertainty and display precision. For example, report 25.37 °C with ±0.15 °C calibrated uncertainty rather than infer ±0.00001 °C confidence from a five-decimal readout. Do not simply round uncertainty after an algorithm has already made an irreversible decision on uncalibrated data. Preserve provenance so later analysis can recompute results when a sensor is recalibrated.

Workload Cost of Oversampling and Filtering

Oversampling exchanges acquisition rate and computation for in-band information. A 16× oversampling ratio on a system that originally processed 20 kS/s can require 320 kS/s raw acquisition, buffering, filtering and transportation of samples. That may change power, memory bandwidth, real-time deadlines and communications link capacity. A decimation filter reduces the output data rate but does not erase the work that occurred upstream. If the original device is limited by analog source settling or DMA throughput, increasing fs can degrade actual precision or violate a latency budget.

Filter length and architecture determine delay: a linear-phase FIR filter of L taps has nominal group delay (L−1)/2 input samples when implemented conventionally. A multistage decimator can economize computation while meeting stopband rejection. Converter datasheets for sigma-delta parts often present multiple noise-versus-rate options because stronger filtering produces lower output noise at the price of longer settling or output latency. Compare expected in-band noise benefit with absolute timing, energy and fault-recovery requirements before accepting an oversampling proposal.

raw_rate=20_000*16
fir_taps=65
group_delay_samples=(fir_taps-1)/2
group_delay_seconds=group_delay_samples/raw_rate
assert raw_rate==320000
assert abs(group_delay_seconds-0.0001)<1e-15
print('65-tap FIR at 320 kS/s, nominal delay:',group_delay_seconds,'s')

Practical Architecture Selection by Accuracy Objective

Select an acquisition architecture by documenting the measurement's useful bandwidth, maximum input magnitude, permissible latency, required SNR/SINAD, largest tolerable spur and allowable uncertainty after calibration. For a slowly varying bridge sensor, a low-rate precision converter with built-in filtering may be attractive if its DC noise and drift meet requirements. For a high-frequency communication receiver, an ADC with sufficient input bandwidth and high-frequency ENOB may be more appropriate even if its nominal bit count is smaller. For a pulse-capture system, a converter with low output noise but long decimation group delay may fail a real-time detection requirement.

A compact selection matrix should compare not just “bits” but frequency-specific performance, input range, power, data rate, filter latency, reference requirements and recovery after overload. Follow the entire signal path through output processing, because a low-noise ADC attached to an inaccurate reference or an unstable coefficient filter does not produce an accurate measurement system. The optimal tradeoff can be application-specific; the important professional practice is to state the governing constraint and its evidence.

A Complete Converter Error Budget

A credible error budget is organized by reference plane and by statistical or deterministic character. Start with a physical measurement quantity, then list transducer sensitivity and error, analog amplification and gain uncertainty, reference error, quantization step and theoretical baseline, thermal input noise, harmonic distortion, clock timing and downstream numerical rounding. Convert contributors into common units or common power ratios only where mathematically justified. For independent random sources, combine mean-square contributions; for worst-case biases, apply the relevant uncertainty model, sensitivity and correlation assumptions.

Next separate the acceptance metrics: absolute accuracy, short-term repeatability, dynamic SINAD, largest spur, latency and in-band noise. Different tests answer different questions. A useful example is an ADC whose quantization noise is 50 µV RMS but whose input amplifier contributes 400 µV RMS: changing from 12 to 16 nominal bits may leave the total noise almost unchanged. The design team's priority should move to the amplifier or its bandwidth. The article's later investigations make this prioritization explicit in numerical scenarios.

A Step-by-Step Decision Framework

The analysis starts with measurement purpose, then quantization model, then real system impairments. It is tempting to begin from a target number of bits, but that approach hides the physical and statistical prerequisites. In a regulated or safety-sensitive system, one should also maintain the provenance of calibration, measurement uncertainty and monitoring of out-of-range conditions. A deterministic spurious signal may demand a notch or clock change; broadband random error may justify filtering; bias requires calibration or redesign; clipping requires range correction.

  1. Write the signal's physical range, useful bandwidth, expected spectrum and required decision accuracy.
  2. Specify quantizer code convention, step q, overload limits and rounding policy.
  3. Estimate ideal uniform q²/12 error only if its assumptions are plausible.
  4. Establish the actual thermal, reference, distortion, timing and DSP error budget.
  5. Choose metrics deliberately: DC noise-free bits, SINAD-based ENOB, SFDR, or calibrated uncertainty.
  6. Model dither, oversampling, noise shaping, filtering, gain staging and associated latency.
  7. Validate with real inputs, coherent or correctly windowed FFTs and long-term DC tests.
  8. Record configuration, uncertainty, data files and failure boundaries; repeat after relevant changes.

Frequent Analytical Errors and Their Corrections

The most common mistake is to read a converter's nominal resolution as if it guaranteed accuracy or even dynamic ENOB. Another is to quote q/√12 as the error of every possible input while failing to acknowledge deterministic code patterns. A third is to assume high sample rate itself prevents aliasing or reduces all noise without properly placed filters. Another is to average identical quantized samples and claim a new bit of detail. On the measurement side, wrong FFT window normalization, incorrect harmonic alias mapping and inconsistent noise bandwidths can generate convincingly precise but invalid ENOB figures.

The remedies are systematic: write down transfer function and endpoint convention; state signal assumptions before using a white-noise model; provide reference planes and spectral bands; distinguish mean bias, RMS, peak-to-peak and spurious terms; and preserve actual test conditions. Calculating one ideal 6.02N + 1.76 dB figure is a useful initial check, but the final engineering assessment must reconcile it with measured performance and the application's own physical tolerance.

Worked Investigation 1: Quantization of a Precision Sensor

A measurement system covers 0–4.096 V with nominal 12-bit straight binary conversion. Under the 2^N-bin convention, q = 4.096/4096 = 1 mV. The ideal uniform-error RMS is 0.289 mV. Suppose the sensor's sensitivity is 20 mV per unit of measurement; then ideal quantization RMS corresponds to 0.0144 units. This is a useful baseline, but the sensor's calibrated sensitivity is 2% uncertain and the input amplifier adds 0.6 mV RMS in the measurement band. The total independent short-term RMS, combining these last two random sources only, is about 0.666 mV or 0.0333 units.

Decision: substituting a 16-bit ADC reduces ideal q by a factor of sixteen, but the analog amplifier still limits repeatability near 0.6 mV. It will not eliminate the 2% scale uncertainty. Before approving a hardware swap, document the source's actual variation, amplifier noise bandwidth, gain error and reference calibration. A production acceptance test should report mean bias, RMS noise and environmental stability as separate figures.

q=4.096/4096
quant_rms=q/math.sqrt(12)
analog_rms=0.0006
combined=math.hypot(quant_rms,analog_rms)
assert abs(combined-0.000666)<0.000002
print('Noise in sensor units:',round(combined/0.02,5))

Worked Investigation 2: Why a DC Code Does Not Gain Resolution by Repetition

A test engineer observes a perfectly stable signal of 0.23 units on a unit-step quantizer, which outputs code 0 repeatedly. A spreadsheet average of 1,000 samples remains 0, with a 0.23-unit bias; the sample standard deviation is zero. The low displayed variance could misleadingly suggest very high precision even though the measurement conveys no position within the code bin. Now add independent rectangular dither before conversion, spanning a single quantization step, and inspect the average reconstructed value. Under ideal threshold behavior, the upper code occurs 23% of the time, yielding an average near 0.23.

Decision: dithered averaging improves information about the mean by accepting increased instantaneous noise and delayed response. It requires input stability and nonoverload throughout the observation. If the application needs to detect an abrupt event within ten milliseconds, a thousand-sample average may not satisfy the response-time requirement. The measurement should state whether the goal is DC mean estimation, instantaneous amplitude or real-time event timing.

x=0.23
n=10000
noise=[(j+0.5)/n-0.5 for j in range(n)]
out=[quantize_nearest(x+d,1.0) for d in noise]
assert abs(sum(out)/len(out)-x)<1e-12
assert sum(out)==2300
print('Threshold crossing fraction:',sum(out)/len(out))

Worked Investigation 3: An Oversampling Procurement Claim

A vendor proposes that fourfold oversampling gives an extra bit to a 12-bit measurement device. Under the ideal uncorrelated-white-quantization-noise approximation, the in-band SNR improvement is indeed about 6.02 dB, equivalent to one ideal bit. But the installation requires a fixed 2 kHz measurement band; increasing sample rate from 4 kS/s to 16 kS/s raises OSR from 1 to 4 only if the analog path and digital filter meet the model assumptions. If an unfiltered 6 kHz interferer enters the ADC at 16 kS/s, it lies inside the raw Nyquist band but outside the 2 kHz useful band; an adequate digital low-pass stage must reject it before downsampling.

Decision: specify not only the 16 kS/s acquisition rate but filter passband, stopband, transition width, latency and output noise. Verify that current output codes show adequate random threshold variation. If the dominant impairment is a fixed 500 Hz spur in the retained band, fourfold oversampling might not improve its relative power at all. The correct acceptance metric is the measured filtered in-band SNR or SINAD, not the mere presence of a higher clock rate.

fs0=4000;fs1=16000;B=2000
osr0=fs0/(2*B);osr1=fs1/(2*B)
assert (osr0,osr1)==(1.0,4.0)
print('Theoretical extra bits:',ideal_bits_from_osr(osr1/osr0))

Worked Investigation 4: Quantizer Noise Versus a Distortion Spur

A waveform capture system reports a 90 dB broadband-noise-limited SNR but only 72 dB SINAD for a particular input frequency. Assume the sine drive and analysis bandwidth match. Since SINAD includes distortion but the quoted SNR excludes harmonics, a distortion contributor must account for much of the difference. Using an approximate independent power decomposition, the distortion-limited ratio is close to 72.1 dB. Switching to a nominally higher-bit ADC without reducing the spur could leave overall dynamic ENOB near 11.7 bits despite a lower white quantization floor.

Decision: inspect spectral lines and sweep input frequency and level. If a third harmonic moves as expected, investigate front-end linearity and input drive. If a fixed spur remains locked to a switching frequency, investigate supply coupling. Report ENOB and SFDR separately: ENOB reveals aggregate noise-plus-distortion, while SFDR identifies the single strongest spur's magnitude. A filter that attenuates the spur may help only if the required signal band excludes its frequency.

snr_db=90.0;sinad_db=72.0
signal_error=10**(-sinad_db/10)
noise_error=10**(-snr_db/10)
harmonic_only=-10*math.log10(signal_error-noise_error)
assert 72.0<harmonic_only<72.2
print('Inferred distortion-only ratio:',round(harmonic_only,3),'dB')

Worked Investigation 5: Low-Bandwidth Delta-Sigma Measurement

An instrument needs 5 Hz of useful bandwidth and can tolerate several hundred milliseconds of settling. Its candidate converter offers a high modulator clock but a decimated output rate of only 20 readings per second. A noise-shaped architecture may be excellent for the task because out-of-band quantization noise can be filtered strongly. However, its catalog word width does not establish meaningful low-frequency accuracy, and the output filter may need additional time after changing the input channel or waking from sleep. The engineer should obtain the noise-versus-output-rate table and a step-response or filter-settling specification.

Decision: compare measured input-referred RMS and noise-free resolution at 20 samples/s, mains rejection near 50/60 Hz, reference stability and settling latency. If the instrument multiplexes eight sensors every 10 ms, a slow filter may never produce a settled reading after each switch. Higher theoretical oversampling gain cannot substitute for correct temporal behavior. A practical qualification should test channel switching, startup, overload recovery and environmental drift, not only a static short record.

Worked Investigation 6: FFT Analysis with Window Leakage

An engineer captures 1,024 samples at 48 kS/s from a 1.7 kHz sine. The FFT bin spacing is 46.875 Hz; 1.7 kHz corresponds to a noninteger bin coordinate and a rectangular-window spectrum distributes substantial energy into neighboring bins. A naive analysis that counts only the largest bin as signal and all other bins as noise can report a severely depressed SINAD. For a precisely coherent tone of k=37 cycles, the frequency would instead be 1,734.375 Hz and a rectangular-window test could isolate an ideal sine in one positive-frequency bin (with the conjugate negative-frequency component).

Decision: choose coherent acquisition with careful timing and source stability, or use a suitable window with correct coherent-gain and ENBW corrections. Retain enough bins around the fundamental to classify leakage appropriately. Do not declare a converter's ENOB until the test fixture and normalization have been checked; FFT analysis error can easily exceed one nominal bit. Sample count and filter settings belong in the published result.

fs=48000; M=1024; f_in=1700
binwidth=fs/M
coordinate=f_in/binwidth
assert abs(binwidth-46.875)<1e-15
assert abs(coordinate-round(coordinate))>0.1
print('1.7 kHz test tone at bin coordinate:',round(coordinate,4))

Worked Investigation 7: Comparing ADCs for an Audio Chain

An audio engineer considers converter A, nominally 24 bits, with measured SINAD 100 dB at a specified input level and bandwidth; converter B is nominally 20 bits with SINAD 105 dB under the same conditions. The equivalent ENOBs are approximately 16.3 and 17.2 bits, respectively. If these numbers are genuinely comparable, device B has better aggregate dynamic performance despite advertising fewer nominal bits. However, the engineer must also inspect low-level spur behavior, analog input requirements, line-level headroom, reference stability, channel crosstalk and latency.

Decision: do not rank by the register width or a bit-derived marketing phrase. Run matched frequency and level sweeps, compare THD and SFDR, and assess the complete audio chain. A converter with a marginally better single-tone SINAD may still be a poorer choice if its analog input driver, clock interface, power consumption or reconstruction requirements are unsuitable. Dither applied during downstream word-length reduction remains a separate design question.

a=enob_from_sinad(100)
b=enob_from_sinad(105)
assert 16<a<17 and 17<b<18 and b>a
print('Equivalent bits:',round(a,2),'versus',round(b,2))

Worked Investigation 8: System-Level Converter Acceptance Test

A production team requires a 0–10 V sensor channel with ±10 mV calibrated accuracy, less than 1 mV RMS short-term noise at 10 Hz useful bandwidth and no spur above −90 dBc relative to a test tone. An engineer proposes using a nominal 16-bit ADC because its q is around 0.153 mV and its ideal quantization RMS is about 0.044 mV. This meets only the theoretical quantization component. It says nothing about whether the voltage reference stays within the ±10 mV accuracy budget, whether the amplifier noise is small enough, or whether digital switching injects an excessive periodic spur.

Decision: write three independent acceptance tests: calibrated DC points across range and temperature, an RMS noise test with controlled 10 Hz bandwidth and sample duration, and a dynamic tone spectrum with a correctly specified stimulus, sample rate and window. Use stored raw output to reproduce the results. If one of the three fails, the remedy must target its actual cause. “16 bits” is not the acceptance criterion; the specified physical behavior is.

Reproducibility Checklist for Converter Tests

Every numerical claim should be reproducible from a defined signal, transfer convention, data record and analysis procedure. A self-contained report records converter identity and configuration, number of bits, voltage range and reference source, stimulus amplitude and frequency, source distortion, sampling rate and clock conditions, digital filter, record length, window and correction, harmonic-bin rules, noise integration bandwidth and environmental temperature. It also records the scope of any uncertainty statement and whether figures are measured, modeled, or guaranteed by the manufacturer.

Archive the raw code stream when practical and retain analysis scripts with precise library or software versions. A plotted spectrum may look stable while numerical SINAD changes with the set of bins counted as fundamental and harmonics. For precision DC work, preserve time series, averaging interval, calibration files and reference traceability. When improvement is claimed, compare before-and-after tests with the same reference plane and workload. A numerical gain without documented conditions is not an engineering result that another team can audit.

For classroom use, attempt each problem independently, then compare the reasoning with its stated answer.

  1. Question: Under the common 2^N-bin convention, what is the nominal LSB step for an N-bit converter with span FSR? Answer: q = FSR/2^N; other endpoint transfer conventions must be identified separately.
  2. Question: What error bound does an ideal nearest-level quantizer offer away from overload? Answer: The in-range output-minus-input error has magnitude no greater than approximately q/2 under the stated tie and endpoint conventions.
  3. Question: Why does clipping invalidate the half-LSB error guarantee? Answer: The nearest supported output may be far from an out-of-range input, giving potentially many LSBs of error.
  4. Question: Is quantization error inherently random? Answer: No. Fixed input and deterministic quantization create a deterministic output and error; noise modeling needs additional signal assumptions.
  5. Question: Under a uniform symmetric error model, what are the mean and variance? Answer: Mean 0 and variance q²/12, hence RMS q/√12.
  6. Question: What input signal is assumed in the familiar 6.02N + 1.76 dB relationship? Answer: An approximately full-scale sinusoid under an uncorrelated uniform quantization-error model over a specified Nyquist band.
  7. Question: Why does one extra ideal bit correspond to roughly 6 dB better SQNR? Answer: Halving q quarters error variance, multiplying the power SNR by four, giving 10log10(4) ≈ 6.02 dB.
  8. Question: How does a −12 dB lower sine amplitude change SQNR under an unchanged white noise floor? Answer: It reduces SQNR by approximately 12 dB; at very low amplitude the white model may fail.
  9. Question: How are SNR and SINAD distinguished in a dynamic converter test? Answer: SNR usually excludes identified harmonic distortion while SINAD includes both specified noise and distortion relative to the fundamental.
  10. Question: What does SFDR describe that ENOB does not identify? Answer: The largest individual unwanted spectral line relative to the fundamental; ENOB aggregates overall noise and distortion.
  11. Question: A converter has 74 dB measured SINAD under the standard sine convention. Estimate ENOB. Answer: (74−1.76)/6.02 ≈ 12.0 equivalent bits.
  12. Question: Does nominal 24-bit converter output guarantee 24 noise-free DC bits? Answer: No. Actual noise-free resolution depends on observed peak-to-peak noise, bandwidth, duration and other system conditions.
  13. Question: Why can constant repeated digital codes not reveal a sub-LSB offset by averaging? Answer: There is no varying threshold information in identical codes; averaging returns the same code.
  14. Question: What is the purpose of introducing dither before quantization? Answer: It decorrelates or randomizes threshold error so low-level means or spectra behave more predictably; non-subtractive methods add output noise.
  15. Question: What is the basic distinction between subtractive and non-subtractive dither? Answer: Subtractive schemes remove the exact known injected dither after quantization; non-subtractive schemes leave its effect in the output.
  16. Question: When is the standard q²/12 uniform-error model least trustworthy? Answer: Constant DC, coherent small tones, overload, periodic code patterns and strongly nonlinear or improperly calibrated quantizers.
  17. Question: For a baseband of width B and sampling rate fs, define a common OSR. Answer: OSR = fs/(2B), provided B is the intended retained low-pass bandwidth and the definition matches the system.
  18. Question: What ideal SNR processing gain follows from OSR = 16 with white quantization noise? Answer: 10log10(16) ≈ 12.04 dB, equivalent to about two ideal in-band bits.
  19. Question: Why must digital low-pass filtering precede decimation? Answer: Without suppressing frequencies outside the new Nyquist band, downsampling aliases their energy into the retained band.
  20. Question: What is the white-noise variance of an M-point equal-weight average? Answer: σ²/M for uncorrelated equal-variance inputs; correlated samples require covariance terms.
  21. Question: What does first-order noise shaping do at low frequencies? Answer: Its ideal NTF 1−z⁻¹ has a zero at DC and pushes quantization-error power toward higher frequencies.
  22. Question: Under ideal first-order shaping, how does in-band noise vary with large OSR? Answer: Approximately as OSR⁻³ under the conventional simplified model, or roughly 9 dB improvement per doubling.
  23. Question: Why can a higher-order noise-shaping formula fail in hardware? Answer: Loop instability, overload, correlated quantizer errors, analog noise and finite circuit performance violate ideal assumptions.
  24. Question: What is an FFT coherent sampling condition for M samples? Answer: Choose a test tone with an integer number k of cycles in the capture, fin=kfs/M, with practical source and clock stability.
  25. Question: Why is an FFT window’s ENBW important? Answer: It alters the physical noise bandwidth represented by bins; failing to correct it biases spectral-density and noise-power estimates.
  26. Question: What happens to a third harmonic at 180 kHz when sampled at 200 kS/s? Answer: It aliases to 20 kHz inside the observed 0–100 kHz Nyquist band.
  27. Question: What is a first-order small-jitter SNR ceiling for a sine signal? Answer: Approximately −20log10(2πfinσt), with fin the sine frequency and σt the RMS sampling-time error.
  28. Question: Name four distinct items that should accompany a published ENOB result. Answer: Examples: measured SINAD, sine level and frequency, analysis bandwidth, sample rate, window, record length, and reference/full-scale convention.
