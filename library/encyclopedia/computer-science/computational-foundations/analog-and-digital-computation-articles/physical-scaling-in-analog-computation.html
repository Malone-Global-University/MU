Why Physical Scaling Is Part of Computation

An analog computation is conducted by a physical apparatus with finite signal ranges, finite speed, limited gain accuracy, and unavoidable disturbances. The abstract mathematical problem may involve a temperature of thousands of kelvin, a length of nanometers, a probability near zero, or a simulated interval lasting centuries. None of these values should be connected literally to an amplifier input as thousands of volts or interpreted as a requirement for centuries of laboratory run time. Physical scaling specifies the mathematical correspondence between the problem and realizable voltages, currents, mechanical coordinates, or other signals.

Scaling is more than a cosmetic change of axis labels. It changes the coefficients of the equations implemented by gain stages and integrators, the frequencies generated in hardware, the voltage margins at internal nodes, and the way measured results are decoded. A network patched from an otherwise correct mathematical model can give a physically useless answer if its coefficients send a stage into saturation or if a useful state change becomes smaller than the apparatus noise and drift. The engineering problem is to find a faithful signal realization over the intended operating region.

This article develops dimensional analysis, state and time transformations, coefficient realization, range budgeting, calibration, and uncertainty propagation. Its equations are general mappings, not claimed performance specifications for a particular laboratory device. Every example names its idealizations and uses synthetic values so that a reader can reproduce the reasoning before substituting a real instrument's data.

The Three Domains of a Scaling Plan

A useful plan distinguishes (1) the problem domain, where variables have their original dimensions; (2) the normalized mathematical domain, where states are divided by characteristic quantities; and (3) the machine domain, where normalized states become actual physical signals. For example, a physical displacement x in meters becomes z=x/X with no dimension and then a voltage v=Vz in volts. The dimensionless factor z has no voltage unit, even though the circuit uses voltage to represent it.

Time requires the same separation. A model time t in seconds is related to dimensionless time θ=t/T and hardware elapsed time τ=Hθ. The reference time T characterizes model dynamics; H is the physical duration chosen to represent that reference time. When a variable is recovered from an oscilloscope trace, both its amplitude scale and its time scale must be reversed. Retaining this three-domain ledger prevents hidden unit conversions from entering feedback coefficients.

Model:         x_i(t)         [native physical unit]
Normalize:     z_i = x_i/X_i     [dimensionless]
Machine:       v_i = V_i*z_i     [volts]
Model time:    t = T*theta      [model seconds]
Machine time:  tau = H*theta    [hardware seconds]
Consequently: t = alpha*tau, alpha = T/H

Units Are Constraints, Not Decoration

Dimensional analysis checks that terms being added have compatible dimensions and that integration or differentiation supplies the appropriate time unit. If x is displacement in meters, then dx/dt has units meters per second; the derivative cannot be added directly to a force measured in newtons. In m x″ + c x′ + k x = F, mass m has kilograms, damping c has kilograms per second, stiffness k has kilograms per second squared, and each term has force units. Scaling an equation without first checking this homogeneity can conceal a modeling error as an arbitrary circuit gain.

Hardware gains may be pure numerical ratios, but they convert representations of physical quantities whose units still matter. A voltage assigned to velocity needs a stated volts-per-(meter/second) conversion; a voltage assigned to acceleration may need a different factor. The numerical ratio of these signals is not automatically a physical conversion from velocity to acceleration. Write units in the model ledger, use dimensionless ratios in the actual circuit calculation, and maintain a documented inverse conversion for reporting.

# Dimensional check for a mass-spring-damper model:
m = 2.0          # kg
c = 1.0          # kg/s
k = 8.0          # kg/s^2
x = 0.1          # m
vel = -0.2       # m/s
F = 2.0          # kg*m/s^2
restoring = k*x  # newtons
damping = c*vel  # newtons
acc = (F-restoring-damping)/m
assert math.isclose(acc, 0.7)  # m/s^2

Choosing Characteristic State Scales

Select for each state xᵢ a nonzero characteristic magnitude Xᵢ in its original unit. The normalized state zᵢ=xᵢ/Xᵢ should occupy a useful range over the experiment. A characteristic scale may be a known peak bound, a trusted design envelope, an equilibrium-to-transient excursion, or a conservative estimate established by simulation. It need not equal the maximum precisely; in fact, using the maximum possible excursion as the nominal full-scale voltage often leaves too little margin for uncertainty.

Some models have a large static baseline and a small scientifically meaningful deviation. In that case, it may be preferable to represent a deviation from a reference x_ref rather than the absolute value. For a signed state crossing zero, a symmetric bipolar encoding is usually simpler than a one-sided voltage assignment. Scaling must preserve the sign, physical interpretation, and required observable quantities; choosing a convenient voltage range is not permission to discard rare but physically important states.

expected_peak = 2.0      # meters, signed displacement
signal_budget = 7.0     # volts, not the whole hardware rail
volts_per_meter = signal_budget / expected_peak
for x in (-2.0, -0.5, 0.0, 1.25, 2.0):
    v = volts_per_meter*x
    assert abs(v) <= signal_budget
    assert math.isclose(v/volts_per_meter, x)

Nondimensionalization Before Circuit Scaling

Nondimensionalization divides dependent and independent variables by characteristic quantities and groups dimensional parameters into dimensionless combinations. For the mass–spring–damper system, choose x=Xz and t=Tθ. Derivatives transform as x′=(X/T) dz/dθ and x″=(X/T²)d²z/dθ². Dividing m x″+c x′+kx=F by mX/T² yields z″+(cT/m)z′+(kT²/m)z=(T²/(mX))F(Tθ). Primes in this transformed equation refer to θ, not model time t.

Choosing T=√(m/k) normalizes the stiffness coefficient to one and makes cT/m a dimensionless damping measure. Such choices often reveal governing physical ratios and keep hardware coefficients near one, but a particular apparatus may still require additional time scaling and voltage scaling. The mathematical normalization and the machine scaling solve related but different problems: one clarifies the equations, while the other makes a practical representation possible.

m, c, k, X = 2.0, 1.0, 8.0, 0.5
T = math.sqrt(m/k)  # model seconds
stiffness = k*T*T/m
damping = c*T/m
forcing_multiplier = T*T/(m*X)
assert math.isclose(stiffness, 1.0)
assert math.isclose(damping, 0.25)
assert math.isclose(forcing_multiplier, 0.25)

Dimensionless Parameters and Similarity

The normalization process can expose a small number of dimensionless groups that determine the qualitative behavior of a system. For a linear oscillator, a damping ratio such as ζ=c/(2√(mk)) controls whether the homogeneous response is underdamped, critically damped, or overdamped when the standard positivity assumptions hold. Different physical systems with the same normalized coefficients may generate the same normalized trajectory when their appropriately scaled initial conditions and forcing coincide.

This notion of similarity is useful for analog computing: a single hardware patch may model different physical parameter sets by changing scales and interpreting outputs accordingly. But similarity is conditional. It does not excuse changes to nondimensional parameter groups, neglected nonlinear terms, boundary conditions, or input histories. State both the normalized equations and the domain in which they approximate the physical process. Matching one dimensionless parameter does not by itself make two systems equivalent.

Amplitude Scales Versus Voltage Full Scale

Let Vᵢ be the voltage assigned to zᵢ=1. Then the output is vᵢ=Vᵢ zᵢ=(Vᵢ/Xᵢ)xᵢ. The conversion factor sᵢ=Vᵢ/Xᵢ has units volts per model-unit; its inverse recovers xᵢ=vᵢ/sᵢ. It is usually advantageous to choose |Vᵢ| smaller than the guaranteed linear output range of the device, so normal states do not touch an output rail. “Ten-volt computer” is not a guarantee that every output or input is linear all the way to ±10 V.

The selected scale must be valid at every signal port that matters. A summer may temporarily form a larger intermediate term than the final state voltage; a multiplier may accept a narrower input range than an integrator output. If one state needs a small voltage scale because its bound is uncertain, the resulting lower signal-to-noise ratio must be assessed. Record both scale and bound: “1 unit = 3 volts, expected |z|≤1.8” is verifiable in a way that “voltage normalized” is not.

X = 4.0       # native model units
V = 6.0       # volts assigned to z=1
s = V / X
assert math.isclose(s, 1.5)       # volts/model unit
assert math.isclose(s * 3.0, 4.5) # hardware volts
assert math.isclose(4.5/s, 3.0)   # decoded model units

The Hardware Time Mapping

A standard convention is t=ατ, with t the model time, τ the elapsed hardware time, and α>0 measured in model seconds per hardware second. The experiment runs α times as quickly as the modeled physical process in these units: α=5 means five seconds of modeled evolution occur during one second of actual run time. Values below one produce a slower-than-real-time representation. Another text may define its time-scaling factor as the reciprocal; disagreements vanish when the explicit mapping is stated.

Writing model time and hardware time on each plot prevents errors when comparing forced waveforms, bandwidth requirements, and derivative gains. A physical frequency measured in hertz is not automatically the model frequency. If the normalized mathematical time θ is in use, t=Tθ and τ=Hθ imply α=T/H. The independent-variable transformation must apply consistently to every state equation and every time-varying external input.

alpha = 5.0       # modeled seconds per hardware second
for tau in (0.0, 0.2, 1.0, 2.5):
    modeled_t = alpha*tau
    assert math.isclose(modeled_t/alpha,tau)
assert math.isclose(alpha*2.5,12.5)

The Chain-Rule Derivation for Any State Equation

For an original initial-value problem dx/dt=f(t,x,u), choose constant positive scales xᵢ=Xᵢzᵢ, vᵢ=Vᵢzᵢ, and t=ατ. The chain rule gives dzᵢ/dτ=(α/Xᵢ) fᵢ(ατ, Xz, u(ατ)). Multiplying by Vᵢ yields the machine-domain law dvᵢ/dτ=(αVᵢ/Xᵢ) fᵢ(ατ, Xv/V, u(ατ)), where componentwise division reconstructs x from v.

This formula is the central design equation. It determines the actual derivative signal to be produced at each integrator input. The factor α accounts for time reparametrization and Vᵢ/Xᵢ for output amplitude conversion. If an inverting physical integrator is used, its input must produce the negative of the desired derivative, including the integrator's own 1/(RC) conversion. Omitting even one factor generally changes the differential equation rather than merely changing how it is displayed.

Model IVP:            dx_i/dt = f_i(t,x,u)
Mapping:              x_i = (X_i/V_i) * v_i;  t = alpha*tau
Machine derivative:   dv_i/dtau = alpha*(V_i/X_i)*f_i(alpha*tau, x, u)
For ideal inverting RC integrator:
                      V_in,i = -(RC)_i * dv_i/dtau
State reconstruction: x_i(t) = (X_i/V_i)*v_i(t/alpha)

Recovering the Original Solution

A scaled analog trace is not the answer in the original variables until it is decoded. If an oscilloscope records voltage vᵢ(τ), the corresponding modeled state is xᵢ(t)=(Xᵢ/Vᵢ)vᵢ(t/α) for t in the supported run interval. A hardware record that lasts two seconds covers α times two model seconds. If the actual time axis is a sampled or externally triggered coordinate, the offset and triggering delay must also be included.

A subtle pitfall is to decode amplitude correctly but use the hardware derivative as though it were the physical derivative. Since dxᵢ/dt=(Xᵢ/(αVᵢ))dvᵢ/dτ, a derivative inferred from a voltage slope requires both scale factors. Similarly, a frequency measured from the trace must be divided by α to obtain model frequency. Document the inverse transform as part of the experiment, not as an afterthought.

X, V, alpha = 0.5, 4.0, 0.5
v_measured, tau = 0.8, 2.0
x_model = (X/V)*v_measured
t_model = alpha*tau
assert math.isclose(x_model,0.1)
assert math.isclose(t_model,1.0)
measured_slope = -0.8 # V / hardware second
model_derivative = X/(alpha*V)*measured_slope
assert math.isclose(model_derivative,-0.2)

Scaling a Linear State-Space System

Let a linear model be dx/dt=A x+B u(t), with x the state vector. Choose diagonal matrices S=diag(sᵢ) with v=Sx, and R=diag(rⱼ) with w=Ru. With t=ατ, differentiation gives dv/dτ=α(S A S⁻¹)v+α(S B R⁻¹)w(τ). The hardware therefore realizes matrix A_h=αSAS⁻¹ and input matrix B_h=αSBR⁻¹. The inverse of a diagonal scale exists only when each assigned scale is nonzero.

Scaling the state coordinates is a similarity transformation on A, so its eigenvalues are unchanged by state scaling alone. Time scaling multiplies those eigenvalues by α, changing physical execution rates while preserving the qualitative signs of their real parts when α is positive. This is a useful consistency check, but it does not establish that a real amplifier will remain within its bandwidth and output swing while implementing the transformed coefficients. A coefficient matrix is only the first approximation to a physical patch.

# Original A=[[0,1],[-4,-0.5]], S=diag(8,3), alpha=0.5
A = [[0.0,1.0],[-4.0,-0.5]]
s = (8.0,3.0)
alpha = 0.5
Ah = [[alpha*s[i]*A[i][j]/s[j] for j in range(2)] for i in range(2)]
assert math.isclose(Ah[0][1], 4/3)
assert math.isclose(Ah[1][0], -0.75)
assert math.isclose(Ah[1][1], -0.25)
assert math.isclose(Ah[0][0], 0.0)

Reference Offsets and Affine State Scaling

Some states have a large nominal operating point and a small dynamic deviation. In that situation choose vᵢ=sᵢ(xᵢ−x_ref,i) instead of vᵢ=sᵢxᵢ. The recovered state is xᵢ=x_ref,i+vᵢ/sᵢ. Because x_ref is constant here, the derivative still obeys dvᵢ/dτ=αsᵢ fᵢ(ατ,x_ref+v/s,u). The offset changes the right-hand-side function evaluated by the circuit; it cannot be discarded from feedback calculations.

Affine scaling is especially useful for operating-point studies in control engineering and for small perturbations around a large temperature, pressure, or voltage. It may also simplify monitoring by centering the variable near zero. If the reference varies with time, an extra derivative term −α sᵢ dx_ref,i/dt appears. Treating a time-varying baseline as fixed changes the underlying equation, which may give plausible but wrong traces.

reference_temperature = 300.0 # kelvin
volts_per_kelvin = 0.25
for kelvin in (295.0, 300.0, 307.5):
    v = volts_per_kelvin * (kelvin-reference_temperature)
    decoded = reference_temperature + v/volts_per_kelvin
    assert math.isclose(decoded, kelvin)
assert math.isclose(volts_per_kelvin*(307.5-300), 1.875)

Scaling Inputs and Forcing Functions

A physical forcing function usually has dimensions different from any state. Choose an independent input scale Uⱼ and define wⱼ=uⱼ/Uⱼ; then represent the dimensionless input by a voltage with its own voltage factor. The state equation transformation must include both the input's amplitude conversion and its shifted time argument. For a sine input u(t)=F₀ sin(ωt), the physical signal generator must produce the equivalent amplitude at angular frequency αω in hardware time.

Using a state scale for an unrelated input can accidentally assume a physically nonexistent conversion. A force input with magnitude F₀ cannot be fed into a velocity channel without the gain of the modeled dynamics. External input calibration also includes phase reference, trigger alignment, DC offset, and any generator or acquisition transfer function. For multiple forcing channels, each can have its own scale while sharing one globally consistent hardware time transformation.

amplitude_newtons = 10.0
omega_model = 2.0 # rad/model second
alpha = 0.5
def model_force(t): return amplitude_newtons*math.sin(omega_model*t)
def normalized_machine_force(tau): return model_force(alpha*tau)/amplitude_newtons
for tau in (0.0,0.3,0.7,1.2):
    assert math.isclose(normalized_machine_force(tau),math.sin(alpha*omega_model*tau))

Worked Derivation: First-Order Exponential Decay

Consider dx/dt=−kx where k is positive in inverse model seconds. Let v=sx and t=ατ. By the general law, dv/dτ=−αkv. The physical decay rate is αk; the hardware solution is v(τ)=v₀ exp(−αkτ). Decoding the trace at modeled time t gives x(t)=x₀ exp(−kt), exactly as intended in the ideal model. Notice that the amplitude scale s cancels from a homogeneous linear decay coefficient; this is not true for every nonlinear or coupled equation.

For an ideal inverting RC integrator whose output is v, the input must be Vin=RC αk v. If RC=1 hardware second and αk=0.5 per hardware second, the input coefficient is 0.5. A sign inversion accidentally added to the feedback turns stable decay into exponential growth. At the same time, an input coefficient that is correct algebraically can be unrealizable if available resistor ratios or signal headroom cannot support it.

k, alpha, x0, s = 0.4, 2.0, 3.0, 1.5
for tau in (0.0,0.5,2.0,4.0):
    v = s*x0*math.exp(-alpha*k*tau)
    x_decoded = v/s
    assert math.isclose(x_decoded,x0*math.exp(-k*(alpha*tau)))
RC = 1.0
required_vin_factor = RC*alpha*k
assert math.isclose(required_vin_factor,0.8)

Worked Derivation: Driven Relaxation

Consider dx/dt=−k(x−x_eq)+b u(t). If a fixed reference x_eq is encoded as the machine offset and v=s(x−x_eq), the derivative is dv/dτ=−αk v+αs b u(ατ). The equilibrium offset is absent from the derivative only because the machine explicitly represents the deviation from equilibrium. If the output is instead v=sx, a constant term αskx_eq must appear in the summer.

This example demonstrates an important benefit of choosing an offset: a model with a large static equilibrium and a small disturbance can occupy a large fraction of the circuit's usable dynamic range. But it also demonstrates a calibration obligation: if the assumed equilibrium is wrong by Δx, the decoded absolute state is shifted even if the deviation dynamics are otherwise accurate. The reference may be a laboratory reading requiring its own uncertainty and drift allowance.

k, alpha, s, x_eq = 0.3,0.5,2.0,300.0
x, u, b = 302.0,1.2,0.1
model_dx = -k*(x-x_eq)+b*u
v=s*(x-x_eq)
machine_dv = -alpha*k*v + alpha*s*b*u
assert math.isclose(machine_dv,alpha*s*model_dx)

Worked Derivation: Oscillator with Unequal State Scales

For x′=y and y′=−ω²x, suppose vₓ=sₓx and vᵧ=sᵧy. Then dvₓ/dτ=α(sₓ/sᵧ)vᵧ and dvᵧ/dτ=−αω²(sᵧ/sₓ)vₓ. The two cross-gains generally differ because position and velocity have different physical dimensions and may need different signal ranges. Using one shared gain merely because both channels carry volts is a dimensional and computational error.

The product of the magnitudes of the two hardware cross-gains is α²ω². Consequently the ideal oscillation frequency in hardware time is αω, independent of the two amplitude scales. This is a valuable diagnostic: state scaling can reshape the phase ellipse but must not change the decoded model frequency. An observed frequency discrepancy may point to time-scale error, coefficient miscalibration, physical bandwidth, or deviations from the model.

omega,alpha,sx,sy=3.0,0.25,4.0,2.0
cross_xy=alpha*sx/sy
cross_yx=-alpha*omega**2*sy/sx
assert math.isclose(cross_xy,0.5)
assert math.isclose(cross_yx,-1.125)
assert math.isclose(math.sqrt(-cross_xy*cross_yx),alpha*omega)

Worked Derivation: Mass–Spring–Damper with Force

Take m=2 kg, c=1 kg/s, k=8 kg/s², displacement x and velocity y, and force F. Use x=(0.5 m)zₓ, y=(1 m/s)zᵧ, with machine voltages vₓ=(4 V)zₓ and vᵧ=(3 V)zᵧ. Normalize force as w=F/(10 N) and choose α=0.5 model second per hardware second. Therefore sₓ=8 V/m and sᵧ=3 V/(m/s). The machine equations become dvₓ/dτ=(4/3)vᵧ and dvᵧ/dτ=7.5w−0.25vᵧ−0.75vₓ, with derivatives in volts per hardware second.

At x₀=0.1 m, y₀=−0.2 m/s and F₀=2 N, the initial signals are vₓ=0.8 V, vᵧ=−0.6 V and w=0.2. The original acceleration is (2−(−0.2)−0.8)/2=0.7 m/s². Hardware derivatives are dvₓ/dτ=−0.8 V/s and dvᵧ/dτ=1.05 V/s, exactly matching the scaled model. The calculation establishes coefficient consistency, not that the laboratory amplifiers can deliver these derivatives with the required precision.

m,c,k=2.0,1.0,8.0
alpha,sx,sy=0.5,8.0,3.0
x,y,F=0.1,-0.2,2.0
vx,vy,w=sx*x,sy*y,F/10.0
model_acc=(F-c*y-k*x)/m
machine_dx=(4/3)*vy
machine_dy=7.5*w-0.25*vy-0.75*vx
assert math.isclose(model_acc,0.7)
assert math.isclose(machine_dx,-0.8)
assert math.isclose(machine_dy,1.05)
assert math.isclose(machine_dx,alpha*sx*y)
assert math.isclose(machine_dy,alpha*sy*model_acc)

Representing Higher-Order and Multistate Dynamics

A higher-order differential equation is normally reduced to a first-order system before assigning distinct state scales. The state components may have different dimensions; for example, angular position, angular velocity, electrical current, and capacitor voltage can coexist in the same model. For n states, derive all n transformed derivatives, establish the physical or normalized ranges of every variable, and budget gain accuracy for every nonzero coefficient in the state-space realization.

Scaling one state independently changes several coupling coefficients, often in opposing directions. Increasing the voltage assigned to a position state reduces its numerical influence when reconstructed inside another derivative but increases coefficients that multiply that state to drive an output. The full matrix should be checked after every change in scale. A local improvement in a single channel can cause a larger internal sum or a wider coefficient ratio elsewhere.

Nonlinear Systems Require Substitution Before Coefficient Selection

For nonlinear dynamics, the scale factors often survive in products and powers. Consider dx/dt=ax−bx² with v=sx and t=ατ. Substituting x=v/s yields dv/dτ=αa v−(αb/s)v². Unlike the linear decay case, changing amplitude scale s changes the required quadratic coefficient. Implementing the old quadratic gain after a voltage-range adjustment changes the physical dynamics, not just the diagram labels.

A general reliable method is to write the original right-hand side symbolically, substitute each xⱼ=vⱼ/sⱼ and each input's scale, multiply by αsᵢ, and simplify. Then compare the resulting terms to the computing elements' supported operations and ranges. If a multiplier produces a normalized product rather than a literal product in volts squared, its transfer coefficient must also be incorporated. Physical multiplier coefficients cannot be inferred from mathematical exponents alone.

alpha, a, b, s=0.5,0.8,0.1,2.0
x=3.0
v=s*x
model_f=a*x-b*x*x
machine_f=alpha*a*v - (alpha*b/s)*v*v
assert math.isclose(machine_f,alpha*s*model_f)
assert math.isclose(machine_f,1.5)

Cross-Products and Nonlinear Coupling

For a term a x₁x₂ in state equation i, the hardware coefficient after scaling is α sᵢ a/(s₁s₂) times v₁v₂, before accounting for the actual multiplier's physical transfer factor. Each state scale appears with an exponent determined by the term's monomial degree. For a cubic reaction term, changing a state scale by a factor of ten may change the required hardware coefficient by a factor of one hundred or one thousand depending on the state being differentiated.

These relationships show why scaling optimization is a system-level exercise, especially in nonlinear analog computing. A choice that avoids state saturation can drive a product node beyond its permitted input or output range. Conversely, rescaling internal multipliers to remain linear can increase sensitivity to device calibration. Bound not only the state variables but also the products, derivatives, summed intermediate quantities, and initialization excursions.

Scaling Coefficients into Physical Gains

A transformed state equation specifies numerical gains with units consistent with the chosen hardware derivative coordinate. It does not directly specify resistor values or amplifier settings. A normalized summer may implement an input gain g as a resistance ratio, a digitally programmable coefficient, or a transconductance relation; an integrator with time constant RC contributes an additional derivative factor. For an ideal inverting integrator, dv/dτ=−Vin/(RC), so Vin=−RC f_h(v,w) when the desired hardware equation is dv/dτ=f_h.

The minus sign and the integrator's units must be kept explicit. A mathematical derivative coefficient of one per hardware second does not imply a physical summer gain of one if RC differs from one second. A device with limited available coefficients may require rescaling, an additional stage, or a different integrator constant. Coefficient realization should be specified by measured small-signal transfer functions where accuracy matters, not solely by nominal resistor markings.

RC = 0.4   # hardware seconds

assert math.isclose(integrator_input,-0.5) # volts
assert math.isclose(-integrator_input/RC,hardware_derivative)

Integrator Sign, Initialization, and Scale Consistency

An analog integrator has three linked properties that a scaling plan must preserve: the sign of its transfer function, its time constant, and its initial state. Consider an inverting stage whose output represents vᵢ=sᵢxᵢ. The required input is minus RC times the calculated machine-domain derivative, and the initial output must equal sᵢxᵢ(t₀) (or an offset-adjusted equivalent). If the network resets to zero when the original model has a nonzero starting value, its later trajectory is the solution to a different initial-value problem.

Where hardware permits explicit initialize, operate, and hold phases, record the switching sequence and settling time. An initial-condition source may have its own gain and offset error, which enters the state before the dynamical integration begins. For coupled systems, the state vector must be initialized coherently; sequentially releasing channels can allow one variable to evolve while another remains clamped. Scaling correctness includes the starting instant, not merely the derivative equations.

Selecting an Analog Run Duration

Choosing α trades hardware speed against runtime and numerical fidelity. If the modeled interval is 0≤t≤T_end, the required hardware time is 0≤τ≤T_end/α. Faster runs reduce laboratory duration but shift every modeled frequency upward and increase physical voltage derivative requirements. Slower runs relieve bandwidth and slew-rate demands but leave more time for offset-driven drift, leakage, low-frequency disturbance, and environmental variation to accumulate.

There is no universal best α. The choice must respect the slowest and fastest significant modeled modes, the apparatus's usable frequency band, switching transients, and measurement time resolution. A hardware run lasting milliseconds may be impractical to trigger or capture; a run lasting minutes may accumulate unacceptable integrator drift. Document the rejected alternatives and the constraints that determine the final run speed.

modeled_duration = 20.0
for alpha in (0.2, 1.0, 5.0):
    hardware_duration=modeled_duration/alpha
    assert math.isclose(alpha*hardware_duration,modeled_duration)
assert math.isclose(modeled_duration/5.0,4.0)

Frequency Translation and Phase Integrity

If a model contains oscillation at angular frequency ω in radians per model second, the corresponding hardware angular frequency is αω. In hertz, f_h=α f_model when both original and hardware coordinates use seconds. If α=10, an oscillation at 2 model hertz appears at 20 physical hertz. All forcing frequencies, filter corner assumptions, sensor response, and phase references must be transformed using the same convention. A signal generator programmed with the unscaled frequency imposes a different forcing function.

Phase shifts introduced by hardware bandwidth and signal-routing delays are not corrected merely by relabeling the time axis. Delay of Δτ hardware seconds represents αΔτ model seconds. For networks whose output depends sensitively on relative phase, such as coupled oscillators or resonant systems, the combination of device phase lag and scaled frequency can materially alter the response. A bandwidth check should consider gain and phase, not just the presence or absence of visible clipping.

Slew Rate Is a Derivative Constraint

Even if a voltage stays inside the allowed output range, an amplifier can fail to track a signal that changes too quickly. If a modeled state x(t) has derivative bounded by D, its scaled physical voltage is v(τ)=s x(ατ), so |dv/dτ|≤|s|α D. A hardware slew-rate limit SR requires the requested voltage slope to remain below the applicable limit with engineering margin. This links amplitude scaling and time scaling: doubling both multiplies the required voltage slope by four.

For a sinusoidal voltage of peak amplitude A and frequency f_h, maximum slope is 2π f_h A. This is an ideal derivative test, not a full small-signal bandwidth check. Fast transitions, startup switching, square-wave forcing, and nonlinear spikes may demand larger slopes than the dominant sinusoidal frequency suggests. The amplifier's data sheet conditions and actual closed-loop circuit matter when deciding whether the computed margin is adequate.

Bandwidth, Phase Lag, and Closed-Loop Dynamics

An op-amp's gain-bandwidth product and phase characteristics limit the frequencies over which the desired summer or integrator approximates its ideal transfer function. The relevant quantity is the circuit's realized closed-loop response under its chosen gain, load, and compensation, not simply a headline amplifier bandwidth. Scaling the simulation upward in speed pushes model dynamics toward higher physical frequencies and can change both gain and phase errors.

A proper bandwidth budget includes the fundamental dynamics, forcing components, nonlinear harmonics, and the integration circuit's own poles and leakage. When a model intentionally contains fast events or switching, a single cutoff frequency may be an incomplete description. Verify behavior over an application-specific spectrum; where possible characterize each block and then test the connected patch, because feedback can amplify errors that look insignificant in an isolated module.

Dynamic Range and Linear Output Headroom

The permissible output range is determined by the actual power rails, output stage, load, temperature, and specified linearity. Choose a working limit V_work below the physical saturation or accuracy boundary, then require |vᵢ(τ)|≤V_work for the intended trajectories, including credible uncertainty excursions. Reserving headroom reduces the risk that unusual inputs, initial-condition mismatch, or coefficient drift make the output clip and thus invalidate the modeled differential equation.

For symmetric bipolar signals, a common design target is to operate substantially below the maximum swing, but no universal percentage is justified without instrument specifications. A mixed-signal apparatus may require separate input headroom, output headroom, and multiplier ranges. A linear model whose true solution grows unbounded cannot be kept within a finite voltage range for all time by a fixed scale; the run interval or modeling objective must be restricted or the state representation changed in a mathematically accounted way.

working_limit=7.0    # V
rail_limit=10.0       # V, illustrative device
predicted_peak=5.8

assert predicted_peak+transient_allowance <= working_limit
assert working_limit < rail_limit

Intermediate Nodes Can Overload Before States Do

A state-voltage budget alone is incomplete. In a weighted sum, two large opposing terms may cancel at the output while each exceeds the linear range of its source multiplier or summer. For a v₁+b v₂, a conservative bound is |a|V₁,max+|b|V₂,max, which may be much larger than the typical net signal. A gain stage that clips before summation destroys cancellation and generates an invalid derivative even when the final integrator voltage remains small.

Build a signal-envelope table for all internal nodes: signed source states, gain outputs, multiplier inputs, multiplier outputs, summed right-hand sides, integrator outputs, display interfaces, and reset voltages. Use realistic worst-case combinations where model constraints provide them; an impossible independent combination need not dictate the design, but any assumption about correlation must be justified. Saturation diagnostics should monitor nodes throughout the chain, not merely the visible final result.

gain_a,gain_b=3.0,-3.0
v1,v2=4.0,4.0
output=gain_a*v1+gain_b*v2

assert math.isclose(conservative_envelope,24.0)
# Zero output does not imply the two 12-V intermediate terms are feasible.

Noise Floor and Useful Signal Resolution

At the low end of a signal range, random electrical noise, quantization in measurement equipment, interference, offset fluctuations, and drift can obscure small state changes. If a measured state is decoded by x=v/s, an additive voltage error δv becomes a state error δx=δv/s. A larger |s| therefore reduces the state-unit effect of a fixed additive voltage error, assuming the apparatus noise characteristics are otherwise unchanged and there is still sufficient headroom. This assumption must be tested: amplifier noise, input impedance, selected gain, and bandwidth can all change when the circuit is rescaled.

Zero crossings require careful interpretation. A bipolar analog state legitimately passes through zero, so an instantaneous signal-to-noise ratio is undefined or very low there; that does not imply the entire trajectory is unusable. Specify a meaningful feature to recover, such as a peak, phase, average, or threshold crossing, and quantify the uncertainty in that feature. When the important observable is a tiny difference between two large states, centering and differential measurement may help more than simply increasing all gains.

additive_voltage_error=0.003 # volts

assert math.isclose(error_large,0.001)

Signal-to-Noise Ratio and Decibel Conventions

For a consistent definition of signal and noise amplitudes at the same measurement point and impedance, one may write SNR_dB=20 log₁₀(A_signal/A_noise). When SNR is defined from powers, the corresponding expression is 10 log₁₀(P_signal/P_noise). The two formulas agree when the underlying amplitudes and impedances are treated consistently. SNR at a selected frequency or bandwidth is not the same as full-scale dynamic range across all possible operating points.

A numerical example: an RMS signal amplitude of 0.1 V and RMS noise of 0.001 V give an amplitude ratio of 100 and 40 dB SNR. If the signal is amplified by 10 along with the noise introduced before amplification, SNR does not improve; amplification alone is not a cure for source noise. If a gain change makes the measured signal larger while a fixed downstream noise floor dominates, the observed ratio may improve. Trace noise sources through the actual signal chain before attributing an improvement to scaling.

signal_rms=0.1
noise_rms=0.001
snr_db=20*math.log10(signal_rms/noise_rms)
assert math.isclose(snr_db,40.0)
# Equal gain on source signal and source noise preserves their ratio.
assert math.isclose(20*math.log10((10*signal_rms)/(10*noise_rms)),snr_db)

Dynamic Range Versus Nominal Precision

Dynamic range typically describes the ratio between the largest usable signal and some operational noise or minimum detectable level. It does not itself establish absolute accuracy. An analog output can be repeatable within a small voltage band yet systematically biased by a miscalibrated gain. Likewise, a circuit with a large nominal output swing may have inadequate usable dynamic range because the multiplier becomes nonlinear or the measurement interface has insufficient precision.

Do not equate the number of decimal places in a digital capture with the accuracy of the analog computation. A digitizer may report many codes while the preceding analog computation exhibits larger systematic error. Conversely, a high-quality continuous-time signal may be measured poorly by a coarse converter. Full-scale ranges, input-referred noise, gain error, nonlinearity, quantization, and uncertainty should be reported as distinct quantities.

Selecting State Scales from Expected Trajectory Envelopes

A practical first pass selects Xᵢ from a credible peak bound on |xᵢ−x_ref,i| and Vᵢ from the permitted physical working swing. The ratio sᵢ=Vᵢ/Xᵢ then places typical peaks near but not at the working limit. If the observable of interest is much smaller than the state envelope, the best scale may be driven by precision rather than absolute maximum amplitude; multiple measurement channels or a deviation encoding can sometimes separate the large baseline from the small feature.

Trajectory bounds should be established from mathematical inequalities, controlled experiments, or trusted numerical reference solutions over the full input and parameter domain of interest. A single smooth nominal run is weak evidence that no other allowed input causes overload. Include startup, reset, forcing peaks, exceptional initial states, and uncertainty in parameters. When upper bounds are not known, state this limitation and include overload detection or conservative guard margins instead of presenting an arbitrary voltage scale as universally safe.

peak_model=1.8
working_voltage=7.0
reserve_fraction=0.15

assert math.isclose(scale,5.95/1.8)
assert scale*peak_model < working_voltage

Optimizing Time Scale Against Bandwidth and Drift

A useful time-scale choice must satisfy competing constraints. Let f_model,max be the highest model frequency that must be represented accurately, and let f_hw,usable be the experimentally established usable frequency of the implemented chain. A necessary simple frequency condition is α f_model,max ≤ f_hw,usable. A slew-rate condition may impose a still smaller α because |dv/dτ| increases linearly with α. In the opposite direction, a fixed maximum hardware-run duration may require α to exceed T_end/τ_max.

These inequalities produce a feasible interval for α only if the maximum of all lower bounds does not exceed the minimum of all upper bounds. A mathematical solution outside that interval is not realizable using the chosen patch and requested interval without changing some design choice: amplitude scales, device bandwidth, measurement method, system model, or run objective. A feasibility certificate should name its assumptions rather than imply that a pleasing-looking waveform establishes it.

model_band_hz=100.0
hardware_usable_band_hz=300.0

max_hardware_duration=10.0

assert math.isclose(alpha_min,2.0)
assert math.isclose(alpha_max,3.0)
assert alpha_min<=2.5<=alpha_max

Fast and Slow Modes: The Scaling Window

Some models contain both fast and slow phenomena. A stiff system may have fast transients essential to accuracy but slow modes that require a long observation interval. Uniform time scaling multiplies every model frequency by α, so it cannot reduce the ratio between the fastest and slowest significant frequencies. Making α smaller can preserve high-frequency accuracy but lengthens the run; making α larger may shorten the run but challenge amplifier bandwidth and slew rate.

A circuit design confronted with this conflict must acknowledge the physical bandwidth ratio. Options include a better amplifier, different partitioning, justified reduced-order modeling, hybrid computation, separate experiments targeting distinct timescales, or a digital reference solver. It is incorrect to claim that time scaling by itself removes stiffness or makes all timescales equally convenient. A rescaled simulation remains a representation of the original dynamics only while its component transfer functions remain sufficiently faithful.

Amplitude Scaling Is a Multivariable Optimization Problem

In a coupled system, choosing scales affects state headroom, derivative headroom, internal gain magnitudes, noise sensitivity, multiplier operating points, and the conditioning of a measured output. These effects form a constrained optimization problem rather than a single rule to maximize every signal. For a linear matrix A, the transformed coefficient α(sᵢ/sⱼ)Aᵢⱼ makes the tradeoff explicit. Increasing sᵢ lowers decoded additive noise in state i but raises outgoing coefficient magnitudes from state j into state i when j is fixed.

A practical objective might minimize the largest required gain while keeping all relevant signal envelopes within defined headroom and meeting an error target for an observable. Different objectives can produce different valid scalings. One should not optimize a single trajectory if the apparatus must handle a family of parameter values and inputs. Retain an explicit constraint table and show which constraint is active at the chosen design point.

A=[[0.0,1.0],[-9.0,-0.4]]
alpha=0.25
scales=[1.0,3.0]
coeff=[[alpha*scales[i]/scales[j]*A[i][j] for j in range(2)] for i in range(2)]
assert math.isclose(coeff[0][1],1/12)
assert math.isclose(coeff[1][0],-6.75)
# A different state-scale ratio trades these two coupling gains.

Negative Scales and Polarity Conventions

The usual formulas assume positive scale magnitudes with signed model states represented by signed voltages. Some physical machines use an explicit polarity reversal or represent a state as a negative voltage. Such a convention can be expressed by allowing a signed factor sᵢ, but every transformed coefficient and initial condition must then carry its sign consistently. The reference convention must be fixed before wiring or programming the patch.

Keeping scale magnitude and polarity as separate fields is often clearer: state i has magnitude factor |sᵢ| and orientation pᵢ=±1, with vᵢ=pᵢ|sᵢ|xᵢ. This reduces accidental double inversion when the physical integrator already inverts its input. Particularly in coupled feedback, two individually plausible sign choices may change stable damping into positive feedback. A polarity ledger should accompany the magnitude-and-time-scale ledger.

Why Normalized and Calibrated Gains Are Different

An ideal equation may call for a gain of 0.75, while the actual circuit realizes 0.747 at the relevant frequency, temperature, loading, and amplitude. The first number is the design coefficient; the second is a measured transfer coefficient. Their difference changes the implemented differential equation. It is not enough to specify the target coefficient in a patch diagram if the device cannot reliably reach it.

Calibration should establish the physical gain corresponding to each programmed or constructed setting, with a stated uncertainty. Where blocks have frequency-dependent response, a DC gain alone may not characterize their dynamic contribution. If coefficient errors are large compared with the desired model accuracy, recalibrate, choose more favorable scales, or state a wider error bound. An internal display of the nominal setting does not substitute for calibration of the realized circuit.

Resistor Ratios and Integrator Constants

In ideal op-amp circuits, a weighted inverting summer can use resistor ratios to implement gains, and an integrator's 1/(RC) factor converts input voltage into output voltage rate. These quantities have different physical meanings: resistor ratios are dimensionless, whereas RC has time units. If the hardware equation requires a coefficient β per hardware second, an integrator of time constant RC needs an input gain of magnitude RC|β| after accounting for sign.

Real resistors and capacitors have tolerances and may vary with temperature, age, voltage, and frequency. Coefficient calibration and error propagation should therefore consider ratio uncertainty and time-constant uncertainty. Correlated changes can cancel or reinforce one another. It is unjustified to sum nominal percentage tolerances in quadrature as though every source were independent and statistical, or to claim guaranteed worst-case performance from a probabilistic estimate alone.

R=100_000.0  # ohms
Cfarads=10e-6 # farads
RC=R*Cfarads
beta=0.4 # 1 / hardware second
input_gain=RC*beta
assert math.isclose(RC,1.0)
assert math.isclose(input_gain,0.4)

Physical Scaling in Mechanical Analog Devices

Electronic voltages are only one possible representation. In a mechanical differential analyzer, angular shaft displacement may represent a quantity, angular speed may represent a derivative, and gearing or disk radii may impose numerical ratios. A planimeter translates a traced boundary and wheel rotation into an area estimate under its geometrical calibration. Physical scaling therefore also encompasses shaft turns per unit length, cam displacement per function value, lever ratios, torque limits, and physical travel ranges.

The same separation between model and apparatus applies: a mathematical variable x can be assigned to a rotation θₛ=s_rot x, and the inverse mapping recovers x from the measured angle. The speed of shaft rotation represents time-scaled derivatives of x and may be limited by inertia, friction, vibration, and the ability to transmit torque. A mechanical machine can overload by exceeding torque or travel limits even when there is no electronic voltage rail.

Current, Charge, and Other Continuous Signal Carriers

In current-mode analog computing, model variables can be represented by electrical currents instead of voltages. Similar scaling rules apply: i=sᵢx, and di/dτ=αsᵢ dx/dt. Integrating current onto a capacitor produces voltage through its charge relation, which introduces an additional physical constant in the transfer. Optical, fluidic, thermal, and other experimental analog substrates may use yet other physical quantities. The mapping is always specific to the actual sensor, actuator, and device dynamics.

One should not assume that changing carrier automatically improves precision or speed. Current-mode circuits have compliance-voltage and noise constraints; mechanical systems have friction and backlash; thermal systems often have slow dynamics and environmental coupling. Specify the carrier, its conversion factor and calibration, the valid operating interval, and the recovery rule. The mathematical scaling remains general, but the engineering limits are substrate-dependent.

Initial-Condition Calibration and Baseline Error

The initial state of an IVP is a fundamental model input. Under v=s(x−x_ref), the target initialization voltage is v₀=s(x₀−x_ref). If the initialization device introduces an additive voltage error δv₀, the model's starting-state error is δx₀=δv₀/s. A small initial-condition error may remain small in a stable contracting system but can grow greatly in unstable or sensitive systems. Hardware settling and switching can produce additional initial transients not described by the ideal state equation.

A well-documented run records the requested initial value, the calculated physical initialization setting, the measured post-reset voltage where available, and the resulting decoded starting-state uncertainty. In systems with multiple integrators, all initial states must correspond to a common model time. A corrected trace that subtracts an initial offset should not hide an actual departure from the specified IVP; the correction method must be declared and its validity justified.

s=2.5 # volts/model unit
x0=3.0
xref=2.0

measured_v0=requested_v0+0.0125
inferred_x0=xref+measured_v0/s
assert math.isclose(requested_v0,2.5)
assert math.isclose(inferred_x0-x0,0.005)

Offset and Drift Accumulate in Integrators

For an ideal integrator, a constant unintended input offset e produces an output drift Δv=−eΔτ/(RC) over hardware run interval Δτ, ignoring feedback and other nonidealities. Such error may be especially damaging when the desired derivative becomes small. A slow physical simulation leaves longer hardware time for the offset effect to accumulate. This shows why choosing α to reduce bandwidth demand may conflict with a low-frequency drift budget.

An actual integrator may contain bias currents, dielectric leakage, finite DC gain, and parasitic paths that make the drift response differ from the ideal linear formula. Operational-integrator application notes describe these mechanisms and their dependence on circuit design. Estimate first-order offset drift from characterized values, but validate the connected hardware over the same duration and temperature conditions as the proposed computation.

offset_input=0.002  # V
RC=0.5                # hardware seconds
run_seconds=20.0

Temperature Dependence and Environmental Conditions

Electronic gains and time constants can change with temperature, supply fluctuations, humidity, mechanical stress, and component aging. A nominal resistor value may be calibrated at one temperature while a long analog computation runs at another. The resulting parameter drift may affect both the computed state and the very scale factor used to decode it. It is therefore important to distinguish a drifting computing coefficient from a drifting measurement conversion, even when both are expressed as a percentage per degree.

Control the environment when feasible and record the actual conditions. For long campaigns, use reference checks before and after runs to detect gross drift; a frequency-sensitive experiment may need in-situ calibration of gain and phase. Any temperature correction should be based on characterized response rather than an assumed universal linear coefficient. Stable bench temperature is helpful but not proof that every active element shares the same temperature or drift behavior.

Sensitivity of the Model to Coefficient Error

A small implementation error does not always produce a small solution error. For x′=ax with x(0)=x₀, the ideal solution is x(t)=x₀e^(at). Sensitivity to a is ∂x/∂a=t x(t), so a small coefficient deviation δa produces first-order trajectory error approximately t x(t)δa. The absolute error can grow with modeled time, and relative error can increase roughly as |tδa| for sufficiently small |tδa|, even if the coefficient calibration seemed excellent.

For nonlinear or coupled systems, sensitivity can be studied by linearizing the dynamics. A relevant Jacobian, forcing perturbations, and initial-condition uncertainties all influence the trajectory. A robust scaling choice reduces hardware errors at the operating point, but it cannot make an inherently sensitive physical model insensitive. Stability analysis and sensitivity analysis are therefore prerequisites to an honest accuracy claim.

a=-0.2
x0=2.0
t=5.0
delta_a=0.001
x=x0*math.exp(a*t)
first_order_delta=t*x*delta_a
actual=x0*math.exp((a+delta_a)*t)-x
assert abs(actual-first_order_delta)<0.0001

Jacobian-Based Local Error Propagation

For a differentiable state equation x′=f(x,p), perturbing the state by δx and parameters by δp gives a local linear approximation δx′≈Jₓδx+Jₚδp, where Jₓ and Jₚ are Jacobian matrices of f with respect to state and parameters. This equation describes how errors evolve locally along a reference trajectory. The effect of physical gain error can enter through δp; state initialization and noise injection enter through δx and forcing terms.

The Jacobian is local, so it should not be treated as an exact global guarantee for large perturbations or strongly nonlinear trajectories. In an unstable or chaotic region, small errors may grow until the linear approximation loses validity. Report the prediction horizon over which the perturbation analysis remains meaningful, and check it against independent reference solutions. When bounds are needed rather than local estimates, use justified norm inequalities or interval methods.

First-Order Uncertainty for Decoding a Voltage

Suppose a decoded state is x=V_meas/s and both the measured voltage and the calibration factor have uncertainty. The derivatives are ∂x/∂V=1/s and ∂x/∂s=−V/s². Under a first-order linear approximation, independent standard uncertainties u_V and u_s give u_x²≈(u_V/s)²+(V u_s/s²)². If the inputs are correlated, a covariance term must be included; omitting it can materially misstate uncertainty.

This statistical calculation is not a guaranteed worst-case range, nor does it automatically cover bias, model error, clipping, or unrecognized drift. The linear approximation may also be inadequate for large relative scale uncertainty. NIST measurement guidance distinguishes input uncertainty, propagation assumptions, and coverage of a result. Declare whether reported values are standard uncertainties, expanded uncertainties, empirical scatter, or conservative engineering bounds.

measured_v=3.0
scale=1.5
u_voltage=0.010
u_scale=0.015
u_x=math.sqrt((u_voltage/scale)**2+
              (measured_v*u_scale/scale**2)**2)
assert math.isclose(measured_v/scale,2.0)
assert math.isclose(u_x,math.hypot(0.010/1.5,3.0*0.015/2.25))

Correlation Between Scales and Measurements

Measurements may share references, power supplies, sensors, temperature dependencies, or calibration steps. In such cases, uncertainties are correlated. For x=V/s the first-order variance includes −2(V/s³) Cov(V,s). This term can reduce or increase the combined estimate depending on the sign of the covariance. Assuming independence because two quantities appear in different columns of a table is not a technical argument.

Correlation is particularly important when a group of analog channels uses the same voltage reference or resistor network. Common-mode gain drift may cancel in some ratios while increasing uncertainty in absolute reconstructed values. When an uncertainty covariance matrix is available, propagate it through the appropriate Jacobian; otherwise state conservative bounds or identify the covariance as an unresolved limitation rather than reporting false precision.

V,s=3.0,1.5
uV,us=0.01,0.015
cov=0.00005 # V times (V/model unit), illustrative
variance=(uV/s)**2+(V*us/s**2)**2-2*(V/s**3)*cov
assert variance>0
uncertainty=math.sqrt(variance)
assert uncertainty<math.hypot(uV/s,V*us/s**2)

Worst-Case Bounds and Statistical Estimates

If a device specification only provides magnitude limits |δV|≤e_V and |δs|≤e_s, a first-order conservative decoded-state bound is |δx|≲e_V/|s| + |V|e_s/s², assuming e_s is sufficiently small compared with |s|. This bound sums absolute contributions rather than treating them as random independent variables. For rigorous finite errors, interval evaluation of (V±e_V)/(s±e_s) is preferable when denominators stay away from zero.

A statistical standard uncertainty and a worst-case bound answer different questions. A standard uncertainty may describe typical dispersion under a measurement model; it does not certify that every run meets that value. Conversely, worst-case addition may be deliberately conservative for independent random errors. Quality decisions should identify which type of assurance is required by the application, especially when outputs control a physical process or support a safety-critical conclusion.

V,s=3.0,1.5
eV,es=0.01,0.015
first_order=eV/s+abs(V)*es/s**2
low=(V-eV)/(s+es)
high=(V+eV)/(s-es)
assert low < V/s < high
assert first_order>0

Monte Carlo Propagation and Its Assumptions

For complex nonlinear decoding functions or coupled uncertain coefficients, a Monte Carlo calculation can sample plausible input parameters and compute the resulting distribution of reconstructed outputs or trajectories. This requires an explicit probability model for each uncertain input, including dependencies where relevant. Sampling a uniform box is one possible engineering stress test, but it is not automatically a calibrated probability distribution of laboratory errors.

Simulation-based uncertainty studies should disclose random seeds, sample counts, parameter distributions, any truncation or rejection rules, and which model discrepancies are outside the study. In an analog computing experiment, a digital Monte Carlo reference can estimate sensitivity to calibration coefficients, but the existence of simulation code does not validate the assumed error distributions. Cross-check analytical approximations in regimes where their assumptions are known to hold.

import random
rng=random.Random(20261123)
base=2.0
samples=[base + rng.uniform(-0.01,0.01) for _ in range(1000)]
assert len(samples)==1000
assert all(1.99<=z<=2.01 for z in samples)
assert abs(statistics.mean(samples)-base)<0.001

Calibration of Amplitude Scale Factors

Calibration connects nominal scaling factors to measured physical response. A simple gain calibration can apply several known input values, measure outputs, and fit or check an assumed linear relationship v=sx+b. The slope s estimates the conversion gain and b estimates offset. Multiple points help identify nonlinearity and range dependence that a single reference measurement could miss. Positive and negative signals should be checked where the computation uses both polarities.

When the reference source has uncertainty, the fitted gain and offset inherit that uncertainty; ordinary least squares with exact input values may be an incomplete metrological model. The accepted calibration must cover the relevant range and conditions, and it should be repeated when hardware settings change. If the analog apparatus includes programmable coefficient banks, calibration may be indexed by gain setting and operating frequency rather than described by one global scalar.

known_x=[-2.0,-1.0,0.0,1.0,2.0]
observed_v=[-5.95,-2.96,0.03,3.02,6.01]
# Exact illustrative affine points, slope 2.99 and intercept 0.03.
slope=(observed_v[-1]-observed_v[0])/(known_x[-1]-known_x[0])
intercept=observed_v[2]
assert math.isclose(slope,2.99)
assert math.isclose(intercept,0.03)
assert all(math.isclose(slope*x+intercept,v) for x,v in zip(known_x,observed_v))

Timebase and Frequency Calibration

A machine's clock, external trigger, signal generator, or recording timebase may have uncertainty that directly affects the α mapping. If a known input frequency is reproduced in hardware, one can compare its measured frequency with the expected α times the model frequency. Measuring a single period with coarse timing may be less precise than observing many periods, but drift and phase jitter can still matter over long runs. The required standard depends on the application: a qualitative demonstration and a precision phase study have very different needs.

Time calibration should account for offsets between the start of the analog integration and the start of recorded data. Where hardware switches from initialize to operate mode, the first sample may not correspond to the theoretical t=0. The event timing must be documented so phase, damping rates, and derivative estimates are not biased. Report both a multiplicative time-scale estimate and any additive start-time uncertainty.

model_frequency=2.5
alpha_requested=4.0
hardware_frequency_expected=model_frequency*alpha_requested
hardware_frequency_measured=9.98
alpha_inferred=hardware_frequency_measured/model_frequency
assert math.isclose(hardware_frequency_expected,10.0)
assert math.isclose(alpha_inferred,3.992)

Calibration of Signed Coupling Coefficients

Coupling coefficients must be checked with their signs as well as magnitudes. A circuit that realizes an intended coefficient −0.75 as +0.75 can change negative feedback to positive feedback, an error far more consequential than a one-percent gain discrepancy. For each nonzero transformed state-space coefficient, document the intended value, sign, realized transfer function, tolerance, relevant bandwidth, and any interaction with other inputs or loading.

One practical procedure injects a known test level into one path at a time while other paths are held at reference conditions, then measures the response. Such block-level testing cannot prove connected-loop stability on its own; subsequent system-level validation is still required. But it isolates mistakes and makes the eventual trajectory comparison interpretable rather than a search through undocumented resistor ratios and signal inversions.

Calibration Traceability and Uncertainty Records

A useful calibration record identifies the measurement reference, equipment identity, conditions, method, fitted coefficient, uncertainty, and date. Where warranted, traceability links measurements through an unbroken chain of calibrated standards with stated uncertainties. Merely reporting that an instrument was “calibrated” without the relevant range and measurement conditions provides limited evidence for a high-accuracy computational result.

The calibration interval should depend on observed drift, device behavior, and application requirements rather than a universal calendar rule. If a coefficient is adjusted during a run, record the change because it alters the differential equation. When comparing multiple experiments, version both the mathematical scaling worksheet and the hardware calibration table. Otherwise a later operator may decode an old trace with a new coefficient and generate an apparently precise but incorrect physical trajectory.

Separating Model Error from Hardware Error

The mathematical model itself is an approximation to reality. Even a perfectly executed analog computation of m x″+c x′+kx=F may disagree with a physical spring if friction is nonlinear, stiffness changes with temperature, or the observed mass is misestimated. Model error is distinct from implementation error caused by gain inaccuracies, offsets, saturation, noise, or bandwidth limitations. It is also distinct from measurement error introduced when recording the analog output.

A validation plan should therefore compare at least three objects where possible: the original physical phenomenon or trusted experimental data, a mathematically independent digital or analytical reference solution of the stated equations, and the decoded analog output. Agreement between analog and digital solvers supports fidelity to the chosen model, not necessarily fidelity of that model to the natural system. Keeping these comparisons separate improves diagnosis and avoids attributing model inadequacy to scaling.

Reconstruction of Units and the Final Plot

The final plotted horizontal axis should use model time when reporting the modeled process, not unlabeled physical wall-clock seconds. The vertical axis should show the original unit and include the stated conversion from measured voltage or other carrier. If a state is represented relative to an offset, reconstruct the absolute quantity explicitly when that is the reported result. A figure may additionally show the raw hardware trace, but readers must be able to distinguish it from the reconstructed model trajectory.

A good measurement dataset retains raw carrier values, hardware timestamps, the scaling metadata needed for inversion, and the calibrated versions used in analysis. This allows an independent reader to update a calibration or inspect a suspected error without recreating the experiment. Converting and discarding the raw data too early can hide clipping, nonlinearity, drift, and timebase alignment issues that are difficult to identify in model units alone.

raw=[(0.0,0.8),(0.5,0.4),(1.0,0.1)] # tau, V
alpha=0.5; volts_per_meter=8.0
reconstructed=[(alpha*tau,volt/volts_per_meter) for tau,volt in raw]
assert reconstructed==[(0.0,0.1),(0.25,0.05),(0.5,0.0125)]

Reference-Model Validation and Residuals

An independent analytical solution or carefully validated numerical ODE solution can serve as a mathematical reference for checking the analog implementation. Compare at matched model times after applying the inverse time transform. For state i, a residual is rᵢ(t)=x_analog,i(t)−x_reference,i(t). Report absolute and normalized errors with appropriate caution near zero-valued reference quantities. A maximum error over a fixed interval, an RMS residual, a phase discrepancy, and an energy-invariant deviation provide different information.

When comparing a damped oscillator, a small frequency error can accumulate into a substantial phase error even if the immediate amplitude discrepancy is modest. For unstable or chaotic dynamics, pointwise long-term trajectory agreement may be an unrealistic requirement, and statistics or invariant measures may be more meaningful. Validation criteria must be selected from the mathematical and practical objective; one favorable snapshot is not an adequate acceptance test.

reference=[1.0,0.8,0.6,0.4]
measured=[1.01,0.78,0.61,0.39]
residuals=[a-b for a,b in zip(measured,reference)]
rms=math.sqrt(sum(r*r for r in residuals)/len(residuals))
maximum=max(map(abs,residuals))
assert math.isclose(rms,math.sqrt(0.000175))
assert math.isclose(maximum,0.02)

Scaling Feasibility Checklist

Before approving a patch, require a complete mathematical model; units for all states, parameters, and inputs; characteristic scales and any offsets; time mapping and run interval; transformed equations in hardware variables; coefficient and polarity ledger; state and intermediate-node envelope; bandwidth and slew-rate estimates; initialization procedure; and a defined inverse reconstruction. The checklist is deliberately redundant: a missing input scale can be caught by dimensional analysis, by coefficient calculations, or by the final test trace.

Next document calibration evidence, noise and drift assumptions, reference-solution strategy, relevant uncertainty methods, and a criterion for rejecting an invalid run. Do not substitute a nominal circuit setting or a visually smooth oscilloscope line for any of these. A credible design is a set of supported constraints and procedures, not a declaration that an analog machine is accurate in general. Reassess feasibility when the input range, model parameters, or required precision changes.

  1. Write the dimensioned ODEs and initial conditions; check every term for dimensional homogeneity.
  2. Choose state scales, possible reference offsets, input scales, and an explicit model-time/hardware-time mapping.
  3. Derive each machine-domain derivative with the chain rule and determine physical integrator input polarity.
  4. Bound all state and intermediate-node amplitudes, slopes, significant frequencies, and hardware run duration.
  5. Calibrate gains, reference voltages and timebase; record limitations and applicable uncertainties.
  6. Decode the trace into model units and validate it against an independent model reference.

Common Failure Modes and Corrective Actions

Frequent mistakes include reversing the reciprocal time-scale convention, forgetting an amplitude factor in a nonlinear product, applying the model frequency directly to a physical input generator, selecting a state-voltage scale from the smallest rather than the largest plausible excursion, and ignoring internal summer saturation. Each mistake has a distinguishable signature: wrong overall frequency, distorted nonlinear trajectory, forced-response phase mismatch, clipping, or apparently inexplicable drift. Diagnostic tests should isolate coefficients and initial conditions before changing multiple gains at once.

A second class of failures concerns interpretation rather than hardware. Examples include plotting hardware seconds as model seconds, calling fitted coefficients exact, treating a standard uncertainty as a guaranteed bound, and citing a good match with a reference ODE solver as proof that the physical model describes nature. Corrective actions must be tied to the error class. Better voltmeters cannot repair an incorrect differential equation, and better mathematics cannot compensate for an uncharacterized clipped multiplier.

A Practical Scale-Selection Workflow

Begin with an analytical or independently simulated envelope over the expected family of inputs, then choose provisional scales with headroom. Calculate the transformed state-space coefficients, locate extreme internal gain ratios, and select a machine-time factor satisfying the relevant frequency, slope, and observation-duration constraints. If no feasible α exists, first determine which physical constraint is contradictory. Consider adjusting amplitude scales or hardware configuration before altering the model itself.

Next calibrate the realized circuit, run low-risk test inputs, and compare recovered traces against known reference trajectories. Update scale factors if necessary, but regenerate the entire transformed coefficient map whenever a scale changes. Freeze a versioned worksheet for the validated design. Finally report the original-unit results with stated uncertainty, accepted operating envelope, and any out-of-scope model conditions. This workflow is iterative, yet every iteration remains traceable to the original equations.

Applied Investigation 1: Scaling a Temperature Control Experiment

Problem. A model evolves an equipment temperature T in kelvin near 300 K, with disturbances expected to remain within ±8 K. The analog state channel is linear within ±7 V. Design a signed deviation mapping using no more than 6 V of that range. Solution. Choose reference 300 K and scale v=(6/8)(T−300) volts. Temperatures 292 K and 308 K map to −6 V and +6 V, leaving 1 V of working margin. If a measured deviation voltage is +2.25 V, the decoded temperature is 303 K.

Analysis. The chosen representation turns a scientifically relevant 8 K excursion into a large enough signal to observe without putting the 300 K baseline directly into the amplifier. But the actual feedback law may depend on absolute temperature, not just deviation, so substitute T=300+v/0.75 everywhere the model uses T. A baseline calibration error of 0.5 K propagates directly into the inferred absolute temperature. A sensor offset may be a different error source than gain drift and should be recorded separately.

reference,range_k,allocated_v=300.0,8.0,6.0
s=allocated_v/range_k
for T in (292.0,300.0,308.0):
    v=s*(T-reference)
    assert abs(v)<=allocated_v
    assert math.isclose(reference+v/s,T)
assert math.isclose(reference+2.25/s,303.0)

Applied Investigation 2: Reproducing the Full Mechanical Model

Problem. A 2 kg mass, 1 kg/s damping, 8 kg/s² stiffness spring, and external force must be simulated. Choose displacement scale 8 V/m, velocity scale 3 V/(m/s), force scale 10 N per normalized input unit, and α=0.5. Solution. Define vₓ=8x, vᵧ=3y, and w=F/10. The machine's first derivative is dvₓ/dτ=(4/3)vᵧ; its second derivative is dvᵧ/dτ=7.5w−0.25vᵧ−0.75vₓ. With x=0.1 m, y=−0.2 m/s, F=2 N, the hardware starts at (0.8 V,−0.6 V) and derivative (−0.8 V/s,+1.05 V/s).

Investigation. Repeat the calculation for x=0.6 m, y=1.5 m/s, F=−5 N. The scaled state becomes (4.8 V,4.5 V), which fits a hypothetical ±7 V state range. Yet inspect each summation term: force contribution −3.75, damping −1.125, and stiffness −3.6 volts per hardware second. Their total magnitude and the amplifiers' required inputs depend on RC and cannot be declared safe from state range alone. Finally compare the reconstructed derivatives to the original model to audit all time and amplitude conversions.

x,y,F=0.6,1.5,-5.0
vx,vy,w=8*x,3*y,F/10
dvx=(4/3)*vy
dvy=7.5*w-0.25*vy-0.75*vx
model_a=(F-1*y-8*x)/2
assert math.isclose(vx,4.8) and math.isclose(vy,4.5)
assert math.isclose(dvx,0.5*8*y)
assert math.isclose(dvy,0.5*3*model_a)

Applied Investigation 3: Accelerating Exponential Decay

Problem. A first-order model x′=−0.2x must represent 60 model seconds, but laboratory measurements must finish within 12 hardware seconds. Its initial state is x₀=4 units, and a state channel has a ±7 V working limit. Solution. Choose α≥60/12=5. If α=5, the physical decay coefficient is αk=1 per hardware second, so an ideal RC=1 s inverting integrator needs input Vin=+v. Assigning 1.5 V per unit makes v₀=6 V, safely inside the nominal ±7 V working envelope.

Feasibility check. The state magnitude falls from 6 V, and its largest initial slope is 6 V/s. Check that the chosen amplifier and integrator support this slope and a dominant decay rate of 1 s⁻¹; a twelve-second run also permits offset and drift to be measured over a shorter duration than a sixty-second real-time run. The nominal calculation does not establish actual accuracy because circuit frequency response, initial-condition error, and offset are not specified. Record those unknowns before accepting the scale.

k,x0,alpha,s=0.2,4.0,5.0,1.5
required_hardware_seconds=60/alpha
initial_voltage=s*x0
initial_slope=alpha*k*initial_voltage
assert math.isclose(required_hardware_seconds,12.0)
assert math.isclose(initial_voltage,6.0)
assert math.isclose(initial_slope,6.0)
for tau in (0,2,6,12):
    assert math.isclose(s*x0*math.exp(-k*alpha*tau)/s,x0*math.exp(-k*(alpha*tau)))

Applied Investigation 4: A Two-Channel Oscillator

Problem. A model has x′=y and y′=−16x, with position x measured in meters and velocity y in meters per second. Choose sₓ=5 V/m, sᵧ=2 V/(m/s), and α=0.5. Solution. The transformed couplings are dvₓ/dτ=1.25vᵧ and dvᵧ/dτ=−3.2vₓ. The product of their absolute magnitudes equals 4 s⁻², giving a hardware angular frequency of 2 rad/s, exactly half the original model's 4 rad/s.

Interpretation. Plotting vᵧ against vₓ may produce an ellipse whose shape reflects unequal channel scales, not a new physical law. If the measured hardware frequency is instead 2.1 rad/s, the decoded model frequency becomes 4.2 rad/s. That discrepancy could reflect coefficient calibration, integrator time constants, delay, or physical nonlinearity; it should not be removed merely by changing the axis labels. The recovered position and velocity must use their respective conversion factors.

sx,sy,alpha,omega=5.0,2.0,0.5,4.0
c_xy=alpha*sx/sy
c_yx=-alpha*omega**2*sy/sx
hardware_omega=math.sqrt(-c_xy*c_yx)
assert math.isclose(c_xy,1.25)
assert math.isclose(c_yx,-3.2)
assert math.isclose(hardware_omega,2.0)
assert math.isclose(2.1/alpha,4.2)

Applied Investigation 5: Scaling Nonlinear Population Growth

Problem. A logistic model is dx/dt=0.8x−0.02x², with population x in thousands. The modeled carrying capacity is a/b=40 thousand. Set v=0.1x volts and α=0.25. Solution. The hardware equation becomes dv/dτ=0.2v−0.05v², because the quadratic coefficient is αb/s=0.25·0.02/0.1=0.05. The equilibrium x=40 maps to v=4 V, and any initial value above zero evolves toward that equilibrium in the ideal positive-parameter logistic model.

Interpretation. Doubling the voltage scale to s=0.2 would double the equilibrium voltage to 8 V and halve the required quadratic hardware coefficient to 0.025. Such a change could overload a ±7 V channel even as it improves the size of small signals. Recomputing only the initial condition and forgetting the nonlinear gain would create a different carrying capacity. This example illustrates that nonlinear dynamics require systematic symbolic substitution for every changed scale.

a,b,s,alpha=0.8,0.02,0.1,0.25
v_equilibrium=s*a/b
linear_h=alpha*a
quadratic_h=alpha*b/s
assert math.isclose(v_equilibrium,4.0)
assert math.isclose(linear_h,0.2)
assert math.isclose(quadratic_h,0.05)
for x in (0.1,2,12,40):
    v=s*x
    assert math.isclose(linear_h*v-quadratic_h*v*v,alpha*s*(a*x-b*x*x),abs_tol=1e-12)

Applied Investigation 6: An Infeasible Time-Scaling Window

Problem. A model must run for 100 modeled seconds on hardware with a maximum practical record of 20 hardware seconds. Its fastest important frequency is 80 model hertz, and the circuit reliably supports at most 300 hardware hertz. Calculation. Runtime requires α≥100/20=5, while the frequency constraint requires α≤300/80=3.75. No positive α satisfies both, even before considering slew rate or drift.

Decision. Do not claim that a compromise of α=4 is acceptable: it violates the runtime requirement and may violate the bandwidth requirement depending on the exact limits. Options include extending the acquisition record, improving the circuit bandwidth, separating observation intervals with reproducible initial conditions, or using a different computational implementation. The redesign must preserve the intended model and its required outputs. A formal feasibility analysis is valuable precisely because it can demonstrate that the current requirements cannot be met simultaneously.

model_duration=100.0
max_record=20.0
highest_model_hz=80.0
max_hw_hz=300.0
lower=model_duration/max_record
upper=max_hw_hz/highest_model_hz
assert math.isclose(lower,5.0)
assert math.isclose(upper,3.75)
assert lower>upper # infeasible

Applied Investigation 7: Decoded Uncertainty and Calibration Drift

Problem. A sensor reports 3 V for an analog state encoded with s=1.5 V per model unit. Assume independent standard voltage uncertainty u_V=0.01 V and scale-factor uncertainty u_s=0.015 V per model unit. Solution. The decoded state is x=2 units. First-order standard uncertainty is sqrt((0.01/1.5)²+(3×0.015/1.5²)²)≈0.0211 model units. The scale-factor contribution dominates here. This is a local standard-uncertainty calculation under the stated independence and differentiability assumptions.

Extension. If ambient temperature shifts the effective scale to 1.53 while the decoding software still uses 1.50, the same 3 V trace is decoded as 2 instead of its true ratio 3/1.53≈1.961. This is a systematic bias, not random standard uncertainty. Simply combining it in quadrature with u_V may be unjustified. Correct it using a calibrated temperature relationship if reliable, or report a suitable bias allowance or bound separately. The investigation illustrates why scaling factors are measured quantities.

V,s,uV,us=3.0,1.5,0.01,0.015
x=V/s
ux=math.hypot(uV/s,V*us/s**2)
assert math.isclose(x,2.0)
assert math.isclose(ux,0.0210818511,rel_tol=1e-6)
true_after_drift=V/1.53
bias=x-true_after_drift
assert math.isclose(bias,2-3/1.53)

Applied Investigation 8: Preparing a Reproducible Analog Run

Scenario. A research group will compare two analog oscillator patches over five hardware seconds. Their nominal time scale is α=4; displacement scale is 6 V per meter, and velocity scale is 2 V per (meter/second). The group needs both phases, amplitudes, and model-frequency estimates. Required protocol. Specify the dimensioned ODE, all initial states, model interval 0–20 s, expected state envelopes, intended gains and integrator RC values, measured coefficient calibration, temperature, hardware trigger origin, and uncertainty of each recorded channel.

Acceptance. Record unmodified hardware traces and reconstruct model states as x=vₓ/6 and y=vᵧ/2 at t=4τ. Compare peak amplitudes, timing of zero crossings, and phase differences against a numerical reference at matching model times; reject runs with clipped internal nodes or mismatched initialization. Include a separate test for timebase synchronization and zero-input integrator drift. Only after these controls should differences between the two patches be interpreted as differences in their mathematical or physical designs.

alpha,sx,sy=4.0,6.0,2.0
sample=(2.0,3.0,-1.5) # hardware time, position V, velocity V
tau,vx,vy=sample
model_t=alpha*tau

assert math.isclose(x,0.5)
assert math.isclose(y,-0.75)

Study Pathways and Application Priorities

A student first encountering the topic should begin with the model–normalized–machine distinction, dimensional homogeneity, the chain-rule scaling equation, and one first-order decay example. Next, derive the two-channel oscillator equations by hand and verify the signs of its cross-couplings. Then implement the fully dimensioned mass–spring–damper example, including force scaling, initialization, and the inverse transform. This sequence develops the central competence: translating a correct original differential equation into a physically realizable and recoverable analog equation.

An engineer assessing an actual apparatus should prioritize measured signal envelopes, internal-node headroom, coefficient calibration, bandwidth and slew-rate limits, drift, correlated uncertainties, and acceptance criteria. For a researcher interested in scaling optimization, formulate the feasible region in state scales and α and examine model sensitivity before choosing an objective. The article's later references discuss programmatic scaling as a constrained optimization problem; such tools do not remove the need to validate the physical assumptions on which their constraints are based.

Review Questions and Worked Answers

The following questions test both numerical transformation and interpretation. Answers are included because many scaling errors are conceptual rather than algebraic: an experiment can appear qualitatively plausible while using the reciprocal time factor, a mismatched offset, or an uncalibrated coefficient. Students should work the formulas independently before consulting the provided responses.

  1. What is physical scaling in analog computation? It is the documented transformation from dimensioned mathematical variables and model time to realizable physical signals and hardware time, together with an inverse mapping.
  2. Why separate a normalized model from a voltage representation? Normalization clarifies physical dimensions and similarity, while voltage assignment concerns the available apparatus range and calibration; they are not equivalent operations.
  3. If x=Xz and v=Vz, what is the direct state conversion? v=(V/X)x, so x=(X/V)v provided X and V are nonzero.
  4. Under t=ατ, what does α=5 mean? Five model seconds pass per hardware second; a 20-model-second interval needs four hardware seconds.
  5. What is the machine derivative of v=sx when x′=f(t,x)? dv/dτ=αs f(ατ,v/s), with other states and inputs substituted with their own scales.
  6. How do you recover x from a recorded v(τ)? At model time t, evaluate v at τ=t/α and calculate x(t)=v(t/α)/s, adding an offset if affine encoding is used.
  7. How is an input sine-wave frequency transformed? Its hardware frequency becomes α times the model frequency when t=ατ.
  8. What is the ideal inverting RC integrator relation? dv/dτ=−Vin/(RC), so the necessary input is Vin=−RC times the desired derivative.
  9. Why might state scaling change nonlinear coefficients? Products and powers contain multiple state-scale factors; for x′=ax−bx² the hardware quadratic coefficient is αb/s.
  10. What is the transformed linear matrix A for v=Sx? A_h=αSAS⁻¹ for nonzero diagonal scales, and an input matrix B_h=αSBR⁻¹ if w=Ru.
  11. What must be checked when two state variables have different units? Choose separate conversions, derive every cross-gain with their scale ratios, and initialize each channel in its own units.
  12. How does an affine encoding v=s(x−x_ref) affect feedback? Reconstruct x=x_ref+v/s inside the model function; constant offset terms may remain in the transformed derivative.
  13. Why is a saturated intermediate node a problem even when final output is small? Clipping before cancellation changes the computed derivative and destroys equivalence with the mathematical sum.
  14. How is maximum slope of a sinusoidal output estimated? For peak amplitude A and hardware frequency f, the ideal maximum voltage slope is 2πfA.
  15. What happens to hardware bandwidth requirements when α increases? Every modeled frequency shifts upward by α, potentially exceeding the circuit’s usable transfer-function range.
  16. Why can decreasing α increase drift error? A fixed modeled interval requires a longer hardware run, giving offsets and low-frequency nonidealities more time to accumulate.
  17. How does fixed additive voltage noise decode to model units? The additive state error is δx=δv/s; a larger |s| reduces this contribution if the physical noise remains fixed.
  18. Why does boosting all signal-chain voltages not guarantee better SNR? Amplifying source signal and source noise by the same factor leaves their ratio unchanged.
  19. What does a 40 dB amplitude SNR mean? The signal amplitude is one hundred times the noise amplitude under consistent measurement definitions.
  20. Why should state envelopes include more than nominal trajectories? Perturbed inputs, parameter uncertainty, initialization transients, and nonlinear products can exceed nominal extrema.
  21. What is a time-scale feasibility interval? A range of α satisfying lower bounds such as run duration and upper bounds such as frequency and slew-rate limits.
  22. How do you detect a timebase calibration discrepancy? Compare a known model frequency times the requested α with the measured hardware frequency, accounting for phase and trigger origin.
  23. What is the first-order independent uncertainty formula for x=V/s? u_x²≈(u_V/s)²+(V u_s/s²)²; correlated inputs additionally require the appropriate covariance term.
  24. Why is standard uncertainty not a guaranteed worst-case error? It describes dispersion under a probability and linearization model; a deterministic bound requires a different justified calculation.
  25. What is the difference between model error and circuit error? Model error comes from the assumed equations failing to describe the target process; circuit error comes from an imperfect realization of those equations.
  26. What are the initial voltage conditions for two differently scaled states? Set v₁=s₁(x₁,0−x_ref,1) and v₂=s₂(x₂,0−x_ref,2), releasing both consistently at the intended start time.
  27. What validation comparison supports correctness of scaling? Decode the analog trace into original units and compare it at matched model times to an independently justified solution of the same specified equations.
  28. What documentation makes a scaling experiment reproducible? Dimensioned model, scale factors and offsets, α, transformed coefficients, gain calibration, ranges, initialization, hardware conditions, raw traces, reconstruction, and uncertainty assumptions.
