Collection Overview

The Algorithm Analysis unit belongs to the Computational Foundations division of the Computer Science volume of the MGU Encyclopedia. This collection develops the mathematical and empirical methods used to describe how algorithms consume computation time, memory, data transfers, and parallel resources as the size or structure of their inputs changes.

The Algorithm Analysis parent overview remains the integrated introduction. The unit's specialist pages pursue the assumptions, proofs, representative examples, and practical limitations behind its topics. An analysis must identify its resource measure, input-size convention, computational model, and case or probability assumptions; a formula without those conditions can be misleading.

Asymptotic growth is not the same as a measured duration. The notation O(f(n)) expresses an upper bound on growth under suitable definitions, whereas a benchmark reports execution in a particular environment on specified workloads. Likewise, average-case, expected, worst-case, and amortized analysis answer different questions. This directory keeps their boundaries explicit.

Within the MGU hierarchy, this page is the Level 4 unit index. Its 15 linked subjects are designated Level 5 subarticles. A specialist page may eventually justify narrower companion subarticles at Level 6 or below, but no such deeper articles are created by this index.

Core Mathematical Analysis

The basic mathematical vocabulary for bounding runtime, expressing input-dependent costs, evaluating loops, and solving recursive cost models. Each article should define the computational model and assumptions it uses, supply worked analysis, and identify what the claimed bound does not establish.

1. Asymptotic Notation

Upper, lower, and tight asymptotic bounds, precise definitions, and disciplined use of growth-rate notation. Begin with functions of a defined input-size variable and positive constants that witness an asymptotic bound. Distinguish O, Ω, and Θ from exact execution counts; explain why a loose upper bound can be true yet uninformative. Develop common growth classes, logarithm bases, products and sums, and the effect of constants and lower-order terms. Include proof examples and counterexamples, making clear that bounds describe a specified resource under explicit assumptions rather than a universal running time.

2. Worst-Case and Average-Case Analysis

Maximum and distribution-dependent costs over inputs, including explicit assumptions about workloads. Define best, worst, and average cases in terms of inputs of a fixed size, then separate these input cases from asymptotic bound notation. An average-case claim requires a probability distribution over inputs; a worst-case claim does not. Compare favorable, pathological, and representative workloads, distinguish mean cost from a reliability guarantee, and clarify how adversarial or skewed distributions can undermine an otherwise reasonable expectation. Relate the analysis to search, insertion-based sorting, and service workloads without assuming random inputs by default.

3. Loop and Summation Analysis

Constant, linear, triangular, geometric, nested, and irregular iteration costs. Translate loop control flow into an explicit sum of counted operations before simplifying its growth rate. Treat sequential loops, nested bounds that depend on outer indices, early termination, logarithmic step changes, and multiplicative loops. Show the difference between two independent loops whose costs add and genuinely nested loops whose costs multiply or sum. Establish off-by-one conventions and the effect of work inside the loop body. When the code is input-dependent, explain which case is being analyzed.

4. Recurrence Relations in Algorithm Analysis

Recurrences obtained from recursive programs, including base cases and nonrecursive work. Form a recurrence by accounting for the number and sizes of recursive subproblems together with the work outside the recursive calls. State base cases, domain assumptions, rounding behavior, and any hidden work in partitioning or combining. Examine substitution, telescoping, induction, and iteration as solution techniques, using the recursion tree as a diagnostic rather than a substitute for proof. Do not assume that every recursive procedure produces a balanced divide-and-conquer recurrence.

5. Recursion Trees and the Master Theorem

Level-by-level recurrence expansion and the scope and limitations of master-theorem shortcuts. Analyze per-level work, tree height, leaf counts, and the sum across levels for standard divide-and-conquer recurrences. Present the Master Theorem as a tool for recurrences of suitable form, with its case conditions and regularity requirements where applicable. Compare balanced merge-style recursion with recurrences that violate standard preconditions. State what happens when unequal partitions, irregular subproblem counts, or atypical nonrecursive work require a different technique.

Advanced Analysis Methods

Proof tools for operation sequences, probabilistic behavior, and lower bounds that rule out entire classes of algorithms under a specified model. Each article should define the computational model and assumptions it uses, supply worked analysis, and identify what the claimed bound does not establish.

1. Amortized Analysis

Aggregate, accounting, and potential-function proofs for sequences of operations. Analyze the total work of a sequence rather than assigning every operation an isolated worst-case price. Develop the aggregate method, credit-based accounting, and the potential method, identifying what is charged, what is saved, and when credits can be spent. Use dynamically resized arrays or similar examples to demonstrate how occasional expensive updates coexist with constant amortized cost per operation. Distinguish amortized guarantees from averages over random inputs: no input distribution is required for a deterministic amortized bound.

2. Randomized Algorithm Analysis

Expected cost, success probability, probability tails, and randomized algorithm contracts. Identify the source of randomness and whether the algorithm, the inputs, or both are random. Differentiate expected runtime from high-probability runtime bounds and from correctness probability. Introduce linearity of expectation and suitable concentration tools while stating their assumptions. Contrast always-correct randomized methods whose runtime varies with methods that may return an incorrect result with bounded probability. Use randomized quicksort or randomized sampling as examples, explicitly retaining possible worst-case outcomes.

3. Lower Bounds and Decision Trees

Comparison-based information requirements, decision trees, and adversary proofs. State the allowed operations and information model before proving that no algorithm in that model can do better. Develop comparison-tree reasoning for sorting distinct keys and explain why the number of distinguishable outcomes yields an Ω(n log n) worst-case comparison lower bound. Introduce simple adversary arguments and compare a lower bound with the cost achieved by a particular algorithm. Do not misapply comparison lower bounds to specialized integer sorting or stronger computational models.

Algorithms and Data Structures

Worked families that show how analytic methods interact with problem constraints, representations, preprocessing, and maintenance operations. Each article should define the computational model and assumptions it uses, supply worked analysis, and identify what the claimed bound does not establish.

1. Sorting Algorithm Analysis

Comparisons, writes, auxiliary space, stability, adaptivity, and hybrid sorting. Compare the running-time cases and auxiliary-memory requirements of representative comparison sorts, then discuss stable ordering, in-place behavior, adaptability to existing order, and treatment of duplicate keys. Explain why worst-case comparison bounds do not always predict measured performance on a particular platform. Separate algorithmic requirements from implementation choices such as pivot selection, temporary buffers, branch behavior, and hybrid thresholds. Use comparison-based lower bounds carefully and identify when noncomparison models permit different limits.

2. Searching and Indexing Analysis

Linear and binary search, tree and hash indexes, preprocessing costs, and maintenance trade-offs. Compare the cost of finding items in unsorted arrays, sorted arrays, trees, hash tables, and persistent indexes. State preconditions such as sorted order, key distribution, load factor, and balancing. Account for initial construction, memory use, insertions, deletions, and range queries rather than presenting lookup as the only operation. Explain why average expected constant-time hashing does not automatically imply a worst-case constant-time guarantee, and why indexing choices should reflect the query-to-update mix.

3. Graph Algorithm Analysis

Vertex-edge complexity, storage representations, traversals, and graph-dependent costs. Define V and E and specify whether the graph is directed, weighted, sparse, dense, static, or dynamic. Explain how adjacency lists and matrices change the costs of traversals, edge queries, and storage. Analyze breadth-first and depth-first search in a representation-aware way, then introduce shortest-path and spanning-tree cost patterns without replicating full algorithm tutorials. State data-structure assumptions for priority queues and other operations that affect asymptotic graph bounds.

Resource Models and Evaluation

The limits of a simple operation-count model and the practical evidence needed to understand memory pressure, parallelism, and real deployments. Each article should define the computational model and assumptions it uses, supply worked analysis, and identify what the claimed bound does not establish.

1. Time-Space Tradeoffs

Precomputation, memoization, indexing, recomputation, and storage-sensitive cost models. Evaluate when extra storage improves query or update performance, and when recomputation saves memory at an acceptable runtime cost. Compare memoization, caching, preprocessing, and succinct representations using total lifecycle costs instead of one isolated operation. Record which memory is counted: input storage, auxiliary working space, output storage, or persistent index structures. Show why the optimal trade-off depends on workload repetition, capacity constraints, update frequency, and available memory.

2. Cache and External-Memory Complexity

Memory transfers, locality, block size, and cache-aware or cache-oblivious algorithms. Distinguish counting elementary operations from counting memory-block transfers in hierarchical storage models. Define block size and memory capacity assumptions and relate them to sequential scans, random access, sorting, and data-structure layout. Explain spatial and temporal locality, cache-aware versus cache-oblivious approaches, and why asymptotically similar algorithms can differ substantially in I/O traffic. Avoid treating a theoretical transfer count as a direct prediction of wall-clock duration.

3. Parallel Algorithm Analysis

Total work, critical-path span, speedup, scheduling overhead, and scalability limits. Define total work and span as separate properties of a parallel computation, and establish the lower bounds they imply for execution on a fixed number of processors. Explain why parallelizing a program does not reduce its total work automatically, and why synchronization, communication, load imbalance, and scheduling may dominate observed speedup. Introduce parallelism as the work-to-span ratio; distinguish theoretical potential from measured throughput and latency. Keep hardware topology and distributed-systems protocols within their neighboring subjects.

4. Algorithm Benchmarking and Profiling

Controlled experiments, repeatable measurements, scaling tests, and profiler interpretation. Build experiments that specify the algorithm, implementation, input generator, input sizes, machine environment, compiler settings, and timing method. Differentiate warmup and steady-state behavior, wall-clock time and CPU time, and median values and tail latency where they matter. Use profiling to locate dominant costs before modifying an implementation. Examine variability, data skew, memory effects, allocation, and error bars; never infer an asymptotic law from a single benchmark or claim that theoretical complexity alone predicts observed runtime.

Analytical Models and Guarantee Comparison

Different techniques can be combined, but they must not be substituted for one another. For example, an expected-time bound can also be an asymptotic bound; “expected” identifies how randomness is aggregated, while “O” or “Θ” describes the growth of the quantity being bounded.

Important distinction: Big-O alone does not mean “worst case,” and Θ alone does not mean “average case.” Neither notation fixes the case being measured. Similarly, an O(n) amortized statement about a whole sequence does not automatically mean each operation individually costs O(1); the aggregation and length of the sequence must be specified.

Illustrative Algorithm Analysis Scenarios

Example 1 — Deriving a triangular-loop bound

Suppose the outer loop chooses i from 0 through n - 1 and the inner loop performs one constant-time operation for every j with 0 <= j < i. The total operation count is 0 + 1 + ... + (n - 1) = n(n - 1)/2, which is Θ(n²) for positive growing n. Simply seeing two loops is not a proof: the bounds and actual counted work determine the sum.

Use Loop and Summation Analysis alongside Asymptotic Notation to connect the exact sum with a tight growth bound.

Example 2 — Solving a divide-and-conquer recurrence

Consider an idealized routine that splits an input evenly into two halves, solves each half, and performs linear work to combine the results. For powers of two with a constant-size base case, the recurrence is T(n) = 2T(n/2) + cn for positive constant c. Each of the log₂ n non-base levels contributes Θ(n) work, and the leaves add Θ(n). Therefore T(n) = Θ(n log n). The same reasoning cannot be transferred to an unbalanced recursion without checking its structure.

Pair Recurrence Relations in Algorithm Analysis with Recursion Trees and the Master Theorem for the model and proof conditions.

Example 3 — Why an occasional costly append can still be amortized constant time

Assume a dynamic array doubles its capacity whenever it becomes full. Most appends write one element, while an expansion additionally copies the existing elements. Across n appends from a constant initial capacity, copies occur in batches of roughly 1, 2, 4, 8, and so on; the total number of copied elements remains below a constant multiple of n. Total append work is O(n), giving O(1) amortized work per append. An individual append that triggers a resize may still take Θ(n) time.

See Amortized Analysis and Time-Space Tradeoffs for the total-cost proof and its memory implications.

Example 4 — Expected performance is not a worst-case guarantee

A randomized-pivot quicksort implementation can achieve expected Θ(n log n) comparisons when pivot choices satisfy the standard random-selection model, yet some pivot sequences can still produce Θ(n²) comparisons. The algorithm's output may remain correct in both cases. A rigorous claim therefore identifies what is random, whether the reported bound is expected or worst-case, and whether any failure probability concerns correctness or only runtime.

Read Randomized Algorithm Analysis, Sorting Algorithm Analysis, and Worst-Case and Average-Case Analysis as a group.

Example 5 — Why equal asymptotic time does not imply equal measured time

Two algorithms may both perform Θ(n) operations but read memory differently: one traverses a contiguous array, while the other follows scattered pointers. Their cache-miss patterns and observed elapsed times can differ sharply, depending on the dataset and hardware. A responsible comparison records the input layout, machine, memory conditions, measurement protocol, and variation across runs rather than assigning a fixed speed ratio to either strategy.

Connect Cache and External-Memory Complexity, Algorithm Benchmarking and Profiling, and Time-Space Tradeoffs. These examples are constructed instructional scenarios, not measured MGU platform results.

Analysis and Investigation Framework

Start with the problem and the promised guarantee. Choose a specialist technique only after determining which modeling assumptions are actually valid.

Methodological discipline: state the input domain, define resource accounting and the computation model, name the guarantee, justify each bound, and then evaluate whether the model captures the deployment question. Do not infer a mathematical worst-case guarantee from a benchmark, or extrapolate a benchmark to untested machines and workloads.

Suggested Study Pathways

Foundations and first formal proofs: begin with the Algorithm Analysis parent overview, then Asymptotic Notation, Worst-Case and Average-Case Analysis, and Loop and Summation Analysis. Work through exact counts before replacing them with asymptotic notation; explicitly distinguish tight and non-tight bounds.

Recursive algorithms and mathematical methods: study Recurrence Relations in Algorithm Analysis followed by Recursion Trees and the Master Theorem. Practice forming recurrences from the actual algorithm and checking theorem preconditions before claiming a result.

Data structures and application algorithms: follow Sorting Algorithm Analysis, Searching and Indexing Analysis, and Graph Algorithm Analysis. Account for preprocessing, updates, representation, and input characteristics, not lookup or main-loop cost alone.

Advanced theoretical guarantees: read Amortized Analysis, Randomized Algorithm Analysis, and Lower Bounds and Decision Trees. Their common theme is rigor, but their claims concern different objects: sequences, randomized behavior, and the minimum possible cost in an explicit model.

Systems performance and empirical work: pair Time-Space Tradeoffs with Cache and External-Memory Complexity and Parallel Algorithm Analysis; finish with Algorithm Benchmarking and Profiling. Keep model-derived conclusions separate from observed timings and explain any mismatch before generalizing.

Related Encyclopedia Topics

These are neighboring encyclopedia topics, not further children of Algorithm Analysis. Each has a distinct instructional ownership boundary.

  • Complexity Theory — computational problem classes, reductions, and the distinction between algorithm-specific bounds and problem difficulty.
  • Discrete Mathematics — proof techniques, functions, sums, counting, induction, and the mathematics used in rigorous analyses.
  • Recursion — recursive definitions, call structure, base cases, and program behavior; recurrence-based cost proofs belong here in Algorithm Analysis.
  • Data Structures — representations and supported operations; this unit focuses on reasoning about the costs of those operations.
  • High-Performance Computing — practical high-throughput computation and architectural systems; this unit owns the general analysis techniques used to characterize them.

Scope control: the Algorithm Analysis unit concerns proofs, resource models, comparative costs, and measurements of algorithms. Complexity Theory addresses the difficulty of problems and classes of computation; Data Structures and other algorithm-family subjects retain their larger content domains.
