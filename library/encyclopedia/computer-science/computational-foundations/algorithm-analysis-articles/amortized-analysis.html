Definition, Purpose, and Editorial Scope

Amortized analysis proves a bound on the total cost of a sequence of operations, then assigns a representative cost to each operation in that sequence. It is especially useful for data structures that perform inexpensive work most of the time but occasionally execute a costly reorganization. The classic examples are a dynamic array that copies elements during expansion, a binary counter that flips many digits on some increments, and a stack whose multipop removes many entries at once. A costly individual operation and a favorable sequence-wide guarantee can both be true.

This is a deterministic mathematical analysis unless the problem explicitly adds randomness. The reasoning does not assume that an expensive operation is statistically rare, and its conclusions need not depend on any distribution of inputs. Instead the proof associates expensive work with earlier progress, limited resources, or a stored potential that subsequent operations consume.

This Level 5 article belongs to the Algorithm Analysis specialist unit in Computational Foundations. The parent overview supplies the general framework, while Worst-Case and Average-Case Analysis and Randomized Algorithm Analysis handle different types of guarantees. The present chapter owns aggregate, accounting, and potential-method proofs and their engineering interpretation.

Why Occasional Expensive Operations Need a Sequence Model

Suppose a dynamic table has capacity eight and holds eight values. A ninth append allocates a table of capacity sixteen, copies eight values, and inserts the new value. In a simplified unit-cost model, that append has cost nine, while most surrounding appends cost one. Declaring every append to cost nine would overcharge the actual sequence; declaring every append worst-case constant would be false.

A sequence proof can show that a table which doubles upon exhaustion performs fewer than twice as many old-element copies as the number of appended values, up to small boundary effects. With one unit to write each new item, the entire sequence needs at most a constant multiple of the number of appends. This establishes O(1) amortized cost per append, not an O(1) upper bound on the slowest append.

The critical question is whether costly rebuilds can happen too frequently. If each append expands capacity by only one location, repeated copying yields a quadratic total. The amortized bound must follow from the precise implementation policy, not from a label such as “resizable array.”

Operation Sequences, States, and Cost Models

Write an operation sequence as σ = (op₁, op₂, …, opₘ). Let Dᵢ denote the data-structure state after operation i, and let cᵢ be the actual modeled cost of that operation. Its total cost is C(σ) = Σᵢ₌₁ᵐ cᵢ. A useful proof bounds C(σ) for every admissible sequence of length m, possibly as a function of its starting state or the values passed to particular operations.

An actual cost must specify the counted work: assignments, element copies, bit flips, comparisons, pointer changes, allocations, or another abstract resource. Counting a table copy as one unit per moved element differs from treating a bulk hardware transfer as constant cost. Likewise, a hash-table insertion may cost more than its resize work when hashing or collision handling dominates.

Not every operation has the same semantic input. A multipop(k) can request deletion of many items, so its one-operation worst case may grow with k. A sequence-wide bound may still be linear in the number of pushes and API calls if each inserted item can be removed only once. Always state whether m counts API calls, elementary actions, or both.

Formal Amortized-Cost Guarantee

A sequence of m operations has amortized upper bound A per operation when every admissible sequence satisfies Σ cᵢ ≤ A·m + B for a constant B independent of m, under the stated initial-state and cost-model assumptions. More generally, analysts assign values âᵢ so that the sum of actual costs is bounded by the sum of assigned amortized costs plus a permitted fixed initial credit.

The quantifiers are important: the inequality concerns the entire operation sequence, not an isolated operation considered without its history. It may hold for every adversarial order of operations. The advertised amortized cost can differ by operation type: push might receive an amortized charge of two and multipop zero, while both still have nonnegative actual costs.

Some analyses produce a bound involving the starting size n₀, such as O(m + n₀). That is meaningful, but is not the same as O(m) from an empty structure. An article or benchmark that suppresses this starting-state term can claim a stronger guarantee than it has established.

Worst-Case, Average-Case, Expected, and Amortized Costs

Worst-case analysis maximizes cost over permitted inputs or states for one operation or an entire algorithm. Average-case analysis takes an expectation under a specified distribution of inputs. Randomized expected analysis takes an expectation over an algorithm’s random choices. Amortized analysis instead aggregates a sequence of related operations and typically requires no probability distribution.

For example, a dynamic-array append has O(n) worst-case cost at size n when growth copies n elements, but O(1) amortized cost over a sequence of doubling-array appends. A separate distributional model could estimate how often a random append arrives at full capacity, yet that expectation is not needed for the deterministic doubling proof.

These guarantees can coexist. A priority queue can have an amortized bound but fail a hard per-operation latency deadline. An operation can have expected time O(1) yet suffer bad behavior under adversarial collisions. Do not reinterpret one form of claim as another.

The Three Classical Proof Methods

The aggregate method counts or bounds the elementary work performed by a complete sequence, and divides or distributes that total over its operations. The accounting method charges selected operations more than they actually cost and saves the difference as credits that pay for later expensive actions. The potential method defines a numerical potential Φ(D) for each state and uses changes in that value to transform actual cost into amortized cost.

These are not competing notions of complexity. They are three proof representations for a shared goal. Aggregate reasoning often offers the most direct argument; accounting provides an intuitive per-object explanation; potential makes state-based telescoping algebra explicit and can handle more complex sequences.

A strong analysis names the operation contract first, constructs one correct proof, and then—if instructive—demonstrates how another method encodes the same limited supply of expensive events. None of the methods permits assuming that the data structure will encounter convenient inputs.

Aggregate Analysis: Count the Total Work

In aggregate analysis, sum the actual costs directly. For a stack, each pushed item can be popped at most once. If m API calls contain p pushes, the total number of element movements caused by pushes and pops is at most 2p. A multipop can remove many elements in one call, but each removed element is accounted for by one earlier push.

For a doubling table, the copying work is bounded by a geometric series. If a counter increments from zero, the least significant bit flips each time, the next flips every second time, and so on. These problems have different state spaces, yet each has a limited number of costly elementary actions across the sequence.

Aggregate analysis can be all that is required when the output is a uniform bound of O(m) on m operations. It is less expressive when a problem needs separate amortized charges for multiple operation types or a carefully tracked initial state.

Accounting Analysis: Prepaid Work and Credit Invariants

In the accounting method, assign an amortized charge âᵢ to each operation. If the charge exceeds the actual cost, store the surplus as credit on a logically defined object or state. When a later operation costs more than its charge, use previously stored credits to make up the difference. The credit balance must never become negative on any permitted execution.

Consider a binary counter: charge two cost units to each increment. One unit pays for the rightmost zero bit becoming one, and one unit is saved on that newly set bit. Later, when that bit changes from one to zero, its credit pays for the flip. All intermediate one-to-zero flips are prepaid. Consequently a potentially long carry chain does not require borrowing from future operations.

Credits are proof devices, not necessarily machine variables or physical money. They should be associated with well-defined events and obey a conservation argument. If an operation consumes a credit that was never deposited, the accounting proof fails even if the final asymptotic statement happens to be true.

Potential Method: A State-Based Accounting Identity

The potential method chooses Φ(D), a real-valued function of the data-structure state. The amortized cost of the i-th operation is âᵢ = cᵢ + Φ(Dᵢ) − Φ(Dᵢ₋₁). A positive change in potential makes the assigned cost larger than the actual cost and banks work for later use. A negative change releases previously stored potential to help pay for a costly operation.

Summing this definition telescopes: Σ âᵢ = Σ cᵢ + Φ(Dₘ) − Φ(D₀). Therefore Σ cᵢ = Σ âᵢ + Φ(D₀) − Φ(Dₘ). If Φ(Dₘ) ≥ 0 and Φ(D₀) = 0, then the total actual cost is at most the total amortized cost. More generally, a known lower bound on the final potential gives a corresponding adjustment.

The central obligation is not merely to write a plausible Φ. Verify the potential on every reachable state, calculate its exact change for every operation case, and show the initial/final conditions necessary for the desired sequence bound.

Telescoping Proof and Its Boundary Conditions

Expanding the sum gives (c₁ + Φ₁ − Φ₀) + (c₂ + Φ₂ − Φ₁) + … + (cₘ + Φₘ − Φₘ₋₁). All interior potential terms cancel. The remaining difference is Φₘ − Φ₀. Thus potential cannot make expensive work disappear; it redistributes where that work is charged within the sequence.

The common sufficient condition Φ(D₀) = 0 and Φ(Dᵢ) ≥ 0 is convenient, but not logically required. A constant shift of Φ leaves every amortized cost unchanged, while altering the displayed initial and final values together. What matters is a uniform lower bound and a controlled initial value, not the arbitrary choice of a zero point.

If an analysis starts with a large pre-populated structure, it must account for the initial potential or work already invested. If its chosen potential can go to negative infinity, bounded amortized charges alone do not establish a total-cost upper bound. This is a frequent error in informal potential arguments.

How to Design a Useful Potential Function

Begin by identifying what makes a future operation expensive: occupied slots that might be copied, one bits that might be cleared, elements awaiting a transfer between two stacks, or partially completed maintenance. Then propose a potential whose value grows during inexpensive preparatory operations and falls when the expensive event occurs.

For a counter, the number of one bits works because an increment with many flips consumes many existing ones. For a two-stack queue, twice the number of elements on the input stack can pay for a later transfer. For a dynamic table, distance from its next resize threshold suggests a piecewise function of occupancy and capacity.

An attractive potential is simple enough to check symbolically. Test it against edge states: empty structures, full capacity, an operation at a threshold, and alternating changes. If a supposed O(1) amortized bound produces an unbounded expression for one case, revise the function or the implementation policy rather than hiding the exception.

Why a Valid Potential Is a Proof, Not a Prediction

Potential is an analytical invariant attached to a state. It need not correspond to preallocated CPU time, actual battery charge, or a forecast of the next operation. A high potential says that the current state has already paid enough under the accounting interpretation to cover certain future work, subject to the stated operation rules.

The proof can tolerate an adversary choosing operations in an inconvenient order because every allowed operation has a bounded amortized charge. But this protection extends only to costs the model includes. A future operating-system pause, memory allocator anomaly, or hash collision is not automatically covered just because the table-resize component was amortized.

Engineering reports should therefore keep the formal logical claim separate from measured throughput and latency. Amortized complexity is a reasoned bound under a model; a performance guarantee for a product requires additional assumptions and validation.

Dynamic Arrays: Capacity and Resize Semantics

A dynamic array stores n elements in a contiguous backing allocation with capacity c ≥ n. An append that finds n < c writes a new element and increments n. When n = c, a doubling policy allocates capacity 2c (or capacity one if c = 0), copies the n old elements, and writes the new item. The copying is the uncommon expensive step.

For this article, the modeled cost is one for each element write or move. Allocation bookkeeping is treated as O(1) overhead; this abstracts away allocator and operating-system behavior. A full append to a table of size n therefore costs n + 1 modeled units. A non-full append costs one. Actual implementations may batch copies, use allocator optimizations, or apply different growth factors.

The amortized analysis applies to append-only sequences from the stated starting state. Deletion and shrinking introduce additional cases; the grow-only proof should not be used unmodified to describe an implementation that contracts its capacity.

Aggregate Proof for Geometric Array Growth

Suppose a table starts empty and doubles capacities 1, 2, 4, 8, … when full. During m appends, old-element copies occur at the transitions from capacity one to two, two to four, four to eight, and so forth. The total copied elements are bounded by 1 + 2 + 4 + … + 2ᵏ, for the largest completed resize. This geometric sum is less than 2m.

Exactly m newly appended values are written, so the modeled total C(m) is less than 3m for m ≥ 1 under the simple schedule. Dividing by m gives a constant amortized append cost. The bound holds no matter what values are inserted; the only relevant input is the sequence length and the doubling policy.

There is no contradiction when a single append copies many elements. Such an expensive append requires the table to have accumulated enough previous appends to reach capacity. A new copy of size 2ᵏ cannot happen again until many more new values have been inserted.

def doubling_append_costs(m):
    size = capacity = 0
    costs = []
    for _ in range(m):
        copies = 0
        if size == capacity:
            copies = size
            capacity = max(1, 2 * capacity)
        size += 1
        costs.append(1 + copies)
    return costs

for m in (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 100):
    total = sum(doubling_append_costs(m))
    assert total <= 3 * m
assert doubling_append_costs(9) == [1, 2, 3, 1, 5, 1, 1, 1, 9]

A Nine-Append Trace with Exact Costs

Starting with size zero and capacity zero, append one element at a time. The first insertion allocates a slot, the second copies one element, the third copies two, the fifth copies four, and the ninth copies eight. Under the unit-copy model, the nine individual costs are 1, 2, 3, 1, 5, 1, 1, 1, 9. Their total is 24: nine writes and fifteen copies.

The ninth operation is nine times as expensive as an ordinary append, yet the average modeled cost across the entire sequence is 24/9, about 2.67. The precise ratio changes with the stopping point: right after a resize it is relatively high, while later cheap appends reduce it. The worst-case bound for a single append is still linear in current size.

A sequence trace is particularly useful for checking proof conventions. Different tables may report capacity before versus after the append, or include allocation overhead. Those choices change small counts but not the O(1) amortized conclusion under geometric growth.

Accounting Proof for Dynamic-Array Appends

An explicit accounting scheme charges three units per append. One pays for inserting the new element; the remaining two units are saved to pay for copies required at the next table doubling. Immediately after a resize from capacity k to 2k, approximately k new insertions must occur before the next full-table resize. Those insertions deposit about 2k credits, which is more than the 2k old elements that must be copied at the following growth.

To make the initial boundary precise, a proof can maintain credits attached to inserted elements or use a potential function with a formal balance. The informal credit story explains the geometric argument but is not a substitute for checking the first allocation and every transition. An accounting invariant must also forbid spending a credit twice.

The point of the method is distribution of charges, not a claim about the physical runtime. A table with an expensive allocation can still have a bounded abstract amortized copy cost, yet the wall-clock latency of the resize may be unacceptable to a time-critical system.

Potential Proof for Append-Only Doubling Tables

Let n be current size and c current capacity. For a grow-only doubling table after its initial allocation, define Φ(n,c) = max(0, 2n − c), with Φ(0,0) = 0. The potential is nonnegative. A non-resizing append adds one element without changing c, so its actual cost is 1, its potential increases by at most 2, and its amortized cost is at most 3.

For a resize from full capacity c ≥ 1, the old state is (n,c) = (c,c), with potential c. The new state is (c+1,2c), with potential 2. The actual work is c copies plus one write. Thus â = (c+1) + (2−c) = 3. The first append from (0,0) to (1,1) has actual cost 1 and potential change 1, so its amortized cost is 2.

All operation cases have amortized cost at most three and the potential begins at zero and remains nonnegative. Telescoping therefore gives C(m) ≤ 3m. This rigorous proof corresponds to the earlier geometric sum but carries the constant bound locally through the state invariant.

def append_with_potential(m):
    n = c = 0
    phi = 0
    for _ in range(m):
        old_phi = phi
        copies = 0
        if n == c:
            copies = n
            c = max(1, 2 * c)
        n += 1
        phi = max(0, 2 * n - c)
        actual = 1 + copies
        amortized = actual + phi - old_phi
        assert 0 <= phi and amortized <= 3
    return n, c, phi

assert append_with_potential(1000)[0] == 1000

Changing the Growth Factor: Correct Constants Matter

A table may use capacity multiplier α > 1 instead of exact doubling. Geometric growth still yields O(m) total element copying across m appends, provided growth reliably increases capacity by a fixed factor greater than one and rounding is handled so that capacity actually grows. The upper-bound constant depends on α and becomes less favorable as α approaches one.

Larger α reduces copying frequency but may reserve more unused memory. Smaller α reduces slack space but performs more frequent capacity growth. Neither choice universally minimizes elapsed time: allocator behavior, cache effects, element size, copy cost, and memory pressure also matter. The asymptotic result omits those constants by design.

An additive policy such as c ← c + 1 is fundamentally different: after m appends, it may copy 1 + 2 + … + (m−1) = m(m−1)/2 elements, giving Θ(m²) total and Θ(m) amortized cost per append. The distinction is multiplicative versus additive capacity growth, not whether the implementation is called a “vector.”

def copies_with_growth(m, policy):
    size = capacity = copies = 0
    for _ in range(m):
        if size == capacity:
            copies += size
            capacity = (capacity + 1 if policy == 'add-one'
                        else max(1, 2 * capacity))
        size += 1
    return copies

for m in range(1, 80):
    assert copies_with_growth(m, 'add-one') == m * (m - 1) // 2
    assert copies_with_growth(m, 'double') < 2 * m

Why Shrinking After Every Deletion Can Cause Thrashing

Suppose a dynamic array doubles when full but halves as soon as its occupancy falls below half capacity. An adversarial sequence can alternate around a boundary: one deletion triggers contraction, the next insertion exhausts the smaller table and triggers expansion. Each operation can then copy Θ(n) elements even though there was little net change in size.

The append-only proof is invalid for this policy because it relied on many cheap insertions separating successive expensive growth operations. Frequent contraction can erase that spacing. One remedy is hysteresis: use different thresholds for expansion and contraction. A common illustrative rule doubles at full capacity and halves only when occupancy reaches one quarter of capacity.

Under a suitable minimum-capacity convention, the quarter-full rule prevents immediate oscillation between expand and shrink after a one-element change. It makes a constant-amortized guarantee possible for both inserts and deletes, though the actual proof must account for elements copied in both directions.

Piecewise Potential for Growing and Shrinking

Let n denote size and c a positive capacity. With doubling on overflow and halving when a deletion leaves n = c/4, choose the illustrative piecewise potential Φ(n,c) = 2n−c when n ≥ c/2, and Φ(n,c) = c/2−n when n < c/2. Define Φ(0,0)=0 if the implementation permits zero capacity. This function is nonnegative in both regions and measures distance toward either expensive threshold.

A normal insert changes n by one and changes Φ by at most two, so its amortized cost is constant. A full-table insert copies c elements, but Φ falls from c to 2 when capacity doubles, paying the copy cost. During a contraction at n = c/4 after deleting one element, the table copies c/4 surviving elements; potential falls by approximately that same amount, paying the transfer.

Rounding, a fixed minimum capacity, and whether shrink happens before or after deletion must be specified for a complete implementation. The formula as written is exact for the idealized even-capacity threshold cases; sensible discrete variations preserve a constant bound with minor boundary adjustments.

A Constructed Shrink-and-Grow Trace

Imagine a table of capacity sixteen containing five elements. One deletion leaves four elements, exactly one quarter of capacity. A quarter-threshold policy contracts to capacity eight and copies the four survivors. Four more append operations can eventually fill that capacity; the next append triggers expansion. The costly events are separated by substantial occupancy movement rather than adjacent insert-delete reversals.

The trace should be analyzed in two ledgers: actual elementary work, including element movement; and potential saved by operations that move toward full or sparse occupancy. Under the piecewise function, a shrink transfers previously accumulated sparse-side potential into the copying cost. Subsequent insertions accumulate dense-side potential before the next expansion.

This is an example of an implementation invariant guiding the choice of data-structure policy. Hysteresis is not merely a user-interface convenience; it establishes the minimum amount of state change necessary between expensive restructuring events.

Binary Counters: One Increment, Many Bit Flips

Represent a nonnegative integer in binary, least significant bit first. An increment changes each trailing one to zero and then turns the first zero to one. If the counter ends in k ones, an increment flips k + 1 bits, which can be large even though the logical action is only “add one.” A fixed-width counter that wraps at overflow has slightly different final behavior but the same basic amortized conclusion.

Starting from zero, the least significant bit changes on each increment, the next changes every two increments, the third every four, and so forth. Across m increments, bit j flips at most ⌊m/2ʲ⌋ times, up to a constant convention at the top bit. Summing these frequencies gives fewer than 2m total bit flips. Hence the aggregate cost is O(m) and the amortized flips per increment are O(1).

The proof measures the number of bit flips, not the cost of arbitrary-precision arithmetic in a real programming language. A production big integer may use machine words, allocation, or nontrivial arithmetic; those effects require their own implementation-level model.

def increment_bits(bits):
    bits = bits[:]   # least-significant bit first
    i = 0
    while i < len(bits) and bits[i] == 1:
        bits[i] = 0
        i += 1
    if i == len(bits):
        bits.append(1)
    else:
        bits[i] = 1
    return bits, i + 1

bits = []
flips = []
for _ in range(64):
    bits, cost = increment_bits(bits)
    flips.append(cost)
assert sum(flips) < 2 * len(flips)
assert flips[:8] == [1, 2, 1, 3, 1, 2, 1, 4]

Binary Counter: Accounting Proof with One-Bit Credits

Charge two units to every increment. When the increment turns its first zero to one, pay one unit for that bit flip and attach one credit to the newly set bit. Any trailing one bits that change to zero during the increment each carry their own saved credits from earlier increments, and those credits pay for clearing them. The balance never goes negative.

This argument shows why the carry chain is affordable. To create k trailing ones, the sequence had to perform work that deposited k distinct credits. A later increment clears them in one burst, but it spends only credit already earned. If the binary representation grows, the newly created high bit receives its own credit as well.

The interpretation is exact under the unit-bit-flip model. It is not an amortized bound for every operation on the counter: resetting all bits in one step, adding an arbitrary integer, or shifting every word might demand a different proof.

Binary Counter: Popcount Potential Proof

Let Φ(D) equal the number of one bits in the current binary representation. An increment with k trailing ones flips k of them to zero and one zero to one, so its actual cost is k+1 and the potential change is 1−k. Its amortized cost is therefore (k+1)+(1−k)=2. The potential begins at zero and never becomes negative.

Telescoping gives fewer than or equal to 2m bit flips across any m increments, with a constant-bound adjustment only for alternative counter conventions. The accounting and potential methods agree: the one-bit credits in the accounting method correspond to the value of Φ in the state method.

In a fixed-width counter at wraparound, an all-ones value may flip every one to zero without setting a new bit. The actual cost is k, potential change −k, and amortized cost zero for that operation; the upper bound still holds. State which representation and overflow semantics are being analyzed.

def potential_counter_trace(steps):
    bits = []
    old_phi = 0
    for _ in range(steps):
        bits, actual = increment_bits(bits)
        phi = sum(bits)
        amortized = actual + phi - old_phi
        assert amortized == 2 and phi >= 0
        old_phi = phi

# Defined independently so this example can run by itself.
def increment_bits(bits):
    bits = bits[:]
    i = 0
    while i < len(bits) and bits[i]:
        bits[i] = 0
        i += 1
    if i == len(bits):
        bits.append(1)
    else:
        bits[i] = 1
    return bits, i + 1

potential_counter_trace(200)

Counter Examples and Boundary Costs

Incrementing binary 0111 (read in ordinary most-significant-bit-first notation) produces 1000 and flips four bits. The preceding increments that created the three trailing ones also prepared enough potential to pay for their removal. In contrast, incrementing 1000 to 1001 changes just the last bit. The single-operation costs vary, but their sum is tightly controlled.

A short exact trace of eight increments from zero has flip counts 1, 2, 1, 3, 1, 2, 1, 4, totaling fifteen flips. The average is 15/8, approaching but never exceeding two under the unbounded representation and this cost model. The unusual four-flip event does not falsify the amortized bound.

When the counter is stored in fixed-size machine words, incrementing one word may be constant time for typical processors, but a multiword carry still may touch several words. The proof can be adapted to count word operations, provided it explicitly accounts for how words are represented and extended.

Stack with Multipop: The Operational Contract

A stack offers push(x) and pop(), plus a multipop(k) operation that removes up to k available items. Define its actual cost as the number of elementary pushes and pops performed; a multipop that removes t entries costs t. On a single call, multipop can cost Θ(n) if the stack contains n entries.

Across an operation sequence beginning with an empty stack, every element removed must previously have been pushed. If there are p pushes in the sequence, there can be at most p actual removals, regardless of how multipop arguments are chosen. The aggregate cost of all pushes and removals is thus at most 2p, and no more than 2m when m counts all API calls.

This conclusion is deterministic and adversarially robust: the caller may issue large multipops at inconvenient times, but it cannot pop the same pushed element twice. A generic multidelete operation that can recreate, copy, or reinsert deleted items would require a different invariant.

def stack_multipop_trace(operations):
    stack = []
    elementary_cost = 0
    for name, value in operations:
        if name == 'push':
            stack.append(value)
            elementary_cost += 1
        elif name == 'multipop':
            count = min(value, len(stack))
            for _ in range(count):
                stack.pop()
            elementary_cost += count
        else:
            raise ValueError('unknown operation')
    return elementary_cost, stack

ops = [('push', i) for i in range(10)] + [('multipop', 8),
       ('push', 10), ('multipop', 100)]
cost, remaining = stack_multipop_trace(ops)
assert cost == 22 and remaining == []

Stack Multipop: Height Potential

Choose Φ(D) = |D|, the current number of stack elements. A push costs one actual unit and increases potential by one, so its amortized cost is two. A multipop removing t elements has actual cost t and decreases potential by t, so its amortized cost is zero. A conventional single pop is the special case t=1 and also has zero amortized cost under this assignment.

With an initially empty stack, the total amortized charge of a sequence is at most two per push, hence at most 2m for m API calls. The fact that a pop may receive amortized charge zero is not an assertion that popping takes no time. It says previously charged pushes have already paid for the eventual removals.

The method depends on the stack not materializing a previously popped element again without a corresponding charged push. It also assumes that the cost of checking whether a stack is empty is either ignored or included as a constant per call; this is easy to add to the bound.

Queue Implemented with Two Stacks

One queue representation uses an in stack for enqueues and an out stack for dequeues. To enqueue, push onto in. To dequeue, pop from out if possible; if out is empty, transfer all entries from in to out (one pop and one push per entry), then pop the oldest item from out. A dequeue that triggers a large transfer can cost Θ(n).

Nevertheless, each enqueued element moves from in to out at most once before it is removed. Consequently, across m queue calls, each enqueued item incurs at most one in-push, one in-pop, one out-push, and one out-pop. Thus the total number of stack primitives is O(m) for an initially empty queue.

This technique is important because it illustrates amortization without global resizing. Expensive work is postponed until a transfer is needed, and stored potential tracks the postponed transfer work. The FIFO order follows from reversing the in-stack order into out and then popping it.

class TwoStackQueue:
    def __init__(self):
        self.in_stack = []
        self.out_stack = []
        self.cost = 0

    def enqueue(self, item):
        self.in_stack.append(item)
        self.cost += 1

    def dequeue(self):
        if not self.out_stack:
            while self.in_stack:
                self.out_stack.append(self.in_stack.pop())
                self.cost += 2  # pop from in and push onto out
        if not self.out_stack:
            raise IndexError('dequeue from empty queue')
        self.cost += 1
        return self.out_stack.pop()

q = TwoStackQueue()
for i in range(10): q.enqueue(i)
assert [q.dequeue() for _ in range(10)] == list(range(10))
assert q.cost == 40

Two-Stack Queue: Potential Proof

Set Φ(D) = 2|in|. An enqueue incurs one actual push and increases potential by two, for amortized cost three. A dequeue with nonempty out incurs one actual pop and leaves the potential unchanged, for amortized cost one. If out is empty and k elements must transfer, the actual cost is 2k+1 including the final pop, while the potential decreases by 2k. Its amortized cost is exactly one.

Therefore every successful queue operation has constant amortized cost under the stated primitive-operation model. When the queue is empty, the API may reject a dequeue or return a special value; that error path should be described separately but can be implemented in constant time.

Notice that the potential method does not forbid one expensive dequeue. It proves that expensive transfers can only arise after enough enqueues have accumulated the potential needed to pay for them. The O(1) amortized result is valid for arbitrary interleavings of enqueues and dequeues.

def queue_potential_check(sequence):
    ins, outs = [], []
    phi = 0
    for kind, value in sequence:
        before = phi
        if kind == 'enq':
            ins.append(value)
            cost = 1
        else:
            cost = 0
            if not outs:
                while ins:
                    outs.append(ins.pop())
                    cost += 2
            if not outs:
                continue
            outs.pop()
            cost += 1
        phi = 2 * len(ins)
        amortized = cost + phi - before
        assert amortized <= 3 and phi >= 0

queue_potential_check([('enq', i) for i in range(20)] +
                      [('deq', None)] * 10 +
                      [('enq', i) for i in range(3)] +
                      [('deq', None)] * 13)

Monotonic Stacks and Limited Removal Work

A monotonic stack maintains a sequence of candidates ordered by a property such as increasing or decreasing value. When a new item arrives, it may pop many earlier items that cannot remain candidates and then pushes the new item. A single arrival can trigger Θ(n) pops, yet each element is pushed once and popped at most once.

For n arrivals, there are n pushes and at most n pops; the total stack work is O(n). This direct aggregate argument explains the efficiency of next-greater-element scans and many linear-time range algorithms. The correctness of removing a candidate is a separate concern: the condition must establish that the popped candidate can never become the desired answer for future positions.

The bound relies on a single-pass structure where popped items are not repeatedly reintroduced. A process that rebuilds the stack after every query can violate the same total-work estimate. Algorithms with multiple stacks or nested sweeps need a fresh accounting of their distinct elementary actions.

def next_greater_indices(values):
    answer = [-1] * len(values)
    pending = []
    pushes = pops = 0
    for i, value in enumerate(values):
        while pending and values[pending[-1]] < value:
            j = pending.pop()
            pops += 1
            answer[j] = i
        pending.append(i)
        pushes += 1
    assert pushes == len(values) and pops <= pushes
    return answer

assert next_greater_indices([2, 1, 3, 2, 4]) == [2, 2, 4, 4, -1]

Union–Find: A More Sophisticated Amortized Bound

The disjoint-set union or union–find structure maintains groups under operations such as make-set, union, and find. With union by rank or size and path compression, the well-known standard amortized analysis gives a near-constant bound involving the inverse Ackermann function: a sequence of m operations on n elements is bounded by O(m α(n)) under customary assumptions and formulations, with details depending on the precise operations and model.

That conclusion cannot be obtained simply by claiming that every parent pointer changes once. Path compression may revisit nodes, and the proof tracks ranks and hierarchical progress through more elaborate amortized arguments. The inverse Ackermann function grows exceptionally slowly, but is not identically constant as a mathematical function.

For this encyclopedia unit, union–find demonstrates the reach of amortized reasoning beyond elementary geometric sums. Its full rank-level proof belongs in a specialist data-structures treatment rather than being replaced with an inaccurate one-paragraph “proof.” When citing a complexity bound, also state the combined heuristics on which it depends.

Splay Trees: Total Cost Versus Individual Access

A splay tree is a self-adjusting binary search tree that rotates accessed nodes toward the root. An individual access or restructuring can take Θ(n) time in an unfavorable tree, but the classic amortized analysis provides logarithmic access bounds over appropriate sequences using a rank-based potential. The proof depends on subtree sizes and a rotation accounting argument.

Unlike a balanced search tree with a strict height invariant, a splay tree does not promise logarithmic worst-case cost for every individual operation. Its amortized guarantee is a different statement: expensive adjustments can improve future structure and their cost is covered within the overall sequence. This can be advantageous for locality-sensitive workloads, but it is not a hard per-request latency guarantee.

This is a case where an informal “each item moves only once” argument is insufficient. Repeated rotations are possible; the correct potential is more subtle. The example illustrates why one must choose a proof method matched to the actual data-structure dynamics.

Hash Tables: Resize Costs and Collision Costs Are Distinct

A hash table may perform occasional rehashes as its capacity grows. Geometric resizing can make the total cost of rebuilding across m insertions O(m) under a constant-cost model for moving or recomputing each entry. This is one amortized component of insertion cost.

Hash lookup and collision resolution introduce a separate question. With suitable randomness or assumptions about key distributions and hash-family properties, expected or average probe costs may be bounded. Under unrestricted adversarial collisions, the search cost can be large even if resize work is amortized constant. A complete insertion claim must combine the appropriate collision analysis and rebuild analysis rather than treating one as proof of the other.

Deletions and tombstones, adversarial key sequences, and rehash policies create further cases. The correct statement is not “hash tables always have O(1) amortized operations,” but rather a conditional cost claim describing the hash model, load-factor policy, and operation mix.

Lazy Deletion, Deferred Cleanup, and Rebuilding

Some structures defer maintenance until a threshold is reached: entries may be marked deleted, logs compacted, caches rebuilt, or invalid records removed in batches. If a maintenance cycle touches work accumulated across many preceding operations, the system may admit an amortized proof using a credit or potential proportional to deferred cleanup.

The proof must identify exactly which actions create maintenance obligations and ensure that the same item cannot trigger unbounded cleanup repeatedly without paying a new charge. For example, if each append creates at most one unit of later compaction work and compaction removes that obligation, a sequence bound may follow. If a policy repeatedly scans unchanged garbage on every read, the desired bound can fail.

An operational batch job that happens to run infrequently is not automatically amortized efficient. A formal proof requires either a bounded total amount of maintenance work or an invariant connecting expensive cleanup to enough preceding charged operations.

String Builders and Buffered Output

Repeatedly concatenating immutable strings may copy the entire prefix on each step in some languages and implementations. Building an n-character result by copying prefixes of lengths 1, 2, …, n can require Θ(n²) total character movement. A geometrically growing mutable buffer instead can use the same aggregate reasoning as a dynamic array to obtain O(n) total appended-character work.

Buffered I/O can also amortize per-call overhead by collecting small writes and flushing a full buffer occasionally. The relevant model may count system calls, bytes copied, block transfers, or a weighted combination. A buffer flush can be expensive in isolation even when the number of flushes per appended byte is bounded.

These patterns show the breadth of amortized reasoning, but the cost claims are implementation-sensitive. Real-world buffering may add latency, durability semantics, encoding work, or concurrency requirements that are not captured by a simple array-copy model.

Amortized Memory Allocation and Capacity Overhead

A dynamic array’s append analysis concerns time spent moving elements, while its space analysis considers allocated capacity. With doubling from a nonempty state, capacity is less than twice the number of stored items for most reachable post-append states, apart from small initialization conventions. Thus capacity is O(n), even though unused slots may be a substantial fraction of the allocation.

Amortized time does not imply amortized memory usage or low peak memory. During reallocation, an implementation may temporarily hold both old and new backing allocations; the peak live allocation can exceed its steady-state capacity. Allocator fragmentation and object references can change the actual memory profile further.

A design review should state both the asymptotic update cost and the operational memory requirements, including peak allocation, per-element overhead, buffer retention after deletion, and the scope of any allocator guarantees.

Credits Are Not Actual Deferred Computations

The accounting method uses “credits” as an abstract proof device. They need not be stored in memory, and operations need not literally perform extra work when charged more. A charged push that receives two abstract units may execute only one actual primitive operation; the surplus represents logical preparation for the future pop.

Conversely, a data structure may physically perform work early—such as incrementally moving elements between backing arrays—so that future operations have a smaller actual latency. That is a different implementation technique from merely proving an amortized upper bound. Incremental rebuilding may transform an amortized guarantee into a stronger worst-case guarantee, but only if every incremental step has a strict work budget and correctness is maintained during migration.

Keeping these ideas distinct prevents an accounting argument from being confused with a scheduler or allocation strategy. A mathematical guarantee can motivate an implementation decision without automatically enforcing its timing.

Accounting for an Arbitrary Starting State

An initially empty stack has zero stored potential. A stack that begins with n₀ elements can perform one multipop(n₀) without any pushes in the observed operation sequence. Its actual cost is n₀, so the total cannot be bounded by O(m) in terms of subsequent call count m alone if m=1 and n₀ is unbounded.

With Φ(D)=|D|, the correct telescoping inequality is C ≤ Σ â + Φ(D₀), since Φ(Dₘ)≥0. Thus an O(m + n₀) bound is natural. The initial n₀ term represents work already “prepared” before measurement began. For grow/shrink arrays, an initial capacity and occupancy may similarly affect the initial potential.

This is particularly important when analyzing persistent services, restored checkpoints, or data structures created from preexisting collections. The choice of sequence start is a mathematical assumption, not a cosmetic reporting preference.

When an Amortized Bound Is Not a Tail-Latency Guarantee

An operation can have constant amortized cost and still block for time proportional to current data size. A table resize may copy millions of elements at once; a two-stack queue may transfer all queued items during a dequeue. These bursts can violate real-time deadlines, interactive latency targets, or service-level objectives even when total throughput is favorable.

For hard real-time or latency-sensitive work, seek a strict worst-case per-operation bound or implement bounded incremental restructuring. Examples include spreading a rehash across operations, moving a fixed number of elements per call, or selecting a data structure with a different representation and cost profile. These implementations require careful state and correctness invariants of their own.

The appropriate question is not whether amortized analysis is “good enough” universally, but whether the application values total work, average throughput, predictable response time, or worst-case deadlines—and which trade-offs the design is permitted to make.

Amortized Guarantees Under Adversarial Sequences

A deterministic amortized proof often bounds every possible sequence of legal operations, including one chosen by an adversary who observes the state and triggers expensive events whenever possible. For example, the two-stack queue potential proof applies to arbitrary enqueue/dequeue interleavings. The adversary can force one expensive transfer but cannot force repeated large transfers without inserting enough new items to fund them.

The guarantee is not immune to changes in the operation contract. If the API adds an unconditional rebuild(), a caller might invoke rebuild repeatedly with no state change, defeating the previous accounting. If a table shrinks too aggressively, alternation can similarly destroy the cheap-operation spacing.

Adversarial robustness therefore means robustness within the explicitly modeled set of operations and state transitions. Every new public operation or background maintenance action must be included in the proof obligations before preserving the same bound.

Why O(1) Amortized Does Not Mean O(1) Per Call

The phrase “O(1) amortized per operation” abbreviates a sequence-wide statement such as C(m) = O(m) for m operations from a defined initial state. It does not assert cᵢ = O(1) for all i. A full dynamic-array append may take Θ(n); its amortized charge remains bounded because accumulated potential offsets the copy cost.

Confusing amortized charges with actual elapsed cost creates downstream errors. It can lead to incorrect worst-case response-time claims, underestimates of tail latency, or unjustified assertions that a single insert cannot trigger a large allocation. The correct performance contract may state both: O(1) amortized append and O(n) worst-case single append.

An analogous distinction applies to stacks with multipop and two-stack queues. An individual multipop or transfer may be linear in the number of currently stored elements even though the total work across related calls is linear in the sequence length.

Variable-Size Operations and Weighted Cost Models

Sometimes operations process objects of different sizes. A resize that copies n references has a different cost from one that copies n large objects with user-defined copy constructors. Similarly, bulk enqueue, variable-length string concatenation, or file compaction can involve work proportional to bytes or records rather than API-call count.

In such cases, define a weighted actual cost, perhaps cᵢ = a·(comparisons) + b·(bytes moved) + d·(allocations), where the weights reflect the chosen abstract model. An amortized statement may then be O(total input bytes + number of calls) instead of O(number of calls). The proof must charge work against the actual growth of the relevant measure.

This distinction matters whenever an implementation claims constant amortized time despite allowing an insertion of an arbitrarily large value. Unless value copying is constant time under the chosen model, the API cannot generally have constant cost independent of value size.

Relationship to Recurrence Relations and Summation Analysis

Recurrence relations describe costs that depend on subproblem sizes, commonly in recursive algorithms. Amortized analysis describes a cost across a sequence of state-changing operations, commonly on a persistent data structure. Both use summations and invariants, but they organize complexity around different mathematical objects.

A resize schedule can be expressed with a geometric sum, and its bound may resemble solving a recurrence. That does not make the amortized proof identical to divide-and-conquer analysis. The relevant “subproblem” in a dynamic table is the state left by earlier operations, not a recursive call solving an independent smaller input.

For formal asymptotic notation and summation methods see Asymptotic Notation and Loop and Summation Analysis. For recursive time equations, use Recurrence Relations in Algorithm Analysis and Recursion Trees and the Master Theorem.

Performance Models: Abstract Work Versus Measurements

An asymptotic amortized proof can count element copies, comparisons, pointer updates, or bit flips, while a benchmark measures elapsed time on a particular runtime, machine, memory system, and workload. The two forms of evidence answer related but nonidentical questions. A code path with O(1) amortized theoretical work may still exhibit long spikes from allocation, garbage collection, locking, or storage I/O.

A productive empirical study separately records operation-level latency distributions, total work over increasing sequence lengths, number of resizes or transfers, allocation peaks, and the exact operation mix. Plotting total modeled cost divided by m can illustrate convergence toward a bounded ratio; it cannot on its own prove the universal guarantee.

The general experimental design belongs to Algorithm Benchmarking and Profiling. Here measurement is a check on the implementation and cost model, not a substitute for the aggregate, accounting, or potential proof.

Concurrency and Shared Data-Structure Guarantees

A concurrent vector, map, or queue introduces costs beyond a sequential abstract structure: lock contention, atomic retries, memory reclamation, cache-line coherence, and scheduler effects. A sequential amortized proof does not automatically establish O(1) concurrent operation latency or even O(m) total multi-threaded implementation work under unrestricted contention.

For lock-free algorithms, unsuccessful retries can be part of a global progress proof, but proving lock freedom is not the same as proving bounded per-thread work. A process may make progress while one thread repeatedly retries. Amortized concurrent analysis requires an explicit scheduler or adversary model and a precise accounting of successful and unsuccessful steps.

When using an amortized bound in a product specification, label whether it applies to abstract sequential operations, concurrent execution, or a distributed protocol. The mathematical scope should be clear before drawing conclusions about user-facing responsiveness.

Practical Proof Checklist

Begin by writing the API and legal states. Choose m as the number of calls or another explicit sequence measure. Identify the primitive work being charged, then derive the exact actual cost of the cheap and expensive branches. Test potential candidates on empty, full, threshold, and adversarially alternating states.

For an aggregate proof, count how often each costly elementary action can occur. For an accounting proof, identify where credits are deposited and why a debit never exceeds the balance. For a potential proof, state Φ(D₀), prove a lower bound on reachable potentials, and calculate âᵢ for every legal operation case. Finally telescope and report the total cost with any initial-state term.

A useful article statement looks like: “Starting empty, with unit-cost element copies and doubling capacity on overflow, m append operations perform at most O(m) writes and copies, so append has O(1) amortized modeled cost; a single full append may cost Θ(n).” That sentence is more informative than an unqualified O(1) label.

Method Selection Matrix

Use the shortest correct proof method rather than automatically applying the most elaborate technique. A one-pass monotonic stack usually admits a direct element-count argument; a two-stack queue is naturally explained with credits or Φ = 2|in|; a grow/shrink table benefits from a piecewise potential because costly events occur at two thresholds.

The methods are interchangeable only at the level of an established invariant, not by verbal substitution. For example, a potential chosen for insert-only arrays may become invalid after deletion; a stack-height potential is unrelated to the cost of hashing keys. State the data-structure dynamics before borrowing a familiar formula.

For instructional purposes, compare proofs by the exact actions they charge and the assumptions they consume. A transparent O(m) aggregate proof is preferable to a complicated potential with an unchecked negative case.

Constructing an Amortized Argument from Source Code

When reading unfamiliar implementation code, first identify the persistent state and all branches that can perform nonconstant work. In an array-backed list, this may be the pair (size, capacity); in a queue, it may be the sizes of two internal stacks; in a binary counter, the number and arrangement of set bits. Draw a state-transition diagram for a normal operation and every threshold event.

Next choose a primitive cost. It is often clearer to count copies, pushes, and pops than to begin with elapsed nanoseconds. Translate loops into exact or bounded elementary actions. Ask whether an expensive event destroys the state that made it expensive: a transfer empties the input stack, a carry clears trailing one bits, and a resize creates fresh unused capacity.

Only after this inspection choose an amortized method. A candidate potential should fall during the expensive transition by an amount comparable to the costly work. If it instead increases substantially during the expensive case, the proposed amortized charge may remain unbounded. Record each case in a small table before claiming a universal bound.

Checking Amortized Claims with a State-Transition Ledger

A state-transition ledger is a useful bridge between mathematics and implementation. Its columns can record the operation, old state, new state, actual cost, old potential, new potential, and amortized cost. It exposes mistakes such as counting a deleted item twice, charging a resize before rather than after insertion, or forgetting the minimum-capacity rule.

For a grow-only array, start from (n,c)=(0,0), take Φ=max(0,2n−c), and calculate at least one ordinary append, the initial allocation, and a doubling resize. For a queue, test both an ordinary out-pop and a transfer from a nonempty in-stack. The maximum amortized charge across these cases is the number that belongs in the theorem statement.

A ledger is not a replacement for proof over all n or all sequences. Its purpose is to reveal missing operation categories. Once the categories are complete, prove the symbolic inequality for arbitrary permitted parameters and then telescope.

Empirical Counterexample: Additive Resizing

A quick way to invalidate an overbroad claim is to find a legal sequence that repeatedly triggers expensive work. Consider a table with capacity incremented by one at every full append. The (k+1)-st insertion must copy k existing elements. Across m insertions from empty, the total copied work is m(m−1)/2, so the amortized copy cost is Θ(m) per append.

A data structure cannot obtain constant amortized append complexity from an accounting method if it does not actually supply enough inexpensive operations between copies. Any proposed constant-charge credit scheme would eventually require negative credit to pay for the growing copying costs. Any nonnegative potential with constant amortized charges would contradict the quadratic aggregate lower bound.

This is a powerful diagnostic: when a hypothesized potential proof seems to “work” for a policy known to copy quadratically, the potential boundary conditions or an operation case must be wrong. Sequence-wide lower-bound examples can expose flawed accounting.

def additive_resize_trace(m):
    capacity = size = total = 0
    expensive = []
    for step in range(1, m + 1):
        if size == capacity:
            total += size  # copy all existing items
            expensive.append((step, size))
            capacity += 1
        size += 1
        total += 1  # write the appended value
    return total, expensive

for m in range(1, 31):
    total, events = additive_resize_trace(m)
    assert total == m * (m + 1) // 2
    assert len(events) == m

Comparing Total Cost with Peak Cost

Suppose a production service performs one million append operations. Under geometric capacity growth, the modeled total number of copied old elements remains proportional to one million. Yet the largest resize can still move hundreds of thousands of elements in one call. Throughput and maximum latency are therefore separated by orders of magnitude in the abstract model.

This is not a paradox; it is the central purpose of amortized analysis. The proof says that one extraordinarily expensive operation cannot recur too many times without additional cheap work. The application must decide whether the one large event is acceptable. For a background batch pipeline, total work may matter most. For a control loop, a strict deadline may dominate the decision.

A careful system design can combine both analyses: report Θ(n) worst-case single-operation work, O(1) amortized work per append from empty, O(n) storage, and the expected real-life resize behavior of the specific library. Each bound addresses a different risk.

Potential Pitfalls: Circularity, Hidden Work, and Negative Balances

A proof can fail even when its formulas look familiar. One failure is circularity: “a resize is cheap amortized because earlier appends have saved enough credits” is merely an assertion until the deposit rate and credit invariant are established. Another is hidden work: a table append that calls a user-defined copying function must account for that function’s cost if the operation model includes it.

A third error is negative potential without an appropriate boundary argument. Choosing Φ = −n for a stack makes multipops look artificially inexpensive, but the final potential can become unbounded below as size grows; telescoping no longer gives the claimed total bound. A fourth mistake is double-counting credit that is paid once but spent by multiple expensive operations.

Finally, proof hypotheses must include all supported mutations. A new remove-all, rebuild, copy-on-write, or shrink operation can invalidate a previous theorem. The final complexity guarantee should be accompanied by the smallest complete set of conditions that makes it true.

Example Study: A Buffered Log Writer

A buffered logger collects short records in an in-memory array and flushes them when a threshold B is reached. Under a model in which appending a record costs one and flushing a full buffer costs B additional units, every block of B records pays for one flush. Over m records, the total modeled work is at most m + B⌊m/B⌋, or O(m), and the amortized cost per appended record is constant.

However, the file system may impose a fixed flush overhead, varying device latency, and durability requirements. If a caller may invoke flush() after each one-record write, then the total number of flushes is no longer bounded by the number of completed full buffers. The API and scheduling policy determine whether the bound applies.

This scenario illustrates how to translate an implementation-specific batching policy into an amortized theorem without suggesting that buffering guarantees a constant real-time write duration.

def buffered_writer_cost(record_count, capacity):
    assert capacity > 0
    buffered = 0
    work = 0
    flushes = 0
    for _ in range(record_count):
        buffered += 1
        work += 1  # append record
        if buffered == capacity:
            work += buffered  # model one action per flushed record
            buffered = 0
            flushes += 1
    return work, flushes, buffered

for records in range(100):
    work, flushes, remaining = buffered_writer_cost(records, 7)
    assert work <= 2 * records
    assert flushes == records // 7

Example Study: An Incremental Maintenance Policy

A system may wish to preserve amortized total work while avoiding large pauses. Instead of rehashing all entries at a single threshold, it can create a new table and transfer a bounded number of old entries on each future API operation. Lookups may need to check both tables during migration, and insertions must avoid losing or duplicating entries.

With enough progress per operation and a disciplined migration invariant, rehash work can be distributed across calls. Whether this establishes a worst-case bound depends on the migration budget, lookup cost, collision policy, and guarantees about how the API is exercised. An operation sequence consisting mostly of reads may require reads to advance migration or an alternative trigger.

Incremental rebuilding is an engineering technique distinct from amortized proof. The latter can show that a bulk rebuild is affordable in total; the former can alter the actual schedule of work. They are complementary but neither automatically proves the other.

Example Study: Grid and Graph Algorithms Using Worklists

Worklist-based graph algorithms often push a node or edge, then process and remove it later. If each vertex is enqueued at most once using a visited marker set at enqueue time, there are at most V enqueue and V dequeue events. Traversing each adjacency list once accounts for O(V+E) total work in the adjacency-list representation.

The resemblance to amortized analysis is useful—each item funds its own later processing—but it is important to distinguish ordinary aggregate counting from a formal claim about amortized cost per arbitrary worklist operation. If duplicates are permitted, a node may be inserted many times and the push count must be bounded separately.

Graph traversal thus provides a caution against casually saying “each item is touched once.” The claim should follow the exact marking policy and data representation. The graph-algorithm costs are treated more comprehensively in the separate Graph Algorithm Analysis specialist article.

Example Study: A Stack-Based Undo History

An editor or transaction manager may retain a stack of undo records. Pushing a record is cheap; a bulk rollback can remove many records at once. If each record is inserted once and removed at most once during the measured sequence, the total number of insertions and removals is linear in the number of pushed records. The same multipop aggregate argument applies.

Actual rollback may perform more than removing a record. It might reconstruct application state, write journal entries, or execute compensating actions whose costs depend on the original operation. An amortized O(1) per-stack-entry removal claim does not give a constant bound on those compensating actions.

This distinction is valuable in engineering documentation: define the narrow data-structure primitive covered by the theorem, then separately model the domain-level work executed when an entry is processed.

Example Study: Workload Drift and Repeated Rescans

Suppose an application stores n deferred jobs and rescans the entire list after every incoming request to decide whether a cleanup is necessary. Even if cleanup itself is rare, the repeated scans cost Θ(n) each when n remains large. Without an operation count or state change limiting rescans, O(1) amortized per request is not justified.

A better design may maintain an explicit counter, a priority queue of deadlines, or a threshold that allows constant-time eligibility tests. The proof must include the cost of updating the auxiliary index, not just the cost of the occasional cleanup. This case illustrates how a seemingly small unmodeled step can dominate total work.

Amortized analysis does not license neglecting routine overhead. It often helps identify which routine work must be avoided so that the aggregate bound is genuinely linear.

Example Study: Choosing a Resizable Container for a Service

Consider a service that stores pending requests in a growable array. A throughput-oriented batch worker may prefer geometric growth because it offers simple constant-amortized append work and contiguous access. An interactive system with strict latency constraints may choose reserved capacity, chunked storage, or incremental resizing to reduce pause risk.

The design should document growth factor, shrink threshold, peak allocation, element-copy semantics, and whether callers can request explicit compaction. A memory-constrained device may prioritize a smaller capacity multiplier, accepting higher constant copying costs. A high-throughput process may accept spare capacity to reduce reallocations.

The theoretical bound narrows the range of reasonable choices but does not dictate the final architecture. Benchmarking, workload sizing, and failure-mode assessment complete the decision.

Six Worked Analytical Investigations

Investigation 1 — array expansion: A grow-only table with doubling capacity starts empty. Show C(m) < 3m under unit-cost writes and copies. Identify the single-operation worst case separately, and trace the ninth append to explain why its cost nine is compatible with the bound.

Investigation 2 — bad shrink policy: Consider alternating insertion and deletion around a half-full threshold. Construct a legal sequence that triggers repeated reallocations. Revise the policy to shrink at one-quarter occupancy and explain how the gap between thresholds prevents immediate oscillation.

Investigation 3 — binary counter: Starting from zero, list bit flips over eight increments. Then set Φ equal to popcount and compute â for an increment with k trailing ones, including the case k=3.

Investigation 4 — multipop: Starting from empty, perform ten pushes, multipop eight, push one, and multipop one hundred. Bound total actual stack work without assuming an average distribution of k.

Investigation 5 — two-stack queue: Enqueue ten elements, then dequeue all ten. Determine the total elementary stack operations and show why the transfer-heavy dequeue receives constant amortized charge.

Investigation 6 — production latency: A request handler uses an array with O(1) amortized appends but must complete every request before a hard deadline. Identify why the mathematical result is insufficient and propose a capacity-reservation or bounded-migration design whose worst-case work can be independently analyzed.

Review Questions with Answer Guidance

The following questions consolidate the definitions, proof methods, and engineering limitations. The answers are concise guides; a complete derivation should include the stated operation model and any initial-state dependence.

QuestionAnswer guidance
1. What does O(1) amortized append mean?O(m) total modeled work over m appends under the stated growth and initial-state policy.
2. Does it imply O(1) worst-case append?No. An individual full append may copy Θ(n) elements.
3. Is amortized analysis probabilistic?Not by definition; its classic guarantees are deterministic over sequences.
4. What is aggregate analysis?Directly bound the sum of actual costs in a complete operation sequence.
5. What is the accounting method?Assign extra charges to cheap operations; save nonnegative credits for later work.
6. Write the potential-method equation.âᵢ = cᵢ + Φ(Dᵢ) − Φ(Dᵢ₋₁).
7. What telescopes in a potential proof?All intermediate potential terms cancel in the total sum.
8. Why require nonnegative potential in common proofs?It ensures the final potential cannot hide unbounded unpaid actual work.
9. What does initial potential contribute?An additive Φ(D₀) term to the total-cost upper bound when final Φ is nonnegative.
10. Why does doubling capacity work?Copies form a geometric series whose total is O(m).
11. Why does additive capacity growth fail?Copies sum to Θ(m²) across m appends.
12. What distinguishes hysteresis?Different grow/shrink thresholds prevent rapid repeated resizing.
13. What is the binary-counter potential?Number of currently set bits.
14. Why does a multipop stack have linear total work?Every removed element must previously have been pushed.
15. What potential suits a two-stack queue?Twice the number of elements on its input stack.
16. Can an amortized guarantee meet hard deadlines by itself?No; one operation may still require nonconstant actual work.
17. Why do hash tables need extra assumptions?Resize work and collision-resolution cost are separate.
18. Is a union–find inverse-Ackermann bound trivial?No; its combined-heuristic analysis needs a sophisticated proof.
19. What invalidates an existing amortized proof?An operation or transition not covered by its accounting invariants.
20. How should models and experiments be related?Prove a model-based bound, then measure implementation-specific costs separately.
21. What if the stack begins with n₀ entries?The total bound can include O(n₀) initial-state work.
22. Does amortized O(1) imply constant peak memory?No; geometric arrays use O(n) capacity and may temporarily duplicate buffers.
23. When is a potential function unhelpful?When it cannot bound all transition cases or has uncontrolled negative final values.
24. How can a costly operation be deamortized?Spread restructuring across calls with a bounded work budget and preserve correctness.
