Collection Overview

This directory is the specialist subarticle index for Definition of a Computer in the MGU Computer Science Encyclopedia. The parent article introduces computers as programmable machines that process information according to instructions, work automatically, maintain state, and interact with their surroundings through input and output. The existing parent article is not altered by this index.

The nineteen planned studies develop those basic ideas in four directions: criteria for recognizing a computer; functions and organization common to computing systems; families of machines that meet the definition in different ways; and historical and conceptual boundaries that make the term more nuanced than “a device with a screen.”

The parent provides the concise definition; these child entries are planned for the deeper questions. The child titles and filenames below are proposed editorial destinations, not claims that the pages have already been published.

A Functional Framework for Defining Computers

There is no single hardware checklist that describes all machines historically or presently called computers. Definitions are most useful when they identify what a system does, how it can be directed, and what forms its operation takes. Modern stored-program digital machines are a central case, but mechanical, analog, embedded, and specially configured systems complicate overly rigid tests.

QuestionWhat it clarifiesImportant qualification
Does the system process information?Distinguishes information transformation from purely mechanical power transmission.Information can be encoded digitally or represented through continuous physical quantities.
Does it operate by defined rules?Identifies a repeatable procedure, control process, or computational mechanism.Random inputs do not prevent rule-governed computation.
Can its behavior be programmed or configured?Explains flexibility and the range of tasks a machine can perform.Not every historical computer is a conventional stored-program machine.
Does it maintain state or intermediate results?Shows how processing proceeds across steps and supports more complex operations.The location and form of state differ by architecture and technology.
What counts as its output?Connects computation to external action, communication, or retained information.An output can be a signal or actuator command, not necessarily a display.

These questions form an explanatory framework, not a legally binding or universal test. When classifying a borderline device, state the definition being used and distinguish a computational component from a general-purpose computer.

Defining Criteria and Capabilities

These studies investigate the functional criteria used to identify a computer and explain why flexibility, automation, and information processing are related but not identical properties.

  1. Automated Information Processing — What it means for a machine to transform information under defined rules; the role of inputs, intermediate state, outputs, and automatic execution. (planned)
  2. Programmability and Reconfigurable Behavior — Programs and configuration as ways to change a system’s behavior; differences among software programming, firmware changes, and hardware reconfiguration. (planned)
  3. Stored-Program Computers and Alternative Designs — Instruction storage, program memory, and why stored-program architecture is enormously influential without being a universal requirement for every computing machine. (planned)
  4. General-Purpose and Special-Purpose Computers — How breadth of programmable tasks distinguishes flexible computers from narrowly optimized machines, including cases where categories overlap. (planned)
  5. Computational State and Execution — How machine state, instructions, inputs, and control rules jointly determine behavior, including deterministic and nondeterministic sources of input. (planned)

Functions and Internal Organization

A functional definition describes what a computer does before specifying one particular processor, instruction set, or memory implementation. These studies introduce the organization shared across many architectures.

  1. Input, Processing, Output, and Storage — The input–process–output–storage model, including control outputs and retained state in systems without conventional keyboards, displays, or disk drives. (planned)
  2. Instruction Execution and Control Flow — Instructions, sequencing, branching, repetition, and the relationship between program behavior and machine-level execution. (planned)
  3. Hardware, Software, and Firmware Boundaries — Physical components and executable descriptions; why firmware and programmable logic complicate simple hardware-versus-software categories. (planned)
  4. Working Memory and Persistent State — Temporary working state, retained configuration, saved files, and the fact that the specific storage arrangement differs among computer classes. (planned)
  5. Computer Input and Output Interfaces — Sensors, network messages, user actions, actuators, and machine-to-machine signals as legitimate information inputs and outputs. (planned)

Major Families of Computing Systems

The categories below apply the functional definition to machines of different sizes, purposes, physical forms, and resource-sharing arrangements.

  1. Personal Computers and Workstations — Interactive general-purpose machines, workstation-oriented workloads, local peripherals, and the distinction between a user role and a strict hardware category. (planned)
  2. Servers and Shared Computing Systems — Computers operated primarily to provide services or resources to other systems; concurrency, remote access, and the distinction between a server role and server hardware. (planned)
  3. Embedded Computers and Microcontrollers — Computing embedded in products and control systems, with constrained resources, sensors, actuators, and domain-specific firmware. (planned)
  4. Supercomputers and Computing Clusters — Large-scale computation across many processing elements; why a cluster can act as a coordinated computing system while containing many separate computers. (planned)
  5. Virtual Computers and Emulated Machines — Virtual machines and instruction-set emulators as software-defined machine abstractions hosted by physical computers; distinctions between logical and physical systems. (planned)

Historical Meaning and Boundary Cases

The concept of a computer has changed as calculating, control, and information-processing technologies evolved. These studies prevent an overly narrow, electronics-only definition.

  1. Human Computers and the History of the Term — The historical occupational meaning of “computer,” manual calculation work, and how the word shifted to describe machines. (planned)
  2. Mechanical, Analog, and Digital Computers — Physical computing mechanisms and continuous versus discrete representations, with attention to the limits of a definition based only on modern binary electronics. (planned)
  3. Fixed-Function Devices and Computational Boundaries — Calculators, timers, control circuits, and programmable devices; tests for whether a device computes and whether it is appropriately called a computer. (planned)
  4. Abstract Computers and Computational Universality — The relationship between practical machines and abstract models, including universality as a significant theoretical capability rather than a prerequisite for every real-world computer. (planned)

Boundary Cases: What Counts as a Computer?

Applying the definition is more informative than assuming that every electronic device—or only a desktop machine—must belong to one category. These examples are prompts for the specialist entries, not substitutes for a detailed technical classification.

ExampleRelevant featuresReasoned classification
Laptop running different applicationsStored programs, automatic instruction execution, general information processing.Unambiguous general-purpose digital computer.
Programmable thermostatSensor input, stored settings or firmware, automated rule execution, actuator output.Embedded computing system, even if its task range is narrow.
Four-function mechanical calculatorPhysical arithmetic mechanism, bounded operations, limited or absent programmability.Calculating machine; whether it is called a computer depends on the definition and historical context.
Fixed digital timer circuitCounting and control behavior, perhaps no changeable program.Computational logic may be present, but it is not automatically a general-purpose computer.
Virtual machineSoftware-defined processor and memory behavior implemented by a physical host.A logical or virtual computer, distinct from the physical machine supporting it.
Scientific analog computerContinuous physical variables represent quantities and enact mathematical relationships.A computer under a broad computational definition, though not a modern binary stored-program system.

The essential distinction is between the abstract function carried out, the mechanism implementing it, and the breadth of programmable behavior. Those attributes should be assessed separately.

Suggested Study Pathways

For introductory computer science: Start with Definition of a Computer, then study automated information processing; programmability and reconfiguration; and input, processing, output, and storage. Apply the framework to a laptop, phone, and embedded controller.

For hardware and systems study: Follow stored-program computers, instruction execution, hardware–software boundaries, memory state, and input/output interfaces. Continue into the existing Computer Architecture Overview and Central Processing Unit articles for component-level explanations.

For technical classification: Compare personal computers, servers, embedded computers, supercomputers, and virtual machines. Ask whether a label describes hardware, a software environment, or the role that a system plays.

For historical and theoretical interpretation: Read about human computers, mechanical and analog devices, fixed-function boundary cases, and computational universality. Compare the history of the word “computer” with the modern mathematical definition of computation.
