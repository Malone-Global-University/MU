Collection Overview

The Discrete Mathematics unit is part of Computational Foundations in the Computer Science volume of the MGU Encyclopedia. This index extends the existing Discrete Mathematics parent overview, which introduces sets, mappings, logic, proof, finite counting, recurrences, modular arithmetic, graph structures, and discrete probability.

Discrete mathematics examines mathematical objects that can be specified through distinguishable elements, combinatorial structures, symbolic rules, and discrete changes. It is not limited to finite objects: infinite sets and sequences are also important. For computer science, the field offers the vocabulary to state requirements precisely, count configurations, analyze algorithms, characterize relations among objects, and prove that specified procedures satisfy their intended conditions.

Sets, Relations, and Functions

The foundational question is what mathematical objects exist and how they connect. Sets identify membership, relations express pairs and structural constraints, and functions assign uniquely determined outputs to inputs. These concepts support later claims about databases, graphs, states, and formal languages. Each article should distinguish the mathematical object from an implementation that stores or computes with it.

1. Sets and Cardinality

Treat sets as collections determined by membership, with order and duplicate notation irrelevant to set identity. Develop union, intersection, difference, complement relative to a specified universe, Cartesian products, and power sets. Distinguish the number of elements in a finite set from the notation used to display them, and explain why a set of n distinct elements has 2n subsets. Use Venn diagrams as an aid but establish identities through element arguments rather than relying on drawings alone.

The specialist article should also explain the distinction between finite, countably infinite, and uncountable cardinality where appropriate, while prioritizing the finite structures used in introductory computing. Apply the framework to unique identifiers, allowable system states, permission sets, and data filtering. Its mathematical scope is membership and size, not the engineering implementation of a hash set or database table.

2. Binary Relations and Equivalence Classes

Define a relation as a subset of a Cartesian product, and for relations on one set examine reflexivity, symmetry, antisymmetry, and transitivity separately. Present relations as ordered pairs, matrices, directed graphs, and logical predicates. Explain equivalence relations through partitions and equivalence classes, while partial orders describe precedence or containment where two elements need not be comparable. Include reflexive, symmetric, and transitive closure as precisely stated transformations.

Worked applications should distinguish grouping records by an equivalence criterion from ordering tasks by dependency. “Connected to,” “same remainder modulo k,” and “must precede” can have different formal properties, so the label relation alone is insufficient. Detailed algorithms for graph reachability belong to the existing Graph Theory or Algorithms topics; this child owns the underlying mathematics of relationships.

3. Functions and Mappings

Define a function by its domain, codomain, and assignment of exactly one output to each input. Compare image and inverse image with inverse functions, and demonstrate that a function can fail to be injective or surjective without ceasing to be a valid function. Explain composition, identity mappings, finite-function counting, and the role of explicit domains when asserting that a mapping is one-to-one or onto.

Use code tables, database identifiers, and state-transition functions as examples, but distinguish a mathematical mapping from a physical subroutine with failures or side effects. Prove when a bijection supports an inverse and show how a change of codomain can change surjectivity without changing the underlying input-output assignments. The specialist scope centers on structural properties, not a survey of programming-language function syntax.

Logic, Proof, and Induction

These three studies supply the reasoning infrastructure of computer science. Formal statements identify the claim; proof methods justify or refute it; induction establishes properties across recursively or sequentially constructed cases. Examples should state a domain, premises, and quantifiers before the proof begins. Testing and simulation may help discover errors, but finite observation alone does not prove a universal assertion.

1. Propositional and Predicate Logic

Begin with propositions, truth valuations, conjunction, disjunction, negation, implication, and biconditional. Explain the difference between logical equivalence and material implication, and demonstrate truth-table reasoning without treating it as a universal method for domains of unlimited size. Extend to predicates, free and bound variables, universal and existential quantification, and the effect of quantifier order.

Use a clear access-policy example: “every approved request has a valid credential” differs from “some approved request has a valid credential.” Show how negating quantifiers changes claims and how a counterexample refutes a universally quantified statement. Binary Logic retains hardware-oriented Boolean realization; this article owns mathematical logical language and interpretation for proofs and specifications.

2. Mathematical Proof Techniques

Organize proofs by what the claim requires: construct a witness for existence, prove every allowed object satisfies a property, establish two implications for an equivalence, or refute an assertion with a valid counterexample. Cover direct proof, contrapositive reasoning, contradiction, proof by cases, and careful treatment of assumptions. Explain why testing many inputs or observing regularity is evidence but is not a proof of a universal mathematical claim.

Examples should include a parity claim, an “if and only if” statement, and a false conjecture disproved by one counterexample. Teach how to identify a logical gap, distinguish converse from contrapositive, and state where an argument uses a theorem already established. Keep this general toolbox distinct from the systematic induction methods developed in the next child.

3. Induction and Structural Proof

Develop the base case, inductive hypothesis, and induction step as a single argument over a well-founded domain. Contrast ordinary induction with strong induction, explain when recursively defined objects suggest structural induction, and show how loop invariants express related reasoning about repeated computation. The specialist should also discuss why a valid step from n to n + 1 is useless if the initial case is not established.

Use the sum of the first n integers, a divisibility statement, and a binary-tree property as separate teaching cases. Explain how a recursive definition supplies the structure that an inductive proof follows, while an actual recursive program additionally needs termination and correct implementation. This article develops proof methods, whereas the sibling Recursion article treats programmatic recursion more broadly.

Counting, Recurrences, and Number Theory

This group deals with the number of possibilities, the growth of quantities across steps, and the properties of integers under arithmetic constraints. While each method may appear in program design, its specialist article should derive the mathematics first and connect to applications second. State whether choices may repeat, what initial conditions apply, and which modulus or input range is assumed.

1. Combinatorics and Counting Principles

Present the sum and product rules with careful attention to whether alternatives are disjoint and choices independent in the counting sense. Distinguish ordered arrangements from unordered selections; explain factorials, permutations, combinations, and the binomial coefficient. Introduce the pigeonhole principle and inclusion–exclusion for overlapping sets, and state whether repetitions are allowed in each example.

A useful computing example counts valid passwords or configurations under explicit alphabet, length, and exclusion rules. Another counts records in the union of two groups without double-counting. Counting outcomes and assigning probabilities to them are distinct operations: this article handles exact enumeration, while the Discrete Probability child handles event measures and assumptions about likelihood.

2. Recurrence Relations and Discrete Growth

Define a recurrence through initial conditions and a rule connecting successive or smaller-indexed values. Distinguish a sequence definition from an algorithm-cost equation, and derive simple linear and divide-and-conquer examples. Compare closed forms with asymptotic estimates, and explain when a geometric sum or recursion-tree interpretation helps solve a recurrence. Include the restrictions on applying standard solution theorems.

A study of halving input size can lead to a logarithmic number of levels, while a recurrence such as T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n for suitable powers of two yields n log n growth. The child should teach how such formulas arise and how their solutions are proved; detailed algorithm-specific lower bounds and benchmarking belong to Algorithm Analysis.

3. Modular Arithmetic and Divisibility

Develop divisibility, greatest common divisors, the division algorithm, congruence modulo m, and the interpretation of residue classes. Show addition and multiplication of congruences, the Euclidean algorithm and its extended form, and the condition for a modular inverse to exist: the chosen value must be relatively prime to the modulus. Explain why an inverse is not always available and why division in modular arithmetic requires care.

Examples should include a cyclic schedule, a simple checksum-like residue calculation, and solving a small linear congruence. Preserve the distinction between modular mathematics and its engineering uses in cryptography, hashing, and clock arithmetic; mathematical congruences alone do not establish security, uniform hashing, or collision resistance.

Graphs, Trees, and Discrete Probability

Graphs and trees represent connected or hierarchical structures; discrete probability assigns weights to uncertain outcomes on explicitly defined spaces. Both rely on the earlier language of sets and relations, but they solve different questions. A graph expresses possibilities or constraints; a probabilistic model adds a distribution. Neither a picture of a network nor a frequency estimate is automatically a rigorous explanation.

1. Graphs, Trees, and Discrete Models

Define vertices, edges, directed and undirected graphs, adjacency, paths, cycles, degrees, and connectedness, with conventions stated whenever terminology differs. Explain trees as connected acyclic undirected graphs, rooted-tree organization, and the relationship between partial orders and directed acyclic dependency networks. Clarify why a general network with cycles or multiple parent relationships is not necessarily a tree.

Use course prerequisites, network links, and file-system hierarchies to connect the formal vocabulary to software models. This child supplies the mathematical foundations and modeling discipline; the independent Graph Theory unit retains deeper traversal algorithms, optimization, connectivity procedures, and application-specific network analysis.

2. Discrete Probability

Define a finite sample space, events as subsets, probability assignments, conditional probability, independence, and the distinction between equally likely outcomes and arbitrary probability distributions. Develop expectation for finite-valued random variables, indicators, and elementary variance where useful. Explain that “random” describes a probabilistic model whose assumptions must be supplied rather than an excuse to ignore the distribution.

Worked problems should consider draws without replacement, randomized selection, and expected counts. Explicitly distinguish a conditional probability from the unconditional one, and show why linearity of expectation does not require independence although calculations of other quantities may. The separate Probability and Statistics for Computing unit owns broader inference, estimation, and statistical decision-making.

Mathematical Methods and Model Comparison

Choosing a discrete mathematical tool begins with identifying the kind of claim rather than memorizing an isolated formula. The same software design may involve a set of possible states, a relation connecting those states, a logical invariant, a count of configurations, and a probability distribution over executions. Each representation answers a different question.

Primary questionAppropriate toolWhat a sound result establishesTypical mistake
Which objects belong to a collection?Sets and CardinalityMembership and, when defined, size or set identitiesCounting duplicates as distinct set members
Which pairs satisfy a rule?Binary Relations and Equivalence ClassesEquivalence, order, reachability, or another specified relation propertyAssuming all relations are symmetric or transitive
How does each input map to an output?Functions and MappingsFunction definition, composition, injectivity, or surjectivityConfusing codomain and image
Is a statement valid for all specified cases?Propositional and Predicate Logic and Mathematical Proof TechniquesA theorem under declared premises, or a counterexampleMistaking extensive testing for proof
Does a property hold for every stage of a construction?Induction and Structural ProofA base-and-step argument across the prescribed domainOmitting base cases or using an unjustified step
How many choices or configurations exist?Combinatorics and Counting PrinciplesEnumeration under explicit overlap and repetition rulesMultiplying dependent or overlapping choices carelessly
How does a quantity change with input or iteration?Recurrence Relations and Discrete GrowthA sequence, bound, or growth law derived from a recurrenceSolving an equation that does not match the algorithm
What happens under cyclic arithmetic?Modular Arithmetic and DivisibilityResidues, divisibility properties, or modular solutionsDividing by a value without a modular inverse
How are dependencies or networks organized?Graphs, Trees, and Discrete ModelsNodes, edges, paths, cycles, and structural constraintsAssuming a general directed graph is a tree

Mathematical versus computational claims: a proven relation identity is not a performance benchmark; a correct recurrence does not establish that code implements its assumed cost; and a graph model is not evidence about an actual network unless its data and interpretation match the system. Treat formal proof, model construction, empirical verification, and engineering validation as separate tasks.

Illustrative Discrete Mathematics Scenarios

The following are constructed instructional examples, not descriptions of MGU production systems. Each starts with assumptions, shows the mathematical decision, and identifies a specialist article for deeper study.

Example 1 — Counting possible subsets of permissions

A system defines four distinct permission labels: read, write, review, and export. A permission set can include any subset of those labels, including the empty set. Each permission either belongs or does not belong, so there are 24 = 16 possible subsets. This is a statement about the theoretical number of subsets, not about which combinations a policy actually permits. If particular permissions must be paired or forbidden, a separate constraint removes some combinations. Study Sets and Cardinality together with Combinatorics and Counting Principles to distinguish unrestricted membership from counted valid configurations.

Example 2 — Grouping identifiers by equivalence

Consider the integers from 0 through 8. Define a ~ b when they have the same remainder after division by 3. The relation is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive, so it partitions the nine integers into the three equivalence classes {0,3,6}, {1,4,7}, and {2,5,8}. A partition groups objects without necessarily ranking them. Contrast that result with a dependency relation such as “must run before,” which need not be symmetric. The relevant foundation is Binary Relations and Equivalence Classes; classifications based on real-world records also require a defensible rule for which objects count as equivalent.

Example 3 — A logical policy and its counterexample

Suppose the written requirement is “every approved submission has a valid credential.” In predicate notation this is ∀x (Approved(x) → ValidCredential(x)). The requirement is false if even one approved submission lacks a valid credential. Finding a valid example does not prove the universal statement; finding a violating case refutes it. The opposite claim “at least one approved submission has a valid credential” uses ∃x and is considerably weaker. This scenario leads from Propositional and Predicate Logic to Mathematical Proof Techniques. It is an example of logical specification, not an assertion that any deployed approval system is correctly implemented.

Example 4 — Proving a summation identity

For positive integers, claim that 1 + 2 + ... + n = n(n + 1)/2. The base case n = 1 gives 1 on both sides. Assuming the expression holds at n, add n + 1 to obtain n(n + 1)/2 + (n + 1) = (n + 1)(n + 2)/2, which is precisely the formula for n + 1. The base case and inductive implication establish the identity for all positive integers. See Induction and Structural Proof. The proof derives a general result; checking the first hundred values would not give the same guarantee.

Example 5 — Accounting for overlapping categories

In a hypothetical cohort of 100 students, 60 participate in group A, 50 participate in group B, and 20 participate in both. The number in at least one group is 60 + 50 − 20 = 90, by inclusion–exclusion. Ten belong to neither group. Merely adding 60 and 50 would count twenty students twice. This illustrates Combinatorics and Counting Principles and Sets and Cardinality. If the problem instead asks for the probability that a randomly selected student belongs to A, an explicit random-selection model is required before using Discrete Probability.

Example 6 — Recognizing discrete growth and modular structure

For input sizes n that are powers of two, suppose a procedure obeys T(1) = 1 and T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n. There are log₂ n non-base expansion levels, each contributing n, followed by n base-case units; therefore T(n) = n log₂ n + n in this exact cost model. Separately, a 12-step recurring schedule places event number 38 at remainder 2 modulo 12. One model explains growth; the other explains periodic equivalence. Read Recurrence Relations and Discrete Growth and Modular Arithmetic and Divisibility, without conflating asymptotic cost and cyclic arithmetic.

Example 7 — A prerequisite network and conditional draw

Let tasks A, B, C, and D have prerequisite edges A → B, A → C, B → D, and C → D. Both A, B, C, D and A, C, B, D respect the dependencies, while placing D ahead of B would not. This is a directed acyclic graph, not a single rooted-tree chain. In an unrelated probability experiment, a bag with two red and three blue tokens is sampled twice without replacement: the probability of two red tokens is (2/5)(1/4) = 1/10. These examples demonstrate separate uses of Graphs, Trees, and Discrete Models and Discrete Probability: structural constraints versus a specified random experiment.

Problem-Solving and Proof Selection Framework

Before using a theorem, choose a representation that captures the intended requirement. The table below connects a research or design question to the mathematical assumptions that must be checked first.

Question to askRequired clarificationStart with
What exactly is the collection?Universe, membership criterion, duplicates, finite or infinite domainSets and Cardinality
Are objects grouped, mapped, or ordered?Relation properties, function domain and codomain, equivalence versus precedenceBinary Relations and Equivalence Classes / Functions and Mappings
What does the requirement assert?Variables, quantifiers, premises, and any necessary or sufficient conditionsPropositional and Predicate Logic
What would prove or disprove the claim?Direct argument, contrapositive, contradiction, cases, or counterexampleMathematical Proof Techniques
Does the result cover arbitrarily many stages?Base cases, transition step, recursive construction, invariantInduction and Structural Proof
How many arrangements are possible?Order, repetition, exclusions, overlaps, and permitted combinationsCombinatorics and Counting Principles
What is the growth rule?Initial conditions, recurrence validity, input parameter, and asymptotic regimeRecurrence Relations and Discrete Growth
Are values constrained by remainders?Modulus, congruence, gcd, invertibility, and rangeModular Arithmetic and Divisibility
Do dependencies create cycles or a hierarchy?Directedness, adjacency, path properties, and tree assumptionsGraphs, Trees, and Discrete Models
Is the question about uncertainty?Sample space, distribution, dependence, and conditioningDiscrete Probability

Proof discipline: write the definition; state all assumptions; identify the mathematical object; show intermediate reasoning; then check the result against edge cases. A proof establishes a claim within its formal model, not a blanket certification of the system that motivated the model. Record such limitations when applying results to real software or research data.

Suggested Study Pathways

Foundations and first-year computer science: begin with the parent overview, then Sets and Cardinality, Binary Relations and Equivalence Classes, and Functions and Mappings. Use small universes to check membership, equivalence classes, and one-to-one functions before generalizing. Add Propositional and Predicate Logic early so that the wording of mathematical claims is explicit.

Proof-centered and theoretical study: follow Propositional and Predicate Logic with Mathematical Proof Techniques and Induction and Structural Proof. Practice proving an equivalence in both directions, refuting a universal statement with a counterexample, and deriving a recursive property by induction. Continue to the neighboring Theory of Computation and Automata Theory overviews.

Algorithm design and performance analysis: pair Combinatorics and Counting Principles with Recurrence Relations and Discrete Growth. A useful exercise derives a recursion tree from an algorithm's actual operations and uses mathematical induction to check its result. The existing Algorithm Analysis article handles broader algorithm-specific performance reasoning; the recurrence specialist should retain its mathematical ownership.

Networking, scheduling, and structured data: study relations before Graphs, Trees, and Discrete Models. Convert prerequisite statements into directed edges, determine whether cycles are allowed, and compare forests, rooted trees, and general graphs. The independent Graph Theory subject then provides broader treatment of network algorithms and graph-theoretic applications.

Integer arithmetic and security foundations: use Modular Arithmetic and Divisibility to understand remainders, divisibility, congruences, and modular inverses. Validate the modulus and gcd assumptions before attempting a calculation. Treat cryptographic uses as a further subject; a congruence calculation is not a proof of cryptographic security.

Randomized computing and uncertainty: first establish finite counting through Combinatorics and Counting Principles, then study Discrete Probability. Compare uniform and nonuniform sample spaces, draws with and without replacement, and linearity of expectation. Follow with Probability and Statistics for Computing for broader statistical inference.
