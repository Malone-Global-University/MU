Collection Overview

This directory organizes the specialist children of Graph Theory within the Computational Foundations division of the MGU Computer Science Encyclopedia. The existing parent article covers the mathematical definitions, principal graph algorithms, applications, and limits of graph-based reasoning; the planned articles below develop particular subjects in greater depth.

The parent article names exactly 15 specialist subarticles. This directory preserves those fifteen titles and their intended filenames without rewriting the parent or introducing a different hierarchy. Four subject groups provide navigational structure, but the intended destination of each child remains directly below computational-foundations/graph-theory-articles/.

Publication distinction: every planned child has an active extensionless hyperlink. A hyperlink denotes its intended publication address, not confirmation that the corresponding child page is already online. The existing Graph Theory overview remains the entry point for available substantive content.

Formal Structures, Connectivity, and Trees

Begin with the objects graph theory studies: vertices, edges, directed and undirected relationships, degrees, walks, paths, components, and trees. These topics establish precise representations and prove the structural properties on which later algorithms rely.

  1. Graph Definitions and Representations — Formal graphs, multigraphs, digraphs, adjacency structures, and modeling conventions. (planned article)
  2. Degree Sequences and Graph Invariants — Handshaking identities, graphical sequences, and structural statistics. (planned article)
  3. Paths, Cycles, and Connectivity — Walks, trails, components, cut vertices, and bridges. (planned article)
  4. Trees and Spanning Trees — Structural equivalences, rooted trees, forests, and spanning subgraphs. (planned article)

Traversal and Directed Dependencies

These articles address how to discover reachable vertices and impose order on directed relationships. Their algorithms depend on whether a graph is directed, whether cycles are allowed, and what information must be returned.

  1. Graph Traversal Algorithms — BFS, DFS, invariants, reachability, and traversal forests. (planned article)
  2. Directed Graphs and Topological Sorting — Reachability, strongly connected components, condensation graphs, and dependency order. (planned article)

Optimization, Flows, Matching, and Coloring

Here graphs become optimization models. A shortest route, an inexpensive connecting network, a feasible flow, a matching, and a conflict-free coloring are distinct mathematical objectives; the correct algorithm follows from the objective and constraints.

  1. Shortest Path Algorithms — BFS, Dijkstra, Bellman–Ford, Floyd–Warshall, and path reconstruction. (planned article)
  2. Minimum Spanning Tree Algorithms — Cut and cycle properties, Kruskal, Prim, and network design. (planned article)
  3. Network Flows and Minimum Cuts — Residual networks, augmenting paths, flow duality, and algorithms. (planned article)
  4. Bipartite Graphs and Matching — Hall's theorem, matchings, covers, and assignment problems. (planned article)
  5. Graph Coloring and Scheduling — Chromatic number, greedy methods, planar coloring, and timetabling. (planned article)

Planarity, Spectral Methods, and Network Analysis

The final group investigates global structure: embeddings and exclusions, eigenvalue-based analysis, random generative models, and measures of importance or clustering. Many quantities depend strongly on modeling assumptions, graph direction, and edge weights.

  1. Planar Graphs and Graph Minors — Embeddings, Euler's formula, obstructions, and structural theorems. (planned article)
  2. Spectral Graph Theory — Laplacians, eigenvalues, connectivity, and partitioning. (planned article)
  3. Random Graphs and Network Models — Probabilistic generation, expected properties, and network structure. (planned article)
  4. Graph Centrality and Community Detection — Centrality, partitions, cluster objectives, and interpretation limits. (planned article)

Model and Algorithm Selection

Graph theory is useful only when the model captures the actual relationship being studied. Direction, multiplicity, weight, time dependence, and feasibility constraints can change both the answer and which algorithm is mathematically justified. The following table helps distinguish similar-looking graph questions.

Research or design questionRelevant specialist topicKey condition or distinction
Can one component reach another, or which vertices are connected?Graph Traversal Algorithms; Paths, Cycles, and ConnectivityBreadth-first search gives shortest paths by edge count in an unweighted graph; depth-first search does not generally do so.
Can prerequisite tasks be put into a valid execution order?Directed Graphs and Topological SortingTopological order exists precisely when the directed graph is acyclic.
What is the least-cost route between two locations?Shortest Path AlgorithmsDijkstra's algorithm assumes nonnegative edge weights; negative weights require a suitable alternative and careful treatment of negative cycles.
What is the least-cost way to connect all network sites?Minimum Spanning Tree AlgorithmsA minimum spanning tree minimizes total selected edge weight, not distances between every pair of vertices.
What is the maximum feasible throughput of a capacitated network?Network Flows and Minimum CutsCapacities and flow conservation matter; the max-flow/min-cut theorem has specific assumptions.
Can jobs be assigned to workers without conflicts?Bipartite Graphs and MatchingA matching selects edges with disjoint endpoints; a perfect matching may not exist.
How few time slots can avoid simultaneous conflicts?Graph Coloring and SchedulingChromatic number characterizes a graph's minimum proper vertex coloring; scheduling constraints may require a richer model.
Is the graph structurally planar or amenable to a spectral partition?Planar Graphs and Graph Minors; Spectral Graph TheoryPlanarity is about crossing-free graph embeddings, whereas spectral analysis draws on matrix eigenstructure.
Which vertices appear most influential, or how strongly do communities emerge?Graph Centrality and Community DetectionCentrality and community scores depend on chosen definitions, sampling, direction, and weight assumptions; they are not direct measures of social value.
How might large networks arise under a probabilistic mechanism?Random Graphs and Network ModelsGeneration models imply particular distributions and assumptions; they do not automatically explain observed networks.

A complete solution should specify its graph definition, what must be optimized or established, the assumptions required by the method, and how its result can be checked. In operational settings, real-world interpretations and data-quality limits also deserve explicit attention.

Recommended Study Pathways

Mathematical foundation: start with Graph Definitions and Representations, continue through Degree Sequences and Graph Invariants and Paths, Cycles, and Connectivity, then study Trees and Spanning Trees. This sequence develops the vocabulary required for proof and algorithm correctness.

Algorithm implementation: proceed from Graph Traversal Algorithms to Directed Graphs and Topological Sorting, then compare Shortest Path Algorithms with Minimum Spanning Tree Algorithms. Understand the objective and algorithmic preconditions before choosing a library routine.

Optimization and scheduling: study Network Flows and Minimum Cuts, Bipartite Graphs and Matching, and Graph Coloring and Scheduling as distinct constraint and optimization families.

Advanced structural analysis: follow Planar Graphs and Graph Minors with Spectral Graph Theory, Random Graphs and Network Models, and Graph Centrality and Community Detection. This pathway benefits from the existing Linear Algebra for Computing and Probability and Statistics for Computing references.

Applied computing: use the existing Routing and Switching article when the goal is network operation, and Data Modeling when the goal is translating real-world entities and relationships into a data structure.

  • Probability and Statistics for Computing — Probabilistic models and inference.

Likewise, Graph Traversal Algorithms should focus on traversal invariants and representations while the separate Algorithm Analysis parent covers general complexity methodology.
