Collection Overview

The Graph Theory unit belongs to the Computational Foundations division of the Computer Science volume in the MGU Encyclopedia. It extends the existing Graph Theory overview, which introduces networks of vertices and edges, fundamental graph properties, traversal, optimization, and applied network questions. The parent remains the authoritative broad introduction; this directory is a specialist navigation and editorial plan.

A graph records entities and relationships under an explicitly chosen model. Its vertices may represent computers, tasks, people, junctions, or mathematical objects; its edges may represent physical links, precedence, similarity, or permitted interaction. The shape of the graph alone does not decide which interpretation is correct. Direction, weights, capacities, time dependence, and missing or uncertain data must be documented before an algorithm can produce a meaningful answer.

This page is a Level 4 unit index. Its 15 designated Level 5 specialist articles follow the existing parent inventory and are arranged in four groups: formal structures; traversal and dependencies; optimization and assignment; and structural or statistical network analysis. The groups are not new encyclopedia divisions, and none of the child titles or destination slugs has been changed.

Publication distinction: the specialist links are intentionally active planned URLs. They identify the expected location of future articles; they do not establish that those articles have been written or deployed. The parent overview and other encyclopedia entries remain unchanged.

Formal Structures, Connectivity, and Trees

The first four studies establish what a graph is, what can be proved about its shape, and which structures survive relabeling or deletion. They introduce graph vocabulary and proof tools before readers choose particular algorithms.

1. Graph Definitions and Representations

Begin from a precise graph specification: vertex set, edge set, whether edges are directed, whether loops or parallel edges are permitted, and what attributes vertices or edges carry. Contrast simple graphs, multigraphs, weighted graphs, and bipartite graphs without implying that one notation works unchanged for all. Study adjacency lists, matrices, edge lists, and incidence structures; compare their storage needs and their costs for adjacency testing, neighbor iteration, and updates. A dense matrix may be appropriate for frequent constant-time adjacency queries, while a sparse adjacency list normally avoids storing nonexistent edges. Explain that edge weights, capacities, and timestamps are additional semantics, not automatically present in an abstract graph.

2. Degree Sequences and Graph Invariants

Establish degrees for undirected graphs and in-degree/out-degree for directed graphs. Use the handshaking identity to derive that the sum of degrees in a finite undirected graph equals twice the number of edges, counting loop contributions under the selected convention. Examine graphical degree sequences and conditions such as those tested by Havel–Hakimi. Introduce invariants including component count, girth, diameter where defined, and degree multisets, while clarifying that matching invariants do not necessarily prove isomorphism. Include careful distinctions between graph labels, properties preserved by relabeling, and stronger methods needed to decide whether two finite graphs are isomorphic.

3. Paths, Cycles, and Connectivity

Distinguish a walk, trail, simple path, and cycle, explaining why some definitions vary by textbook. For undirected graphs, develop connected components, articulation vertices, bridges, and vertex- versus edge-connectivity. For directed graphs, distinguish one-way reachability, strong connectivity, and weak connectivity. Present what a cut separates, what a path certifies, and how structural decompositions support fault analysis in networks. Use examples to show why a graph may remain connected after one failed link but not after the removal of an articulation vertex. Keep shortest-path optimization in its own specialist article; this entry owns the definitions and existence of connecting structures.

4. Trees and Spanning Trees

Develop the equivalent finite undirected-tree properties: connected and acyclic; a unique simple path between each pair of vertices; and exactly n−1 edges for n vertices when connected. Explain rooted terminology—parent, child, depth, ancestor, leaf—as a choice of orientation on an underlying tree, not a different graph definition in every setting. Compare tree structures with general graphs and directed arborescences. A spanning tree selects all vertices of a connected graph using an acyclic subset of its edges; a disconnected graph instead has a spanning forest. This article treats mathematical structure, whereas the separate Minimum Spanning Tree Algorithms article owns weighted optimization.

Traversal and Directed Dependencies

Traversal converts formal relations into discoverable paths and orders. Direction, reachable state, and cycle constraints determine which procedural conclusions are justified.

1. Graph Traversal Algorithms

Explain queue-based breadth-first search and stack- or recursion-based depth-first search, with the colors or visitation states used to prevent repeated work. Describe BFS layers and why BFS returns minimum edge-count distances in an unweighted graph from a chosen source; do not extend that guarantee to arbitrary weighted graphs. Show how DFS discovery/finish times help expose cycles, components, and certain decomposition structures. With adjacency lists, each method can run in O(V+E) time when all vertices and edges are considered under standard assumptions. Distinguish a visit order from a graph invariant, and address traversal of disconnected graphs, memory usage, and recursion-depth limits.

2. Directed Graphs and Topological Sorting

Study how directed edges encode one-way dependencies, influence, or reachability. Define a directed acyclic graph (DAG) and show that a topological order exists exactly when a finite directed graph has no directed cycle. Compare Kahn's in-degree method with a DFS-based approach; both support linear-time processing with suitable representations. Explain why multiple valid topological orders may exist and why an ordering does not by itself solve resource-constrained scheduling. Introduce strongly connected components as maximal vertex sets mutually reachable by directed paths, whose condensation graph is acyclic. Tie these ideas to build systems, prerequisite structures, and workflow validation.

Optimization, Flows, Matching, and Coloring

These five articles study distinct objectives—minimum route cost, minimum connection cost, maximum throughput, maximum compatible assignments, and minimum conflict labels. Similar diagrams can encode different mathematical optimization problems.

1. Shortest Path Algorithms

Start by defining what path cost means and whether edge weights may be negative. Contrast BFS for unweighted edge counts, Dijkstra's algorithm for nonnegative edge weights, Bellman–Ford for graphs admitting negative edges, and all-pairs methods such as Floyd–Warshall under their assumptions. Give special attention to negative cycles reachable from a source: a finite shortest walk may not exist for affected destinations. Discuss predecessor trees, unreachable vertices, and route reconstruction. Separate shortest paths from minimum spanning trees: minimizing one route's cost does not generally minimize the total weight of an infrastructure network. Include the difference between theoretical path weights and variable real-world travel times.

2. Minimum Spanning Tree Algorithms

Define the minimum spanning tree problem for a connected, weighted, undirected graph: choose n−1 edges connecting all vertices without cycles and with minimum total weight. Explain why Kruskal's algorithm sorts eligible edges and uses disjoint-set data structures to avoid cycles, while Prim's algorithm grows a tree using a frontier of candidate edges. Present cut and cycle properties as the mathematical justification for greedy decisions, including how equal-weight edges may allow more than one optimal tree. Clarify that an MST need not minimize the path length between selected vertices; it solves a total connection-cost problem. Treat spanning forests explicitly when the graph is disconnected.

3. Network Flows and Minimum Cuts

Introduce source, sink, directed edge capacities, feasible flows, and conservation at intermediate vertices. Trace residual capacities, augmenting paths, and the idea that reverse residual edges allow earlier choices to be revised. State the max-flow/min-cut theorem for a standard finite capacitated network: the largest feasible source–sink flow equals the capacity of a minimum source–sink cut. Include a small numerical example and explain why a graph edge's capacity is not automatically its latency or construction cost. Clarify how flow algorithms differ from shortest-path and matching questions, even though several problems can be reduced to one another under precise transformations.

4. Bipartite Graphs and Matching

Define a bipartite graph by a partition into two vertex sets with edges only across the partition. Explain matching, maximum matching, perfect matching, and weighted matching as distinct objectives. Use a worker–task assignment example to build an augmenting path, showing why an initially plausible greedy assignment can be revised to increase cardinality. Introduce Hall's marriage theorem as a condition for matching every vertex on one designated side, and distinguish that condition from merely having equal partition sizes. Discuss the connection to network flow and note that additional time slots, skill constraints, preferences, or multiple assignment capacities can require extensions beyond a simple bipartite matching model.

5. Graph Coloring and Scheduling

Define a proper vertex coloring in which adjacent vertices receive different colors and the chromatic number as the minimum number of colors needed. Explain how an exam conflict graph maps courses to vertices and conflicting enrollments to edges; colors then represent nonconflicting time slots under a simplified model. Show why greedy coloring depends on vertex order and may use more colors than optimal. Include elementary bounds, structural examples such as bipartite graphs, and the distinction between vertex and edge coloring. For real schedules, warn that capacities, room assignments, instructor availability, and preferences add constraints that simple graph coloring does not capture.

Planarity, Spectral Methods, and Network Analysis

The advanced group examines graph embeddings, algebraic structure, random generation, and descriptive network measures. Mathematical guarantees, model-dependent findings, and empirical interpretations must be carefully separated.

1. Planar Graphs and Graph Minors

Explain planarity as the existence of a crossing-free drawing, not the absence of crossings in one particular drawing. For a connected planar graph, introduce Euler's formula V−E+F=2 with a clear convention that includes the outer face. Examine consequences for simple planar graphs, such as E≤3V−6 for V≥3, and discuss why parallel edges require caution with such bounds. Introduce Kuratowski-style forbidden subdivisions and graph-minor ideas at a conceptual level without collapsing two different characterizations into one claim. Distinguish graph topology from geographic coordinates: a road-network drawing may appear messy even when the underlying graph is planar.

2. Spectral Graph Theory

Define the adjacency matrix and (combinatorial) graph Laplacian for an undirected graph, then interpret basic spectral properties. The Laplacian has a zero eigenvalue for each connected component under the standard unweighted undirected formulation; the second-smallest eigenvalue of a connected graph is often used as a measure of connectivity. Discuss normalized Laplacians where vertex degrees vary and where comparisons require specific conventions. Show how eigenvectors can guide clustering or partitioning while stressing that an algorithmic partition is not automatically a ground-truth community. Keep linear algebra prerequisites explicit and distinguish structural eigenvalue facts from statistical inference.

3. Random Graphs and Network Models

Introduce the Erdős–Rényi G(n,p) model, in which each potential undirected edge is included independently with a specified probability, and contrast it with configuration-based, preferential-attachment, and small-world models. Explain expected degree in G(n,p), stochastic variability, and how model assumptions affect degree distributions, clustering, and path-length behavior. The appearance of an observed degree pattern does not establish its generating process. Clarify the difference between a random graph used to prove a mathematical existence result and a probabilistic generative model fitted to data. Include guidance on reporting graph size, sampling frame, edge definition, uncertainty, and reproducibility.

4. Graph Centrality and Community Detection

Compare centrality measures by the specific property they quantify: immediate degree, distance-based closeness, shortest-path brokerage, or recursive link importance. Warn that directed, weighted, and disconnected graphs require explicit conventions; ordinary closeness is not straightforwardly comparable when some nodes cannot be reached. Explain community detection as a family of objectives and heuristics—modularity-based methods, spectral techniques, and other clustering models—rather than a unique true partition. Discuss sensitivity to edge construction, missing data, sampling, and resolution choices. Avoid equating high centrality with authority, trustworthiness, or human merit without independent evidence.

Graph Models and Method Comparison

Before choosing a graph algorithm, distinguish the underlying graph model from the optimization or inference question. The following comparisons are especially important because superficially similar diagrams can require different mathematical tools.

Graph settingTypical representation or taskWhat must be specified
Undirected, unweighted graphComponents, shortest number of hops, connectivityWhether parallel edges or loops are permitted; whether all edges count equally
Directed graphReachability, precedence, topological orderEdge orientation; whether cycles are possible; strong versus weak connectivity
Weighted graphLeast-cost route or connection networkMeaning and sign of weights; shortest path versus minimum spanning tree
Capacitated networkMaximum feasible source–sink flowSource and sink, direction, capacities, conservation, and any lower bounds
Bipartite graphOne-to-one matching across two distinct setsSide membership, allowed pairs, and whether cardinality or cost is optimized
Conflict graphProper coloring and simplified schedulingWhich pairs conflict; capacities and other scheduling requirements not modeled
Observed or sampled networkCentrality, communities, or model fittingHow edges were measured; missing data; statistical assumptions

Analytic distinction: a minimum spanning tree can connect all sites cheaply without producing the shortest route between two sites; graph coloring can prevent pairwise conflicts without guaranteeing sufficient rooms or staff; and a centrality score is a model-dependent statistic rather than an independent proof of real-world importance.

Illustrative Graph Theory Scenarios

These examples are constructed teaching cases. They illustrate how to move from a real question to the correct mathematical object and then to a justified answer. They are not observations from a particular MGU network, classroom, or deployment.

Scenario 1 — Building an accurate road-map graph

Suppose a small campus has five intersections and six walkways. If each walkway can be traversed in both directions, an undirected graph may be appropriate; if a service road is one-way, at least that edge requires direction. A matrix could encode adjacency for all five vertices, but an adjacency list may be more economical for a much larger and sparse map. The first step is not to run a routing algorithm—it is to define intersections, edges, accessibility rules, and whether travel cost means number of segments, distance, or time. Consult Graph Definitions and Representations before Shortest Path Algorithms.

Scenario 2 — Testing a network for resilience

An undirected communications graph contains two internally well-connected areas joined by a single edge. That edge is a bridge: removing it increases the number of connected components. An engineer should first run or reason about connectivity, then identify critical edges or articulation vertices. Simply measuring the number of links at each site does not establish resilience: a high-degree vertex can coexist with one vulnerable inter-area link. Paths, Cycles, and Connectivity provides definitions; Graph Traversal Algorithms supplies systematic searches that support practical discovery.

Scenario 3 — Scheduling courses with prerequisites

Consider prerequisites A before B and C, followed by both B and C before D. The directed dependency graph has edges A→B, A→C, B→D, and C→D. Both A, B, C, D and A, C, B, D are valid topological orders. The sequence B, A, C, D is invalid because it violates A→B. A cycle such as D→A would eliminate every topological order. This formal method checks precedence, not the actual semester, instructor, or room allocation; those are additional constraints. See Directed Graphs and Topological Sorting.

Scenario 4 — Choosing routes versus infrastructure

Let three sites form a triangle with edges of weights 2, 3, and 10. The minimum spanning tree takes the edges of weights 2 and 3, making a connection of total weight 5. That is a total-network objective. When a person asks for the shortest route between two sites, the best path must be computed between the particular endpoints and can differ from the route along a selected spanning tree. In larger graphs the distinction is even more consequential. Read Minimum Spanning Tree Algorithms and Shortest Path Algorithms side by side.

Scenario 5 — Assigning people to tasks

Three technicians can perform different subsets of three maintenance tasks. Draw a bipartite graph with technicians on one side and tasks on the other; add an edge only for an authorized, feasible assignment. A matching is a collection of nonconflicting technician–task pairs. If a greedy first assignment blocks another task, an augmenting path may allow earlier choices to be rearranged. Hall’s condition helps decide when every task can be matched in a simple one-to-one case. For multi-shift or multiple-capacity work, the model may need expansion. Start with Bipartite Graphs and Matching.

Scenario 6 — Distinguishing maximum flow from cut capacity

A directed network has source S, sink T, two intermediate branches, and capacities on each link. Sending too much along one branch violates a capacity constraint even if another branch is underused. A feasible flow must conserve total inflow and outflow at intermediate vertices. A source–sink cut provides an upper bound on all feasible flows; the max-flow/min-cut theorem guarantees equality at the optimum under the standard model. This method does not automatically account for packets, queueing, or variable travel times. Study Network Flows and Minimum Cuts.

Scenario 7 — Interpreting an apparent network community

A sample of online interactions appears to contain two clusters. A community-detection algorithm returns a partition, but the result depends on which interactions were recorded, whether edges are weighted or directed, and the algorithm’s resolution preferences. Degree centrality may favor heavily active accounts while betweenness centrality may emphasize connectors between clusters. Random-network baselines can help test whether a structure is unusual under a chosen model, not establish its social meaning. Combine Graph Centrality and Community Detection with Random Graphs and Network Models and Spectral Graph Theory.

Model and Algorithm Selection Framework

A productive investigation identifies the graph, its semantics, and the desired result before choosing an algorithm. Use this decision table as an entry point, then confirm the assumptions in the specialist article.

QuestionPrimary methodKey assumption or caution
Are two vertices reachable?Paths, Cycles, and ConnectivitySpecify direction, and distinguish reachability from shortest distance.
In what order can dependent tasks execute?Directed Graphs and Topological SortingA topological ordering exists only for a directed acyclic graph.
What is the fewest number of links from S to T?Graph Traversal AlgorithmsBFS minimizes edge count when edges are unweighted.
What is the cheapest weighted path?Shortest Path AlgorithmsCheck whether edge weights are nonnegative and whether negative cycles matter.
What is the minimum-weight network connecting all sites?Minimum Spanning Tree AlgorithmsUse a weighted undirected model; distinguish total tree cost from route cost.
How much flow can pass from a source to a sink?Network Flows and Minimum CutsCapacities, direction, and conservation must be modeled.
Can all tasks be matched one-to-one?Bipartite Graphs and MatchingCheck the bipartition and augmenting-path or Hall conditions.
How many nonconflicting slots are needed?Graph Coloring and SchedulingA proper coloring covers pairwise conflicts, not every operational constraint.
Can the graph be drawn without crossing edges?Planar Graphs and Graph MinorsPlanarity concerns existence of an embedding, not one drawing.
How do matrix properties reveal graph structure?Spectral Graph TheoryState the matrix and normalization convention.
What generative mechanism might explain edge patterns?Random Graphs and Network ModelsProbabilistic mechanisms must be checked against observations and sampling bias.
Who appears central and which clusters are present?Graph Centrality and Community DetectionDefinitions and data quality determine the meaning of reported scores.

Recommended procedure: (1) identify real-world entities and relations; (2) decide direction, weight, capacity, and time assumptions; (3) select the exact mathematical objective; (4) justify the algorithm and its complexity under the chosen representation; (5) verify results with invariants or certificates; and (6) evaluate how missing data or omitted constraints affect the practical conclusion. For difficult optimization problems, distinguish proven optimality from heuristic quality.

Suggested Study Pathways

Foundational mathematical pathway: start with the Graph Theory parent overview, then Graph Definitions and Representations, Degree Sequences and Graph Invariants, Paths, Cycles, and Connectivity, and Trees and Spanning Trees. Practice stating graph conventions, proving basic identities, and detecting counterexamples to overgeneralized statements.

Algorithms and software engineering pathway: follow Graph Traversal Algorithms and Directed Graphs and Topological Sorting with Shortest Path Algorithms. Connect adjacency representations to time and memory costs; verify that a traversal order, dependency order, or predecessor tree matches the intended question. The separate Algorithm Analysis entry owns general proof and complexity techniques.

Infrastructure and operations pathway: study Minimum Spanning Tree Algorithms, Network Flows and Minimum Cuts, and Bipartite Graphs and Matching. Compare connection cost, throughput, and assignment feasibility as different optimization goals. Use Graph Coloring and Scheduling where pairwise conflicts define a useful first approximation to scheduling.

Advanced structural pathway: proceed to Planar Graphs and Graph Minors and Spectral Graph Theory. Record when theorems apply to undirected simple graphs, whether connectedness is assumed, and what matrix normalization or embedding convention supports a claim.

Networks and empirical research pathway: read Random Graphs and Network Models before Graph Centrality and Community Detection. Separate observed network properties from the proposed mechanism that generated them; include data provenance and sensitivity to sampling, graph construction, and parameter choices.

Instructional application: for each pathway, write down a tiny graph, compute a result by hand, and compare it with a clearly stated formal definition. Then change one assumption—direction, an edge weight, a missing observation, or a dependency—and explain why the answer or the appropriate method changes.

The dedicated networking and data-system subjects retain their operational applications.
