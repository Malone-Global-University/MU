Collection Overview

This directory is the companion collection for the Linear Algebra for Computing overview in the Computational Foundations category of the MGU Computer Science Encyclopedia. It organizes the mathematical principles and numerical methods used to represent vectors and matrices, solve linear systems, analyze transformations, and build computational applications.

The parent article specifies 15 planned subarticles. This index retains their exact titles, descriptions, and destination filenames, and divides them into four instructional groups. The hierarchy is intentional: each child examines one part of the parent subject in greater depth rather than duplicating an established neighboring parent such as Graph Theory or Machine Learning.

Editorial status: the links below are active destination links to planned specialist pages. Inclusion does not establish that those pages have been written or published. For introductory coverage, use the existing parent overview.

Vectors, Spaces, and Linear Structure

Build the language of vectors, linear maps, and solvability before choosing a numerical algorithm. Coordinate conventions and matrix dimensions matter because many apparent mathematical errors are representation errors.

  5. Rank, Nullspaces, and Linear Independence — Four fundamental spaces, nullity, and numerical rank. (planned subarticle)

Orthogonality, Factorizations, and Fitting

These subjects provide the numerical and geometric tools for solving systems, projecting observations, and fitting data. A matrix decomposition is not merely an algebraic identity: its assumptions and numerical properties determine when it is useful.

  2. LU, QR, and Cholesky Factorizations — Structured direct methods and their assumptions. (planned subarticle)

Selecting a Mathematical Method

Problems in computing often involve a matrix, but the existence of a matrix does not identify the correct method. First characterize the question, the structure and size of the data, the assumptions, and the accuracy requirement. The following table is a planning aid rather than a substitute for a numerical analysis.

Problem to solveStart withImportant qualification
Find an exact symbolic solution or establish whether a small linear system is consistent.Gaussian Elimination and Linear Systems; Rank, Nullspaces, and Linear IndependenceFloating-point computation can alter the apparent rank of an ill-conditioned matrix.
Solve a well-posed dense linear system repeatedly.LU, QR, and Cholesky FactorizationsCholesky requires an appropriate positive-definite matrix; pivoting and numerical stability affect other cases.
Fit a model when measurements outnumber unknown parameters.Least Squares and Pseudoinverses; Orthogonality and Projection MethodsRank deficiency, outliers, and measurement quality can affect interpretation and solution choice.
Identify major directions of variance or approximate a matrix at lower rank.Singular Value Decomposition; Principal Component AnalysisPCA needs attention to centering, scaling, and interpretability; large variance is not necessarily predictive value.
Estimate stability or dominant modes of a transformation.Eigenvalues and EigenvectorsNot all matrices are diagonalizable, and eigenvalue sensitivity can be substantial.
Solve very large sparse systems.Sparse Matrices and Iterative SolversConvergence and preconditioning depend on matrix properties; no solver is universally best.
Differentiate a matrix-valued model or optimize its parameters.Matrix Calculus and OptimizationSpecify dimensions and derivative conventions; account for numerical and optimization error.
Apply matrix operations to rendering, embeddings, or learned systems.Linear Algebra for Machine Learning and GraphicsSeparate mathematical transformations from the domain-specific conventions of model training or graphics pipelines.

Numerical Stability and Matrix Conditioning is a cross-cutting subject. A small computed residual alone does not always imply a small forward error; sensitivity of the mathematical problem must also be considered.

Suggested Study Pathways

Foundations pathway: Begin with Vectors, Norms, and Inner Products; then study Vector Spaces, Bases, and Dimension and Matrices and Linear Transformations. Continue to Gaussian Elimination and Linear Systems, followed by Rank, Nullspaces, and Linear Independence. These articles establish the vocabulary and proof ideas needed throughout the collection.

Scientific and numerical computing pathway: After the foundations, study Orthogonality and Projection Methods, LU/QR/Cholesky Factorizations, and Least Squares and Pseudoinverses. Pair these with Numerical Stability and Matrix Conditioning before moving to Sparse Matrices and Iterative Solvers for large problems. Verify numerical behavior with examples rather than relying only on symbolic reasoning.

Data and machine-learning pathway: Build the foundations, then progress from Least Squares and Pseudoinverses to Singular Value Decomposition and Principal Component Analysis. Continue to Matrix Calculus and Optimization and Linear Algebra for Machine Learning and Graphics. Distinguish descriptive dimensionality reduction from supervised predictive modeling.

Graphics and transformations pathway: Emphasize Matrices and Linear Transformations, Vectors/Norms/Inner Products, and Orthogonality and Projection Methods before exploring transformation conventions, camera matrices, and numerical implementation in Linear Algebra for Machine Learning and Graphics.

Each route is a suggested sequence, not a prerequisite system imposed on unfinished articles. The parent article remains the current broad reference for the subject.

Scope and Related Encyclopedia Topics

Linear Algebra for Computing explains mathematical structure, numerical representation, and associated computational methods. Its specialist articles should avoid duplicating the entire Graph Theory, Machine Learning, or Computer Graphics curricula. Instead, they should deepen the matrix and vector foundations of those subjects and cross-reference the established overviews where disciplinary detail begins.

  • Discrete Mathematics — Sets, functions, proof techniques, and discrete structures supporting mathematical computation.
  • Graph Theory — Graph structures and algorithms; adjacency and Laplacian matrices are selected bridges into linear algebra.
  • Algorithm Analysis — Correctness, time and space growth, and performance evaluation distinct from numerical conditioning.
  • Complexity Theory — Complexity classes and computational feasibility as distinct from error sensitivity.
  • Probability and Statistics for Computing — Random variables, estimation, and statistical inference that complement PCA and least squares.
  • Data Modeling — Semantic and structural organization of data before its numerical representation.
  • Information Retrieval — Vector search, rankings, and matrix-based information methods.
  • Machine Learning — Learning from data through mathematical models and computational optimization.
  • Computer Graphics — Geometric transformations, projections, and visual computational systems.

The difference between a mathematical problem and a computational algorithm is central to this collection. An algorithm may solve a mathematically well-posed problem poorly because of finite precision, and a mathematically correct formula may be impractical for large data. Readers should assess both the problem's conditioning and the method's stability.
