Collection Overview

The Linear Algebra for Computing unit belongs to the Computational Foundations division of the Computer Science volume in the MGU Encyclopedia. It extends the established Linear Algebra for Computing parent overview, which introduces vectors, matrices, linear systems, orthogonality, decompositions, eigenproblems, numerical computation, and applications. The parent remains the introductory synthesis; this directory organizes deeper specialist treatment.

Linear algebra describes relationships that are linear in an explicitly chosen space, but those relationships serve many different purposes. A vector may encode a geometric displacement, a collection of measurements, a feature embedding, or a numerical state. A matrix may express a system of simultaneous equations, a change of basis, a discrete operator, or a learned transformation. The mathematical forms overlap, but their dimensions, assumptions, units, and validation criteria differ. A practical computing treatment must identify those assumptions before recommending a numerical method.

The titles, grouping, and extensionless public destination slugs are preserved from the previous unit index and parent plan. Vectors, Spaces, and Linear Structure

The first group establishes the objects and equalities on which later numerical methods depend. The articles proceed from vectors and spaces to linear maps, systems, and the rank structure that governs solvability. Mathematical definitions are distinguished from the behavior of finite-precision software.

1. Vectors, Norms, and Inner Products

Explain vector addition, scalar multiplication, dot products, inner products, Euclidean and general norms, distances, orthogonality, and the distinction between vectors and their coordinate descriptions. Show how cosine similarity compares directions while discarding magnitude and why normalization may be useful for embedding comparisons. Distinguish geometric intuition in two or three dimensions from arbitrary-dimensional spaces and introduce complex inner-product conventions where relevant. Numerical examples must state shape, norm, and units before drawing conclusions.

Article boundary: Primary editorial boundary: this article owns vector measures and geometry; it does not become a full treatment of feature engineering, search ranking, or statistical estimation.

2. Vector Spaces, Bases, and Dimension

Develop subspaces, span, linear independence, bases, dimension, coordinates, and change of basis. Prove why the number of vectors in a basis is fixed for a finite-dimensional space and explain how a basis expresses the same abstract vector through different coordinate systems. Examples should include a subspace of a Euclidean space, column-space interpretation, and a dependent generating family. Distinguish exact linear dependence from near-dependence detected by a floating-point computation.

Article boundary: Primary editorial boundary: emphasize the mathematical structure of spaces; numerical decisions about effective rank belong principally to Rank, Nullspaces, and Linear Independence and numerical-stability studies.

3. Matrices and Linear Transformations

Treat matrices as coordinate representations of linear transformations while also explaining matrix storage, dimensions, transpose, multiplication, and composition. Illustrate how a matrix maps a vector, why multiplication order matters, and how changes of basis alter the matrix but not the underlying linear map. Differentiate a linear transformation from an affine translation; introduce homogeneous coordinates only as a bridge to graphics applications. Show how shape mismatches reveal invalid operations before any numerical calculations begin.

Article boundary: Primary editorial boundary: matrix operations and linear maps are the focus; detailed rendering pipelines and image transformations remain topics of the Computer Graphics overview.

4. Gaussian Elimination and Linear Systems

Introduce the equation Ax = b and classify existence and uniqueness through pivots, row operations, augmented matrices, and reduced echelon form. Derive elimination on small systems, discuss partial pivoting and singular or inconsistent cases, and explain how a computed solution may be checked by the residual b − Ax. For computation, distinguish solving a system from forming an explicit matrix inverse and distinguish exact arithmetic demonstrations from stable library-backed numerical solvers.

Article boundary: Primary editorial boundary: the mechanics of elimination and solvability take precedence; factorization reuse, conditioning, and sparse methods are developed in their dedicated children.

5. Rank, Nullspaces, and Linear Independence

Develop column rank, row rank, kernel/nullspace, rank–nullity, image, and the four fundamental subspaces of a real matrix. Connect the rank of an m × n matrix to consistent systems, free variables, degrees of freedom, and uniqueness of a least-squares fit. Separate structural rank over an exact field from effective numerical rank under measurement noise and floating-point thresholds. Use a small redundant-column example to explain how a matrix can represent fewer independent directions than its dimension suggests.

Article boundary: Primary editorial boundary: center rank and nullspace analysis; extensive singular-value algorithms belong in Singular Value Decomposition.

Orthogonality, Factorizations, and Fitting

The second group studies geometry and direct numerical methods for fitting observations and solving systems. It treats orthogonality as a structural tool, not merely a diagram, and describes which matrix assumptions justify particular factorizations.

1. Orthogonality and Projection Methods

Define orthogonal and orthonormal vectors, projections onto lines and subspaces, and projection matrices. Explain how an orthogonal projection separates a vector into fitted and residual components with a geometric orthogonality condition. Present Gram–Schmidt and modified Gram–Schmidt as conceptually related methods, noting that classical implementations can lose orthogonality in finite precision. Connect projections to QR decomposition and least squares without duplicating their complete computational treatments.

Article boundary: Primary editorial boundary: the projection geometry and construction of orthonormal bases are central; estimation objectives and fit interpretation are concentrated in Least Squares and Pseudoinverses.

2. LU, QR, and Cholesky Factorizations

Compare LU with pivoting, QR, and Cholesky as structured ways to decompose matrices for repeated solving, stability, or specialized structure. State that Cholesky requires a symmetric or Hermitian positive-definite matrix, while QR is useful for least squares and orthogonal transformations. Explain why a numerical library may select different routines for square, rectangular, sparse, and rank-deficient inputs. Describe computational cost and storage qualitatively, and ensure any claimed advantage is paired with its prerequisites.

Article boundary: Primary editorial boundary: this article owns the factorization methods and assumptions; broader solver selection, sparse storage, and measurement sensitivity are cross-referenced rather than repeated.

3. Least Squares and Pseudoinverses

Explain why an overdetermined system may lack an exact solution and how least squares minimizes the squared Euclidean residual norm. Derive the orthogonality condition Aᵀ(Ax − b) = 0, describe least-squares fitting with QR and SVD, and note why solving normal equations explicitly can magnify conditioning problems. Introduce Moore–Penrose pseudoinverses and minimum-norm solutions for underdetermined or rank-deficient systems. Distinguish fitting error, predictive validity, and the meaning of the chosen loss function.

Article boundary: Primary editorial boundary: matrix fitting and minimum-norm solutions are the focus; statistical inference, model selection, and machine-learning evaluation belong to neighboring subjects.

Eigenproblems and Dimensionality Reduction

The third group concerns important directions, invariant subspaces, matrix decompositions, and reduced representations. A spectral quantity is meaningful only relative to the chosen matrix, normalization, and analytical objective.

1. Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

Define eigenpairs Av = λv with nonzero v, characteristic equations, eigenspaces, diagonalization conditions, and geometric meaning. Discuss symmetric or Hermitian matrices and their orthogonal eigenbases, while warning that arbitrary real nonsymmetric matrices need not have real eigenvalues or a full eigenbasis. Connect eigenvalues to repeated transformations, stability, vibration modes, and certain graph matrices. Explicitly distinguish eigenvectors from singular vectors, which are defined through SVD for general rectangular matrices.

Article boundary: Primary editorial boundary: focus on eigenproblems and their valid interpretations; PCA and numerical conditioning have dedicated specialist treatments.

2. Singular Value Decomposition

Present the decomposition A = UΣVᵀ in the real case, singular values in nonincreasing order, left and right singular vectors, and its availability for rectangular matrices. Explain the link between singular values, matrix norm, rank, the pseudoinverse, and low-rank approximation. Show why truncating small singular values can reduce noise sensitivity but also discard information, depending on the task. Clarify that SVD may support PCA after suitable data centering and that singular vectors need not be eigenvectors of A itself.

Article boundary: Primary editorial boundary: SVD owns the general factorization and low-rank mathematics; PCA owns statistical preprocessing and component interpretation.

3. Principal Component Analysis

Describe PCA as a method for finding orthogonal directions of maximal sample variance after choosing how observations and features are represented. Explain centering, the role of scaling, the covariance matrix, eigenanalysis or SVD implementation, and variance explained. Illustrate how a data matrix with redundant dimensions may be approximated in a lower-dimensional space. Discuss interpretability, sensitivity to outliers and units, and why high explained variance is not a guarantee of predictive relevance or fairness.

Article boundary: Primary editorial boundary: the statistical objective and interpretation of PCA are central; the mechanics of SVD and model training pipelines are treated elsewhere.

Numerical Methods and Computing Applications

The fourth group connects the mathematics to reliable large-scale computation. It studies sparsity, floating-point sensitivity, gradients, and applied matrix workflows without duplicating the broad machine-learning or computer-graphics disciplines.

1. Sparse Matrices and Iterative Solvers

Explain sparse representations such as compressed sparse row and compressed sparse column layouts, and how they avoid storing every zero. Contrast direct sparse factorizations with iterative families such as conjugate gradient and GMRES, specifying the symmetry and positive-definiteness requirements relevant to each method. Explain stopping tolerances, residuals, preconditioners, fill-in, matrix-free operators, and why algorithm choice depends on structure and available memory. Illustrate sparse graph and discretized physical-system matrices without equating all sparse matrices with adjacency matrices.

Article boundary: Primary editorial boundary: focus on representation and numerical solution of sparse systems; graph traversal and graph-theoretic properties remain the scope of Graph Theory.

2. Numerical Stability and Matrix Conditioning

Distinguish problem conditioning from algorithmic stability: a well-designed algorithm may still yield a sensitive result when the input matrix is nearly singular. Introduce absolute and relative errors, residuals, backward error, floating-point roundoff, and condition numbers. Explain why small residuals do not guarantee small forward errors and why accurate matrix shapes, units, and scales matter. Use a nearly dependent linear system to illustrate uncertainty amplification and discuss practical diagnostics rather than promising numerical certainty.

Article boundary: Primary editorial boundary: this article supplies cross-cutting numerical judgment applicable to all matrix computations; it is not an isolated proof of the superiority of any particular factorization.

3. Matrix Calculus and Optimization

Develop matrix and vector differentiation with clear shape conventions for gradients, Jacobians, and Hessians. Use the squared-error objective to derive a gradient, then connect it to gradient-based optimization, regularization, and differentiable computational graphs. Distinguish symbolic matrix calculus from software automatic differentiation and show how transposes and broadcasting can cause shape errors. Discuss smoothness and nonconvexity as modeling issues rather than assuming that a gradient method always identifies a global optimum.

Article boundary: Primary editorial boundary: differential tools for matrix-valued problems are central; complete optimization theory and machine-learning training remain in their own encyclopedia areas.

4. Linear Algebra for Machine Learning and Graphics

Trace practical uses of vectors, matrices, embeddings, linear layers, and geometric transformations in computing applications. Explain how data are arranged into samples and features for matrix multiplication and how graphics pipelines use transformations and homogeneous coordinates. Compare shared algebraic building blocks with differing application-specific meanings: a matrix may encode a 3D transform, a feature projection, or a learned weight map. Note dimensional correctness, normalization, data dependence, numerical cost, and downstream validation.

Article boundary: Primary editorial boundary: act as a concrete applications bridge, not a duplicate of the full Machine Learning or Computer Graphics parent subjects.

Illustrative Computational Linear Algebra Scenarios

The examples below link a mathematical question to the specialist article that can develop it. The numerical data are deliberately small so that the reasoning can be checked by hand; real implementations need validated library routines, appropriate precision, and performance testing.

Example 1 — Two perpendicular vectors and a similarity score

Let a = (3, 4) and b = (4, −3). Their dot product is 3×4 + 4×(−3) = 0; both Euclidean norms are 5. Therefore their Euclidean cosine similarity is 0 / (5×5) = 0, and the vectors are orthogonal. This is a geometric conclusion under the specified inner product, not a guarantee that records represented by those vectors have no meaningful relationship. Start with Vectors, Norms, and Inner Products and connect the interpretation to Vector Spaces, Bases, and Dimension.

Example 2 — A system of equations and its unique solution

Consider 2x + y = 5 and x − y = 1. Adding the equations gives 3x = 6, so x = 2 and y = 1. The coefficient matrix [[2,1],[1,−1]] has nonzero determinant −3, agreeing with a unique solution in exact arithmetic. For larger or floating-point problems, the correct solver needs pivoting and numerical judgment. Compare Gaussian Elimination and Linear Systems, Rank, Nullspaces, and Linear Independence, and LU, QR, and Cholesky Factorizations.

Example 3 — Fitting a line to inconsistent observations

Suppose three observations have input values 0, 1, 2 and measured outputs 1, 2, 2. No single line passes through all three points. A least-squares line has slope 1/2 and intercept 7/6, predicting 7/6, 5/3, 13/6. The residuals observed minus predicted are −1/6, 1/3, −1/6, whose sum is zero and whose input-weighted sum is also zero: the residual vector is orthogonal to the two design-matrix columns. Read Orthogonality and Projection Methods and Least Squares and Pseudoinverses. Statistical fitness is a separate question from the mathematical minimizer.

Example 4 — A nearly singular matrix and misleading accuracy

Consider the matrix [[1,1],[1,1.00000001]]. Its columns are very close to linearly dependent, so small input or rounding changes can have outsized effects on a computed solution. A solver may produce a tiny residual while the estimated solution remains sensitive to perturbations. Exact rank, numerical rank, residual size, and condition number answer different questions. Study Rank, Nullspaces, and Linear Independence, Singular Value Decomposition, and Numerical Stability and Matrix Conditioning before trusting the number of printed digits.

Example 5 — PCA finds redundant coordinates

Four centered sample points (2,0), (1,0), (−1,0), (−2,0) lie on a single axis. Their variance in the second coordinate is zero; the horizontal direction captures all sample variance. A one-dimensional PCA representation is therefore lossless for these four points under the chosen Euclidean coordinates. This does not show that an unseen dataset will have the same structure or that the retained dimension is useful for a particular predictive task. Compare Principal Component Analysis with Singular Value Decomposition.

Example 6 — Why sparse structure changes computational cost

A tridiagonal 10,000 × 10,000 matrix has 10,000 diagonal entries and 9,999 entries on each adjacent diagonal: at most 29,998 stored nonzero positions if those coefficients are nonzero. A dense representation allocates space for 100,000,000 positions. The sparse layout avoids storing structural zeros, although indexing, arithmetic, fill-in, and convergence determine the actual resource savings. An appropriate choice of sparse direct or iterative solver depends on symmetry, definiteness, and the problem's numerical properties. See Sparse Matrices and Iterative Solvers.

Example 7 — Translating a graphic with homogeneous coordinates

A two-dimensional point (2,1) translated by (3,−2) becomes (5,−1). Translation is affine rather than linear on ordinary 2D vectors because it does not map the origin to the origin. A 3 × 3 homogeneous-coordinate matrix can represent the operation by multiplying (2,1,1). The construction illustrates why graphics uses matrix operations together with clear coordinate conventions, and why a transformation's algebraic type matters. Read Matrices and Linear Transformations and Linear Algebra for Machine Learning and Graphics.

Example 8 — Differentiating a matrix-based loss function

For the real-valued least-squares objective f(x) = ½‖Ax − b‖²₂, the gradient is Aᵀ(Ax − b). It has the same shape as the parameter vector x, so a shape check is part of verifying the formula. A stationary point satisfies the normal-equation condition, but computing the solution numerically by explicitly forming AᵀA may be less stable than using QR or SVD. This bridges Matrix Calculus and Optimization and Least Squares and Pseudoinverses without treating differentiation as a complete optimization plan.

All eight scenarios are instructional constructs. Where a mathematical identity is exact, finite-precision implementations still require tolerances and validated numerical routines.

Mathematical Method Selection and Investigation Framework

Matrix computation should begin with a precise problem statement. The questions below help identify the right part of the specialist directory before a technique is chosen.

Question to answerDistinction requiredRead first
What is the mathematical object?Vector, subspace, matrix, linear map, sampled data, or affine transformVectors, Norms, and Inner Products
What exactly must be produced?Solution, projection, minimizer, dominant direction, factorization, or derivativeMatrices and Linear Transformations
Are all constraints compatible?Consistent, inconsistent, underdetermined, or rank-deficient systemRank, Nullspaces, and Linear Independence
Can special matrix structure be used?Symmetric/Hermitian, positive definite, triangular, sparse, or rectangularLU, QR, and Cholesky Factorizations
Is the result an approximation?Least-squares residual, truncation error, retained variance, or statistical uncertaintyLeast Squares and Pseudoinverses
Could input or rounding error dominate?Backward versus forward error, conditioning, precision, and scalingNumerical Stability and Matrix Conditioning
Is the matrix too large for dense operations?Sparsity, fill-in, memory budget, solver stopping rules, and preconditioningSparse Matrices and Iterative Solvers
Does the application need interpretable principal directions?Eigenvectors, singular vectors, or variance-based PCA componentsPrincipal Component Analysis
Will gradients guide an iterative method?Variable shapes, differentiability, objective, and termination criterionMatrix Calculus and Optimization

Suggested investigation order: define dimensions and data types; verify units and orientation; state the exact mathematical objective; identify structure, rank, and numerical conditioning; choose a library-backed algorithm; check residuals or reconstruction error; compare against an independent small example; then evaluate resource use and domain-specific meaning. A method should not be promoted merely because it produces a numerically plausible answer on one dataset.

Suggested Study Pathways

Foundational computer-science pathway: Begin with the parent overview, then Vectors, Norms, and Inner Products, Vector Spaces, Bases, and Dimension, and Matrices and Linear Transformations. Continue to Gaussian Elimination and Linear Systems and Rank, Nullspaces, and Linear Independence. The objective is to recognize valid operations, understand dimension and dependence, and test a small system's solvability before considering scalable implementation.

Numerical and scientific computing pathway: Study Orthogonality and Projection Methods, LU, QR, and Cholesky Factorizations, and Least Squares and Pseudoinverses. Then pair Numerical Stability and Matrix Conditioning with Sparse Matrices and Iterative Solvers. Compare exact hand calculations with numerical outputs and document whether the dominant limitation comes from the mathematical problem, available memory, or the chosen algorithm.

Data analysis and dimensionality reduction pathway: After a grounding in vectors and matrices, follow Singular Value Decomposition, Principal Component Analysis, and Least Squares and Pseudoinverses. Include Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors to understand covariance eigenproblems and links between different decompositions. Study centering, scaling, sample variability, reconstruction error, and the meaning of high or low variance before interpreting components as discoveries.

Machine learning and differentiable computation pathway: Combine Matrices and Linear Transformations with Matrix Calculus and Optimization and Linear Algebra for Machine Learning and Graphics. Review Numerical Stability and Matrix Conditioning whenever training or inference involves nearly singular data, mixed precision, or repeated matrix operations. Matrix algebra explains transformations; the validity and effects of a trained model require separate application-level evidence.

Geometry and computer graphics pathway: Emphasize Vectors, Norms, and Inner Products, Matrices and Linear Transformations, and Orthogonality and Projection Methods before Linear Algebra for Machine Learning and Graphics. Distinguish vectors, points, coordinate systems, linear maps, and affine transformations; document homogeneous-coordinate and row-versus-column conventions when transferring computations between graphics systems.

Graph and sparse computation pathway: Combine matrix fundamentals with Sparse Matrices and Iterative Solvers and Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors. Compare this mathematical formulation with the separate Graph Theory overview, which owns the broad structural graph subject. A graph's adjacency or Laplacian matrix may serve as an input to numerical linear algebra, but graph connectivity and graph-algorithm correctness have their own conceptual framework.
