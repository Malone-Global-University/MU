Collection Overview

The Data Representation unit belongs to the Computational Foundations division of the Computer Science volume of the MGU Encyclopedia. It extends the existing Data Representation parent article, which introduces the conventions by which numbers, symbols, images, sounds, and other information become patterns a computer can store, process, and exchange. That comprehensive introduction remains unchanged.

Representation is a contract, not merely a collection of bits. The same eight bits 11111111 can mean unsigned 255 or signed −1 in eight-bit two’s complement; the interpretation depends on the declared type. Likewise, a four-byte sequence may identify different multibyte values under different byte-order rules, and a sequence of text code points may have a normalized equivalent without being byte-identical. Correct design therefore names the value type, layout, encoding, and relevant semantic rules before interpreting data.

This page is the Level 4 unit index. It preserves 18 previously designated Level 5 specialist article titles and destination slugs, organized into numerical representation, binary layout, text and multimedia, and semantic interoperability. The corresponding hyperlinks are intentionally active future publication routes. This index does not establish that the child articles are already written or available online.

The unit’s scope is the relationship between information and its representation. Compression, encryption, physical storage, and end-to-end data processing have their own encyclopedic treatments. These topics intersect in practice, but they are not interchangeable. A file format may combine several techniques; analysis should identify which part of the system is responsible for each transformation.

Numerical Values and Finite Precision

A numeric format is a contract between a mathematical value and the finite operations a computer can perform. These five specialist articles explain positional notation, signedness, scaling, rounding, and exceptional range behavior. Their common boundary is the representation of a value, not a full treatment of numerical algorithms.

1. Positional Number Systems and Base Conversion

Explain how a radix assigns weights to digit positions and how an integer can be written in binary, octal, decimal, or hexadecimal without changing its mathematical value. Develop repeated-division and place-value conversion techniques, binary grouping, and the relationship between a written hexadecimal dump and the byte pattern it describes. Clarify that a displayed numeral is a notation, whereas a machine representation additionally specifies width, interpretation, and sometimes endianness. Treat fractions cautiously: a terminating decimal expansion need not terminate in base two.

2. Signed Integer Representation and Two’s Complement

Compare sign-and-magnitude, ones’ complement, and two’s complement under a fixed bit width. Derive the usual n-bit two’s-complement range from −2^(n−1) to 2^(n−1)−1, and show why the top bit contributes a negative positional weight in this interpretation. Discuss negation, zero, sign extension, and the difference between interpreting bits as a signed value and changing the bits themselves. Separate mathematical wraparound properties from the overflow rules of a particular programming language or processor.

3. Fixed-Point Representation and Scaling

Establish the scale factor that maps a stored integer to its represented value, including decimal scales for currency and binary scales for control or signal processing. Explain units, rounding when converting between scales, intermediate-width requirements during multiplication, and why integer storage does not by itself prevent overflow. Contrast explicit decimal-currency requirements with binary fixed-point signal pipelines. Show how an apparently harmless change of scale can silently change the meaning of an entire field unless its metadata travels with it.

4. Floating-Point Representation and Rounding

Introduce sign, exponent, and significand as cooperating parts of a finite floating-point format; distinguish normal values, subnormals, zeros, infinities, and NaNs where the format supports them. Explain rounding-to-nearest and directed rounding as policies rather than hidden implementation accidents. Include the classic nonexact representation of decimal 0.1 in binary floating point and the consequences of repeated arithmetic, cancellation, and summation order. Keep sophisticated numerical-analysis algorithms in their own disciplinary treatment.

5. Numeric Range, Overflow, and Underflow

Define representable range separately from precision and distinguish signed overflow, unsigned modular arithmetic, saturating behavior, exceptions, and implementation-specific results. For floating-point formats, explain the difference between overflow to an exceptional value and loss of magnitude near zero, including gradual underflow where subnormal numbers are available. Develop safe-arithmetic design questions: which operations require wider intermediates, what should happen beyond a declared range, and how should errors be signaled or validated? Avoid claiming one universal overflow policy.

Editorial boundary: These entries own the representation and finite-domain semantics of numbers; detailed numerical algorithms and theoretical computational cost proofs remain with the corresponding mathematics and algorithm-analysis subjects.

Binary Layout and Machine-Level Interpretation

A meaningful number or field can be corrupted by an incompatible byte layout even when every transmitted bit is intact. These five entries investigate byte order, bit-level packing, memory-layout rules, compact integer coding, and portable serialization. They distinguish an in-process representation from a defined exchange format.

6. Byte Order and Endianness

Distinguish the logical numeric value from its ordered sequence of bytes. Show how the same 32-bit value can be stored most-significant-byte-first or least-significant-byte-first, and explain why individual bit numbering is a separate convention. Discuss network or file-format byte-order declarations, byte swaps, field alignment, and explicit decoding. Make clear that endianness cannot be repaired by guessing from arbitrary data: it must be specified or reliably derived from a defined protocol.

7. Bit Fields and Packed Data

Use a compact status byte to demonstrate masks, shifts, and field extraction with explicitly documented bit positions. Contrast a normative wire-format bit layout with native compiler bit-field placement, whose order and padding may vary by language and implementation. Cover signed extraction, reserved bits, version flags, and how a packing choice trades storage efficiency for decoding complexity. Keep the conceptual focus on representing and interpreting fields rather than on gate-level circuitry, which belongs to Binary Logic and Digital Logic.

8. Alignment, Padding, and Memory Layout

Explain how alignment constraints may create internal and trailing structure padding and how field reorderings can change native object size and offsets. Distinguish language-level data types, application binary interfaces, and on-disk or on-wire records. A direct memory image is generally an unsafe substitute for portable serialization: it may encode padding, pointers, host byte order, and compiler choices. Develop a concrete offset calculation under stated example alignment assumptions while warning that actual layouts require the platform ABI or compiler specification.

9. Variable-Length Integer Encodings

Introduce why small magnitudes can benefit from encodings that use fewer bytes than a fixed-width integer. Describe the general idea of continuation bits, groups of payload bits, and signed mappings such as ZigZag in schemes that define them. Address maximum encoded length, overflow during decoding, truncated inputs, and noncanonical multiple encodings of the same value. Clarify that there is no single universal variable-length format: interoperability depends on specifying an exact scheme and its edge-case rules.

10. Binary Serialization and Deserialization

Follow a typed record as it becomes a deterministic sequence of bytes and is decoded by another implementation. Define field order, lengths, tags, endianness, integer widths, strings, optional fields, and version behavior; distinguish self-describing formats from schema-dependent formats. Discuss safe bounds checks before allocating or indexing, compatibility tests, and preservation of unknown fields when required. Explain why copying a raw process structure is not a sound cross-platform encoding unless its layout is itself the explicitly defined protocol.

Editorial boundary: These entries own bytes, fields, portable layouts, and decoding contracts; physical memory devices, operating-system memory managers, and full network protocols remain in their own encyclopedia subjects.

Text, Images, Sound, and Video

Symbolic and sampled media require distinct interpretive rules. These five entries cover characters and text equivalence, pixels, audio samples, colors, and time-indexed images. They emphasize the boundary between a decoded representation and the separate systems that compress, render, transmit, or play it.

11. Unicode Code Points and Normalization

Separate Unicode code points from code units, encoded bytes, glyphs, and user-perceived characters. Explain how visually similar text may be represented using a precomposed character or a base character plus combining marks. Introduce normalization forms and the reasons normalization does not itself solve case folding, language-sensitive collation, grapheme segmentation, or security confusables. Direct the reader to the independent Character Encoding article for the broader coverage of UTF-8, UTF-16, and encoding standards.

12. Raster Images and Pixel Formats

Specify width, height, pixel order, channel semantics, bit depth, alpha convention, and row stride for a raster image buffer. Show why width × height × bytes per pixel is only a lower-level payload calculation when row padding or metadata may be present. Distinguish an uncompressed pixel-buffer description from a complete file format with headers, color metadata, or compression. Avoid implying that equal raw RGB triplets necessarily create equal appearance without the correct transfer function and color profile.

13. Digital Audio Sample Formats

Distinguish sampling frequency from sample precision: one describes how often a signal is represented, while the other constrains each sample's numerical form. Cover integer and floating-point PCM, mono and multichannel interleaving, endianness where relevant, and the computation of uncompressed payload rates. Discuss clipping, headroom, and the role of metadata in mapping channels to speakers. Leave codec algorithms and psychoacoustic compression to the existing Data Compression treatment.

14. Color Spaces and Component Representation

Compare RGB component values with color spaces defined by primaries, white points, and transfer functions; distinguish device-dependent values from a fully specified colorimetric interpretation. Introduce Y′CbCr as a component representation commonly used in video without treating it as equivalent to unqualified RGB. Discuss quantization, alpha compositing assumptions, and round-trip color conversion limits. Explain why a numeric triplet alone is insufficient to guarantee visual matching across displays.

15. Video Frame and Temporal Representation

Explain how video is an ordered collection of spatial data and timestamps or presentation intervals, not merely a stack of interchangeable still images. Distinguish frame rate from time base, progressive frames from field-based representations, and pixel component subsampling from resolution. Discuss presentation timestamps, variable-frame-rate content, synchronization with audio, and the role of container metadata. Keep compression, motion estimation, and playback implementation outside this representation-focused scope.

Editorial boundary: These entries own the definitions and interpretation of media values and samples; broad media compression, signal processing, and graphics-rendering systems belong to their respective disciplinary units.

Meaning, Validation, and Interoperability

A valid sequence of bytes is not necessarily valid information. These three studies focus on missing-value policies, types and schemas, and evidence that data retains its intended meaning during conversion and exchange. They bring the earlier encoding and layout choices together into portable data contracts.

16. Nullability, Missing Values, and Sentinel Codes

Compare a legitimate zero value with no value supplied, an unknown value, a measurement failure, and a deliberately reserved sentinel. Explain how nullability rules and missingness metadata affect calculations, filters, validation, and storage. Discuss the risks of using a possible real-world value, such as −1, as an overloaded signal for 'not measured.' Clarify that SQL NULL, a floating-point NaN, and a language-specific optional type have different formal semantics and should not be treated as interchangeable.

17. Data Types, Schemas, and Semantic Interpretation

Follow an encoded number from bit pattern through declared numeric type, field name, unit, permitted range, and business or scientific definition. Explain how schema versions, optional fields, enumerations, and precision expectations determine whether a receiver may safely interpret a record. Distinguish syntactic validity from semantic validity: a 16-bit integer can decode correctly yet represent an impossible temperature or an amount in the wrong currency. Leave full database schema design and data governance to the relevant Data Systems units.

18. Representation Fidelity, Validation, and Portability

Define what it means for encoding and decoding to preserve meaning rather than merely reproduce bytes. Explore exact, lossless, and bounded-loss transformations; canonical encodings; equality after normalization; and acceptance or rejection of malformed inputs. Design cross-platform tests for extremes, signed values, absent data, unknown fields, precision changes, and schema migrations. Explain the difference between bit-for-bit fidelity, numeric equivalence within tolerance, and domain-equivalent information, including when each standard is appropriate.

Editorial boundary: These entries own the interpretation of encoded values and preservation of meaning; enterprise data governance, database operations, and application-specific regulations remain in neighboring units.

Representation and Encoding Comparison

Representations are selected to preserve specified information under real constraints. No format maximizes range, precision, compactness, interoperability, and computational convenience for every workload. The first table contrasts common representation families; the second distinguishes representation layers that are frequently conflated.

Fixed-width unsigned integerNonnegative integer with declared bit widthSimple finite range and direct bit operationsOverflow, unintended signed reinterpretation, width mismatch
Fixed-width two’s-complement integerSigned integer with declared widthUniform representation of positive and negative integersAsymmetric endpoints, sign extension, overflow semantics
Scaled or fixed-point integerInteger plus specified scale and unitControlled fractional precisionWrong scale, rounded rescaling, narrow intermediates
Binary floating pointFinite significand/exponent representation with roundingWide dynamic rangeNonexact decimal fractions, NaNs, underflow, loss of significance
Explicit serialized recordField order, type, length, version, and encoding rulesInteroperable exchange across implementationsUnbounded lengths, unknown fields, schema or byte-order mismatch
Unicode text and media samplesDeclared text or signal interpretation and metadataRich symbolic and sampled contentText normalization, color-profile, channel-layout or timestamp mismatch
LayerQuestion it answersIllustrative distinction
Mathematical valueWhat quantity or concept is intended?One hundred versus the numeral 100
Machine representationWhich finite patterns can encode the value?Signed 8-bit 100 versus unsigned 8-bit 100
Byte and field layoutWhere and in what order are patterns placed?Little-endian versus big-endian multibyte integer
Media or text encodingHow do symbols and samples map to data?Unicode code point versus its UTF-8 bytes
Semantic contractWhat does the receiver believe the field means?Temperature in degrees Celsius versus degrees Fahrenheit

A bit-preserving transfer can be semantically wrong if the receiver assumes an incompatible interpretation. Conversely, a meaning-preserving conversion may legitimately change the bytes, as when text is normalized or an integer changes its documented serialized width. The purpose of a format specification is to make these differences deliberate and testable.

Illustrative Representation Scenarios

The cases below are constructed teaching examples. They are designed to identify hidden assumptions that can make formally valid bytes unreliable when interpreted by another program, machine, or user.

Example 1 — The same byte carries two numbers

A diagnostic dump shows 11111111 for an eight-bit field. Under an unsigned interpretation, its value is 255; under standard eight-bit two’s-complement interpretation, it is −1. Neither reading is valid without a declared field type. If the receiver later widens the signed value, sign extension must preserve −1 rather than zero-extending it to 255. This is a representation problem, not proof of data corruption. Start with Signed Integer Representation and Two’s Complement and Representation Fidelity, Validation, and Portability to establish type and interpretation.

Example 2 — A decimal amount and an approximate fraction

Suppose an invoicing application records $19.95. Storing an integer count of 1,995 cents can represent that amount exactly under a declared cent scale, provided arithmetic respects range and rounding policies. A typical binary floating-point format cannot represent 0.1 exactly, so apparently simple decimal calculations can yield tiny approximation differences. That does not mean floating point is universally wrong: scientific workloads may require its dynamic range. Read Fixed-Point Representation and Scaling, Floating-Point Representation and Rounding, and Numeric Range, Overflow, and Underflow to evaluate scale, precision, and overflow together.

Example 3 — The integer is correct but the byte order is not

A four-byte big-endian encoding of the illustrative value 0x12345678 is 12 34 56 78 (hexadecimal bytes); the corresponding little-endian byte order is 78 56 34 12. If a receiver assumes the opposite order while reading those same four bytes as one 32-bit integer, it obtains a different value. A portable file or protocol must declare the byte order and define a decoder; copying native memory silently transfers host assumptions. See Byte Order and Endianness, Binary Serialization and Deserialization, and Alignment, Padding, and Memory Layout.

Example 4 — A text string looks the same but fails equality

Two strings can display as “é” while using distinct code-point sequences: the precomposed U+00E9 or U+0065 U+0301. A byte-for-byte comparison may find them different even when Unicode canonical normalization treats them as equivalent. A system that keys records by names, searches user input, or deduplicates text must specify its normalization and comparison policy. Normalization is not the same as locale-sensitive sorting, case folding, or the detection of deceptive lookalike characters. Begin with Unicode Code Points and Normalization and Representation Fidelity, Validation, and Portability.

Example 5 — Sampling formats have calculable payloads

A hypothetical uncompressed stereo PCM stream sampled at 48,000 samples per second per channel, with 16 bits per sample, has a raw sample payload of 48,000 × 2 × 16 = 1,536,000 bits/s, or 192,000 bytes/s, excluding headers and other transport overhead. This calculation does not say anything about the audible quality or the size of a compressed file. Likewise, image payload size requires a declared row layout, and video interpretation requires timing. Continue with Digital Audio Sample Formats, Raster Images and Pixel Formats, and Video Frame and Temporal Representation.

Example 6 — An absent measurement is not zero

An environmental sensor reports zero for a valid measurement, while another sensor fails to return any value. If a pipeline encodes both situations as numeric zero, subsequent averages and alarms can become misleading even though the stored bytes remain perfectly valid. A reliable record distinguishes valid zero, missing, invalid, and out-of-range readings; it specifies units, range, and null-handling rules before calculations begin. Use Nullability, Missing Values, and Sentinel Codes, Data Types, Schemas, and Semantic Interpretation, and Representation Fidelity, Validation, and Portability to develop and test the contract.

Example 7 — A file survives a schema upgrade

A service writes a binary record with a version number, typed lengths, an integer count, and an optional status field. A newer application adds an optional field but must still decode older records. Success requires defined handling for unknown fields, lengths, missing values, and defaults, plus explicit numeric widths and byte order. A round-trip test can verify whether a value survives encode → decode without semantic loss; byte-for-byte identity may be a separate requirement. Read Binary Serialization and Deserialization, Data Types, Schemas, and Semantic Interpretation, and Representation Fidelity, Validation, and Portability.

These are not descriptions of deployed MGU systems or claims about a particular vendor implementation. Each example assumes the stated encoding, precision, and layout rules.

Design and Interpretation Framework

Before selecting a data format, state what its receivers must reconstruct, what forms of loss are acceptable, and which errors are prohibited. A robust representation decision is made across the full data path, not by choosing a convenient type name in isolation.

Design or investigation questionRequired distinctionRead first
What exact mathematical value or domain entity is intended?Type, range, sign, precision, and unitSigned Integer Representation and Two’s Complement
Which radix or finite numeric format is appropriate?Notation versus encoding; fixed versus floating precisionPositional Number Systems and Base Conversion
How will arithmetic behave at the limits?Overflow, underflow, rounding, exceptional valuesNumeric Range, Overflow, and Underflow
How are fields arranged into transferable bytes?Byte order, padding, field widths, and layout contractsByte Order and Endianness
Should small values use a compact encoding?Length bounds, signed mapping, noncanonical encodingsVariable-Length Integer Encodings
Will a record survive software and platform changes?Field tagging, versioning, explicit decoding, validationBinary Serialization and Deserialization
Are text and media interpreted consistently?Code points, samples, channels, colors, timestampsUnicode Code Points and Normalization
How are missing or invalid values distinguished?Nullability, sentinel values, value-state semanticsNullability, Missing Values, and Sentinel Codes
What evidence shows meaning survived conversion?Round-trip invariants, limits, portability and edge casesRepresentation Fidelity, Validation, and Portability

Recommended procedure: (1) define the value and its semantic constraints; (2) specify numeric or symbolic encoding; (3) declare byte order, widths, lengths, and alignment-independent layout; (4) establish error and missing-value rules; (5) document versioning and metadata; and (6) test boundaries and round trips across independent implementations. Explicitly distinguish a decoder accepting input from that input being safe, valid, and meaningful for downstream use.

Suggested Study Pathways

Readers can approach this unit through a sequential curriculum or by following the representation failure encountered in a specific system. The pathways below name the most useful combinations of specialist subjects; they do not imply that the linked planned articles are already published.

Foundational computer science: Read the parent overview, then Positional Number Systems and Base Conversion, Signed Integer Representation and Two’s Complement, Fixed-Point Representation and Scaling, Floating-Point Representation and Rounding, Numeric Range, Overflow, and Underflow. Trace how mathematical values are constrained by bit width, scale, rounding, and exceptional behavior. This route establishes the vocabulary for every later study.

Systems and low-level programming: Follow Byte Order and Endianness, Bit Fields and Packed Data, Alignment, Padding, and Memory Layout, Variable-Length Integer Encodings, Binary Serialization and Deserialization. Practice describing a byte stream without referring to the native memory layout of the producing process. Pair representation choices with explicit decoding rules and bounded input validation.

Multimedia and user-interface systems: Study Unicode Code Points and Normalization, Raster Images and Pixel Formats, Digital Audio Sample Formats, Color Spaces and Component Representation, Video Frame and Temporal Representation. Distinguish pixels from color appearance, samples from compression, and images from timestamped video frames. Ask which metadata is needed for a receiver to reproduce intended behavior.

Database and data-integration teams: Begin with Nullability, Missing Values, and Sentinel Codes, Data Types, Schemas, and Semantic Interpretation, Representation Fidelity, Validation, and Portability, then return to numeric precision and serialization. Define what zero, absent, invalid, and unknown mean before aggregating or exchanging records. Test schema revisions on old and new readers.

Interoperability and format design: Combine Binary Serialization and Deserialization, Byte Order and Endianness, Variable-Length Integer Encodings, Unicode Code Points and Normalization, Data Types, Schemas, and Semantic Interpretation, Representation Fidelity, Validation, and Portability. Write a canonical format contract, bounded parser tests, and cross-platform round-trip cases. Verify all required invariants rather than assuming that success on one machine proves general portability.

Precision and safety-sensitive applications: Pair Fixed-Point Representation and Scaling, Floating-Point Representation and Rounding, Numeric Range, Overflow, and Underflow, Nullability, Missing Values, and Sentinel Codes, Representation Fidelity, Validation, and Portability. Establish representable ranges, tolerated error, overflow policies, and explicit error signaling. A representation is appropriate only when the requirements can be checked and failure conditions handled.

For a complete learning cycle, write down a field definition, encode a representative value, decode it in a different environment, check its meaning, and repeat the experiment with extreme or malformed inputs. This approach exposes assumptions that ordinary demonstration data may conceal.
