Collection Overview

This Level 4 unit index is a specialist directory within Computational Foundations of the MGU Computer Science Encyclopedia. It extends the existing Probability and Statistics for Computing parent overview, which introduces random events, uncertainty, sample-based inference, statistical models, and computing applications. The parent article remains the broad introduction; the studies linked here take individual methods and claims into deeper technical detail.

The directory preserves the 15 existing planned Level 5 subarticles and their original destination slugs. The four established subject groups proceed from probability foundations through sample-based inference, computational modeling, and experimental or production-system evaluation. Each linked article title represents a publication assignment rather than a claim that the article is already live.

Probability and statistics answer related but different questions. A probability model describes uncertainty under explicit assumptions; a statistical procedure uses selected observations to estimate quantities or test claims; a computational method approximates or implements those calculations with finite resources. Correct numerical output does not rescue a biased sample, invalid experimental assignment, or misspecified model. This distinction governs the scope of every specialist entry.

The intended reader should identify the unit of analysis, the target population or process, the stochastic assumptions, the method of collecting data, the uncertainty measure, and the decision context before selecting a procedure. When a task has several possible methods, the tables, examples, and pathways below direct readers to the most relevant articles.

Probability Foundations and Distribution Models

The five foundational articles establish the language of uncertainty: events and conditional probabilities, updating evidence, discrete and continuous distributions, and moments of those distributions. They should state model assumptions before applying formulas, and keep probability definitions separate from statistical claims inferred from observed samples.

1. Probability Spaces and Conditional Probability

Define sample spaces, events, probability measures, and the rules connecting unions, complements, intersections, and conditioning. Work from finite outcome spaces to general event models, showing when a conditional probability is defined and why conditioning changes the reference population. Develop precise contrasts among independence, mutual exclusivity, and conditional independence. Use contingency tables and simple probability trees to show that outcomes can be dependent even when no direct causal mechanism has been established. The article should make assumptions explicit before presenting applications to reliability, detection, and decision systems.

2. Bayesian Reasoning and Base Rates

Explain how a prior event rate, a test likelihood, and new evidence combine through Bayes’ rule. Include rare-event classification, false positives, diagnostic alerts, and posterior probability calculations with both algebraic and count-based examples. Clarify that a high true-positive rate does not imply a high probability that a flagged case is genuine when the underlying event is rare. Compare probability updating with decisions governed by loss and cost thresholds. This article owns Bayes’ rule as a reasoning tool; the deeper modeling of priors and full posterior distributions belongs to Bayesian Statistical Inference.

3. Discrete Random Variables and Distributions

Establish probability mass functions, cumulative distributions, and common count or trial models: Bernoulli, binomial, geometric, negative binomial, and Poisson. Match each family to a well-defined random experiment instead of choosing a distribution by superficial resemblance. Examine independence, equal trial probabilities, event-rate assumptions, support, parameter interpretation, expectation, and variance. Distinguish the counts generated by a model from an empirical histogram. Small computing cases can include retries until success, event counts per time window, and binary test outcomes.

4. Continuous Random Variables and Distributions

Develop probability density functions and cumulative distribution functions, emphasizing that continuous probabilities concern intervals and that a density value is not a point probability. Compare normal, exponential, and gamma distributions and discuss when positive-only or skewed models are more appropriate. Use numerical integration, quantiles, and units to connect mathematical distributions to measurements such as response time or component lifetime. Address tail behavior, censoring, measurement resolution, and model fit. Keep continuous mathematics distinct from a claim that real sensors have infinite measurement precision.

5. Expectation, Variance, and Covariance

Treat expectation as an average under a probability model and variance as a measure of dispersion around that average. Develop linearity of expectation, covariance, correlation, and the impact of dependence on sums and averages. Connect these concepts to capacity planning, randomized algorithm analysis, and uncertainty propagation. Explain that covariance depends on units, correlation does not establish causation, and an expected value is not a bound on any single observation. Include the law of total expectation and variance as ways to decompose mixed populations.

Sampling, Estimation, and Statistical Inference

These four entries move from how observations are collected to what can responsibly be inferred about a population or process. Sampling design, estimator behavior, interval procedures, and posterior analysis address different aspects of evidence; none independently establishes that a sample is unbiased or that a model is correct.

1. Sampling Theory and Survey Design

Start with the target population, sampling frame, unit of observation, and mechanism by which an observation enters a dataset. Compare simple random, stratified, cluster, and systematic designs, then introduce sampling weights and design effects. Distinguish sampling variation from coverage error, nonresponse, measurement error, and convenience-selection bias. Show why collecting more of the wrong observations does not reliably repair a biased design. The article should provide a procedural approach to documenting inclusion probabilities, subgroup representation, and missingness.

2. Statistical Estimation and Maximum Likelihood

Define parameters, statistics, estimators, likelihoods, and the distinction between a fitted estimate and the underlying population quantity. Cover bias, consistency, efficiency, identifiability, and the role of optimization in maximum-likelihood fitting. Work a simple Bernoulli likelihood example, then explain why numerical solvers require convergence checks and can be unstable under separation, sparse data, or weakly identified parameters. Inference claims should be grounded in a specified data-generating model rather than in an optimizer’s output alone.

3. Confidence Intervals and Statistical Testing

Present confidence intervals as procedures with stated long-run coverage under model assumptions. Explain hypotheses, test statistics, p-values, Type I and Type II errors, effect size, power, and the distinction between a statistical difference and a meaningful operational change. Provide comparisons of one- and two-sided tests, interval methods, and multiplicity adjustments. Warn against interpreting a p-value as the probability that the null hypothesis is true. Account for assumptions, dependent observations, data-driven stopping, and post hoc comparisons before reporting a result as evidence.

4. Bayesian Statistical Inference

Develop the posterior as the combination of a likelihood and prior distribution, then introduce posterior summaries, credible intervals, posterior prediction, and representative conjugate examples. Explain the role of computational approximations when closed-form analysis is unavailable. Assess prior sensitivity, model checking, convergence diagnostics, and the limitations of posterior certainty under misspecification. Contrast a Bayesian credible interval with a frequentist confidence interval without presenting either as universally superior. This article treats inference frameworks, while Bayesian Reasoning and Base Rates provides the more accessible probability-updating entry.

Regression, Resampling, Simulation, and Stochastic Systems

This group covers computational methods that fit relationships, approximate sampling distributions, simulate uncertain outcomes, or track changing states. The relevant questions are which distribution generates the data, how dependence is represented, and which numerical or statistical errors remain after computation.

1. Regression and Generalized Linear Models

Explain how regression represents relationships between predictors and a response. Introduce linear models and generalized linear models for binary and count responses, including link functions, coefficient interpretation, and fitted-value computation. Cover residual checks, functional form, uncertainty, multicollinearity, exposure offsets, and distributional assumptions. Distinguish predictive association from causal identification and discuss omitted variables, data leakage, and dependence across observations. The technical objective is trustworthy model interpretation, not only minimizing a training loss.

2. Resampling, Bootstrap, and Permutation Tests

Show how repeated resampling of observed data can approximate the sampling behavior of a statistic, and how label permutation can construct a reference distribution under an appropriate exchangeability assumption. Define a resampling unit before selecting a procedure. Contrast ordinary, stratified, clustered, block, and paired resampling as needed; independent-row bootstrap is not valid for every correlated or time-ordered dataset. Explain percentile intervals, Monte Carlo variability, permutation p-values, and cases where sparse samples or boundary estimates make naive resampling misleading.

3. Monte Carlo Methods and Random Number Generation

Introduce Monte Carlo estimation as numerical approximation using simulated draws from a specified model. Explain pseudorandom number generators, seeds, reproducibility, sampling error, variance reduction, and the relationship between sample count and approximation precision. Use demonstrations such as estimating a simple event probability or integrating a function. Differentiate statistical-quality generators from cryptographically secure randomness, and distinguish simulation uncertainty from errors in the underlying model. Record seeds, generator versions, assumptions, and convergence evidence in reproducible computational reports.

4. Markov Chains and Stochastic Processes

Define states, transition probabilities, time indexes, and the Markov property. Develop finite transition matrices, multiperiod transitions, stationary distributions where they exist, and examples involving system health, service queues, and reliability. Distinguish memoryless modeling assumptions from claims that real processes have no history. Introduce absorbing states, irreducibility, periodicity, and when stationarity is inappropriate. Model validity requires checking that chosen states encode enough information and that estimated transitions remain stable for the question of interest.

Experiments, Machine Learning, and Computing Systems

The final two specialists apply statistical reasoning to deliberate interventions and ongoing operational evaluation. Their editorial responsibility is to distinguish measured outcomes from causal or predictive claims, and to document assignment, validation, uncertainty, and changes in system behavior after deployment.

1. Experimental Design and A/B Testing

Explain random assignment, treatment and control, estimands, experimental units, assignment integrity, sample size, power, and outcome measurement for online and offline experiments. Cover predeclared primary metrics, guardrails, and the distinction between percentage-point and relative effects. Examine interference, selection bias, repeated looks at accumulating data, multiple comparisons, attrition, and sample-ratio mismatches. A/B testing should be connected to credible causal inference only when its assignment and measurement assumptions hold; implementation errors can invalidate an apparently precise result.

2. Statistical Methods for Machine Learning and Systems

Develop statistical evaluation of predictive models and operational computer services, including suitable train–validation–test splits, calibration, cross-validation, uncertainty intervals, time-series forecasting, and performance monitoring. Consider class imbalance, subgroup performance, false alarms, correlated observations, changing data distributions, and deployment drift. Distinguish ranking metrics from calibration and classification thresholds, and separate model evaluation from causal performance claims. The article owns statistical reliability and evidence quality rather than full machine-learning architecture.

Critical distinction: statistical significance concerns evidence under a defined procedure; practical significance concerns the size and consequences of an effect. Prediction concerns future or held-out outcomes, while causal attribution additionally requires an identification strategy. An accurate prediction does not by itself establish why an outcome occurred.

Illustrative Probability and Statistics Scenarios

The following are constructed instructional scenarios, not published studies, observed MGU operating data, or empirical claims about real systems. Each gives enough information to show a calculation or modeling decision while explicitly naming the assumptions that would require verification in practice.

1. Rare-event screening and base rates

Suppose 1% of events are genuine incidents. A classifier catches 90% of genuine incidents (sensitivity) but falsely flags 5% of non-incidents. In a hypothetical population of 10,000 cases, the expected counts are 100 actual incidents, about 90 correctly flagged incidents, and about 495 false alarms among 9,900 non-incidents. The probability that a flagged case is genuine is therefore 90 ÷ (90 + 495) ≈ 15.4% under these assumptions. High sensitivity is compatible with low precision when the base rate is small. The example is not a measurement of a deployed system; it connects Bayesian Reasoning and Base Rates to Probability Spaces and Conditional Probability.

2. A sample of server errors

An engineer observes 20 failed requests in a sample of 1,000. The observed failure proportion is 2%, but that number does not say whether the sample represents all users, all hours, or all request types. Under an illustrative independent Bernoulli model, the plug-in standard error is approximately 0.44 percentage points; operational clustering or changing load could increase uncertainty. An appropriate confidence interval and sampling plan are needed before reporting a population error rate. Read Sampling Theory and Survey Design, Statistical Estimation and Maximum Likelihood, and Confidence Intervals and Statistical Testing.

3. Survey results change after weighting

A fictional survey has 80% participants from Group A and 20% from Group B, although the intended population consists of 40% A and 60% B. If positive-response rates are 90% in A and 50% in B, the unweighted mixture reports 82%, while a correctly specified population mixture would be 66% (0.4 × 0.9 + 0.6 × 0.5). Weighting addresses this particular composition mismatch; it does not automatically fix nonresponse, measurement error, or an incomplete sampling frame. The example highlights Sampling Theory and Survey Design and Expectation, Variance, and Covariance.

4. A/B test conversion differences

Two hypothetical randomized groups each contain 1,000 users. Version A has 100 conversions and Version B has 120: the observed rates are 10% versus 12%, a difference of 2 percentage points and a relative increase of 20%. The difference alone is not proof of a genuine treatment effect; under simple independent-binomial assumptions the estimated standard error of the difference is about 1.4 percentage points. Assignment, power, repeated monitoring, guardrails, and confidence intervals determine what can responsibly be concluded. Continue to Experimental Design and A/B Testing and Confidence Intervals and Statistical Testing.

5. Long-tail response times

Seven illustrative service latencies are 80, 85, 90, 95, 102, 110, and 500 milliseconds. The median is 95 ms, but the mean is about 152 ms because one very slow response raises the average. Both are valid summaries, and neither alone describes the tail, the causal mechanism, or the user population. An analysis should report suitable quantiles and examine the sampling window, correlated requests, and load conditions. This case links Continuous Random Variables and Distributions, Expectation, Variance, and Covariance, and Statistical Methods for Machine Learning and Systems.

6. Simulating an event probability

For two fair six-sided dice, exactly six of the 36 equally likely ordered outcomes sum to seven, so the exact probability is 1/6. A Monte Carlo program could generate pairs of simulated dice, count the proportion summing to seven, and compare the result with 1/6. The simulation is an approximation with sampling error, not a substitute for the exact derivation. It should record its pseudorandom generator and seed for reproducibility, and its measured error should decline as the number of simulated draws grows. See Monte Carlo Methods and Random Number Generation and Discrete Random Variables and Distributions.

7. A two-state reliability chain

Consider a simplified service with states Healthy and Degraded. In each step, a healthy service stays healthy with probability 0.9 and becomes degraded with 0.1; a degraded service recovers with probability 0.3 and stays degraded with 0.7. The transition matrix by row is [[0.9, 0.1], [0.3, 0.7]]. Under a time-homogeneous Markov assumption, its stationary distribution is 75% Healthy and 25% Degraded, found by solving πP = π. This is a model-derived long-run fraction, not an observed service-level agreement. Read Markov Chains and Stochastic Processes and Statistical Methods for Machine Learning and Systems.

8. Validation that leaks future information

A forecasting model is evaluated by randomly splitting time-ordered transactions, allowing examples from the future to enter training while earlier observations appear in testing. The resulting score may look strong but fail in actual forward deployment. A more credible evaluation holds out later periods, fits preprocessing only on training data, and checks subgroup and temporal drift separately. Even this design does not remove every structural change in the environment. Start with Statistical Methods for Machine Learning and Systems and Regression and Generalized Linear Models.

Across all eight cases, useful evidence requires a declared target quantity, traceable observations, a defensible uncertainty calculation, and an account of assumptions. Numerical neatness by itself is not evidence of external validity.

Statistical Investigation and Method Selection Framework

Method selection begins with an analytical objective and an evidence-generating process, not a preferred formula or software library. Claims about causation require a suitable design rather than simply a small p-value or high predictive score.

Suggested Study Pathways

The pathways below are suggested routes for readers with different objectives. They are curricular sequences, not publication timelines. An unreleased specialist page can still appear as an active planned destination; the parent overview supplies the broad introductory context.

Foundations of uncertainty: Begin with the parent overview, then Probability Spaces and Conditional Probability, Bayesian Reasoning and Base Rates, Discrete Random Variables and Distributions, and Continuous Random Variables and Distributions. Finish with Expectation, Variance, and Covariance. This sequence teaches readers to define events, distributions, and dependence before interpreting observed numerical results.

Surveys and evidence from samples: Follow Sampling Theory and Survey Design with Statistical Estimation and Maximum Likelihood and Confidence Intervals and Statistical Testing. Compare designed probability samples with convenience samples, then practice describing uncertainty in ways consistent with the actual inclusion mechanism. Use Bayesian Statistical Inference to understand posterior-based statements as a distinct interpretive framework.

Applied modeling and prediction: Study Regression and Generalized Linear Models and then Statistical Methods for Machine Learning and Systems. Focus on model diagnosis, held-out evaluation, calibration, leakage, and changing conditions. Avoid treating an association as a causal effect or a stable training metric as evidence that future system performance is guaranteed.

Simulation and stochastic systems: Pair Monte Carlo Methods and Random Number Generation with Resampling, Bootstrap, and Permutation Tests and Markov Chains and Stochastic Processes. Begin with an exact small-probability example, simulate it, then introduce resampling appropriate to the data structure and transition models for evolving systems. Record generator assumptions, error measures, and convergence evidence.

Experiments and product decisions: Begin with Experimental Design and A/B Testing, then connect it to Confidence Intervals and Statistical Testing, Sampling Theory and Survey Design, and Statistical Methods for Machine Learning and Systems. Distinguish the effect identified by a controlled experiment from a prediction made by an observational model; discuss interference and sequential monitoring before making an intervention decision.

Systems reliability and performance: Study Continuous Random Variables and Distributions, Expectation, Variance, and Covariance, Markov Chains and Stochastic Processes, and Statistical Methods for Machine Learning and Systems. Apply them to error rates, latency tails, transient failures, and monitored service health. State the workload and measurement units and avoid extrapolating a short monitoring window to every future operating condition.

  • Discrete Mathematics — Sets, counting, functions, logic, and proof methods underpinning formal probability.

  • Algorithm Analysis — Expected running time, randomized algorithms, asymptotic bounds, and benchmarking.

  • Computing Ethics — Accountable use of evidence, transparency, privacy, and harms in data-driven decisions.

Editorial boundaries matter. The present unit owns probabilistic reasoning, statistical estimation, tests, experiment design, and the reliability of data-derived conclusions. Discrete Mathematics retains its general formal structures; Algorithm Analysis treats efficiency proofs; Data Engineering treats operational pipelines; and Machine Learning treats the broader learning-method and application domain. The two subjects can intersect without being organizational duplicates.
