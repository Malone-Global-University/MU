Collection Overview

This directory is the specialist companion to the State Machines parent overview in the Computational Foundations division of the MGU Computer Science Encyclopedia. The parent identifies 20 specialist child topics. Their titles, destination filenames, and descriptive outlines are preserved here as the canonical planned collection.

A state machine represents behavior through states and permitted transitions. Different families serve different ends: a finite automaton recognizes strings, a Mealy or Moore machine produces outputs, an extended state machine tracks data and guards, and a timed or probabilistic model introduces constraints beyond plain discrete transitions. A productive design begins with the question being asked and selects only the model richness required to answer it.

The collection is organized into four areas: formal models and regular languages; notation and structural design; verification and quantitative extensions; and implementation, testing, and security. It is not a substitute for the parent article or for the neighboring Automata Theory and Digital Logic articles, which pursue different emphases.

Finite Models, Languages, and Observable Output

A machine description identifies a collection of states, permitted events, and a transition relation. The first five children establish the core formal distinctions among generic labeled systems, deterministic and nondeterministic recognition, regular-language expressiveness, and stateful output. Formal acceptance and operational output should not be conflated.

  1. Formal Transition Systems — States, labels, paths, and relations. (planned subarticle) Editorial depth: Define states, transition relations, initial states, paths, labels, reachable states, and model boundaries; separate a state graph from its interpretation.
  2. Deterministic Finite Automata — Total transition functions and recognition. (planned subarticle) Editorial depth: Develop total transition functions, accepted strings, execution traces, and explicit treatment of the input alphabet and accepting states.
  3. Nondeterministic Finite Automata — Branching, epsilon moves, subset construction. (planned subarticle) Editorial depth: Explain branching runs, epsilon transitions, language acceptance by some run, and subset construction for equivalent deterministic recognition.
  4. Regular Languages and Automata — Automata equivalence and formal expressions. (planned subarticle) Editorial depth: Connect automata with regular expressions and regular languages, including closure operations, expressive limits, and equivalence results.
  5. Mealy and Moore Machines — Output-producing finite-state transducers. (planned subarticle) Editorial depth: Compare transition-labeled and state-labeled output, conversion principles, observable behavior, and output timing assumptions.

Specification, Extended State, and Structural Refinement

Engineering practice requires readable specifications as well as rigorous semantics. These subjects treat transition diagrams and tables, guarded extended state, hierarchical statecharts, the SCXML execution framework, and minimization. They connect implementable behavior to clear documentation and appropriate abstractions.

  1. State Machine Diagrams and Tables — Notation and coverage techniques. (planned subarticle) Editorial depth: Standardize symbols, event and guard notation, table completeness, traceability, and methods for reviewing missing transitions.
  2. Extended Finite-State Machines — Variables, guards, and side effects. (planned subarticle) Editorial depth: Define guards, data variables, update actions, invariants, and the circumstances in which extended state creates a larger effective state space.
  3. Hierarchical Statecharts — Nested and concurrent reactive behavior. (planned subarticle) Editorial depth: Explain nested states, orthogonal regions, event propagation, and the complexity of concurrency and priority in reactive behavior.
  4. SCXML Execution Semantics — Event dispatch, entry and exit effects. (planned subarticle) Editorial depth: Examine SCXML processing, event dispatch, eventless transitions, entry/exit actions, history, and implementation conformance questions.
  5. Automata Minimization — Equivalent states and partition refinement. (planned subarticle) Editorial depth: Study observational equivalence, distinguishing inputs, partition refinement, and conditions under which distinct states may be merged.

Verification, Temporal Properties, and Quantitative Extensions

A state machine can be syntactically well formed yet still permit unsafe or stalled behavior. These specialist topics study reachable states, deadlock, safety, liveness, temporal logic, model checking, and the added modeling obligations of time and probability.

  1. Reachability and Deadlock Analysis — Graph exploration and blocked execution. (planned subarticle) Editorial depth: Explore graph searches, unreachable transitions, deadlock classifications, and the distinction between an intentional terminal state and a blocked one.
  2. Safety and Liveness Properties — Invariants, progress, and fairness. (planned subarticle) Editorial depth: Express invariants and progress requirements, illustrate counterexamples, and distinguish unconditional progress from fairness assumptions.
  3. Temporal Logic for State Machines — Logical specifications over execution traces. (planned subarticle) Editorial depth: Introduce trace properties with temporal operators and explain differences between path-based and branching-time specifications.
  4. State-Space Exploration and Model Checking — Explicit-state and symbolic verification. (planned subarticle) Editorial depth: Compare explicit exploration and symbolic representations; identify counterexamples, state explosion, and abstraction trade-offs.
  5. Timed Automata — Clock constraints and real-time behavior. (planned subarticle) Editorial depth: Model clocks, guards, resets, time-bounded behavior, and real-time requirements, including timing nondeterminism.
  6. Probabilistic State Machines — Markov transitions and uncertain outcomes. (planned subarticle) Editorial depth: Use transition probabilities to reason about stochastic behavior while distinguishing probabilistic models from nondeterministic choices.

Distributed Behavior, Testing, Security, and Implementation

Real systems must account for asynchronous messages, retries, persistence, security boundaries, and executable code. This final group translates formal state models into defensible implementation and assurance workflows without assuming that a diagram alone proves runtime correctness.

  1. State Machines in Distributed Systems — Messages, retries, failures, and persistence. (planned subarticle) Editorial depth: Model message delivery, retries, failures, recovery, event order, durable state, and the limits of local-state reasoning.
  2. State-Based Software Testing — Transition coverage and generated sequences. (planned subarticle) Editorial depth: Build transition and state coverage plans, generate tests from models, and identify the limits of coverage as evidence.
  3. State Machine Security — Authorization, workflow bypass, and trust boundaries. (planned subarticle) Editorial depth: Analyze state-dependent authorization, workflow bypass, replay, invalid transitions, idempotency, and attacker-controlled events.
  4. State Machine Implementation Patterns — Table-driven, event-driven, and durable designs. (planned subarticle) Editorial depth: Compare switch statements, transition tables, event-driven handlers, persistence, typed transitions, and auditability.

State-Machine Model Selection Guide

The most important modeling decision is whether the system is being treated as a recognizer, an output-producing controller, an event-driven workflow, or an object of verification. These purposes overlap but are not interchangeable. The table suggests a starting point and the assumption most likely to be overlooked.

Modeling objectiveRecommended specialist entryCritical design or proof question
Recognize whether a finite input sequence belongs to a language.Deterministic Finite Automata; Nondeterministic Finite Automata; Regular Languages and AutomataWhat is the input alphabet, and does acceptance depend on a single run or some possible run?
Transform incoming events into visible responses.Mealy and Moore MachinesIs each output attached to a state, a transition, or a combination of current state and input?
Represent application behavior with guards and internal variables.Extended Finite-State Machines; State Machine Diagrams and TablesCan guards overlap or fail to cover an event? Which actions may change state?
Control complex reactive workflows with nesting and concurrent regions.Hierarchical Statecharts; SCXML Execution SemanticsWhich event priority, dispatch, entry/exit, and concurrency semantics govern execution?
Detect inaccessible states and executions that cannot proceed.Reachability and Deadlock Analysis; State-Space Exploration and Model CheckingIs a blocked state an intentional final condition, or an unintended deadlock?
Specify absence of bad behavior and eventual progress.Safety and Liveness Properties; Temporal Logic for State MachinesWhich environmental and fairness assumptions are necessary for the progress claim?
Describe deadlines and quantitative uncertainty.Timed Automata; Probabilistic State MachinesAre time and probabilities explicit model parameters rather than informal annotations?
Build a persistent distributed service subject to retries.State Machines in Distributed Systems; State Machine Implementation PatternsCan out-of-order or repeated messages violate invariants or cause duplicate effects?
Evaluate behavioral tests and trust boundaries.State-Based Software Testing; State Machine SecurityDo tests exercise forbidden transitions and attacker-controlled event sequences, not just expected paths?

A finite diagram alone does not establish correctness. A valid assurance argument also defines state invariants, event assumptions, transition semantics, and the relationship between the model and the deployed system. Likewise, formal equivalence between two machines requires an explicitly chosen notion of observable behavior.

Worked Comparisons of State-Machine Designs

Example 1 — Turnstile Access Control

Consider a turnstile with two abstract states: locked and unlocked. A validated credential changes the state from locked to unlocked. A passage event from unlocked restores locked. A denied credential should not change the state; a passage attempt from locked must not authorize passage. An explicit transition table forces the designer to say how each event behaves in each state instead of relying on undocumented default cases.

A simple two-state Mealy model can emit an open or deny command on transitions; a Moore model could instead associate an access-enable output with the unlocked state. In a real facility, actuator failures, tailgating, emergency egress, and credential expiration require richer modeling. Security depends on verifying that no event sequence permits unauthorized movement, not merely on the visible diagram's simplicity.

Example 2 — Publication Approval Workflow

A publication workflow may use the states draft, under review, approved, published, and withdrawn. The invariant “only approved content can be published” requires both a sound transition specification and role-based enforcement at the action boundary. A submission event from draft may lead to under review, but a direct draft-to-published transition should be rejected unless an expressly authorized exceptional process exists.

The same workflow illustrates liveness. “Every submitted item will eventually be approved” is usually false because reviewers may reject an item; “every valid submission eventually receives a decision” is a more defensible goal, but it still relies on scheduling and service assumptions. A timed model may impose an escalation deadline, and a testing model can enumerate approval, rejection, resubmission, withdrawal, and unauthorized-action paths.

Example 3 — Network Retry Protocol

A network client could move among idle, request sent, awaiting response, completed, and failed. Timeout events should be distinguished from negative responses. Retries may reissue messages after the server has already performed an action, so idempotency and duplicate detection matter independently of the state diagram's transition validity.

A local client state machine cannot, by itself, guarantee a globally consistent distributed outcome. Failures and delays can make client and server observations differ. The specialist article on distributed state machines therefore must specify message assumptions, persistent state, retry boundaries, and recovery behavior; the implementation article must describe how those semantics survive restarts.

Across these examples, the workflow is the same: define observable events and state, write a complete transition relation or table, specify invariants and required progress, identify the environment's assumptions, and test or formally explore paths that violate the intended behavior.

Suggested Study Pathways

Foundations and formal languages: Begin with Formal Transition Systems and State Machine Diagrams and Tables. Continue to Deterministic Finite Automata, Nondeterministic Finite Automata, and Regular Languages and Automata. Finish with Automata Minimization to understand behavioral equivalence and compact recognition models.

Reactive application design: Study Mealy and Moore Machines before Extended Finite-State Machines. Then progress to Hierarchical Statecharts and SCXML Execution Semantics. Use a simple approval workflow to compare how explicit transitions, guarded data, nesting, and event queues express increasingly complex behavior.

Formal assurance: Follow Formal Transition Systems with Reachability and Deadlock Analysis. Study Safety and Liveness Properties, Temporal Logic for State Machines, and State-Space Exploration and Model Checking. Add Timed Automata or Probabilistic State Machines only when the question requires deadlines, stochastic transitions, or quantitative properties.

Secure implementation: Start with State Machine Diagrams and Tables and Extended Finite-State Machines, then continue to State Machine Implementation Patterns and State-Based Software Testing. Pair these with State Machine Security to examine invalid inputs, bypassed authorization checks, and sequences that malicious users can construct.

Distributed and operational workflows: Read State Machines in Distributed Systems alongside State Machine Implementation Patterns. Use the timed and verification material when retries, deadlines, recovery, and progress guarantees become essential to system design.

The study paths are recommendations rather than a rigid dependency graph. Each eventual child article should stand independently while pointing to the completed State Machines overview when the reader needs shared definitions.

Scope and Related Encyclopedia Topics

The State Machines specialist collection is principally about explicit behavioral models and the engineering discipline of deriving reliable implementations from them. It is distinct from the broader formal-language scope of Automata Theory, the hardware focus of Digital Logic, and the general algorithmic foundations of Graph Theory or Algorithm Analysis. A child title may touch those subjects, but should develop state-machine-specific methods rather than reproduce an adjacent parent article.

The following links are established neighboring MGU Encyclopedia topics, not additional children of this directory:

  • Automata Theory — Language recognition, grammars, automaton equivalence, and computability.
  • Computational Models — Foundational models of computation and their limits.
  • Discrete Mathematics — Relations, functions, graphs, and proof methods for transition systems.
  • Graph Theory — Reachability, paths, cycles, and graph-search algorithms.
  • Binary Logic — Boolean conditions, guards, and digital logic.
  • Algorithm Analysis — Algorithmic correctness and the costs of state-space exploration.
  • Digital Logic — Physical sequential logic and register-based circuit behavior.
  • Control Flow — Program execution branches, iterations, and structured control.
  • Testing and Debugging — Software execution diagnostics and test investigations.
  • Quality Assurance — Evidence, test planning, and systematic lifecycle assurance.
  • Processes and Threads — Operating-system execution contexts and concurrency.
  • Network Protocols — Communications rules and message-processing behavior.
  • Industrial Control Systems — Operational control systems and their timing constraints.
  • Identity and Access Management — Authorization policy and access-state enforcement.
  • Database Transactions — Atomic updates, persistent state, and consistency.

Editorial boundaries to preserve: distinguish a machine's abstract state from its physical implementation; separate nondeterminism from probabilistic choice; separate a safety invariant from a liveness guarantee; and explicitly state whether a “state” includes only a control location or also data variables, timers, queues, and persistent records.
