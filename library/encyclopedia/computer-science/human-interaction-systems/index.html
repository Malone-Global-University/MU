Division Overview and Taxonomy

Human Interaction Systems is a Level 3 division of the Computer Science volume in the MGU Encyclopedia. It examines how people perceive computational output, formulate goals, issue commands, interpret system behavior, recover from errors, and accomplish tasks. The core question is not simply whether a technical system can execute an operation, but whether a person can understand, control, and successfully use that capability in the real context of work or daily life.

This discipline combines human-computer interaction (HCI), cognitive and behavioral research, interface engineering, visual and alternative input methods, user experience design, accessibility, and human factors. It bridges the human and computational sides of a complete interaction loop: user intention, input action, system state, output feedback, human interpretation, and the next decision. Quality depends on both technically correct behavior and interaction that is understandable, discoverable, recoverable, and inclusive.

Taxonomy: this page is the Level 3 division index. Each named topic is a division-level subject overview. Its associated <subject>-articles/ folder is the intended Level 4 specialist unit, whose own index.html should govern Level 5 subarticles. A topic overview explains the whole field; a unit index organizes scoped specialist study; individual specialist pages develop a single child topic. The specialist-directory links are active hyperlinks to intended destinations, consistent with MGU planning conventions; a proposed destination is not evidence that its index or child pages already exist.

Human-Computer Interaction and Interaction Models

These subjects describe the structure of the user–system relationship before focusing on individual controls. HCI is the broader empirical discipline; interaction models give explicit representations of actions, feedback, states, and transitions.

1. Human-Computer Interaction

What this unit studies: Study human abilities and behavior, interactive-system principles, and empirical methods for designing and evaluating the human–machine relationship. Principal inquiry: How can a system support people, tasks, and context instead of forcing users to accommodate the machine?

Ownership and boundaries: Human factors, cognitive and behavioral models, task analysis, interaction principles, empirical evaluation, and research methodology. Boundary: UI owns particular controls and presentation; UX owns the end-to-end product journey; interaction models provide formal or conceptual representations.

Planned specialist scope: Mental models and cognition; human factors and ergonomics; task analysis; usability engineering; empirical HCI methods; participatory design; evaluation of interactive systems.. These are editorial subject areas for eventual Level 5 articles, not a representation of completed children.

Illustrative case: A control-room team studies why operators misread status changes despite technically correct telemetry.

Read: subject overview. Level 4 directory target: Explore specialist directory (planned / verify) human-computer-interaction-articles/index.html (local status unverified). Neighboring subjects: Interaction Models, User Interface Design, User Experience Design.

2. Interaction Models

What this unit studies: Model the relationship between user goals, actions, feedback, system state, and interpretation so interactive behavior can be reasoned about. Principal inquiry: What structured model explains a user action, the resulting system transition, and the feedback loop?

Ownership and boundaries: Conceptual and formal interaction models, interaction cycles, action–feedback sequences, user-system dialogue, and model-based evaluation. Boundary: HCI develops and tests wider human factors; UI implements controls; UX organizes experience across tasks and touchpoints.

Planned specialist scope: Norman-style action and evaluation cycles; interaction loops; state-transition models; direct-manipulation models; command and dialogue models; multimodal coordination; conceptual models and user expectations.. These are editorial subject areas for eventual Level 5 articles, not a representation of completed children.

Illustrative case: A form wizard must explain navigation, validation, save state, cancellation, and error recovery without trapping users.

Read: subject overview. Level 4 directory target: Explore specialist directory (planned / verify) interaction-models-articles/index.html (local status unverified). Neighboring subjects: Human-Computer Interaction, User Interface Design, Input Method Design.

Input Methods and Display Technologies

Interaction depends on how users deliver intent and how systems present results. These two subjects focus on the user-facing behavior and requirements of input and output modalities; the physical engineering of devices is treated elsewhere.

1. Input Method Design

What this unit studies: Design how human intent enters a system through commands, text entry, direct manipulation, voice, touch, gesture, and alternative controls. Principal inquiry: How should a person express an action accurately, comfortably, and accessibly?

Ownership and boundaries: Input interaction vocabulary, command mapping, precision, discoverability, feedback, multimodal coordination, and alternate ways to act. Boundary: Device manufacturing and hardware sensor internals belong to Hardware Systems; general interaction theory belongs to HCI.

Planned specialist scope: Keyboard and pointer interaction; touch and gesture patterns; voice and conversational input; command shortcuts; error tolerance; input ergonomics; switch and adaptive input.. These are editorial subject areas for eventual Level 5 articles, not a representation of completed children.

Illustrative case: A mobile form needs usable entry for a gloved worker, a speech-input user, and a person navigating by switch.

Read: subject overview. Level 4 directory target: Explore specialist directory (planned / verify) input-method-design-articles/index.html (local status unverified). Neighboring subjects: Accessibility, User Interface Design, Display Technologies.

2. Display Technologies

What this unit studies: Explain how visual output technologies affect the legibility, visual fidelity, responsiveness, and usability of human-facing systems. Principal inquiry: Which visual output characteristics support the actual task and viewing environment?

Ownership and boundaries: User-perceptual display needs, visual performance tradeoffs, readability, color and motion presentation in context, and display-mediated usability. Boundary: Panel fabrication, circuitry, electrical interfaces, and physical device engineering belong principally to Hardware Systems.

Planned specialist scope: LCD, OLED, and electronic-paper displays; pixel density and viewing distance; brightness and contrast; refresh and response characteristics; color presentation; spatial displays; human perceptual limits.. These are editorial subject areas for eventual Level 5 articles, not a representation of completed children.

Illustrative case: A field-service tablet must remain readable in sunlight while preserving clear alarm and status communication.

Read: subject overview. Level 4 directory target: Explore specialist directory (planned / verify) display-technologies-articles/index.html (local status unverified). Neighboring subjects: Accessibility, User Interface Design, Input Method Design.

User Interface and User Experience Design

UI and UX share techniques but answer different questions. Interface design defines the immediate interaction surface and behavior; user experience design assesses the wider journey of goals, touchpoints, support, and outcomes.

1. User Interface Design

What this unit studies: Design the visible and interactive presentation layer: controls, information hierarchy, layout, status, navigation, feedback, and interface behavior. Principal inquiry: How should the immediate interface communicate actions, system state, and results?

Ownership and boundaries: Navigation controls, form elements, visual hierarchy, status communication, responsive layouts, interface patterns, and coherent component behavior. Boundary: UX owns the larger user journey; Input Method Design owns modality-specific command design; Accessibility supplies cross-cutting inclusion requirements.

Planned specialist scope: Visual hierarchy and layout; navigation patterns; forms and validation; feedback and system status; responsive UI; component libraries; design systems; error and empty states.. These are editorial subject areas for eventual Level 5 articles, not a representation of completed children.

Illustrative case: A dashboard needs to distinguish live status, historical reports, actionable alarms, and irreversible controls.

Read: subject overview. Level 4 directory target: Explore specialist directory (planned / verify) user-interface-design-articles/index.html (local status unverified). Neighboring subjects: User Experience Design, Accessibility, Input Method Design.

2. User Experience Design

What this unit studies: Design and evaluate the complete sequence of user goals, journeys, services, expectations, and outcomes before, during, and after a particular interface session. Principal inquiry: Can users accomplish their intended outcome across the whole service journey?

Ownership and boundaries: User research, journey and service mapping, information architecture, task flows, research synthesis, testing strategy, and experience-quality assessment. Boundary: UI owns specific interface elements and visual behavior; business domain divisions own specialized institutional objectives.

Planned specialist scope: User research and interviews; personas and evidence-based segmentation; journey mapping; information architecture; prototypes; usability testing; service blueprinting; experience metrics.. These are editorial subject areas for eventual Level 5 articles, not a representation of completed children.

Illustrative case: A student can register for classes only after identity verification, course selection, payment, and confirmation succeed as one coherent flow.

Read: subject overview. Level 4 directory target: Explore specialist directory (planned / verify) user-experience-design-articles/index.html (local status unverified). Neighboring subjects: Human-Computer Interaction, User Interface Design, Accessibility.

Accessibility and Inclusive Interaction

Accessibility is both a dedicated technical subject and a requirement across every other unit. Inclusive evaluation must reflect real users, assistive technologies, and contexts rather than assume a single standard way of seeing, hearing, moving, or thinking.

1. Accessibility

What this unit studies: Remove barriers to perceiving, navigating, understanding, and operating a system across varied abilities and conditions. Principal inquiry: Can people with differing capabilities complete equivalent essential tasks?

Ownership and boundaries: Inclusive interaction requirements, accessible content and controls, assistive-technology compatibility, accommodations, and testing with affected users. Boundary: The engineering of input and output devices remains in Hardware Systems; computing-wide ethical duties are covered by Computing Ethics.

Planned specialist scope: Accessible navigation and keyboard operation; screen-reader semantics; captions, transcripts, and multimodal alternatives; cognitive accessibility; assistive-technology interoperability; accessibility testing.. These are editorial subject areas for eventual Level 5 articles, not a representation of completed children.

Illustrative case: A public application portal must work through keyboard navigation, screen reading, zoom, and low-bandwidth assistive use.

Read: subject overview. Level 4 directory target: Explore specialist directory (planned / verify) accessibility-articles/index.html (local status unverified). Neighboring subjects: User Interface Design, User Experience Design, Input Method Design.

Essential distinctions: HCI studies the relationship between people and interactive technology; interaction models articulate the action/feedback structure; input methods translate human intent; displays present perceptible visual output; UI specifies immediate interface composition and controls; UX evaluates the whole journey; and accessibility tests whether the experience remains operable for people with varied needs. A successful design must integrate these without treating any one of them as a synonym for the others.

Illustrative Design and Evaluation Scenarios

These constructed teaching cases illustrate the division's subject boundaries. They do not describe verified projects, measured MGU performance, or specific documented failures.

Scenario 1 — A public benefits application excludes keyboard-only users

A public portal appears functional with a mouse but cannot complete a required confirmation using the keyboard alone. Accessibility owns the equivalent access requirement, semantics, focus order, error messaging, and assistive-technology testing. User Interface Design owns the form controls and state presentation; Interaction Models explains the broken action-state transition; User Experience Design considers the consequences across the full application journey. A visual pass is not evidence of successful task completion by users of alternate input.

Scenario 2 — A payment interface displays confirmation before settlement

A customer reads “completed” when a service has only received an instruction and the next step may still fail. User Interface Design owns precise status labels and feedback hierarchy, while Interaction Models formalizes what state was actually reached and which transitions remain. User Experience Design examines anxiety, support calls, and recovery across the journey. The Applications of Computing division retains responsibility for financial-domain transaction meaning and reconciliation. Clear interaction must reflect real backend states.

Scenario 3 — A worker uses a tablet with gloves and limited connectivity

A technician must record readings outdoors using protective gloves, intermittent network access, and a small screen. Input Method Design evaluates touch targets, voice alternatives, command precision, and safe offline input. Display Technologies addresses glare, contrast, motion, and readability; User Interface Design addresses forms and saved-state feedback; Accessibility ensures alternatives accommodate relevant capability constraints. Reliable input and display choices must be validated under actual working conditions.

Scenario 4 — A control-room screen hides critical alarm priority

An operator receives multiple alarms but the interface makes an urgent fault visually indistinguishable from routine notifications. Human-Computer Interaction investigates attention, workload, and operator behavior. User Interface Design establishes alarm hierarchy and acknowledgement feedback; Display Technologies addresses legibility and viewing distance; Interaction Models formalizes acknowledgement, escalation, and recovery states. Physical-control safety requirements remain in the appropriate industrial-control domain.

Scenario 5 — A university redesigns student enrollment

Course search, prerequisites, permissions, billing, and confirmation all work individually, yet students abandon the end-to-end process. User Experience Design owns the journey map, research, handoffs, task completion, and iterative testing. User Interface Design improves screen-level navigation and feedback; Human-Computer Interaction supports empirical task evaluation; Accessibility checks comparable participation by all users. Academic-record logic remains in the education application domain.

Scenario 6 — A new voice assistant frequently misunderstands commands

A hands-free device accepts spoken commands but misinterprets them in noisy locations and makes errors difficult to correct. Input Method Design owns recognition-related interaction behavior, confirmations, ambiguity resolution, and alternate controls. Interaction Models defines dialogue states and recovery transitions; Human-Computer Interaction studies user expectations and observed error patterns; Accessibility evaluates whether voice-only operation excludes people who cannot or prefer not to speak.

Scenario 7 — An immersive training platform causes disorientation

A spatial training simulation is visually impressive but some participants cannot navigate it comfortably. Display Technologies examines latency, motion presentation, field of view, and perceptual considerations, while Input Method Design addresses movement and selection controls. Human-Computer Interaction supports task-based studies of orientation and workload; Accessibility considers nonimmersive and alternate interaction routes. Underlying headset engineering belongs to Hardware Systems.

Scenario 8 — A subscription flow uses confusing cancellation controls

A service makes purchase easy but hides renewal terms and makes cancellation hard to find. User Experience Design assesses the total consumer journey and misleading friction; User Interface Design examines wording, button prominence, confirmation, and path consistency; Human-Computer Interaction investigates observed comprehension and user behavior. Computing Ethics within Applications of Computing provides a distinct analysis of manipulation, autonomy, and fair choice. High conversion does not by itself imply a well-designed or ethical experience.

Recurring lesson: begin with the user's goal and context, identify the action/feedback sequence, select the human-facing modality, assess interface and journey quality, and validate access and recovery for real users. Do not infer usability from technical functionality or appearance alone.

Interaction Design and Evaluation Framework

The following decision gates can structure analysis of a new product, a failed interaction, or an existing service redesign. They are intentionally cross-disciplinary: a team should not complete an input-only or UI-only check and presume the human interaction problem has been solved.

Decision gatePractical questionExpected evidenceStarting subject
1. Define people, tasks, and contextWho uses the system, for which goal, in what environment, and with which constraints?User groups; representative tasks; context observationsHuman-Computer Interaction
2. Map intended outcomesWhat counts as successful completion, not merely a successful click?Task success criteria; journey map; completion checkpointsUser Experience Design
3. Model actions and stateWhich operations and transitions are possible, and what happens after errors?State model; user actions; system responses; recovery pathsInteraction Models
4. Choose input modalitiesHow can each user express commands and data, including alternate methods?Input map; precision needs; fallbacks; error handlingInput Method Design
5. Define perceptible outputWhat must users see or otherwise perceive, at which distance and conditions?Visibility and display requirements; alternatives and redundancyDisplay Technologies
6. Design interface structureWhere are actions, information, navigation, warnings, and results presented?Wireframes; component specifications; status and error patternsUser Interface Design
7. Validate accessible operationCan users with varied capabilities complete every material task?Assistive-technology testing; keyboard paths; captions; inclusive testingAccessibility
8. Test with representative participantsWhat do real users understand, do, miss, or misinterpret?Task observations; time, errors, completion; qualitative findingsHuman-Computer Interaction
9. Check the complete serviceDo handoffs, interruptions, support, and later returns make sense?Journey evidence; abandonment analysis; service recoveryUser Experience Design
10. Revise and govern the designWho owns defects, changing contexts, new devices, and ongoing improvement?Prioritized fixes; retesting; change records; design ownershipUser Interface Design

Evidence discipline: task success, task time, error rate, recovery success, assistive-technology interoperability, user comprehension, and user satisfaction measure different aspects of quality. A single aesthetic score or aggregate conversion figure cannot establish accessibility, safety, fairness, or suitability across all user groups. Identify critical user groups and tasks before interpreting metrics.

Suggested Study Pathways

These pathways are structured reading sequences through existing or indexed subject overview destinations. They do not assume that any proposed specialist directory or child article has already been created. The Interaction Models link is retained from the original index and should be verified locally before publication.

Orientation for new readers

Start with the general discipline, distinguish UI from UX, and examine accessibility as a foundational requirement. Suggested sequence: Human-Computer Interaction → User Interface Design → User Experience Design → Accessibility.

Empirical human factors and interaction theory

Study human behavior, formal action/feedback descriptions, and the relationship between design choices and observed outcomes. Suggested sequence: Human-Computer Interaction → Interaction Models → Input Method Design → User Interface Design.

Visual interface and product design

Move from perception and interface composition toward complete journeys and the evidence needed to refine them. Suggested sequence: Display Technologies → User Interface Design → User Experience Design → Accessibility.

Input and multimodal interaction

Investigate how users issue commands, how systems respond, and how input and output choices affect inclusive usability. Suggested sequence: Input Method Design → Interaction Models → Display Technologies → Accessibility.

Accessibility-first product assessment

Begin with accessibility requirements, then examine the controls, complete task sequence, and user research needed to validate equitable operation. Suggested sequence: Accessibility → User Interface Design → User Experience Design → Human-Computer Interaction.

Human-facing system evaluation and editorial production

Use the subject overview to identify ownership; then verify each Level 4 specialist directory and develop its planned Level 5 child collection without duplication. Suggested sequence: Human-Computer Interaction → Accessibility → Input Method Design → User Experience Design.

Related Computer Science Divisions and Topics

These are neighboring divisions, not additional Human Interaction Systems units. They house general-purpose technology, implementation methods, application requirements, and ethical analysis that interaction specialists often need to consult.

  • Computer Science volume — Parent volume for all Computer Science divisions.
  • Hardware Systems — Physical input/output devices, displays, sensors, processors, and device engineering; human-facing interaction requirements are studied in this division.
  • Software Systems — Operating systems and applications that implement user-facing functions and controls.
  • Programming and Development — Implementation, testing, component development, and maintenance practices for interactive software.
  • Applications of Computing — Domain-specific systems and institutional requirements, including Computing Ethics as a cross-cutting evaluative discipline.
  • Data Systems — Representation, organization, retrieval, transformation, and stewardship of the information presented by interfaces.
  • Security Systems — Authentication, authorization, and protective controls; interaction design must make the security workflow usable without redefining security architecture.
  • Networking Systems — Communication technology, latency, and availability influencing the perceived responsiveness of connected interfaces.
  • Computational Foundations — Algorithms and formal models that may underlie interactive behavior.
  • Computer Architecture — System organization and technical performance constraints relevant to human-facing processing.
  • Specialized and Emerging Systems — Advanced and emerging technologies that introduce new interaction modalities and evaluation challenges.
  • Basics — Independent introductory computing guides, not replacements for encyclopedic HCI and UX coverage.

Editorial ownership: this division owns the human-facing interaction and evaluation problem, not all technology used by an interface. For example, a speech interface belongs here for dialogue design and error correction; underlying recognition algorithms belong to their relevant computational subject. Display panels are studied here in relation to human visibility and use, while their electronics and construction remain in Hardware Systems. User privacy and manipulation risks should cross-reference Computing Ethics rather than duplicate its full normative treatment.
