Unit Overview and Taxonomy

Accessibility studies how computing systems can be perceived, understood, navigated, and operated by people with diverse sensory, motor, cognitive, speech, and situational needs. Its principal question is: Can people with differing capabilities achieve the essential task with meaningful independence, comparable information, and an effective path for recovery? Accessibility is not a visual theme, a single assistive device, or a checklist that substitutes for usable outcomes.

Within the MGU Encyclopedia hierarchy, Computer Science is a volume; Human Interaction Systems is its Level 3 division; Accessibility is the division-level overview; and this page is the Level 4 specialist unit index. Its 26 prescribed child articles are Level 5 topics with physical targets of accessibility-articles/<slug>.html. Public links are extensionless. Any future narrowly scoped companion article should sit under the appropriate Level 5 subject rather than multiply peer units without a clear boundary.

Evidence and editorial distinction: the existing public Accessibility overview was observed while defining this unit; it discusses keyboard access, screen readers, alternative input, text scaling, media alternatives, inclusive design, and standards. The 26 specialist children below are a new editorial plan, not an assertion that these 26 files already exist. No recursive inventory of accessibility-articles/ was supplied for this build. Hyperlinks to planned child destinations are intentionally active in accordance with the encyclopedia's hierarchy convention.

Scope boundary: this unit owns accessible access to human-facing tasks, including applicable technical semantics and evidence of use. User Interface Design owns general interface layout and components; User Experience Design owns the entire product/service journey; Input Method Design studies input modalities generally; Display Technologies owns visual-output technology; and Computing Ethics owns general rights and normative governance. Shared topics are cross-referenced rather than cloned.

Foundations, Inclusion, and Conformance

Define the populations, environments, obligations, and evidence that make accessibility a design requirement rather than a cosmetic preference.

Accessibility Fundamentals and Disability Models

Governing question: How do impairments, environmental barriers, and task contexts interact to produce exclusion?

Specialist scope: Establishes the distinction among accessibility, usability, accommodation, and inclusive participation. Covers sensory, motor, cognitive, speech, and combined needs; permanent, temporary, and situational constraints; and the social and functional models of disability without treating any user group as a single homogeneous category.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns the vocabulary and conceptual frame for identifying barriers. Detailed remediation techniques, assistive technologies, and compliance testing are developed in later articles.

Illustrative task: Compare whether a silent instructional video excludes a viewer because of a hearing limitation, a noisy train, or both; the missing textual alternative is the actionable design barrier.

Planned child: accessibility-fundamentals-and-disability-models. This active destination is prescribed by the unit index; its child file has not been supplied or confirmed in the available inventory.

Inclusive Design and Universal Design

Governing question: How should product decisions accommodate variation without promising that one configuration will suit everyone?

Specialist scope: Examines inclusive and universal-design principles, adaptable interfaces, equivalent task outcomes, user choice, edge cases, and the limits of one-size-fits-all solutions. Relates accessibility requirements to iterative product design, avoiding the claim that a single universal layout can eliminate every barrier.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns cross-user design strategy and the design of alternatives. The empirical procedure for recruiting and engaging participants belongs to the research article.

Illustrative task: Design a service that permits both visual and text-led navigation, while ensuring that neither path loses a required transaction step.

Planned child: inclusive-design-and-universal-design. This active destination is prescribed by the unit index; its child file has not been supplied or confirmed in the available inventory.

Accessibility Requirements and Participatory Research

Governing question: Whose needs are represented, which tasks matter, and how are requirements validated in realistic use?

Specialist scope: Covers recruiting and compensating participants with different access needs, task-based research, informed consent, contextual inquiry, co-design, representative environments, and translating observed barriers into verifiable acceptance criteria. Distinguishes research with people from assumptions derived from personas alone.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns discovery, participation, task evidence, and acceptance requirements. Formal WCAG interpretation and technical testing methods reside in their own articles.

Illustrative task: Observe people using a form by keyboard, speech, magnification, and a screen reader; document each task-level obstruction and the required alternative.

Planned child: accessibility-requirements-and-participatory-research. This active destination is prescribed by the unit index; its child file has not been supplied or confirmed in the available inventory.

Accessibility Standards and Conformance

Governing question: Which technical requirements apply, how are conformance claims substantiated, and what are their limits?

Specialist scope: Explains WCAG 2.2 principles and testable success criteria, levels A/AA/AAA, the role of WAI-ARIA and native platform guidance, the difference between standards and laws, and the meaning of scoped conformance statements. Covers exceptions and partial-support disclosures without presenting automated scores as proof of conformance.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns standards literacy and defensible conformance claims. Legal duties vary by jurisdiction; this is not a substitute for legal advice or the implementation-level testing article.

Illustrative task: Evaluate a procurement claim of “WCAG AA compliant” by asking which WCAG version, pages, states, technologies, test methods, and known exceptions are covered.

Planned child: accessibility-standards-and-conformance. This active destination is prescribed by the unit index; its child file has not been supplied or confirmed in the available inventory.

Semantics, Navigation, and Operable Controls

Explain how interface structure becomes perceivable and operable through the browser, platform accessibility APIs, keyboards, and assistive technologies.

Semantic HTML and the Accessibility Tree

Governing question: How does program structure communicate meaning to assistive technology?

Specialist scope: Examines native HTML semantics, headings, landmarks, lists, tables, labels, reading order, and how user agents expose an accessibility tree. Explains where source order, CSS layout, DOM order, and perceived navigation order can diverge, and why native controls are generally preferable to custom replacements.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns source structure and semantic mapping. ARIA attributes and their design-system conventions are treated in the following article.

Illustrative task: Compare an unlabeled sequence of styled divs with a form using actual headings, labels, and fieldsets; inspect the resulting keyboard and screen-reader experience.

Planned child: semantic-html-and-the-accessibility-tree. This active destination is prescribed by the unit index; its child file has not been supplied or confirmed in the available inventory.

Accessible Names, Roles, States, and Properties

Governing question: How can an interface convey what a control is, what it is called, and what has changed?

Specialist scope: Covers accessible name computation, descriptions, role semantics, state and property communication, live region use, and common ARIA failure modes. Establishes that ARIA can communicate semantics but does not automatically supply keyboard behavior or repair broken interface logic.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns names, role/state communication, and accessible API semantics. Composite-widget interaction behavior belongs to the interactive-components article.

Illustrative task: Test whether a search button with only an icon exposes a useful name and whether an expanded disclosure conveys its state without excessive announcements.

Planned child: accessible-names-roles-states-and-properties. This active destination is prescribed by the unit index; its child file has not been supplied or confirmed in the available inventory.

Keyboard Access and Focus Management

Governing question: Can a person complete the entire workflow without a pointing device and without losing their place?

Specialist scope: Explains sequential focus, visible focus, operable controls, shortcuts, logical focus order, focus trapping risks, dialog focus return, skip mechanisms, and avoidance of keyboard traps. Separates keyboard availability from quality: every control may technically receive focus while the journey is still incoherent.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns keyboard-level task execution and focus behavior. Hardware switches and alternative physical input devices are covered in the motor-accessibility article.

Illustrative task: Complete a multi-step application using only Tab, Shift+Tab, Enter, Space, arrows where applicable, and Escape; record where focus is hidden or stranded.

Planned child: keyboard-access-and-focus-management. This active destination is prescribed by the unit index; its child file has not been supplied or confirmed in the available inventory.

Accessible Navigation and Information Architecture

Governing question: How do people discover location, structure, and the next viable action in large interfaces?

Specialist scope: Studies page landmarks, headings, orientation cues, navigation consistency, link purpose, breadcrumb meaning, skip links, search and filtering, clear pathways, and orientation after navigation or dynamic updates. Includes task structure for both visual and nonvisual navigation.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns discoverability and navigation structure. Information architecture as a general UX discipline remains within User Experience Design.

Illustrative task: Compare a 100-page service with repetitive unstructured links against one using meaningful headings, landmarks, and predictable navigation names.

Planned child: accessible-navigation-and-information-architecture. This active destination is prescribed by the unit index; its child file has not been supplied or confirmed in the available inventory.

Accessible Interactive Components and ARIA Patterns

Governing question: How do dynamic widgets preserve predictable operation and semantics?

Specialist scope: Covers dialogs, menus, disclosure controls, tabs, tree views, comboboxes, sortable grids, carousels, and dynamically inserted content. Uses WAI-ARIA Authoring Practices patterns while emphasizing that a role alone does not implement focus behavior, event handling, or status announcements.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns composite component contracts and interaction patterns. Detailed basic keyboard rules are a prerequisite; component styling belongs primarily to User Interface Design.

Illustrative task: Design a modal that announces its name, moves initial focus intentionally, supports Escape appropriately, and returns focus to the invoking control.

Planned child: accessible-interactive-components-and-aria-patterns. This active destination is prescribed by the unit index; its child file has not been supplied or confirmed in the available inventory.

Visual, Nonvisual, and Media Accessibility

Ensure information does not depend on a single visual, auditory, or presentation channel.

Color Contrast and Visual Presentation

Governing question: How can information remain distinguishable when color vision, lighting, or contrast sensitivity differs?

Specialist scope: Covers textual and nontext contrast, not using color as the sole indicator, focus indicator visibility, visual hierarchy, high-contrast settings, color themes, and viewing-environment variability. Distinguishes legibility from aesthetics and acknowledges that luminance contrast alone does not guarantee full usability.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns visible information differentiation. Physical screen performance and device display engineering belong to Display Technologies.

Illustrative task: Assess an error state shown by red borders alone, then add a text message, programmatic association, and a visibly distinguishable indicator.

Planned child: color-contrast-and-visual-presentation. This active destination is prescribed by the unit index; its child file has not been supplied or confirmed in the available inventory.

Text Resizing, Zoom, and Responsive Reflow

Governing question: Can content adapt to different visual scales without obscuring critical tasks?

Specialist scope: Investigates browser zoom, text-only resizing, reflow, spacing overrides, magnification, flexible layouts, overflow, overlapping controls, and preservation of content/function when the viewport is constrained. Emphasizes testing with real content, controls, and bidirectional scroll conditions.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns content adaptability at the interface layer. Device pixel-density and display hardware analysis remain in Display Technologies.

Illustrative task: Increase text size and zoom on a checkout form, then verify that the total, error summary, and submit control remain perceivable and usable.

Planned child: text-resizing-zoom-and-responsive-reflow. This active destination is prescribed by the unit index; its child file has not been supplied or confirmed in the available inventory.

Emphasizes variation across assistive-technology/browser combinations.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns nonvisual reading and operation experience; programmatic name and role implementation is developed in the semantics group.

Illustrative task: Ask a reader to locate a required form section by headings and landmarks rather than listening sequentially to the entire page.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns accessible media alternatives and their information quality. Media encoding and distribution infrastructure belong to Entertainment Computing.

Illustrative task: Evaluate a training video where an on-screen diagram changes but the narration never describes it; provide a suitable visual description alongside accurate captions.

Accessible Images, Charts, and Nontext Content

Governing question: What textual or structured representation communicates the function of information carried by a visual?

Specialist scope: Covers decorative versus informative images, alternative text, image buttons, diagrams, data visualizations, charts, infographics, equations, icons, and complex relationships requiring adjacent text or tables. Explains why generic alt text and raw data dumps are not always adequate.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns nontext information equivalence. Broad scientific visualization techniques belong to Scientific Computing; these pages focus on accessible interpretation.

Illustrative task: For a multi-series chart, supply a concise trend summary and accessible underlying values while preserving the visual presentation for sighted readers.

Tasks, Cognition, Motor Control, and Sensory Safety

Governing question: Can people identify fields, recognize errors, correct them, and complete high-stakes tasks?

Specialist scope: Explores visible and programmatic labels, instructions, grouped controls, validation timing, error summaries, status messages, input assistance, review/confirmation, and preserving entered data after errors. Pays particular attention to lengthy enrollment, payment, and eligibility workflows.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns completion and error recovery in user-entered tasks. General form aesthetics and conversion optimization belong to User Interface Design and User Experience Design.

Illustrative task: Submit a form with missing required information; determine whether each problem is announced, linked to its field, understandable, and recoverable without data loss.

Worked Accessibility Scenarios

Each scenario is constructed for instruction, not presented as a reported incident or a verified defect on an MGU service.

Scenario 1. Public benefits application with keyboard-only use

An applicant navigates without a pointer and must complete eligibility questions, upload a document, and receive a confirmation. Test focus order, visible focus, accessible error messages, field groupings, session expiration, and the final confirmation state. The responsible article cluster is keyboard access, accessible forms, cognitive accessibility, and testing; a visually attractive portal that cannot be submitted by keyboard fails its core service task.

Scenario 2. Streaming lecture with missing nonverbal information

A lecture has captions for speech but demonstrates a chemical process solely through moving images. Correct captions and speaker labels are necessary but not sufficient: a description of visually important changes, accessible playback controls, and an equivalent text or tactile representation may also be required. The media-alternatives and nontext-content articles share the analysis.

Scenario 3. Financial dashboard read through a screen reader

A dashboard communicates account risk using green and red icons and an interactive chart. The required reading includes meaningful names, non-color risk indicators, a text explanation of the trend, accessible values, and a logical heading order. Mere exposure of each pixel as an image label does not establish equivalent decision support.

Scenario 4. Hospital intake kiosk with motor limitations

A patient must sign in on a touch kiosk that requires a narrow swipe and a short timeout. Evaluate a large and operable target, a non-gesture path, sufficient completion time, privacy of assistive output, and staff-supported alternatives where needed. Device hardware is relevant, but the unit owns the accessibility of the task and service.

Scenario 5. University course PDF with apparent headings

A scanned course guide looks well organized but has no tagged structure or accessible text. Students cannot reliably navigate chapter headings, tables, or references. A remediated and verified source format, export procedure, reading order, and alternative text is needed; an online delivery platform does not automatically repair the underlying document.

Scenario 6. Product dialog that appears usable but traps focus

A purchase confirmation modal is drawn on screen but focus remains behind it or never returns when it closes. Correct design requires a named dialog, appropriate initial focus, keyboard operation, predictable focus containment, status communication, and return of focus. ARIA attributes alone cannot implement these behaviors.

Scenario 7. Voice-controlled mobile banking with label mismatch

A button visibly says “Pay bill,” but the interface exposes only an unrelated internal label to speech-control software. A user cannot activate it by speaking its visible name. Evaluate the label/name relationship, voice-independent completion route, confirmation semantics, privacy, and recovery from recognition error.

Scenario 8. AI-generated captions and visual summaries

A platform auto-generates captions and chart descriptions at scale. Test omissions, misleading terminology, speaker attribution, sensitive-content disclosure, editing workflows, and user access to corrections. Automation can accelerate production but should not be treated as proof that essential meaning has been preserved.

Common lesson: classify the barrier by the essential task that fails, record the circumstances under which it fails, supply a viable alternative when necessary, then demonstrate that a representative user can complete the task after remediation.

Assessment and Implementation Framework

This ten-gate sequence links introductory design work, acceptance criteria, accessibility engineering, testing, and ongoing ownership. Use it to scope a new system or diagnose an existing one; it does not certify conformance on its own.

Decision gatePractical questionExpected evidence
1. Identify essential tasksWhich real outcomes must every user be able to accomplish?Task inventory, critical path, affected roles, environment, foreseeable constraints.
2. Include representative usersWhich access needs, assistive tools, and situational conditions are relevant?Participatory research plan; recruitment and consent; contextual findings.
3. Specify appropriate standardsWhat technical criteria and jurisdiction-specific obligations will be evaluated?Versioned criteria, scoped assumptions, legal/policy review where needed.
4. Preserve programmatic meaningCan the platform expose names, roles, states, relationships, and reading order?Semantic specification and accessibility-tree inspection.
5. Guarantee independent operationCan tasks be completed through keyboard and meaningful alternative input?Keyboard sequence, visible focus, gesture alternatives, target review.
6. Supply information alternativesCan people perceive critical content through nonvisual, nonauditory, or simplified pathways?Text alternatives, reflow, contrast, captions, descriptions, clarity.
7. Support recovery and choiceCan users understand errors, pause activity, and complete tasks after interruptions?Validation and recovery flows, session behavior, reduced motion, clear feedback.
8. Test beyond automated scansDo representative users and assistive technologies succeed in realistic conditions?Automated results plus manual inspection, real task evidence, compatibility matrix.
9. Assign defects and release decisionsWho owns barriers, temporary alternatives, acceptance criteria, and communication?Prioritized backlog, responsible owner, mitigation, due date and release gate.
10. Monitor after deploymentHow are regressions, platform changes, and user-reported obstacles handled?Periodic retests, user feedback channel, maintenance ownership, corrective action.

Decision principle: passing a scanner or producing an accessibility statement is not equivalent to verified access. Evidence should include important states, complete tasks, the applicable platform and assistive technologies, observed failures, and accountable follow-up.

Suggested Study Pathways

These sequences serve distinct reading needs. A planned link may not yet resolve until its child file is authored; the sequence specifies editorial reading order rather than asserting publication.

Foundational reader

Begin with the barrier model, then the design philosophy, empirical discovery, and only afterward the normative framework. Planned sequence: Accessibility Fundamentals and Disability Models → Inclusive Design and Universal Design → Accessibility Requirements and Participatory Research → Accessibility Standards and Conformance.

Semantic and front-end engineering

Use programmatic meaning as the base layer; verify operability and dynamic state rather than relying on ARIA syntax alone. Planned sequence: Semantic HTML and the Accessibility Tree → Accessible Names, Roles, States, and Properties → Keyboard Access and Focus Management → Accessible Interactive Components and ARIA Patterns → Accessibility Testing and Assistive-Technology Compatibility.

Content and communication

Follow the transformation from visual presentation to adaptable content, media equivalents, and transferable document structure. Planned sequence: Color Contrast and Visual Presentation → Text Resizing, Zoom, and Responsive Reflow → Accessible Images, Charts, and Nontext Content → Captions, Transcripts, and Audio Description → Accessible Documents and Electronic Publications.

Complex public-service workflows

Prioritize essential task completion, user comprehension, alternative controls, and reproducible evaluation. Planned sequence: Accessible Navigation and Information Architecture → Accessible Forms, Errors, and Recovery → Cognitive Accessibility and Clear Language → Motor Accessibility and Alternative Input → Accessibility Testing and Assistive-Technology Compatibility.

Mobile and emerging interaction

Distinguish the accessibility contract from the hardware and algorithms; maintain alternative routes when novel modalities fail. Planned sequence: Mobile, Touch, and Gesture Accessibility → Native Applications and Operating-System Accessibility → Speech Input and Voice Accessibility → Immersive, Spatial, and Emerging Accessibility → AI-Assisted Accessibility and Adaptive Interfaces.

Institutional ownership and procurement

Progress from evidence of user needs through scoped acceptance claims to repeatable testing and remediation governance. Planned sequence: Accessibility Requirements and Participatory Research → Accessibility Standards and Conformance → Accessibility Testing and Assistive-Technology Compatibility → Accessibility Governance, Procurement, and Remediation.
