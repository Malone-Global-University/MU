Interaction Models

MGU Encyclopedia — Computer Science — Human Interaction Systems

Encyclopedic entry for Interaction Models.

Interaction models explain how people form goals, communicate actions to digital systems, perceive system responses, and decide whether an intended outcome has been achieved. They serve as practical tools for designing, analyzing, and evaluating the relationship between user behavior and computer behavior.

Definition and Scope

Interaction models are conceptual or analytical representations of how people communicate with a computer system, how the system interprets their actions, and how its responses become understandable to them. In human-computer interaction (HCI), a model abstracts a complicated exchange into identifiable participants, goals, actions, translations, states, and feedback. It provides a vocabulary for investigating why an interface succeeds or fails.

People do not ordinarily operate software for the purpose of pressing a button. They have goals, such as saving a document, locating information, entering an order, or completing a lesson. Interaction models connect these human goals to the controls, commands, signals, and system responses that make the goals achievable. A useful model must describe both what the user is trying to accomplish and what the computer makes possible and visible.

The term has more than one established usage. A process model describes the sequence of human action and interpretation; an interaction framework identifies the user, input, system, and output components; an interaction style describes a recognizable way to communicate with software; and a task or predictive model examines the operations involved in completing a task. These are related but not interchangeable categories. Nor is an interaction model the same thing as a software architecture such as Model–View–Controller.

Why Interaction Models Matter

Interaction models make the behavior of an interface easier to reason about before, during, and after implementation. They help designers identify missing actions, ambiguous controls, invisible state changes, excessive steps, and weak recovery paths. Engineers can use them to define state transitions and system responses; usability researchers can use them to diagnose difficulties observed during testing.

Without a model, development teams may focus on individual screens while overlooking the larger sequence through which a user completes a task. A control can be visually attractive but poorly connected to the user's goal. A process can be technically correct but fail to communicate whether anything happened. These problems are especially consequential in financial software, medical devices, public-service systems, education platforms, and other environments in which mistakes have practical costs.

Interaction models are not automatic prescriptions for a particular interface. They provide structured explanations and questions; the appropriate design still depends on the task, user population, context, safety requirements, and evidence from actual use.

The Goal–Action–Feedback Loop

At the simplest level, interaction forms a repeating cycle: a person establishes a goal, chooses an action, provides input, receives a system response, interprets the result, and decides what to do next. The cycle may repeat many times during a single activity.

  1. Goal: determine the desired outcome, such as moving a file to another folder.
  2. Action selection: identify an available control, command, or gesture.
  3. Input: activate the control through a keyboard, pointing device, touch surface, speech interface, or other mechanism.
  4. System processing: interpret the input, apply rules, and update application state when appropriate.
  5. Feedback: communicate the relevant result through the interface.
  6. Interpretation and evaluation: assess whether the action achieved the original goal, then continue, correct, or stop.

For example, clicking Save is an input event, but the meaningful outcome is whether the intended document was stored in the intended location. A brief confirmation, a changed file-status indicator, or a visible file entry can help the user evaluate the result. A click that produces no observable response leaves the final stages of the cycle uncertain.

This basic loop is an instructional simplification rather than a complete theory of cognition. More formal frameworks distinguish additional stages and translations.

Norman’s Execution–Evaluation Cycle

Donald Norman's execution–evaluation cycle is among the most influential models used to describe human action around interactive systems. It organizes interaction into seven stages, beginning with a goal and ending with evaluation of the resulting state. The stages are analytical distinctions: in real activity they may overlap, repeat, or proceed rapidly rather than forming a rigid sequence.

  1. Forming the goal: identifying what outcome is wanted.
  2. Forming the intention: deciding generally what action will produce that outcome.
  3. Specifying an action sequence: choosing particular operations that the interface permits.
  4. Executing the action: carrying out the operations.
  5. Perceiving the system state: noticing what the interface now displays or communicates.
  6. Interpreting the system state: understanding what the observed result means.
  7. Evaluating the outcome: comparing the interpreted result with the original goal.

Consider a person who wants to print two copies of a document. The goal is to obtain two printed copies; the intention is to use the application's print function; specification involves finding the print command and choosing the copy count; execution involves submitting the request. The person must then perceive and interpret the print status and output before concluding that the goal was achieved.

The model demonstrates that a failure can occur before the computer receives a command or after the computer has successfully processed one. It is therefore valuable for diagnosing issues that cannot be attributed solely to program logic.

The Gulfs of Execution and Evaluation

Two related concepts describe the distance between human goals and a system's available behavior. The gulf of execution is the difficulty of determining how to act on a system to achieve a goal. The gulf of evaluation is the difficulty of determining what state the system is in and whether the goal has been accomplished. These concepts were articulated by Edwin Hutchins, James Hollan, and Donald Norman in their 1986 work on direct manipulation and were further popularized through Norman's writing on design.

A large gulf of execution arises when an action is hidden behind obscure terminology, an unlabeled icon, or a complex command sequence that users cannot infer. Designers can narrow the gulf by providing meaningful labels, discoverable controls, consistent mappings, examples, and progressive guidance.

A large gulf of evaluation arises when feedback is absent, delayed, unclear, or easily misinterpreted. A file-upload service that silently accepts a file but does not indicate its status leaves users unsure whether they should retry. Designers can narrow this gulf with truthful status information, clear success and error messages, visible state, and a reliable means of inspecting results.

These gulfs interact. A person who misunderstands the current state may choose the wrong next action even if the controls themselves are clearly presented. Effective design supports both acting and interpreting, rather than treating feedback as a decorative addition.

The Abowd–Beale Interaction Framework

The Abowd–Beale framework, associated with Gregory Abowd and Russell Beale, extends analysis beyond the user's action cycle to include four interacting components: the user, input, system, and output. These components communicate through translations between representations or “languages.” The emphasis is on the complete human–machine exchange rather than solely on the person's experience of an interface.

  1. Articulation (user to input): the user expresses a goal by selecting an interface action, such as typing a command or pressing a control.
  2. Performance (input to system): the system interprets that interface input as an operation to perform.
  3. Presentation (system to output): the system translates its state or computation into an observable representation.
  4. Observation (output to user): the user perceives and interprets the presented information.

In a spreadsheet, a user may type a formula (articulation), the application parses and calculates it (performance), a numeric result or error appears in the cell (presentation), and the user reads and judges the result (observation). Difficulty at any translation boundary can undermine the overall experience. For example, a valid calculation presented in an ambiguous number format may cause an interpretation error even when the computation is correct.

The framework is especially useful when designing an interface that must mediate between the terminology of its users and the technical representations used internally by software.

Common Interaction Styles and Paradigms

An interaction style is a customary pattern through which users express requests and receive responses. The style is not identical to the device: a touchscreen can present menus, forms, direct-manipulation objects, and conversational controls. Most contemporary products mix styles according to the task.

  • Command language: users enter explicit commands, often with arguments and options. Command shells can be efficient, expressive, repeatable, and scriptable, but unfamiliar syntax increases the burden on new users.
  • Menu selection: users choose among displayed options. Menus favor recognition over memorization, although deeply nested choices can make frequent operations slow.
  • Form-based interaction: users enter or review information in structured fields, often with validation. Forms suit records, applications, and transactions but can be cumbersome if requirements are unclear or errors erase prior work.
  • Direct manipulation: users act upon visible representations of objects, as when dragging a file, resizing a window, or moving a shape in an editor.
  • Question-and-answer or wizard interaction: a system guides the user through a defined sequence of prompts and decisions. This can reduce complexity for unfamiliar tasks but may constrain expert workflows.
  • Conversational interaction: a user expresses requests in natural language through text or speech. Flexible phrasing can lower the barrier to entry, while ambiguity and variable system interpretation create new design challenges.
  • Touch, gesture, and spatial interaction: controls respond to taps, swipes, tracked movement, gaze, or gestures. These may support embodied tasks but require attention to precision, fatigue, and discoverability.

The familiar desktop WIMP pattern—windows, icons, menus, and pointer—is one graphical interface paradigm, not a definition of all graphical or all human-computer interaction. A single application may combine a WIMP interface with keyboard commands, search, drag-and-drop operations, voice control, and automation.

Direct Manipulation and Reversibility

Direct manipulation became a foundational idea in interface design through the work of Ben Shneiderman and other HCI researchers. Rather than asking users to describe an operation in specialized syntax, the interface presents objects and allows people to act upon their representations. Typical characteristics include the visibility of objects, incremental action, rapid feedback, and operations that can be reversed where feasible.

In a drawing application, moving a shape by dragging it illustrates the idea: the selected object remains visible, its location changes as the pointer moves, and the user can see the consequence without executing a separate textual command. File managers use related ideas when they let people drag items between folders.

Direct manipulation is not universally superior. Drag-and-drop may be difficult for someone using a keyboard, switch device, or limited-precision pointing device. It may also be imprecise for large sets of objects or actions requiring complex parameters. Robust systems often provide equivalent menu commands, keyboard operations, or explicit move dialogs. Undo, confirmation for consequential actions, and clear feedback strengthen the interaction without depending exclusively on gestures.

Dialogue Models, States, and Transitions

Some interaction models concentrate on the dialogue between a person and an application: which actions are permitted at a given moment, how the interface changes after an action, and which outcomes return control to the user. A state-transition model represents the interface as a set of states connected by events or actions.

Consider an online submission process. Its states might include not started, draft saved, validation failed, submitting, submitted, and submission failed. The action Submit may be allowed only when required information is present. A network error should move the interface to a recoverable failure state rather than falsely indicating success.

State-transition diagrams, task flows, and dialogue specifications help developers find missing paths, unreachable screens, inconsistent back-navigation, and unsafe duplicate actions. They can also be used to design tests. For example, the transition from submitting to submitted should be triggered by actual confirmation of the transaction, not merely by a button press.

A state machine describes permitted behavior, but does not by itself establish whether people will understand that behavior. Technical correctness and user comprehension remain separate evaluation questions.

Mental Models and Conceptual Models

A mental model is a person's internal understanding of how a system works, formed through prior knowledge, observation, and experience. A conceptual model is the system's organizing explanation—communicated through its design, labels, behavior, and documentation—of what users can do and what will happen. Interaction succeeds more reliably when the interface helps users form a mental model that predicts important outcomes accurately.

For instance, a folder metaphor helps people understand file organization by connecting unfamiliar digital behavior to the familiar idea of grouping objects in containers. The metaphor is useful but incomplete: digital files can be copied, linked, synchronized, and accessed remotely in ways that physical documents cannot. A good design uses familiar concepts while teaching the differences that matter.

Users do not all begin with the same experiences or expectations. Localization, prior software exposure, expertise, disability, and task context can change what seems intuitive. Designers should therefore test their proposed conceptual model with representative users rather than assume that an interface is self-explanatory.

Task Models and Predictive Analyses

Interaction research also uses models that decompose a task into component operations. GOMS stands for Goals, Operators, Methods, and Selection rules. It is a family of approaches for describing the knowledge and procedures a person uses to perform tasks, often in relatively structured expert interaction. Related methods, including the Keystroke-Level Model (KLM), can estimate task execution time under specified assumptions.

Suppose two interfaces let a trained worker locate a record. One requires several mouse selections; another permits a keyboard shortcut followed by a typed identifier. A task model can make the sequence of operations explicit and support a reasoned comparison. Such estimates are not measurements of every person's real performance. They depend on assumptions about user expertise, task structure, interruptions, and the operations included in the model.

Unlike Norman's action cycle, which emphasizes action and interpretation, predictive task models often focus on the operations and methods needed to achieve a goal. They complement, rather than replace, observation, usability testing, and accessibility evaluation.

Multimodal, Adaptive, and AI-Mediated Interaction

Multimodal interaction combines or offers different input and output modalities, such as speech, touch, keyboard input, visual displays, audio feedback, and haptic signals. A driver might speak a destination while receiving a spoken confirmation and a map; a student might use a keyboard to navigate a lesson while a screen reader announces changes. The value lies in enabling effective interaction in different contexts, not in maximizing the number of modalities.

Adaptive systems may alter their presentations or recommendations according to device characteristics, user preferences, or observed behavior. Such adaptation can reduce work, but frequent unannounced changes can weaken predictability and interfere with learned workflows. Important actions should remain understandable and controllable.

Conversational and AI-mediated interfaces introduce an additional translation stage: software must interpret a natural-language request and decide how, or whether, to act. When interpretation is uncertain, a clarifying question or preview may be more appropriate than an irreversible action. A generated response should not be mistaken for verified execution. Clear distinctions among suggested action, action in progress, and confirmed outcome are essential for trustworthy interaction.

Accessibility and Inclusive Interaction

Interaction models are useful only if they account for different ways people perceive and operate a system. Users may interact through a mouse, keyboard, touch screen, screen reader, voice recognition, switch controls, magnification, or other assistive technologies. A design that assumes one physical action is available to everyone excludes legitimate users and contexts.

Inclusive design calls for keyboard-operable controls, discernible labels, visible focus, meaningful feedback, sufficient time where appropriate, and alternatives to gesture-only actions. Information such as success or error state should not be conveyed solely through color, sound, or animation. A status update that is visually obvious may need a programmatically available equivalent so assistive technologies can present it.

The W3C's Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2 and ARIA Authoring Practices Guide provide relevant technical guidance for web interactions. Their provisions do not amount to one general HCI theory, but they translate broad interaction principles into practical requirements and design patterns. Accessibility evaluation should involve actual keyboard and assistive-technology operation, not visual inspection alone.

Worked Example: Uploading an Assignment

Consider a student uploading a completed assignment through a university learning platform. The student's goal is not merely to select a file; it is to deliver the correct work and receive dependable confirmation that submission is complete.

  1. Discover the action: the interface presents a clearly labeled Upload assignment control, with accepted file types and any size limits.
  2. Provide input: the student chooses a file with the keyboard, pointer, or operating system's file picker; optional drag-and-drop is an alternative rather than the only method.
  3. Validate: the platform checks the selected file and explains any correctable errors without discarding the student's work.
  4. Process: while the transfer is occurring, the interface accurately identifies it as pending and prevents unintended duplicate submission.
  5. Present the result: after the server confirms receipt, the platform displays the file name, submission status, and relevant timestamp.
  6. Enable evaluation and recovery: the student can inspect the submitted file or replace it when policy allows; failures have a clear retry path.

Norman's model asks whether the student can identify the appropriate action and understand its result. Abowd and Beale's framework examines how the selected file and submission request travel through input processing and output presentation. A state-transition model checks that uploading, failed, and submitted are distinguished correctly. Accessibility testing determines whether the same task is practical without a mouse or visual-only feedback.

The example demonstrates why several models may be applied to the same feature: each reveals different classes of failure.

Applying Interaction Models in Design and Evaluation

Teams can apply interaction models through a disciplined workflow that connects theory with observed behavior. The first step is to define the user's actual goal, the context of use, and the consequences of failure. Next, map the interaction's actions, states, feedback, and recovery routes. Identify points at which a user must remember a command, infer hidden behavior, wait without feedback, or interpret an unfamiliar technical term.

Design reviews and cognitive walkthroughs can ask whether a person will know what to do, recognize the correct control, understand the effect of an action, and realize when the goal is complete. Heuristic evaluation considers established usability principles, while usability testing observes representative users attempting representative tasks. Neither an expert review nor a theoretical model substitutes for evidence from real users.

Useful measures may include task completion, elapsed time, error and recovery rates, requests for assistance, and users' understanding of system status. These measures must be interpreted in context. A faster process is not necessarily better if it increases costly errors, hides important choices, or excludes users of alternative input methods.

Limits and Distinctions

Interaction models simplify reality. They may underrepresent emotion, social collaboration, interruptions, institutional rules, cultural expectations, privacy concerns, and the long-term development of expertise. A model suited to a single user manipulating an object may require extension for shared documents, remote collaboration, automated agents, or systems that perform work while the user is absent.

It is important to distinguish interaction models from adjacent terms. Information architecture concerns the organization and labeling of information; interface design concerns the actual controls and presentation; user experience design considers the broader experience over time; input method design concentrates on capturing and interpreting user actions; and Model–View–Controller is an application architecture for separating responsibilities in software. These disciplines can inform one another without being synonymous.

No interaction model proves an interface usable or safe. A sound design combines suitable models, explicit implementation rules, representative testing, and ongoing revision when evidence identifies a problem.
