1. Definition, Scope, and Governing Question

Accessibility standards translate access needs into requirements that authors, evaluators, purchasers, and institutions can discuss using shared terms. Conformance is the determination that specified content satisfies the applicable normative requirements of a named standard at a declared level and within the scope permitted by that standard. It is not an impression that a page feels usable, a score produced by a scanning tool, or a vendor's assurance that accessibility was considered. A conformance assertion needs a precise target, an identifiable subject, evidence, and an account of what the evaluation did not establish.

The governing question of this specialist article is: Which technical requirements apply, how are conformance claims substantiated, and what are their limits? An acceptable answer states the standard and version; the target level; the product, pages, and states in scope; technologies relied upon; the methods and conditions of evaluation; the unresolved findings; and, where applicable, the separate legal or contractual obligation. Without these distinctions, phrases such as “accessible,” “AA ready,” and “fully compliant” can obscure more than they explain.

The central technical reference is the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2, a W3C Recommendation published in October 2023 and updated subsequently. WCAG defines technology-neutral, testable success criteria for web content. Other references address assistive-technology semantics, authoring tools, user agents, native software, information-and-communications technology, documentation, and procurement. Different products and jurisdictions can legitimately cite different baselines at the same time. A newer recommendation does not automatically change an older version incorporated into law or a contract.

Three additional questions must remain separate. First, does the product meet a technical standard? Second, does the organization meet its legal and contractual duties? Third, can affected people actually complete important tasks in their real environments? Positive evidence for one question supports, but never automatically establishes, the others. The Accessibility Requirements and Participatory Research article owns research with people and task-derived requirements. The Accessibility Testing and Assistive-Technology Compatibility article owns specific testing procedures and compatibility engineering. The present article owns standards literacy, the logic of conformance, evidence interpretation, and the discipline of making claims proportionate to findings.

2. Standards, Guidance, Regulation, and Contracts: Distinct Sources of Authority

Accessibility requirements arrive through several instruments. A technical specification describes what must be true of a system to satisfy its criteria. An informative guide illustrates techniques, testing procedures, and design rationale without necessarily adding normative obligations. A law or regulation identifies whom an enforceable duty applies to, which activities are covered, which exceptions exist, and sometimes which technical standard is incorporated. A procurement contract or policy can impose requirements beyond a statutory minimum. A conformance report records results against selected criteria; it is not itself the source of the criteria.

The distinction matters because a development team may implement a generally respected accessibility pattern without satisfying the success criterion relevant to its actual implementation. Conversely, a valid technique other than one illustrated in an official guidance document can satisfy a technology-neutral criterion. A government procurement baseline might cite WCAG 2.0 while the internal engineering roadmap targets WCAG 2.2. Both statements can be true, but a report must say which is being evaluated and which is legally required.

2.1 Normative and informative text

The principal success criteria and conformance requirements of WCAG are normative. Explanatory examples, advisory techniques, and many notes help the reader understand those rules but are not additional pass–fail obligations. The W3C documentation on WCAG resources and About WCAG Techniques make this division explicit. An evaluation records whether the normative condition is met, not whether a team used one favored framework or copied an example verbatim.

2.2 Responsibility for interpretation

Standards bodies publish specifications and guidance; legislators and regulators determine some legal obligations; courts and enforcement bodies may interpret law; purchasers define contracts; evaluators examine concrete implementations. Product owners must not collapse these authorities. When a question concerns statutory coverage, a contractual exception, undue burden, equivalence under the law, or another legal defense, competent jurisdiction-specific review is necessary. A technical auditor can describe functional failures without issuing a legal ruling.

3. WCAG's Four Principles: Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, Robust

WCAG 2 is organized around four principles, often abbreviated POUR. These principles are not four individually scored tests. Each contains guidelines, which contain testable success criteria. Their value is diagnostic: they explain why a defect matters and help organize corrective work. A single experience may implicate several principles at once.

3.1 Perceivable

Information and user-interface components must be presented in ways users can perceive. This addresses text alternatives for meaningful images, captions and alternatives for time-based media, adaptable content structure, distinguishability of text and controls, visual contrast, and the ability to separate foreground and background. A chart with a visual legend but no equivalent description can be perceptually unavailable to a nonvisual reader, even though its colors and labels are visually polished. A video that is captioned but omits information expressed only in imagery may still have an unmet media-access need. The specific criterion, content type, and level determine the formal requirement.

3.2 Operable

Users must be able to operate interface components and navigation. Relevant concerns include keyboard access, focus behavior, sufficient time, avoidance of flashing hazards, predictable navigation, pointer gestures, dragging alternatives, and pointer target size. An apparently simple image-button toolbar fails users when the controls cannot receive keyboard focus. A sticky heading can obscure the focused element and create a new barrier; WCAG 2.2 adds specific focus-obscuration criteria. Operability must be evaluated in changing states, not inferred from an initial screenshot.

3.3 Understandable

Users must be able to understand information and the operation of an interface. This includes readable language indications, predictable behavior, identified input errors, meaningful labels and instructions, error prevention in relevant contexts, consistent help, avoidance of redundant re-entry, and accessible authentication. A form can have perfect contrast and still be unusable because a rejected field is not identified, a required step is unexplained, or previously entered information must be typed again without a permitted reason. The goal is reliable understanding and action, not merely short prose.

3.4 Robust

Content must be robust enough to be interpreted reliably by relevant user agents, including assistive technologies. Programmatic names, roles, and values are especially important for custom widgets. Robustness does not mean that every assistive-technology product must work identically under every possible configuration; it requires satisfying the relevant technical criteria and the standard's accessibility-support rules. Valid native semantics often reduce ambiguity. The WCAG 2.2 removal of the older 4.1.1 Parsing criterion does not eliminate the need for correct structure, relationships, names, roles, or states.

A practical team should ask both “Which criterion governs this failure?” and “What is the actual access consequence?” The first question supports defensible conformance interpretation; the second prevents remediation from becoming an exercise in meeting numbers without restoring meaningful use.

4. Anatomy of a Success Criterion and the Evidence It Requires

A WCAG success criterion has an identifier, a name, an assigned conformance level, and normative conditions. The identifier is stable within the version in which it appears. For example, 1.1.1 Non-text Content (A) concerns text alternatives under specified conditions; 1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum) (AA) describes minimum text contrast with exceptions; 2.1.1 Keyboard (A) concerns keyboard operability; and 4.1.2 Name, Role, Value (A) concerns programmatic interface information. The exact wording, definitions, exceptions, and applicability determine the finding. A paraphrase is an orientation, not a substitute for the specification.

A requirement is not necessarily applicable to every page in the same way. A page containing no prerecorded video does not fail caption-related criteria merely because it lacks captions. A site containing video must evaluate the relevant media criteria according to the type of media and required level. Some criteria apply only to particular interactions, technologies, or content structures; others, including fundamental keyboard operability, affect nearly every interactive workflow. “Not applicable” therefore demands a reason tied to actual content, not a convenient label used to reduce reported failures.

1.2.2 Captions (Prerecorded) A Relevant prerecorded synchronized media Caption timing, speech coverage, and important sound information
1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum) AA Applicable text and images of text Calculated contrast under the actual colors, font size, weight, and exceptions
2.1.1 Keyboard A Interaction without a pointing device Completing essential controls and sequences with keyboard input

4.1 Success criteria versus implementation techniques

A product team might satisfy a text-alternative requirement using a native HTML mechanism, a longer equivalent description, or another permitted construction, depending on the content. The relevant question is whether the result meets the criterion. W3C techniques show sufficient approaches and common failures, but the set of techniques is not exhaustive and not independently mandatory. A team's approved engineering patterns may impose stricter internal conventions for maintainability; those are legitimate design controls but should be labeled as internal controls rather than falsely attributed to the standard.

4.2 Defects, severity, and pass–fail results

The severity of an observed failure is useful for remediation priority, yet WCAG conformance is not awarded by severity-weighted averaging. An unresolved Level A failure on a covered page is not offset by dozens of satisfied Level AA criteria. A tester might classify the failure as “critical for task completion,” “high-frequency,” or “limited to a rare branch” to determine repair order, but such labels do not change a criterion's normative status. Report both the conformance finding and the operational consequence.

5. WCAG 2.0, 2.1, 2.2, and the Developing WCAG 3 Model

WCAG 2.0 became a W3C Recommendation in 2008 and provided a durable foundation for technology-neutral success criteria. WCAG 2.1, published in 2018, expanded coverage particularly for mobile access, low vision, and cognitive and learning needs. WCAG 2.2, first published as a Recommendation on October 5, 2023, introduces additional success criteria addressing focus visibility and occlusion, dragging and pointer target interactions, consistent help, redundant entry, and authentication barriers. The W3C recommends WCAG 2.2 for new or updated web accessibility policies when possible. This recommendation is not itself a change to every preexisting legal mandate.

WCAG 2.2 was designed to remain backward compatible in substantive accessibility requirements with WCAG 2.1 and 2.0. One narrow exception needs explicit handling: success criterion 4.1.1 Parsing, present in prior versions, is obsolete and removed from WCAG 2.2. A project reporting directly against WCAG 2.0 or WCAG 2.1 should still attend to that version's formal criterion and not assume the older text was legally amended by publication of a newer standard. The W3C explains the parsing change in its WCAG 2 FAQ.

Version Initial W3C Recommendation Expansion or defining feature Reporting caution
WCAG 2.0 December 2008 Original WCAG 2 POUR structure and success criteria Still referenced by revised U.S. Section 508
WCAG 2.1 June 2018 Mobile, low-vision, cognitive and learning-related additions Cited in the U.S. ADA Title II web rule and the European harmonized baseline discussed below
WCAG 2.2 October 2023 Nine additional success criteria and removal of obsolete 4.1.1 Strong current engineering target; do not claim it is mandated wherever older versions remain incorporated
WCAG 3.0 Working Draft as of September 2026 Developing approach and wider accessibility outcomes Not a finished W3C Recommendation or replacement conformance baseline

The September 2026 WCAG 3 Working Draft is important for horizon scanning, but its approaches and conformance model remain in development. A vendor advertising “WCAG 3 certified” is making a claim that should be questioned: there is no completed WCAG 3 Recommendation establishing that as a normal WCAG 2-style conformance status as of this article's preparation on November 23, 2026.

Version management is a lifecycle responsibility. The report header must record the actual target version and date; the organization must maintain an explicit change log when upgrading to a newer target. The engineering team can deliberately implement criteria beyond the legally referenced baseline and report this separately. That is more credible than replacing all versions with a vague statement such as “meets the latest WCAG.”

6. The Nine New WCAG 2.2 Success Criteria

The nine new WCAG 2.2 success criteria form an important bridge between modern interface behavior and technical evaluation. The numbers below correspond to WCAG 2.2 and their levels in that Recommendation. These short descriptions are teaching summaries, not the normative wording and not a substitute for checking the criteria's exceptions and defined terms.

Criterion Level Central access issue Illustrative verification question
2.4.11 Focus Not Obscured (Minimum) AA Keyboard focus must not be entirely hidden by author-created content Does the focused control remain at least partially visible after opening a sticky bar?
2.4.12 Focus Not Obscured (Enhanced) AAA Keyboard focus is not obscured by author-created content Is the entire focused component visible in the tested state?
2.4.13 Focus Appearance AAA Focus indicators must satisfy defined appearance requirements Does the focus treatment meet the criterion's geometry and contrast conditions?
2.5.7 Dragging Movements AA Dragging-dependent functions need a non-dragging pointer alternative unless an exception applies Can a list item be moved with discrete controls instead of dragging?
2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum) AA Pointer targets need sufficient size or separation, subject to exceptions Are small adjacent icon controls separated or otherwise eligible for an exception?
3.2.6 Consistent Help A Repeated help mechanisms occur in a consistent relative order Is help discoverable in the same relative position across a multi-step service?
3.3.7 Redundant Entry A Previously entered information in a process is not unnecessarily requested again Can an applicant avoid re-entering the same address at a later step, unless an exception applies?
3.3.8 Accessible Authentication (Minimum) AA Authentication avoids required cognitive function tests unless a permitted alternative or exception applies Can a person authenticate without memorizing or transcribing a secret from memory as an unavoidable requirement?
3.3.9 Accessible Authentication (Enhanced) AAA Stronger restrictions on cognitive-function tests in authentication Does the sign-in workflow meet the enhanced criterion without relying on only the minimum-level allowances?

Two features make this list especially useful. First, not all nine criteria are Level AA: some are A and several are AAA. Second, a legitimate exception is not a blanket exemption. For example, the Dragging Movements criterion includes exceptions for essential dragging and user-agent-controlled behavior; Target Size has its own defined exceptions. The evaluator must examine the normative conditions rather than infer failure from the mere presence of a small icon or a draggable interface.

WCAG 2.2 also requires care in product planning. A document might state an older WCAG 2.1 AA target because of a current statutory reference; a product roadmap can additionally target 2.2 AA so that focus obstruction, target size, authentication, and other barriers receive explicit attention. These are different reporting sentences and should not be merged into an unsupported “all standards met” declaration.

7. Understanding Conformance Levels A, AA, and AAA

WCAG 2 conformance levels are cumulative. A claim of Level A requires all applicable Level A success criteria to be satisfied. Level AA requires all applicable A and AA criteria; Level AAA requires A, AA, and AAA criteria. A page does not reach AA because “most AA tests passed,” because a sample of pages looked good, or because a vendor resolved all findings ranked critical. The standard includes the possibility of a conforming alternate version under its specific requirements, but that is not permission to substitute an inaccessible primary experience with a vague offer of telephone assistance.

Level AA is a common policy and procurement target because it covers many important barriers while providing an attainable baseline for a wide range of products. W3C cautions against requiring Level AAA conformance as a general policy across an entire site: some content cannot satisfy every AAA success criterion. This does not diminish the value of individual AAA practices. For example, better focus appearance, sign-language alternatives, enhanced contrast, and clearer language may be strategically valuable even where formal AAA conformance is not claimed.

7.1 Conformance is not a score

Imagine two sites evaluated against the same target. Site A has a 98% automated-check success figure but a blocked login button that cannot receive keyboard focus. Site B has a lower scanner score due to repeated low-risk warnings but permits all core tasks and passes a complete standards-based review. The percentage alone is not an acceptable basis for a conformance claim about either site. Tools differ in rule coverage, checks performed, elements counted, and how false positives are managed. Failure of an applicable criterion remains a failure regardless of the percentage of other checks that passed.

7.2 Documenting progress without overstating conformance

A product owner can accurately disclose that “the evaluated pages satisfy WCAG 2.2 Level A, with unresolved Level AA issues at specific locations,” if evidence supports that wording. It can also state “most tested Level AA criteria passed, but the evaluated page does not conform at Level AA,” provided the sampling and criterion-level findings are described. The first formulation describes an achieved level; the second describes progress toward a higher one. In neither case should an isolated component test be promoted to a statement about all pages of a product.

8. The Five Requirements for WCAG 2.2 Conformance

An evaluator must consider five conformance requirements, not merely the checklist of individual success criteria. The normative WCAG 2.2 conformance section supplies the controlling details.

8.1 Conformance level

The page must satisfy the relevant A, AA, or AAA success criteria in full, or have a qualifying conforming alternate version. A higher-level declaration incorporates the lower levels. The denominator is not a customizable set of convenient checks chosen by the vendor.

8.2 Full pages

Conformance is evaluated for the full page, not a selected panel, header, or widget while the rest of the page is ignored. Responsive variations automatically presented for different screen sizes are included in this requirement. It is legitimate to describe a narrower evaluation scope covering specified pages, but not to say a page conforms while excluding a known failing feature within that page.

8.3 Complete processes

When pages form a sequence necessary to accomplish an activity, the relevant pages of that process must conform at the stated level. A checkout's product selection and billing screens do not qualify independently if the final authorization step is inaccessible. Similarly, login, payment, appointment booking, and multi-stage eligibility applications must be examined as coherent workflows, including consequential branches.

8.4 Accessibility-supported ways of using technologies

Only uses of technologies supported by relevant accessibility features of user agents and assistive technologies can be relied upon to satisfy success criteria. For example, if important form instructions are presented through a novel mechanism that common supported configurations cannot expose, an alternative accessible-supported representation may be necessary. The aim is not an arbitrarily large list of operating systems; it is a defensible account of the technology patterns relied upon and the environments in which those patterns are accessible.

8.5 Non-interference

Content that is not relied on to satisfy accessibility requirements, or technologies used in non-conforming ways, must not block access to the conforming portions of the page. A decorative animation that triggers a seizure hazard or a keyboard trap inside an optional panel can defeat use of an otherwise accessible page. WCAG identifies particularly serious non-interference criteria that apply to content even when that content is not otherwise relied upon. A product cannot treat “optional” as synonymous with “permitted to interfere.”

These requirements also distinguish a formal page-level conformance claim from a practical statement that a design system component has been tested against selected criteria. Component libraries can provide valuable evidence, but their successful test records do not confer conformance on every assembled page or complex task using them.

9. Page Scope, Dynamic States, Responsive Variants, and Complete User Processes

A modern service is rarely a single static page. Interfaces change when dialogs open, data loads, forms are submitted, validation fails, authentication expires, language changes, or layout adapts to a narrower display. A static scan of the default route may omit the most consequential accessibility barriers. Reliable scope definition therefore records the deployed release, product areas, user roles, supported languages, page templates, content types, interaction states, and complete processes relevant to the claim.

A representative-sampling evaluation can provide disciplined evidence about a larger website, but one must distinguish evaluating a sample from proving that every page conforms. The evaluator should include common templates, high-risk tasks, forms, media, documents, embedded services, and unusual states, and record unknown areas that were not checked. Where a product has thousands of pages, sampling can be a reasoned strategy; it is not a magic license to assume every untested instance shares the results. WCAG-EM 2.0 explicitly addresses structured and random sampling, complete processes, and limitations of claims.

Scope element Weak practice Defensible practice
Page inventory Test the homepage only Define product boundaries and select varied templates and content types
Responsive variants Assume desktop success transfers to mobile Evaluate relevant screen sizes, zoom states, and layout changes
Authentication Exclude sign-in as “third party” without analysis Record identity provider dependency and whether the process is within the claimed service
Error states Test only a successful form submission Include invalid input, errors, recovery, timeout, and confirmation
Media and files Ignore PDFs, recordings, and third-party components Record content types, ownership, exceptions, and evaluated conditions
User roles Test an anonymous visitor only Cover important authenticated and permission-specific tasks
Deployed change Reuse last year's report without review Attach results to versions and reassess changes affecting accessibility

9.1 A worked claim boundary

Suppose an institution evaluates its public course catalog, student login, and application form. It finds that the catalog template and public search conform, but that a custom date picker in the authenticated application creates a keyboard trap. A statement that “the entire admissions website conforms to WCAG 2.2 AA” would overreach. A statement that “the named public catalog pages were evaluated and found to conform at WCAG 2.2 AA, while the full application process does not conform because of the documented keyboard failure” is more useful. The second statement identifies a technical fact and an actionable boundary rather than masking the failed essential workflow.

10. Accessibility Support, User Agents, and Assistive Technology

Accessibility support concerns whether a technology is used in ways that make its accessibility features available to users through relevant user agents and assistive technologies. It is not identical to popularity, browser market share, or availability of a library. A custom web widget can be visually operable yet expose no role, label, or state to an accessibility API. Conversely, a native input can expose important semantics without manually reproducing them in script. Evaluating the mechanism and resulting information is more reliable than endorsing a framework by brand name.

A support baseline should identify realistic combinations of browsers, operating systems, devices, input methods, accessibility settings, and assistive technologies relevant to the service's audience and technologies. It is possible to define documented support boundaries, but those boundaries should not be manipulated to exclude a user group solely to justify a conformance assertion. International services, public-sector platforms, and high-stakes applications may require broader testing than an internal single-platform product. Some technologies rely on platform accessibility APIs rather than web semantics; the evaluative question then involves the appropriate normative reference and platform support.

Examples include a screen reader announcing the accessible name of a button, a speech-input system matching a visibly displayed label, keyboard navigation following a meaningful order, a screen magnifier retaining access to the focused control, and captions functioning in the actual media player. The WAI-ARIA standard governs additional web semantics; the ARIA Authoring Practices Guide provides useful, informative interaction patterns. Implementation details properly belong to the Semantic HTML and the Accessibility Tree and Accessible Interactive Components and ARIA Patterns specialist articles.

10.1 Do not confuse compatibility with conformance

Successful use by one screen reader is useful evidence about one configuration; it does not establish compliance with every applicable success criterion. A valid WCAG success-criterion test may also detect a problem before it becomes visible in limited user research. Both are useful: conformance testing examines defined requirements, whereas user testing reveals task burdens and unmet needs that criteria may not fully capture.

11. Conforming Alternate Versions, Third-Party Content, and Partial Conformance Statements

WCAG allows a conforming alternate version under specified conditions. Such a version must satisfy the relevant conformance level and the standard's requirements for equivalent information and functionality, language, currency, and access from the nonconforming page. A rough text-only summary, a phone number without an equivalent pathway, or an alternate document that omits crucial steps is not automatically a valid conforming alternate version. The engineering goal should ordinarily be to make the primary experience accessible rather than force affected users into a segregated secondary product.

A second distinction concerns statements of partial conformance for certain content outside the author's control. WCAG describes a specific kind of partial-conformance statement for web pages where third-party or user-generated content can cause barriers; it does not convert the page into a page that fully conforms. Another form addresses situations in which accessibility support for a language is unavailable. The formal conditions and recommended wording matter. A vendor cannot simply label known defects in its own required checkout widget as “exceptions” and then pronounce the page conformant.

Also distinguish a technical WCAG statement from statutory exemptions or defenses. A regulation may have categories of exempt content, conditions for undue burden or fundamental alteration, or obligations to provide alternate access. The boundaries of such rules must be read in the governing instrument and never presumed from a WCAG note about alternate versions. In the U.S. ADA Title II rule, for example, certain exceptions concern preexisting archived content, preexisting conventional electronic documents, some third-party content, individualized password-protected documents, and preexisting social-media posts; exceptions are qualified and do not automatically remove duties to provide access to services. An evaluation should identify the exact basis of any proposed exception and refer legal questions to qualified review.

The safest reporting method separates: (a) full conformance established for identified pages, (b) evaluation findings on sampled content, (c) statements of partial conformance expressly permitted by the standard, (d) contractual limitations, and (e) potential legal exceptions. These are not interchangeable levels of certainty or equivalent kinds of claim.

12. ARIA, Native Interface Guidance, Documents, and Adjacent Standards

The ecosystem of accessibility standards is broader than web-content success criteria. WAI-ARIA defines roles, states, and properties used to communicate additional semantic information about web interfaces to user agents and assistive technologies. The HTML platform has native elements and behaviors that often provide the correct semantics, keyboard interaction, and accessible properties with less custom code. ARIA does not create a separate WCAG conformance level, nor does adding attributes automatically make a control accessible. WCAG evaluates the resulting experience against its criteria; the ARIA specification has its own technical requirements and processing model.

The ARIA Authoring Practices Guide documents approaches for widgets such as dialogs, menus, tabs, listboxes, and tree views. These patterns are informative design resources, not a new independent product certification. Teams should use them as implementation and testing support, then test behavior and programmatic meaning in the target environment. A correctly named button that never responds to keyboard activation can still fail operability requirements. A keyboard-operable custom listbox that exposes incorrect selection state can still fail robust semantics.

Other W3C initiatives address accessibility beyond authored page content. The Authoring Tool Accessibility Guidelines (ATAG) relate to tools that help people author accessible content and can enable authors with disabilities to use those tools. The User Agent Accessibility Guidelines (UAAG) concern browsers and other user agents. These do not automatically substitute for WCAG when evaluating a public page; they answer different system-design questions. Digital documents and non-web software may be governed by adapted WCAG requirements or other technical clauses under regulations and ICT standards. Native mobile and desktop applications also depend on platform accessibility APIs, gestures, focus systems, and operating-system conventions.

A procurement office considering a packaged learning management system, for example, should examine not just the learner's web screens but also the instructor's content-authoring functions, embedded documents, mobile app, external authentication, and administrative software. WCAG alone may be necessary but insufficient to describe the full product's accessibility commitments. Its scope and the additional referenced standard must be identified explicitly.

12.1 Multiple standards can apply to one service

A single public-benefits portal may be evaluated against WCAG for its website, a jurisdiction-specific ICT standard for its non-web documents or kiosk software, a contract for vendor reporting, and task-based accessibility requirements derived from residents' needs. These obligations should form a traceable matrix rather than a single checkbox labeled “accessibility.” Where requirements differ, the matrix must preserve their source, applicability, version, and acceptance evidence.

13. United States Regulatory Context: Revised Section 508 and ADA Title II

The United States provides an instructive example of why the technically newest WCAG version and the legally incorporated version are not necessarily the same. Revised Section 508 standards apply to covered federal information and communication technology, including qualifying electronic content and software. The U.S. Access Board's Revised 508 Standards generally incorporate WCAG 2.0 Levels A and AA for covered web and non-web content and software, subject to specified scope provisions and exceptions. A procurement team should examine the applicable Section 508 clauses, the subject product type, and any additional agency or contract requirements. The shorthand “the federal government requires WCAG 2.2” is not a correct description of the baseline merely because WCAG 2.2 is a newer W3C Recommendation.

The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) Title II governs state and local government services, programs, and activities. A U.S. Department of Justice final rule published April 24, 2024 establishes WCAG 2.1 Level AA as its technical requirement for covered web content and mobile applications, subject to regulatory scope, exceptions, and defenses. Crucially, the April 20, 2026 interim final rule extended the compliance dates. As of November 23, 2026, the DOJ's official Title II web rule fact sheet identifies the following dates:

State or local government category Current Title II web/mobile rule compliance date as of Nov. 23, 2026 Incorporated technical baseline
Public entities with population of 50,000 or more (subject to the rule's classification provisions) April 26, 2027 WCAG 2.1 Level AA
Public entities with population below 50,000 and special district governments April 26, 2028 WCAG 2.1 Level AA

Do not use the original 2026/2027 dates from older summaries without checking the amendment. The obligations of state and local governments under Title II do not begin and end with those new technical compliance dates; existing nondiscrimination and effective-communication duties remain relevant. Nor does the rule automatically convert every private business into a Title II public entity. ADA Title III, which governs many places of public accommodation, is a separate legal context; there is no justification for applying the Title II-specific population schedule to every private website. Other laws, state rules, education or healthcare statutes, settlements, and contracts may affect a particular service.

13.1 Exceptions must be analyzed narrowly

The Title II rule includes defined exceptions such as certain archived web content and certain preexisting conventional electronic documents. But an old document still actively used to apply for a government benefit may fall outside the preexisting-document exception. A vendor's control of a payment or scheduling interface does not automatically make that interface exempt under the exception for certain third-party postings. The DOJ guidance provides concrete examples. A legal reviewer should confirm applicability and continued obligations before an institution relies on an exception; a technical report can record the functional barrier regardless.

13.2 A sensible dual-baseline strategy

For organizations with a WCAG 2.0 or 2.1 statutory baseline, verify and report that required baseline, then consider using WCAG 2.2 AA as an additional engineering target to address newer barriers. If a service meets all 2.2 AA conditions but the legal instrument references 2.1 AA, document the mapping and attend to the 4.1.1 Parsing difference rather than asserting without qualification that the new document rewrote the statute. This avoids two opposite errors: falling behind current good practice, and claiming a legal update that has not occurred.

14. European ICT Accessibility: EN 301 549 and Legal Harmonization

Europe presents a different standards relationship. EN 301 549 is an accessibility standard for information and communication technology products and services, covering more than websites. It includes requirements relevant to web content, electronic documents, software, and other ICT contexts. It operates alongside different European legal and procurement instruments, including the Web Accessibility Directive (WAD) and European Accessibility Act (EAA). The scope and conditions of each law are not identical, and the standards relevant to a legal presumption of conformity depend on formal publication and citation processes.

In September 2026, a new edition, EN 301 549 v4.1.1, was published. It updates its web, document, and software clauses in alignment with WCAG 2.2. At the date of this article, however, the new version had not yet been cited in the Official Journal of the European Union as the harmonized legal reference for the EAA and WAD. Official AccessibleEU guidance published September 7, 2026 states that the applicable cited reference remains EN 301 549 v3.2.1 (2021), linked to WCAG 2.1 Level AA, until the formal citation changes. Thus “the newest published edition” and “the edition currently used as a harmonized legal reference” are distinct concepts.

A multinational supplier may reasonably maintain testing records for both the legally referenced edition and the newer 4.1.1 edition. It should identify which report supports an applicable European presumption of conformity, which reflects proactive preparation for a later standard, and which product clauses were actually evaluated. Merely passing selected web checks cannot justify an ACR covering software and documents that were never examined. Similarly, a published standards update does not by itself create a new EAA compliance deadline.

The relevant European Commission WAD standards and harmonization guidance and the official citation in the EU Official Journal should be checked when issuing a time-sensitive legal or procurement claim. Product teams can prepare for the newer benchmark, while legal claims must follow the formally applicable text at the time and in the relevant jurisdiction.

15. Choosing the Applicable Baseline Across Jurisdictions and Product Types

The correct conformance target is not selected simply by choosing the largest WCAG version number. Begin with the regulated entity, offered service, product type, geographic market, contract, and publication or procurement date. Then identify the current legal or contractual instrument and the version it incorporates. Only after that should the team set additional engineering targets. If a service crosses borders, the requirements register may need multiple columns rather than one winner.

16. A Reproducible Evaluation Methodology

Evaluation must be systematic enough that another qualified evaluator can understand how the result arose. The W3C WCAG Evaluation Methodology (WCAG-EM) 2.0, published as a W3C Group Note on July 23, 2026, provides a structured approach. It is an informative evaluation methodology, not an additional set of normative WCAG success criteria. It explains how to define a digital product, choose a conformance target, establish an accessibility-support baseline, explore functionality, select representative samples, evaluate those samples, and report findings.

16.1 Step 1: Define the evaluation scope

Document which product and release are evaluated, which domains, routes, screens, and processes are included, the target version and level, and the technologies relied upon. Include a method for identifying generated or authenticated views that are not obvious from a public sitemap. Where there are multiple applications under a single brand, define whether each is a distinct product or part of a coherent service. Resist an evaluation contract that promises a whole-site result but allocates time to inspect only three unrepresentative pages.

16.2 Step 2: Explore the product

Examine common views, essential functionality, different content types, and interactions that can change page behavior. A service might include a content catalog, media player, date picker, map, invoice PDF, dashboard, chat component, and payment form. Each represents a distinct risk category. Discovering scope before deep testing improves both efficiency and the honesty of the eventual conclusions.

16.3 Step 3: Select representative samples

Use both structured selection and a random or otherwise less predictable selection according to the methodology. Cover common templates, unusual widgets, content producers, languages, and all relevant pages within selected complete processes. Sampling should reflect the real product, not merely the areas the developers expect to pass. If an unexpected failure appears in a random sample, revisit the sample assumptions and expand coverage where warranted.

16.4 Step 4: Evaluate the samples

Test the applicable success criteria and all five conformance requirements. Include interaction states rather than limiting examination to initial markup. When the product changes under the user's actions, follow the process and evaluate the new state. The methodology helps organize the examination; it does not automatically test the product or turn a scan result into a complete finding.

16.5 Step 5: Report findings and limitations

Record the evaluator, date, product release, scope, target, accessibility-support baseline, sampling design, failures, evidence, and important limitations. Group repeating problems by root cause without erasing distinct user consequences. If an evaluator has not examined all necessary surfaces, say so. If an issue was corrected after the initial evaluation, distinguish the original finding, the repair, and the verified retest. The most credible report is one that can be acted upon and independently understood.

17. Automated Checks, Manual Technical Review, and User Research

A conformant product cannot be established by running an accessibility tool and accepting a headline score. Automated tools detect certain classes of machine-checkable failures: missing programmatic names, some invalid attribute combinations, color contrast in determinable contexts, some structural errors, or incorrectly used roles. They cannot reliably judge every meaningful text alternative, full task comprehension, nuanced focus behavior, accessible authentication, equivalent content, appropriate error recovery, or whether a task succeeds with assistive technology. Some findings require human interpretation even when a machine detects the pattern.

A credible evaluation typically combines three forms of evidence. Automated analysis provides breadth and repeatable early detection. Manual technical evaluation checks requirements that need judgment, dynamic interaction, keyboard navigation, visual adaptation, and programmatic behavior. Research or task-based testing with people with disabilities identifies lived barriers and service outcomes that may remain outside the precise coverage of WCAG. Each produces a different kind of conclusion. A task participant's successful completion is not proof that every normative criterion passes; a standards checklist that passes does not prove universal usability.

Evidence method Strength Common blind spot Appropriate claim
Automated scanner Repeatable checks across many routes and components Meaning, task context, dynamic branches, test-rule coverage “These machine-detectable rules were evaluated; these issues were found”
Manual WCAG review Criterion interpretation and state-specific observation May miss unselected routes or user-specific strategies “These identified screens and states were examined against these criteria”
Keyboard and assistive-technology checks Real interaction and exposure of semantics Limited to tested configurations and paths “The recorded task and control behaved this way in these configurations”

17.1 Separating tests from conclusions

A browser extension showing “zero violations” is a statement about the extension's checked rule set on the tested state, not a formal WCAG AA finding. A manual audit of every accessible name on a page is stronger for name semantics, but alone says nothing about keyboard traps in a dialog that was never opened. A complete methodology combines coverage, requirement-level examination, evidence of critical workflows, and explicit limitations. More details on executing those checks belong to the sibling Accessibility Testing and Assistive-Technology Compatibility article.

18. Evidence Traceability: From Requirement to Finding, Repair, and Retest

A conformance finding is useful when it can be traced back to a defined requirement and forward to its resolution. A disciplined register contains the applicable WCAG version and criterion, the rule's applicability, the product route or state, observed behavior, expected behavior, test environment, reproducible steps, user consequence, responsible team, remediation plan, and retest outcome. This is not bureaucracy for its own sake; it prevents an issue from becoming an untraceable assertion that “accessibility was addressed.”

Consider a custom modal dialog opened on an appointment page. A review finds that the Escape key does not dismiss the dialog and the keyboard focus moves behind the overlay. The evaluator should avoid marking an arbitrary list of criteria failed without reading their wording. Instead, record the exact reproducible behavior, examine the applicable keyboard and focus-related requirements, note whether the interface prevents further operation, and link to the appropriate criterion-level conclusions. The development team fixes the focus trap, implements a correct close path, and reruns the affected test in the original environment and relevant related paths. A report documenting only that the issue was “resolved” is weaker than a dated record of the verified change.

The register should also distinguish “passes,” “fails,” “not applicable,” “not tested,” and “cannot determine from evidence.” Those categories carry different epistemic meanings. “Not tested” is never a synonym for “passed,” and “not applicable” should have a specific reason. Technical conformance depends on the normative requirement; severity, confidence, and fix effort may be tracked as additional operational fields.

19. Conformance Claims, Accessibility Statements, ACRs, and VPAT®

Different reporting artifacts serve different audiences and should not be conflated. Under WCAG 2.2, a formal conformance claim is optional even when content actually conforms. If such a claim is made, WCAG requires the claim date; the guidelines title, version, and URI; the level satisfied; a concise description of the web pages within the claim; and the web content technologies relied upon. A conformance logo is itself a claim and must be supported by the required elements. A cautious institution should include additional useful facts such as tested environments, known constraints, product version, method, and review date, even when these are not all mandatory claim fields.

An accessibility statement is a public communication about accessibility features, standards targets, known problems, support channels, and improvement commitments. It may contain a conformance claim, but it need not. A useful statement tells a reader where barriers might remain and how to obtain help or report a defect. It should not assert “100% accessible” simply because a contractor delivered a checklist or because an institution has a published policy.

The Voluntary Product Accessibility Template (VPAT®) is a structured template maintained by the Information Technology Industry Council (ITI). When completed for a particular product and its support of named standards, it becomes an Accessibility Conformance Report (ACR). An ACR can assist procurement teams in comparing products and asking follow-up questions. There is no official VPAT or ACR certification, approval, or pass–fail status. ITI's VPAT information explains this distinction directly. A report should identify the product, tested edition, author, date, methods, criterion-level support descriptions, remarks, and limits. The existence of a report does not establish that the product was independently tested, that a specific deployment is free of barriers, or that every claim is accurate.

19.1 Example of a careful public statement

Illustrative wording, not an actual conformance claim: “The named public-information pages of Product X, release 4.2, were evaluated during November 2026 against WCAG 2.2 Level AA using documented automated and manual checks. The tested catalog pages met the applicable success criteria; the authenticated application workflow was outside that evaluation. Separate tests identified unresolved focus-order and document-accessibility issues in the application. For assistance, use the published accessibility contact channel.”

This statement helps readers understand the boundary of evidence. It does not claim that the authenticated application conforms, and it does not imply a legal determination. A formal WCAG claim would need the actual named web pages, the required technologies-relied-upon list, and valid evidence that the full conformance requirements were met for the claimed scope.

20. Accessibility Due Diligence in Procurement and Supplier Management

Accessibility requirements are often weakened at the moment organizations purchase external software. A buyer may accept an ACR describing a different product edition, a report dated several years earlier, a statement that only the marketing website was evaluated, or a row of “supports” entries without explanatory evidence. The right response is neither automatic rejection nor uncritical trust. It is a requirements-driven review, with follow-up testing and enforceable remediation commitments where the risks warrant them.

A purchaser should determine the essential functions; identify the applicable laws, standards, and contract requirements; request a current report; map the report's product scope to the intended deployment; examine known limitations; arrange representative demonstrations or trials with relevant configurations; and include accessibility obligations in acceptance testing, upgrades, and support. For high-stakes systems, include affected users in evaluating real workflows. An administrator's inaccessible configuration page can still be an institutional barrier even if end-user screens appear compliant.

20.1 Questions to ask when a supplier says “WCAG AA compliant”

  1. Which WCAG version, and which level? “AA” without a version and an identified product does not define the target.
  2. Which exact product release and surfaces? Web app, mobile app, embedded widget, documents, and admin tools may differ.
  3. Which pages, states, and complete processes were evaluated? A scanned homepage is not a checkout or enrollment workflow.
  4. What did the evaluation actually do? Ask for the methodology, manual review, assistive-technology configurations, and limitations.
  5. Who conducted the review, when, and under what access? Independent assessments can provide useful perspective, but independence alone does not compensate for weak scope.
  6. What are the unresolved findings? Request criterion-specific remarks, severity for users, ownership, and remediation timelines.
  7. What product dependencies remain? Third-party identity, media, maps, payment, and content-authoring tools may affect the service.
  8. What will the contract require after upgrades? Specify reporting frequency, defect response, retesting, and remedies.

Vendor declarations are evidence to be examined rather than a transfer of organizational responsibility. The sibling Accessibility Governance, Procurement, and Remediation article takes up procurement processes and organizational ownership in detail. Here the central issue is how to interpret a conformance assertion and identify the evidence needed to rely upon it responsibly.

21. Eight Worked Conformance and Standards Scenarios

These are constructed educational cases, not reports of actual organizational reviews. Their purpose is to practice distinguishing a visible barrier, an applicable technical rule, an appropriate evaluation claim, and the separate institutional decision that follows. In each case the exact WCAG criterion must be verified against the normative text rather than inferred solely from the short descriptions below.

Case 1 — A city portal still cites the original ADA deadline

Situation. A medium-sized U.S. municipality is updating a public-service portal in November 2026. A project spreadsheet says that WCAG 2.1 AA compliance was required in April 2026, and the team is preparing a public statement that the municipality is already legally noncompliant solely because that date passed. The portal's actual accessibility is still being evaluated.

Analysis. The DOJ published an interim final rule on April 20, 2026 extending the date for the relevant population category to April 26, 2027. The old schedule is not the current one. The team's document should cite the revised rule and verify the government's category. It should not infer that the current website is accessible from the extension; nor should it infer that all existing ADA accessibility obligations were suspended. The correct actions are to update the compliance calendar, continue a task-based accessibility review, document barriers, prioritize essential services, and seek legal guidance on the full institutional duties.

Transferable lesson. Technical targets, statutory due dates, and existing nondiscrimination duties are three different variables. A date change alters one variable, not the others.

Case 2 — A supplier has a “100%” scanner score

Situation. A student enrollment software supplier shows a scanner dashboard with a score of 100 and declares WCAG 2.2 AA compliance. A student using only a keyboard cannot dismiss a payment dialog; a student using speech input cannot activate an unlabeled icon controlling the same payment step.

Analysis. The scanner's result pertains only to its implemented checks in the tested states. The payment dialog and speech-input mismatch require criterion-level examination, including operability and labeling as applicable. The full enrollment process may fail to conform; meeting numerous automated checks cannot cancel the failure. The buyer should request reproducible test evidence, updated ACR remarks, and verified repair before treating the product as a reliable accessible enrollment system.

Transferable lesson. A score is a measurement of a tool's outputs, not a conformance level or a proxy for completed tasks.

Case 3 — A checkout page works, but the final step does not

Situation. A retailer evaluates its product listing and cart. Both pages seem to meet relevant Level AA criteria. At the last step, a dynamic payment challenge places focus behind an overlay. The retailer wants to claim that the first two pages conform while describing the checkout workflow as accessible overall.

Analysis. Under WCAG's complete-process requirement, pages forming the relevant purchasing sequence must be considered together for a conformance claim. The last step's issue cannot be treated as irrelevant if the service requires it to finish a purchase. A transparent finding can describe the observed behavior on the preliminary pages and state that the full checkout process did not meet the claimed level. The team should identify whether the challenge is a vendor-controlled integration and repair the process rather than shift the barrier to an inaccessible telephone-only alternative.

Transferable lesson. Conformance scope follows a meaningful activity as well as individual page boundaries.

Case 4 — A research dashboard has a nonconforming third-party chart

Situation. A research site hosts a chart embedded from an external provider. Its accessible text summary is incomplete, and the provider resists changes. The site owner intends to say the entire dashboard conforms except for the third-party chart.

Analysis. “Except for” is not a full WCAG conformance claim. The owner must investigate whether it controls the embedding, whether it can supply equivalent information and functionality, whether the chart is required for the page's purpose, and whether a qualifying alternate version is feasible. The W3C's partial-conformance statement for uncontrollable third-party content has specific conditions and does not recategorize the page as fully conforming. Any statutory third-party-content exception must be analyzed separately under the governing law. The report should identify the chart and the information the user cannot access.

Transferable lesson. Ownership constraints are relevant operational facts but are not a universal technical exemption.

Case 5 — A federal software contract requires an older baseline

Situation. A vendor has tested its web interface using WCAG 2.2 AA and submits the resulting summary to a federal purchaser whose contract refers to revised Section 508. The vendor assumes that every newer claim proves the applicable Section 508 requirements without additional mapping.

Analysis. The revised federal standard incorporates WCAG 2.0 A and AA in covered contexts and includes requirements for covered non-web content and software. The vendor should map the product and test results to the requirements that apply to the actual procurement. In particular, it should attend to the formal treatment of 4.1.1 Parsing in prior versions and whether non-web and documentation components were examined. The 2.2 work is highly valuable engineering evidence, but the acquisition package should not leave the applicable standard ambiguous.

Transferable lesson. Evidence may be reusable across baselines, but the baselines and their scope must remain identifiable.

Case 6 — An EU vendor treats the 2026 EN update as already harmonized

Situation. A software supplier releases an ACR for EN 301 549 v4.1.1 in October 2026. Its marketing site asserts that this automatically establishes conformity with all European Accessibility Act obligations because the updated standard has been published.

Analysis. Publication of v4.1.1 does not, by itself, mean it is already cited as a harmonized reference in the EU Official Journal. AccessibleEU's September 2026 guidance identifies v3.2.1 as the current cited edition for the stated legal reference, pending formal citation of v4.1.1. In addition, conformity under European laws may involve requirements beyond selected web criteria. The supplier should separate new-edition readiness from legally operative claims and seek current review of the relevant product obligations.

Transferable lesson. “Published standard” and “legally harmonized reference” are statuses with different consequences.

Case 7 — A PDF report contains accessible-looking headings

Situation. A university's annual report PDF contains large bold headings and high-contrast body text. A scanner of the linked HTML landing page finds no errors. Screen-reader users report that headings inside the PDF are not represented as navigable document structure, and the tables lose their row and column relationships.

Analysis. Scanning the HTML landing page says little about the PDF's own accessibility. The university must determine the applicable document baseline and evaluate the document's actual structure, reading order, alternatives, table relationships, and task purpose. If the document is within a regulated or contracted scope, exceptions and technical clauses must be assessed precisely. A remediation plan should repair the source and generated artifact, not merely modify the landing-page link.

Transferable lesson. The accessibility boundary includes actual informational artifacts, not only the website hosting their links.

Case 8 — A product uses a good ARIA pattern but fails a user task

Situation. An application uses a custom listbox modeled after a published ARIA pattern. The visible selection changes and role attributes are present. In the deployed interface, keyboard users cannot discover the available options after filtering, and screen-reader users do not hear updated results.

Analysis. Following a pattern is a design input, not an automatic certification. Evaluators should inspect actual keyboard behavior, accessible names, programmatically exposed state, dynamic announcements where required, and task completion. The team might satisfy some semantic criteria while failing others or creating a functional usability barrier that standards checks only partly capture. A robust decision will combine precise normative evaluation with task evidence, then record the repairs and retests.

Transferable lesson. Standards provide accountable thresholds; inclusive task success still requires examination of the implementation in context.

23. Ten-Step Conformance Decision and Reporting Workflow

A practical decision process should begin before a claim is drafted. The following workflow integrates standards selection, evidence, and responsible disclosure.

  1. Define the service and accountable owner. Identify the product, task, audience, public or private entity, contractual relationship, and operating jurisdiction. A web portal, mobile application, SaaS administrative console, and document archive may have distinct requirements.
  2. Inventory authoritative obligations. Identify the relevant legislation, regulation, procurement conditions, internal policy, and technical standards. Record exact editions and dates. Do not assume that “latest” is the version incorporated in an older law.
  3. Set technical targets explicitly. Record a mandatory baseline and any higher voluntary engineering target as separate fields. For example: “Title II rule: WCAG 2.1 AA” and “internal forward target: WCAG 2.2 AA.”
  4. Define product scope and exclusions. Inventory routes, component libraries, authentication, user roles, responsive variants, media, documents, and external providers. Document evaluation boundaries without excluding portions of pages in a way that contradicts the full-page requirement.
  5. Identify critical complete processes. List tasks such as account creation, application submission, bill payment, scheduling, learning assessment, or incident reporting. Include errors and consequential branching behavior.
  6. Select an evaluation method. Use a recognized methodology such as WCAG-EM where appropriate, supplemented by technical inspection and affected-user research. Match the sample design to product complexity and risk.
  7. Collect requirement-level evidence. For each applicable criterion, record observations, environments, pass/fail/applicability, uncertain results, and barriers in meaningful context. Keep the method and tested release discoverable.
  8. Remediate and retest. Address issues with high access impact and shared root causes first, but do not equate risk prioritization with a formal exemption. Verify correction in the specific affected state and related workflows.
  9. Write an evidence-bounded disclosure. If full conformance is established, state all required claim elements. If not, disclose tested areas, unresolved issues, support contacts, and the limits of the evaluation. Keep an ACR or accessibility statement distinct from a certification.
  10. Maintain the result over time. Reevaluate after substantial UI changes, third-party updates, new content, revised procurement requirements, or a changed standards reference. Date reports and record version dependencies; accessibility is a lifecycle obligation, not a one-time badge.

The completion test for this workflow is simple: can an independent stakeholder tell what was required, what was evaluated, what passed or failed, what remains unknown, and who is accountable for next action? If these answers are missing, a grand conformance label is likely premature.

24. Common Interpretation and Governance Failures

24.1 Treating “WCAG compliant” as a complete specification

This phrase omits the version, level, object of evaluation, and conformance boundaries. Repair it by supplying the exact standard reference, achieved level, qualifying page set, date, technologies relied upon, and the relevant findings. Where the claim is about procurement rather than a WCAG page-level assertion, identify the applicable ACR edition and product release as well.

24.2 Treating an automated score as formal evidence of full conformance

Scanning can identify many problems efficiently but does not cover all conditions. Replace the score-based conclusion with criterion-level checks, task states, complete processes, documented assumptions, and evidence from appropriate manual examination.

24.3 Treating a newer WCAG Recommendation as an automatic legal amendment

Technical standards evolve on their own publication schedule. A regulation, procurement term, or harmonized standard may reference a particular edition until formally changed. Preserve the distinction between forward-looking design target and currently applicable legal baseline.

24.4 Treating a vendor ACR as a certification

An ACR is a report prepared from a template; it is not an ITI-issued approval or badge. Examine dates, versions, test coverage, remarks, unresolved support gaps, and evidence rather than taking its existence as conclusive.

24.5 Treating a successful component test as a whole-product outcome

A design-system button may be well labeled and keyboard operable, while the actual checkout containing it is blocked by a modal or inaccessible authentication. Reevaluate composition, page state, responsive changes, and complete processes.

24.6 Confusing applicability, exception, and untested status

A criterion that does not apply is different from one that was not tested. A legal exception is different from a technical pass. A third-party partial-conformance statement is different from WCAG conformance. Reports must preserve these distinctions even when a procurement dashboard would prefer fewer categories.

24.7 Ignoring people whose needs are not fully captured by the criteria

WCAG acknowledges that it does not address every combination of access needs. A page that meets its technical criteria can still have unclear instructions, harmful cognitive load, insufficient language support, or task-level barriers for a particular user. Inclusive research and product improvement remain essential even when a formal level has been met.

24.8 Failing to maintain evidence after deployment

An accessibility report from an earlier release can become misleading after a redesigned component, added payment service, changed content pipeline, or new mobile behavior. A credible organization maintains traceability between the evaluated release and the live product, and has a path for reporting and correcting newly discovered barriers.

26. Applied Exercises, Study Questions, and Editorial Practice

Exercise A — Distinguish five claims

Consider these statements: “zero scanner errors,” “WCAG 2.2 AA,” “meets Section 508,” “VPAT certified,” and “users completed the registration task.” For each, identify whether it refers to an automated finding, formal standard conformance, a legal or procurement baseline, a reporting artifact, or task evidence. Rewrite each statement to make its scope and basis clear. Explain why “VPAT certified” is an unsuitable term.

Exercise B — Draft a conformance evidence request

A supplier offers a web application, a native mobile app, and a collection of exported PDFs. Write a procurement request asking for the named standard and version; target level; product editions; tested surfaces and user roles; included complete processes; assistive-technology configurations; ACR or equivalent report; unresolved issues; and retesting commitments. Explain why a website-only ACR would not establish support for every other delivered artifact.

Exercise C — Test the boundary of a public claim

A university evaluated its public homepage, course catalog, and two enrollment screens. It did not test payment authorization or the student records dashboard. Draft two sentences: one accurately describing the evaluated material and one describing the untested areas. Identify additional evidence required before the university could credibly claim that the entire enrollment process conforms at Level AA.

Exercise D — Track a regulatory update

For a fictional U.S. municipality with a population above 50,000, list the updated Title II compliance date from the April 2026 interim final rule. Distinguish that date from the rule's technical WCAG 2.1 AA baseline and from continuing ADA duties. Write a short decision log stating why a 2024 publication may contain a deadline that is no longer current.

Exercise E — Compare European standards statuses

Explain what changed in EN 301 549 v4.1.1 in September 2026 and why its publication did not immediately replace the cited v3.2.1 baseline for the European legal instruments identified in current AccessibleEU guidance. Develop a two-column reporting header: “edition evaluated for current harmonized reference” and “new-edition readiness testing.” State which official source should be rechecked before a new contract is signed.

Exercise F — Inspect a hypothetical dynamic flow

A public application shows a four-step form, an upload widget, an eligibility result, and a confirmation. List the initial and error states needed for the evaluation sample, note the complete-process requirement, and propose a reasonable combination of automated, manual, and user-based evidence. Explain why a pass on step 1 alone cannot support a whole-process conformance claim.

Exercise G — Classify a partial-conformance statement

An embedded map changes outside the author's control, cannot be navigated with a keyboard, and displays location information essential to the service. Identify the questions that must be answered before invoking WCAG's third-party partial-conformance mechanism. Distinguish this inquiry from a law's third-party-content exception and from building a properly equivalent accessible alternative.

Exercise H — Build a release sign-off record

Create a one-page internal release form containing the product version, required standard and engineering target, evaluated routes, complete tasks, test environments, findings and exceptions, known untested material, owner, repair dates, and sign-off authority. Explicitly disallow the term “fully accessible” unless its meaning is tied to defensible scope and evidence. Include an escalation route for barriers reported after release.

Self-check questions. Why is a success criterion different from a sufficient technique? What are the five WCAG 2 conformance requirements? What does Level AA include? Why does a screen-reader demonstration not establish whole-product conformance? Which change to WCAG 2.2 complicates a simplistic backward-version checklist? How is a partial-conformance statement different from full conformance? Why are the current U.S. Title II rule dates and the new European EN 301 549 edition particularly important to version-controlled reporting in 2026?

28. Related Topics and Cross-Division Context

The Human Interaction Systems division provides the larger setting for this specialist unit. Its Accessibility overview introduces the overall discipline. Human-Computer Interaction studies how people engage with interactive systems; User Experience Design addresses the experience of whole tasks and services; User Interface Design addresses interface structure and presentation.

The Computing Ethics overview in the Applications of Computing division examines rights, inclusion, accountability, and institutional consequences. Software Systems, Programming and Development, and Security Systems provide neighboring technical perspectives on implementation, maintenance, authentication, and security. Accessibility conformance must often be reconciled with security and privacy controls, but it should not be subordinated to them by default: a secure service that qualified users cannot operate is not an adequate service.

Taxonomy distinction: this article is neither a general HCI introduction nor a legal advice manual. It clarifies which technical accessibility instruments exist, how their requirements interact, how to evaluate claims, and how to avoid overstating the evidence. Detailed interaction techniques, testing execution, participatory research, and operational remediation have separately identified owners in the Accessibility unit.
