1. Definition, Scope, and Governing Question

Accessibility requirements are statements of the conditions a digital product, content service, or interaction must satisfy so people with differing functional abilities can achieve essential outcomes. Participatory research encompasses approaches that invite people affected by design decisions to shape the research questions, describe their context, examine alternatives, interpret findings, and, where appropriate, share authority over decisions. Together these practices convert a generalized commitment to accessibility into concrete, accountable knowledge about users, tasks, environments, barriers, and acceptable solutions.

The central question is whose experience establishes what the system must do, and what evidence will establish that it works? A product team might begin with “our application should be accessible,” but that goal cannot settle how a blind applicant locates application status, how a person who uses speech input activates controls, how a person with impaired dexterity corrects an accidental selection, or how someone who enlarges text completes a long form. Each situation requires an explicit account of the task, interface behavior, environment, and outcome. Technical criteria matter, but so do the real circumstances in which a product must operate.

This article owns the discovery-and-requirements portion of the MGU Accessibility unit. It addresses recruitment, representative participation, consent and compensation, accessible research instruments, observation of meaningful tasks, co-design, synthesis, acceptance criteria, evidence traceability, and uncertainty. Separate articles own detailed WCAG interpretation, implementation of individual interface patterns, formal accessibility testing, and institutional remediation governance. The boundary matters because observing a problem does not, by itself, identify its technical cause or demonstrate complete conformance.

Consider a user who cannot submit a scholarship application after enlarging text. A diagnosis-based requirement such as “support low-vision users” is too broad. The team needs to know which page, zoom or text setting, viewport, browser, task stage, and information dependency causes failure; whether the submit control becomes unreachable; whether the problem occurs for other users or configurations; and what successful completion must look like. Research supplies an evidence-based explanation, and a requirement makes the corrective obligation verifiable.

2. Human-Centered Design, Disability Expertise, and Research Roles

2.1 Human-centered design as a repeated activity

Human-centered design is not an interview at the beginning of a project followed by months of unexamined implementation. It is an iterative discipline: understand contexts of use, specify user requirements, produce designs that address them, and evaluate the resulting experience against those requirements. ISO 9241-210:2019 provides a widely recognized framework for human-centered design of interactive systems. In accessibility work, the loop must include people whose use of technology differs from that of the designers, testers, or procurement staff.

Research should influence the problem definition, not merely validate a nearly finished solution. Early interviews might reveal that a college's “simple sign-up” requires an inaccessible identity document upload, while a later design workshop might identify a viable mobile capture route, a keyboard-operable file chooser, and an assisted alternative with equivalent service. These discoveries change the requirements and the workflow before development costs become entrenched. A team that invites people only to applaud a completed design is conducting demonstration, not meaningful inquiry.

2.2 Lived expertise is essential but not interchangeable

People with disabilities bring direct knowledge of their own access practices, priorities, environmental constraints, workarounds, and the consequences of exclusion. Accessibility engineers contribute knowledge of platforms, standards, programmatic semantics, compatibility, and test methods. Domain professionals contribute knowledge of legal, clinical, administrative, or operational duties. None of these forms of expertise automatically substitutes for another. The productive research question is not which contributor is universally authoritative, but which contributors must be involved in each decision and what evidence supports the conclusion.

A participant who uses a screen reader is not appointed as a spokesperson for all blind people, all assistive technologies, or all disability communities. Nor is a specialist whose job is auditing WCAG entitled to overrule a participant's account of an inaccessible process simply because a checklist passed. A dependable program combines diverse lived experience, technical competence, and carefully bounded claims. User input can also conflict for legitimate reasons: a highly customized interaction may help one group and interrupt another group's established method. Recording such differences is more informative than forcing a single consensus.

2.3 Research participants, advisers, and co-designers

A research participant may perform tasks or describe experiences under a study protocol. An adviser may review the research plan, evaluate tradeoffs, or identify neglected issues across a sustained relationship. A co-designer may generate options, build representations, interpret data, and share substantive influence over decisions. The terms should reflect the work and authority actually granted. Using the word “co-design” for a one-time preference poll obscures who shaped the system and who retained control over the resulting decisions.

Teams should describe up front which decisions are open, which are constrained by law or technical dependencies, who will receive findings, and how participant recommendations will be accepted, adapted, or declined. Where disabled contributors perform professional design or advisory labor, compensation should reflect the contribution and arrangement, not be reduced to a token appreciation payment. Equally, not every accessibility study must use co-governance: tightly defined usability sessions remain valuable when their purpose and limits are clear.

3. Framing the Research Problem Around Essential Tasks

3.1 Move from generic access claims to service outcomes

A requirement begins with something the person has a legitimate reason to accomplish: register for a course, read a diagnostic result, make a payment, respond to an emergency notice, approve a workplace request, or receive a government decision. The researcher should identify the start state, information the person is assumed to possess, relevant choices, possible errors, and the final state that counts as completed. This avoids treating successful navigation of a sample screen as proof that a service works. It also reveals cross-channel barriers, such as a keyboard-accessible form whose final confirmation can only be received by an inaccessible telephone call.

Meaningful tasks differ by stakes. A streaming-platform playlist failure produces inconvenience, while inability to renew a medication or submit a time-sensitive benefits application may have more serious consequences. The research plan should state the consequences of delay or error, whether a workaround preserves privacy and autonomy, and which users may have limited opportunities to retry. This does not imply that researchers must obtain personal diagnoses or sensitive histories; those are generally unnecessary to document interface barriers.

3.2 Identify context before evaluating the interface

A context of use includes the person, goal, device, software, assistive technology, physical setting, language, connectivity, institutional expectations, and time pressures. These elements interact. Someone using magnification may experience a navigation problem at a large text setting that is invisible at the designer's default. Someone using voice input may work successfully in a quiet room but not in a shared office where speaking sensitive data is inappropriate. Someone using an augmentative communication device may need longer to explain choices than the interview script anticipates.

Questions should therefore distinguish stable preferences from momentary constraints and identify what the system may reasonably control. A product cannot eliminate every environmental condition, but it can avoid preventable dependencies on sight, sound, dexterity, short working memory, or rapid response. An understanding of context also prevents researchers from attributing failures to the user when the environment, task instructions, or prototype itself produced the obstruction.

3.3 Design diagnostic questions without presupposing the answer

“Did the screen reader work?” is too vague for a study of an application. Better prompts are “How would you locate the application deadline?” and “Show me how you would review and correct this answer before submitting.” Questions should leave room for an unexpected method. The researcher should follow up on observable behavior: What information did the participant expect? What did the interface communicate? Which action was attempted? Where did the process stop? What did recovery require?

Leading prompts such as “Wasn't the new navigation easier?” bias interpretation toward the design team's preferred outcome. For an unfamiliar service, do not hand participants hidden instructions that real users would never receive unless the research purpose is specifically to evaluate training. Conversely, do not create artificial obstacles merely to prove that a participant can work around them. Tasks should be realistic, safe, and proportionate to the study's ethical approvals.

4. Recruitment, Representation, and Sampling Limits

4.1 Recruit by relevant access practice, not diagnosis alone

An inclusive recruitment plan identifies the interaction characteristics that matter to the product: keyboard-only navigation, screen-reader use, screen magnification, captions, signing or text communication, speech recognition, switch control, limited fine-motor precision, cognitive supports, adjustable pacing, or combinations of these. People also differ in assistive-technology proficiency, work role, age, familiarity with a domain, and willingness to use particular channels. Recruiting one individual for every broad impairment category would still leave substantial variation unobserved.

For each task, write a sampling matrix identifying interaction requirements, experience level, task exposure, technology environment, and inclusion rationale. A workplace accounting system may need practiced keyboard and screen-reader users familiar with financial records. A public benefits application should also be studied with people unfamiliar with the portal, since expert shortcuts can conceal poor onboarding. Depending on scope, include people who use more than one access method, people with fluctuating capabilities, and people whose contexts expose low-bandwidth or mobile limitations. The matrix is a rationale for purposeful coverage, not a claim that the sample statistically represents a population.

4.2 Recruit without coercion or unnecessary disclosure

Recruitment messages should explain the organization, the research purpose, expected activities and duration, compensation, requested accommodations, how to contact the researcher, and that participation is voluntary. Screening should ask only what is needed to determine relevant user experience and arrange access—for instance, whether someone uses screen magnification and on what device—not demand a medical record as proof. Sensitive details about disability or health should not be collected by default and should be protected when a project has an unusually strong reason to collect them.

Potential participants may be contacted through disability-led organizations, professional associations, research panels, service networks, or open calls. Partners must not pressure members, employees, patients, or benefit recipients to participate. When the researcher controls something important to a potential participant, such as workplace evaluation or service eligibility, use safeguards that preserve a genuine ability to decline without adverse consequences. Do not purchase unconsented disability lists or treat a gatekeeper's permission as equivalent to an individual's consent.

4.3 Small samples produce findings, not universal guarantees

Small qualitative studies can reveal severe and actionable barriers. If one participant cannot submit a form because the only action is pointer-operated, that observation is sufficient to justify investigating and correcting a serious defect; it is not necessary to prove that a particular percentage of the population is affected. But a sample of three screen-reader users does not warrant the claim that “all blind users prefer this menu” or that every assistive-technology combination is supported. Report the sample, selection method, assistive technologies, environments, tasks, and exclusions.

Study size should follow the research question and the diversity of configurations, not a fixed magic number. Additional recruitment may be needed when user groups differ, the task is high-stakes, or findings remain contradictory. A separate quantitative study may estimate prevalence if it uses an appropriate sampling design and sufficient statistical rigor. A team should never use a small formative study to declare a system universally accessible, conformance-certified, or legally compliant.

5. Informed Consent, Compensation, Privacy, and Power

5.1 Consent is a process, not merely a signed form

Participants should understand the purpose of the study, what they will do, what is optional, expected time, known burdens, recording practices, who will see their responses, what will be retained, and how findings may be shared. Supply consent information in accessible formats before the session where feasible, allow time for questions, and provide supported ways to register a decision. If a participant needs an interpreter, communication partner, or accessible text to understand the study, provide that access without presuming the person lacks agency.

Consent to participate is distinct from consent to record audio, video, screen content, or identifiable quotations. A person may agree to an interview while declining video capture. A participant should be able to stop a session or skip a question without losing earned compensation merely for declining an optional activity, subject to the disclosed payment terms. Research teams should specify how withdrawal affects already collected data and should not promise removal in circumstances where it cannot be honored. Institutional review, when required, is separate from ordinary product-study planning and must be handled through the applicable process.

5.2 Fair compensation and accessible participation costs

Participants expend time, cognitive energy, transportation resources, communication effort, and sometimes the use of specialized tools. Fair compensation recognizes that contribution; travel reimbursement, interpreters, captioners, accessible venues, and assistance needed to attend are separate logistics rather than substitutes for payment. Co-designers and advisory contributors who provide sustained professional input should have agreements and compensation proportionate to their role. Clarify payment timing, taxes or benefit implications when relevant, and options that do not impose unexpected complexity on participants.

Research schedules should allow for setup of assistive technology, changing between tasks, pauses, and communication at a participant's preferred pace. A study that excludes someone because their communication method makes a rigid thirty-minute script difficult may bias the findings toward the easiest participants to accommodate. The solution is not to treat every session as identical in duration, but to preserve comparable research goals while transparently documenting accommodations that change the procedure.

5.3 Protect both participant information and incidental personal data

Assistive-technology demonstrations may reveal passwords, contacts, health notifications, browsing history, account balances, or voice data unrelated to the study. Use representative test accounts and synthetic task information whenever possible. Minimize recording, avoid capturing unrelated screens, limit access to raw material, establish a retention schedule, and separate direct identifiers from research notes when feasible. A transcript that removes a participant's name but retains a unique diagnosis, workplace, and city may still be identifying.

Research agreements should define who has access to recordings, whether third-party transcription or AI summarization services are permitted, and whether training or secondary use is excluded. Do not assume a participant who consents to a design study has also consented to public demonstrations or the reuse of assistive-technology footage. When findings are distributed, prefer de-identified task descriptions that preserve the barrier's technical meaning without unnecessarily revealing personal facts.

5.4 Avoid tokenism and extractive participation

Participation has little substance if the team repeatedly requests testimony but never reports what changed. Establish a feedback loop: show participants or advisory representatives how insights were interpreted, offer opportunities to correct misreadings, and explain material decisions that depart from their recommendations. Avoid putting a single disabled staff member in the position of approving every design choice or carrying the entire accessibility workload. Institutional responsibility remains with the organization that commissions, builds, and operates the product.

6. Accessible Research Instruments and Session Design

6.1 The study itself must be operable

Recruitment forms, calendars, consent materials, prototypes, interview platforms, surveys, and follow-up messages are part of the research experience. If the invitation form does not work by keyboard, the study can systematically exclude the people whose barriers it intends to investigate. Researchers should preflight materials with multiple access methods and offer equivalent scheduling or response routes. Plainly written questions, meaningful headings, labeled form fields, transcripts, captioning, sign-language interpretation where appropriate, and flexible pacing support valid participation.

Researchers should not ask participants to use inaccessible collaboration software merely to satisfy a uniform documentation process. For example, a visual sticky-note board may exclude participants who rely on screen readers or switch input. Offer a structured document, accessible virtual whiteboard, verbal or text-based contribution route, tactile material where appropriate, or an individual facilitated session. The research task is to capture ideas and decisions, not to require every contributor to manipulate the same representation.

6.2 Remote, in-person, and hybrid tradeoffs

Remote research can reduce travel demands and allow someone to use their own equipment, familiar settings, preferred input tools, and private assistive configuration. It can also introduce screen-sharing limitations, unstable audio, inaccessible conferencing controls, and difficulty observing behavior that is not visually apparent. In-person research permits richer observation of physical environments, kiosks, and multiple devices but requires accessible travel, venues, orientation, and safety planning. A hybrid protocol should not assume these settings are interchangeable; note how the format could affect findings.

Let participants describe whether a problem originates in the product, the prototype, the meeting platform, or the laboratory arrangement. For screen-reader research, observers often learn more from structured verbal walkthroughs and participant-directed explanations than from visual screen capture alone. For magnification research, the size and placement of the viewport matters. For speech-control research, permissions and microphone quality can change the result. Preserve enough context to make the finding reproducible without turning the participant's home or medical condition into the subject of the study.

6.3 Moderation that respects agency

Begin with an orientation that explains that the interface is under study, not the participant. Describe when the moderator will intervene, how to request a break, and how to stop. Prefer naturalistic task prompts and observe before asking interpretive questions. If someone is blocked, document the state and offer a safe way to proceed rather than prolonging frustration to collect dramatic footage. Never move a participant's mobility device, change screen-reader settings, or operate their personal computer without permission.

Thinking aloud may be useful but is not always comfortable or feasible, particularly when speech itself is an input method, when a participant must listen closely to a screen reader, or when maintaining focus takes substantial effort. Offer alternatives: retrospective explanation, periodic check-in, written comments, a researcher who narrates observed events for confirmation, or a participant-defined pause protocol. A method that changes the behavior being measured should be documented in the study notes.

7. Interviews, Contextual Inquiry, Observation, and Task Studies

7.1 Interviews establish meaning and expectations

Semistructured interviews are useful for understanding how users define a successful outcome, which tasks matter most, where they anticipate difficulty, and what existing workarounds cost. Ask participants to reconstruct a recent real-world journey when appropriate: What started the need? What channel did they choose? Where did they need help? What information did they have to retain? How did they verify completion? Interviews can reveal procedural barriers hidden outside the interface, such as a support requirement that cannot be completed privately.

Interviews also have limits. A participant may not remember the exact sequence of actions that produced a failure, or may describe a preferred design without experiencing its actual implementation. Treat stated preferences as important evidence of values, needs, and perceived constraints, but combine them with observation where the study aims to diagnose operational usability. A respondent's report that “the process always times out” is a lead to investigate, not proof of a specific timeout duration.

7.2 Contextual inquiry captures the setting of work

Contextual inquiry examines a task in the environment where it normally occurs. A university employee may reconcile student accommodation records across two systems; a blind commuter may consult bus arrival information while walking and listening to traffic; a person with tremor may use a banking app with a stylus and device stabilization. Observing the context can expose interruptions, device transitions, shared-terminal privacy, the role of colleagues, and external deadlines. These observations often become nonfunctional requirements that a screenshot-based review would miss.

Contextual observation should respect privacy and the rights of bystanders. Prefer simulations, redacted materials, or test data when real records would create disproportionate risk. If observation occurs at a service site, secure necessary organizational permissions without letting a manager's approval replace each participant's informed choice. Document relevant conditions such as network stability and room noise while avoiding speculative medical explanations for individual behavior.

7.3 Task observation reveals interaction dependencies

Assign a realistic objective rather than a control-level instruction. “Find the last payment date and download a copy of the receipt” permits observation of navigation, interpretation, and completion, whereas “press the Export button” skips essential steps. Record attempted actions, interface feedback, unexpected state changes, detours, recovery, assistance, and whether the final artifact is usable. For screen readers, note how headings, landmarks, controls, and announcements communicate the same information that visual observers see spatially.

Distinguish task success from success with unusual coaching. A participant who completes the task only after being told where a hidden menu is located has provided valuable diagnostic evidence, but that coached outcome should not be scored as independent completion. Researchers may explicitly test a supported training flow, but should report it separately. Similarly, the ability to improvise a workaround does not eliminate the burden of an inaccessible default.

7.4 Mixed methods and measurement discipline

Time on task, assistance events, completion rate, error recovery, and self-reported confidence can help describe usability if the measures are appropriate and conditions are documented. Faster is not always better: a participant who takes longer because speech output is sequential may complete the service reliably and independently. A simple average across incompatible interaction modes can hide barriers. Qualitative notes explain mechanisms, while quantitative measures describe outcomes within the limits of the sample and procedure.

Where testing compares two designs, keep task wording, prerequisite information, and environmental conditions sufficiently consistent to support interpretation. Record whether participants have prior exposure to one design, how learning effects were handled, and when moderators intervened. Do not manufacture precision by attaching percentages to very small purposive samples without explaining the denominator and sampling limits.

8. Participatory Workshops and Shared Design Decisions

8.1 Co-design is structured influence, not decoration

In participatory design, affected people collaborate in defining problems and developing solutions rather than solely responding to options made by professionals. A practical workshop may compare several ways of presenting transaction histories, identify what information must be announced by a screen reader, and propose a strategy for preserving state when changing from keyboard to speech input. The team should state which decisions participants may make, recommend, or veto, and which boundaries are externally fixed. Genuine participation is compatible with technical constraints, but it requires a clear account of those constraints.

Equal opportunity to contribute does not mean identical methods. Participants may sketch, dictate, label physical cards, annotate a shared document, use structured lists, or respond asynchronously. A researcher should be cautious when converting someone's contribution into a visual artifact: check that the translation preserves the participant's meaning, rather than allowing the designer's assumptions to become the official record. Materials may need verbal descriptions, keyboard-operable editing, large print, accessible diagrams, or extended review time.

8.2 Preserve disagreement and identify the underlying need

Participants may disagree about density, audio feedback, level of guidance, navigation shortcuts, default timeouts, or information disclosure. The analytic task is to identify which preferences are optional and which requirements protect essential access. One group may prefer detailed inline instructions, while another needs an uncluttered workflow; the solution could be instructions that are consistently available but selectively expandable, with robust semantic labeling under both conditions. In other cases, separate interface modes or alternative workflows may be necessary.

Do not resolve a conflict by voting across a convenience sample as though majority preference determines whether a smaller group receives access. Requirements that prevent exclusion and risks that affect privacy, dignity, or safety deserve independent evaluation. A design decision record should identify proposed alternatives, the access need each serves, implementation cost, unintended effects, consultation history, and the evidence needed to validate the final choice.

8.3 Close the feedback loop

After a workshop, share an accessible summary: what the team learned, which options were developed, which assumptions remain uncertain, and what decisions follow. Invite corrections from contributors and explain how their ideas will be credited or anonymized under the study agreement. When a proposed change is not adopted, provide a reason tied to evidence or constraints rather than quietly omitting it. This accountability encourages durable partnership and reduces the risk that disabled contributors are repeatedly asked to narrate barriers without seeing any response.

9. From Research Observations to Accessibility Requirements

9.1 Separate observations, interpretation, and obligation

A strong synthesis preserves three different statements. An observation records what happened: “At 200% text enlargement, the applicant scrolled to the end of the form but did not find the confirmation control.” An interpretation proposes why: “The fixed footer obscured the primary action when content reflowed.” A requirement specifies the behavior that must be achieved: “The applicant must be able to locate and activate the final action at supported text enlargement and viewport settings without lost information or functionality.” Blending these statements encourages premature fixes and makes later evidence difficult to examine.

Observations should include task context, date or build version, input method, environmental factors, the participant's account, assistance supplied, and any uncertainty. Interpretations should be labeled as hypotheses until corroborated by review, additional observation, or debugging. A requirement can be justified before the root cause is fully known if the essential outcome is clearly blocked, but the eventual engineering solution should be tested against its full effect rather than only the initially observed symptom.

9.2 Distinguish user needs, system behavior, and implementation choices

A user need describes a condition for meaningful participation: “I need to know whether the submission succeeded without relying on color or sound alone.” A functional requirement states what the system must do: “When submission succeeds, the interface provides persistent text confirmation and a programmatically exposed status.” An implementation decision chooses a mechanism, such as an appropriately announced status message and visible confirmation panel. Treating implementation decisions as though they were original needs can prevent the team from considering an equally effective alternative.

Some obligations may come from standards, contracts, product policies, or organizational duties rather than directly from a small study. Mark the origin of each requirement. Research-derived requirements may identify barriers that lie beyond the minimum of a technical checklist; standards-derived requirements protect a broad range of users who may never appear in a particular study. Neither source should be silently reduced to the other.

9.3 Write for a verifiable outcome

Use clear actors, contexts, triggers, and observable results. “The page shall be easy to use” cannot support acceptance. “A user operating the account settings by keyboard can reach every actionable control, identify focus, save changes, and receive confirmation without requiring a pointer” is testable. “Provide an accessible alternative” is incomplete until the team specifies the alternative's actual functions, timeliness, privacy, cost, and entitlement. A telephone-only alternative may not be equivalent for someone who cannot use voice telephony or who requires a private transaction.

Requirements should also cover failure paths: invalid data, lost connectivity, session expiration, multiple errors, cancelled actions, authentication challenges, and state restoration. A service that works only along the happy path may remain effectively inaccessible. Researchers should ask which failure states users already encounter, but designers should anticipate plausible failures even when no observed participant happens to trigger them.

10. Structuring a Requirements Register and Traceability Record

10.1 A durable requirement has more than a title

A practical requirements register gives each item an identifier, service or component scope, essential user task, applicable contexts, functional statement, source of obligation, observed barrier, priority rationale, owner, proposed verification method, acceptance criteria, known constraints, and resolution status. An identifier such as A11Y-REQ-014 should remain stable when text is revised; version history should record material changes. The source may be an interview note, a participant workshop decision, an existing standard, or a documented incident, with the original evidence stored under appropriate privacy controls.

Attribution needs precision. “User requested a larger button” might obscure a problem with target size, activation accuracy, or touch orientation. Before prescribing larger controls everywhere, document what the participant actually attempted, the action's importance, and whether the proposed change conflicts with other interface constraints. Retain a link between the observation and the chosen design rationale so a future reviewer can reconstruct why the requirement exists.

10.2 Trace the requirement to design, test, and release

Requirements should not end as a spreadsheet separate from development. Link each to affected service journeys, interface patterns, design-system components, content rules, user stories, code changes, test cases, and release decisions. A reusable dialog component may satisfy part of the requirement for focus handling across many products, but a checkout journey still needs evidence that the dialog is invoked and dismissed correctly in context. Component-level correctness and end-to-end task completion are different levels of evidence.

When a change introduces a new barrier, traceability supports impact analysis. For example, replacing a native select control with a custom searchable list may affect keyboard behavior, screen-reader interaction, mobile touch, cognitive demands, and error recovery. A requirements register makes it harder for a team to optimize visual appearance while unknowingly dropping an established access route.

11. Acceptance Criteria, Test Oracles, and Meaningful Equivalence

11.1 Define what would count as success

An acceptance criterion describes a condition a delivered feature must satisfy for an identified requirement. For accessibility, this often includes more than the presence of a visible control. A submission flow is not successful if a person can complete fields but cannot inspect errors, confirm answers, submit without a pointer, or receive an understandable result. Criteria should cover initiation, interaction, recovery, and completion, and should distinguish tests of a component from tests of the whole user journey.

Where appropriate, use scenario-based structure: Given a user employs a supported keyboard access path, when a modal confirmation opens, then focus enters an appropriate location, all available actions are operable, and focus returns predictably when the modal closes. This language is useful because it identifies preconditions, triggers, and observations. It is not a substitute for detailed accessibility specifications where specific technical behavior must be verified.

11.2 Equivalent outcomes matter more than identical gestures

An interaction may be provided through pointer, keyboard, switch, speech, or touch while preserving the same essential service. The exact gesture need not be identical, but the user's agency and access to information should remain comparable. Consider a visual drag-and-drop budget planner. A keyboard-operable series of controls could allow the same reordering and save the same result; a text-only alternative that merely displays the list but does not permit editing would fail the essential outcome.

Equivalence must also consider cost, time, privacy, accuracy, and autonomy. “Ask an employee to read the page aloud” is not automatically an adequate substitute for independent access to financial or medical information. A separate accessible route should not impose extra fees, unnecessary delays, or reduced functionality because of how the user interacts. Requirements must identify which dimensions of equivalence are essential for the particular service.

11.3 Design tests for normal and adverse conditions

Include a standard path, incorrect input, interruption, return visits, changes in orientation or input mode, and system feedback. Test plans should describe relevant software versions and configurations without assuming that one screen reader or one browser can represent all environments. At the requirements stage, the goal is to specify the evidence needed; detailed execution protocols and compatibility judgments belong to the Accessibility Testing specialist article.

A passing user task in one session is evidence for the studied configuration, not proof of conformance. Similarly, a failed task may reflect an interaction between a prototype and a research platform rather than the final product alone. The test report should distinguish observed experience, reproducible system behavior, technical diagnosis, and any limits on generalization.

12. Using Accessibility Standards Without Replacing User Research

12.1 Standards and lived experience serve complementary purposes

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) establish technical success criteria for web content. They address broad classes of barriers and create a common basis for evaluation. Research with people with disabilities identifies how specific tasks, combinations of tools, institutional processes, and unfamiliar interfaces behave in practice. A standards review may reveal missing accessible names across hundreds of controls; a participant study may reveal that a theoretically accessible workflow requires remembering information spread across five pages. Both matter, and neither makes the other unnecessary.

W3C guidance explicitly recommends combining standards-based conformance work with user involvement. Study participants cannot collectively represent every disability, technology, and situation, and a checklist cannot anticipate all real-world difficulties or explain every usability problem. In a mature project, standards-related requirements are incorporated into the design contract before testing, while participant research helps prioritize, contextualize, and validate task outcomes.

12.2 Do not treat conformance as a popularity vote

A researcher should not drop a required technical behavior merely because none of the recruited participants needed it during a short study. A low-incidence access need may be fundamental to a group excluded from the sample. Conversely, passing technical checks does not justify dismissing reports of failed tasks. Distinguish standards-based obligations, additional product quality commitments, and evidence of user impact, and document all three without exaggerating what each proves.

WCAG 2.2 is a W3C Recommendation and a useful technical reference for web accessibility work. Other platforms, procurement conditions, jurisdictions, or product categories may involve different criteria and legal obligations. The specific standard edition and required conformance target should be stated for a project, not inferred from a researcher mentioning “accessibility.” Detailed level mapping and formal conformance claims belong to the companion Accessibility Standards and Conformance article.

12.3 Research should expose gaps in existing patterns

When observing an unexpected barrier, examine whether it results from a violation of an established criterion, an implementation problem outside the criterion's narrow scope, a usability problem that disproportionately affects a particular group, or a broader service-design obstacle. A person may be able to use every control in a banking interface yet be unable to obtain help because the sole fraud-recovery channel requires intelligible speech. That finding is a service requirement even when the individual web controls pass tests.

13. Prioritizing Barriers, Risk, and Remediation

13.1 Prioritize the consequence of exclusion

Research findings compete with functionality requests, deadlines, and engineering constraints. A principled prioritization method considers the essential task affected, seriousness of the outcome, whether the failure is blocking or degradative, availability and fairness of alternatives, frequency of encounter, affected contexts, and legal or contractual duties. Product teams should not treat the number of participants who happen to encounter a barrier in a purposive study as its sole importance measure. A barrier blocking an essential emergency message may deserve urgent correction even if observed once.

Severity labels should be supported by an explanation. “Critical” could mean that a required service cannot be completed without outside assistance, that essential information is omitted, or that an error creates safety or financial exposure. “Moderate” might describe a task completed independently only through burdensome repeated navigation. The labels can support triage, but they must not disguise the actual functional impact or turn accessibility into a purely numerical score.

13.2 Make tradeoffs explicit

Sometimes improvements interact. An expanded explanation can help some people understand a form while adding cognitive burden for others. A larger touch target can improve activation accuracy but consume limited viewport space. A session timeout protects sensitive data but may exclude users who need additional time. Research can identify the underlying access needs, while engineering and product teams must design a solution that respects multiple constraints—for instance, secure extension warnings and draft preservation rather than simply removing a timeout.

A decision record should distinguish tradeoffs that are unavoidable from barriers caused by habit, budget assumptions, or missing design work. Cost alone does not establish that an inaccessible path is acceptable. Where a requirement cannot be fulfilled by a scheduled release, assign a responsible owner, explain the interim mitigation, maintain an accessible support route where feasible, and establish how and when remediation will be revisited. Whether a mitigation satisfies a legal duty is a separate, context-dependent determination.

13.3 Risks of alternative channels

Alternatives can protect access, but they may also create a secondary service of lower quality. Requiring users to telephone for a routine action available online to others can introduce delay, dependency, privacy loss, or an incompatible communication method. When an alternative is proposed, test it with the same rigor as the primary path: can the user complete the same essential action, receive the same result, retain records, correct errors, and do so without unjustified burden?

14. Evidence Quality, Bias, and Research Limitations

14.1 Differentiate a discovery study from a prevalence study

A formative study is designed to find problems, understand behaviors, and improve a design. It can be rigorous without producing population estimates. A representative survey or appropriately designed quantitative evaluation addresses a different question, such as how commonly a behavior occurs in a defined audience. The study report should name its purpose and avoid sliding from “observed in this sample” to “true of all people with this disability.” The absence of a reported barrier is not proof that the system is accessible for people who were never recruited.

14.2 Common sources of distortion

Recruitment through one advocacy network may overrepresent particular skill levels or technology ecosystems. A lab with fast internet may hide problems in rural connectivity. Participants who are expert technology users may use shortcuts that novices cannot discover. Moderators may cue the desired answer, or treat silence as satisfaction when a participant is waiting for speech output. A prototype may behave differently from the shipped system. These threats do not invalidate research, but they should shape design and claims.

Research logs should record deviations from the protocol, unanticipated moderator assistance, changes in accessibility settings, service interruptions, and participant comments that contradict the team's initial interpretation. When several contributors independently experience a related barrier, assess whether the same mechanism is involved rather than treating similar symptoms as proof of one cause. Negative cases—participants who succeed where others fail—can reveal important differences in strategy, context, or configuration.

14.3 Triangulate without silencing the participant

Triangulation means examining a problem with more than one source or method: participant accounts, observation, product logs where appropriate, expert inspection, code review, and repeat tasks. It should improve explanation rather than become a means of dismissing lived experience. An expert may not reproduce a barrier because their machine uses different settings; the correct next step is to investigate the interaction, not conclude that the original report was mistaken.

Reports should identify the degree of confidence in each interpretation. A directly observed blocking condition may be strongly evidenced while its root cause remains uncertain. A participant's predicted preference for an unbuilt concept is a hypothesis requiring prototype evaluation. A standards mapping may be provisional until a qualified reviewer confirms applicability. Accurate uncertainty makes the work useful to later engineers and auditors.

16. Applications Across Public Services, Education, Health, and Employment

16.1 Public and civic services

Public-service research must account for eligibility requirements, deadlines, identity verification, document upload, notice delivery, appeals, and assisted channels. Participants may depend on these services and may reasonably fear that criticism could affect benefits or outcomes. Researchers should make the separation between voluntary study participation and service decisions explicit. Prototype scenarios should avoid using real beneficiary accounts or inducing accidental submissions. The acceptance plan should include the complete path to a receipt, decision, or appeal record.

16.2 Education and training

Educational platforms combine content access with assessment, discussion, progress records, and time-limited deadlines. A learner may be able to read a lecture yet be unable to complete a timed quiz or interpret an inaccessible diagram. Research should involve learners and instructors, and possibly accessibility support staff, without allowing administrative assumptions to substitute for the learner's experience. Requirements should preserve the learning objective while allowing equivalent ways of interacting with materials and demonstrating knowledge where appropriate to the educational purpose.

16.3 Health and clinical communication

Health applications involve sensitive information and potentially safety-relevant decisions. Accessibility requirements may include private communication, understandable instructions, identification of critical changes, reliable appointment management, and access to clinical documents. Research should avoid gathering actual health records when simulated cases will suffice. Clinical professionals must validate medical content and safety implications; participants with disabilities should still influence how information and actions are communicated. A technically operable interface is not acceptable if it misrepresents an urgent clinical message.

16.4 Employment and internal systems

Internal tools often have high-frequency workflows, specialized terminology, and interdependent systems. Research should examine real job roles and assistive practices while protecting employee privacy and avoiding manager pressure. A visually simple replacement may be slower than a keyboard-efficient legacy system for a skilled worker. Acceptance criteria should address productivity, independent access, training needs, and equal ability to perform authorized work, rather than assuming that the design team knows what employee efficiency means.

16.5 Consumer platforms and customer support

Commerce and entertainment services include discovery, purchase, cancellation, returns, account recovery, and customer service. A flow should not become less accessible at the point of payment or dispute. Researchers should treat recovery from mistakes and access to human assistance as part of the product journey. A consumer who can select an item but cannot cancel a subscription through an accessible channel has not received equivalent control over the transaction.

17. Worked Research and Requirements Scenarios

These eight constructed cases illustrate methodological choices and the journey from observation to acceptance. They are educational scenarios, not reports of verified defects in MGU products or other named organizations. In each case, the point is to locate the accountable task and preserve uncertainty rather than infer user behavior from a diagnostic label.

Case 1 — A public-benefits portal excludes keyboard applicants at the final step

Research brief: The service must let an eligible applicant complete identity questions, upload evidence, submit, and retain confirmation. Interviews indicate the application is widely regarded as “accessible” because the landing page supports keyboard navigation. The team recruits people who use keyboards, switch devices, and screen readers at varied levels of experience, with test documents and safe accounts. Researchers first inspect the prototype for obvious blockers, then observe each participant undertaking the full journey without coaching.

Observed barrier: In a simulated session, a custom document-upload widget offers no usable non-drag route, and an error summary appears visually but is not announced when submission fails. Participants respond differently: an advanced user discovers a hidden fallback, while a newer user abandons the task. Derived requirements: Users can upload documents without dragging; errors are programmatically conveyed, described in text, and linked to the relevant fields; successful submission produces a durable confirmation. Acceptance: Demonstrate full completion, recovery from a deliberately invalid attachment, and independently discoverable confirmation with representative input methods. The advanced user's workaround is documented but not treated as fulfillment of the main requirement.

Case 2 — A university timetable breaks under text enlargement

Research brief: Students need to locate the correct room, start time, and change notices from phone or desktop. The research plan includes magnification users, people who increase text in the browser, and students with unfamiliar campus terminology. Researchers test representative views at enlarged settings and ask participants to complete an authentic sequence: identify a class, compare two schedule changes, and update a calendar.

Observed barrier: At some combinations of viewport size and text enlargement, the timetable requires horizontal movement that makes row headings difficult to retain; a sticky overlay hides the update control. The team does not conclude that “magnification is incompatible with timetables.” It records the specific layout, controls, and task stages involved. Derived requirements: Students retain access to the association among class, time, room, and status at supported enlargement settings; any necessary scrolling preserves understandable context; update controls remain operable. Acceptance: Verify the task using the intended presentation variants, with a structured list alternative if the grid cannot responsibly support all contexts. Evaluate whether a proposed compact view omits information required for the student's decision.

Case 3 — A banking customer uses speech input but cannot confirm payment

Research brief: Customers must be able to create a payment, review its recipient and amount, authorize it, and obtain a receipt. Researchers include people who use voice control, as well as keyboard users and customers who prefer private nonspoken input. Participants use test accounts and artificial transaction data. The team asks what they would do next rather than directing them to known controls.

Observed barrier: The visible control says “Confirm payment,” but the programmatic name is an internal term not spoken by the customer. A voice-control command based on the visible wording does not activate the action. The participant's preference for speech is not itself the problem. Derived requirement: A control's accessible name supports activation using its visible label, and a non-voice route is also available for privacy-sensitive settings. Acceptance: Demonstrate authorization with speech control in a representative configuration, with keyboard operation and accurate receipt details tested separately. Include the wrong-recipient correction path because authorization is a consequential decision.

Case 4 — A hospital check-in kiosk assumes rapid touch gestures

Research brief: Patients must confirm arrival, protect health information, and request assistance without unnecessary disclosure. The team recruits people using touch with limited precision, people who rely on physical switches, and users who need additional response time. Research occurs with a realistic kiosk mockup and synthetic personal information, avoiding actual registration transactions. Staff members contribute knowledge of clinic flow but do not speak for patients.

Observed barrier: A slide gesture is the only way to advance, and a fixed short timeout discards completed fields without notice. Some participants can continue only by asking another person to operate the kiosk, creating a privacy burden. Derived requirements: The essential gesture has a non-gesture alternative; users are warned about inactivity and can preserve work within the service's security policy; assistance can be requested discreetly. Acceptance: Complete the same check-in task without sliding, verify timeout recovery, and assess the proposed staff-support route for equal treatment and confidentiality. Patient research does not replace clinical safety or organizational review.

Case 5 — An employee cannot complete monthly reporting with a redesigned dashboard

Research brief: A new dashboard is marketed as visually cleaner and more intuitive, but a workforce member reports that it takes much longer to complete required tasks. The team recruits employees who perform the actual workflow, including practiced screen-reader and keyboard users; participation is voluntary and separated from performance appraisal. The study compares the existing system, an early redesign, and the full submission journey using realistic anonymized records.

Observed barrier: The redesigned page exposes dozens of repeated buttons with indistinguishable names, removes efficient table navigation, and requires frequent focus returns to the top. A participant can eventually complete the report, but only after unusually burdensome repeated actions. Derived requirements: Repeated controls are identifiable in context, data relationships remain available nonvisually, and the reporting sequence supports efficient predictable navigation. Acceptance: Verify task completion and investigate navigation burden with representative expert users, while confirming technical semantics. Comparing only mouse completion times would mischaracterize the impact.

Case 6 — A public consultation workshop excludes some of its intended co-designers

Research brief: A municipality seeks input about a new transit alert service. The facilitators plan a single two-hour workshop with rapid brainstorming on digital sticky notes. Invitees include blind commuters, people with cognitive access needs, people using augmentative communication, and people who cannot attend a fixed evening session. The research team recognizes that its workshop design would prevent some invitees from participating at the same level.

Redesigned method: Participants receive accessible background materials in advance, can choose between a moderated group, individual interview, and asynchronous structured responses, and can propose alerts in text, audio, or facilitated formats. Compensation is established for contributors and accessibility support is funded separately. Evidence and requirement: The team traces themes to individual functional needs without publishing identifiers. Rather than voting on a single alert format, it specifies that time-sensitive service changes must be available through equivalent visual and nonvisual means. Acceptance: The proposed alert service is later evaluated with actual tasks and environments, including noise and limited connectivity. The inclusive workshop enables better decisions but is not itself proof of finished-product accessibility.

Case 7 — A mobile account-recovery flow depends on inaccessible memory tasks

Research brief: Users who change devices must restore account access without compromising security. Researchers invite people who use cognitive supports, screen readers, magnification, and nonstandard input methods, as well as people with varying familiarity with multifactor authentication. Studies use nonproduction accounts, and participants are not asked to disclose credentials.

Observed barrier: The recovery flow requires repeated transcription of short-lived codes while navigating between applications and displays an ambiguous error when time runs out. Some participants repeatedly fail even though they know their account information. Derived requirements: The service offers supported secure authentication paths that do not impose avoidable memory or transcription burdens, communicates time-sensitive steps clearly, preserves safe progress where possible, and provides a practical recovery alternative. Acceptance: Verify task completion under realistic device switching and error conditions, then subject the proposed change to security review. An accessibility improvement must not be assumed safe merely because it reduces steps, nor should security be used as an unexamined explanation for an arbitrary barrier.

Case 8 — A scientific data chart is usable visually but inaccessible for analysis

Research brief: Research staff need to identify a peak, compare two series, extract values, and cite the finding in a report. The study includes screen-reader users, people who depend on high contrast or magnification, and researchers familiar with the underlying data. Participants are asked to answer the same substantive analytical questions using realistic but nonsensitive data.

Observed barrier: An automatically generated description says that “values generally increased” but does not preserve peak location, series labels, units, or exceptions. A separate CSV contains the numbers but no explanation of column relationships. Derived requirements: Essential chart semantics, data relationships, and decision-relevant features are available without relying on the visual plot alone. Acceptance: Participants can answer the specified analytic questions using accessible text, structured data, or another suitable presentation, and the information is checked for accuracy by domain specialists. Automated alternative text is treated as a draft to be verified, not as an accessibility guarantee.

18.1 Confidence and claims ladder

Level A — observed incident: “One participant could not operate this control in this configuration.” Level B — corroborated design problem: “Reproduction and technical inspection identify an operability barrier.” Level C — validated task outcome: “Representative participants completed these tasks under the documented conditions after the change.” Level D — scoped technical conformance statement: “A defined set of pages or journeys was evaluated against specified criteria, with results recorded.” These are different claims, not a single linear grading scale; a product can have strong evidence for one and weak evidence for another.

18.2 A complete evidence packet

For an important release, preserve a research brief, participant coverage rationale, consent and data-handling plan, sanitized observation log, alternative design analysis, requirements register, applicable standards mapping, acceptance criteria, review results, limitations, and owner decisions. The packet should be readable by someone who was not present at the research sessions and should not expose sensitive details beyond what that person's role requires. This enables accountability without confusing a polished report with a universally valid conclusion.

19. Practical Decision Framework for Accessibility Research

Use this diagnostic sequence whenever a product team proposes a new feature, resolves an accessibility complaint, or claims an inclusive redesign:

  1. Define the essential outcome. Specify the service task and what completion means for the person, not simply which screen is under review.
  2. List the access contexts. Identify likely input, output, assistive-technology, device, environmental, and skill variations; record omitted groups.
  3. Ask who shaped the problem. Identify whether affected users defined the question or were consulted only after decisions had been made.
  4. Choose methods by decision. Use interviews for experience and expectations, contextual inquiry for settings, observation for actual performance, and co-design for alternatives.
  5. Make participation accessible and voluntary. Review recruitment, materials, scheduling, privacy, compensation, and avenues to decline.
  6. Capture the barrier precisely. Record task, attempted action, feedback, assistance, configuration, and consequences without diagnosing the participant.
  7. Separate evidence from explanation. Identify untested assumptions and decide whether replication, inspection, or additional research is needed.
  8. Write a functional requirement. State what the system must permit or communicate; distinguish the requirement from a proposed visual or technical solution.
  9. Define acceptance and standards obligations. Specify success conditions, supported configurations, error states, task-level evidence, and any distinct technical rules.
  10. Return to users and owners. Test the resulting change, explain unresolved limitations, close the consultation loop, and assign responsibility for future regressions.

If a team cannot explain which participant evidence motivated a change, how the change will be accepted, and what limitations remain, the research has not yet been operationalized into a defensible product decision. If a team cannot identify an accountable owner, even good research may have no route into implementation.

20. Glossary

Accessibility requirement
A stated condition necessary to remove or prevent barriers to meaningful use by people with disabilities.
Acceptance criterion
An observable condition used to decide whether a delivered feature or service satisfies a requirement.
Access modality
A means by which a person perceives information or operates a system, such as visual reading, screen-reader output, keyboard input, speech input, or switch control.
Assistive technology
Technology that supports an individual's interaction, such as a screen reader, magnifier, speech-recognition system, or switch interface.
Co-design
A participatory process in which affected people substantially contribute to the formulation of problems and development of solutions.
Conformance evaluation
A structured determination of whether a defined target satisfies a specified technical standard or requirement set.
Context of use
The combination of users, goals, tasks, technology, physical and social environments, and organizational circumstances of an interaction.
Contextual inquiry
Research that examines tasks and practices in the settings in which people normally perform them.
Critical incident
An event that exposes significant success, failure, error, recovery, or consequence within a task journey.
Evidence traceability
The ability to follow a design decision from research source through requirement, implementation, and verification.
Functional need
An access-related capability or condition required to accomplish a task without assuming a specific medical diagnosis.
Formative study
Research intended to discover problems and improve an evolving design rather than estimate population prevalence.
Informed consent
Voluntary agreement based on accessible understanding of research activities, risks, data use, and withdrawal conditions.
Nonfunctional requirement
A condition governing system quality or behavior, such as availability, compatibility, privacy, performance, or accessibility in context.
Participant coverage matrix
A record of which access modes, environments, experience levels, and tasks a study intends to include or exclude.
Participatory research
Inquiry that involves affected people in substantive parts of evidence generation and interpretation, potentially including shared design authority.
Research protocol
The documented plan for recruitment, consent, activities, observation, data handling, and reporting.
Task success
Completion of an essential outcome under specified conditions, distinguishing independent performance from coached or workaround-based performance.
Triangulation
Use of more than one evidence source or method to investigate a research finding or explanation.
Usable accessibility
An approach combining technical accessibility with actual ease and effectiveness of use by people with disabilities.

21. Study Questions and Applied Exercises

Review questions

  1. Why can a person-centered design study and a technical standards review produce different but compatible findings?
  2. How does recruiting by functional interaction need differ from recruiting solely by diagnosis? What important variation might remain?
  3. What makes a consent process accessible and voluntary, and why is recording permission a separate question?
  4. How would you distinguish an observed barrier from a hypothesized cause and from a functional requirement?
  5. Why should assistive-technology expertise, professional accessibility expertise, and domain expertise be considered separately?
  6. When is a proposed alternative route not meaningfully equivalent to the original service?
  7. Why can an individual blocking failure justify action even when a study is too small for population claims?
  8. What conditions must be documented before comparing task times or error rates across participants?
  9. What does genuine co-design require beyond collecting feedback from disabled participants?
  10. How should an organization preserve accountability after a research team completes its report?

Applied exercise A — Turn an observation into a testable requirement

A participant using screen magnification cannot identify the current step in a multi-page application after switching to a narrow viewport. Write four distinct statements: the observation, at least two possible causes, the underlying user need, and a requirement with observable acceptance criteria. Specify what additional evidence would distinguish the proposed causes. Do not assume the participant's visual condition determines the cause.

Applied exercise B — Design an ethical recruitment plan

Design a study for an online course registration service. Produce a functional-needs coverage matrix, recruitment message, three screening questions that avoid unnecessary medical disclosure, compensation and accessibility-support budget categories, and a plan for handling recordings. Explain how students can decline without affecting academic services. State which populations will not be represented and why.

Applied exercise C — Build a small traceability register

Using the banking, hospital, and public-benefits cases, create three requirement records with identifiers, source notes, user task, expected system behavior, alternative paths, acceptance evidence, and a responsible owner. Identify one conformance criterion or standard-related area to review separately for each. Explain why a passing task session would not by itself establish formal conformance.

Applied exercise D — Facilitate accessible disagreement

Two advisory participants want opposing instruction densities in a complex form. Prepare an accessible workshop exercise that clarifies the common need, explores alternatives, preserves each contribution, and produces a decision record. Define how you would verify the resulting design rather than relying on a workshop vote.

Applied exercise E — Critique a claim of universality

A product team writes, “Five disabled users tested our checkout; therefore our checkout is fully accessible.” Rewrite the statement as a defensible description of evidence. Identify missing information about selection, tasks, browsers, assistive technologies, error paths, and standards evaluation. Propose a next round of evidence collection suitable to the product's risk.
