1. Definition, Scope, and Governing Question

Accessible documents and electronic publications are files whose information, structure, controls, and reading sequences remain usable by people with varied sensory, motor, cognitive, and technological needs. Their accessibility cannot be established from appearance alone. A page that looks orderly on a monitor may contain no recognizable headings, an illogical reading sequence, unlabeled figures, and tables that cannot be navigated by row or column. A visually simple file may nevertheless present serious barriers if it consists of scanned images rather than extractable text. Conversely, a carefully authored file can support screen readers, braille output, keyboard navigation, magnification, read-aloud tools, personalized typography, translation, and reliable conversion between formats.

The governing question is: can a reader obtain and use the same substantive information and complete the intended document task independently, in the published format and with their chosen technology? The answer requires examining the source document, conversion process, file structure, reader software, and task outcome. This Level 5 article belongs to the MGU Encyclopedia's Human Interaction Systems → Accessibility → Platforms, Documents, and Emerging Interfaces group. It concentrates on word-processing documents, presentations, spreadsheets, accessible PDF, EPUB, electronic textbooks, and structured archival publications. Web-page semantics are covered elsewhere; the distinctive problem here is how meaning survives a transition from authoring software into portable, distributable documents.

A document can be accessible to some readers yet fail others. A PDF with excellent tagging but tiny fixed visual text may be difficult for a low-vision reader on a phone; an EPUB that reflows beautifully may lack useful descriptions for figures. A credible review therefore considers several modes of consumption, not just a tool's pass/fail display.

2. The Document Accessibility Contract

A document establishes an interaction contract even when it has no clickable controls. Readers must be able to locate a section, distinguish headings from body text, follow a table, identify links, understand graphics, choose a reading order, search content, and determine when an item is complete. Forms, interactive publications, and multimedia documents add actions, validation, playback, and focus movement. These tasks depend on both machine-readable structure and editorial clarity.

Dimension Accessible behavior Common failure Evidence
Content Real, selectable text with meaningful alternatives Whole page is an image Extract and read a representative paragraph
Structure Headings, lists, tables and landmarks preserve relationships Font size alone denotes a heading Inspect document structure and navigation views
Sequence Reading order follows intended meaning Columns interleave or captions precede wrong figures Read linearly using assistive software
Navigation Working table of contents, bookmarks and meaningful links Printed page numbers with no electronic navigation Move among sections without searching visually
Presentation Text can be enlarged and colors remain distinguishable Fixed tiny type or low-contrast labels Test magnification and reflow where supported
Interaction Controls have labels, states and usable activation Fillable fields are unidentified Complete a representative form with keyboard and screen reader
Portability Conversion retains structure and language Export strips tags or headings Compare source with final distributed copy

The contract should be written into acceptance criteria before authoring begins. For example, “Every chapter heading appears in the navigation pane; every complex figure has a text explanation; all financial tables expose header relationships; readers can locate a citation and return to the passage.” These are stronger than “run an accessibility checker” because they describe observable outcomes.

3. Readers, Technologies, and Reading Environments

People may read documents with NVDA, JAWS, VoiceOver, TalkBack, Narrator, braille displays, screen magnifiers, text-to-speech software, switch input, adaptive keyboards, speech commands, simplified readers, or combinations of these. A reader may use a laptop and a PDF viewer one day and a mobile reading application the next. Performance varies by document format, application, browser, operating system, assistive technology, and version. The same accessible file can behave differently in two reading systems.

Design for modes of use rather than a supposed average reader. Someone who enlarges text may need text to reflow into one column. Someone using braille needs a reliable text stream and explicit mathematical notation. Someone navigating by headings needs semantic titles in the correct order. Someone who becomes fatigued may need a stable navigation position and a way to resume a long reading task. A reader with limited network access may download the file and use it offline. These needs are not special exceptions; they are ordinary operational requirements for a durable publication.

A publishing team should record the intended operating environments: downloadable DOCX, PDF viewed in a browser, EPUB read on a dedicated device, and LMS-integrated documents are different targets. Include minimum supported reading systems and any known limitations in a documented test matrix. Do not infer universal support from a successful demonstration in one desktop application.

4. Document Formats and Their Distinct Responsibilities

Portable distribution is not a single technology. DOCX and ODT are editable document containers organized around structural styles and objects; PDF is a fixed-layout representation with an optional semantic tagging layer; EPUB is a packaged electronic publication generally built from XHTML, CSS, navigation documents, and metadata. Presentation formats arrange objects across slides. Spreadsheets encode grids, formulas, labels, sheets, and relationships. Each can be accessible or inaccessible depending on construction and the reader's software.

Format Strength Recurring accessibility challenge Preferred source of repair
DOCX/ODT Editable, structured text, familiar authoring Fake headings, floating objects, complex tables Native styles, paragraphs and object properties
Tagged PDF Stable pagination and print fidelity Bad tags, order, metadata, forms Source regeneration or PDF structure repair
EPUB 3 Adaptable text, navigation, publishing metadata Broken navigation, inaccessible images, fixed layouts Source XHTML, package metadata and CSS
Slides Visual presentation and speaker context Object order, dense graphics, inaccessible speaker notes Original slide file and companion text
Spreadsheets Detailed data, formulas and calculations Unlabeled axes, merged cells, nonobvious ranges Workbook semantics and accompanying explanation
Scanned document Preserves historical visual artifact No reliable machine-readable content OCR plus human correction and structural tagging

When publication requires both a downloadable PDF and an EPUB, maintain one authoritative editorial source and format-specific exports. Do not assume that an exported PDF or EPUB automatically inherits all accessible properties from the source. A publishing process is successful only after the final artifact has been inspected.

5. Standards and Guidance: An Accurate Hierarchy

Different standards govern different questions. WCAG 2.2 provides web-content accessibility success criteria and evaluation conventions; the W3C WCAG2ICT Group Note explains their application to non-web documents and software but is informative rather than an independent mandate. PDF/UA is an ISO standards family addressing accessible PDF structure. EPUB Accessibility defines discoverability and content-conformance expectations for EPUB publications. Platform and procurement policies may incorporate some of these specifications, but their legal effect depends on jurisdiction, date, policy text, and the particular product or document.

Reference Scope 2026 status Interpretive caution
WCAG 2.2 Web-content success criteria W3C Recommendation Applicability to non-web deliverables may be defined by law, policy or WCAG2ICT guidance
WCAG2ICT Interpretation for non-web documents/software W3C Group Note Informative guidance, not a new standalone conformance certification
ISO 14289-1:2014, PDF/UA-1 Tagged PDF for PDF 1.7 Published ISO standard Distinct from PDF/UA-2 and not a synonym for all PDF accessibility
ISO 14289-2:2024, PDF/UA-2 Tagged PDF in PDF 2.0 Published ISO standard Different technical baseline and ecosystem support
EPUB Accessibility 1.1 EPUB content conformance/discoverability W3C Recommendation (2024) Conformance depends on all relevant criteria, not metadata alone
EPUB Accessibility 1.2 Updated EPUB framework Candidate Recommendation Draft (November 11, 2026) Work in progress, not yet a W3C Recommendation

An evaluator must name the exact version and level of a standard and the files or content to which a claim applies. “WCAG compliant PDF” or “accessible EPUB” alone is too vague to describe which requirements were checked. In procurement, require evidence of actual test coverage and remediation rather than treating a format label as proof of accessibility. The standards have complementary functions: one supports document-specific structure; another governs content requirements or evaluation; another explains how to report features to prospective readers.

6. WCAG Principles in Non-Web Documents

The four familiar WCAG principles—perceivable, operable, understandable, and robust—remain useful for files. Perceivable means text, images, color-dependent information, and media have suitable alternatives. Operable means links, controls, navigation, and any embedded interactions can be reached and used. Understandable means content and its instructions are intelligible and predictable. Robust means machine-readable semantics survive into relevant software and assistive technologies.

The WCAG2ICT note helps interpret concepts such as “web page,” “set of web pages,” and user interface components outside the browser. Its examples and interpretations must not be mistaken for legal requirements on their own. A DOCX heading can satisfy the underlying need for structure without containing an HTML <h2> tag; a PDF must express structural semantics using its own tag representation. The information relationship is portable, but the implementation mechanism is not. Where a particular public-sector rule cites WCAG or an accessibility standard, evaluate the actual normative reference rather than assuming the current W3C guidance automatically updates the rule.

For editorial teams, organize the document's requirements by reader task and then map each to the applicable format-specific technique. For example, “identify an image's function” maps to appropriate alternative text in Word, /Alt or equivalent association in tagged PDF when appropriate, and an XHTML alt or extended description in EPUB. The semantic requirement remains stable; the file-specific implementation changes.

7. Source-First Authoring and Durable Content Structure

Accessible publication is easier when the authoring source is already structured. Begin with a document template containing named heading styles, body text, list styles, callout conventions, caption styles, table designs, language metadata, and instructions for alternative text. The template should make correct authoring the default. Editors should not have to reconstruct a document's logical organization after thousands of pages have been laid out for print.

Use a consistent heading hierarchy. Write one document title, followed by sections and subsections in a meaningful order. In a large publication, headings are not merely decoration; they are primary navigation targets. Preserve actual paragraph boundaries. Use list functions rather than typing bullet glyphs into separate paragraphs. Keep a figure adjacent to its caption in the semantic order. Make links descriptive. Avoid using blank paragraphs, excessive spaces, or tabs to create visual layout; those characters can affect navigation and reading output.

A source-first approach also improves change management. When a chapter moves or a figure is replaced, automatic headings and references can be regenerated. When the original source is accessible, PDF and EPUB production requires fewer manual repairs. This is an economic advantage, but it is not a guarantee: a converter may mis-handle mathematical notation, multi-column arrangements, footnotes, and unusual objects, so final-format testing remains essential.

8. Headings, Paragraphs, and Logical Outline

A heading conveys section identity and hierarchy, not just larger or bold text. “Chapter 4” may be a Heading 1 within a textbook, followed by Heading 2 section titles and Heading 3 subtopics. A reader navigating headings should encounter a logical outline that matches the content's meaning. Numbers alone are insufficient: a paragraph manually formatted to resemble “4.2 Analysis” does not become a heading for an assistive technology just because a sighted reader recognizes it as such.

Avoid using headings solely to obtain a desired font appearance. Create or modify a style that looks right while retaining the correct semantic role. A “Note” label inside a paragraph is not necessarily a heading. Conversely, important chapters should not be buried in nonsemantic text boxes. Long documents benefit from consistent chapter and subsection granularity; too many headings can make navigation burdensome, while too few leave readers stranded within long blocks of text.

Intended content Appropriate semantic representation Misleading shortcut
Chapter heading Heading style at appropriate level Bold 18-point body paragraph
Subsection Next meaningful heading level Arbitrarily skip levels to adjust appearance
Enumerated requirements True ordered list Manual numbers with tabs
Definition Term plus meaningful explanatory paragraph or definition structure All-caps text treated as structure
Quotation Quotation paragraph style/semantic grouping Indentation only, without clear attribution

Review the outline using the authoring application's navigation pane and the distributed file's heading navigation. If these differ materially, the export may have lost structure.

9. Reading Order, Columns, and Floating Objects

Printed pages invite the eye to jump between columns, sidebars, captions, and figures. Assistive technologies need a linearizable order. In a two-column research report, the correct sequence is generally the first column top-to-bottom, then the second, not line one from the left followed by line one from the right. A footnote, margin note, or pull quote must be connected to the relevant main text in a predictable way. The answer depends on editorial purpose; there is no universal rule that every sidebar belongs immediately after its nearest visible line.

Floating figures, text boxes, and layered objects are especially risky. Authoring software may store them near an insertion anchor that does not reflect their visual position. A PDF converter may emit an unexpected content order. An EPUB converter may move them into the main reading stream with insufficient context. Inspect reading order after export, not merely object positions in the source file. Preserve meaningful relationships by placing figures in-line where feasible or by establishing explicit reference and reading order.

A useful acceptance test asks a reader to encounter the document without looking at the page: do they understand the argument, data, callouts, and cross-references in the order presented? If not, fix the content model rather than hoping the reader will infer the layout visually.

10. Document Navigation, Contents, and Bookmarks

Long publications should provide several ways to move. A structured table of contents generated from real headings is more reliable than a manually typed list of titles and printed page numbers. Hyperlinked contents let a reader jump to a chapter; PDF bookmarks permit navigation in supporting viewers; EPUB navigation documents define the reading system's navigation interface. Indexes, cross-references, hyperlinks, footnotes, and citation back-links may add essential paths in scholarly works.

A logical structure is not automatically an adequate navigation system. A 600-page volume may have accurate headings but still require chapter-level bookmarks, linked lists of figures, and a subject index. Internal links need discernible purposes. “See Table 7” should resolve to the correct table, not merely the next printed page. Where the target is far away, readers may need a practicable way to return to the reference location. Do not break a link by changing a heading or regenerating identifiers without retesting its destination.

A comprehensive navigation review should start with no mouse and no visual scanning: find Chapter 6, open its third subsection, locate an associated chart, and return to the citation. This checks the actual task rather than the existence of a contents page alone.

11. Metadata, Title, Language, and Publication Identity

Metadata helps assistive technologies and library systems identify a document. A meaningful title should appear as document content and in file properties when supported. Set the primary document language; identify language changes for passages that require a different pronunciation or braille translation. Include an appropriate document description, author or responsible organization, revision or edition, and rights information where useful. Avoid assuming a filename such as final-v7.pdf is a meaningful public title.

The metadata supported by a DOCX file, PDF information dictionary or XMP data, and EPUB package is not identical. A PDF's displayed title may depend on viewer behavior; an EPUB reader may expose the package title, creators, language, identifier, and accessibility metadata; a Word document's language may be applied to specific text runs. Therefore inspect both metadata and user-visible presentation. Identify the main language accurately even if the entire publication is technically valid Unicode.

For academic and government publishing, maintain an edition record that connects the accessible file to the correct source revision. This prevents a remediated PDF from becoming obsolete while a newer inaccessible print export is distributed. Accessibility is part of publication version control.

12. Multilingual Text, Reading Pronunciation, and Localization

Multilingual documents require more than translating visible words. Screen readers use language metadata to select pronunciation rules; braille output may depend on language and notation. A report written in English but quoting Arabic, French, Japanese, or Latin may need localized language assignments. Language tags should reflect actual content, not simply the author's interface locale. Symbols, abbreviations, and names may still require editorial care because pronunciations vary across readers.

In EPUB XHTML, the document's primary language commonly appears on the root element, with lang and xml:lang used according to the applicable EPUB/XHTML context. In a word processor, selected spans may need their own proofing or language setting; in PDF, appropriate language metadata and marked content can be relevant. A machine translation should not be treated as an accessibility accommodation when meaning, legal obligations, scientific terminology, or proper names may be distorted. Review translated versions as real publications.

Consider bidirectional text, mixed scripts, ruby annotation, mathematical symbols, and font coverage. A publication that displays correctly but produces gibberish after extraction has not preserved useful text. Test representative passages with copy/paste, read-aloud, search, and braille or screen-reader output where applicable.

13. Lists, Quotations, Footnotes, and Academic Apparatus

Scholarly and professional documents contain structures more varied than ordinary paragraphs. Ordered and unordered lists should be encoded as lists, so readers hear item boundaries and understand the number of items. Nested lists require meaningful levels, not leading spaces or manual symbols. Quotations should retain attribution; definitions should not rely solely on visual indent. Footnotes and endnotes need clear references and a navigable return path. Glossaries, bibliographies, numbered equations, and index entries must also be understandable without relying on printed page layout.

The difficulty increases during conversion. A word processor may preserve footnote objects, while a converter might flatten them into end-of-file text with no backlinks. An EPUB export might place notes at the end of a chapter, but readers need to return without losing the main passage. In PDF, a reading system may expose footnotes as tagged notes, artifacts, or other content depending on implementation. Evaluate whether the published structure remains understandable and operable; merely detecting the word “footnote” is not sufficient.

For MGU textbooks, editorial planning should include a stable convention for source notes, glossary terms, figure references, and study exercises. The convention should serve both print and accessible electronic editions rather than being added manually for each conversion.

14. Accessible Tables and Data Relationships

Tables should represent relationships among rows and columns, not function as general-purpose layout grids. A simple data table usually needs clearly identified column headings and, when necessary, row headings. Readers must be able to associate each numeric value with its corresponding labels. Avoid merged cells, empty header cells, and deeply nested tables when a simpler structure would communicate the same information. Complex statistical tables may need introductory text explaining units, categories, and the interpretation of missing values.

In Word and common office editors, apply the relevant table-header settings; when exported, verify those relationships in the target file. In tagged PDF, logical table structures such as table, row, header cell, and data cell roles are important, and complex spans may require more careful associations. In EPUB, native XHTML table markup supports semantic headers, captions, scopes, and programmatic relationships when authored appropriately. A screenshot of a table loses those relationships and prevents convenient searching or extraction.

Region Q1 Q2 Q3
North 18 22 25
South 13 17 21

This illustrative table is useful only if the publication also identifies what the values measure, whether they are counts or percentages, and how periods are defined. The absence of units is a content defect, even if headers are technically encoded. Long tables can benefit from a short summary of the key trend and a link to machine-readable data.

15. Figures, Charts, Maps, and Meaningful Alternatives

An image's alternative must communicate its purpose in the surrounding argument. A decorative border may have an empty alternative; a photograph illustrating a historical artifact needs context-specific identification; a statistical chart may require both a short summary and a detailed data table. The goal is not to narrate every pixel but to preserve the evidence, relationship, or decision that the figure contributes. Automatically generated captions are starting points, not editorial substitutes for verifying the chart's interpretation.

For complex graphs, maps, timelines, engineering diagrams, and scientific figures, provide a two-layer approach: a concise title or short alternative identifying the subject and a nearby detailed description or accessible dataset. Identify axes, units, key comparisons, exceptions, and uncertainty. Maps should explain relevant spatial relationships, not merely list place names without direction or scale. A mathematical graph needs accessible expressions and explanation of what can be inferred. Diagram descriptions should follow the logical flow of the system rather than the arbitrary sequence of graphical drawing objects.

Keep figures connected to captions and references during conversion. A screen reader that announces “Figure 8: Trade volume” after the paragraph analyzing Figure 9 creates confusion even when the individual alt texts are accurate. The companion MGU article Accessible Images, Charts, and Nontext Content treats graphic alternatives in more technical detail.

16. Mathematical, Chemical, and Technical Notation

Equations are integral information, not decorative images. Readers may need to listen to an expression, navigate its structure, inspect it character by character, or render it in braille. A screenshot of an equation rarely supports these tasks. Use a native equation model or an appropriate machine-readable mathematical representation, and preserve the semantic structure through conversion. Mathematical content may require MathML or format-specific equations, but actual support depends on the export pipeline and reading system. Neither a tool's claim to “support MathML” nor a correct visual rendering proves that the expression is navigable in the delivered file.

For a sample relationship, the statement “average velocity equals displacement divided by elapsed time” helps readers orient themselves to the notation without replacing a fully accessible equation. Units, variables, assumptions, and equations referred to from paragraphs need clear definitions. Complex matrices, subscripts, superscripts, summation, chemical reactions, and diagrams need specialist review; naive text alternatives can change meaning. An equation that loses a minus sign or exponent creates more than inconvenience—it can corrupt the academic claim.

A technical-publication workflow should test a representative set of equations with the intended screen reader and mathematical speech or braille system. Check whether a reader can distinguish numerators from denominators and superscripts from neighboring text. Record exceptions and offer an accessible alternate representation where complete native support is unavailable.

17. Descriptive Links, Cross-References, and Source Citations

Links in electronic documents should communicate their destination or purpose out of context. A reference list full of “click here” labels forces a screen-reader user to inspect surrounding paragraphs repeatedly. Use descriptive article titles, chapter headings, resource names, or actions. For raw web addresses that are meaningful as citations, it may be appropriate to show a short labeled link while preserving the full URL in the underlying target or bibliography. The link's programmatic target must match the text's promise.

Internal links should go to the correct chapter, table, note, glossary term, or figure. If a publication spans multiple files, verify that the target remains resolvable offline and in common readers. PDF destinations and bookmarks may behave differently from EPUB fragment identifiers; a print PDF's page references may not make sense in a reflowable EPUB where the reader changes font size. Use semantic anchors and section identifiers for electronic navigation rather than relying exclusively on print pagination.

Citations and bibliography systems should preserve source attribution when exported. External web links can become unavailable; the bibliographic identity must still be understandable without the network. Treat a failed external URL as an availability issue, not a reason to remove the citation itself.

18. Forms, Signatures, and Interactive Documents

A fillable document is an application-like workflow inside a file. Each input needs a meaningful programmatic label, clear instructions, a sensible keyboard sequence, and a predictable indication of what has been entered. Checkbox and radio-group relationships, required fields, errors, and submission outcomes need explicit exposure. A visually labeled rectangle that accepts typing is not inherently an accessible field. Check whether the finished PDF or office form still exposes the name, role, state, and value in the intended viewer.

High-stakes forms require additional care: financial authorizations, benefit applications, legal acknowledgments, and medical paperwork may have irreversible consequences. Readers should be able to review entries, identify corrections, and understand what signing or submitting means. Digital signature interfaces can depend on third-party components and certificate dialogs outside the document itself; test the complete task, including the system dialog where possible. Provide an accessible alternative process when a required signing workflow cannot be made operable in supported software.

Avoid distributing a document whose security settings block essential operations such as filling, extracting accessible text, or interacting with assistive tools without a justified reason. Security and accessibility need to be designed together. A form that looks compliant but cannot be submitted with keyboard or assistive input does not provide an accessible service.

19. Visual Presentation: Contrast, Text, Layout, and Reflow

A document's semantic structure and its visual presentation are complementary. Low-contrast text can remain unreadable even when perfectly tagged. Dense paragraphs, tiny type, decorative fonts, and unexplained color coding can obstruct readers who do not use screen readers. Use distinguishable foreground/background combinations, accessible chart palettes, clear labels and symbols, and sufficient spacing. The exact criteria applicable to a non-web document depend on the governing standard or policy, but the user's functional need is straightforward: information must remain perceivable.

Word-processing documents and reflowable EPUB usually offer more layout flexibility than fixed-page PDF. Authors should avoid artificial line breaks, locked text boxes, and multi-column constructs that collapse poorly when fonts enlarge. In EPUB, CSS that fixes every line's height or prevents text resizing may undermine the format's primary accessibility advantage. PDF viewers may provide zoom or reflow capabilities, but results depend on structure and viewer implementation; a PDF should not be assumed to provide the same adaptable experience as a well-built reflowable publication.

Test long words, tables, footnotes, equations, and captions under increased text size. A publication that adapts paragraphs but hides the answer key behind an overlay remains defective. Do not trade off essential content to preserve an exact print-like appearance on every device.

20. Cognitive Accessibility and Editorial Comprehension

Accessible document production includes editorial comprehension, not only technical tagging. Readers benefit from clear section goals, defined terminology, consistent references, meaningful summaries, and instructions that do not demand remembering earlier pages without navigational support. Short explanatory paragraphs, useful headings, and transparent definitions can help readers with limited working memory, attention variability, or unfamiliarity with the field. This does not mean removing technical depth; it means explaining relationships and reducing unnecessary ambiguity.

Tables should include units and definitions. Forms should provide examples of acceptable input. A long procedure should state prerequisites, the expected result, and what to do if an error occurs. In textbooks, review questions should identify which chapter concepts they assess. In policy reports, distinguish observed evidence from projections and recommendations. A clear content architecture can reduce the assistive-technology workload of everyone, especially people reading one section at a time.

Use the companion article Cognitive Accessibility and Clear Language for full treatment of language, predictability, and working-memory support. The current article owns the publication-level implementation of those principles, such as navigable abstracts, readable document controls, accessible notes, and consistent section structures.

21. Accessible Microsoft Word and DOCX Production

Word is widely used because authors can build structured documents without writing code. Its strengths include paragraph and heading styles, real lists, table headers, alternative text, language settings, a document navigation pane, and accessibility checking tools. A robust workflow begins with styles, not hand formatting. Apply Heading 1 and Heading 2 to genuine structural headings; use caption and list tools where available; verify that important diagrams have context-sensitive alternatives; and set the appropriate document language.

Microsoft's Accessibility Checker can identify many common problems, including certain missing alternative texts and structural issues. It cannot determine whether the author's descriptions are accurate or whether an argument's reading sequence is coherent. A document can pass automatic checks while having misleading link text or a confusing table. Use it as an early defect finder, followed by manual structure inspection and task-based reading.

Practical workflow: create from an accessible template; enter content with real styles; build navigation and references; review nontext alternatives; run the checker; inspect headings and tables; save a controlled source version; export to the requested format; test the exported file independently. If the resulting PDF has bad reading order, investigate source text boxes and floating objects before attempting extensive manual repair downstream.

22. Google Docs, OpenDocument, and Other Editable Formats

Accessible authoring principles also apply to collaborative web editors and OpenDocument tools. The feature names differ, but the core expectations remain: document title, structured headings, real lists, meaningful table organization, explicit language where supported, images with alternatives, and a predictable reading sequence. Collaborative comments, suggested edits, annotations, and version histories introduce additional accessibility considerations beyond the final publication. A reviewer should be able to identify changes and comments using a supported workflow, not just see colored marks in a margin.

Export behavior may vary by product and version. A Google Docs file exported to PDF may not preserve every relationship in the way a dedicated tagged-PDF workflow would; an ODT document converted to DOCX may alter list styles or object anchors. Treat such changes as conversion risks to investigate, not universal claims about a product. If multiple organizations edit the same manuscript, agree on a single canonical source, style vocabulary, citation convention, and export responsibility.

A sound handoff package can include the original editable document, the final accessible distribution copy, a note identifying version and tested software, and a list of unresolved limitations. Accessible collaboration and accessible publication are related but different quality gates.

23. Presentations, Speaker Notes, and Slide Reading Order

Slides often derive their meaning from spatial layout and live speech. A screen reader generally needs a usable object order and alternatives for meaningful diagrams. Use built-in slide layouts when they provide reliable title and content placeholders. Give each slide a descriptive title. Inspect reading order, especially when objects were added late or copied from other decks. Decorative shapes should not become distracting stops; meaningful shapes must communicate their purpose. Charts may need accessible data or a textual summary rather than an unhelpful name such as “Chart 2.”

A live presentation and a standalone downloadable deck have different obligations. When a speaker verbally explains all the information in a diagram, a person attending the presentation may receive useful context; someone downloading the slides later may not. Include speaker notes, a transcript, or an accessible companion document where essential reasoning otherwise disappears. For synchronized video or recorded presentation, captions and audio description may also be relevant.

After exporting a deck to PDF, verify slide titles, text object order, figure alternatives, and links in the PDF itself. A slide deck should not be considered accessible solely because the source application has an accessibility checker or because the font is large enough for projection.

24. Spreadsheets and Data-Heavy Office Publications

Spreadsheets challenge readers because meaning is distributed over cells, formula ranges, sheet names, labels, and sometimes charts. Name sheets descriptively; identify the main table or model; provide units and explanatory instructions; avoid large regions of empty decorative cells; and use stable headers. Where an input workbook has multiple tabs, explain the purpose of each and the order in which a user should inspect or edit them. Consider protection and validation carefully: a locked cell may be appropriate, but the user needs a clear way to recognize editable inputs.

A complex financial model can be computationally sound yet inaccessible if a screen reader encounters thousands of unlabeled cell coordinates. A small introductory worksheet with definitions, navigation guidance, and clear model assumptions can materially improve access. For a public report, offer a structured HTML table or accessible text summary alongside the downloadable workbook where feasible. Preserve formulas, source data, and model assumptions rather than replacing substantive information with a screenshot.

Evaluate a task such as “find the forecast revenue for Q3 and compare it with Q2” using keyboard and screen reader. Can the reader identify the sheet, column, row, number format, and unit without visual inference? If not, the file's data architecture needs additional meaning.

25. Tagged PDF: The Separation of Appearance and Structure

PDF maintains a page-oriented visual representation, but accessibility depends heavily on additional information. In an appropriately tagged PDF, structural elements distinguish paragraphs, headings, lists, tables, figures, links, notes, and other roles. These tags relate the visible and extractable content to meaningful structure so assistive technologies can navigate and interpret it. The tags are not simply colored markers added by an editing tool; they are part of the file's semantic architecture.

A common misconception is that selecting text proves PDF accessibility. A file may permit copy/paste but have no useful heading or table structure. Another misconception is that a PDF tagged by an automated program is necessarily accurate. The program can label a paragraph as a heading incorrectly, reverse the intended reading order, or fail to describe a diagram. Conversely, a PDF that passes a technical syntax checker may still have a confusing human experience if essential meaning is not present in text.

When creating a PDF from a structured source, use a supported tagged-PDF export path, then inspect the result. For existing PDFs without a source, specialized remediation may be necessary. The most economical and reliable solution depends on whether the original content can be reconstructed from an editable manuscript.

26. PDF/UA-1, PDF/UA-2, and Conformance Boundaries

PDF/UA-1 corresponds to ISO 14289-1:2014 and is based on PDF 1.7 technology. PDF/UA-2 corresponds to ISO 14289-2:2024 and uses the PDF 2.0 foundation. They are distinct standards with differing technical details, not interchangeable grades of one certification. A file created under one baseline should not automatically be described as conforming to the other. Tool support, receiving-system requirements, and the chosen PDF version matter when setting the correct target.

PDF/UA focuses on conditions that enable accessible use of PDF documents. It is closely related to—but not identical with—the full scope of usability, WCAG evaluation, or every legal requirement. Claiming PDF/UA conformance requires appropriate technical and semantic evidence for the chosen version. A document that passes one mechanical validator still needs human review of alt text, reading order, table meaning, language, and workflow. A good accessibility statement discloses exactly what has been evaluated and, where relevant, the limits of tested viewers.

For long-lived institutional collections, record the edition, PDF standard targeted, validator version, known exceptions, and remediator. Avoid marketing terms like “universal PDF accessibility” that imply identical rendering in every reader. The standard establishes a file-based technical target, while users still depend on compatible software and correct content.

27. Rebuild at Source or Repair the Existing PDF?

The choice between rebuilding and manual remediation should be based on available sources, document complexity, future maintenance, and the quality of existing structure. If an editable source exists and the PDF's faults originate in that source, correcting the source and regenerating the PDF is generally preferable. It prevents the same errors from recurring during the next revision. If the original document no longer exists, targeted PDF repair may be necessary, especially for archival or externally supplied publications.

Condition Likely best starting action Main risk to monitor
Editable manuscript available, poor tagging Correct source styles and regenerate Converter does not preserve complex relationships
Strong tagged PDF with isolated figure defect Repair figure description or tag locally Manual fix lost in later export
Inaccessible table repeated across a long report Redesign table at source if possible Repaired cells remain semantically ambiguous
Scanned image-only historical report OCR, proofread and build structure OCR errors alter names or numbers
Legally archived document with no edit rights Preserve original and produce accessible companion as appropriate Alternate version may not be equivalent or legally accepted

Prioritize semantic correctness over making every validator indicator green by superficial manipulation. Reclassifying all complex tables as paragraphs to silence warnings would destroy relationships. Maintain an authoritative record of repairs and ensure the actual distributed file, not a private working copy, is the one tested.

28. Scanned Documents, OCR, and Historical Collections

A scan preserves appearance but usually does not preserve useful machine-readable structure. Optical character recognition (OCR) can create searchable text, yet OCR is not synonymous with accessibility. It may misread historical type, handwritten annotations, accented characters, tables, mathematical notation, and page numbers. It can also place text in an order that is impossible to follow. A searchable scan whose word “interest” becomes “intere5t” in a financial contract is both an access failure and a content integrity risk.

An archival workflow should distinguish the preservation master from the accessible reading edition. The master may retain original imagery for authenticity; the reading edition should provide corrected text, logical headings, meaningful page references, descriptions of significant marginalia, and captions for archival images. Where a handwritten passage cannot be reliably transcribed, disclose that uncertainty and avoid inventing its contents. Public institutions may offer both the facsimile and a verified accessible transcription.

OCR confidence scores and automatic correction are helpful triage tools, but human review is essential for names, dates, prices, formulas, and legal clauses. If a historical diagram cannot be fully recovered, describe available evidence and uncertainty. The obligation is faithful access to the source's meaning, not the fabrication of missing information.

29. PDF Reading Order, Tags, Artifacts, and Tables

A tagged PDF may have several overlapping notions of order: content stream order, tag-tree order, and the order selected by a particular viewer or assistive technology. Editors must understand which tool is showing which representation. Inspect logical tags, text extraction, headings, tables, figure associations, and reading sequence in representative readers. Decorative page numbers or running headers might be marked as artifacts in an appropriate context, but real information should not be hidden merely because it repeats.

Complex tables demand special care. The table's visual row boundaries may not automatically produce a meaningful structure; header cells and span relationships should be interpreted correctly. Multi-column pages can place figure captions into the wrong order. Footnotes or equation numbers may be read as disconnected objects. A document's tag tree should follow the author's intended content relationships without omitting meaningful notes or attachments.

A practical review begins with one representative chapter, one dense table, one illustrated page, and one page containing notes. Check with a structure inspector and then with an actual screen reader. If the same systemic defect recurs throughout the document, correct the conversion method or source instead of fixing hundreds of instances manually without addressing the cause.

30. PDF Forms, Annotations, and Security Restrictions

Interactive PDF features add further responsibilities. Form fields need visible and programmatic labels, field roles and values, instructions, and predictable keyboard order. Tooltips and field names should communicate the actual question rather than internal database codes. Annotation links and comments need a usable way to discover and activate them. Popups and signature widgets should not become hidden traps for non-mouse users. A file that requires printing, handwriting, and scanning to submit an application may be inaccessible as a service even if its static reading text is well tagged.

Security mechanisms should protect data without unnecessarily blocking access. Some DRM, encryption, and copy restrictions can interfere with reading technologies or text extraction in particular software. Evaluate the actual result rather than claiming all security measures are incompatible with accessibility. Sensitive forms should use minimal data collection, clear retention explanations, and authenticated transmission appropriate to risk. If a document is opened from a browser, the accessibility of the browser's download and viewer interfaces can be part of the complete user journey.

Include sample-field completion in the acceptance test. A tester should locate a field, understand its question, enter and correct a value, identify required status, and save or submit. Checking that field rectangles exist is insufficient.

31. Extractable Text, Character Integrity, and Fonts

Text must remain text in more than a visual sense. A PDF can display a sentence correctly while using a defective font encoding that produces scrambled characters when copied, searched, read aloud, or exported. Similarly, embedded fonts in an EPUB may look attractive but lack characters required by the content. A document's text layer should preserve real Unicode content and, where supported by the format, the mappings needed for extraction. This is particularly important for mathematics, historical sources, multilingual passages, punctuation, and specialist notation.

Use standard authoring practices and well-supported fonts. Verify that copied text matches the source and that search finds the words readers expect. Inspect ligatures, superscripts, fractions, directionality, and symbols separately; they are common places for unintended substitutions. Do not substitute private-use symbols or decorative icons for text with no textual alternative. If a glyph is integral to a technical definition, explain it and provide a trustworthy textual representation.

Font licensing and portability also matter. Embedding, substitution, and digital-rights restrictions can change the reading experience. The goal is not to mandate one font family, but to preserve meaning and usability when the document moves between environments. Preserve the original source for future re-export if a reader needs a version with a different typeface.

32. EPUB 3 as a Structured Electronic Publication

EPUB 3 packages publication resources—commonly XHTML content documents, CSS, images, navigation, and metadata—into a distributable file. A reading system uses the package to present books and other publications. The format can support reflow, adaptable typography, semantic navigation, footnote links, media, and metadata describing accessible features. Unlike a fixed-layout PDF, a reflowable EPUB can usually adapt line lengths and page breaks to device size and reader preferences. Those advantages depend on correct authoring and capable reading software.

An EPUB contains both content and publication machinery. Its navigation document should identify the table of contents. The spine expresses a primary reading sequence. Metadata identifies the publication and makes accessibility features discoverable. XHTML provides semantic elements for chapters, headings, paragraphs, tables, and links. CSS determines presentation but should not defeat user settings. Optional JavaScript, media overlays, or embedded resources introduce additional compatibility concerns. Each layer contributes to access; no single layer compensates for missing semantic content elsewhere.

Do not equate EPUB with automatic accessibility. A book containing scanned page images inside an EPUB container remains difficult for nonvisual users. A book that uses fixed viewport dimensions may severely constrain text enlargement. A correct XHTML heading structure can still be undermined by misleading navigational labels or broken intra-book links.

33. EPUB Accessibility 1.1 and the Developing 1.2 Specification

The W3C EPUB Accessibility 1.1 Recommendation, published October 17, 2024, establishes discoverability and conformance expectations for EPUB publications. It addresses metadata and references WCAG-based content requirements. A publication should be evaluated as a complete work, including all content required for the claimed level. Accessible titles, navigation, media, alternatives, and reading-system compatibility should not be assessed as isolated fragments.

As of November 23, 2026, EPUB Accessibility 1.2 is a W3C Candidate Recommendation Draft dated November 11, 2026, not a final Recommendation. It is legitimate to study and pilot its newer requirements, but a publisher should not describe the draft as a fully adopted W3C Recommendation. Compare specifications by exact version and publication status; do not assume that “latest” means final or that a change in an editor's draft automatically changes every contractual requirement. Where a vendor or procurement rule specifies EPUB Accessibility 1.1 or another version, test against that stated baseline and document any additional voluntary testing.

EPUB conformance is not demonstrated simply by including accessibility metadata. Metadata is a discoverability claim about the publication; the actual content must support the claimed experience. A statement such as “contains alternative text” should be truthful for the figures in the relevant work, not merely copied from a template.

34. EPUB Accessibility Metadata and Reader Discoverability

Readers need to know whether a publication will work for them before they acquire or open it. EPUB accessibility metadata can communicate information such as access modes, accessibility features, possible hazards, and sufficient access modes. A human-readable accessibility summary can explain unusual limitations. For example, an illustrated chemistry textbook may include textual descriptions for most diagrams yet require a particular interactive resource for a few exercises. Stating that limitation is more useful than pretending the book has no barriers.

Accessibility metadata belongs in the proper publication package using the specification's defined properties. Some metadata describes a feature (such as alternative text or a table of contents); other metadata identifies the ways content can be accessed. Distinguish a publication's required perception modes from combinations sufficient to obtain its meaning. A book containing images does not necessarily require vision if every meaningful image has an effective textual equivalent. The reverse can also be true when essential visual material is not described.

The following illustrative package fragment shows the idea of metadata in an EPUB OPF file; values and the permitted syntax must be validated against the targeted EPUB specification before production.

<package xmlns="http://www.idpf.org/2007/opf"
         version="3.0" unique-identifier="pub-id">
  <metadata xmlns:dc="http://purl.org/dc/elements/1.1/">
    <dc:identifier id="pub-id">urn:example:coursebook-01</dc:identifier>
    <dc:title>Introduction to Accessible Publishing</dc:title>
    <dc:language>en</dc:language>
    <meta property="schema:accessMode">textual</meta>
    <meta property="schema:accessibilityFeature">tableOfContents</meta>
    <meta property="schema:accessibilityFeature">alternativeText</meta>
  </metadata>
</package>

This is not a complete valid EPUB package or a full conformance declaration. In real publishing, namespace or prefix handling, required package components, manifest, spine, and accessibility claim strings must meet the selected specification. Do not publish an invented conformsTo value to manufacture the appearance of certification.

35. EPUB Navigation, XHTML Semantics, and Reading Sequence

A reflowable textbook needs a meaningful chapter hierarchy, an accessible table of contents, and reliable links to figures, notes, references, exercises, and bibliographies. In EPUB XHTML, use real heading elements, lists, tables, and descriptive anchors. EPUB-specific semantics can supplement generic structure where appropriate, but should not substitute for native HTML semantics that are widely understood. An EPUB navigation document helps reading systems expose chapters and subsections, while the spine supplies the primary reading sequence.

A minimal illustrative chapter excerpt could use the following markup:

<section id="chapter-3" aria-labelledby="chapter-3-title">
  <h1 id="chapter-3-title">3. Principles of Accessible Publication</h1>
  <p>Readers need a usable sequence and discoverable structure.</p>
  <h2 id="section-3-1">3.1 Document headings</h2>
  <p>A heading identifies the next section for navigation.</p>
  <a href="notes.xhtml#note-7">Read note 7</a>
</section>

The snippet illustrates hierarchy and links, not a complete EPUB XHTML content document. A real export must include required namespaces, document wrappers, publication identifiers, resources, and navigation components. In production, examine whether a reading system announces headings in the intended sequence and returns from notes without disorienting the reader. Verify that internal links survive file splitting and rebuilding; EPUB often distributes one logical volume across multiple XHTML resources.

36. Reflowable and Fixed-Layout EPUB

Reflowable EPUB allows text to adapt to reader settings. Fixed-layout EPUB preserves page composition more closely, which can be useful for complex illustration or designed educational content, but can also constrain enlargement, navigation, and text extraction. The decision should follow content needs rather than treating fixed layout as the default for an attractive printed original. A historical atlas, children's illustrated book, and text-heavy law treatise do not present the same layout problem.

If fixed layout is necessary, avoid using it as an excuse to provide essential content solely as page images. Supply meaningful textual structure and alternatives, and ensure that reading order and navigation remain intelligible. When users need to magnify heavily or read on small screens, a complementary reflowable edition or structured HTML version may provide a better experience. Such an alternate must be substantively equivalent and maintained with revisions; a stale simplified version is not a reliable accessibility solution.

A useful format-selection question asks whether the text is the primary artifact or whether the exact relation of objects on a page is fundamental to interpretation. Even where precise geometry matters, relevant relationships can often be explained in structured text. Test the actual target readers before locking the distribution format.

37. Audio, Video, Media Overlays, and Synchronization

Electronic books and digital documents may contain audio narration, embedded video, read-along highlighting, or synchronized media overlays. These features can support readers with different needs, but only if auditory and visual information is available through suitable alternatives. Videos with speech may require captions; visually essential actions may require descriptions; standalone audio may need a transcript depending on context and applicable requirements. Reader controls for starting, stopping, seeking, and adjusting media should remain accessible to keyboard and assistive input.

Media overlays can synchronize text with narration. The system should not rely on synchronized color highlighting as the only way to tell a reader where they are, and a loss of synchronization should not prevent ordinary reading. Avoid automatic audio that surprises readers or prevents use of screen-reader speech. Multimedia content inside EPUB or a PDF should be tested in the actual delivery environment because reader support varies considerably.

For formal instruction, include stable section or paragraph identifiers so a reader can move between the narrated passage and the text. Verify that captions, transcripts, and descriptions represent the published edition, not an older recording. Refer to Captions, Transcripts, and Audio Description for broader media production methods.

38. DRM, Reading Systems, and Device Interoperability

A correctly prepared EPUB or PDF can still be inaccessible in a particular reading application. Reading systems vary in their support for accessible navigation, text-to-speech, braille, mathematical content, note handling, image descriptions, and personal preferences. Digital rights management may restrict copying or alter how accessibility services access content in particular implementations. The correct evaluation is empirical: test the documented combination of file, license, reading app, operating system, and assistive technology.

Publishing contracts should explicitly require accessibility support through the delivery chain. The distributor and reading platform share operational responsibility even when the publisher produced a strong file. If a reader cannot open the book with a supported screen reader because a DRM wrapper blocks necessary access, the published service has a critical barrier that must be addressed. The response need not be abandoning rights protection altogether; it can involve compatible protection, a different delivery route, or an authorized equivalent accessible edition.

Keep known support limitations visible at acquisition time, not buried after purchase. A clear accessibility summary and support channel let readers make informed decisions. Do not equate vendor marketing claims with independently validated outcomes.

39. Cross-Format Conversion and Semantic Preservation

Conversion is a transformation of both presentation and information architecture. A DOCX-to-PDF pipeline may flatten a table, lose headings, or replace a formula with a visual object. An HTML-to-EPUB pipeline may introduce empty navigation entries or change IDs. A PDF-to-Word conversion may reconstruct paragraphs by spatial heuristics and yield incorrect order. Each conversion therefore needs a mapping between source semantics and destination semantics, with documented exceptions and a measurable acceptance process.

Source object Desired PDF result Desired EPUB result Conversion failure to test
Heading 2 Correct structural heading tag Correct XHTML heading Presented as large paragraph only
Numbered list List and item relationships Native ordered list Numbering read as detached text
Data table Table structure and header associations Structured XHTML table Table becomes layout image
Figure + caption Appropriate figure semantics and placement Image alternative and caption association Caption reads with wrong image
Footnote Discoverable reference and note Navigable note with return path Note is moved to unrelated page
Equation Usable text/semantic representation Accessible mathematical representation Formula rasterized with no equivalent
Language change Language metadata/marked content where supported XHTML language attributes Screen reader pronounces with wrong rules

Maintain one authoritative content model when possible, but allow format-specific editorial adjustments. Structural parity does not mean identical file bytes, visual arrangement, or interaction mechanism. It means each delivered format supports the same essential meaning and tasks.

40. Automated Checking: Strengths and Limits

Automated tools are useful for broad defect discovery. Microsoft and other office suites have accessibility checkers; PDF tools can examine tags, metadata, structural expectations, and selected PDF/UA conditions; EPUBCheck checks EPUB packaging and validity; Ace by DAISY reports many EPUB accessibility issues. These tools differ in scope and should be selected for their relevant target rather than treated as interchangeable certification engines.

Checker or approach Good at detecting Cannot establish alone
Office accessibility checker Missing alt text flags, some structure and contrast issues Correctness of an explanation or practical task
PDF validator Tag syntax, structure conditions, selected PDF/UA checks Whether a diagram description conveys its meaning
PDF visual/structure inspector Order, tags, associations for review Cross-reader user performance without further testing
EPUBCheck EPUB package conformance, manifest and syntax issues WCAG/EPUB Accessibility conformance overall
Ace by DAISY Many EPUB accessibility defects and metadata issues Full conformance or usability by itself
Screen reader and manual walkthrough Task accessibility and human interpretation Every possible device, language, and user situation

A green automated report is a useful milestone, not the final verdict. A false-negative risk exists whenever a tool cannot evaluate meaning; a false-positive may exist where a tool lacks context. Record results and manually adjudicate them. Avoid “gaming” the checker by removing essential structure just to eliminate a warning.

41. Manual Evaluation with Assistive Technologies

Manual evaluation should follow realistic reader tasks and representative content. Begin with a keyboard-only pass. Open the document, locate its title, navigate headings, inspect a table, activate a link, read a caption, and return to the previous section. Repeat selected tasks using a screen reader and, where relevant, magnification, braille, or voice access. Use an actual PDF reader or EPUB app supported by the delivery process. An accessibility-tree or structure inspector can explain defects but cannot substitute for the lived reading workflow.

Select tests based on the file's components. A history textbook may require chapter navigation, quotation/footnote handling, timelines, maps, and foreign-language passages. A spreadsheet requires identifying row/column context and making data comparisons. A PDF form requires entry, review, correction, and completion. A research article needs equations, figures, references, and possibly datasets. The file's size does not remove these obligations; sample systemic properties where appropriate while inspecting unique high-risk components individually.

Document observed behaviors, software versions, and steps to reproduce. Instead of “screen reader doesn't work,” record: “After navigating to Table 4 in Reader X with Screen Reader Y, the value 12.6 is announced without its ‘Operating margin’ row label.” Specific evidence supports correction and defensible release decisions.

42. Evaluation Matrix and Evidence-Based Acceptance

A credible acceptance matrix combines standards references, file tests, and reader outcomes. Evidence should tie each requirement to an artifact version and test condition. A source document may be accessible yet export incorrectly, so the matrix must refer to the final distribution file. Use severity to distinguish critical blockers (inability to reach essential information or complete a form) from lesser issues (nonessential decorative announcement). Prioritize the actual consequences for readers rather than only counts of automated warnings.

Gate Acceptance question Evidence Escalation if failed
Content integrity Are all words, symbols and numbers preserved? Text-extraction comparison and spot checks Return to source or OCR review
Logical structure Do headings, lists and tables retain meaning? Structure inspection and screen-reader review Repair source or semantic tags
Graphics Are significant figures understandable without vision? Reviewed short/detailed alternatives Author subject-matter revision
Navigation Can a reader find and return to key sections? Recorded navigation tasks Repair navigation destinations

Keep a record of limitations. A known incompatibility should be characterized precisely—for example, an equation readout issue in a specified reader—rather than hidden or generalized into “all EPUB readers are inaccessible.”

43. Publishing Lifecycle, Procurement, and Institutional Governance

Accessibility needs an owner across manuscript development, editing, design, production, distribution, and maintenance. Assign responsibilities for source structure, figure descriptions, conversion, evaluation, correction, accessibility metadata, and reader support. Ensure contractors and third-party vendors provide actual remediation support and evidence for their deliverables. A publication management system should prevent unreviewed exports from silently replacing corrected versions.

Set up gates rather than a one-time final repair event. During authoring, require structured styles and meaningful figures. During editorial review, verify clarity and cross-references. During production, validate the finished DOCX, PDF, and EPUB. During release, check the actual download URLs and filenames, accessibility notices, and delivery platform. During maintenance, correct errata in all supported accessible formats. Track the ratio of source-origin versus converter-origin defects to prioritize process improvements.

An institution with hundreds of publications may need a risk-based remediation queue: prioritize active enrollment materials, application forms, required public information, time-sensitive notices, and high-use reference books. This is operational prioritization, not permission to mislabel the rest of the catalog as accessible. Procurement terms should require versioned standards, evidence, ownership, and remediation service levels.

44. Frequent Publication Failures and Their Root Causes

Many defects recur because publication workflows reward appearance before structure. Fake headings, manually spaced columns, image-only tables, unreviewed automatic descriptions, and inaccessible scanned appendices often begin in the source. Other defects originate during export: tags omitted, link targets rewritten, footnotes detached, or math flattened. A third category originates during distribution, including broken downloads, incompatible reader settings, or DRM behavior.

Observable failure Likely underlying cause Better corrective action
Headings never appear in navigation Text was hand-formatted instead of styled Rebuild source outline and re-export
Table is read as random numbers Header relationships missing or flattened Rebuild as semantic table with proper labels
Image description says “graphic” Placeholder alt text or blind auto-generation Author context-specific alternative
PDF search finds no terms Scan or damaged character mapping OCR/proofread or repair text encoding
EPUB TOC jumps to wrong place Broken fragment IDs or navigation export Repair source anchors and rebuild package
Footer is read between sentence halves Broken tagging/order Repair semantic order or export layout
Screen reader stops at license prompt Inaccessible reading-system/DRM interface Resolve delivery dependency or provide supported access
Updated edition has old captions Version drift across formats Regenerate from controlled source and verify edition

Do not apply blanket fixes without investigating causation. An author-created table might require restructuring; a publication-wide failure to recognize headings could result from an export flag. Root-cause analysis reduces maintenance costs and protects content meaning.

45. Design Pattern: Accessible Textbook Chapter Source

The most reliable textbook workflow starts with a semantic source template. A chapter contains a title, learning objectives, real heading hierarchy, paragraphs, lists, tables, meaningful figures, notes, review questions, and bibliography. The source should avoid making layout geometry the only expression of information. A short conceptual example follows:

<article aria-labelledby="chapter-title">
  <h1 id="chapter-title">Chapter 7: Industrialization</h1>
  <p><strong>Learning objective:</strong> Explain three causes
     and two social consequences of industrial growth.</p>
  <h2 id="causes">Causes</h2>
  <p>Economic change involved capital, technology and labor.</p>
  <figure>
    <img src="output-chart.svg"
         alt="Factory output increased each decade; see data table below.">
    <figcaption>Figure 7.1. Illustrative factory output trend.</figcaption>
  </figure>
  <table>
    <caption>Illustrative output by decade, in index units</caption>
    <thead><tr><th scope="col">Decade</th><th scope="col">Output</th></tr></thead>
    <tbody><tr><th scope="row">1880s</th><td>100</td></tr>
    <tr><th scope="row">1890s</th><td>135</td></tr></tbody>
  </table>
</article>

This example demonstrates structural relationships, not historically verified output data. Its goal is to show how content can remain meaningful when styling changes. When publishing to PDF or EPUB, preserve the accessible figure description, table relationships, and heading hierarchy. For real historical publications, cite actual data and provide enough context to interpret the chart and source limitations.

46. Worked Scenario 1: A University Converts Lecture Notes into Accessible PDF

A university department has fifty-page lecture packets in Word. Authors used bold paragraphs as headings and positioned equation images with floating text boxes. The school exports them as PDFs and receives reports that screen-reader navigation is nearly impossible. The defective output is not solved by adding a “download accessible PDF” link to the same file. The team first audits the source: reassigns real heading styles, adds equation descriptions or accessible mathematical representations, places callouts in meaningful sequence, and checks the tables. It then exports tagged PDF using an appropriate workflow.

A reviewer opens the resulting PDF, navigates by heading, reads a selected equation, follows a reference, and inspects the tag structure. The team also tests copy/paste accuracy of technical symbols. Success is evidenced by those task results, not merely by the presence of tags. The corrected Word template becomes the required starting point for future lecture packets, preventing the same failure in the next semester.

47. Worked Scenario 2: A Government Form Contains Unlabeled Fields

A public agency distributes a fillable PDF with boxes for a person's address, household details, and eligibility information. Sighted users see nearby labels, but the fields are announced only as “Text field 14” and “Checkbox 6.” Keyboard order jumps between pages and the submit action is inaccessible. The remedy requires more than changing the visual font. Staff define proper field descriptions, required-state instructions, logical tab order, clear error messaging, and an accessible submission route. Where signatures depend on an external service, that service must be tested as part of the same workflow.

Acceptance is a complete form task performed through keyboard and a representative screen reader, including the ability to review and correct a sample entry. Sensitive real applicant data is not needed for testing. The agency documents which PDF viewers support the required interactions and provides a viable alternate channel where needed. A technically improved file with an unusable submission portal would not satisfy the full service objective.

48. Worked Scenario 3: A Publisher Produces a Reflowable EPUB Textbook

A publisher has an illustrated science textbook designed for print. Its first EPUB edition preserves entire pages as screenshots to maintain typography. Students cannot enlarge individual paragraphs or navigate by headings, and figure captions are merged into images. The publisher returns to structured chapter content and produces a reflowable EPUB. Headings become XHTML headings; figures receive context-sensitive alternatives; charts have text summaries and datasets; note references support return navigation; and the package includes accurate accessibility metadata.

Before release, the team runs EPUBCheck, Ace by DAISY, and manual review. It tests font enlargement, chapter navigation, a complex table, and image interpretation in supported reading systems. The team does not simply declare “EPUB Accessibility 1.2 certified,” because the November 2026 1.2 specification remains a Candidate Recommendation Draft. Its conformance statement names the finalized standard actually evaluated and discloses any unresolved figure limitations. The result is an improved publication, not a promise that every reading app behaves identically.

49. Worked Scenario 4: A Museum Digitizes a Historical Newspaper

A museum scans a nineteenth-century newspaper with narrow columns, advertisements, images, and damaged print. OCR identifies many words but interleaves columns and converts several names incorrectly. The museum preserves the facsimile for historical authenticity while preparing a structured reading edition. Human editors proofread names, dates, and quotations; assign article boundaries and headings; distinguish captions from advertisements; and add descriptions for significant images. They preserve uncertainty where the source is illegible instead of silently guessing.

Users can search the corrected text, navigate between articles, and refer back to image coordinates or page identifiers in the preservation master. The museum evaluates both faithful scholarly representation and practical accessibility. A machine-searchable text layer alone would not have met the goal because its reading sequence and factual transcription were unreliable. The accessible edition includes a revision record linked to the source scans so later historical corrections can be audited.

50. Worked Scenario 5: A Financial Report Contains Dense Tables

A financial institution issues a PDF annual report whose financial statements contain multilevel headers and many merged cells. An automatic checker reports partial table structure, but a screen reader reads certain numeric amounts without clear row or column context. The production team first simplifies the source table layout where editorially feasible. It provides explicit units and reporting dates, uses stable table labels, and adjusts the PDF tag structure to preserve required header relationships. For particularly complex statements, a supporting accessible spreadsheet or HTML table may help readers analyze data.

Acceptance asks readers to locate net revenue in two periods, compare the values, and identify the correct units and footnotes. Merely locating two numbers is not sufficient; the labels must remain associated. The institution verifies that the values match its audited source and that accessible companion files correspond to the same reporting edition. This avoids a common risk: an accessible table that inadvertently changes the financial meaning during remediation.

51. Worked Scenario 6: A Multilingual Research Article Has Equations and Notes

A scholarly journal publishes a technical paper containing English argumentation, Arabic quotations, mathematical expressions, and footnotes. The PDF looks excellent but text extraction corrupts an equation and the screen reader mispronounces Arabic text because language changes are not conveyed. The editor records the actual language of each substantial passage, checks font/character mappings, preserves a usable mathematical representation, and ensures notes can be found and returned from. The same controls are examined in its EPUB edition where one is offered.

A reviewer follows the argument without vision, reads a selected equation, distinguishes a footnote from the main text, and obtains the original non-Latin quotation accurately. Where a reading system has a known limitation in mathematical speech, that limitation is documented with the tested software. The journal does not downgrade the entire article to image-only presentation to retain typographic fidelity; it balances scholarly precision with accessible meaning.

52. Worked Scenario 7: A Training Slide Deck Is Viewed Without a Presenter

An organization releases a slide deck from a training session. The slides contain short headings, visually grouped process diagrams, and references such as “as shown here” that made sense only during the live talk. A screen-reader user downloading the deck later cannot reconstruct the process or identify the next step. The training team revises slide titles, orders slide objects logically, adds descriptions for meaningful diagrams, and publishes accessible speaker notes or a structured companion document that explains the process and decision points.

The revised deck is tested both in its editable format and in the PDF exported for public distribution. A reader should be able to describe the sequence of the process without access to the speaker's gestures or spoken explanations. Where a recording is published, the team separately evaluates captions and visual description. The acceptance criterion is an understandable training task, not merely an attractive presentation design.

53. Worked Scenario 8: A Library's E-Book Platform Blocks Accessible Reading

A library licenses an EPUB edition with accurate headings, alt text, and accessibility metadata. The file passes publisher checks, but a specific mobile reading application prevents navigation by screen-reader headings after a DRM-enabled download. The library investigates the entire acquisition path: catalog descriptions, account authorization, download, decryption, reader controls, and assistive-technology operation. It reproduces the issue with a controlled device/version matrix and requests a vendor correction or an authorized supported delivery route.

The publisher retains responsibility for a correctly authored file; the distributor and reading system retain responsibilities for access to the delivered publication. Neither party can close the case by pointing to a good EPUB validation report. The library provides an accessible support process and communicates limitations honestly before acquisition where possible. Success means the reader can actually obtain and use the book within the supported licensing environment.

54. A Twelve-Stage Accessible Publication Framework

Accessible publishing is most reliable when implemented as a repeatable sequence rather than as final-stage cosmetics. The framework below applies across documents while recognizing format-specific requirements.

  1. Define the audience and tasks. Identify what readers must find, understand, compare, complete, or revisit and which document types they will receive.
  2. Set a versioned technical baseline. Name the standards and internal requirements that actually apply; distinguish formal obligations from guidance.
  3. Choose the authoritative source. Establish a structured manuscript or data model, an owner, and edition identifiers.
  4. Build semantic content. Use meaningful headings, lists, tables, notes, language metadata, and ordered content.
  5. Provide alternatives and explanations. Author contextual image descriptions, chart data, accessible mathematics, and media alternatives.
  6. Plan navigation. Generate tables of contents, bookmarks, linked references, indexes, and note return paths where appropriate.
  7. Design adaptable presentation. Review contrast, typography, reading scale, reflow, and device-specific constraints.
  8. Export for each format. Use supported tagged PDF, EPUB, DOCX, or other pipelines rather than assuming one export works for all readers.
  9. Run structural validators. Apply suitable format and accessibility tools; record versions, warnings, and actions.
  10. Perform human and assistive-technology review. Complete representative tasks in the actual distributed file and supported readers.
  11. Record acceptance and limitations. Link resolved defects, unresolved barriers, standards, and test evidence to the exact release artifact.
  12. Publish and maintain. Verify real download paths, accessibility notices, user support, errata, and synchronized updates across formats.

Do not skip the final two stages. It is common for a validated working copy to be replaced by a different export or for one format to be updated while another remains stale. Version-controlled publication records and clear ownership prevent the accessible version from becoming the exception that disappears during routine updates.

56. Applied Exercises and Study Questions

The exercises below can be completed on a public-domain sample or an institutionally authorized document. Do not use confidential records or live applicant information for demonstration.

  1. Select a three-page word-processing file. Identify every visually styled heading and determine which are genuine structural headings. Repair the outline and document the before/after navigation.
  2. Export the same structured source to PDF. Compare the reading order and heading structure of the original and final PDF with available inspection tools and a screen reader.
  3. Find a complex chart and create a concise alternative, a longer explanation, and an accompanying data table. Explain the distinct audience tasks each supports.
  4. Test a document's table of contents, footnotes, bibliography, and cross-references with keyboard-only navigation. Record any missing return route.
  5. Compare a reflowable EPUB and a fixed-layout PDF on a narrow display with enlarged text. Identify which relationships are preserved or broken in each.
  6. Run an EPUB validator and an accessibility checker on an example publication. Classify each reported issue as format validity, accessibility evidence, or a matter requiring human interpretation.
  7. Examine an example accessibility statement. Does it identify the exact version, standard, scope, tested reader environments, and known exceptions? Rewrite it to eliminate unsupported claims.
  8. Prepare an accessibility plan for scanning a damaged archival newspaper. Separate OCR, proofreading, semantic reconstruction, figure description, and scholarly uncertainty.
  9. Evaluate a fillable form using keyboard and screen reader. Document one successful task and one defect with specific steps and expected behavior.
  10. Draft procurement acceptance criteria for outsourced PDF and EPUB conversion, including source control, PDF/UA target, EPUB standard version, validation, manual testing, and remediation obligations.

Discussion prompts: When should a publisher offer more than one format? What evidence is sufficient for a release? Why does a format-level technical pass not prove equitable reader access? Which document features create the greatest consequence if misrepresented—an academic citation, a financial number, a mathematical symbol, or a legal checkbox—and why?

59. Related Topics and Disciplinary Boundaries

Data Systems addresses machine-readable datasets, metadata management, encoding, and integrity.
