1. Definition, Scope, and Governing Question

A chart can be legible, attractive, and numerically correct while withholding its principal finding from readers who cannot see it. A button containing only a magnifying-glass picture can look familiar yet have no accessible name. A complex map may offer a concise label but still conceal locations, routes, and relationships needed to make a decision. These failures share a cause: information or functionality is encoded visually without a usable way to recover its meaning.

Accessible Images, Charts, and Nontext Content studies the representation of static and interactive visual content so that essential information and actions remain available in text, semantic structures, and other suitable modalities. It addresses ordinary photographs, diagrams, logos, graphical controls, charts, maps, equations presented as images, raster and vector formats, canvas renderings, and visualizations generated by software. Its governing question is What information or function would be lost if this visual were unavailable, and which alternative allows a person to achieve the same relevant purpose?

The answer depends on why a visual was placed in a particular document or interface. A photograph might identify a person, establish architectural detail, supply historical evidence, decorate a page, or open another location when selected. Those purposes require different treatments even when the underlying image file is identical. Accessibility is not achieved by attaching a generic caption to every object, nor by generating a verbose account of every pixel. It requires an editorial decision about relevance, a technical implementation that exposes that decision, and real evaluation of what a user can learn or do.

This is Article 14 in the Accessibility specialist unit, under Human Interaction Systems. The preceding article, Captions, Transcripts, and Audio Description, owns time-based media alternatives. This article owns static and programmatically generated nontext information, including images embedded in larger task flows. Visual styling and color contrast receive full independent treatment in Color Contrast and Visual Presentation.

2. Users, Tasks, and Different Ways of Accessing Visual Information

Visual alternatives support people who are blind or have low vision, including people who use screen readers and braille displays, but the benefits are broader. A learner who struggles with visual complexity may understand a concise explanation more readily than a crowded infographic. Someone using magnification may need to inspect local detail while reading a textual summary of the whole. Images may fail to download; automated systems may need to index the content; a printout may lose its colors; and a narrow display may crop or shrink labels. These situations are not all identical, yet each reveals a risk when visual presentation becomes the only carrier of meaning.

Consider two readers consulting the same statistical plot. One needs the most important trend and a few relevant comparisons. Another needs all the values to conduct independent analysis. A single phrase such as “sales increased” serves the first partially and the second poorly. A lengthy paragraph listing every value may be difficult for both. A layered alternative—short title, summary of findings, structured data table, and optional methodological explanation—meets a wider range of requirements without forcing everyone through the same dense representation.

Reader or task Barrier in an unadapted visual Effective alternative or supplement Evidence of success
Screen-reader user checking quarterly revenue Chart drawn without exposed data Short chart summary and labeled data table Can identify both the trend and a precise quarter value
Braille reader examining a process diagram Relationships shown only as arrows Ordered steps, decisions, and transition descriptions Can reconstruct the logic without an image
Low-vision reader inspecting a map Tiny symbols, labels, and intricate legend Reflowing location list and readable directions Can identify destinations and routes at high magnification
Cognitive-accessibility user reading an infographic Many ungrouped icons and claims Concise headings, grouped facts, explicit conclusions Can locate the relevant claim without decoding symbols
Keyboard user selecting chart data Hover-only markers Keyboard-operable data controls or HTML table Can retrieve the same values without a pointer
Reader with images disabled Blank graphical navigation Functional text names and visible headings Can complete the page’s principal task

A useful acceptance test therefore starts with a task: compare two data series, follow a path, select an action, understand a mechanism, or evaluate an illustration. The implementation should be judged against task completion and information integrity, not merely the presence of an alt attribute.

3. Classification: Decorative, Informative, Functional, and Complex

The W3C WAI Images Tutorial organizes alternatives by an image’s purpose. Informative images convey information that needs to be expressed; decorative images do not add relevant information; functional images operate as buttons, links, or other controls; images of text need special attention; and complex images such as diagrams and graphs often require richer explanations than an alt attribute can reasonably hold. Groups of images and image maps introduce additional relationships and interactions.

A classification is contextual, not permanently attached to an asset in a media library. A company logo used as ornament on an awards page may be decorative if the organization’s name is already presented. The same logo by itself inside a home-page link must convey the destination. A photograph of a bridge could be purely atmospheric on a city landing page but substantive evidence in a civil-engineering case study. If a visual has two functions, such as identifying a product and linking to details, the accessible alternative must preserve both the identifying and functional information as appropriate.

Image category Primary authoring question Common approach Common error
Decorative Would removing the image change the task or meaning? alt="" for an HTML image; avoid unnecessary accessibility-tree entries Announcing decorative filenames or repeated adjectives
Informative What fact, object, or event is the reader meant to learn? Brief purpose-based alt Listing irrelevant surface features while omitting the fact
Functional What action does selecting the image perform? Accessible name of the button or link “Blue magnifying glass” instead of “Search”
Complex What relationships, values, or reasoning are essential? Brief label plus structured extended alternative Putting a dense dataset into a single unreadable alt value
Image of text Is rendered text itself the information? Prefer real text; when justified, provide the exact meaningful text Relying on tiny unscalable letters inside a bitmap
Group Does one image explain the set, or does each add evidence? Group-level and/or item-level alternatives as appropriate Six duplicates announcing “chart” or hiding unique information

The WAI alt Decision Tree is a reliable editorial starting point. It is not a substitute for analyzing unusual scientific figures, legal exhibits, culturally significant images, or complex interactive visualizations with their subject experts.

4. WCAG 2.2: Relevant Requirements and Their Boundaries

WCAG 2.2 is the principal normative reference for this article. Success Criterion 1.1.1 Non-text Content, Level A, requires a text alternative for nontext content serving an equivalent purpose, with special provisions for controls, time-based media, tests, sensory experiences, CAPTCHAs, and purely decorative or formatting content. Importantly, not every relevant requirement is in 1.1.1. Graphical controls, color-only encoding, visual text, data structure, pointer use, and content resizing can trigger other criteria.

WCAG 2.2 criterion Level Visual-content implication
1.1.1 Non-text Content A Supply purposeful text alternatives; apply specified cases and exceptions correctly
1.3.1 Info and Relationships A Preserve meaningful structure and relationships programmatically or in text
1.3.3 Sensory Characteristics A Do not make instructions rely solely on shape, color, size, location, orientation, or sound
1.4.1 Use of Color A Color alone cannot be the only visual method of conveying information
1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum) AA Meet specified contrast requirements for text, including relevant images of text
1.4.5 Images of Text AA Prefer text to images of text where presentation is achievable with text, subject to exceptions
1.4.11 Non-text Contrast AA Meet relevant contrast requirements for essential graphical objects and UI component states
2.1.1 Keyboard A Interactive graphical content must be operable by keyboard, unless its underlying function requires path-dependent input
2.4.4 Link Purpose (In Context) A Linked image names must make the destination or purpose understandable in context
4.1.2 Name, Role, Value A Custom interactive graphics must expose correct names, roles, states, and values

A successful alternative to a chart is not an excuse to make its interactive controls impossible to focus. Likewise, a screen-reader-friendly table does not automatically fix red and green bars that are indistinguishable to users with some forms of color-vision difference. The applicable requirements are evaluated together. The table is an instructional map, not a complete reproduction of normative criteria or a legal conclusion.

Special cases need care. Under 1.1.1, a test whose validity would be defeated by giving its content as text, or a work primarily meant to create a specific sensory experience, is treated differently from an ordinary informative photograph. That does not authorize a meaningless label or permit unrelated controls to remain inaccessible. A CAPTCHA has its own specified alternative-modality requirements. Since a CAPTCHA can still create practical exclusion despite nominal alternatives, avoiding unnecessary challenge-response barriers remains a sound design choice.

5. The Meaning-First Authoring Workflow

Writers should compose the core explanation before writing an alternative attribute. Define the visual’s contribution: identify something, compare quantities, illustrate a relationship, establish evidence, show a procedure, or initiate an action. Record the intended claim and what would be misunderstood if the visual disappeared. Then determine whether the necessary information already exists in readable nearby text. If it does, avoid announcing a duplicate. If it does not, choose the least cumbersome representation capable of supporting the task.

For a chart, capture the chart title, source, time period, units, axes, scales, categories, series, exceptions, major trend, notable comparisons, and source dataset where available. For a procedure, capture the stages and their transitions. For a map, capture the meaning of location, proximity, and routing information rather than attempting a complete verbal inventory of pixels. A short label is the entry point; an extended explanation or data structure is the place for detail.

A reliable handoff has three layers: editorial content (what the visual means), technical implementation (how the alternative is attached and reached), and evaluation evidence (whether the result works with assistive technologies and for representative users). The person entering alt text may not know enough about the chart to interpret it; the subject expert may not know how the interface exposes a figure caption. Both roles must contribute.

Example acceptance statement: “The quarterly margin graphic has a concise accessible label; a text summary states that margin fell to 11.5% in Quarter 3 before recovering; a semantic table exposes each quarter’s value; and the source notes distinguish actuals from forecasts.” This is testable and avoids the vague requirement “provide accessible graphics.”

6. Writing Effective Short Alternatives

An alt value should convey an image’s purpose and essential information in its context, in language suitable for the audience. Short does not mean incomplete or deliberately generic; it means concise enough to be understood as an image’s immediate replacement. Avoid redundant prefaces such as “image of” when the user’s technology already announces the object type. For a technical diagram, a phrase like “Three-stage filtration system; detailed sequence follows” can orient the reader without forcing the entire explanation into the attribute.

Compare a photograph in a newspaper article titled “Floodwater reaches the courthouse steps.” An unhelpful alt says “city building”; an overlong alternative narrates weather, architectural decoration, and clothing unrelated to the story; a useful one might say “Floodwater covers the lower courthouse steps while pedestrians stand behind a temporary barrier.” The language should not assert causes, identities, or exact measurements the image does not support.

<figure>
  <img src="courthouse-flood.jpg"
       alt="Floodwater reaches the courthouse steps behind a temporary barrier">
  <figcaption>Flooding near the courthouse after overnight rainfall.</figcaption>
</figure>

Here the alternative identifies what is visually demonstrated; the caption supplies general narrative context. Editors should remove unnecessary repetition if the adjacent article already states precisely the same point. alt is not a hidden SEO field, a place to stack keywords, or an invitation to infer attributes of depicted people that are not relevant and verified.

7. Decorative and Redundant Images

A decorative image can supply atmosphere, visual rhythm, or emphasis without contributing unique content or interaction. For an ordinary HTML image, the standard way to mark it as decorative is an empty alt value (alt=""), not an omitted attribute. Omitting alt can cause screen readers to fall back to a filename or URL, depending on implementation. A meaningless name such as “decorative divider” needlessly interrupts reading. When a purely decorative shape is delivered through CSS, it may not need an accessibility-tree representation at all.

<section aria-labelledby="funding-title">
  <img src="abstract-wave.svg" alt="">
  <h2 id="funding-title">Funding opportunities</h2>
  <p>Review grants, eligibility, and application deadlines.</p>
</section>

The image could be removed without loss of the section’s meaning. However, a badge showing “Approved” beside an otherwise unlabeled application is not decorative; it communicates status. A green checkmark inside a button with the visible label “Submit application” may be redundant if the control already has a complete name, but the same checkmark used alone to indicate submission success needs a textual status.

Do not assume that every background image is decorative. A CSS background may carry a warning, chart, or instructional illustration. If important meaning lives only in the CSS image, it must also be expressed in accessible content. The implementation should move meaning into the document or provide a corresponding text presentation, not simply rely on the same invisible background image becoming perceivable through assistive technology.

8. Informative Photographs, Historical Images, and Visual Evidence

An informative photograph does not need a mechanical inventory of all visible objects. Its alternative should tell the reader why the photograph matters in that context. In a biography, a portrait may identify an individual. In a construction report, the same framing may demonstrate a cracked foundation, damaged equipment, or a safety condition. In a history textbook, a photograph may require a description of visible banners, objects, attire, or spatial arrangement to support interpretation. Authorship and date belong in a caption or citation when those claims come from source records rather than the visible pixels.

A visual description can accidentally transform uncertainty into fact. “Workers protest a government decree” asserts motive and historical context; the pixels may only show a crowd holding placards. If contextual evidence establishes the event, put it in the caption or surrounding prose and attribute it properly. The alt text can say what is visible: “Marchers carrying placards walk past a civic building.” A photograph may also depict sensitive information, such as a license plate or medical screen; the decision about what to include in an alternative should follow the same privacy and editorial rules that govern the visible publication.

Editorial test: read the surrounding paragraph with the image removed. If the article’s conclusion still makes sense and the photograph serves only illustration, a short alternative is enough, and in some contexts the image may be redundant. If the argument relies on something the photograph proves or shows, the alternative must preserve that evidentiary role. This is a content-accuracy question as much as an accessibility question.

9. Functional Images: Buttons, Links, and Controls

When an image does something, its alternative should communicate the action or destination, not merely describe appearance. A search icon operating a search button should be named “Search,” not “Magnifying glass.” A download icon linked to a report should communicate the download, file identity, or both as needed. The most robust pattern often includes visible text next to an icon, enabling users of speech input and magnification to find the same labeled action.

<button type="button" aria-label="Search courses">
  <img src="search.svg" alt="">
</button>
<a href="/library/encyclopedia/computer-science/">
  <img src="mgu-cs-mark.svg" alt="Computer Science encyclopedia home">
</a>

In the first example the button receives its accessible name from aria-label, so the nested image is decorative. In the second the linked image alone names the destination. These examples demonstrate labeling, not complete production styling or search-function JavaScript. Avoid supplying both a full accessible name on the parent and a second, redundant informative name on its child unless the combined name has a deliberate purpose.

Functional diagrams are more demanding than functional icons. A selectable map with ten hotspots requires accessible controls for each destination. A chart that opens a drill-down panel should expose which series or point is selected and how the panel can be reached. Making the outer image identifiable is not sufficient if the action it contains remains available only to pointer users.

10. Logos, Identity, and Image-Only Links

Logos are a frequent source of unnecessary repetition and ambiguous navigation. If a recognizable brand mark appears beside the same name in visible text and both are part of the same link, the image can often have empty alt text because the link text already supplies the name. If the logo is the link’s only content, it needs meaningful text. Sometimes the destination must be included, as when a company mark links to the main site from a different organizational microsite.

<a href="/" aria-label="Malone Global University home">
  <img src="mgu-logo.svg" alt="">
</a>

<a href="/about">
  <img src="mgu-logo.svg" alt="">
  <span>About Malone Global University</span>
</a>

The first uses an explicit link name; the second relies on visible text and suppresses a redundant announcement from the image. A heading consisting entirely of a logo image is less adaptable than visible text and should be questioned where the visual design allows a semantic heading. A trademark or identity mark may be artistically protected, but that does not remove the obligation to expose the relevant name to users.

11. Groups, Galleries, and Image Sequences

Some sets of images communicate a combined idea, while others contribute independent facts. A five-star rating graphic can be expressed once as “Rated 4 out of 5” rather than five separately announced stars. A collage of three archaeological artifacts may require each object to be identified, plus a caption stating why the set is being compared. A series of before-and-after images may need an explicit statement about which features changed, not only labels “before” and “after.”

<figure>
  <img src="roof-before.jpg" alt="Roof before repairs, with a gap beside the vent">
  <img src="roof-after.jpg" alt="Roof after repairs, with the gap sealed">
  <figcaption>Roof repair comparison, photographed from the same viewpoint.</figcaption>
</figure>

This figure deliberately provides two distinct alternatives because each image contains evidence. Where a group works only as one visual symbol, the group may have one meaningful label and decorative child images instead. The correct decision depends on whether independent examination is part of the reading task. Collections should also retain a sensible order, grouping labels, and predictable navigation; adding alt text to every thumbnail cannot fix a gallery with inaccessible controls or unexplained image order.

12. Images of Text, Posters, and Scanned Labels

Text rendered as pixels is much less adaptable than real text. It may become blurred when enlarged, resist user-selected fonts and spacing, become invisible when images fail, and remain unavailable to screen readers unless an accurate alternative is provided. WCAG 2.2 criterion 1.4.5 generally requires actual text rather than images of text when the desired presentation can be achieved with text, subject to its exceptions, including essential presentation and user-customizable cases.

An academic poster with headings, charts, and conclusions is not made accessible by an alt text reading “Research poster.” The better publishing model includes an HTML version with semantic headings, readable prose, described figures, and accessible datasets; the visual poster can remain a supplemental format. A photographed historic sign may require preserving the wording as part of the source evidence, while its visual character and material condition might also be relevant. A scanned label on a product photo can be transcribed in prose where the label content affects the reader’s decision.

For a simple text logo, the accessible name generally conveys the words or identity. For a complex equation, merely copying a screenshot’s characters into alt text may lose mathematical grouping, exponent relationships, or reading order; see the separate treatment of mathematical content below. Where the text image is essential, preserve the user’s ability to obtain the message through another route, rather than assuming the visual exception removes all alternative-content requirements.

13. Complex Visuals: Short Labels, Long Descriptions, and Structured Alternatives

A complex image normally requires more than a brief alt string. The purpose of the short alternative is to identify the figure, announce its topic, and direct attention to a detailed explanation. The longer alternative may consist of paragraphs, a table, an ordered list, a decision tree, or a combination. This is consistent with W3C’s Complex Images guidance, which presents several ways of providing descriptions and data that cannot fit comfortably into a concise label.

<figure>
  <img src="quarterly-enrollment.png"
       alt="Quarterly enrollment chart; summary and exact values follow">
  <figcaption>Enrollment grew over four quarters, with a pause in Quarter 3.</figcaption>
</figure>
<p id="enrollment-description">
  Enrollment rose from 820 in Quarter 1 to 950 in Quarter 2,
  remained at 950 in Quarter 3, and reached 1,080 in Quarter 4.
</p>
<p><a href="#enrollment-table">Read the exact enrollment figures</a>.</p>

The example places the summary directly in normal reading order, so all users can inspect it; a linked data table would follow at the referenced anchor in a full implementation. Avoid relying only on an inaccessible hover tooltip, a very long title attribute, or a hidden panel that cannot be operated. The HTML longdesc attribute is not a universal substitute for visible, reliable navigation to the explanation. A visible “Data and description” link offers users a predictable choice and supports ordinary browsing.

14. Statistical Charts: Findings, Scales, and Exact Values

A chart’s accessible alternative should preserve what is measured, how it is measured, the comparisons made, and the relevant conclusion. A bar chart of enrollment by program might warrant a concise description identifying the highest and lowest categories, together with an accessible table giving all values. A time-series plot may require the direction of a trend, a turning point, a material anomaly, and important qualifications. A map-based heat scale may need a text classification by region or a geographic table. An author should not select “chart type” as the whole alternative.

Graphs can mislead when units or scales are omitted. “Revenue doubled” means little without the time interval, currency, and starting value. A log-scale chart must identify that scale; otherwise an alternative that describes equal visual intervals as equal absolute changes is wrong. A dual-axis chart needs careful explanation of which variables belong to which axis. If a dataset contains missing, estimated, or suppressed values, the alternative should not silently invent or interpolate data.

Chart type Minimum meaningful text Often necessary deeper alternative
Bar chart Categories, measure, important comparison Exact values by category in table form
Line chart Time span, units, trend and turning points Time-indexed values; uncertainty and missing data
Pie or donut Meaning of portions, dominant shares Labeled category and percentage table; explain rounding
Scatterplot Variables and main relationship Data points or grouped summaries; outliers, model caveats
Histogram Variable and bin definition Bin limits, counts, distribution shape
Heatmap Dimensions and intensity meaning Accessible matrix, thresholds, exceptions
Box plot Groups and principal distribution statistics Median, quartiles, whisker definitions, outliers
Network diagram Nodes and link meaning Adjacency or relationship list and interpretation

A text alternative should support the claim being made and appropriate independent examination. It should not assert statistical significance merely because two lines appear different. Scientific inference belongs in the interpretation, with evidence and uncertainty reported separately.

15. Data Tables as a Companion to Visualizations

A semantic HTML table is often the most useful complement to a graph because it preserves exact values for users of screen readers, braille, magnification, copy/paste, and ordinary reference. It is not necessary to replace every chart with a table when the data are already stated elsewhere in an accessible, inspectable form, but high-stakes analytics should not require users to infer numbers from visual geometry alone.

<table>
  <caption>Illustrative annual program enrollment</caption>
  <thead>
    <tr><th scope="col">Year</th><th scope="col">Program A</th><th scope="col">Program B</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><th scope="row">2023</th><td>420</td><td>360</td></tr>
    <tr><th scope="row">2024</th><td>470</td><td>390</td></tr>
    <tr><th scope="row">2025</th><td>510</td><td>445</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

This example uses a caption, column headers, and year row headers. Production datasets may require separate units, a table summary outside the table, irregular-header markup, downloadable data, and source and update notes. Do not replace true tabular values with a large string of numbers in an aria-label; doing so discards the navigation advantages of a table. For large datasets, make sure users can reach, filter, and export the same records available through the visual chart, with corresponding keyboard and focus behavior.

16. Reporting Trends, Uncertainty, and Data Provenance

Charts are interpretations, not neutral pictures of a dataset. An accessible description must preserve assumptions that would matter to a careful reader: whether an axis begins at zero, whether a scale is logarithmic, whether values were adjusted for inflation, what is forecast versus observed, whether error intervals appear, and whether totals reconcile to the source. A chart can be rendered correctly in code yet mislead both sighted and nonvisual readers through an inaccurate textual summary.

Imagine two trend lines with overlapping uncertainty intervals. A summary reading “Treatment A is significantly better” would be unsupported by the visual alone; a safer description would identify the plotted estimates and that their uncertainty intervals overlap, then refer to the statistical analysis for the strength of any inference. This distinction is especially important where readers may use the accessible summary without consulting the graphic. An alternative should neither exaggerate a finding nor disguise uncertainty merely to be concise.

For dashboards that update automatically, treat the alternative as part of the same data pipeline as the chart. If the chart’s values change on refresh but its caption remains static, the alternative becomes stale. Store a versioned description or derive bounded facts from the same validated dataset, then have a subject expert review the language. Where analytical conclusions cannot safely be generated, provide the data and a carefully limited description of what can be stated without interpretation.

17. Process Diagrams, Flowcharts, and Decision Trees

A process diagram typically communicates sequence, branches, loops, dependencies, and exception handling. A useful text alternative must preserve those relationships rather than merely list visible labels. The phrase “Process from application to approval” says nothing about what triggers rejection, which steps can repeat, or who makes the decision. An ordered list with explicit conditions often supplies a much stronger equivalent.

Illustrative procurement workflow, written as text rather than solely shown as arrows:

  1. The requester submits a purchase justification and estimated value.
  2. The department manager checks the business purpose. If information is missing, the request returns to the requester for revision.
  3. Approved justifications move to purchasing for vendor and contract review.
  4. Purchases above the institutional threshold require a separate financial approval; those below it proceed without this extra gate.
  5. If all required approvals are present, the order is issued. If a review fails, the requester receives the reason and correction route.

A flowchart’s legend, arrow direction, gateways, and exception paths should either be described or transformed into the text structure. A small linear diagram may be sufficiently conveyed by one sentence. A complex system architecture may need a list of components followed by a connection table. When the diagram itself is used to operate a workflow, the alternative must also preserve available actions, not only the abstract logic.

<figure>
  <img src="approval-flow.svg"
       alt="Procurement approval flow; the full decision sequence follows">
  <figcaption>Manager review precedes purchasing review; high-value purchases require additional approval.</figcaption>
</figure>
<ol>
  <li>Requester submits justification.</li>
  <li>Manager approves or returns it for revision.</li>
  <li>Purchasing reviews the approved request.</li>
  <li>High-value requests receive financial approval before order issuance.</li>
</ol>

The example is deliberately simplified; in a real system, the list and image must agree about every material decision branch. Editorial ownership belongs to the subject expert who can validate the process, not only the graphic designer.

18. Maps, Location Data, and Spatial Relationships

Maps communicate positions, boundaries, distance, orientation, concentration, and routes. Not all of these need a word-by-word translation. The appropriate alternative is driven by the reader’s actual decision: choosing a nearby facility, comparing region-level outcomes, finding a campus building, or understanding a historical frontier. A static labeled map may be complemented by a list of places and their relationships. An interactive locator may require a searchable list containing the same locations, addresses, categories, and essential action links.

A campus map used for wayfinding should not offer only “map of campus.” It might supply building names, street access, accessible entrances, an ordered route between relevant points, and constraints such as closed entrances or elevators. A choropleth map comparing regional unemployment rates may be best accompanied by an accessible table of each region and rate plus a short summary of the geographic pattern. Geographic relationships matter: saying “the three highest values occur in the northern districts” may be more informative than supplying only the ranked values.

Maps should not be treated as automatically accessible because a geographic provider supplies zoom buttons or a screen-reader label on the map container. Pin selection, keyboard movement, announcements, coordinate changes, legend meaning, and alternate list access need separate evaluation. If an exact route depends on live geography or current conditions, the alternative must not invent travel times, paths, or accessibility features. Preserve a clear difference between static educational cartography and live navigational services.

19. Organizational Charts and Relationship Graphs

Organizational charts represent reporting lines, divisions, positions, and sometimes temporary or matrix relationships. A flat list of person names fails to show who reports to whom. A useful alternative may begin with a hierarchy: executive director; three directly reporting divisions; each division’s departments. When several people report jointly to two leaders, a hierarchical outline may be insufficient, so include a relationship table with explicit fields such as position, reports to, coordinates with, and office.

Position Reports to Functional responsibility
Executive director Governing board Institutional leadership
Academic director Executive director Teaching and academic quality
Technology director Executive director Infrastructure and digital services
Accessibility coordinator Academic director Academic access requirements; consults Technology director

The final row illustrates why prose may be necessary alongside the tree: consultation is not the same as a reporting relationship. Network diagrams pose similar challenges. An accessible representation can name nodes, define the type of edge, and present an adjacency list or connection matrix. Where the exact network is large, offer filters, search, and exportable records. A complete recitation of hundreds of connections is rarely an effective immediate alternative, but hiding the relationships entirely is not acceptable when users need to analyze them.

20. Scientific and Engineering Figures

Scientific figures commonly contain multiple panels, markers, equations, legends, error bars, annotations, and domain-specific conventions. A description should tell the reader the experiment or phenomenon, units, conditions, measured quantities, and main observation. More detailed alternatives may expose numeric values and methodology. The figure caption should identify panels and sources; the text alternative should make the visual evidence available to readers who cannot see it.

In a materials-testing graph, the shape of a stress-strain curve may be essential: linear region, yield point, peak stress, and fracture region. A description that says “line graph of force versus extension” omits the relevant engineering result. In a microscopy image, observed cell patterns might matter, but precise diagnosis cannot be inferred unless supported by the accompanying analysis. For an electrical schematic, the topology and component connections are far more important than the physical placement of boxes on the page.

Scientific figure handoff record: include figure number and title; description of each panel; variables, units, and scales; visual encodings; key observations; underlying numerical data where possible; uncertainty; and an accountable reviewer who understands the disciplinary content. For an image of a mathematical formula, provide accessible mathematical notation in a format supported by the target publication and reading tools, not merely a picture captioned “formula.” This article addresses the representation principle; specialist mathematical semantics belong to accessible math and document-production resources.

21. Infographics, Timelines, and Multi-Panel Posters

Infographics combine content structure with visual storytelling. They may communicate facts through placement, typography, pictograms, arrows, size, and color. Rewriting every label as one long alt string produces a difficult listening experience and hides the intended section order. A stronger solution reconstructs the information as normal document content: a meaningful title, ordered headings, short paragraphs, lists or tables, and an accessible summary of the infographic’s key message.

For a historical timeline, preserve dated events in chronological order and state which events overlap or precede others. For a policy poster, separate its headline claim, evidence, recommendations, contact information, and citations. For a comparison infographic, use a table if categories align or paired subsections when the comparison is qualitative. If the original graphic is retained, its brief alternative can point to this structural equivalent.

When a poster is also published as a PDF, the accompanying HTML explanation does not automatically make the PDF file itself accessible. Readers who need to download or annotate the document may encounter a distinct structure and tagging problem. The Accessible Documents and Electronic Publications companion addresses document-format accessibility. The web article must avoid claiming that a supplementary HTML transcript cures every inaccessible downstream format.

22. Mathematical Expressions and Scientific Notation in Images

Equations, chemical structures, and technical notation cannot always be faithfully conveyed by a short natural-language substitute. For a simple expression, readable text may suffice; for a more complex one, mathematical markup, a structured verbal description, and an explanation of variables may be needed. A screenshot of an equation with alt="equation" is insufficient. A string such as “x slash a plus b” can be ambiguous about grouping unless parentheses and operators are clarified.

<p>The area of a circle is given by:</p>
<math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML">
  <mi>A</mi><mo>=</mo><mi>π</mi><msup><mi>r</mi><mn>2</mn></msup>
</math>
<p>Here <var>r</var> is the radius, and <var>A</var> is the area.</p>

MathML provides machine-readable mathematical structure; actual speech and braille presentation vary by browser and assistive technology and should be tested. When a discipline depends on precise symbolic manipulations, provide text explanations of notation and relationships rather than assuming a visual rendering alone is enough. Chemical reaction schemes, circuit notation, and music notation may require specialized semantic representations beyond the scope of a generic image alternative. Preserve the exact technical meaning and consult subject specialists.

23. Accessible Static SVG and Vector Graphics

SVG supports scalable graphics and, when used inline, can expose titles and other markup in the document tree. It does not guarantee accessibility simply because its shapes are resolution independent. A static SVG intended as a single figure should have an appropriate accessible image name and, where useful, a visible associated description. Decorative vector art can be hidden from assistive technologies if it carries no independent meaning.

<figure>
  <svg viewBox="0 0 200 80" role="img"
       aria-labelledby="sales-svg-title">
    <title id="sales-svg-title">Illustrative revenue rises from 40 to 65 units</title>
    <rect x="25" y="38" width="45" height="32" fill="currentColor"/>
    <rect x="115" y="18" width="45" height="52" fill="currentColor"/>
  </svg>
  <figcaption>Illustrative two-period revenue comparison. Exact data are 40 and 65 units.</figcaption>
</figure>

This shows the labeling approach rather than a publication-grade chart; a real chart needs meaningful visual axes, data validation, and all applicable contrast requirements. The SVG <title> can provide a concise name, and <desc> may supply additional information, but support for particular SVG description techniques differs among browser and assistive-technology combinations. Avoid duplicating the same text through several mechanisms so that it is announced repeatedly. A visible HTML explanation is a robust default for complex material.

If the SVG is used through an ordinary HTML <img> element, the HTML alt attribute is the typical accessible labeling mechanism for that embedded image. If the SVG is inline and contains separately interactive regions, the situation changes: those regions need suitable roles, labels, focusability, and keyboard behavior rather than one label for the entire drawing.

24. Interactive SVG: Focusable Regions, States, and Actions

An interactive vector chart may allow users to select bars, inspect data points, toggle series, or reveal details. Calling the entire SVG a single role="img" object may conceal the separate interaction semantics of its descendants. The better architecture may be a noninteractive SVG paired with normal HTML controls and a data table, especially when the chart library does not expose each target predictably. If the graphic itself must be interactive, treat every actionable region as a genuine control with keyboard handling, focus indication, accessible naming, and synchronized state.

The underlying data and interaction model should be separable from its drawing engine. A keyboard user who chooses “Quarter 2” through a native button should receive the same detail as a pointer user who clicks the visual bar. Programmatic announcements should describe the selected category, value, and any expanded detail without overwhelming the user with every data point. Navigation should identify a stable order and explain how to move between points if a custom composite interaction is necessary.

Implementation caution: browser and screen-reader exposure of SVG child nodes is not uniform, and attaching a tabindex to an arbitrary <rect> does not automatically supply a button role or keyboard click semantics. Teams should choose established, tested HTML control patterns where possible. The ARIA components article owns broader composite-widget design, and Keyboard Access and Focus Management owns focus mechanics.

25. Canvas, Rasterization, and Visually Rendered Applications

The HTML <canvas> element is a rendering surface, not a substitute for a semantic document. A chart drawn onto canvas may expose pixels without meaningful underlying categories or values. Canvas content can be paired with accessible HTML alternatives, including a labeled table and controls. In some circumstances authors can provide suitable fallback content inside the canvas element, but fallback content must be evaluated in the intended browser and assistive-technology configurations; do not presume that anything placed inside a canvas will automatically behave like an ordinary visible interface.

<figure>
  <canvas id="orders-chart" width="480" height="240"
          role="img" aria-label="Quarterly orders; data table follows">
    <p>Quarterly order counts are listed in the table immediately after this chart.</p>
  </canvas>
  <figcaption>Illustrative quarterly orders. Exact data appear below.</figcaption>
</figure>

The code is a conceptual fragment. The ordinary HTML data table referenced by the label would need to exist immediately afterward, and a real drawing routine would need to render the image. For keyboard-operated diagrams, canvas fallback text alone cannot replace independent access to controls or hit regions. An alternative control panel or semantic HTML layer may be necessary. The correct benchmark is whether the same decisions and actions remain possible, not whether the canvas can be announced as one image.

26. Charting Libraries, Dashboards, and Data-Driven Graphics

Modern charting libraries can create SVG, canvas, or combinations of HTML and graphics. Their accessibility behavior varies with versions and configuration. Some automatically label series and axes; others require explicit authorship; still others offer keyboard point navigation that must be enabled. No general-purpose library should be described as wholly accessible or inaccessible solely from its rendering technology. Inspect the actual output and supported interaction model.

For an institutional dashboard, define an accessible chart contract before selecting a library: a meaningful title, summary, exact data access, clear units and scales, keyboard access to needed interactive features, exposed filter names and state, usable focus behavior, visible non-color distinctions, and consistent results across displays. Where users can change chart data by year, region, or program, the alternative table and summary must update to reflect the same selection. A stale text alternative is a data integrity defect.

Data-heavy dashboards often need progressive disclosure. Readers may first encounter a brief accessible summary, then choose a table, search among categories, or inspect a specific series. Dumping hundreds of values into a live region after every filter change is not accessible. Keep announcements concise, make the complete values reachable, and let the user control the degree of detail. Publication review should include keyboard, screen-reader, zoom, and no-color tests on the actual application, not only documentation about the charting package.

27. Image Maps, Hotspots, and Clickable Regions

An image map divides an image into several separately selectable regions. Each region is a distinct action and therefore needs its own accessible name and purpose. The WAI Image Maps tutorial provides guidance for client-side image maps. The enclosing graphic can orient users to the subject, while the <area> elements must communicate the destinations of hotspots. Complex map geometry can be difficult to use at high magnification or by touch; an equivalent textual list can be far more practical.

<img src="campus-zones.png" usemap="#campus-zones"
     alt="Campus zones; each selectable destination is also listed below">
<map name="campus-zones">
  <area shape="rect" coords="10,10,130,80"
        href="/campus/library" alt="Campus library">
  <area shape="rect" coords="145,10,270,80"
        href="/campus/student-services" alt="Student services office">
</map>
<ul>
  <li><a href="/campus/library">Campus library</a></li>
  <li><a href="/campus/student-services">Student services office</a></li>
</ul>

The labels are illustrative and the hotspots require real geometry appropriate to the deployed map. Redundant links are intentional as an alternate route, but keyboard users should not be forced through an excessively duplicated navigation sequence where a simpler implementation is possible. The list may be the primary accessible interaction, with the map as a supplemental visual aid. Any zoom behavior, responsive resizing, or coordinate transformation must preserve the alignment between target regions and visible locations.

28. Icons, Status Indicators, and Visual Design Systems

A scalable icon system should define the semantic role of each icon in the design system. Icons may be decorative beside explicit text, function as controls, indicate status, or provide part of an instruction. Their meaning may change by context. A star inside a “Save” button is not an adequate control name; a warning triangle beside text reading “Deadline missed” need not be announced separately; and a colored circle indicating payment status must have a textual equivalent for the state.

Design-token governance should cover not only sizes and colors but also the accessible names and placement rules of reusable components. A component named IconButton should require a meaningful label or visible text at its point of use. A StatusBadge should generate or accept meaningful status text and not depend exclusively on hue or outline. A ChartLegend should present categories with clear labels and, when necessary, patterns or direct annotations.

<p><span aria-hidden="true">●</span> <strong>Payment pending</strong></p>
<button type="button" aria-label="Remove item from saved list">
  <svg aria-hidden="true" focusable="false" viewBox="0 0 24 24">
    <path d="M4 12h16" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"/>
  </svg>
</button>

These examples illustrate a text-present status and a labeled icon button. In real production, the second button must be connected to its intended action and have a visible focus indicator. Speech-input users may benefit from a visible label matching the accessible name. Consistency matters: if an icon means “expand” on one screen but “open another page” on the next, users cannot form reliable expectations.

29. CSS Images, Pseudo-Elements, and Generated Content

CSS background-image, ::before, and ::after are useful for decoration, but they are not reliable channels for essential information. A warning inserted only as a CSS exclamation mark may disappear when styles are disabled, may be interpreted inconsistently by assistive technologies, and may be missed by users who cannot distinguish its color. The semantic content should carry the warning; the visual decoration can reinforce it.

<p class="warning"><strong>Deadline passed:</strong> This application is closed.</p>

.warning::before {
  content: "⚠";
  margin-inline-end: 0.35em;
}

Here the visible message is complete without the symbol. Because some assistive technology may announce CSS-generated content, avoid designs whose semantics require pseudo-elements to be read exactly once. Evaluate how decorative symbols are exposed and simplify them where possible. Background maps, instructional diagrams, and alerts should not be embedded in stylesheets as their only substantive presentation.

A similar problem appears when designers place important text inside images loaded by CSS media queries. At narrow widths, one background may be replaced by another, silently changing what the page communicates. If that content is essential, the accessible text must remain synchronized across responsive states.

30. Automated Alt-Text Generation, OCR, and Human Review

Computer vision systems and language models can suggest descriptions, detect objects, extract some printed words, or create draft captions. They can accelerate inventory triage, but their output is not authoritative. A model may misidentify a place, invent a relationship between people, miss a chart’s units, transpose a number, omit an important exception, or describe details irrelevant to the publication’s purpose. Optical character recognition can extract wording from a poster yet lose layout, reading order, or the distinction between a heading and a footnote.

A safe workflow treats automated descriptions as drafts requiring context-aware review, not as direct replacements for subject expertise. An editor should verify names, numerical claims, chronology, specialized symbols, and any sensitive inference. For a complex chart, programmatic access to its source dataset is usually more reliable than reading values from pixels. For a scientific diagram, a domain expert should validate the relationships and terminology. Where the graphic is decorative, automatic description may actually harm access by cluttering the reading experience.

Automated output Typical risk Appropriate review action
“Two people meeting in an office” Misses that one person is giving safety instructions Compare against article purpose and source captions
“Revenue rises steadily” Ignores a downturn or incorrect axis Review validated data and chart scale
OCR of an infographic Mixes headings, figures, and footnotes Rebuild semantic reading order
AI-generated map description Invents distance, route, or accessibility features Confirm against map data and location sources
Generated icon label Names appearance instead of function Check control’s actual action and visible label
Generated formula text Loses exponents, grouping, or symbols Verify against the source expression with a subject specialist

Automation is most useful when it produces a candidate that a responsible author can correct. An operational system should record who reviewed the alternative, which source version it describes, and whether subsequent asset changes require renewal.

31. Multilingual Content and Cultural Interpretation

Visual alternatives are language-dependent content. A page available in several languages should not silently announce English alt text inside an otherwise translated interface when a natural equivalent can be supplied. The alternative should preserve local terminology, date formats, units, and culturally specific references where these matter to comprehension. Translating a chart title while leaving its legend categories untranslated can split the meaning across languages.

Cultural images require additional editorial care. Descriptions of historic dress, ceremonies, religious objects, national symbols, or artistic representations should distinguish visible features from interpretation, use respectful naming supported by reliable sources, and avoid assuming that one audience already understands the symbolism. An image caption may appropriately contain attributed historical interpretation; the alt text can preserve what is visibly represented. Neither should invent provenance or identity.

For an interactive multilingual chart, localized labels, accessible names, descriptions, and data headers must all update together. Test the actual language configuration and text direction. The image’s embedded words may be fixed in one language; when that is important for the task, supply an accessible translation and identify whether it is a translation or a direct transcription.

32. Responsive Visuals, Magnification, and Nontext Contrast

A graphic may satisfy a text-alternative requirement yet become unusable visually when enlarged, viewed at high zoom, displayed under forced-color settings, or reduced to a narrow viewport. Chart labels may overlap, legends may disappear, and a map’s small icons may become untappable. Preserve user control over scaling, provide robust text and tabular routes, and avoid embedding essential small text into a low-resolution raster image where a scalable or textual presentation would work better.

WCAG 2.2 criterion 1.4.11 concerns nontext contrast for specified graphical objects and user-interface components. It is not a blanket requirement that every decorative pixel meet a 3:1 contrast ratio. For a chart, the visual features necessary to understand a data series must be distinguishable; in addition, color must not be the sole visual channel of information under 1.4.1. Patterns, direct labels, differences in shape, and explicit values can improve readability for a wider audience.

Responsive acceptance scenario: Open a multi-series chart at a desktop width and at a narrow viewport; enlarge the interface; switch to a high-contrast or forced-colors environment when supported; inspect whether labels and plotted series remain identifiable; use the alternative table without horizontal layout traps. The evaluation must consider both the actual graphic and the independent information path. A text summary should not be praised as an excuse to leave an otherwise usable visual needlessly poor.

33. Screen-Reader, Braille, and Nonvisual Evaluation

A screen reader can announce that an image has a name while leaving the reader unable to understand its meaning. Evaluation therefore has two stages: inspect the accessibility exposure of the figure, and then attempt the actual information task. Use the browser accessibility tree to inspect role and accessible name; inspect adjacent visible descriptions and semantic tables; then navigate through the page with a supported screen reader, using both ordinary reading and structural commands. A graph may be announced as an image followed by an unrelated table; if the connection is not clear, the user might never know the data exist.

Check the natural reading order. Are figure title and summary encountered at the right point? Can users navigate table headers to compare values? Is a link named “View full data” specific enough to identify its target? If a graphic is updated through filters, does the alternative update without unexpectedly moving focus? Where a visual has selectable regions, can each region be reached by keyboard and identified by name and current state? Does an announcement convey the selected value rather than simply saying “expanded”?

Braille output reinforces the need for real text. A description created as an audio clip may not serve readers who need a braille-accessible equivalent. A well-structured HTML table may support systematic examination of figures, but unusually wide tables can be cumbersome on small braille displays, requiring an alternative list or downloadable data. Nonvisual evaluation is best conducted with representative tasks and, where possible, people who use these modalities regularly. A successful automated accessibility check is only one piece of evidence.

34. Visual Readability and Interoperable Alternatives

Some readers depend on an enlarged visual rather than a spoken alternative. For them, the best implementation may combine a scalable figure, readable labels, generous separation, color-independent encodings, and nearby prose. Direct labels are often easier than a distant legend that forces a reader to repeatedly move across a magnified screen. A chart’s text description should not be reserved for screen-reader-only users; it can assist readers who process a complex graphic more effectively in prose.

Visual and nonvisual alternatives should be mutually consistent. A chart depicting four series should not have a table showing only two without saying so. A map’s legend that marks a building closed must match the accessible list of facilities. If the graphic is translated or updated, the alternatives require the same review. In a high-stakes setting, discrepancies between the visual and textual representations constitute substantive content defects even if each representation is independently well formatted.

A strong design also permits multiple forms to coexist without forcing one method. Some readers will use the graphic for rapid comparison, then consult a table for precision. Others will first read the summary, then examine individual items. This is not redundant duplication when each representation supports a distinct legitimate task. The editorial burden is to keep them synchronized and clearly connected.

35. Testing Methods and Acceptance Evidence

Accessibility assessment should combine authoring review, automated detection, semantic inspection, assistive-technology use, and task-level testing. Automated scanners can find images without alt, some missing names, and certain contrast problems. They generally cannot determine whether a description accurately conveys the conclusion of a graph, whether a photograph’s historical label is truthful, or whether an alternative covers every meaningful branch of a decision diagram. Those require human judgment and domain knowledge.

Testing documentation should record the figure identifier, source revision, intended task, findings, test configuration, person responsible, remediation outcome, and any unresolved limitation. A change to the data must trigger review even when the rendering markup itself is unchanged.

36. Editorial Operations, Content Management, and Release Governance

An organization publishing many visual assets needs a repeatable process. At acquisition or creation, record the image’s purpose, rights and source, where it will be used, who owns the domain meaning, and whether it requires an alternative description or structured dataset. Do not store a single immutable alt text in a shared media library as though that wording will fit every usage. The library can store descriptions and metadata as candidates; the final alternative belongs to the context of the individual article or component.

During production, assign a content author, subject reviewer, implementation owner, and accessibility evaluator where the asset’s significance warrants those roles. Ordinary decorative icons can be handled through design-system rules; a research figure, map used for navigation, or life-critical operational dashboard merits stronger review. Treat data provenance, chart revision, caption changes, and translated labels as synchronized artifacts in the publishing workflow.

At release, require an information-equivalence check: list what an informed sighted reader can learn or do, verify how a nonvisual reader can obtain the essential information and actions, and document differences. A passing technical scan does not by itself close the review. Post-publication, monitor edits to source data, new chart interactions, localization, and templates that may alter the accessibility tree. These are ordinary maintenance obligations, not one-time special projects.

37. Worked Scenarios I: A University Enrollment Dashboard and a Financial Report

Scenario 1 — Enrollment by faculty. A university publishes a stacked bar chart with five faculties and four academic years. The original alt text says “Enrollment data,” which identifies neither units nor trends. The team first creates a summary noting that total enrollment has increased and identifying the faculty with the largest change. It then publishes a table containing enrollment by faculty and year, with row and column headers, totals, and a data-source date. If a filter changes academic years, the same dataset updates the visual and table; the summary either updates after validated calculation or remains neutral about trends it cannot establish. Acceptance evidence: a nonvisual reader identifies the largest year-over-year increase and retrieves an exact faculty total without guessing from bar sizes.

Scenario 2 — Financial performance chart. A corporate report displays quarterly revenue and operating margin in a two-axis graph. A draft alternative says “Revenue and profitability improved,” even though margin fell in the second quarter. The subject reviewer separates the two measures, specifies currency and percentages, documents the dual-axis convention, and provides a table. The revised summary reports revenue growth and the temporary margin decline without implying causal relationships. Acceptance evidence: readers can distinguish the two axes, state the margin low point, and identify which figures are audited actuals versus forecasts. A visually dramatic slope is not treated as a substitute for numerical context.

These examples illustrate a common governance rule: an alternative must be materially faithful, even when the underlying chart was originally designed to emphasize a particular conclusion. Accessibility is not a marketing rewrite of evidence.

38. Worked Scenarios II: A Public-Service Map and a Safety Flowchart

Scenario 3 — Public-service locations. A regional website uses an interactive map to show service centers, but the pins have no names and selection depends on a mouse. An accessible alternative offers a searchable list of centers with names, locations, service categories, and links to verified directions and hours; it also explains the map’s geographic pattern at a high level. Each filter changes both representations. Acceptance evidence: a keyboard and screen-reader user can locate a center providing the specified service and reach its detail page, without interacting with map geometry. No opening times or accessible entrances are invented where source data are absent.

Scenario 4 — Emergency procedure diagram. An industrial training manual depicts an alarm-handling flow with orange diamonds and thick arrows. The graphic’s original alternative says “Emergency response process,” omitting the central decision. The team provides an ordered set of steps: recognize the alarm, check the documented safety condition, follow the designated shutdown procedure when its specified criteria are met, and escalate according to the approved operational protocol. The textual steps are reviewed against the authoritative manual; the graphic is not treated as a source for inventing procedural advice. Acceptance evidence: a learner can explain the branch and escalation route after reading the structured alternative. Any operational procedure must be approved by the responsible safety authority.

These two tasks show why information versus interaction is an important distinction. The training diagram primarily communicates an approved sequence; the service locator also initiates actions. Their accessible alternatives need not look alike.

39. Worked Scenarios III: A Historical Photo Essay and a Scientific Figure

Scenario 5 — Historical photographs. An encyclopedia chapter presents three images of a city street in different decades. The initial alt text repeats “Historic street photograph” three times. The historian identifies what can be visually observed in each photograph: paved versus unpaved roads, changes in transport, and distinct architectural features. Captions separately supply verified dates and attribution. A short comparative paragraph explains the change over time without assigning undocumented causes. Acceptance evidence: a reader can compare the visible street features and distinguish observation from historical interpretation. The final descriptions avoid guessing personal identities or affiliations from appearance.

Scenario 6 — Research microscopy panels. A paper presents four microscope panels with a scale bar and color-coded markers. A generic alternative says “Microscope images of cells.” The subject expert supplies panel-by-panel conditions, scale information, what the colored markers indicate, and the principal observed pattern. A table or structured list conveys quantitative counts drawn from the study data, rather than estimates made by an image-description model. Acceptance evidence: readers can identify each panel’s experimental condition, understand the scale, and distinguish observations from author interpretation. If fluorescence colors carry the comparison, corresponding labels and a non-color cue are supplied.

A scholarly figure’s accessible text is part of the research record. It should receive the same rigor as captions, tables, and statistical claims, including correction when the source data are revised.

40. Worked Scenarios IV: A Product Gallery and an Interactive SVG Chart

Scenario 7 — Product photographs. An online store displays four photographs of the same item: front, back, connection ports, and packaging. The original alternatives all repeat the product’s name, making it impossible for a reader to discover what differs. The revised descriptions identify the relevant view and visible features, while separate structured specifications record product dimensions and compatibility claims supported by official information. A selection control indicates the current image and permits keyboard movement through the gallery. Acceptance evidence: a buyer can determine which image shows a required port and navigate to the corresponding product details. Claims not visible or verified are not invented in alt text.

Scenario 8 — Interactive SVG comparison. An education analytics page renders a scatterplot whose points reveal student-group metrics only on hover. The alternative is not one enormous spoken list of hundreds of points. The team creates a filterable semantic data table, provides an accessible chart description, and exposes the same selected subset through HTML controls. The visual plot remains useful for spatial pattern recognition, but no essential selection is trapped behind pointer hover. Acceptance evidence: a keyboard user can choose a category, inspect its numerical values, and return to the filter without losing focus; a nonvisual reader can inspect outliers and understand the metric definitions.

The common outcome is equivalent relevant opportunity rather than identical sensory presentation. Visual and textual methods can differ in navigation while still offering the needed information and control.

No single technique suits every image. The following matrix is intended for editorial triage, not as a replacement for the normative standards or judgment about particular content.

42. Ten-Step Implementation and Evaluation Framework

  1. Identify the task. Determine what the user must know, compare, decide, or operate after encountering the visual.
  2. Classify contextual purpose. Distinguish decoration, information, action, complex explanation, and any combination of purposes.
  3. Verify source meaning. Confirm facts, variables, units, provenance, and interpretations with the responsible content owner.
  4. Author the short alternative. Supply a concise name or summary that fits the image’s role in the surrounding content.
  5. Select deeper access where needed. Use semantic paragraphs, lists, tables, structured notation, location records, or another appropriate representation.
  6. Preserve relationships. Convey chart series, flowchart branches, map associations, and other meaning beyond the labels themselves.
  7. Preserve user action. When graphics are interactive, offer named, keyboard-operable controls and accurate state feedback.
  8. Evaluate visual access. Check zoom, reflow, contrast, color independence, legibility, and responsive behavior.
  9. Evaluate nonvisual access. Inspect accessibility-tree exposure and use real screen-reader, keyboard, and relevant assistive-technology workflows to complete a defined task.
  10. Maintain integrity over time. Reconcile graphics and alternatives after data revisions, localization, redesign, or platform updates; record unresolved gaps and accountable owners.

A short acceptance record can state the image identifier, audience task, classification, text alternative, linked deeper representation, applicable criteria, evaluator, evidence, defects, and disposition. This framework scales from a single course figure to an institution-wide collection of data products when consistently embedded in authoring workflows.

44. Study Exercises and Review Questions

Applied exercise A — Image triage: select ten real images from an educational website. For each, identify its context-specific function and write either a justified empty alternative, a short informative description, or an action name. Explain why reusing the same label for every occurrence of one logo may be wrong.

Applied exercise B — Chart reconstruction: choose a published chart with known underlying data. Write a short alternative, a two-paragraph summary of its main finding and limitations, and a semantic table for the key values. Verify that your summary accurately reports units, dates, and unusual values. Repeat the task with the graphic hidden.

Applied exercise C — Process and map alternatives: translate a decision flowchart into an ordered process with explicit branches. Separately create a list alternative for a map used to find local services. Explain why the flowchart primarily requires logical relationships while the service locator also requires interaction and verified location data.

Applied exercise D — Technical implementation: compare an <img> with alt, an inline SVG using accessible naming, a canvas visualization with companion data, and a linked icon-only button. Inspect the accessibility tree and keyboard behavior. Record the difference between a graphic being announced and a task being operable.

Applied exercise E — Editorial governance: draft an acceptance record for a 12-figure history chapter. Specify who validates fact-dependent descriptions, how tables and figure titles are reconciled after revisions, how localized versions will be reviewed, and what constitutes a release-blocking defect.

Review questions: Why is a decorative image sometimes best given empty alt text? Why should the purpose of a linked icon be described instead of its appearance? When does a complex image need a long description or table? What relationships must be preserved in a flowchart? Why is a screenshot of an equation different from MathML? Why can chart data and textual interpretation diverge after a filter changes? How can a visually accessible chart still fail screen-reader users? What new obligations arise when an SVG is interactive? Why is an automatically generated description not authoritative? How should a production team respond when a chart changes but its alternative does not?

47. Related Topics and Cross-Division Boundaries

This article is centered on the accessibility of meaning and interaction in graphics. General visual composition belongs to User Interface Design and wider journey design to User Experience Design. Display Technologies addresses display hardware and visual-output properties; it is not a substitute for alternative descriptions or usable interface semantics. Input Method Design covers input technology more broadly.

Data modeling, analytical computation, and visualization software may have their own specialized treatments in the Data Systems and Applications of Computing divisions. The present article owns the human-facing availability of that output. A charting application may be technically accurate and still inaccessible; an accurate text alternative may coexist with a graph that needs improved analytical design. Computing Ethics covers the larger normative questions of information rights, representation, and organizational accountability.

Boundary rule: image accessibility is neither a visual-design afterthought nor a substitute for domain correctness. Graphic meaning, underlying data, semantic alternatives, and user interaction must be addressed by their responsible specialists and kept coherent at publication.
