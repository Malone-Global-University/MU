1. Definition, Scope, and Governing Question

A lesson with excellent narration can remain inaccessible when it presents information solely through moving diagrams. A video with a readable transcript can still exclude a viewer who needs synchronized captions to follow dialogue at the moment it occurs. A captioned broadcast may remain inaccessible to a blind viewer when essential visual evidence is never spoken. These are not interchangeable defects. They concern different information channels, different ways people encounter media, and different requirements for usable alternatives.

Captions, Transcripts, and Audio Description examines how the meaningful information in audio and video can be made perceivable in more than one form. It addresses prerecorded and live media, spoken and unspoken sound, significant moving imagery, caption and transcript authoring, described video, sign-language access, the surrounding player, and an organization's responsibility to maintain quality after revision. The governing question is: Can a person who cannot hear, cannot see, or cannot use the default presentation still acquire the information and carry out the intended task?

The term equivalent refers to preservation of relevant meaning and task opportunity, not identical sensory experience or a requirement that every poetic or aesthetic element be conveyed in precisely the same way. Different media forms have different affordances. The aim is to preserve essential dialogue, sound cues, visible actions, displayed data, and decision-making context, while offering useful choices to people with different access needs.

This is a specialist article within the Accessibility unit. It complements Screen Readers and Nonvisual Navigation, which studies assistive-technology navigation, and Accessible Images, Charts, and Nontext Content, which treats static visual alternatives. It does not attempt to replace entertainment encoding, streaming infrastructure, media licensing, or specialist legal advice.

2. Human Needs, Environments, and Meaningful Media Access

Media access is not a binary division between viewers who can see and hear and those who cannot. Deaf and hard-of-hearing people have different language and caption preferences; blind and low-vision people may listen to audio while needing descriptions of visual action; DeafBlind people may depend on text that can be processed by a braille display. Some people have difficulty processing rapid speech, use media in an additional language, or need to search and reread at their own pace. Others may simply be in an environment where sound cannot be played.

A useful design inquiry separates information needs from assumptions about a diagnosis. If a lecture contains spoken equations, the equations may need accurate text representation. If a scene relies on a silent hand signal, it may require description. If dialogue overlaps, viewers may need clear speaker identification. If a player contains inaccessible controls, the presence of an excellent caption file will not ensure users can activate it.

Situation Access barrier Possible provision Verification question
Prerecorded lecture with spoken examples Audio cannot be heard Accurate synchronized captions, usable transcript Do examples and meaningful sounds survive transcription?
Silent visual demonstration Key action is only visible Described visual information or full text alternative Can the action be understood without seeing the frame?
Documentary with dialogue and visual clues Information is split across channels Captions plus audio description; descriptive transcript Can users follow cause and effect by their chosen mode?
Livestream with spoken instructions No realtime text Live captions with monitoring and correction process Are instructions intelligible while the event unfolds?
Video for a DeafBlind learner Spoken and visual cues are inaccessible Full descriptive transcript in accessible text Can a braille reader follow the sequence independently?
Multilingual conference Language barrier and audio access Separate language tracks and suitable captions Are track labels, language, and translations truthful?

Access requirements should be derived from the content and intended use, not merely the existence of a video file. A silent decorative loop and an instructional surgical demonstration demand fundamentally different analysis.

3. The Media Alternative Matrix: Four Distinct Functions

Captions are time-aligned text that communicates speech and important nonspeech audio while the media plays. Transcripts allow audio content, and where applicable visual description, to be read independently of playback. Audio description communicates visual information through speech or another audible presentation. Sign-language interpretation conveys linguistic content through a signed language and serves users whose primary language is that signed language. A language-translation subtitle may be useful but is not necessarily equivalent to an accessibility caption that includes meaningful audio cues.

Provision Primary information transformed Timing and modality Does not necessarily replace
Captions Speech and meaningful audio Synchronized visual text Full descriptive transcript; description of silent visual content
Translation subtitles Speech, translated Synchronized text in another language Captions for nonspeech sounds and speaker cues
Basic transcript Speech and relevant audio Independently readable text Synchronized captions; descriptions of visuals
Descriptive transcript Spoken and significant visual information Independently readable structured text Audio description required for prerecorded synchronized video at WCAG AA
Audio description Essential visual information Additional spoken information Captions for users unable to hear dialogue
Sign-language interpretation Spoken linguistic content and appropriate context Synchronized signed video Captions, transcripts, or visual descriptions

A complete media-access plan may require several provisions simultaneously. A text transcript can be superior for searching terminology; synchronized captions can be indispensable for following a classroom demonstration; and an audio description can make a silent action meaningful to a listener. Selecting only one based on the idea that it is a universal substitute is an architectural error.

4. WCAG 2.2: Time-Based Media Requirements by Conformance Level

The World Wide Web Consortium's WCAG 2.2 Recommendation is the normative standard used for this article. Its Guideline 1.2, Time-based Media, differentiates what is required by media type and conformance level. The provisions below are abridged teaching descriptions, not the full normative wording. Exceptions and precise definitions must be checked in the Recommendation before making a formal conformance claim.

Criterion Level Core obligation in scope
1.2.1 Audio-only and Video-only (Prerecorded) A Provide an equivalent alternative for prerecorded audio-only, or a time-based media alternative / audio track for prerecorded video-only, subject to the criterion's exception
1.2.2 Captions (Prerecorded) A Caption prerecorded audio in synchronized media, subject to the media-alternative-for-text exception
1.2.3 Audio Description or Media Alternative (Prerecorded) A For prerecorded synchronized video, supply audio description or a complete alternative for time-based media, subject to the stated exception
1.2.4 Captions (Live) AA Caption live audio content in synchronized media
1.2.5 Audio Description (Prerecorded) AA Supply audio description for prerecorded video content in synchronized media where visual information needs description
1.2.6 Sign Language (Prerecorded) AAA Supply sign-language interpretation for prerecorded audio in synchronized media
1.2.7 Extended Audio Description (Prerecorded) AAA When regular pauses do not allow necessary description, provide extended description
1.2.8 Media Alternative (Prerecorded) AAA Provide an alternative for time-based media for prerecorded synchronized media and video-only media
1.2.9 Audio-only (Live) AAA Provide an equivalent alternative for live audio-only content

A common error is to assume that a descriptive transcript always satisfies 1.2.5 at Level AA. It does not: the Level A criterion 1.2.3 offers a choice between description and an alternative for time-based media, but Level AA criterion 1.2.5 calls for audio description. Conversely, when the ordinary audio already communicates all essential visual information, there may be nothing further to add by separate audio description. The determination is based on actual content, not a blanket exemption for a media genre. WCAG 2.2's other requirements—including keyboard access, pause mechanisms, contrast, labels, focus, and operation—also apply to the surrounding player as relevant.

WCAG 3.0 remained a Working Draft during this article's November 2026 editorial window. Its evolving media methods can inform research, but they should not be misrepresented as completed replacement conformance requirements for WCAG 2.2.

5. Planning Accessible Media Before Recording

The cheapest accessibility intervention is often to change the script before the first recording session. A presenter who says, “The upper curve rises from 20 to 45 units, while the lower curve stays near 25,” has preserved meaning that would otherwise be carried only by a plot. A presenter who says “click here” while pointing to the screen has not. If accessibility is deferred until export, editors may need to rewrite narration, re-record speakers, construct descriptions around crowded dialogue, or reconstruct terminology from imperfect automatic speech recognition.

A media brief should identify intended audience and languages, prerecorded versus live delivery, expected users and controls, the information communicated only by sound, the information communicated only by sight, available source scripts, and ownership of alternate formats. Establish a caption editor and visual-description reviewer, select a player that can expose alternatives, and budget both transcription review and post-publication maintenance. These are content-management requirements rather than optional production embellishments.

Storyboard review example: A training segment consists of narration, a cursor moving around a spreadsheet, and a red highlighted cell. The script says only, “Now use this result.” Before filming, revise it to identify the cell and its meaning: “In the monthly totals row, the operating surplus appears in cell F14; the value is 12,400.” Include the instruction in the caption script and transcript. The visual highlight can remain as a helpful cue without carrying unique information.

For complex performance, drama, or documentary media, it may be necessary to author a separate description track rather than continually interrupt the principal audio. But early planning still yields clear dialogue intervals, access to uncompressed audio, accurate character names, and a reliable content list of visual details to convey.

6. Captions as Synchronized Meaning, Not Merely Displayed Words

Captions convey spoken dialogue and the audible context necessary to understand a scene or lesson. A transcript of dialogue dumped onto the screen as a paragraph is not an adequate caption implementation: timing, speaker changes, and association with relevant action matter. Captions normally include appropriate punctuation and meaningful non-speech information such as a door slam that triggers a reaction, a distant alarm that causes evacuation, or music that is essential to understanding a mood or event.

Accurate captions convey terminology, names, numbers, units, and distinctions between speakers. They preserve meaning even when a speaker corrects a claim mid-sentence. Editing out a significant correction can mislead viewers; leaving every hesitation and false start mechanically untouched can also impair comprehension. The appropriate practice is a documented editorial policy that preserves substantive meaning while providing readable text.

Consider a recorded safety tutorial: “Turn off the main breaker” is spoken while an electrical warning buzzer sounds. Captioning only the spoken instruction omits an environmental cue relevant to the demonstration. The cue might be rendered as [warning buzzer sounds], with timing that aligns with the event. In a different scene, quiet music that carries no instructional content need not be transcribed exhaustively merely to fill a caption track. The author must make evidence-based judgments about meaningful sound.

Captions should not be confused with the accessibility of the caption controls. A correctly timed file does no practical good if the viewer cannot locate the captions button, tell whether captions are active, or choose the appropriate language.

7. Captions, Subtitles, Open Captions, and Closed Captions

Usage varies by region and platform, but the instructional distinction is useful: accessibility captions cover dialogue and relevant audio cues; translation subtitles often focus on translated speech. A translated subtitle track can also be authored as full captions if it includes the appropriate sound information. Never assume the track is functionally complete because a file or menu item is labeled “subtitles.”

Closed captions are generally selectable or toggled by a user. Open captions are embedded in the visual image and cannot be independently disabled or restyled. Closed captions offer flexibility, while open captions may be useful in tightly constrained exhibition environments. Embedded captions are part of the picture: their size, contrast, cropping, and positioning may deteriorate when the video is scaled or viewed on a small device. Selectable text tracks also support multiple languages and, depending on the player, user preferences for presentation.

Toggle or select language Often available through player Not independently toggleable Often available through player
Change visual styling Depends on player/OS, sometimes possible Usually not without re-editing video Depends on player/OS
Meaningful sound effects Can be included Can be included May be omitted

Accessibility teams should record precisely which function a media asset provides, and should not designate a track as accessible purely because it contains synchronized text.

8. Caption Timing, Segmentation, and Reading Load

A caption must arrive when it is useful. Text appearing five seconds after an instruction may arrive only after the demonstrated action has ended. Captions displayed too far ahead can reveal an outcome prematurely or confuse which speaker is talking. Timing evaluation therefore considers entry and exit times, sync with dialogue, action boundaries, shot changes, and the ability to read at a manageable pace.

Avoid fragmenting fluent speech into isolated words that flicker rapidly, and avoid covering an entire short sentence with one late cue. Break lines at natural linguistic boundaries when possible; do not separate a negation from what it modifies in a way that invites error. A practical readability review observes the actual render at intended display sizes, rather than relying only on a numeric characters-per-second threshold. Requirements may vary by language, audience, media genre, and a distribution platform's specifications.

Before: the speaker says, “Do not open the valve,” and the caption breaks into Do / not open / the valve with long delays. A reader may temporarily receive a dangerous or misleading instruction. After: display a coherent instruction aligned with its utterance, preserving the negation and context. For high-stakes instruction, verify the caption and video together with a reviewer competent in the language and subject.

For live events, latency may be unavoidable, but it remains a quality attribute to measure. Track delay and dropped words separately from transcription accuracy; two files can have identical text while providing different access due to timing.

9. Speaker Identification, Sound Effects, and On-Screen Placement

When multiple voices can be heard, viewers need to know who speaks. The required precision depends on context. A short interview may use speaker labels, while a film might use spatial positioning, character names, or other techniques to avoid unnecessary repetitive labels. Identifiers should not depend on color alone, because captions may be viewed in altered color schemes and by people with color-vision differences.

Meaningful sound descriptions explain effects rather than merely report that a sound exists. “[metal latch clicks]” can explain why a character realizes a door is locked. “[alarm beeps three times]” may be important in a device tutorial. Where music establishes a vital narrative change, concise notation can communicate the shift. However, over-captioning incidental ambient sound can obscure dialogue and impede reading. A review should separate information essential to the task from atmosphere that may be summarized.

Placement must protect both captions and underlying content. Captions that cover an equation, the name of a speaker, or an emergency warning create a new barrier. Conversely, placing captions too high may collide with a playback overlay or appear outside a user's visible area. When a media player permits repositioning or user-defined styles, inspect the actual result on small screens, zoomed views, and full-screen modes.

A useful editorial log records each high-risk segment: overlapping dialogue, scene changes, on-screen titles, music that affects meaning, numerical statements, and shots where caption position conflicts with important visual information. The log becomes a concrete revision queue rather than a subjective “captions look fine” approval.

10. Caption Accuracy, Human Review, and Quality Evidence

Automated speech recognition can accelerate draft creation, but it does not establish accuracy. A transcript may substitute an ordinary word for a specialized acronym, invert “can” and “cannot,” misstate units, merge speakers, or omit quiet speech. Captions can also be grammatically correct yet semantically wrong because they fail to register humor, uncertainty, irony, or a demonstrator's correction.

Review should involve someone who can listen to the media and understands its language and technical terminology. For specialist material, supply a terminology list and consult a subject-matter reviewer. Check names and numbers against authoritative scripts or source records where available. A reviewer must also inspect timing, sound descriptions, legibility, display over significant content, and accurate language labeling. The existence of a provider quality score is not a substitute for observing the final published video.

An approval record should identify media version, languages reviewed, date, responsible reviewer, defects corrected, and remaining limitations. Media accessibility is a content-quality process with accountable deliverables.

11. Live Captions, Latency, and Correction During Events

Live captions differ operationally from prerecorded captions. There may be no time to pause a speaker, resolve technical vocabulary, or correct every word before display. A high-quality service needs preparation, event vocabulary, reliable audio feeds, caption production, monitoring, technical fallback, and a channel for reporting breakdowns. Human realtime captioning and automated systems have different strengths and constraints; either can fail under overlapping speech, poor microphones, unusual names, noise, or rapid turn-taking.

WCAG 2.2 criterion 1.2.4 (Level AA) applies to live audio content in synchronized media. It is not identical to criterion 1.2.9, which addresses an alternative for live audio-only content at Level AAA. The distinction matters when choosing how to deliver an accessible livestream and what formal claim to make.

Organizers should obtain presenter names and pronunciation, key terms, slides and abbreviations beforehand; encourage speakers to identify themselves and use a microphone; keep questions audible; and design a caption area that does not obscure poll results or shared visuals. When a caption service disconnects, the host needs a defined response: announce the access failure through an available text channel, pause or provide an alternative when appropriate, and avoid pretending that the event remains fully accessible.

After the event, a machine-produced transcript of the livestream should not automatically become the public archive. Archive captions require a second pass to correct terminology and timing. If edits change the visual sequence, the described-video and transcript assets should also be updated.

12. Transcripts: Audio Text, Descriptive Text, and Task Independence

A transcript is an independent text representation of media content. A simple audio transcript conveys speech, speaker changes, and meaningful nonspeech sounds. A descriptive transcript adds visual information required to understand the presentation: onscreen charts, physical actions, important gestures, captions appearing inside the image, visible labels, and sequence information. A full descriptive transcript can be particularly valuable for a person who accesses content through braille or who needs to move between reading and playback.

Transcripts are useful beyond strict conformance: they support search, indexing, translation, slow reading, quoting, glossary construction, review of technical terminology, and locating specific topics. But a transcript does not provide synchronized display alongside video by itself. A student watching a complicated demonstration while unable to hear may still need captions at the moment of action, even if a complete transcript appears elsewhere.

A robust transcript identifies speakers when relevant, contains headings or segments for navigation, provides a textual account of important visual changes, and retains equations, values, and instructional steps with adequate precision. It should not collapse an entire lecture into a summary if the result is presented as a full alternative. For lengthy media, useful structural boundaries might be “Introduction,” “Procedure,” “Worked Example,” and “Questions,” with text that can be searched and read in ordinary document order.

Task test: Turn off the media, use only the transcript, and ask a reviewer to complete the instructional objective. If the transcript says “a green indicator appears” but never states what green signifies, the alternative is incomplete. If it uses only the phrase “as shown” to refer to a complex illustration, the burden has simply been transferred from a video player to a text page.

13. Making Transcripts Themselves Accessible

A transcript is a document and must be accessible as a document. Placing text inside a scanned image or an untagged PDF is not equivalent to providing selectable, navigable HTML text. Good HTML transcripts use semantic headings, paragraphs, ordered lists for steps, properly marked tables for data, and explicit labels for any download or interactive navigation. Use an appropriate page language, preserve reading order, and ensure that keyboard and screen-reader users can reach the transcript from the media page.

If a video contains an equation, a transcript should express it accurately. For example, “The formula is E = mc²” can be represented in a text sentence, with further explanation if the notation is essential. If the equation is complex, a carefully structured text rendering or an accessible math representation may be needed. “The formula appears on screen” cannot support the same educational task. If the media contains a comparison chart, describe the relevant trend, figures, axes, and conclusion rather than repeating “see chart.”

Consider the markup for a short companion transcript:

<section aria-labelledby="training-transcript">
  <h2 id="training-transcript">Transcript: Equipment Setup</h2>
  <h3>00:00 — Preparation</h3>
  <p><strong>Instructor:</strong> Disconnect the equipment from power.</p>
  <p><strong>Visual description:</strong> The indicator above the
    power switch changes from amber to dark.</p>
  <h3>00:18 — Inspection</h3>
  <p><strong>Instructor:</strong> Check the locking tab on the left.</p>
</section>

This example is illustrative, not a transcript of a real media file. The timestamp labels help readers cross-reference playback but should not be the only navigational mechanism. Offer the transcript in a readily accessible page location rather than burying it behind an unlabeled icon. Where downloadable documents are provided, identify the format and make sure the downloadable version is accessible too.

14. Interactive Transcripts and Time-Linked Navigation

An interactive transcript can synchronize transcript passages with playback and let readers jump to an exact media position. This is an enhancement, not a substitute for a complete accessible text version. It can support students locating a term, journalists reviewing a quotation, and people who need to replay only one part of a long video. But an interactive transcript can introduce new problems if every word is a separate tab stop or if playback forcibly moves the user's keyboard focus through a transcript as captions update.

Separate transcript reading from playback tracking. Highlight the currently spoken passage if the user wants it, but do not unexpectedly scroll away from a person reading another part of the text. If a “Follow playback” mode is available, make it understandable and reversible. Time-jump controls should be labeled with the relevant section and time, and they must work by keyboard. Ensure that the transcript can be searched using ordinary browser functionality and remains meaningful when JavaScript is unavailable.

A practical control might read “Play section: Troubleshooting, 04:26.” Another control might be “Disable automatic transcript following.” A page that merely colors the active paragraph without announcing a new section may still be usable if the basic transcript remains accessible; a page that automatically hides all noncurrent paragraphs undermines independent study.

Implementation principle: the transcript's semantic reading order is the primary document. Playback synchronization adds optional navigation and highlighting. If synchronization fails, the user should still have the transcript and an operable player. The interface should not replace complete text with a sequence of inaccessible dynamic fragments.

15. Audio Description: What Sighted Presentation Communicates

Audio description is spoken information that conveys significant visual meaning not otherwise included in the main soundtrack. It can identify a person who is entering a scene, an unspoken gesture, a change in visual evidence, text displayed without narration, a sign that explains the setting, or a plotted trend that alters the interpretation of a lesson. It should enable a listener to follow the event or task without needing to infer essential information from silence.

Good descriptions are selective. They do not attempt to catalog every color, object, texture, or camera movement. They identify what matters to understanding the plot, instructional procedure, argument, evidence, or decision. In educational media, data, diagrams, labels, and directional actions can be more important than aesthetics. In a dramatic scene, action, body language, identity, and relationships may be necessary to understand the narrative, while a long list of decorative furnishings may not be.

Descriptions are timed relative to existing speech and sounds. If important dialogue occupies the entire soundtrack, a conventional description cannot always be inserted without masking it. In those cases the producer may integrate the description into the original narration, create a different described version, or use an extended-description approach. Merely uploading a descriptive text file to a platform that cannot read it aloud is not equivalent to delivering an audible described experience.

Illustration: A video shows a chart with a blue line that steepens sharply after year three. The narration says, “As you can see, the result is decisive.” An appropriate revision might say, “After year three, the intervention group rises from 30 to 75, while the comparison group remains near 35.” That narration communicates the evidence rather than asking listeners to see it.

16. Integrated Description: Writing Sight into the Primary Narration

For many educational and explanatory videos, integrated description is the most efficient solution. Instead of scheduling a separate voice between spoken segments, the writer includes relevant visual content directly in the main narration. This approach can serve everyone: people who are not watching continuously, those viewing on small screens, listeners with low vision, and people reviewing the material while performing a practical task.

Non-integrated version: “Place that part there. This is the correct result.”

Integrated version: “Insert the silver connector into the left-hand socket until the black locking tab clicks. The status indicator beside the socket changes from red to steady green.”

The added words must correspond to the actual demonstration and not introduce new inaccuracies. For a chart, use data or trends rather than speculative interpretations; for a safety procedure, name the relevant control and context. Filmmakers can create room for description by adjusting the shot list, allowing a few seconds after a significant visual action, or choosing narration that does not compete with crucial sound effects.

Integrated description does not mean reading every visual label verbatim. It means delivering the information necessary for comprehension. A displayed employee name could be read when identification matters, while a decorative background logo might not require a spoken announcement. A review process should ask whether the ordinary audio conveys the same essential information as the image. When it does, additional audio description may not be necessary.

17. Standard, Extended, and Alternative Described Versions

Standard audio description is usually inserted into natural pauses in the original audio. When meaningful visual action occurs during uninterrupted dialogue, the available pause may be too short. Extended audio description allows the video timeline to pause or expand so that information can be conveyed without talking over the original soundtrack. WCAG 2.2 criterion 1.2.7 (AAA) specifically addresses cases in which pauses are inadequate for necessary description.

Other implementation patterns include a separate described audio track synchronized to the video, a distinct audio-described version of the video with narration mixed in, or integrated narration planned at production. Selection should account for both content and playback support. A separate audio track may preserve visual quality and offer choice, but it requires a player that exposes the track, maintains synchronization, and permits users to activate it. A complete described video is simpler to verify in some environments because description is already mixed into the audio, but it creates an additional asset that must be maintained whenever the original edit changes.

Description strategy Appropriate use Operating risk
Integrated description Training, explanation, planned lectures Writer fails to name critical visual data
Standard inserted description Narrative with clear dialogue pauses Description masks speech or is truncated
Separate described audio track Player with supported audio-track selection Control undiscoverable or track out of sync

Extended description Dense visuals with insufficient audio gaps Video pauses or alternate timing not supported

When the project includes translations, a described track may also require translation and new narration. A caption file for the original audio does not automatically include material newly spoken in a described version; check the actual combined playback and provide the necessary caption representation.

18. Timed Text Descriptions and Their Support Limits

The HTML <track> element allows a kind="descriptions" text track, and WebVTT can carry timed description cues. However, the existence of such a track does not prove that any browser or player will speak it. Text-based description depends on a media player implementation that exposes the track and reads or presents cues at the right time; in some contexts the player also needs to pause the video while the description is spoken. Browser and screen-reader combinations vary, and the W3C explicitly advises checking player support before choosing this method.

A conceptual HTML declaration looks like this:

<video controls preload="metadata">
  <source src="/media/setup-described.mp4" type="video/mp4">
  <track kind="captions" src="/media/setup.en.vtt"
         srclang="en" label="English captions">
  <track kind="descriptions" src="/media/setup-description.en.vtt"
         srclang="en" label="English description cues">
</video>

The descriptions declaration alone is not a working audio-description implementation. The example assumes a media player or custom system that actually delivers the descriptions. If that support is unavailable, provide an integrated or separately described audiovisual asset, or another conforming solution appropriate to the target level. Do not rely on text-only cues that stay hidden from the people they are intended to serve.

To test this feature, close your eyes, activate the actual published described mode without visual assistance, and listen to the entire affected passage. Confirm that controls identify the mode, descriptive speech is audible, timing does not obscure essential dialogue, and the alternative still works on the relevant mobile and desktop platforms.

19. Diagrams, On-Screen Text, Demonstrations, and Visual Evidence

Educational media frequently introduces information that is present neither in narration nor in a conventional transcript. A presenter may highlight a column in a table without naming it, point to the right side of a machine, or circle a branch on a process diagram. An inaccessible video can be technically captioned and still omit the core lesson for listeners who cannot see the frame.

Use a visual-information inventory during script review. Record each critical title, label, data value, chart change, physical manipulation, gesture, and visual-only conclusion. Decide whether it should appear in the main narration, separate audio description, descriptive transcript, or several of these. In some subjects, more information may be necessary than can fit comfortably into the audio track. A detailed transcript or independently accessible data table can complement spoken description without pretending to replace the audio description required at the target conformance level.

For plots, identify the variables, units, relevant magnitudes, and trend. For an instructional interface, identify the control's name and position only as much as needed to perform the action: “Open Settings, then select Export” is usually more useful than “Click the little icon over there.” For a procedure, articulate changes in the state of equipment and meaningful warning indicators. For time-dependent demonstrations, communicate when an action occurs relative to an instruction or observation.

Boundary: This article owns alternative presentation of visual meaning within time-based media. A static image on a webpage may require alternative text and a separate detailed description; those decisions are developed further in Accessible Images, Charts, and Nontext Content.

20. Sign-Language Provision and Linguistic Access

A sign language is a complete natural language, not simply a word-for-word visual rendering of the majority spoken language. Deaf viewers who use sign language as their primary language may find written captions useful but still prefer or require signed communication for equivalent comprehension in specific contexts. Vocabulary, grammar, culture, and regional language varieties matter; an interpreter appropriate for one signed language is not automatically appropriate for another.

WCAG 2.2 criterion 1.2.6 (AAA) addresses sign-language interpretation for prerecorded audio in synchronized media. This is a separate provision from ordinary captions. Institutions may have additional duties or service commitments beyond WCAG, depending on jurisdiction, audience, and context; those obligations should be determined in the applicable legal and institutional framework rather than inferred solely from the WCAG table.

If signing is provided in a dedicated picture-in-picture pane, it must be large and clear enough to understand and should not be obscured by captions, promotional overlays, or controls. If the user can choose to show or hide an interpreter pane, the control must be accessible. Evaluate whether the pane remains readable on smaller screens and whether signing becomes blurred, poorly framed, or cropped during compression. If more than one signed language is offered, label the tracks accurately.

A video with signing can still need accurate captions, a descriptive transcript, and visual description. The interpreter translates spoken language but may not communicate independent visual clues such as an unspoken chart change. An inclusive media strategy allocates resources by audience and content rather than declaring any one mode sufficient for everyone.

21. Prerecorded Audio-Only, Video-Only, and Live Audio-Only

Time-based media include distinct cases often obscured by the general term “video accessibility.” For a prerecorded audio-only item, such as a podcast episode, criterion 1.2.1 (A) requires an alternative for time-based media that presents equivalent information, unless the relevant exception applies. A suitable transcript must communicate the meaningful sound and speech, not merely the episode title or summary. An ordinary audio-only podcast does not require audio description, because there is no visual track to describe.

For prerecorded video-only content, such as a silent hand-assembly demonstration, criterion 1.2.1 (A) allows an alternative for time-based media or an audio track that presents equivalent information. A sequence of silent images may require text or spoken narration explaining gestures, steps, and changes. If the silent content is merely decorative, the assessment should still identify the content's actual role and how it appears in the page rather than assigning requirements without context.

For live audio-only content, criterion 1.2.9 at Level AAA requires an alternative for time-based media that presents equivalent information. This should not be confused with live synchronized video captioning at Level AA. A practical organization may provide text access to a live audio program even when its target conformance level does not expressly require the AAA criterion, because real users may need it.

A team should classify each item at intake: prerecorded or live; audio-only, video-only, or synchronized; primary content or clearly labeled media alternative for text; informative or decorative; target conformance level. The classification controls which alternatives must be developed and verified.

22. Media Players as Accessibility Systems

A media player's accessibility is part of the learner's access, not an independent technical luxury. Users must be able to start, pause, resume, seek, change volume, turn captions on or off, choose language and audio description, and manage full screen using accessible controls. When a custom player is used, each control needs an appropriate accessible name, role, state, and keyboard interaction. A visual “CC” icon without an accessible name or state does not fulfill the same function as an operable captions button.

Native browser controls can be useful starting points, but their specific accessibility support and language-track functionality should be tested in target environments. A custom interface must not assume that a styled <div> is a button. Correct use of HTML <button> and consistent state changes is preferable to recreating controls unnecessarily with ARIA. This article focuses on media-specific consequences; Accessible Names, Roles, States, and Properties and Keyboard Access and Focus Management explain the general component architecture.

Player operation should be tested with the complete content and alternatives, not an empty preview player. A described track that works only in one proprietary client is a documented deployment constraint, not proof of universal compatibility.

23. HTML Video, Caption Tracks, and a Working WebVTT Baseline

HTML supports associated text tracks through <track>, usually supplied as WebVTT (.vtt) files. For a basic instructional video, the browser can be given a prerecorded captions track with kind="captions", a valid language tag, a descriptive track label, and a relative asset path. The example below shows source markup; the referenced media and cue files are illustrative and must be created and hosted before it can play.

<figure>
  <video controls preload="metadata"
         aria-describedby="demo-media-note">
    <source src="/media/safety-demo.mp4" type="video/mp4">
    <track kind="captions"
           src="/media/safety-demo.en.vtt"
           srclang="en" label="English captions">
    Your browser does not support HTML video.
  </video>
  <figcaption>Equipment safety demonstration, 4 minutes.</figcaption>
</figure>
<p id="demo-media-note">
  <a href="/media/safety-demo-transcript">Read the full
    descriptive transcript of this demonstration</a>.
</p>

The caption file could begin with:

WEBVTT

00:00:00.000 --> 00:00:03.500
Instructor: Disconnect the machine from power.

00:00:04.000 --> 00:00:06.900
[warning tone sounds]

00:00:07.100 --> 00:00:10.600
Instructor: Confirm that the status light is dark.

This example intentionally does not assert that the video or VTT files already exist. A deployed implementation must ensure they are served with appropriate media types, accessible to authorized users, included in a cache or offline distribution plan as appropriate, and kept in sync with the current media edition. The transcript URL should resolve to meaningful HTML or another accessible representation; a placeholder hyperlink alone does not constitute delivery.

The default attribute on a <track> may influence initial track selection, but it does not ensure that every browser will display or announce captions identically. Test how the browser and player expose the track choices. Also do not mislabel translated speech-only subtitles as full captions simply because the markup accepts a kind="captions" value.

24. WebVTT Structure, Encoding, and Caption Rendering

WebVTT is a timed-text format with a WEBVTT header and cues defined by start and end timestamps. It can include cue identifiers, voice annotations, positioning, and limited formatting, subject to support in the actual playback environment. A .vtt file should use correct syntax and character encoding so names and punctuation survive. SubRip .srt files are common in production pipelines but are not simply interchangeable with .vtt without appropriate conversion; the HTML text-track workflow expects WebVTT for broad interoperable browser use.

A further illustrative example shows timed speaker turns:

WEBVTT

opening
00:00:01.200 --> 00:00:04.400
<v Instructor>Begin by checking the pressure gauge.</v>

response
00:00:04.900 --> 00:00:07.700
<v Technician>The needle reads forty kilopascals.</v>

00:00:08.100 --> 00:00:10.600
[compressor switches off]

When a team uses voice annotations or styling, it should verify how those appear on target platforms and whether speaker identity remains comprehensible where styling is unavailable. Do not depend on color or positioning alone to identify speakers. Ensure cues do not overlap accidentally, contain invalid negative durations, or appear after the media ends. Some overlaps are intentional, but they should be inspected as a composed visual result rather than approved only from a file parser.

The most useful validation is a combined inspection: parse for syntax errors, listen to the media while reading captions, inspect small-screen layout, and compare the source against current recorded content. Caption authoring software may facilitate this process, but the substantive judgment of what a viewer needs remains a human editorial responsibility.

25. Multiple Languages, Alternate Audio, and Discoverable Choices

Multilingual media needs explicit language labels and a policy for both speech translation and accessible audio information. A file that translates dialogue but omits the sound of an approaching vehicle may be appropriate as dialogue subtitles but incomplete as accessibility captions. Organizations should avoid using imprecise language labels such as “English 2” when the actual distinction is “English captions,” “Spanish subtitles,” or “English audio description.”

A hypothetical player could expose two text tracks:

<video controls preload="metadata">
  <source src="/media/lecture.mp4" type="video/mp4">
  <track kind="captions" srclang="en"
         label="English captions"
         src="/media/lecture.en.vtt">
  <track kind="subtitles" srclang="es"
         label="Subtítulos en español"
         src="/media/lecture.es.vtt">
</video>

This markup demonstrates classification and labeling only. It does not itself prove the quality of either track, nor does it implement selection of an alternate spoken-language or described audio track. Audio selection depends on the media packaging and player, and must be verified for each intended platform. Different languages may require different reading pace and line breaks, so blindly reusing timestamps from one language may harm usability.

When the organization publishes a new edit of the video, track versioning is essential. The original language captions, translated text, described audio, and transcripts should all identify the same content revision. A reader must not be left with captions explaining an experiment that no longer appears in the current video. Language and revision are integral parts of content integrity.

26. Keyboard, Screen-Reader, and Touch Operation of Media Controls

A captioned video must remain operable for people who cannot use a mouse, cannot see the icons, or rely on touch exploration. The essential sequence is practical: locate the player; understand its title and duration; start playback; pause it; select a text or audio alternative; adjust the position; and leave the player without being trapped. The failure of any one of these steps can prevent access to the alternative media the team painstakingly produced.

Native interactive elements should be preferred when implementing custom controls. A caption-state toggle might use a button whose text identifies the current action and whose state is exposed consistently. Some interfaces use aria-pressed for toggle behavior; others use a menu item or selection control. The correct pattern depends on the actual interaction. Whatever pattern is selected, the displayed icon, programmatic state, visible status, and media track must agree.

The following is a structural illustration, not a complete caption-track switching algorithm:

<div role="group" aria-label="Video playback options">
  <button type="button" aria-pressed="false" id="captions-toggle">
    Captions off
  </button>
  <a href="/media/lesson-transcript">Read transcript</a>
</div>

A working application must implement the actual track selection, keep aria-pressed and visible text synchronized, and handle media and browser events correctly. Publishing only the markup would be misleading. It is also important not to steal keyboard focus each time captions update: caption changes are part of media playback, not repeated reasons to move the user's interaction cursor.

Testing should include keyboard-only, screen-reader, touch exploration, zoomed and narrow views, and full-screen operation on the platforms the institution supports. If a player has inaccessible controls, a transcript link may mitigate some information loss but should not be represented as a repair for all interaction requirements.

27. Playback Preferences, Audio Control, Motion, and Interference

People should control when media plays and how it competes with other tasks. Unexpected autoplay can disrupt screen-reader speech, startle users, consume bandwidth, and make it difficult to find the source of sound. WCAG 2.2 criterion 1.4.2 addresses audio that plays automatically for more than three seconds by requiring a mechanism to pause or stop it, or control its volume independently of system volume. Other criteria may apply to moving, blinking, or auto-updating content. The precise applicability depends on behavior and context.

A person using audio description may need to raise speech volume relative to original media; a viewer using captions may want audio muted without losing the caption stream. Where practical, player preferences should preserve the user's selected caption language, display style, and audio description choice across episodes or sessions. Such preferences should not require invasive tracking or additional personal data. Avoid assuming that defaults inferred from browser language accurately represent an individual's preferred caption or signed language.

The surrounding page must also cooperate. A floating autoplay trailer can mask captions from the primary video. An announcement from another component can overlap a spoken description. A full-screen transition can unexpectedly hide the transcript link or move focus to an inaccessible place. A content team needs end-to-end task tests because these defects are often produced by interactions between a correct media asset and an incorrect interface.

Design rule: the player should offer a predictable, recoverable media experience. The reader must not have to fight motion, unexpected sound, or inaccessible controls merely to obtain the information in an accessible format.

28. Distribution, Platform Support, and Cross-Device Verification

Media is often distributed through an embedded player, a content management system, a mobile application, an external streaming platform, and downloaded files. These environments may handle alternative tracks differently. One may expose captions and transcript links clearly while another drops a caption file, uses an inaccessible language selector, or lacks a means of enabling separate described audio. A successful playback test in one browser cannot certify all other platforms.

Establish a supported-environment matrix based on actual readers: desktop browsers and operating systems, mobile browsers, any native applications, relevant screen readers, and network conditions. Test the content's real destination URL and the route used to reach it. A file can exist on a server while being inaccessible because it is blocked by authentication, omitted from an application manifest, mistyped, cached from an old revision, or not discovered by the player.

Delivery context What to verify Typical remediation owner
Embedded web player Track controls, keyboard interaction, labels, transcript Web product / frontend team
Learning platform Caption ingestion, mobile playback, transcript placement Learning platform administrator
External hosting Which alternate audio and captions are actually available Content publisher and platform contract owner
Native app Screen-reader announcements, route focus, track switching Mobile product team
Downloaded video Accompanying transcript and available described edition Distribution/editorial team
Offline package Alternative assets retained and correctly linked Packaging and deployment team

Platform constraints should be recorded and communicated; however, “the vendor cannot display it” is not equivalent to providing an accessible service. The team should select an alternative delivery arrangement when required information cannot be reached through the chosen player.

29. Authoring Workflow, Roles, and Revision Governance

A credible media workflow assigns the same quality obligations to alternatives as to the original video. At intake, classify the media and determine the target accessibility outcomes. During scripting, remove avoidable dependence on sight or hearing. During production, capture clean audio and retain accurate scripts, names, diagrams, and source material. During postproduction, develop captions, transcripts, descriptions, and signed content where needed. During publication, confirm that the website and player actually expose all assets. During maintenance, track change requests and revisions together.

A practical role model is:

Role Responsibility Evidence
Subject owner Identifies information essential to understand the lesson Reviewed script and terminology register
Caption editor Produces accurate, timed captions Approved caption file and defect log
Description writer / narrator Communicates relevant visual meaning Description script, recording, review notes
Transcript editor Produces navigable and complete text alternative Accessible transcript page or file
Publishing engineer Connects media assets to an accessible player Published environment walkthrough
Accessibility reviewer Evaluates alternatives with target users and assistive tech Task outcomes and remaining issues
Content owner Ensures accessibility after content updates Version history and maintenance accountability

High-volume institutions benefit from metadata that records source media identifier, revision, duration, spoken language, caption languages, described editions, transcript location, sign-language support, review date, and responsible owner. This metadata should not become a bureaucratic substitute for user testing. It exists to make it possible to locate, maintain, and replace inaccessible assets at scale.

An editor who changes a narrated number must update every dependent alternative. A producer who replaces footage may need to revise descriptive narration even when the audio transcript stays unchanged. A translation provider who inserts a mistranslated warning may need independent specialist review. These dependencies belong in change management, not in occasional retrospective repair.

The most useful evaluation question is not “Do captions exist?” but “Can the user successfully understand and act on the media?” Formal criteria are essential, yet task-level examination reveals defects that a file inventory cannot show. Testing should cover representative content segments, high-risk instructions, alternate language tracks, the beginning and end of media, and the actual user journey from the host page into playback and back.

31. Worked Scenario 1 — A Training Video with a Silent Chart Change

Situation. A five-minute public-health training video has accurate English captions. At minute two, an on-screen chart changes from weekly totals to age-adjusted rates, but the narration continues, “This shows the real difference.” A blind listener hears no indication that the measure changed. A text-only transcript reproduces the dialogue without describing either chart.

Diagnosis. The caption track may fulfill the immediate speech-access role, but neither the audio nor transcript conveys the essential visual data. The defect is not repaired by making captions larger or by relabeling the player. It is a failure of visual-information description. A Level A assessment for prerecorded synchronized media examines whether the relevant audio description or complete media alternative is provided; a Level AA assessment examines audio description.

Response. Revise the main narration to say, “The first chart shows weekly counts; the second adjusts for age. On the adjusted measure the difference is smaller, approximately four percentage points.” If a new narration cannot be recorded, produce a separate described version or track that gives the same information in an appropriate interval. Update the descriptive transcript to include the switch and values.

Acceptance test. Ask a reviewer to listen without looking and state what changed and why it matters. Verify the same outcome from the descriptive transcript alone. Confirm that the replacement narration does not contradict the displayed data and that captions remain aligned after editing. The case illustrates why captioned speech and described visual information serve different purposes.

32. Worked Scenario 2 — A University Lecture with Badly Timed Automatic Captions

Situation. A statistics lecturer uploads an hour-long recording. Automatic captions exist, but crucial terms are wrong: “variance” becomes “various,” negative signs are dropped, and equations are split into short unreadable fragments. The university's publishing checklist records “captions present,” and the course is released.

Diagnosis. Asset presence has been mistaken for content accuracy and teaching equivalence. The errors are concentrated in high-stakes terminology, and the timing fragments meaningful mathematical phrases. A student using captions receives materially different content from the student hearing the lecture.

Response. Supply terminology and slide references to a caption editor. Correct definitions, numbers, units, negations, and speaker turns. Adjust segmentation so complete concepts remain together. Publish an HTML transcript with accurately rendered formulas and brief descriptions of diagrams. Have a subject specialist review sections where transcription errors could change an answer or method.

Acceptance test. Select representative segments plus all high-risk formula demonstrations and compare caption content with the lecture. Ask someone who cannot rely on sound to explain the method and answer a sample question. Check that the revised captions belong to the correct video edition. The result should be more than an improved automated confidence score: it should preserve the instructional content.

33. Worked Scenario 3 — A Live Town Hall Loses Its Caption Service

Situation. A local government streams a town hall with synchronized video and sound. Live captions function for the first twenty minutes and then stop after a captioning connection failure. Speakers continue reading questions about a public benefit application. The stream archive later contains no record of the interruption.

Diagnosis. A real-time accessibility service has failed at the moment it is needed. The fact that the recording can be captioned after the meeting does not necessarily restore live participation for people who needed text while decisions and questions were occurring. The failure involves service operations, not simply caption-file authoring.

Response. Assign staff to monitor a live viewer-facing caption feed, not merely confirm that a vendor is connected. Establish a fallback: announce the service interruption through accessible text channels, pause or adjust the meeting when appropriate, and restore access before continuing with material requiring participation. Record the incident, preserve questions, and publish a corrected accessible archive promptly.

Acceptance test. Run a scheduled failure drill: disconnect the caption feed and observe whether the host detects it, communicates it, restores the service, and protects participants' opportunity to respond. Distinguish a live-service outcome from subsequent archive quality.

34. Worked Scenario 4 — An Interview with Overlapping Speakers and Music

Situation. A documentary segment features three interviewees, an off-camera question, and music that changes abruptly before a revelation. The captions contain accurate words but no speaker labels or meaningful sound descriptions. A person unable to hear cannot determine whether the last statement was made by the interviewer or an interviewee.

Diagnosis. Literal transcription has not preserved relationships between utterances and the story. Meaningful non-speech audio and speaker identity are missing. In addition, captions are positioned over the only visible name identifiers, so reading both the captions and lower thirds is difficult.

Response. Add concise speaker labels at transitions where identity is ambiguous, describe a significant musical change if it carries narrative meaning, and move captions to avoid obscuring names. Review punctuation and overlap so the sequence remains comprehensible. Ensure the caption policy works in monochrome and in players without sophisticated positioning.

Acceptance test. Watch with sound off and identify who said each contested line, why the scene changed, and what the significant sound implied. Test the same segment on a narrow viewport. The goal is not to annotate all music continuously but to preserve the audible context necessary to follow the argument.

35. Worked Scenario 5 — A Silent Maintenance Demonstration

Situation. A machine-maintenance clip has no narration. It shows a technician removing a guard, testing a latch, and replacing a panel. A team assumes it has no accessibility obligations because there is no dialogue to caption, so the media page contains only a title and playback button.

Diagnosis. The clip is a prerecorded video-only instructional resource. Its information is located in the action sequence, not in speech. The absence of audio does not mean the information has no need for an accessible alternative.

Response. Create an accessible step-by-step text alternative describing the necessary actions and visible verification points, or create an audio narration that conveys the same information. Where the actions involve safety, confirm technical accuracy with an authorized subject expert; accessibility editing must not invent procedural instructions. Consider producing a narrated version as the default so the instructions are accessible to more people.

Acceptance test. Without watching the clip, ask a qualified reviewer to explain the sequence and identify the key verification cues based on the alternative. Confirm that the text is navigable by headings and lists. The case demonstrates the distinct WCAG treatment of prerecorded video-only media.

36. Worked Scenario 6 — A Multilingual Caption Menu That Lies

Situation. A platform offers “English,” “Español,” and “Audio Description” in a single menu. English captions work; the Spanish item contains only an automatic translation of dialogue, omitting important alarm cues; the “Audio Description” selection loads a timed text file that the player never speaks. The selection UI looks polished and passes a superficial visual inspection.

Diagnosis. The asset names are not reliable descriptions of the experiences they provide. Translation and full accessibility captions have been conflated. A description text track has been mistaken for working audible description, despite the player's lack of functionality.

Response. Correct the menu labels, revise the Spanish access provision according to the intended function, and use a player-supported described audio or described video asset for the audible description. Keep the descriptive transcript accessible as an additional text option. Test each selection using the actual target platform and, for descriptions, with the display out of view.

Acceptance test. Confirm that the selection changes playback in the represented way and that each language track contains its promised information. A menu item that exists but performs no accessible function must not be counted as successful delivery.

37. Worked Scenario 7 — A DeafBlind Reader Needs a Complete Transcript

Situation. An educational program offers accurate captions and a separate described audio mix. A learner using a refreshable braille display cannot use the visual caption stream and cannot hear the descriptions. The linked transcript contains only dialogue, with no account of the charts and gestures used to teach the procedure.

Diagnosis. Two correct media-specific provisions do not automatically produce an independently usable textual account. The learner needs speech, sound cues, and essential visual information in a single accessible text sequence. The issue is especially visible for DeafBlind access but may also affect anyone who needs to read the lesson without playing the media.

Response. Produce a descriptive transcript that integrates relevant visual events, titles, numerical findings, speech, and meaningful sounds in reading order. Use headings to divide sections and lists for the actual steps; preserve precise mathematical or procedural terminology. Provide it in accessible HTML, test braille navigation, and confirm the transcript is not merely an inaccessible export.

Acceptance test. Have a reviewer navigate the text without audio or video and answer the lesson's key questions. Inspect whether the text retains the information needed to complete the intended task. This is a case where accessible choices must be composed across modalities rather than handled in isolation.

38. Worked Scenario 8 — A Revised Course Video Leaves Alternatives Behind

Situation. An instructor replaces an outdated twelve-minute recorded lecture with a nine-minute revision. The content management system retains the old caption and transcript files. The former first chapter remains in the transcript, and cue timings now point to unrelated scenes. A described audio edition still narrates a diagram removed from the video.

Diagnosis. This is a version-governance failure. Each alternative may have passed review when it was authored, but none is synchronized with the current source. A generic “accessibility complete” status attached to the media URL obscures the mismatch between editions.

Response. Treat captions, translations, descriptions, transcripts, and signed editions as dependent assets of a specific media revision. On publication, require a version check and content review. Retire or replace obsolete files, invalidate stale caches as appropriate, and verify alternate access in the player after deployment. Keep an editorial record of which assets were changed and when.

Acceptance test. Select early, middle, and late points of the current video and verify the corresponding captions, description, and transcript, including changes to scene order and duration. Check alternate language tracks and downloaded editions. The lesson is that accessible publication must remain accessible throughout the content lifecycle.

39. A Ten-Step Media Accessibility Evaluation Framework

This framework combines classification, normative requirements, content review, actual operation, and remediation ownership. It is intended for educational and publishing teams; it is not itself a formal certification standard. The steps should be repeated after substantive changes to media content, captioning, or player implementation.

Step Required decision Evidence to retain
1. Classify media Audio-only, video-only, synchronized; live or prerecorded Media inventory and content purpose
2. Establish target obligations Relevant WCAG 2.2 criterion and institutional requirements Requirement map with versions and exceptions
3. Inventory sound meaning Speech, speakers, alarms, sound effects, meaningful music Audio-information notes
4. Inventory visual meaning Charts, changes, gestures, onscreen text and actions Visual-information register
5. Choose alternatives Captions, transcript, description, signed content, languages Access provision plan
6. Author accessible assets Produce language-appropriate, meaningful deliverables Asset files with named owners
7. Review accuracy and timing Validate technical and semantic correctness Editorial corrections and review notes

A program should prioritize defects by their effect on understanding and task completion. Missing emergency instructions are a different risk from a minor typo in background music notation. That does not make minor issues irrelevant; it establishes a rational remediation order while preserving the full accessibility objective. Where a formal conformance claim is made, evaluate the applicable criteria across the complete scope, not only the high-priority defect list.

41. Applied Exercises and Review Questions

These exercises can be completed in a controlled training environment using rights-cleared sample media or material created by the learner. Record assumptions, target conformance level, user tasks, and relevant observations. Do not use a public video as proof of compliance merely because an exercise finds one passing feature.

  1. Caption audit: Select two minutes of instructional video. Compare the full audible content against its captions. Identify at least five substantive categories of information to check: dialogue, terms, negations, speakers, and meaningful sounds. Record errors and propose corrected cues.
  2. Visual-information inventory: Watch a short training segment without sound, then listen without seeing it. List all pieces of significant information that exist only in the image and decide which should be included in integrated narration, separate description, or a descriptive transcript.
  3. WebVTT construction: Write a three-cue .vtt file with a valid WEBVTT header, times, speaker text, and a meaningful nonspeech sound. Explain how cue alignment affects a learner's understanding of the instruction.
  4. Transcript independence: Convert a short media script into a semantic HTML transcript. Include speaker turns, at least two visual descriptions, and headings for navigation. Test whether the transcript can be followed without running the video.
  5. Description-method selection: Compare integrated description, standard inserted description, a separate audio track, and a complete described edition for a densely narrated diagram demonstration. State which method you would deploy and what the actual media player must support.
  6. Player-operation test: With a keyboard and a screen reader, evaluate whether a player enables caption selection, pause, seek, description activation, and transcript discovery. Record whether each control's announced name and state match what happens.
  7. Live contingency exercise: Draft a procedure for a university livestream when captions stop, including detection, participant notice, event handling, restoration, and archive repair. Identify the person responsible for each action.
  8. Multilingual comparison: Compare a translated subtitle file and a complete accessibility caption file for the same material. Identify which sound and speaker information the translation does or does not provide.
  9. Revision-control exercise: A video removes 90 seconds from its middle. Design a checklist for updating captions, transcripts, audio descriptions, subtitles, and the publication record before releasing the edit.
  10. Standards classification: For a prerecorded audio podcast, a silent tutorial, a live video conference, and a prerecorded audiovisual documentary, identify the relevant WCAG 2.2 time-based-media criteria at A, AA, and AAA, including any clearly applicable exception.

Review questions: Why does a transcript not automatically replace captions? When is separate audio description unnecessary? What information does speaker identification preserve? Why can a correctly formatted kind="descriptions" track still fail users? How should live-caption interruptions be handled? What makes a transcript descriptive rather than a dialogue-only export? What evidence would show that a new video revision has left its alternatives stale? Why must the actual media player be included in the evaluation scope?

Editorial task: Prepare a one-page accessible-media acceptance record for a hypothetical MGU instructional clip. Include classification, alternative assets, WCAG targets, reviewer names or roles, a small content-and-player test matrix, unresolved defects, and the release decision. Such records should be concise enough to use operationally but detailed enough to support correction after an incident.

43. Related Topics and Cross-Division Boundaries

Media accessibility depends on disciplines across the Computer Science volume. Cross-references should clarify where design responsibility lies rather than duplicate every adjacent technical domain.

  • Human–Computer Interaction: investigates interaction behavior, human needs, and evaluation of systems.
  • User Interface Design: covers player interface layout, feedback, control discoverability, and component composition.
  • User Experience Design: addresses the complete media-access journey, including finding a transcript, choosing a language, and completing an instructional task.
  • Input Method Design: studies alternative methods of operating player controls and content.
  • Entertainment Computing: owns media production pipelines, streaming distribution, encoding, and entertainment platform operations; the current article owns accessible alternative content and access quality.
  • Educational Computing: owns the educational deployment, instructional outcomes, and learning-service requirements of course media.
  • Computing Ethics: examines access, equity, accountability, and consequences of excluding users from media-based information.
  • Computer Science Encyclopedia: provides the parent volume and the wider computing taxonomy.

Taxonomy rule: a video encoding defect is not automatically an accessibility-content defect, although encoding may affect caption visibility or timing. A transcript created for a course is an educational resource, although its semantic structure is a document accessibility problem. An inaccessible media control is a user interface defect, even where the caption and description assets are correct. The responsible owner should be named according to the failure mechanism, while service outcomes should be assessed end-to-end.
