Unit Overview and Taxonomy

Display Technologies studies the equipment, optical processes, electrical and computational pipelines, and viewing environments that transform digital information into visible output. The central question is not simply which panel has the newest specification, but how well an output system presents meaningful information to people performing identifiable tasks under actual operating conditions. A monitor, projection wall, retail display, smartphone screen, and near-eye headset can have different optimal characteristics even when built from some of the same materials.

In the Computer Science volume, Human Interaction Systems is a Level 3 division. The division-level subject overview is Display Technologies; this document is its Level 4 specialist unit index. The 16 linked Level 5 pages below have planned physical filenames display-technologies-articles/<slug>.html. Public page links use the extensionless form and unit indexes use trailing slashes.

Source and publication boundary: the existing MGU Display Technologies overview documents resolution, pixel density, brightness, contrast, gamut, accuracy, refresh, response, viewing angle, latency, power and immersive output. The 16 specialist filenames were proposed during the November 23, 2026 Human Interaction Systems backlog planning and are formally organized by this new index. The supplied local repository inventory shows the parent overview but does not list a display-technologies-articles/ directory or any of these 16 child HTML files. Accordingly, the active links here define editorial destinations; they do not certify that those pages are deployed.

How to use the directory: read the overview to understand the full field, select a child below by the measurement or design problem to be solved, and use the related-discipline links when the inquiry is primarily about interface design, an input modality, content authoring or accessibility. Differences between laboratory specifications and observed task success are treated as recurring research problems rather than reasons to reject measurement.

Five specialist groups: the physical/perceptual foundation; spatial detail and time; color and luminance; public/touch display environments; and near-eye, calibration and lifecycle quality. This order helps readers avoid comparing products before defining viewing conditions and the information they must communicate.

Display Fundamentals and Physical Technologies

Begin by separating the mechanisms that create light or modulate it from the perceptual tasks performed by the viewer. The foundational articles define the vocabulary needed to interpret manufacturer claims without equating a panel category with an automatically superior user experience.

Display Systems and Human Visual Perception

Governing question: How do emitted light, viewing geometry, ambient conditions, and perception jointly determine whether visual information can be understood?

Specialist scope: The image-forming chain from digital scene or interface values through signal processing, panel or projector output, the viewing environment, and human perception. Introduces luminance, adaptation, spatial acuity, contrast sensitivity, visual attention, accommodation, and the importance of a task-dependent viewing distance. Explains why a visually spectacular demonstration is not a controlled test of legibility, accuracy, or response.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns the display-side link between physical output and perception. General visual hierarchy and interface composition belong to User Interface Design; disability accommodations and equivalents belong to Accessibility.

Illustrative investigation: Compare the readability of a status panel viewed at desktop distance and the same nominal pixel resolution viewed across a control room; document angular size and ambient illumination before proposing a replacement.

Evidence to collect: Viewing distance, angular detail, ambient illuminance, viewing angle, target recognition and comprehension. Destination: display-systems-and-human-visual-perception. This is a planned child file, not a verified publication.

Display Panel Technologies: LCD, OLED, and MicroLED

Governing question: How do transmissive and emissive panel mechanisms create different engineering trade-offs?

Specialist scope: Liquid-crystal panels with backlight systems, self-emissive organic light-emitting diodes, and direct-view inorganic microLED architectures. Covers local dimming, pixel emission, black level, reflectance, response behavior, lifetime, image retention, thermal limits, manufacturing yield, repairability, and distinctions between panel technology and product implementation. Treats marketing names as product descriptors that require verification.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns panel construction and performance consequences. Physical semiconductor fabrication details belong to Hardware Systems; the interface’s color choices belong to UI Design.

Illustrative investigation: Evaluate a long-running financial dashboard showing static cells and a mobile entertainment display with variable scenes; distinguish operational duty cycle from claims based only on contrast or brightness.

Evidence to collect: Static-image duty cycle, luminance behavior, local dimming, temporal response, power and retention risk. Destination: display-panel-technologies-lcd-oled-and-microled. This is a planned child file, not a verified publication.

Reflective and Electronic-Paper Displays

Governing question: When is ambient-light-driven or bistable presentation a better choice than continuously refreshed emissive output?

Specialist scope: Reflective liquid crystal, electrophoretic and related e-paper approaches; ambient illumination, front lights, monochrome/color variants, update modes, partial refresh, image persistence, ghosting, and power behavior over realistic duty cycles. Explains that refresh and touch responsiveness remain separate properties and that bistability does not remove the cost of controllers or front lighting.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns display-output behavior in reflective and low-refresh systems. Input hardware and gesture handling belong to Input Method Design; accessible alternate presentation belongs to Accessibility.

Illustrative investigation: Select a shelf label and a real-time dispatch console: determine which content is sporadic and which cannot tolerate delayed or ghosted state changes.

Evidence to collect: Update latency, residual image, readability in bright settings, total energy per update and environmental fit. Destination: reflective-and-electronic-paper-displays. This is a planned child file, not a verified publication.

Spatial Detail and Temporal Responsiveness

Pixel count, refresh cadence, pixel transitions, and feedback delay describe different mechanisms. The articles in this group quantify what viewers can resolve, what motion actually looks like, and how long an action takes to appear on the display.

Resolution, Pixel Density, and Interface Scaling

Governing question: What is the difference between physical pixels, addressable signal resolution, and the apparent size of interface elements?

Specialist scope: Pixel dimensions, pixel pitch, density, angular resolution, physical dimensions, aspect ratio, subpixel structures, operating-system scale, CSS or logical pixels, fractional scaling, text rasterization and asset resolution. Distinguishes sharper text from smaller text and explains cross-monitor transitions and remote-desktop scaling limitations.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns display dimensions and scaling at the output pipeline. Responsive application layout belongs to UI Design; zoom and assistive magnification requirements belong to Accessibility.

Illustrative investigation: A laptop is connected to a high-density external monitor: identify why a fixed-pixel settings dialog becomes too small even though both panels advertise high resolution.

Evidence to collect: Pixel pitch, viewing distance, logical-size consistency, clipping and readability. Destination: resolution-pixel-density-and-interface-scaling. This is a planned child file, not a verified publication.

Refresh Rate, Motion Clarity, and Response Time

Governing question: Why can two displays with the same advertised refresh rate reproduce moving objects differently?

Specialist scope: Frame delivery, refresh frequency, scanout, variable refresh, sample-and-hold persistence, pixel transitions, overshoot and inverse ghosting, backlight strobing, motion blur, judder, frame pacing and flicker trade-offs. Separates refresh timing from pixel response measurement and perceived motion clarity. Treats laboratory response-time numbers as method-dependent, not sufficient proof of total motion quality.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns temporal presentation by the output device. Rendering pipelines and game software are handled by Software Systems and Entertainment Computing.

Illustrative investigation: Compare two 144 Hz displays during fast text scrolling; measure readability and visible artifacts rather than ranking them solely by a single response-time specification.

Evidence to collect: Frame-time variation, pursuit-camera motion clarity, overshoot, response distributions and flicker behavior. Destination: refresh-rate-motion-clarity-and-response-time. This is a planned child file, not a verified publication.

Display Latency and Interaction Feedback

Governing question: How does delay between user action, system update, and visible output affect control and trust?

Specialist scope: Input-to-photon timing, rendering queues, synchronization, panel processing, scanout, networked/remote display delay, feedback recognition and temporal consistency. Shows how high refresh rate can reduce one component of latency without guaranteeing low total end-to-end delay. Includes instrumentation concepts and task-dependent acceptance thresholds without asserting a universal threshold.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns the display contribution to perceived feedback and end-to-end measurement. Input devices belong to Input Method Design; application-state feedback design belongs to User Interface Design.

Illustrative investigation: A digital drawing kiosk feels sluggish despite a high-refresh display: separate stylus sampling, application rendering, connection latency and screen presentation before making a hardware purchase.

Evidence to collect: Action-to-visible-feedback delay distribution, frame pacing, error frequency and task performance. Destination: display-latency-and-interaction-feedback. This is a planned child file, not a verified publication.

Color, Luminance, and Visual Conditions

This group addresses how emitted or reflected light becomes intelligible information. Color range, color fidelity, local contrast, and ambient conditions are related but cannot substitute for one another. The same panel may produce different results after calibration or when installed in a different environment.

Color Reproduction, Gamut, and Color Management

Governing question: How do a display’s physical color capabilities, signal encoding, and color-management pipeline combine to reproduce intended colors?

Specialist scope: Color spaces and gamuts, primaries, white point, electro-optical transfer characteristics, profiles, calibration versus characterization, gamut mapping, application-to-operating-system color management, bit depth, gradients and workflow conditions. Explains why supporting a wide gamut does not establish color accuracy and why an untagged asset may render inconsistently across devices.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns display color-reproduction behavior and its measurement. Color choice as a communication design decision belongs to UI Design; color-independent meaning is covered by Accessibility.

Illustrative investigation: A design team reviews a campaign illustration on a wide-gamut workstation and a non-managed public monitor; identify the color-space, profile, and viewing-condition assumptions before diagnosing a mismatch.

Evidence to collect: Gamut coverage, white-point error, color difference under stated conditions, uniformity and profile handling. Destination: color-reproduction-gamut-and-color-management. This is a planned child file, not a verified publication.

Brightness, Contrast, HDR, and Tone Mapping

Governing question: How should luminance range and transfer functions be judged in real content rather than by a single peak-brightness number?

Specialist scope: White/black luminance, contrast within and across scenes, local dimming, blooming, electro-optical transfer functions, standard and high dynamic range, mastering metadata, tone mapping, ambient compensation, window-size dependence and certification claims. Discusses that HDR capability depends on the full signal/display chain and that headline tier labels do not describe every use case.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns output-device luminance and HDR performance. Video creation workflows belong to Entertainment Computing; contrast accessibility requirements belong to Accessibility.

Illustrative investigation: A museum display shows bright windows but loses details in dark maps: examine black level, flare, local dimming and tone mapping before increasing the maximum brightness setting.

Evidence to collect: Peak versus sustained luminance, black level, intra-scene contrast, bloom and tone-mapping consistency. Destination: brightness-contrast-hdr-and-tone-mapping. This is a planned child file, not a verified publication.

Viewing Angle, Glare, and Ambient Light

Governing question: How does the real installation environment alter the performance that a display achieves on a test bench?

Specialist scope: Angular dependence of luminance, color and contrast; specular and diffuse reflections; surface treatment; direct sunlight and interior lighting; mounting orientation; user position; lens and screen reflections; privacy filters; and spatial uniformity. Explains how a display that appears adequate front-on may fail for multiple viewers or under elevated ambient light.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns optical performance in situ. Workplace layout and ergonomic postures are addressed with the ergonomic specialist and Human-Computer Interaction.

Illustrative investigation: Place a check-in screen opposite a bright window, then evaluate legibility at the positions of short, tall and seated visitors across a day.

Evidence to collect: Reflected luminance, contrast under ambient light, angular shifts, lighting variation and observer position. Destination: viewing-angle-glare-and-ambient-light. This is a planned child file, not a verified publication.

Typography and Display Legibility

Governing question: Why do the same font sizes and layouts read differently across screens and viewing distances?

Specialist scope: Visual legibility as affected by apparent size, resolution, pixel arrangement, text antialiasing, contrast, motion, contrast adaptation, line length, font stroke construction, and viewing conditions. Discusses reader tasks such as scanning tables and following long-form text without turning this into a duplicate guide to editorial typography.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns the intersection of font rendering and physical display conditions. General typography design belongs to User Interface Design; content readability, resizing and reflow accommodations belong to Accessibility.

Illustrative investigation: A transit kiosk uses condensed labels that appear clear in a developer’s screenshot but fail when viewed from an accessible approach distance; revise test conditions before declaring the typeface inadequate.

Evidence to collect: Recognition time, reading errors, viewing distance, scaling and environmental robustness. Destination: typography-and-display-legibility. This is a planned child file, not a verified publication.

Touch Integration and Shared Display Environments

Large and interactive displays impose geometric, social and maintenance constraints beyond the usual single-user desktop. These articles examine the output system in the environment while referring input gestures and the broader workflow to their owning disciplines.

Touchscreen Display Integration and Parallax

Governing question: How do display optics, cover layers and touch sensing produce apparent disagreement between the place touched and the place acted upon?

Specialist scope: Display/touch stack alignment, optical bonding, parallax from glass thickness, calibration, lamination, occlusion, edge effects, glove and moisture conditions, touch sensing with visible feedback and inspection of input-to-visual mapping. Explains why a bright responsive panel can still feel inaccurate if the coordinate transform or viewing geometry is wrong.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns visual-output alignment and stack geometry. Gesture selection and recognizer algorithms belong to Input Method Design; adequate target sizes and non-touch alternatives belong to Accessibility.

Illustrative investigation: A public kiosk registers taps below the apparent buttons when viewed from an angle: isolate sensor calibration, front-glass geometry and visual target placement before replacing the touch controller.

Evidence to collect: Point-to-render offset, edge accuracy, approach angle, calibration drift and task completion. Destination: touchscreen-display-integration-and-parallax. This is a planned child file, not a verified publication.

Large-Format and Multi-Display Environments

Governing question: What changes when one visual workspace spans many surfaces or must be read by a group?

Specialist scope: Video walls, multi-monitor desktops, digital signage, tiled display seams, color and luminance uniformity, bezel interruption, signal distribution, synchronization, desk and room geometry, screen sharing and failure isolation. Considers whether the information remains interpretable when one panel is unavailable or several observers view from different positions.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns output topology and measurable presentation consistency. Dashboard semantics and alert hierarchy belong to User Interface Design; infrastructure resilience belongs to Networking and Hardware Systems.

Illustrative investigation: A control room replaces two monitors with a wall of smaller panels: map the critical alarms and ensure they are not divided by bezels or lost during a single-panel failure.

Evidence to collect: Uniformity, cross-screen mismatch, seam visibility, synchronization, readable field of view. Destination: large-format-and-multi-display-environments. This is a planned child file, not a verified publication.

Projection Displays and Public Information Systems

Governing question: How can projected content remain legible when surface properties and lighting determine the apparent image?

Specialist scope: Projector technology families, throw and keystone geometry, screen gain, focus, optical brightness, ambient light rejection, image mapping, maintenance, interactive projection, temporary installations and public-display duty cycles. Distinguishes manufacturer optical claims from measured in-room image results; relates audience size to viewing geometry.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns projected-image formation and environmental constraints. Content planning and presentation design belong to User Experience Design and educational or entertainment application units.

Illustrative investigation: A lecture hall has a high-brightness projector but unreadable tables during daytime: measure screen area, contrast in the room and font angular size before changing projection hardware.

Evidence to collect: In-room contrast, peak/average projected luminance, geometry, edge focus, uniformity and audience legibility. Destination: projection-displays-and-public-information-systems. This is a planned child file, not a verified publication.

Near-Eye Systems, Calibration, and Lifecycle Quality

The final articles address spatial displays and the quality practices that keep real-world output reliable. They introduce acceptance testing, perceptual comfort, maintenance and energy use, then connect physical measurements back to meaningful human tasks.

Stereoscopic, Head-Mounted, and Near-Eye Displays

Governing question: Which display and optical limitations matter when digital images are presented separately to the eyes or optically overlaid on the world?

Specialist scope: Stereoscopic depth cues, interpupillary fit, field of view, lens distortion, optical focus, vergence-accommodation conflicts, motion-to-photon latency, passthrough, see-through optics, brightness and ocular comfort. Separates display hardware and optical effects from immersive content, tracking and interface navigation.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns spatial image delivery and optical limits. XR interaction design belongs to Human-Computer Interaction and Input Method Design; alternative participation and sensory safety belong to Accessibility.

Illustrative investigation: A training headset delivers convincing depth but induces discomfort after repeated sessions: investigate fit, focus, latency and task design without presuming a single cause.

Evidence to collect: Optical clarity, binocular alignment, field of view, motion-to-photon delay, discomfort rates. Destination: stereoscopic-head-mounted-and-near-eye-displays. This is a planned child file, not a verified publication.

Display Calibration and Quality Assessment

Governing question: How can teams distinguish display configuration, characterization, measurement uncertainty and documented acceptance criteria?

Specialist scope: Calibration tools, reference conditions, colorimeters and spectroradiometers, profile creation and verification, white-point and grayscale measurements, luminance and black-level testing, pixel defects, uniformity, temporal measurements, acceptance thresholds, uncertainty budgets, maintenance schedules and longitudinal drift.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns evidence-based display testing and configuration. UI usability testing belongs to HCI and UX; WCAG conformance and assistive-technology testing belong to Accessibility.

Illustrative investigation: A print proofing workstation displays changing skin tones after a software update: document the target conditions, instrument state, profile association and before/after measurements.

Evidence to collect: Measurement repeatability, profile agreement, drift, uniformity and acceptance evidence. Destination: display-calibration-and-quality-assessment. This is a planned child file, not a verified publication.

Display Ergonomics, Eye Comfort, and Energy Use

Governing question: How should working conditions, visual comfort and operational energy demands influence display selection?

Specialist scope: Viewing distance, posture and positioning, brightness adaptation, flicker and temporal modulation, reflection management, screen placement, breaks, environmental lighting, energy measurement, power management, duty cycles, service life and disposal considerations. Avoids unsupported medical claims and distinguishes comfort reports from clinical diagnosis.

Ownership and boundaries: Owns display-related operating conditions and resource trade-offs. General workplace human factors belong to Human-Computer Interaction; medical advice belongs outside this encyclopedia unit.

Illustrative investigation: An all-day operations team reports fatigue after replacing monitors: compare lighting, placement, scaling, reflections and task schedules rather than assuming screen technology alone is responsible.

Evidence to collect: Comfort reports, task errors, measured power across realistic workloads, maintenance and environmental fit. Destination: display-ergonomics-eye-comfort-and-energy-use. This is a planned child file, not a verified publication.

Subject Ownership and Boundary Map

Display technologies are one layer of the human-facing system. Assign an inquiry to the discipline that owns its main causal question; cross-reference neighboring disciplines when a display constraint affects outcomes. A higher-resolution panel does not itself repair a confusing interface, and stronger panel contrast does not make inaccessible data charts understandable to nonvisual readers.

Boundary example: a command center reports missed alarms. If an alert exists but is visually buried, start with User Interface Design; if a bright room washes out the display, start with Viewing Angle, Glare, and Ambient Light; if the alert is entirely inaccessible without sight, start with Accessibility. Use this table to avoid the common mistake of choosing a screen by a single headline number. Most selection problems are multi-criterion: brightness may improve visibility under some conditions but raise power demand; high pixel density may improve edge definition yet create smaller logical elements if scaling is poorly configured; lower nominal response time may coexist with visible overshoot. A useful specification identifies both what was measured and under what conditions.

Worked Display Evaluation Scenarios

These eight cases are constructed teaching examples, not claims that incidents occurred at MGU or at any named institution. They show how to select the owning specialist article, isolate a cause, and collect evidence before making a purchasing or design decision.

Scenario 1: University lecture theater with unreadable projected spreadsheets

A lecture room installs a new projector rated for high output, but students in the back report that spreadsheet labels disappear when room lights remain on. Start with the real image size, audience positions, room illuminance, projection screen characteristics and apparent text size rather than the projector’s advertised brightness. Inspect focus and geometry at the edges, then compare contrast with lights in the actual teaching configuration.

Start with: Projection Displays And Public Information Systems · Typography And Display Legibility · Viewing Angle Glare And Ambient Light.

Acceptance evidence: An in-room test set with readable sample content, a maximum audience distance, observed contrast and a documented replacement or room-lighting decision.

Scenario 2: Mobile field application on a high-density handset

An inspection app renders crisp icons on a high-density screen, but technicians cannot reliably select them while wearing protective gloves. Screen pixel density does not determine hit target size or input sensing. Record logical UI scaling, target dimensions, environmental glare and the touchscreen stack before referring the input-gesture design to its owner. Confirm that visual feedback corresponds to the intended command.

Start with: Resolution Pixel Density And Interface Scaling · Touchscreen Display Integration And Parallax · Display Latency And Interaction Feedback.

Acceptance evidence: A task-based test across representative operating conditions, including visible target position and measured activation errors.

Scenario 3: Color-critical production team disagrees about final artwork

A digital publisher finds that two workstations show noticeably different image colors, even though each monitor is marketed as wide gamut. Compare source tagging, software color management, assigned profiles, white points, calibration targets and room lighting. Distinguish inherent gamut capability from the accuracy achieved after characterization; record the state of every display and application used to approve the image.

Start with: Color Reproduction Gamut And Color Management · Display Calibration And Quality Assessment · Viewing Angle Glare And Ambient Light.

Acceptance evidence: Profile and workflow evidence, controlled visual references and repeatable color measurements at accepted targets.

Scenario 4: Control room loses alarm visibility across a video wall

A plant control room spans alerts across a multi-panel wall. Staff at side stations report that alarm colors shift and critical text intersects panel seams. Assess viewing angles, cross-panel luminance and color mismatch, seam placement and time synchronization. Verify that alarms remain visible after a panel fails, then involve the dashboard team to redesign placement where hardware constraints cannot be eliminated.

Start with: Large Format And Multi Display Environments · Viewing Angle Glare And Ambient Light · Typography And Display Legibility.

Acceptance evidence: Observed alarm recognition from multiple positions, a panel-failure rehearsal and an installation-specific visual acceptance matrix.

Scenario 5: Research lab compares smooth scrolling at equal refresh rates

Two 120 Hz monitors both satisfy the advertised frame cadence, but scrolling plots appear sharper on one and show bright halos on the other. Check frame pacing, pixel transitions, overdrive behavior, sample-and-hold persistence, software presentation mode and any backlight strobing. A single response-time specification is insufficient to explain the perceived motion result.

Start with: Refresh Rate Motion Clarity And Response Time · Display Latency And Interaction Feedback.

Acceptance evidence: Matched motion patterns, artifact observations and measurements under documented refresh and overdrive settings.

Scenario 6: Public electronic signage must minimize operating cost

A transport operator considers replacing a continuously powered display with an electronic-paper sign for timetables. Compare how often the information changes, whether emergency notices must appear instantly, readability under sunlight and artificial light, residual images, environmental limits and energy use per realistic update schedule. A low-refresh panel may fit stable signs but not every safety-critical alert.

Start with: Reflective And Electronic Paper Displays · Display Ergonomics Eye Comfort And Energy Use · Display Systems And Human Visual Perception.

Acceptance evidence: An update-rate contract, emergency-response test, readability observations and measured energy per operating cycle.

Scenario 7: Remote stylus workspace feels delayed despite a premium display

An illustrator uses a stylus with a remotely hosted graphics workstation. The local monitor offers a high refresh rate, yet pen strokes visibly trail the hand. Break total delay into stylus sampling, application/rendering, encoder, network, decoding, queueing and display stages. Replacing only the panel may achieve little if the bottleneck is remote processing or transport. Validate with representative strokes, not menu animations.

Start with: Display Latency And Interaction Feedback · Touchscreen Display Integration And Parallax · Refresh Rate Motion Clarity And Response Time.

Acceptance evidence: A trace of the entire interaction pipeline, measured delays at stages and an improvement test after the highest-impact bottleneck is addressed.

Scenario 8: Immersive training causes discomfort in a mixed group

A training team uses a near-eye display and finds that some participants report blur, disorientation or fatigue after several exercises. Separate fit, binocular alignment, lens clarity, refresh and motion-to-photon latency from content motion, interaction demands and duration. Provide a conventional alternative while investigating, and avoid inferring a medical diagnosis from the device report alone.

Start with: Stereoscopic Head Mounted And Near Eye Displays · Display Ergonomics Eye Comfort And Energy Use · Display Calibration And Quality Assessment.

Acceptance evidence: Device configuration and comfort observations, task-completion evidence, opt-out pathways and a repeatable acceptance protocol.

Display Selection and Evaluation Framework

Use these twelve gates for either new equipment procurement or diagnosis of a deployed display. The order is intentional: first define the visual task and conditions, then translate them into measurable requirements, and only afterward compare technology options. Document where results are inferred, measured or reported by users; these are not equivalent evidence types.

Decision gatePractical questionEvidence / acceptance artifact
1. Identify the human taskWhich information, action or decision must the viewer accomplish, and what are the consequences of missing it?A documented task, user groups, environment and definition of successful perception.
2. Record viewing geometryWhere will people stand or sit, how far away, and at what range of angles?An installation drawing or field measurement with representative observer positions.
3. Characterize ambient conditionsWhat variations in light, reflections and surrounding surfaces will affect the image?Lighting and reflection observations at peak and normal operating conditions.
4. Define the output workloadIs the content mostly static text, moving graphics, color-critical images, live alerts or stereoscopic material?Representative content set and duty-cycle assumptions rather than a single demo loop.
5. Establish usable image detailWhat apparent text and symbol size can users recognize at the intended distance?Scaling and legibility tests plus screen dimensions and effective angular resolution.
6. Determine temporal requirementsWhich refresh, motion clarity and action-feedback delays are important to task completion?Temporal measurements and user-task observations under realistic frame delivery.
7. Specify luminance and color needsDoes the content require particular black levels, highlight handling, color fidelity or gamut?Documented target lighting, profiles and test images with measurement criteria.
8. Check accessibility and alternativesDoes essential information remain available if the display cannot be perceived or comfortably used?A handoff to accessibility testing, zoom/reflow and nonvisual or alternate-channel requirements.
9. Account for interaction integrationIf touch, pen, controller or gaze is involved, do visible targets align with sensed input?Cross-modality tests, registration measurements and a plan for alternative controls.
10. Test representative configurationsHave vendor claims been evaluated at the actual settings, mounting positions and input conditions?Site-specific acceptance checks; instrument settings and test dates recorded.
11. Evaluate lifecycle and resource costWhat happens under long duty cycles, changing lighting, software updates, failures and power restrictions?Maintenance, energy, drift, retention, replacement and warranty assumptions.
12. Decide, document and revisitWhich trade-offs are accepted, who owns them, and when must the decision be reviewed?An explicit decision record with evidence, limitations, owner and follow-up milestones.

Interpretation rule: a certification, display model name or specification value supports only the property and test conditions it actually covers. A product’s advertised high dynamic range does not demonstrate legibility in a bright lobby; a high refresh rate does not validate a remote stylus workflow; a wide color gamut does not prove color-managed accuracy. Validate the complete operational pipeline.

Governance handoff: when a proposed display changes a user task, involve User Experience Design; when a display feature must be accessible without sight or precise touch, involve Accessibility; and when reliability depends on distributed transport, involve Networking Systems.
