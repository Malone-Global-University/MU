Collection Overview

The Educational Computing unit belongs to the Applications of Computing division of the Computer Science volume of the MGU Encyclopedia. It is subordinate to the existing Educational Computing overview, which introduces the use of digital systems for instruction, assessment, student records, institutional operations, and accountable educational services. This page extends that summary through a specialist directory; it does not replace the parent or redefine the wider division.

Educational computing is a coordinated institutional system, not merely the selection of a website for lessons. A learning management system may host a course, while a student information system authoritatively records enrollment and grades; an identity service governs permissions, assessment tools report results, a credentialing system describes achievements, and procurement and accessibility policies affect every stage. These components interact, but none should be assumed to share data, legal authority, or educational purpose automatically.

This page is the Level 4 unit index for 14 planned Level 5 specialist subarticles. It preserves all fourteen titles and intended child destinations from the uploaded index and organizes them into four editorial groups: teaching platforms and curriculum; student records and integration; assessment and learning support; and accessibility and governance. Every child URL is active so the editorial destination is clear before publication. Active links do not establish that the substantive child articles exist online.

The subject must be approached from both technical and educational perspectives. A system can correctly transmit data while applying a flawed assessment rubric; it can produce a high-performing predictive score while failing to improve learner outcomes; or it can meet a procurement checklist while remaining unusable for a learner with an assistive technology. Each specialist should specify its operating role, evidence requirements, participants, institutional responsibilities, and failure modes before recommending tools or metrics.

Teaching Platforms, Curriculum, and Learning Environments

These entries describe how educational experiences are assembled, presented, revised, and made available to learners. They treat the LMS as a delivery and coordination tool rather than a replacement for curriculum design; they separate content stewardship from a course shell, and distinguish simulated practice from proof of competence in the physical world.

1. Learning Management Systems and Course Architecture

A course platform organizes learning activities, schedules, participants, feedback, and evidence of completion. The specialist article should separate the logical course design from vendor-specific screen layouts: content modules, release conditions, assessments, instructor workflows, discussion areas, grading views, and accessibility checks can be configured differently in each product. It should distinguish LMS course membership from the registrar’s authoritative enrollment record and explain why a transfer of grades or attendance is an integration event rather than a simple copy of an interface label. A well-designed course has accessible navigation, explicit sequencing, defined learner support channels, and a sustainable archival policy. Assess its reliability through actual course tasks and role-based tests, not just the presence of a feature in a purchasing brochure.

2. Digital Curriculum and Open Educational Resources

Digital curriculum involves more than converting printed pages into files. This article should show how learning objectives, prerequisites, content versions, reusable activities, metadata, accessibility, attribution, and assessment alignment form a maintainable collection. Open educational resources carry identifiable licensing conditions that must be checked before reuse or adaptation; “available online” does not automatically mean “openly licensed.” Examples should distinguish a source lesson from a localized revision, and a student-facing reading from the instructor’s answer key or grading criteria. Portability depends both on content formats and on the ability to reconstruct context when material moves between course platforms. Editorial coverage should focus on content stewardship and reuse, not recapitulate all of instructional theory or general copyright law.

3. Online, Hybrid, and Mobile Learning Systems

Online and hybrid delivery changes which activities occur together, which can be asynchronous, and how participation is recorded. A useful specialist study compares on-campus, remote, blended, and mobile-first designs in terms of connectivity, device constraints, interaction, scheduling, and support needs. A low-bandwidth design may offer downloadable readings, asynchronous submissions, captions or transcripts, and graceful recovery after an interrupted upload; those accommodations must still preserve assessment integrity and student privacy. The article should distinguish engagement indicators from demonstrated mastery, and technical availability from educational access. Course completion may depend on accessible practice, instructor response capacity, and institutional continuity plans just as much as reliable web hosting. Different learners may legitimately require different modalities for the same objective.

4. Educational Simulations and Virtual Laboratories

A virtual laboratory supports learning through simulated equipment, experiments, environments, or constrained interactive tasks. This study should assess educational fidelity against the specific skill being taught rather than treat photorealistic graphics as an automatic measure of scientific accuracy. A simulation can safely expose difficult or expensive scenarios but may conceal physical uncertainty, unsafe operating practices, or material constraints if the model is over-simplified. Students should know which parameters are idealized, which outputs are computed, and what observations would differ in a real laboratory. The article should also address accessibility alternatives, offline or low-bandwidth participation, learner feedback, reproducible experiment settings, and assessment of skill transfer. The neighboring Scientific Computing unit retains the deeper algorithms used to construct simulations.

Student Records, Identity, Interoperability, and Credentials

These entries establish the institutional data and trust boundaries: which system is authoritative, how people receive access, how tools exchange records, and how academic claims may be issued and verified. Good system integration needs stable identifiers, provenance, permissions, exception handling, and accountable record correction.

1. Student Information Systems and Academic Records

A student information system generally holds the authoritative institutional records for registration, program membership, academic standing, and transcript-related processes. A specialist treatment should explain the differences among a draft grade in a course platform, a submitted final grade, an approved change, and a certified academic record. The system needs stable identifiers, effective dates, correction histories, restricted roles, and clear reconciliation when an external learning tool supplies data. The article should examine data stewardship, audit trails, retention schedules, and the consequences of mismatched enrollment periods or course section codes. Different institutions and jurisdictions impose different recordkeeping requirements; any example policy should be labeled with its scope rather than presented as universally binding.

2. Educational Identity, Rostering, and Access

Educational identity links a person to the right set of accounts and permissions across platforms. The planned article should trace admission or registration through account activation, role assignment, course rostering, transfer between sections, suspension, graduation, and eventual deprovisioning. It must distinguish authentication from authorization: proving an identity does not itself grant access to every course, grade record, or administrative function. Students, faculty, teaching assistants, guardians, and contractors may need different visibility. Automated roster synchronization has failure modes, including stale enrollments, duplicate persons, late withdrawals, and improper exposure of sensitive information. Good instructional examples should show who owns each change, how exceptions are resolved, and how staff can detect unauthorized access without retaining unnecessary personal details.

3. Educational Interoperability and LTI

Institutional education technology often depends on several connections that solve distinct problems. Learning Tools Interoperability (LTI) concerns integrations such as launching an external tool in a learning-platform context; rostering formats such as OneRoster address sharing class membership and related information; learning-event standards may describe activities and outcomes. These mechanisms must not be presented as interchangeable. The specialist article should diagram identity claims, authorized scopes, grade-return pathways, event schemas, and trusted system boundaries without making undocumented claims about a particular vendor’s implementation. A launch working in a test account does not establish secure roster updates, durable grade sync, or privacy compliance. Contract tests should cover renamed courses, removed participants, missing permissions, retries, and rollback after failed upgrades.

4. Digital Credentials and Learner Portfolios

Digital credentials may describe degrees, certificates, badges, competencies, or documented experiences, while learner portfolios organize supporting evidence across time. The specialist article should separate an issuer’s authorization to make a claim from the format used to transport or verify it. A PDF copy, a database record, and a cryptographically verifiable credential can have different authenticity and revocation properties; none is automatically proof that the claimed learning occurred. Useful coverage includes recipient identity matching, issuance criteria, correction and replacement, expiration where applicable, verification workflows, privacy-preserving sharing, and long-term portability when an institution changes platforms. Learner agency matters: a portable artifact should communicate what the qualification actually means and should not disclose unrelated confidential academic records.

Assessment, Analytics, and Adaptive Instruction

These entries examine how learning evidence is elicited, processed, and used. Their common lesson is that technical events and algorithmic estimates are not automatically valid measures of learning. Instrument design, privacy, interpretation, fairness, and actual support interventions determine whether a computational output becomes educationally useful.

1. Digital Assessment and Item Interoperability

Digital assessment systems manage prompts, response formats, item metadata, test assembly, scoring, feedback, and results exchange. This article should introduce the difference between formative practice and high-stakes judgment, then explain how item banks and transfer formats such as QTI support reuse across compatible tools. Interoperability does not guarantee equivalent scoring: partial credit, randomization rules, time limits, item dependencies, and accommodation settings require explicit agreements and testing. The article should also distinguish correct technical marking from educational validity, reliability, and fair interpretation. Versioned question banks need safeguards against inadvertent answer disclosure, obsolete distractors, and grade changes after item corrections. The intended contribution is the assessment technology boundary, not an all-purpose treatise on psychometrics.

2. Learning Analytics and Student Support Systems

Learning analytics transforms logs and course records into indicators intended to support instruction or student services. The specialist article should distinguish a click, a viewed page, a submitted assignment, and an achieved learning outcome: these are not equivalent measurements. Before creating a risk score, investigators must identify the decision it will inform, available support capacity, data permissions, missed cases, and the burden of false alarms. Dashboards should present data quality, uncertainty, and meaningful comparisons rather than a single unexplained color. Human review and intervention evaluation are essential, particularly when attendance patterns or device access correlate with circumstances beyond learner control. The subject should emphasize responsible student support, not surveillance, and identify when a model must not be treated as a disciplinary finding.

3. Adaptive Learning and Intelligent Tutoring

Adaptive learning systems alter questions, explanations, pacing, or practice based on an estimated learner state. A specialist account should compare explicit rules, mastery thresholds, knowledge tracing, item-response-informed approaches, and more elaborate tutoring systems without assuming any one method reliably infers understanding. It should show how the adaptation loop uses evidence, selects the next task, records the response, and revises its estimate. Sparse data, guessing, practice effects, changes in curriculum, and accessibility differences can make estimates unreliable. Useful evaluation distinguishes immediate exercise accuracy from durable learning, equitable opportunities, and performance on independent assessments. Instructor override, clear feedback, and fallback activities are important design choices, particularly when an adaptive rule might keep a learner from accessing intended material.

Accessibility, AI Oversight, and Institutional Governance

These entries define cross-cutting responsibilities that cannot be delegated entirely to a vendor: accessible participation, review of AI-generated or AI-assisted decisions, purchasing controls, and long-term accountability. They provide policies and evaluation processes to guide implementation of the other specialist systems.

1. Educational Accessibility and Universal Design

Accessibility is a practical requirement for learners who use varied devices, sensory modalities, motor controls, and assistive technologies. This study should connect technical web-content criteria, including WCAG, with educational approaches such as Universal Design for Learning while clarifying that the two frameworks serve different purposes. Workflows include accessible documents, keyboard operability, meaningful heading structure, text alternatives, captions, compatible math notation, and accessible quiz interactions. An instructor may also need a process to deliver specific accommodations that the general course design does not provide. Accessibility cannot be certified by a single automated scan, and a vendor’s stated support does not prove that every configuration is usable. The intended article should offer observation-based testing using common learner tasks, alongside institutional responsibilities and respectful feedback mechanisms.

2. Artificial Intelligence Governance in Education

AI-assisted education includes drafting practice material, providing feedback, supporting staff workflows, and sometimes evaluating student submissions. The planned specialist study should establish which uses are permitted, which data are sent to a third party, whether outputs are checked, and how learners can challenge consequential errors. Automated generation can produce plausible but incorrect explanations or biased grading suggestions; therefore institutional policies should distinguish assistance from final authoritative decisions. Coverage should address academic integrity, disclosure expectations, accessibility, information security, staff training, procurement obligations, and continuous monitoring of system behavior. A risk register needs ownership and escalation routes, not merely a statement that staff should “use AI responsibly.” The article should not assume that generative output is a verified record or substitute for a teacher’s professional judgment.

3. Educational Technology Procurement and Governance

Institutional procurement must evaluate educational fit, security, privacy, accessibility, supportability, integrations, financial exposure, and exit costs together. The specialist article should describe a staged process from defined learner and instructor requirements through pilot evaluation, data agreements, vendor due diligence, contract negotiation, implementation, renewal, and retirement. Vendor claims about compliance or interoperability should be verified against the institution’s actual configuration and obligations. A complete cost picture includes migration, staff training, accessible alternatives, identity management, data export, help-desk demands, and service continuity, not just a per-student subscription fee. Governance requires named owners for incidents, policy exceptions, academic records, and periodic outcomes review. Evaluation should include observable instructional results and constraints, not a technology-first assumption that any new platform improves teaching.

Boundary rule: distinguish what a system records, what it is authorized to decide, and what its measurements can validly establish. An educational platform can report that a learner opened a lesson; only an appropriately designed assessment may support a claim of demonstrated competence, and the institution remains responsible for the validity of the resulting academic record.

Illustrative Educational Computing Scenarios

The following are constructed instructional examples, not accounts of specific MGU systems, deployed integrations, audited compliance, or actual student records. They demonstrate how to locate the right specialist topic and reason across institutional boundaries.

Example 1 — Launching a low-bandwidth online course

A department intends to publish a mobile-accessible course for learners with unstable connections. The first task is to name the instructional objectives and essential activities, not select a video-conferencing product. The LMS should provide an organized sequence, clear deadlines, and accessible communication; the digital curriculum should offer appropriately licensed and attributed materials; and the delivery design should support asynchronous tasks and reliable resumption of interrupted submissions. A team should test screen-reader navigation, keyboard use, captions, file sizes, and common learner devices before launch. A log of page visits is not a measure of completion or mastery. Read Learning Management Systems and Course Architecture, Digital Curriculum and Open Educational Resources, Online, Hybrid, and Mobile Learning Systems, and Educational Accessibility and Universal Design together.

Example 2 — Posting a final grade to an academic record

An instructor grades a final project in the course platform and the score appears in the LMS gradebook. The institution must determine whether it is provisional, subject to moderation, or approved for formal posting. A controlled transfer should match the learner, course section, academic period, grade schema, and authorization. If a student withdraws, transfers sections, or receives a corrected grade, synchronization needs an explicit exception and audit process. Testing should include mismatched student identifiers, repeated submissions, omitted fields, and incomplete transfers. The SIS remains the academic record authority; the LMS supplies instructional evidence. Pair Student Information Systems and Academic Records, Educational Identity, Rostering, and Access, and Educational Interoperability and LTI.

Example 3 — Integrating a third-party learning application

A campus wants to launch an external practice tool directly from a course. An LTI-based launch may convey context and authorized identity claims, but the team must separately establish how the roster is maintained, how a score is returned, and what happens after a learner drops the course. A working first launch proves little about privacy or grade transfer. An integration agreement should state the minimum shared data, trust relationships, permitted actions, token lifetime, staff support channels, and expected failure behavior. Test a late enrollment, a removed user, a course rename, and a retried score submission. The relevant specialist treatment is Educational Interoperability and LTI, supported by Educational Identity, Rostering, and Access and Digital Assessment and Item Interoperability.

Example 4 — Building and transferring an item bank

Faculty prepare a question bank for an introductory course and want to reuse items in a second assessment platform. A compatible interchange format can help preserve prompts, response options, and metadata, but the institution must test scoring rules, partial credit, randomized variants, feedback visibility, time limits, and accommodation behavior. For example, a technically imported question may produce a different score if the receiving system ignores an item’s response-processing semantics. Content authors should version items and document any changes in the answer key so historical student results remain interpretable. The task spans Digital Assessment and Item Interoperability, Digital Curriculum and Open Educational Resources, and Student Information Systems and Academic Records.

Example 5 — Designing a student-support dashboard

A retention team observes that some learners stop opening online activities. Before treating the pattern as a risk score, it should ask whether activity logs are complete, whether students can learn offline, and whether missing activity may reflect accessibility or connectivity rather than disengagement. Any proposed intervention should have a responsible staff member, privacy controls, a voluntary or proportionate support channel, and measures of whether it actually helps. A classifier with many false positives can consume scarce advising capacity; a model may also miss students who struggle despite frequent logins. Analytics should inform human support, not impose an automatic disciplinary label. Consult Learning Analytics and Student Support Systems, Adaptive Learning and Intelligent Tutoring, and Artificial Intelligence Governance in Education.

Example 6 — Evaluating a virtual laboratory

A science course pilots a simulated instrument to provide practice without immediate access to expensive equipment. The instructional team identifies the learning objective: interpreting an experiment, selecting valid controls, or safely performing physical procedures. A simulation may model the first two well yet omit noise, calibration drift, and tactile hazards relevant to the third. Instructors should disclose assumptions, provide accessible alternatives, preserve reproducible settings, and compare student performance on independent tasks rather than rely only on satisfaction scores. A positive pilot is evidence for specified outcomes, not proof that the simulation can replace every physical lab. Study Educational Simulations and Virtual Laboratories, Digital Assessment and Item Interoperability, and Educational Accessibility and Universal Design.

Example 7 — Introducing AI-assisted feedback

An institution proposes using a generative system to draft comments on student submissions. Before pilot deployment, decision-makers define permissible input data, whether students are notified, how instructors verify feedback, which outputs may enter official records, and how to correct a misleading comment. A risk review should consider unequal feedback quality, accessibility, third-party data retention, and the possibility that a fluent explanation is factually wrong. Evaluation compares the accuracy and educational usefulness of reviewed feedback against an existing process; mere speed is not sufficient. A designated instructor remains responsible for consequential academic judgments. See Artificial Intelligence Governance in Education, Educational Technology Procurement and Governance, and Digital Assessment and Item Interoperability.

Example 8 — Issuing a transferable certificate

A continuing-education program wants learners to share verified completion records with employers and other institutions. The institution must state the requirements for award, identify the authorized issuer, determine how the recipient’s identity is matched, and retain a process for correcting or withdrawing erroneous claims. A portfolio may offer examples of work, whereas a credential asserts a defined achievement; one should not be mistaken for the other. Verification requires the receiving organization to understand the issuer and qualification, not merely confirm that a file has not changed. The implementation also needs privacy-respecting sharing and a long-term export strategy. Use Digital Credentials and Learner Portfolios, Student Information Systems and Academic Records, and Educational Technology Procurement and Governance.

Across these scenarios, distinguish an available platform feature from a verified operational capability, and distinguish a computational result from an educational conclusion. Actual implementation requires local records, learner circumstances, institutional policy, and applicable legal requirements.

Select a specialist article by the practical decision or failure being investigated. Start with ownership and intended educational outcome, then identify the evidence and interfaces needed to test whether the system meets that outcome.

Six gates for evaluating a proposed education-technology workflow

  1. Learning purpose: specify the learner task and instructional result to be improved, including students who need alternative participation modes.
  2. Authoritative sources: list systems of record, roles, identifiers, data provenance, correction procedures, and access limits.
  3. Interoperability contract: identify which system exchanges which facts, with what permissions, version compatibility, error handling, and auditability.
  4. Trust and accessibility: assess data minimization, privacy, learner consent where relevant, assistive-technology support, incident response, and human appeal routes.
  5. Operational evidence: run realistic pilots involving late enrollments, revoked access, ambiguous grades, failed uploads, and accessibility problems; assess outcomes, not merely successful logins.
  6. Institutional ownership: assign decision-makers, staff capacity, contract renewal and exit plans, records governance, and scheduled outcome review.

Evaluation principle: an educational technology decision is defensible when its proposed benefit, evidence, implementation constraints, and accountability are explicit. Public standards may inform compatibility, but their practical scope and the applicable institutional policies must be checked for each deployment. Standards and regulations should not be treated as interchangeable.

Suggested Study Pathways

New instructors and course builders: begin with the Educational Computing parent overview, then Learning Management Systems and Course Architecture, Digital Curriculum and Open Educational Resources, and Online, Hybrid, and Mobile Learning Systems. Add Educational Accessibility and Universal Design before the first course pilot, not after receiving accessibility complaints. The practical endpoint is one accessible unit with clear objectives, consistent navigation, and a tested submission process.

Registrars, platform administrators, and integration engineers: study Student Information Systems and Academic Records, Educational Identity, Rostering, and Access, Educational Interoperability and LTI, and Digital Credentials and Learner Portfolios. Trace a learner from admission through course access and grade posting to an issued credential; write down where each record becomes authoritative and how corrections propagate.

Assessment designers and faculty evaluators: use Digital Assessment and Item Interoperability, Learning Analytics and Student Support Systems, and Adaptive Learning and Intelligent Tutoring. Build a distinction between a learning objective, an assessment item, an observed response, a statistical estimate, and an intervention. Compare results against independent evidence and check which assumptions a score cannot justify.

Academic leadership and technology procurement teams: pair Educational Technology Procurement and Governance with Artificial Intelligence Governance in Education, Educational Accessibility and Universal Design, and Student Information Systems and Academic Records. The purpose is to design approval, pilot, contracting, monitoring, and exit criteria that follow educational risk and institutional responsibility rather than vendor marketing categories.

Technical and laboratory program designers: study Educational Simulations and Virtual Laboratories, Online, Hybrid, and Mobile Learning Systems, and Digital Assessment and Item Interoperability. Test whether a virtual task teaches the intended operational skill, what physical effects it abstracts away, and how evidence of learning will be collected without excluding students with limited devices or incompatible accessibility tools.

Education data and student-support researchers: begin with Learning Analytics and Student Support Systems, continue to Adaptive Learning and Intelligent Tutoring and Artificial Intelligence Governance in Education, and then return to Educational Identity, Rostering, and Access. Define the population, intended decision, access permissions, uncertainty, support capacity, and potential inequities before collecting additional data. A useful result explains what evidence warrants action and who is accountable for acting.

All pathways refer to planned specialist destinations. Until individual child pages are authored, the existing parent overview provides the common conceptual starting point. Study should proceed by problems and institutional responsibilities, not by assuming the order of a vendor interface is an academic curriculum.

Taxonomy boundary: the Educational Computing unit owns the institutional application of technology to teaching, learning, assessment, academic records, and program governance. General-purpose business workflows, cloud infrastructure, interaction design, computing ethics, and research computation belong to their already established encyclopedia parent subjects. The educational accessibility child applies broader accessibility principles to learning materials, course workflows, assessment, and accommodations; it does not replace the independent Accessibility parent. The AI-governance child likewise addresses educational decision structures rather than claiming ownership of all artificial-intelligence research.

Related does not mean subordinate: an LMS may be an enterprise system and a cloud-hosted service, but its course-delivery and educational-record responsibilities are the focus here. A learning simulation may use methods from Scientific Computing, while the educational question is whether the simulated activity achieves a defined learning objective. Explicit boundaries make the specialist directory useful without flattening the encyclopedia into repeated cross-disciplinary articles.
