Definition and Scope of Ethical Theories in Computing

Ethical theories in computing are systematic approaches to deciding what computing professionals, organizations, and institutions ought to do when the design or use of technology affects people. They provide reasons for judgments about acceptable purposes, legitimate methods, fair allocation of risks, and duties after a system causes harm. Consequentialist theories evaluate results; deontological theories emphasize duties and constraints; virtue ethics evaluates moral character and professional judgment; care ethics calls attention to relationships and vulnerability; and theories of justice ask whether benefits, burdens, opportunities, and decision procedures are fair. Other influential approaches, including contractualism, rights-based ethics, and capabilities theory, illuminate problems that these classifications do not fully capture.

Computing ethics is not a substitute for computer science, evidence, law, security engineering, or sound organizational management. An ethical judgment about a medical scheduling system requires facts about error rates and patients as well as a defensible account of what counts as fair service. Conversely, compliance with technical specifications or legislation does not by itself establish that a system is morally justified. The question is not merely whether software works, but whether its purposes, operating rules, consequences, and distribution of authority can be defended to the people whose lives it affects.

The Computing Ethics parent article introduces this wider field. The present entry develops normative reasoning in greater depth, connects it to systems design and governance, and examines how decision makers should respond when ethical principles pull in different directions.

Why Computing Creates Distinctive Moral Problems

Computers make rules executable at speed and scale. A human clerk may make an inconsistent decision about one application, whereas a flawed eligibility rule can deny thousands of applications before anyone sees the pattern. Networked systems combine records across settings, infer characteristics never disclosed by users, and make outcomes difficult to contest. Interfaces shape which choices are visible; optimization objectives select whose interests matter; infrastructure decisions determine who can participate. These capabilities generate familiar concerns about welfare, consent, truthfulness, and justice in technically unfamiliar forms.

The philosopher James H. Moor characterized computer ethics as involving policy vacuums and conceptual muddles created by computing's expanding capabilities. The important insight is not that old moral principles become obsolete. Rather, organizations encounter arrangements for which settled practices or concepts are inadequate: is a location trace a personal diary, a service log, or an advertising asset? Who is responsible when a sequence of independently designed components produces a harmful automated outcome? Ethical theories help articulate the competing claims before organizations formalize policies. The philosophical argument supplies reasons; empirical inquiry determines what is actually happening; institutional processes determine what can be changed and by whom.

Moral Questions, Empirical Questions, and Legal Questions

Three questions must be separated. An empirical question asks whether a model's error rate is different across populations, whether a warning is understood, or whether monitoring improves safety. A normative question asks what error disparity is unjust, whether monitoring is proportionate, and what persons are owed when mistakes occur. A legal question concerns the rules of an applicable jurisdiction or contract. One question cannot answer the others. A finding that workplace surveillance improves measured productivity does not establish that it respects employees; a moral objection does not establish that a specific statute has been violated.

Ethical theory also differs from metaethics, which asks about the nature of moral judgments and reasons, and from descriptive ethics, which studies actual moral beliefs or conduct. A survey showing that most users tolerate a data practice describes attitudes; it is not conclusive moral authorization. Teams should record factual assumptions, normative commitments, and legal requirements separately. That separation makes revisions possible when evidence changes without pretending that every policy dispute is a technical bug.

From Abstract Values to Operational Requirements

Values such as autonomy, privacy, safety, fairness, and accountability often appear in corporate statements but do not implement themselves. The statement “respect autonomy” must be translated into choices about notice, defaults, opt-out routes, nonretaliation, and how a refusal affects access to essential services. “Avoid harm” can imply careful testing, meaningful incident reporting, monitoring for downstream consequences, and suspension thresholds. “Treat people fairly” requires defining relevant groups and outcomes, examining unequal access, and establishing a remedy for mistaken decisions.

The transition from value to requirement involves several disciplines: philosophy clarifies what the value means; social research examines the affected context; engineering identifies feasible controls and limitations; governance allocates authority and resources. These activities must remain connected. A sophisticated fairness metric cannot compensate for a dataset that omits people excluded from the system. A privacy policy cannot cure hidden secondary use. Good practice therefore makes ethical requirements traceable to design decisions, test evidence, operational owners, and review dates.

Stakeholders, Power, and Moral Standing

The purchaser of a computing product is not necessarily its only stakeholder. A hiring system affects applicants who never chose the vendor, a campus camera network affects visitors and bystanders, and a database purchased by an employer affects former employees whose records remain stored. Stakeholder analysis should identify direct users, nonusers subject to decisions, people represented in training data, employees expected to follow new workflows, suppliers, downstream recipients, and people who bear environmental or social costs. Their interests may be very different.

Power matters because nominal agreement can conceal dependency. A worker may click a monitoring consent form to retain a job; a student may accept profiling to enroll; a tenant may have no practical alternative to a digital portal. The ethical question is not only whether permission was formally recorded, but whether the person could understand, refuse, or challenge the practice without unreasonable loss. Engagement should not treat every preference as decisive—affected groups may disagree—but a decision process that never hears the most burdened people lacks essential evidence and legitimacy.

Consequentialism: Judging Outcomes

Consequentialism is a family of moral theories according to which the moral status of actions or rules depends on the value of their consequences. In computing, consequentialist analysis compares feasible designs by their expected effects on safety, welfare, opportunity, privacy, service quality, and social resources. For example, a hospital might compare appointment reminder systems by missed visits, patient burden, costs, erroneous notifications, and accessibility. The morally relevant outcome is broader than a vendor's conversion rate or the number of automated calls completed.

Consequentialism encourages decision makers to look beyond good intentions. A system developed to widen access might create exclusion if its interface is inaccessible or its predictions systematically misclassify certain users. But consequences are difficult to predict, measure, and value. Assessments must identify affected populations, the time horizon, plausible failure modes, spillovers, and the baseline against which improvement is claimed. Observed correlations should not be treated as causal benefits without suitable analysis. Classical utilitarianism evaluates well-being in aggregate, but consequentialism also includes theories with different accounts of value and different kinds of consequences. It is inaccurate to use “consequentialism” and “maximize corporate profit” as synonyms.

Utility, Aggregation, and the Limits of a Single Score

A project business case often assigns points or monetary values to benefits and losses. Such calculations can discipline judgment by making assumptions visible, yet ethical importance is not fully represented by a spreadsheet. A small average benefit distributed over a large population may outweigh a severe and concentrated injury under a simplistic sum, while the people bearing that injury would regard the trade as unacceptable. Privacy, dignity, and democratic participation may resist credible conversion into money. Uncertainty about long-term harm may also be much larger than a forecast suggests.

A responsible consequentialist assessment disaggregates consequences. It reports average benefit alongside worst-case harms, distribution across groups, who consented, reversibility, and effects on those with little ability to avoid exposure. It considers alternative designs that achieve most of the benefit with fewer harms. When evidence is weak, organizations should distinguish measured results, modeled estimates, and conjecture; they should not artificially increase precision to make a favored project appear justified. Ethical assessment should include who is entitled to choose what counts as a benefit.

Act and Rule Consequentialism

Act consequentialism evaluates a particular action in light of its consequences. Rule consequentialism assesses conduct through rules whose general adoption would have desirable consequences. The distinction is important in systems engineering. Sharing one customer's data without permission might appear beneficial in a specific emergency; a routine organizational rule allowing unauthorized sharing whenever employees predict useful outcomes may erode trust and invite abuse. Rules establish expectations, limit opportunistic behavior, and reduce the cost of case-by-case deliberation.

Neither form makes every decision mechanical. Rule systems still require determining what a rule means and how exceptions are governed. A prohibition on all disclosure, without any provision for imminent danger or lawful obligations, may also cause harm. Reliable organizations define narrow, reviewable exception procedures rather than letting users quietly invent exemptions. Consequential reasoning is particularly useful for evaluating incident-response alternatives, recovery strategies, software release timing, and the societal effects of pervasive technology, but it should be interpreted alongside the legitimate claims of individuals.

Consequences Under Uncertainty and Irreversibility

Computing projects often scale before their long-term effects are known. New recommender systems may change behavior as people adapt to them; security controls can shift attackers toward unexpected methods; historical training data can reproduce past patterns after formal discrimination ends. A one-time predeployment assessment is therefore not enough. Decision makers should estimate which harms are foreseeable, which are hard to observe, and which are difficult to reverse once data are distributed or institutions depend on the system.

Uncertainty calls for proportional safeguards rather than paralysis or reckless optimism. Low-impact experiments can use staged release and clear monitoring. High-impact systems may require independent evaluation, stronger evidence, controlled pilots, human review, or refusal to deploy. A system that cannot explain whom it affects or cannot correct mistaken outcomes is not made ethically acceptable merely by claiming innovation. Evaluators should specify what new evidence would lead them to stop, redesign, or restrict the system and who has authority to enforce that decision.

Deontology: Duties, Rules, and Constraints

Deontological ethics evaluates choices through duties, rights, prohibitions, and permissible conduct, rather than treating outcomes as the sole determinant of rightness. An engineer may have an obligation not to deceive users, even if the deception increases engagement. A security administrator may have a duty to respect authorized access boundaries even when additional personal information would make the administrator's job easier. Some deontological accounts hold that certain acts are wrong in themselves; others assign importance to commitments, roles, intentions, and moral constraints.

This approach has practical force where maximizing a metric would otherwise justify unacceptable treatment. A platform cannot routinely take private correspondence without authorization merely because advertising revenue finances other services. Yet duties also conflict: maintaining confidentiality may compete with preventing imminent injury; obeying an employer may compete with an obligation to warn the public. The existence of a duty does not end deliberation. It demands careful reasoning about its source, scope, exceptions, priority, and the procedure for resolving competing obligations.

Rights, Human Dignity, and Autonomy

Rights-based analysis asks what claims people possess against systems and institutions. Claims concerning privacy, expression, freedom of association, nondiscrimination, due process, and meaningful participation can limit otherwise profitable uses of data. In technical settings, a right may imply concrete capabilities: control over who can access sensitive records, an understandable explanation of consequential decisions, an effective route to correct errors, or a usable way to decline optional tracking. Legal rights vary by jurisdiction; moral rights arguments are not automatically statements of existing enforceable law.

Respect for autonomy means treating individuals as agents capable of forming and pursuing their own purposes, not merely as variables to be managed. Autonomy is undermined when interfaces exploit confusion, when essential choices are hidden, or when systems make consequential decisions without realistic appeal. Genuine choice depends on comprehensible information and feasible alternatives. Rights are not absolute in every philosophical or legal system, but a serious rights analysis requires more than showing that a platform's average benefit exceeds an individual's loss.

Universalization and Respect for Persons

Kantian reasoning asks whether a proposed principle of conduct can be defended as a general rule and whether persons are treated as ends rather than merely as instruments. A team considering undisclosed behavioral experiments might ask whether it could will a general practice in which service operators secretly manipulate users whenever a metric improves. It should also ask whether people are being used as tools for someone else's objectives without regard for their agency. These questions highlight problems that a narrow financial benefit calculation may miss.

Universalization does not mean that every person must receive identical treatment under every circumstance. Different levels of security screening can be justified when the underlying risks differ materially. What matters is whether the rule is coherent and can be applied without arbitrary favoritism, and whether its justification respects the standing of persons subject to it. A seemingly neutral technical rule can fail this test if the relevant inputs are proxies for morally irrelevant characteristics or if no reasonable person could understand or challenge how it operates.

Professional Roles and Conflicting Obligations

Professional ethics attaches duties to expertise, authority, and public trust. Computing practitioners may owe fidelity to clients, employers, colleagues, users, and the public. These duties do not always agree. An engineer asked to release a system despite unresolved safety defects must decide whether schedule commitments can override a duty to report foreseeable harm. A researcher offered unrestricted access to a sensitive dataset must assess whether technical availability establishes appropriate authorization; it usually does not.

The ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct and the ACM/IEEE-CS Software Engineering Code provide professional principles, not algorithms that eliminate judgment. They emphasize the public good, avoiding harm, honesty, privacy, fairness, competence, and responsible leadership. Professionals need credible escalation channels, documentation practices, protected reporting where applicable, and procedures for declining or revising unsafe work. Loyalty to an organization is not a license to ignore the consequences of its products.

Virtue Ethics: Character and Practical Wisdom

Virtue ethics emphasizes character traits and the qualities of good judgment rather than focusing solely on acts or rules. Relevant professional virtues include honesty about uncertainty, competence, courage in reporting risk, humility before unfamiliar communities, diligence in testing, fairness toward colleagues, and willingness to correct mistakes. A skilled engineer may know the formal procedure for filing a defect report yet need courage to resist pressure to conceal it. Virtue ethics helps explain why ethical computing depends on the character of practitioners and leaders, not only on institutional paperwork.

The idea of practical wisdom, associated with Aristotelian ethics, concerns sound judgment in context. Neither indiscriminate disclosure nor indefinite secrecy is always appropriate for a newly discovered vulnerability. A responsible professional considers severity, affected users, exploitability, availability of a remedy, coordination with maintainers, and foreseeable effects of publication. Virtue ethics does not supply a numerical formula, and appeals to a person's “good character” can conceal structural failures. A mature institution supports and tests virtues through incentives, peer review, transparency, and protection from retaliation.

Organizational Virtues and the Culture of Engineering

Individual integrity is not enough when promotion criteria reward speed at any cost or when teams are punished for reporting defects. Organizational habits teach people which reasons count. If an executive publicly praises privacy but measures employees solely on data collection, the operating culture privileges extraction. Conversely, a team that documents unsuccessful tests, learns from incidents, and gives reviewers the authority to block unsafe changes develops institutional habits aligned with responsible practice.

Virtue-informed governance examines how leaders allocate time, which dissenting evidence is heard, how mistakes are acknowledged, and how junior employees can challenge technical decisions. It resists the false comfort of character declarations unconnected to outcomes. Useful measures include whether risk reports receive timely responses, whether known defects remain unresolved, whether release decisions document disagreement, and whether affected users obtain real remedies. These measures do not quantify virtue itself; they provide evidence about the conditions that either support or suppress sound judgment.

Care Ethics: Relationships, Dependence, and Context

Care ethics draws attention to relationships, dependency, vulnerability, and the particular circumstances in which people need assistance. It challenges accounts that imagine every affected person as an independent negotiator with equal information and power. In computing, a disabled resident using an online public-service portal, an elderly patient receiving automated reminders, and a child learning through software may each face constraints that ordinary usability tests overlook. A care-oriented design asks whether people are actually supported when the system fails, not only whether its interface formally offers the same options to all users.

Care does not mean sentimental preference for familiar people or unlimited paternalism. It requires listening to affected individuals, recognizing asymmetries in knowledge and power, and sustaining responsive support while preserving autonomy. For example, a welfare portal may offer plain-language guidance, human escalation, and assistance through multiple channels without depriving people of the ability to make their own choices. Care ethics makes the quality of relationships and the burden of obtaining help morally salient, extending beyond the narrower question of whether a policy is applied consistently.

Care Beyond Direct Users

A digital service may transfer work to families, caregivers, frontline employees, and community organizations without recognizing them in a cost model. When a hospital automates appointment booking, some patients may benefit while others must ask a relative to navigate repeated authentication failures. The institution's measured savings then partly reflect unpaid labor performed elsewhere. Care ethics asks whose time, uncertainty, emotional burden, and dependence are hidden by a metric such as reduced call-center staffing.

A useful evaluation records failure rates for people with limited connectivity, nonstandard schedules, language barriers, or accessibility needs. It identifies escalation paths and measures whether people who request help actually obtain resolution. Designers should also avoid forcing vulnerable users to disclose more personal information than necessary to receive support. Attentiveness to particular needs should coexist with safeguards against arbitrary favoritism and with capacity planning that makes quality assistance available at scale.

Justice: Distribution of Benefits and Burdens

Distributive justice concerns the fair allocation of resources, opportunities, risks, and burdens. A facial-recognition system may produce aggregate convenience while exposing certain communities to more mistaken identifications. A broadband investment plan may achieve high average coverage while leaving low-income or remote households with no practical service. A just evaluation must identify whose circumstances improve, whose worsen, and which differences can be defended under a coherent principle of fairness.

There are competing theories of justice. Egalitarian approaches emphasize equality in relevant respects; prioritarian views assign greater moral weight to benefits for those who are worse off; sufficientarian approaches emphasize ensuring that everyone reaches an adequate threshold; desert-based approaches examine what people deserve; and Rawlsian accounts emphasize fair basic liberties, fair equality of opportunity, and institutional arrangements that benefit the least advantaged under specified conditions. These approaches do not yield identical answers. Ethical review should say which principle it is applying rather than using “equity” as an unexplained slogan.

Procedural Justice, Recognition, and Contestability

Fair outcomes are not the whole of justice. Procedural justice concerns how decisions are reached, who can present evidence, whether rules are consistently applied, and whether corrections are possible. A benefits eligibility model with impressive accuracy still raises concerns if applicants cannot discover what evidence was used, correct outdated records, or reach a human reviewer. Formal appeals that take months while a person loses housing or medical care may be ineffective in practice.

Recognition concerns whether institutions take the experiences and social standing of affected people seriously. Groups may be misrepresented by categories chosen without their input, or excluded from product research because they are expensive to recruit. Contestability requires a real path from objection to review and potential remedy. It is not enough to publish a general statement about explainable AI while employees lack authority to overturn wrong decisions. Data access, appeals, correction, and institutional accountability are ethical design choices as well as possible legal obligations in particular contexts.

Equality, Equity, and the Problem of Metrics

Equality may require the same rule or right for everyone; equity may require attending to differences in obstacles and starting conditions. Neither concept has a single universally agreed operational definition. A security system that requires a modern smartphone from everyone applies one rule but may exclude people who cannot afford one or who cannot use it. A technically different authentication option may better protect equal access. In another setting, separate treatment could facilitate stigmatization or reduce privacy. The design rationale and consequences matter.

Statistical fairness metrics help identify patterns but do not establish the correct moral principle on their own. Equal selection rates, equal error rates, and calibration answer different questions and may be mutually incompatible under certain conditions. Before measuring, teams must examine how target labels were constructed, whether prior institutional decisions shaped the data, whether groups were excluded, and whether the modeled outcome itself is legitimate. Justice requires scrutinizing the entire decision process, not just optimizing a chosen model metric.

Capabilities, Opportunity, and Effective Access

A capabilities approach, associated especially with Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum, asks what people are substantively able to do and be, not merely which resources or formal rights they possess. Applied to computing, it shifts attention from whether software was made available to whether a person can actually use it to obtain education, participate in a civic process, manage personal finances, or communicate with others. A digital service that is free but unusable with assistive technology or low-bandwidth connections may fail to expand meaningful opportunity.

This perspective is especially useful for public infrastructure, educational computing, accessibility, and international development. It encourages measurement of actual participation and outcomes rather than raw registrations, devices shipped, or accounts created. But capabilities are plural: greater access to an automated service may undermine privacy or independent decision making if the service requires excessive surveillance. Institutions therefore need both practical measures of access and a public account of which opportunities they seek to support and which tradeoffs they will not impose.

Contractualism and Justification to Affected Persons

Contractualism is associated with the idea that morally acceptable principles must be justifiable to others and, in T. M. Scanlon's influential formulation, not reasonably rejectable by people motivated to find principles for mutual regulation. It is not the same as treating a click-through contract as morally conclusive. The question is whether an institution can offer defensible reasons for a general rule to each person subject to it. A platform might argue that comprehensive tracking improves service quality; people facing serious exposure, coercion, or discrimination may have objections that aggregate user-satisfaction figures fail to address.

Contractualism invites the test: could a person who bears the greatest foreseeable burden reasonably reject the proposed rule, considering the burdens others would bear under alternatives? A rule permitting unrestricted secondary use of health information may be hard to justify to patients who risk stigma, while a narrowly restricted, audited research use might survive a different evaluation. The theory does not supply an automatic veto for every complaint. It supplies a discipline of interpersonal justification that prevents decision makers from collapsing individuals into anonymous units of benefit.

Moral Pluralism and Conflicts Among Values

Real computing decisions often engage several legitimate values that cannot be fully reduced to one scale. A transparency requirement can conflict with security if revealing anti-fraud details would enable evasion. A privacy-preserving system can reduce the data available for detecting discrimination. A recommendation algorithm optimized for relevance may narrow informational exposure or entrench existing preferences. Moral pluralism recognizes that there may be several genuine reasons for action without assuming one master value can always absorb the rest.

Pluralism is not an excuse for indecision. A practical decision should state which interests are in tension, rule out plainly unacceptable alternatives, seek redesigns that reduce conflict, and explain the residual burden of the choice made. It is often possible to change the architecture of a problem: provide an accessible alternative to mandatory biometrics, disclose reasons for a denial without exposing the complete fraud model, or audit sensitive information without distributing it across departments. Ethics becomes constructive when it reshapes design options, not merely ranks a fixed menu.

Technology, Social Power, and Institutional Structures

Computing systems distribute authority. A platform determines whose speech is amplified, an employer determines which behaviors count as productivity, and an identity service can become a gatekeeper to employment, housing, or education. Ethical analysis should ask who sets rules, who may challenge them, who owns the relevant infrastructure, and which populations have alternatives. A well-calibrated model can still serve an unjust purpose; a technically secure database can institutionalize a surveillance relationship that was never publicly justified.

Approaches often grouped under critical ethics or political philosophy examine how the benefits and costs of computing follow existing patterns of economic and institutional power. These perspectives should be supported by specific evidence rather than treating every unequal outcome as proof of deliberate wrongdoing. They prompt investigation into procurement incentives, governance representation, data ownership, labor bargaining power, state authority, and concentration among providers. Those questions complement rather than replace the individual-level analysis of intention, duty, character, and consequences.

Value Sensitive Design and Participatory Methods

Value Sensitive Design is an approach to technology development that examines human values systematically across design. It commonly integrates conceptual investigations of values and stakeholders, empirical investigations of people's experiences and social context, and technical investigations of what system architectures enable or constrain. A concept such as autonomy therefore leads not only to a policy statement but also to research on meaningful choice and engineering work on defaults, permission boundaries, and alternatives. Batya Friedman and David G. Hendry provide a substantial methodological treatment of this approach.

Participatory methods can reveal concerns that technical teams miss: field staff may know that a supposedly optional workflow is in practice mandatory; students may explain why an automated alert feels stigmatizing; residents may identify conditions that make a portal unusable. However, participants must not be treated as an approval stamp. Recruitment, compensation, accessible participation, confidentiality, and disclosure of decision authority matter. Organizations should report which feedback changed requirements, which recommendations were rejected, why, and what remaining remedies exist.

Moral Responsibility in Complex Systems

A harmful system is rarely the product of one decision. Data may have been collected by one company, labeled by contractors, assembled into a model by a vendor, integrated by a different team, approved by management, and used by frontline workers. Diffuse involvement can create a responsibility gap if every participant claims that another party controlled the decisive step. Ethical theory asks what each actor knew or reasonably should have known, which choices were within that actor's authority, and which duties attach to expertise and control.

Responsibility must be assigned prospectively, not invented after a scandal. System owners should maintain records of datasets, constraints, intended uses, approval decisions, evaluation evidence, exceptions, and incident escalation. Procurement contracts should distinguish the vendor's obligations from the deploying institution's duties. Human oversight is ineffective when the named reviewer has neither sufficient information nor authority to intervene. An explanation that “the algorithm decided” is not a defensible account of responsibility for an institutional decision.

Case Study: Automated Student Support and Risk Scores

Consider a university that proposes a model predicting which students are unlikely to complete a term. The proposed benefit is to send timely assistance and improve retention. Consequentialist analysis asks whether the intervention produces better student outcomes than simpler alternatives and whether false alarms or missed cases create harm. Deontological analysis examines acceptable collection and sharing of sensitive information, whether students are deceived about the model's purpose, and whether they have a legitimate basis to challenge records. Virtue ethics asks whether staff demonstrate humility about the model's uncertainty and resist treating predictions as judgments of character.

Care ethics asks whether assistance is supportive rather than stigmatizing, whether struggling students can reach a person, and whether the system recognizes differences in circumstances. Justice asks whose records were represented in the training data, whether error burdens differ across student groups, and whether scarce services are allocated fairly. Contractualist analysis asks whether students could reasonably reject an opaque profiling arrangement given alternatives such as voluntary advising. A defensible redesign might restrict the model to voluntary support, prohibit punitive uses, minimize data, monitor outcomes, and provide student-facing explanation and correction. A high prediction score alone would not justify deployment.

The case also shows that an ethical theory does not identify a single engineering tool. The appropriate control may be a service policy, a model restriction, a better intake process, staff training, or abandonment of the prediction model. Ethical evaluation should compare those alternatives before assuming that technical optimization is the solution.

Case Study: Coordinated Disclosure of a Serious Vulnerability

Suppose a researcher discovers a flaw that allows unauthorized access to customer records in widely used software. Immediate public disclosure may help users understand their exposure, but it may also enable exploitation before a remedy is available. Silence may temporarily protect unprepared users while leaving them exposed without informed choice. Consequentialism examines expected exploitation, patch readiness, mitigations, attacker knowledge, and effects of each disclosure schedule. Deontology emphasizes honesty, confidentiality obligations that are ethically defensible, and a duty not to misuse access or deceive affected parties.

Virtue ethics emphasizes competence, restraint, courage, and willingness to document uncertainty. Care ethics asks about organizations with limited resources to patch and individuals harmed by unavailable services. Justice asks whether smaller providers and marginalized communities are left behind during remediation. A reasoned response often involves prompt private reporting to an appropriate maintainer, coordination with affected parties and capable response organizations where suitable, realistic remediation time, and public communication proportionate to risk. The exact timetable depends on the facts; neither indefinite concealment nor automatic immediate release follows from the theories alone.

The ethical requirement is to document why disclosure or delay is expected to protect people, what evidence supports the decision, how it will be reviewed, and when the organization will reconsider. Legal duties and organizational policies must be evaluated separately from the normative assessment, particularly when personal data are compromised.

Case Study: Workplace Monitoring and Productivity Analytics

A multinational employer proposes monitoring keyboard activity, application use, location, and message metadata to identify productivity problems. A narrow efficiency analysis might predict fewer idle hours, but the project could also produce false accusations, employee stress, exclusion of nonstandard work, and data useful for unrelated discipline. Consequentialism must include those costs and comparison with less intrusive performance measures. Rights-oriented and deontological approaches question pervasive observation, secondary use, surveillance of private communications, and whether dependent workers have meaningful choice.

Virtue ethics asks whether managers act with honesty and fairness rather than treating numerical activity as a proxy for trustworthiness. Care ethics draws attention to caregivers, disabled workers, and employees whose work consists of reflection, conversation, or support that telemetry poorly captures. Justice asks whether monitoring is applied consistently, whether its burden falls disproportionately on certain roles, and whether people have effective appeals against misclassification. A contractualist asks whether affected workers could reasonably reject constant monitoring when job-quality reviews, service outcomes, and narrower safety controls are feasible.

A better approach might define legitimate business purposes, restrict collection, prohibit sensitive inference and reuse, use aggregate measures where sufficient, validate metrics with employees, publish retention rules, and allow correction. Even this design requires deciding whether monitoring is necessary at all. The absence of a specific legal prohibition would not by itself satisfy the moral case.

Case Study: Consent Interfaces and Behavioral Design

An online service offers a large, prominent button for broad data sharing and hides the refusal choice behind several screens. The company cites a high consent rate as evidence of acceptance. Consequentialism asks whether the net benefits of personalization outweigh privacy costs, but must account for manipulation and loss of trust. Deontology questions whether engineered confusion is consistent with honesty and respect for autonomy. Virtue ethics asks whether the designers are behaving with integrity or merely exploiting user habits.

Care ethics highlights people with limited digital literacy, language differences, cognitive disabilities, or little time. Justice examines whether these burdens are distributed unequally and whether access to essential services is conditioned on unnecessary collection. Contractualism asks whether users could reasonably reject a general rule permitting organizations to make refusal deliberately difficult. A more defensible design uses clear alternatives, comparable prominence for materially different choices, purpose-specific settings, intelligible language, and straightforward revocation. It also limits collection independently of whether every checkbox can be made legally valid.

The example demonstrates why usability is not morally neutral. Interface placement, wording, default settings, and the cost of refusing a choice are ways in which organizational power becomes executable. A successful interface metric can thus be evidence of manipulation rather than evidence of ethically acceptable behavior.

Case Study: Resource Allocation in a Public Digital Service

Imagine a public agency with limited funding for modernizing an emergency-information service. One option would improve a high-traffic website for most residents; another would fund multilingual alerts, low-bandwidth delivery, and disability access for a smaller population facing greater barriers. A simple aggregate-benefit model may favor the first option, while prioritarian or sufficientarian justice may favor ensuring that those currently excluded can receive essential information. Rights-based analysis may identify access obligations and constraints that should not be reduced to a discretionary return-on-investment score.

Care ethics directs attention to people dependent on caregivers, staff assistance, or intermittent connectivity. Virtue ethics asks whether leaders are candid about budget tradeoffs and uncertainty. Contractualism examines which principle for allocating access the affected populations could reasonably accept. The agency should investigate combined and phased options, such as a lighter shared infrastructure with accessible, multilingual distribution, before treating the choices as mutually exclusive. It should record who will remain underserved, the risk of that choice, and a timetable and owner for remediation.

The moral analysis turns on the purpose of the service. An emergency communication channel does not have the same ethical threshold as a nonessential entertainment feature. Severity of harm, dependency, and the existence of alternatives should influence the standard of evidence and what counts as a justified minimum service.

Case Study: Generative AI in Institutional Publishing

A university department proposes generating encyclopedia articles with an AI assistant to accelerate publication. Consequentialist reasoning recognizes potential gains in coverage and accessibility but also the risk of fabricated sources, biased representation, unsafe explanations, and loss of reader trust. Duties of honesty require that a publication not pretend to have verified claims it has not checked. Rights and intellectual-property considerations require appropriate treatment of quotations, licensing, personal information, and attribution, while recognizing that specific legal obligations depend on context.

Virtue ethics emphasizes epistemic humility: editorial teams should admit uncertainty and revise errors rather than treating fluency as proof. Care ethics asks how misleading health, safety, or public-policy content might affect inexperienced readers. Justice examines whether historically underrepresented people are described fairly and whether low-resource languages receive weaker verification. Governance should assign a human editor accountable for factual claims, document reliable sources, maintain correction channels, and distinguish drafting assistance from independent verification.

A proper publication workflow can use AI for outlining, formatting, and candidate explanations while enforcing source checks for contested facts. Prohibiting all tools may not be necessary, but automating publication with no meaningful editorial review would transfer institutional authority to an unaccountable process. The ethical question is not merely whether generated prose reads well; it is whether the institution can substantiate what it publishes and correct it when challenged.

Comparative Framework: Questions and Blind Spots

The following matrix is a practical comparison rather than a declaration that all ethical theories are interchangeable. Each framework directs attention to a different kind of moral consideration and faces characteristic implementation difficulties.

Approach Central question Useful evidence Typical blind spot if used alone
Consequentialism What will the action or rule cause? Outcomes, risk ranges, affected populations, counterfactuals Severe individual harms can disappear inside aggregate benefits
Deontology and rights Which duties and claims constrain the action? Authorization, truthfulness, rights, commitments, access rules Duties can conflict and require interpretation
Virtue ethics What would competent and principled practitioners do? Decision culture, dissent, professional conduct, incentives Good character alone cannot repair unjust institutions
Care ethics Who depends on whom, and what support is required? Lived experience, accessibility, assistance burden Particular care can overlook consistency and wider allocation
Distributive justice Who receives benefits and carries burdens? Group outcomes, access barriers, opportunity distribution A chosen distributive metric may ignore procedure or autonomy
Procedural justice Is the decision process legitimate and contestable? Notice, appeals, records, review authority, remedy Fair procedures may still produce morally unacceptable purposes
Contractualism Can the rule be justified to each affected person? Reasons, objections, feasible alternatives, unequal burdens Reasonable rejection is not reducible to a simple score
Capabilities What can people genuinely do and access? Effective participation, access, barriers, opportunity Expansion of one capability can compromise another value

Organizations should not treat this table as a weighted checklist that automatically declares the winning theory. It is a structured aid for identifying neglected reasons, incompatible premises, missing stakeholders, and possible redesigns. Where values conflict, the record should state which obligations prevail, why, and how unresolved harms will be controlled and reviewed.

A Practical Ethical Deliberation Process

A defensible evaluation can proceed through seven connected stages. First, specify the proposed decision and a realistic nondeployment or lower-risk alternative. Second, identify direct and indirect stakeholders and whose voice is missing. Third, establish reliable facts about purpose, data, error rates, permissions, costs, and dependencies. Fourth, identify duties, rights, foreseeable consequences, vulnerabilities, and distributive effects. Fifth, compare options through multiple moral frameworks and search for designs that reduce the conflict rather than merely trade harms away. Sixth, assign an accountable decision maker and record the reasons, objections, limits, and conditions. Seventh, monitor outcomes and reopen the decision when evidence or circumstances materially change.

The stages are not a claim that philosophical disagreement can be solved mechanically. They are a governance discipline for preventing premature closure. For instance, a review might find a technically accurate model whose purpose is unjustifiable, or a morally attractive intervention whose implementation evidence is too weak. In either case, the result can be redesign, controlled experimentation, or a decision not to proceed. An ethical process that cannot say no is not a substantive review process.

Evidence, Measurement, and Moral Uncertainty

Ethical assertions depend on facts, but facts often have uncertainty. A team may not know how a recommender system influences long-term choices; a security project may have sparse evidence about rare but catastrophic attacks. Analysts should classify what is observed, modeled, assumed, or unknown. They should report the scope of available data, uncertainty ranges where useful, which groups are missing, and whether observed differences have plausible explanations. Different ethical views may also disagree about which evidence is decisive.

When a harm is difficult to measure, it should not be silently assigned zero. Loss of dignity, manipulation, chilling effects, and institutional distrust can be real even when they are absent from performance dashboards. Conversely, unverified speculation should not be presented as established injury. Review records should distinguish credible pathways of harm from demonstrated incidents and should identify research or pilots capable of resolving uncertainty. The standard of proof should be proportional to the consequence of error and the reversibility of deployment.

Translating Ethical Judgments into Technical Controls

Ethical requirements have operational consequences. If privacy matters, data minimization, role-based access, retention limits, and deletion procedures may be necessary. If fairness matters, representative evaluation and monitoring for affected populations may be required. If autonomy matters, defaults and alternatives must be intelligible, with real opt-out paths when appropriate. If transparency matters, decision records and meaningful explanations should be accessible without disclosing secrets that would compromise security. If care matters, human support and assisted access need staffing rather than being relegated to a dead-end help page.

The control must match the moral problem. Encrypting a database does not make excessive data collection ethical; publishing an abstract fairness statement does not remedy exclusion; placing a human reviewer in a workflow does not help if the reviewer rubber-stamps outputs. Controls should have acceptance criteria, ownership, maintenance budgets, and evidence that they work. Ethical design is demonstrated through changing the conditions of use, not merely through naming values in a requirements document.

Documentation, Oversight, and Public Accountability

A substantive ethical review records the system's intended purpose, affected populations, alternatives, source and quality of evidence, competing values, rejected options, approval authority, residual risk, and conditions requiring reconsideration. For consequential decisions, it may include stakeholder consultation, an impact assessment, test reports, model or data documentation, and appeal procedures. Different systems require different levels of documentation. The objective is a traceable justification and a basis for correction, not producing paperwork for its own sake.

Oversight must be proportionate to scale and significance. A low-risk internal formatting tool needs less scrutiny than a system determining access to housing, employment, education, or health care. Independent review is particularly valuable when the team seeking deployment controls the evidence and the financial incentives. Institutions should authorize reviewers to demand redesign or halt release; otherwise an ethics committee can become a symbolic endorsement. Public-facing explanations should be accurate about what the organization knows, what is uncertain, and how affected people can seek remedy.

Professional Dissent, Whistleblowing, and Escalation

Ethical disagreements sometimes remain after ordinary review. A junior engineer may identify a safety concern that management dismisses; a researcher may discover that a project exceeds the consent obtained from participants. A responsible escalation path begins with factual documentation and appropriate internal reporting when feasible, followed by independent channels or other lawful and appropriate mechanisms where necessary. The proper response depends on severity, urgency, confidentiality obligations, available protections, and the risk of exposing additional people to harm.

Virtue ethics highlights courage and integrity; deontology emphasizes duties of truthfulness and public welfare; consequentialism examines the likely consequences of disclosure; care ethics asks how vulnerable people may be affected; and justice examines whether existing channels systematically silence particular groups. These frameworks converge on a practical need for safe reporting, competent investigation, and nonretaliation. They do not authorize careless publication of personal data, fabricated allegations, or disregard of the legitimate security interests of others.

Common Ethical Reasoning Failures

Recurring errors include metric substitution, where a proxy such as engagement or prediction accuracy is treated as the moral goal; false aggregation, where average benefit hides serious concentrated injury; formalistic consent, where a click is treated as meaningful freedom; role diffusion, where no actor accepts responsibility; ethical washing, where public principles do not affect design; and technological determinism, where a choice made by managers is described as an inevitable consequence of software. Such failures are not always malicious. They can arise from incentive structures, poor evidence, limited expertise, and failure to ask whose perspective is missing.

Another error is theory shopping: selecting whichever framework most easily justifies a decision already made. A company may appeal to aggregate welfare when defending surveillance, then invoke inviolable contractual rights when users challenge the same system. Responsible analysis applies moral standards consistently to alternatives, explains exceptions, and makes explicit when competing values are being balanced. Criticism should remain open to revision: a careful ethical argument names what observation, objection, or new evidence could alter its conclusion.

The Precautionary Principle and High-Consequence Systems

The precautionary principle is invoked when a proposed action may create serious or irreversible harm despite incomplete evidence. In computing, it is relevant to autonomous control systems, essential service infrastructures, highly invasive surveillance, and large-scale automated decisions. Versions of the principle differ: some assign a strong burden of proof to proponents of risky deployment; others recommend safeguards and research proportionate to plausible damage. It is not a blanket requirement that every new technology demonstrate zero risk, an impossible standard that could itself foreclose life-saving improvements.

A credible precautionary evaluation identifies the proposed benefit, plausible hazards, severity, populations exposed, reversibility, fallback mechanisms, and safer alternatives. A small experiment with clear human control differs from an irreversible institution-wide rollout. If the organization cannot observe mistakes before people suffer substantial consequences, the system may require additional validation or must not be deployed for that use. Precaution complements consequentialism by giving greater practical attention to tail risks and epistemic uncertainty; it complements duties and justice by recognizing that some burdens should not be imposed without legitimate authority and protections.

Moral Luck, Foreseeability, and Accountability After Harm

An organization may act responsibly but still experience an unanticipated failure; another may neglect essential precautions yet escape harm by chance. Moral luck describes part of the tension between evaluating conduct according to circumstances beyond an actor's control and evaluating responsibility according to knowledge, intention, and reasonable care. In computer operations, the presence or absence of a breach is not the sole indicator of an organization's ethical quality. A firm that tests recovery, restricts access, and reports weaknesses may demonstrate responsible conduct even if attacked, while a firm that escapes attack despite reckless practices has not thereby demonstrated integrity.

This distinction matters for incident reviews. Investigators should separate whether a harmful outcome occurred, whether the harm was foreseeable, whether safeguards matched the risk, and how the organization responded. Moral responsibility does not necessarily imply unlimited blame for every downstream event, nor does lack of malicious intent excuse negligence. Decision records, testing histories, warnings, and escalation evidence can reveal whether professional duties were discharged. Ethical learning improves when organizations can acknowledge error without scapegoating the least powerful person in a chain of decisions.

Public Reason, Democratic Legitimacy, and Critical Infrastructure

Some computing systems function as de facto public infrastructure. Identity systems, communication platforms, large educational networks, and public-service portals may establish rules that affect civic participation even when operated by private entities. Here, ethical legitimacy depends partly on whether affected people have meaningful representation, access to reasons, and opportunities to contest institutional choices. An efficiently administered rule can remain objectionable if it is imposed without legitimate authority or if its operation prevents the public from exercising rights and freedoms.

Public-reason approaches emphasize justifications that persons with different beliefs can assess, rather than expecting agreement on a single comprehensive moral doctrine. Engineers cannot decide questions of democratic authority through software testing. They can, however, make governing rules visible, design transparent audit mechanisms, avoid undisclosed policy enforcement, and preserve routes for oversight and remedy. Public participation should be proportional to the system's reach. A local internal scheduling tool need not have the same consultation process as a national digital identity scheme, but even small systems can inherit substantial public obligations if they control essential access.

Environmental Ethics and Duties to Future Generations

Computing has physical and social material costs: electricity, cooling, water use in some facilities, device extraction and manufacturing, electronic waste, and infrastructure replacement. A narrow project calculation that counts only the purchaser's hosting bill may omit harms borne by communities or future generations. Consequentialism asks about the full lifecycle and alternatives; distributive justice asks who bears local burdens and who enjoys the benefits; duties and rights-based perspectives consider the legitimate claims of affected people. Care ethics extends attention to dependencies outside the immediate consumer relationship.

These concerns do not entail that computation itself is morally objectionable. The evaluation must compare realistic alternatives, including the benefits of digital substitution, possible reductions in resource use, and the effects of not providing a useful service. Ethical procurement may favor longer device lifetimes, repairability, transparent resource reporting, efficient architecture, or a lower-impact deployment model. The organization should be cautious about claims of net environmental benefit based on one metric, because energy, water, materials, and social impacts are not interchangeable. Long-term obligations require considering burdens that may appear after the current project team has dispersed.

Cross-Cultural Ethics and the Limits of a Universal Template

Much of the standard introductory taxonomy of consequentialism, deontology, and virtue ethics is developed through particular European philosophical traditions. Computing operates across societies with diverse moral, legal, political, and intellectual traditions. Relational obligations, community responsibilities, personal autonomy, public duties, and concepts of well-being may be emphasized differently. Researchers should avoid assuming that a single corporate ethics policy or a survey designed in one country adequately captures values in another. At the same time, the existence of disagreement does not establish that harmful coercion or deception is beyond criticism.

Cross-cultural ethical practice requires respectful engagement with local stakeholders, attention to language and social context, and clear documentation of how contested values shape design. A service developed for individual account holders may encounter settings where several people routinely share devices. A privacy model assuming constant personal connectivity may be inappropriate where households depend on intermediaries. Good design acknowledges these realities without accepting unjustified intrusions or exclusion as inevitable cultural facts. Ethical theories are most useful when applied with intellectual modesty and tested against the lived experience of those subject to a system.

Refusal, Retirement, and the Duty Not to Automate

Engineering organizations often frame ethical review as a choice among safeguards for a system already scheduled to launch. This framing can conceal the most important alternative: do not build, do not collect, or retire the system. A biometric identification tool may be technically feasible and accurate yet unnecessary for the service it purports to provide. A workplace scoring algorithm may produce no legitimate advantage over supportive human supervision. A data archive may create accumulating exposure without a defensible continuing purpose. Responsible organizations evaluate necessity before debating optimization.

A decision not to deploy can follow from any major theory. Consequentialism may find expected harms exceed benefits; deontology may find that rights would be infringed; care ethics may identify unacceptable dependency or distress; justice may show a systematically unfair burden; contractualism may find that the operating rule cannot be justified to affected people. Refusal is not simply failure to innovate. It can be a positive exercise of institutional responsibility when no available implementation is ethically acceptable. Retirement also requires planning: records may need lawful retention or deletion, users may need continuity, and harmful effects from past decisions may need remediation after the software is switched off.

Education and Professional Practice

Ethical reasoning should be integrated into programming, databases, user-experience design, AI, cybersecurity, and organizational decision making. Students can compare how the same design responds to consequentialist, deontological, virtue, care, and justice-based questions. A useful classroom exercise starts with a concrete system, identifies the data it uses and the authority it exercises, then forces students to propose competing architectures and explain their moral and technical tradeoffs. Assessment should reward precise arguments, sound evidence, and recognition of uncertainty rather than recitation of slogans.

In professional organizations, education should be connected to implementation. Staff need examples of when to seek approval, how to record a decision, where to report unsafe behavior, and who can correct errors. Leaders should model acknowledgement of mistakes and ensure sufficient time for review. Ethics improves when people practice justified decisions with consequences, not only when they memorize standards. Related specialist entries in the Computing Ethics collection develop professional codes, privacy, surveillance, fairness, transparency, accessibility, AI governance, and ethical impact assessment in greater detail.

Conclusion: Ethical Reasoning as Design and Institutional Practice

No single ethical theory answers every question raised by modern computing. Consequentialism demands attention to effects; duties and rights place limits on acceptable means; virtue ethics examines the judgment of practitioners; care ethics reveals dependency and neglected burdens; justice evaluates the distribution and legitimacy of outcomes; contractualism tests whether rules can be justified to those subject to them; and capabilities approaches assess meaningful opportunity. Their disagreements are intellectually valuable because they reveal what one-sided technical evaluations omit.

The practical purpose of ethical theory is not to make an institution appear morally sophisticated. It is to change decisions: to prevent an unjustified system from being built, to select a less harmful architecture, to allocate safeguards and support, to preserve avenues for dissent and appeal, and to revise systems when their effects contradict their stated values. Computing professionals exercise moral judgment whenever they decide which problems deserve automation, whose data may be used, what counts as success, and who is empowered to correct failure.

Review Questions and Further Study

  1. A predictive model improves average service speed but increases false denials for a small, vulnerable group. How would consequentialist, deontological, and prioritarian analyses differ?
  2. What factual evidence is needed before concluding that monitoring employees is necessary or proportionate? Which questions remain normative even after the evidence is obtained?
  3. Can a technically accurate and transparent automated decision still be unjust? Explain using the distinction between procedural and distributive justice.
  4. Compare how virtue ethics and an organizational control system address pressure to conceal serious defects before a product release.
  5. Describe a case where care ethics identifies a burden overlooked by standard usability and cost metrics.
  6. How could a platform redesign a conflict between anti-fraud transparency and the need not to reveal exploitable details?
  7. Why does obtaining a click-through agreement not automatically satisfy rights-based, care, or contractualist analysis?
  8. Construct a seven-stage ethical review for an institution proposing AI-assisted triage of student or customer requests. Name the decision owner and at least three conditions that would trigger reconsideration.

Glossary

These definitions are intended for ethical reasoning about computing systems; philosophical traditions may use some terms in more specialized ways.

Act consequentialism
A consequentialist approach that evaluates a particular act by its consequences.
Algorithmic bias
Systematic distortion or unequal effects in data, models, objectives, or deployment.
Appeal
A process by which an affected person may challenge a decision and seek reconsideration.
Autonomy
A person’s meaningful capacity to deliberate, choose, and act on their own purposes.
Capabilities approach
An approach emphasizing what people are substantively able to do and be.
Care ethics
An ethical approach emphasizing relationships, dependence, responsiveness, and vulnerability.
Consent
Permission whose moral adequacy depends on comprehension, voluntariness, specificity, and context.
Consequentialism
A family of ethical theories assessing the rightness of acts or rules by their consequences.
Contractualism
An approach evaluating principles by whether they can be justified to affected persons.
Counterfactual
A reasoned account of what would happen under an alternative decision or no intervention.
Deontology
A family of ethical theories emphasizing duties, rights, and constraints on permissible conduct.
Digital exclusion
Inability to meaningfully use digital services because of barriers in access, design, or support.
Distributive justice
Fair allocation of resources, opportunities, risks, and burdens.
Duty
An obligation to act or refrain from acting for morally relevant reasons.
Egalitarianism
A family of views assigning importance to equality in relevant moral or distributive respects.
Empirical ethics
Inquiry into people’s actual ethical beliefs, behavior, or lived experiences.
Environmental ethics
The moral study of human relationships with ecosystems, resources, and nonhuman nature.
Epistemic humility
Recognition of the limitations of one’s knowledge and willingness to revise judgments.
Equity
Fairness responsive to morally relevant differences in circumstance, need, or obstacles.
Ethical impact assessment
A structured inquiry into a system’s possible moral consequences and required remedies.
Ethical washing
Public expression of ethical commitments without meaningful operational implementation.
Externality
A consequence borne by people who are not party to the focal exchange or decision.
Fairness metric
A quantitative criterion used to evaluate one aspect of differential outcomes or errors.
Formalistic consent
Treating procedural acceptance as sufficient without assessing meaningful choice.
Human oversight
An arrangement in which authorized humans can understand, review, and intervene in decisions.
Justice
An ethical concern with fair claims, institutions, processes, treatment, and distribution.
Kantian ethics
An approach associated with duties, universalizability, and respect for persons.
Metaethics
Inquiry into the nature, meaning, and justification of moral claims.
Moral agent
An entity or person regarded as capable of bearing moral responsibility under an ethical theory.
Moral luck
The problem of how circumstances outside one’s control affect judgments about moral responsibility.
Moral pluralism
Recognition that several distinct moral values or principles may be genuinely important.
Normative ethics
Study of what people ought to do, what they owe others, and what counts as good conduct.
Policy vacuum
A gap in established rules for practices made possible by new technologies.
Practical wisdom
Context-sensitive, well-informed judgment about what responsible conduct requires.
Precautionary principle
A family of approaches recommending safeguards against potentially serious harm amid uncertainty.
Prioritarianism
The view that benefits to people who are worse off have greater moral weight.
Procedural justice
Fairness in processes of decision making, participation, review, and correction.
Professional responsibility
Duties connected to expertise, entrusted roles, and the effects of professional conduct.
Proportionality
The requirement that a measure’s intrusiveness or burden be warranted by its legitimate purpose.
Public reason
Justification of collective rules using reasons that people holding diverse reasonable views can assess.
Reasonable rejection
In contractualism, a test of whether a principle could be rejected on defensible grounds.
Recognition
Taking persons and communities seriously as holders of dignity, experience, and valid interests.
Remedy
An effective measure to correct or repair harm or an unjustified decision.
Responsibility gap
A situation in which diffuse roles obscure who is accountable for a harmful system outcome.
Rights-based ethics
Ethical reasoning grounded in claims that others or institutions must respect.
Risk appetite
The level and kinds of risk an institution authorizes within defined limits.
Rule consequentialism
An approach assessing conduct through rules whose general use is justified by consequences.
Secondary use
Use of information for a purpose distinct from that for which it was originally obtained.
Socio-technical system
A system in which technologies interact with people, organizations, rules, and practices.
Stakeholder
A person or group whose interests or legitimate claims are affected by a system.
Sufficientarianism
An approach emphasizing that everyone should meet an adequate threshold of resources or opportunity.
Theory shopping
Selecting ethical principles opportunistically to defend a predetermined conclusion.
Traceability
Ability to connect a requirement or decision to its reasons, evidence, owner, and implementation.
Universalizability
A test of whether the principle behind an action can be defended as generally applicable.
Utilitarianism
A consequentialist tradition that assesses actions through their contribution to overall well-being.
Value Sensitive Design
A design approach using conceptual, empirical, and technical inquiry into human values.
Virtue ethics
An approach emphasizing character, dispositions, flourishing, and good practical judgment.
