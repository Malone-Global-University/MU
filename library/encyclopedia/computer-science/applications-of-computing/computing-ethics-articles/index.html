Collection Overview

The Computing Ethics unit is located in the Applications of Computing division of the Computer Science volume of the MGU Encyclopedia. Its foundational Computing Ethics parent article surveys ethical theories, professional obligations, privacy, fairness, accessibility, safety, public welfare, and governance. This index gives readers a navigable specialist directory for deeper treatment.

Computing ethics asks not only whether a system works, but whether it should be built or used as proposed; who sets the objectives; whose data, labor, autonomy, access, and safety are affected; and what happens when an institution gets the decision wrong. A credible evaluation identifies stakeholders, plausible harms and benefits, applicable duties, competing values, alternatives, evidence, responsible decision-makers, and routes to contest or repair consequences. Ethical acceptability is not identical to technical functionality, legal compliance, or commercial success.

Under the MGU Encyclopedia taxonomy, this page is the Level 4 unit index. The fifteen linked sibling articles are Level 5 unit subarticles of the Computing Ethics overview; their four group headings on this page are instructional arrangements, not new taxonomy levels. More granular companion subarticles may be warranted later, but no Level 6 structure is commissioned here.

Ethical Foundations, Professional Duty, and Information Rights

These entries supply the normative vocabulary and professional obligations needed to evaluate technology before a design or deployment decision is made. They connect arguments about consequences, duties, and justice to concrete information practices, but do not substitute ethical reflection for the technical details of a system.

1. Ethical Theories in Computing

Establish how different moral theories illuminate a computing decision: who receives benefits, who bears risks, what obligations are non-negotiable, and whose relationships or circumstances need attention. Use a common case to contrast outcomes under consequentialist, deontological, virtue, care, and justice perspectives. No framework mechanically resolves a dispute; disagreements and assumptions must be made explicit.

Practical output: A transparent ethical justification and a record of unresolved trade-offs.

2. Professional Codes of Computing Ethics

Explain how professional codes, including ACM and IEEE/IEEE-CS traditions, guide decisions on competence, honesty, confidentiality, risk disclosure, conflicts of interest, and public welfare. Illustrate the difference between an employer's instruction, a legal minimum, and a professional's independently exercised duty of care. Include practical escalation, documentation, and responsible objection without implying that codes automatically determine every outcome.

Practical output: A documented professional rationale, escalation path, and accountability record.

3. Privacy, Consent, and Data Protection Ethics

Analyze what data are collected, the context in which collection occurs, what affected people reasonably expect, and who may receive the information later. Cover meaningful consent and its limits, proportionality, necessary uses, retention limits, secondary use, and the distinction between formal compliance and ethically justified data practices. Technical encryption or data modeling is supporting context, not the sole subject.

Practical output: An information-flow map with purposes, permissions, limits, and review points.

4. Ethics of Surveillance and Biometrics

Distinguish targeted security monitoring from pervasive behavioral surveillance and biometric identification or verification. Assess necessity, proportionality, error and differential error, consent under unequal power, retention, oversight, and function creep. Explain why high technical accuracy does not by itself justify a particular monitoring practice or settle a civil-liberties dispute.

Practical output: A necessity-and-proportionality assessment with alternatives and safeguards.

Fairness, Agency, and Inclusive Participation

These entries examine unequal impacts, the ability to understand and challenge decisions, interface autonomy, and the conditions for meaningful participation. They separate a system's measured behavior from the institutional and social remedies needed when its use is harmful.

5. Algorithmic Fairness and Discrimination

Study how sampling, historical labels, proxy variables, optimization targets, deployment policies, and feedback loops can produce unequal outcomes. Compare group-based and individual-based fairness concepts while acknowledging that desirable metrics can be incompatible in realistic settings. Connect measurement to stakeholder participation, root-cause analysis, institution-level change, and recourse rather than framing fairness as a single score.

Practical output: A context-specific fairness evaluation and remediation plan.

6. Transparency, Explainability, and Appeals

Separate disclosure that a system is in use from explanations that are intelligible and actionable to affected people. Compare model-level documentation, decision-specific reasons, audit access, and effective appeal procedures. Consider the limits of explanation when decisions depend on institutional policy, contested data, security constraints, or limited human review capacity.

Practical output: A notice, explanation, appeal, and correction workflow.

7. Ethics of User Interface Design

Examine interface defaults, asymmetric friction, hidden choices, pressured consent, addictive engagement loops, and misleading affordances. Distinguish helpful persuasion from exploitation by asking whose objectives the interface advances, whether alternatives are realistic, and whether the user can reverse a decision. Evaluate interface behavior with affected users, not only conversion or engagement data.

Practical output: An interface review with alternatives, user-control checks, and redesign criteria.

8. Accessible and Inclusive Computing

Explain barriers affecting visual, auditory, motor, cognitive, language, and situational access without assuming that all users share one preference. Study inclusive research, assistive technology compatibility, multiple interaction modes, and accessibility as an ongoing practice rather than a one-time checklist. Distinguish technical conformance from the real ability to complete essential tasks and participate on fair terms.

Practical output: An inclusive testing plan and barrier-removal priorities.

Knowledge Stewardship, Research, and AI Ethics

These entries address responsible use and production of intellectual work, legitimate experimentation, institutional oversight of AI, and trust in synthetic information. Their shared concern is evidence, attribution, accountability, and the people affected by technological claims.

9. Intellectual Property and Software Ethics

Assess ethical obligations surrounding proprietary and open-source licenses, author credit, reuse of code and data, dependency stewardship, and the public benefits of access to knowledge. Distinguish permission under a legal license from professional courtesy and broader duties to contributors, users, and downstream maintainers. Address software-supply-chain provenance without conflating open source with unrestricted use.

Practical output: A provenance, attribution, license-review, and stewardship record.

10. Research Ethics and Responsible Disclosure

Analyze informed participation, risks to research subjects, dataset handling, experimentation without adequate notice, and responsible communication of security discoveries. Differentiate research validity from ethical acceptability, and disclosure decisions from unauthorized exploitation. Include coordinated reporting, documentation, reproducibility, and safeguards appropriate to the people or systems exposed.

Practical output: An ethics protocol and responsible reporting or disclosure plan.

11. Artificial Intelligence Ethics and Governance

Examine the full AI lifecycle from problem framing and dataset selection to evaluation, deployment, monitoring, retirement, and remedy. Distinguish technical model performance from institutional justification, appropriate human authority, documentation, incident response, and accountability for outsourced systems. Avoid implying that human-in-the-loop language is sufficient when reviewers lack information or power to intervene.

Practical output: An AI governance dossier with owners, evaluations, controls, and review triggers.

12. Generative AI and Information Integrity

Evaluate generated text, images, audio, and other synthetic content in contexts where authenticity, accuracy, attribution, or reputational harms matter. Address fabricated citations, misleading impersonation, unclear provenance, automated amplification, and editorial verification. Treat watermarking or content labeling as partial measures, not guarantees of truth or ownership.

Practical output: A publication and provenance policy with evidence-checking requirements.

Labor, Global Consequences, and Institutional Governance

These entries examine power and resource distribution across organizations, workers, communities, supply chains, and jurisdictions. They close the loop from ethical concern to accountable review, documented decisions, and practical remedies.

13. Labor, Automation, and Platform Ethics

Study how automation reallocates tasks, bargaining power, income, surveillance, safety risks, and access to remedies. Examine opaque performance scores, automated scheduling, platform ranking, task allocation, and changes to occupational skill. Consider who participates in decisions and how workers can challenge errors, while distinguishing job redesign and transition support from simply predicting aggregate employment trends.

Practical output: A worker-impact assessment and participation, appeal, and transition plan.

14. Sustainable and Global Computing Ethics

Assess computing across procurement, operation, maintenance, disposal, and international supply chains. Distinguish direct operational energy from lifecycle impacts, and consider water, rare materials, repairability, export of waste, uneven infrastructure access, and power imbalances across regions. Prefer measurable assumptions and identified stakeholders over generalized claims that a technology is inherently sustainable.

Practical output: A lifecycle-and-equity assessment with measurable mitigation commitments.

15. Ethical Impact Assessments and Technology Governance

Turn ethical reasoning into a repeatable institutional process: define system purpose, map affected groups, characterize foreseeable harms, examine alternatives, appoint accountable decision-makers, set thresholds, and document decisions. Include independent challenge, change management, continuous monitoring, incident response, and accessible remedy. Separate an assessment document from evidence that controls are actually effective.

Practical output: An auditable impact-assessment record and governance/recourse process.

Ethical analysis can proceed through different lenses: normative, rights-based, empirical, institutional, and remedial. The table is a navigation aid rather than a claim that each question has one correct technique. A single real-world decision can require several rows simultaneously.

Two distinctions matter throughout: an observed statistical disparity is not, by itself, a complete explanation of its causes or legitimacy; and a documented policy or assessment is not proof that the system conforms to it. Both claims require contextual investigation and evidence of operation.

Illustrative Ethics Scenarios

The following cases are constructed instructional scenarios, not reports of MGU operations or verified incidents. They illustrate why technical success, legal conformity, individual consent, and ethical justification need separate examination.

Example 1 — A school proposes automated exam surveillance

A university proposes webcam monitoring, identity checks, and automated flags during examinations. The immediate design question is not just detection accuracy: the institution must consider necessity, alternatives, device and home conditions, disability access, false accusations, retention, and whether a student can contest a flag. A proportionate review asks whether less intrusive assessment methods can meet the educational purpose. The institution should document scope, human review authority, retention limits, disability accommodations, and a meaningful appeal route.

Relevant specialist readings: Ethics of Surveillance and Biometrics, Privacy, Consent, and Data Protection Ethics, Accessible and Inclusive Computing, Transparency, Explainability, and Appeals, Ethical Impact Assessments and Technology Governance.

Example 2 — A benefits eligibility model denies applications

A public agency uses a risk score to prioritize investigation, but certain applicants experience more denials and longer delays. A defensible investigation checks training labels, proxy features, evaluation subgroups, administrative policy, reviewer incentives, and appeal outcomes. Publishing a model card alone does not repair invalid records or restore benefits. Measure both system-level disparities and the effectiveness of corrected decisions and restitution.

Relevant specialist readings: Algorithmic Fairness and Discrimination, Transparency, Explainability, and Appeals, Artificial Intelligence Ethics and Governance, Ethical Impact Assessments and Technology Governance.

Example 3 — A consent interface pushes people toward tracking

A website places a prominent “accept all” control beside a much less visible refusal path and bundles optional analytics with an essential service. Assess whether the choice is intelligible, freely made, symmetric in effort, and reversible, and whether the data flow matches what was presented. The relevant redesign is not merely a shorter privacy notice; it may require equal prominence, granular choices, reduced collection, and retention limits.

Relevant specialist readings: Ethics of User Interface Design, Privacy, Consent, and Data Protection Ethics, Ethical Theories in Computing.

Example 4 — A security researcher finds an exploitable defect

An independent researcher identifies a vulnerability in an internet-facing institution. The ethical issue spans authorization, affected populations, safe evidence collection, disclosure timing, coordination, and public risk if correction is delayed. The researcher should avoid unnecessary access to personal data, preserve a minimal reproducible record, use appropriate reporting channels, and avoid treating public disclosure or secrecy as universally correct independent of context.

Relevant specialist readings: Research Ethics and Responsible Disclosure, Professional Codes of Computing Ethics, Ethical Impact Assessments and Technology Governance.

Example 5 — A publisher releases AI-assisted explanatory material

An editorial team drafts material with a generative system that produces a plausible citation and a quotation unsupported by the cited document. The workflow must verify source existence, claim support, attribution, rights, and disclosure before publication. The relevant control is evidence-grounded editorial accountability: a watermark or the presence of a human reviewer does not guarantee accuracy, and remediation should include corrections when errors are discovered.

Relevant specialist readings: Generative AI and Information Integrity, Intellectual Property and Software Ethics, Artificial Intelligence Ethics and Governance.

Example 6 — An employer deploys automated worker scoring

A logistics operator uses software to allocate shifts and identify low performers. Scores may depend on unreliable activity traces, inaccessible review procedures, or factors workers cannot control. The organization should examine power imbalance, safety, privacy, reasonable accommodations, error correction, worker involvement, and transition support. Productivity gains and formal employment-law compliance do not settle whether the process is just.

Relevant specialist readings: Labor, Automation, and Platform Ethics, Algorithmic Fairness and Discrimination, Ethics of Surveillance and Biometrics, Transparency, Explainability, and Appeals, Accessible and Inclusive Computing.

Example 7 — A service works well for most users but excludes some

A public service portal succeeds in standard usability tests yet cannot be completed without a mouse or under some assistive-technology configurations. An ethical review should pair technical accessibility assessment with task completion by affected users and examine multilingual, cognitive, and bandwidth barriers when relevant. The decision is whether access to the essential service is equitable, not whether the team can report a high aggregate satisfaction score.

Relevant specialist readings: Accessible and Inclusive Computing, Ethics of User Interface Design, Ethical Theories in Computing, Ethical Impact Assessments and Technology Governance.

Example 8 — A computing program claims climate benefits

A company moves workloads to a new data center and announces that its platform is “green” based on an efficiency benchmark alone. A responsible claim must disclose measurement boundaries and examine energy demand, grid mix, hardware procurement, cooling and water, equipment longevity, disposal, and affected communities. Compare alternatives and assess whether actual resource use fell, rather than assuming greater computational efficiency necessarily lowers total consumption.

Relevant specialist readings: Sustainable and Global Computing Ethics, Ethical Impact Assessments and Technology Governance, Professional Codes of Computing Ethics.

Ethical Assessment and Governance Framework

An ethical evaluation should precede deployment, be revised when conditions change, and lead to enforceable operating decisions. The following eight gates can structure reviews across product development, public services, academic research, procurement, and organizational operations. It is an educational process model, not a substitute for jurisdiction-specific law or professional standards.

Assessment gateRequired inquiry or evidencePrimary starting article
1. Define the activity and claimed benefitState what the technology does, the institution's actual objective, the proposed context of use, the affected decisions, and plausible alternatives. A vaguely defined “efficiency improvement” is not enough to establish necessity.Ethical Theories in Computing
2. Identify rights, duties, and affected peopleIdentify users, nonusers, bystanders, workers, marginalized groups, suppliers, and downstream recipients. Record duties and power asymmetries as well as likely benefits.Professional Codes of Computing Ethics
3. Map information flows and monitoringTrace collection, inference, access, retention, onward transfer, monitoring, and potential expansion beyond the original purpose. Establish who can refuse or challenge use.Privacy, Consent, and Data Protection Ethics
4. Evaluate disparate and exclusionary impactsTest relevant subpopulations and failure modes using contextual measures. Seek participation from affected people and examine how organizational choices create or worsen harms.Algorithmic Fairness and Discrimination
5. Examine user control and contestabilityAsk whether choices are informed and reversible, whether the interface is accessible, what explanations are available, and whether appeals can actually change results.Transparency, Explainability, and Appeals
6. Review provenance, research ethics, and AI riskAssess evidence sources, licenses, experiment protections, model limitations, synthetic content claims, and the limits of automation or human oversight.Research Ethics and Responsible Disclosure
7. Evaluate labor, environmental, and wider effectsInclude foreseeable worker changes, resource demand, procurement, global supply chains, and externalized burdens, with explicit measurement boundaries.Sustainable and Global Computing Ethics
8. Make an accountable, reversible decisionRecord alternatives, owners, approval conditions, unacceptable risks, independent challenge, testing, launch thresholds, monitoring, incident response, and available remedy. Revise or withdraw the system when evidence warrants.Ethical Impact Assessments and Technology Governance

Decision record: a useful record identifies the system version and use case; supporting evidence and uncertainty; who was consulted and omitted; rejected alternatives; accountable approvers; operating controls; measurable thresholds; review dates or change triggers; affected-person notification; and channels for correction or redress. An assessment should be reopened after material changes in purpose, data, audience, scale, autonomy, vendors, or observed harms.

Approval is not proof of safety or fairness. Monitoring, external challenge, incident learning, and proportionate remedy are continuing obligations. Institutions should distinguish having a formal policy from demonstrating that the policy works for the people affected.

Suggested Study Pathways

Readers can follow the tracks below as coherent sequences; links remain within the completed sibling collection. Begin with the Computing Ethics overview if the terminology is unfamiliar.

1. Foundational ethics and professional practice: Ethical Theories in Computing → Professional Codes of Computing Ethics → Ethical Impact Assessments and Technology Governance. Begin with the ethical vocabulary and professional obligations, then learn how principles enter review and decision records.

2. Privacy, monitoring, and civil liberties: Privacy, Consent, and Data Protection Ethics → Ethics of Surveillance and Biometrics → Ethics of User Interface Design → Transparency, Explainability, and Appeals. Trace collection and surveillance purposes, interface-based choice, and the ability to challenge outcomes.

3. Fairness and public-sector decision systems: Algorithmic Fairness and Discrimination → Transparency, Explainability, and Appeals → Accessible and Inclusive Computing → Ethical Impact Assessments and Technology Governance. Link measurement of disparate outcomes to explanations, meaningful participation, and institution-level remedy.

4. Product design and accessible digital services: Ethics of User Interface Design → Accessible and Inclusive Computing → Privacy, Consent, and Data Protection Ethics → Professional Codes of Computing Ethics. Study autonomy and inclusive completion before extending the analysis to data use and organizational duties.

5. Research, software stewardship, and trustworthy publication: Research Ethics and Responsible Disclosure → Intellectual Property and Software Ethics → Generative AI and Information Integrity → Professional Codes of Computing Ethics. Address legitimate research conduct, reuse and attribution, evidence-grounded synthetic media, and professional responsibility.

6. AI governance and high-impact deployments: Artificial Intelligence Ethics and Governance → Algorithmic Fairness and Discrimination → Transparency, Explainability, and Appeals → Generative AI and Information Integrity → Ethical Impact Assessments and Technology Governance. Integrate evaluation, fairness, explanation, content integrity, and review authority across a deployed system's lifecycle.

7. Workplace, environmental, and global justice: Labor, Automation, and Platform Ethics → Sustainable and Global Computing Ethics → Ethics of Surveillance and Biometrics → Ethical Impact Assessments and Technology Governance. Examine workers, communities, monitoring, supply chains, resource use, and institutional accountability.

Editorial boundary: Computing Ethics owns normative evaluation and actionable accountability. It does not duplicate the technical mechanisms of machine learning, data engineering, interface construction, or application-specific systems. For example, Algorithmic Fairness and Discrimination examines justice, measurement choices, and remedies; the Machine Learning subject develops model-training and statistical techniques. Accessible and Inclusive Computing evaluates duties of inclusion and equitable participation; the independent Accessibility subject addresses implementation and testing methods in broader technical depth.

Ethical concerns also cross every other Applications of Computing unit. A financial or medical decision system can be studied in its application unit for specialized requirements and in this unit for autonomy, information rights, discrimination, and governance. Cross-reference without duplicating the neighboring subject's technical treatment.
