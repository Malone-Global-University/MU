Definition and Scope of Generative AI and Information Integrity

Generative AI and information integrity concerns the creation, alteration, circulation, verification, and responsible use of text, images, audio, video, code, and structured information produced or transformed with generative systems. Generative models can expand access to communication and creative tools, but they also make persuasive content easier to produce without the observation, expertise, or documentary foundation that audiences may assume. Information integrity is not a demand that every statement be free from disagreement. It refers to the practices that make significant claims traceable, accurately contextualized, corrigible, and open to meaningful scrutiny. Those practices include attribution, source verification, transparent editing, correction procedures, and appropriately qualified communication.

This entry examines the ethical and operational choices of developers, publishers, governments, educators, researchers, employers, and ordinary users. Its primary subject is trustworthy information practice rather than model architecture; technical mechanisms are explained because their strengths and limits determine responsible deployment. The companion Generative Artificial Intelligence encyclopedia entry addresses generation technology, while this article asks how its outputs should be interpreted, disclosed, checked, preserved, and governed.

Authenticity, Accuracy, Provenance, and Trust

Four concepts should not be conflated. Authenticity concerns whether an artifact is what it claims to be, such as an actual recording of a meeting or a declared fictional reenactment. Accuracy concerns whether its propositions correspond to relevant evidence. Provenance describes a chain of creation, modification, transfer, or publication claims. Trustworthiness is the audience's justified confidence in a particular source or assertion given its provenance, conduct, methods, and independent corroboration. Each can diverge from the others. An authentic photograph can be captioned with the wrong date; a synthetic diagram can accurately explain a scientific mechanism; a correctly signed document can contain false numbers.

These distinctions determine the right response to uncertainty. Authenticity checks can assess whether files and signed metadata correspond; factual review must test claims against independent evidence; editorial review evaluates whether omission, framing, or presentation misleads. A rigorous institution records which property has been checked and avoids announcing a universal verdict such as “verified” when only one narrow technical signal has passed.

Historical Background: Automation Before Foundation Models

Manipulated photographs, fabricated quotations, forged documents, unreliable rumors, and propaganda long predate generative AI. Desktop publishing and image-editing software reduced the cost of sophisticated manipulation. Search engines and social platforms accelerated distribution, while automated ranking changed which audiences encountered which claims. Advances in machine learning subsequently enabled convincing synthetic voices, photorealistic faces, translation, and plausible long-form prose at scales previously unavailable to most publishers.

The novelty is not that deception has become possible; it is that the cost, speed, apparent polish, and adaptability of production have changed. A false document can now be tailored to different audiences or generated rapidly in many languages. The same capabilities support beneficial applications: captioning, translation, accessibility, educational illustration, simulation, and creative experimentation. Sound information policy must address the specific risk of a use and its surrounding power relationships rather than treating all synthetic content as inherently improper.

The Sociotechnical Chain of Information

Information enters public understanding through a sequence: someone observes or asserts a fact, records or generates an artifact, supplies context, publishes it through a channel, and an audience interprets or redistributes it. Generative AI may operate at any stage. It can invent an initial assertion, rewrite a source inaccurately, change a photograph's apparent setting, create a deceptive voice message, or synthesize an authoritative-looking summary from several incompatible accounts. Errors can also originate with humans who misuse genuine model outputs or publish without adequate checking.

An institutional integrity assessment therefore maps the complete chain: source, originator, editor, model and retrieval service, reviewer, publisher, platform, and audience. It identifies who has authority to publish, what evidence accompanies a claim, what changes are recorded, and how corrections propagate. Integrity is not a feature that can be purchased as a single detector; it is a maintained process with technical support and accountable human decisions.

Legitimate Synthetic Content and Disclosure Context

Synthetic content is not synonymous with misinformation. Educational reconstructions, concept art, language translations, fictional dramas, interface prototypes, and accessible narration may clearly serve legitimate purposes. Ethical duties arise from the likelihood that an audience will mistake the content's origin or factual status, not merely from the use of a generative tool. An altered historical photograph passed off as evidence is different from an explicitly labeled illustration of an event; both may look convincing, but their relationship to historical truth is different.

Disclosure should be tailored to the audience and function. A background spelling correction ordinarily does not require the same label as a simulated eyewitness interview. A documentary reconstruction should identify what was reconstructed and which details are unsupported. A commercial advertisement using a synthetic spokesperson may need to distinguish fictional endorsements from an actual person's experience. The most relevant question is whether knowing about generation or alteration would materially change a reasonable audience's assessment of the claim, person, or evidence.

Synthetic Text, Confabulation, and False Precision

Language models can produce fluent prose with invented citations, incorrect dates, fabricated quotations, or unsupported causal explanations. The persuasive quality of the writing does not measure its evidentiary quality. A generated answer may also combine true statements into a misleading conclusion through omitted qualifications or a shift in the meaning of terms. An apparent citation may name a real article while assigning it a conclusion the article never reached. The problem is amplified when users assume that a grammatically confident answer reflects a verified search.

For consequential publishing, each material claim requires a separate verification pathway. Quotations should be compared with the underlying text and context; quantitative claims should be recomputed or checked against identifiable datasets; dates and institutional status should be checked with suitable primary records. Editors should distinguish quotation, paraphrase, synthesis, inference, and speculation. If evidence cannot be recovered, the publisher should remove the claim, narrow it to what can be supported, or label the uncertainty rather than inventing a plausible substitute.

Synthetic Images and Visual Evidence

Image generation and editing can construct events that never occurred or alter genuine scenes by adding, removing, or transforming people and objects. Even an innocuous edit can change evidentiary meaning. Cropping may conceal the surrounding crowd; a simulated fire or uniform can falsely imply culpability; color changes can affect how audiences judge conditions. The moral issue is the representational claim being made, not whether a mathematical model was used in the editor.

Publishers should distinguish an actual photograph, a manipulated photograph, a composite, a reconstruction, an illustration, and fully generated imagery. A caption should accurately communicate the category when the image could be mistaken for an observation. For evidence-dependent uses, retain the original acquisition, capture context where available, edit history, licensing information, and a route to an independent source. Synthetic illustrations can be valuable in instruction when they are visibly or textually identified as illustrations and do not substitute for evidence of a specific event.

Synthetic Audio, Voice Cloning, and Apparent Authority

Synthetic speech can provide language accessibility, assistive reading, dramatic narration, and voice restoration. It can also falsely impersonate a family member, public official, executive, clinician, or journalist. Listeners often use familiar vocal characteristics as evidence of identity, but modern synthesis weakens this heuristic. A recording can be fabricated in full or assembled from authentic fragments that change the speaker's apparent meaning. The fact that a voice sounds familiar does not establish authorization or authenticity.

Institutional controls should separate recorded voice from verified authorization. A finance team must not accept a payment instruction merely because it sounds like an executive. Schools and hospitals should establish independent callback or authenticated-channel procedures for urgent changes; media editors should seek the original recording and source context. Consent to use someone's voice for a specific production does not automatically authorize arbitrary future impersonation, redistribution, or misleading endorsement.

Synthetic Video, Deepfakes, and Fabricated Events

Synthetic video combines visual appearance, motion, speech, scene construction, and editing into an artifact that audiences may interpret as an eyewitness record. Motion defects or facial irregularities are not reliable universal tests: genuine video suffers compression and lighting artifacts, and generated video continues to improve. Deliberate manipulators may mix genuine footage with fabricated segments or recaption old footage to imply a new event. A perfectly genuine clip can become disinformation through false context.

Verification must therefore consider the uploader, original event, precise date, location, independent witnesses, associated official records, and any available technical provenance. When an alleged event matters for public safety, reputation, or policy, uncorroborated video should not be promoted as proof. Equally, real footage must not be dismissed as fabricated solely because it is embarrassing, politically inconvenient, or lacks a technological authenticity tag.

Synthetic Personas, Testimonials, and Identity Fraud

A constructed identity may combine generated photographs, resumes, posts, chat messages, and audio to appear to be a real employee, customer, expert, or community member. Synthetic testimonials and fabricated consensus can mislead procurement decisions, consumer choices, and public debate. The ethical issue includes impersonation, manipulation of the audience's perception of social evidence, and appropriation of trust ordinarily earned by a person or institution. Not all anonymous or pseudonymous expression is deceptive; anonymity may protect vulnerable speakers and whistleblowers.

An evaluation should distinguish a clearly fictional character from an account falsely claiming real credentials or experiences. Institutions should validate claimed professional authority through independent channels, document the origin of testimonials, and avoid using synthetic identities to simulate public approval or suppress criticism. Verification policies must preserve legitimate privacy and dissent, rather than making intrusive universal identification the default response to synthetic media.

Misinformation, Disinformation, and Malinformation

Misinformation generally refers to false or misleading information circulated without regard to a speaker's intention to deceive; disinformation is deliberately deceptive information; and malinformation is often used for genuine information deployed in a harmful or misleading manner, such as invasive publication of private details. Their exact definitions vary across frameworks. Generative systems can facilitate all three, but the model does not independently establish an author's intention or the social context of dissemination.

A response should be proportionate to the problem. An accidental error may call for a correction and a clearer citation. Coordinated impersonation may require a protective warning, investigation, and restriction of abusive distribution. Publication of accurate but unlawfully disclosed private information may raise privacy and safety concerns rather than factual falsity. Content governance should distinguish falsehood, misleading context, uncertainty, privacy invasion, and lawful unpopular opinion to avoid substituting broad censorship for disciplined integrity review.

Citation Laundering and Artificial Evidence Chains

An AI-produced article can falsely elevate an unsupported claim by attaching citations that resemble scholarly authority. Subsequent websites may copy the claim, after which a later system retrieves those copies and presents them as multiple independent sources. This is citation laundering: the appearance of corroboration grows while the underlying evidentiary base remains one unsupported assertion. A similar pattern occurs when an unverified social-media post is repackaged into summaries, infographics, and search snippets.

Editors should trace claims backward to the earliest recoverable source and identify independent evidence, not simply count URLs. A peer-reviewed paper may support one limited result, not the sweeping assertion attached to it. A page that repeats another page is not independent corroboration. Citation checks should include author, title, date, exact proposition, original methods or data, and any corrections. An integrity workflow should be able to record “source found but does not support claim,” a distinct outcome from “source not found.”

Scale, Ranking, and the Attention Economy

The social effect of synthetic information depends on distribution as well as production. Recommendation systems, paid promotion, search ranking, coordinated reposting, and repeated exposure influence the visibility of claims. Generative tools lower the cost of producing numerous tailored variants, potentially making low-effort spam, fraudulent reviews, and manufactured consensus more economical. High production volume can overwhelm verification capacity and make it difficult for genuine sources to receive attention.

Platform and publisher responsibility should include rate limits where appropriate, reporting channels, transparent commercial sponsorship, integrity signals, and investigation of coordinated manipulation. These controls must not equate high posting volume with wrongdoing or penalize legitimate community campaigns without evidence. Measures of success should consider the reach of demonstrably misleading content, correction exposure, and user comprehension—not solely the quantity of removed posts. Public-interest safeguards should account for uneven effects on less-resourced languages and communities.

Political Expression, Elections, and Public Debate

Political speech demands careful treatment because societies have a strong interest in open disagreement, satire, investigative reporting, and the exposure of official wrongdoing. At the same time, forged announcements, fabricated candidate statements, and impersonated election officials can cause material harm, especially when they mislead people about voting procedures or immediate safety. The problem is not that synthetic media expresses a viewpoint; it is that it may falsely claim that an identifiable person or institution said or did something.

An ethically defensible response distinguishes political advocacy from deceptive impersonation, genuine satire from plausible forged evidence, and disputed interpretation from falsifiable procedural claims. Officials and journalists should link to authenticated primary statements where feasible and publish corrections promptly. Platforms should explain enforcement categories and provide appeals. Measures must be applied consistently across viewpoints, with attention to their effects on marginalized speakers and on legitimate anonymous political criticism.

Journalism, Documentary Practice, and Editorial Evidence

Journalistic use of generation can assist transcription, translation, indexing, draft organization, accessibility, and visual explanation. It can also introduce fabricated statements or obscure whether an image is documentary evidence. The editorial obligation remains with identifiable people who decide what to publish. A newsroom should specify which uses are permitted, what disclosure is expected, who checks sources, and whether generated images may appear in contexts where readers reasonably expect photographs of actual events.

A robust chain for consequential allegations retains interviews, recording metadata, contemporaneous notes, independently obtained documents, and confirmation from appropriate parties. Generated summaries are leads, not citations. Quotations must be checked against recordings or written records. Corrections should preserve an understandable history rather than silently rewriting public evidence. Documentary reconstructions should identify their illustrative status and avoid inventing distinctive details that could materially alter public perceptions of responsibility or harm.

Scientific Research, Data Integrity, and Reproducibility

Generative AI can help researchers draft code, search literature, formulate hypotheses, and make complex findings accessible. It can also fabricate experiments, images, references, statistical outputs, or data points. A scientific figure carries a special evidentiary claim: readers expect it to represent recorded observations or an explicitly described simulation. Generating plausible microscopy images and presenting them as experimental measurements is a different practice from creating an openly labeled conceptual schematic.

Research organizations should retain raw data, protocol versions, transformation logs, instrument details, and documented analytical decisions. AI-assisted processing should be described at an appropriate level so that methodological significance and reproducibility can be assessed. Publication ethics guidance, including that of the Committee on Publication Ethics, assigns responsibility to human authors and calls for transparent disclosure of relevant AI use. A model cannot assume authorship responsibility, answer misconduct inquiries, or stand behind unsupported experimental conclusions.

Historical Records, Archives, and Cultural Memory

Historical documents and images often carry authority because they appear to have survived from a particular time and place. Generated facsimiles can support teaching, exhibition design, and reconstruction when explicitly identified. Problems arise when synthetic pages, invented oral histories, or altered photographs are inserted into archives as authentic artifacts. Even subtle modernization of wording can erase uncertainty, dialect, or historical context. Artificial “restoration” may invent features not present in the surviving record.

Archivists and historians should preserve originals, catalog source and acquisition information, distinguish transcription from translation or reconstruction, and document editorial interventions. When a damaged photograph is restored with inferred visual detail, the result should not be presented as a direct observation of the missing content. Exhibitions may display synthetic scenes as interpretations while still connecting audiences to surviving evidence. Public access should not depend on deceptive claims of provenance or certainty.

Education, Assessment, and Learning Integrity

Generative assistance can enable explanations at multiple reading levels, language support, practice problems, and adaptive feedback. It can also create false sources or obscure whether a student's submitted work demonstrates the intended learning outcome. Ethical educational policy must distinguish legitimate assistance from undisclosed substitution where independent reasoning is being assessed. Blanket prohibitions may be impractical or exclude helpful accessibility tools, while unchecked use can undermine fairness and mastery.

Institutions should specify allowed assistance for each task, teach verification practices, use assessment formats consistent with course objectives, and provide a practical path to contest allegations of misuse. Automated AI-writing detectors should not be treated as conclusive proof of misconduct. Instructors should seek proportionate evidence such as drafts, oral explanation, work history, and documented criteria, while protecting student privacy and avoiding punitive inferences from language background or writing style.

Institutional Communications and Public Trust

Businesses, universities, agencies, and nonprofit organizations increasingly use generative systems for newsletters, policy summaries, customer service, press releases, and internal briefings. Such content may be published under a trusted institutional identity even when its factual basis has not been checked. The institution's reputation and authority amplify the risk: a false scholarship deadline, incorrect maintenance notice, or fabricated policy quotation can affect real decisions.

Governance should define publication authority, source requirements, review tiers, and emergency retraction procedures. Low-risk wording edits may need ordinary editorial review; legal, financial, medical, safety, or public-policy statements require competent subject-matter validation. A content system should preserve approved text and supporting evidence, identify the official channel, and track updates. Labels that say “AI assisted” are useful in some contexts but cannot replace the basic obligation to publish accurate, appropriately sourced communications.

Emergencies and High-Velocity Rumors

During emergencies, the value of fast communication is high, but incorrect messages can redirect people into danger or overwhelm response services. Synthetic recordings may impersonate officials, altered imagery may exaggerate or invent damage, and automatically summarized alerts may omit critical conditions. A single misleading map or shelter notice can have operational consequences even when the publisher meant to help.

Emergency communication should privilege known authoritative channels, explicitly identify time and location, distinguish confirmed information from preliminary reports, and make corrections highly visible. Organizations can establish preauthorized templates and redundant confirmation routes in advance. Automated systems may help translate or format approved notices, but must not manufacture missing instructions. A practical validation rule is that a consequential claim should either trace to an authorized incident source or be withheld with clear uncertainty until it can be corroborated.

Confabulation, Calibration, and Overconfidence

A language model may produce a detailed answer despite insufficient evidence because it is optimized to generate plausible continuations or responses, not inherently to verify facts. This behavior is often described as confabulation or hallucination. Confident language can lead readers to overestimate reliability. The challenge is not solved by asking the model to “be careful”: evaluation, source constraints, uncertainty handling, and human judgment remain necessary. In high-stakes contexts, a model should have a safe path to abstain.

Organizations should test outputs on realistic tasks, including unavailable information, ambiguous questions, outdated sources, adversarial inputs, and cases outside the approved domain. Performance must be measured against the quality of the underlying evidence and the harm of incorrect answers, not merely user satisfaction. A model that accurately states uncertainty may be safer and more useful than one that always supplies a polished response. Verification criteria should be designed before deployment and revisited when sources, models, or workflows change.

Prompt Injection, Retrieval Contamination, and Spoofed Sources

Systems that retrieve webpages, documents, messages, or files may encounter text crafted to act like instructions rather than evidence. A malicious passage can tell an assistant to ignore verification rules, hide a citation, or invent a conclusion. This is prompt injection when untrusted material attempts to redirect a model's behavior across a trust boundary. Retrieval contamination also occurs accidentally when outdated or duplicated content is ranked as authoritative.

The system should treat retrieved content as evidence to evaluate, not as authorization to change editorial policies or take actions. Controls include source restrictions, permission separation, prompt-injection testing, output validation, and review of consequential actions. Editors still need to inspect the original cited passage. An apparently well-formatted AI answer can contain both a real URL and a fabricated claim, so technical retrieval success must not be mistaken for factual verification.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation and Its Limits

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) provides selected external passages or database results to a generative model. It can improve an answer's grounding when sources are relevant, accurate, current, and faithfully interpreted. It does not guarantee accuracy. Retrieval can omit crucial material, include irrelevant pages, confuse conflicting definitions, or supply a document whose title appears authoritative despite weak content. The model may misread retrieved text or attach a citation to a claim not actually supported by the source.

A dependable RAG workflow should track document identity, source date, version, relevant passage, retrieval time, and the relation between claim and evidence. For numeric or legal facts, it should support a direct check of the original authoritative source. Summaries should identify important disagreement rather than smoothing incompatible findings into an apparent consensus. In sensitive cases, the system should abstain or refer to an accountable professional if sources do not support a defensible conclusion.

Evidence Hierarchies and Source Independence

Sources differ in authority depending on the claim. A law's current text is usually best established from an official legal publication, an instrument reading from the original measurement record, and a claim about a scientific finding from the relevant research and its methods. Expert commentary can provide interpretation, while journalism can provide valuable independently reported facts. A source's official appearance is not a guarantee that its statements are complete or unbiased. A useful hierarchy prioritizes the most direct, independently inspectable evidence available for the proposition at issue.

Independent corroboration is particularly important for contested claims. Two pages citing the same press release do not supply two independent observations. Editors should look for separate underlying records, witnesses, analyses, or methods. When credible sources disagree, the publication should state the disagreement and its basis. A source ledger should distinguish a fact directly established by a document from an inference made by the author, editor, or model.

Verification of Quotations, Numbers, and References

A plausible source citation is not enough to validate a statement. Quoted words must occur in the cited source and not be detached from qualifications that reverse their meaning. Statistical comparisons should specify the population, period, units, denominators, and method. A model-generated bibliography should be treated as an unverified suggestion until each work's existence, author, publication details, and relevance have been confirmed. DOI strings and journal names can be hallucinated as readily as ordinary prose.

A practical review distinguishes four statuses: verified in the original source; partially supported or requiring qualification; contradicted by the source; and unsupported or not located. The last two must not be silently combined. For tables and calculations, a reviewer should reproduce the arithmetic and check whether the chosen inputs apply to the intended date and population. For historical claims, editors should verify dates and referents rather than rely on repeated summaries that may share a common error.

Claim-by-Claim Editorial Verification

An institution can make verification operational through a claim ledger. Each consequential assertion is assigned an identifier, source document, precise passage or dataset, check result, reviewer, date, and publication location. Citations that merely describe a topic are not accepted as evidence for a specific statistic. The ledger also records whether a claim is a confirmed fact, methodological interpretation, forecast, opinion, or illustration. Such distinctions are especially valuable when several people or AI systems contribute to a long document.

The checking effort should be risk-based. A generic definition can often be reviewed against reputable reference literature; a claim affecting criminal accusations, medical treatment, electoral access, or substantial financial decisions requires stronger corroboration and expert judgment. Editorial review should include misleading omissions, title-to-body consistency, tables, captions, and summaries—not only whether hyperlinks resolve. A final publication gate should ensure every unresolved high-risk claim has been removed, qualified, or explicitly referred for additional review.

Human Editorial Responsibility and Escalation

Responsibility cannot be delegated to a generative system in the same sense that it can be assigned to an accountable editor or institution. A model cannot independently accept professional consequences, protect a confidential source in accordance with organizational policy, or authorize publication under a legal mandate. The human publisher must establish who approves content, who checks sensitive facts, who manages corrections, and who may suspend a publication workflow. Merely inserting a nominal human click before release is insufficient if the reviewer lacks time or evidence to challenge the output.

Organizations should designate escalation triggers: unsupported quotations, identity-sensitive allegations, synthetic likenesses of real people, urgent safety claims, unverified statistics, and indications of intentional manipulation. Reviewers need access to original sources and authority to stop release. Management should measure whether the process catches and corrects problems, not reward teams only for publication throughput. A transparent correction culture supports accuracy better than pressuring staff to preserve an appearance of infallibility.

Identity, Signatures, and Authenticated Channels

Publisher identity helps audiences decide whether an artifact came from a claimed institution, but visual branding alone is weak evidence. Logos, email signatures, web layouts, and voice characteristics can be imitated. A stronger approach combines official domains, authenticated accounts, cryptographic signatures where appropriate, documented distribution channels, and independent confirmation for unusual or high-impact requests. Even an authenticated channel can be compromised, so verification must include reasonable assessment of the message's content and context.

Digital signatures can establish relationships between data, keys, and defined trust infrastructures. They do not demonstrate that a signer's underlying claims are true or that the signing organization performed appropriate editorial checks. An official correction should be traceable to an official channel, with a clear statement of what changed. When a synthetic executive announcement appears, an organization should be able to issue a prompt authentic notice through known channels rather than rely solely on public speculation about audiovisual artifacts.

C2PA Content Credentials and Signed Provenance

The Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA) specifies mechanisms for conveying cryptographically verifiable assertions about the creation and editing of digital assets. Content Credentials can include claims about an asset's source, software processes, ingredients, and subsequent transformations. The C2PA technical specification has evolved through multiple versions; version 2.5 was published in October 2026, including additional asset-format and live-media capabilities. Implementations must account for supported formats, validation rules, trust lists, and the compatibility of different readers.

The basic logic is that a claim generator signs a manifest bound to the asset, permitting a compatible validator to examine signature and binding status. Assertions may describe actions, ingredients, or AI involvement, but they are assertions made through a particular provenance process. An institution using Content Credentials should define who signs content, how keys and credentials are governed, what actions are recorded, how transformations are handled, and how end users can inspect the results. Technical adoption should be tested in the actual publishing workflow, not assumed from product marketing.

What Content Credentials Do Not Prove

A valid signed provenance chain is not a guarantee of factual truth. A trusted institution can publish a sincerely mistaken report and sign it correctly; a deceptive author can sign content that accurately records its own generation. Credentials generally establish a narrow relationship among declarations, asset bindings, and signing infrastructure. They do not necessarily prove the civil identity of every person involved, the truth of every caption, or that the original event occurred as described. Conversely, a real photograph may lack Content Credentials because its capture device did not support them or a platform removed metadata.

Institutional policy must therefore distinguish signature validity, trusted signer, recorded editing history, and verified substantive claim. Each answers a different question. A validator's negative or unknown result may indicate missing data, unsupported versions, an incomplete chain, or a genuine integrity issue; it should not automatically be interpreted as proof of forgery. C2PA's own specification explicitly avoids providing moral judgments about provenance assertions. Source verification and editorial accountability remain indispensable.

Watermarking and Embedded Identification Signals

A digital watermark embeds a detectable pattern into generated content or its representation. Some methods aim to survive moderate resizing, cropping, compression, or editing; others are fragile. A detector may use access to a particular provider's keys or model-specific knowledge. Watermarks may help identify a supported generation pathway or attach information that can be matched with external records, but no universal watermark covers every generator, media type, or subsequent transformation.

Verification must reflect the particular technology's documented scope and error characteristics. A missing watermark is not proof of human origin. A positive watermark signal does not independently establish whether the content is misleading or whether a person's likeness was used with consent. Publishers may combine watermarking with authenticated distribution, C2PA provenance, public disclosures, and source review. These layers serve different objectives and should not be described as interchangeable technical guarantees.

Automated Deepfake Detection and Error Rates

Deepfake and AI-content detectors estimate whether observed features resemble those of known synthetic or human-created material. Their accuracy depends on training data, the generator, language, compression, editing, adversarial adaptation, and the population being assessed. A score returned by a detector is an inference under a specified testing condition, not a definitive determination of origin. False positives can unjustly accuse a student or journalist, while false negatives can allow deception to pass through a screening process.

An institution should require documented validation for its own media types and use cases before using detection results to make consequential decisions. Review should include representative benign content, diverse speakers and languages, accessibility transformations, and deliberately altered examples. Thresholds must be chosen with awareness of the real-world prevalence of synthetic content. A low-prevalence setting may yield many false accusations even when a detector appears accurate in a balanced laboratory benchmark. Automated scores should be one input to accountable human inquiry.

Forensic Analysis and Independent Corroboration

Digital forensics can examine file structure, timestamps, image compression patterns, audio discontinuities, sensor traces, edit histories, or relationships among versions. Its usefulness depends on artifact quality and a defensible chain of custody. Many indicators have alternative explanations: repeated compression, screen recording, legitimate video editing, or transmission through a messaging service can alter characteristics without implying deception. Forensic conclusions should explain methods, uncertainty, and competing hypotheses rather than present visual defects as a universal authenticity test.

Independent corroboration is often more informative than a single detector. A claimed public event may leave multiple recordings, location evidence, witness accounts, schedules, or official records. Verification should seek independent pathways that do not all originate from the suspicious artifact. In high-stakes disputes, preserve the original file and metadata, use qualified examiners where available, and avoid altering evidence during analysis. Evidentiary limitations should be reported explicitly.

Metadata, Platforms, and Broken Provenance Chains

Metadata may describe file creation, camera settings, software, authors, or edits, but it can be omitted, stripped by platforms, or modified. A screenshot can preserve the visible appearance of an image while discarding many original technical attributes. A social platform may resize a file or reencode a video, breaking the straightforward link between the uploaded artifact and its original signature. Some provenance systems offer ways to recover or match associated records, but those mechanisms depend on implementation and availability.

Publishers should test how files survive each distribution channel and whether provenance remains intelligible to the audience. A public archive may retain an original master, publish a derivative for accessibility, and maintain a manifest linking the two. It should describe any loss of verifiable information introduced by distribution. Absence of metadata should be reported as an evidentiary limitation, not treated as proof of improper alteration.

Chain of Custody and Preservation of Evidence

When content may serve as evidence, preserve the received original before converting, compressing, annotating, or analyzing it. Record acquisition time, source URL or channel, collector, relevant context, and subsequent custody. Cryptographic hashes can help demonstrate that a particular file has not changed since hashing, but a hash by itself does not establish that the initial file was authentic. Access and editing privileges should be restricted, and every derived copy should be distinguishable from the source.

An institutional record can maintain a linked set of originals, transcripts, translations, analyzed versions, verification notes, and final publications. Where privacy or safety considerations prevent disclosure of raw evidence, document the restriction and provide an appropriate independently reviewable account. Chain of custody is especially important for journalism, scientific records, legal investigations, and research misconduct inquiries. Accurate preservation makes later correction possible when methods or interpretations change.

Provenance Versus Veracity: An Editorial Decision Matrix

Provenance and factual accuracy create four broad cases. A signed artifact may be accurate, signed but false, unsigned yet well corroborated, or unsigned and unverified. Each calls for a different explanation. The presence of a signature should prompt examination of the signer's identity and claims; it must not override contrary empirical evidence. The absence of a signature may simply mean the file has a different technological history. Likewise, technically authentic footage can be paired with a misleading caption.

Provenance state Substantive claim state Appropriate editorial action
Validated record from an appropriate source Independently corroborated Publish with clear sourcing and contextual qualifications
Validated record from an appropriate source Contradicted or unsupported Withhold or correct the claim; document the discrepancy
No usable provenance signal Independently corroborated Publish with ordinary sourcing; disclose provenance limitations where relevant
No usable provenance signal Not independently corroborated Do not present as established fact; investigate or label uncertainty

The matrix is an editorial reasoning aid, not a probability calculator. A “validated record” still depends on a particular validator and trust configuration; “corroborated” depends on the quality and independence of evidence. Editors should not manufacture certainty by combining two weak signals.

Direct Disclosure, Labels, and Audience Comprehension

Direct disclosure tells an audience when content has been generated or materially altered, what type of intervention occurred, and how to interpret it. A meaningful label should appear close enough to the affected material to inform interpretation before a user relies on it. Generic wording such as “AI may have been used” can be inadequate when an artifact depicts a person making a statement they never made. Conversely, needlessly labeling routine spell-checking may bury important notices in noise.

Good disclosure is specific and actionable: “Illustrative AI-generated reconstruction; not historical footage,” or “Audio synthesized for accessibility from an approved transcript.” When content includes both genuine and generated material, the disclosure should indicate which portions differ from observation. Labels should not suggest that generation automatically makes content false or that a provenance check makes it true. Partnership on AI's synthetic-media framework emphasizes responsible creation and sharing practices, including disclosure that respects context and audience needs.

Accessible and Multilingual Integrity Signals

Disclosure fails if the affected audience cannot perceive or understand it. A label embedded only in an image may be invisible to a screen reader; color alone may not communicate warning status; low-contrast text may be inaccessible; and a disclosure in one language may not reach a multilingual audience. Audio content can require spoken contextual information or an accessible transcript. Video may need captioned disclosures and sufficient display duration for comprehension.

Organizations should evaluate labels with actual users, including people with disabilities and people accessing content on small screens or low-bandwidth connections. The disclosure should explain a material fact about content origin without forcing readers through technical jargon. Where access barriers prevent reliable disclosure, the publisher should consider alternative formats or distribution methods. Effective integrity information is part of usable interface design, not an afterthought appended to files after publication.

Uncertainty, Abstention, and Calibrated Language

A dependable publication communicates what is known, inferred, disputed, or unavailable. It should not collapse uncertainty into artificial confidence, but neither should it create false balance by treating all claims as equally credible. Language such as “according to the agency's preliminary statement,” “the image's original source has not been established,” or “the estimate depends on the following assumptions” helps audiences interpret evidence responsibly. Quantitative confidence figures require defensible meaning rather than arbitrary numerical decoration.

Generative tools should be designed to decline unsupported high-stakes claims, ask for missing evidence within appropriate workflows, or provide clearly qualified alternatives. Human editors should know when an uncertainty statement is warranted and when the most ethical choice is not to publish. If additional evidence later changes the assessment, the institution should issue a traceable correction. The objective is accurate public understanding rather than an appearance of certainty.

Privacy, Confidential Sources, and Source Protection

Verification can create privacy risks. A newsroom may verify an authentic recording by inspecting metadata that exposes a source's identity, precise location, or personal contacts. An institution may upload confidential records to an external AI service to check them without realizing how the provider processes that information. A provenance record can itself reveal sensitive information about creators or editing workflows. Therefore, maximal disclosure is not always ethically justified.

Organizations should use data minimization, secure storage, permission controls, documented retention, and restricted verification channels. Public-facing provenance can be narrower than internal evidence records. Editorial decisions should balance accountability with the need to protect vulnerable sources, students, patients, or abuse survivors. A refusal to publish personally identifying details can be consistent with rigorous verification if an independent reviewer can appropriately assess the underlying evidence.

Attribution, Copyright, and Synthetic Reuse

Content authenticity intersects with intellectual property but does not resolve it. A generator's ability to reproduce a recognizable style, character, passage, or voice does not establish the user's right to publish the result. Copyright ownership and fair-use questions depend on jurisdiction and facts; a “generated by AI” label is not a substitute for rights clearance. In the United States, the Copyright Office's January 2025 report explained that human-authored contributions may be protected even when AI assists a work, while purely machine-determined expressive elements raise different authorship questions.

Responsible publishing keeps track of human contributions, source materials, licenses, permissions, and attribution expectations. Editors should avoid generating false endorsements or misrepresenting a living person's participation. A provenance manifest may help document a workflow, but it is not a legal permission grant. The companion Intellectual Property and Software Ethics article explores licensing and rights-management questions in greater depth.

Consent, Likeness, and Person-Specific Synthesis

Synthetic representations of identifiable people may create reputational, privacy, employment, or safety harms even when the output is not a literal copy of an existing recording. Ethical review should ask whether the subject consented to the proposed use, what expectations were created, and whether an audience might mistake simulation for actual behavior. Consent to use a likeness in one advertisement or documentary scene does not establish open-ended permission for future political, commercial, or intimate impersonation.

Legitimate public-interest satire and criticism require careful differentiation from deceptive false attribution. The ethical analysis should consider context, material risk, audience understanding, and less intrusive alternatives. Institutions should offer a clear route to report unauthorized likeness use and document how complaints are investigated. Rights and remedies vary by jurisdiction, so governance rules should not be presented as universal legal determinations.

Abuse, Harassment, and Nonconsensual Synthetic Media

Generative technology can be used to manufacture humiliating images, defamatory statements, or harassing messages that falsely depict real people. Harm is not limited to whether observers believe the content; victims may experience distress, reputational damage, coercion, or increased risk of targeting. A synthetic image circulated as a joke can still cause serious harm. Conversely, careless automated enforcement can suppress lawful reporting or incorrectly accuse creators of misconduct.

Organizational responses should prioritize safety, documented reporting channels, evidence preservation, restricted redistribution of harmful material, proportionate takedown processes, and appeals. Assistance should not require victims to republish abusive content broadly. Public communications should avoid sensationally amplifying the fabrication. Prevention combines moderation, consent safeguards, employee training, and prompt institutional review—not merely after-the-fact detection.

Platforms, Moderation, and Governance of Distribution

Platforms influence information integrity through ranking, advertising, sharing functions, content moderation, recommendation algorithms, and design of reporting systems. Synthetic-content labels can provide useful context, but they are not substitutes for enforcing policies against impersonation, fraud, or coordinated deceptive activity. A platform's rule should state whether it addresses factual falsity, identity deception, harmful manipulation, undisclosed commercial sponsorship, or another defined category.

Enforcement must be proportionate and allow redress. Documentary evidence, artistic expression, parody, and legitimate political criticism may involve editing or AI assistance without crossing the same ethical boundary as fraudulent impersonation. Platforms should examine whether integrity tools work in different languages and accessible formats. Transparency reports should distinguish reported content, confirmed violations, appealed decisions, reversals, and genuine exposure changes rather than treating removal volume as a simple measure of safety.

Organizational Integrity Governance and Decision Rights

An institution using generative systems should maintain a clear policy defining permitted activities, content classifications, responsible reviewers, retention, disclosure, security controls, and correction obligations. Roles need to extend beyond the technical team. An editor is responsible for published claims, a subject expert for specialized factual validation, a privacy officer for sensitive data handling, a security owner for authenticity infrastructure, and senior management for risk acceptance. Smaller institutions may combine roles but should preserve a real ability to challenge and stop high-risk publication.

A usable governance design establishes approval thresholds. Ordinary drafts may undergo standard editing; safety announcements, allegations, scientific evidence, legal statements, and impersonation-sensitive content require stronger review. The policy should specify how AI use is documented, when synthetic media must be labeled, where primary evidence is retained, and which incident types require immediate escalation. Formal controls are effective only if people can use them during realistic deadlines and if managers respond when reviewers identify unresolved risk.

Supplier Evaluation and Contractual Accountability

Generative AI providers differ in data handling, supported provenance mechanisms, logging, retention, model changes, enterprise permissions, and claims about output reliability. Procurement should verify, rather than assume, how a system uses confidential inputs, whether customers can disable specified retention or training uses, and which administrative controls are available. A supplier's benchmark may not reflect the languages, source types, or false-claim costs of the customer's environment. Contract terms should identify responsibilities for service changes, security incidents, and exit or portability.

An institution should test the end-to-end use case before adoption. This includes generation, source retrieval, human review, content export, provenance preservation through publishing platforms, and the ability to retract or correct outputs. Supplier assurances should be compared with observed controls. When a vendor offers an authenticity badge, reviewers must understand exactly what it verifies and what happens when files are transformed downstream. Critical editorial decisions should not rely on opaque supplier claims without independent checks or fallback methods.

Monitoring, Corrections, and Incident Response

Integrity failures may be discovered after publication: a fabricated quotation reaches an archive, a synthetic emergency announcement circulates, or an AI-generated image is mistaken for an event photograph. Institutions need a repeatable process for intake, triage, evidence preservation, containment, investigation, correction, and learning. The response should identify affected channels and audiences, not merely replace the original page. A correction may need to reach syndication partners, search results, mailing lists, or social accounts that propagated the error.

Incident severity should reflect actual and potential harms, including safety, reputational accusations, financial reliance, and vulnerable populations. An incident record should preserve the claim, source history, discovery route, reviewer findings, affected outputs, correction notice, and preventive changes. Public correction language should state what was wrong and what is now supported. Concealing errors undermines trust; publishing excessive unverified detail during an investigation can amplify the original harm. Governance must balance timely warning with evidentiary discipline.

Integrity Metrics and Performance Evaluation

A publishing workflow should measure whether it produces reliably supported claims and effective corrections. Candidate indicators include the proportion of audited consequential claims supported by primary evidence, citation mismatch rate, time to correct material errors, percentage of high-risk content receiving documented expert review, provenance preservation through distribution, and recipient comprehension of synthetic-content labels. Metrics should be evaluated by content category rather than aggregated into a single score that hides serious failures.

Operational targets must not encourage gaming. A team judged by the number of citations may add irrelevant links; a team rewarded for low correction rates may suppress corrections. Independent sample review, documented error severity, source checks, and periodic user testing can counteract these incentives. For detectors, report the false-positive and false-negative rates under the actual decision threshold and population. Data collected to evaluate integrity must itself be handled with privacy safeguards and consistent definitions.

Trade-offs: Expression, Privacy, Accessibility, and Security

Integrity policy can conflict with other legitimate values. Mandatory real-name verification may discourage fraud but expose whistleblowers. Public provenance details may inform audiences but reveal private locations or confidential contributors. Strict removal of ambiguous synthetic material may suppress satire, political criticism, or archival restoration. Minimal disclosure may preserve narrative flow but mislead audiences about whether a depicted event occurred. No single technical default resolves these conflicts.

Institutions should document the purpose, plausible harms, alternative measures, evidence of effectiveness, and available remedies for each significant control. A proportional response might verify an official publisher's identity without forcing every reader or speaker to disclose theirs; it might publish an edited-source description without exposing protected metadata. Accessibility and language equity should be tested rather than assumed. Values conflicts require accountable judgment and ongoing review, not merely a longer checklist.

Cross-Border Distribution and Different Legal Regimes

A single generative media artifact can cross jurisdictions with different privacy, election, copyright, consumer-protection, defamation, and platform-governance laws. Institutions should distinguish general ethical obligations from specific legal duties applicable to particular services, locations, and dates. A disclosure rule that is legally required in one context may be advisable but not legally mandated elsewhere. Global organizations need a means to review jurisdiction-specific requirements without sacrificing consistent basic commitments to truthfulness, consent, and contestability.

Policies can provide a common ethical floor—accurate context, verified high-impact claims, nondeceptive attribution, source protection, and effective corrections—while local legal review determines particular notices and restrictions. Localization must address more than translation: local information authorities, cultural expectations, vulnerable groups, and distribution channels can differ. A regional publisher should have enough decision authority to correct locally harmful misinformation even when the original content originated elsewhere in the organization.

Worked Case: Fabricated Emergency Notice at a University

A university communications office discovers an image circulating online that resembles an official campus alert, claims a building is unsafe, and directs students to an unofficial website. The visual branding looks convincing, but the university's alert system contains no matching notice. An unverified AI-detector score should not become the central finding: the important immediate question is whether the instruction came from an authorized source. The communications team checks the authenticated alert log, contacts campus safety, and preserves the circulating artifact.

The institution issues a short notice through established channels identifying the message as unauthorized, states verified safety information, and warns people not to follow the embedded link. Security personnel investigate the website without exposing students' private data. Later, the team determines whether the artifact was synthetic, copied, or manually edited, but immediate safety actions do not depend on a conclusive deepfake classification. The incident closes only after the office reviews its official-channel guidance and measures whether students understood the correction.

Worked Case: Synthetic Candidate Speech During an Election

A video appears to show a political candidate making a sensational statement. It spreads through several accounts, some describing it as authentic and others calling it satire. Responsible journalists obtain the earliest available upload, seek the candidate's authenticated public response, examine the video's event context, and compare it with independently recorded footage if available. They do not declare the clip fake solely because an automated detector assigns a high synthetic probability; nor do they assume it is genuine because it lacks an AI label.

If the video is a clearly identified parody, reporting should preserve that context. If it falsely attributes statements to the person, coverage should explain the verified discrepancy and avoid repeatedly amplifying the deceptive recording without need. Platform action should be justified by a defined policy such as deceptive impersonation, not by the speaker's political position. Any correction should be traceable and available to readers who encountered the first claim. The case demonstrates that evidence, context, expression, and public-interest scrutiny must be assessed together.

Worked Case: Generative AI in an Educational Encyclopedia

An educational encyclopedia uses AI to draft an entry about a historical discovery. The draft contains accurate background, a fabricated journal title, an unsupported quotation, and an overconfident date. Because the prose is fluent, a hurried reviewer might publish it without recognizing the errors. A disciplined editorial workflow assigns each claim to an underlying source, verifies the existence and details of references, checks direct quotations against original documents, and distinguishes historical scholarly disagreement from factual certainty.

The editor removes unsupported text, corrects the date using a reliable source, replaces the false citation, and retains a revision history. If an illustrative AI image is used, its caption states that it is a reconstruction rather than surviving evidence. Where institutional policy calls for disclosure of AI assistance, the disclosure identifies its role without suggesting that a model authored or verified the scholarship. The governing lesson is that machine assistance can increase production capacity only when the institution maintains independent control of evidence, authorship, and correction.

Worked Case: AI-Assisted Clinical Education Material

A medical training group drafts a patient-facing explanation of a common diagnostic procedure with a generative system. The draft is readable but includes a dosage-like instruction borrowed from an unrelated context and an inaccurate claim about a complication rate. Because a patient might treat a polished document as professional guidance, ordinary copyediting is inadequate. The institution routes the content to a qualified clinical reviewer, checks quantitative claims against appropriate current sources, and removes unsupported instructions.

The final material records its clinical approval date, scope, and review owner; it directs patients to their care team for individualized decisions. A synthetic illustration is labeled where needed to prevent readers from mistaking it for a clinical record. The publishing workflow is tested for errors introduced during translation into other languages. If the group later learns that its information is obsolete, the correction process updates distributed versions and informs relevant recipients. Model fluency plays no role in authorizing medical advice.

Worked Case: Executive Voice Impersonation and Payment Fraud

A regional finance employee receives a voice message that sounds like the chief executive directing an urgent wire transfer to a newly named account. The message contains familiar expressions and references a real contract. Rather than attempting to classify the voice by ear or rely exclusively on an audio detector, the employee follows the organization's independent approval process: confirm the transaction through a known contact route, validate beneficiary details, and require the established segregation of duties.

The message is preserved and reported to security. The verified executive disclaims the instruction, and the payment is not executed. The organization then reviews which public recordings or organizational details might have assisted the impersonation, but it does not punish employees merely for encountering plausible synthetic audio. Training emphasizes that urgent authority claims are not authentication. The result is an operational safeguard that remains useful even if the fraudulent message was assembled without AI.

Worked Case: False Citation in a Government Policy Brief

A public agency prepares a policy briefing using a generative summarization tool. The model cites a legitimate statistical report but incorrectly attributes a headline percentage to a population not measured by that report. An analyst traces the quotation to the original table and finds that the denominator and period are different. Several derivative news summaries repeat the incorrect number, giving an illusion of independent support. The agency must decide whether the claim is material to its recommendation.

The reviewer recomputes the relevant measure, records the actual scope, and revises the briefing's conclusion to match the evidence. If the mistake has already influenced public comments or decisions, an amendment should identify the numerical correction and what it changes. The agency updates its citation-checking rules so future summaries must include the source passage, units, period, and population. The case illustrates why independent quantitative review cannot be replaced by a list of plausible URLs.

Comparative Integrity Controls and Their Limits

No single control supplies complete protection. Different techniques address distinct failures, and institutions should select combinations based on whether the central risk is deception, inaccurate factual claims, identity fraud, source loss, or operational reliance. This comparison captures the difference between origin evidence and substantive verification.

Control Principal question answered Typical limitation Complementary safeguard
Source verification Does reliable evidence support the claim? Original evidence may be incomplete or contested Independent corroboration and explicit uncertainty
C2PA Content Credentials Can specific signed provenance assertions be validated? Signed claims do not establish truth; metadata may be absent Editorial fact-checking and trusted distribution
Digital watermark Is a supported generation signal detectable? Limited generator coverage and transformation sensitivity Provenance review and independent source checks
AI-content detector Does the artifact resemble known synthetic examples? False positives, false negatives, and domain shift Human inquiry and original-file examination
Human editorial review Is the communication appropriate and adequately supported? Reviewers can be rushed, biased, or lack expertise Source ledger, independent challenge, and escalation
Correction and incident process Can material errors be remedied and audiences informed? Corrections may not reach every downstream copy Distribution tracking and proactive notices

The table is a decision aid rather than a certification scheme. Institutions should test each control against actual publication routes, error costs, and user understanding.

Twelve-Stage Information Integrity Review Framework

A complete operational review can be organized into twelve stages: (1) define the publication purpose and affected audience; (2) classify the risk and whether synthetic-origin disclosure matters; (3) identify the accountable publisher and approvers; (4) locate original sources and record evidence provenance; (5) verify quotations, dates, figures, and citations; (6) inspect authenticity and technical provenance signals where relevant; (7) evaluate privacy, consent, and rights clearance; (8) test captions, labels, and accessibility; (9) secure independent review for consequential claims; (10) authorize release through approved channels; (11) monitor distribution, complaints, and emerging contradictions; and (12) correct, archive, or retract with an auditable record.

Each stage should produce a proportionate evidence artifact rather than a ceremonial checkbox. A school newsletter may need only a brief source record and editor approval, while a public safety alert or allegation against an identifiable person requires stricter validation and escalation. A workflow is incomplete if it describes prepublication checking but has no mechanism to notify readers when a claim is later disproved.

Model Editorial Decision Record

A concise decision record makes editorial judgment explainable. It identifies the article or asset, proposed claim, generation tools used, original evidence location, corroborating sources, material uncertainties, provenance or watermark results if obtained, rights and consent status, reviewer, approval date, and correction contact. A claim whose evidence is unresolved receives a disposition of withhold, qualify, revise, or publish after independent validation. The record should also note important dissent or alternative interpretations.

For a synthetic image, the record might state: “Illustrative reconstruction, not documentary capture; no real individual depicted; editor verified caption accuracy; provenance retained in the archive; disclosure appears beneath the image.” For an analytical paragraph, the record might say: “Table figures independently reproduced from the source dataset; historical period and denominator confirmed.” Such notes are internal evidence of a process, not proof of universal truth. They become especially valuable during corrections, staff turnover, or external review.

Questions for Study and Institutional Practice

Students and practitioners should be able to explain why a signed provenance record does not guarantee factual accuracy, why missing watermarks do not establish human origin, and why a detector's false-positive rate matters before accusing a person of misconduct. They should compare the editorial treatment of a labeled reconstruction, a genuine image with a misleading caption, and a fabricated official announcement. An institutional review should ask whether a user can identify the original source and whether a material correction can reach the audience that relied on a false claim.

Further questions concern competing values: When could public provenance metadata endanger a confidential source? When should an author disclose AI assistance in a scientific paper or encyclopedia entry? How can a platform protect political satire while responding to fraudulent impersonation? What makes two citations independently corroborating rather than copied? These are not merely technical puzzles; they require evidence, proportionality, public-interest reasoning, and accountable decisions.

Conclusion: Integrity as an Institutional Capability

Generative AI changes how quickly persuasive information can be produced, personalized, and distributed, but it does not remove the core obligations of human communication. An authentic file can be false, a synthetic explanation can be true, a signed artifact can mislead, and an unsigned artifact can be well evidenced. Information integrity depends on keeping these categories distinct and building practical institutions that can verify claims, interpret provenance, protect people, disclose significant synthesis, correct errors, and justify decisions.

The strongest strategy is layered rather than singular: establish legitimate editorial purposes; require appropriate primary evidence; use C2PA, watermarks, and detectors according to their demonstrated scope; maintain human oversight; protect source privacy; and provide accessible disclosures and remedies. The resulting capability should serve education, research, journalism, commerce, and public administration without treating technological novelty as proof of reliability or as grounds for dismissing genuine evidence.
