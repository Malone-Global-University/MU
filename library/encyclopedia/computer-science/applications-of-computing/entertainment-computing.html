Definition and Scope of Entertainment Computing

Entertainment computing is the application of computational systems to the creation, performance, distribution, interpretation, and enjoyment of cultural and recreational media. It encompasses video games, recorded and live audiovisual entertainment, interactive fiction, computer animation, digital music production, streaming platforms, immersive environments, audience participation, and the software infrastructure connecting creators with audiences. Its organizing question is not which device is used but how computing changes expressive form and the processes by which experiences are made and delivered.

An entertainment system can be interactive or principally presentational. A game may calculate an evolving world in response to player actions; a streaming service may select, package, and deliver prerecorded audiovisual programs; a theater may combine rendered scenery with tracked performers. These applications share foundational technologies—graphics, audio processing, data management, networks, interfaces, and rights management—but impose different priorities. The field studies their integration, including technical performance, creative freedom, accessibility, business arrangements, and the social consequences of digital entertainment.

Entertainment Computing and Neighboring Disciplines

The subject sits at the intersection of computer science, artistic practice, media studies, design, and engineering. Computer graphics studies image construction and rendering; human–computer interaction studies how people use and experience systems; networking examines communication protocols; software engineering supplies development methods. Entertainment computing brings these components together for creative and recreational purposes, while also investigating audience behavior, distribution, and production constraints.

It is broader than video-game development, but gaming remains a major component. It is also different from entertainment industry management: a financial licensing agreement or marketing strategy has computing implications only when its terms shape the systems that implement distribution, reporting, or access. This article provides the parent-level synthesis. Existing encyclopedia entries on Computer Graphics, Cloud Computing, and Human–Computer Interaction retain their own disciplinary focus.

Historical Development of Digital Entertainment

Entertainment computing developed from early electronic games, computer music experiments, numerical animation, and digitally controlled production equipment into a broad networked industry. Mainframes and arcade devices demonstrated interactive play; dedicated consoles and personal computers expanded real-time simulation, graphical interaction, and home media creation. Digital audio workstations transformed recording and editing, while computer-generated imagery became integral to cinema, television, and animation. These were overlapping developments, not a single linear replacement of analog arts by digital machines.

During the growth of the internet and mobile computing, distribution and participation changed as much as production. Downloadable games, online multiplayer worlds, user-generated video, social streaming, digital storefronts, and mobile apps moved entertainment from physical carriers toward continuously operated services. More recent developments include cloud rendering, virtual production, spatial computing, and generative models. The historical pattern is recurrent: better processing and storage create new forms, but economic arrangements, platform compatibility, accessibility, artistic skills, and reliable networks determine whether those forms become durable.

The Digital Entertainment Value Chain

A digital entertainment work moves through several interdependent stages: concept and rights acquisition; design and authorship; asset production; technical assembly and testing; publication or live operation; discovery and distribution; audience interaction; support, preservation, and eventual retirement. Games add simulation state and interactive systems; film and music emphasize capture, composition, editing, and timed presentation; live events depend on synchronization and production control. No one technology platform necessarily owns the entire chain.

Every stage produces information that the next stage must interpret correctly. A game artist's animation must be compatible with the engine's skeleton and build process. A video master must be transcoded into deliverable variants with sound, captions, and rights metadata. A virtual concert must align live signals with interactive clients under time pressure. The economic and engineering challenge is maintaining continuity across organizational handoffs. A viable architecture therefore includes asset identifiers, versioning, quality thresholds, access controls, and clearly assigned responsibilities, not merely powerful content-creation software.

Interactive, Linear, and Hybrid Experiences

Linear entertainment normally follows a predetermined temporal sequence: a recorded episode or film has a defined order even when viewers control playback. Interactive entertainment responds to actions and may produce different sequences, outcomes, viewpoints, or challenges. Many products combine these modes: interactive documentaries, branching narrative media, performances with audience voting, games with cinematics, and concerts with optional social or immersive participation.

The distinction changes system architecture. A video player must synchronize decoding, buffering, captions, and output; a game must update state, accept inputs, and render results within a repeated deadline. Hybrid experiences may need both prerecorded media delivery and authoritative state transitions. Designers must decide whether audience choices alter the underlying work, select among prepared alternatives, or only change presentation. These choices affect testing complexity, content inventory, accessibility, save-state design, rights clearance, and the evidence required to reproduce a particular viewing or play session.

Platforms, Devices, and Distribution Environments

Entertainment computing operates across consoles, personal computers, mobile devices, televisions, browsers, arcade cabinets, immersive headsets, theater installations, and remote cloud services. Each environment imposes distinct capabilities and limitations: available memory, processing rate, input hardware, display characteristics, network access, application-store rules, and upgrade cycles. A game designed for local console execution differs fundamentally from one streamed as compressed video from a remote server.

Cross-platform development requires more than compiling the same code in several environments. Control layouts, text size, audio output, power consumption, privacy prompts, connectivity assumptions, and entitlement validation may need adaptation. Common engines and web standards reduce duplicated work, but platform-specific testing remains necessary. Distribution itself may involve physical installations, downloads, subscriptions, stores, broadcast or multicast channels, or adaptive HTTP streaming. The architectural boundary between production platform and consumer platform should be explicit, since content tools and audience devices have different security and operational requirements.

Real-Time Performance and Experience Budgets

Interactive experiences have deadlines perceptible to the user. At 60 displayed frames per second, a frame occupies roughly 16.7 milliseconds; at 120 frames per second, about 8.3 milliseconds. These are display intervals rather than guarantees of actual rendering time. Simulation, input processing, animation, rendering, audio, and presentation all compete for resources. A high average frame rate can still feel poor if occasional frames take much longer than neighboring frames.

Media playback has different timing requirements. Audio crackles when playback buffers underrun, and video may stutter when decoding or network delivery falls behind. Designers track frame-time distributions, input-to-photon delay, audio latency, bitrate, rebuffering, and audiovisual synchronization rather than treating processor utilization as the sole quality metric. The relevant metric depends on the artistic experience: rhythm games are sensitive to timing, narrative films to uninterrupted playback, and multiplayer games to action responsiveness. Performance budgets should be specified for target hardware and real operating conditions, not only ideal development machines.

Game Loops and Interactive State

Most real-time games repeatedly collect inputs, advance simulation, update audiovisual presentation, and deliver a frame to the user. This recurring process is called the game loop. A fixed-step simulation can improve repeatability by advancing game logic in consistent increments, while rendering can interpolate between states. Variable-step approaches are simpler in some circumstances but require care when simulation behavior depends on fluctuating frame duration.

Game state contains more than a visible position. It may include resources, inventory, health, progression flags, mission objectives, physics variables, timers, artificial agents, and persistence markers. Reliable design separates transient visual effects from authoritative state and defines how state is initialized, saved, recovered, and migrated after an update. Input handling must account for device variation, remapping, simultaneous actions, and pauses. A game loop is thus a coordination system with both artistic and correctness requirements; a beautiful rendering pipeline cannot compensate for unpredictable state changes or missed user inputs.

Entities, Components, and Scene Organization

Interactive worlds commonly represent characters, objects, locations, and effects as entities with associated data and behaviors. Some engines use object-oriented scene graphs; others employ entity–component–system designs that keep identity, data components, and processing systems relatively separate. Neither approach is universally superior. Choice depends on the expected number of entities, runtime performance, editor usability, team expertise, and complexity of interactions.

Scene organization also determines asset loading and memory use. A large world may stream geometry and textures into memory as the player moves, while a smaller game may load complete levels. Developers must define object ownership, update order, collision layers, camera references, save compatibility, and event delivery. Poorly scoped dependencies create defects that are difficult to reproduce, such as an animation assuming an object still exists after its scene has been unloaded. Clear component contracts help teams compose interactive behavior without making every gameplay feature dependent on a single monolithic object hierarchy.

Physics, Collision, and Animation Behavior

Game physics approximates selected properties of motion and interaction. Systems may model rigid bodies, particle fields, deformable materials, or character controllers. Collision detection determines candidate contacts, while a solver approximates constraints, impulses, and resulting movement. Simulation choices balance plausibility, responsiveness, numerical stability, and runtime cost; entertainment physics is rarely a full scientific representation of the physical world.

Animation adds a separate layer of expression through skeletal rigs, blending, procedural motion, inverse kinematics, and authored keyframes. A player's input can control an animated character while the physics engine enforces collision boundaries, creating coordination challenges when animations and collision state disagree. Networked games face further difficulty because floating-point calculations and event ordering may vary across machines. Developers should define which system owns movement, how transitions occur, and what information is serialized. Testing should include extreme velocities, crowded scenes, low frame rates, and repeated save/load cycles.

Game Mechanics, Rules, and Player Feedback

A game mechanic is a rule governing what players can do and how the system responds. Movement, resource exchange, turn ordering, scoring, crafting, and combat are examples; mechanics combine into feedback loops through which players learn and make decisions. Computing implements these rules as state transitions, input mappings, event handlers, and presentation cues. Good technical implementation makes the rules consistent and discoverable without dictating a single creative style.

Feedback may be visual, auditory, haptic, or textual. A player needs to understand whether an input registered, whether an objective changed, and why an action failed. The system must distinguish intentional uncertainty, which can be part of a game, from technical ambiguity created by lag or inconsistent interfaces. Balancing and playtesting evaluate whether rules produce intended experiences across skill levels and control configurations. Designers often use telemetry and controlled experiments, but metrics such as time-on-task or retention should not substitute automatically for enjoyment, autonomy, or artistic objectives.

Graphics Pipelines and Rendering Methods

Computer-generated entertainment images typically pass through a graphics pipeline that transforms scene descriptions into displayed pixels. Geometry, camera parameters, materials, lighting, shaders, rasterization or ray tracing, post-processing, and display output are organized into coordinated stages. Real-time rendering prioritizes timely frame production; offline rendering can spend substantially longer to achieve an image. Many contemporary workflows combine rasterization with selected ray-traced effects or other approximations.

Graphics APIs provide interfaces to graphics hardware, but the application's rendering architecture determines what passes are scheduled, which resources are shared, and how data travels between processor and GPU. Rendering teams manage overdraw, geometry complexity, texture bandwidth, shader cost, memory pressure, and synchronization hazards. Artistic requirements matter as much as benchmark scores: stylized lighting may communicate narrative and improve clarity better than computationally expensive physical realism. Correct color management and predictable output across devices are essential for preserving the intended visual work.

Shaders, Materials, and Visual Effects

Shaders are small programs or stages that perform graphics calculations, such as transforming vertices or evaluating material appearance. Material models represent properties including surface color, roughness, metalness, transparency, and emission. Physically based rendering establishes useful conventions for light–surface interaction, but artists may deliberately depart from them to achieve animation, illustration, or nonphotorealistic styles.

Visual-effects systems add particles, volumetric lighting, fluid approximations, screen-space compositing, and procedural effects. These systems create technical tradeoffs: transparency sorting and dynamic lighting can be expensive, and effects that obscure gameplay information can harm usability. A production workflow therefore maintains material libraries, shader variants, profiling targets, and visual regression images. Accessibility should also inform presentation: repeated intense flashes, insufficient contrast, or meaning conveyed only through color may exclude viewers or cause discomfort. Performance optimization must protect the visual intention while reducing unnecessary computational work.

Animation, Rigging, and Virtual Characters

Digital animation represents changing positions, poses, deformations, and camera views over time. Artists may use keyframed animation, motion capture, procedural blending, skeletal rigs, and simulation-based secondary motion. Animation data passes through asset pipelines that preserve skeleton conventions, coordinate systems, frame rates, retargeting rules, and export versions. Character performance is an expressive collaboration between animation, sound, writing, and interaction design.

Realtime characters create further constraints because animations must respond plausibly to unforeseen actions. A player may stop suddenly, switch weapons, climb, or enter a conversation while a prior animation is active. Animation graphs and blending logic define legal transitions, while inverse kinematics adjusts hands, feet, or gaze to the current environment. Film and game workflows overlap but differ in acceptable control: a film can render a final controlled performance; a game must accommodate many possible input sequences. Both require clear procedures for approving assets and preserving the rights associated with performances and captured likenesses.

Digital Audio Production and Spatial Sound

Digital audio workflows include recording, editing, synthesis, mixing, effects processing, mastering, and playback. Audio samples represent signal amplitudes at discrete times; sample rate, bit depth, compression, and buffering influence storage and processing. A digital audio workstation arranges tracks, effects, automation, and project metadata, while a game audio engine triggers sounds in response to state changes. Low-latency output is especially important for instruments, interactive music, and tightly synchronized actions.

Spatial audio can vary sound according to listener and source positions, acoustic models, and reproduction hardware. Techniques include channel-based mixes, object-oriented rendering, and binaural headphone processing. A design should consider loudness normalization, intelligible dialogue, safe listening defaults, accessible captions or transcripts where suitable, and fallback behavior on ordinary speakers. The engineering objective is not merely accurate signal processing but a controlled auditory experience that remains intelligible across devices, rooms, hearing profiles, and bandwidth conditions.

Video Encoding, Containers, and Playback

Digital video systems represent successive visual frames, usually accompanied by audio and timed metadata. A codec defines procedures for compressing and decompressing media, while a container format organizes encoded tracks, timing, metadata, and sometimes subtitles into deliverable files or segments. These concepts are distinct: different codecs can be carried in a compatible container, and a decoder supported on one device may not be available on another.

Video encoding exchanges bitrate, resolution, frame rate, compression efficiency, quality, encoding time, and playback complexity. High-motion scenes and fine textures may require more bits than static scenes at similar perceptual quality. An asset pipeline should produce a mezzanine or other suitable high-quality master, validate audio and caption synchronization, encode required device profiles, and preserve documented color primaries and transfer characteristics. Media failures may arise from malformed timestamps, incompatible profiles, missing tracks, incorrect color interpretation, or decoder constraints—not just from insufficient network bandwidth. Testing must therefore cover both the encoded files and the playback path.

Adaptive Streaming and Playback Quality

Adaptive-bitrate streaming divides media into segments or fragments available in multiple quality representations. A player estimates network and buffer conditions, then selects representations that balance fidelity against the risk of playback interruption. MPEG-DASH and HTTP Live Streaming are prominent approaches; Common Media Application Format packaging can support interoperable workflows when the relevant profiles and signaling agree. A manifest describes available representations and how segments are obtained.

Adaptive delivery is not equivalent to a guarantee of uninterrupted playback. Congested networks, origin failures, expired authorization, incorrect segment boundaries, and device decoding limits can still prevent service. Operational monitoring therefore considers startup time, rebuffering ratio, rendition changes, playback errors, and completed viewing sessions. Excessively aggressive bitrate selection may raise short-term image quality while increasing stalls. A responsible design considers accessibility tracks, low-bandwidth audiences, and predictable behavior when the network is temporarily unavailable. Publishers should also verify that a player can switch tracks without losing accurate timestamps, audio continuity, or caption alignment.

Live Streaming, Broadcast, and Production Control

Live entertainment introduces timing and operational risks that prerecorded distribution can partially avoid. Cameras, microphones, graphics generators, switching equipment, encoders, contribution links, origin services, and playback systems must collaborate while an event is occurring. Production crews manage source selection, transitions, overlays, lower thirds, cueing, and audio mixing. A fault during the event may not be recoverable by re-encoding an asset after the fact.

System designers distinguish contribution—the path from the event or studio toward production—from distribution to viewers. Contribution may prioritize low delay and preservation of quality; mass distribution may prioritize scalable HTTP delivery and compatibility. End-to-end latency depends on capture, encoding, transport, packaging, buffering, and display, so optimizing one stage does not establish the final delay. Remote audience interaction, such as voting or real-time questions, may require a separate low-latency communication channel. Rehearsal, redundancy, monitoring, fallback feeds, and explicit operator handoffs are essential parts of live-production reliability.

Creative Production and Digital Asset Pipelines

Entertainment production depends on reusable digital assets: artwork, scenes, models, textures, recordings, scripts, music, animations, effects, and localization files. Assets are often authored in specialized software and transformed into formats suitable for engines, editing suites, distribution, or archival repositories. Consistent metadata identifies ownership, license constraints, versions, language, dependencies, and approval status. A large project can fail operationally even when each individual creative tool works correctly.

A robust pipeline separates original source files from generated derivatives and documents every major transformation. An animation may require retargeting, compression, preview rendering, and platform-specific packaging; a film might require editing, grading, captioning, audio mastering, and transcoding. Automated checks can identify missing dependencies, naming conflicts, incompatible geometry, unlicensed samples, or incorrect exports before these reach consumers. Human creative approval remains necessary: technically valid content can still violate artistic intent, contain harmful mistakes, or fail to communicate a narrative. Pipeline design should therefore support iteration without sacrificing provenance.

Virtual Production and Computer-Generated Performance

Virtual production uses digital environments, tracking systems, rendering engines, and synchronized display technologies to support physical performances or filming. In-camera visual-effects workflows can display generated environments on large LED volumes, while other workflows combine live performers and digital scenes through compositing. Motion tracking and timecode connect cameras, performers, and digital backgrounds so their perspectives remain aligned. This can make creative decisions visible earlier in production, but it does not eliminate the need for careful lighting, compositing, and post-production.

Technical issues include display refresh behavior, camera shutter characteristics, color calibration, tracking error, parallax, moiré patterns, and discrepancies between physical and virtual illumination. The production team must identify which images are final, which are provisional references, and which require later replacement. High fidelity on a studio monitor is not sufficient if the recorded camera sees different colors or timing artifacts. Asset management and authority over scene revisions are therefore essential. Virtual production is best understood as a coordinated production method, rather than the simple use of an engine to display scenery.

Version Control and Collaborative Creative Work

Entertainment projects combine code, prose, images, large binary assets, and production documents. Traditional source-control systems support change history, review, branching, and reproducibility, but large media files may require specialized storage or asset-management workflows. Teams must know which revision of an asset belongs to a particular game build, trailer, or localization delivery. A file name such as final-v3-new does not provide a reliable provenance system.

Collaborative controls should distinguish an editable source asset, an approved master, a rendered derivative, and an externally published artifact. Changes to music, voice performances, or character likenesses may also require rights review. Continuous integration can validate code and asset compatibility, but generating every high-resolution derivative on each change may be prohibitively expensive. Staged build pipelines, caching, selective validation, and human sign-off help control cost without obscuring accountability. A reproducible release record should identify the committed sources, tools, processing versions, dependencies, test evidence, and distribution entitlements needed to restore or patch a particular edition.

Testing and Quality Assurance in Entertainment Software

Entertainment quality assurance examines both objective system correctness and subjective experience. Functional tests verify rules, menu behavior, save files, achievements, network transitions, subtitles, purchases, and device compatibility. Performance tests measure frame time, memory pressure, loading duration, audio glitches, and crash frequency. Exploratory playtests ask whether users understand a challenge, feel appropriately rewarded, or encounter confusing interactions. No single test type substitutes for all the others.

A robust release program tests representative hardware and network conditions, accessibility settings, localization variations, account states, and long-running sessions. Multiplayer products need simulations of packet loss, high latency, server restarts, duplicate messages, and reconnects. Media services need tests of rendition switching, entitlement expiration, playback resume, caption language changes, and regional rights restrictions. Bug severity must reflect user impact: an infrequent loss of purchased access or corrupted save file may be more damaging than a visible but harmless graphics artifact. Quality is measured against intended experience and recovery behavior, not only successful installation.

Interaction Design and Audience Experience

Entertainment interfaces organize attention and establish how audiences control a work. Game menus, playback controls, subtitles, inventory systems, controller prompts, recommendation cards, and interactive overlays must communicate affordances without overwhelming the creative experience. Interaction design includes information architecture, sensory feedback, spatial navigation, and user expectations. Some interfaces should be highly visible, while others are designed to disappear during attentive play or viewing.

Research can combine observation, interviews, usability tasks, accessible-device testing, and restrained telemetry. A high click-through rate or prolonged session can reflect satisfaction, confusion, or manipulative friction; the meaning of a metric must be interpreted in context. Designers must accommodate interrupted sessions, limited bandwidth, different devices, and viewers who prefer not to participate socially. Patterns such as autoplay, darkened dismissal options, or unnecessary purchase interruptions may serve a short-term commercial goal while reducing trust. Good user experience aligns controls with user intentions, not merely organizational conversion targets.

Accessibility and Inclusive Entertainment Design

Accessible entertainment offers meaningful participation to people with different visual, auditory, motor, cognitive, and sensory needs. Typical provisions include captions and transcripts, adjustable text size and contrast, configurable controls, alternative input, reduced-motion settings, clear status feedback, and audio descriptions where appropriate. The relevant solution depends on the medium: game interaction may require remapping controls or adjusting difficulty barriers, while streaming video commonly needs timed captions, accessible player controls, and compatible assistive technology.

Accessibility should influence requirements, asset production, testing, and maintenance from the beginning. Retrofitting captions after all video is delivered, or changing controls after core mechanics are frozen, can be costly and incomplete. The W3C Web Content Accessibility Guidelines provide established guidance for web content and synchronized media; the IGDA Game Accessibility SIG provides game-focused design resources. Neither should be misrepresented as a universal technical certification for every platform. Evaluation requires real tasks with relevant assistive technologies and users, especially when visual, auditory, or motor demands are central to an experience.

Controllers, Haptics, and Immersive Interaction

Interactive entertainment may accept touch, mouse, keyboard, motion sensing, speech, eye tracking, specialized controllers, or physical installations. Input devices differ in precision, feedback, latency, accessibility, and environmental constraints. Haptics adds tactile sensations through vibration or force feedback, while immersive systems use head tracking and stereoscopic displays to create spatial experiences. Their value depends on consistent mapping between action, simulated response, and sensory output.

Immersive design requires special care with motion and perceptual comfort. Tracking errors, unstable frame times, conflicting visual and vestibular signals, and poor spatial interfaces can cause discomfort. Audio cues, guardian boundaries, seated alternatives, and adjustable movement modes support broader participation. Designers also need to minimize unnecessary collection of motion, gaze, or biometric-like sensor information, since such signals may expose sensitive behavioral patterns. Not every entertainment experience benefits from maximal immersion; a simpler interface may improve comprehension, reduce hardware cost, and better serve the artistic purpose.

Multiplayer Worlds and Authoritative State

Multiplayer games coordinate interactions across devices that experience different network delays and failures. An authoritative server commonly maintains the official version of important game state, resolving movement, scoring, inventory, and other contested operations. Clients send input or action requests, receive updates, and display approximations between authoritative messages. Some games employ alternative peer-to-peer or hybrid designs, but those bring different trust and synchronization problems.

State architecture must define what can be predicted locally, what requires confirmation, and how disagreements are corrected. A player should not be able to create unlimited currency by replaying a purchase request or alter an opponent's health by editing local memory. Servers therefore validate consequential actions and maintain auditability where appropriate. Designers balance server cost, fairness, perceived responsiveness, geographic proximity, and user privacy. Shared virtual worlds also need policies for identity, moderation, persistence, account recovery, and orderly retirement. Technical synchronization alone does not make an online community safe or sustainable.

Latency Compensation, Prediction, and Rollback

Networked action games often hide some communication delay by predicting likely outcomes on the player's device and later reconciling them with authoritative information. Interpolation smooths movement between received states; extrapolation estimates missing updates; rollback techniques can re-simulate a recent interval when delayed inputs arrive. Each technique has tradeoffs in complexity, visual stability, CPU cost, bandwidth, and fairness. These are alternative designs selected for specific mechanics, not universal settings that every online game should enable.

Latency compensation cannot repeal network physics. A sudden high-delay interaction may still feel unfair or cause visible correction. Deterministic simulation can support synchronized replays but requires careful treatment of randomness, numerical behavior, and input ordering. Server-side time windows and validation prevent some abuses but also introduce policy judgments about plausible actions. Teams must test high-loss networks, geographic disparities, and adversarial conditions, then expose clear feedback when disconnection or synchronization failure affects play. Responsiveness and integrity should be evaluated together, not optimized independently.

Matchmaking, Social Systems, and Community Infrastructure

Online entertainment includes social relationships and identity systems alongside the playable simulation. Matchmaking may consider connection quality, player preference, relative skill, group size, region, platform, and queue duration. No algorithm can maximize every criterion simultaneously. Highly restrictive matching may improve some aspects of fairness while increasing waiting time, excluding sparse regions, or limiting user choice.

Community systems support invitations, chat, voice, groups, spectatorship, reporting, and moderation. They introduce distinct requirements for privacy, access control, retention, abusive conduct, and safeguarding minors. Identity design can separate public handles from administrative account records, while moderation teams require constrained access to evidence and documented appeals. Ranking systems should communicate uncertainty when they estimate player ability from limited or uneven matches. Responsible operation recognizes that a successful multiplayer platform is a social environment as well as a distributed service, with obligations extending beyond low latency and accurate scores.

Cloud Gaming and Remote Rendering

Cloud gaming executes interactive simulation and rendering on remote hardware, then streams the resulting audiovisual output to the player while receiving controller inputs over the network. Unlike prerecorded streaming, the next frames depend on the viewer's actions. End-to-end responsiveness therefore includes input transmission, simulation, GPU rendering, video encoding, network delivery, decoding, and display. Improving encoder throughput does not remove latency elsewhere in the path.

This approach can broaden access to demanding games without equivalent local graphics hardware, but it creates dependencies on data centers, regional proximity, network stability, service subscriptions, and device compatibility. It can also increase operational energy and infrastructure costs for providers. Cloud gaming should not be confused with online multiplayer: a locally executed multiplayer game can use networked servers without remote video rendering, and a single-player game can be streamed from a remote machine. Deployment decisions require target-device testing, regional capacity planning, licensing analysis, and clear disclosure of the consequences of connectivity loss.

Streaming Platforms and Content Delivery Networks

A streaming service connects media catalogs, entitlement systems, playback applications, encoding workflows, origins, caches, and content delivery networks. A CDN places copies or cached segments closer to audiences to reduce repeated origin traffic and improve delivery reliability. The viewer's device still needs a compatible decoder, correctly timed media, and valid access rights. Delivery architecture must distinguish a cacheable media segment from personalized account responses, secrets, and payment information.

Capacity planning considers expected simultaneous viewers, geographic distribution, peak events, codec profiles, caption and audio track variants, and the cost of outbound traffic. Cache-key mistakes can serve incorrect assets, while insufficient invalidation can leave withdrawn or corrected material available longer than intended. Operators should test failover paths, origin shielding, geographic rights enforcement, and recovery from malformed manifests. A content delivery system is successful only when the intended audience can reliably receive the correct authorized work; impressive theoretical bandwidth does not compensate for broken playback or missing accessibility tracks.

Rights Management, Licensing, and Digital Entitlements

Digital entertainment combines technical distribution with legally defined permission. A publisher may possess rights to stream a film in one territory and time window but not another; a game may use licensed music with restrictions that differ from the game's overall sale. Systems implement these agreements through catalog metadata, contracts, entitlement checks, geographic rules, release dates, and content withdrawal procedures. Licensing rights are not inferred from possession of a media file.

Digital rights management can use encryption, key distribution, secure playback components, and device authorization to make unauthorized access more difficult. Encrypted Media Extensions supplies a web interface connecting playback applications to content-decryption mechanisms; it does not itself define a publisher's rights or guarantee that copying is impossible. The system must also address legitimate user needs: offline access, accessibility, account recovery, and clear purchases or rental terms. Strong controls on paper are inadequate if they conceal lawful consumer expectations or prevent appropriately licensed educational and archival use. Legal questions require jurisdiction- and contract-specific analysis.

Catalog Metadata, Discovery, and Preservation

Audiences find entertainment through titles, contributors, genres, language, region, cast, ratings, accessibility features, artwork, recommendations, and search. These fields form a media catalog whose integrity is crucial to distribution. A program may exist in storage yet be effectively unavailable if its title is misspelled, its language tracks are not described, or its rights window is incorrectly modeled. Identifiers should distinguish a work, its edition, encoded asset, localized version, and individual release.

Long-term preservation adds requirements beyond ordinary availability. Source masters, project files, codecs, manifests, rights documentation, software dependencies, and contextual metadata may all be required to reproduce an experience later. Games are particularly difficult to preserve when essential servers are retired or platform authentication disappears. Migrations between storage formats must preserve meaningful technical and cultural features, not merely yield files that can be opened. A preservation plan identifies what constitutes the work: a playable simulation, a particular release, a recording of a performance, or some combination of these.

Recommendation Systems and Audience Discovery

Entertainment platforms frequently recommend works based on content features, viewing histories, explicit preferences, popularity, or learned representations. Collaborative filtering estimates relationships among users and items; content-based methods use attributes of works; hybrid systems combine signals. Search and recommendation are different functions: search attempts to satisfy a stated query, while recommendation proposes options without requiring a precise request. Both depend on catalog quality and reliable interpretation of user intent.

Evaluation should consider relevance, diversity, novelty, coverage, long-term satisfaction, and fairness rather than clicks alone. A recommender trained mostly on dominant languages or popular genres may systematically obscure independent creators. Personalization introduces privacy concerns when listening, viewing, or interaction patterns reveal habits or sensitive interests. Systems should minimize unnecessary tracking, support clear controls, and consider exploratory options that let users escape a narrow feedback loop. There is no universally optimal recommendation metric: a public-service archive, a children's educational platform, and a commercial music service have materially different objectives.

Artificial Intelligence in Game Behavior and Media Systems

Artificial intelligence has long supported entertainment through state machines, pathfinding, behavior trees, planning, procedural decisions, and statistical models. A non-player character may choose a route through a map, react to changes in combat state, or follow scripted narrative rules. More advanced models can help classify media, detect defects, organize assets, or adapt difficulty. These uses vary greatly in computational cost and in the degree of creative and operational control they allow.

AI-driven behavior should remain testable and bounded by game rules or production expectations. A pathfinding agent that chooses legal routes can still behave implausibly if it ignores social context, while an adaptive difficulty system may frustrate users if it continuously changes objectives without disclosure. In a media platform, automated classification errors may mislabel language, accessibility, or audience suitability. Developers should establish which decisions require human approval, what evidence is retained, and how users can contest consequential errors. Good AI integration is defined by useful outcomes and accountable control, not by the mere presence of sophisticated models.

Generative Media, Authorship, and Provenance

Generative systems can help draft dialogue, concept art, textures, background elements, music sketches, animation variations, and production metadata. They can also produce inconsistent character designs, incorrect citations, misleading performances, or materials that infringe contractual obligations. Different systems create different legal and technical risks, and model-generated output should not be assumed to be original, accurate, or fully cleared for commercial use merely because it was newly generated.

Professional workflows require policies for permitted training and input data, contributor consent, provenance, review, revision, and credit. Voice cloning and likeness synthesis raise especially significant questions when a person's recognizable performance is recreated without appropriate permission. A studio may treat a model as an assistive production tool while keeping a human author or art director responsible for final decisions. Claims about ownership and copyrightability require attention to the applicable legal rules, contracts, and jurisdictions. Entertainment computing should explain these risks as matters of technical design and governance rather than presume that one universal policy resolves them.

Security, Cheating, Piracy, and Service Abuse

Entertainment systems face familiar cybersecurity threats as well as domain-specific misuse: account takeover, in-game economy fraud, manipulated leaderboards, bot-driven purchases, piracy, stream credential theft, and attacks on community systems. A client device cannot be trusted as the sole authority for valuable game state. Server-side validation, rate limits, secure authentication, encrypted communication, careful patch distribution, and anomaly investigation reduce some risks, but each introduces operational costs.

Anti-cheat technology may inspect local processes or device behavior, creating privacy and compatibility concerns. Its deployment should be proportional, transparent, and subject to security review; invasive monitoring is not justified merely by competitive pressure. Content protection may reduce unauthorized distribution without eliminating it, and excessive restrictions can harm legitimate users. Incident response must account for players who purchased access, performers whose recordings are exposed, and organizations whose live events depend on uninterrupted operations. Security design should protect people and systems while preserving understandable avenues for correction and appeal.

Privacy, Safety, and Online Community Governance

Entertainment applications can collect account identities, viewing histories, social graphs, messages, device identifiers, location approximations, telemetry, and payment-related records. Some immersive systems also collect motion or gaze data. Privacy engineering asks what information is necessary for a defined function, how long it must be kept, who can access it, and how rights or deletion requests are handled. No benefit is gained by retaining sensitive raw data indefinitely when aggregate performance information would answer the actual operational question.

Safety governance addresses harassment, threats, stalking, nonconsensual content, grooming risks, and coordinated manipulation. Reporting systems should route urgent risks appropriately without treating automated detection as infallible evidence. Moderation policies require consistency, language coverage, documentation, proportional measures, and appeal mechanisms. The responsibilities of a social multiplayer world differ from those of a single-player offline game, yet both should explain their data and safety practices. Trust depends on predictable enforcement and transparent user controls as much as sophisticated detection technology.

Children, Age Suitability, and Family Controls

Entertainment experiences intended for children and adolescents warrant design decisions appropriate to developmental stage and relevant laws. Content ratings describe categories of material and may provide interactive-feature information, but an age rating is not an all-purpose guarantee of privacy or platform safety. Product teams should distinguish exposure to content, access to social communication, purchase capabilities, targeted advertising, and collection of information about children.

Useful family controls can include adjustable communication permissions, spending authorization, clear account recovery, session scheduling, and accessible reporting. Default design should not rely on children to interpret complex consent notices or resist deliberately confusing purchase flows. Age-assurance approaches involve tradeoffs among accuracy, privacy, access, and potential exclusion; teams must assess applicable legal obligations rather than adopt one technical method without review. When children and adults share a platform, policies must address mixed-age spaces and prevent a nominal parental control from silently changing after an update.

Business Models, Virtual Goods, and Creator Economies

Digital entertainment is financed through combinations of direct sales, subscriptions, advertising, licensing, ticketing, sponsorships, in-app transactions, virtual goods, and creator revenue sharing. Computing systems implement the contracts behind these models: who can purchase, access, transfer, revoke, or resell a digital entitlement. Virtual items may be purely cosmetic or affect gameplay; their technical treatment and economic implications differ. Payment and inventory records must be reconciled so retries or failures do not duplicate charges or eliminate valid entitlements.

Creators operating on platforms may depend on distribution algorithms, moderation decisions, royalty calculations, and shifting technical interfaces. A platform can expand an independent creator's reach while controlling access to the audience and the terms of monetization. Product design should state when an item is licensed rather than owned outright, explain renewal or expiration rules, and avoid deceptive purchase mechanics. Where randomness affects paid outcomes, disclosure and regulatory obligations may apply. Financial analysis belongs beside user welfare because a profitable transaction design is not necessarily an ethical or sustainable entertainment experience.

Advertising, Metrics, and Measurement Limits

Advertising-supported entertainment systems may record impressions, completed plays, engagement events, conversions, and campaign attribution. Subscription services monitor acquisition, cancellations, viewing or play patterns, and technical quality of service. Game operators track progression, matchmaking queues, technical stability, and purchases. These metrics describe aspects of system behavior, but they do not directly measure cultural significance, satisfaction, consent, or artistic quality.

Measurement design should distinguish technical incidents from user preferences. An abandoned video may signal a playback failure rather than dissatisfaction; a long game session may reflect positive immersion or unwanted pressure. Privacy-preserving aggregation and shorter retention periods can provide valuable operational information while reducing exposure. Experiments should have explicit hypotheses and guardrail metrics, such as accessibility failures or involuntary spending, rather than optimize a single click or retention rate. Editorial judgment and qualitative research remain necessary, especially when a product's mission includes education, public culture, or artistic exploration.

Platform Economics and Industry Structure

Entertainment platforms exhibit economies of scale in software development and content delivery, while expensive original production and marketing create substantial risk. Network effects can increase the value of multiplayer communities and creator marketplaces as participation grows, but they also reinforce platform dependence. Catalog depth, recommendation quality, rights exclusivity, hardware compatibility, and regional licensing determine competitive position. Technical decisions about APIs, storefront access, and portable identities can shape market outcomes.

Creators face a practical tradeoff between control over distribution and access to established audiences. Proprietary tools or formats may accelerate production within one ecosystem yet raise switching costs later. An independent studio should compare licensing fees, revenue shares, online-service costs, content moderation obligations, porting effort, and contractual restrictions before selecting a platform. The economics of a streaming service differ from those of a shipped offline game, which may have low per-session server costs. Sustainable architecture therefore accounts for business arrangements without allowing commercial incentives to erase creative, accessibility, or preservation obligations.

Reliability, Operations, and Incident Response

Entertainment services often operate continuously after their first release. Online games, streaming platforms, social worlds, and ticketed digital events require monitoring, support, patch deployment, and incident response. Operators establish service objectives for availability, playback success, login completion, matchmaking, payment integrity, and latency. Some experiences can degrade gracefully: an optional recommendation service may fail while catalog search and authorized playback remain available. Others depend on near-total continuity during a live performance.

Operational plans should identify critical dependencies, fallback paths, on-call ownership, backup restoration, incident communication, and post-incident learning. Emergency changes must be reviewed carefully because a rushed fix to authentication or entitlements can deny access to paying audiences. Teams should rehearse scenarios involving CDN disruption, corrupted game-state migration, compromised creator accounts, and widespread client crashes. Reliable entertainment is not achieved only by adding servers. It depends on understanding which user experiences are essential and organizing software and human operations around their recovery.

Environmental Impact and Sustainable Production

Digital entertainment consumes energy in production hardware, data centers, network delivery, devices, cooling, and electronic manufacturing. Real-time graphics, high-resolution encoding, remote rendering, and unnecessary repeated processing can increase resource use. Conversely, efficient compression, asset reuse, longer hardware support, and practical device settings may reduce work per useful experience. Environmental comparisons must consider the full system boundary; local execution and cloud execution each shift costs rather than making them disappear.

Sustainability evaluation can examine energy per rendered session, compute utilization, content delivery patterns, hardware replacement cycles, and storage retention. A release requiring the latest hardware may increase user costs even if server infrastructure is efficient. Teams can profile representative workloads, reduce idle polling, optimize encoded variants, and avoid unnecessary always-on services. Such work should be balanced with accessibility and quality rather than impose lower resolution or performance indiscriminately. Environmental responsibility also includes asset disposal, durable archival formats, and maintenance policies that extend the usable life of supported software.

Cross-Platform Production and Release Management

Shipping entertainment on several platforms requires compatible builds, certification processes, input configurations, store assets, and entitlement rules. A new patch can change assets, save formats, multiplayer protocols, or behavior in ways that make older clients incompatible. Teams must plan version negotiation, update channels, rollback capability, and regional release coordination. Console certification and application-store requirements are contractual platform processes, not substitutes for independent security, accessibility, or quality evaluation.

Localization multiplies the release surface: dialogue length, typography, subtitles, cultural references, audio performances, and age classifications can change by market. Build systems need consistent asset manifests and release records, while operations staff need a clear account of which variant is active in each region. Cross-play adds identity and fairness questions when device input methods differ. A defensible release process includes representative device testing, explicit ownership of each store or channel, incident communications, and a documented procedure for reverting a harmful release without destroying valid player data.

Immersive Worlds, Mixed Reality, and New Interfaces

Virtual reality and augmented or mixed reality combine computer-generated content with tracked human movement and, in some cases, the surrounding physical environment. Interactive installations can make digital works spatial, collaborative, or physically situated. These systems share graphics and input technologies with games but add constraints involving calibration, tracking continuity, safety boundaries, device comfort, and perceptual alignment.

The long-term importance of immersive entertainment should not be mistaken for a guarantee of mass adoption of any device class. Cost, ergonomics, social context, accessible alternatives, privacy, and the value of the creative content govern actual use. New interfaces also challenge preservation: an installation designed for obsolete tracking hardware may require emulation or a documented reinterpretation. As immersive technologies change, creators should distinguish the essential artistic experience from incidental implementation details, preserving the ability to migrate works without silently altering their meaning.

Worked Example: Independent Cross-Platform Game

Consider a small studio developing a cooperative puzzle game for personal computers and consoles. Its production pipeline must coordinate code, art, audio, localization, and accessibility testing. The game uses locally rendered graphics but relies on a hosted service for group invitations and optional shared progression. The team defines authoritative rules for puzzle completion and inventory, supports reconnect after transient network loss, and keeps solo mode available without continuous connectivity where feasible.

The studio sets a frame-time budget on target hardware, tests input remapping and captions, and measures crash-free sessions alongside player comprehension during playtests. A build pipeline validates asset references, automated logic tests, and compatible save versions before release candidates are approved. Business review includes platform fees, ongoing network costs, licensing for music and fonts, and a contingency plan if the hosted component must close. This example illustrates that entertainment quality arises from aligning creative design, technical state, accessibility, and long-term promises; a high review score for visual art cannot repair broken entitlements or unrecoverable saves.

Worked Example: Documentary Streaming Collection

Consider a public-interest media organization distributing documentaries in several languages. It maintains a preservation master, creates compressed playback representations, produces subtitles and audio-description assets where needed, and records rights windows by territory. The content catalog distinguishes titles, editions, language variants, and approved publicity images. A playback application uses an adaptive streaming manifest and checks entitlements without confusing those checks with ownership of the underlying intellectual property.

The organization measures startup delay and rebuffering by device and network class, but it also verifies whether captions can be selected and whether search finds less-promoted subjects. Its CDN architecture caches public media segments while protecting personal account information from shared caching. The preservation team retains accessible masters, project metadata, and rights documents separately from transient delivery derivatives. When a license expires, withdrawal affects the authorized distribution entry without requiring destruction of materials retained lawfully for institutional records. This case demonstrates that media delivery is simultaneously an engineering, editorial, accessibility, and rights-management operation.

Worked Example: Interactive Live Performance

Consider a live concert streamed to remote participants who can select camera views and vote on optional visual effects. The production chain captures multiple camera feeds, synchronizes audio, encodes a primary program, and distributes it using scalable media delivery. A separate interactive service collects authorized votes with rate limits and clear closing times. Operators define which effects the votes can influence while retaining final artistic and safety control over the performance.

Because streaming latency varies among viewers, the system cannot assume that everyone sees the same frame at the instant they submit an action. Polls therefore use well-defined windows and server timestamps instead of attributing fairness to instantaneous shared viewing. A backup program feed is prepared for encoder or connectivity failures. Accessibility staff verify captions and accessible voting controls, while moderation protects performers and participants from abuse. Rehearsals test a failed origin, a flood of duplicate votes, and loss of the interactive overlay. The event succeeds when the music continues reliably even if optional participation temporarily degrades.

Architectural Decision Matrix

Entertainment applications should choose technologies according to the experience they promise. This matrix is illustrative, not a universal scorecard; performance targets and licensing obligations must be established for the actual product.

Application Primary technical objective Critical dependency Essential quality evidence
Offline story game Consistent simulation and local rendering Supported device and recoverable saves Frame-time distribution, playtesting, save integrity
Competitive online game Fair authoritative play with responsive inputs Low-latency network and operational servers Packet-loss tests, cheat resistance, reconciliation behavior
Subscription video service Reliable authorized playback Catalog, encoding, CDN, device decoding Startup success, rebuffering, subtitles, entitlement tests
Interactive live event Uninterrupted program with optional participation Production control, contribution and delivery paths Rehearsal, fallback validation, synchronization tests
Immersive installation Comfortable and aligned spatial experience Tracking, rendering, calibrated hardware Motion comfort, accessibility, tracking recovery

The matrix also clarifies important separations. A content delivery network may improve film distribution while offering little help with a local game's simulation correctness. Anti-cheat measures may matter in competitive gaming but not in a documentary archive. Selecting a technology because it is common in entertainment projects is insufficient; it must be justified by user experience, constraints, and operating responsibility.

Editorial and Technical Implementation Framework

A useful project assessment begins with an explicit creative proposition: what should the audience perceive, control, learn, or feel? It then identifies the product's presentation model, target devices, audience constraints, anticipated scale, and rights. Engineering translates these into measurable quality attributes such as frame timing, playback success, accessibility, recoverability, data minimization, and service continuity. Producers identify which outputs require human artistic approval and which can be validated automatically.

Before launch, the team should test representative devices and networks, confirm asset provenance and permissions, exercise incident recovery, and document ownership of each service. After launch, it should collect proportionate performance evidence, respond to user reports, maintain accessibility features, and review monetization and data practices. At retirement, it should honor entitlements and preservation commitments according to agreements and law. This framework treats entertainment computing as a lifecycle discipline: a work is not technically complete merely because the first public release can be played or viewed.

Conclusion: Entertainment Computing as a Creative Systems Discipline

Entertainment computing is not merely the application of faster processors to games or the replacement of physical media with streaming. It is the design and stewardship of computational systems that transform artistic creation, audience participation, and cultural distribution. Its technical core includes simulation, graphics, audio, encoding, networking, interfaces, and data infrastructure, but these components gain meaning through creative intent and human experience.

An encyclopedia account must therefore connect engineering choices with access, rights, labor, privacy, sustainability, and preservation. The strongest systems balance performance with accessibility, personalization with autonomy, security with legitimate use, and commercial viability with cultural responsibility. As tools and distribution platforms change, those design tensions remain central. The enduring subject is how computation can support creative expression and reliable entertainment without reducing audiences or creators to mere inputs of a technical platform.

Glossary

Adaptive-bitrate streaming
Delivery method that changes between media representations according to player and network conditions.
Animation blending
Combining animation sequences or poses to produce a controlled motion transition.
Asset pipeline
System of tools and approvals that converts creative source materials into usable production and release assets.
Authoritative server
Service that maintains final control over specified multiplayer state or transactions.
Binaural audio
Headphone sound reproduction designed to approximate direction-dependent human hearing cues.
Bitrate
Number of encoded bits transmitted or stored per unit of time.
Branching narrative
Interactive story structure in which user actions or state select among different narrative paths.
Buffer underrun
Condition in which playback consumes data faster than the system supplies it.
Caption
Timed text describing spoken content and relevant nonspeech sounds in synchronized media.
Client prediction
Temporary local simulation of expected outcomes before remote authority confirms them.
Cloud gaming
Remote execution of game simulation and rendering with audiovisual output streamed to the player.
Codec
Method for coding and decoding digital audio or video, usually involving compression.
Color management
Processes for preserving intended color appearance across creation, rendering, and display workflows.
Content delivery network (CDN)
Distributed servers that cache or deliver content nearer to audiences or network edges.
Content entitlement
Recorded permission allowing a user or account to access a particular digital product or service.
Content moderation
Policies and processes for handling harmful or rule-violating user-generated activity.
Contribution link
Path carrying live production media from an event or source into the production system.
Cross-play
Multiplayer participation across different hardware or software platforms.
Digital audio workstation (DAW)
Software environment for recording, editing, processing, and mixing audio.
Digital rights management (DRM)
Technical measures used to control access to or use of protected digital works.
Encoding ladder
Set of media renditions created at different bitrates and often resolutions for adaptive playback.
End-to-end latency
Total delay between an initiating event and its perceivable result at the recipient.
Engine
Runtime and development environment that integrates simulation, rendering, interaction, and asset processing.
Entity–component–system (ECS)
Software organization separating entities, associated data components, and processing systems.
Frame time
Elapsed time required to produce or present one frame, usually measured in milliseconds.
Game loop
Repeated sequence that processes inputs, updates game state, and advances presentation.
Geometry shader
Graphics processing stage operating on geometric primitives in APIs that support it.
Haptic feedback
Tactile or force-based output that conveys information through touch.
HTTP Live Streaming (HLS)
HTTP-based adaptive media streaming approach using playlists and media segments.
Immersive computing
Interactive systems that create a spatial or embodied sense of presence.
Input-to-photon latency
Delay between a user action and the corresponding displayed pixels.
Inverse kinematics
Technique for calculating joint positions or rotations needed to reach a defined target.
Jitter
Variation in the timing or arrival intervals of network packets or other events.
License window
Contractually authorized period during which media may be distributed or accessed.
Localization
Adaptation of an experience to languages, regions, and cultural contexts.
Manifest
Machine-readable description of media tracks and segments available to a player.
Matchmaking
Method of organizing players into shared sessions according to selected criteria.
Mezzanine master
High-quality intermediate media file used for further processing or distribution encoding.
Motion capture
Recording of physical movement for analysis or use in digital animation.
MPEG-DASH
MPEG family of standards for Dynamic Adaptive Streaming over HTTP.
Origin server
Primary source from which content delivery infrastructure obtains authorized media assets.
Physics engine
Runtime subsystem approximating movement, collisions, and selected physical interactions.
Playback buffer
Stored media data awaiting scheduled presentation.
Procedural generation
Algorithmic creation or variation of game content, environments, or media elements.
Provenance
Traceable origin, history, rights, and transformations of a creative or technical asset.
Rasterization
Rendering process that converts geometric primitives into pixel or sample coverage.
Rebuffering
Playback pause occurring while a player waits for additional media data.
Reconciliation
Correction of a predicted or local state after authoritative state information arrives.
Rendition
Encoded representation of media for a particular quality, device, or compatibility profile.
Rollback networking
Technique that re-simulates earlier states when delayed remote input becomes available.
Rights metadata
Information describing ownership, permissions, geographic restrictions, and terms applicable to a work.
Shader
Program executed in a graphics pipeline to evaluate geometry, material, or pixel-related calculations.
Spatial audio
Audio rendering that communicates direction, distance, or spatial position.
Streaming segment
Timed media fragment that a player downloads or receives as part of a presentation.
System of record
Authoritative store for a designated class of operational information.
Timecode
Structured timing reference used to identify and synchronize media positions.
Transcoding
Conversion of media between encoding formats, profiles, or representations.
Virtual production
Production method combining tracked real-world activity with live or planned digital scenes.
Visual effects (VFX)
Computational or composited imagery and transformations used to create or modify scenes.
WebRTC
Web technology framework supporting real-time communications among browsers and associated systems.
