Scientific Computing

Computational models, numerical methods, scientific data, credible simulation, and research infrastructure.

Scientific computing connects mathematical theory, experimental evidence, numerical algorithms, and computing systems. This reference emphasizes trustworthy scientific results: how computational studies are designed, executed, evaluated, documented, and interpreted.

Definition and Scope of Scientific Computing

Scientific computing is the disciplined use of algorithms, numerical methods, software, and computing infrastructure to investigate scientific questions. Its tasks include solving mathematical models, analyzing measurements, generating simulations, optimizing systems, and producing quantitative evidence. The goal is not simply a result that a program can calculate; the result must have an interpretable relationship to a scientific question, assumptions, measurement conditions, and error. Scientific computing is thus both a computational practice and part of scientific methodology.

It overlaps with computational science, scientific programming, numerical analysis, and data science without being identical to any of them. Numerical analysis investigates approximation algorithms and their mathematical properties. Computational science combines disciplinary theory with algorithms and experiments. Data science emphasizes extracting information from data, often with statistical learning. Scientific computing includes all these activities when they form part of a scientifically accountable computation. A simulation can run on an ordinary laptop; a laboratory data-reduction job can require a supercomputer. Scale alone does not define the discipline.

Historical Development of Computational Research

Scientific computation predates electronic computers. Astronomers and engineers used tables, mechanical calculators, and human computation to perform repetitive calculations. Electronic machines made large systems of equations, iterative approximations, and increasingly detailed simulations practical. Numerical weather prediction, early nuclear calculations, structural engineering, and celestial mechanics were important catalysts. The development of programming languages designed for formula translation, especially Fortran, helped make scientific procedures portable enough to evolve beyond one laboratory or machine.

As storage, networks, and processors improved, the field expanded from calculations on isolated computers to shared research facilities, parallel machines, graphical workstations, experimental instruments, and geographically distributed teams. The mathematical method did not become irrelevant as computers became faster. Greater resolution introduced new challenges: numerical instability, uncertain inputs, large outputs, complex software dependencies, and the difficulty of checking an answer when no exact solution exists. Modern scientific computing therefore integrates software engineering and research governance with algorithms and hardware.

Computational Inquiry and the Scientific Method

Computational research begins with a question and a description of what would count as evidence. A researcher may want to estimate heat flow, compare candidate molecules, detect a weak signal, or forecast a regional environmental process. The problem must be translated into measurable variables, physical or statistical assumptions, boundary conditions, an algorithm, and a plan for assessing results. Computation can generate hypotheses, make predictions, explore mechanisms, or interpret observations; it does not replace the need to distinguish those purposes.

A credible study records assumptions, parameter choices, reference data, experimental settings, and the intended interpretation of outputs. Researchers must ask whether a result would remain materially similar under plausible alternative resolutions, parameters, numerical methods, or datasets. Sensitivity is not necessarily a defect; it can be a scientifically important property of the modeled system. But unsupported confidence in a calculation is not evidence of precision. Scientific computing is strongest when its output is linked to a transparent chain of reasoning and appropriate comparison with observed reality.

Mathematical Models and Levels of Abstraction

A model is a selective representation of a system. It identifies which quantities and relationships matter for the question and leaves out detail considered unimportant or infeasible to represent. A climate model, a protein interaction model, and an electrical network model operate at very different scales, yet each simplifies reality through variables, mathematical relationships, parameters, and idealizations. The best model is not always the most detailed one. A simple, well-understood model can be more useful than a highly resolved simulation whose behavior cannot be independently assessed.

Models may be deterministic, stochastic, mechanistic, statistical, empirical, or hybrids. Mechanistic models express proposed governing processes, while statistical models summarize relationships learned from observations. Deterministic equations can still produce unpredictable outcomes when initial conditions are uncertain or dynamics are sensitive. Scientific interpretation depends on model purpose: fitting observations, explaining causation, and predicting unobserved conditions are different achievements. A model should be evaluated for its intended use, operating range, and known limitations rather than judged by a single aggregate accuracy number.

Research Workflows and Computation Pipelines

A scientific workflow connects acquisition, preparation, computation, analysis, visualization, and interpretation. Its stages may run interactively on a workstation or automatically across clusters. Some computations produce a single final result; others generate large ensembles and intermediate datasets. A workflow plan identifies required inputs, expected outputs, dependencies, units, versioned software, resource needs, and checks at each boundary. Without these controls, a scientifically sound algorithm can be undermined by mislabeled files or an incorrect transformation upstream.

Workflows may form directed graphs: one task produces a dataset consumed by another, with branches representing alternative configurations. Robust orchestration distinguishes an incomplete job from a completed calculation, preserves the circumstances of failure, and avoids silently mixing outputs from incompatible experiment runs. Research teams benefit from machine-readable configurations and immutable run identifiers, but no single workflow engine is universal. The design should reflect actual reproducibility needs, scale, instrumentation, collaboration, and the cost of re-running each stage.

Scientific Measurement, Units, and Metadata

Numerical data derive their meaning from measurement processes. A column of numbers is not scientifically interpretable without knowing the measured quantity, units, calibration state, sampling period, spatial reference, instrument, and treatment of missing values. A sensor reporting temperature in Celsius cannot safely be combined with a model expecting Kelvin without conversion. A field measured at the surface cannot automatically be compared with a vertical average. Scientific metadata therefore form part of evidence, not just documentation added after an analysis.

Units, coordinate systems, and uncertainty should be captured as early as possible. Where suitable, software can use typed quantities or validation rules to catch incompatible values, but researchers still need to understand the semantics of each measurement. Time stamps require time-zone and clock conventions; spatial datasets require projections and reference frames. Calibration histories and provenance can explain systematic differences among instruments. Good scientific computing preserves distinctions among zero, below detection limit, missing, and not applicable instead of coercing them into a common numeric placeholder.

Experimental Design and Data Acquisition

The structure of a dataset is often determined before any scientific program runs. Experimental design determines treatments, controls, randomization, replication, observations, and collection timing. Instrument sampling rates, imaging exposure conditions, detection thresholds, and data acquisition software can affect the patterns later found by algorithms. Computational techniques cannot reliably recover information never measured or correct a study whose design creates unresolvable confounding. Thus scientific computing includes the careful translation of experimental context into data structures and analysis plans.

Research systems may ingest laboratory measurements, sensor streams, field observations, surveys, telescope exposures, or outputs from previous simulations. Acquisition pipelines should record provenance and validate data boundaries. Range checks can catch implausible values, while domain-specific checks distinguish a real extreme event from a malfunctioning instrument. Raw measurements should be retained when feasible, so revised calibration or preprocessing can be applied without irreversibly rewriting the underlying evidence. Requirements differ for sensitive human data and instruments with regulated operating conditions.

Data Cleaning and Preprocessing

Raw scientific data often contain missing samples, duplicated records, instrument artifacts, inconsistent units, background noise, and incomplete annotations. Preprocessing may involve filtering, calibration correction, normalization, registration, resampling, segmentation, or feature extraction. Each operation has assumptions and can alter the evidence. Removing observations identified as outliers without transparent criteria can erase genuine rare phenomena. Aggressive smoothing can suppress signals; resampling can introduce correlations or distort spatial features.

A reproducible pipeline preserves a distinction between raw records, derived intermediates, and analysis-ready datasets. Researchers should record transformation order, parameters, inclusion rules, and the reason for excluding observations. Software tests are particularly important when data shape, column naming, or instrument firmware changes. If an analysis evaluates a predictive model, transformations learned from a dataset—such as scaling based on sample statistics—must generally be fitted only on the relevant training partition to avoid information leakage into evaluation. Documenting these decisions is as important as choosing a sophisticated model.

Numerical Representation and Floating-Point Arithmetic

Computers represent most non-integer scientific quantities with finite precision. Binary floating-point types approximate many decimal fractions and support arithmetic over a wide dynamic range, but rounding, overflow, underflow, cancellation, and exceptional values can affect results. For example, repeatedly adding many tiny values to a very large accumulator can lose contributions that would survive a more stable summation strategy. Two mathematically equivalent expressions can produce different answers because their operations and rounding histories differ.

Researchers should choose numeric types according to the desired error budget, not merely the highest available precision or fastest representation. Double precision is common, but some workloads use single or mixed precision and others require higher precision. Floating-point behavior matters especially for nearly singular systems, long integrations, iterative solvers, and massively parallel reductions. Comparisons should normally account for tolerances rather than assume exact equality of computed real numbers. Nondeterministic reduction order across processors can change low-order bits without necessarily invalidating a scientific conclusion.

Error, Conditioning, and Numerical Stability

Numerical error has several origins. Measurement error comes from observations; model error comes from approximating reality; discretization error comes from representing continuous quantities on finite meshes or time steps; rounding error comes from finite arithmetic; and solver error comes from incomplete iterative convergence. These components cannot automatically be merged into a single meaningful uncertainty interval. A scientifically defensible error statement depends on which errors are present, their relationships, and how they are estimated.

Conditioning describes how sensitive the mathematical problem's answer is to changes in its input. Stability concerns whether an algorithm introduces or magnifies error beyond what the problem itself requires. An ill-conditioned problem can be difficult even for a stable algorithm, whereas an unstable algorithm can fail on a well-conditioned problem. For example, solving a linear system by explicitly computing a matrix inverse is often inferior to using an appropriate factorization. Researchers must distinguish the trustworthiness of the mathematical formulation from the behavior of the computational procedure used to solve it.

Linear Algebra in Scientific Computing

Matrices and vectors are central to scientific computation. They represent transformations, measurements, networks, coupled equations, optimization constraints, and discretized physical systems. Scientific software uses factorizations, eigensolvers, singular-value decomposition, least-squares methods, and iterative solution algorithms. Linear algebra underlies applications as diverse as tomography, quantum mechanics, finite-element analysis, parameter estimation, and principal component analysis.

Method selection depends on dimensions, sparsity, conditioning, precision, hardware, and whether one or many right-hand sides must be solved. Dense routines can be efficient for moderate systems because well-optimized libraries exploit contiguous memory and parallel arithmetic. Sparse systems instead store mostly nonzero entries and often rely on specialized factorization or iterative methods. Accuracy must be evaluated using residuals and, where possible, estimates of forward error. A small residual alone does not guarantee that the computed answer is close to the true answer when the system is poorly conditioned.

Sparse Matrices and Iterative Solvers

Discretized scientific models frequently produce matrices with enormous numbers of rows but relatively few nonzero entries per row. Storing all elements would waste memory and time. Sparse representations such as compressed sparse row or compressed sparse column formats preserve patterns efficiently, although the preferred layout depends on access operations. The graph of interactions among variables can determine solver performance as strongly as arithmetic throughput does.

For large systems, iterative solvers such as conjugate-gradient methods or Krylov-subspace approaches build approximate solutions through repeated matrix-vector operations. Applicability depends on matrix properties; conjugate gradient, for example, assumes a symmetric positive-definite system in its standard form. Preconditioning transforms a problem to improve convergence without changing its intended solution. The convergence criterion should be connected to scientific accuracy rather than an arbitrary iteration count. Parallel implementations must also account for communication costs and numerical changes caused by operation ordering.

Numerical Optimization and Parameter Estimation

Optimization seeks parameter values that minimize or maximize a specified objective subject to constraints. In scientific research, objectives may measure model-data misfit, energy, geometric error, resource usage, or a posterior penalty. Some methods use gradients and curvature, while others use derivative-free searches when evaluations are discontinuous, costly, or noisy. A method that performs well on a smooth convex objective may fail on a highly nonconvex landscape with numerous local solutions.

Parameter estimation adds the scientific question of whether the parameters are identifiable from available observations. Multiple parameter combinations can fit a dataset equally well but make different predictions. Regularization introduces additional preferences or constraints, often improving stability but also imposing assumptions that must be stated. Researchers should report objective definitions, bounds, stopping conditions, initialization, and uncertainty or sensitivity. The smallest optimization objective does not by itself establish that a model's assumptions, data, or interpretation are correct.

Numerical Integration and Quadrature

Numerical integration approximates quantities such as area, accumulated flux, probability, energy, or the value of an integral without a convenient closed-form solution. Quadrature methods approximate an integral through weighted evaluations of a function. Their accuracy depends on smoothness, sampling, singularities, and the choice of evaluation points. Adaptive methods concentrate evaluations in regions that appear difficult, but they need stopping criteria and safeguards for discontinuities or sharply peaked contributions.

High-dimensional integration presents a different challenge because regular grids grow exponentially with dimension. Monte Carlo and quasi-Monte Carlo methods can offer practical alternatives, with performance depending on properties of the integrand and sampling design. Some computations require integrating noisy experimental observations, where interpolation and measurement uncertainty may dominate theoretical quadrature error. The analyst should distinguish estimates of algorithmic truncation error from uncertainty already present in the measured input and should avoid reporting a numerical precision not warranted by the evidence.

Ordinary Differential Equations and Dynamic Models

Ordinary differential equations describe changes of quantities with respect to one independent variable, often time. Examples include population dynamics, reaction kinetics, orbital motion, and electrical circuits. Numerical solvers advance an approximate state using initial conditions and derivative information. Fixed-step methods are easy to understand, while adaptive solvers vary the step size to manage estimated local error. The appropriate method depends on timescales, smoothness, events, conserved quantities, and stability requirements.

Stiff systems contain processes evolving on very different timescales; explicit methods may need extremely small steps for stability even when the desired solution changes slowly. Implicit methods can address stiffness but require solving equations at each step. A solver's successful return status is not sufficient evidence of a reliable scientific prediction. Investigators should examine conservation laws, compare tolerances and methods, detect discontinuities, and assess sensitivity to initial conditions. Chaotic systems may remain useful for statistical or structural inference even when long-term point trajectories diverge.

Partial Differential Equations and Field Simulation

Partial differential equations describe quantities varying over space and often time. Heat diffusion, fluid flow, elasticity, electromagnetism, and transport are classic applications. To compute approximate solutions, a continuous region is represented by a mesh, grid, or other discrete structure, and the governing equations are translated into algebraic operations. Initial and boundary conditions are part of the model: a missing or physically implausible boundary can invalidate a sophisticated solver.

The choice of representation balances geometry, accuracy, conservation, and cost. Finite-difference, finite-volume, finite-element, and spectral methods emphasize different properties and are not universally interchangeable. Mesh refinement can reduce discretization error only when the scheme and solution have the properties needed for convergence. Complex geometry, shocks, nonlinearities, and coupled processes can complicate the analysis. Scientific workflows need to document the domain, mesh generation, material parameters, boundary assumptions, numerical scheme, and the evidence used to judge whether the calculation is sufficiently resolved.

Discretization, Meshing, and Resolution

Discretization replaces continuous quantities with a finite collection of degrees of freedom. A mesh may be regular, unstructured, adaptive, or hybrid. Fine resolution can reveal small-scale effects but increases computation and memory costs. Coarse resolution can be suitable when the question concerns large-scale behavior or when a validated reduced model captures unresolved effects. What matters is whether the mesh and numerical representation resolve the features required by the research objective.

Mesh quality affects accuracy and solver behavior. Highly distorted elements, abrupt changes in cell size, and poor treatment of interfaces can produce misleading solutions. Grid convergence studies compare results across systematically refined resolutions; a result that appears visually smooth is not necessarily converged. Adaptive mesh refinement concentrates detail in regions where an error indicator suggests it matters, but refinement criteria themselves require justification. A trustworthy paper should report sufficient information for readers to understand resolution choices and distinguish physical structures from artifacts of the numerical grid.

Time-Stepping, Conservation, and Stability

Time-dependent simulations advance their state through a sequence of numerical steps. A large step reduces computational effort but can miss rapid events or violate stability conditions. A very small step may be costly and can amplify accumulated rounding or other numerical problems. Some discretizations must respect limits related to physical propagation speed and grid spacing, often described through Courant-type conditions. The precise limit depends on the governing equations and numerical scheme; no single safe step size applies to every model.

Conservation properties provide useful checks. In a closed system, the numerical behavior of mass, charge, momentum, or energy should be evaluated against the equations and the intended approximations. A simulation may deliberately use sources, sinks, or dissipative models, so numerical conservation should be interpreted in context. Monitoring invariants, accepted error measures, and convergence across step sizes provides stronger evidence than visual agreement with an expected animation. Event handling, checkpoint restart, and boundary updates must also be included in reproducibility plans.

Stochastic Simulation and Random Processes

Stochastic scientific models explicitly represent randomness or unresolved variability. Examples include particle transport, epidemic spread, biochemical reaction counts, and uncertainty in environmental inputs. A simulated outcome is then a draw from a distribution under stated assumptions, not a unique deterministic answer. Multiple runs and appropriate summary statistics may be necessary. Random-number generators are algorithmic; their sequences depend on algorithms, seeds, and sometimes parallel execution details.

Good practice includes documenting generator choices, seed handling, burn-in or convergence procedures when applicable, and the distinction between variability across simulated trajectories and uncertainty about underlying model parameters. Repeatedly running a flawed model does not remedy structural bias. Parallel simulations need deliberate stream management to avoid accidental correlations or repeated random sequences. Scientific claims should be based on enough independent information to estimate the quantities of interest, with uncertainty intervals appropriate to the model and sampling method.

Monte Carlo Methods and Ensemble Computation

Monte Carlo methods use sampled values to approximate distributions, expectations, integrals, or possible outcomes. They are attractive when deterministic methods become impractical, especially for high-dimensional or uncertain problems. An ensemble consists of related computations using varied initial states, parameters, assumptions, or stochastic realizations. Ensembles can reveal sensitivity and probabilistic behavior, but only when their variations represent scientifically defensible alternatives.

Accuracy should not be inferred from the number of samples alone. Dependence, poor mixing, extreme events, and biased sampling can invalidate naive error estimates. Variance-reduction methods such as importance sampling or stratification can improve efficiency but require careful design. Ensemble workflows also create operational challenges: run tracking, storage demand, resource scheduling, and aggregation without mixing incompatible variants. A reproducible ensemble records the generating distributions and choices of scenarios, not just an average result and an attractive uncertainty band.

Inverse Problems and Scientific Inference

Forward problems predict observations from a specified model and parameters. Inverse problems work in the opposite direction: they infer hidden quantities from available measurements. Examples include reconstructing an image from projections, identifying subsurface properties from wave responses, and estimating a model's material parameters. Inverse problems can be ill-posed, meaning solutions may not exist uniquely or may vary sharply with small changes in data. Their outputs must therefore be interpreted in light of assumptions and uncertainty.

Regularization and Bayesian approaches incorporate prior information or constraints to stabilize inference. They can be scientifically justified, but they do not transform insufficient evidence into objective certainty. Identifiability analysis asks which parameters the measurements actually constrain. Researchers should distinguish the fitted response from the inferred physical property, check alternative explanations, and evaluate predictions on observations not simply recycled from fitting. When inference supports safety, environmental, or medical decisions, sensitivity to model structure and prior assumptions deserves explicit attention.

Uncertainty Quantification and Sensitivity Analysis

Uncertainty quantification examines how imperfect knowledge affects computed results. Sources can include measurement variability, uncertain parameters, model discrepancy, random processes, numerical approximation, and incomplete coverage of scenarios. Aleatory uncertainty describes variability treated as inherent in a process; epistemic uncertainty concerns limited knowledge and may be reducible by additional evidence. The distinction is useful but can depend on model scope and research purpose.

Sensitivity analysis estimates how outputs change when inputs or assumptions vary. Local methods examine changes near a selected point, while global methods explore a broader input space and interactions among variables. Uncertainty propagation can use analytical approximations, sampling, polynomial methods, or surrogate models. Results should report assumptions about input distributions and dependence. An uncertainty interval based only on numerical solver tolerance is not a complete predictive interval, and a narrow confidence band is not reliable if major structural model errors were excluded.

Verification, Validation, and Model Credibility

Verification asks whether the equations and algorithms are implemented and solved correctly to an appropriate accuracy. Validation asks whether the model represents the relevant real-world behavior adequately for its intended use. These questions are related but not interchangeable. A perfectly implemented model can be a poor description of reality; a model that matches selected measurements can still contain coding errors or compensate for incorrect assumptions. Method-of-manufactured-solutions tests, reference problems, convergence studies, and code comparisons can support verification.

Validation commonly involves comparison with independent experimental or observational evidence and requires an evaluation of measurement and model uncertainty. A model may be credible for one operating regime but unsupported outside it. A numerical result can be useful even without exhaustive validation if the limitations and decision context are clear. Researchers should define evaluation criteria before inspection of results when feasible, preserve validation datasets, and state what a test can and cannot establish. Confirmation of a single expected curve is weaker than evidence gathered across independently challenging cases.

Calibration, Data Assimilation, and Updating

Calibration chooses model parameters to improve agreement with selected observations. Data assimilation combines model predictions with incoming observations to estimate the current state of a dynamic system. Such procedures are used in atmospheric prediction, ocean science, navigation, and many engineering applications. They require assumptions about observation errors, model errors, and how information should be weighted. Assimilation can improve state estimates without demonstrating that the underlying model is universally valid.

A calibration process should distinguish the data used to fit parameters from data reserved for validation. Continual retuning against the same target can conceal structural problems and overstate predictive skill. Data assimilation systems should record observation timing, quality flags, update mechanisms, and handling of missing readings. When observations contradict a model, the discrepancy may indicate a sensor defect, a regime change, or an inadequate physical representation. Scientific judgment is needed to interpret the residual rather than automatically forcing the computation toward every observed value.

Scientific Statistics and Experimental Analysis

Statistical reasoning supports the design, analysis, and interpretation of scientific computations. Descriptive summaries help reveal data properties; inferential methods assess claims under assumptions about sampling and variability. Experimental analysis may include confidence intervals, estimation of effects, regression, hypothesis tests, hierarchical models, and methods for repeated measurements. A p-value does not measure the probability that a scientific hypothesis is true, and a statistically significant result can be practically negligible.

Computation makes complex statistical models feasible, but it also facilitates extensive searching over variables and analyses. Multiple testing, selection bias, missing-data mechanisms, and post hoc model choices can distort findings. Research workflows should make exploratory and confirmatory stages distinguishable. Reporting should emphasize assumptions, effect sizes, uncertainty, robustness, and data provenance rather than rely on a single threshold. Where a scientific study incorporates human participants or sensitive information, statistical reproducibility must be balanced with applicable ethical and privacy constraints.

Scientific Machine Learning and Surrogate Models

Machine learning can approximate difficult mappings, discover patterns, accelerate simulations, or support analysis of large experimental datasets. Surrogate models replace an expensive computation with an approximation trained from previous runs or measurements. Physics-informed methods use equations or scientific constraints within a learning procedure, but incorporating physical language into an algorithm does not automatically guarantee physical validity. Neural networks can interpolate well within a training domain yet produce unreliable predictions for rare events or conditions outside their experience.

Scientific machine learning should be evaluated against the purpose for which it will be used. An emulator for uncertainty quantification may need accurate distributional summaries, while a surrogate for optimization needs reliable ordering of candidates. Test sets should reflect deployment conditions and remain separate from training and tuning. For high-consequence decisions, researchers should examine uncertainty, calibration, physical constraints, conservation, dataset shift, and failure modes. A faster model is beneficial only when its error is quantified and acceptable for the scientific task.

Signal Processing and Scientific Imaging

Scientific instruments produce time series, spectra, spatial images, and other signals that need computational interpretation. Processing may include filtering, transforms, denoising, reconstruction, calibration, registration, and feature detection. Fourier transforms provide a powerful framework for studying frequency content, while wavelet and other methods support localized phenomena. Sampling theory determines which frequencies can be represented without aliasing under particular conditions, and calibration helps distinguish a physical pattern from instrument response.

Image reconstruction can be an inverse problem in which the desired image is inferred from incomplete or noisy measurements. Smoothing, sharpening, and contrast enhancement can aid interpretation but may alter scientifically important features. A processed image should retain documentation of transformations, original measurements, spatial scale, and quantitative uncertainty when appropriate. For automated detection and segmentation, evaluation against independent reference data is essential. Apparent visual clarity is not a substitute for measurement fidelity or verified reconstruction properties.

Visualization and Scientific Communication

Visualization turns numerical output into interpretable forms such as plots, maps, volume renderings, trajectories, and uncertainty distributions. It helps researchers identify errors, explore patterns, and communicate findings. Visualization choices can also mislead. Truncated axes, inappropriate color scales, unreported interpolation, projection distortions, and hidden missing data can change a viewer's interpretation. Effective scientific graphics clarify dimensions, units, sampling, reference frames, and uncertainty without exaggerating precision.

Three-dimensional rendering can be useful for complex spatial fields, but it may obscure internal structure or imply detail not supported by resolution. Interactive exploration should preserve traceability from displayed values to source data. A paper's static figure is only one view of a model and often cannot communicate the full parameter sensitivity of the underlying calculation. Visualization belongs within the scientific evidence chain and should be tested for correct transformations, accessibility, and understandable explanations for non-specialist audiences when the research affects public decisions.

Scientific Data Formats and Interoperability

Scientific computing relies on files and interfaces that represent arrays, coordinate axes, experiment metadata, simulation checkpoints, and other complex objects. Formats such as HDF5 and netCDF are widely used for structured scientific datasets, but each has conventions and limitations that must be managed explicitly. A file format can preserve numerical values without preserving their semantic meaning if units, coordinate systems, and variable descriptions are omitted. Interoperability requires shared understanding, not merely successful file opening.

Storage choices reflect workload requirements. Chunking can improve access to selected subregions of large arrays, while compression can reduce storage at a cost in processing and sometimes precision. Parallel writes require coordination and appropriate library capabilities. The permanence of a format is not equivalent to the permanence of dependent software. Scientific repositories need documentation, format migration plans, persistent identifiers, and compatibility checks so that future teams can understand both raw and derived material.

Research Data Stewardship and FAIR Principles

The FAIR principles encourage research objects to be findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable. They concern metadata, identifiers, standards, and machine-actionable access—not an unconditional requirement to make every dataset publicly downloadable. Sensitive patient records, protected locations, proprietary research, and security-restricted measurements may require controlled access while still benefiting from descriptive metadata and durable provenance.

A data stewardship plan identifies ownership, retention, access restrictions, documentation, versioning, and long-term repository responsibilities. Researchers should cite datasets and software as scientific products where appropriate. Reuse requires enough context to know which collection methods, quality flags, calibrations, and transformations produced the available variables. Merely preserving a file is insufficient if the instruments, units, software, or policy conditions needed to interpret it have been lost. The value of data stewardship is scientific continuity as well as administrative accountability.

Provenance, Versioning, and Reproducible Environments

Provenance records how outputs were generated from inputs and methods. A result may depend on a specific dataset snapshot, source-code revision, compiler, numerical library, parameter file, operating environment, and hardware. Capturing these relationships helps investigators explain differences, locate mistakes, and update analyses. Source control and dependency management are therefore core research practices, even when scientific software is produced by a small laboratory rather than a professional development team.

Reproducible environments may use lockfiles, containers, package managers, or described system images. Containers simplify distribution but do not guarantee identical floating-point behavior across architectures or eliminate external dependencies. A complete record includes execution commands, inputs, random seeds, computational resources, and the intended tolerance for comparing outputs. Researchers should protect confidential information when sharing workflows and avoid putting secrets or restricted datasets inside public code repositories. Reproducibility is a managed scientific property, not merely an artifact of running the same script twice.

Reproducibility, Replicability, and Independent Evidence

The National Academies' 2019 report distinguishes computational reproducibility—obtaining consistent results from the same data and computational steps—from replicability, which concerns consistent findings through independent studies addressing the same question. These concepts should not be collapsed. Re-running a flawed analysis can reproduce the same error; an independent experiment can provide valuable evidence even when it does not reproduce an earlier result in every numerical detail.

Scientific computing supports both by making methods inspectable and results easier to evaluate. Researchers can share workflows, test cases, code, documentation, and uncertainty estimates, subject to legitimate access restrictions. Scientific software must also manage change: a corrected algorithm may intentionally alter output. Versioned results and change notes allow readers to distinguish genuine revisions from uncontrolled variation. In performance-sensitive parallel computing, a sensible numerical equivalence criterion may be more defensible than requiring bitwise-identical outputs on all hardware.

Programming Languages and Scientific Libraries

Scientific programming uses languages selected for numerical performance, expressiveness, interoperability, and the needs of particular research communities. Fortran and C/C++ remain important in established simulation codes; Python, Julia, R, MATLAB, and other languages support a wide range of analysis and modeling. A single workflow may use multiple languages, calling compiled numerical kernels through a high-level interface. Choice should depend on library quality, maintenance capacity, validation requirements, team skills, and long-term sustainability.

Scientific libraries encode extensively tested methods for linear algebra, optimization, integration, statistics, and signal processing. Using a mature library can reduce duplication, but analysts must understand method assumptions and available diagnostics. Array broadcasting, automatic differentiation, and vectorized operations can make code concise while also hiding memory allocations or unit errors. Version changes can alter numerical results or APIs, so archived analyses should identify dependencies. Code clarity and independent tests matter alongside throughput; the fastest implementation may be the wrong choice if its scientific behavior cannot be explained.

Parallel Algorithms and Concurrency

Parallel scientific computing distributes calculations across processors or accelerators to reduce elapsed time or handle larger problems. Data parallelism partitions a dataset, task parallelism runs independent operations, and pipeline parallelism overlaps stages of a workflow. Parallelization introduces synchronization, contention, communication, memory consistency, and load-balancing considerations. A computation with a large serial portion cannot scale indefinitely simply by adding processors; the attainable benefit depends on the workload and its decomposition.

Numerical correctness can also change under parallel execution. Reduction order affects floating-point results; asynchronous updates can violate assumptions of iterative algorithms; and data races may produce intermittent faults. Tests should address both scientific accuracy and concurrency behavior. Speedup must be compared with the correct serial or lower-parallel baseline and reported with problem size and hardware context. Some applications are better served by running many independent ensemble members than by parallelizing one tightly coupled calculation.

Message Passing and Distributed-Memory Computing

Distributed-memory systems assign separate address spaces to participating processes. They communicate by sending messages or using supported collective operations. The Message Passing Interface (MPI) is a widely used standard for portable scientific parallel programming; MPI 5.0 was approved in 2025. MPI does not remove the need to design a good decomposition or determine which data must cross process boundaries. Communication patterns and synchronization can become bottlenecks when computations are spread across many nodes.

Applications commonly divide a simulation domain among processes and exchange information near partition boundaries. Collective operations may combine partial results, while parallel I/O moves datasets to and from shared storage. A stalled rank or failed node can disrupt many participants, depending on implementation and resilience design. Researchers must distinguish application-level correctness from the success of message transport. Distributed-memory scientific computation is a specialized form of distributed computing whose principal goal is coordinated numerical work rather than general-purpose network service delivery.

GPUs, Accelerators, and Mixed Precision

Graphics processing units and other accelerators provide high arithmetic throughput when calculations expose sufficient parallelism and regular memory access. They are useful in matrix operations, particle simulations, image analysis, and machine learning. However, moving data between host and accelerator memory can outweigh the savings for small workloads or frequent transfers. Performance depends on kernel launch overhead, occupancy, available memory bandwidth, and the ability to organize computations efficiently.

Mixed-precision methods use different numeric precisions across a workflow to improve speed or memory efficiency. They require evidence that accumulated error remains acceptable. Certain operations may need higher precision for stability, even if other stages tolerate lower precision. Differences among hardware libraries, reduction strategies, and compiler optimizations also affect reproducibility. An accelerator is therefore not an automatic improvement: researchers should benchmark representative cases, test numerical sensitivity, and document whether an optimized kernel changes the scientific meaning of its output.

High-Performance Computing Systems

High-performance computing (HPC) combines processors, memory, fast interconnects, storage, schedulers, and operations teams to support demanding workloads. Supercomputers are one form of HPC infrastructure, but scientific work also runs on institutional clusters and specialized servers. Researchers typically submit batch jobs requesting cores, accelerators, memory, and wall time. Resource allocations are finite, so queue delays and data movement are often as important to a project's calendar as raw calculation speed.

HPC systems require strategies for software environments, parallel libraries, checkpointing, large-output management, and fair resource use. A model that runs efficiently on a single node may scale poorly across nodes because communication becomes dominant. Scientific facilities also face energy and cooling constraints, equipment turnover, and cybersecurity obligations. Effective projects plan the entire workflow, including pre-processing and analysis, rather than treating the central simulation as the only resource-consuming stage. HPC is a tool for scientific goals, not a synonym for scientific credibility.

Cloud, Hybrid, and Remote Research Infrastructure

Cloud platforms provide configurable computation, storage, and networking for scientific projects. They can be especially useful for irregular workloads, geographically distributed collaboration, or access to specialized hardware. Hybrid arrangements may keep sensitive or high-throughput experimental data on premises while using external services for selected processing. Data transfer charges, access restrictions, startup overhead, resource availability, and reproducibility all affect whether cloud infrastructure is appropriate.

Moving a simulation to the cloud does not inherently make it scalable or reliable. A tightly coupled calculation may need a low-latency interconnect; a data-intensive workflow may be constrained by the time and cost of moving inputs. Identity management, encryption, retention, and scientific provenance must be carried across administrative boundaries. Workload selection should consider total research cost, not just virtual-machine hourly rates. Institutions benefit from budgets, project-specific access controls, audit trails, and policies governing deletion and archival of outputs after a funded computation ends.

Scientific Workflow Scheduling and Checkpointing

Long simulations and large analyses can take hours or weeks. Scheduling systems allocate resources, enforce quotas, and coordinate jobs across shared infrastructure. Checkpointing periodically saves enough state to restart without repeating the entire computation. Choosing checkpoint intervals involves a tradeoff: frequent writes consume time and storage, while infrequent writes increase the work lost during failure. Some algorithms can restart from a simple state vector; others also need random generator states, decomposition metadata, solver history, or intermediate caches.

Checkpoints should be distinguished from durable scientific outputs. A valid restart file may be tied to a particular software version or processor configuration and may not be suitable as a long-term research artifact. Restart tests are essential, because a checkpoint that has never been used to recover a job provides limited assurance. Workflow systems should connect resumed jobs to the original experiment identity and prevent accidental double counting of outputs. Data integrity checks and documented retention policies support both recovery and reproducibility.

Performance Modeling and Scalability

Scientific software performance can be limited by computation, memory bandwidth, network communication, storage access, or synchronization. Roofline-style analysis compares arithmetic intensity with hardware capabilities and can help identify whether optimizing computation or data movement is likely to matter. Profiling measures where time is actually spent. The performance of a small benchmark may not predict the behavior of a full research run with larger data, different sparsity patterns, or expensive output operations.

Strong scaling measures the effect of adding resources to a fixed total problem; weak scaling examines how performance changes when the work per processing unit remains approximately constant as total size grows. Both are informative and should be reported with problem dimensions and system context. Optimizations require correctness comparisons, because faster code can change numeric behavior. Energy usage, queue time, and maintenance complexity can also influence the best architecture. The objective is trustworthy scientific throughput: useful results produced reliably at a justified cost.

Scientific Software Engineering and Automated Tests

Scientific software often begins as a research prototype, yet successful models can become long-lived institutional infrastructure. Code reviews, version control, documentation, modular interfaces, and automated testing reduce dependence on individual researchers and help prevent undetected regressions. Unit tests cover small functions; integration tests cover component interactions; reference benchmarks check established scientific behavior. Metamorphic tests examine relationships among outputs when a precise expected answer is unavailable.

Testing numerical software needs tolerances and physically meaningful invariants rather than unconditional exact equality. Property tests may check nonnegative concentrations, symmetry, conservation, or monotonic relationships under stated conditions. A continuous integration pipeline can build software and run selected fast tests, while expensive validation cases are scheduled separately. Scientific validity cannot be fully automated: a passing test suite confirms only the particular checks implemented. Teams should preserve evidence of algorithm changes, failed tests, and known operating ranges to support responsible reuse.

Research Security, Privacy, and Responsible Conduct

Scientific systems may handle unpublished results, hazardous material specifications, personal data, geolocation of protected resources, or restricted intellectual property. Security controls should reflect the specific risks and obligations. Role-based access, strong authentication, encryption, audit trails, secure software dependencies, and appropriate data minimization can protect research without unnecessarily obstructing collaboration. Availability matters as well: a compromised compute environment can invalidate results or disrupt time-sensitive experiments.

Responsible conduct includes honest reporting of uncertainty, methodological limitations, conflicts of interest, and negative results. Computation can create a false appearance of objectivity when datasets are biased, models omit affected groups, or a persuasive visualization hides uncertainty. Researchers must distinguish observed evidence from model-based inference and resist overstating what an algorithm proves. Ethical oversight is especially important where computational results inform health, environmental, public safety, or resource-allocation decisions. The correct governance framework depends on the application, not merely the fact that software is used.

Scientific Computing Across Physical Sciences

Physics and chemistry use scientific computing to examine systems from atomic interactions to galaxies. Researchers model particle transport, fluid flow, electronic structure, thermal phenomena, spectroscopy, and reactions. Different scales require different theories and algorithms. A molecular calculation cannot be assumed to predict a laboratory-scale material property without considering approximations and scale bridging. A high-resolution plasma simulation may be appropriate for investigating mechanisms but too expensive for broad uncertainty studies.

Validation in the physical sciences often requires comparisons with experiments, conservation laws, benchmark solutions, or independent simulation methods. Complex models can combine uncertain inputs with costly numerics. Research teams may use simpler surrogate systems to understand which parameters dominate the result and reserve expensive high-fidelity computation for questions where detail changes conclusions. Scientific computation is an instrument of reasoning and measurement, not a substitute for established physical interpretation.

Biological, Chemical, and Biomedical Computation

Biological research uses computing for sequence analysis, molecular structures, population modeling, microscopy, experimental design, and complex network investigation. Data may be heterogeneous, noisy, incomplete, and affected by laboratory protocols. Scientific computing supports the algorithms that make large datasets interpretable, while biological interpretation requires attention to sampling, species, tissues, assays, and experimental context. Statistical associations in high-throughput data do not by themselves demonstrate biological mechanisms.

Computational biology often combines simulations with experimentally derived datasets. Model validation may require new experiments or comparison with independently acquired observations. Sensitive biomedical data also bring privacy and governance obligations that may prevent unrestricted replication by outside teams. Transparent documentation, controlled-access repositories, shared software, and appropriate evaluation datasets can preserve scientific rigor within these constraints. Medical Computing addresses clinical systems and patient-care applications separately; this scientific-computing entry focuses on research methodology and computation.

Earth, Climate, and Environmental Systems

Earth systems science combines numerical models, satellites, monitoring networks, and historical datasets to study atmosphere, oceans, land, water, and ecological processes. Models operate across spatial and temporal scales and may couple physical, chemical, and biological components. Regional forecasts and long-horizon climate projections address different questions and should not be evaluated with identical expectations. Uncertain initial conditions, boundary conditions, parameterizations, and future scenarios can each contribute to the spread of computational results.

Environmental decision-making often requires uncertainty and communication tailored to stakeholders. A flood model may be accurate for observed river conditions but unsupported for a newly altered landscape. Climate simulations can describe plausible trajectories under explicit emissions scenarios without assigning a single inevitable future. Data assimilation and ensemble methods improve estimates and characterize uncertainty, but model interdependence complicates naive interpretation of ensemble spread. Research reports must make scenario assumptions and limitations visible when results inform infrastructure or public policy.

Astronomy, Space Science, and Remote Sensing

Astronomy and space science depend on computation to reduce instrument measurements, reconstruct images, model dynamics, and search large datasets. Telescope readings require calibration, background subtraction, coordinate transformations, uncertainty propagation, and artifact detection. Simulations may explore orbital stability, astrophysical fluid dynamics, galaxy formation, or spacecraft trajectories. The enormous range of relevant scales makes direct simulation of every physical process infeasible; numerical approximations and subgrid representations are therefore common.

Remote sensing similarly links instruments and physical measurements with geospatial computation. Pixel values must be interpreted using sensor characteristics, geometry, atmospheric corrections, and acquisition conditions. Automated classification can support mapping, but algorithms must be evaluated across regions, seasons, and sensor types. Scientific claims should distinguish a model-estimated property from a directly measured observable. Archiving calibration information, coordinate metadata, processing versions, and uncertainty estimates enables later researchers to reinterpret observations as techniques improve.

Worked Example: A Heat-Diffusion Simulation

Consider an engineering team studying how heat moves through a metal component. The research question is whether the component exceeds a critical temperature during a defined operating cycle. The team begins by identifying material properties, geometry, heat sources, initial conditions, and thermal boundary conditions. It chooses a mathematical heat-transfer model and discretizes the component. A small benchmark with a known analytical solution tests the basic solver, while a mesh-refinement study checks sensitivity to spatial resolution.

The team also measures temperature using calibrated sensors under representative conditions. Those observations support model validation, with their uncertainty recorded separately from discretization error. Different material-conductivity values and boundary assumptions are explored to assess sensitivity. Final reporting includes the maximum predicted temperature, its location and timing, an uncertainty statement, and the range of conditions for which the model has evidence. If the simulation is later used to support a safety decision, the required confidence and validation coverage may be substantially greater than for an exploratory classroom exercise.

Worked Example: A Research Laboratory Data Pipeline

A laboratory collects time-series signals from several instruments to study a chemical reaction. Each file includes a device identifier, acquisition time, calibration record, and measurement units. An automated pipeline checks file completeness, registers instrument versions, corrects known baseline offsets, flags suspect samples, and stores both raw and processed datasets. Subsequent software estimates reaction rates and compares them with a kinetic model. The lab retains code versions, numerical tolerances, and a manifest of which source files contributed to each plotted result.

When instruments disagree, the workflow does not average measurements blindly. Researchers examine calibration, synchronization, detection limits, and differences in sampling location. A new preprocessing rule is tested on archived reference cases before adoption. The published analysis distinguishes technical repeatability from the scientific claim that a reaction mechanism is supported. By preserving provenance and allowing controlled reruns, the institution can explain how a reported value changed after a calibration update without losing the historical record of its original calculation.

Worked Example: An Environmental Forecast Ensemble

An environmental agency estimates possible river conditions over the coming week. Observations provide current flow and rainfall, while several weather scenarios supply future inputs. A hydrological model translates those inputs into estimates of water level at selected points. The agency runs an ensemble using plausible variations in initial conditions and parameter values, with automated checks for missing observations and failed simulations. Forecast output includes uncertainty bands and clear definitions of what the bands reflect.

Before communicating flood risk, analysts compare recent forecasts with later measurements and review model skill at different flow levels. They avoid interpreting a single ensemble average as a guaranteed outcome. If extreme events are poorly represented in historical data, that limitation is stated. The system also has a fallback workflow for unavailable instruments or interrupted compute infrastructure. The final scientific product is not just a map: it is an evidence chain connecting measurements, models, computational runs, uncertainty, and an operational decision.

No single numerical method or infrastructure design is best for all scientific problems. Research Review and Decision Framework

An institution reviewing a scientific-computing project should begin with purpose, not hardware purchasing. What scientific question is being answered? Which outputs influence a decision? What observations and accepted theory support the model? What is the acceptable error for the intended use? Which results require independent validation? The team should next review software sustainability, data governance, execution costs, and recovery plans. These questions determine whether a laptop, campus cluster, specialized accelerator, or external facility is appropriate.

The review should also consider the consequences of failure. A visualization used to explore possible hypotheses is not held to the same assurance standard as software used in a safety-critical engineering determination. Even an exploratory calculation needs provenance and honest limitations, but assurance effort should scale with risk. Clear acceptance criteria, designated scientific ownership, preserved evidence, and periodic review improve research quality more reliably than a narrow focus on processor performance or fashionable computational methods.

Scientific Computing as an Institutional Capability

Scientific computing can increase the reach of research by making previously intractable problems analyzable, generating useful forecasts, integrating complex measurements, and allowing systematic comparisons among hypotheses. Its strengths depend on several systems working together: sound mathematics, well-characterized data, dependable software, appropriate infrastructure, and researchers able to interpret results critically. The presence of advanced numerical algorithms does not remove the need for independent evidence, and computational speed is not a measure of truth.

A mature scientific-computing institution treats code, data, metadata, infrastructure, and human expertise as part of its research assets. It sustains methods over time, tests important models against independent evidence, tracks uncertainty, and communicates results at the level of confidence they warrant. This combination of computational power and scientific accountability makes the discipline an essential part of contemporary research and engineering.

Review Questions and Further Study

The following prompts encourage evaluation of numerical methods, research evidence, and model limitations.

  1. How does scientific computing differ from both numerical analysis and general Data Science?
  2. Why is greater simulation resolution not automatically evidence of a more credible prediction?
  3. What distinguishes numerical stability from mathematical conditioning?
  4. Identify measurement, discretization, rounding and model-form errors in a selected simulation.
  5. When might an iterative sparse solver be preferable to a dense direct solver?
  6. Why can a dynamic model require an implicit rather than explicit integration method?
  7. How can a simulation satisfy a conservation check yet still be physically inaccurate?
  8. Distinguish verification of numerical software from validation against independent physical observations.
  9. How would an ensemble represent uncertainty, and which uncertainty sources might it leave out?
  10. What information is needed to reproduce a calculation beyond the source code alone?
  11. Explain why bitwise-identical output is not always achievable or scientifically necessary across parallel platforms.
  12. What differences separate strong and weak scaling?
  13. How could calibration against one dataset give misleading confidence about predictions?
  14. When should a scientific team choose cloud resources over a campus HPC cluster?
  15. What ethical obligations arise when a model affects public safety or environmental policy?
  16. How do FAIR metadata and controlled-access data restrictions coexist?
  17. What tests would you use to verify a numerical heat-diffusion code?
  18. How should a study report the scientific limitations of a learned surrogate model?

Glossary

These concise definitions are provided for reference. Technical meaning and acceptable tolerances must always be interpreted in the relevant scientific context.

Adaptive mesh refinement
Dynamic allocation of finer discretization where estimated errors or features justify it.
Aleatory uncertainty
Uncertainty associated with variability modeled as inherent to a process.
Algorithm
Finite, specified procedure for transforming inputs into outputs.
Analytical solution
Mathematical expression describing an exact solution under stated assumptions.
Assimilation
Combination of model forecasts and measurements to estimate the state of a system.
Boundary condition
Constraint specifying a model behavior at a spatial or other domain boundary.
Calibration
Estimation or adjustment of instrument or model parameters against reference information.
Checkpoint
Saved computational state sufficient for a defined restart procedure.
Computational reproducibility
Ability to obtain consistent computational results from the same data and analysis steps under specified conditions.
Conditioning
Sensitivity of the mathematical problem to perturbations in its inputs.
Convergence
Approach of an approximation or iterative sequence toward a specified solution or limiting behavior.
Courant condition
Scheme-dependent relationship between discretization, wave or transport speed, and time-step stability.
Data assimilation
Use of observations and model dynamics to update estimates of a system state.
Data provenance
Recorded origins and transformation history of datasets and derived results.
Deterministic model
Model whose specified state and inputs determine its subsequent outcome under the given rules.
Discretization
Replacement of a continuous problem with a finite numerical representation.
Discretization error
Difference caused by representing a continuous problem with a discrete method.
Ensemble
Collection of related calculations representing variations in inputs, assumptions or realizations.
Epistemic uncertainty
Uncertainty arising from limited knowledge or incomplete representation.
Error budget
Account of identified error contributions and their possible effect on results.
FAIR principles
Guidelines encouraging digital research objects to be findable, accessible, interoperable and reusable.
Finite difference method
Numerical technique approximating derivatives using values at discrete points.
Finite element method
Method approximating fields using basis functions over a partitioned domain.
Finite volume method
Discretization based on balances of quantities across computational control volumes.
Floating point
Finite-precision representation of real-valued quantities by exponent and significand.
Forward problem
Calculation of predicted observations from model structure, parameters and inputs.
High-performance computing
Use of coordinated advanced computational resources for demanding workloads.
Implicit solver
Solver whose next state participates in equations that must be solved at each step.
Initial condition
Specified starting values for a dynamic mathematical model.
Inverse problem
Inference of hidden states, properties or parameters from available observations.
Iterative solver
Numerical method that approaches an approximate solution through repeated updates.
Mesh
Discrete partition of a spatial domain used for numerical approximation.
Message Passing Interface (MPI)
Standardized set of interfaces supporting parallel programming with message passing.
Metamorphic test
Test of relations among outputs under controlled transformations when exact answers are unavailable.
Model discrepancy
Difference between the modeled representation and real behavior not explained by selected random effects alone.
Model validation
Evaluation of model adequacy for a specified real-world purpose using relevant evidence.
Model verification
Evaluation of whether the implemented equations and numerical algorithms are correctly solved.
Monte Carlo method
Computational approach using sampling to estimate quantities or model distributions.
Numerical integration
Approximation of an integral or cumulative quantity by computational methods.
Numerical stability
Property of an algorithm relating how approximation and rounding errors develop during computation.
Parallel reduction
Operation combining partial values computed on separate processing units.
Parameter identifiability
Degree to which available observations distinguish alternative parameter values.
Parameterization
Representation of unresolved effects or relationships through selected model quantities or formulas.
Partial differential equation (PDE)
Equation involving partial derivatives of functions of multiple variables.
Preconditioner
Transformation intended to improve iterative solver convergence.
Provenance
Documented origin, processing and contextual history of information or output.
Quadrature
Numerical approximation of a definite integral from weighted function evaluations.
Random seed
Initial value used to initialize a pseudorandom generation procedure.
Regularization
Constraint or preference introduced to stabilize or structure an inverse or optimization problem.
Replicability
Agreement of findings across independent studies addressing the same question, as defined in a stated framework.
Residual
Difference remaining when an approximate solution is substituted into a modeled equation.
Resolution
Spatial, temporal or other level of detail represented in data or a numerical model.
Restart validation
Test demonstrating that a saved state can recover the intended calculation correctly.
Roundoff error
Error introduced by finite precision arithmetic and representation.
Scientific computing
Use of computation to model, simulate, analyze and interpret scientific systems with accountable evidence.
Sensitivity analysis
Evaluation of how changes in inputs or assumptions affect outputs.
Sparse matrix
Matrix storing relatively few nonzero elements compared with all possible entries.
Stiff differential equation
Dynamical problem requiring attention to widely separated numerical timescales for stable solution.
Strong scaling
Performance change when computing resources increase while total problem size remains fixed.
Surrogate model
Computationally cheaper approximation of a more expensive model or process.
Truncation error
Error introduced when an infinite or continuous representation is approximated by finite terms or steps.
Uncertainty quantification
Assessment of uncertainty sources and their influence on modeled or estimated outcomes.
Validation dataset
Evidence reserved to assess whether a model adequately represents its intended target.
Variance reduction
Sampling design or estimator strategy intended to reduce estimation variance.
Version control
Systematic tracking of revisions to software, configurations or other artifacts.
Weak scaling
Performance change as both resources and total work grow while work per unit remains approximately stable.
