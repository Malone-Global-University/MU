What Is Cloud Computing?

Cloud computing is a model for obtaining computing capabilities—such as servers, storage, databases, networking, software, and data-processing services—over a network from a provider that maintains and allocates the underlying resources. Instead of purchasing and operating every component as a permanently assigned local asset, an organization can request capacity, configure it through software, and release or adjust it as requirements change. The term cloud refers to this service and operating model, not to a particular brand, building, or type of computer.

A cloud service still runs on physical equipment. Data centers contain processors, memory, storage devices, switches, power supplies, cooling infrastructure, and specialized accelerators. What changes is the relationship between the underlying equipment and its users: the provider presents resources through standardized interfaces, allocates them among customers, meters their use, and assumes defined portions of operating responsibility. A user may work with a virtual machine, a managed database, or a web-based application without administering the servers that support it.

Cloud computing is closely connected to computer networking, distributed computing, and virtualization, but it is not synonymous with any of them. Networking connects machines; distributed computing organizes work across them; virtualization abstracts computing resources. Cloud computing combines these and other techniques into an on-demand service model with defined operational and commercial characteristics.

Cloud services support activities ranging from individual document synchronization to enterprise information systems, scientific analysis, media streaming, and large-scale artificial intelligence. The underlying principles remain the same even though different workloads demand different architectures.

The Defining Characteristics of Cloud Services

The United States National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) identifies five essential characteristics in its influential definition of cloud computing. Together, these distinguish a genuine cloud service from the mere remote hosting of a conventional server. The characteristics are an analytical framework, not a guarantee that every commercial product implements them equally well.

  1. On-demand self-service: a consumer can provision capabilities—such as computing time or storage—without requiring a separate human interaction with the provider for every request.
  2. Broad network access: capabilities are available through network mechanisms suitable for different kinds of client devices and software.
  3. Resource pooling: a provider serves multiple customers from a shared pool, dynamically assigning and reassigning resources according to demand and isolation rules.
  4. Rapid elasticity: capacity can expand or contract promptly, sometimes automatically, as workload requirements change.
  5. Measured service: the system monitors and reports resource use, supporting accountability, operational measurement, and commonly usage-based charging.

Elasticity should not be confused with unlimited capacity. Providers have physical limits, quotas, network constraints, and service-specific restrictions; automatic scaling may also require advance configuration. Likewise, a service being accessible through a web browser does not automatically make it a cloud service. The complete operating model matters.

NIST also sets out the traditional three service models and four deployment models. They remain useful categories even as contemporary cloud products combine features that cross those boundaries.

From Shared Computing to Commercial Clouds

Cloud computing draws on several earlier developments. Large institutional computers once served multiple users through time-sharing systems, demonstrating that computing capacity could be treated as a shared resource. Later, networked client–server applications, hosting providers, utility-computing proposals, and the growth of the commercial internet made remote computing more practical. Improvements in parallel computing architectures, automated provisioning, and hypervisor technology helped transform fixed servers into more flexible resource pools.

During the 2000s, commercial platforms increasingly exposed storage and computing resources through programmable services. Over time, providers added managed databases, application platforms, container services, analytics, identity systems, and specialized processors. Organizations could assemble systems from these services rather than constructing every underlying layer independently.

The history is not a transition from local computers to a world without local infrastructure. Personal devices, enterprise data centers, private networks, edge systems, and public clouds continue to coexist. Hybrid approaches emerged because institutions often have existing investments, performance requirements, regulatory obligations, or operational constraints that prevent a complete move to external services.

The distinctive historical change is the increased ability to treat infrastructure as configurable services: resources can be requested through APIs, described as code, operated with automation, and measured against requirements for reliability, security, performance, and cost.

How a Cloud System Is Organized

A cloud system can be understood as several cooperating layers. At the foundation lie physical facilities and hardware. Above them are mechanisms that allocate and isolate resources, including hypervisors, container runtimes, storage controllers, and virtual networking. Service-management software then exposes products that customers create and administer through consoles, command-line tools, or application programming interfaces (APIs). Applications and data operate on top of those services.

The distinction between control plane and data plane is especially important. The control plane accepts commands such as “create a database,” “change an access policy,” or “increase capacity.” The data plane performs the actual work: transferring packets, processing queries, serving application requests, and storing or retrieving information. A service may continue processing existing traffic during a temporary control-plane interruption, but this depends on its design.

Resources are commonly organized into regions and availability zones. A region identifies a broad geographic deployment area; a zone is a provider-defined, relatively isolated failure domain within a region. Their exact meanings and failure-independence guarantees vary by provider. Placing multiple copies of a workload in separate zones can improve resilience, but it does not automatically eliminate application, configuration, or region-wide failures.

NIST's cloud reference architecture also distinguishes actors: the cloud consumer, provider, broker, auditor, and carrier. These categories clarify who consumes, supplies, intermediates, assesses, and transports cloud services. They are conceptual roles rather than a requirement that five separate companies participate in every cloud deployment.

The deeper topics of cloud networking and infrastructure as code examine how the layers are connected and controlled.

Service Models: IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS

Cloud services differ in how much of the technology stack the customer is expected to manage. The standard categories describe a continuum of operational responsibility rather than mutually exclusive product boxes.

Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS)

IaaS provides configurable infrastructure such as virtual servers, storage volumes, and networking. The customer typically chooses operating systems, installs applications, manages most guest-system configuration, and controls many network and security settings. The provider operates the underlying physical equipment and virtualization platform. IaaS is useful when an organization needs significant control over its software environment but does not want to own the servers.

Platform as a Service (PaaS)

PaaS supplies an environment for deploying applications without requiring the consumer to administer every operating-system or runtime component. The platform may provide language runtimes, deployment mechanisms, scaling features, or managed development facilities. Developers concentrate on application code, configuration, dependencies, and data while the provider assumes more of the supporting platform work.

Software as a Service (SaaS)

SaaS gives consumers access to a functioning application operated by a provider—for example, collaborative document software, customer relationship management, or a hosted communication platform. Customers generally configure accounts, permissions, data, and application settings rather than managing the servers or application runtime.

Managed databases, container platforms, and hosted AI services may span or supplement these categories. A customer can use IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS products simultaneously. The important questions are what capability is delivered, which configuration choices remain with the consumer, and who is responsible for each layer's operation and protection.

Public, Private, Community, Hybrid, and Multi-Cloud

A deployment model describes who can use a cloud environment and how its infrastructure is organized. The traditional NIST categories are public, private, community, and hybrid clouds.

  • Public cloud: infrastructure offered for general use by a provider, with customer resources logically separated on shared or dedicated underlying systems as contracted.
  • Private cloud: cloud infrastructure provisioned for the exclusive use of one organization, whether operated internally or by a third party.
  • Community cloud: infrastructure shared by organizations that have common concerns, such as governance, mission, or security requirements.
  • Hybrid cloud: a combination of distinct cloud infrastructures connected through mechanisms that permit appropriate application or data portability.

Multi-cloud is a commonly used additional strategy involving services from more than one cloud provider. It is not itself one of NIST's original four deployment models. An organization may employ multiple providers without integrating them into a single hybrid architecture. Conversely, a hybrid deployment may combine an internal private cloud with one public provider.

Deployment choices involve tradeoffs in control, workforce skills, data location, integration, dependence on providers, and cost. Private cloud does not automatically mean greater security; public cloud does not automatically mean lower expense. The result depends on the specific controls, workloads, and organizational capacity involved.

Compute Resources, Virtual Machines, and Containers

A cloud compute resource supplies processing capacity for applications and data workloads. Providers may offer general-purpose virtual processors, memory-intensive machines, specialized accelerators, and physical servers dedicated to one customer. Consumers choose resources according to workload characteristics, performance requirements, availability, and cost.

With virtualization software, a hypervisor can present one physical server as multiple isolated virtual machines (VMs). Each VM typically includes a guest operating system and an allocation of CPU, memory, storage, and networking. This enables workload separation and flexible placement, though virtualization still imposes overhead and does not eliminate risks from poor configuration or software vulnerabilities.

Containers package applications with their required dependencies while generally sharing the host operating-system kernel. They can start quickly and support consistent software delivery across environments. Container isolation and virtual-machine isolation are not identical, and containers are not automatically a stronger security boundary. Platforms such as Kubernetes can schedule and maintain containerized workloads across multiple machines.

Specialized accelerators, including GPUs, support computationally intensive rendering, machine learning, scientific simulation, and large-scale data processing. These workloads connect cloud computing to high-performance computing, but cloud capacity is not inherently faster than dedicated equipment: performance depends on architecture, networking, storage, and workload placement.

Detailed treatments belong in Virtual Machines and Cloud Compute and Containers and Cloud Orchestration.

Cloud Networking and Traffic Delivery

Cloud services depend on physical and logical networks linking users, applications, servers, storage systems, and providers. Inside a cloud platform, a customer often defines a virtual private cloud or equivalent isolated virtual network, then organizes subnets, routing tables, security rules, public-facing gateways, and private connections. These are software-defined abstractions implemented on the provider's network infrastructure.

Applications may need different exposure levels. A public website requires an internet-accessible entry point; a database usually needs access restricted to authorized application components. Load balancers distribute incoming requests among healthy service instances, while domain name systems (DNS) help clients locate services. Content delivery networks (CDNs) distribute copies of suitable content closer to audiences, reducing some network delays and origin-server workload.

Performance is affected by latency, bandwidth, congestion, geographic distance, and traffic charges. Data transferred between locations or outside a provider's network can be more expensive than data retained within a service boundary. The same architecture that appears efficient from a computing standpoint may therefore be inefficient from a network-cost or data-residency standpoint.

Cloud networking builds on network protocols and internet infrastructure; its specialized configuration topics are reserved under Cloud Networking and Virtual Private Clouds.

Cloud Storage, Databases, and Data Movement

Cloud storage is not a single universal file system. Providers commonly distinguish object storage, block storage, and file storage, each with different interfaces and performance characteristics. Object storage manages named objects and metadata through service APIs and is widely used for media, archives, backups, and data lakes. Block storage exposes addressable volumes appropriate for virtual-machine disks and some databases. File storage offers shared file-system semantics for applications requiring directories and familiar file operations.

A hosted application may also use a managed database for structured records. Providers offer relational, document, key–value, analytical, and other database services; their consistency, indexing, replication, backup, and query capabilities differ. A managed database reduces certain administrative duties but does not remove the need to design schemas, access controls, data lifecycles, and application behavior.

Data locality matters. Replicating data across zones or regions can improve availability and support recovery, but it may increase cost and introduce consistency or compliance questions. Data residency—where information is stored or processed—is distinct from data sovereignty, which concerns the legal and governmental controls applicable to that information. Neither should be assumed from the marketing term “cloud.”

Copies of data used for replication, snapshots, and backup serve different purposes. Replication may reproduce deletions or corruption; a backup should provide a recoverable historical state protected from the same failure. Related infrastructure is explained in Data Storage Methods and File Systems; cloud-specific storage and backup architecture belong in Cloud Storage Services.

Cloud Applications, APIs, and Managed Services

Cloud computing supports both traditional applications moved from local servers and software designed around managed services. A monolithic application may run successfully on one or several VMs. A more distributed design may divide responsibilities among distinct services—for example, a user interface, authentication service, transaction processor, and database. Neither style is universally superior; decomposition improves some kinds of independent scaling while introducing network dependencies and operational complexity.

APIs allow applications to request services and exchange data. Asynchronous queues and event streams can separate components so that a temporary delay in one component does not immediately stop every other component. Such mechanisms introduce questions of message ordering, duplicate processing, retries, idempotency, and failure handling. Reliable architecture therefore depends on explicit assumptions about delivery and consistency, not merely on attaching more services.

Managed services can accelerate delivery by outsourcing database operation, message queues, authentication tooling, monitoring, or other platform responsibilities. The tradeoff is greater dependence on a provider's APIs, configuration rules, service limits, and pricing structure. Development teams must determine which dependencies are acceptable and which need portable interfaces or an exit strategy.

This area intersects with software engineering and middleware. The deeper cloud-native application patterns are assigned to Cloud-Native Application Architecture.

Serverless Computing and Infrastructure Automation

Serverless computing is a service model in which developers invoke code or managed functionality without administering the servers running it. In a common form, functions as a service (FaaS), a provider executes code in response to events such as an HTTP request, file upload, or queue message. The servers still exist; the name refers to the abstraction and operational responsibilities presented to the consumer.

Serverless systems can be economical for intermittent or event-driven workloads, but their suitability depends on execution limits, startup behavior, runtime support, sustained traffic, and pricing. Long-running or predictable workloads may be better served by provisioned compute. Architectural choices should account for observability, security permissions, and how state is stored outside ephemeral execution environments.

Infrastructure as code (IaC) represents resource configurations in version-controlled files or declarative templates. Instead of manually creating infrastructure through a series of undocumented console actions, teams can review desired changes, validate them, and apply them consistently across environments. IaC does not guarantee correctness: flawed templates can reproduce errors at scale, so reviews, tests, authorization, and rollback planning remain essential.

Cloud delivery increasingly joins IaC with continuous integration, deployment pipelines, policy checking, and operational feedback. For deeper treatment, consult the proposed Serverless Computing and Infrastructure as Code sub-articles.

Reliability, Availability, and Disaster Recovery

Cloud platforms make redundant capacity available, but high availability must still be designed. An application with a single database, one network route, or an untested configuration may fail even when its provider operates many data centers. Reliability depends on removing critical single points of failure, preparing for expected faults, monitoring outcomes, and testing recovery procedures.

Availability describes whether a service can be used when needed; durability concerns preservation of data; resilience is the ability to continue or recover despite disruption. These are related but different properties. A highly durable object store does not guarantee that an application using it will always be available. Likewise, redundant servers do not guarantee protection from accidental deletion or logical corruption.

Organizations define a recovery point objective (RPO) to express the maximum acceptable loss of recent data, and a recovery time objective (RTO) for the acceptable time to restore operations. Achieving either objective requires suitable backups, replication, runbooks, application design, and tests. Service-level agreements can clarify contractual commitments, but credits under an agreement do not replace a business continuity plan.

Distributing workloads across availability zones may tolerate selected infrastructure failures. Multi-region designs can address a broader category of regional disruption but create complexity in data synchronization, routing, security, and operating costs. The objective is proportionate resilience, not redundancy without a defined business need.

Security, Shared Responsibility, and Governance

Cloud computing shifts the location and division of technical work; it does not transfer all security duties to the provider. Under the shared responsibility model, providers generally secure infrastructure that they operate, while customers remain responsible for the configuration, identities, data, and workloads within their scope. The exact boundary changes by product and service model. A SaaS customer typically manages fewer underlying components than an IaaS customer, but still must protect accounts, permissions, and information.

Identity and access management (IAM) governs which people, services, and automated processes may perform which actions. Strong designs rely on least privilege, multi-factor authentication where appropriate, short-lived credentials, separation of duties, and regular reviews. An overly broad role assignment or exposed secret can compromise resources even if the provider's infrastructure is functioning correctly. These principles connect to Access Control and Authentication Systems.

Data protection involves encryption in transit and at rest, key management, backup protection, security logging, and appropriate handling of sensitive information. Network segmentation, software updates, vulnerability management, and incident response remain necessary. The cloud's programmable management interfaces create useful automation opportunities, but a compromised administrative identity can also cause rapid, large-scale changes.

Governance assigns ownership for accounts, cost centers, permissible services, data classifications, compliance requirements, vendor contracts, and retention rules. An organization should verify where data is processed, how access is audited, what provider commitments cover, and how records will be exported or deleted when service ends. Independent assessments can inform decisions but do not eliminate customer obligations or establish universal compliance.

Operations, Observability, and Change Management

Cloud infrastructure is a continuing operational responsibility, not a deployment that can be forgotten after launch. Teams need visibility into what is running, whether users can complete important tasks, and how systems behave during incidents. Monitoring observes selected signals, while observability combines evidence—often metrics, logs, traces, and events—to investigate the internal behavior of complex systems.

Operational teams establish objectives for service performance, investigate incidents, track configuration changes, and maintain escalation procedures. Cloud platforms make some of this evidence accessible through centralized management services, but blind spots remain if instrumentation is incomplete. More telemetry is not automatically better: excessive logging can increase costs, expose sensitive data, or obscure important signals.

Autoscaling adjusts resource counts or capacity in response to predefined rules and measurements. It can handle some load changes but requires sensible thresholds, workload testing, and controls that prevent runaway cost or cascading failure. Scheduled scaling, capacity reservations, and load testing may be necessary where demand is predictable or startup time matters.

Reliable change management includes configuration review, staged rollouts, health checks, rollback procedures, and periodic verification that deployed resources match organizational policy. A production system is defined as much by its operating practices as by the infrastructure listed in its architecture diagram.

Cloud Economics and Financial Management

Cloud computing can reduce the need to purchase some infrastructure in advance, but it does not make computation free or necessarily inexpensive. Costs may accrue for provisioned or consumed computing time, stored data, operations against storage and databases, network transfers, monitoring, support, and managed services. A small instance used continuously may cost more over time than a larger temporary workload; a seemingly inexpensive design may become costly when it moves data frequently.

Cloud spending is influenced by unit economics: the cost of serving a transaction, active user, processed record, training job, or other meaningful unit of work. This perspective is more informative than simply asking whether the monthly bill increased. Usage growth may be economically healthy if the output grows faster than associated expense, while unused provisioned capacity is usually waste.

FinOps refers to the collaborative discipline of managing cloud technology value and spending through financial accountability, shared information, and iterative optimization. Typical practices include tagging and allocating expenses, forecasting, identifying idle resources, selecting appropriate capacity, and maintaining responsibility across engineering, finance, and operational teams.

Cost optimization must be evaluated alongside reliability, security, performance, and sustainability. Aggressive cost reduction can introduce fragile architecture, excessive staff work, or unacceptable risk. The appropriate question is whether the service delivers the required outcome at a justified total cost, not whether it achieves the lowest possible cloud invoice.

Practical Applications and an Illustrative Architecture

Cloud computing supports a wide variety of workloads. Retail organizations may host digital storefronts and inventory services; research institutions may provision temporary computing for large analyses; manufacturers may collect equipment telemetry; educators may run learning platforms; and media companies may distribute content to geographically dispersed audiences. These applications differ in scale, data sensitivity, and performance requirements, so they do not share a single ideal design.

Illustrative example: a growing online service

Consider a university operating an online learning platform for several thousand students. Rather than maintaining one permanently oversized server, the university might place its web application behind a load balancer, run the application on multiple compute instances, store uploaded assignments in object storage, and use a managed relational database for enrollment and coursework records. A CDN could cache eligible static resources near students. Monitoring would track response time, error rates, resource use, and costs.

  1. Entry: a student requests the platform using a domain name and secure network connection.
  2. Delivery: a CDN may serve eligible static files; dynamic requests reach an application service.
  3. Processing: the application authenticates the student, applies course permissions, and performs the requested work.
  4. Data: the application reads or updates a database and stores uploaded files in a suitable storage service.
  5. Operations: monitoring detects failures or high latency, while tested backups and recovery plans support continuity.

This is a conceptual design, not a production configuration. It omits details that would require dedicated engineering: identity federation, database transaction guarantees, data retention, accessibility, service quotas, network controls, and security testing. The example shows the cloud principle of combining separately managed services while remaining accountable for how they work together.

Limitations, Tradeoffs, and Emerging Directions

Cloud computing offers flexibility but introduces dependencies. Applications may rely on particular provider APIs, network routes, support arrangements, and regional capacity. Vendor lock-in can arise when specialized services simplify development but make later migration expensive. Portability is not an all-or-nothing property: data may be exportable while application logic, deployment automation, and operating procedures still require extensive rework.

Security and privacy also depend on institutional decisions. A poorly configured cloud system can be vulnerable despite the provider's technical capabilities. Service outages, contract changes, pricing revisions, and data-transfer limits are business risks that require contingency planning. Organizations should not assume that moving a workload to the cloud automatically improves its reliability, performance, or regulatory posture.

Cloud architectures increasingly intersect with edge computing, where processing takes place nearer users or devices, and with specialized services for machine learning, analytics, and high-performance workloads. Hybrid and multi-cloud operations continue to expand the challenge of managing identity, networking, telemetry, and data consistently across environments. Automation may reduce repetitive work, but it also increases the importance of correctness, oversight, and change control.

Cloud computing is therefore best understood as an engineering and management capability: a method of organizing access to computing resources, dividing operational responsibilities, and adapting systems to changing requirements. Whether it is the appropriate solution depends on the workload and the organization, not on the popularity of the delivery model.
