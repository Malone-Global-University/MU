Definition, Purpose, and Scope

The Agile Manifesto is a short statement of comparative priorities for software development, composed by seventeen software practitioners in February 2001. It is accompanied by twelve principles that explain how teams can turn those priorities into delivery practice. The document is not a legally binding standard, a certification program, a project-management framework, or a mandate to organize all work into sprints. It is a statement about the choices that make effective software development possible when customers, technology, and operating environments change.

Agile development principles are the working implications of the Manifesto: deliver useful results early, invite learning, maintain close collaboration, build quality into the product, support sustainable teamwork, and revise a plan when evidence warrants it. They challenge two common forms of organizational error: treating a detailed process as proof of progress and treating an approved plan as more important than solving the underlying customer problem. An effective team nevertheless retains disciplined planning, records, testing, controls, and accountability.

This encyclopedia article interprets the original four comparative values and twelve principles, explains their limits and operational consequences, and offers decision frameworks and worked scenarios. Its perspective is the contemporary software organization: product owners, engineers, designers, testers, operations personnel, customers, institutional managers, auditors, and executives who must distinguish productive adaptation from ungoverned change. For the surrounding methods and companion subjects, see Agile Development.

Historical Origins and Intellectual Context

The Manifesto emerged from a gathering of seventeen practitioners at Snowbird, Utah, in February 2001. They represented overlapping but distinct approaches to iterative and human-centered development, including Extreme Programming, Scrum, Crystal, and other lightweight methods. Their agreement was notable precisely because they did not settle on a single procedure. Instead, the participants expressed shared comparative values that could guide many different ways of developing software. The accompanying principles made those preferences more operational.

The Manifesto did not invent iteration, incremental delivery, customer consultation, automated testing, or collaborative engineering. Earlier research and practice had explored evolutionary development, rapid feedback, small releases, and other adaptive approaches. Nor did the Manifesto make every predictive or stage-gated technique obsolete. Its contribution was a compact synthesis and a public language for prioritizing direct collaboration and delivered value when the operational context contains uncertainty.

Historical accuracy requires separating the original 2001 text from later commercial practices. Mandatory daily status rituals, a particular ticketing product, story-point estimation, enterprise scaling certifications, and a fixed sprint length do not appear as universal requirements of the Manifesto. Some are features of specific frameworks, some are optional techniques, and others are local administrative inventions. See the original Manifesto and the authors' twelve principles for primary evidence.

How to Read Comparative Values

The Manifesto uses four paired comparisons. In each, the left-hand concern receives greater emphasis than the right-hand concern; it does not declare the right-hand concern worthless. The difference matters because a careless reading can be used to justify abandoning documentation, contract obligations, schedules, professional specialization, or security reviews. A sound reading requires balancing outcomes with the controls that make those outcomes reliable.

These are priority rules for judgment, not a scorecard on which one side always wins. Consider a payment service subject to retention, security, and regulatory controls. The team should collaborate and deliver a secure increment; the required evidence and contractual constraints remain part of what “valuable, working” means. Agile discipline is the ability to achieve the outcome without confusing bureaucracy with assurance.

Value One: People, Interactions, and Organizational Design

Software work relies on tacit knowledge: why a rule exists, what a user actually needs, which risks matter, and what a design assumption excludes. A workflow system can record tasks, but it cannot automatically resolve contradictory stakeholder interpretations. The first value therefore encourages direct conversations at the moment uncertainty appears, rather than a chain of documents that arrives too late to influence a decision.

Operational consequence: give the people closest to the work clear routes to the people who can answer questions; reduce unnecessary approval layers; create visible decision records after important conversations. Product leaders should explain desired outcomes, engineers should make technical risk intelligible, and stakeholders should engage with actual product behavior. Remote teams may use live sessions, written discussions, recorded demonstrations, and concise decision logs according to time-zone and accessibility requirements. The principle is effectiveness of human communication, not compulsory physical co-location.

Boundary: a process is valuable when it protects safety, makes quality repeatable, or clarifies ownership. An incident-response checklist, an accessibility acceptance criterion, or a controlled production release can enable, rather than obstruct, collaboration. The warning sign is a team that completes administrative steps while misunderstandings and defects remain unresolved.

Value Two: Usable Software and Evidence of Completion

A technical design that has not been implemented remains a hypothesis about software behavior. “Working software” directs attention to an integrated result that can be used, demonstrated, tested, and evaluated in a realistic environment. A feature is not complete merely because its task is marked done, code has been written, or a presentation claims readiness. It must behave correctly for the promised use case and meet the applicable standard of quality.

Operational consequence: define acceptance through examples and observable outcomes, create a verifiable increment in small steps, and distinguish demonstration from operational readiness. Automated tests, accessible interfaces, failure recovery, logs, security validation, and maintainable code may all be prerequisites for a credible increment. A prototype can legitimately be “working” for a narrowly stated experiment without being production ready, but the distinction must be explicit.

Boundary: documentation still matters when it transfers knowledge, records decisions, helps operators, enables accessibility, establishes audit evidence, or supports continuity. The choice is not between software and documents; it is between evidence of functional value and an accumulation of documents used as a substitute for value. Good teams produce enough accurate documentation to operate responsibly, update it as the product changes, and avoid producing volume with no decision value.

Value Three: Customer Collaboration and Accountable Agreements

Customers may discover what they need only after viewing a working example. An arrangement in which requirements are negotiated once and sealed off from later learning often preserves an obsolete interpretation of the problem. Agile collaboration therefore treats users, purchasers, domain experts, and other legitimate stakeholders as continuing participants in validation, prioritization, and acceptance.

Operational consequence: establish accessible review channels, clarify whose input is authoritative, show increments frequently, and make the cost of alternatives visible. Contracts can support adaptation by defining bounded scope changes, risk-sharing, acceptance procedures, spending limits, intellectual property rights, and escalation paths. In public procurement or heavily regulated work, recorded change decisions and traceability are often essential to preserve legitimacy.

Boundary: the word customer does not refer to a single infallible voice. The buyer may differ from the end user, the administrator, or the person harmed by a defect. A university lesson platform, for example, must reconcile instructor requirements, student usability, disability access, safeguarding, and institutional data rules. Product judgment consists of making these tensions visible and resolving them through evidence and authorized decision rights.

Value Four: Responsive Planning and Disciplined Change

Plans are models of a future that has not happened. Their usefulness declines when the initial assumptions are disproved by users, production behavior, new law, supplier changes, or technical investigation. The fourth value asks teams to revise a plan when new evidence changes the best path to the desired outcome. It rejects inflexible attachment to a baseline, not the practice of having a baseline in the first place.

Operational consequence: maintain a product goal, a prioritized body of work, release forecasts with stated assumptions, and a clear change protocol. Reassess work at an appropriate cadence. Protect near-term focus while allowing future scope to change. Distinguish a discovery that benefits the customer from an uncontrolled interruption that transfers risk and destroys predictability.

Boundary: adaptation is not an excuse to disregard funding decisions, promise dates that were never feasible, or keep changing direction without learning. Institutional leaders need a stable decision frame: the outcome sought, constraints that cannot be violated, the uncertainty being tested, and the point at which new evidence justifies a new commitment. An agile plan should be revisable and auditable rather than either immutable or nonexistent.

Principle 1: Early and Continuing Customer Value

The first principle establishes the governing purpose: value for the customer should be delivered early and repeatedly, rather than held back until every intended feature is finished. Early delivery reduces the duration between investment and feedback. Frequent delivery also limits the damage if the team has misunderstood the problem. The term value is not identical to the number of functions shipped. A small, reliable function that resolves a common failure can deliver more value than a large catalog of rarely used options.

Implementation: describe outcomes in the language of users or operations; select the smallest complete scenario that can test an important assumption; verify quality; and observe what changes after delivery. For an enrollment workflow, a first increment might allow a learner to retrieve a confirmed enrollment record, with appropriate authorization and accessibility. The team can then measure successful retrievals and failure reports before investing in sophisticated dashboards.

Failure test: if the release plan produces only unfinished fragments for months, or reports success solely through tasks completed, the organization may be optimizing activity rather than value. A research experiment, however, may yield learning rather than deployable software; its stated purpose should be identified clearly so it is not falsely represented as a finished product increment.

Principle 2: Welcome Change Without Abandoning Commitments

Changes in requirements can reveal new information: an accessibility barrier, a compliance obligation, a user behavior that contradicts an assumption, or an emerging market need. Agile processes seek to use such discoveries advantageously, including late in development when a rigid plan would resist them. Welcoming change is not the same as accepting every request immediately. It means examining the evidence and adjusting priorities in a controlled way.

Implementation: create an accessible path for proposing changes, record their rationale and affected outcomes, compare them with existing commitments, and decide through authorized product leadership. When work is already in progress, consider the disruption cost and the opportunity to include the change in the next increment. An urgent security vulnerability may warrant immediate intervention; a cosmetic preference may not.

Failure test: a team that calls all change “scope creep” can become unresponsive; a team that treats every suggestion as an emergency can become unable to finish anything. The managerial skill is to protect the goal while making the path to it revisable.

Principle 3: Deliver Integrated Software Frequently

Frequent delivery creates repeated opportunities to see whether different parts of a system work together. Integration that occurs only at the end allows incompatible design decisions to accumulate. Short feedback cycles also make errors easier to trace because fewer changes have been introduced since the last trusted state. In the original principle, delivery is discussed in periods of weeks or months, with shorter intervals preferred; later organizations have sometimes moved to much more frequent deployments where operationally appropriate.

Implementation: favor narrow, end-to-end slices, maintain an automated build and verification pipeline, and keep the product in an integrable state. Distinguish integration, delivery readiness, and production deployment. A team can create frequent releasable increments while releasing to users at a separate cadence mandated by safety, policy, or customer coordination.

Failure test: if every release requires weeks of manual integration and emergency testing, the team may be producing small tasks but not small working increments. Conversely, deploying often without adequate monitoring and rollback may increase risk rather than agility.

Principle 4: Continuous Business–Technical Cooperation

Business and technical personnel approach the same problem with different information. Product representatives know organizational needs, service obligations, and customers; engineers know implementation tradeoffs, technical constraints, and uncertainty. Continuous cooperation allows each to correct the other's assumptions before they harden into software. In practice, collaboration should include design, operations, safety, accessibility, and other domain specialists when relevant.

Implementation: establish named decision-makers, prompt clarification routines, demonstrable acceptance examples, and shared review of significant tradeoffs. A domain specialist should be available when the meaning of a requirement is genuinely ambiguous; the team should not rely on a hierarchy of tickets that separates authority from evidence. Document decisions that affect policy, compliance, or future maintenance.

Failure test: watch for engineers who cannot explain the user's need or sponsors who cannot explain what the product currently does. Weekly meetings alone do not guarantee cooperation if decisions remain unowned or are made after the implementation has already proceeded.

Principle 5: Motivation, Support, and Trust

Adaptive work requires people to recognize new problems, judge tradeoffs, and propose solutions. Constant micromanagement suppresses those capabilities and increases the time needed for every decision. The fifth principle therefore links good results with capable, motivated individuals who have the resources, supportive environment, and trust necessary to work well.

Implementation: establish clear outcome responsibilities and non-negotiable quality boundaries, then let practitioners choose suitable implementation methods. Provide technical mentoring, time to address defects, reliable development environments, access to domain expertise, and authority to escalate blockers. In a team with apprentices or junior engineers, trust includes appropriate review, not unattended autonomy on critical production functions.

Failure test: autonomy without goals becomes confusion; goals without autonomy become queues for permission. Management should assess whether people have the means to meet a commitment and whether significant decisions are delegated at the proper level.

Principle 6: Direct and Effective Communication

The original principle emphasized conversation in person as a particularly effective way to convey complex information. Its broader engineering lesson is that ambiguous matters benefit from responsive, interactive communication: participants can ask questions, check interpretations, expose assumptions, and notice where agreement is missing. Contemporary distributed and accessible teams should not turn a 2001 statement into a blanket exclusion of remote or asynchronous participation.

Implementation: choose the channel that fits the issue. A live design discussion may settle a disputed architecture faster than twenty comments; a written decision record can preserve that settlement for people who were absent. Critical instructions should not exist solely in ephemeral speech. Support captions, transcripts, keyboard-accessible collaboration, and time-zone fairness when teams require them.

Failure test: a communication pattern is poor when the same question repeatedly returns, decisions are invisible, or people who must implement a change were excluded from the discussion. Measure shared understanding and decision latency rather than merely counting meetings.

Principle 7: Functioning Software as the Main Progress Indicator

A development project can appear busy while producing little usable change. The seventh principle makes working software the primary indicator of progress because it is the strongest ordinary proof that ideas have become functioning capabilities. This does not imply that research, security work, documentation, migration planning, or operational preparation have no value; it means that claimed product progress should ultimately be anchored to verifiable behavior.

Implementation: define what a successful increment must demonstrate, use acceptance criteria and tests, and inspect the product under realistic conditions. Include the ability to handle error states, privacy boundaries, and accessibility requirements when these are part of its intended use. Track exceptions explicitly: an experimental prototype may prove a hypothesis without satisfying full release controls.

Failure test: status reports that rely only on hours expended, backlog items closed, design documents approved, or “percent complete” can conceal unintegrated code. Ask to see the working capability, the remaining known limitations, and the evidence supporting readiness.

Principle 8: Sustainable Development Pace

A heroic burst of overtime can temporarily increase visible output while weakening future delivery through fatigue, errors, turnover, and deferred maintenance. The eighth principle aims for a pace that sponsors, developers, and users can maintain. This is an economic and engineering commitment, not merely a wellness slogan: a system that depends on exceptional labor to meet routine obligations is difficult to forecast and vulnerable to failure.

Implementation: reserve capacity for defects, operational support, learning, and technical improvement. Set realistic work-in-progress limits, investigate repeated emergency work, and include maintenance demands in forecasts. A temporary incident response may require extraordinary effort, but it should be treated as exceptional and followed by recovery and prevention rather than normalized.

Failure test: observe whether quality deteriorates near deadlines, whether overtime becomes a planning assumption, and whether improvement work is regularly canceled to make room for new commitments. Throughput without sustainability is not a stable delivery capability.

Principle 9: Technical Excellence and Good Design

Adaptation becomes expensive when the codebase is poorly tested, highly coupled, opaque, or fragile. The ninth principle makes technical quality a condition for future agility. It connects short-cycle delivery with architecture and engineering discipline: small increments work best when each change can be integrated, tested, understood, and revised without destabilizing the entire product.

Implementation: use suitable automated testing, code review, refactoring, dependency management, observability, secure design, and controlled releases. Choose architectural decisions based on expected change, operational needs, and reversibility. Do not infer that every team needs microservices or a large framework; complexity should be justified by real constraints and supported by adequate operations capability.

Failure test: if a modest requirement change forces widespread edits and unpredictable regressions, the organization has likely accumulated design friction. A team that appears fast because it postpones verification may be borrowing time from future releases at high interest.

Principle 10: Simplicity and Avoidance of Unnecessary Work

Development capacity is scarce, and every feature creates maintenance, testing, security, documentation, and support obligations. The tenth principle encourages the deliberate exclusion of work that does not advance a validated purpose. Simplicity does not mean choosing the cheapest initial implementation regardless of consequences; it means designing for the actual problem and avoiding unsupported generality.

Implementation: remove low-value scope, defer speculative features, reuse established components when appropriate, and create small experiments before investing heavily. A team may decide not to build a custom notification engine when an existing vetted platform meets the requirement. The saved development time can be directed toward reliability, accessibility, or a more valuable feature.

Failure test: ask of every major component, “Which observed requirement justifies this complexity?” Beware of “simplifications” that merely transfer manual work to users or operators. The simplest responsible system is not necessarily the one with the fewest source-code lines; it is the one that meets the obligation with the least avoidable total complexity.

Principle 11: Emergent Architecture and Self-Management

Good designs often arise when people with different technical and domain knowledge work through a problem together. The eleventh principle recognizes the capacity of self-organizing teams to discover workable architectures, requirements, and designs rather than receiving every detail from a distant authority. Modern guidance commonly uses self-management to emphasize autonomy in how work is performed within a stated purpose and organizational constraints.

Implementation: give teams access to architectural context, domain expertise, quality standards, and systems that reveal consequences. Encourage review of alternatives, record consequential design decisions, and establish organization-wide interoperability rules where shared systems require them. Shared architectural constraints should be explicit rather than silently enforced through a sequence of vetoes.

Failure test: central design control can become a bottleneck, but distributed decisions can also fragment standards. A good governance model identifies decisions that a product team can safely make alone and those needing coordination because they affect security, data contracts, customer experience, or other teams.

Principle 12: Reflection and Deliberate Improvement

Teams cannot assume that a process remains optimal simply because it once worked. The twelfth principle institutionalizes periodic reflection on effectiveness and purposeful modification of work practices. It is a continuous-improvement mechanism: the team examines evidence, identifies a constraint, runs a bounded improvement, and checks whether the result is better.

Implementation: select a small set of operational signals—such as escaped defects, blocked work age, user failure rates, review delays, or team interruption load—alongside qualitative observations. Choose one or two concrete interventions with an owner and a review date. Record whether the change helped and revise it if evidence is inconclusive. Protect the opportunity to raise uncomfortable issues without turning the retrospective into a performance tribunal.

Failure test: a retrospective that produces the same list of complaints every cycle but never changes conditions has become a ritual. Equally, a team that changes its process each week without measuring effects cannot distinguish improvement from instability. The principle demands both adaptation and disciplined learning.

Values and Principles Versus Frameworks and Methods

Agile values are normative guidance: they indicate priorities when a team must choose. A framework supplies defined accountabilities, artifacts, and events; an engineering method supplies specific techniques; a governance system specifies approval rights and duties. Confusing these levels produces inaccurate claims, such as “the Manifesto requires story points,” “Scrum requires daily manager status reports,” or “Kanban cannot be agile.”

The 2020 Scrum Guide describes a specific framework based on empiricism and lean thinking; it should not be misrepresented as the text of the original Manifesto. The 2025 Kanban Guide defines Kanban as a strategy for optimizing flow of value through a process. Either may be employed in a way that supports agile principles—or in a way that contradicts them if practices displace outcomes.

Empiricism: Transparency, Inspection, and Adaptation

Empirical development proceeds through observation and revision. A team forms a plausible plan, produces an inspectable result, compares evidence with expectations, and adapts. This contrasts with assuming that a detailed initial analysis can predict every product requirement or operating condition. Empiricism is particularly useful for complex problems in which cause and effect cannot be fully known before implementation.

Transparency means stakeholders understand the goal, current product behavior, important assumptions, and unresolved risks. Inspection examines meaningful evidence rather than solely the presentation of progress. Adaptation changes the work or its governing assumptions when inspection reveals a better course. The three reinforce one another: undisclosed failure makes inspection misleading; inspection that never affects a decision consumes attention without learning.

Good empirical work is not anti-scientific or unstructured. It uses explicit hypotheses, credible measures, confounder awareness, and appropriate decision thresholds. A team may test whether a new enrollment form increases completed applications, while recognizing that a seasonal intake surge could explain part of the change. It should record how the result was evaluated and what will be tried next.

Iteration, Increments, and Feedback Loops

Iteration is repeated learning and revision; an increment is an addition to the product's usable capability. A team can iterate without delivering an increment—such as repeatedly redesigning mockups—or produce increments without fixed-duration iterations. An agile delivery model aims to combine learning and verified capability frequently enough to guide the next investment.

A practical loop has seven elements: select an outcome; identify the riskiest assumption; choose a small testable increment; implement and verify it; expose it to authorized feedback; measure what was learned; and reorder the next decision. The loop can operate within Scrum Sprints or in continuous flow. The cadence should match the cost and urgency of learning, not an arbitrary organizational target.

Feedback has levels. Code-level feedback discovers defects quickly. Integration feedback reveals conflicts between components. Usability feedback reveals comprehension and accessibility barriers. Production feedback shows performance and operational behavior. Strategic feedback asks whether investment is actually improving outcomes. Optimizing only the fastest loop may still miss the largest business problem.

Turning Principles into a Product Operating System

An organization needs more than declared values: it needs a repeatable operating system that allocates authority, limits risk, and produces evidence. For an agile software product, that system can be understood through six connected decisions. The product leadership function defines the problem and expected outcome. The team explains technical options and constraints. A work-selection mechanism orders changes. Quality controls determine what counts as completed. Review mechanisms assess behavior and customer results. Governance establishes spending authority, privacy, security, and operating obligations.

Useful governance separates outcome constraints from implementation prescriptions. Leadership may require compliant enrollment records and an availability objective without prescribing every database function. Engineers can choose the implementation within those constraints and disclose costs. Conversely, teams should not redefine customer objectives unilaterally because a preferred technology is easier to build.

A minimal operational package might include a one-page product objective; a visible prioritized queue; a written acceptance and release standard; named decision owners; a regular feedback demonstration; and a concise record of risks, evidence, and changes. The package should scale to the severity of the product, not to the popularity of agile ceremonies.

Requirements, Acceptance, and the Meaning of Done

Agile development does not eliminate requirements. It encourages requirements to become more precise as the team learns. A useful requirement states the need, context, constraints, and observable success condition. A user story may be a helpful conversation prompt, but it is not a substitute for domain rules, edge-case analysis, privacy protections, accessibility requirements, or acceptance tests.

Consider a student applying for a course. “Improve enrollment” is an objective; “a qualified student can submit a form and receive an accessible confirmation without duplicated records” is a testable capability. The team may discover that a prospective learner has no permanent email address or uses assistive technology. These observations revise requirements and acceptance examples rather than being dismissed as late interference.

A Definition of Done is a shared quality standard used in Scrum and often adapted elsewhere. It is not part of the Manifesto's four values, but it helps operationalize the requirement that software function. For this example, done may require validated authorization, functional error recovery, accessibility checks, automated regression tests, deployment evidence, and an updated support procedure. A demonstration that bypasses these gates should be labeled a prototype, not passed off as ready for production.

Collaboration, Authority, and Stakeholder Conflicts

Customer collaboration is most productive when participants know who can decide what. A product sponsor may control the economic objective, a product leader may order backlog work, engineering may own implementation design, and a security function may set non-negotiable controls. Without a decision model, frequent meetings can multiply contradictory instructions rather than resolve them.

Recommended decision protocol: describe the contested outcome; list the affected stakeholder groups; identify the policy or technical constraints; request evidence; assign an accountable decision-maker; record the decision and its review trigger. If a user preference conflicts with accessibility, privacy, legal duties, or documented safety limits, do not treat the most vocal participant as the only customer. Escalate the tradeoff to a legitimate authority.

Cross-functional collaboration does not require every specialist to attend every meeting. It requires the right knowledge to enter a decision before it becomes expensive to reverse. Teams should periodically map delays caused by missing expertise, approval bottlenecks, or unclear responsibilities.

Planning Horizons, Forecasts, and Risk

Planning in an agile organization occurs at several horizons. A strategic horizon defines why the product is funded. A product horizon describes outcomes to pursue and possible themes. A near-term horizon specifies what work is feasible given capacity, dependencies, and operational obligations. Each horizon has a different degree of uncertainty; commitments should not imply identical precision at all levels.

Use forecasts as conditional statements: “With current staffing and service interruptions, these four capabilities are likely by the end of the period; the fifth depends on a vendor decision.” Record assumptions and update them when facts change. A fixed statutory deadline or contract milestone must be treated as a constraint to plan around, not as a reason to fabricate confidence. Scope, staffing, risk tolerance, or timing may have to be renegotiated.

Technical risk can be reduced through small experiments, architecture spikes with explicit outcomes, early integration, realistic test environments, and incremental releases. These activities may not themselves create visible features, but they support the value of eventual working software. Keep them proportionate, time-bounded, and tied to a named uncertainty.

Technical Excellence as a Delivery Capability

Technical excellence makes change cheaper. When tests are trustworthy, build pipelines are fast, code is comprehensible, interfaces are stable, and production systems reveal failures, teams can explore new requirements without placing every release at risk. When technical debt accumulates unchecked, even minor changes can require large coordination efforts and slow validation.

Practices should match context: version control and code review; automated build verification; unit, integration, accessibility, and security testing; refactoring; deployment automation; observability; controlled dependencies; and incident learning. Continuous integration is particularly important for reducing divergence between developers' work, but it is not a complete guarantee of correctness. A pipeline that runs only superficial checks can report success while the product remains unsafe or unusable.

Leadership should avoid a false choice between features and quality. Engineering work can be framed in terms of avoided failure risk, faster future modification, lower support effort, and continuity of service. A useful indicator is whether product changes become easier, safer, and more predictable over time—not simply whether the team appears to code faster.

Ethics, Accessibility, Security, and Compliance

Agility creates a danger if “move faster” is interpreted as permission to defer responsibilities that affect people. In products handling educational records, financial information, health data, public services, or critical operations, privacy and security requirements are part of the value proposition. Accessibility likewise belongs in the definition of usable software, not in a perpetual final-phase remediation backlog.

Integrate controls into ordinary delivery: threat modeling where justified; authorization tests; secure defaults; secrets management; dependency review; retention and deletion policies; traceable changes; and accessible interaction and content verification. Separate evidence needed for legal or institutional accountability from ceremonial paperwork that offers little assurance. Teams can adapt how evidence is produced without abandoning the obligation to produce it.

An agile team can operate within approved budgets, purchasing rules, and audit trails. The relevant question is whether those constraints are visible early enough to shape design and increments. Late-stage discovery of a required control is usually a failure of cross-functional product planning, not evidence that governance is incompatible with agile development.

Measurements That Support Learning

Measurement should reveal whether a team is delivering useful outcomes and preserving its capability to deliver more. No single metric proves agility. Counting story points, meetings, tickets, or code commits mainly measures a chosen activity representation and can easily be gamed. A balanced view combines customer outcomes, delivery health, product quality, and sustainable team operations.

The current DORA guidance identifies five software delivery performance measures. Its model evolved from an earlier four-metric set, so references to “the four DORA metrics” may describe a historical formulation rather than the current guidance. These measures are useful in context, especially at the level of an application or service; turning them into quotas or cross-team leaderboards can distort behavior.

Worked Case Study: An Online Lesson Platform

Initial situation: a university is modernizing its online lesson platform. Administrators propose a twelve-month project with fifty defined features. Students report difficulty resuming lessons after connectivity loss; instructors report errors publishing assessment revisions; disability support staff report forms that do not announce errors properly. A schedule dominated by administrative feature counts may miss these urgent problems.

Outcome framing: instead of accepting the fifty-feature list as the sole success criterion, the cross-functional group defines three near-term outcomes: reliable lesson resumption, safe assessment revision, and accessible recovery from form errors. These outcomes have testable behaviors and affect different stakeholder groups. Product leadership authorizes a narrow pilot, subject to the university's data and accessibility requirements.

  1. First learning cycle: the team interviews students with unreliable connections, identifies failure conditions, creates a small resumable-progress increment, and verifies it across selected devices and low-connectivity scenarios.
  2. Second cycle: the team adds recovery messaging and accessible error announcements after observation shows users cannot reliably distinguish saved progress from pending uploads.
  3. Third cycle: instructors test a controlled assessment revision workflow; the team discovers a requirement for preserving audit history and includes it before general release.
  4. Review: the team compares task success and failures with the baseline, documents remaining problems, and revises the broader roadmap under the original budget ceiling.

Interpretation: early working increments provide feedback (principles 1, 3, and 7); new evidence changes priorities (principle 2); participants collaborate across functions (principles 4–6); accessibility, maintainability, and controlled records establish quality (principle 9); the team limits unnecessary scope (principle 10); and a retrospective changes the testing workflow (principle 12). The pilot is not proof of universal success: the group must still examine sample limitations, operational stability, support demand, and whether benefits persist after scale-up.

Worked Example: A Change Request Near Release

Suppose a course-search feature is scheduled for release in two weeks. A pilot shows that screen-reader users cannot reliably identify search-result categories. The existing plan includes a decorative personalization feature, but its customer value is uncertain. A manager argues that no requirements can change after the deadline is announced.

DecisionRigid-plan responsePrinciple-aligned response
Interpret the discoveryClassify it as late scope expansionRecognize an unmet usability and accessibility requirement
Choose priorityKeep cosmetic feature to protect scheduleReorder uncertain work; protect required quality
Manage commitmentClaim delivery success when ticket closesDisclose release impact and seek an authorized decision
Verify outcomeReview screenshots onlyTest keyboard and assistive-technology use with defined acceptance cases
Capture learningMove on after releaseChange design review and test coverage to prevent repetition

Agile reasoning does not automatically require postponement. If the issue can be corrected and verified safely inside the authorized release window, proceed. If it cannot, the accountable decision-maker may need to revise scope or timing. The governing question is whether the product is fit for its intended use under its actual obligations—not whether every original line item remains intact.

Selecting an Approach: A Decision Framework

No universal method follows directly from the Manifesto. The right delivery approach depends on the nature of the work, the cost of failure, dependency structure, degree of uncertainty, and availability of feedback. A product with a stable, inspectable backlog may benefit from Scrum's regular planning and review structure. A support platform receiving unpredictable incidents may benefit from continuous flow and explicit work-in-progress controls. A critical migration may need more up-front coordination, dry runs, rollback plans, and controlled cutovers while retaining short feedback cycles.

Decision contextReasonable starting pointNon-negotiable safeguard
Uncertain user experienceSmall prototypes and frequent user evaluationClear distinction between experiment and release
Complex product with stable multidisciplinary teamRegular outcome-based inspection and iterative incrementsIntegrated quality and a single transparent priority system
High-volume operational requestsVisualized workflow, service classes, bounded WIPVisible escalation rules and operational ownership
Regulated or safety-critical softwareIncremental engineering inside formal assurance gatesRequired review, records, validation, and authorization
Known, repeatable deployment sequenceAutomated pipeline and planned checkpointsRollback, monitoring, and change accountability
External deadline fixed by law or contractRisk-first sequencing and transparent conditional forecastsNo false claims of certainty or unauthorized control bypass

Selection rule: choose practices because they improve feedback, safety, and customer value; keep them only while evidence supports their usefulness. A methodology becomes counterproductive when adherence is valued more than the outcomes it was selected to achieve.

Common Misreadings and Failure Modes

  • “Agile means no planning.” A team needs goals, cost and capacity assumptions, dependencies, constraints, and conditional forecasts. Agile planning is revisited rather than treated as infallible.
  • “Documentation is anti-agile.” The issue is excessive documentation that substitutes for functional evidence; accurate records, legal artifacts, operating runbooks, and knowledge transfer remain necessary.
  • “Every change must be accepted immediately.” Changes require evaluation, prioritization, decision rights, and respect for existing obligations.
  • “Fast releases guarantee customer value.” Speed increases the frequency of learning opportunities; it cannot establish that the chosen problem or solution is worthwhile.
  • “Scrum and agile are the same thing.” Scrum is one framework; the Manifesto is broader. Specific Scrum requirements should be attributed to the Scrum Guide.
  • “Story points measure developer productivity.” Relative estimation is context-bound and manipulable. It should not become an individual performance ranking.
  • “Good teams have no architecture.” The ninth and eleventh principles support good design and responsible team-level decision-making, not architectural neglect.
  • “Remote teams cannot be agile.” Collaboration can be direct and effective through accessible, synchronous and asynchronous channels; the objective is shared understanding.
  • “Retrospectives are complaint sessions.” Reflection must lead to observable, assigned changes rather than a recurring unowned list.
  • “Security reviews prevent agility.” Risk-based automated and human review can be integrated into short cycles; omission of mandatory safeguards is not evidence of responsiveness.

Organizational anti-patterns often combine several errors: a manager demands fixed scope and fixed dates, requires teams to increase velocity every period, calls all change agile, and simultaneously removes the capacity for testing and refactoring. The result is neither predictable delivery nor responsible adaptability. The corrective action is to align funding, incentives, product decisions, and technical quality with observed outcomes.

Implementation Checklist for Organizations

The following operational questions can be used by a development team or governing body to evaluate whether its practices substantively implement the principles. They are diagnostic prompts, not a branded certification or a mandatory universal procedure.

  1. Purpose: Can the team explain which user or operating outcome the next increment is expected to improve?
  2. Feedback: Is there a credible, accessible mechanism for learning from actual use before large sunk costs accumulate?
  3. Cadence: Can the team integrate and verify a small change without a lengthy end-stage rescue effort?
  4. Authority: Are ordering decisions, technical design choices, and mandatory controls assigned to named accountable parties?
  5. Quality: Does “done” require the tests, security, accessibility, maintenance, and release conditions necessary for intended use?
  6. Planning: Are forecasts documented with their assumptions and revised when those assumptions no longer hold?
  7. Change: Can an important new requirement be evaluated without either automatic rejection or chaotic interruption?
  8. Capacity: Does the routine plan leave room for incidents, defects, and improvements?
  9. Learning: Does reflection result in specific changes whose effects are later inspected?
  10. Evidence: Can leadership distinguish outcome progress from production volume, meeting attendance, or administratively closed tasks?

Several “no” answers do not establish that a team is incapable; they identify governance and process constraints worth investigating. Improvement should be staged. Attempting to change every role, meeting, reporting format, and technical pipeline simultaneously can overwhelm a team and obscure which intervention actually worked.

Comparison: Agile Principles and Predictive Controls

Predictive planning and agile delivery need not be absolute opposites. Some work has stable dependencies, mature specifications, long procurement lead times, or irreversible physical constraints. Those features can justify more detailed early planning. Other work contains unknown customer behavior and fast-changing technology, making short learning cycles essential. Large programs frequently contain both conditions.

QuestionPredictive emphasisAgile emphasisResponsible combination
What is specified early?Detailed deliverables and sequenceOutcome, constraints, near-term incrementFix obligations; revise uncertain implementation details
How is progress inspected?Milestone completionVerified working result and learningTrack authorized milestones with product evidence
How are changes handled?Formal baseline controlContinuous evidence-led reprioritizationUse a risk-based change process with clear authority
How is quality assured?Specified reviews and final acceptanceIntegrated verification each incrementEmbed tests early and retain required final sign-offs
How are uncertainties treated?Assumptions to reduce before executionHypotheses to evaluate during executionFront-load irreversible risk; experiment where reversible

The strongest model is the one that reliably governs the actual task. Calling a practice “agile” or “traditional” does not establish its suitability. Organizations should ask which uncertainty is being managed, how early mistakes can be detected, and what evidence an authorized stakeholder needs to release the next investment.

Discussion Questions and Applied Exercises

  1. Comparative values: Give an example in which keeping a formal document is essential to effective agile delivery. Explain why this does not contradict the Manifesto.
  2. Principle mapping: A team has completed thirty backlog tickets but cannot demonstrate an integrated feature. Identify at least three principles at risk and propose a corrective plan.
  3. Decision rights: An engineer wants to ship a new authentication flow before security review. Which agile principles are relevant, and why is bypassing the control not an appropriate interpretation?
  4. Communication: Redesign a remote-team approval process so that decisions are timely, accessible, and retrievable after the conversation.
  5. Change management: A customer discovers a serious usability flaw ten days before release. Write a brief decision memo specifying options, evidence, authority, and effect on the forecast.
  6. Delivery cadence: Distinguish iteration frequency, software integration frequency, release readiness, and production deployment. Explain why each may occur on a different schedule.
  7. Measurement: Define two customer-outcome measures and two engineering-health measures for a student lesson platform. Describe one way each metric could mislead.
  8. Simplicity: Compare a custom workflow engine with a vetted existing service. Specify what evidence would justify building the custom option.
  9. Sustainability: Identify leading signs that short-term output is being achieved by accumulating maintenance debt or overloading staff.
  10. Reflection: Draft one improvement experiment with an owner, a baseline, a review date, and a criterion for retaining or reversing the change.

Glossary of Core Terms

Acceptance criterion
A condition used to determine whether specified behavior satisfies a requirement.
Agile Manifesto
The 2001 statement of four comparative software-development values, supported by twelve principles.
Backlog
An ordered or managed collection of proposed work; the exact definition depends on the method used.
Continuous integration
A development practice of integrating changes frequently and validating them through automated checks.
Customer value
A benefit realized or credibly expected by a legitimate stakeholder or user through product behavior.
Definition of Done
A shared understanding of the quality conditions an Increment must meet; a defined commitment in Scrum.
Empiricism
Learning and decision-making based on observed evidence and revisable assumptions.
Feedback loop
A sequence in which a result is observed, interpreted, and used to improve a subsequent decision.
Increment
An integrated addition to a product's capability that meets its relevant quality standard.
Iteration
A bounded cycle of work or learning that informs the next cycle; not necessarily a deployment.
Lead time
Elapsed time between defined starting and ending events; definitions vary by system and metric.
Product outcome
An observable change in user or organizational results, distinct from the quantity of work delivered.
Refactoring
Improving internal code structure while preserving intended externally visible behavior.
Release readiness
The state in which software meets the organization's conditions for authorized release.
Self-management
The team's authority to decide how to perform its work inside agreed purpose, responsibilities, and constraints.
Technical debt
Future effort or risk introduced by design and implementation compromises, whether intentional or accidental.
Throughput
Number of defined work items completed within a stated period.
Work in progress (WIP)
Work started but not yet completed in a specified workflow.
