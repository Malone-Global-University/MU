Definition, Purpose, and Scope

Work-in-progress (WIP) limits and flow management concern the deliberate control of how many work items may be started but not yet finished within a defined service. A team may have fifty valid requests, but that does not mean it should begin all fifty. A limit creates a capacity boundary: completing or advancing an item frees a slot for another. Effective WIP management is therefore an operating policy for finishing valuable work, not merely a counter displayed above a column on a task board. The goal is a delivery system that produces usable outcomes with reasonable speed, reliability, quality, and workload sustainability.

The distinction between demand, committed work, and completed value is fundamental. Demand includes requests, ideas, defects, incidents, and opportunities. Committed work consists of items that have crossed the service's defined started point. Delivered work meets its defined finished point, with the required verification or other evidence. WIP is the count in between. An overloaded organization often confuses a large backlog with a large amount of work already underway. Such a system accumulates queues, context switching, coordination overhead, and aging tasks even while employees appear fully occupied.

This article specializes the companion topic Kanban Workflow Design. Its focus is the mechanisms and economics of controlling unfinished work: where limits belong, how to respond to congestion, which metrics reveal flow, when limits need modification, and what happens when teams confuse local utilization with organizational results. It treats numerical scenarios as instructional examples rather than universal targets or evidence about a particular organization.

The Historical Logic of Limiting Unfinished Work

The underlying problem is older than software development. Manufacturing lines, hospitals, transportation networks, contact centers, and computer systems all face a tension between incoming demand and finite service capacity. When arrivals reach or exceed what a system can complete, queues tend to grow, even if every individual worker remains busy. In the Toyota Production System, replenishment signals helped prevent upstream activity from producing without a corresponding downstream need. Knowledge-work Kanban adapts the pull principle to information-intensive services, while recognizing that software tasks differ in complexity, risk, and required collaboration.

Modern Kanban systems combine visibility, explicit movement policies, WIP controls, and feedback from observed results. The May 2025 Kanban Guide requires an explicit mechanism to control WIP and defines four core flow metrics. Kanban University's broader Official Guide to the Kanban Method, version 2.1 (November 2026), emphasizes limiting work in progress and managing the movement of work within a service-oriented improvement model. These publications overlap in purpose but should not be represented as identical standards. A method can share flow concepts without imposing the same terminology, practices, or reporting requirements.

The central historical lesson is practical: more starts do not necessarily produce more finishes. A development manager who approves ten simultaneous features may improve apparent activity while delaying the point at which any feature becomes useful. A service manager who leaves twenty incidents open may increase the inventory of unresolved risk. WIP control changes the conversation from how many activities are underway to what is preventing a valuable item from moving to a defensible finish.

Value Flow Versus Resource Utilization

Resource utilization measures how much available time or capacity is occupied. Value flow measures the progress of work toward useful delivery. The two are related but not equivalent. A team can operate at high utilization while its completed output remains low because items spend most of their elapsed time waiting for reviews, approvals, environments, decisions, or access to a specialist. Busy workers do not automatically imply a healthy delivery system.

Consider three developers who each start several changes to keep themselves occupied. Every change eventually needs the same security reviewer. Even if that reviewer works continuously, the queue of pending reviews can expand faster than it clears. Beginning another change does not increase review capacity; it makes more unfinished work visible or, worse, hides more work in personal branches. A flow-oriented policy may ask developers to help complete reviews, simplify the change set, improve automated checks, or pause intake until the queue is under control.

A sustainable system also preserves slack for unplanned incidents, quality investigation, mentoring, improvement, and recovery. Treating one hundred percent nominal utilization as a permanent performance objective ignores unavoidable variability in arrivals, service times, and dependencies. The appropriate balance depends on service criticality, the cost of delay, the variability of demand, and the economic cost of keeping capacity available. A WIP limit is an instrument for learning that balance, not a command to eliminate useful collaboration or leave valuable requests unattended.

Defining the Measurement Boundary

No WIP figure is meaningful until the service defines when an item starts and when it finishes. A team counting a work item as started upon entering development will show a smaller WIP inventory than a customer-facing service counting it as started when a request is formally accepted. Neither is automatically wrong; each describes a different segment of the value stream. A governance report becomes misleading when it combines boundaries without explanation or quietly changes them from month to month.

Write a boundary statement before setting limits. For example: “This workflow starts when a website change is accepted for implementation and ends when the updated page is available publicly and required publication checks have passed.” Then define what constitutes one work item. A tiny text correction, a multi-page redesign, and a new identity service are not interchangeable in effort or risk. Throughput counts them as separate completed items, so forecasting should account for changing mixtures and substantial differences in size.

Work before commitment is normally options or backlog inventory, not WIP in that delivery workflow. Blocked work after commitment remains WIP unless the Definition of Workflow explicitly and consistently establishes a different measurement boundary. Merely moving a card to “Waiting” does not make its delay disappear. Cancellation and abandonment also need recorded rules: an item removed without delivery is not counted as successfully finished work. Each policy should be accessible to contributors and users of the metrics.

The Definition of Workflow and WIP Control

The May 2025 Kanban Guide identifies six minimum elements of a Definition of Workflow (DoW): the work items that move; started and finished points; at least one state between them; how WIP is controlled; explicit movement policies; and a service level expectation (SLE) consisting of an elapsed period and an associated probability. A board is the visualization of this shared definition. There is no mandatory board layout, numeric WIP threshold, or software product in the guide.

A WIP policy should state the scope of the limit, the items that consume it, its value or other control rule, the authority to admit work, the signal for replenishment, the behavior required when the limit is reached, and the conditions under which exceptions are permitted. “WIP limit: 6” by itself leaves many operational ambiguities. Does “Waiting for Test” count? Are incidents included? Can one person move a card backward? Does a blocked ticket reserve capacity? Who records an exceptional seventh item and its impact?

A useful written policy might read: “From development start to verified release, no more than six feature-change items may be active; all blocked items remain counted; a new item is selected only after a slot becomes free; urgent production incidents may use the separately defined expedite capacity with a visible exception record.” The particular number six is only an example. The strength of the policy lies in its unambiguous relationship to the whole workflow, its explicit exception rule, and its applicability when real pressure occurs.

What Counts as Work in Progress

At any moment, WIP equals the number of items inside the agreed start-to-finish boundary. Items being coded, checked, reviewed, tested, reworked, queued for deployment, or awaiting an external approval may all belong to that inventory. If the finish point requires deployment and acceptance evidence, then passing a code review is not equivalent to completion. Counting merely “busy hands” or only items in a column marked Active can exclude most of the actual delay.

The WIP metric and the WIP limit policy are distinct. The metric is observed inventory, which may be three, six, or eight. The limit is a rule governing what the system should permit, perhaps a systemwide cap of six. A team above its limit has not necessarily invalidated its measurement; it has surfaced a condition requiring explanation or correction. Do not erase cards, reclassify work opportunistically, or move work into a shadow queue to make the dashboard conform to policy.

A carefully modeled system may distinguish several inventories: upstream options, committed WIP, pending downstream release, and post-delivery support. Each should be labeled and tied to decisions. In multiteam environments, a feature can be “done” for one development component yet unfinished for the customer. Track both local state and end-to-end obligation. Reporting only a local team’s WIP while ignoring cross-team queues can shift congestion rather than resolve it.

Systemwide Limits and State-Level Limits

A systemwide limit caps all items between the defined start and finish. It directly constrains total committed inventory and makes the trade-off between starting and finishing visible. State-level limits restrict particular activities such as development, peer review, or validation. A system may use both: total WIP no greater than eight, with no more than three items awaiting or undergoing review. A shared reviewer might benefit from a tighter queue, while a flexible development group needs some freedom to shift effort.

State limits can expose bottlenecks but can also create perverse behavior if treated mechanically. If the testing column is full, developers may move unfinished items into a synthetic “ready” column with no limit and continue opening work. The overall queue then grows while each displayed state nominally obeys its rule. Systemwide controls counter this kind of inventory leakage. Conversely, an overall limit alone may allow one state to monopolize every available slot, leaving downstream capacity idle. A layered policy is often worth testing.

The correct design is the least complicated arrangement that reliably changes decisions in favor of completion. A team should be able to explain the controls without a specialist interpreting a board configuration.

Pull Systems and Capacity Signals

In a push system, an upstream party moves another task into the next stage regardless of the capacity available. In a pull system, downstream availability signals when another item may be accepted. The receiving service has an explicit say in how much work it can handle. Pull does not mean that every person individually chooses whatever is easiest; priority, quality, risk, and allocation policies still govern which eligible item is selected.

Suppose the review stage is capped at two items. Two are present and one new implementation is ready. The developer should not silently insert a third review item or declare the work finished. The team could assist with review, hold the item in its preceding counted state, or renegotiate the work allocation while preserving the control. When a reviewed item moves onward and the review count drops to one, the vacant slot becomes a signal to pull the next eligible item. The signal travels upstream, preventing unlimited queues from being pushed downstream.

For effective pull, the team needs explicit entry criteria, exit criteria, ordering criteria, and treatment of exceptions. “Take the next available” is not enough when one item is security-critical, another has a fixed contractual date, and another can be deferred safely. Capacity without a prioritization policy creates arbitrary service; priority without capacity creates overcommitment. Both belong in the Definition of Workflow.

Choosing a Starting WIP Limit

There is no universal formula that yields a correct WIP limit from head count. For a new service, begin with what actually exists: average active inventory, service staffing, demand types, stage capacities, blockers, and recent completion behavior. If reliable records are absent, set an explicit provisional limit, observe what happens, and revise using evidence. The objective is not to select an impressively low number; it is to constrain unnecessary starting without starving delivery of useful work.

One pragmatic experiment is to compare the current average WIP with what the team can regularly complete over an interval. If the team usually finishes four similar items per week and carries sixteen active items, ask what produces the four-week average implied by the simple long-run relationship. A trial cap of ten or twelve may reveal whether earlier review, smaller changes, or less multitasking improves completion. That cap is not mathematically “proved”; it is a policy hypothesis tested against throughput, cycle-time distribution, item aging, incidents, quality, and staff experience.

Make starting conditions explicit. A cap that is so low that necessary specialists cannot work simultaneously on complementary pieces may degrade throughput. A cap so high that no one ever encounters a pull constraint is unlikely to change behavior. The team must also define what to do when the control is met: swarm to finish, unblock an existing item, reduce batch size, improve a test environment, or work on genuinely upstream discovery that does not count as committed delivery. Simply creating an untracked project is not a valid escape.

WIP Limits and Team Collaboration

A WIP limit is not a rigid rule that each ticket must be owned by one isolated worker. A feature with a deployment dependency may need a developer, tester, designer, and operations specialist working together. If the board has no available capacity for a new item, collaboration on a shared unfinished outcome can reduce overall elapsed time. A pair review, joint test session, or coordinated release may create more value than beginning three unrelated tasks.

The important distinction is between parallel work that genuinely accelerates one deliverable and multitasking that merely increases the number of open deliverables. Two developers can work together on a complex defect without counting it as two separate finished items. However, slicing one large ticket into hidden sub-tasks and treating each as a completed feature distorts measurement. Use task checklists beneath a stable work-item boundary when needed and record the completion status of the actual outcome.

Management should resist turning WIP limits into a moral judgment about an individual's busyness. A healthy pull system makes congestion a collective system problem and encourages temporary help where it has the highest impact. The flow conversation becomes “What must finish?” rather than “Why are you not starting something new?” Such a policy supports cross-training, explicit handoffs, and recognition of less visible review and maintenance work.

Queueing, Waiting, and Variability

A queue forms whenever requests for a service arrive before that service can attend to them. Even when long-run average demand is lower than average capacity, variability can generate substantial waits. Two ten-minute tasks might arrive simultaneously to a one-at-a-time reviewer; one must wait. If a large security review takes several hours while more requests arrive, the queue can grow despite a reviewer who is fully occupied. This is why WIP and timing distributions matter as much as nominal staffing.

Queueing delays generally become more sensitive to variability as a service approaches saturation. High utilization can be financially attractive in repetitive settings but fragile in services where a rare incident, a difficult integration, or a sudden batch of tasks can consume capacity unpredictably. WIP caps do not make work disappear. They prevent excessive commitment to it and encourage upstream demand shaping, smaller batches, faster feedback, better review coverage, and deliberate capacity reservation.

A useful distinction is waiting caused by lack of capacity versus necessary elapsed time, such as a regulatory review window or an external vendor's fixed processing delay. Both are included in customer-facing cycle or lead time if they fall within the boundary, but the remedies differ. A team cannot fix a third-party deadline by demanding more local coding speed. It may instead submit earlier, parallelize permitted checks, clarify required information, or build contractual buffers into service expectations.

Little's Law and the Flow Relationship

Little's Law relates average inventory, average throughput, and average time in a stable system with consistent boundaries: average WIP = average throughput × average cycle time. In symbols, L = λW, where L is average items in the system, λ is the average output rate in items per time, and W is average elapsed time in that system. One practical rearrangement is average cycle time = average WIP ÷ average throughput. All terms must describe the same workflow and compatible units.

Suppose an established delivery service carries an average of twelve committed items and completes four comparable items per week. The observed long-run relationship corresponds to an average system time of 12 ÷ 4 = 3 weeks. If a subsequent period truly supports an average WIP of eight while throughput remains four per week under the same boundary and stable conditions, the corresponding average system time is 8 ÷ 4 = 2 weeks. This is a conditional relationship, not a promise that an order to cut inventory by one-third will automatically reduce elapsed time by one-third.

Little's Law is not a deterministic per-item delivery formula and does not justify arbitrary day-by-day guarantees. Interpretation is especially unreliable when arrivals and departures differ materially, the inventory is growing, work is repeatedly abandoned, item definitions change, the measurement window is too short, or the flow contains inconsistent boundaries. Useful application is empirical: measure the relationship, make one informed policy change, and observe whether throughput, average WIP, and elapsed time move together as expected. Use percentiles and aging data when forecasting individual requests.

Throughput: What Actually Finishes

Throughput is the count of work items finished per unit of time. If a service completes nineteen accepted changes in a five-week period, its observed average throughput is 19 ÷ 5 = 3.8 changes per week. This metric must use the agreed finish definition; moving a task into “Ready for Test” does not count when release and verification are still outstanding. Nor should a single issue be counted repeatedly as it cycles through rework.

Throughput differs from story-point velocity, hours billed, commits made, messages sent, and individual activity counts. An organization can generate many commits while finishing few customer-meaningful changes. It can also finish fewer large, valuable outcomes than small routine items. Item count remains useful for flow, but reports should show the work-item mix, unusual batch sizes, rework, and business outcomes where those materially change interpretation. Combining dissimilar populations without labeling them can produce misleading forecasts.

When assessing a WIP experiment, look for sustainable throughput rather than merely a one-week spike. A team that closes many tiny tickets while postponing every difficult dependency might show increasing throughput and worsening stakeholder outcomes. Pair the count with aging of unfinished items, escaped defects, service-critical incidents, value evidence, and a view of the oldest obligations. A management dashboard should neither reward cosmetic item slicing nor punish teams for taking necessary high-risk work.

Cycle Time and Distribution Shape

Cycle time, as used in the May 2025 Kanban Guide, is the elapsed time from the DoW's started point to its finished point for a completed item. If an item starts on Monday morning and finishes the following Thursday afternoon, its time includes weekends or nonworking hours depending on the published elapsed-time convention. Avoid silently mixing working days with calendar days or changing the finish point after analyzing the results. A separate customer lead time can begin earlier, at the request or commitment point, if that is what stakeholders need to know.

A single average conceals variation. Imagine six finished items with elapsed times of 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 28 calendar days. The mean is 8 days, while the median is 4.5 days; neither alone exposes the long tail. A customer told simply “the team finishes in eight days” may reasonably infer a level of certainty unsupported by the data. Histograms, scatterplots, percentiles, and aging exceptions help distinguish consistent performance from occasional very long delays. The 28-day case deserves investigation even if its cause was legitimate.

Keep a visible record of completed items, their work types, dates, exceptions, and documented boundary changes. Do not remove inconvenient outliers merely to create a more attractive forecast. If unusual items form a different class of service—such as security investigations rather than ordinary fixes—separate their distributions on defensible, predeclared grounds. Trustworthy process improvement depends on preserving both the center and the tail of performance.

Work Item Age and the Risk of Invisible Stagnation

Work Item Age is elapsed time since an item crossed the started point when it has not yet finished. It is not the historical cycle time of a completed item. Age is operationally important because an item can become dangerously old long before its eventual completion affects the cycle-time distribution. A service reporting good completed-item averages may be quietly leaving its hardest or least convenient obligations unresolved.

Compare each in-progress item's age to a relevant service-level expectation (SLE). If historical evidence suggests that 85% of comparable items finish within ten calendar days, an unfinished item on day nine warrants attention, but not automatic blame. Ask what is blocking progress, whether the downstream slot is available, what remaining work or risk exists, and whether the original expectation still applies. An item that is already fifteen days old should be visibly escalated, not hidden by redefining its start date or moved to an uncounted backlog.

An aging review should prioritize the oldest, most at-risk, or most costly commitments rather than mechanically prefer the easiest ticket. This does not imply that every urgent incident must wait behind an old feature. Risk-aware policies can explicitly allow expedite work. The core discipline is to account for the effect of interruptions on all other unfinished work and preserve an honest explanation of each decision.

Service Level Expectations and Statistical Honesty

An SLE expresses a period and a probability: for example, “Based on the last six months of comparable completed changes, approximately 85% finished within ten elapsed days.” It is a probabilistic planning aid rather than a universal benchmark or a contractual service-level agreement. The May 2025 Kanban Guide requires an SLE in the DoW and permits an openly labeled best guess until adequate history is available. Once data exist, teams should update the expectation transparently.

An SLE should be grounded in the observed cycle-time distribution for the same start and finish definitions and a reasonably comparable work population. The sample size, observation period, outages, changed policies, and mix of demand affect how much confidence to place in it. A high percentile can be informative for risk-sensitive delivery, but the chosen percentile is a governance judgment, not a magical guarantee. A stakeholder needs both the range of outcomes and the conditions under which the estimate may fail.

For actual deadline commitments, add relevant dependencies and tail risk. If an external approval cannot be completed within three days, an SLE derived from tasks without that approval may not apply. Inform decision-makers when a date is speculative, when a fixed-date request must be prioritized, and when expedited treatment will delay other work. Good forecasting exposes uncertainty instead of turning statistical measures into promises about a particular ticket.

Flow Efficiency and the Anatomy of Delay

Flow efficiency is sometimes defined as active processing time divided by total elapsed time for a work item. A change requiring two days of actual implementation, review, and verification but spending ten elapsed days in the system has a simple flow-efficiency measure of 2 ÷ 10 = 20%. The remaining eight days are not necessarily wasted: some waiting is legitimate or unavoidable. The ratio is most useful as a diagnostic clue about where further investigation is warranted.

Distinguish touch time, queue time, blocked time, and necessary waiting. Logging these precisely may be burdensome; teams can sample cases or use board transitions to estimate where delay accumulates. Do not rely on automated timestamps without checking whether the columns represent genuine work states. A card held in “In Progress” may spend most of its time waiting for one person's review. A simplistic stage-duration report would incorrectly label the entire interval as active work.

A better diagnostic asks what changes could reduce avoidable waiting without weakening assurance. Earlier review, smaller changes, ready test environments, clear acceptance criteria, fewer approval handoffs, and streamlined permissions may help. Eliminating a required accessibility or security check simply to raise a flow-efficiency percentage would undermine the delivery definition. The objective is better useful delivery, not a cosmetically high ratio.

Cumulative Flow Diagrams and Congestion Patterns

A cumulative flow diagram (CFD) plots the cumulative number of items that have crossed successive stages over time. The vertical distance between relevant cumulative curves can visualize the inventory in a stage or across a defined portion of the service. When the band for review grows, items may be entering review faster than they exit. When a band remains wide and approximately flat, the queue may be persistent even if it is not growing. A finished-work line with a sustained upward slope indicates continuing departures.

Interpretation requires careful boundary definitions and an understanding of arrivals, cancellations, reopened items, and work-item changes. A growing band can reflect a temporary planned batch, an operational incident, or a real bottleneck; the chart alone does not identify the cause. Repeatedly counting moved-back items as new arrivals can corrupt the picture. If the team skips intermediate stages or bypasses the workflow, the apparent data may be cleaner than reality.

Combine CFDs with an aging work-item view and a throughput history. The CFD reveals accumulation patterns, aging points to specific obligations, and throughput shows actual completion capability. A team should investigate when one stage expands while downstream stages remain steady, or when starting work increases while the finish line flattens. These are signals for conversation and experiments, not automatic grounds for assigning fault to a particular worker.

Bottlenecks, Constraints, and Starved Stages

A bottleneck is a constraint that restricts end-to-end throughput under current operating conditions. It may be a specialist reviewer, an unstable integration environment, a slow external dependency, a release window, or unclear acceptance criteria that cause repeated rework. It is not necessarily the column with the largest card count; queue size and service time must be interpreted together. A downstream state can appear empty because an upstream decision process is starved or blocked, rather than because it has unlimited capacity.

When a constraint is identified, protect its useful capacity. Feed it work that is actually ready, reduce avoidable interruptions, prevent rework from upstream, cross-train where appropriate, and prioritize clearing its output. Once the constraint improves, the bottleneck may move. Raising a WIP limit upstream without changing the constraining service can enlarge the queue without increasing completion. Conversely, lowering WIP too far upstream may starve the constraint and reduce useful delivery.

Do not optimize only one team's queue at the expense of another. A development group that ships fifty unreviewable requests may report excellent local throughput while the organization's total flow deteriorates. Map the customer-meaningful finish point and investigate congestion across the complete service. Organizationally, a cross-team dependency that repeatedly delays every feature may be a better improvement target than another request for faster code writing.

Batch Size and Handoffs

Batch size is how much work is bundled before a transition, review, test, integration, or release occurs. Large batches can reduce fixed setup overhead but increase the time work waits for the batch to be ready, enlarge the consequences of defects, and complicate diagnosis. Small batches can enable earlier feedback and more frequent finishing, but excessively tiny units may create avoidable coordination and transaction costs. The appropriate batch size depends on technical architecture, risk, automation, and service economics.

In a software service, a large pull request often imposes a prolonged review, increasing the chance that the author starts another task while waiting. Smaller coherent changes can be easier to verify and integrate, reducing the age of each item. In a content publishing service, releasing one page at a time may be safe, while coordinated policy changes require a synchronized batch. The goal is independently useful, verifiable flow—not arbitrary fragmentation into tickets that create no deliverable on their own.

A practical experiment is to compare the elapsed time, rework rate, reviewer effort, and release risk of different change sizes. If small batches improve flow but create many partial deployments, the team may need feature flags, backward-compatible database migrations, or controlled release techniques. Batch-size decisions and WIP controls reinforce one another: smaller items often permit a given cap to produce more frequent reliable completion without abandoning required assurance.

Blocked Work and Exception Handling

A blocked item is work unable to progress because a required condition is absent. Examples include waiting on access, a third-party answer, a failed shared build, an approval, or an unresolved dependency. If the item is already started, it normally continues to consume WIP in the declared boundary. Removing it from the count rewards concealment and allows the service to begin replacements until the real inventory becomes unmanageable.

Record at least the blocker description, when the block began, the person or organization responsible for clearing it, the next action, and the date it will be reconsidered. Avoid treating every pause as equal. A five-minute test wait needs a different response from a six-week vendor approval. Where an item must be explicitly suspended and its service commitment renegotiated, define and publish that policy; do not use status changes solely to improve metrics.

Exception policies should be visible before crises. A declared expedite lane may reserve one active slot for an urgent production incident, or the team may permit a temporary overflow with a named decision-maker and recorded reason. The exception should reveal which other commitments are delayed. If expedited work repeatedly becomes routine, the service's demand mix or capacity assumptions are wrong. Retrospectively relabeling ordinary work as urgent weakens trust and defeats the constraint.

Classes of Service and Cost of Delay

Different work types may carry different economic consequences for delay. A security exposure under active exploitation, a scheduled legal filing, an important customer defect, and a speculative feature should not necessarily use the same sequencing rule. Classes of service provide explicit handling policies—for example, standard, fixed-date, expedite, or intangible/risk-reduction work. They do not remove the need for transparent trade-offs or create additional staff capacity.

Use a deliberate policy for selecting a ready item when a slot is free. Consider impact, urgency, dependency risk, stakeholder need, and any promised date. An expedite route should be narrow, documented, and reviewed. Protect some capacity for reliability maintenance if chronic postponement would create future incidents. Do not assume that urgency signals are accurate simply because they came from the most senior requester.

A useful decision record states: “We pulled the incident ahead of the planned feature because service was unavailable to paying users; the feature's delivery expectation increased by two days; the Product Owner and affected stakeholder were informed.” Such records keep governance aligned with actual flow. They also reveal whether repeated urgent work signals missing upstream quality, insufficient resilience, or an unrealistically full portfolio.

Dependencies and Multiteam WIP

Work often crosses organizational boundaries. A feature may require design approval, application code, infrastructure, security review, data migration, and customer communications. Each group may impose its own local WIP limit, but the feature's end-to-end cycle time includes waits between groups. A system with five independently optimized boards may still have an excessive collective inventory and unclear ownership of unfinished outcomes.

Introduce visible dependency links, explicit handoff acceptance criteria, and a shared view of the oldest end-to-end commitments. Where practical, organize work as small deliverable slices crossing relevant specialties rather than a series of enormous departmental batches. If a dependent team has no capacity, do not start a high-risk feature that cannot progress unless the economic reason is explicit. Distinguish work on critical-path dependencies from unrelated preparation that consumes scarce attention.

At scale, portfolio WIP may be more important than local task counts. An organization attempting fifteen simultaneous major programs can exhaust decision-making and integration capacity even when individual teams appear well managed. A portfolio policy might limit strategic initiatives in implementation, reserve funding for maintenance, and require a clear completion or suspension decision before another initiative begins. This is a management design choice, not a number imposed by the Kanban Guide.

WIP Limits Within Scrum

Scrum prescribes accountabilities, events, artifacts, and commitments, while Kanban's flow techniques can complement Scrum. The Kanban Guide for Scrum Teams explains using a Definition of Workflow, explicit WIP limitations, active item management, and inspection of flow within the existing Scrum framework. Adding WIP controls does not eliminate Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum, Sprint Review, Sprint Retrospective, Product Backlog, or the Definition of Done.

A Scrum Team can use a Sprint Backlog board with states such as ready, development, peer review, verification, and done. A systemwide cap or state-specific limits can prompt Developers to finish Sprint work before selecting more tasks. The Daily Scrum can inspect aging work and blockers in relation to the Sprint Goal, while Sprint Reviews and Retrospectives provide opportunities to adapt delivery and working policies. If the Sprint Goal is at risk, the team may shift collaboration to the most critical unfinished items rather than distribute attention evenly across all stories.

Do not confuse a Sprint scope forecast with permission to start every selected item immediately. Multiple Product Backlog items may be planned within a Sprint while only a smaller number are active at a time. Conversely, WIP limitations do not authorize cutting quality or allowing an unfinished Increment to masquerade as done. A flow-oriented Scrum Team values feedback, usable outcomes, and transparency above keeping every task in motion.

WIP Limits in Continuous Delivery

Continuous integration and delivery pipelines create their own queues: changes awaiting merge, automated checks, staging environments, release approval, deployment windows, or post-release verification. Treat these as parts of the delivery service where they affect the defined finish. A code change that passes local tests but cannot merge because the main branch is broken is still unfinished for most product-delivery purposes.

Useful controls include limiting outstanding pull requests, automating reliable checks, enforcing a manageable merge queue, and reducing the size of individual changes. A deployment pipeline with an explicit capacity limit prevents hundreds of purportedly releasable changes from collecting in an unexamined buffer. However, automatic build concurrency limits are not identical to limits on business work items: one feature can trigger many jobs, and one job can test changes related to several items. Keep machine-job counts and deliverable counts distinct.

Some organizations improve flow with trunk-based development, feature flags, compatible schema transitions, and independent deployability. These methods reduce the cost of small, frequent completions. When regulatory approval or a fixed maintenance window is required, model it as a real stage and plan accordingly. The target is continuous readiness for a safe release, not indiscriminate deployment speed.

Security, Accessibility, and Quality Controls

WIP reduction should never mean excluding security review, accessibility evaluation, privacy assessment, testing, or mandatory approvals merely because they increase elapsed time. These activities may prevent harm and costly rework. Instead, design the workflow so risk assessment occurs early enough to inform implementation and required evidence is produced as part of delivery rather than assembled at the last minute.

A vulnerability remediation service might reserve incident capacity and use shorter review paths for urgent patches while retaining peer review, appropriate automated checks, and rollback planning. An accessible web-service team may integrate semantic markup checks and assistive-technology testing into standard completion rules, preventing a growing queue of “accessible later” work. A regulated service may establish a specific approval state with explicit records and authorized decision-makers. The board should tell the truth about the obligations required for safe delivery.

Track failure demand and rework alongside speed. A lower cycle time achieved by shipping defects that generate urgent repairs is not a durable improvement. A healthy WIP policy preserves bandwidth for prevention, recovery, and inspection; it does not use a low work count as proof that the organization has adequately managed its risks. When capacity is constrained, make quality-preserving trade-offs visible to leadership rather than silently omitting controls.

Measurement Design and Data Integrity

Choose a single authoritative record for each item identifier, timestamps, state changes, blocker events, and finish evidence. If work is tracked across a source repository, issue system, and deployment platform, agree on how those records map to a deliverable. Use stable references rather than duplicated cards that inflate throughput or hide cross-team delay. Automate collection where it makes facts more reliable, but audit timestamps when tools change or teams bypass their normal workflow.

Document whether elapsed time uses calendar days, hours, business days, or another unit. Report the observation window, which items were included, and the major exclusions. Measure average WIP from repeated snapshots or inventory-time data rather than treating a single end-of-month count as the period average. If items were canceled, explain their disposition and remove them from success counts while preserving a visible history. If an item is reopened after being declared finished, record the rework or defect instead of silently changing the original history.

Metrics deserve safeguards against Goodhart's law: when a measurement becomes a high-stakes target, people may optimize the number rather than the purpose. A team punished for high WIP may hide blocked tasks. A team rewarded for throughput may split tickets artificially. Governance should reward honest disclosure, high-quality delivery, risk reduction, and improvement of stakeholder outcomes instead of gaming any single flow measure.

Worked Case Study I: An Overloaded Review Queue

Scenario (illustrative). A five-person application team can implement many routine changes but relies on one qualified reviewer for architecture and security implications. At the start of a week, ten changes are in development and seven more wait for review. The reviewer completes about four checks per week, while the team keeps beginning six additional changes weekly. The review queue grows because, even before accounting for rework, its arrivals exceed its departures by approximately two per week.

Diagnosis. Counting developers as fully utilized disguises the bottleneck. A new “review complete within forty-eight hours” target is not credible with unchanged arrivals, service capacity, and review requirements. The team defines a stable start and finish boundary and records each item as WIP through verification. It introduces a systemwide cap, makes review-stage capacity explicit, reduces change size, and shifts developers into joint review preparation where authorized. During the transition, it concentrates on finishing aged changes rather than authorizing every incoming feature.

Experiment. For four weeks, the team stops admitting routine items when the system cap is reached; makes review readiness criteria visible; and tracks review queue length, item age, throughput, escaped defects, and completed cycle-time distribution. If review output increases sustainably to six per week and arrivals are managed to match, the queue can stabilize rather than grow. That outcome is a hypothesis to verify, not a guaranteed effect of the cap. High-risk changes still receive appropriate independent evaluation; control is not a pretext to waive security authority.

Worked Case Study II: University Content Publication

Scenario (illustrative). An educational publication service has twenty drafted encyclopedia articles, eight in editorial review, and five awaiting web integration. The publishing group advertises that writers produced twenty articles, while readers receive only two or three completed pages weekly. The service's actual finished point is public availability with correct navigation, rendering, and editorial sign-off. Drafting a document therefore does not constitute publication throughput.

Diagnosis. The service separates upstream manuscript ideas from accepted production items and then defines WIP across writing, review, integration, and deployment. It discovers that several articles wait on missing cross-links and inconsistent HTML structure, not on slow writing. Merely doubling the writing rate would increase unfinished inventory. A first policy caps active production articles and requires each draft to include the canonical URL, intended folder path, navigation links, and content completeness before entering editorial review.

Experiment. Editors and developers temporarily collaborate to finish the five oldest near-ready articles, using short integration batches and reproducible publication checks. The team watches the number of pages that actually become available, the age of every accepted article, and correction rates after release. It does not count the same article twice when a minor revision occurs. The success condition is a shorter, more reliable path from accepted topic to usable encyclopedia page, not a permanently low visible number of tasks.

Worked Case Study III: Payment-Processing Incident Capacity

Scenario (illustrative). A service team maintains a payment application with a mixture of feature changes, routine defects, and occasional critical incidents. It carries eight routine changes in progress when a severe transaction outage occurs. A simplistic rule, “No new work while WIP is eight,” would prevent an urgent response; a simplistic exemption, “Everything urgent ignores the cap,” would make ordinary flow collapse. The team needs a risk-conscious, explicit exception policy.

Diagnosis. The service defines two relevant work categories and establishes clear severity and authorization criteria. An active incident can occupy reserved expedite capacity or trigger a documented temporary cap exception. Routine items remain counted and their stakeholders receive revised expectations. The organization applies incident containment, evidence preservation where required, independent checks appropriate to risk, and recovery validation before considering the incident completed. It distinguishes restoration of service from completion of subsequent root-cause remediation work.

Experiment. After recovery, the team reviews how many features aged because of the intervention, whether the expedite rule was correctly invoked, and what preventive work should enter the normal service. If incidents recur, it examines capacity reservation, fragile dependencies, change safeguards, and prioritization. The WIP policy's role is to make these trade-offs disciplined and visible; it cannot replace incident command, technical risk management, or required regulatory notification.

Worked Case Study IV: A Cross-Team API Program

Scenario (illustrative). A platform group commits to a large API modernization initiative needing backend implementation, frontend adaptation, security approval, and infrastructure changes. Each department has a local board and each launches several subprojects simultaneously. The backend reports almost all items done, but the final user capability cannot deploy because migration scripts and authorization integration remain unfinished. Local activity looks healthy while the end-to-end program is late.

Diagnosis. Leaders identify an integrated customer-visible work item and track it through all dependencies. They establish a portfolio-level WIP cap for concurrent capability slices, visible handoff rules, and a shared impediment review. Rather than starting five more API endpoints, the team concentrates on completing one end-to-end vertical slice, including compatible contracts, tests, deployment, documentation, and access checks. The complete slice produces evidence about true delivery capacity and exposes integration risk earlier.

Experiment. Over two release cycles, the program compares end-to-end elapsed time, blocked dependencies, rework, and real user availability before and after reducing parallel initiatives. It does not declare success merely because one department's queue shrank. If reduced concurrency leaves critical specialists idle for extended periods, the policy can be refined, but only with a transparent assessment of downstream capacity and the total number of committed initiatives.

Worked Calculation: WIP, Throughput, and Cycle Time

Consider an illustrative service that has maintained stable boundaries and sufficiently steady behavior over a long interval. Its weekly finished-item counts are 3, 5, 4, 4, 3, and 5. The total is 24 finished items over six weeks, so average throughput is 4 items/week. Suppose repeated inventory sampling also finds average WIP of 12 items during the same period. The Little's Law relationship yields an observed average system time of approximately 12 items ÷ 4 items/week = 3 weeks.

A manager proposes lowering average WIP to eight items. If the service preserves a throughput of four items per week and the required conditions remain suitable, the corresponding average time would be 2 weeks. But if an overly tight cap reduces throughput to two items per week, the same WIP of eight corresponds instead to 4 weeks. This contrast shows why inventory reduction must be evaluated against output capability and variation, not treated as an independent performance guarantee.

Condition Average WIP (items) Average throughput (items/week) Relationship: average time
Observed baseline 12 4 3 weeks
Conditional successful experiment 8 4 2 weeks
Conditional overly tight control 8 2 4 weeks

These are illustrative combinations satisfying the arithmetic, not a claim that a specific WIP decision will create the stated throughput. A sound post-change assessment measures the real service, considers any demand mix changes, and checks whether work-item age, quality, and long-tail delays improved.

Worked Calculation: A Service-Level Expectation

Suppose a service has twenty completed routine requests with cycle times (in elapsed days): 2, 2, 3, 3, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 5, 6, 6, 6, 7, 7, 8, 8, 9, 11, 15. Seventeen out of twenty finished within eight days, so the observed proportion at eight days is 85%. The team might communicate a provisional statement: “In this small sample, 85% of comparable requests finished within eight days.” This is a transparent description, but not a guarantee that 85% of all future requests will do so.

The sample is small and may not reflect seasonal demand, urgent interruptions, staffing changes, or rare long-tail events. If a request has an external security sign-off unlike the observed routine items, its forecast should not be borrowed blindly from the same distribution. More history may justify a different SLE or separate categories. The chosen percentile and elapsed-time convention must remain visible, especially when a stakeholder interprets the statement as a delivery commitment.

A current request already aged nine days has passed the observed eight-day reference; it should be reviewed for risk and an updated expectation. The team's response should be to understand the actual delay, communicate with the stakeholder, and remove an impediment where practical. Reclassifying it as a newly started item to preserve an 85% statistic would destroy the value of the SLE and the trustworthiness of flow reporting.

Worked Calculation: Queue Growth and Stabilization

A review service receives an average of six items each week but finishes only four. If conditions persist, its unfinished queue can increase by approximately two items per week. Over five weeks that is roughly ten additional waiting items, apart from initial inventory and any cancellations. A WIP cap does not erase this unmet demand: it moves the waiting boundary upstream, making a decision about acceptance and timing unavoidable.

The service has several options. It may improve the review process without weakening assurance, increase qualified review capacity, reduce work arriving through better upstream selection, or negotiate expectations with stakeholders. If it admits only four items per week while retaining the four-per-week completion rate, committed WIP may stabilize, but rejected or deferred demand must still be managed honestly. Such management may involve a separate options queue, transparent priorities, and explicit rejection or postponement decisions.

This illustrates a general rule: a limit on committed work is compatible with a large population of uncommitted options. Its value is preventing the organization from representing every request as actively underway. Managers must still answer whether available capacity can satisfy the institution's obligations and whether additional staffing, automation, scope reduction, or service redesign is economically justified.

A recurring failure is limit theater: the organization displays a cap but authorizes invisible work in chats, private documents, untracked branches, or emergency channels. This creates the appearance of disciplined control while increasing actual commitments. An honest WIP policy recognizes every legitimate category of active work and offers a documented means of making exceptions. If the control is repeatedly overrun, redesign the service rather than teach contributors to conceal exceptions.

Another failure is premature optimization of the cap. Teams sometimes argue about whether the correct number is seven or eight while their actual start and finish dates are unreliable, their work-item definitions are inconsistent, and a shared review queue remains unmeasured. Resolve the measurement and boundary defects first. The value of the limit is the behavior and learning it creates; the precise number is secondary to operational truth.

Implementation Roadmap: The First Thirty Days

Days 1–5: establish the service. Identify the requesting stakeholders, actual work types, meaningful started and finished points, completion evidence, and current intake channels. Capture every existing accepted item, including blocked tasks and hidden handoffs. Agree on an initial DoW and assign owners to clarify policy decisions. Publish the initial WIP count and distinguish it from the backlog of uncommitted options.

Days 6–10: make queues visible. Map real workflow states, shared resources, and repeated handoffs. Mark blocked items and record their ages. Define how items are selected and what “done” means. Capture baseline throughput and cycle times from available trustworthy history. If history is incomplete, say so; do not invent an SLE from idealized plans. The team should understand where congestion appears before changing policies.

Days 11–20: run one bounded experiment. Adopt a provisional WIP control, specify whether it is systemwide or stage-specific, and name permissible exceptions. Stop selecting routine work when the relevant capacity is full. Encourage collaboration on old or blocked items. Track daily WIP, completed items, age, blockers, and quality issues. Record major operational disruptions that could explain observed changes.

Days 21–30: assess and decide. Compare output, elapsed-time patterns, customer outcomes, overtime, and quality with baseline evidence. Determine whether the cap is too restrictive, too loose, or simply exposing a deeper constraint. Change only what the evidence justifies, communicate to stakeholders, and establish a review cadence appropriate to the service. Do not declare a universal success from a few days of activity; the first month begins disciplined observation rather than ending the improvement process.

A Manager's WIP and Flow Decision Framework

Use the following sequence in a delivery discussion. First, ask whether the work item sits inside the agreed service boundary. Second, ask whether capacity is genuinely available under the policy. Third, if the answer is no, identify which existing obligation can move toward completion or which explicit exception is economically and ethically justified. Fourth, inspect blockers and age before rewarding new starts. Fifth, reassess the decision using throughput, risk, and quality evidence rather than task count alone.

Observed condition Immediate diagnostic question Defensible next step
WIP at limit; new request arrives Is this an exception under published policy? Defer intake or invoke an accountable exception
WIP far below limit; low output Is a constraint starved or is demand absent? Inspect readiness, staffing, handoffs, and capacity
WIP increasing; throughput flat Where are items accumulating? Map queues and stop unnecessary starts
Throughput rises; age rises too Are old, difficult items being ignored? Inspect tail items and mix of work
Cycle time falls; defects rise Were completion or verification rules weakened? Restore assurance and investigate root cause
One state congested; others idle Is there a shared-resource bottleneck? Reallocate effort, cross-train, or change the workflow
Urgent incidents repeatedly overflow Is the demand model unrealistic? Review capacity reservation and prevention investments

The framework is deliberately conditional. “Reduce WIP” is often a useful experiment, but not the right first response in every circumstance. A service with insufficient demand may need better intake. A service with mandatory fixed-time review may need better coordination. A system with unreliable dates needs measurement repair. Operational judgment lies in choosing the response that changes real completion, not merely the visible shape of the board.

Governance, Human Factors, and Ethical Measurement

A service leader owns the health of the system but should not use process statistics to rank people simplistically. Individual task counts are distorted by complexity, collaboration, specialization, and risk. Publicly identifying a single person as “the bottleneck” can discourage transparent reporting and reduce willingness to help others. Instead, examine which knowledge, permissions, tools, or approval responsibilities are concentrated and make a measured plan to improve capacity without weakening accountability.

WIP limits affect stakeholder promises and budgets. An executive should understand that starting fewer initiatives may require saying no to attractive proposals and may reveal uncomfortable demand-capacity mismatches. Portfolio owners must communicate which commitments are funded, accepted, deferred, or rejected. When delivery risk affects customers, suppliers, employees, or the public, process improvement should consider transparency and fair treatment, not only cost savings. In regulated environments, separate process advice from legally required compliance decisions.

Governance should protect the integrity of historical data. A leader can ask for forecasts with uncertainty, explicit policy exceptions, and documented quality criteria. Retrospectives and service reviews should encourage correction of flawed assumptions. A culture that penalizes honest WIP counts will produce understated inventories and worse decisions. A culture that values responsible finishing, shared learning, and documented trade-offs can use the same metrics to improve service reliability.

Advanced Questions: Variability, Stability, and Tail Risk

A stable average does not imply predictable individual delivery. Two teams may both finish four items per week and have average WIP of eight, yet one may complete most items in a narrow two-week range while another finishes many quickly and leaves a few waiting for months. Both satisfy the same long-run averages under suitable conditions; their stakeholder risk differs markedly. Use percentiles, aging scatterplots, blocker analysis, and work-type segmentation to understand that difference.

For variable demand, arrival rate is as important as completion rate. A system with average capacity of five items per week may become congested if requests arrive in bursts or require highly variable work. An aggregate WIP control can help absorb variability only if downstream capacity and service design support a useful response. Extremely low limits can create starvation or idle handoffs, while extremely high limits allow queues to grow unnoticed. The relationship between WIP and throughput is empirical and can change as staffing, skills, tooling, or item mix changes.

Consider also dependency tail risk. A small percentage of work may depend on external parties, hard-to-reproduce defects, specialized review, or regulatory deadlines. A service can maintain an attractive median while these items become much older than ordinary requests. A good board surfaces them through aging and explicit classes of service rather than deleting them from the sample. Risk-sensitive forecasts should not be derived from a handpicked set of easy items.

Applied Exercises and Study Questions

Exercise 1 — classify the inventory. A delivery board shows three items in development, two in review, one blocked awaiting a vendor, and four unaccepted requests. The start point is acceptance into development and the finish point is deployment. Determine WIP; explain why the blocked item counts and the four requests do not. Indicative answer: WIP is six active or waiting accepted items; the four upstream options are outside the defined boundary.

Exercise 2 — evaluate the arithmetic. A stable service has average WIP of fifteen items and throughput of five items per week. What average elapsed time corresponds to these values under Little's Law? What must remain true before one may interpret that number as the service's average cycle time? Indicative answer: Three weeks; consistent boundaries, throughput and inventory measurement, and suitably stable system behavior. Do not infer an individual deadline.

Exercise 3 — detect policy evasion. A team caps development at four and review at two, but moves excess completed coding into an unlimited “Ready for Review” column. Explain what the chart may hide and design a safeguard. Indicative answer: Hidden queue growth and long cycle times; introduce an end-to-end cap and include the ready queue in WIP and aging visibility.

Exercise 4 — separate quality and speed. A team cuts peer review to improve throughput, then sees a rise in production defects. What measurements and decisions would demonstrate real improvement? Indicative answer: Restore required review, inspect rework and escaped defects alongside throughput and age, and reduce unnecessary review waiting through smaller changes or better preparation rather than waiving assurance.

Exercise 5 — assess an SLE. Of twenty completed tasks, seventeen finished within eight elapsed days. Can a manager promise the next item will finish within eight days? Indicative answer: No; the observed rate of 85% is a sample proportion, not a deterministic promise. Check comparability, uncertainty, and current blockers.

Discussion questions. (1) When might a lower WIP limit reduce throughput? (2) How can an expedite policy be fair to stakeholders whose work is interrupted? (3) Which stage of a service would you measure first and why? (4) Why is personal utilization an incomplete proxy for delivery performance? (5) How should a team react when its oldest item is also its most uncertain? (6) What would prove that a WIP experiment improved customer outcomes, rather than only the appearance of the board?

Conclusion: Finish Work to Improve Value Flow

Work-in-progress control is a discipline of making the cost of unfinished commitments visible. Its purpose is not to make boards look empty or force every person to be perpetually busy. It creates an opportunity to prioritize finishing, share scarce capacity, uncover hidden queues, shorten avoidable waits, and improve credible delivery expectations. The limit must sit inside a clearly defined workflow with honest boundaries, explicit pull and exception rules, and the quality controls necessary to deliver usable outcomes.

An effective team treats WIP, throughput, cycle time, and Work Item Age as complementary evidence. It uses queueing insights and Little's Law to frame hypotheses, not to announce guaranteed dates. It measures actual departures, investigates old and blocked items, and adjusts constraints in light of risk, customer value, and service capacity. The practical rule is simple but demanding: admit new commitments when capacity and priority justify them; otherwise help existing commitments reach a defensible finish.
