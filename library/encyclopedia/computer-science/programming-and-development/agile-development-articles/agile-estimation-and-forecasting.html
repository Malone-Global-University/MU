Definition, Purpose, and Decision Context

Agile estimation and forecasting are related but distinct ways of reasoning about uncertain future work. Estimation assesses the likely size, duration, difficulty, or resource demands of an activity before all details are known. Forecasting uses explicit assumptions and available evidence—such as throughput, cycle times, observed team capacity, and remaining scope—to describe plausible future results. Neither practice removes uncertainty from software delivery. Their value lies in allowing a product organization to make informed decisions about scope, timing, funding, risk, and trade-offs as new facts emerge.

A useful planning conversation begins with a decision rather than a number. A product leader may need to decide whether a release could plausibly be ready before an enrollment period; a team may need to determine how much work to select for its next Sprint; a governance group may need to decide whether to narrow the scope of an external integration. These are different questions and demand different measures. “How large is this item?” is not the same as “When will this set of twenty items finish?” or “How many verified changes might be completed within six weeks?” The method must match the decision.

Agile planning is often described as adaptive because a forecast is revised when learning changes the assumptions. A good forecast includes its measurement boundary, range, confidence language, scope, dependencies, and decision date. It is not an unqualified delivery promise. This article examines qualitative and quantitative approaches; explains story points, velocity, flow metrics, and probabilistic simulation; and provides worked calculations and organizational controls for using the resulting information without rewarding false precision.

Historical Background: From Predictive Plans to Empirical Feedback

Software has long used work breakdowns, expert judgment, effort budgets, and calendar schedules to coordinate projects. Early predictive planning attempted to represent most work in a comprehensive schedule before execution. That approach remains useful for tasks with stable inputs and clear dependencies, but it can become misleading when requirements are discovered through use, architecture evolves during implementation, or integration risks cannot be fully known in advance. A detailed chart is no more reliable than the assumptions behind its estimates.

Agile methods increased the cadence of feedback by delivering useful increments and inspecting the results. The Agile Manifesto emphasizes responding to change, customer collaboration, and frequent delivery, but it does not prohibit forecasts or require any particular estimation scale. Scrum formalizes empirical inspection and adaptation and permits various ways to forecast progress. Kanban makes flow metrics and service-level expectations explicit. Different approaches therefore share a goal—more responsible decisions under uncertainty—without requiring a common formula or software tool.

By the 2010s, story points and team velocity had become common, and interest in throughput-based forecasting, Monte Carlo simulation, and no-estimates approaches grew in response to poor uses of point systems. These are not simply competing ideologies. A team with poorly understood work may use brief relative sizing to expose uncertainty, then use historical completion data to forecast delivery. Another team with consistently small, comparable items may forecast directly from throughput and avoid numerical item sizing. The quality criterion is whether the method improves real planning decisions and can be checked against actual results.

The Three Different Questions: Size, Quantity, and Date

Three planning questions should remain separate. Sizing asks how demanding an item appears relative to other items or in an absolute unit such as staff-days. How-many forecasting asks how many completed items may be delivered by a stated date. When forecasting asks when a specified set of items may be completed. A forecast can also concern a single item's elapsed time or the probability of achieving a product outcome. One planning meeting may need more than one answer, but the answers must not be blended casually.

For example, suppose a university website team has twelve ready-to-implement accessibility changes. The team may place each change into Small, Medium, and Large categories to detect risk and decide which changes should be divided. To anticipate how many will be done by the end of the month, it might examine completed changes per week. To anticipate when all twelve will finish, it could simulate sequences of weekly completions. To understand the likely delivery time for one newly started change, it could use a cycle-time distribution. Each method relies on different evidence.

Uncertainty, Variability, and the Limits of Precision

An estimate has at least two kinds of uncertainty. Epistemic uncertainty comes from limited knowledge: unclear requirements, unknown integrations, immature technology, or incomplete stakeholder decisions. It can sometimes be reduced through research, a prototype, or a narrow experiment. Aleatory variability is variation that persists even when the process is well understood: interrupts, individual work-item differences, defects, uneven arrivals, and changes in the operating environment. Statistical distributions help describe the second kind but cannot automatically remedy the first.

A team that has never connected to a particular identity provider may not have useful historical integration data. Running ten thousand simulations on unrelated user-interface stories adds numerical sophistication, not relevant knowledge. First isolate and examine the high-risk integration. Conversely, a team with fifty comparable completed maintenance requests may need little discussion of individual effort; the observed distribution could provide a useful starting estimate of what similar work takes in practice.

Forecast uncertainty should be visible in both numbers and language. A statement such as “the work is expected to finish around December 12” conceals a range of possibilities. A clearer decision product might be “the current model, assuming the work mix and team remain similar, places 50% of completions by approximately December 12 and 85% by approximately December 19.” Such dates should be computed from an actual model; the hypothetical wording demonstrates communication, not a real organizational prediction. The accompanying assumptions must include how changes in scope or capacity will be handled.

Product Outcomes, Scope, and Delivery Forecasts

Completing a number of stories does not itself demonstrate increased customer value. Product outcomes include more successful enrollments, fewer payment errors, improved accessibility, lower operating cost, or faster processing. Output forecasts estimate how many deliverables can be produced; outcome forecasts estimate whether the intended change in user or organizational results will occur. The second requires additional hypotheses, evidence, and sometimes controlled experiments.

A product team may be confident it can complete ten onboarding improvements but less confident that these will improve successful enrollment by 15%. The first uncertainty concerns engineering delivery; the second concerns user behavior and impact. Combining both into a single “90% likely” claim without a coherent model makes the figure meaningless. Maintain distinct outcome hypotheses, engineering delivery scenarios, and measurement plans. An early release or experiment can improve the outcome model before a large investment is approved.

Scope is also a moving input, not always a fixed object. New legal obligations, defects, usability findings, and service incidents can add work while product discovery removes low-value items. Thus a release forecast should state whether the scope is frozen, allowed to grow, or governed by explicit trade-offs. For a fixed date, negotiate the minimum valuable and compliant scope; for a fixed scope, let the date remain uncertain; for a constrained budget, revisit both scope and schedule using transparent evidence.

Relative Sizing and Story Points

Story points are a relative sizing unit that some teams assign to backlog items. A point does not correspond to a universal number of hours. Teams may consider effort, complexity, uncertainty, and dependency exposure in different proportions. A story rated five points is judged more demanding than a three-point story according to that team's conventions, not scientifically 1.67 times as long. Scales such as 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, and 13 are common aids, not required Agile or Scrum standards.

A sound relative-sizing session compares a proposed item with a small set of reference items previously understood by the team. If one login change required a modest user-interface adjustment and a new one also involves identity-provider policy, the discussion can reveal a hidden security concern. The benefit of points lies mainly in the conversation and the ability to aggregate consistently over time; the assigned number is a lossy summary of a multidimensional problem. Numbers selected under pressure or without a shared understanding may convey more confidence than the evidence warrants.

Point systems are team-local. Ten points on Team A need not equal ten points on Team B. Even within a team, points may drift if work types, tools, or composition change. Comparing teams by velocity or ranking individual developers by points creates incentives to inflate estimates, split work artificially, or underinvest in invisible quality. The Scrum Guide does not require story points, Planning Poker, a Fibonacci sequence, or a prescribed estimation technique. It does state that the Developers who do the work are responsible for sizing Product Backlog items, while the Product Owner can help clarify trade-offs.

Planning Poker, Reference Stories, and Facilitated Judgment

Planning Poker uses independent initial judgments followed by discussion of differing assessments. Participants review a backlog item, reveal a size estimate, and examine large disagreements. The conversation might uncover a missing audit-log requirement, testing environment risk, or an assumption about which users can authorize a change. After clarifying the item, the group may estimate again or decide the item is too uncertain and must first be investigated.

Useful facilitation separates dissent from confrontation. Ask the highest and lowest estimators what assumptions they made, then test those assumptions against requirements and prior work. Record the significant facts uncovered. An authoritative person revealing an estimate first can anchor the conversation; independent estimates can reduce that effect. Consensus is not evidence that the estimate is accurate, and an excessively long argument over five versus eight points may cost more than it helps. Teams can use a timebox, a range, or an “unknown” category instead of forcing agreement.

Reference stories should be revisited after a substantive technology or workflow change. A small interface adjustment that once required a week of manual regression work might later take far less time with automated testing. The reference remains a useful illustration of scope, but historical performance needs reinterpretation. Facilitators should preserve uncertainty and encourage splitting large items into independently useful, verifiable increments rather than compressing technical work into an unrealistically low point estimate.

T-Shirt Sizing, Three-Point Estimates, and Rough Orders of Magnitude

Not every decision warrants a numeric points session. T-shirt sizing classifies work as Small, Medium, Large, or Extra Large, sometimes with “Needs Discovery.” It is particularly useful for early product options, where broad distinctions matter more than numerical precision. A product leader may be deciding which investments merit further exploration, not which day implementation will finish. T-shirt categories should have team-specific examples rather than universal conversion tables.

Three-point estimation frames an optimistic, most likely, and pessimistic assessment. These can be useful for revealing uncertainty but become misleading if converted mechanically into a guaranteed date. A common weighted expression is (optimistic + 4 × most likely + pessimistic) / 6. This is a conventional approximation sometimes associated with PERT-like planning, not an empirical proof about a particular task. It does not automatically create a valid probability distribution or account for correlations among tasks. The assumptions behind the three values need as much attention as the weighted arithmetic.

Uncertainty Spikes and Decomposing Work

When the dominant difficulty is missing information, the best estimate may be unknown. A time-limited discovery activity, often called a spike, can answer a narrow technical or product question: whether a legacy API supports required permissions, whether a new rendering approach meets performance needs, or whether a proposed data conversion preserves records. The objective is evidence and reduced decision risk, not a speculative promise to deliver a complete feature in the discovery timebox.

An effective spike has a question, a limited budget, a repeatable test, a documented finding, and a follow-up decision. It may conclude that the feature is feasible, requires architectural changes, or should be abandoned. After the spike, the team can revise its size estimate or split the work into smaller increments. Simply naming a large uncertain task a spike does not remove risk. The distinction is between producing knowledge and claiming product functionality has been completed.

Vertical slicing decomposes work into increments that cross the necessary system layers to deliver an observable capability. For a new identity workflow, one slice might permit a particular authorized user to sign in securely, with logging, tests, and accessible error handling. Dividing instead into “database week,” “API week,” and “frontend week” can postpone integration surprises until the end. Smaller complete slices often produce more useful completion data, improve forecasting, and create earlier evidence about user outcomes.

Team Velocity and Its Proper Interpretation

Velocity is the sum of points associated with completed, qualifying work within an iteration, according to a team's local point system. It can support short-term forecasting if work definitions and conditions remain sufficiently comparable. Suppose a team completed 18, 21, 16, 22, and 23 points across five Sprints. The arithmetic average is 20 points per Sprint. If 80 points of comparable scope remain, dividing 80 by 20 gives a rough central projection of four Sprints, not a four-Sprint commitment. Variation, interruptions, dependencies, and changing scope make the actual result uncertain.

The completed-work rule matters. If an item estimated at eight points is partly implemented but fails its Definition of Done, it is not an eight-point completed increment. Counting partial points as if delivered can cause optimistic forecasts and conceal quality debt. Some teams reestimate incomplete work, but a change in the accounting approach must be explained and tracked. Otherwise the resulting velocity series mixes inconsistent rules.

A team should monitor whether its velocity remains meaningful after significant changes in staffing, product domain, test automation, or item-splitting policy. Comparing the current velocity with the average of an earlier era may mislead more than a simple informed range. Rather than set velocity targets, ask whether the team consistently achieves useful Sprint Goals and whether its flow and product outcomes are improving. Velocity can inform planning without becoming a performance quota.

Velocity Versus Throughput

Throughput counts the number of work items that cross a defined finished point in a unit of time, regardless of their point values. A team that finishes eight comparable changes in one week has a throughput of eight items that week. The advantage is low measurement overhead: a clear finished criterion and a reliable completion timestamp often suffice. The limitation is that items can differ substantially. Ten tiny fixes are not always interchangeable with ten large platform migrations.

Both velocity and throughput depend on item boundaries. Teams can inadvertently or deliberately inflate either measure by subdividing work differently. For forecasting, an item should represent a meaningful, consistent unit of delivered work; changes to the counting rules must be disclosed. Some teams reduce variance by slicing work into relatively comparable, verifiable items. Others segment the data by service type or size category. Segmenting creates smaller samples, so finer classification does not necessarily yield better predictions.

Cycle Time, Lead Time, and Work Item Age

Cycle time measures the elapsed time between a workflow's defined start and finish points for a completed item. Work item age measures elapsed time since the defined start for an item that has not finished. Lead time is used differently across organizations: some define it from customer request to delivery and others use it synonymously with cycle time. A publication or governance report must define its boundary rather than assume everybody shares the same terminology.

Consider a change requested on Monday, accepted into implementation on Thursday, and released the following Wednesday. If cycle time starts on Thursday, the completed cycle time is approximately six calendar days; if the customer-facing lead-time boundary starts when the request arrived, lead time is approximately nine calendar days. Exact measures depend on timestamps, time zone, and whether weekends are counted. This example is illustrative. The system should consistently record dates and calculate elapsed periods rather than rely on memory.

Cycle-time history supports empirical service-level expectations for comparable work. Work item age, in contrast, helps a team spot an unfinished item whose current age is approaching or already exceeds an expected threshold. The observation that 85% of past items finished in ten days does not imply that an eight-day-old unresolved item necessarily has an 85% chance of finishing within two more days. Its conditional prospects depend on the process, blockers, work type, and age distribution. Avoid using unconditional percentiles as individual-item guarantees.

The Kanban Guide and Service-Level Expectations

The May 2025 Kanban Guide defines a service level expectation (SLE) as a forecast of how long it should take a work item to flow from the started point to the finished point. The expectation has two parts: an elapsed duration and an associated probability. For example, “85% of comparable work items finish within eight calendar days” means roughly 85 of 100 comparable historical completions met that duration; it is not a contractual guarantee for a new individual item. The guide recommends using observed cycle times to establish the SLE and visualizing it in the Definition of Workflow.

An SLE is not a service-level agreement. An agreement may impose an enforceable obligation, remedy, or financial consequence, while an SLE is an empirical planning and feedback expectation. An organization might choose to make an SLE part of a contract, but the contract's terms then govern the obligation. The team should maintain a precise distinction between its internal probabilistic forecast and any external guarantee.

For example, an observed sample of twenty completed items may contain seventeen items finished within eight days. This produces an empirical success proportion of 17 / 20 = 85% in that sample. Twenty observations do not establish that the future true probability is exactly 85%, especially if work types or process conditions change. Instead of presenting the proportion as scientific certainty, record the sample size, date window, exclusions, and relevant workflow conditions. Continue calibrating the SLE as the service evolves.

Forecasts as Probability Distributions

A single projected date compresses many possible futures into one number. A probability distribution represents a range of possible outcomes and the relative frequency or probability assigned to each. For a release date, the distribution can describe the chance of completing the defined scope by successive dates. For delivery quantity, it can describe how many items may finish by a deadline. The forecast only deserves probabilistic language to the extent its data and assumptions support it.

A median, or 50th-percentile outcome, is not the same as a high-confidence commitment. If the model's median completion date is week four, approximately half the simulated outcomes finish later. The 85th percentile is a later date by which around 85% of modeled outcomes finish, given the assumptions. Leaders should decide how much risk they can accept, not label an optimistic percentile as “the plan.” A high-stakes external deadline may warrant more conservative thresholds and additional contingency responses.

Distribution shape also matters. A few unusual delays can create a long right tail that is obscured by an average. A histogram, cumulative probability chart, or table of selected percentiles can make that asymmetry visible. The quality of the visualization does not compensate for poor input data; transparent uncertainty and back-testing remain essential.

Sampling Historical Throughput

A simple throughput forecast starts by collecting counts of completed work items over consistent time periods. Suppose a service completed 4, 6, 5, 3, 7, 5, 4, and 6 comparable items over eight consecutive weeks. These values form an empirical sample of weekly capacity. They should be drawn from the workflow and team conditions that will plausibly apply to the work being forecast. Importantly, weeks with zero completions must be represented; excluding them systematically overstates future throughput.

The sample average is 40 / 8 = 5 completed items per week. A crude forecast for 30 remaining comparable items is 30 / 5 = six weeks. This central calculation may be useful as a baseline, but it does not indicate the probability of delivery in six weeks. The range of observed counts, serial correlation, seasonal changes, unplanned work, scope churn, and item variation all matter. If releases occur in batches, weekly completion counts may display artificial spikes; daily or Sprint-level intervals may be more representative of the true process.

Before simulating, check whether the sample contains periods with unusual outage response, holidays, onboarding, or a major architectural migration. Do not automatically delete unfavorable weeks: those events may recur and are part of operating risk. Instead, determine which conditions are genuinely nonrepresentative, document exclusions, and consider separate scenarios. A forecast can be transparent about thin data and still be useful for making a reversible decision.

Stable Systems, Changing Conditions, and Data Segmentation

Historical performance is informative only to the extent the future delivery system resembles the one that produced the history. A team might be adding members, migrating to a new platform, splitting responsibilities, entering a regulatory review period, or moving from minor patches to complex integrations. Each change may alter throughput or cycle-time distributions. A model that precisely reproduces last quarter's maintenance work may be poorly suited to next quarter's security redesign.

Stationarity is a simplifying assumption that the relevant underlying process is sufficiently stable for historical data to inform the future. Agile delivery is rarely perfectly stationary, but forecasts can still be useful over intervals when inputs and output conditions are comparable. Investigate visible trends, sudden changes, blocked-work patterns, and correlations between consecutive weeks. A rolling window can make a model more responsive but leave fewer observations. A long history increases sample size but may mix different systems. Neither choice is universally superior.

Segment by work type when distinctions materially affect duration and there is enough data to support them. Customer-facing features, incidents, dependency upgrades, and access reviews may each have different completion patterns. Separate forecasting models can be appropriate for large recurring categories; small or highly irregular categories may require scenario-based expert judgment. Preserve the ability to explain each segment's data source and sample size. Excessively complicated segmentation can create an illusion of precision without improving decisions.

Monte Carlo Forecasting: Concept and Preconditions

Monte Carlo forecasting generates many possible delivery outcomes by repeatedly sampling from an explicitly specified model of future work. In a throughput-based model, the inputs commonly include a historical distribution of completed items per period, a starting count of remaining items, a time horizon, and the treatment of scope changes. Each simulated future draws plausible throughput values for successive periods until the defined condition is met. Repeating this process thousands of times yields a distribution of completion dates or quantities. The simulation does not discover future facts; it propagates assumptions and observed variation into decision-relevant possibilities.

The simplest version resamples the observed weekly throughput counts with replacement. If the historical sample is 3, 5, 4, 6, and 2 completed items, each future week can draw one of those five values. One simulated sequence might be 4, 3, 6, 5, 2, reaching twenty completed items at the end of week five. Another might be 2, 2, 3, 4, 5, 3, 4, reaching twenty-three completed items at week seven. Many simulated sequences form the estimated date distribution for that fixed twenty-item scope. The model should start from a defined item count and account for zero-output periods when they occur.

Sampling each week independently is a modeling assumption, not necessarily a property of the actual system. A long incident can affect several weeks; team vacations occur in blocks; a major dependency may constrain many items simultaneously. Resampling independent historical weeks can understate persistent disruptions and correlate poorly with seasonal changes. A sophisticated model may use block resampling, explicit capacity scenarios, or separate work classes—but more inputs also mean more assumptions. Document the chosen method and avoid implying that ten thousand simulation runs equal ten thousand independent observations of reality.

Monte Carlo: How Many Items by a Fixed Date?

A how-many forecast asks how many items will likely reach the defined finished point by a future date. For a six-week horizon, one simulation draws six weekly throughput values and sums them. Run that procedure many times, then sort the results. An 85%-confidence lower-bound result can be communicated as the number of items achieved or exceeded in approximately 85% of the modeled outcomes. It is distinct from the 85th percentile of the quantity distribution, which is a high rather than low delivery count. Mixing these conventions reverses the risk interpretation.

For an illustrative six-week scenario with week-by-week outcomes of 4, 5, 3, 6, 4, and 5, total completed output is 27 items. That single sample path does not mean 27 is the expected total, and it does not establish any percentile. A real forecast requires the full ensemble of modeled outcomes, chosen percentiles, and a statement of which work items count as completed. Also establish whether the starting WIP, incident queue, and current release work are included in the modeled inventory.

A useful stakeholder statement might be: “For the next six weeks, the model's 85%-confidence lower bound is 20 completed small enhancements, assuming similar demand mix, team capacity, and acceptance standards.” Only use the number 20 if actual computation supports it; here it is an example of how to structure a result. If a decision requires a minimum of 28 completed enhancements, compare the modeled probability of reaching 28 with the organization's tolerance for delay, then examine scope reduction and capacity changes. Do not select a percentile after seeing which one makes the desired plan appear feasible.

Monte Carlo: When Will a Defined Batch Finish?

A when forecast begins with a defined batch, such as twenty-four remaining backlog items, and accumulates simulated period throughput until at least twenty-four completions occur. The first period at which the threshold is reached is the simulated completion time. After many runs, a table of cumulative percentiles can show when 50%, 70%, 85%, and 95% of simulated paths have finished the batch. These are conditional probabilities from the model, not independent guarantees.

A basic method is: record the start date and calendar convention; select a representative historical throughput sample; set the remaining scope; for each simulation draw successive weekly counts until the cumulative number reaches the scope; store the number of weeks; repeat; and summarize the empirical completion-time distribution. Maintain a fixed random seed for reproducible analytical reviews, but do not present seed selection as a way to reduce real uncertainty. If a simulated path repeatedly draws zero output, the model may require a maximum horizon and a transparent label for unfinished paths.

If the batch contains items with widely different sizes and dependencies, simple counting may give a falsely narrow forecast. Split the batch into comparable classes, explicitly model large items, or report scenarios with wider intervals. Work that must be completed in a particular order is not generally equivalent to an arbitrary collection of independent items. A release that needs every critical-path item may be delayed even when the overall item count progresses well. A sound model reflects the decision's actual definition of “finished.”

Forecast Intervals, Percentiles, and Risk Language

Percentile-based results require careful interpretation. An 85th-percentile completion date from a correctly structured when-forecast model is a date at or before which approximately 85% of simulated completions occur. An 85th-percentile throughput quantity is a relatively high output count, which is achieved or exceeded in only about 15% of simulations when outcomes are continuously distributed. If the question is “How much can we safely promise by the deadline?”, report a conservative lower quantity bound rather than an upper tail result.

For discrete counts and dates, exact percentile language depends on the quantile rule and ties. A forecast can present a decision table instead of arguing about mathematical notation. For example: “Likelihood of finishing all required work by week five: 35%; by week six: 62%; by week seven: 82%; by week eight: 93%.” These figures are hypothetical and should not be reused as facts about an actual team. Their purpose is to show that confidence increases as the allowed deadline moves later. The required confidence threshold is a governance decision based on consequences, not a universal Agile standard.

Distinguish confidence in the modeled outcome from confidence in the model itself. An 85% simulated success rate is not necessarily an 85% real-world success rate when data are scarce, scope is unstable, or the system is about to change. Mark the forecast with a date, a model revision, a lookback period, and key assumptions. Use simple statements such as “moderate model confidence; limited history” when a calibrated numerical reliability claim is unavailable. Do not conceal major modeling doubts inside a decimal percentage.

Forecast Calibration and Back-Testing

A forecast is useful partly because it can be evaluated. Back-testing runs the forecasting method against historical points in time while using only data available at those points, then compares predicted outcomes with what subsequently happened. A team can ask whether 70%-confidence delivery dates were met roughly 70% of the time across an adequate set of comparable forecasts. Repeated misses in one direction may reveal optimistic assumptions, changing scope, omission of zero-output periods, or improper treatment of dependencies.

Avoid hindsight leakage. If a team tests a March forecast using data collected in April, the test exaggerates predictive skill. Also distinguish between a model that was poorly calibrated and one affected by an explicitly documented change of conditions. A new release freeze, a key staff departure, or newly discovered high-risk work can make previous inputs inappropriate. That does not make every forecast miss acceptable; it identifies which assumptions should be updated and whether communication was timely.

Calibration is harder with small samples. If only three historical forecasts exist, a single miss can swing the observed success rate substantially. Track coverage over time, record forecast updates and reasons, and compare simple baselines with more complex methods. If a straightforward median-throughput forecast performs as well as a complicated simulation, the simpler method may be preferable until there is evidence that added complexity improves decisions.

Capacity, Holidays, Interruptions, and Maintenance Work

A team has fewer available delivery hours than the total hours implied by head count. Time off, support duties, required training, operational incidents, mentoring, release verification, and cross-team collaboration consume capacity. Forecasts based on previous periods may already incorporate typical interruptions, in which case subtracting a large general “overhead factor” again would double-count them. Conversely, a known two-week training program or holiday closure should be represented if it differs from the historical conditions.

For short-term Sprint selection, the Developers can inspect known availability and the Sprint Goal alongside past outcomes. For longer-term forecasting, explicit scenarios may be more defensible than one precise capacity multiplier. A base scenario assumes typical operating conditions; a constrained scenario accounts for a known platform migration or major staff absence; a favorable scenario assumes an automation improvement has been demonstrated, not merely planned.

Work type matters. A team responsible for software delivery and production support might reserve capacity for incidents rather than pretending support will not occur. Incident demand can be simulated or bounded using observed frequency and duration, but correlated emergency events remain possible. Do not improve a forecast by excluding security remediation, accessibility work, maintenance, regression testing, or operational readiness when these obligations are essential to the Definition of Done.

Dependencies, Critical Paths, and Correlated Risk

A batch-counting model is most useful when delivery items can progress with limited sequencing constraints. Dependencies change that condition. A payment-service feature may require a vendor authorization decision; a student-record migration may require an approved schema and a coordinated cutover window. Finishing many unrelated items does not satisfy the release if the prerequisite integration remains blocked. This is why throughput forecasting should be complemented by dependency mapping and milestone review.

The critical path is the longest chain of dependent work that determines the earliest possible completion under a particular schedule model. In adaptive software development, the network itself can change when learning reveals new work or alternatives. For a high-consequence release, record prerequisite decisions, external dates, and the items that cannot proceed until they are resolved. Distinguish a dependency with a fixed external wait from an internal capacity shortage; the mitigation strategies differ.

Risks can be correlated. A failing shared test environment could halt ten nominally separate items. A release policy change could affect several teams at once. Sampling every item duration or weekly throughput independently would understate the possibility of simultaneous delay. Consider scenario shocks—for example, a full week of delayed integration—or model shared constraints explicitly. The goal is not to build an enormous mathematical system; it is to identify the risks that can invalidate a simple forecast and make them visible before a decision is locked.

Scope Changes, Replenishment, and Date-Driven Planning

Forecasts must define the scope boundary. In a fixed-scope when forecast, the item count remains constant except for verified changes to the defined list. In a replenished backlog, new items continuously enter the system. A model that forecasts clearing thirty items assumes a different question from one that predicts the completion of all work while new requests arrive every day. If replenishment persists at or above throughput, the total backlog may never reach zero, even if the team is functioning well.

When scope expands, the forecast should be updated with the additional items and their risk characteristics. When lower-value features are dropped, report that the scope changed rather than treating the earlier forecast as vindicated. Maintain a change log with the date, decision authority, reason, and effect on confidence. For an externally imposed deadline, use the probability of delivery under the current scope as evidence for negotiation. Possible trade-offs include reducing nonessential scope, staging rollout, removing an unreliable dependency, or changing the date. Required quality and legal obligations are not optional buffer variables.

Rolling-wave planning keeps near-term work detailed while leaving distant scope open to learning. This limits the waste of sizing every hypothetical feature years in advance. It also requires disciplined decision rights: who can add new work, who can reorder the backlog, which work is mandatory, and when the next forecast will be reviewed. The Product Owner's accountability for ordering the Product Backlog in Scrum should be preserved even when a steering group sets high-level funding constraints.

Estimates, Commitments, and Contractual Obligations

A numerical estimate is not a commitment merely because it appears in a spreadsheet. A forecast describes plausible results under stated assumptions; a commitment expresses an accountable undertaking; a deadline is a constraint set by customers, contracts, or governance; and a target is an objective that management wishes to achieve. Treating these terms as interchangeable causes conflict and can encourage teams to hide uncertainty. A legal or contractual service obligation remains binding according to its terms even if the engineering forecast predicts difficulty meeting it.

A responsible commitment process has decision authority, scope boundaries, measurable completion criteria, known dependencies, contingency options, and escalation triggers. For example, a governance board may approve a migration target subject to a security review and vendor certification. The team can maintain a forecast showing the likelihood of meeting the target, while management decides whether to fund a contingency and whether contractual changes are necessary. Forecast updates do not silently change the obligation; they inform the decision to renegotiate or mitigate it.

Use confidence levels proportional to the consequences of missing the date. A low-cost internal experiment can tolerate a high probability of revision. A public service transition serving vulnerable users may need significant buffers, verification capacity, fallback arrangements, and explicit stop conditions. In every case, the organization's ethical duty is to convey material uncertainty before others commit resources or rely on the date. A forecast should inform accountable leadership, not substitute for it.

Agile Estimation within Scrum

The November 2020 Scrum Guide defines an empirical framework rather than a prescribed estimation formula. The Product Backlog contains an ordered, emergent set of work. Refinement can add description, order, and size, with the attributes varying by domain. The Developers who do the work are responsible for sizing Product Backlog items. During Sprint Planning, Developers select items in discussion with the Product Owner, considering past performance, upcoming capacity, and the Definition of Done. The entire Scrum Team collaborates on the Sprint Goal.

Scrum permits techniques such as burn-down charts, burn-up charts, and cumulative flow charts for forecasting. These indicators do not replace inspection of usable Increments, Product Goal progress, or adaptation through Scrum events. The Sprint Backlog is a plan by and for Developers; it can change as new information emerges, provided the Sprint Goal remains protected. The Scrum Guide does not require story points, an average velocity threshold, or the selection of a fixed number of points in every Sprint.

The right question for Sprint Planning is not “How do we fill 100% of capacity?” but “Which coherent set of work gives us a credible path to a valuable Sprint Goal while maintaining the Definition of Done?” A team may select fewer items when an external dependency is uncertain or planned support duty is substantial. If the planned scope changes mid-Sprint, the Developers and Product Owner can negotiate that scope without endangering the Sprint Goal. Misusing velocity as an executive performance target contradicts the purpose of empirical planning.

Kanban Forecasting and Managing Flow

Kanban uses explicit workflow boundaries, policies, and empirical flow metrics. Under the May 2025 Kanban Guide, the four basic metrics are work in progress, throughput, work item age, and cycle time. The system's Definition of Workflow includes an SLE with elapsed time and probability. Flow data can support both single-item expectations and multi-item forecasts, provided the model respects the actual process and avoids inventing certainty.

Work-in-progress limits can affect forecasting by reducing uncontrolled starts and exposing blocked or aging items. A service that finishes six items one week and zero the next may have a less predictable future than one completing four to five each week, even if their long-run averages are similar. Changes in WIP, batch size, release cadence, review queues, and item composition can alter the distribution. A forecast should therefore be refreshed after a material workflow change and not assume an improved process has already demonstrated its benefits.

The companion articles Kanban Workflow Design and Work-in-Progress Limits and Flow explain the operational controls behind these measurements. Here the focus remains how the resulting evidence supports choices about dates, quantities, commitments, and risk. A Kanban board is not itself a forecasting engine; the meaningful data come from consistently defined and observed workflow behavior.

No-Estimates Approaches and Their Trade-Offs

The phrase #NoEstimates refers to a family of practices and debates, not one universally prescribed method. Some teams avoid assigning individual numerical sizes and instead make work items small enough to be counted, forecast with observed throughput, and refine only as needed. Others use short discovery conversations but decline to transform size guesses into elaborate task-hour budgets. These approaches can reduce meeting overhead and gaming, particularly where work is repetitive and delivery boundaries are clear.

Omitting item estimates does not eliminate uncertainty, the need for planning, or the obligation to explain decisions. A manager still needs to know which items are ready, which are unusually large, which depend on external approvals, and how likely a deadline is. If new work has a substantially different risk profile from past work, raw throughput cannot be assumed representative. A rough “small enough or split” policy may be an efficient way to distinguish tractable work from exceptions requiring deeper investigation.

A practical organization can choose between relative sizing and no-estimates forecasting on evidence. Compare preparation cost, forecast calibration, decision quality, and team behavior. For a complex, one-off security migration, explicit technical risk assessment may be essential even if the team does not use story points. For a steady stream of small web fixes, counting completions might outperform repeated point assignment. The method should serve the decision, not become a badge of methodological identity.

Worked Calculation I: Velocity-Based Release Projection

Assume a team completes 18, 21, 16, 22, and 23 story points over five consecutive Sprints. The mean observed velocity is (18 + 21 + 16 + 22 + 23) / 5 = 20 points per Sprint. With 80 estimated points remaining, the basic arithmetic projects 80 / 20 = 4 Sprints. A manager should not conclude that the team is guaranteed to finish in exactly four Sprints. The observed range is 16–23 points, and the next five Sprints could include interruptions, changes in scope, and unusually difficult work.

If the team uses the historical minimum of 16 points as a simple conservative planning rate, the calculation is 80 / 16 = 5 Sprints. But the minimum of only five observations is not a statistically validated confidence threshold. It is one sensitivity scenario. A plan should document whether the remaining eighty points are comparable, what happens when scope changes, and which work must be integrated and verified before the release counts as complete.

Scenario Assumed points per Sprint Arithmetic for 80 points What this means
Lower historical rate 16 5 Sprints Sensitivity case, not a measured percentile
Mean historical rate 20 4 Sprints Central rough calculation, not a promise
Upper historical rate 23 3.48 Sprints Cannot finish in part of a whole Sprint if release occurs only at Sprint boundaries; at least 4 Sprints under this rate

Do not use the upper case to impose a delivery target or the lower case to claim high confidence without a calibrated model. If stakeholders require a guaranteed deployment date, the organization must plan quality gates, integration dependencies, scope trade-offs, and contingency actions beyond point arithmetic.

Worked Calculation II: Throughput and a Fixed Batch

Suppose an educational platform team has eight observed weeks of comparable completions: 4, 6, 5, 3, 7, 5, 4, 6. Total throughput is forty items in eight weeks, so the average is five items per week. Thirty remaining small work items yield a rough central forecast of six weeks, using 30 / 5. This calculation is intentionally transparent and incomplete: it does not assign a probability to the date, model scope arrival, or account for any special integration work.

A scenario forecast can expose the difference between a central rate and a delivery boundary. At three items per week, thirty items take ten weeks; at five, six weeks; and at six, five weeks. These are hypothetical constant-rate scenarios, not percentiles from a probability distribution. In actual practice, each week can differ, and the chance of sustaining the lower or upper rate for several periods must be assessed rather than assumed.

Constant-rate scenario Items per week Weeks to reach at least 30 items Interpretation
Constrained 3 10 Illustrates slower capacity
Central 5 6 Matches the historical mean
Favorable 6 5 Depends on sustained higher throughput

For a probabilistic forecast, sample complete weekly outcomes from the historical data and accumulate them until the thirty-item threshold is crossed. Then report the resulting completion-time percentiles with the lookback window, calendar assumptions, and any special work classes. If the current backlog includes a large payment-service redesign unlike the observed small changes, do not force it into the same count model without an explicit adjustment.

Worked Calculation III: Cycle-Time Percentiles and an SLE

Assume a team recorded the following completed-item cycle times, in calendar days: 2, 3, 3, 4, 4, 5, 5, 6, 7, 9, 10, 13, 15, 19, 22, 30, 32, 39, 45, 61. The twenty observations are sorted for inspection. Exactly 17 of 20 completed within 32 days; therefore, in this sample 85% finished in 32 days or less. A provisional SLE might be expressed as “85% within 32 calendar days,” explicitly identifying it as an empirical result for this sample, not a guarantee for future work.

Only 10 of 20 completed in 9 days or less, so “50% within nine days” is another sample statement. A team that publicizes only the median may appear more predictable than it really is when the distribution has a long right tail. A new item that has already been active for thirty days needs attention; the SLE does not imply it is certain to finish within the next two days. The age of unfinished work is a separate signal for investigation.

The analysis should identify whether the data represent comparable work and whether unusually long items correspond to genuine exceptions, blocked reviews, or inconsistent measurement. Twenty observations support only a provisional empirical statement. The sample must be updated as more items finish, and deviations should trigger discussion of workflow behavior rather than retrospective rewriting of the definition of “done.”

Worked Calculation IV: A Reproducible Monte Carlo Model

A minimal throughput Monte Carlo model can be specified clearly enough for a spreadsheet or a script. Define the remaining batch as 24 comparable items and the historical weekly throughput observations as [3, 5, 4, 6, 5, 7, 4, 2]. For each of 10,000 simulation runs, start the cumulative completed count at zero; sample one of the eight weekly values uniformly with replacement; add it to the count; and repeat until the count reaches or exceeds 24. Record the number of sampled weeks. Sort the 10,000 completion times to summarize the distribution. Use a fixed random seed when reproducing an analysis.

The algorithm is intentionally simple: it assumes the eight weeks are representative, each has equal sampling weight, consecutive weeks can be treated independently, the twenty-four items are comparable, and no additional scope enters the batch. It also assumes that once twenty-four items have finished, all release requirements and dependencies have been satisfied. Those assumptions need review, especially for a cross-system launch with a critical-path security approval.

observed_throughput = [3, 5, 4, 6, 5, 7, 4, 2]
remaining_items = 24
completion_weeks = []
repeat 10,000 times:
    finished = 0
    weeks = 0
    while finished < remaining_items:
        finished += sample_with_replacement(observed_throughput)
        weeks += 1
    completion_weeks.append(weeks)
report percentiles(completion_weeks, 50, 70, 85, 95)

Executed illustration. With a Python pseudorandom generator seeded at 20261123, 10,000 runs of the specified simple resampling model yielded a median finish of six weeks and an 85th-percentile and 95th-percentile finish of seven weeks (nearest-rank rule). 82.92% of simulated runs finished by six weeks and 97.95% finished by seven weeks. These numerical results are model outputs for the eight illustrative inputs, not predictions for a real organization. Different seeds may yield slightly different results. A governed implementation should store the seed, publish inputs and completion criteria, and compare subsequent actual delivery with forecasts. Simulation precision must not be mistaken for evidence precision: a model based on eight historical periods still has only eight observed periods, even after 10,000 simulated paths.

Worked Case Study I: University Course-Platform Release

A university platform team must improve course enrollment before a registration window. The proposed scope includes account verification, accessible confirmation messages, roster synchronization, and staff exception handling. At first, management proposes a single date based on the number of features on a presentation slide. The team instead separates the work into a minimally usable release, optional enhancements, and mandatory integration and compliance requirements. It identifies a student-information-system dependency that must be verified before declaring the release ready.

The team records twenty completed changes from recent iterations but discovers that most were small interface improvements. The roster-integration component is larger and less familiar. Rather than treating all work as comparable, the team prototypes the roster exchange, tests error recovery, and records the external system's authorization window. It uses throughput history for the small backlog items and a scenario range for the high-risk integration. A release forecast then includes the dependency gate, end-to-end accessibility checks, and rollback preparation.

At each review, leaders compare the distribution of plausible delivery dates with the fixed registration date. If confidence falls below their agreed tolerance, they remove optional reporting filters or stage their release; they do not redefine “complete” to exclude mandatory verification. This example illustrates how a team can make a useful commitment through scope management and evidence while avoiding claims that any arbitrary set of twenty items represents the same amount of work as another.

Worked Case Study II: Payments Reliability and an External Deadline

A payment-processing team is asked to deliver a new authorization workflow before a contractual integration date. Engineers initially estimate the work as a handful of small changes. A discovery session reveals three correlated risks: the payment provider's sandbox has intermittent failures, a security review depends on a shared specialist, and transaction reconciliation must remain accurate across retries. The planning problem is not simply the number of tickets; it is the sequence of prerequisites and the consequence of failure.

The team separates independent implementation changes from provider certification and a mandatory reconciliation exercise. It uses historical cycle-time data for comparable code changes but creates distinct scenarios for the external dependency. In the favorable scenario, the provider's test environment is stable; in a constrained scenario, certification is delayed and a fallback integration remains necessary. Management receives a dated forecast with two credible milestones: an internal verified implementation and externally certified readiness. These dates are not interchangeable.

The governance decision is to establish an escalation point well before the contractual deadline and preserve fallback capacity. If the constrained scenario becomes likely, the responsible commercial representatives can consider a contractual amendment or a staged activation. Security checks and transaction integrity remain mandatory. The lesson is that credible forecasts expose the risks that matter, even when doing so creates an uncomfortable conversation about a preannounced delivery date.

Worked Case Study III: A Small-Team Maintenance Service

A three-person development team maintains several internal websites. Requests include broken links, small content changes, minor accessibility defects, and occasional release errors. Most items are independently verifiable and finish quickly. The team has tried estimating each request with story points, but the size meetings consume time without changing prioritization or the order in which tasks finish. A throughput forecast may be a better fit for this service.

The service defines its start point as acceptance into active maintenance and its finished point as the corrected page being publicly accessible after required checks. It records completion counts by week, including zero weeks, and keeps incidents separately tagged. Over time it creates an empirical service-level expectation for routine requests and uses work item age to identify items blocked by external approvals. The team can now answer “How many routine changes can we likely finish before the end of the month?” without estimating each one numerically.

However, a request to rebuild site authentication is not a routine maintenance item. It should not be forecast from the small-fix throughput sample. The team identifies it as a separate piece of project work requiring discovery, security review, and perhaps external implementation capacity. This case shows both the value of count-based forecasting and the importance of refusing an invalid comparison when the incoming work no longer resembles the historical sample.

Worked Case Study IV: Coordinating Several Product Teams

Three product teams contribute to a shared registration system. The first owns user experience, the second owns identity services, and the third owns academic records integration. Each has its own backlog and delivery history. A program manager considers adding all three teams' story points into one cumulative velocity target. That total would be misleading because the teams use different reference stories and different Definition-of-Done boundaries.

Instead, the organization identifies the shared release outcome and the specific integration dependencies among teams. Each team publishes its own forecast with assumptions and evidence. The program view combines required milestones, interface readiness, cross-team test capacity, and the critical path. A dependency that can block all three teams receives more governance attention than its local story-point size might suggest. Common software-delivery criteria and cross-system test evidence make the actual state of readiness inspectable.

Leadership maintains a program-level risk register without turning team estimates into individual performance rankings. The integrated release forecast is updated when a dependency changes or when the organization modifies scope. Rather than promise that parallel teams automatically triple delivery capacity, this arrangement identifies which activities truly can proceed concurrently, which need sequencing, and which cross-team risks need explicit contingency plans.

Common Failure Modes and Practical Corrections

Estimation and forecasting fail most often because of incentives, inconsistent definitions, and unjustified assumptions rather than sophisticated mathematical errors. Teams can be rewarded for appearing busy, for increasing points completed, or for conforming to an announced deadline instead of delivering useful and verified outcomes. Under those conditions, dashboards can improve while delivery reliability deteriorates. A functioning measurement system needs transparent rules and a culture in which revising a forecast is acceptable when new evidence appears.

Failure mode Why it distorts a forecast Practical correction
Story points treated as staff-hours Removes meaning of relative sizing Keep team-local definitions and avoid automatic conversions
Partial stories counted as complete Inflates apparent delivery Count only work meeting the finished or Done criterion
Different teams ranked by velocity Encourages gaming and invalid comparisons Evaluate product outcomes, quality, and team-specific trends
Only productive weeks sampled Creates optimistic throughput distribution Include zero-output weeks and explain exclusions
Backlog grows without model adjustment Fixed-scope forecast no longer answers the real question Log changes and update remaining scope
Very large items mixed with tiny fixes Item-count model assumes false comparability Slice, classify, or use explicit scenarios
Shared dependencies sampled independently Understates correlated delays Model dependency gates or scenario shocks
Percentile claims exceed evidence Produces spurious confidence Label small samples and back-test calibration
Deadline substituted for the model result Conceals delivery risk Present the likelihood of meeting the deadline
Quality work quietly omitted Misrepresents what finished means Include testing, security, accessibility, and operational readiness

A useful corrective question is: What actual decision would change if this number moved? If the answer is nothing, the metric may be ceremonial rather than operational. The aim is to make relevant uncertainty discussable, not to create a permanently green executive report.

Designing a Forecast Governance Record

A forecast should be portable: another reviewer ought to understand what question it answers and how it was produced. An operating record can include the forecast ID, product or service, owner, decision supported, as-of date, time zone, remaining scope, start and finish boundaries, source data period, sample size, work-class treatment, model method, planned confidence thresholds, dependencies, exclusions, and review cadence. For a simulated forecast, also store the model version and reproducibility settings. These fields are useful even if the forecast is maintained in a simple spreadsheet.

Separate the technical model owner from the person who accepts business risk. Developers and delivery specialists are accountable for understanding system behavior and identifying capacity constraints; product leadership determines value, scope trade-offs, and sequencing; accountable organizational authorities approve external commitments and financial exposure. In Scrum, these governance arrangements must not displace the Product Owner's accountability for Product Backlog management or Developers' accountability for sizing and Sprint planning.

The record should preserve the original forecast and its revisions. A replaced slide that erases earlier risk predictions prevents learning about calibration. When forecasts materially change, report both the new range and its cause. For example: “The model's 85th-percentile completion date moved by two weeks because twelve additional items entered scope and the integration environment was unavailable.” This is more useful than silently publishing a new target and treating the earlier one as if it never existed.

A Practical Thirty-Day Adoption Sequence

During the first week, select a real planning decision, agree on the measurement boundary, and confirm what qualifies as finished. Gather recent completion timestamps, record zero-output periods, and identify work types that cannot reasonably be compared. Ask whether the team actually needs item estimates or whether throughput and age provide sufficient information. Resist importing a complicated dashboard before agreeing on the meaning of an item.

In the second week, inspect the data: chart throughput over time, cycle-time distribution, WIP, and aging items; examine unusual periods and changing workflow conditions. Produce a simple baseline forecast first, with a clearly labeled range and assumptions. If there is enough representative data and the decision justifies additional analysis, create a reproducible simulation. Explain the difference between a modeled probability and a contractual obligation in the next stakeholder conversation.

During the third week, test the forecast on a near-term question and record what actions it changes: removing a low-value feature, reducing WIP, resolving a dependency, or reserving capacity for verification. In the fourth week, compare early results with expectations, identify obvious model failures, revise the data collection policy if needed, and establish a lightweight review rhythm. The thirty-day sequence is an illustrative adoption plan; teams with little history or large one-off initiatives may need more discovery before quantitative confidence is warranted.

Forecasting Decision Framework for Leaders

A forecast should be chosen by the nature of the demand, the available evidence, and the cost of being wrong. Begin with the decision: an internal iteration selection, a public launch date, a budget proposal, or a risk-trigger threshold. Then determine whether the scope is fixed, replenishing, or intentionally adaptive. Verify whether the work is comparable to the historical records. Only then choose a method, decide which percentiles or scenarios matter, and define who can accept the remaining risk.

For a low-risk, short-horizon planning question, a simple throughput range may be adequate. For a fixed date with considerable scope uncertainty, provide a series of scope scenarios and clearly state the mandatory minimum. For an individual work item, cycle-time percentiles and work-item aging may be more useful than team velocity. For a multi-team launch, the critical path and integrated quality evidence may dominate the probability of finishing a given number of items.

Situation First-choice approach Escalation trigger
Routine comparable requests Throughput and cycle-time history Sustained change in work mix or throughput
Sprint selection with stable point conventions Developer judgment plus past outcomes Key capacity/dependency change
Fixed set of small comparable items Throughput-based when forecast Scope changes or persistent blockers
Target date with flexible nonessential scope How-many forecast and minimum viable scope Modeled likelihood falls below tolerance
Novel technical integration Discovery, dependency scenarios, risk register Unknown becomes a critical-path blocker
High-consequence cross-team launch Integrated dependency and readiness plan Security, quality, or external certification gate at risk

The most credible approach is not necessarily the most numerically complicated. It is the one whose assumptions can be inspected, whose decision consequences are clear, and whose prediction can be checked after delivery.

Measurement Ethics and the Risks of Incentive Gaming

Measurement changes behavior. A management team that rewards high estimates completed may unintentionally encourage the inflation of story points. A service desk rewarded solely for item throughput may split requests into trivial units or close unresolved tickets. Teams penalized for reporting forecast deterioration may conceal dependencies until a missed release becomes unavoidable. A credible forecasting system therefore requires organizational protection for accurate reporting and transparency about model limits.

Do not use story points, velocity, or throughput as direct measures of individual productivity. These metrics are shaped by the work system, collaboration, quality controls, interruption load, and how items are defined. Ethical use includes balanced attention to customer outcomes, defects, security, accessibility, worker sustainability, and the consequences of delayed delivery. Forecasts that make the organization faster at producing unusable features have failed the larger objective.

When forecasts affect customers, employees, or regulated processes, decision-makers should disclose material limitations and preserve accountable review. A confidence percentage should never become a device for obscuring an institution's responsibilities. Record what the team knows, what remains uncertain, and what can be done if an adverse scenario materializes. That practice strengthens trust more effectively than presenting a precise but ungrounded date.

Advanced Issues: Model Risk and Forecast Horizons

Longer forecasts generally require more assumptions about future demand, team composition, architecture, and operating conditions. A six-week model based on a stable recent workflow may be useful; extending the same rate mechanically across three years is rarely defensible. Forecast horizons should match the stability of the system and the reversibility of the decision. Long-horizon portfolio plans are often better expressed as conditional investment stages and decision checkpoints rather than fixed lists of exact delivery dates.

Model risk includes bad input definitions, small samples, inappropriate independence assumptions, unmodeled dependencies, and selection bias. Even a statistically calibrated model can become obsolete after a technological or organizational change. Track drift in throughput and cycle time, compare forecast errors by work class, and schedule reviews after major process changes. Do not compensate for bad inputs merely by increasing the number of simulated runs or using more decimal places.

In some environments, a distribution of delivery dates is not the most important output. A decision may depend on the probability that a required compliance test passes, that an external vendor responds by a deadline, or that a deployment can be reversed safely. These questions require domain-specific evidence beyond cycle-time records. Agile forecasting should sit alongside technical risk management, operational resilience planning, user research, and sound governance rather than replace them.

Applied Exercises and Study Questions

  1. A team completes 12, 18, 15, 11, and 19 story points in five Sprints. Calculate its mean velocity. Explain why the mean does not establish a guaranteed completion rate.
  2. Define two different workflow start points for a website change: customer request received and implementation begun. Explain how the choice affects lead time, cycle time, and perceived responsiveness.
  3. A service completes 2, 0, 5, 4, 0, and 7 items across six weeks. Calculate its observed average throughput. Explain what happens to the answer if zero-output weeks are improperly removed.
  4. Explain why the 85th percentile of simulated completion dates can represent a relatively conservative date while the 85th percentile of completed item quantities is not a conservative quantity promise.
  5. Design a brief Monte Carlo pseudocode procedure for thirty remaining items using a fixed historical throughput sample. List at least four assumptions that must be disclosed.
  6. A release is due in six weeks, and the model assigns only a 45% chance of completing all current scope. Identify at least three responsible management choices that do not reduce required quality or security controls.
  7. A team has many completed small accessibility fixes but no previous experience with a new single-sign-on provider. Explain why a raw throughput forecast is insufficient for the proposed identity migration.
  8. Draft a forecast governance record for a product launch, including measurement boundary, data window, owner, confidence levels, and review triggers.
  9. Explain the difference among an estimate, a forecast, a target, and a contractual deadline. Give an example in which all four exist at once.
  10. Compare relative sizing, throughput forecasting, and discovery-first scenario planning. Which would you use for routine text corrections, a stable Scrum feature backlog, and a novel external payment integration?

Suggested study path. Begin with the companion articles on Product Backlog Management, Sprint Planning and Goal Management, and Work-in-Progress Limits and Flow. Then apply a forecast to a real backlog with representative observations, explicitly checking the difference between modeled confidence and actual decision risk.

Conclusion: Forecast for Decisions, Not for Appearances

Agile estimation and forecasting exist to support better commitments and trade-offs under uncertainty. Story points and qualitative sizing can stimulate valuable questions about work that is not yet understood. Velocity can help a stable team plan within its own conventions. Throughput and cycle-time observations can inform service expectations and probabilistic completion forecasts. Monte Carlo simulation can express plausible distributions, but it does not turn a thin dataset into certainty or make an invalid set of assumptions true.

The central discipline is to define what “finished” means, choose evidence representative of the actual decision, state uncertainty in useful language, and revise the plan as new facts emerge. A sound forecasting culture rewards reliable learning rather than the appearance of exact prediction. Leaders should use its outputs to manage scope, dependencies, quality, financial exposure, and customer obligations—while preserving the agency of teams who perform the work and the integrity of the product outcomes they deliver.

Primary Sources and Further Reading

Standards and evidence base. This reference distinguishes Scrum's explicit framework requirements from optional forecasting techniques. The November 2020 Scrum Guide remains the official current Scrum Guide as of November 2026 and assigns Product Backlog item sizing to Developers. The May 2025 Kanban Guide defines four core flow metrics and a probabilistic service level expectation. Agile Alliance and ProKanban material offers practical explanations of relative sizing, velocity, and empirical forecasting; such material is guidance and analysis rather than a mandatory universal standard. Sources checked November 2026. All case studies, sample throughputs, and point values are illustrative unless explicitly described as an executed model demonstration.

  1. Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland. The Scrum Guide (November 2020). Empiricism, Product Backlog refinement, Developer sizing responsibility, Sprint Planning, and adaptive forecasts.
  2. Kanban Guides. The Kanban Guide (May 2025). Explicit workflow boundaries, work in progress, throughput, work-item age, cycle time, and SLEs.
  3. Agile Alliance. Story Points. Relative sizing and the danger of confusing team-local sizes with fixed duration commitments.
  4. Agile Alliance. Velocity. Completed point totals and limitations of using historical team velocity for future plans.
  5. Agile Alliance. Estimation and Forecasting. Relative sizing, historical throughput, distributions, and Monte Carlo applications.
  6. ProKanban. When Can I Start Telling Customers When Work Will Be Done? Empirical throughput, model stability, forecasting ranges, and calibration.
  7. UK Government Digital Service. Planning in Agile. Adaptive planning and the connection between product vision and iterative delivery.
  8. NIST. Secure Software Development Framework, SP 800-218. Security work should be incorporated into delivery processes, not excluded to produce attractive timelines.

Limitations and application. Story points are not standardized time or productivity units, and percentiles from a simulation describe outcomes conditional on its assumptions. A forecast should be revised after material scope, data, or system changes. SLEs are empirical expectations and do not by themselves form legally enforceable agreements. Contractual and regulatory requirements must be evaluated within their governing context. Illustrations herein are instructional and not individualized financial, legal, or operational assurances.
