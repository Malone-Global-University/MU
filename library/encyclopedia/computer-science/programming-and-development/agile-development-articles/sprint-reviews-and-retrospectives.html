Definition, Scope, and Governing Framework

Sprint Review and Sprint Retrospective are distinct but complementary Scrum events that occur near the end of a Sprint. The Review examines the outcome of the Sprint with key stakeholders and supports decisions about the product's direction. The Retrospective examines how the Scrum Team worked and develops ways to improve quality and effectiveness. Both embody empirical product development: make relevant evidence visible, examine it honestly, and adjust future behavior. They differ in the object of inspection, the people involved, and the adaptations they seek. Neither is a ceremonial report intended merely to prove that everyone was busy.

This article uses the November 2020 Scrum Guide by Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland, the current official version identified by Scrum Guides as of November 23, 2026. Its framework descriptions are distinguished throughout from optional techniques. Facilitation exercises, feedback templates, voting methods, research instruments, health surveys, action trackers, and sample agendas can be useful; Scrum does not prescribe those mechanisms. The Guide does require the events themselves as elements of Scrum. Changing their fundamental purposes or routinely omitting them reduces the framework's ability to expose and address problems.

The subject belongs within the Agile Development unit of the MGU Computer Science Encyclopedia. It presumes familiarity with the Agile Manifesto and Development Principles, Scrum Framework and Accountabilities, and Sprint Planning and Goal Management. The companion Product Backlog Management article develops the decisions that frequently follow a Review.

Why Two Different Inspection Events Exist

The central distinction is product adaptation versus team improvement. A team might deliver a technically sound enrollment feature but learn from users that the feature solves the wrong bottleneck. A Sprint Review can redirect the product backlog toward a faster identity-verification experience. Separately, the team might discover that the feature required unnecessary manual test coordination and ambiguous deployment approvals. A Retrospective can change how the team organizes test environments, defines readiness, or works with support specialists. The two observations have different owners and different corrective actions. Combining them into a generic meeting risks overlooking one dimension or inviting stakeholders into discussions that require a different level of candor.

Each event also prevents a common organizational failure. Without substantive Reviews, teams can become efficient at implementing assumptions no one checks with actual users. Without Retrospectives, teams can repeat avoidable quality failures while treating velocity or ticket closure as proof that their process works. Both failures may coexist: a fast team can build the wrong product, and a strategically well-directed team can accumulate unmanageable defects and burnout.

The events are not the only occasions for feedback. Developers, the Product Owner, and stakeholders should share information as needed throughout the Sprint. The Review and Retrospective establish a regular minimum cadence for two complementary kinds of adaptation. If a safety-critical fault emerges on Tuesday, the team should not wait until Friday's meeting to address it. Regular meetings are safeguards against neglect, not excuses for postponing timely decisions.

Empiricism: Transparency, Inspection, and Adaptation

Scrum is founded on empiricism and lean thinking. Experience and observed results provide a better basis for decisions than confidence unsupported by evidence. Three connected pillars make this approach operational. Transparency means the state of work and value is visible enough for meaningful decisions. Inspection evaluates what that evidence implies. Adaptation changes the plan, product, or practices when inspection reveals a need. An event that reports ten completed tickets but conceals that none are usable is not transparent; a meeting that identifies a recurring defect but changes nothing completes only the first two steps.

A Review may inspect a working Increment, customer behavior, changed market or regulatory conditions, operational performance, and progress toward the Product Goal. Its adaptations concern future product opportunities, priority, experiments, or course corrections. A Retrospective may inspect cross-functional handoffs, lead time, testing defects, interactions, tools, the Definition of Done, interruptions, and working conditions. Its adaptations concern how the team will work more effectively. Neither event requires a particular dashboard, and neither should reduce the complexity of product decisions to a single metric.

Empiricism also requires treating causal explanations as hypotheses. A slow release may correlate with an overloaded test environment, unclear acceptance criteria, architectural coupling, or external approvals. A team that decides why before examining the evidence can solve the wrong problem. Instead, ask what happened, what was expected, what alternative explanations remain, what small change could test the hypothesis, and how its effects will be assessed. This logic carries directly from retrospective discussion into the next Sprint's actual work.

Event Sequence and Official Timeboxes

A Sprint is a fixed-length Scrum event of one month or less. Sprint Planning opens it, the Daily Scrum supports daily inspection by Developers, the Sprint Review is the second-to-last event, and the Sprint Retrospective concludes the Sprint. A new Sprint begins immediately after the prior Sprint concludes. The Review and Retrospective therefore occur inside the Sprint, not in an unplanned gap between Sprints. Teams may continuously release completed work, and release timing is not defined by either event.

The Sprint Review has a maximum four-hour timebox for a one-month Sprint; the Sprint Retrospective has a maximum three-hour timebox for a one-month Sprint. The Scrum Guide says both are usually shorter for shorter Sprints. For a two-week Sprint, a 60–90-minute Review and a 60–75-minute Retrospective are illustrative scheduling choices, not prescribed limits. The timebox is an upper bound, not a mandated duration or a target to fill. A team that can inspect evidence and decide responsibly in less time need not manufacture discussion.

Keeping the events distinct helps avoid a misplaced authority structure. Stakeholders are essential to the Review's product conversation. The Retrospective is an event for the Scrum Team to inspect its own effectiveness. The Scrum Master supports constructive participation and the Scrum framework; the Product Owner and Developers are members of the team, not outsiders who must wait for a status report. The order matters because product feedback from the Review may inform which working arrangements need improvement and what constraints the team faces next.

Sprint Review Sprint outcome, product progress, changing context Scrum Team and key stakeholders Product opportunities and Product Backlog direction 4 hours
Sprint Retrospective Team interactions, processes, tools, quality and Definition of Done Scrum Team Changes to team quality and effectiveness 3 hours

Scrum Values and Respectful Participation

The Scrum values—commitment, focus, openness, respect, and courage—affect whether these events produce accurate knowledge. Openness enables a Developer to say a demonstrated workflow is fragile rather than presenting it as finished. Respect enables a stakeholder to question an assumption without humiliating the team. Courage allows the Product Owner to admit that a favored product hypothesis is not supported by observed behavior. Commitment directs the group toward a valuable product and tangible improvement rather than personal credit.

A Review becomes distorted when seniority determines whose feedback counts, when people hide unfinished work, or when attendees treat the session as acceptance theater. A Retrospective becomes distorted when remarks are later used in employee performance disputes, managers press individuals for admissions, or participation is reduced to a happiness score. The Scrum Master can help remove these impediments, but organizational leaders must reinforce a climate where useful dissent is possible. A team cannot be instructed to become candid while being punished for giving inconvenient evidence.

Respect does not require avoiding hard questions. Product quality, missed commitments, privacy defects, repeated production incidents, and chronic overwork may need explicit discussion. The difference between responsible inspection and blame is whether participants examine conditions and decisions in order to change them, rather than seek a culprit. Neither psychological safety nor collaboration means ignoring accountability for professional standards. It means separating learning from public humiliation and identifying who can take a justified next action.

Sprint Review: Purpose and Decision Boundary

The purpose of the Sprint Review is to inspect the outcome of the Sprint and determine future adaptations. The Scrum Team presents the results of its work to key stakeholders; together they discuss progress toward the Product Goal. They examine what was accomplished, what changed in the surrounding environment, and what to do next. The Product Backlog may change as a result. This is a working session, not merely a sequence of slides or a scripted product demonstration.

A good Review answers several questions: What Increment exists now? Which product assumptions did we test? What evidence indicates benefit or disappointment? What external conditions have changed? How close are we to the Product Goal, and is it still the right direction? What will the Product Owner need to reconsider? The final question requires a decision pathway, not automatic agreement. Stakeholders contribute information, while the Product Owner remains accountable for effective Product Backlog management and maximizing value.

The Review is not an internal sign-off gate for partially completed work. An Increment can be delivered to stakeholders before the end of the Sprint, and the Review should never be regarded as a gate to releasing value. Nor does the Review substitute for security controls, accessibility assessment, contractual acceptance, or statutory approval where those are required. A demonstration cannot turn undone work into Done. The event's authority is to inspect results and inform adaptations within Scrum, while product-specific release, operational, and legal controls continue to apply.

The Increment and Definition of Done at Review

An Increment is a concrete step toward the Product Goal that is usable and verified to meet the applicable Definition of Done. Multiple Increments may be created within a Sprint, and the sum of Increments forms the product's current state. Work that fails the Definition of Done is not part of an Increment and cannot be presented as completed product work. The Review can still discuss unfinished risks or learning, provided the difference between verified capability, experiment, prototype, and unfinished work is transparent.

This distinction matters when using live demonstrations. Suppose an enrollment page looks polished but its validation rules have not been tested for screen-reader navigation or duplicate submissions. If accessibility and error-handling checks belong to the Definition of Done, presenting it as a finished Increment creates false confidence. The team may show the prototype as a research artifact and explain the remaining requirements, but should not relabel it as Done simply to produce an impressive Review. Likewise, a backend change with no visible interface can be demonstrably valuable if its verified behavior is relevant to the product goal.

A Review need not reperform every automated test. It should make relevant product evidence credible: a representative workflow in the working product, key acceptance conditions, usage or reliability observations, known constraints, and any unresolved threats to value. Recording a short evidence summary can assist absent stakeholders, but the existence of a slide deck or test report is not itself a Scrum artifact. The core question remains whether the product outcome supports a different decision.

Stakeholders and Representation in the Review

Key stakeholders are people whose knowledge and interests are needed to understand whether the product creates value and what should change. They may include actual users, customers, operations staff, accessibility specialists, data stewards, support agents, compliance professionals, service owners, or budget sponsors. Representation should follow the product decision, not job title alone. For a student information system, a student advisor who handles exceptions may provide more relevant detail about a broken workflow than an executive who only sees quarterly adoption charts.

The Product Owner has a central responsibility to make the product direction understandable and engage stakeholders with useful evidence. Scrum Masters may help remove collaboration barriers, and Developers can explain product behavior and technical constraints. The group need not turn into a committee that votes every backlog item into a fixed order; the Scrum Guide makes the Product Owner one accountable person, not a multi-person board. Stakeholder expectations and organizational obligations remain essential inputs, but they are not excuses to override this accountability.

Review organizers should also consider power and inclusion. A live customer may hesitate to criticize a feature in front of the vendor who built it. Remote attendees may not have equal access to a hands-on product. Frontline staff may have limited time while executives can remain for the whole event. Brief asynchronous evidence gathering, accessible demonstrations, and targeted interviews before the Review can widen the information base. These are optional supporting practices, not substitutes for collaborative inspection at the Review itself.

Preparing Review Evidence Without Rehearsed Theater

A little preparation makes a Review easier to understand: ensure the Increment is demonstrable, state the Sprint Goal, identify relevant baseline measures, distinguish known defects from new questions, and invite stakeholders able to contribute. Preparation should improve the fidelity of inspection rather than manufacture a success narrative. A carefully choreographed walkthrough that avoids real data, error paths, or representative user circumstances may make the team look polished while hiding the conditions stakeholders need to evaluate.

A useful evidence bundle can be small: (1) one-sentence Product Goal and Sprint Goal; (2) verified change since the last Review; (3) relevant qualitative or quantitative observations; (4) constraints and uncertainty; and (5) two or three genuine questions requiring stakeholder input. The bundle should separate observation, interpretation, and proposed action. For example: “Ten of twenty trial participants completed registration without assistance” is an observation; “the progress indicator may be unclear” is an interpretation; “test an alternate progress indicator with a larger cohort” is a proposed action.

Avoid treating completed story points, ticket counts, or developer hours as primary proof of customer value. Those figures may inform team capacity but do not establish whether a feature is useful or safe. Where the product is early, credible evidence may include direct observation of representative tasks or technical feasibility rather than business-scale outcome metrics. Evidence standards should be proportionate to what the team claims. Product learning is often strongest when the team can identify what remains unknown.

A Practical Review Working-Session Structure

One optional structure for a two-week Sprint's Review is to open with the product direction, inspect the Increment, discuss the environment and feedback, and identify adaptations. The facilitator should reserve sufficient time for stakeholder interaction rather than using the entire event for narration. A useful Review does not need an elaborate agenda; it needs visible product evidence and real collaborative decisions.

For example, after demonstrating a revised class-registration flow, an academic advisor might explain that the improvement helps new students but does nothing for returning students with prerequisite overrides. The team should inspect that evidence, not simply append the advisor's comment to the end of a slide deck. The Product Owner can assess whether override support should rise in the Product Backlog. When multiple requests conflict, the team can show the tradeoffs and make the competing needs explicit instead of promising to deliver all requests at once.

Collaborative Inspection Rather Than Approval Theater

Approval theater occurs when the Review is framed as a one-way report to leadership, when stakeholder questions are deferred until the next month, or when the team solicits only praise. A Scrum Review is not an executive scorecard, and stakeholder attendance should not convert it into an audit of individual productivity. Participants need the freedom to question utility, detect changed priorities, and propose future experiments. Some Reviews will validate the current direction; others will expose mistaken assumptions. Both outcomes can be valuable.

A useful facilitator distinguishes discussion categories: what is observable, what it means, and which decision follows. If a sponsor says the interface is too slow, ask which workflow and context produce that judgment, whether performance measures exist, and what user harm results. If Developers explain that a requested feature adds security risk, ask what concrete failure mode matters, which safeguards are feasible, and how the backlog might balance those constraints. The aim is not to win an argument; it is to improve the quality of the next product decision.

Avoid making every insight a commitment. The Product Owner may need more evidence before reordering major work. Conversely, repeatedly deferring obvious adaptation because all dates were promised months ago violates the spirit of empirical planning. A Review should surface decision rights and constraints visibly. Organizational funding approvals, regulatory review, and contracts may constrain options, but they should not be hidden under a fictional consensus that implies all feedback has been accepted.

Product Goal, Sprint Goal, and Backlog Adaptation

The Product Goal provides a longer-term objective for the Scrum Team, while the Sprint Goal expresses why the current Sprint is valuable. At Review, progress is not evaluated solely as a percentage of tasks completed. The more useful question is whether the delivered Increment and observed environment support movement toward the Product Goal. A technically successful Sprint can reveal that the next step should differ from the one previously imagined.

The Product Backlog is an emergent, ordered list of what is needed to improve the product. After a Review, the Product Owner may clarify a previously misunderstood need, elevate an urgent accessibility defect, introduce an experiment, retire a low-value assumption, or alter sequencing. The guide does not require the Review to end with a fully reordered backlog or a formally approved plan. It does require inspection and an opportunity for adaptation. Important decisions may continue with further investigation after the event.

Consider an online learning product whose Product Goal is to help first-time students reach their first completed lesson. A Sprint produces a new onboarding checklist, but the Review reveals that most trial users leave during account verification, before seeing the checklist. The checklist may be Done, but the Product Owner should examine whether identity verification is now the higher-value intervention. That choice follows from observed behavior, not from pretending the completed checklist has no technical value. A meaningful Review can therefore protect future investment by challenging a previously plausible plan.

Distinguishing Feedback, Evidence, and Decision

Stakeholder feedback is useful data, but it is not automatically a fact about all users. A single complaint may expose a severe accessibility barrier or may reflect an uncommon preference. A favorable demonstration response may represent genuine value or a participant's reluctance to criticize. A mature Review evaluates the source, scope, frequency, severity, and consequences of feedback before translating it into backlog decisions. This is especially important when the product serves diverse populations and when some user groups have less voice in ordinary meetings.

A simple decision record can capture: observation; affected user segment; current evidence; risk of inaction; alternative explanations; proposed follow-up; and decision owner. The record should not become a bureaucratic gate for routine collaboration. It can, however, prevent the team from confusing “stakeholder requested” with “validated requirement.” The Product Owner decides how to use that information for the Product Backlog; Developers contribute feasibility and quality implications; the Scrum Master supports transparent decision-making without taking over product ordering.

Three questions improve the transition from feedback to action. First, what can we now say with confidence? Second, what remains uncertain and could reverse our choice? Third, what is the smallest responsible next action? The answer might be immediate remediation, a targeted research session, a monitored rollout, or leaving an idea unordered until more evidence exists. The Review is successful when that reasoning is more informed than it was before the meeting—not when a large number of new tickets is created.

Reviewing Product Risks and Nonfunctional Qualities

Product feedback is not limited to visible features. Reliability, privacy, security, accessibility, recoverability, maintainability, and operational performance can determine whether a product is valuable at all. An enrollment service that appears fast in a demonstration may fail under peak registration demand; a useful recommendation feature can expose sensitive student data through a poorly designed permission boundary. These are not secondary concerns to be deferred until a “hardening Sprint.” They belong in the work needed to create a usable Increment consistent with the Definition of Done and product obligations.

When stakeholders bring specialized expertise, the Review can reveal that a feature's intended outcome creates additional constraints. An accessibility specialist might demonstrate that keyboard-only navigation takes twice as many actions as expected. Operations staff may show that payment retries cause duplicate notifications. Such observations can lead the Product Owner to reorder the Product Backlog, while the team may separately inspect its testing practices at the Retrospective. The Review remains a discussion of product outcome and adaptation; it need not become a comprehensive security-audit meeting.

Evidence should distinguish confirmed defects from hypothetical failure modes. If a privacy review has not occurred, say so; do not substitute a dashboard screenshot or reassuring declaration. If a production log is unavailable, state the measurement gap. When a legal or contractual acceptance process exists, document the responsible authority outside Scrum rather than claiming the Sprint Review satisfies that process. Honest risk disclosure allows stakeholders to make better decisions while preserving the role of proper assurance controls.

Review Metrics: Outcomes Before Output Counts

An effective Sprint Review uses measures aligned to the decision under consideration. Product adoption may matter for an onboarding change; task completion, error frequency, time to recover, or customer-reported friction may matter for an operational workflow; accessibility test outcomes may matter for inclusion. Different measures can provide contradictory signals: registrations may rise while successful first lessons decline. This is a reason to inspect the relationship, not to choose the metric that creates a favorable status slide.

Useful review evidence can combine product outcomes, operational safeguards, and qualitative context. A team's “number of stories Done” may indicate throughput, but it is not proof that a user obtained a benefit. Velocity is especially vulnerable to misuse as a productivity score because story points are local estimates rather than a universal unit of value. Comparing two teams' points as if they were standardized output produces incentives to manipulate estimates. Review questions should focus on what changed for users and what should happen next.

A disciplined evaluation states the measure's definition, population, time window, baseline, and limitations. If a feature is available only to twenty invited users, do not claim the results predict adoption across an entire university. If an accessibility incident was reported once, do not dismiss it because it is statistically infrequent; severe harms can justify immediate action without a large sample. If data collection would compromise privacy or consent, use a less invasive measure or rely on permitted qualitative observation. Data should support judgment, not displace it.

Demonstrations, Experiments, and Product Learning

A demonstration helps make the Increment concrete, but the Review's purpose is broader than showing a happy path. Teams may invite stakeholders to attempt a workflow, compare expected and actual outcomes, inspect a production metric, or discuss a monitored experiment. A live feature can be valuable even when its effect remains uncertain. In that case, the Review should separate the capability built from the outcome not yet demonstrated. “Search filtering now works” and “search filtering improved discovery” are different claims, requiring different evidence.

Experiments may be designed in the Product Backlog and carried out during a Sprint. They should have a question, a responsible method, appropriate safeguards, and a decision rule. For a learner dashboard, a team might ask whether a clearer next-action prompt reduces abandonment. The Review might reveal ambiguous early evidence and lead to additional user research rather than immediate expansion. In settings where randomized testing is unsuitable, interviews or structured observations may still reduce uncertainty. Scientific language should not be used to overstate confidence in weak designs.

The same discipline applies to technical demonstrations. A new cache layer may reduce median response time under a load-test scenario yet increase worst-case latency during invalidation. Stakeholders can inspect both findings and decide whether the next product investment should prioritize operational resilience or new features. The Review is a place to discuss the implication for product value. The Retrospective can later consider why the team initially selected inadequate performance tests and what practices to change.

What a Review Can Produce—and What It Cannot

A Sprint Review may yield a clearer understanding of customer needs, updated relative priorities, newly recognized risks, research questions, alternative delivery choices, or changes to the Product Backlog. Its output is not required to be a signed acceptance document, a fixed release plan, a verbatim transcript, or a committee-approved set of user stories. The Product Owner remains accountable for maintaining and ordering the Product Backlog, including after stakeholders express different preferences.

A lightweight follow-up record can improve organizational continuity. Record the decisive observation, who owns the next decision, what remains unverified, and the date or event at which new evidence should be considered. In regulated settings, a separate compliance record may also be necessary, but a meeting note should not be described as regulatory approval. The distinction protects the team from confusing evidence of discussion with evidence of control execution. Where participants make commitments affecting the organization, the specific authority and dependency should be explicit.

Do not equate a Review with “permission to release.” Scrum recognizes that an Increment can be delivered before the Sprint ends and that the Sprint Review is not a release gate. Actual releases may require deployment readiness, customer notice, change controls, or security procedures depending on the product's context. Those operational conditions should be designed into the product's life cycle, not used to keep fully Done features unavailable simply because a recurring meeting has not occurred.

Sprint Retrospective: Purpose and Scope

The purpose of the Sprint Retrospective is to plan ways to increase quality and effectiveness. The Scrum Team inspects how the previous Sprint went across individuals, interactions, processes, tools, and the Definition of Done. It discusses what went well, what did not, how problems were handled or left unresolved, and which assumptions proved misleading. The team identifies the changes most likely to improve its effectiveness and addresses significant improvements as soon as possible. Some of that work may be included in the next Sprint Backlog.

The Retrospective concludes the Sprint and follows the Review in the official event sequence. Its membership is the Scrum Team: Developers, Product Owner, and Scrum Master. This is not a managerial appraisal conference, customer acceptance forum, or meeting for assigning fault. The subject is the system of work and the team's capacity to generate better results, including relationships, skill access, technical practices, infrastructure, and organizational impediments. Individuals may acknowledge their own decisions, but the meeting must not become a public performance ranking.

The Retrospective's effect should be visible in future behavior. If a team repeatedly identifies “we need better communication,” but does not determine what will change, when, by whom, and how success will be recognized, the meeting becomes a ritual. A retrospective improvement can be modest: testing a shared escalation channel for blocked production changes, reserving protected code-review capacity, or adding a reproducible accessibility check. The method is optional; the objective of real improvement is intrinsic to the event.

Retrospective Participants and Decision Rights

All three Scrum accountabilities belong in the Retrospective because each influences how value is created. Developers can speak about architecture, testing, coordination, and interruptions. The Product Owner can discuss stakeholder demands, clarity of product direction, and backlog quality. The Scrum Master can facilitate inquiry, help identify organizational barriers, and coach self-management. The team should not assume only Developers have process problems or only the Scrum Master is authorized to suggest process changes.

A Retrospective normally does not include outside stakeholders because the Scrum Team is the specified participant group. Teams can seek outside input at other times, and a cross-team coordination forum may be useful, but replacing the team's Retrospective with a management-led review changes the event's character. An executive may have legitimate responsibility for staffing or funding; that does not require their presence while team members discuss difficulties candidly. If an obstacle requires external authority, the Scrum Master or an agreed representative can elevate the issue with enough context to seek a remedy without exposing every personal comment.

Responsibility for improvements must match decision rights. Developers may control how they organize code reviews, but may not be able to purchase new infrastructure without approval. The Product Owner may clarify stakeholder priorities but cannot single-handedly remove a third-party procurement delay. A good Retrospective distinguishes changes the team can implement immediately, changes requiring organizational support, and uncertainties needing investigation. The meeting should not create false commitments from people without the power or capacity to fulfill them.

Psychological Safety and Professional Accountability

Retrospectives require sufficient trust to discuss failure mechanisms honestly. A team that conceals incidents to avoid ridicule cannot learn from them. Teams can establish working agreements such as describing observable events rather than personalities, avoiding interruptions, allowing participants to decline to discuss sensitive personal details, and prohibiting retaliatory use of personal disclosures. These are practical facilitation options, not formal Scrum rules. Their purpose is to keep difficult discussions useful rather than to make all comments agreeable.

Psychological safety and accountability are compatible. When a developer repeatedly bypasses essential quality safeguards, the team should examine the behavior and the conditions that enabled it. It might find unrealistic deadlines, unclear controls, inadequate training, or an individual decision that requires direct correction. The Retrospective can address systemic learning; formal misconduct, harassment, or personnel matters may require appropriate confidential organizational processes outside the event. A working session about process improvement should not be used as a substitute investigation of serious allegations.

Confidentiality should be communicated accurately. A facilitator cannot promise unconditional secrecy when safety, legal, or workplace reporting obligations may apply. Similarly, anonymous surveys may not truly preserve anonymity on a small team. Respectful practice includes collecting only necessary information, making notes proportionate to the improvement goal, and allowing people to raise concerns through suitable protected channels. Productive candor grows when the team sees that issues lead to appropriate corrective actions rather than embarrassment or inaction.

Data for Retrospectives: Events, Patterns, and Conditions

A useful Retrospective can combine several evidence types: observed incidents, repeated bottlenecks, defect escape patterns, review wait times, production changes, coordination delays, and direct experience. No mandatory metrics set exists. Data should illuminate the team's own work rather than compare people or departments using incompatible measures. A high number of tickets completed alongside repeated urgent hotfixes may indicate fragility. A lower throughput alongside a well-resolved reliability risk may indicate valuable investment in quality.

Teams should avoid treating correlation as diagnosis. Suppose work consistently remains in “ready for test” for three days. Possible explanations include a shared environment with limited availability, test ownership ambiguity, late defect discovery, or a measurement that includes planned waiting periods. The observation suggests a question, not an automatic conclusion that testers are the bottleneck. Examining multiple recent cases, including exceptions, helps reveal the mechanism. Participant recollections can complement event timestamps but should be acknowledged as partial.

The most useful measure is sometimes the effect of the team's previous improvement experiment. If the team introduced smaller pull requests to shorten review delay, it can compare representative cases before and after, note differences in change size and staffing, and decide whether to keep, modify, or abandon the policy. The goal is not to prove every improvement succeeded. Learning that a proposed fix did not work is itself valuable when it prevents continued waste. As with Review metrics, collect only data needed to make a responsible decision.

A Practical Retrospective Working-Session Structure

One optional Retrospective sequence is to establish a constructive discussion context, collect observations, identify a small number of meaningful patterns, analyze likely causes, select improvements, and agree how to inspect the outcome. The team should adapt the sequence to its needs. The Scrum Guide does not mandate “start, stop, continue,” dot voting, affinity mapping, or a particular retrospective board. A short direct conversation can be better than an elaborate game when a serious operational issue needs attention.

Illustrative phase Main question Useful output
Establish context What was this Sprint's work and what boundaries make conversation productive? Common factual frame
Collect observations What happened, including exceptions and successes? Specific events and supporting evidence
Investigate causes Which mechanisms could explain recurring difficulty? Tested or testable hypotheses
Select an improvement What is the most consequential feasible change? One or two bounded actions
Assign follow-through Who will enable the action and when will the result be inspected? Visible ownership and review point

For example, if reviews of pull requests frequently stall, the team can examine whether requests arrive too late, whether ownership is unclear, whether CI feedback is slow, and whether reviewers are constantly interrupted. An action like “communicate better” gives no meaningful test. An action like “split changes into independently reviewable units and reserve a daily 20-minute review window for the next two Sprints” has an observable mechanism. It may not solve the problem, but the team can learn from its result.

Root-Cause Analysis Without Inventing Certainty

Root-cause analysis seeks the conditions that produce undesirable outcomes, not a person to blame. Methods such as five whys, causal diagrams, timelines, and incident reconstruction can help but are not Scrum requirements. Each method has limitations. Asking “why” repeatedly may create an overly simple chain when software failures emerge from interacting conditions. A timeline can reveal sequence while still omitting hidden dependencies. The strongest conclusion is one supported by observations, plausible alternatives, and an intervention that can be tested.

Suppose a production deployment required an emergency rollback. “The developer made a mistake” is unlikely to be a sufficient explanation. Further examination may reveal that the change lacked a reliable staging dataset, code review focused on style rather than behavior, a feature flag was absent, and rollback steps had never been practiced. The team can consider several feasible improvements: representative test data, safer rollout, dependency checks, or better review prompts. The chosen action should address the most influential and controllable failure mode, not every symptom at once.

A Retrospective should also inspect what went well and why. Teams sometimes solve difficult cross-functional problems through informal practices worth preserving. For example, paired debugging between an accessibility specialist and a frontend developer may have exposed hidden assumptions early. Identifying the enabling conditions—shared availability, a reproducible test case, and direct communication—can help the team reproduce that strength. Continuous improvement includes protecting successful practices, not only fixing failures.

Choosing High-Leverage Improvement Experiments

A Retrospective often surfaces more problems than the team can address. Selecting too many actions creates another unmaintained backlog. Prefer a small number of improvements with a plausible mechanism, meaningful expected benefit, and capacity to implement. The team can consider severity, frequency, influence on product quality, feasibility, and time to learn. These criteria are decision aids, not a prescribed Scrum scoring system. A rare event with severe privacy implications may deserve priority over a frequent but harmless annoyance.

A useful improvement hypothesis has four parts: problem and baseline, change to try, expected observable effect, and review point. Example: “Three of the last four Sprints saw overnight deployment failures related to missing environment variables. For the next two Sprints, run a predeployment configuration check in a representative environment. We expect configuration-caused failures to decline; we will review all deployment attempts at the next Retrospective.” This is much more actionable than “improve deployments.”

Not every action needs a metric. A team may need to clarify a working agreement after a harmful interruption or establish a private escalation route. The test can be qualitative: whether participants understand the agreement, whether the expected communication occurs, and whether the recurring problem becomes less disruptive. If the action is beyond the team's authority, identify a sponsor or impediment owner and avoid pretending it is already solved. Follow-through, not the volume of ideas, differentiates improvement from ceremony.

Retrospective Actions and the Next Sprint Backlog

The Scrum Guide says the most impactful improvements should be addressed as soon as possible and may even be added to the Sprint Backlog for the next Sprint. This language matters: it does not require a fixed number of retrospective action items, a mandatory “improvement story” every Sprint, or automatic inclusion of every idea in the Product Backlog. Some improvements require no ticket; others involve substantial product-related engineering work and should be planned transparently. The team chooses a form that supports real execution.

Suppose the team agrees to add an automated schema-compatibility check. Developers can plan the technical work, Product Owner can understand its value and capacity effect, and the Scrum Team can consider how it supports the next Sprint Goal. A commitment to change code, tests, or operations should not be hidden from the people who depend on delivery forecasts. Conversely, updating a meeting working agreement may require only a direct decision and a reminder rather than a separate artifact. The improvement method should fit its risk and cost.

A strong follow-up loop inspects previous decisions before adding new ones. Did the action occur? What effect did it have? Was the original diagnosis right? Did the change create a new cost? What should be kept or adapted? Teams can abandon an ineffective tactic without abandoning their improvement objective. If the same issue appears repeatedly with no progress, inspect whether the missing ingredient is authority, capacity, skill, willingness, or data. Recurrence without learning is itself a retrospective signal.

Engineering Quality and the Definition of Done

The Retrospective explicitly includes the Definition of Done among its inspection subjects. The team may discover that its current quality standard misses an important failure mode, that automated checks are unreliable, or that required evidence is gathered too late. Improving the Definition of Done can strengthen transparency, but it must be done responsibly. The Scrum Guide states that if the Definition of Done is part of organizational standards, Scrum Teams must follow it as a minimum; otherwise, the Scrum Team must create an appropriate Definition of Done for the product. Multiple Scrum Teams working on the same product must mutually define and comply with the same Definition of Done.

A Retrospective might reveal that features consistently pass functional tests but fail keyboard navigation after integration. The improvement could be to include keyboard-access tests earlier in the development path, train Developers to use automated and manual checks, and ensure the Definition of Done reflects the necessary accessibility outcome. The actual product work still must meet the applicable quality boundary. A retrospective resolution is not itself proof that the product became compliant.

The team should resist the tempting “solution” of weakening quality criteria to make forecasts look better. Quality does not decrease during a Sprint; the Definition of Done is a formal commitment attached to the Increment. Genuine improvement may include removing wasteful duplicate steps if they do not protect quality, but reducing necessary verification without risk assessment distorts transparency. Teams should document material standard changes and coordinate with product and organizational authorities where required.

Flow, Coordination, and Technical Debt

Many retrospective problems originate in the flow of work rather than an individual's speed. Excessive work in progress, long review queues, test-environment contention, brittle integrations, and frequent context switching can make a competent team unpredictable. A useful Retrospective can examine where work waits, how often items are reopened, which dependencies recur, and how much capacity is absorbed by unexpected repair. The underlying question is whether the team's working arrangement enables timely delivery of usable value at sustainable cost.

Technical debt is relevant when design shortcuts or deferred maintenance make future changes more expensive or dangerous. Not every workaround is irresponsible; some are explicit tradeoffs made under uncertainty. A Retrospective can identify when a short-term choice has become a persistent obstacle. Rather than declaring “all technical debt must be paid,” the team can articulate a concrete effect—for example, duplicate authorization code causes changes to require three separate patches, increasing regression risk—and determine which remediation is proportional to the risk and future product needs.

Flow improvements may include limiting concurrent work, reducing batch sizes, strengthening automated integration feedback, changing review policies, or improving dependency visibility. These are optional tactics consistent with agility, not prescribed Scrum mechanics. The team should test whether they actually reduce waiting or improve quality; a WIP limit can be ineffective if managers continuously override it. The Review may later show the product benefit of improved reliability, while the Retrospective inspects whether the underlying work process is healthier.

Distributed and Asynchronous Teams

Distributed Scrum Teams face additional constraints: time zones, unreliable video access, differing spoken languages, assistive technology needs, and uneven access to test environments. Both events should remain accessible working sessions in which relevant participants can inspect evidence and contribute meaningfully. A remote Review might provide an accessible recording of a representative workflow beforehand, clear links to a safe demonstration environment, and time for live discussion. The recording supports participation; it should not replace collaborative inspection with stakeholders.

A remote Retrospective may use a shared board to collect observations before the meeting, followed by synchronous discussion of patterns and improvement choices. Facilitators should avoid assuming that visible enthusiasm in video calls measures the quality of participation. Some people contribute better through structured writing, especially when conversations are multilingual or require reflection. Anonymity claims should be accurate, and asynchronous submissions should not become surveillance records. Documentation must be sufficiently clear for team members who cannot attend, without treating the private discussion as a transcript intended for broad redistribution.

Time-zone fairness is itself a possible improvement topic. If one region always joins outside working hours, the team can experiment with rotating schedules, pre-reading, or alternating live participation modes while preserving the purpose of the events. Technical limitations may justify changes to facilitation, but not perpetual elimination of stakeholder collaboration or introspection. Distributed work should shape the method used to achieve transparency, inspection, and adaptation—not become an assumption that these goals are no longer possible.

Working Across Multiple Scrum Teams

When several Scrum Teams work on the same product, they should share the Product Goal, Product Backlog, and Product Owner. They must also have a mutually defined and followed Definition of Done for their common product. The local details of Reviews and Retrospectives may vary with team size and organizational arrangements, but each team still needs effective inspection and adaptation. A cross-team demonstration can illuminate integrated product results; it should not conceal component gaps or make one team responsible for another team's professional commitments.

Integrated product inspection matters when separate teams' features work alone but fail together. A shared Review may expose authentication incompatibility, duplicated user workflows, or conflicting data assumptions. The Product Owner must be able to use that information to order one coherent Product Backlog. If every team holds a Review attended only by its own internal customers, product-wide feedback may remain fragmented. The organization can coordinate reviews or product-level evidence as needed, but should not impose a fixed scaling ceremony and claim it is required by the Scrum Guide.

Cross-team patterns discovered in Retrospectives—shared build instability, contested staging access, inaccessible design assets, or delayed service contracts—may require a joint improvement discussion. Such a discussion supplements rather than replaces each Scrum Team's Retrospective. It should identify who can act on the shared impediment and how individual teams will inspect their own effectiveness. The aim is an integrated product and effective teams, not a hierarchy of meeting reports that filters out uncomfortable evidence before leadership can hear it.

Leadership, Governance, and Organizational Impediments

Executives and institutional leaders can improve Scrum effectiveness by protecting decision rights and removing obstacles rather than using Reviews as performance hearings. Management may appropriately ask whether a product is achieving its intended outcomes, what investment is justified, and whether serious risks are controlled. Those questions belong in the product governance context and can be informed by Review evidence. Demanding commitments to a predetermined roadmap despite contradictory user evidence, however, undermines adaptation and may produce distorted reporting.

The Product Owner remains accountable for maximizing product value and Product Backlog management. The Scrum Master supports the Scrum framework and effectiveness, including helping the organization understand empirical planning and remove barriers. Developers own professional implementation and quality responsibilities. These accountabilities do not eliminate legal or contractual authorities: a chief security officer may have control obligations, an accessibility owner may have compliance responsibilities, and budget owners may impose spending limits. The challenge is to make these constraints transparent while preserving legitimate team decisions.

A Retrospective might reveal that planned work is constantly interrupted by executive requests. The team's immediate action could be an intake policy that routes new demands to the Product Owner for negotiation. If the organization refuses to respect that boundary, it is an impediment requiring leadership engagement; no amount of clever retrospective facilitation will solve it within the team. Conversely, a product decision that conflicts with a binding security requirement should not be justified as agile flexibility. Responsible governance clarifies boundaries while enabling learning within them.

Common Sprint Review Anti-Patterns

Demo-only Review. The team shows features, receives applause, and ends without inspecting environmental change or future direction. Remedy: reserve real discussion time, frame decisions, and invite relevant stakeholders.

Completion theater. Partially verified prototypes are presented as Done. Remedy: state the Definition of Done, distinguish research artifacts from usable Increments, and show relevant quality evidence.

Status interrogation. Leaders ask each Developer why tickets moved slowly. Remedy: keep the event focused on product outcomes and collaborative adaptation; address personnel matters through appropriate channels.

Stakeholder capture. The loudest attendee converts every suggestion into a promised backlog item. Remedy: document competing needs and retain Product Owner accountability for prioritization.

Proxy-only evidence. An executive reports what users want while frontline users and support staff are absent. Remedy: broaden the evidence base with representative user research and appropriate participation.

Review-as-release-gate. The team delays delivery until the meeting because it assumes the Review grants permission. Remedy: separate Scrum inspection from real deployment and approval controls.

Metrics without context. The presentation advertises ticket closure or story points as customer value. Remedy: show relevant product effects and acknowledge limitations.

These anti-patterns are not prohibited meeting labels; they are ways an event can lose its purpose. A Review may include a demonstration or management representatives and still be valuable. The diagnostic test is whether the session enables inspection of the actual product outcome and informed adaptation rather than merely celebrating the prior plan.

Common Sprint Retrospective Anti-Patterns

Complaint collection without action. The same issue is raised repeatedly without a feasible improvement hypothesis or owner. Remedy: select one or two changes and inspect results at a future Retrospective.

Blame and performance grading. Individuals are compared or pressed to admit fault in front of colleagues. Remedy: examine conditions and patterns, route serious personnel issues appropriately, and build trust in learning.

Endless idea lists. Every observation becomes a task despite limited capacity. Remedy: distinguish themes, choose high-leverage actions, and preserve the ability to execute them.

Premature certainty. A team attributes a delay to one person or tool without considering alternative causes. Remedy: reconstruct representative events and test causal explanations.

Ritualized optimism. Participants feel compelled to say everything is fine. Remedy: make disagreement safe, ask about specific events, and examine objective evidence alongside experience.

Process-only focus. A team ignores quality and its Definition of Done while optimizing meeting duration. Remedy: connect any efficiency gain to usable Increments, technical excellence, and sustainable work.

Externalized ownership. Every action is assigned to a manager who is not present and has not agreed. Remedy: identify controllable local actions and escalate genuine external impediments through appropriate channels.

A Retrospective is not judged by the novelty of a facilitation technique. Its quality is shown by whether the team learns something consequential and changes behavior in response.

Worked Case Study: University Course Enrollment

Consider a fictional university software product that helps students register for courses. The Product Goal is to increase the percentage of eligible students who complete registration without support intervention. During a two-week Sprint, a Scrum Team completes a new course-search filter, verifies keyboard use and error states, and deploys it behind a controlled rollout. Its Sprint Goal was to make prerequisite-compatible options easier to locate. The team brings a working Increment, two short user observations, and initial support inquiries to the Sprint Review.

During the Review, an advisor explains that transfer students still cannot find equivalent course names. One student finds the filter more readable; another fails to understand the synonym matching. The Product Owner asks whether the team should pursue synonym discovery ahead of a previously planned recommendation panel. Developers explain that synonym support can be tested through a small curated dataset, but a full equivalency engine requires collaboration with academic records specialists. The Product Owner records the evidence and determines that a targeted experiment deserves further refinement. The Review ends with a better-informed product direction—not a demand to implement every idea immediately.

During the Retrospective, the Scrum Team examines why the change needed late accessibility fixes. It finds that product examples used in early development lacked keyboard-only workflows, even though accessibility tests were part of the Definition of Done. The team selects an improvement experiment: add keyboard-only acceptance walkthroughs during backlog refinement and inspect the next two suitable Product Backlog items. The action affects how the team works; it is distinct from the product prioritization discussed with advisors. At the following Review, the team can report product findings; at the following Retrospective, it can inspect whether earlier accessibility checks reduced late rework. This separation creates two genuine learning loops.

Worked Case Study: Payment Retry Reliability

A second fictional team maintains a subscription-payment service. The Product Goal is to reduce failed renewals without introducing duplicate charges. During one Sprint the team completes idempotency handling for a defined set of payment retry requests and strengthens audit logging. It verifies the Increment against the Definition of Done. The Sprint Review includes an operations representative, support lead, and product sponsor. A representative test shows that repeated client retries now produce one authoritative transaction outcome for the supported path.

The Review reveals a tradeoff: support incidents have declined in the test population, but retry latency has risen and the current dashboard does not distinguish deliberate user retries from network retransmissions. The Product Owner and stakeholders discuss whether the next investment should expand the coverage or first improve observability. Developers explain what can be measured reliably. The Product Owner may choose an observability-focused backlog item before broad rollout. No one should infer production-wide duplicate-charge prevention from a narrow test or treat Review comments as release authorization.

The Retrospective examines how the team discovered ambiguous retry semantics only after integration. Members reconstruct three cases and identify a missing shared contract between service and client teams. They choose to define an example-based retry contract and test it during the next implementation cycle. They also decide to review error and timeout behavior earlier with operations. These actions are within the team's influence and should reduce future misunderstandings. They do not replace the product decision about rollout scope. This case illustrates the difference between inspecting the service's customer outcome and improving the engineering process that produced it.

Worked Case Study: Low Trust and Management Pressure

A third fictional Scrum Team faces pressure to report consistently rising story-point velocity. Developers have begun inflating estimates and avoiding complex reliability work. At the Sprint Review, a department leader presents the velocity chart as proof that the product is improving. Yet production support reports recurring outages, and users report difficulty completing essential tasks. The first corrective move is to reframe the Review around product behavior and outcomes: actual uptime, workflow success, unresolved operational constraints, and the Product Goal. A velocity trend cannot answer those questions.

The Retrospective requires a more delicate intervention. Team members are unlikely to describe the pressure candidly if the same leader observes their conversation or later uses notes in performance reviews. The Scrum Master can establish an appropriate discussion setting, clarify limits of confidentiality, and invite fact-based examination of incentives and effects. The team may identify a testable change—stop publishing story-point comparisons as performance ratings, improve reliability work visibility, and track incident recurrence. If leaders control reporting policy, the Scrum Master must address the impediment with the organization, rather than asking the team to conceal the truth.

The desired outcome is not a superficially pleasant meeting. It is a system in which professionals can report what has been learned, quality problems receive attention, and leaders understand the relationship between investment and value. If trust does not improve, the team must inspect that failure too. A Retrospective cannot itself guarantee organizational reform, but it can make the relevant conditions visible and guide accountable next steps.

Decision Framework: Choosing the Right Event and Action

When an issue emerges, first ask what is being inspected. If the question concerns user benefit, stakeholder needs, product results, or future product options, it primarily belongs to the Sprint Review. If it concerns how the team collaborates, verifies quality, manages dependencies, or learns from a failure, it primarily belongs to the Sprint Retrospective. Some issues belong in both, but they require different questions and actions. An accessibility defect can trigger a Product Backlog adaptation to repair the product and a process experiment to detect such defects earlier.

Next, ask how urgent the matter is. Active security incidents, unsafe releases, or production failures should be addressed when discovered rather than delayed until either event. Then ask who is accountable: the Product Owner for Product Backlog decisions, Developers for creating Done Increments and their implementation plan, the Scrum Master for helping establish Scrum and effectiveness, and organizational authorities for obligations outside those accountabilities. Finally, ask which observation will show whether the chosen adaptation worked. This turns a meeting outcome into a learning cycle.

Planning the Next Cycle of Learning

The strongest connection between the events is not a rigid handoff of tickets; it is an improved ability to plan based on current evidence. The Review may reveal that the next valuable product problem differs from the one anticipated. The Retrospective may reveal that the team needs a new testing practice to deliver that product change safely. When Sprint Planning begins the next Sprint, these findings can inform the Product Owner's priorities and the Scrum Team's decisions about the Sprint Goal and a credible plan.

This is still an adaptive system, not a mechanical conveyor belt. Some Review findings need further discovery, some retrospective improvements can begin immediately, and some organizational constraints persist across several Sprints. The team should avoid converting every observation into obligatory Sprint scope. Instead, ensure that important discoveries are not lost, that decision rights remain clear, and that the selected Sprint Goal can be pursued without sacrificing quality. A short feedback loop is useful only if it can influence behavior.

Scrum's recurring events create a minimum rhythm of transparency and adaptation. Product feedback should continue outside the Review; engineering improvement should occur whenever problems are detected; and the Definition of Done remains a quality boundary throughout the Sprint. The recurring cycle works when these events strengthen continuous learning rather than restrict learning to scheduled meetings. It fails when the team waits for the ceremony while known problems worsen.

Facilitator and Team Preparation Checklist

The following is an optional operating checklist, not a formal Scrum artifact or compliance standard. It can help a team sustain the intent of the events without prescribing a single template.

Before the Sprint Review: clarify the Product Goal and Sprint Goal; identify usable Increment evidence and important limitations; invite stakeholders with relevant knowledge; provide accessible material; consider environmental changes; leave time for dialogue; and distinguish decisions that belong to the Product Owner from matters requiring outside approval.

During the Sprint Review: inspect the actual outcome; ask who benefits and who may be excluded; consider product risks and uncertainty; allow relevant disagreement; discuss what has changed; identify candidate adaptations; and avoid using story points, ticket counts, or presentation polish as proxies for value.

Before the Sprint Retrospective: bring representative observations about interactions, tooling, process, quality, and the Definition of Done; review the effects of earlier improvement actions; establish a respectful discussion context; and consider where the team can act independently versus where organizational help is needed.

During and after the Sprint Retrospective: investigate causes without unwarranted certainty; select a manageable number of consequential improvements; identify how each will be enabled; make follow-through visible as appropriate; escalate external impediments; and inspect whether the change helped in a later cycle. Preserve space for immediate corrective action when risk cannot wait.

Misconceptions and Boundary Cases

“The Sprint Review is the demo.” A demo can support the event, but the event is a collaborative inspection of Sprint outcome, product direction, and future adaptation. A polished product presentation with no stakeholder contribution is insufficient.

“The Review approves unfinished work.” No. Only work meeting the Definition of Done counts as an Increment. A Review is not a substitute verification or release gate.

“The Retrospective is an optional meeting for teams with problems.” The event is an element of Scrum and is relevant to strong teams as well as struggling ones. Improvement includes protecting effective practices.

“Scrum requires a retrospective action ticket every Sprint.” The 2020 Guide does not mandate that artifact or number of tasks. It says impactful improvements should be addressed promptly and may enter the next Sprint Backlog.

“Stakeholders should attend the Retrospective because they attended the Review.” The Guide describes the Scrum Team inspecting its own effectiveness in the Retrospective. External feedback is valuable, but it should not replace the team's setting for candid process inspection.

“A one-hour Review and a one-hour Retrospective are official timeboxes.” No. The official upper bounds are four and three hours, respectively, for a one-month Sprint; shorter Sprints usually use shorter events.

“The Product Owner must obey every Review request.” The Product Owner is accountable for value and Product Backlog management. Stakeholder input matters, but it does not turn backlog ordering into a committee vote.

“A team must release only after the Review.” No. Scrum permits delivering usable Increments before the Sprint concludes, subject to applicable product and organizational controls.

Study Questions and Applied Exercises

  1. Compare the primary object of inspection, participants, and expected adaptation in the Sprint Review and the Sprint Retrospective. Why would merging them into a single status meeting be risky?
  2. An Increment meets its Definition of Done but user interviews show little benefit. What should the team discuss at Review, and what should it not infer from the technical completion?
  3. A prototype is visually impressive but fails accessibility criteria in the Definition of Done. How may the team describe it honestly at Review?
  4. Stakeholders request three conflicting features. Who remains accountable for Product Backlog ordering, and how should the competing evidence be evaluated?
  5. A Retrospective identifies repeated deployment failures. Construct a causal hypothesis and a two-Sprint improvement experiment with an observable outcome.
  6. A leader uses velocity comparisons to rank individual Developers. Explain how this could damage empirical learning and what governance changes might help.
  7. Which concerns should be escalated immediately rather than postponed until a scheduled Review or Retrospective?
  8. A two-week Sprint has a 90-minute Review and a 60-minute Retrospective. Are these times required, prohibited, or optional? Justify with the official Guide.
  9. Draft an evidence note distinguishing observation, interpretation, and action for a user who cannot complete an online application.
  10. Multiple Scrum Teams work on the same product and repeatedly encounter integration defects. What should be inspected in product-wide review evidence, local Retrospectives, and cross-team improvement discussions?
  11. Identify three differences between a compliance audit and a Sprint Review, explaining where the processes may exchange evidence but cannot replace one another.
  12. Design a small follow-through record for a retrospective improvement. What information is useful, and what unnecessary surveillance data should be excluded?

Applied exercise A—Product inspection: Select a familiar digital service. Define its Product Goal, one plausible Sprint Goal, two observable Increment behaviors, and three stakeholder questions. Explain how answers could change the next Product Backlog decision.

Applied exercise B—Team improvement: Reconstruct a recurring delay or defect in a fictional team's workflow. Identify at least two alternative causes, choose a bounded intervention, specify who can authorize it, and describe the evidence needed to decide whether it helped.

Applied exercise C—Decision boundary: For a fictional payment-service Sprint with rising retry errors, write separate Review and Retrospective agendas. Name who should participate, what each event should inspect, and what decision must occur outside Scrum if formal security or legal authorization is required.
