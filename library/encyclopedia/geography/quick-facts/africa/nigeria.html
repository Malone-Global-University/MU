Country Overview

Nigeria, officially the Federal Republic of Nigeria, is a large and highly populous country in West Africa, extending from the Gulf of Guinea northward into the Sahel. Its federal capital is Abuja, while Lagos is a leading commercial center. Nigeria gained independence from the United Kingdom on October 1, 1960 and became a republic on October 1, 1963. Its contemporary economy includes petroleum and natural gas, agriculture, manufacturing, finance, telecommunications, trade and major cultural industries. [4] [24] [16]

Under the 1999 Constitution, Nigeria comprises 36 states and the Federal Capital Territory. As verified November 5, 2026, its president is Bola Ahmed Tinubu and its vice president is Kashim Shettima. Nigeria is distinct from its northern neighbor, the Republic of Niger. [11] [9] [8]

United Nations geographic subregion
Western Africa. [4]
Position
Coastal West Africa on the Gulf of Guinea; the Niger and Benue river systems cross the country. [4]
Precolonial historical breadth
Nok archaeology, Hausa states, Kanem–Bornu, the Oyo Empire, the Kingdom of Benin, Igbo societies and numerous other polities predate the modern federation.
Early nineteenth-century change
The Sokoto Caliphate expanded across large parts of northern Nigeria after the jihad led by Usman dan Fodio.
Atlantic trade and colonial expansion
Coastal commerce, Atlantic slavery, abolition-era politics and British colonial expansion shaped nineteenth-century economic and political change.
Colonial consolidation
Britain combined the Northern and Southern Nigeria protectorates in 1914, with Lagos included in the colonial administrative system.
Self-government and constitutional development
Constitutional reforms and regional political movements expanded participation before independence in 1960.
Independence
October 1, 1960, from British colonial rule.
Independence Day
October 1.
First prime minister
Abubakar Tafawa Balewa, head of government at independence.
First ceremonial governor-general
Nnamdi Azikiwe became governor-general in 1960; he later became Nigeria’s first president.
Republic established
October 1, 1963, when Nigeria adopted a republican constitution.
First president of the republic
Nnamdi Azikiwe (1963–1966).
1966 military coups
Coups in January and July 1966 ended the first civilian republic and intensified regional political tensions.
Nigerian Civil War
1967–1970, following the attempted secession of Biafra; the conflict brought extensive loss of life and humanitarian suffering.
Return to civilian politics, 1979
The Second Republic operated under a presidential constitution before a new military takeover in 1983.
June 12 election crisis, 1993
The annulment of the 1993 presidential election became a landmark crisis in Nigeria’s democratization.
Current democratic era
Civilian constitutional rule resumed on May 29, 1999, beginning the Fourth Republic. [11]
Current constitutional basis
Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria, 1999, as amended. [11]
2015 electoral milestone
An opposition presidential candidate, Muhammadu Buhari, defeated the incumbent Goodluck Jonathan in a nationally recognized transfer of power.
2023 inauguration
Bola Ahmed Tinubu assumed the presidency on May 29, 2023. [9] [16]
UN admission
October 7, 1960. [4]
National flag
Vertical green–white–green tricolor. [13]
National anthem
“Nigeria, We Hail Thee,” reinstated in May 2024; older references may incorrectly list “Arise, O Compatriots.” [13]
National motto
Unity and Faith, Peace and Progress.

Historical distinction: British colonial amalgamation (1914), independence (1960), establishment of the republic (1963) and the beginning of the Fourth Republic (1999) identify separate milestones. [11] [4]

Eastern highlands
Cameroon-border highland zones and the Mambilla Plateau include cooler elevations.
Highest peak
Chappal Waddi, commonly reported at approximately 2,419 meters above sea level.
Northern ecological zones
Sahel and Sudan savanna, progressively drier toward the northern border. [24]
Middle ecological zones
Guinea savanna and mosaic farming landscapes.
Southern ecological zones
Tropical forest, coastal mangroves, freshwater swamp forest and delta wetlands.
Climate
Mainly tropical; regional variations from equatorial coastal humidity to seasonally drier northern savanna and Sahel. [24]
Rainfall seasonality
Wet and dry seasons vary by location; monsoon circulation and the harmattan influence conditions.
Natural hazards
River flooding, coastal erosion, drought, heat, local landslides and changing rainfall patterns.
Environmental pressures
Deforestation, urban air and water pollution, soil degradation and petroleum pollution in parts of the Niger Delta. [16]
Federal subdivisions
36 states plus the Federal Capital Territory. [11] [8]
Local government framework
768 constitutionally listed local government areas within states plus six FCT area councils, usually described together as 774. [11] [12]
Six geopolitical zones
North Central, North East, North West, South East, South South and South West; widely used planning groupings, not additional constitutional states.
Major cities
Lagos, Kano, Ibadan, Abuja, Port Harcourt, Benin City, Kaduna, Enugu and other state capitals. [24]
Abuja capital transfer
Federal capital operations shifted from Lagos to Abuja in 1991.
Urban economy
Metropolitan Lagos is a leading commercial, port, cultural and financial hub. [24]
Internal migration
Movement from rural districts to expanding cities is shaped by work, education, family networks and security conditions. [16]
Coastal ports
Lagos-area ports and ports in the Niger Delta and eastern coast serve commerce and energy exports.
Niger River regional significance
The Niger basin connects Nigeria to several West African countries and supports agriculture, hydropower and ecological systems.
Water infrastructure
Major river basins support dams, irrigation and drinking-water supply, with uneven service coverage.

This Nigeria Quick Facts entry uses identified datasets and legal or institutional references from the World Bank, United Nations, WTO, Nigeria’s government and statistical bodies, Pew Research Center, IMF, UNESCO and UNDP. Each statistical observation is assigned its own reporting year; the verification date is not an observation year. Facts may change and national accounts may be revised.

The World Bank’s 2026 first-half growth assessment is not a final calendar-year result. [1] [3] [15] [4] [16]
