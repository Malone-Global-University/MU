Country Overview

Sudan, officially the Republic of the Sudan, is a northeastern African country extending from the Sahara and Sahel to the Red Sea and the middle Nile basin. Its capital is Khartoum, at the confluence of the Blue and White Nile rivers. Sudan became independent on January 1, 1956, and South Sudan separated in 2011. Civil war between the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF), which began in April 2023, has divided effective control of territory and caused mass displacement and an acute humanitarian emergency. In November 2026, Abdel Fattah al-Burhan heads the internationally recognized transitional authority, with Kamil Idris as prime minister; the RSF-backed coalition claims a rival administration in Nyala. Sudan’s economy includes agriculture, livestock and gold exports, while its historical heritage includes Kushite and Nubian archaeological sites. [5] [11] [9] [18] [7] [21]

Early state formation
The modern republic inherited the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan territorial administration, within which distinct regions had very different political histories. [5]
Nile Valley civilisations
Ancient Kushite states included Kerma, Napata and Meroe; archaeological sites remain central to Sudanese history. [21]
Medieval historical landscape
Christian Nubian kingdoms flourished along the Nile before Islam and Arabic expanded through successive centuries.
Early modern institutions
The Funj sultanate centred on Sennar and the Darfur sultanate were influential regional polities.
Nineteenth-century transformations
Egyptian-Ottoman rule, the Mahdist revolution and British-Egyptian reconquest reshaped the territory.
Anglo-Egyptian condominium
Joint British–Egyptian administration, formally established in 1899, ended at independence in 1956.
1960s–1980s transitions
Civilian, military and coalition governments alternated amid profound centre–periphery conflicts.
1989 regime change
Omar al-Bashir came to power through a military coup; his government continued until April 2019.
2019 political transition
Mass protest and military intervention ended Bashir’s rule, followed by an attempted civilian–military transitional arrangement. [9]
2021 military takeover
A military takeover disrupted the earlier civilian transition. [9] [10]
2023 war outbreak
Open conflict between the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) began April 15, 2023. [9] [25]
2025 institutional fragmentation
The RSF-aligned coalition announced a rival administration in 2025, deepening the division of authority. [10]
National continuity
Sudan remains a United Nations member state notwithstanding the conflict and fragmentation of effective administration. [4] [9]

People and Society

Population and identities
Ethnically and linguistically diverse country with Arab-identifying and numerous non-Arab African communities. [20]
Languages in widespread use
Sudanese Arabic varieties are widely spoken; numerous regional languages remain vital. [20]
Official languages — country profiles
Arabic and English are named official languages by the African Union Mission (September 2026) and SESRIC; wartime administrative use varies. [12] [13]
Arabic
Dominant language of national public communication and much administration. [20]
English
Used in education, international affairs and some state settings; official-working-language formulations have changed across constitutional periods. [20]
Selected indigenous languages
Beja, Fur, Nobiin and other Nubian languages, Masalit, Zaghawa and various Nuba Mountain languages. [20]
Official-language legal caveat
Historical constitutional texts differ; wartime legal uncertainty makes it inappropriate to treat any earlier multilingual clause as a freshly verified 2026 enactment. [9] [20]
Main religious tradition
Predominantly Sunni Islam, with diverse Sufi orders and practices. [20]
Other religious communities
Christian communities and indigenous religious traditions are also present. [20]
Religious share caution
No current representative nationwide census permits reliable 2026 percentage breakdowns. [20]
Religious and cultural diversity
Faith affiliations, customary practices and language patterns differ markedly by region. [20]
Major communities
Fur, Beja, Nubian, Nuba, Zaghawa, Masalit and many others, alongside diverse Sudanese Arab communities. [20]
Community percentages caution
Broad “Arab” and “non-Arab” estimates depend on identification and survey methods and may flatten internally diverse identities. [20]
Social organisation
Extended families, rural communities, urban neighbourhood networks and pastoral groups are influential social structures.
Traditional livelihoods
Farming, herding, fishing, trade and handicrafts. [7]
Urban life
Khartoum’s metropolitan region has historically been a cultural, commercial and educational hub; war severely damaged its services. [9] [20]
Cultural expressions
Music, oral literature, poetry, dance, textiles, cuisine and locally distinctive artistic traditions.
Common staple foods
Sorghum and millet dishes, flatbreads, beans and regional stews.
Education
Public schools and higher education have been disrupted by violence, displacement and damaged facilities. [18]
Healthcare
Medical access is severely constrained by destroyed facilities, shortages, insecurity and disrupted supply chains. [19]
People needing humanitarian assistance
33.7 million estimated for 2026 in UN humanitarian planning (UNICEF mid-year reporting). [18]
Children needing humanitarian assistance
17.3 million (UNICEF 2026 mid-year reporting). [18]
Internally displaced benchmark
Approximately 9.14 million internally displaced people recorded in UNICEF’s 2026 mid-year situation report; definitions and dates matter. [18]
Health assistance need
Approximately 21 million people needing health assistance in the WHO 2026 assessment. [19]
Conflict and food systems
Agricultural disruption, displacement, restricted trade and rainfall deficits undermine food security. [26] [20]
Protection concerns
UN reporting documents grave violence against civilians, including the 2025 RSF offensive on El Fasher. [17]
UNESCO World Heritage properties
Three inscribed properties as of November 2026. [21]
UNESCO cultural property — 2003
Gebel Barkal and the Sites of the Napatan Region. [21]
UNESCO cultural property — 2011
Archaeological Sites of the Island of Meroe. [21]
UNESCO natural property — 2016
Sanganeb Marine National Park and Dungonab Bay – Mukkawar Island Marine National Park. [21]
Heritage protection
Archaeological and cultural assets face conservation and security challenges in a country experiencing sustained conflict. [21] [16]

Government and Institutions

Form of state
Republic with military-dominated transitional institutions and rival de facto wartime administrations; not a functioning ordinary constitutional democracy. [9] [10]
Internationally recognized state framework
Transitional Sovereignty Council and the SAF-aligned “Government of Hope” administration. [9] [11]
Head of state
Abdel Fattah al-Burhan, president/chair of the Transitional Sovereignty Council and commander of the Sudanese Armed Forces. [11] [10]
Head of government
Prime Minister Kamil Idris, appointed in May 2025. [11] [9]
Prime minister’s November 2026 location
The Sudan News Agency reported the prime minister carrying out official meetings in Khartoum in early November 2026. [15]
Foreign affairs portfolio
Mohi El-Din Salem Ahmed Ibrahim in the Australian foreign ministry’s current head-of-government listing. [11]
Defense command
Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF), led by Abdel Fattah al-Burhan. [10]
Major rival armed force
Rapid Support Forces (RSF), led by Mohamed Hamdan Dagalo (“Hemedti”). [10]
RSF-aligned political organisation
Sudan Founding Alliance (Taasis), linked to the self-proclaimed Government of Peace and Unity. [10]
Rival administration seat
Nyala in South Darfur was identified as the base of the RSF-led parallel government. [9]
Rival administration’s claimed president
Mohamed Hamdan Dagalo, as head of its self-proclaimed presidential council (not internationally recognized as Sudan’s head of state). [10]
Rival administration’s claimed prime minister
Mohammed Hassan Osman al-Taishi was appointed by the RSF-aligned coalition in 2025; status remains subordinate to the non-recognized rival authority. [10]
International recognition caveat
UN, African Union and other partners have rejected the declaration of a competing Sudanese government. [10] [27]
Current sovereignty
Sudan remains one sovereign UN member state despite competing territorial administrations. [4] [27]
National parliament
No ordinary nationally elected legislature exercising effective authority throughout the country under wartime conditions. [9]
Constitutional status
Transitional and contested; the 2019 civilian–military constitutional framework was disrupted by the 2021 takeover and subsequent conflict. [9] [10]
National elections
No verified completed nationwide democratic election establishing a post-war government at the November 5, 2026 cutoff. [9]
Administrative divisions
Eighteen states, although service delivery and effective oversight are fragmented by conflict. [20] [9]
Darfur states
North Darfur, South Darfur, East Darfur, West Darfur and Central Darfur. [20]
Kordofan states
North Kordofan, South Kordofan and West Kordofan. [20]
Central Nile states
Khartoum, Gezira, White Nile, Blue Nile and Sennar. [20]
Northern and eastern states
Northern, River Nile, Red Sea, Kassala and Gedaref. [20]
Local governance
State and local administrative institutions vary by military control, displacement, staff availability and service conditions. [9]
Judicial and legal administration
Courts and legal services have suffered wartime interruption and uneven effective jurisdiction. [9] [20]
Protection of civilians
UN reporting identifies attacks on noncombatants, civilian infrastructure and humanitarian operations. [16] [17]
Conflict frontlines — October 2026
North and South Kordofan and Blue Nile were identified as major active areas in a UN Security Council briefing. [16]
Capital military situation
SAF regained Khartoum in 2025, but many public services and buildings remained damaged in 2026. [25] [20]
Peace and civilian transition
International diplomatic efforts emphasize ceasefire, humanitarian access, civilian participation, and preservation of national unity. [27]
Civil society
Sudanese civilian coalitions, professional groups, women’s networks and local committees remain important despite repression and displacement. [9]
Political reporting caution
Territorial control, appointments and institutional operation can change quickly; this snapshot is dated November 5, 2026. [9] [16]

Governance status: Al-Burhan and Idris are identified by diplomatic and European institutional references as leaders of the recognized transitional government; the RSF-backed rival entity is self-proclaimed and not internationally recognized. “Head of state” does not imply a popularly elected presidency. Refugees and displaced people

Civilian protection and accountability

Food security risks

World Heritage engagement

Economic integration obstacles

Data verification date
November 5, 2026; humanitarian, political and economic conditions are especially volatile. [16] [25]
