Country Overview

Afghanistan is a landlocked Asian state centered on the Hindu Kush mountain system, with Kabul as its capital. Its history links the Iranian plateau, Central Asia and South Asia. The Taliban have exercised de facto national power since August 2021, calling their administration the Islamic Emirate of Afghanistan; this designation should not be confused with Afghanistan’s continuing membership in the United Nations or universal recognition of the Taliban government. The country faces deep humanitarian and development pressures, alongside major archaeological and cultural heritage at Bamiyan and Jam. [3] [13] [17] [9] [15]

Identity and State Formation

Common international name
Afghanistan. [3]
Name used by de facto authorities
Islamic Emirate of Afghanistan. This is the Taliban administration’s self-designation; it should not be conflated with universal international diplomatic recognition. [13] [12]
Former republic-era name
Islamic Republic of Afghanistan, the designation of the constitutional system established after 2001 and displaced in August 2021. [14] [13]
Continent
Asia. The United Nations places Afghanistan in Southern Asia for statistical purposes; political and historical geography also frequently links it to Central and Southwest Asia. [3]
Capital
Kabul. [3]
Largest city
Kabul. [3]
Historical sovereignty milestone
19 August 1919 is observed historically as Afghan Independence Day, marking the end of British control over Afghan external affairs after the Third Anglo-Afghan War.
Modern state background
The Durrani dynasty emerged in 1747 under Ahmad Shah Durrani; subsequent state formation involved changing empires, dynasties, constitutional experiments and regional interests.
Monarchy ended
1973, when Mohammad Daoud Khan proclaimed a republic after a coup that ended the reign of Zahir Shah.
Soviet war period
Soviet military intervention began in December 1979; withdrawal of Soviet troops was completed in February 1989.
First Taliban government
The Taliban controlled Kabul from 1996 until the government’s overthrow in 2001. [13]
Republic-era constitution
A new constitution entered into force in 2004; it is a historical constitutional reference rather than the operative basis of Taliban administration in 2026. [14] [19]
Return of Taliban control
15 August 2021: Taliban forces entered Kabul as the Islamic Republic’s government collapsed. [5] [13]
Governing status, November 2026
Taliban de facto authorities exercise central governing power; broad diplomatic recognition of the government is absent, despite recognition by Russia and contacts with other governments. [13] [12]
Nationality / demonym
Afghan; “Afghani” denotes the currency. [3]
ISO 3166 codes
AF (alpha-2); AFG (alpha-3). [3]
Currency abbreviation
AFN (Afghan afghani). [3]
International telephone code
+93.
Internet country domain
.af.
Time zone
Afghanistan Time (AFT), UTC+04:30; standard time is used year-round.
Traditional flag / recent state symbols
The de facto Taliban administration uses a white banner with an Islamic declaration of faith; the former republic used a black-red-green tricolor with an emblem. Symbols can indicate opposing political claims. [14] [13]
Former republic-state flag rule
Article 19 of the 2004 Constitution specified a black, red and green vertical tricolor. That historical rule should not be represented as a current Taliban law. [14]
Official language precedent
Pashto and Dari were specified as the state’s official languages by Article 16 of the 2004 Constitution; both remain major languages in public life. [14]
Religious and legal context
The de facto authorities apply their interpretation of Islamic law and decrees. No elected constitutional republic or functioning national legislature operates as the pre-2021 system did. [19] [13]

Geography and Population

Location
Landlocked at the crossroads of South Asia, Central Asia and the Iranian plateau. [3]
Land borders
Pakistan, Iran, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan, Tajikistan and China (six states).
Eastern and southern neighbor
Pakistan; the frontier includes mountainous corridors and cross-border Pashtun populations.
Western neighbor
Iran.
Northern neighbors
Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan and Tajikistan.
Northeastern neighbor
China, at the eastern end of the narrow Wakhan Corridor.
Total surface area (UN convention)
652,864 km²; the UN country profile uses a surface-area series and flags its observation-year footnotes. [3]
Land area (World Bank / FAOSTAT)
652,230 km², World Bank land-area series for 2024. Not identical to the UN surface-area measure. [4] [3]
Coastline
None; Afghanistan has no seaport.
Landscape
The Hindu Kush mountains dominate central and northeastern Afghanistan; broad valleys, northern plains and deserts occupy other regions.
Highest peak
Noshaq (Noshakh), approximately 7,492 m, in the Hindu Kush near the northeastern border.
Important mountain systems
Hindu Kush and adjoining ranges; foothills and upland valleys complicate transportation.
Wakhan Corridor
Long narrow northeastern panhandle linking Afghanistan to China and separating Tajikistan from Pakistan across part of the region.
Major river systems
Amu Darya along portions of the north; Helmand River in the southwest; Hari Rud (Harirud) in the west; Kabul River flowing toward the Indus system.
Notable inland basin
Helmand–Sistan basin, characterized by endorheic drainage and sensitive wetland-water relationships.
Climate
Generally arid to semi-arid, with cold highland winters, hot lowland summers and strong regional differences.
Major environmental hazards
Droughts, floods, destructive earthquakes, landslides and severe winter conditions. [8] [9]
Provinces
34 provinces (wilayat), conventionally used in administrative geography.
Principal cities
Kabul, Kandahar, Herat, Mazar-i-Sharif, Jalalabad and Kunduz.
Capital population
UN profile lists approximately 4.114 million for Kabul, but the cited observation is 2019 rather than a 2025 city count. [3]
Population, 2025
43,844,111, the 2025 World Bank demographic estimate; not a census enumeration. [1]
UN population projection, 2025
About 43.844 million; UN country-profile footnote identifies this as a medium-fertility projection. [3]
Population growth, 2025
Approximately 2.8% in the World Bank country-indicator series; estimates vary by population-model vintage. [1]
Statistical census limitation
Population totals are estimates; no recent complete, uniformly documented national census underpins current 2025 counts. [3] [20]
Young population
The UN country profile estimates about 42.6% under age 15 in its 2025 projected population. [3]
Fertility indicator
UN profile projects about 4.7 births per woman for 2025 under its stated demographic scenario. [3]
Rural/urban pattern
Historically predominantly rural; the UN profile’s 25.8% urban figure is marked as 2019 and must not be misrepresented as a 2025 observation. [3]
Population density
UN profile reports approximately 67.5 people per km² for 2025, using its population and area convention. [3]
Water and irrigation
Irrigated farming depends heavily on river and groundwater systems; drought poses severe risks to crops, livestock and settlements. [8] [5]
Forest area
About 1.9% of total land area in the World Bank 2024 series. [1]
Electricity access
87.8% of the population in the World Bank 2024 modeled access indicator; access does not imply uninterrupted supply. [1]
Internet use
16% of people in 2024 in the World Bank country indicator. [1]

Economy and International Trade

Economic category
Low-income economy with a large agricultural and informal sector, aid-reliant public services and trade-finance constraints. [5]
Currency
Afghan afghani (AFN). [3]
Central bank
Da Afghanistan Bank, Afghanistan’s central bank; its access to international reserves and financial infrastructure has been constrained since 2021. [5]
Nominal GDP, 2024 (World Bank WDI)
Approximately US$17.78 billion (current U.S. dollars); later World Bank outlook and UN releases can reflect different revisions and bases. [2] [3]
GDP per capita, 2024 (World Bank WDI)
Approximately US$416.9 (current U.S. dollars). [1]
Real GDP growth, 2024 (WDI)
1.9% in the World Bank country indicator; later outlook updates use other estimates. [1] [5]
2025 updated World Bank growth estimate
World Bank’s May 26, 2026 Development Update estimated 4.8% real GDP growth for 2025, compared with a 4.3% earlier April 2026 country-overview estimate; estimates are subject to revision. [22] [5]
Economy since 2021
Severe 2021–22 contraction followed by partial recovery; per-capita welfare is constrained by rapid population growth, returnee inflows and low investment. [5]
Agriculture
Wheat, vegetables, grapes, pomegranates, nuts, livestock, fruit and irrigated regional crops support rural livelihoods. [5] [6]
Major traditional cash crops
Dried fruit, nuts, grapes, saffron and other high-value horticultural exports. [6]
Saffron
A high-value agricultural export associated especially with cultivation in western Afghanistan. [6]
Livestock and textiles
Sheep, goats, animal products, wool and carpets remain economically and culturally relevant. [6] [5]
Mining potential
Coal, talc, marble, copper, iron ore and lithium-bearing resources have attracted interest; mineral endowment is not equivalent to proven commercially producing reserves. [5]
Construction and services
Construction, commerce, small-scale processing, transportation and services contributed to the post-2021 partial recovery. [5]
Banking constraints
Fragile liquidity, access to international payments and limitations on private credit remain obstacles. [5]
2024 consumer-price inflation
Annual average CPI change of approximately −6.6% in World Bank WDI (deflation). [1]
March 2026 inflation
World Bank Economic Monitor reports 7.6% year-on-year headline inflation in March 2026; this is a monthly year-on-year measure, not the 2024 calendar-year average. [6]
Unemployment indicator, 2025
13.4% of the modeled labor force in the World Bank/ILO unemployment estimate; actual employment distress may be broader. [1]
Women’s economic participation
Severe restrictions on women’s employment and education substantially constrain human capital and future growth. [5] [11]
International assistance
UN agencies and partner institutions deliver humanitarian and basic services while restrictions and reductions in aid complicate financing. [9] [5]
Trade access
Landlocked trade corridors run through Pakistan, Iran and Central Asia; border interruptions can redirect flows and raise costs. [6] [7]
Customs-based merchandise exports, FY2025
Approximately US$1.8 billion cumulatively for Afghanistan FY2025 as reported in World Bank March 2026 monitoring; not total goods-and-services exports. [6]
Food-group share of FY2025 goods exports
Approximately 83% in the World Bank March 2026 monitoring aggregate; product composition varies across data systems. [6]
Leading FY2025 goods-export market
India, approximately 43.6% of cumulatively reported export value. [6]
Second-leading FY2025 goods-export market
Pakistan, approximately 22.8% of cumulatively reported export value. [6]
Third-leading FY2025 goods-export market
Uzbekistan, approximately 8.0% of cumulatively reported export value. [6]
Primary imported products
Petroleum and fuels, foodstuffs, capital machinery, construction inputs, textiles, chemicals and consumer goods. [7]
Leading import origin, FY2025-to-December 2025
Iran, approximately 30.3% of customs-reported imports through the period reviewed in the December 2025 World Bank Economic Monitor. [7]
Second import origin, FY2025-to-December 2025
United Arab Emirates, approximately 18.5% in the same World Bank series. [7]
Economic data caveat
Do not mix 2019 UN Comtrade data shown on some WTO summary pages with 2025 Afghan customs monitoring or UN balance-of-payments statistics. [6] [7]

People and Society

Major languages
Dari (Afghan Persian) and Pashto; Uzbek, Turkmen, Hazaragi, Balochi, Pashayi, Nuristani and Pamiri languages are among other languages in use. [14]
Republic-era language framework
Article 16 of the 2004 Constitution identified Pashto and Dari as official and provided for additional official status of certain languages in qualifying localities; it is a historical legal source in 2026. [14]
Language diversity
Multilingual communities, regional dialects and varying literacy complicate standardized national-language surveys. [14]
Ethnolinguistic communities
Pashtuns, Tajiks, Hazaras, Uzbeks, Turkmens, Baloch, Nuristanis and other groups; no definitive contemporary ethnic-percentage census is available. [19] [3]
Religion
Islam is followed by the overwhelming majority of the population; Sunni and Shia traditions are both present. [19]
Estimated Sunni Muslim share
Approximately 84.7–89.7% in the cited religious-freedom estimates; not a 2026 census result. [19]
Estimated Shia Muslim share
Approximately 10–15% in the cited religious-freedom estimates; these ranges are uncertain. [19]
Other religious communities
Small Hindu, Sikh, Ahmadiyya and other communities have existed; displacement and restrictions make current counts unreliable. [19]
Religion and state
The Taliban de facto authorities enforce a restrictive interpretation of Islamic law; legal protections and religious freedom are contested. [19] [10]
Life expectancy at birth
About 66.3 years, 2024 World Bank estimate. [1]
Women’s post-primary education, 2026
Taliban restrictions bar girls from general secondary education beyond grade six and women from university; UNESCO reports this policy remains in place in 2026. [11]
Girls excluded from secondary education
UNESCO estimated roughly 2.4 million Afghan girls excluded from secondary education as of August 2026. [11]
Primary education
Primary schools operate, but access and quality vary because of poverty, displacement, staffing and infrastructure. [9]
University sector
Kabul University, Herat University, Balkh University and other institutions have been major centers; women are barred from university attendance under de facto restrictions. [11]
Women’s workplace restrictions
Many types of paid employment, especially public or NGO-facing roles, are restricted for women; exceptions and local implementation vary. [11] [10]
Freedom of movement and clothing
The UN documented arrests, detentions and threats targeting women and girls for alleged dress-code violations in 2026. [10]
Humanitarian need, 2026
21.9 million people estimated to require humanitarian assistance under the UN-led 2026 planning framework. [9] [8]
Children in humanitarian need, 2026
UNICEF estimated about 11.6 million children among people needing help. [9]
Health priorities
Maternal and child care, malnutrition treatment, immunization, communicable disease response and essential rural care. [9] [8]
Food security
Drought, poverty, displaced populations and market disruptions expose households to acute food insecurity. [8] [5]
Acute food insecurity, winter 2026–27
An October 16, 2026 FAO/WFP statement projects approximately 19.2 million people facing IPC Phase 3 or worse in November 2026–April 2027, including approximately five million at emergency level. This later seasonal forecast differs from the original 2026 humanitarian-plan sector estimates. [21] [8]
Displacement and returns
Large return movements from Pakistan and Iran have added pressure to housing, work, services and host communities. [5] [9]
Water and sanitation
Reliable water, sanitation and hygiene services remain major humanitarian and development needs. [8] [9]
Main grain staples
Wheat is prominent; breads and rice accompany regional dishes, dairy, pulses and seasonal vegetables.
Known traditional dishes
Kabuli (Qabili) palaw, mantu, ashak, bolani and various kebabs, with significant regional variation.
Traditional sports
Buzkashi (horseback sport) has a long cultural history; cricket, football and other sports also have important followings.
Cultural craft traditions
Carpet weaving, embroidery, ceramics, metalwork, miniature painting and manuscript arts are longstanding forms. [15]
Music and oral heritage
Regional song, instruments, storytelling and poetry reflect Persianate, Turkic and Pashtun heritage, among others.
Heritage access and risks
Conflict, illicit traffic, environmental hazards and conservation funding challenges threaten archaeological and architectural sites. [15] [16]

Government and Institutions

Current effective regime
Taliban de facto administration calling itself the Islamic Emirate of Afghanistan; it exercises authority without a national democratic electoral mandate. [13] [12]
Leader of Taliban
Hibatullah (Haybatullah) Akhundzada, identified as the supreme or overall Taliban leader. [12]
Acting prime minister
Mohammad Hassan Akhund, according to the U.S. World Leaders September 2026 roster. [12]
Acting deputy prime minister
Abdul Ghani Baradar (economic portfolio), listed by the September 2026 roster. [12]
Additional acting deputy prime minister
Abdul Salam Hanafi, listed by the September 2026 roster. [12]
Acting minister of foreign affairs
Amir Khan Muttaqi, as listed in September 2026. [12]
Acting interior minister
Sirajuddin Haqqani, as listed in September 2026. [12]
Acting minister of defense
Mohammad Yaqoob (Yaqoub), as listed in September 2026. [12]
Executive structure
Religious leadership and appointed ministers dominate governance; consultation and provincial administrative arrangements exist but differ sharply from an elected parliamentary system. [13] [19]
National parliament status
The pre-2021 bicameral National Assembly does not operate as an elected legislature under Taliban de facto rule. [13] [14]
Constitution status
The 2004 Islamic Republic constitution no longer governs operationally; the Taliban use decrees and their interpretation of sharia rather than the former constitutional arrangements. [19] [14]
National electoral timetable
No competitive national parliamentary or presidential elections under the de facto Taliban administration as of the edition date. [13]
De facto administrative divisions
34 provinces administered by governors and subnational authorities within a centralized system. [13]
Local judicial structure
De facto courts operate under Taliban interpretation of Islamic jurisprudence, not the pre-2021 republic’s independent constitutional-court system. [19]
De facto ministry for morality enforcement
Ministry for Promotion of Virtue and Prevention of Vice carries out and enforces aspects of the Taliban’s social-control policies. [12] [10]
Women in national political administration
Women’s participation in senior public governance has been severely curtailed since 2021. [11] [10]
Religious minorities and civil rights
Religious practice, expression and participation face restrictions under de facto rule. [19] [10]
International recognition status
The Russian Federation formally recognized the Taliban government in July 2025; October 2026 analysis described it as the only state to have done so. [13]
U.S. recognition position
The United States does not recognize the caretaker Taliban government, according to the September 2026 U.S. government leadership roster. [12]
Diplomatic representation caveat
Diplomatic contacts, embassy operations and acceptance of envoys do not necessarily constitute legal recognition of a government. [13]
UN representation caveat
Afghanistan’s UN membership continues, but UN member-state status is distinct from acceptance of a Taliban claim to represent it. [17] [13]
Human-rights assessments
UN and independent monitors document severe restrictions affecting women, girls and other vulnerable populations. [10] [11]
Government-source methodology
Names and portfolios are time-sensitive; the roster source was updated September 3, 2026 and should be checked again on later republication. [12]

International Profile

United Nations member since
19 November 1946, admitted during the Afghan monarchy. [17]
World Trade Organization accession
29 July 2016; WTO member status is a property of Afghanistan as a state, not a statement about Taliban recognition. [18]
UNESCO World Heritage Convention
Ratified 20 March 1979. [15]
UNESCO World Heritage properties
Two inscribed World Heritage properties, both cultural, in Afghanistan. [15]
Cultural Landscape and Archaeological Remains of the Bamiyan Valley
Inscribed in 2003; includes remains associated with the giant Buddhas destroyed in 2001 and surrounding archaeological landscape. [15]
Minaret and Archaeological Remains of Jam
Inscribed in 2002; a medieval Ghurid-period heritage site in mountainous western Afghanistan. [15]
UNESCO danger-list status
Both inscribed World Heritage properties were retained on the List of World Heritage in Danger in the 2026 UNESCO decision. [16]
Herat, Balkh and other cultural locations
Important historical cities and sites include Herat and Balkh, but tentative-list candidates are not currently counted as inscribed World Heritage properties. [15]
Regional trade diplomacy
Relationships with Pakistan, Iran, India, China and Central Asian countries shape trade transit and humanitarian logistics. [6] [13]
Most important FY2025 export destination
India leads recorded customs exports by value in World Bank monitoring. [6]
Second major export destination
Pakistan, despite border closure disruptions. [6]
International humanitarian partners
UNICEF, other UN agencies, national NGOs and international NGOs continue to provide services across Afghanistan. [9] [8]
International aid needs, 2026
The United Nations-led 2026 plan sought US$1.71 billion to assist a planned 17.5 million people; requirements are targets, not funding secured or delivered. [8]
Regional migration
Returns and deportations from neighboring states have substantially affected population estimates and social-service demand. [5] [9]
International banking linkages
Access to external banking and finance remains impaired despite ongoing regional economic exchange. [5]
International diplomatic recognition
As of October 2026, Russia was described as the only country that had formally recognized the Taliban-led government; other partners engage at varying levels. [13]
International development agenda
Food security, primary education, basic health care, protection, disaster resilience, employment and safe water are central priorities. [8] [9]
International statistical caveat
UNdata, the World Bank, trade customs reports and relief agencies employ different years, population denominators and concepts; do not treat unrelated indicators as a single synchronous snapshot. [3] [1] [6] [8]

Sources and Methodology

Editorial cutoff: November 6, 2026 (Pacific Time). This profile uses World Bank 2025 population estimates, 2024 nominal GDP and selected indicators, UN geographic tables, World Bank Afghan FY2025 merchandise-export monitoring published in March 2026, UN-led humanitarian planning for 2026, a September 2026 leadership roster, October 2026 analysis of recognition, and UNESCO heritage information. No indicator is silently relabeled as a 2026 census measurement or synchronized with an unrelated statistical series.

Political designation: The names “Afghanistan,” “Islamic Republic of Afghanistan” and “Islamic Emirate of Afghanistan” are used with their relevant international, historical, or de facto context. Continuity of the Afghan state and its UN membership does not imply diplomatic recognition of the current de facto rulers. Legal provisions quoted from the 2004 Constitution describe the prior republic-era legal text, not current enforcement.

Numerical comparability: The UN’s surface-area and World Bank’s land-area series are distinct; GDP data reflect different source vintages; estimated population and humanitarian planning denominators cannot be treated as one census; 2025 Afghan customs merchandise exports are not comparable to 2019 WTO/Comtrade data or balance-of-payments goods-and-services exports. The UNdata country profile’s “2025” column contains older observation years as indicated by individual footnotes. Percentages are rounded and may not sum to 100.
