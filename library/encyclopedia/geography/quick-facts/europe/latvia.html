Country Overview

Latvia is a Baltic state on the eastern shore of the Baltic Sea, classified by the United Nations in Northern Europe. Its modern republic was proclaimed on 18 November 1918. Following Soviet occupation and annexation during the Second World War, Latvia restored its independence in 1990–1991. Riga is the capital, and the country belongs to the European Union, NATO, euro area and Schengen area. [6]

Livonian historical context
Finnic-speaking Livonian communities inhabited parts of the territory before later political consolidation and remain an important cultural heritage.
Medieval Baltic political orders
German crusading and ecclesiastical authorities shaped medieval Livonia, but those institutions were not the modern Latvian republic.
Livonian Confederation
Medieval religious orders, bishoprics, and towns operated within a larger political mosaic on the eastern Baltic coast.
Riga as Hanseatic city
Medieval Riga participated in northern European trading networks, helping establish the city's commercial significance.
Polish-Lithuanian period
Following the collapse of medieval Livonian authority, portions of modern Latvia came under the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth.
Swedish-era Vidzeme
Part of present-day Latvia was governed by Sweden in the seventeenth century, while other territories followed different political paths.
Duchy of Courland
The Duchy of Courland and Semigallia was a distinct early modern polity, not equivalent to the modern national territory.
Russian imperial incorporation
By the late eighteenth century, the lands of modern Latvia had largely passed into the Russian Empire through different annexations.
Latvian national awakening
Language, education, cultural organization, and national political consciousness developed in the nineteenth century under imperial rule.
First World War disruption
War, displacement, and military occupation accelerated political change and the emergence of independence movements.
State proclamation 1918
The Republic of Latvia was proclaimed on November 18, 1918 in the turmoil surrounding the First World War's end.
War of Independence
The 1918–1920 conflicts involved multiple armies and political claims before Latvian independence was consolidated.
1920 peace treaty
The 1920 peace agreement with Soviet Russia recognized Latvian independence in the postwar international settlement.
Satversme adoption
Latvia's constitution was adopted in 1922, establishing a republican parliamentary framework later restored after Soviet rule.
Interwar parliamentary period
The first republic experienced multiparty politics and economic change before an authoritarian domestic turn in the 1930s.
Ulmanis regime
Kārlis Ulmanis established authoritarian rule in 1934, ending the prior competitive parliamentary system until World War II.
Soviet occupation 1940
Soviet forces occupied and annexed Latvia in 1940, disrupting the sovereign republic and leading to repression and deportations.
Nazi occupation 1941–1944/45
Nazi German occupation included the Holocaust and mass violence; the historical record requires acknowledgment of victims and collaborators.
Second Soviet occupation
Soviet control was reimposed in 1944–1945 and continued through late twentieth-century political and social transformation.
Postwar deportations
Forced deportations and state repression affected Latvian communities, including major deportation operations in 1941 and 1949.
Singing Revolution
Civic demonstrations, cultural mobilization, and independence organizations supported Baltic political change during the late Soviet period.
Baltic Way demonstration
The 1989 human chain linking Baltic capitals symbolized demands for freedom and the rejection of the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact's consequences.
May 1990 declaration
On May 4, 1990, Latvia adopted a declaration restoring independence with a transition period during Soviet rule.
August 1991 restoration
Full effective independence was asserted in August 1991 amid the failed Soviet coup and followed by broad diplomatic recognition.
Legal continuity doctrine
Latvia treats post-1991 independence as restoration of the prewar republic rather than the establishment of an unrelated new state.
1993 constitutional resumption
The Satversme resumed full effect during the post-independence transition and has been amended under constitutional procedures.
National commemorations
November 18 marks the proclamation of the republic, while May 4 commemorates the restoration declaration; the two serve distinct purposes.

Historical context: Latvian state formation followed centuries under Baltic German, Polish-Lithuanian, Swedish and Russian imperial rule. Independence was declared in 1918 and defended during the Latvian War of Independence. The republic was occupied by the Soviet Union in 1940, by Nazi Germany in 1941–1944/45 and again by the Soviet Union until the restoration of state independence in 1991. Latvia entered the European Union and NATO in 2004 and adopted the euro in 2014. [9]

Continuity rather than a new state. The modern republic began in 1918 but experienced an interruption of sovereignty under Soviet occupation from 1940. The restoration process began formally in May 1990 and was consolidated after the events of August 1991. A historically grounded account must also include the 1922 Satversme, the interwar authoritarian period, Nazi occupation and the Holocaust, Soviet deportations, and the Baltic movement for independence. The legal-continuity doctrine distinguishes Latvia from states treated as entirely new successors to the Soviet Union.

Multiple commemorative dates. November 18 celebrates the 1918 proclamation, May 4 the 1990 restoration declaration, and August 21 an additional milestone in securing full independence. None of these dates can replace the others without changing what is being commemorated. Latvia's location at the Baltic commercial and political crossroads has shaped its cultural life for centuries.

A lowland Baltic state. Latvia combines the Baltic coast, the Gulf of Riga, large forests, river basins, farmland, peatlands, and inland lakes. Riga is the largest economic and population center, but Latvia's historic regions have differing urban systems and cultural identities. Long-term decline and ageing in some areas contrast with population growth in parts of the capital region, which complicates any single nationwide description of demographic change.

Changing population methodology. The CSB introduced a revised administrative-register-based system in 2025 and reported approximately 1.845 million residents at the start of 2026. Language and citizenship require precision. Latvian is the state language, while Russian and other languages are part of everyday community life. Latvian citizenship, ethnic identification, use of a minority language, and the special noncitizen status are separate legal or demographic variables. Educational language policy has evolved, and a publication should refer to the school level and year rather than treat all schools as having identical arrangements.

Religious and cultural diversity. Lutheran, Catholic, Orthodox, Old Believer, and secular traditions vary by region and history, and the country's Jewish heritage must be described alongside wartime destruction. Contemporary religion surveys and church records do not form a complete standardized population census. Music festivals and the Latvian Song and Dance Celebration help connect regional cultures to national civic life.

Integration came in stages. Latvia regained independence in 1991, joined NATO and the European Union in 2004, entered the Schengen area in 2007, and adopted the euro in 2014. Each step has separate legal consequences for territorial defense, market regulation, travel, and monetary policy. The country is also embedded in Nordic–Baltic partnerships and multilateral institutions wider than the EU.

Security, logistics, and heritage. Latvia's borders with Russia and Belarus have acquired major security significance. The ports, energy networks, digital infrastructure, and planned north–south rail connections influence international economic resilience. Cultural diplomacy also connects the country with the UNESCO system, including Riga, Kuldīga, and the transnational Struve Geodetic Arc. [11]
