Country Overview

Belgium is a densely populated Western European constitutional monarchy and federal parliamentary democracy, situated between the North Sea, France, Luxembourg, Germany and the Netherlands. Brussels is the federal capital and a major seat of European Union and NATO institutions. A Dutch-speaking north, a predominantly French-speaking south, a constitutionally bilingual capital region and a smaller German-speaking community underpin an intricate system of linguistic and territorial autonomy. Belgium is internationally connected through the Port of Antwerp-Bruges, pharmaceutical and chemical industries, European transport networks, specialized manufacturing, finance, and knowledge-intensive services.

The present Belgian state emerged from the revolution of 1830 and the Constitution of 1831. Its past also includes early industrialization, the two world wars, European integration, and Belgian involvement in the colonization of Central Africa, subjects that remain important to historical understanding and public debate. Federal reforms gradually transformed a unitary monarchy into a multilevel constitutional system. Pre-Roman and Roman history
Celtic-speaking communities and Roman provinces left urban, road and settlement legacies; the territorial boundaries of today’s Belgium were not those of a distinct Roman nation-state.
Medieval principalities
Flanders, Brabant, Liège, Hainaut and other principalities developed different legal, commercial and political traditions before later dynastic consolidation.
Burgundian Netherlands
Burgundian rule in the fifteenth century coordinated institutions across numerous Low Countries territories while preserving substantial urban and provincial privileges.
Habsburg Netherlands
Habsburg governance and the sixteenth-century revolt divided the northern and southern Low Countries, with the southern territories undergoing a different confessional and political trajectory.
Austrian and French periods
Eighteenth-century Habsburg rule and revolutionary and Napoleonic French administration introduced reforms that influenced the subsequent territorial and legal order.
United Kingdom of the Netherlands
The territory was joined with the northern Netherlands after 1815; disputes about religious interests, language and political representation contributed to the 1830 revolution.
1830 Belgian Revolution
Insurrection and political negotiations led to the declaration of independence; international diplomacy helped establish the new state’s recognized position.
1831 constitutional monarchy
The Constitution established a constitutional monarchy with parliamentary institutions; Leopold I took the oath on July 21, 1831.
1839 Treaty of London
Great-power agreements confirmed Belgian independence and addressed neutrality and territorial arrangements in a changing European balance.
Nineteenth-century industrialization
Coal, iron, steel, railways and textile production helped Belgium become an early continental industrial center, with important labor and urban social changes.
Expansion of political rights
Property-based voting gradually gave way to broader suffrage, with major twentieth-century reforms affecting universal suffrage and women’s full electoral participation.
Congo Free State, 1885–1908
King Leopold II held personal sovereignty over the Congo Free State, where coercive extraction and atrocities inflicted severe suffering on African communities. [14]
Belgian Congo, 1908–1960
Belgium annexed the Congo Free State in 1908; colonial administration continued until Congolese independence in 1960, leaving enduring political and ethical questions. [14]
Rwanda-Urundi colonial administration
Belgian control in the twentieth century preceded the separate independence of Rwanda and Burundi in 1962; the history is distinct from Belgian Congo. [14]
First World War
Germany invaded neutral Belgium in 1914; occupation, military operations and civilian suffering became central to national and international war memory.
Second World War
Germany occupied Belgium from 1940 to 1944; resistance, collaboration, deportation and liberation form different chapters of public historical inquiry.
Benelux cooperation
Economic cooperation with the Netherlands and Luxembourg anticipated broader European integration, though Benelux and the EU remain legally distinct organizations.
Federalization process
Constitutional reforms beginning in 1970 and continuing through later decades transferred powers to language communities and regions, changing the form of the state.
Contemporary constitutional identity
Belgium remains one sovereign state with a shared monarchy and federal institutions, despite strong regional autonomy and recurrent disputes over responsibilities.
National emblems
The black-yellow-red tricolor, national coat of arms and Brabançonne anthem are part of the state’s official symbolic heritage.

Belgian state formation involved constitutional liberalism and negotiated international recognition, but it was also shaped by language-based inequalities and a nineteenth-century pattern of industrial development concentrated in particular regions. Colonial history cannot be separated from King Leopold II’s personal control of the Congo Free State before 1908 and the subsequent Belgian colonial administration. Modern Belgian identity is plural rather than reducible to a single ethnic or linguistic tradition.

Historical context: Belgium’s early modern territory formed part of several larger European polities. The 1830 revolution created a separate state; later history includes industrialization, imperial rule over Congo, two world wars, and federal reforms responding to linguistic and regional political demands. Founding in 1830 and the 1831 constitutional monarchy are distinct dates. [6]

Geographic setting
Borders France, the Netherlands, Germany, and Luxembourg; has a short North Sea coastline
Physical features
Low coastal plain, central plateau, Ardennes uplands, Scheldt and Meuse river systems
Major urban centers
Brussels, Antwerp, Ghent, Charleroi, and Liège
North Sea location
A short maritime coastline and major inland navigation routes support international shipping, offshore activities, tourism and flood-management infrastructure.
Coastal plain and polders
Low elevations and reclaimed land along the coast require pumping, drainage, dunes and engineered defenses against storms and rising water.
Central plateau
A mixed landscape of towns, agriculture, waterways and transport infrastructure occupies much of the transitional zone between coastal lowlands and southern uplands.
Ardennes region
Wooded hills, deeply cut valleys and relatively sparse settlements characterize a large portion of southeastern Belgium and influence land use and tourism.
High Fens plateau
The Hautes Fagnes–Hohes Venn landscape contains wetland habitats, protected peat environments and some of the state’s highest upland terrain.
Highest point
Signal de Botrange reaches approximately 694 meters above sea level, illustrating Belgium’s modest relief compared with Alpine countries.
Scheldt basin
The Scheldt and its tributaries are central to Antwerp’s maritime access and a network of industrial, agricultural and urban watersheds.
Meuse basin
The Meuse valley connects Liège, Namur and industrial settlements; its tributaries and floodplain require river-management cooperation.
Canal networks
Inland waterways connect major ports, industrial zones and neighboring countries, with locks and canals supporting freight transport.
Neighboring states
Belgium borders the Netherlands, Germany, Luxembourg and France; freight, labor and passenger movements cross the surrounding EU region.
Three territorial regions
The Flemish Region, Walloon Region and Brussels-Capital Region govern different sets of territorial policy responsibilities under federal constitutional arrangements.
Flemish provinces
Antwerp, Limburg, East Flanders, West Flanders and Flemish Brabant form the five provinces of the Flemish Region.
Walloon provinces
Hainaut, Liège, Luxembourg, Namur and Walloon Brabant form the five provinces of the Walloon Region.
Brussels-Capital territory
Brussels-Capital consists of nineteen municipalities and does not form part of either the Flemish or Walloon territorial region.
Major urban areas
Brussels, Antwerp, Ghent, Charleroi, Liège, Bruges, Leuven, Mons and Namur contribute distinct functions to the national urban network.
2026 national population
Statbel recorded 11,867,634 inhabitants on January 1, 2026; this official register total differs from the 2025 World Bank international estimate. [11]
2025 national population change
Statbel registered a rise of 42,083 inhabitants during 2025, despite a negative natural balance; international net migration offset the difference. [11]
2026 regional population figures
Statbel listed 6,898,350 in Flanders, 3,713,450 in Wallonia and 1,255,834 in Brussels-Capital on January 1, 2026. [11]
Population ageing
Statbel reported that people aged at least 65 comprised approximately 20.67% of the national population on January 1, 2026. [12]
Population density interpretation
More than 380 residents per square kilometer in national totals masks dense Brussels neighborhoods, industrial corridors and much less densely settled uplands.
Temperate maritime climate
Atlantic weather produces relatively mild winters, variable rainfall and moderate summers, with regional differences in exposure and elevation.
Flooding and water risk
River flooding and intense rainfall present hazards for urban and rural communities; drainage capacity, catchment planning and regional policy are essential.
Energy and environmental management
Air quality, industrial emissions, waste treatment, biodiversity decline and low-carbon transition policy involve multiple levels of government.
Agricultural geography
Mixed livestock, dairy, horticulture, cereals and intensive farming systems vary by region and are influenced by land prices and environmental regulation.
Transport crossroads
Railways, roads, inland waterways and airports connect Benelux, the Rhine basin, northern France and major European markets.

Physical geography influences economic concentration: lowland ports and inland waterways feed an extensive transport network, while the Ardennes uplands support forestry, smaller settlements and nature tourism. National totals obscure sharp differences between the Brussels-Capital, Flemish and Walloon regions. The January 2026 national population register and World Bank annual population estimates are different series with different dating conventions.

Data interpretation: Belgium’s national statistics office and Eurostat issue population figures with different dates and coverage. The population above is the World Bank 2025 series for cross-country comparison, not the 1 January 2026 Statbel count. [1]

Advanced, high-income, internationally integrated economy with substantial logistics, services, chemicals, pharmaceuticals, and specialized manufacturing

Economic hubs
Port of Antwerp-Bruges, Brussels, and industrial clusters across Flanders and Wallonia
2025 goods exports
Belgian government economic statistics report approximately €503.7 billion in merchandise exports for 2025; the figure is not the same as goods-and-services exports. [13]
2025 goods imports
The same 2025 goods trade summary lists roughly €477.9 billion in imports, illustrating Belgium’s position as both a destination and transit hub. [13]

Foreign trade statistical caution
National, customs, balance-of-payments and regional trade accounts can differ in valuation, reporting boundary, re-exports and treatment of merchanting. [13]
National Bank of Belgium
The central bank participates in the Eurosystem and provides financial stability analysis, statistics and macroeconomic research.
Euro adoption history
Belgium entered the original euro area in 1999 and introduced euro notes and coins in 2002, replacing the Belgian franc in cash circulation.
Industrial geography
Chemical and petrochemical clusters, Flemish ports, pharmaceutical facilities and Walloon industrial zones reflect the country’s long manufacturing legacy.
Pharmaceutical industry
Medicines, biotechnology and vaccines are significant high-value manufacturing and export activities; output may be concentrated in large international firms.
Chemicals and materials
Petrochemicals, specialty chemicals, plastics and materials industries rely on imported feedstocks, integrated transport and environmental compliance.
Port of Antwerp-Bruges
Major North Sea terminals handle containers, vehicles, liquid bulk and industrial cargo; port throughput should not be equated with Belgian exports. [16]
Logistics and distribution
Warehousing, freight forwarding, rail links and inland shipping connect domestic production to the wider European market.
Financial and professional services
Banking, insurance, corporate services, legal work and EU-related professional activity form important service-sector employment and output.
Automotive supply chains
Vehicle assembly, imports, exports and logistics remain connected to broader European manufacturers and distribution networks.
Agriculture and agri-food
Meat, dairy, fruit, vegetables, beer, chocolate and processed foods involve domestic farming and manufacturing as well as imported ingredients.
Diamond trade
Antwerp is a longstanding diamond trading and cutting center; gross diamond trade flows must not be confused with local value added.
Research and innovation
Universities, biotechnology clusters, materials laboratories and engineering firms connect public research with domestic and international business.
Small enterprises
Small and medium-sized firms employ many workers in retail, construction, professional services and manufacturing alongside multinational groups.
Labor productivity
High-value industry and specialized services support productivity, but productivity per worker is conceptually different from wages or median household income.
Employment and skills
Regional labor conditions, language requirements, educational qualifications and commuting patterns can produce marked geographic differences.
Fiscal structure
Federal and federated governments raise and allocate resources under complex intergovernmental arrangements, with pensions and health spending prominent.
Public debt
Debt-to-GDP ratios measure accumulated liabilities relative to annual economic output; they should not be interpreted as household indebtedness.
Energy infrastructure
Import terminals, electricity interconnectors, natural gas infrastructure and offshore wind projects support industrial and household energy supply.
Tourism economy
Brussels, Bruges, Antwerp, Ghent, Flanders war memorials, the Ardennes and coastal resorts generate tourism and hospitality activity.
Brussels international employment
EU and international institutions support local office, diplomacy and services employment, but their expenditures are not Belgian government spending.
GDP interpretation
The 2025 World Bank nominal GDP estimate is an international U.S.-dollar series; exchange rates and revisions affect comparisons with Belgian euro figures.

Belgium’s export statistics are especially sensitive to the distinction between domestic production and goods passing through its major maritime and distribution infrastructure. Regional totals, national exports and national-accounts exports of goods and services do not have identical scopes. The 2025 merchandise-export figure below is an official goods series, and pharmaceuticals, vehicles and petroleum categories can vary sharply with prices and distribution arrangements.

People and Society

Official languages
Dutch, French, and German
Language geography
Dutch is predominant in Flanders; French in Wallonia; the Brussels-Capital Region is officially bilingual Dutch–French; a German-speaking community lies in eastern Belgium
Religious landscape
Historically Roman Catholic, with Protestant, Orthodox Christian, Muslim, Jewish, other faith, and unaffiliated populations
Religious statistics
Belgium does not use a mandatory religious affiliation population census; survey estimates vary with question, age range, and survey year
Regional and cultural identity
The Flemish, French-speaking, and German-speaking communities have distinct educational and cultural institutions
Belgian language areas
The constitution recognizes Dutch, French, German and a bilingual Brussels administrative framework in different legal language areas.
Dutch-speaking Flanders
Dutch is the principal language of administration, education and public life in most of the Flemish Region, with specific legally protected exceptions.
French-speaking Wallonia
French predominates in most Walloon municipalities, but the region also contains the separate German-language area in the east.
German-speaking Community
German-language educational and cultural institutions serve communities in eastern Belgium under a constitutionally recognized autonomous community.
Bilingual Brussels
Dutch and French have official status in Brussels-Capital despite differing everyday usage; bilingual public services have specific legal obligations.
Language facilities
Some municipalities offer language accommodations under special laws, making linguistic rules more complex than a simple one-language-per-region map.
Regional culture
Flemish and Francophone institutions support media, museums, literature, festivals and education; boundaries do not always correspond to personal identity.
Higher education
Universities in Leuven, Ghent, Brussels, Liège, Louvain-la-Neuve and other centers pursue teaching and research under different community systems.
School governance
Communities have extensive education competences, while constitutional guarantees and other cross-cutting policies shape national educational standards.
Healthcare financing
Compulsory health insurance and social protection operate within national rules alongside health-service delivery and public-health competences at other levels.
Social security
Pensions, unemployment protection and other benefits reflect a substantial welfare state; eligibility and financing follow separate statutory arrangements.
Migration background
Belgian residents include European and non-European immigrant communities, naturalized citizens, and multiple generations of Belgian-born descendants.
Population measurement
Citizenship, country of birth, parental origin and self-identified ethnicity are different categories and should not be merged without source definitions.
Religious traditions
Catholic Christianity remains historically influential alongside Protestant, Orthodox, Muslim, Jewish, secular and other communities.
Religious data caution
Belgium does not publish a mandatory comprehensive religion census; opinion polls are sampled estimates with varying survey populations.
Jewish cultural history
Antwerp and Brussels have longstanding Jewish institutions, while the Holocaust and wartime persecution remain central historical and educational subjects.
Arts and painting
Flemish Renaissance, Baroque and modern art traditions include major contributions to European painting, sculpture and graphic arts.
Architecture
Gothic towns, Art Nouveau buildings, industrial monuments and modern architecture preserve different periods of urban transformation.
Comics and popular culture
Belgium’s comics and graphic-narrative industry has international influence in both Dutch- and French-language markets.
Music and festivals
Classical music, jazz, electronic music and regional festivals contribute to a diverse contemporary cultural economy.
Food and gastronomy
Belgian brewing, chocolate, waffles, fries, regional cheeses and other culinary traditions combine artisanal culture with commercial industries.
Football and cycling
Association football, road cycling and other sports hold prominent positions in popular culture and international competition.
Urban inequality
Housing costs, educational access, employment opportunities and poverty vary across municipalities and regions despite national social protections.
Historical memory
Colonialism, collaboration and resistance during World War II, and language-related disputes are continuing subjects of scholarship and public commemoration.
Civic diversity
National, regional, municipal, European and community-level affiliations coexist, and political identities cannot be inferred from a resident’s language alone.

Language policy is institutional rather than a simple informal choice: education, courts, administration and electoral organization depend in part on legally defined language areas and community competences. Surveyed religious beliefs do not amount to a population census of faith. Higher education, health and social-security policy combine federal and federated responsibilities in ways that are important for interpreting public services.

Demographic interpretation: No single contemporary religion percentage is presented as a definitive national census result. Survey values must be attributed to the exact respondent population and year. [6]

Government and Institutions

System
Federal parliamentary constitutional monarchy
Head of state
King Philippe (King of the Belgians since 2013)
Head of government
Federal prime minister; Bart De Wever was appointed in February 2025 (leadership is subject to change during government crises)
Federal parliament
Bicameral: Chamber of Representatives (150 directly elected members) and Senate (60 members, primarily designated through subnational bodies)
Subnational institutions
Three regions—Flanders, Wallonia, Brussels-Capital—and three language communities—Flemish, French, German-speaking
Constitutional design
Federal authority shares powers with regions and communities; the communities and regions exercise distinct constitutionally assigned responsibilities
Judiciary
Independent courts, including a Constitutional Court and Court of Cassation
Federal constitutional monarchy
The monarch is head of state under constitutional rules, while politically accountable ministers and elected institutions exercise day-to-day governing authority.
King Philippe
Philippe has been King of the Belgians since July 21, 2013, succeeding Albert II; royal powers are governed by constitutional conventions and law.
Prime Minister Bart De Wever
Bart De Wever took office as federal prime minister in February 2025 and is listed on the official federal government composition page at the cutoff. [10]
Council of Ministers
Federal ministers coordinate government policy under coalition arrangements and parliamentary confidence rules.
Chamber of Representatives
The 150-seat directly elected lower house is the principal federal parliamentary chamber for most lawmaking and government accountability.
Reformed Senate
The 60-member upper house mainly represents federated entities and has narrower constitutional functions than an equal-power chamber.
Flemish Region
The regional government is responsible for designated territorial issues including parts of economic policy, transport and spatial planning.
Walloon Region
Walloon institutions exercise specified regional powers over territory, economy, infrastructure and the environment.
Brussels-Capital Region
The bilingual capital region governs distinct metropolitan responsibilities across nineteen municipalities.
Flemish Community
Cultural, linguistic, educational and other community responsibilities operate within constitutional rules and institutional arrangements.
French Community
The French Community, commonly styled the Fédération Wallonie-Bruxelles, manages important language-linked public-policy fields.
German-speaking Community
Community institutions in the east exercise their own recognized cultural and educational competences.
Institutional asymmetry
Flemish regional and community institutions are merged in practice, while French-speaking and Walloon institutions remain more distinct.
Ten provinces
The five Flemish and five Walloon provinces provide an administrative tier, but Brussels-Capital does not belong to a province.
Municipal government
Communes and gemeenten handle numerous locally delivered services, with powers and supervision depending on region.
Constitutional Court
The Constitutional Court reviews specified federal and federated legislative norms for conformity with constitutional rules.
Court of Cassation
The Court of Cassation is a senior judicial institution dealing with questions of law rather than serving as a routine factual retrial court.
Council of State
The Council of State reviews administrative legal disputes and issues advisory opinions in defined legislative processes.
Coalition formation
Multiple parties and linguistic constituencies can necessitate prolonged negotiations; election winners do not automatically govern alone.
Electoral geography
Federal, regional, community, provincial and municipal electoral institutions differ, and some elections occur on synchronized but separate ballots.
Public accountability
Parliamentary questions, courts, audit institutions and media oversight operate alongside statutory disclosure and anti-corruption arrangements.
Separation of authorities
EU law may directly affect Belgian institutions, but European Commission, Council and Parliament are not federal organs of the Belgian state.
Federal financing
Fiscal transfers, taxation powers and spending competences involve complex arrangements negotiated between federal and federated authorities.
Language and public administration
Official language requirements shape courts, administration, public employment and services according to jurisdiction and location.

The Belgian federation is unusual because regions and language communities overlap geographically but are not identical jurisdictions. Constitutional revisions transferred powers from the national level over several decades, and the Senate no longer resembles a directly elected second chamber. Government formation often requires multipartite negotiations across linguistic constituencies; federal and regional elections produce different executives.

Institutional descriptions refer to the legal and constitutional design; incumbents and cabinet composition may change between updates. [6]

International travel documents

Belgium’s role in European integration reflects geography, international diplomacy and postwar institutional design. Its territory hosts major EU functions as well as NATO headquarters, but the presence of an institution in Brussels does not make that body part of Belgian government. Cultural heritage lists contain transnational and serial properties; constituent monuments should not be added as if each were a separate UNESCO inscription.
