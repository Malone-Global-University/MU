Country Overview

Ukraine is an Eastern European republic with its capital at Kyiv and a Black Sea coastline. Modern independence was declared in 1991 after the dissolution of the Soviet Union. Russia occupied and purported to annex Crimea in 2014 and launched a full-scale invasion in February 2022; hostilities and occupation continued at the editorial cutoff of November 2026. Ukraine’s internationally recognized borders encompass Crimea and other regions under foreign occupation. War has disrupted statistical collection, population location and economic activity; estimates are explicitly qualified throughout this entry. [8]

Ukraine is a sovereign Eastern European state whose internationally recognized territory includes Crimea and the other Ukrainian areas occupied or claimed by Russia. The full-scale invasion begun by Russia in February 2022 continued as of 6 November 2026 and has transformed population movements, public finance, border access, economic output and the reliability of survey evidence. The World Bank 2025 population estimate appearing in the original source page must not be treated as a census of people physically living in Ukraine's government-controlled areas, and wartime area comparisons must state whether they concern recognized territory or effective control. President Volodymyr Zelenskyy remained head of state at the editorial cutoff. The Verkhovna Rada appointed Sergii Koretskyi as prime minister on 16 July 2026, replacing the government led by Yulia Svyrydenko. Ukraine opened four negotiating clusters in its EU accession process and continues reform work; it was not yet an EU member. The 2025 official goods trade figures are separately reported from GDP and critical financial assistance.

Identity and State Formation

Ukraine's historical identity encompasses the medieval Kyivan polity, later Ruthenian communities in the Grand Duchy of Lithuania and Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, Cossack political formations, and territories governed by the Russian and Habsburg empires. These overlapping histories cannot be reduced to one modern national origin. During the revolutionary years of 1917–1921, Ukrainian governments sought sovereign statehood, while wars and changing armies fragmented the territory. The Ukrainian Soviet Socialist Republic participated in the founding of the United Nations in 1945, although sovereignty was constrained within the USSR. The independent state emerged through the declaration of 24 August 1991 and its confirmation in the December referendum. The Holodomor, the Holocaust, mass deportations and wartime occupation are essential parts of historical memory. Ukraine's parliamentary and civic history includes the Orange Revolution of 2004 and the Euromaidan movement of 2013–14. The Russian seizure of Crimea in 2014 and the full-scale invasion beginning in February 2022 violated Ukraine's territorial integrity. The current edition clearly separates internationally recognized sovereignty from military control and documents historical, legal and social continuity amid war. [24]

Revolutionary governments asserted Ukrainian independence in 1917–1921, but much of Ukraine was incorporated into the Soviet Union. Stalin-era collectivization and the Holodomor famine, World War II mass violence, and Soviet rule deeply shaped Ukrainian society. Russia occupied Crimea in 2014, supported armed groups in eastern Ukraine and invaded on a much larger scale on 24 February 2022. Ukraine has defended its sovereignty while enduring enormous losses, population displacement and damage to housing, infrastructure and heritage. [12]

Geography and Population

Ukraine stretches from the Carpathian uplands across central river basins and agricultural plains to the Black Sea and Sea of Azov. The Dnipro watershed supports major cities, ports, reservoirs and hydropower infrastructure; the Danube delta in the southwest crosses international conservation and transport networks. The Carpathians contain forests, highland settlements and transboundary biodiversity, while the Black Sea coast has experienced major wartime threats to ports, ecosystems and cities. Ukrainian law recognizes 24 oblasts, the Autonomous Republic of Crimea and the cities with special status of Kyiv and Sevastopol, though military control is not coextensive with the legal map. Estimates of residents by locality remain uncertain because millions have moved internally or abroad, some areas are inaccessible, and people may return or relocate repeatedly. The World Bank's population projection does not resolve these problems. Rural land use, mined fields, damaged irrigation and water infrastructure, and energy-network attacks illustrate why economic geography in 2026 cannot be inferred from a prewar atlas alone. Border data should always distinguish land frontiers, international recognition and current operational crossings. [23]

Economy and International Trade

Before and during the invasion, Ukraine's economy combined major agricultural exports, metal and machinery production, mining, IT services, energy, transport and domestic consumption. Wartime destruction of industrial installations, ports, housing and energy infrastructure has altered that production mix and increased the importance of international grants, loans and security-related procurement. The Ukrainian export-support portal, citing the State Customs Service, reported 2025 merchandise exports of US$40.37 billion and imports of US$84.74 billion, a goods deficit of US$44.37 billion. Those totals must not be called the current-account deficit or the country's entire external earnings. Sunflower and soybean oils, cereals, iron-related products and electric wiring remain important categories, but shipments depend on security, logistics and the agricultural harvest. IMF analysis recorded real GDP growth of 1.8% in 2025 and a sharply uncertain 2026 outlook. Exchange-rate and population-denominator assumptions matter to GDP-per-capita comparisons. Rebuilding productive capacity will require finance, demining, infrastructure repair, skilled workers and reliable transport. Statements about recovery should distinguish observed 2025 output, projected 2026 growth and uncertain future reconstruction scenarios. [19]

People and Society

Ukrainian is the state language, but the historical linguistic landscape includes Russian, Crimean Tatar, Romanian, Hungarian, Polish, Bulgarian and other languages in different communities and regions. Language use, ethnicity, political identity, citizenship and religion must not be equated. Orthodox Christian, Ukrainian Greek Catholic, Roman Catholic, Protestant, Muslim, Jewish and secular communities have been affected by displacement and the damage to religious and cultural buildings. A prewar census cannot give a reliable 2026 nationwide share for every religious affiliation while large numbers of people are abroad, displaced or living under occupation. The Crimean Tatar people and other Indigenous communities have distinct political and cultural histories and have faced special risks under Russian occupation. Women, children, displaced households, veterans, people with disabilities and older residents face different needs in education, shelter, employment and healthcare. Universities, libraries, schools, theaters and heritage agencies continue to operate and adapt under extraordinary pressures. International humanitarian figures, when cited, require a date, population coverage and clear distinction between refugees abroad and internally displaced persons; they should never be casually summed into a supposed national population. Government and Institutions

The Constitution of 1996 establishes Ukraine as a unitary republic with a directly elected president, a unicameral Verkhovna Rada and a Cabinet of Ministers. Under wartime martial-law arrangements, ordinary elections and some administrative processes have been subject to legal restrictions, which must be explained by reference to law rather than assumed to erase constitutional continuity. President Volodymyr Zelenskyy remained in office at the November 2026 cutoff, while the legislature appointed Sergii Koretskyi prime minister on 16 July 2026 following Yulia Svyrydenko's government resignation. The prime minister leads a cabinet responsible through constitutional parliamentary institutions, distinct from presidential command and diplomacy. Ukraine's judiciary includes the Supreme Court and Constitutional Court, while specialized corruption-control institutions perform separate statutory functions. Decentralization gave municipalities and territorial communities roles in public services, now affected differently by damage and displacement. The legal designation of Crimea and occupied regions as Ukrainian territory remains unchanged by Russian annexation claims. Policies concerning defense, conscription, mobilization, civil liberties and national reconstruction require careful distinction between constitutional design, martial-law legislation and implementation on the ground. International Profile

Ukraine has held its own seat in the United Nations since 1945, originally as the Ukrainian SSR; independence in 1991 did not mean that it first joined the UN that year. The General Assembly has repeatedly reaffirmed the country's territorial integrity within internationally recognized borders and rejected Russia's attempted annexations. Ukraine is not a member of NATO, but it maintains extensive cooperation with NATO countries; the alliance's obligations do not apply as though Ukraine were a treaty member. The EU granted candidacy in 2022 and opened accession talks in 2024. By November 2026, the European Commission reported four clusters opened and ongoing work on democratic institutions, rule of law, minority protections and sectoral alignment; formal EU membership was not complete. The Council of Europe, OSCE, WTO, international financial institutions and bilateral partners have distinct roles. International humanitarian assistance, military support and reconstruction finance must be classified separately rather than combined into a single trade figure. UNESCO lists eight Ukrainian World Heritage properties on its state page, some affected by threats or military hostilities. International recognition of territory is a legal issue that cannot be replaced by a temporary map of military positions. [20]

Source reconciliation and 2026 editorial decisions

  • November EU accession review: Four negotiation clusters opened; the 2026 enlargement assessment requests further reforms rather than announcing accession. [20]
