Country Overview

The Holy See and Vatican City State are closely connected but are not the same entity. The Holy See is the supreme ecclesiastical authority and a sovereign legal person with international diplomatic relations; the State of Vatican City is the territorial enclave established in 1929 to guarantee its independence. The Pope leads both, yet the Roman Curia’s worldwide ecclesiastical functions must be distinguished from the Governorate’s administration of the small territorial state. This encyclopedia page uses the internationally recognized Holy See as its principal subject and labels territorial figures as Vatican City data.

The Governorate’s dated public release reports 882 Vatican City residents at December 31, 2024, while separately describing 673 citizens, some living abroad. No comparable World Bank GDP, GDP-per-capita or HS-coded export series is reported, and none has been invented here. Pope Leo XIV was elected in May 2025 and promulgated a new Fundamental Law of Vatican City on July 31, 2026. That legal document clarifies civil government and expressly distinguishes the state from the Roman Curia. The Holy See remains a permanent non-member UN observer, not a voting UN member.

Identity and State Formation

The Holy See: juridical and diplomatic personality of the central authority of the Catholic Church [1]

Historical sovereignty
The Holy See retained international diplomatic personality after the Papal States lost Rome in 1870, prior to Vatican City State’s creation [1]
Lateran Treaty

Contemporary papacy

State identifiers
VA (ISO alpha-2), VAT (ISO alpha-3), numeric 336; Holy See (Vatican City State) as UN statistical designation [9]

Holy See as legal person
The Holy See exercises international personality as the central governing authority of the Catholic Church. It should not be described simply as a religious ministry of the territorial Vatican state.
Vatican City as territory
The State of Vatican City provides an independent territorial guarantee for the Holy See, whose international role predates the state created in 1929.
Ancient Christian Rome
The historical importance of the bishops of Rome developed within early Christianity, long before papal temporal rule or a modern territorial sovereign jurisdiction existed.
Early papal institutions
Ecclesiastical administration, councils and papal correspondence evolved over centuries; present-day offices should not be projected unaltered into late antiquity.
Medieval temporal rule
Papal claims to temporal government over lands in central Italy produced political institutions later described as the Papal States, distinct from universal religious jurisdiction.
Renaissance Rome
Papal patronage helped shape major artistic, architectural and scholarly projects, while politics, finance and church reform generated considerable controversy.
Reformation and Catholic reform
Sixteenth-century religious conflict and reform changed the Catholic Church’s institutions and diplomacy, including the Council of Trent and the expansion of missionary networks.
Roman Question
The capture of Rome by the Kingdom of Italy in 1870 ended the Papal States’ remaining temporal control, creating a prolonged dispute over the Pope’s territorial independence.
1870–1929 diplomatic continuity
Despite the loss of territorial control over Rome, the Holy See maintained international relations and institutional continuity before the Lateran settlement.
Lateran Pacts
The 1929 settlement between the Holy See and Italy included a treaty addressing statehood and sovereignty and additional arrangements on relations with the Italian state.
Treaty ratification
The Lateran arrangements were signed on 11 February 1929 and ratified in June, making the legal sequence different from the origin of papal religious authority.
Unique state purpose
Vatican City was established principally to ensure the Holy See’s independence, rather than to accommodate an ordinary national economy or a large permanent population.
Second Vatican Council
The 1962–1965 council reshaped Catholic approaches to liturgy, ecumenism and contemporary society, with consequences extending far beyond Vatican City’s residents.
John Paul II pontificate
The 1978–2005 papacy was marked by wide international travel, dialogue and engagement with political developments, including late Cold War change.
Benedict XVI transition
Benedict XVI’s resignation in 2013 demonstrated the distinction between ecclesiastical succession procedures and ordinary hereditary or electoral constitutional monarchies.
Francis pontificate
Pope Francis, elected in 2013, emphasized pastoral reform, migration, climate responsibility and administrative accountability before his death in 2025.
2025 conclave
The cardinals elected Pope Leo XIV on 8 May 2025 following the papal vacancy, according to ecclesiastical voting procedures rather than a resident-state popular election.
Pope Leo XIV
Leo XIV exercises the office of Bishop of Rome, supreme pastor of the Catholic Church and sovereign of Vatican City State, which are related but legally distinguishable capacities.
Continuing ecclesiastical law
Canon law regulates many matters of church governance globally; it is not identical to the civil criminal and administrative laws of Vatican City State.
Territorial civil law
Vatican City maintains its own legal framework for civil administration, security and courts; that framework does not automatically govern every Catholic institution worldwide.
2023 Fundamental Law
The 2023 Fundamental Law supplied an earlier constitutional framework that was expressly replaced by the new Fundamental Law promulgated on 31 July 2026.
2026 Fundamental Law
Leo XIV’s July 2026 Fundamental Law reaffirmed the state's instrumental purpose and clarified legislative, executive and judicial institutions, without creating a new sovereign country.
State flags and symbols
The yellow-and-white state flag uses the papal tiara and crossed keys under the 2026 Fundamental Law; symbols should be distinguished from church-wide insignia.
National and ecclesiastical names
'Holy See', 'Apostolic See', 'Vatican', and 'Vatican City State' can refer to different legal entities or locations; context determines accurate usage.
Documentary chronology
Early church history, the 1870 loss of the Papal States, the 1929 treaty and the 2026 Fundamental Law represent distinct kinds of institutional change.

This entry addresses an unusual relationship among religious jurisdiction, international personality and physical territory. Papal institutions developed across centuries, while the modern state originates in a twentieth-century treaty with Italy. The Holy See maintained diplomatic continuity after losing Rome in 1870, before Vatican City existed. A new Pope or a new civil fundamental law does not imply a newly founded Holy See. Legal terminology is therefore essential: the Holy See conducts most international diplomacy, and Vatican City supports the independent exercise of its central ecclesiastical mission.

Historical context: Papal territorial authority in central Italy was reduced during Italian unification and lost Rome in 1870, but the Holy See continued diplomatic activity. The 1929 settlement established a small independent territory, not the first-ever creation of the Holy See. [1]

Access points link Vatican City with surrounding Rome, but internal security and entry rules differ from the ordinary international borders of neighboring sovereign countries.
St Peter’s Square
The square is associated with the Vatican complex and has special practical and treaty arrangements involving Italian access and public gatherings.

This major pilgrimage and worship site is central to public religious life; its functions are not reducible to a commercial tourism attraction.
Apostolic Palace
Papal offices and ceremonial rooms form part of the administrative and historic built environment, with access determined by institutional role and preservation requirements.
Vatican Museums
Collections occupy extensive historic buildings within Vatican City and provide public cultural access under conservation, security and visitor-management rules.
Sistine Chapel
Michelangelo’s ceiling and the chapel’s historical and liturgical roles make it both a protected artistic space and a location associated with papal conclaves.
Vatican Gardens
Gardens and landscaped grounds occupy a significant portion of the enclave and present maintenance, biodiversity and water-management considerations.
Vatican Library
Library collections and scholarly access support historical research, preservation and cataloging, distinct from museum admissions or ordinary municipal library services.
Vatican Apostolic Archive
Archival holdings document ecclesiastical and diplomatic history; research access is governed by specialized archival policy and opening periods.
Rome extraterritorial sites
Certain basilicas, palaces and institutional buildings outside the state receive treaty-based protections without forming contiguous additions to the 44-hectare enclave.
Lateran Basilica
The cathedral of the Diocese of Rome is outside Vatican City and enjoys a special legal status under the Lateran arrangements.
Santa Maria Maggiore
This major papal basilica in Rome is located on Italian territory with special treaty protections, not within the perimeter of Vatican City State.
Saint Paul Outside the Walls
The basilica is part of Rome’s historic ecclesiastical landscape and a UNESCO-related property, despite its location outside Vatican City’s walls.
Castel Gandolfo
Papal and associated properties at Castel Gandolfo lie outside Vatican City; treatment of such sites requires distinguishing ownership and treaty protections from sovereign territory.
Residence versus citizenship
A person may hold Vatican citizenship without living inside the state, and a resident may lack citizenship; the two categories are not interchangeable.
2024 dated residents
Governorate statistics enumerate 882 Vatican City residents as of 31 December 2024, including both citizens and non-citizens; this is not a 2026 direct count. [source]
Reported citizenship count
The Governorate separately describes 673 citizens on its public page, some living elsewhere because of diplomatic or official functions; avoid presenting it as a same-date census total. [source]
Swiss Guard residence
Members of the Pontifical Swiss Guard are part of the special service-related resident community; their professional status differs from ordinary national armed forces.
Daily population fluctuation
Pilgrims, visitors, employees, journalists and contractors enter the small territory, so daytime presence is not equivalent to resident population.
No conventional municipalities
The state does not have a hierarchy of towns, provinces, villages or a rural-urban population split like that used for ordinary country profiles.
Climate and urban context
Vatican City shares Rome’s Mediterranean climate and urban heat conditions, with localized impacts on building conservation and outdoor visitor facilities.
Heritage preservation
Dense concentrations of irreplaceable buildings and artifacts require fire protection, environmental monitoring, conservation and crowd management within limited space.
Density caution
Dividing residents by 0.44 square kilometers produces a mathematical density, but the figure tells little about functional land use dominated by offices, monuments and gardens.

Vatican City’s 44 hectares cannot be analyzed through the usual national categories of countryside, major cities, resource basins and provincial populations. The legal perimeter lies inside Rome, whereas special extraterritorial protections apply to some sites outside it. Daytime numbers fluctuate as visitors and employees arrive from Italy, and citizenship frequently accompanies office or service rather than birth on the territory. The official 2024 count is a resident figure. It is not a 2026 census and does not include the worldwide Catholic population.

Data interpretation: The 882 figure is a dated Vatican City State resident count, not a count of every Vatican citizen or a population estimate for the worldwide Catholic Church. The Holy See as a juridical person has no analogous resident-population boundary. [2]

Employment categories

Financial interpretation

A rigorous Holy See economic profile should explain what is not measurable rather than supply artificial international comparisons. Vatican City operates services and cultural institutions, while multiple Holy See entities manage donations, property, investments and central administration. Their accounts have differing legal boundaries. The euro is used through a monetary agreement, not EU or Eurosystem membership. Neither museum admissions, charitable transfers, global parish collections nor collectible coin revenue can legitimately substitute for gross domestic product or a national merchandise-export basket.

Religious and institutional identity
The Holy See governs the Roman Catholic Church; Vatican City supports the independent exercise of that ministry [1]
Administrative working language
Italian is extensively used in Vatican City administration; Latin has a distinctive role in Holy See ecclesiastical law and official usage [1]
Other working and diplomatic languages
Multiple languages are used in liturgy, diplomacy and international communications; no general resident language survey is inferred
Religious affiliation percentages
No representative census-based breakdown of Vatican City residents by faith or denomination is claimed
Population characteristics
Residents include clergy, members of religious communities and lay personnel, with nationality and residence governed by specialized rules [2]
Citizenship qualification
Often tied to office, service and residence arrangements, rather than normal jus soli or multigenerational citizenship rules [2]
Ecclesiastical community
Vatican City’s permanent community is structured around offices and service to central church institutions, not an ordinary multigenerational national population.
Universal Catholic Church
The Holy See serves a global religious community, but global baptized membership numbers do not constitute the population of Vatican City State.
Papal pastoral role
The Pope’s universal religious leadership reaches far beyond the enclave, while his sovereign functions apply to the civil state under its own legal order.
Clergy and laypeople
Administrators, chaplains, religious communities, conservators and lay employees perform differentiated tasks within the Vatican environment.
Women in institutions
Women work across administration, scholarship and cultural roles; specific appointment powers and ecclesiastical ministries require precise institutional terminology.
Raffaella Petrini
Sister Raffaella Petrini serves as President of the Governorate and Pontifical Commission in 2026, a civil-state leadership role separate from the papacy. [source]
Swiss Guard
The guard has ceremonial and security duties connected with the Pope and Vatican institutions; its membership rules are unlike an ordinary conscript national army.
Gendarmerie
Vatican police protect public order and security, separate from the Swiss Guard and from security services of the Italian Republic.
Italian usage
Italian is widely used for local administration and day-to-day operations, while institutional communication takes place in multiple languages.
Latin in documentation
Latin has a special place in Catholic liturgy, law and formal texts; describing it as the universal daily mother tongue of residents would be inaccurate.
Multilingual public events
Papal audiences, documents and media broadcasts commonly use many languages to serve an international public and worldwide dioceses.
Citizenship by function
Vatican citizenship is commonly linked to office or service rather than unconditional birthright or a conventional long-term path to naturalization.
Nonresident diplomats
Members of the Holy See’s diplomatic service can be citizens while serving abroad, reinforcing the distinction between citizenship and territorial census.
Resident employees
Some people serving Vatican institutions live in Rome, not Vatican City; a workplace count cannot be treated as a resident-population tally.
Pilgrimage culture
Pilgrims and visitors create major seasonal activity around liturgical celebrations, but they are temporary visitors rather than members of the resident community.
Artistic heritage
Collections spanning antiquity, Renaissance art and global religious material attract research and ethical questions about provenance, conservation and public interpretation.
Sacred music
Liturgical music and choir traditions connect contemporary ceremonies with centuries of musical composition and scholarship.
Research communities
The Vatican Library, Apostolic Archive and scholarly institutions provide research access under specialist rules for catalogs, manuscripts and historic records.
Education networks
Catholic universities and seminaries worldwide have distinct legal and financial structures; they are not public schools administered as a Vatican national district.
Healthcare provision
Medical support for Vatican residents, staff and visitors relies on specialized institutions and relationships, not a statistically comparable national hospital network.
Media and communications
Vatican News, radio, digital channels and formal publications disseminate church information globally, beyond the scope of local resident media.
Religious diversity metrics
A resident census of detailed religious affiliation is not comparable with nationwide survey systems; no fabricated denominational percentages are appropriate.
Culture and religious freedom
Vatican City is an unusual confessional microstate; its rules cannot be generalized as typical constitutional arrangements for the diverse states where Catholics live.
Built-environment accessibility
Historic architecture, security requirements and high visitor volumes complicate accessibility and maintenance, prompting practical conservation and visitor-service planning.
Global engagement
Charitable and pastoral relationships across continents reflect the Holy See’s international ecclesiastical role, not demographic migration into the enclave.
Vatican Museums collections
Museum collections include archaeology, classical sculpture, Renaissance painting, ethnographic material and the Sistine Chapel; display access and conservation differ across collections and historic spaces. [source]

The enclave is a special-purpose institutional community, not an ordinary nation with a settled, statistically large household population. Some Vatican citizens serve overseas, and substantial numbers of staff commute from surrounding Rome. Scholarly, diplomatic, cultural and liturgical activity involves many languages. Religious and civic roles are distinguishable, and the worldwide Catholic Church is neither a Vatican population denominator nor a territorial extension of the state. The Vatican Museums, Library and Apostolic Archive support important global cultural and research engagement.

Population caveat: Vatican City is not a statistically typical sovereign population; worldwide Catholic membership counts do not measure Vatican City residents. [2]

The Pope (Roman Pontiff), presently Leo XIV; sovereign of Vatican City State and supreme pastor of the Catholic Church [6]

Sovereign pontiff
Pope Leo XIV holds supreme authority in Vatican City State under the 2026 Fundamental Law, while leading the universal church through the distinct institutions of the Holy See.
Holy See versus Governorate
The Roman Curia assists the Pope in the Church’s central administration; the Governorate manages defined Vatican civil-state functions under separate law.
2026 constitution
The Fundamental Law promulgated on 31 July 2026 replaced the 2023 law and confirmed the distribution and delegation of Vatican City legislative, executive and judicial functions. [source]
Supreme powers

Pontifical Commission
The Commission carries out legislative responsibilities under the fundamental constitutional framework, with membership and presidential arrangements shaped by recent reforms.
Commission president
The President of the Pontifical Commission also heads the Governorate and directs executive activities within the law and under papal authority.

The civil administration provides security, public health, environmental services, infrastructure, museums and other functions specified in Vatican City law.
Secretary General
The Governorate’s Secretary General assists the president, with powers and delegation arrangements under the 2026 legislation and subsidiary rules.
State representation
The Pope, acting through the Secretariat of State, conducts Vatican City’s diplomatic and treaty representation in matters specified by the Fundamental Law.
Extraterritorial authority
Some legally protected sites outside the enclave are covered by specialized international guarantees, without automatically becoming new sovereign Vatican territory.
Tribunal
The state has a tribunal hearing relevant legal proceedings under Vatican law; its competence differs from ecclesiastical tribunals that hear canon-law cases.
Court of Appeal
Vatican City’s judicial organization includes an appellate court under applicable legislation, separate from ecclesiastical appellate structures.
Court of Cassation
The Vatican state’s highest judicial body forms part of a specialized legal order, with functions defined by its judicial-system law.
Promoter of Justice
The Promoter of Justice exercises investigation and prosecutorial functions specified in Vatican criminal procedure, distinct from pastoral disciplinary authority.
Papal judicial reservation
The 2026 Fundamental Law reserves certain exceptional judicial functions to the Pope; this differs markedly from a separation-of-powers constitutional republic.
Due process commitments
The 2026 law expressly addresses judicial impartiality, the defense and adversarial proceedings; their operation should be evaluated within the specialized legal system.
Sede vacante
During a papal vacancy the College of Cardinals maintains continuity under specified limitations; the arrangement is not a general parliamentary caretaker cabinet.
Conclave process
Cardinal electors select a new Pope through canonically regulated procedures, distinct from elections by Vatican citizens or international Catholics.
Swiss Guard duties
The Swiss Guard serves protective and ceremonial functions under specialized command structures and should not be conflated with state police administration.
State Gendarmerie
The Gendarmerie handles policing and public security and can work with Italian counterparts under operational agreements and applicable law.
Secretariat of State
The Vatican's central diplomatic and administrative coordinating body operates within Holy See governance, separate from the civil executive in the Governorate.
Dicasteries
Roman Curia departments carry out designated ecclesiastical functions in fields such as doctrine, bishops, evangelization and charity; their mandates are not ordinary state ministries.
Civil and canon law
Civil legislation for the territory must be distinguished from canon law governing global church administration, sacramental discipline and ecclesiastical tribunals. [source]
Legal transparency
Published fundamental laws and decrees provide authoritative information on state institutions, while personnel, delegated regulations and procedures require further dated verification.
State continuity
Changes in popes or fundamental laws do not imply the disappearance and re-creation of the Holy See’s diplomatic personality or Vatican City’s sovereignty.
Swiss Guard institutional mandate
The Pontifical Swiss Guard protects the Pope, guards accesses and serves during a vacant see under its specified command structure; it is not an ordinary territorial army. [source]

The July 31, 2026 Fundamental Law affirms supreme papal sovereignty while delegating operational functions to specialized state bodies. The Pontifical Commission legislates, the Governorate carries out civil executive duties, and defined Vatican tribunals exercise judicial functions under the law. In parallel, the Roman Curia assists the Pope in governing the universal Church. Canon-law tribunals and the Vatican City judicial system are not interchangeable. A vacancy of the papacy also triggers special rules for continuity, not a normal parliamentary succession.

The Holy See maintains dialogue with other Christian traditions, world religions and secular institutions; doctrinal and diplomatic exchanges are different activities.
Humanitarian diplomacy
Papal appeals and Holy See engagement address conflict, migration, poverty and disaster response, but should not be equated with a national foreign aid budget.
Ecological diplomacy
Catholic social teaching and Holy See statements address climate and environmental stewardship, distinct from Vatican City’s own small-scale sustainability operations.
Peace and mediation
The Holy See sometimes offers diplomatic good offices or moral appeals, without implying authority to enforce settlements against sovereign states.
Religious liberty discourse
International statements emphasize freedom of conscience and worship, while the Holy See’s particular ecclesiastical character raises distinct constitutional comparisons.
Global diocesan network
Dioceses are locally governed church jurisdictions across sovereign states, not overseas provinces of Vatican City State.
Vatican citizenship abroad
Diplomatic appointments can place Vatican citizens overseas; their residence and representation must not be treated as migration from a typical national household population.
Consular limitations
The Holy See’s diplomatic networks serve defined church and diplomatic functions rather than all ordinary passport and overseas welfare duties of a large population state.
International communications
The Vatican maintains .va and the +379 assigned code, though Vatican telephone services commonly use Italian +39 routing; designations differ from a national trade classification.
Scholarly partnerships
Libraries, archives, research bodies and universities exchange expertise internationally, expanding cultural influence beyond the tiny territorial footprint.

The Holy See’s diplomacy is not limited by the acreage of its enclave. It maintains relations with sovereign states, participates in multilateral forums and engages internationally in ecclesiastical and humanitarian matters. The Holy See’s permanent non-member observer role at the United Nations differs from UN membership. Vatican City uses the euro but is outside the European Union. UNESCO lists two World Heritage properties associated with the Holy See, including sites in Rome with extraterritorial status; counting each constituent building separately would misstate the number of inscriptions.

The Vatican City World Heritage property covers the full 44-hectare state territory. The second Holy See-related inscribed property spans part of Rome and properties with special legal protections. [5]
