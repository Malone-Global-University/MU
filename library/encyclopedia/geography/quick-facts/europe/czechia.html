Country Overview

Czechia, formally the Czech Republic, is a Central European parliamentary republic combining the historic lands of Bohemia, Moravia and Czech Silesia. Prague anchors a network of manufacturing and university cities extending to Brno, Ostrava, Plzeň, Olomouc and other regions. Its industrial profile includes passenger vehicles, machinery, electronics, materials, information technology and high-value services. The landscape ranges from the Bohemian basin and forested mountain borders to Moravian valleys and lowlands; all four international borders are land borders.

The state became independent on January 1, 1993 when Czechoslovakia peacefully divided, although the historical line runs through medieval Bohemian government, Habsburg rule, the Czechoslovak republic established in 1918, Nazi occupation, communist rule and the Velvet Revolution of 1989. Czechia is an EU, NATO and Schengen member but retains the koruna rather than the euro. Early settlement and antiquity
Celtic and Germanic populations and later Slavic communities lived in the Czech lands; no single ancient population maps directly onto the modern national state.
Great Moravia
The ninth-century polity of Great Moravia connected parts of Moravia and neighboring lands with political and religious networks that predated the later Bohemian kingdom.
Christianization
Missions associated with Cyril and Methodius and subsequently western Latin Christianity influenced language, liturgy and institutions in the early medieval region.
Přemyslid dynasty
Přemyslid rulers consolidated Bohemian political authority and shaped relations with the Holy Roman Empire during the medieval era.
Kingdom of Bohemia
Bohemian kingship developed within the Holy Roman Empire and became a major Central European power, notably under Charles IV in the fourteenth century.
Charles IV and Prague
The founding of Charles University in 1348 and major building projects helped make medieval Prague a center of education, trade and imperial government.
Jan Hus and reform
Criticism of church practices associated with Jan Hus preceded the Hussite movement and the fifteenth-century wars over religion and political authority.
Hussite wars
Conflicts beginning in 1419 involved religious reform, estates, royal authority and international crusading forces; the consequences shaped Czech political culture.
Habsburg rule
From 1526 the crown lands became integrated into Habsburg dynastic government, with institutional arrangements changing over subsequent centuries.
Battle of White Mountain
The 1620 defeat of Bohemian estates was followed by repression, confiscations and confessional restructuring during the Thirty Years’ War.
National revival
Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Czech language scholarship, cultural associations and political movements reshaped identities within the Habsburg monarchy.
Industrial development
Coalfields, textile manufacture, engineering and transport made the Czech lands important manufacturing regions of the late Habsburg economy.
1918 Czechoslovak republic
The collapse of Austria-Hungary in World War I allowed Czech and Slovak leaders to establish a new republic on October 28, 1918.
Interwar democracy
The First Czechoslovak Republic developed electoral institutions and industrial capacities amid minority and regional inequalities.
Munich Agreement of 1938
Western powers accepted the transfer of predominantly German-speaking border regions to Nazi Germany without Czechoslovakia participating as an equal signatory.
Occupation and resistance
Nazi Germany occupied the remaining Czech lands in March 1939; persecution, forced labor and the Holocaust devastated communities.
Postwar displacement
Following World War II, large numbers of ethnic Germans were expelled or transferred; the process involved coercion and serious human suffering.
Communist takeover of 1948
The Communist Party consolidated one-party control under Soviet influence, replacing the postwar multiparty political order.
Prague Spring 1968
Reform efforts under Alexander Dubček and the subsequent Warsaw Pact invasion marked a decisive conflict over political liberalization.
Velvet Revolution 1989
Mass demonstrations and opposition mobilization helped end communist one-party rule and restore competitive constitutional politics.
Velvet Divorce 1993
Negotiated separation of the Czech and Slovak republics produced two sovereign states on January 1, 1993, without a war of secession.
European integration milestones
NATO membership in 1999 and European Union accession in 2004 followed major diplomatic, legal and economic transitions.
National commemoration distinction
October 28 commemorates Czechoslovakia’s 1918 creation, while January 1 is observed for restoration of the independent Czech state in 1993.
Symbols and heritage
The white-red-blue flag, national coat of arms and anthem “Kde domov můj” are state symbols; their historical trajectories differ from the 1993 founding date.

The date 28 October 1918 commemorates the creation of Czechoslovakia; 1 January 1993 marks the sovereign Czech Republic. Neither is the origin of Czech communities, language or statecraft. A country profile should distinguish continuous cultural history from modern constitutional succession, while recognizing both persecution under Nazi occupation and the repression and dissent of communist decades.

Historical context: The medieval Czech lands were an important part of Central European political and cultural history. They became constituent lands of Czechoslovakia in 1918, endured Nazi occupation during World War II, and entered the communist period after 1948. The peaceful 1989 Velvet Revolution preceded the consensual 1993 separation from Slovakia. The 1918 national commemoration must not be mistaken for Czechia’s sovereign-state founding date in 1993. Boundaries
Germany, Poland, Slovakia, and Austria; no coastline
Physical features
Bohemian Basin and surrounding uplands, Bohemian Forest, Sudetes and Krkonoše ranges, Moravian lowlands; Elbe (Labe), Vltava, Morava and Oder (Odra) river systems
Major urban centers
Prague, Brno, Ostrava, Plzeň, Liberec, and Olomouc
Central European location
The country lies between Germany, Poland, Slovakia and Austria, without direct access to a sea or ocean.
Bohemia
Western Czechia includes a basin encircled by higher border ranges, with the capital Prague in the Vltava basin.
Moravia
Eastern and southeastern districts include hills, river valleys and plains connecting the Czech interior to Slovakia and the Danube region.
Czech Silesia
Northeastern territory includes the Ostrava and Opava areas, with an industrial and cultural history distinct from Bohemia and much of Moravia.
Krkonoše Mountains
The Giant Mountains on the Polish frontier are associated with high-elevation habitats, winter tourism and protected landscapes.
Highest summit
Sněžka, in the Krkonoše, rises to approximately 1,603 meters, the country’s highest mountain.
Šumava
The Bohemian Forest on the German and Austrian frontier contains extensive woodland, peatlands and ecological corridors.
Bohemian-Moravian Highlands
A broad upland transition shapes road networks, agriculture and watersheds between the western and eastern regions.
Elbe catchment
The Labe (Elbe) drains much of Bohemia toward the North Sea through Germany.
Vltava river
The Vltava crosses Prague and is a major Elbe tributary; reservoirs and urban flood defense are nationally significant.
Morava and Danube linkage
The Morava flows south toward the Danube, connecting parts of eastern Czechia hydrologically with the Black Sea basin.
Oder catchment
The Odra (Oder) drains the northeastern Czech lands northward toward Poland and the Baltic Sea.
River basin division
Czechia’s waters reach three maritime basins, which matters for water management and international environmental coordination.
Climate
Temperate conditions combine Atlantic, continental and altitude-related influences; summers and winters vary by elevation and region.
Heat and drought
Recent dry periods and heat waves have affected agriculture, forestry, groundwater and urban infrastructure.
Flood vulnerability
The 1997 Moravian and 2002 Bohemian floods illustrate the risk from extreme rainfall and river flooding.
Protected areas
Krkonoše, Šumava, Podyjí and České Švýcarsko national parks conserve contrasting mountain, forest and river environments.
Land and surface distinction
Eurostat total geographical area incorporates categories different from FAO land-only area; values should be labeled by the publishing source.
Regional administration
Fourteen regions (kraje), including Prague, form the regional level; districts and municipalities have different administrative functions.
Urban network
Prague, Brno, Ostrava, Plzeň, Liberec, Olomouc, Hradec Králové and České Budějovice are significant population or service centers.
Population at end of 2025
CZSO counted 10,915,839 residents on December 31, 2025; a dated national count, not the same as the World Bank 2025 annual series. [12]
2025 net migration
CZSO registered about 42,040 more international arrivals than departures, offsetting a negative natural population change. [13]
2025 natural change
There were approximately 35,701 more deaths than births in 2025, according to CZSO demographic statistics. [13]
2025 births and deaths
CZSO counted 77,636 live births and 113,337 deaths during calendar 2025; vital statistics use their own registration rules. [13]
Average age
CZSO reported a mean population age of 43.3 years at the end of 2025, indicating sustained ageing. [12]
Older adults aged 65 and above
The 2,272,755 persons aged 65 or older represented about 20.8% of the 2025 year-end population. [12]
Population density
National density is approximately 138 persons per km² using 2025 national population and conventional total area; concentration varies considerably.
Migration and settlement
Migration has supported population totals even as natural increase turned negative, with Prague and metropolitan corridors attracting newcomers.
Natural resources
Brown coal, building materials, forests and industrial minerals have economic importance, but extraction has environmental liabilities.

Population geography is shaped by migration and ageing: the CZSO measured a small overall increase in 2025 despite more deaths than births. Border uplands, industrial basins, the Bohemian-Moravian Highlands and fertile southern lowlands experience different economic and ecological pressures. Geographic surface, land area and watershed size are separate measures.

Data interpretation: Population is the harmonized 2025 World Bank series; Eurostat reports a separate 2026 population snapshot and a 2026 geographical-area estimate. Land area excludes inland waters, unlike total surface area. [1]

Central bank

High technology goods exports
CZSO placed 2025 high-tech goods exports at CZK 569.836 billion, or 11.9% of its total goods-export series. [15]
High tech imports
CZSO reported CZK 746.978 billion of high-tech goods imports in 2025; industrial integration includes substantial imported technology inputs. [15]
Automotive manufacturing
Major automotive assembly and suppliers support employment and foreign trade, with vehicle production dependent on European demand and supply chains.
Industrial machinery
Czech firms manufacture machine tools, mechanical apparatus and specialist equipment for domestic and international clients.
Electrical equipment
Wiring, motors, electrical apparatus, transformers and automation components supply industrial and energy networks.
Computers and electronics
Electronics manufacturing includes assembled devices and components, whose domestic value added is not identical to gross export value.
Steel and engineering
Traditional industrial centers have diversified from mining and heavy production into modern engineering and subcontracting.
Information technology
Software, cybersecurity, business services and digital product development contribute services exports and knowledge-sector employment.
Financial services
Banking and financial intermediation support investment, housing and commerce, within EU banking and regulatory arrangements.
Foreign direct investment
International-owned manufacturing facilities link domestic labor and infrastructure to company networks headquartered in other countries.
Export destination structure
Germany remains the most important merchandise partner; Slovakia, Poland, Austria and other EU economies are closely linked.
Tourism
Prague, spa towns, castles, protected landscapes and regional historic centers attract travelers and support services rather than goods exports.
Agriculture
Cereals, oilseeds, livestock and specialized crop production operate under EU agricultural regulation and domestic environmental constraints.
Beer and brewing
Brewing combines domestic culture, agriculture, brands and export activity; beverage shipments are a subset of total manufactured goods.
Energy transition
Reliance on nuclear generation, fossil-fuel facilities and growing renewables raises investment and grid-modernization questions.
Nuclear electricity
Domestic nuclear generation is an important electricity source; safety, waste management and expansion policy involve national and European oversight.
Lignite legacy
Brown-coal mining and coal-fired electricity have provided industrial energy while creating emissions, land restoration and transition challenges.
Research and training
Technical universities, industrial research and skilled workers help sustain manufacturing innovation and higher-value employment.
Wage versus GDP measure
Nominal GDP per person is average national output per resident, not a measurement of individual disposable income or salary.
Trade composition caution
Cross-border components, re-exports, service fees and exchange rates mean merchandise totals should not be confused with industrial value added.

Czechia is unusually integrated into cross-border manufacturing networks. The value of a vehicle assembled locally may include imported components; export sales, value added and final corporate profits should therefore not be equated. The revised CZSO ownership-based 2025 series supersedes earlier provisional trade totals. High-technology products form an identifiable subset of all merchandise exports.

People and Society

Official language
Czech
Other languages and communities
Slovak, Ukrainian, Polish, Romani, Vietnamese, German, and other minority and migrant languages; Czech sign language has legal recognition
Religious belief, 2021 census
Of persons who answered the voluntary religion question, 68.3% reported no religious belief and 18.7% reported adherence to a church or religious society; the latter excludes believers without church affiliation
Nonresponse, 2021 census
30.1% of residents left the voluntary religion question unanswered; response percentages must not be stated as shares of every resident
Societal context
Historically Catholic and Protestant traditions coexist with large religiously unaffiliated populations; modern society also includes growing migrant communities
Language and public administration
Czech is the primary language of national administration, education and public discourse; other languages have varying minority and community roles.
Slovak language
Czech–Slovak historical, educational and family ties have long supported mutual linguistic familiarity, without eliminating each language’s distinct identity.
Ukrainian communities
Longstanding and more recent Ukrainian migration contributes to schooling, employment, health service and local-language access needs.
Roma communities
Czech Roma communities have varied identities and histories, with unequal outcomes and continuing debate over housing, education and discrimination.
Polish minority
The northeast contains Polish-speaking communities, including the Těšín/Cieszyn region with cross-border cultural institutions.
Vietnamese community
Vietnamese migration and entrepreneurship have become a lasting part of the urban and regional social landscape.
German heritage
German-speaking communities and historical German-Czech relations remain important to cultural memory, border cooperation and industrial history.
2021 census religion question
Religious affiliation was a voluntary census item and its response distribution is not comparable to a mandatory all-resident faith register.
Religion nonresponse
Approximately 30.1% of the 2021 census population did not answer the voluntary religion question; the omitted group affects reported belief shares.
No-belief respondents
Among those answering the 2021 religion item, a large share reported no religious belief; response-based proportions are not the share of every resident.
Christian traditions
Catholic, Protestant, Hussite and other Christian institutions have important historical roles despite widespread contemporary nonaffiliation.
Jewish heritage
Synagogues, cemeteries, museums and archives document Czech Jewish life and the devastating effects of the Holocaust.
Education system
Compulsory schooling leads to secondary, vocational and higher-education pathways with public and private institutions.
University network
Charles University, Masaryk University, Czech Technical University and regional institutions contribute research and professional training.
Public health
Compulsory health insurance and a mixed provider landscape support clinical care, with regional workforce and access pressures.
Population ageing
Rising shares of older residents influence health, pension provision, labor availability and intergenerational household support.
Housing markets
Affordability and rental pressures are pronounced in Prague and other high-demand urban areas; smaller towns face different market conditions.
Transport access
Extensive rail services and metropolitan public transport connect regions, but travel frequency and accessibility differ between urban and rural locations.
Cultural literature
Franz Kafka, Karel Čapek, Jaroslav Hašek and other writers illustrate a multilingual and politically complex literary inheritance.
Music and performance
Smetana, Dvořák, Janáček and later traditions connect Czech performance institutions with wider European culture.
Film and animation
Czech cinema, puppetry and animation have developed distinctive international reputations across political eras.
Architecture
Gothic, Renaissance, Baroque, nineteenth-century, functionalist and postwar buildings coexist in historic and industrial districts.
Sports
Ice hockey and football have prominent national followings alongside athletics, winter sports and local sporting organizations.
Regional cuisine
Bread, dumplings, soups, meats and brewing reflect local tradition but vary significantly by household and region.
Civil society
Voluntary associations, unions, charities and local groups participate in public debate, education and community life.

National identity, mother tongue, citizenship and census self-identification are different statistical categories. The 2021 census religion question was voluntary, and a large nonresponse group makes simplistic national belief percentages misleading. Employment, housing and service access differ between Prague, industrial regions and rural communities.

Demographic interpretation: The Czech 2021 census religion question was voluntary. Answer-based percentages differ substantially from percentages of the full census population and should be interpreted with nonresponse included. [8]

Government and Institutions

System
Unitary parliamentary republic with directly elected head of state
Head of state
President, directly elected for a five-year term
Head of government
Prime minister, leading a cabinet responsible to the Chamber of Deputies
Legislature
Bicameral Parliament: Chamber of Deputies (200 seats) and Senate (81 seats)
Administrative divisions
14 regions (kraje), including the capital Prague; municipalities constitute local government
Constitutional order
Constitution effective 1 January 1993, together with the Charter of Fundamental Rights and Freedoms within the constitutional order
Judiciary
Constitutional Court and independent general court system
President as of cutoff
Petr Pavel was serving as president in November 2026; the president is directly elected for a five-year constitutional term. [17]
Prime minister as of cutoff
Andrej Babiš headed the Czech government at the November 6, 2026 editorial cutoff, as recorded in the government’s cabinet listing. [16]
2025 cabinet formation
Andrej Babiš became prime minister in December 2025 following the 2025 Chamber of Deputies election and government formation. [16]
Parliamentary responsibility
A government must maintain parliamentary confidence; presidential appointment does not eliminate the lower chamber’s accountability role.
Chamber of Deputies
The lower house has 200 elected seats and is central to ordinary legislation, budget approval and government confidence.
Senate
The upper house has 81 seats, with staggered six-year terms and elections for one third of senators every two years.
Bicameral review
Senate review affects ordinary legislation while the constitutional rules distinguish between ordinary bills and constitutional acts.
Presidential elections
Since constitutional reform, the president is chosen by direct popular vote rather than by joint parliamentary ballot.
Prime minister’s functions
The prime minister coordinates cabinet activity and directs executive policymaking through ministers and government decisions.
Cabinet ministries
Ministries administer sectors including finance, interior, industry, education, foreign relations and social affairs under statute and cabinet policy.
Constitutional Court
The Constitutional Court reviews constitutional disputes and rights cases within powers granted by the constitutional order.
General judiciary
Courts decide criminal, civil and administrative disputes under legal rules, with different appellate and specialized jurisdictions.
Supreme Administrative Court
Administrative justice reviews decisions and other legally defined questions involving public authority.
Supreme Audit Office
The national audit institution scrutinizes specified public expenditure and financial management in accordance with law.
Czech National Bank independence
The central bank has legally defined responsibilities distinct from elected executive departments.
Regions and municipalities
Fourteen regions and thousands of municipalities exercise separate self-government functions, budgets and legal competences.
Prague self-government
The capital has both city-level and regional functions under its special administrative arrangements.
Political party system
Parties compete through proportional electoral arrangements for the lower chamber; coalition formation can affect cabinet durability.
Election oversight
Judicial and administrative processes govern election conduct and disputes within statutory deadlines.
Civil rights charter
The Charter of Fundamental Rights and Freedoms forms part of the constitutional order alongside the 1993 Constitution.
Freedom of information
Public access to state records and transparency procedures is governed by national law and relevant EU obligations.
Local government finance
Municipal budgets depend on taxation, allocations, service obligations and capital infrastructure needs.
EU legislative obligations
EU regulations and transposed directives influence domestic law, but EU legislative institutions differ from the Czech parliament.
Security and defense
Armed forces and civilian security agencies operate under statutory and constitutional constraints within NATO commitments.

The Czech president is directly elected but does not govern as an executive president; government requires support in the elected Chamber of Deputies. The 200-seat lower chamber and 81-seat Senate have different terms and competencies. Cabinet leadership can change without altering the underlying constitutional model.

Institutional descriptions refer to the legal and constitutional design; incumbents and cabinet composition may change between updates. [10]
