Country Overview

New Zealand, also known as Aotearoa, is an island country in the southwestern Pacific comprising the North Island, South Island and numerous smaller islands. Māori communities developed distinctive iwi and hapū societies following Polynesian settlement, while sustained European encounters and British colonization profoundly reshaped landholding, politics and demography. The 1840 Treaty of Waitangi / Te Tiriti o Waitangi remains central to constitutional and Treaty-settlement debates. Wellington is the seat of national government, Auckland the largest metropolitan area, and services, agriculture, food exports, tourism and technology support an internationally integrated economy. New Zealand is a constitutional monarchy and parliamentary democracy led, at the November 6, 2026 cutoff, by Prime Minister Christopher Luxon, with Dame Cindy Kiro serving as governor-general. Parliament was dissolved on October 1 before the general election scheduled for November 7; the outcome is not yet established at this editorial cutoff. The 2023 census, Stats NZ June 2026 population estimate, World Bank 2025 annual indicators and Stats NZ trade releases are different observations and should not be conflated.

Identity and State Formation

Official name
New Zealand; Aotearoa is a widely used Māori name for the country [11]
Capital
Wellington, seat of national executive, parliamentary and major judicial institutions [2]
UN regional location
Oceania; Australia and New Zealand subregion under UN M49 conventions [15]
First settlement
Polynesian navigators settled the islands centuries before sustained European contact, forming the ancestors of Māori
Māori political communities
Iwi and hapū maintain complex histories of kinship, authority, land stewardship and cultural practice
Māori customary frameworks
Tikanga, whakapapa and mana inform community relationships and customary institutions in differing contexts
Māori navigation
Ocean voyaging and knowledge of stars, currents and winds supported trans-Pacific settlement
European voyages
Abel Tasman encountered New Zealand in 1642; James Cook charted coasts in the late eighteenth century
Missionary contact
Christian missions, schooling and written Māori-language publishing expanded in the nineteenth century
Declaration of Independence
He Whakaputanga of 1835 asserted chiefly independence, with complex relationships to later British claims
Treaty of Waitangi
Te Tiriti o Waitangi and the English-language Treaty were signed in 1840, with differing interpretations [12]
Treaty signatures
Different chiefs signed copies in different places; Treaty understandings were not uniform [12]
British colonial institutions
The Crown expanded authority, settlement and colonial law during and after the 1840s
New Zealand Wars
Nineteenth-century conflicts over land and sovereignty had major military, social and demographic effects
Land confiscations
Confiscation and purchase systems led to significant Māori dispossession and enduring claims
Representative government
Nineteenth-century elected institutions and responsible government formed under colonial legislation
Women’s suffrage
Women gained the right to vote in parliamentary elections in 1893, a landmark in electoral history
Māori parliamentary seats
Dedicated Māori representation was established in 1867 and remains part of election arrangements
Dominion status
New Zealand was designated a Dominion in 1907; this was not the same moment as full legislative autonomy
Statute of Westminster
New Zealand adopted the Statute of Westminster in 1947, clarifying legislative autonomy
World wars
Military participation and losses in the world wars shaped commemorations and external relationships
Postwar migration
Urbanization, immigration and changing social rights substantially transformed the population
Treaty of Waitangi Act
The 1975 law established the Waitangi Tribunal to examine Treaty-related claims [12]
Historical Treaty settlements
Negotiated settlements address particular historical Crown breaches; these are not single nationwide reparations [12]
Constitution Act 1986
Modern parliamentary institutions are shaped by statutes, common law and constitutional conventions [11]
Electoral reform
Mixed-member proportional representation began at the 1996 general election
2017–2023 governments
Jacinda Ardern and Chris Hipkins led Labour governments before the November 2023 transition
2023 government change
A National-led coalition formed in November 2023 under Christopher Luxon [7]
2026 election calendar
Parliament was dissolved October 1 ahead of voting on November 7, 2026 [9]
National holiday
Waitangi Day is observed February 6; Anzac Day and other commemorations have separate meanings
National emblems
The flag uses the Union Jack and Southern Cross; silver fern and kiwi are prominent national symbols
ISO codes and domain
NZ, NZL, numeric 554; country-code internet domain .nz [15]

Treaty interpretation: the 1840 English and Māori-language texts differ in important respects, and modern Treaty principles arise from subsequent law, tribunal work and historical debates. November 7, 2026 is an upcoming election at the November 6 cutoff.
