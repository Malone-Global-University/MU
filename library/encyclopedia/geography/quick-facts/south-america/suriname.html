Country Overview

Suriname is a sovereign republic on the northern coast of South America between Guyana and French Guiana, with Brazil along its southern frontier and the Atlantic Ocean to the north. Its small coastal population contrasts with an extensive tropical-forest interior forming part of the Guiana Shield. Indigenous peoples and communities descended from Africans who escaped slavery possess enduring territorial, linguistic and cultural traditions; plantation history, Dutch colonial rule, South Asian and Javanese indentured migration, and the 1975 achievement of independence help explain its unusually multilingual society.

Paramaribo is the capital. Gold production, petroleum, services and smaller agricultural and forestry sectors underpin the economy, while the large GranMorgu offshore oil project was still under development in November 2026, with first production projected for 2028 rather than realized in 2025. Following the 2025 parliamentary election, Jennifer Geerlings-Simons became the country’s first woman president. International annual estimates, the 2012 national census and merchandise-trade series are separate measurements and are identified accordingly. [4] [10]

Identity and State Formation

Official name
Republic of Suriname (Republiek Suriname), independent republic on the northeast South American mainland
Capital
Paramaribo, at the lower Suriname River, with government functions and major commercial and cultural institutions [2]
UN geographic region
South America under the UN M49 geographic classification; Suriname also participates extensively in Caribbean institutions [14] [13]
First peoples
Arawak-speaking and Cariban-speaking Indigenous peoples and other communities occupied the territory long before European settlement
Indigenous continuity
Lokono, Kali’na, Trio, Wayana and other Indigenous peoples retain distinct languages, social institutions and territorial relationships
Early European contact
Spanish, English, Dutch and other expeditions participated in seventeenth-century competition for trading access and colonial footholds
English colonial phase
English colonists established plantations before Dutch control became established through seventeenth-century Anglo-Dutch agreements
Dutch colony
Suriname became an important Dutch Caribbean plantation colony; European rule remained uneven in the large forest interior
Plantation system
Sugar, coffee, cacao and other plantation crops relied heavily on the coerced labor of enslaved Africans
Maroon communities
People escaping slavery founded autonomous communities along interior rivers; Saamaka and Ndyuka are among several distinct Maroon peoples
Eighteenth-century treaties
Agreements between colonial authorities and some Maroon groups recognized forms of autonomy while conflict and exploitation continued
Emancipation
Slavery was abolished in the Dutch colony in 1863; a decade of state-supervised transition followed for many formerly enslaved people
Indentured migration
Contract laborers arrived particularly from British India and the Dutch East Indies, shaping modern Indo-Surinamese and Javanese communities
Colonial constitutional change
The 1954 Charter of the Kingdom of the Netherlands granted internal autonomy within the kingdom
Independence
Suriname became independent of the Netherlands on November 25, 1975, with Paramaribo as capital [13]
First republic
Johan Ferrier served as first president after independence; the new state faced difficult economic and political adjustments
1980 military coup
A coup led by military figures including Dési Bouterse displaced the civilian government and began an authoritarian period
December 1982 killings
The extrajudicial killings of fifteen prominent critics became a major issue of memory, justice and accountability
Interior War
Armed conflict involving the military and Jungle Commando during 1986–1992 harmed many interior communities
1987 Constitution
Constitutional rule was formally restored under the 1987 framework, with later elections and political reforms
Civilian multiparty period
Competitive political organizations and coalition bargaining returned as central features of state politics after military rule
2025 parliamentary election
The May 25, 2025 election filled 51 National Assembly seats under a new nationwide electoral method [5]
2025 presidential transition
Jennifer Geerlings-Simons was elected by the National Assembly on July 6 and inaugurated July 16, 2025 [4] [5]
First woman president
Geerlings-Simons became the first woman to hold the presidency of independent Suriname [4]
National holiday
Independence Day, November 25, commemorates the transition from Dutch colonial government to sovereignty
Official language
Dutch is the language of government and the main official language; extensive multilingual communication occurs across society
National symbols
A five-striped red, green and white flag with a central yellow star, national coat of arms and anthem
UN admission
Suriname became a United Nations member on December 4, 1975 [2]
ISO and domain
SR (alpha-2), SUR (alpha-3), 740 (numeric); internet domain .sr [14]

Chronology: Suriname gained sovereignty on November 25, 1975; the 1980 coup and subsequent Interior War belong to the independent republican era. The July 2025 election produced the first woman president, installed with the vice president on July 16. [4]

Geography and Population

Surface area
163,820 km² according to UN country statistical reporting; land-only measures differ from total surface area [2]
Land borders
Guyana to the west, Brazil to the south and French Guiana, an overseas department of France, to the east
Atlantic frontage
Low-lying northern coastal plain includes estuaries, mangrove habitats, mudflats, wetlands and port facilities
Guiana Shield
Much of the interior overlies ancient crystalline rock associated with minerals, waterfalls and forested uplands
Coastal belt
Densely settled lowlands support Paramaribo, agriculture, transportation and critical infrastructure but are exposed to flooding
Northern savannas
Sandy savanna and forest mosaics create ecological transitions between the coastal plain and rainforest
Southern interior
Large areas remain sparsely settled with river communities, protected landscapes and logistical access limitations
Highest mountain
Julianatop reaches approximately 1,230 meters in the Wilhelmina Mountains
Other highlands
The Wilhelmina, Eilerts de Haan and Lely ranges form parts of interior terrain and watersheds
Major rivers
Corantijn/Courantyne, Nickerie, Coppename, Saramacca, Suriname, Commewijne and Marowijne/Maroni are major drainage systems
River transport
Boats are important for interior passenger travel, commerce, health services and access to remote settlements
Brokopondo reservoir
The Afobaka Dam created a large artificial lake that supports hydropower but inundated former settlement and forest lands
Tropical climate
Warm, humid tropical conditions prevail, with rainfall patterns varying regionally and seasonally
Rainy seasons
The region experiences distinct wetter and relatively drier periods, although rainfall timing changes year to year
Forest cover
World Bank reports approximately 94.5% national forest cover, among the highest shares globally [9]
Central Suriname Nature Reserve
UNESCO-listed landscape protects extensive tropical forest and important upper-river watersheds [12]
Biodiversity
Forest habitats contain jaguars, tapirs, monkeys, birds, amphibians and exceptionally diverse plants
Protected land
Conservation areas include Brownsberg and reserves with different management mandates, access and ecological conditions
Gold-mining impacts
Legal and informal mining can cause deforestation, sedimentation and mercury pollution in interior watersheds
Coastal climate risk
Sea-level rise, extreme rainfall, poor drainage and saltwater intrusion threaten communities and agricultural assets
District organization
Ten administrative districts: Paramaribo, Wanica, Nickerie, Coronie, Saramacca, Commewijne, Marowijne, Para, Brokopondo and Sipaliwini
Major urban settlements
Paramaribo and adjacent Wanica concentrate population; Nieuw Nickerie, Moengo and other settlements serve regional functions
2012 census population
The General Bureau of Statistics recorded 541,638 usual residents in the final 2012 census [3]
2012 enumeration distinction
The reported 541,638 figure is the de jure resident population, not the separate de facto census-night count [3]
2025 modeled population
The World Bank annual total of 639,850 is a later estimate, not the result of a national census in 2025 [1]
Population density
Approximately 3.9 residents per km² using 2025 World Bank population and total UN area; actual coastal density is much greater
Settlement distribution
Most people live in the northern coast and lower river regions, while southern Sipaliwini has low population density
Border-zone complexity
Some upper-river areas are subject to long-running boundary disagreements with neighbors and should not be mistaken for settled territorial reallocations
Resource distribution
Gold deposits, onshore oil fields, timber and offshore hydrocarbon discoveries have markedly different footprints and impacts
Natural hazards
Flooding, drought, coastal erosion and forest degradation pose risks despite comparatively low tropical-cyclone exposure

Demographic method: the ABS 2012 de jure census recorded 541,638 people, while the World Bank 2025 total of 639,850 is an annual international estimate. They are neither the same date nor the same collection method. [3] [1]

Economy and International Trade

Economic structure
Commodity-dependent small economy with gold, petroleum, services, trade, agriculture and forestry; resource shocks influence public finances
Nominal GDP
US$4.52 billion in 2025 World Bank current-dollar national-accounts series [1]
GDP per capita
US$7,069.9 in 2025 current US dollars; not average household earnings or a purchasing-power adjustment [1]
2025 GDP growth
1.8% real annual growth in the World Bank 2025 series, which can differ from later national and IMF revisions [1]
2025 consumer inflation
9.2% in the World Bank annual consumer-price indicator, distinct from end-of-year inflation [1]
ILO-modeled unemployment
7.8% in the World Bank's 2025 ILO-based model; this does not enumerate all informal work [1]
Currency
Surinamese dollar (SRD); exchange-rate shifts affect imported goods, prices and national income converted to USD [2]
Central bank
Centrale Bank van Suriname oversees monetary and financial functions within the national framework
Gold exports
Nonmonetary gold dominates recorded merchandise shipments; legal and informal operations have different regulatory and social effects [7]
2024 WTO merchandise exports
WTO's UN Comtrade-based profile reports approximately US$1.484 billion for 2024; coverage and revisions differ by system [7]
2024 gold-export share
Unwrought nonmonetary gold represented about 65.4% of the WTO product-classified 2024 merchandise series [7]
Oil production today
Existing onshore petroleum output is distinct from planned deepwater output not yet in production by late 2026
State oil company
Staatsolie is a central national enterprise in petroleum production, investment and government revenue
Offshore oil discovery
Significant offshore discoveries have created a prospective investment and fiscal transition, not yet a realized production boom
GranMorgu project
Deepwater Sapakara and Krabdagu field development about 150 km offshore targets first oil in 2028 [10]
Project capacity
GranMorgu design includes a floating production unit with approximately 220,000 barrels-per-day processing capacity, not present 2026 production [10]
2026 development status
Equipment and construction advanced during 2026; September 2026 operator update retained a 2028 first-oil timetable [11]
Bauxite legacy
Historic bauxite mining and alumina refining shaped infrastructure and employment; former operations should not be presented as current dominant exports
Agriculture
Rice, bananas, vegetables and smaller crops support domestic production and specialized regional trade
Fisheries
Coastal and estuarine fisheries supply domestic consumption and export markets, with fisheries-management and habitat concerns
Forestry
Timber extraction can provide export income but presents sustainability, land-rights and traceability challenges
Electricity system
Hydropower, thermal generation and distribution infrastructure support homes and industry with different reliability challenges
Merchandise imports
Fuel, equipment, food, machinery and oil-project capital goods contribute to import dependence
Inflation and fiscal policy
Fiscal consolidation, debt restructuring and central-bank stability remain key macroeconomic challenges [8]
2026 IMF outlook
IMF's early-2026 consultation projected stronger medium-term growth but emphasized fiscal, exchange-rate and natural-resource risks [8]
Resource-revenue governance
Contract terms, royalties, taxes, national savings and transparent budgeting are central to future offshore receipts
Tourism potential
Historic Paramaribo, rainforest tourism and cultural heritage attract visitors, subject to transport and conservation constraints
Trade partners and reporting
Export destination shares vary strongly by reporting system and intermediary gold trade; avoid inferring ultimate consumer markets [7]
Services versus goods
Hotel stays, professional services and shipping are not included in merchandise-export totals
Informality and employment
Small businesses, subsistence production and informal mining may be incompletely represented in formal labor indicators
Climate and investment
Coastal flood protection, roads, broadband and skills training will affect capacity to convert resource investment into broad development

People and Society

Official language
Dutch is the official language used by most national institutions, schools and much formal administration
Sranan Tongo
Widely used creole lingua franca serves informal and intercommunity communication across many social settings
Sarnami Hindustani
Indo-Surinamese languages connected to North Indian linguistic histories remain important in homes and cultural institutions
Javanese languages
Descendants of contract migrants from Java maintain language and cultural traditions alongside Dutch and Sranan Tongo
Maroon languages
Saamaka, Ndyuka and other Maroon languages preserve complex African-influenced linguistic histories
Indigenous languages
Lokono, Kali’na, Trio, Wayana and additional languages require differentiated education and cultural-protection approaches
Multilingual society
An individual may use several languages across household, education, worship and government; these measures should not be conflated
Indigenous peoples
Indigenous communities maintain governance, knowledge and territorial claims that are distinct from broad national ethnicity labels
Maroon peoples
Communities descended from formerly enslaved Africans who established autonomous interior settlements have distinct identities and institutions
Indo-Surinamese
Families with ancestry in nineteenth- and early twentieth-century Indian indentured migration are a major component of society
Javanese Surinamese
Migration from the former Dutch East Indies contributed another major language, religious and cultural inheritance
Creole communities
Diverse Afro-Surinamese urban and rural histories reflect enslavement, emancipation, city formation and political change
Other communities
Chinese, Lebanese, European, Brazilian, Guyanese and other populations contribute to commerce and social diversity
Religious traditions
Hindu, Christian, Muslim, Indigenous and other religious communities coexist with nonreligious populations
Religious pluralism
Mosques, Hindu temples and churches illustrate religious diversity; census affiliations are not identical to active practice
Education system
Public and private schools provide Dutch-language instruction, with challenges for children whose home languages differ
Higher education
Anton de Kom University of Suriname and other institutes provide higher education, professional training and research
Health services
Paramaribo hosts major hospitals while interior care depends on air and river access and local health providers
Traditional knowledge
Forest-based ecological expertise and traditional medicine remain culturally significant but require ethical representation
Urban-rural equity
Service delivery, housing, sanitation and secondary education differ considerably between the coast and interior
Gender equality
Political representation and workforce participation shape equality debates; the 2025 first woman presidency is a distinct milestone [4]
Historical trauma
The legacy of slavery, the Interior War and 1982 political killings influences community memory and justice discussions
Cuisine
Pom, roti, moksi alesi, satay and other dishes reflect Afro-Surinamese, Indian, Javanese, Indigenous and wider Caribbean influences
Music and performance
Kaseko, kawina, gospel, Hindustani and Javanese musical forms contribute to a plural cultural landscape
Sport
Football, swimming and athletics are prominent; swimmer Anthony Nesty won Suriname’s first Olympic gold in 1988
Diaspora
Strong family and cultural networks connect Suriname with the Netherlands and neighboring Caribbean and American countries
Cultural heritage
UNESCO-listed Paramaribo and Jodensavanne preserve histories of architecture, colonialism, Jewish communities and migration [12]
Media and civic life
Dutch-language journalism coexists with multilingual broadcasting and digital political debate
Social inclusion
Indigenous and Maroon land interests, migration, education and urban opportunity remain material issues for institutions

Cultural classification: Maroon, Indigenous, Indo-Surinamese, Javanese and other identities describe communities with distinct histories. Language, ancestry, self-identification and citizenship use different statistical definitions.

Government and Institutions

Constitution
The 1987 Constitution as amended underpins Suriname's republican institutions and national legal framework
System of government
Republic with an executive president elected indirectly by the National Assembly under constitutional voting rules
President
Jennifer Geerlings-Simons became president on July 16, 2025, following parliamentary election on July 6 [4]
Vice president
Gregory Rusland was installed as vice president alongside President Geerlings-Simons in July 2025 [4]
Historical significance
The current president is Suriname's first woman head of state and government in the presidential system [4]
Executive branch
The president and cabinet direct national administration, appointments and fiscal policy, subject to law and Assembly oversight
Presidential selection
An initial supermajority parliamentary vote is required; constitutional procedures address failure to achieve the threshold
National Assembly
De Nationale Assemblée is a unicameral legislature with 51 directly elected members [6]
2025 election date
National parliamentary voting took place May 25, 2025 [5]
2025 electoral system
A nationwide electoral constituency replaced earlier district-based allocation for National Assembly seats [5]
Coalition politics
Multiple parties shared the assembly; the election produced a coalition rather than a single-party majority [5]
Assembly leadership
Michael Ashwin Adhin was elected speaker when the new Assembly first convened in June 2025 [5]
Presidential term
The constitutional office is generally filled for five-year cycles, subject to constitutional succession and electoral procedures
Judicial branch
The Court of Justice and subordinate courts apply law, with institutional independence a recurring governance concern
Constitutional review
Constitutional institutions have developed amid efforts to strengthen judicial and administrative oversight
Administrative districts
Ten districts are headed by appointed district commissioners; local participation and resources vary
Local representation
District and resort councils have statutory roles distinct from the central National Assembly
Electoral administration
Independent electoral bodies administer ballots and tabulation under a framework revised before the 2025 vote
Legal pluralism
National law and traditional community leadership intersect in debates over land tenure and customary authority
Indigenous land rights
Collective land claims and access to free, prior and informed consultation remain prominent policy questions
Maroon territory
Riverine communities seek recognition of customary land and resource rights within national legal structures
Natural-resource oversight
Licensing, environmental assessment and public reporting are crucial for mining and offshore petroleum management
Fiscal scrutiny
Parliamentary oversight of contracts, debt and royalties is especially consequential before expected offshore petroleum revenues
Security institutions
Police and national defense bodies have separate public-order and territorial functions under state law
Historic accountability
Public debate over authoritarian-era abuses, trials and political reconciliation remains part of the democratic landscape
Diplomatic administration
Ministries coordinate Caribbean, South American, Dutch and broader global relationships

Institutional update: the 2025 legislature consists of 51 seats, and the president and vice president were installed in July 2025 following a parliamentary selection process. [5] [4]

International Profile

UN membership
Suriname joined the United Nations on December 4, 1975, shortly after independence [2]
CARICOM membership
Full member of the Caribbean Community since July 1995 despite geographic location on South America's mainland [13]
Caribbean identity
Dutch, English-creole and wider Caribbean social relations connect Suriname to Caribbean institutions
South American relationships
Borders and commerce tie the country to Guyana, Brazil and France through French Guiana
Guyana boundary
The upper Courantyne/New River area involves an unresolved territorial disagreement; attribution depends on legal and cartographic sources
French Guiana boundary
The Marowijne/Maroni river system includes stretches with historically disputed tributary interpretation
Brazil relations
South-border links connect rainforest protection, Indigenous communities, gold-mining controls and diplomacy
Netherlands relations
Colonial history, migration, education and finance produce enduring cultural and bilateral connections
UNESCO properties
Three World Heritage sites are inscribed: Central Suriname Nature Reserve, Historic Inner City of Paramaribo, and Jodensavanne [12]
UNESCO categories
The three sites consist of two cultural and one natural property, not three additional categories of the same site [12]
Caribbean climate diplomacy
Coastal flood and sea-level risks create common interests with other low-lying Caribbean states
Guiana Shield conservation
Biodiversity and forest preservation support cooperation across neighboring jurisdictions
Amazon cooperation
Suriname participates in wider Amazonian environmental and river-basin discussions
Global climate role
Exceptionally high forest cover is relevant to carbon accounting, but emissions and forest sequestration depend on methodology [9]
Offshore energy diplomacy
Investment partners, maritime rules and environmental safeguards affect the developing oil economy [10]
Gold-market networks
Refineries, intermediaries and global commodity markets matter more than simple geographic destination rankings
Trade statistics
WTO merchandise figures are based on reported customs categories; services and investment flows are separate [7]
Regional migration
Movement to and from Guyana, Brazil, Caribbean countries and the Netherlands creates labor and integration questions
World Bank engagement
Bank-supported development work addresses poverty, infrastructure and resilience to commodity shocks [9]
IMF relationship
Economic surveillance and previous IMF-backed arrangements have influenced debt and stabilization debates [8]
Geographic time zone
Suriname Time (SRT), UTC−3 throughout the year without general daylight-saving time
Telephone and web
International dialing code +597; country-code internet domain .sr
Flag symbolism
Green and white bands and central yellow star are associated with unity, hope and national identity
Cultural diplomacy
Music, language, sporting achievements and diasporic connections complement formal diplomatic relations

Heritage interpretation: UNESCO lists three Suriname World Heritage properties, including Jodensavanne inscribed in 2023. A named component within a site is not a separate fourth inscription. [12]
