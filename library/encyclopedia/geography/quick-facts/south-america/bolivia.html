Country Overview

Bolivia, officially the Plurinational State of Bolivia, is a landlocked South American republic that extends from the high Andean Altiplano and Lake Titicaca eastward into the Amazon Basin, subtropical valleys and Gran Chaco. Its national history encompasses ancient Andean societies, the powerful Tiwanaku civilization, Inca expansion, Spanish colonial silver mining, nineteenth-century independence and subsequent conflicts over territory and political representation. The constitutional order adopted in 2009 identifies Bolivia as a unitary, plurinational state with regional autonomy and recognition of Indigenous nations and languages. The presidency changed hands following the 2025 election. Mining, hydrocarbons, soybeans, agriculture, commerce and services remain economically significant. Contemporary statistical comparisons are particularly sensitive to the discrepancy between the 2024 national census count and the World Bank 2025 population series, and to different customs and balance-of-payments definitions of merchandise exports.

Identity and State Formation

Official name
Plurinational State of Bolivia (Estado Plurinacional de Bolivia) since the 2009 constitutional order [6]
Constitutional capital
Sucre is the constitutional capital and seat of the Supreme Tribunal of Justice; it is not the usual seat of the national executive [6]
Seat of national government
La Paz hosts the presidency, cabinet and Plurinational Legislative Assembly, making its role distinct from Sucre's [6]
UN geographic region
South America under UN M49; the country's lack of direct ocean access is central to trade geography [10]
Precolonial Andean centers
Tiwanaku developed near Lake Titicaca, alongside many other Andean, Amazonian and lowland political and cultural formations [9]
Aymara and Quechua histories
Aymara-speaking communities, Quechua-speaking societies and many other Indigenous nations predate the modern national state
Inca period
Parts of modern Bolivia formed part of the Inca realm before sixteenth-century Spanish conquest and colonial transformation
Colonial naming
The jurisdiction often called Upper Peru was connected to Spanish imperial administrations, with mining central to fiscal and labor systems
Potosí silver
Cerro Rico and the city of Potosí were major colonial silver-production centers, built upon coercive labor, trade and extensive social change [9]
Independence war
Insurgencies and campaigns beginning in the early nineteenth century culminated in the establishment of a new republic
Independence
August 6, 1825; the republic was named for Simón Bolívar, with Antonio José de Sucre central to the founding military and political settlement [11]
Republican era
Nineteenth-century politics saw disputes over constitutional design, regional power, taxation, Indigenous land and control of mineral resources
War of the Pacific
War with Chile in 1879–1884 and subsequent treaty settlement resulted in Bolivia's loss of Pacific coastal territory
Chaco War
Conflict with Paraguay from 1932 to 1935 led to massive casualties, territorial settlements and major political consequences
1952 National Revolution
The revolution expanded suffrage, transformed mining ownership and launched agrarian reforms, although outcomes differed by community and region
Military and democratic periods
Twentieth-century military governments and authoritarian intervals preceded the durable reestablishment of electoral democracy in the 1980s
2009 Constitution
Plurinational Constitution adopted by referendum in 2009, recognizing multiple Indigenous nations, autonomy arrangements and collective rights [6]
Plurinational constitutional identity
Legal recognition does not make all Indigenous peoples a single homogeneous group; institutions and territorial jurisdictions remain varied [6]
Recent election transition
Rodrigo Paz Pereira won the 2025 presidential election and assumed office November 8, 2025 [7]
National Day
Independence Day, August 6, commemorating the 1825 proclamation
National emblems
Red, yellow and green national tricolor; the wiphala is additionally recognized as a state symbol under the Constitution [6]
United Nations
Bolivia joined the United Nations in 1945 [12]
ISO and country domain
BO (alpha-2), BOL (alpha-3), 068 (numeric); .bo domain [10]

Two capitals in different senses: Sucre is the constitutionally designated capital and seat of major judicial institutions, whereas La Paz is the seat of the executive and legislature. The 1825 republic and the 2009 plurinational refoundation are different historical milestones.

Geography and Population

Physical location
Landlocked central-western South America bordering Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Paraguay and Peru
National surface area
1,098,581 km² in Bolivia's central bank statistical profile; includes diverse highland and lowland landscapes [3]
Highland Altiplano
Upland plateau between Andean ranges includes La Paz, Oruro, the Salar de Uyuni, Lake Titicaca and substantial rural high-altitude settlement
Cordillera Occidental
Western Andean range includes active and dormant volcanoes and high plateau topography
Cordillera Oriental
Eastern Andean belts include steep mountains, basins and valleys where agriculture and settlement concentrate in particular elevations
Highest mountain
Nevado Sajama rises approximately 6,542 metres in the western Andes
Lake Titicaca
Large high-altitude lake shared with Peru, culturally and environmentally important and linked to the Desaguadero system
Salar de Uyuni
Extensive salt flat in Potosí Department, important to tourism, salt extraction and lithium-resource debates
Amazon lowlands
Northern and northeastern tropical landscapes encompass forests, floodplains, cattle ranching, river systems and Indigenous territories
Gran Chaco
Southern and southeastern lowland region contains dry forests, agricultural settlements, hydrocarbon infrastructure and biodiversity pressures
Yungas
Moist eastern Andean slopes exhibit steep environmental gradients and intensive local agricultural activity
Principal rivers
Beni, Mamoré, Madre de Dios, Pilcomayo and Paraguay basin systems; river connectivity spans national boundaries
Drainage basins
Amazon, Río de la Plata and interior Altiplano watersheds have different seasonal and ecological regimes
Climate zones
Tropical lowlands, temperate valleys, alpine mountains and dry high plateaus, with strong local variation by altitude
Climate exposure
Drought, floods, frost, glacier retreat, wildfire and land degradation affect food systems, water resources and infrastructure
Agricultural geography
Eastern Santa Cruz plains contain extensive commercial farming while highlands and valleys support diversified and Indigenous farming systems
2024 national census
11,365,333 residents enumerated in the official 2024 Population and Housing Census, results presented in August 2025 [2]
2025 World Bank population
12,581,843 in the World Bank 2025 annual model, significantly higher than the 2024 census count [1]
Why population figures differ
Different reference years, data sources, coverage and estimation methods make the national direct census and the international annual series noninterchangeable [2] [1]
Population density
Approximately 10.35 per km² using 2024 national census count and national area; 11.45 per km² using the World Bank 2025 population; both derived ratios [2] [3] [1]
Administrative departments
La Paz, Chuquisaca, Cochabamba, Oruro, Potosí, Tarija, Santa Cruz, Beni and Pando [6]
Major urban centers
Santa Cruz de la Sierra, El Alto, La Paz, Cochabamba and Sucre are important metropolitan centers with different economic bases
Settlement distribution
Concentrated cities in the western metropolitan corridors and Santa Cruz coexist with sparsely populated Amazonian and Chaco districts
Census demographic aging
Age 65+ represented about 7.4% of the 2024 census population, compared with 5.0% in 2001 [2]
Life expectancy
About 69 years at birth in the World Bank 2024 series; national conditions vary by access to care and regional inequalities [1]
Hydrocarbon geography
Natural gas fields and pipelines are concentrated in southern and southeastern departments, connected to domestic use and exports
Protected areas
Madidi, Noel Kempff Mercado, Sajama and other protected landscapes encompass globally significant biodiversity [9]

Population comparability: The definitive 2024 census enumerated 11,365,333 people. The World Bank 2025 annual estimate is 12,581,843. The large numerical difference cannot be assigned to migration or natural population change without reconciling reference dates, data collection and modeling.

Economy and International Trade

Economic structure
Mixed economy with mining, natural gas, farming, manufacturing, commerce, logistics and services, alongside state-owned enterprises and informal activity
Nominal GDP
Approximately US$64.8 billion in the 2025 World Bank current-dollar series [1]
GDP per capita
Approximately US$5,148 in 2025 World Bank data; not a measure of average personal earnings [1]
Real GDP growth
Approximately −1.6% in the World Bank 2025 annual series, indicating a contraction [1]
Central-bank national GDP
Banco Central de Bolivia gives preliminary 2025 nominal GDP about US$65.241 billion; its national compilation differs slightly from World Bank conversion/vintage [3]
Price environment
Bolivia recorded substantial inflationary pressures in 2025, with BCB reporting 20.40% accumulated consumer inflation; definitions differ from international annual means [3]
National currency
Boliviano (BOB), symbol Bs; current regulations and operational foreign-exchange access should be checked separately from the formal quoted reference rate [3]
Monetary authority
Banco Central de Bolivia issues currency and publishes monetary, balance-of-payments and reserve statistics [3]
Mining production
Silver, zinc, tin, gold, lead and other minerals are central to visible exports and mining communities
Silver concentrates
Silver-ore concentrates were the largest listed product by 2025 export value in a Spanish government compilation using Bolivian INE data [5]
Zinc and lead
Ore concentrates and associated metals are linked to highland mining, processing and global industrial demand [5]
Gold products
Unwrought gold and jewelry are important reported exports; valuation can change sharply with world precious-metal prices [5]
Tin
Tin mining and smelting have deep historical roots and remain an important source of manufactured mineral exports [5]
Natural gas exports
Long-established gas pipeline connections to neighboring countries; earnings declined as production and price conditions changed [5]
Soy agribusiness
Soybean meal, crude soybean oil and related products support eastern lowland agricultural processing and exports [5]
Brazil nuts and agroforestry
Brazil nuts and other forest products link Amazonian rural households with international food markets [5]
Lithium resources
Salt-flat brines create major strategic interest, but reserves, pilot projects, commercial output and environmental impacts must not be conflated
2025 customs exports
Approximately US$9.701 billion in INE's preliminary 2025 export statistics including customs re-export adjustments [4]
2025 customs imports
Approximately US$10.029 billion in the paired preliminary INE customs import dataset [4]
2025 customs trade balance
Roughly US$328 million goods deficit by subtraction of INE's matched preliminary 2025 export and import values [4]
Different BCB export basis
BCB reported approximately US$9.563 billion FOB goods exports on its balance-of-payments treatment; not the same as INE customs export total [3]
Total goods and services exports
BCB published approximately US$10.775 billion in 2025 goods-and-services exports, wider coverage than merchandise alone [3]
Trade dependence
Mining commodity prices, gas output, fuel imports, foreign-exchange constraints and road disruptions can materially affect trade and supply chains
External market relations
Brazil, Argentina, China and other buyers and suppliers feature in energy, minerals, agricultural and manufactured trade
Informal economy
Small-enterprise, informal and household production are economically important; formal trade totals cannot quantify all domestic activity
Industrial development
Agricultural processing, refining, chemicals, textiles, metalworking and assembly are important but vary widely by region
Development challenges
Access to foreign exchange, investment, logistics, infrastructure, social services and diversified jobs remain significant policy challenges
Tourism services
Tiwanaku, Uyuni, La Paz, Sucre and Amazonian landscapes attract visitors; tourism receipts are services and excluded from customs goods exports

People and Society

Constitutional languages
Spanish and 36 named Indigenous languages are official languages under Article 5 of the 2009 Constitution, though language proficiency and public-sector use vary [6]
Quechua
Widespread Andean language family with substantial cultural and educational presence in highland and valley communities
Aymara
Prominent Indigenous language and cultural tradition across highland Bolivia and adjoining Andean countries
Guaraní and eastern languages
Guaraní, Bésiro and other eastern, Amazonian and Chaco languages belong to distinct language communities [6]
Indigenous governance
Indigenous nations and peoples have varied institutions, communal organizations and legally recognized autonomy claims under the plurinational order
Census self-identification
The 2024 census provides self-identification and linguistic data under specific questions; language ability and national identity are distinct measures [2]
Population composition
Indigenous, mestizo, Afro-Bolivian and other communities have plural social histories, migration patterns and regional identities
Afro-Bolivian heritage
Afro-Bolivian communities maintain distinctive historical experiences and cultural traditions, notably in Yungas districts
Rural–urban mobility
Internal movement to Santa Cruz, El Alto and other cities has reshaped housing, services, employment and social organizations
Religious freedom
Constitution guarantees freedom of religion and spirituality and declares the state independent of religion [6]
Catholic traditions
Roman Catholicism has a long historical role, while present religious practice is diverse and should not be reduced to colonial institutions
Protestant and evangelical communities
Evangelical and Protestant communities have grown across a range of urban and rural districts
Indigenous spiritual traditions
Andean, Amazonian and Chaco spiritual practices coexist with Christian traditions, sometimes within shared or mixed ritual life
Religious-data caution
No single national 2026 religious percentage is asserted here without an identified comparable census or survey series
Education
Public and private schooling, universities and intercultural bilingual programs operate under a national system with varied regional implementation
Healthcare
Access and quality differ by urban–rural geography, institutional coverage and household resources
Regional cuisine
Potatoes, maize, quinoa, Andean camelids, eastern lowland produce and diverse cuisines reflect environmental and cultural variety
Music and festivals
Carnival of Oruro, Andean music, lowland festivals and regional forms of dance have strong local and international influence
Textiles and crafts
Weaving, traditional dress, ceramics, silverwork and other crafts are tied to specific communities, markets and historical traditions
Urban cultural centers
La Paz, Santa Cruz, Cochabamba, Sucre, Potosí and smaller towns host distinct cultural and educational institutions
Social policy priorities
Housing, inflation, rural services, Indigenous land rights, migration, environmental protection and urban employment shape public debate
Poverty and inequality measures
Official and international poverty lines measure different concepts; a single nominal GDP per capita number cannot substitute for household welfare

Religion and language methods: The constitutional list of 37 official languages establishes legal recognition, not actual shares of fluent speakers. Religious affiliation is not treated as a current population percentage in the absence of a directly comparable recent survey.

Government and Institutions

Constitutional structure
Unitary plurinational republic with autonomy arrangements under the Constitution of 2009 [6]
President
Rodrigo Paz Pereira assumed office on November 8, 2025 following the 2025 election; president as of editorial cutoff November 6, 2026 [7]
2025 electoral transition
Rodrigo Paz succeeded Luis Arce after the 2025 vote; election and inauguration are distinct dates [7]
National executive
President and cabinet direct national executive policy under the constitutional system [6]
Vice presidency
Constitution provides for a vice president who also presides over the Plurinational Legislative Assembly; the office is distinct from cabinet ministries [6]
Presidential terms
Constitutional rules define five-year presidential mandates and reelection conditions; legal interpretation and political debates require period-specific context [6]
Legislature
Bicameral Plurinational Legislative Assembly includes Chamber of Deputies and Chamber of Senators [6]
Chamber of Deputies
Constitution sets a chamber of 130 deputies elected through prescribed district and proportional arrangements [6]
Senate
36 senators, four from each of the nine departments, in the constitutional framework [6]
Subnational departments
Nine departments governed by elected departmental authorities in the autonomous territorial system [6]
Other autonomies
Municipal, regional and Indigenous-origin peasant autonomies have defined legal competences and political procedures [6]
Judicial bodies
Supreme Tribunal of Justice, Plurinational Constitutional Tribunal and specialized jurisdictions possess distinct constitutional roles [6]
Judicial elections
Several senior judicial posts are filled under special electoral procedures, which differ from ordinary executive or legislative elections [6]
Electoral oversight
Plurinational Electoral Organ and Supreme Electoral Tribunal administer elections and related legal responsibilities [8]
Constitutional recognition of peoples
Indigenous nations and Afro-Bolivian communities are explicitly recognized in the plurinational framework [6]
Role of Sucre
Supreme Tribunal of Justice is based in Sucre; its constitutional capital status is not a claim that the presidency is located there
Role of La Paz
Presidential offices, cabinet ministries and the national legislature are based in La Paz, separating administrative seat from capital title
Public-sector economic functions
State-owned enterprises and public agencies have major roles in hydrocarbons, infrastructure and resource development
Human-rights institutions
Ombudsperson, courts, legislature and constitutional rights provisions supply domestic oversight and remedies
Governance challenges
Regional autonomy, institutional independence, public accountability, economic stabilization and inclusion remain areas of political contestation
Constitutional change over time
The 2009 refoundation does not erase earlier republican statehood dating to 1825; modern institutions developed through successive constitutional periods

Political transition: Rodrigo Paz assumed office November 8, 2025, after electoral victory that year. Bolivia’s autonomous departments and Indigenous jurisdictions do not constitute separate sovereign countries; their competences derive from the national constitutional framework.

International Profile

United Nations
Bolivia is a longstanding UN member, participating in global diplomacy and international-development institutions [12]
Andean Community
Member of the Andean Community with regional trade and regulatory cooperation arrangements [13]
MERCOSUR relationship
Bolivia entered a process of fuller MERCOSUR membership with a transition for its treaty and trade obligations; status and deadlines should be stated by date [14]
World Trade Organization
Member of the WTO and participant in global rules for merchandise and services trade [15]
Organization of American States
OAS member involved in hemispheric diplomacy and electoral and legal issues
South American land borders
Borders Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Paraguay and Peru; trade corridors require international transit cooperation
Chile and maritime access
The loss of a Pacific coast in the nineteenth century underpins a long-running diplomatic dispute and Bolivian claims for sovereign ocean access
International Court of Justice
The 2018 ICJ ruling found no legal duty for Chile to negotiate sovereign access in the particular claim litigated; it did not settle all bilateral policy questions [16]
Paraguay relations
Chaco War history and contemporary roads, agriculture and energy connections shape the bilateral relationship
Brazil relationship
Natural gas pipelines and commercial ties make Brazil a significant external economic partner
Argentina relationship
Gas, transit, migration and regional trade have linked Bolivia with Argentina over many decades
China relationship
Chinese demand for minerals and manufactured exports, and Chinese supplied equipment, feature in trade and investment
Mining supply chains
Silver, zinc, tin, gold and lithium developments tie Bolivia to global raw-material and processing markets
UNESCO World Heritage
Seven inscribed properties, including Potosí, Tiwanaku, the Jesuit Missions of Chiquitos and Noel Kempff Mercado National Park [9]
Cultural-diplomatic identity
Indigenous arts, Andean heritage and national festivals are part of broader diplomacy and tourism
Climate negotiations
Tropical-forest conservation, glaciers, vulnerable water supplies and commodity-linked deforestation inform environmental policy
Multilateral lenders
World Bank, IMF, Inter-American Development Bank and Andean financial institutions publish differing program and data definitions
International telephone code
+591; internet country-code domain .bo
Time-zone convention
Bolivia Time UTC−4 nationwide with no routine seasonal daylight-saving schedule

Maritime status: Bolivia is landlocked. Longstanding demands concerning access to the Pacific should be identified as political and legal claims; the 2018 ICJ decision is not a grant of sovereign coastal territory.
