Country Overview

Costa Rica is a Central American republic between Nicaragua and Panama, with coastlines on the Caribbean Sea and the Pacific Ocean. It is known internationally for biodiversity conservation, extensive protected areas, renewable-electricity development and a constitutional prohibition on a permanent army. Indigenous societies existed before the Spanish colonial period; independence was declared in 1821 as part of the wider Central American political transformation, while the modern republic was established in 1848. The country’s present constitutional framework dates to 1949. Agriculture and tourism remain important, but manufacturing and international services—especially medical devices and business services—now form major parts of its export economy. The president and unicameral legislature are directly connected to a four-year electoral cycle.

Identity and State Formation

Official name
Republic of Costa Rica (República de Costa Rica)
Common name
Costa Rica; Spanish name means Rich Coast, reflecting terminology from the Spanish colonial era
Capital city
San José, in the central highland region and the country's principal metropolitan area [2]
Continent / UN M49 subregion
North America in the MGU continent index; Americas / Central America in United Nations M49 classification [10]
Precolonial communities
Indigenous societies—including ancestors of today's Bribri, Cabécar, Maleku and other peoples—preceded Spanish invasion and settlement
Spanish colonial period
The territory was incorporated into Spain's Central American colonial system; settlement and economic conditions differed between the Central Valley and peripheral areas
Separation from Spain
September 15, 1821 is celebrated as Independence Day, reflecting Central America's break with Spanish colonial rule
Early post-independence politics
The territory passed through shifting Central American constitutional arrangements, including participation in the Federal Republic of Central America
Republican proclamation
August 31, 1848: Costa Rica was declared a sovereign republic, distinct from the earlier 1821 independence milestone
Late nineteenth-century transformations
Coffee production, infrastructure investment and export commerce reshaped national institutions, landholding and external relations
1948 civil conflict
A short civil war was followed by a founding political settlement and constitutional reconstruction
Abolition of the permanent army
December 1, 1948 is the traditional commemorative date; prohibition of a permanent army was incorporated into Article 12 of the 1949 Constitution [8]
Current Constitution
Constitution of November 7, 1949, the basic framework for contemporary republican government [8]
National Independence Day
September 15; observed throughout the Central American region, with country-specific national celebrations
National symbols
National flag has red, white and blue horizontal bands; the yigüirro is the national bird, and the guaria morada the national flower [5]
United Nations admission
November 2, 1945; earlier than the present 1949 constitutional framework [2]
ISO 3166-1 codes
CR (alpha-2), CRI (alpha-3), and 188 (numeric) [10]
Internet country-code domain
.cr; the country uses the international dialing code +506

Historical milestones are distinct: September 1821 independence from Spain, August 1848 declaration as a republic, abolition of the standing army in 1948 and the 1949 Constitution each refer to separate political events. None should be collapsed into a single founding date.

Geography and Population

Country region
Central America, bounded by Nicaragua to the north, Panama to the southeast, Caribbean Sea to the northeast and Pacific Ocean to the southwest
Territorial area
51,100 km² in the United Nations statistical profile; the figure is a national surface-area convention [2]
World Bank population
5,152,950 for 2025 in the annual country series, not a new national population census [1]
Population density
Roughly 100.8 people per km² calculated from 2025 World Bank population and the United Nations total-area figure [1] [2]
Population growth
Modest demographic growth in the recent period; specific annual rates are available as separately dated World Bank indicators [1]
Administrative provinces
Seven provinces: San José, Alajuela, Cartago, Heredia, Guanacaste, Puntarenas and Limón
Local government
Provinces are subdivided into cantons and districts; local municipalities govern defined competencies within the national framework
Principal urban areas
San José metropolitan region, Alajuela, Cartago, Heredia, Liberia, Puntarenas and Limón
Central Valley
Important concentration of population, institutions and industry, surrounded by volcanic and mountain systems
Mountain ranges
Guanacaste, Tilarán, Central Volcanic and Talamanca ranges are principal upland systems
Highest summit
Cerro Chirripó, approximately 3,820 metres above sea level, within the Talamanca mountain region
Volcanoes
Poás, Irazú, Arenal, Turrialba and other volcanic systems shape landscapes, environmental risks and tourism
Principal rivers
Reventazón, Tempisque, San Juan border area and other Pacific and Caribbean watershed systems
Coastlines
Both Caribbean and Pacific marine environments, with ports, mangroves, estuaries and varied coastal settlement
Offshore territory
Cocos Island lies far into the eastern Pacific and is part of Costa Rica's territory and marine conservation responsibilities
Climate
Tropical lowland conditions vary with elevation, mountain rain shadows, Caribbean rainfall and seasonal Pacific precipitation
Protected nature
National parks, biological reserves and other protected areas conserve diverse forests, wetlands, beaches and marine systems [9]
Biodiversity significance
High diversity of flora and fauna relative to national area, supported by geographic, climatic and elevation gradients
Natural hazards
Earthquakes, volcanic activity, landslides, floods, storms and climate-linked coastal pressures affect infrastructure and communities
Environmental policies
Forest recovery, conservation finance, electricity generation and climate adaptation are important policy fields, though not every energy use is renewable

Territorial comparison: The 51,100 km² reported by the United Nations is a country-wide area convention. An internal density calculation using 2025 World Bank population is not a measured average for the densely settled Central Valley, sparsely settled protected areas or distant Cocos Island.

Economy and International Trade

Development profile
Upper-middle- to high-income transition economy with strong international manufacturing and service links, and substantial environmental and social-policy investment
Nominal GDP
Approximately US$102.9 billion for 2025 in the World Bank current-dollar series [1]
GDP per capita
Approximately US$19,970 in 2025 current U.S. dollars; this is national GDP divided by resident population, not a measure of household pay [1]
Real GDP growth
4.6% for calendar 2025 in the World Bank country series [1]
Consumer-price change
About −0.1% for the annual 2025 World Bank inflation indicator, reflecting near-flat average price changes under that definition [1]
Modeled unemployment
6.8% for 2025 in the ILO-modeled measure reported through the World Bank; domestic labor-survey estimates may differ [1]
National currency
Costa Rican colón (CRC), represented by the symbol ₡ and used for domestic monetary transactions
Central bank
Banco Central de Costa Rica, responsible for monetary-policy and currency functions
Economic sectors
Medical devices and precision manufacturing, business services, information technology, tourism, agriculture, food processing, retail and logistics
Goods exports, revised annual series
US$22.731 billion for 2025 in an April 8, 2026 COMEX release using PROCOMER reporting; goods only [3]
Earlier 2025 goods estimate
A January 2026 preliminary COMEX release cited US$22.855 billion; the April release gives a later figure, showing revision effects [3] [4]
Services exports, stated coverage
US$12.512 billion for 2025 in the COMEX release, specifically non-travel services; it is not an all-in figure for tourism plus other services [3]
Published combined subtotal
COMEX reports US$35.243 billion for goods plus non-travel services in 2025; this is not identical to all exports including travel [3]
Leading manufactured exports
Medical devices and precision equipment occupy a major share of goods-export earnings, aided by foreign investment and specialized supply chains [3]
Agricultural exports
Pineapples, bananas, coffee and related agroindustrial food products remain major external trade items [3]
Business-services exports
Corporate support, information technology and knowledge-intensive services form an expanding portion of non-travel service trade [3]
Tourism receipts
Travel is a separate service category and should not be added to goods exports without using compatible balance-of-payments accounting [3]
Main goods market
The United States is a leading goods-export destination and significant investment and tourism partner
Other trading relationships
European Union, Central American neighbors, Mexico and Asian partners are important for commerce and specialized inputs
Free-trade arrangements
Central American regional and bilateral trade frameworks, CAFTA-DR and other agreements influence access to export markets
Economic vulnerabilities
External demand for medical devices, global travel, exchange-rate movements, supply-chain inputs and climate-related agriculture disruptions influence output
Development priorities
Productivity, public finances, education, transport, decarbonization, export upgrading and access to decent employment are recurrent issues

People and Society

Official language
Spanish is the language of nationwide public institutions and general government communication
Indigenous languages
Bribri, Cabécar, Maleku, Ngäbere and other Indigenous languages have distinct communities and preservation needs
Afro-Caribbean heritage
English-based Limonese Creole and Afro-Caribbean traditions have historical roots particularly in Limón and neighboring Caribbean districts
Other spoken languages
English is used in tourism, technology and some professional settings; immigration adds additional languages and traditions
Constitutional religious status
Roman Catholicism has the status of state religion under Article 75 of the Constitution, alongside religious freedom protections [8]
Actual religious affiliation
Catholic, Protestant/Evangelical, other religious and nonreligious communities are present; no invented 2026 census percentages are assigned to these categories
Church–state distinction
Formal constitutional support for the Catholic religion is not proof that every resident identifies with or practices Catholicism [8]
Indigenous peoples
Distinct territorial, linguistic and governance traditions remain important to cultural rights and public policy
Demographic change
Fertility decline, population aging and internal mobility affect education, health and labor-force planning
Education system
Public primary, secondary and university institutions are complemented by private institutions and technical training; quality and access vary
Health system
National social-insurance and public health institutions, including the Caja Costarricense de Seguro Social, play central roles in health care delivery
Cultural practices
Traditional crafts, foodways, music, rural agricultural practices, soccer and regional festivities contribute to national identity
Environmental identity
Ecotourism and conservation are important national economic narratives, while land-use conflicts and local environmental pressures remain real
Settlement patterns
Central Valley urbanization coexists with coastal towns, farming districts, Indigenous territories and more remote border settlements
Societal priorities
Public security, inequality, employment access, migrant integration, affordable housing and services are major policy discussions

Religious interpretation: Article 75 creates a legal status for Roman Catholicism while protecting other religious worship; it does not constitute a current statistical census of affiliation. Indigenous language and culture facts should not be conflated with the official use of Spanish.

Government and Institutions

Form of government
Unitary presidential constitutional republic, with elected executive and separate legislative and judicial branches [8]
Constitution
Political Constitution of November 7, 1949, as subsequently amended [8]
President of the Republic
Laura Fernández Delgado, in office for the 2026–2030 constitutional period after the February 2026 election [5] [6]
Presidential inauguration
May 8, 2026, the start of the four-year constitutional term [6]
First Vice President
Francisco Ernesto Gamboa Soto, elected on the 2026 presidential ticket [6]
Second Vice President
Douglas Soto Campos, elected on the same ticket [6]
Executive cabinet
President and appointed ministers direct the national executive administration and policy implementation [5]
Legislature
Unicameral Legislative Assembly of 57 deputies, elected for four-year terms [7]
Legislative election
Deputies are elected on a provincial basis under the electoral rules; legislative terms differ in responsibilities from presidential office [7]
Judiciary
Supreme Court of Justice and lower courts exercise judicial powers subject to constitutional law
Supreme Electoral Tribunal
Independent constitutional electoral authority administers national elections and certifies the official results [6]
Administrative divisions
Seven provinces subdivided into cantons and districts; cantonal municipalities manage local responsibilities
Permanent military forces
Article 12 of the Constitution prohibits a permanent army as a standing institution, while maintaining provision for public-security and exceptional defense arrangements [8]
Civilian security
Police, border, maritime and other security bodies have statutory responsibilities; the absence of a standing army does not mean absence of security forces
Constitutional religion provisions
Article 75 recognizes Roman Catholicism as the state religion while requiring protection of other religious worship [8]
Democratic succession
The presidential transition from the 2022–2026 administration to Laura Fernández occurred in May 2026, not at the date of the February election [6] [5]

Security-system interpretation: Costa Rica did not eliminate all uniformed security services. Constitutional Article 12 bans a permanent army while civilian police and other security authorities perform legally authorized functions.
