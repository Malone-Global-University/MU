Country Overview

The Republic of Trinidad and Tobago is a sovereign two-island state at the southern end of the Caribbean archipelago, immediately northeast of Venezuela. Trinidad, the larger and more industrialized island, anchors much of the national population, government, petroleum and natural-gas economy, while Tobago has its own elected House of Assembly and a stronger tourism-and-marine orientation. Indigenous communities predated European conquest; Spanish rule, British colonialism, African slavery, post-emancipation Indian indentured labor and later migrations shaped a particularly diverse Caribbean society. The country gained independence from Britain on August 31, 1962 and became a parliamentary republic in 1976. Its economy combines energy extraction and the manufacture of liquefied natural gas and petrochemicals with services, food production, maritime activity, culture and tourism. Contemporary statistics require care: the national population estimate and World Bank series are both approximately 1.37 million for 2025, whereas UN demographic projections use a substantially different population series. Energy merchandise exports are not the same as services receipts, and Tobago’s devolved institutions operate within a single sovereign republic.

Identity and State Formation

Official name
Republic of Trinidad and Tobago; a unitary state with constitutionally recognized arrangements for Tobago [6]
Common name and demonym
Trinidad and Tobago; citizens are Trinidadians and Tobagonians, with regional identities also reflected in national usage
National capital
Port of Spain on Trinidad; the central legislature, presidential residence and national executive institutions are based there [5]
Continental and subregional placement
North America in the MGU country directory; Caribbean under the United Nations M49 geographic standard [23]
Earlier Indigenous societies
Archaeological evidence documents centuries of settlement and contact with the South American mainland; Indigenous communities did not disappear from history when European rule began
Spanish conquest and colonization
Spain asserted authority over Trinidad from the late fifteenth century; the pattern of settlement, landholding and labor changed significantly under later colonial administrations [21]
European competition over Tobago
Tobago changed hands among competing European colonial powers before the island was consolidated under British imperial rule
British acquisition of Trinidad
British forces captured Trinidad in 1797; the 1802 Treaty of Amiens confirmed the transfer from Spain to Britain [21]
British rule over Tobago
Tobago passed into British rule through eighteenth- and nineteenth-century imperial settlements; its history differed from Trinidad’s earlier Spanish governance
African enslavement
Enslaved Africans were exploited in sugar and other plantation production; plantation institutions had lasting effects on land, labor, culture and inequalities
Emancipation and apprenticeship
British abolition legislation took effect in the 1830s; full emancipation followed the end of apprenticeship in 1838, without ending the economic disadvantages of former enslaved workers
Indian indentureship
Contracted labor migration from India began in 1845 and continued until 1917, profoundly influencing religion, foodways, language, festivals and demography [21]
Administrative union
Trinidad and Tobago were joined under one British colonial administration in 1889; this union was not an act of independence [21]
West Indies Federation
The territory participated in the short-lived West Indies Federation from 1958 until 1962, preceding its separate independence
Internal self-government
Constitutional and electoral changes in the 1940s–1960s expanded representative government and ministerial responsibility
Independence
August 31, 1962: Trinidad and Tobago became a sovereign state independent from the United Kingdom [7] [4]
First prime minister
Eric Williams, founder of the People's National Movement, served as head of government from independence until his death in 1981 [7]
Republican constitution
A republican Constitution came into effect on August 1, 1976; the Queen ceased to be head of state and a locally chosen President replaced the governor-general [6]
Republic Day
September 24 is observed as Republic Day; it commemorates the republican institutional milestone, rather than the original 1962 independence date [6]
National flag and symbolism
Red field crossed by a black diagonal stripe edged in white; the national flag was adopted at independence, while the coat of arms includes the scarlet ibis and cocrico
National holidays
Independence Day on August 31 and Republic Day on September 24 are distinct state holidays; religious and cultural holidays also form part of the calendar
UN admission
Trinidad and Tobago joined the United Nations on September 18, 1962, shortly after independence [4]
ISO and internet identifiers
TT (alpha-2); TTO (alpha-3); 780 (numeric); .tt country-code domain [23]

Historical sequence: Spanish and British colonial rule, African enslavement, Indian indentured labor and the 1889 union preceded independence in 1962. August 1, 1976 is the effective date of the republican Constitution; September 24 is the annual Republic Day observance. These are distinct legal and commemorative milestones.

Geography and Population

Regional location
Southern Lesser Antilles between the Caribbean Sea and Atlantic Ocean, just northeast of Venezuela and south of Grenada [5]
Territory and islands
Two main islands, Trinidad and Tobago, and a number of smaller coastal islands; no international land frontier [5]
Area: national convention
5,128 km² in the Ministry of Foreign and CARICOM Affairs profile; UNData publishes a slightly different 5,127 km² surface-area figure [5] [4]
Island-area distinction
Trinidad accounts for approximately 4,828 km² and Tobago around 300 km²; these rounded components should not be mistaken for precisely mapped subnational surveys [17]
Main island
Trinidad has the greater population, most industrial installations and the major east–west and north–south transportation corridors [5]
Tobago
Northeastern island with a prominent Main Ridge, forest reserve, coastal communities and beaches; Scarborough is its principal administrative town [12]
Other islets
The Bocas, Five Islands and other small islands support protected habitats, shipping navigation, recreation or conservation
Highest point
Cerro del Aripo in Trinidad’s Northern Range rises about 940 metres above sea level [17]
Northern Range
Mountain belt along the north of Trinidad with forested catchments, steep valleys and important biodiversity [17]
Central and southern Trinidad
Plains, hills, agricultural districts, energy facilities and urban development create a contrasting landscape beyond the Northern Range
Tobago Main Ridge
Forested interior ridge with montane habitat; the Main Ridge Forest Reserve is a long-established protected area [17]
Gulf of Paria
Sheltered waters between western Trinidad and Venezuela are central to shipping, fishing, hydrocarbon activity and port development
Major watersheds
Caroni, Ortoire and other river systems drain the island’s highlands, lowlands and agricultural districts
Wetlands and mangroves
Caroni Swamp and coastal mangrove zones provide wildlife habitat, fisheries support, flood buffering and carbon storage
Coral reef geography
Buccoo Reef, Speyside and associated Tobago marine areas are important for biodiversity, recreation and tourism; coral ecosystems face climate and human pressures
La Brea Pitch Lake
Notable natural asphalt lake in southwest Trinidad, linked to petroleum geology and industrial history; on UNESCO’s tentative list [18]
Climate
Tropical maritime climate with wet and dry seasons, strong local differences in precipitation and mountain exposure, and year-round warm temperatures
Hurricane exposure
The islands lie near the southern edge of the usual Atlantic hurricane belt, but tropical storms, intense rain, surge and severe winds remain hazards
Other natural hazards
Flooding, landslides, coastal erosion, seismic activity and climate-related sea-level rise affect settlement and infrastructure planning
2011 census benchmark
1,328,019 usual residents enumerated in the January 2011 national Population and Housing Census; a direct count, not a 2025 estimate [3]
2025 CSO mid-year estimate
1,367,764 persons as at June 30, 2025, according to the Central Statistical Office’s age-and-sex estimates [2]
2025 World Bank population
1,367,764 in the World Bank annual population series; the displayed figure coincides with the CSO total in this release [1]
UN demographic-model distinction
UNData lists about 1.511 million residents in its separate 2025 UN projection series; it is not the same series as the CSO mid-year figure [4] [2]
Approximate population density
Roughly 267 persons per km² using 1,367,764 divided by the national 5,128-km² area; this is a calculated ratio, not a fresh census density [2] [5]
Principal population centers
Port of Spain, Chaguanas, San Fernando, Arima, Point Fortin and the greater East–West Corridor; Tobago settlements include Scarborough
Air and sea gateways
Piarco International Airport on Trinidad and A.N.R. Robinson International Airport on Tobago, with commercial ports and inter-island ferries
Natural resources
Natural gas, petroleum, asphalt, marine ecosystems, fisheries, fertile agricultural land and biodiversity underpin divergent development opportunities
Development and conservation
Coastal construction, water supply, wildfire and flood management, and the environmental legacy of extractive industries are ongoing planning concerns

Population comparability: The 2011 census enumerated 1,328,019 usual residents, and the CSO/World Bank 2025 series reports 1,367,764. The UNData 2025 estimate is roughly 1.511 million, reflecting a separate population methodology. No difference between the modelled series should be described as measured migration without reconciliation.

Economy and International Trade

Development profile
Upper-middle/high-income regional industrial economy with a comparatively large natural-gas and petrochemical export base; output remains sensitive to production and global prices
Nominal GDP
Approximately US$25.94 billion in calendar 2025, World Bank current U.S.-dollar series [1]
GDP per capita
Approximately US$18,967 in 2025, measured as national output per resident; it is not median salary or household disposable income [1]
Real GDP growth
−0.8% for 2025 in the current World Bank annual series; revised statistical releases and forecast vintages may differ [1]
2025 consumer-price inflation
1.0% in the World Bank 2025 annual consumer-price measure; price dynamics vary by food, housing and imported inputs [1]
Modeled unemployment
3.3% for 2025 in the World Bank’s ILO-modeled unemployment indicator; not necessarily identical to a quarterly national labor-force estimate [1]
Currency and monetary authority
Trinidad and Tobago dollar (TTD), issued by the Central Bank of Trinidad and Tobago [4] [22]
Exchange-rate reference
UNData’s reported reference is about TT$6.8 per US dollar; this is a statistical exchange-rate observation rather than a promise of unlimited currency convertibility [4]
Energy-industry structure
Offshore and onshore natural gas and petroleum production, gas processing, liquefied natural gas and downstream chemicals anchor the export economy [13]
Natural-gas production
Natural gas feeds electricity, LNG export trains and industrial inputs including ammonia and methanol; upstream supply is critical to downstream utilization
Liquefied natural gas
Atlantic LNG facilities process natural gas for international sale; cargo values vary with contracts, output, shipping and benchmark prices
Petrochemicals
Ammonia, urea and methanol make the country a significant industrial exporter beyond crude oil and refined fuels
Oil sector
Crude petroleum production, storage, petroleum services and associated infrastructure contribute to revenues alongside the gas-focused supply chain
Energy-related fiscal risks
Hydrocarbon output, tax receipts, foreign exchange receipts and investment decisions are linked to volatile global prices and aging fields
Non-energy manufacturing
Food and beverages, chemicals, plastics, cement and other manufactured goods complement hydrocarbon exports and supply Caribbean markets
Agricultural production
Domestic farms produce vegetables, fruit, cocoa and other goods; food imports and farm productivity are persistent policy subjects
Cocoa heritage
Trinidad’s fine-flavor cocoa heritage, including the Trinitario type, connects estate history, specialized agriculture and international chocolate value chains
Services economy
Wholesale trade, finance, professional services, logistics, education, health, public administration and communications create substantial domestic value
Tourism differences
Tobago relies heavily on tourism and related local services; Trinidad hosts business travel, cultural events and other visitor activities
Industrial sites
Point Lisas industrial estate, Port of Spain port facilities and energy installations support production and international commerce
2024 merchandise exports
US$10.0191 billion for calendar year 2024 in the Ministry of Finance Review of the Economy’s external-accounts discussion; not a reported full-year 2025 figure [13]
2024 energy exports
US$8.0014 billion in the same calendar 2024 ministerial series, about four-fifths of that merchandise export total [13]
2024 non-energy merchandise exports
US$2.0177 billion reported in the Ministry of Finance 2025 Review of the Economy, up 5.2% from 2023; distinct from the energy goods subtotal [13]
2024 visible-trade exports
TT$64.6798 billion in the Ministry of Finance visible trade appendix for 2024 (provisional), a distinct currency and statistical presentation [14]
2024 visible-trade imports
TT$48.4524 billion in the same provisional 2024 visible-trade table; these are goods, not imports of services [14]
2024 visible-trade balance
TT$16.2274 billion surplus, calculated and reported on the same visible-trade table’s paired exports and imports [14]
2024–25 partial-year trade
TT$38.8364 billion in exports and TT$32.9623 billion in imports for October 2024–June 2025 (nine months), provisional; not annual 2025 totals [14]
2024 current-account balance
About US$1.233 billion surplus in the Ministry of Finance’s 2025 Review of the Economy, including goods, services and other current-account transactions [13]
Goods versus services
Natural-gas and chemical shipments are merchandise exports; travel, air and maritime transport, and financial or professional services are recorded separately
Imports and supply chains
Food, capital machinery, industrial feedstocks, consumer goods, vehicles and manufactured inputs enter through import channels
Main external markets
Trading relationships include the United States, CARICOM markets, European markets and industrial energy importers; relative ranks vary across goods types and years
CARICOM goods trade
Trinidad and Tobago supplies fuels and a diverse range of manufactured products to nearby CARICOM economies; the regional account is distinct from the worldwide goods ledger [15]
Industrial transition
Renewable energy, value-added manufacturing, gas supply security and wider diversification are central economic-policy questions
Fiscal and external accounts
Public borrowing, heritage-fund management, exchange-market access and the balance of payments should be assessed separately from headline GDP growth

People and Society

Principal language
English is the official language of administration, legislation, schooling and national public communication
Everyday speech
Trinidadian English Creole and Tobagonian English Creole are common in informal settings, with differences across communities and regions
Historical multilingualism
Trinidad Hindustani, Bhojpuri-derived heritage speech, French Creole and Spanish have historical or contemporary influence; current fluency cannot be inferred from cultural origins
Indigenous heritage
The First Peoples’ presence and continuing cultural revival, including the Santa Rosa First Peoples Community, form part of national identity
African-descended communities
Histories of slavery, emancipation, migration and cultural innovation shape Afro-Trinidadian and Afro-Tobagonian life
Indian-descended communities
Descendants of nineteenth- and early twentieth-century indentured laborers and later migrants contribute to religious, culinary, musical and civic institutions
Other ancestral communities
Mixed-heritage, European-, Chinese-, Syrian/Lebanese- and other communities reflect centuries of Caribbean trade and settlement
Religion: census context
Detailed national religion percentages commonly cited in public references derive from the 2011 Census; they are not current 2026 measures [16]
Roman Catholic identification
285,671 persons, about 21.6% of 2011 non-institutional census respondents, identified with Roman Catholicism [16]
Hindu identification
240,100 persons, about 18.2% of the 2011 non-institutional census population, reported Hinduism [16]
Muslim identification
65,705 persons, about 5.0% of the 2011 non-institutional census population, identified with Islam [16]
Pentecostal and evangelical
159,033 persons, approximately 12.0% of the 2011 non-institutional population, were in the Pentecostal, Evangelical or Full Gospel category [16]
Spiritual Baptist heritage
The Baptist–Spiritual Shouter census category included 75,002 persons in 2011; the religious tradition has a distinct national cultural and political history [16]
Religion and non-response
The 2011 figures include 146,798 religion responses classified as not stated, a material share that prevents simplistic addition of selected groups into a current national total [16]
Carnival
Annual pre-Lenten Carnival is a major public performance tradition involving masquerade, calypso, soca, costumes, music, craft production and tourism [19]
Steelpan and steelband
The steelpan developed in Trinidad during the twentieth century and is a distinctive acoustical instrument and educational and performance tradition [19]
Music and literature
Calypso, soca, chutney, rapso, jazz, literature, spoken-word performance and theater reflect cultural interactions across the islands
Religious holidays
Divali, Eid-ul-Fitr, Christmas and Easter are nationally important; the public calendar also recognizes Spiritual Baptist Liberation Day
Cuisine
Doubles, roti, pelau, callaloo, bake and shark, curry dishes and Tobago specialties reflect African, Indian, Indigenous and wider Caribbean histories
Schooling
Universal public education and substantial state-supported and denominational schooling coexist; the University of the West Indies St. Augustine campus is a principal tertiary institution
Healthcare
Public health services operate through national ministries and regional health authorities, alongside private providers and specialized institutions
Population aging
Central Statistical Office’s 2025 estimate includes 122,587 residents aged 65 and older, with 281,598 aged under 15; these age categories are not census shares [2]
International migration
Diaspora communities are present especially in North America and the United Kingdom, while regional migration and Venezuelan displacement affect society
Local identity
Tobago has distinctive speech, community histories, performing arts and administrative traditions within one republic; island identities should not be collapsed into a single stereotype
Sports and recreation
Cricket, football, athletics and aquatic sports are prominent, with internationally recognized athletes and active community organizations
Social challenges
Crime prevention, inequality, access to housing and education, youth opportunity and disaster resilience are material public-policy concerns

Religion and census coverage: Catholic, Hindu, Muslim and other religious shares above derive from the 2011 non-institutional census population (1,322,546), not the larger 2011 total population or the 2025 population estimate. Religion reported in a census measures identification, not actual observance.

Government and Institutions

Constitutional order
Unitary parliamentary republic under a written republican Constitution, effective from August 1, 1976, with subsequent amendments [6]
Head of state
President Christine Carla Kangaloo, in office since March 20, 2023; the presidency is distinct from the executive role of the prime minister [11]
Head of government
Prime Minister Kamla Persad-Bissessar, sworn in May 1, 2025 following the April general election [10] [9]
Executive and Cabinet
The prime minister heads the Cabinet and is normally supported by the confidence of a majority in the elected House of Representatives [7]
Selection of president
President is chosen by an electoral college consisting of members of both chambers of Parliament under constitutional procedure; not directly elected by popular vote [6]
National Parliament
Bicameral national legislature consisting of the House of Representatives and the Senate, alongside the constitutional role of the President [7]
House constituencies
Forty-one directly elected geographic constituencies contested in the April 28, 2025 election; the separately selected Speaker may count as an additional House member [9] [7]
Senate membership
Thirty-one appointed senators: sixteen on advice of the prime minister, six on advice of opposition leader and nine independent senators appointed at presidential discretion [8]
Electoral system
First-past-the-post single-member election of constituency representatives; general elections subject to the constitutional life of Parliament and any earlier dissolution [7]
April 2025 election
United National Congress won 26 of the 41 elected House seats; the People's National Movement won 13; Tobago People's Party held the remaining two [9]
2025 transfer of power
Kamla Persad-Bissessar returned as prime minister on May 1, 2025, succeeding the short-serving Stuart Young administration [9] [10]
Speaker of the House
Jagdeo Singh was elected Speaker on May 23, 2025, and may be counted separately from the 41 directly elected MPs [9]
President of the Senate
Wade Mark was elected President of the Senate on May 23, 2025; temporary acting presidential duties do not change the titular elected national president [9]
Judicial system
Supreme Court structure includes High Court and Court of Appeal; the Caribbean Court of Justice has an original CARICOM jurisdiction, while the Privy Council remains the final appellate court in national cases
Local government on Trinidad
Municipal corporations administer cities, boroughs and regional areas, with public functions distinct from central ministries
Status of Tobago
Tobago has an elected House of Assembly and executive departments with devolved administrative responsibilities under the Tobago House of Assembly Act, 1996 [12]
Tobago House of Assembly election
January 12, 2026 elections renewed the Tobago House of Assembly; inauguration occurred January 15, 2026 [12]
Tobago Assembly composition
The January 2026 inauguration involved fifteen elected assembly members and three appointed councillors; these are distinct from national Parliament seats [12]
Tobago Chief Secretary
Farley Chavez Augustine leads the Tobago House of Assembly Executive Council in the 2026–2030 term [12]
Tobago Deputy Chief Secretary
Faith Brebnor was sworn in as Deputy Chief Secretary in January 2026 [12]
Tobago executive responsibilities
Portfolios include tourism, community development, agriculture, fisheries, health coordination and public works within the national legal framework [12]
National defense
Trinidad and Tobago Defence Force includes army, coast guard, air guard and reserve elements supporting security and disaster response
Public accountability
Independent offices and oversight processes include an Auditor General, Ombudsman, electoral authorities, courts and parliamentary committees
Human rights and citizenship
Constitutional rights, citizenship law, judicial remedies and electoral participation are governed by national legal rules and institutions
Governmental priorities
Public safety, fiscal management, health, infrastructure, energy reform, migration and Tobago’s administrative autonomy remain important policy areas

Parliament and Tobago: The 2025 general election contested 41 elected national House seats, although an externally selected Speaker can bring the membership count to 42. Tobago’s 2026 Assembly comprises 15 elected representatives and three appointed councillors and is not a second sovereign legislature.

International Profile

United Nations membership
Admitted September 18, 1962; its UN membership followed independence by less than three weeks [4]
CARICOM membership
Founding member of the Caribbean Community and participant in regional political, trade and economic coordination [20]
Founding Treaty of Chaguaramas
The original 1973 Caribbean Community treaty was signed in Chaguaramas, Trinidad and Tobago, a landmark in Caribbean regional integration [5]
CARICOM Single Market
Participates in regional arrangements for qualifying movement of goods, services, capital and designated categories of skilled labor [20]
World Trade Organization
Member of the multilateral trading system; energy, non-energy manufacturing and customs policy interact with WTO rules [24]
Commonwealth
Member of the Commonwealth of Nations despite becoming a republic in 1976; membership does not imply allegiance to the British monarch
Association of Caribbean States
Participates in Caribbean Sea regional consultation concerning trade, shipping, disasters and sustainable development
Organization of American States
Member of the OAS, which supports diplomatic dialogue, electoral and regional governance initiatives
Multilateral financial institutions
Works with the IMF, World Bank, Caribbean Development Bank and Inter-American Development Bank on economic and policy issues
United States links
Energy trade, diaspora networks, educational ties, investment, financial regulation and security cooperation make the United States an important partner
Venezuela relationship
Nearby maritime borders and migration flows give Venezuela strategic relevance to border management, humanitarian policy and energy cooperation
Energy diplomacy
Natural gas, LNG, ammonia and methanol link the country to global industrial importers and pricing cycles
Caribbean industrial exports
Refined petroleum and chemical goods supply CARICOM economies, but trade statistics must distinguish primary exports from re-export and transit flows [15]
Climate diplomacy
Small-island conditions, coastal flooding and industrial emissions shape policy on adaptation, mitigation, finance and disaster resilience
UNESCO World Heritage status
Zero inscribed World Heritage properties as of the 2026 UNESCO State Party listing; proposed sites are not inscriptions [18]
UNESCO tentative-list sites
Banwari Trace Archaeological Site, La Brea Pitch Lake and Tobago Main Ridge Forest Reserve are on the tentative list [18]
Cultural diplomacy
Steelpan music, Carnival and the diaspora contribute to cultural exchange; Port of Spain belongs to the UNESCO Creative Cities Network as a City of Music [19]
International calling code
+1 868 within the North American Numbering Plan; international internet domain is .tt
Time-zone convention
Atlantic Standard Time, UTC−4, used throughout the country without regular seasonal daylight-saving adjustment
Regional maritime issues
Gulf of Paria shipping, fisheries, coastal pollution, security and marine-boundary management require cross-border cooperation

UNESCO listings: Trinidad and Tobago has zero inscribed World Heritage properties but three sites on UNESCO’s tentative list. The separate Creative Cities programme and the country’s living cultural traditions are not substitutes for an inscribed World Heritage property.
