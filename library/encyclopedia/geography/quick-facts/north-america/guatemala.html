Country Overview

Guatemala is a Central American republic with Pacific and Caribbean coastlines, volcanic highlands, forested lowlands and one of the region’s largest and most diverse populations. The territory was central to the development of Maya civilizations, whose languages, communities and cultural institutions remain fundamental to national life. Following Spanish conquest in the sixteenth century, Guatemala became an administrative center of colonial Central America. It joined the 1821 independence process and later emerged from the Central American federation as a separate republic. The twentieth century included political upheaval and a long internal armed conflict formally ended by the 1996 peace accords. Today the country is governed under the 1985 Constitution and faces continuing debates over the rule of law, inequality, public services and Indigenous rights. Its economy combines manufacturing, agriculture, retail and services with major remittance inflows and growing regional trade.

Identity and State Formation

Official name
Republic of Guatemala (República de Guatemala), a sovereign republic of Central America [2]
Common name and demonym
Guatemala; Guatemalan (guatemalteco or guatemalteca in Spanish)
Capital city
Guatemala City (Ciudad de Guatemala), seat of national government and the primary metropolitan economic center [2]
Continent / UN M49 region
North America in MGU indexing; Central America in UN M49, within the Americas statistical region [3]
Maya civilizational foundations
Maya urban centers, languages, calendars, agriculture, writing and political systems developed for centuries before European contact, especially in Petén and the highlands
Tikal and the lowlands
Tikal was a major Classic Maya center whose monumental urban and ceremonial complex forms part of modern archaeological research [9]
Spanish conquest
Military campaigns beginning in the 1520s transformed political power, settlement and religious administration while Indigenous communities resisted and adapted
Colonial regional capital
Guatemala served as a key administrative center of the Captaincy General of Guatemala within the Spanish imperial system
Independence from Spain
September 15, 1821, shared with the other Central American provinces; this date does not mark adoption of the current 1985 Constitution [11]
Central American federation
Participated in the nineteenth-century Federal Republic of Central America, which disintegrated amid regional political disputes
Consolidation of republic
Separate republican institutions emerged during the dissolution of the federation in the late 1830s and 1840s
Nineteenth-century agriculture
Coffee plantations and export agriculture reshaped land tenure, Indigenous communities, labor relations and international trade connections
1944 revolution
The October Revolution of 1944 ended the Jorge Ubico-era political order and opened a decade of reform governments
1954 political crisis
A United States-supported coup against President Jacobo Árbenz profoundly changed Guatemalan institutions and later political conflict [11]
Internal armed conflict
Civil war and state repression extended across much of 1960–1996, causing extensive killing, displacement and particularly severe abuses against many Maya communities
Peace agreements
Final peace accords signed December 29, 1996, addressed demobilization, rights and institutional reform; implementation remains an ongoing issue [11]
Current constitutional foundation
Constitution enacted in 1985, effective in 1986, with later legal changes; it establishes a republican separation of powers [8]
National Independence Day
September 15, celebrated alongside other Central American independence commemorations
National flag and emblems
Vertical blue-white-blue stripes, a central coat of arms featuring the quetzal bird and a scroll marking independence on September 15, 1821
United Nations membership
Joined the United Nations on November 21, 1945, distinct from the earlier independence date [2]
ISO identifiers
GT (alpha-2), GTM (alpha-3), 320 (numeric); national internet country-code domain .gt [3]

Founding chronology: September 1821 independence from Spain, the post-federal separate republic, the 1944 revolution, the 1985 constitutional order and the 1996 peace agreements are separate milestones. Ancient Maya societies cannot be reduced to the history of the modern nation-state.

Geography and Population

Total surface area
108,889 km² in UN country data, including inland waters; this should not be substituted with estimates of usable agricultural land [2]
Regional setting
Borders Mexico to the north and west, Belize to the northeast, Honduras to the east and El Salvador to the southeast
Coastlines
Pacific Ocean frontage to the south and short Caribbean Sea access around the Gulf of Honduras and Amatique Bay
Territorial dispute context
Guatemala's historic territorial, insular and maritime claim regarding Belize is before the International Court of Justice; recognized Belizean sovereignty is not negated by the existence of the dispute [14]
Major relief units
Sierra Madre volcanic chain, central and western highlands, eastern valleys, northern Petén lowlands and two distinct coastal plains
Highest peak
Volcán Tajumulco in western Guatemala, approximately 4,220 metres high and the highest summit in Central America
Volcanic hazard
Fuego, Pacaya and Santiaguito are among prominent volcanoes; eruptions, ash fall and lahar risks require continuing hazard planning
Seismic exposure
The regional tectonic setting produces earthquakes and related landslide threats, with destructive events recorded across modern history
Principal lakes
Lake Atitlán, Lake Izabal and Petén Itzá are major inland water bodies with different ecological and cultural contexts
Rivers and watersheds
Motagua, Usumacinta, Polochic and other rivers drain to the Caribbean, Gulf of Mexico and Pacific systems
Forest regions
Petén forests form part of the wider Maya Forest and host protected habitats, archaeological landscapes and pressures from deforestation
Administrative departments
Twenty-two departments and hundreds of municipalities administer regional and local affairs under the unitary constitutional state
Key departments
Guatemala, Alta Verapaz, Huehuetenango, Quiché, Petén, Quetzaltenango, San Marcos and Escuintla illustrate major geographic and population diversity
Principal cities
Guatemala City, Mixco and Villa Nueva in the capital metropolitan region; Quetzaltenango, Escuintla, Cobán and Puerto Barrios are other important centers
Climate
Tropical lowlands, comparatively temperate highlands and humid Caribbean-facing zones produce strong seasonal and elevation-driven contrasts
Natural hazards
Flooding, hurricanes, drought, landslides, earthquakes and volcanic eruptions can create overlapping emergency-response needs
2025 estimated population
18,687,881 in the World Bank annual international series, rather than a direct headcount from the latest decennial census [1]
2018 official census
14,901,286 enumerated persons in the 2018 population and housing census, reflecting that year's census scope and methodology [4]
2025 population growth
About 1.5% in the World Bank series; population growth is not identical to natural increase because migration and statistical revisions also matter [1]
Population density
Approximately 172 residents per km² calculated from 18,687,881 people and the UN surface area; highland cities and sparsely populated forest regions vary greatly [1] [2]
Life expectancy
Approximately 73 years at birth in the World Bank 2024 reference; this is a national period indicator with regional and socioeconomic variation [1]
UNESCO World Heritage
Four inscribed properties: Antigua Guatemala, Tikal National Park, Archaeological Park and Ruins of Quirigua, and Tak’alik Ab’aj [9]

Census versus estimate: the 2018 INE direct enumeration of 14,901,286 and the 2025 World Bank population estimate of 18,687,881 have different reference years and methods. Calculated population density based on total surface area is not the same as land-only density.

Economy and International Trade

Economic classification
Large mixed Central American economy spanning agriculture, food processing, export manufacturing, retail, trade logistics, finance and public services
Nominal GDP
Approximately US$123.31 billion in 2025 according to the World Bank current-US-dollar series [1]
GDP per capita
US$6,598.2 in 2025, calculated from overall economic output and population; not a measure of a typical worker's wages [1]
Real GDP growth
4.3% in 2025 in the World Bank annual national-accounts series, which differs from dollar-value growth caused by prices and exchange movements [1]
Inflation
1.5% consumer-price growth in the 2025 annual World Bank series; national inflation rates can mask variations among regions and goods [1]
ILO-modeled unemployment
2.6% in 2025 according to the modeled World Bank headline, which does not measure the full burden of informal work or underemployment [1]
Personal remittances
Received personal remittances equaled about 20.8% of GDP in 2025 (World Bank), reflecting extensive family ties to international labor markets [1]
National currency
Guatemalan quetzal (GTQ, commonly Q), with monetary management by Banco de Guatemala
Monetary authority
Banco de Guatemala supports national monetary and exchange-rate policy and collects foreign trade and payments data
Services sector
Domestic trade, transportation, finance, telecommunications, real estate, professional services, tourism and public administration generate substantial output
Agricultural sectors
Coffee, bananas, sugarcane, cardamom, palm products, vegetables and grains contribute to employment and rural exports
Textile manufacturing
Clothing and apparel industries operate in export chains linked to North America and nearby markets, alongside domestic fashion and textile production
Food processing and edible oils
Processed sugar, palm oils, food products and packaged goods connect farm production, factories and international customers
2025 merchandise exports
US$15,587.4 million in 2025 under Guatemala's national foreign-trade reporting, up from US$14,556.3 million in 2024 [6]
2025 merchandise imports
US$34,609.3 million in the paired 2025 national merchandise series, reflecting imports of fuels, equipment, vehicles and consumer goods [6]
2025 goods trade gap
Approximately US$19,021.9 million deficit using the rounded annual 2025 national customs export and import totals; not the full current-account balance [6]
Merchandise export growth
Annual export value increased about 7.1% from 2024 to 2025 in the national reporting series; growth in sales value is not necessarily volume growth [6]
Important export categories
Apparel, coffee, bananas, sugar, edible fats and oils, plastics, cardamom and assorted processed manufactured products [6]
Regional export destinations
Central American markets collectively and the United States were leading outlets in 2025, in addition to Mexico, Europe and other global markets [6]
Trade agreements
CAFTA–DR and Central American integration arrangements shape tariffs, market access and supply chains; agreement membership differs from a customs union
Merchandise versus services
Physical goods exports are counted in national merchandise tables; tourism receipts, transport and professional services belong to other categories
Investment and industry
Construction, logistics, food processing, consumer manufacturing and electricity projects attract domestic and foreign capital under changing risk conditions
Infrastructure and ports
Puerto Quetzal on the Pacific and Santo Tomás de Castilla / Puerto Barrios on the Caribbean connect Guatemala to ocean shipping networks
Tourism economy
Archaeological places, colonial architecture, lake and mountain landscapes, traditional markets and eco-tourism generate service receipts rather than commodity exports
Distribution of economic benefits
Rural poverty, unequal land access, Indigenous community exclusion and regional public-service gaps affect welfare despite national GDP growth
Fiscal and development policy
Schooling, healthcare, transportation, energy reliability, judicial confidence and disaster adaptation shape public investment choices

People and Society

Official state language
Spanish has official constitutional status; the state recognizes and protects languages of Indigenous peoples under national language and education frameworks
Maya languages
Twenty-two recognized Mayan languages, including K'iche', Q'eqchi', Kaqchikel and Mam, are important to public life and community identity [5]
Other recognized Indigenous languages
Xinka and Garífuna are associated with distinct population histories and cultural rights and should not be grouped as dialects of Mayan languages [5]
2018 Maya identification
Approximately 41.7% of persons in the 2018 census identified as Maya; this is a self-identification category rather than a count of a single language community [4]
2018 Ladino identification
Approximately 56.0% identified as Ladino in the 2018 census; the concept does not correspond exactly to terms used in other countries [4]
Other 2018 census identities
Xinka accounted for about 1.8%, Garífuna about 0.1%, and Afrodescendant/Creole/Afromestizo about 0.2%; small percentages can conceal significant community histories [4]
Language and identity caveat
Ethnic self-identification, first language, language used at home and bilingual capacity measure different characteristics; one percentage cannot replace another
Religious affiliations
Roman Catholic and numerous evangelical or Protestant denominations are important; religion categories and shares depend on survey year, question wording and respondent age
Historical religious survey example
A 2016 ProDatos survey, cited in a U.S. State Department summary, estimated roughly 45% Catholic and 42% Protestant; it is not a verified 2026 share [10]
Maya spiritual traditions
Indigenous ceremonial and community practices persist and sometimes coexist with Catholic or evangelical affiliations, requiring care when classifying practices
Education provision
Public, private and community schooling systems operate in Spanish and selected bilingual or intercultural programs; language access remains uneven
Literacy and learning gaps
Rural location, household income, school funding and Indigenous-language provision influence learning opportunities; no undated literacy rate is asserted
Healthcare delivery
Public and private health services coexist, with substantial disparities between urban and rural regions in staffing, travel time and coverage
Cultural heritage
Textile arts, weaving, pottery, markets, oral literature, music and Maya ceremonial life contribute to a diverse national cultural landscape
Major cultural observances
Independence Day, Holy Week processions, local patron-saint celebrations and community rituals are prominent, with regional variation
Food traditions
Maize-based tortillas and tamales, beans, pepián, kak'ik and highland and coastal regional dishes reflect Indigenous and colonial culinary exchange
Migration and remittances
Family connections to the United States and other countries affect household economics, education, consumption and rural development [1]
Demographic age structure
The 2018 census documented a relatively youthful age profile, while the estimated population and age distribution continue evolving [4]
Urban and rural differences
Metropolitan industry and services contrast with highland smallholder agriculture and dispersed villages; the same service-access indicators are not uniform nationally
Memory of armed conflict
Communities continue to confront displacement, disappearances, human rights violations and demands for truth, justice and reconciliation after the 1996 accords

2018 census identities are self-reported and specifically dated. The ProDatos religious percentages cited here refer to a 2016 survey, not to the 2025 population or 2026 religious composition. Indigenous-language speakers are not interchangeable with all persons identifying as Maya.

Government and Institutions

Constitutional framework
1985 Constitution, effective in 1986, establishes a democratic representative republic with separate executive, legislative and judicial powers [8]
President
Bernardo Arévalo, inaugurated January 14, 2024 for the 2024–2028 presidential term; as of the November 2026 editorial cutoff
Vice president
Karin Herrera, elected on the presidential ticket in 2023 and inaugurated alongside Arévalo in January 2024
Executive branch
President, vice president and government ministers oversee national policy and administration subject to constitutional and statutory controls
Legislature
Unicameral Congress of the Republic, comprising 160 directly elected deputies serving four-year terms [8]
Legislative representation
National and district electoral systems elect Congress deputies; internal bloc changes do not change the total authorized 160 seats [8]
Judicial system
Constitutional Court, Supreme Court and other courts exercise distinct constitutional and ordinary jurisdictions, with institutional independence a recurrent public debate
Constitutional Court
Corte de Constitucionalidad addresses constitutional interpretation and claims; it is distinct from the Supreme Court for ordinary judicial matters
Electoral authority
Supreme Electoral Tribunal (Tribunal Supremo Electoral) organizes national electoral processes under electoral law
2023 presidential election
Bernardo Arévalo and Karin Herrera were elected after a competitive runoff; the transfer of power occurred in January 2024
Administrative divisions
Twenty-two departments governed through central and local institutions, with municipalities as the basis of local administration
Municipal governments
Locally elected municipal authorities exercise functions under the Municipal Code and national law; departmental administration is not the same as a federal state
Indigenous political rights
Indigenous communities assert collective rights, cultural protections, ancestral authorities and participation in local and national decision-making
Human-rights legacy
The 1996 peace accords and historical commission findings remain central to debates about violence, accountability and institutional reform
Security institutions
Police and national armed forces have distinct mandates; legal oversight and civil control remain important constitutional principles
Public finance
Congress considers budgets and public spending, while national revenue, procurement and audits involve several distinct executive and oversight entities
Political tensions
Anti-corruption reform, prosecutorial independence, legislative bargaining and challenges to electoral results have been important issues since 2023
Law and access to justice
Court access, case delays, inequality, language interpretation and community legal traditions are recurring governance concerns
International obligations
Guatemala participates in inter-American rights mechanisms and UN conventions that interact with national constitutional safeguards

The present 1985 constitutional republic is institutionally different from both the earlier federal Central American experiment and the post-1996 peace process. Constitutional law and actual access to institutions should be treated separately in research.

International Profile

United Nations
Member since November 21, 1945, distinct from independence in September 1821 [2]
Central American Integration System
Participates in SICA and related regional political, customs and development coordination
Central American Common Market
Regional economic integration connects Guatemala with neighboring markets and shared road and manufacturing corridors
CAFTA–DR
Party to the United States–Central America–Dominican Republic trade agreement covering trade, investment and associated rules
United States economic relationship
Remittances, immigration, manufacturing supply chains, agriculture and diplomacy make the United States an important partner [1]
Mexico relationship
Land borders, migration routes, trade and environmental watersheds create major bilateral cooperation issues
Belize territorial dispute
The international court process concerns historical territorial, insular and maritime claims; the unresolved matter should not be presented as settled [14]
Organization of American States
Participates in the inter-American diplomatic and human-rights system
International economic bodies
Member of the WTO, IMF and World Bank, participating in international financial and trade standards
UNESCO World Heritage
Four properties: Tikal, Antigua Guatemala, Quirigua, and Tak’alik Ab’aj, with inscription dates from 1979 through 2023 [9]
Environment and climate diplomacy
Forest conservation, regional watersheds, disaster-risk reduction and the Maya Forest involve cross-border cooperation
Maritime and trade connections
Pacific and Caribbean port access facilitates trade with Central America, North America and wider global markets
Standard time
Central Standard Time (UTC−6), used year-round without a normal national daylight-saving transition
Telephone and domain
International dialing prefix +502 and internet country-code top-level domain .gt
Migration and diaspora diplomacy
Labor mobility, family reunification, protection of citizens abroad and trafficking prevention feature in cross-border policy agendas

The World Heritage tally includes the mixed cultural-natural Tikal National Park, alongside three cultural sites. UNESCO inscriptions differ from tentative nominations; the continuing Belize dispute is a legal question, not a substitute for an agreed sovereignty boundary.
