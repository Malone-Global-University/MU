Country Overview

Panama is an isthmian republic joining Central and South America and separating the Caribbean Sea from the Pacific Ocean. Its position enabled an enduring network of Indigenous trade routes, colonial Spanish overland connections, nineteenth-century railway transport and the twentieth-century Panama Canal. Panama declared separation from Colombia in 1903; later negotiations and public movements culminated in full Panamanian control of the Canal at the end of 1999. Today its economy is especially dependent on logistics, ports, financial and professional services, maritime transport and regional commercial services, alongside agriculture, tourism and domestic merchandise exports. Panama has a national balboa unit at parity with the U.S. dollar, while U.S. dollar banknotes circulate in everyday life. This profile distinguishes the Panamanian economy’s large international service flows and Colón Free Zone re-exports from the much smaller registered domestic goods-export category, and separates the 2023 national census from 2025 demographic estimates.

Identity and State Formation

Official name
Republic of Panama (República de Panamá), independent sovereign republic on the American isthmus [7]
Common name and nationality
Panama; Panamanian (panameño or panameña), with diverse national, Indigenous and Afro-descendant identities
Capital and seat of government
Panama City (Ciudad de Panamá) hosts the presidency, national legislature and central administrative institutions [2]
Continent and UN statistical classification
North America in the MGU geographic directory; Central America in the United Nations M49 scheme [11]
Indigenous histories
Guna, Emberá, Wounaan, Ngäbe, Buglé, Naso and other peoples maintain distinctive histories predating European colonization
Spanish colonial foundation
Panama City was founded in 1519 as a major trans-isthmian colonial city, associated with imperial traffic between oceans
Colonial trans-isthmian route
Overland routes carried trade and precious-metal shipments between Caribbean and Pacific ports, with Indigenous labor and coercive colonial systems [10]
Independence from Spain
November 28, 1821 declaration ended Spanish sovereignty, after which the region joined the larger Colombian republican polity [12]
Nineteenth-century political status
The isthmus remained connected to successive New Granadan and Colombian states during much of the nineteenth century
1855 railway
The Panama Railroad linked the two oceans before the modern Canal and accelerated commerce, travel and strategic foreign interest
French canal attempt
A late-nineteenth-century French attempt to build a canal failed after major engineering, disease and financing difficulties
Separation from Colombia
November 3, 1903 marks Panama’s declaration of separation and the creation of the modern republic [12]
1903 canal treaty
The Hay–Bunau-Varilla Treaty granted extensive U.S. rights over the Canal Zone, creating a prolonged sovereignty controversy
Canal opened
The Panama Canal opened to traffic in 1914, reorganizing global shipping routes and national economic life [5]
1964 flag demonstrations
January 1964 clashes over flag and sovereignty symbolized the dispute over canal administration and territorial rights
1977 Torrijos–Carter treaties
Signed September 7, 1977, the treaties provided a framework for transferring canal operation and returning the former Canal Zone [5]
Full Canal handover
Panama assumed administration of the Canal at noon on December 31, 1999, completing the agreed transfer schedule [5]
1989 intervention
United States military intervention overthrew Manuel Noriega’s regime, with civilian casualties and long-term debate over sovereignty and accountability
Modern constitution
1972 Constitution as subsequently amended remains the national legal framework, subject to elections and constitutional change [7]
2024 presidential transition
José Raúl Mulino won the May 2024 presidential election and assumed office July 1, 2024 [6]
National days
November 3 marks separation from Colombia; November 28 marks independence from Spain; November 4 is Flag Day
National emblems
Four-part red-white-blue national flag with stars; coat of arms and national anthem Himno Istmeño
United Nations membership
Panama joined on November 13, 1945 [2]
ISO and internet identifiers
PA (alpha-2), PAN (alpha-3), 591 (numeric), and the .pa internet domain [11]

Distinct independence milestones: 1821 separation from Spain was followed by association with Colombia; the 1903 separation created the present republic. The Canal transfer completed in 1999 and should not be treated as the founding of Panama itself.

Geography and Population

Strategic location
Panama spans the narrow American land bridge between Costa Rica in the west and Colombia in the east, bordered by two oceanic basins
Western international boundary
Costa Rica borders the west; mountain and coastal regions contain ecological corridors and cross-border connections
Eastern international boundary
Colombia borders the east near the Darién region, where dense forest and limited road connections affect migration and conservation
Total surface area
75,320 km² under the United Nations geographic surface-area convention, distinct from separately reported land-only measures [2]
Caribbean shoreline
The northern coast includes Bocas del Toro, the Colón region, Guna Yala and significant reefs, ports and mangrove systems
Pacific coastline
The southern coast includes the Gulf of Panama, Gulf of Chiriquí, Azuero Peninsula and numerous islands
Panama Canal corridor
Waterway joins the Atlantic and Pacific through locks, lakes and excavated channels, with a complex reservoir and water-management system [5]
Gatun Lake and reservoir system
Artificial lakes supply navigation and water-management functions, illustrating the interaction of water security and shipping
Canal expansion
The expanded Neopanamax lock system began operation in 2016, enabling larger vessels than the original lock chambers [5]
Darién Gap
Forested eastern frontier prevents a continuous road link in the Pan-American Highway; protected areas and migrant routes require distinct policies
Chiriquí highlands
Mountainous western lands support high-elevation farming, tourism and cloud-forest ecosystems
Highest peak
Volcán Barú rises to approximately 3,475 metres, offering highland environments and volcanic geological history
Central mountain spine
The Cordillera Central and other ranges shape rainfall patterns, transport corridors and river watersheds
Major rivers
Chagres River supplies Canal watershed water; other systems include Tuira, Chucunaque and regional Pacific and Caribbean rivers
Island systems
Archipiélago de Bocas del Toro and Pearl Islands illustrate distinct coastal ecologies and settlement histories
Climate
Tropical climate with seasonal rainfall differences, wetter Caribbean regions and locally cooler high-elevation areas
Flood and drought exposure
Rainfall extremes and seasonal dry conditions affect Canal water levels, agriculture, urban drainage and household supply
Ocean biodiversity
Coral reefs, mangroves, marine parks and fisheries support biodiversity, coastal livelihoods and conservation efforts
2023 population census
4,064,780 residents in the 2023 national census; a separate enumeration from the 2025 international annual population series [3]
2023 Indigenous self-identification
698,114 people, 17.2% of the 2023 census population, identified as Indigenous under the census question [3]
2023 Afro-descendant self-identification
1,286,857 people, 31.7% of the 2023 census population, reported Afro-descendant identity under the census definition [3]
2025 World Bank estimate
4,571,189 people in the World Bank annual population series; it uses a different date and population-estimation method [1]
Calculated overall population density
Approximately 61 residents per km² using 2025 estimated population and UN surface area; Panama City and western corridors are much denser [1] [2]
Major urban settlements
Panama City metropolitan area, San Miguelito, Colón, David and La Chorrera serve differing commercial and administrative roles
Administrative divisions
Ten provinces plus designated Indigenous comarcas or regions with special legal status; the latter are not simply ordinary provinces [7]
Environmental policy priorities
Forest protection, coastal degradation, fisheries, urban pollution, rainfall extremes and Canal watershed conservation are interlinked
UNESCO World Heritage
Five inscribed properties at the 2026 cutoff, including the Colonial Transisthmian Route of Panamá listed in 2025 [10]

Population and identity definitions: the 2023 census enumerated 4,064,780 persons, while the World Bank’s 2025 annual population estimate was 4,571,189. Indigenous and Afro-descendant self-identifications reflect particular census questions; percentages should not be interpreted as mutually exclusive ethnicity blocks.

Economy and International Trade

Economic structure
High-income services-centered economy with global shipping, port services, finance, logistics, real estate, retail, tourism and regional commercial activities
Nominal GDP
US$90.46 billion in the World Bank 2025 current-dollar annual national accounts [1]
GDP per capita
US$19,789.7 for 2025 World Bank current dollars, an output-per-person average rather than median wages or income [1]
Real economic growth
Approximately 4.4% in 2025, measured in the World Bank annual real GDP growth indicator [1]
Consumer-price change
About −0.2% annual CPI inflation in 2025 in the World Bank comparable series; not a universal decline in all prices [1]
ILO-modeled unemployment
Approximately 8.4% in 2025 under the comparable World Bank modeled labor series, distinct from national survey measures [1]
Dual monetary practice
Balboa (PAB) at parity with the United States dollar; U.S. dollar banknotes circulate while balboa coinage is issued domestically [2]
Monetary-system distinction
Panama does not operate a conventional national central bank issuing a separate paper currency or independent exchange-rate policy
Panama Canal operations
Canal tolls, transit services, water management and operating expenditures are substantial service-sector and public-revenue matters [5]
International maritime logistics
Cargo handling, maritime services, warehousing, bunkering and shipping agency activities form commercial segments beyond domestic goods production
Colón Free Zone
The free zone imports and re-exports goods through a large distribution network; re-export values should not be added as if all were Panama-produced goods [13]
Ports and transshipment
Balboa, Colón-area terminals and other port facilities handle transshipment that is counted differently from national merchandise production
International finance
Banking, insurance and professional services serve domestic and regional customers within financial and regulatory frameworks
Tourism
Urban heritage, highland destinations, beaches, marine environments and ecotourism provide international travel service receipts
Construction and real estate
Urban building, infrastructure and housing contribute to output but respond to financing, demand and land-use conditions
Agricultural economy
Bananas, coffee, sugar, cattle, rice, palm products and fisheries remain significant in nonmetropolitan livelihoods
2025 registered goods exports
B/.980.1 million in officially registered merchandise exports excluding copper, in the February 5, 2026 Commerce Ministry report [4]
Year-on-year goods change
Officially registered exports excluding copper rose about 10.1% in 2025 against the revised 2024 comparison [4]
Frozen-shrimp exports
Frozen shrimp led individual six-digit tariff subheadings with about 13.3% of registered goods exports in 2025 [4]
Crude palm oil
Crude palm oil accounted for roughly 7.7% of the same registered export subset, not of GDP or Panama Canal revenues [4]
Banana exports
Bananas accounted for about 7.5% of 2025 registered exports; disruption in the banana sector affected annual composition [4]
Coffee and specialty crops
Regional highland coffee and selected horticultural products support farm-sector exports, though annual category shares vary
Copper-trade caveat
Panama’s registered 2025 export totals cited here explicitly exclude copper, and historical copper shipments should not be extrapolated into current active output [4]
Canal earnings excluded
Transit tolls are receipts for services; they are not part of the B/.980.1 million registered merchandise-export total [5] [4]
Free-zone re-exports excluded
Colón Free Zone re-exports are measured under a different statistical trade perimeter and must not be described as domestic export production [13] [4]
International services
Canal-related transportation, cargo handling, financial and professional services form significant exports not captured by the merchandise-only headline
Inflation and currency
The balboa-dollar parity means domestic currency conditions differ from those of countries with independent exchange rates and monetary-policy instruments
Trade-account methodology
Registered goods exports, national re-exports, balance-of-payments totals and international shipping services have different scopes and cannot be combined without reconciliation

People and Society

Official language
Spanish is the official language of national government and widely used in public life, education and commerce [7]
Indigenous languages
Guna, Ngäbere, Buglere, Emberá, Wounaan and Naso/Teribe languages are among the country's distinctive regional languages
English-language use
English is used in shipping, tourism, finance and some Afro-Caribbean communities, with regional differences in fluency
Indigenous nationality and identity
Guna, Ngäbe, Buglé, Emberá, Wounaan, Naso and Bribri groups preserve varied institutions and community histories
Indigenous census share
The 2023 census counted 698,114 self-identified Indigenous people, 17.2% of enumerated residents [3]
Afro-Panamanian census share
The 2023 census counted 1,286,857 self-identified Afro-descendants, 31.7% of the enumerated population [3]
Census category interpretation
Indigenous and Afro-descendant measures arise from different self-identification questions and should not automatically be treated as mutually exclusive
Colonial and migration histories
Spanish colonial society, the nineteenth-century railroad, Canal construction and later migration produced diverse urban and regional communities
Caribbean cultural influence
Afro-Antillean communities have contributed language, religious practice, food, music and labor history, particularly along the Canal and Caribbean coast
Religious life
Roman Catholic and evangelical Protestant Christianity are prominent, alongside other Christian, Jewish, Muslim and other traditions
Religion methodology
No undated denominational share is presented as a validated 2026 population estimate; survey scope and practice differ from affiliation
Education system
Primary and secondary education is administered through public and private institutions with significant rural and Indigenous access disparities
Higher education
University of Panama, Technological University of Panama and other institutions provide professional, technical and research training
Health access
Public and private healthcare networks serve urban and rural areas; geographic access remains uneven in remote Indigenous territories
Income inequality
Large international-service and urban sectors coexist with pronounced inequalities between metropolitan and rural Indigenous communities
Arts and music
Tamborito, congo performance, regional music, visual arts and crafts reflect African, Indigenous, Spanish and Caribbean influences
Traditional clothing
The pollera and regional clothing traditions have historical and ceremonial significance without representing every ethnic community
Cuisine
Rice, maize, plantains, seafood, tropical fruits and regional dishes vary by coast, highland and ethnic tradition
Cultural festivals
Carnival, local religious observances and Indigenous celebrations reveal different regional calendars and artistic forms
Sports
Baseball and football are widely followed alongside basketball, boxing and regional sporting activity
Migration corridors
The Darién is both a protected forest and a route used by migrants; movement totals, refugee status and resident census counts are different measures
Urban development
Panama City metropolitan growth produces opportunities and housing, mobility, wastewater and environmental challenges

Ethnocultural comparison: the 2023 census reported 17.2% Indigenous and 31.7% Afro-descendant self-identification. These definitions and denominators should accompany each number; religion and language use are different population concepts.

Government and Institutions

State form
Unitary presidential constitutional republic under the Constitution of 1972, amended on multiple occasions [7]
Constitutional system
Executive, legislative and judicial powers are defined in law; public administration includes national and local government
Head of state and government
President José Raúl Mulino, elected May 5, 2024 and inaugurated July 1, 2024, serving as of November 2026 [6]
Presidential term
Five years under regular constitutional rules; the president is elected in nationwide voting [7]
Executive branch
President, vice presidency when applicable under constitutional rules, Council of Ministers and national administrative agencies
National legislature
Unicameral National Assembly (Asamblea Nacional), with 71 deputies elected through constituency systems [9]
Judiciary
Supreme Court of Justice and lower courts exercise judicial authority subject to the constitutional appointment and disciplinary framework [7]
Electoral administration
Tribunal Electoral is the autonomous electoral authority for elections and civil registration within its legal mandate [8]
Administrative provinces
Ten provinces have governors and local administrative institutions under national law [7]
Indigenous comarcas
Special Indigenous territorial arrangements recognize distinct governance practices; the legal scope varies by comarca [7]
Municipal government
Districts and corregimientos support local administration, public services and elected representation
Constitutional rights
Civic and political rights are legally protected, while practical policy debates concern corruption, due process and accountability
Canal administration
Autoridad del Canal de Panamá is a constitutionally grounded autonomous public authority for Canal operations [5]
1999 transfer
Panama took control of Canal administration on December 31, 1999 under the Torrijos–Carter treaty framework [5]
Security forces
Panama maintains a public-force arrangement rather than a conventional permanent standing army following the post-1989 security transformation
Fiscal responsibilities
Central government budgets, public agencies and Canal-related transfers are subject to differing statutory oversight and reporting
National elections
Presidential, legislative and local elections are held under rules administered by the Electoral Tribunal
Public policy agenda
Canal water management, regional logistics, social inequality, health systems, environment and fiscal governance feature in current debates
Sovereignty and diplomacy
Canal neutrality, maritime trade and relations with neighboring countries are core external governance issues

Canal governance: the Canal is operated by an autonomous Panamanian authority; after the December 31, 1999 transfer, continuing international shipping and neutrality obligations do not imply foreign sovereign ownership of the waterway.

International Profile

United Nations
Member since November 13, 1945, participating in international institutions and multilateral agreements [2]
Central American relations
Links with Costa Rica and other regional states include trade, transportation, customs and environmental cooperation
Colombia relations
Eastern border policies connect rainforest protection, security, migration and Indigenous community issues
United States relationship
Long historical relationship shaped by Canal treaties, commerce, migration and sovereignty negotiations [5]
Canal neutrality framework
Torrijos–Carter treaty arrangements and related obligations are central to Panama’s international maritime status [5]
Global shipping role
Commercial vessels from many economies use the Canal, subject to draft, lock capacity, toll and hydrological constraints
World Trade Organization
WTO membership situates Panama in multilateral trade law and customs-related commitments
Organization of American States
Member of the OAS and participant in inter-American diplomacy and institutional cooperation
Regional economic organizations
Central American and Latin American cooperation involves financial, customs, infrastructure and investment partnerships
Global financial institutions
IMF and World Bank participate in economic monitoring and development-policy discussions
Colón commercial networks
Distribution and re-export arrangements connect Panama to regional Latin American and Caribbean markets [13]
UNESCO World Heritage
Five listed properties in 2026; the 2025 Colonial Transisthmian Route is included in the current official total [10]
Shared natural heritage
Talamanca Range–La Amistad Reserves / La Amistad National Park is a transboundary World Heritage property with Costa Rica [10]
Darién National Park
UNESCO-inscribed natural property with biodiversity and Indigenous-cultural significance, separate from border transit statistics [10]
Marine conservation
Coiba National Park and its Special Zone of Marine Protection illustrate oceanic conservation commitments [10]
Climate and Canal watersheds
International maritime trade relies on environmental conditions and freshwater availability managed under national institutions
Standard time
Eastern Standard Time, UTC−5, ordinarily observed year-round without national daylight saving
International telephone and domain
International dialing code +507; Panama’s country-code internet top-level domain is .pa

World Heritage count: UNESCO lists five inscribed properties including the 2025 Colonial Transisthmian Route of Panamá. Proposed nominations and additions to previously listed properties are not separately counted without a new inscription.
