New Orleans Neighborhood Crews and Drug Markets, 1990s–2010s

Overview

New Orleans' drug and gang history from the 1990s through the 2010s differs from cities dominated by a few nationally branded organizations. Federal intelligence repeatedly described a city in which criminal groups were commonly organized around housing projects, neighborhoods, streets, and small alliances.

The Project-Based Market

Public housing developments became important retail markets for crack cocaine and later heroin. Crews used courtyards, streets, nearby commercial corridors, and runners to sell drugs while attempting to insulate senior members from direct transactions.

Crack Era

During the late 1980s and early 1990s, crack cocaine was deeply embedded in the city's street economy. Federal treatment and enforcement data indicate that Orleans Parish reached a peak in cocaine/crack treatment admissions around 1991.

1995 REDRUM Investigation

The REDRUM Task Force's sixteen-month crack-cocaine investigation targeted a violent drug organization operating in two New Orleans housing projects. The case produced five arrests and became an early model of concentrating federal resources on a small, violent drug network.

Heroin Expansion

By the late 1990s, heroin use and distribution were rising sharply in New Orleans. Federal intelligence reported that New York-based Dominican wholesalers supplied heroin to local street gangs and that black-tar heroin also reached the city from Oakland.

Local Crews Rather Than National Gangs

Federal analysts specifically noted that New Orleans had relatively few nationally affiliated gangs. Instead, groups such as the Mosley Gang, Perry Francois Gang, Dillon Gang, and Got It Boys were identified by neighborhood or project affiliation.

Central City

Central City became an important setting for later organizations. The Hankton Enterprise sold drugs there, while Young Melph Mafia members were associated with the former Melpomene Housing Development.

Hankton Enterprise

Federal courts documented the Hankton Enterprise as a long-running drug organization dating to the 1990s. Its conflict with rival dealers evolved into a violent feud, and the later RICO case added witness intimidation and obstruction to the federal theory of the enterprise.

39ers Alliance

The 39ers represented a different organizational model: an alliance between G-Strip and 3NG. Federal prosecutors alleged that the alliance was formed to strengthen both drug trafficking and violent capability.

Multi-Agency Enforcement

By the 2010s, the city increasingly used coordinated federal, state, and local gang investigations. The Multi-Agency Gang Unit and federal RICO prosecutions targeted individual groups rather than attempting to define one overarching citywide gang.

Geographic Interpretation

Magnolia, Calliope, Melpomene, the Ninth Ward, Central City, and other places should be treated as recurring geographic nodes. Different criminal networks occupied or used those places at different times, and cultural identity associated with a neighborhood should never be equated automatically with criminal membership.

Assessment

The most useful model for New Orleans is a network-of-crews approach: relatively small local organizations connected by rivalries, temporary alliances, wholesale suppliers, housing-project markets, and neighborhood geography. That model is more accurate than forcing the city into the hierarchical gang structures seen in Chicago or the large national trafficking systems seen in Atlanta and Miami.
