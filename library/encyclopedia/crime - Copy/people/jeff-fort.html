Jeff Fort

Overview

Jeff Fort was the dominant leader of the Chicago organization that evolved from the Blackstone Rangers into the Black P. Stone Nation and later the El Rukns. Federal appellate decisions describe Fort as the “Imam” at the top of a carefully structured El Rukn hierarchy.

Blackstone Rangers and Black P. Stone Nation

The organization emerged on Chicago's South Side during the 1960s. Its names and internal structure changed over time. Federal courts later summarized the progression as Blackstone Rangers in the 1960s, Black P. Stone Nation in the 1970s, and El Rukn in the 1980s.

Those labels should be used chronologically rather than treated as perfectly interchangeable. Membership, leadership roles, ideology, and criminal activity evolved across the different phases.

El Rukn Leadership

By the 1980s, Fort was described in federal court as the undisputed leader of the El Rukns. The organization's hierarchy placed Fort at the top, followed by generals, officer muftis, ambassadors, and soldiers. Its principal headquarters at 3947 South Drexel Boulevard was referred to as the Mosque.

Leadership from Prison

Fort began serving a federal sentence in Texas in 1984 but continued communicating extensively with Chicago leaders by telephone. Court records state that Fort and senior associate Melvin Mayes developed coded language for telephone conversations because authorities were monitoring calls.

Libya Conspiracy

In 1986, El Rukn members traveled to Libya and discussed obtaining financial support in exchange for proposed terrorist activity in the United States. Federal investigators used recorded prison calls, informants, undercover activity, and a controlled weapons purchase to build the case.

Fort and four co-defendants were convicted in November 1987 on conspiracy and weapons-related charges. Fort received an additional 80-year prison sentence.

Historical Significance

Fort's history spans several major phases of Chicago street-organization development: the large federated gangs of the 1960s, increasingly sophisticated criminal enterprises in the 1970s and 1980s, narcotics-related violence, and ultimately a federal national-security prosecution.
