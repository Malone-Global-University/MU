Baltimore

Overview

Baltimore, Maryland, developed one of the most persistent large-city heroin markets in the United States during the second half of the twentieth century. Its location on the Northeast Corridor connected the city to New York and Philadelphia while interstate and port geography facilitated access to broader supply networks.

Heroin Economy

Heroin was deeply embedded in Baltimore's illicit economy by the 1960s and 1970s. Melvin “Little Melvin” Williams became one of the city's most prominent traffickers, while other major organizations—including the networks later prosecuted around Clarence Meredith, Thomas Ricks, and Maurice King—distributed heroin through neighborhood territories.

Organizational Structure

Federal cases from the 1980s document several organizational forms: long-running kingpin-centered enterprises, street-corner lieutenant systems, stash-house operations, family-assisted processing, and later multilayered conspiracies with suppliers in other states.

Cocaine Expansion

By the mid-1980s, cocaine had become increasingly important alongside heroin. Tommy Lee Canty's organization purchased cocaine and heroin from suppliers in New York, Miami, and Los Angeles, showing how Baltimore's local markets were integrated into a national trafficking network.

Law Enforcement

Baltimore narcotics investigations increasingly combined local police with federal agencies. Undercover purchases, cooperating insiders, wiretaps, financial investigations, asset seizures, and multi-defendant conspiracy cases became central enforcement tools.

Historical Interpretation

Baltimore's drug history is best understood as a succession of overlapping networks rather than a single continuous organization. Individual organizations rose and fell, but the market infrastructure—suppliers, street territories, customers, neighborhood crews, and interstate connections—persisted.
