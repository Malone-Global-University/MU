History of African American Churches

The development of African American churches from the colonial era through slavery, emancipation, migration, civil rights, and contemporary religious life.

Overview

The history of African American churches spans more than three centuries, from the earliest encounters between enslaved Africans and Christianity in the English colonies of North America to the diverse landscape of Black congregations and denominations in the United States today. Across that span, African American churches have functioned simultaneously as sites of worship, centers of community life, engines of education and mutual aid, and platforms for political organization. Their history cannot be separated from the larger history of slavery, emancipation, segregation, migration, and civil rights in the United States, and it has produced some of the nation's most consequential institutions, denominations, and leaders.

The phrase Black church is useful as a broad historical description, but it does not refer to a single institution or a uniform body of belief. African American Christians have belonged to many denominations and independent congregations, differed in theology and worship, and disagreed over political strategy, gender roles, social reform, and the relationship between religion and public life. The history of African American churches is therefore best understood as the history of a diverse network of religious institutions that developed under shared but changing historical conditions.

Origins and the Colonial Period

Enslaved Africans encountered European Christianity in the English colonies of North America beginning in the early seventeenth century. Conversion was neither immediate nor uniform. Many enslavers resisted or neglected Christian instruction, while some feared that baptism might create a claim to freedom or otherwise unsettle the legal order of bondage. Colonial legislatures addressed that concern directly. Virginia, for example, declared in 1667 that baptism did not change an enslaved person's legal status.

Once colonial law more clearly separated baptism from emancipation, some enslavers became more willing to permit or encourage Christian instruction, sometimes because they believed Christian teaching could reinforce obedience. Missionary organizations also attempted to evangelize enslaved Africans. The Society for the Propagation of the Gospel in Foreign Parts, an Anglican missionary organization founded in 1701, instructed some of its missionaries in the colonies to catechize enslaved people, although the results were uneven.

Many enslaved Africans and their descendants retained, adapted, or reinterpreted elements of African religious traditions even as they encountered Christianity. Religious change therefore did not proceed as a simple replacement of one belief system by another. Practices, memories, forms of communal worship, and understandings of the spiritual world could persist and interact with Christian teaching in different ways across regions and generations.

Religion under Slavery

The religious life of enslaved African Americans changed substantially during the eighteenth- century revivals associated with the First Great Awakening and again during the Second Great Awakening in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries. Evangelical preaching that emphasized personal conversion, emotional religious experience, and direct engagement with scripture reached many enslaved people. Baptist and Methodist preachers in particular gained significant followings among Black worshippers.

Enslaved Christians did not simply absorb the religion presented to them. They interpreted Christianity through their own experiences and communities, combining elements of African religious culture with biblical narrative and Christian theology. Rhythm, call and response, communal testimony, spiritual song, and other expressive forms became important features of worship in many communities. Enslavers and ministers sympathetic to slavery often emphasized obedience and submission, while enslaved worshippers frequently found meaning in biblical narratives of deliverance, especially the Exodus story, the prophetic tradition, and promises of divine justice.

Because Black religious assemblies were often restricted, monitored, or prohibited, important forms of religious life developed outside formal church structures. Enslaved Christians met in cabins, woods, brush arbors, and other informal settings for prayer, preaching, singing, and testimony. Historians have often described this network of self-directed religious activity as the invisible institution. These gatherings created a sphere of spiritual autonomy within the constraints of slavery and helped nurture Black religious leadership before emancipation.

Independent Black Congregations

Formally organized Black congregations began to appear in the late eighteenth century, including Baptist congregations in Virginia, South Carolina, and Georgia and independent Black religious organizations in Northern cities. Their development reflected both the growth of free Black communities and the determination of Black Christians to worship under their own leadership.

The push toward independent institutions intensified in places where Black worshippers experienced discrimination within white-controlled churches. One of the best-known episodes occurred at St. George's Methodist Episcopal Church in Philadelphia. Richard Allen later recalled that Absalom Jones was seized while kneeling in prayer after Black worshippers had been directed away from an area reserved for white members. Allen, Jones, and other Black members withdrew from the congregation and developed independent religious institutions.

In 1787, Allen, Jones, and other Black Philadelphians helped organize the Free African Society, a mutual-aid organization that became an important institutional foundation for independent Black religious life in the city. Jones later became associated with the African Episcopal Church of St. Thomas, while Allen pursued an independent Methodist path that eventually produced the African Methodist Episcopal Church.

The African Methodist Episcopal Church and the African Methodist Episcopal Zion Church

Richard Allen's struggle for independent Black Methodist worship led to the establishment of Bethel African Methodist Episcopal Church in Philadelphia. In 1816, Allen brought together Black Methodist congregations from several states to form the African Methodist Episcopal Church, commonly known as the AME Church, with Allen as its first bishop. The AME Church became the first fully independent Black denomination in the United States, with authority over its clergy, property, governance, and denominational life.

A parallel development occurred in New York City. Black Methodists who faced discrimination and restrictions within white-controlled Methodist institutions established their own congregation, which eventually became the foundation of the African Methodist Episcopal Zion Church. The denomination was formally organized in 1821. AME Zion developed a strong antislavery reputation, and prominent abolitionists including Frederick Douglass and Harriet Tubman had important connections to the denomination.

Both AME and AME Zion expanded among free Black communities before the Civil War and grew rapidly in the South after emancipation. Their denominational structures allowed them to send clergy, establish congregations, organize schools, and build institutions across state lines, making them major national organizations in African American religious life.

Black Baptist Churches

Black Baptist congregations followed a somewhat different institutional path. Because Baptist governance is traditionally congregational rather than episcopal, individual Black Baptist churches could form and operate with significant local autonomy. Independent Black Baptist congregations appeared during the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries in both the North and South, although Black religious independence in slaveholding states was often constrained by law and white supervision.

National organization developed gradually. Black Baptist churches formed associations, conventions, missionary societies, and educational organizations during the nineteenth century. In 1880, Black Baptists organized the Baptist Foreign Mission Convention, one important predecessor in the development of national cooperation. In 1895, several national Baptist bodies merged in Atlanta to form the National Baptist Convention.

Black Baptist institutional life continued to diversify during the twentieth century as disagreements over leadership, governance, political engagement, and organizational priorities produced additional national bodies. Despite these divisions, Baptist congregations and conventions remained among the largest and most influential components of African American Christianity.

Emancipation and Reconstruction

The destruction of slavery during the Civil War and the ratification of the Thirteenth Amendment in 1865 transformed African American religious life. Formerly enslaved Christians withdrew from many white-controlled congregations and established or joined independent Black churches. AME and AME Zion missionaries entered the South, while Black Baptist churches and associations expanded rapidly among freed communities.

Churches became central institutions during Reconstruction because they combined religious authority with organizational capacity, property ownership, literacy networks, and community leadership. Congregations often hosted schools, political meetings, mutual-aid activities, conventions, and civic organizations. Rather than existing outside political life, Black churches frequently helped support the unprecedented expansion of African American voting, officeholding, and political participation that occurred during Reconstruction.

Ministers were prominent in Reconstruction politics, but church-based leadership extended beyond ordained clergy. Congregations provided meeting places, communication networks, and institutional continuity at a time when freedpeople were building schools, families, businesses, political organizations, and community institutions under difficult and often violent conditions.

Reconstruction also produced another major historically Black Methodist denomination. In 1870, Black members and clergy formerly connected to the Methodist Episcopal Church, South organized the Colored Methodist Episcopal Church in Jackson, Tennessee. The denomination later changed its name to the Christian Methodist Episcopal Church while retaining the initials CME. Alongside AME and AME Zion, the CME Church became one of the principal historically Black Methodist bodies in the United States.

Jim Crow

The end of Reconstruction and the rise of disfranchisement and legal segregation across the South changed the environment in which Black churches operated. As white-controlled governments and institutions restricted African American political participation and access to public life, Black churches became even more important as autonomous institutions.

Churches were among the most important Black-controlled spaces in many communities. They hosted schools, mutual-aid organizations, missionary societies, civic meetings, and cultural programs. They also provided opportunities for property ownership, public speaking, administration, and institutional leadership. At the same time, African American communities built newspapers, businesses, fraternal organizations, colleges, professional associations, and other independent institutions, so the church should be understood as central to a broader Black institutional world rather than as its only autonomous component.

Women were indispensable to this institutional development. Through missionary societies, fundraising organizations, educational work, benevolent associations, and denominational conventions, Black women built and sustained much of the infrastructure of church life. The Woman's Convention of the National Baptist Convention, organized in 1900, became one of the most important national organizations of Black Baptist women. Debates over women's formal authority and ordination continued across denominations, with practices varying widely.

The Great Migration

Between roughly 1910 and 1970, several million Black Southerners moved to cities in the North, Midwest, and West in the movement known as the Great Migration. Migrants carried their religious traditions with them, reshaping African American religious life in cities such as Chicago, Detroit, New York, Philadelphia, Los Angeles, and other urban centers.

Existing Black congregations in Northern cities did not always receive migrants without tension. Some established churches had developed formal worship styles and middle-class institutional cultures that differed from the expressive religious practices of many Southern newcomers. Migrants created new congregations, including numerous storefront churches, that more closely reflected familiar styles of preaching, music, testimony, and community support.

The Great Migration therefore did more than move existing churches from one region to another. It changed the social composition of urban congregations, accelerated denominational competition, stimulated new religious movements, and helped create new forms of gospel music, preaching, and church-based community life adapted to the industrial city.

Holiness and Pentecostalism

The Holiness movement emerged from nineteenth-century Methodism and emphasized sanctification, often described as a second work of grace following conversion. Holiness teaching attracted significant numbers of Black Christians and helped create new networks of congregations, ministers, camp meetings, and independent religious organizations.

Pentecostalism developed from this broader Holiness environment in the early twentieth century. The Azusa Street Revival in Los Angeles, beginning in 1906 under the leadership of the Black preacher William J. Seymour, became one of the most influential centers of early Pentecostalism. Worship at Azusa emphasized the baptism of the Holy Spirit, speaking in tongues, healing, and other spiritual gifts. The revival also became known for a degree of interracial worship that was striking in the context of early twentieth-century segregation.

The Church of God in Christ predates the Azusa Street Revival. Charles Harrison Mason and other Holiness ministers organized the denomination in 1897. Mason visited the Azusa revival in 1907 and accepted Pentecostal teaching concerning Spirit baptism and speaking in tongues. His position produced a division with ministers who rejected Pentecostal doctrine, after which the Church of God in Christ developed as a major Pentecostal denomination under Mason's leadership.

COGIC became the largest historically Black Pentecostal denomination in the United States. Holiness and Pentecostal churches more broadly expanded rapidly during the twentieth century, especially among working-class and migrant communities, while contributing substantially to African American preaching, music, worship, and religious broadcasting.

The Civil Rights Movement

The importance of Black churches to African American civic life became especially visible during the modern civil rights movement of the 1950s and 1960s. Church buildings provided meeting spaces for boycotts, mass meetings, voter-registration campaigns, fundraising, training, and organizing. Because churches possessed local membership networks and were often among the largest Black-controlled institutions in Southern communities, they could provide organizational infrastructure that other institutions could not easily match.

Black ministers supplied much of the movement's public leadership. Martin Luther King Jr., pastor of Dexter Avenue Baptist Church in Montgomery and later co-pastor of Ebenezer Baptist Church in Atlanta, became the movement's most internationally recognized figure. In 1957 he helped establish the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, which drew heavily on Black church networks and clergy leadership. Ralph Abernathy, Fred Shuttlesworth, C. T. Vivian, and many other ministers also played major roles.

The movement, however, was never simply a movement of ministers. Lay organizers and women were essential to its development. Jo Ann Robinson helped organize the Montgomery bus boycott; Ella Baker challenged overly centralized models of leadership and helped cultivate student activism; Septima Clark developed citizenship-education programs; and Fannie Lou Hamer connected religious conviction with voting-rights activism in Mississippi. Local churchwomen across the South raised money, organized transportation, prepared food, maintained communication networks, and sustained campaigns over long periods.

Black clergy were also not unanimous in supporting direct-action protest. Some ministers feared economic retaliation, violence, congregational division, or political entanglement, while others disagreed with particular tactics or leaders. The relationship between the Black church and the civil rights movement was therefore powerful but not uniform.

The church's symbolic and organizational importance also made it a target of white supremacist violence. In 1963, the bombing of Sixteenth Street Baptist Church in Birmingham, Alabama, killed four girls attending Sunday school and became one of the most notorious acts of racial terrorism of the civil rights era.

The Late Twentieth Century

After the major legislative victories of the civil rights movement, African American churches continued to play important civic and political roles while adapting to changing social conditions. Suburbanization, deindustrialization, the growth of mass media, changing patterns of denominational affiliation, and the expansion of evangelical and Pentecostal Christianity all affected Black congregational life.

Black Theology became an important academic and ecclesial movement during this period. The theologian James H. Cone argued that Christian theology should be interpreted from the historical experience of Black oppression and the struggle for liberation. His work helped establish Black Theology as a major field while provoking substantial debate within African American churches and the broader Christian academy.

The period also saw the increasing visibility of Black megachurches, continued growth of Pentecostal and nondenominational congregations, and sustained engagement with electoral politics, community development, education, and social services. Debates over women's ordination and ecclesiastical leadership became increasingly visible, although Black women had long exercised substantial religious authority through missionary organizations, education, music ministries, congregational administration, preaching, and informal leadership.

Contemporary Black Churches

Black churches in the twenty-first century remain highly diverse in theology, worship style, denominational affiliation, class composition, political orientation, and social mission. The landscape includes historic Black Protestant denominations, Black congregations within historically white denominations, Pentecostal churches, independent megachurches, storefront congregations, and a growing number of nondenominational churches.

African Americans have historically reported high levels of religious commitment relative to the United States population as a whole, but Black religious life is changing alongside broader national trends. Younger adults are more likely than older generations to identify as religiously unaffiliated, and patterns of church attendance and denominational loyalty have become less uniform. These changes coexist with continued vitality in many congregations and with substantial differences by region, age, theology, and social background.

Contemporary Black churches continue to combine worship with social engagement. Congregations sponsor food programs, housing and community-development initiatives, schools, mentoring, criminal-justice advocacy, health programs, disaster relief, voter mobilization, and other forms of public service. At the same time, churches remain divided over political strategy, sexuality, gender, economic policy, partisan affiliation, and the proper relationship between religious institutions and political movements.

African American churches have also remained targets of racial violence. In 2015, nine Black worshippers were murdered during Bible study at Emanuel African Methodist Episcopal Church in Charleston, South Carolina. The attack recalled earlier violence against Black churches and underscored the continuing symbolic importance of the church as both a sacred institution and a center of African American community life.

Historical Significance

Across more than three centuries, African American churches have functioned as far more than religious institutions in a narrow sense. Under slavery, Black Christians created spaces of spiritual autonomy and interpreted scripture through experiences of bondage and hopes for deliverance. During emancipation and Reconstruction, churches became major institutions through which Black communities built schools, organized politically, developed leadership, and expanded networks of mutual aid.

During Jim Crow and the Great Migration, churches helped sustain community institutions while adapting to segregation, urbanization, migration, and new religious movements. In the civil rights era, church buildings, clergy, lay organizers, women, musicians, and congregational networks supplied essential resources for political mobilization, even as Black Christians debated the proper role of churches in protest and public affairs.

The historical significance of African American churches therefore lies not in the existence of a single unified "Black church," but in the extraordinary durability and adaptability of a broad religious institutional tradition. Across changing denominations, regions, classes, theological positions, and political commitments, Black churches have helped shape African American community life and the larger history of the United States.
