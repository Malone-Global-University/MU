African American Religious History

Explore the development of African American religious life from slavery and the early republic through independent Black churches, emancipation, migration, civil rights, new religious movements, and the contemporary United States.

Overview

African American religious history examines the beliefs, institutions, communities, leaders, movements, and practices through which people of African descent in the United States have expressed religious faith and organized religious life. The field includes the history of African American Christianity and the Black church, but it also extends to African-derived religious traditions, Islam, Jewish religious life, new religious movements, theological developments, and changing forms of religious identity.

Religious institutions became especially important centers of African American community life. Black congregations frequently served not only as places of worship but also as centers of education, mutual aid, leadership development, civic participation, political organization, cultural expression, and social reform. Their importance became particularly visible during emancipation and Reconstruction and continued through the era of segregation, the Great Migration, and the modern civil rights movement.

This section approaches African American religious history as both religious history and American history. It follows the development of institutions and denominations while also examining the relationship between religion and slavery, freedom, migration, education, racial discrimination, political participation, music, family life, social movements, intellectual history, and the continuing debate over the place of religion in public life.

Studying African American Religious History

The subject is best studied through multiple kinds of evidence. Church records, sermons, autobiographies, denominational publications, newspapers, oral histories, music, photographs, organizational records, government documents, and material culture can reveal different dimensions of religious experience. Because religious communities have never been uniform, interpretation should distinguish among denominations, regions, historical periods, theological positions, class backgrounds, gender, migration patterns, and individual experiences.

The phrase Black church is useful when discussing the broad historical importance of African American Christian institutions, but it should not be treated as though all Black Christians belonged to a single church or shared identical beliefs. Likewise, African American religious history should not be reduced to Christianity alone. The purpose of this index is to preserve those distinctions while showing how religious institutions and ideas interacted with the larger history of the United States.

The Black Church in African American Religious History

The African American church has been one of the most important institutions in Black American life, dating back to the era of slavery and continuing to shape culture, politics, and community life today.

Origins under slavery

Christianity spread among enslaved Africans mostly in the 1700s and 1800s, often through the Great Awakenings. Enslaved people blended Christian teachings with African spiritual traditions, creating distinctive worship styles built around music, call and response, and embodied expression. Many enslavers used religion to encourage obedience, but enslaved people found in the Bible, especially stories like the Exodus, a message of liberation. Worship often happened in secret, in what became known as the "invisible institution," away from white oversight.

The birth of independent Black churches

Even before the Civil War, free Black Christians began forming their own congregations and denominations because they were excluded or mistreated in white churches. Richard Allen founded the African Methodist Episcopal (AME) Church in Philadelphia in 1816 after facing discrimination at a white Methodist church. The African Methodist Episcopal Zion (AME Zion) Church and independent Black Baptist congregations also emerged in this period.

After emancipation

Following the Civil War, Black Christians left white controlled churches in large numbers and built their own institutions rapidly. The National Baptist Convention, still one of the largest Black denominations, formed in 1895. Churches became more than places of worship. They were schools, banks, social service centers, and the main public space where Black communities could organize independently of white control.

Twentieth century and the Civil Rights Movement

The church remained central through the Great Migration, as Black southerners carried their religious traditions to northern cities, and through the rise of the Pentecostal movement, including the founding of the Church of God in Christ. The church's role became especially visible during the Civil Rights Movement, when it supplied leadership, meeting spaces, and moral authority. Martin Luther King Jr., Ralph Abernathy, and many other leaders were pastors, and churches served as organizing hubs for boycotts, marches, and voter registration drives.

Continuing significance

Today the Black church remains a major force in community life, politics, and culture, even as religious affiliation patterns shift among younger generations. It has also long been a target of racist violence, from the bombing of the Sixteenth Street Baptist Church in 1963 to the Charleston church shooting in 2015, underscoring its symbolic and real importance as a center of Black life and resistance.
