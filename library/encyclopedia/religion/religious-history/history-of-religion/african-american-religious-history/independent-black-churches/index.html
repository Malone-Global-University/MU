The Rise of Independent Black Churches

The movement from white-controlled congregations toward Black-owned, Black-governed churches and denominations in the United States from the late eighteenth century through the antebellum era.

Overview

Between the late eighteenth century and the eve of the Civil War, Black Christians in the United States moved increasingly from subordinate participation in white-controlled churches toward the creation of independent Black congregations and denominations governed by Black leadership. This article traces that institutional transition in detail: the discrimination that helped provoke it, the individuals and communities that led it, and the new structures of ownership, governance, ministry, and denominational organization that resulted.

The focus here is deliberately narrow. This is not a full history of African American churches across every later period. Instead, it examines the formative era, roughly the 1770s through the 1850s, in which Black Christians established durable institutions under their own control. Those institutions became critical foundations for the far larger expansion of independent Black Christianity after emancipation.

Black Christians within White-Controlled Churches

Before independent Black denominations existed, free and enslaved Black Christians often worshipped within congregations controlled by white members and white clergy. Across both Northern and Southern colonies and later states, Black congregants were frequently placed in subordinate positions through segregated seating, exclusion from church governance, restrictions on ordination, and unequal access to authority within congregational life.

Black membership nevertheless grew substantially during the revivals associated with the First and Second Great Awakenings. Evangelical Methodist and Baptist preaching attracted large numbers of free and enslaved Black converts even as the institutions they joined often denied them meaningful control over worship, property, discipline, or clergy.

In some congregations, Black worshippers became a substantial portion of the membership while remaining excluded from leadership and decision-making. That imbalance created persistent tension. The movement toward independence therefore grew less from theological separation than from demands for institutional equality, ownership, authority, and control.

Early Black Baptist Congregations

Because Baptist church governance was traditionally congregational rather than centrally hierarchical, Black Baptist communities could sometimes organize independently earlier than Methodist or Episcopal congregations, whose structures depended more heavily on formal systems of ordination and episcopal oversight.

George Liele, a formerly enslaved man who became a licensed Baptist preacher in Georgia during the 1770s, helped organize one of the earliest Black Baptist communities in Savannah before relocating to Jamaica, where he continued missionary work among enslaved people. His ministry helped establish a model of Black religious leadership outside direct white control.

Andrew Bryan, converted through Liele's preaching, became a founder and principal early leader of the Savannah congregation. He was ordained in 1788, when the congregation received formal recognition. The church that developed from this community became First African Baptist Church, one of the oldest continuing Black congregations in the United States. Bryan and other Black worshippers faced arrest, whipping, and harassment from white authorities suspicious of independent Black religious assembly.

These early Baptist congregations demonstrate that Black ecclesiastical independence did not begin in a single city or through a single event. It emerged through multiple local movements shaped by denominational structure, regional law, Black leadership, and the degree of tolerance or hostility shown by white authorities.

Discrimination at St. George's Methodist Episcopal Church

One of the most widely remembered turning points in the rise of independent Black churches occurred in Philadelphia during the 1780s. Richard Allen, a formerly enslaved man who purchased his freedom in 1783, became an important Methodist preacher among the city's growing free Black population. Absalom Jones, another formerly enslaved Black religious leader, was also active within the same religious community.

As Black attendance increased at St. George's Methodist Episcopal Church, church authorities moved to enforce segregated seating more rigidly. During a service in 1787, an usher attempted to move Absalom Jones while he was kneeling in prayer. Jones asked to be allowed to finish praying, after which he and other Black worshippers withdrew from the church.

The episode was one instance within a broader pattern of discrimination, but it became a powerful symbol of the demand for Black ecclesiastical independence. It demonstrated that spiritual participation without institutional equality was no longer acceptable to many Black Christians who had built substantial communities within white-controlled churches.

The Free African Society

The withdrawal from St. George's did not immediately produce a new denomination. On April 12, 1787, Richard Allen and Absalom Jones helped establish the Free African Society, a mutual-aid organization created without strict denominational affiliation. Its purpose was to strengthen Philadelphia's free Black community through collective organization independent of white institutional control.

Members contributed regular dues to support the sick, widows, orphans, burial expenses, and other community needs. The Society also encouraged education, apprenticeship, mutual assistance, and moral discipline. It became one of the most important early examples of Black-controlled civic organization in the United States.

During the 1793 yellow fever epidemic in Philadelphia, Black caregivers associated with Allen, Jones, and the wider community provided nursing, transportation, and burial services while many residents fled the city. After accusations were later made against Black caregivers, Allen and Jones published a defense in 1794 documenting the community's service and challenging hostile portrayals of their conduct.

The Free African Society also developed religious functions. Over time, some of its practices reflected Quaker influence, including periods of silent worship. Allen, whose religious commitments remained strongly Methodist, withdrew in 1789 with others who preferred a different style of worship. The Society nevertheless remained the organizational seedbed from which Philadelphia's first major independent Black churches emerged.

St. Thomas African Episcopal Church and Mother Bethel

The religious activity surrounding the Free African Society gave rise to two major Black congregations in Philadelphia in 1794. Absalom Jones led the formation of the African Episcopal Church of St. Thomas, which affiliated with the Episcopal Church while retaining Black congregational identity and leadership.

Jones was ordained a deacon in 1795 and, on September 21, 1802, was ordained an Episcopal priest, becoming the first African American priest in the Episcopal Church. His ministry demonstrated that Black Christians could pursue institutional independence while remaining connected to an established denomination.

Richard Allen established Bethel African Methodist Episcopal Church, commonly known as Mother Bethel. Allen remained Methodist in doctrine and worship but insisted on Black ownership and control of the congregation's property and internal affairs. Mother Bethel became the institutional center from which the African Methodist Episcopal Church would later emerge.

The Mother Bethel property at Sixth and Lombard Streets in Philadelphia is recognized as the oldest parcel of land continuously owned by African Americans in the United States. That continuity of ownership illustrates why property rights were central to the movement for ecclesiastical independence.

The Formation of the African Methodist Episcopal Church

Mother Bethel's independence remained contested for more than two decades because the Methodist Episcopal Church's hierarchical structure gave white denominational authorities continuing claims over Black congregations nominally under their oversight. Disputes centered especially on property, pulpit control, and the authority to appoint clergy.

Allen and Mother Bethel pursued a prolonged legal and institutional struggle to secure independent status. Important Pennsylvania court decisions in 1807 and 1815 strengthened the congregation's control over its property and governance and helped establish the practical basis for full denominational independence.

In 1816, Allen convened representatives from Bethel and several other independent-minded Black Methodist congregations to organize the African Methodist Episcopal Church. The AME Church became the first fully independent Black-governed Christian denomination in the United States, and Allen was consecrated as its first bishop.

The denomination could now control its own clergy, discipline, conferences, property, and institutional expansion without white Methodist oversight. That combination of Black ownership, ordination, governance, and denominational continuity made the AME Church one of the most important institutional developments in early African American history.

The African Methodist Episcopal Zion Church

A parallel movement developed in New York City among Black Methodists connected to the John Street Methodist Episcopal Church. Beginning in 1796, Black worshippers organized separate meetings in response to restrictions on worship, leadership, and ordination.

In 1801, Zion incorporated as an African Methodist Episcopal congregation. Over the next two decades, its leaders moved steadily toward full independence. In 1820, the congregation adopted its own discipline, and in 1821 representatives held the first annual conference of what became the African Methodist Episcopal Zion Church. By 1822, the denomination had obtained ordination for Black ministers, allowing it to operate independently of white Methodist authority.

AME Zion remained distinct from Allen's AME Church even though the two shared Methodist theology and similar origins in resistance to racial exclusion. The denomination developed a strong antislavery reputation, and prominent abolitionists including Frederick Douglass and Harriet Tubman maintained important connections to AME Zion communities.

Why Institutional Independence Mattered

The movement toward independent Black churches was not primarily a doctrinal dispute. AME and AME Zion remained Methodist in theology and practice, and early independent Black Baptist congregations shared much of the same theology as white Baptist congregations. What independence secured was institutional control.

Property. Black ownership protected meeting houses, land, and other assets from being controlled, sold, reassigned, or repossessed by white trustees or denominational authorities.

Worship. Independent congregations could determine their own preaching, music, prayer, schedules, discipline, and styles of religious expression without white supervision.

Clergy. Black churches and denominations could recognize, train, ordain, appoint, and support Black ministers according to standards established by Black religious authorities.

Discipline and governance. Congregations could regulate membership, finances, moral conduct, leadership, and internal disputes through institutions they controlled themselves.

Institutional continuity. Black-controlled churches could outlast the tenure of any individual minister because property, records, finances, governing structures, and denominational networks belonged to the institution and community rather than to outside white authorities.

Mutual Aid, Education, and Abolition

Independent Black churches quickly assumed functions extending beyond formal worship. Following patterns established by the Free African Society, congregations supported the sick, widows, orphans, burial expenses, and families facing economic hardship. Churches also created or supported schools, literary societies, and educational initiatives in communities where formal schooling for Black Americans was restricted or unavailable.

Independent churches became important centers of antislavery activity. Clergy and congregants in AME, AME Zion, Baptist, Episcopal, and other Black religious communities participated in abolitionist organizing, conventions, public speaking, publishing, fundraising, and assistance to people escaping slavery.

Sarah Allen, Richard Allen's wife and an important organizer in her own right, provided assistance to freedom seekers and used her home at Lawrence Court as a place of refuge. Mother Bethel itself later became associated with Underground Railroad activity and functioned as an important sanctuary within Philadelphia's Black abolitionist community.

Institutional Expansion before the Civil War

From their founding centers in Philadelphia and New York, AME and AME Zion congregations expanded steadily through the early and mid-nineteenth century. Their strongest bases developed in Northern states, but congregations and missions also appeared in selected slave states and eventually farther west, including California.

Black Baptist congregations expanded differently. Because Baptist governance remained strongly congregational, independent churches multiplied locally rather than through a single centralized denominational structure. Regional associations and cooperative networks gradually developed and later laid the groundwork for national Black Baptist organization.

By the outbreak of the Civil War in 1861, independent Black congregations and denominations formed a substantial institutional presence in many Northern Black communities. In the South, many enslaved Black Christians continued to worship under white supervision, within biracial congregations, or in clandestine and semi-independent religious gatherings.

After emancipation, these antebellum institutions became part of a much larger transformation. Formerly enslaved Christians created independent congregations, joined expanding AME and AME Zion networks, reorganized Black Baptist churches, and established additional Black-controlled denominational structures throughout the South.

Legacy

The Black churches and denominations established between the 1770s and the 1850s created several of the principal institutional models on which the later expansion of African American Christianity was built. Black-owned property, Black-ordained clergy, community-controlled governance, mutual aid, education, abolitionist organizing, and durable denominational networks gave African American Christians institutional resources that extended far beyond worship alone.

The era's major developments, including the early Baptist communities associated with George Liele and Andrew Bryan, the withdrawal from St. George's, the Free African Society, St. Thomas, Mother Bethel, and the formation of the AME and AME Zion denominations, mark a decisive transition in African American religious history.

That transition did not create a single institutional model. Baptists, Methodists, Episcopalians, and later denominations developed different structures of governance and authority. What they increasingly shared was the principle that Black Christians could own, govern, finance, staff, discipline, and sustain religious institutions for themselves.

The rise of independent Black churches therefore represented more than separation from white congregations. It created enduring institutions through which African Americans could exercise leadership, preserve community autonomy, organize against slavery, educate future generations, and build the religious infrastructure that would expand dramatically after 1865.
