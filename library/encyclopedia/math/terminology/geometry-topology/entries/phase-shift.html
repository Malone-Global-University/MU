Phase Shift

Subfield: Geometry & Topology

Etymology & Roots

A phase shift is a horizontal displacement of a periodic pattern, especially a sine wave, cosine wave, or other oscillatory function. It changes where the cycle begins relative to the independent-variable axis without changing the fundamental shape of the cycle.

$$ \boxed{ f(x) \longrightarrow f(x-h) } $$

represents a horizontal shift by $h$ units to the right when $h>0$.

The word phase refers to a position or stage within a repeating cycle. In periodic phenomena, two oscillations can have the same amplitude and frequency but be at different stages of their cycles at the same input value.

The word shift indicates translation. Thus phase shift literally means a translation in where the cyclic phase is located.

$$ \boxed{ \text{phase shift} = \text{horizontal displacement of a periodic cycle} }. $$

In a sinusoidal function written:

$$ \boxed{ y = A\sin \left( B(x-h) \right)+D }, $$

the value $h$ is the phase shift in the $x$-units of the graph.

The related form:

$$ y = A\sin(Bx+C)+D $$

contains the same information, but the horizontal shift must be extracted by factoring the coefficient of $x$:

$$ \boxed{ h = -\frac{C}{B} } $$

when $B\neq0$.

The terminology is especially common in trigonometry, wave mathematics, signal analysis, periodic motion, electrical engineering, acoustics, and physics.

Operational Nuance

Standard Horizontal-Shift Form

For a periodic parent function $f$, the transformation:

$$ \boxed{ g(x) = f(x-h) } $$

shifts the graph horizontally by $h$ units.

Right Shift

If $h>0$:

$$ \boxed{ f(x-h) = \text{shift right by }h }. $$

Left Shift

If:

$$ f(x+h), \qquad h>0, $$

then the graph shifts left by $h$:

$$ \boxed{ f(x+h) = \text{shift left by }h }. $$

Why the Sign Looks Reversed

To reproduce the parent output $f(a)$ using:

$$ g(x)=f(x-h), $$

the inner input must satisfy:

$$ x-h=a. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ x=a+h }, $$

so the corresponding graph feature appears $h$ units farther right.

Sinusoidal Standard Form

A general sinusoidal model can be written:

$$ \boxed{ y = A\sin \left( B(x-h) \right)+D }. $$

Here:

  • $|A|$ controls amplitude;
  • $B$ controls period and horizontal scale;
  • $h$ controls phase shift;
  • $D$ controls vertical shift.

Cosine Form

$$ \boxed{ y = A\cos \left( B(x-h) \right)+D }. $$

The phase shift is again $h$.

Expanded Argument Form

A sinusoid is also commonly written:

$$ \boxed{ y = A\sin(Bx+C)+D }. $$

Extracting the Shift

Factor $B$:

$$ Bx+C = B \left( x+\frac{C}{B} \right) = B \left( x-\left(-\frac{C}{B}\right) \right). $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ h = -\frac{C}{B} }. $$

Example

Consider:

$$ y = \sin(2x-\pi). $$

Factor $2$:

$$ 2x-\pi = 2 \left( x-\frac{\pi}{2} \right). $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \text{phase shift} = \frac{\pi}{2} \text{ to the right} }. $$

Common Shortcut

For:

$$ A\sin(Bx+C)+D $$

or:

$$ A\cos(Bx+C)+D, $$

use:

$$ \boxed{ \text{phase shift} = -\frac{C}{B} }. $$

Why $-C$ Alone Is Usually Wrong

If $B\neq1$, the horizontal scale changes. The constant inside the angle must be divided by the coefficient of the independent variable.

Example of the Error

For:

$$ \cos(4x-\pi), $$

the shift is not $\pi$.

Instead:

$$ 4x-\pi = 4 \left( x-\frac{\pi}{4} \right), $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ h = \frac{\pi}{4} }. $$

Left-Shift Example

Consider:

$$ y = \cos(3x+2). $$

Then:

$$ 3x+2 = 3 \left( x+\frac{2}{3} \right) = 3 \left( x-\left(-\frac{2}{3}\right) \right). $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ h = -\frac{2}{3} }. $$

The graph shifts left by $2/3$ unit.

Period

For:

$$ y = A\sin(Bx+C)+D, $$

the fundamental period is:

$$ \boxed{ T = \frac{2\pi}{|B|} }. $$

Phase Shift Does Not Change Period

Replacing $x$ with $x-h$ relocates the cycle but does not alter its length:

$$ \boxed{ T_{\text{shifted}} = T_{\text{parent-scaled}} }. $$

Phase Shift Does Not Change Amplitude

Horizontal translation does not alter the vertical extent of a sinusoid.

Phase Shift Does Not Change Midline

The midline remains:

$$ \boxed{ y=D }. $$

Phase Shift vs. Vertical Shift

$$ \boxed{ x-h \Longrightarrow \text{horizontal shift} } $$
$$ \boxed{ +D \Longrightarrow \text{vertical shift} }. $$

Phase Shift vs. Period

Phase shift determines where a cycle begins; period determines how far the input must advance before the cycle repeats.

$$ \boxed{ \text{phase shift} = \text{cycle position} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{period} = \text{cycle length} }. $$

Phase Shift vs. Phase Angle

In:

$$ y = A\cos(\omega t+\phi), $$

$\phi$ is often called the phase angle or phase constant. It is measured in angular units.

The corresponding time shift is:

$$ \boxed{ h = -\frac{\phi}{\omega} }. $$

Angular Phase vs. Horizontal Shift

These are related but have different units:

$$ \boxed{ \phi = \text{angle} } $$
$$ \boxed{ h = \text{input displacement} }. $$

Time-Domain Sinusoid

A signal:

$$ \boxed{ x(t) = A\cos(\omega t+\phi) } $$

can be rewritten:

$$ x(t) = A\cos \left( \omega \left( t+\frac{\phi}{\omega} \right) \right). $$

Thus the time shift is:

$$ \boxed{ t_0 = -\frac{\phi}{\omega} }. $$

Time Delay

A delayed signal:

$$ \boxed{ x(t-\tau) } $$

is shifted right in time by $\tau$.

Corresponding Phase Change

If:

$$ x(t) = A\cos(\omega t), $$

then delaying by $\tau$ gives:

$$ x(t-\tau) = A\cos(\omega t-\omega\tau). $$

Therefore the phase change is:

$$ \boxed{ \Delta\phi = -\omega\tau }. $$

Time Advance

A signal:

$$ x(t+\tau) $$

is advanced by $\tau$ and corresponds to:

$$ \boxed{ \Delta\phi = +\omega\tau }. $$

Phase Difference

Two sinusoids with the same angular frequency:

$$ x_1(t) = A_1\cos(\omega t+\phi_1) $$

and:

$$ x_2(t) = A_2\cos(\omega t+\phi_2) $$

have phase difference:

$$ \boxed{ \Delta\phi = \phi_2-\phi_1 }. $$

Corresponding Time Shift

$$ \boxed{ \Delta t = -\frac{\Delta\phi}{\omega} } $$

when interpreting the second signal relative to the first under the stated sign convention.

Phase Difference as a Fraction of a Cycle

Since a complete cycle corresponds to $2\pi$ radians:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\Delta\phi}{2\pi} = \text{fraction of a cycle} }. $$

Time Shift as a Fraction of Period

Because:

$$ T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega}, $$

a shift of magnitude $\Delta t$ corresponds to:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{|\Delta t|}{T} = \frac{|\Delta\phi|}{2\pi} }. $$

Quarter-Cycle Phase Difference

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{1}{4} \text{ cycle} }. $$

Half-Cycle Phase Difference

$$ \boxed{ \pi = \frac{1}{2} \text{ cycle} }. $$

Full-Cycle Phase Difference

$$ \boxed{ 2\pi = 1 \text{ full cycle} }. $$

In Phase

Two same-frequency sinusoids are in phase when their phase difference is an integer multiple of $2\pi$:

$$ \boxed{ \Delta\phi = 2\pi n, \qquad n\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Opposite Phase

A phase difference of $\pi$ corresponds to opposite points of the cycle:

$$ \boxed{ \Delta\phi = \pi \pmod{2\pi} }. $$

Common Language: 180 Degrees Out of Phase

$$ \boxed{ \pi\text{ radians} = 180^\circ }. $$

Two same-frequency sinusoids differing by this phase are often said to be 180 degrees out of phase.

Quadrature

A phase difference of:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\pi}{2} = 90^\circ } $$

is often called quadrature.

Sine and Cosine as Phase-Shifted Versions

$$ \boxed{ \sin x = \cos \left( x-\frac{\pi}{2} \right) }. $$

Thus sine can be viewed as cosine shifted right by $\pi/2$.

Equivalent Identity

$$ \boxed{ \cos x = \sin \left( x+\frac{\pi}{2} \right) }. $$

Why Phase Shift Is Not Unique for Periodic Functions

Because periodic functions repeat, shifts differing by a whole period can describe the same graph.

For sine:

$$ \sin(x-h) = \sin \left( x-(h+2\pi n) \right), \qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}. $$

Equivalent Phase Shifts

If $T$ is a period, then:

$$ \boxed{ h \sim h+nT } $$

in the sense that the resulting periodic graph is unchanged.

Canonical Phase Shift

A problem may choose one representative shift within a specified interval, such as:

$$ 0\leq h<T $$

or:

$$ -\frac{T}{2} < h \leq \frac{T}{2}. $$

Such a restriction is a convention, not an inherent uniqueness property.

Negative Amplitude Can Alter Apparent Phase

Since:

$$ -\sin x = \sin(x-\pi), $$

a vertical reflection can also be represented as a half-period phase shift.

Cosine Example

$$ \boxed{ -\cos x = \cos(x-\pi) }. $$

Parameter Nonuniqueness

A sinusoid can often be represented using different combinations of amplitude sign and phase shift.

Example

$$ \boxed{ -2\sin x = 2\sin(x-\pi) }. $$

Why Standardization Helps

When comparing sinusoidal models, it is often useful to require:

$$ \boxed{ A>0 } $$

and place the phase shift in a chosen interval. This reduces representational ambiguity.

Phase Shift from a Graph

For sine, one common reference point is the upward midline crossing of the parent graph.

In:

$$ y = A\sin \left( B(x-h) \right)+D, \qquad A>0, \quad B>0, $$

the point:

$$ \boxed{ (h,D) } $$

is an upward midline crossing.

Cosine Reference Point

For:

$$ y = A\cos \left( B(x-h) \right)+D, \qquad A>0, $$

the cycle begins at a maximum:

$$ \boxed{ (h,D+A) }. $$

Negative Amplitude Caveat

If $A<0$, the corresponding cosine reference point at $x=h$ is a minimum rather than a maximum.

Quarter-Period Structure

Once the phase shift $h$ and period $T$ are known, key sinusoidal points are spaced by:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{T}{4} }. $$

Sine Key Points

For a standard positive-amplitude sine cycle beginning at $x=h$:

$$ \boxed{ h,\quad h+\frac{T}{4},\quad h+\frac{T}{2},\quad h+\frac{3T}{4},\quad h+T }. $$

Cosine Key Points

The same quarter-period spacing applies, but the cycle starts at an extremum rather than a midline crossing.

Phase Shift and Periodic Motion

In periodic motion:

$$ x(t) = A\cos \left( \omega(t-t_0) \right), $$

the time shift $t_0$ determines when a chosen phase of the motion occurs.

Initial Conditions

Phase shift is often determined from initial position and velocity.

Example Framework

If:

$$ x(t) = A\cos(\omega t+\phi), $$

then:

$$ x(0) = A\cos\phi. $$

The initial velocity is:

$$ \boxed{ v(0) = -A\omega\sin\phi }. $$

Together these conditions can determine the phase angle $\phi$ up to the normal periodic equivalence.

Phase Ambiguity

Because:

$$ \phi \quad\text{and}\quad \phi+2\pi n $$

describe the same sinusoidal phase:

$$ \boxed{ \phi \equiv \phi+2\pi n \pmod{2\pi} }. $$

Wave Phase

A traveling wave may be written:

$$ \boxed{ y(x,t) = A\cos(kx-\omega t+\phi) }. $$

Temporal Phase Shift

At fixed $x$, $\phi$ changes the time placement of the oscillation.

Spatial Phase Shift

At fixed $t$, the same phase constant also changes where the wave pattern is located in space.

Spatial Shift from Phase

For:

$$ A\cos(kx+\phi), $$

the horizontal spatial shift is:

$$ \boxed{ h = -\frac{\phi}{k} }. $$

Wavelength Relation

Since:

$$ \lambda = \frac{2\pi}{|k|}, $$

a phase difference corresponds to a fraction of a wavelength:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{|\Delta x|}{\lambda} = \frac{|\Delta\phi|}{2\pi} }. $$

Signals

In signal processing, phase shift describes relative displacement between periodic signals of the same frequency.

Lead

A signal whose corresponding cycle feature occurs earlier is said to lead another signal.

Lag

A signal whose corresponding cycle feature occurs later is said to lag another.

Lead-Lag Sign Conventions

Sign conventions depend on whether a model is written with:

$$ \omega t+\phi $$

or:

$$ \omega t-\phi. $$

Therefore verbal statements such as "leads by $\phi$" should be tied explicitly to the chosen formula.

Electrical AC Models

Voltage and current may be represented:

$$ v(t) = V_0\cos(\omega t+\phi_v) $$

and:

$$ i(t) = I_0\cos(\omega t+\phi_i). $$

Their phase difference is:

$$ \boxed{ \phi_i-\phi_v }. $$

Acoustics

Phase shift can describe the relative timing of sound waves with the same frequency.

Interference

Relative phase influences whether waves reinforce or cancel.

Constructive Alignment

Equal-frequency sinusoids with:

$$ \Delta\phi = 2\pi n $$

have their peaks aligned.

Opposing Alignment

Equal-frequency sinusoids with:

$$ \Delta\phi = \pi+2\pi n $$

have peaks aligned with troughs.

Phase Shift as Translation Symmetry

Geometrically, phase shift is simply the horizontal translation:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y) \mapsto (x+h,y) }. $$

Phase Shift vs. General Horizontal Shift

Every phase shift is a horizontal shift, but the term phase shift is most natural when the function represents cyclic or periodic behavior.

$$ \boxed{ \text{phase shift} \subset \text{horizontal-translation language conceptually} }. $$

In ordinary prose, it is better to say that phase shift is the periodic-function use of horizontal translation rather than treating the two terms as formally different transformation operations.

Phase Shift and Coterminal Angles

A phase angle can be changed by whole cycles without changing the sinusoid:

$$ \boxed{ \phi \longrightarrow \phi+2\pi n }. $$

Phase Shift Modulo the Period

Horizontal shifts of a $T$-periodic function are naturally considered modulo $T$.

$$ \boxed{ h_1 \equiv h_2 \pmod{T} } $$

informally means their difference is an integer multiple of the period.

Example

For sine:

$$ \frac{\pi}{2} \quad\text{and}\quad \frac{5\pi}{2} $$

differ by $2\pi$, so they produce equivalent horizontal placements.

Phase Shift and Tangent

Tangent has period $\pi$, so its equivalent phase shifts are considered modulo $\pi$ rather than $2\pi$.

Tangent Form

$$ \boxed{ y = A\tan \left( B(x-h) \right)+D }. $$

The phase shift is $h$, while the period is:

$$ \boxed{ T = \frac{\pi}{|B|} }. $$

Cotangent Form

$$ \boxed{ y = A\cot \left( B(x-h) \right)+D }. $$

Asymptote Shift

For tangent and cotangent, phase shift moves the repeating asymptote structure horizontally along with the rest of the graph.

Secant and Cosecant

Phase shifts of cosine and sine transfer directly to their reciprocal functions, subject to their repeating domain exclusions.

Phase Shift and Domain Features

Horizontal shifting relocates zeros, extrema, and discontinuities by the same displacement.

Shifted Zeros

If $x_0$ is a zero of $f$, then $x_0+h$ is a zero of:

$$ g(x)=f(x-h). $$

Shifted Maxima

A maximum originally at $x=x_0$ moves to:

$$ \boxed{ x=x_0+h }. $$

Shifted Asymptotes

A vertical asymptote originally at $x=a$ moves to:

$$ \boxed{ x=a+h }. $$

Phase Shift Does Not Mean the Object Physically Moves Sideways

In a time-based model, a horizontal graph shift represents a change in timing or phase, not necessarily a literal translation through physical space.

Common Error: Reading the Sign Directly

In:

$$ f(x-h), $$

a minus sign produces a right shift, not a left shift.

Common Error: Using $C$ as the Phase Shift

In:

$$ A\sin(Bx+C)+D, $$

$C$ is an angular offset inside the argument. The horizontal phase shift is:

$$ \boxed{ -\frac{C}{B} }. $$

Common Error: Forgetting to Factor $B$

The expression:

$$ 3x-6 $$

should be written:

$$ \boxed{ 3(x-2) } $$

before identifying the shift as $2$.

Common Error: Confusing Phase Shift with Period

A function can have the same period but many different phase shifts.

Common Error: Confusing Phase Shift with Amplitude

Amplitude changes vertical size; phase shift changes horizontal placement.

Common Error: Confusing Phase Shift with Vertical Shift

The constant $D$ outside a trigonometric expression controls the vertical midline, not phase.

Common Error: Assuming Phase Shift Changes Frequency

Translation does not alter how frequently the cycle repeats.

Common Error: Assuming Phase Shift Is Always Positive

A negative phase shift represents movement to the left under the usual $f(x-h)$ convention.

Common Error: Assuming Phase Shift Is Unique

For a periodic function, shifts differing by an integer multiple of the period are equivalent.

Common Error: Confusing Phase Angle with Phase Shift

A phase angle such as $\phi$ is angular. A phase shift such as $h$ is measured in the independent variable.

Common Error: Ignoring Units

If the independent variable is time, phase shift may be measured in seconds. If the independent variable is position, it may be measured in meters. A phase angle is instead measured in radians or degrees.

Common Error: Saying $\phi/\omega$ without Checking the Sign

For:

$$ \cos(\omega t+\phi), $$

the horizontal time shift is:

$$ \boxed{ -\frac{\phi}{\omega} }. $$

Common Error: Mixing Degree and Radian Phase

The phase and angular-frequency units must be compatible.

Common Error: Calling Every Horizontal Shift a Phase Shift

The term is most meaningful for cyclic or periodic phenomena. For a nonperiodic function, horizontal shift is usually the clearer term.

Common Error: Assuming Negative Amplitude and Phase Shift Are Independent Unique Features

Periodicity identities can allow a negative amplitude to be absorbed into a half-period phase shift.

Common Error: Comparing Phase of Different Frequencies as a Fixed Quantity

If two sinusoids have different frequencies, their relative phase generally changes with time rather than remaining constant.

Common Error: Calling Two Signals In Phase Merely Because They Cross Zero Together Once

In-phase behavior requires consistent cycle alignment, not a single accidental intersection.

Common Error: Confusing Phase Lead and Graph Shift Sign

Lead and lag terminology depends on the exact sign convention used in the sinusoidal expression, so the formula should be stated explicitly.

Common Error: Treating a Full-Period Shift as a Visibly New Phase

A shift by exactly one period reproduces the same periodic function.

$$ \boxed{ f(x-T)=f(x) } $$

for a $T$-periodic function.

Common Misconception: The phase shift in $A\sin(Bx+C)+D$ is not simply $C$. The phase shift is the horizontal translation obtained after factoring the argument: $h=-C/B$. The quantity $C$ is an angular phase constant, while $h$ is measured in units of the independent variable.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$f(x-h)$ "F shifted horizontally by h units to the right when h is positive."
$A\sin(B(x-h))+D$ "A sinusoid with phase shift h."
$h=-C/B$ "The phase shift is negative C divided by B."
$A\cos(\omega t+\phi)$ "A cosine with phase constant phi."
$t_0=-\phi/\omega$ "The corresponding time shift is negative phi divided by omega."
$\Delta\phi=\phi_2-\phi_1$ "The phase difference is phi two minus phi one."
$\Delta\phi=\pi/2$ "The signals differ in phase by ninety degrees, or one quarter cycle."

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"shifted right by }h\text{"} } $$

corresponds to:

$$ f(x-h). $$

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"shifted left by }h\text{"} } $$

corresponds to:

$$ f(x+h). $$

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"phase difference of }\pi\text{"} } $$

means a half-cycle separation.

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"ninety degrees out of phase"} } $$

means:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\pi}{2} \text{ radians} }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Rewrite trigonometric arguments in factored form before identifying a phase shift. Distinguish the angular phase constant $C$ or $\phi$ from the horizontal displacement $h$. State whether the independent variable is time, distance, or angle, because the units of phase shift depend on that variable. When discussing lead or lag, specify the sinusoidal sign convention to prevent ambiguity.

In standard factored form: $$ \boxed{ y = A\sin \left( B(x-h) \right)+D }, $$ the phase shift is directly: $$ \boxed{ h }. $$ In expanded form: $$ \boxed{ y = A\sin(Bx+C)+D }, $$ it is: $$ \boxed{ h = -\frac{C}{B} }. $$ In time-domain sinusoidal models, this distinguishes angular phase from actual time displacement: $$ \boxed{ A\cos(\omega t+\phi) = A\cos \left( \omega \left( t+\frac{\phi}{\omega} \right) \right) }, $$ so the corresponding time shift is: $$ \boxed{ -\frac{\phi}{\omega} }. $$ Phase shift therefore connects graph transformations, periodicity, trigonometry, periodic motion, waves, and signal timing through one central idea: moving the cycle along the independent-variable axis without changing its repeating form.
