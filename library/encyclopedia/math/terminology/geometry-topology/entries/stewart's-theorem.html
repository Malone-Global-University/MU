Stewart's Theorem

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

Stewart's Theorem is named after the Scottish mathematician Matthew Stewart (1717–1785), who published the result in 1746 in Some General Theorems of Considerable Use in the Higher Parts of Mathematics. The theorem concerns a line segment drawn from a vertex of a triangle to the opposite side and provides an exact relationship among the resulting lengths.

The historical attribution is more complicated than the name suggests. Sources on the history of geometry indicate that closely related results may have been known earlier, and later historical discussions have questioned how much originality should be assigned to Stewart personally. The modern name nevertheless became attached to the formula through Stewart's eighteenth-century publication.

The segment appearing in the theorem is called a cevian: a segment joining a vertex of a triangle to a point on the opposite side. Medians, angle bisectors, and altitudes are all special kinds of cevians. Stewart's Theorem is therefore a general theorem about cevian lengths rather than a theorem tied to only one special triangle construction.

Operational Nuance

Let $\triangle ABC$ have side lengths:

$$ BC=a, \qquad AC=b, \qquad AB=c. $$

Let a cevian $AD$ meet side $BC$ at the point $D$. Suppose:

$$ BD=m, \qquad DC=n, \qquad AD=d. $$

Since $D$ lies on $BC$:

$$ a=m+n. $$

Stewart's Theorem states:

$$ \boxed{ b^2m+c^2n = a(d^2+mn) } $$

Equivalently:

$$ b^2m+c^2n = (m+n)(d^2+mn). $$

The theorem is especially useful when the side lengths and the way the cevian divides the opposite side are known, but the cevian length is unknown. Solving for $d^2$ gives:

$$ d^2 = \frac{b^2m+c^2n}{m+n} - mn. $$

Therefore:

$$ d = \sqrt{ \frac{b^2m+c^2n}{m+n} - mn }. $$

For example, suppose:

$$ b=7, \qquad c=5, \qquad m=3, \qquad n=2. $$

Then:

$$ a=m+n=5. $$

Stewart's Theorem gives:

$$ 7^2(3)+5^2(2) = 5(d^2+3\cdot2). $$

Simplifying:

$$ 147+50 = 5(d^2+6) $$ $$ 197=5d^2+30 $$ $$ 167=5d^2 $$ $$ d^2=\frac{167}{5}. $$

Thus:

$$ d=\sqrt{\frac{167}{5}}. $$

One of the useful features of Stewart's Theorem is that the cevian need not have any special property. It does not have to bisect the side, bisect an angle, or meet the opposite side at a right angle.

Common Misconception: Stewart's Theorem is not only a formula for a median. A median is merely one important special case. The theorem applies to a general cevian $AD$ dividing the opposite side into lengths $m$ and $n$.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$AD=d$ "The cevian A D has length d."
$BD=m$ "B D has length m."
$DC=n$ "D C has length n."
$a=m+n$ "Side a equals m plus n."
$b^2m+c^2n=a(d^2+mn)$ "B squared times m plus c squared times n equals a times the quantity d squared plus m n."
$d^2=\frac{b^2m+c^2n}{a}-mn$ "D squared equals b squared m plus c squared n, divided by a, minus m n."

The notation is easy to misuse unless the side labels are tied carefully to the diagram. With the conventional setup:

$$ BC=a=m+n, $$ $$ AC=b, $$ $$ AB=c, $$ $$ AD=d. $$

The segment $m$ lies adjacent to vertex $B$, while $n$ lies adjacent to vertex $C$. Accordingly:

$$ b^2m+c^2n $$

pairs side $b=AC$ with $m=BD$ and side $c=AB$ with $n=DC$. This cross-pairing is one of the most common places students make errors.

A traditional classroom mnemonic for Stewart's formula exists, but mnemonic forms depend heavily on the chosen lettering system. For a terminology reference, the structural relationship:

$$ b^2m+c^2n = (m+n)(d^2+mn) $$

is safer than memorizing a phrase without understanding which length each symbol represents.

Defense Notation Advice: Draw and label the triangle before substituting into Stewart's Theorem. There is no universal law requiring every textbook to assign $a,b,c,d,m,n$ exactly as shown here. The formula is reliable only when the symbols are interpreted consistently with the diagram.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • General Cevians: Stewart's Theorem determines the length of a cevian from the side lengths of the triangle and the two segments into which the cevian divides the opposite side: $$ b^2m+c^2n = a(d^2+mn). $$
  • Medians: If $AD$ is a median, then: $$ m=n=\frac{a}{2}. $$ Substituting into Stewart's Theorem produces: $$ b^2\frac{a}{2} + c^2\frac{a}{2} = a\left( d^2+\frac{a^2}{4} \right). $$ Dividing by $a$ and simplifying gives: $$ d^2 = \frac{2b^2+2c^2-a^2}{4}. $$ Hence the median length is: $$ d = \frac{1}{2} \sqrt{2b^2+2c^2-a^2}. $$
  • Apollonius' Theorem: The median case of Stewart's Theorem yields the classical relation known as Apollonius' Theorem: $$ b^2+c^2 = 2d^2+\frac{a^2}{2}, $$ where $d$ is the median to side $a$.
  • Angle Bisectors: If the cevian is an angle bisector, the Angle Bisector Theorem provides: $$ \frac{m}{n} = \frac{c}{b}. $$ This ratio can be combined with Stewart's Theorem to derive a formula for the length of an internal angle bisector.
  • Altitudes: If the cevian is perpendicular to the opposite side, Stewart's Theorem remains valid. In that special configuration, however, direct applications of the Pythagorean theorem are often simpler.
  • Triangle Geometry: The theorem serves as a general framework connecting four important lengths: the three sides of a triangle and an interior cevian. Because many named triangle segments are cevians, Stewart's Theorem can unify several formulas that otherwise appear unrelated.
  • Law of Cosines: Stewart's Theorem can be derived using the Law of Cosines on the two smaller triangles created by the cevian. The angle terms can be eliminated, leaving a relationship involving only lengths. This shows that Stewart's Theorem is closely connected to the metric geometry encoded by the Law of Cosines.
  • Pythagorean Derivation: Another derivation drops an altitude from the vertex containing the cevian and applies the Pythagorean theorem to the resulting right triangles. Algebraic elimination of the altitude leads to Stewart's formula.
  • Extended Cevians: More general signed-length versions of Stewart's Theorem can be used when the point $D$ lies on the line containing $BC$ but outside the triangle. In that setting, directed or signed segments allow the various configurations to be represented within a unified formula.
  • Problem Solving: Stewart's Theorem is particularly valuable when a geometry problem gives all three side lengths together with the location where a cevian meets the opposite side. It often avoids the need to calculate angles before determining the cevian length.
  • Terminological Contrast: A cevian is any segment joining a vertex of a triangle to the opposite side. A median is a cevian that bisects the opposite side. An angle bisector divides the vertex angle into two equal angles, and an altitude is perpendicular to the opposite side. Stewart's Theorem applies to the general cevian and therefore includes all of these configurations as special cases when their additional conditions are imposed.
