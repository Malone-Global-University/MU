Ceva's Theorem

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

Ceva's Theorem is named after the Italian mathematician Giovanni Benedetto Ceva (1647–1734), who published the result in 1678 in his work De lineis rectis se invicem secantibus statica constructio. The theorem gives a precise condition under which three lines drawn from the vertices of a triangle meet at one common point.

The historical story is more complicated than the modern name suggests. A form of the result had already appeared centuries earlier in medieval Islamic mathematics, including work attributed to the eleventh-century mathematician and ruler Yusuf al-Mu'taman ibn Hud of Zaragoza. Ceva independently developed and published the result in seventeenth-century Europe, and his name became attached to it.

The theorem belongs to the geometry of cevians. A cevian is a line segment drawn from a vertex of a triangle to the opposite side or its supporting line. The word cevian itself was coined later from Ceva's name because of the theorem's close association with these vertex-to-side lines.

Operational Nuance

Let $\triangle ABC$ contain points:

$$ D\in BC, \qquad E\in CA, \qquad F\in AB. $$

Draw the three cevians:

$$ AD,\qquad BE,\qquad CF. $$

Ceva's Theorem states that these three cevians are concurrent—that is, they all pass through one common point—if and only if:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{BD}{DC} \cdot \frac{CE}{EA} \cdot \frac{AF}{FB} = 1 } $$

Thus:

$$ AD,\ BE,\ CF \text{ concurrent} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{BD}{DC} \frac{CE}{EA} \frac{AF}{FB} =1. $$

The phrase if and only if is important. Ceva's Theorem works in both directions:

  • if the three cevians are known to be concurrent, the product of the three ratios must equal $1$;
  • if the three ratios have product $1$, the three cevians must be concurrent.

For example, suppose:

$$ \frac{BD}{DC}=2 $$ $$ \frac{CE}{EA}=3. $$

If the three cevians are to be concurrent, then:

$$ 2\cdot3\cdot\frac{AF}{FB}=1. $$

Therefore:

$$ \frac{AF}{FB} = \frac{1}{6}. $$

This illustrates one of the theorem's most common uses: two side-division ratios determine the third ratio required for concurrency.

The theorem also immediately explains why the three medians of a triangle are concurrent. If $D$, $E$, and $F$ are the midpoints of their respective sides, then:

$$ \frac{BD}{DC} = \frac{CE}{EA} = \frac{AF}{FB} = 1. $$

Therefore:

$$ 1\cdot1\cdot1=1, $$

so Ceva's Theorem guarantees that the three medians meet at a single point. That point is the centroid.

Common Misconception: Ceva's Theorem is not fundamentally a theorem about side lengths alone. The ratios describe how three points divide the sides, but the theorem's geometric conclusion concerns the three cevians drawn from the opposite vertices. The result tells us when those lines meet at one common point.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$D\in BC$ "D lies on side B C."
$AD,\ BE,\ CF$ "The cevians A D, B E, and C F."
$AD\cap BE\cap CF=\{P\}$ "A D, B E, and C F meet at the common point P."
$\frac{BD}{DC}$ "The ratio B D to D C."
$\frac{BD}{DC}\frac{CE}{EA}\frac{AF}{FB}=1$ "B D over D C times C E over E A times A F over F B equals one."
$\Longleftrightarrow$ "If and only if."

The cyclic order of the ratios helps prevent errors. Move around the triangle:

$$ B\to D\to C, $$ $$ C\to E\to A, $$ $$ A\to F\to B. $$

This produces:

$$ \frac{BD}{DC} \cdot \frac{CE}{EA} \cdot \frac{AF}{FB}. $$

Some textbooks use different labels, such as $X$, $Y$, and $Z$ instead of $D$, $E$, and $F$. The letters themselves are not important. What matters is that each point lies on the side opposite the vertex from which its cevian is drawn.

Defense Notation Advice: Keep a consistent cyclic orientation when forming the three ratios. Inverting only one ratio changes the product and produces an incorrect test. If a source uses directed segments or points on side extensions, follow that source's sign convention explicitly rather than mixing signed and unsigned formulations.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Concurrency: Ceva's Theorem is fundamentally a concurrency criterion: $$ AD,\ BE,\ CF \text{ concurrent} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{BD}{DC} \frac{CE}{EA} \frac{AF}{FB} =1. $$ It converts the geometric question "Do these three lines meet at one point?" into a numerical ratio calculation.
  • Medians: Every median divides the opposite side into two equal parts, so each ratio equals $1$. Hence: $$ 1\cdot1\cdot1=1, $$ proving that the three medians are concurrent at the centroid.
  • Angle Bisectors: If the three cevians are internal angle bisectors, the Angle Bisector Theorem gives: $$ \frac{BD}{DC}=\frac{AB}{AC}, $$ with analogous formulas at the other vertices. Multiplying the three resulting ratios causes the side lengths to cancel, giving $1$. Ceva's Theorem therefore proves that the three internal angle bisectors are concurrent at the incenter.
  • Altitudes: The three altitudes of a triangle are also concurrent, meeting at the orthocenter. Trigonometric or length-based forms of Ceva's Theorem can be used to establish this concurrency.
  • Perpendicular Bisectors: Perpendicular bisectors are not cevians in the ordinary sense because they do not generally originate at the vertices. Their concurrency at the circumcenter is therefore not a direct application of the basic form of Ceva's Theorem.
  • Finding Unknown Ratios: If concurrency is known and two ratios are given, the third can be found from: $$ \frac{AF}{FB} = \frac{1}{ \left(\frac{BD}{DC}\right) \left(\frac{CE}{EA}\right) }. $$
  • Proving Concurrency: Conversely, a geometry proof may first calculate three side-division ratios. If their product is $1$, Ceva's converse establishes that the three associated cevians meet at one point.
  • Trigonometric Ceva: A powerful variant expresses concurrency through sines of angles. If cevians $AD$, $BE$, and $CF$ meet inside $\triangle ABC$, then one form is: $$ \frac{\sin\angle BAD}{\sin\angle DAC} \cdot \frac{\sin\angle CBE}{\sin\angle EBA} \cdot \frac{\sin\angle ACF}{\sin\angle FCB} = 1. $$ This version is particularly useful when angle information is known but side ratios are not.
  • Area Form: Because triangles sharing the same altitude have area ratios equal to the ratios of their bases, the side ratios in Ceva's Theorem can also be interpreted through ratios of triangle areas. This often provides elegant geometric proofs.
  • Mass Points: Ceva's ratio relationships are closely related to the technique known as mass points. Assigning suitable masses to triangle vertices can encode the same balance relationships that determine how concurrent cevians divide the sides.
  • Directed Segments: More general versions permit points to lie on extensions of the sides. Directed or signed segment conventions allow internal and external cevians to be treated within a unified framework, provided the sign convention is applied consistently.
  • Relationship to Menelaus' Theorem: Ceva's and Menelaus' theorems use strikingly similar products of side ratios but describe different geometric configurations. Ceva concerns three cevians meeting at one point; Menelaus concerns three side-intersection points lying on one line.
  • Ceva vs. Menelaus: A useful conceptual memory device is: $$ \boxed{ \text{Ceva} \longrightarrow \text{concurrent at one point} } $$ $$ \boxed{ \text{Menelaus} \longrightarrow \text{collinear on one line} } $$ The two theorems are closely related and often studied together in advanced triangle geometry.
  • Affine & Projective Geometry: Ceva-type relationships extend beyond elementary Euclidean geometry and have formulations in affine, projective, spherical, and higher-dimensional settings. The elementary triangle theorem is the most familiar representative of this broader family of concurrency principles.
  • Terminological Contrast: A cevian joins a triangle vertex to the opposite side or its supporting line. Concurrency means several lines meet at one common point. Collinearity means several points lie on one common line. Ceva's Theorem characterizes concurrency; Menelaus' Theorem characterizes collinearity.
