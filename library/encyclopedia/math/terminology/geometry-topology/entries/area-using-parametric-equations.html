Area Using Parametric Equations

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

Area using parametric equations refers to finding planar area when a curve is described indirectly by two functions of a third variable:

$$ \boxed{ x=x(t), \qquad y=y(t) }. $$

The variable $t$ is called a parameter. As $t$ changes, the point:

$$ (x(t),y(t)) $$

traces a curve through the plane.

For an ordinary function $y=f(x)$, area under a nonnegative curve is:

$$ A=\int y\,dx. $$

In a parametric representation:

$$ dx=x'(t)\,dt. $$

Therefore the corresponding parametric area formula becomes:

$$ \boxed{ A= \int_\alpha^\beta y(t)x'(t)\,dt }. $$

This expression is naturally a signed area. Its sign depends on the direction in which the curve is traced.

Operational Nuance

Parametric Representation

A plane curve may be written:

$$ \boxed{ x=f(t), \qquad y=g(t) }. $$

Rather than making $y$ directly a function of $x$, both coordinates are determined by the parameter $t$.

Area under a Parametric Curve

Start with:

$$ A=\int y\,dx. $$

Since:

$$ dx=x'(t)\,dt, $$

substitute:

$$ \boxed{ A= \int_\alpha^\beta y(t)x'(t)\,dt }. $$

This formula directly gives the usual positive area under a nonnegative curve when the curve is traced from left to right, so that $x'(t)\ge0$ throughout the relevant interval.

Bounds of Integration

The limits $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are values of the parameter, not necessarily $x$-coordinates.

$$ \boxed{ t=\alpha \quad\text{to}\quad t=\beta }. $$

The corresponding endpoints are:

$$ (x(\alpha),y(\alpha)) \qquad\text{and}\qquad (x(\beta),y(\beta)). $$

Why the Derivative x'(t) Appears

In an ordinary integral, a thin vertical strip has width $dx$. Under a parametrization:

$$ dx= \frac{dx}{dt}\,dt = x'(t)\,dt. $$

Thus:

$$ y\,dx = y(t)x'(t)\,dt. $$

The factor $x'(t)$ converts change in the parameter into horizontal change.

Example: Reparametrizing an Ordinary Function

Consider:

$$ x=t, \qquad y=t^2, \qquad 0\le t\le2. $$

Then:

$$ x'(t)=1. $$

Therefore:

$$ A= \int_0^2 t^2(1)\,dt. $$

Hence:

$$ A= \left[ \frac{t^3}{3} \right]_0^2 = \boxed{ \frac83 }. $$

A More Genuinely Parametric Example

Suppose:

$$ x=t^2, \qquad y=t^3, \qquad 0\le t\le1. $$

Then:

$$ x'(t)=2t. $$

The area under the curve is:

$$ A= \int_0^1 t^3(2t)\,dt. $$

Therefore:

$$ A= 2\int_0^1t^4\,dt = \frac25. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ A=\frac25 }. $$

Connection to Eliminating the Parameter

In the previous example:

$$ x=t^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad t=\sqrt{x} $$

for $t\ge0$, so:

$$ y=t^3=x^{3/2}. $$

The ordinary Cartesian calculation gives:

$$ \int_0^1x^{3/2}\,dx = \frac25, $$

confirming that the parametric and Cartesian methods agree.

Orientation Matters

Suppose the same geometric curve is traced from right to left. Then:

$$ x'(t)<0. $$

Consequently:

$$ \int y(t)x'(t)\,dt $$

may be negative even when the geometric region lies above the $x$-axis.

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \int y\,dx \text{ is orientation-sensitive} }. $$

Reversing the Parameter Direction

Reversing the limits changes the sign:

$$ \boxed{ \int_\beta^\alpha y(t)x'(t)\,dt = - \int_\alpha^\beta y(t)x'(t)\,dt }. $$

Geometric Area

If a curve is traced monotonically from left to right and remains above the $x$-axis, the integral is already positive geometric area.

More generally, geometric area may require splitting the parameter interval wherever:

  • $y(t)$ changes sign,
  • $x'(t)$ changes sign,
  • the curve reverses horizontal direction,
  • the boundary of the intended region changes.

Area below the x-Axis

If the curve is traversed left to right but:

$$ y(t)<0, $$

then:

$$ y(t)x'(t)<0. $$

The integral is negative, while geometric area is positive.

The relevant geometric contribution is then:

$$ \boxed{ -\int y(t)x'(t)\,dt } $$

over that portion, assuming $x'(t)>0$ there.

Total Area against the x-Axis

When $x(t)$ moves monotonically across the interval, total geometric area between the curve and $x$-axis can be written:

$$ \boxed{ A= \int_\alpha^\beta |y(t)|\,|x'(t)|\,dt } $$

provided the parametrized trace does not introduce overlapping or repeated regions that would cause area to be counted more than once.

Area between a Parametric Curve and a Horizontal Line

If the lower boundary is:

$$ y=c, $$

then the signed area contribution is:

$$ \boxed{ A= \int_\alpha^\beta [y(t)-c]x'(t)\,dt }. $$

Area between Two Parametric Curves

Suppose two curves share the same horizontal parameterization:

$$ x=x(t), $$

with upper and lower functions:

$$ y=y_{\text{upper}}(t), \qquad y=y_{\text{lower}}(t). $$

If $x'(t)\ge0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ A= \int_\alpha^\beta \left[ y_{\text{upper}}(t) - y_{\text{lower}}(t) \right] x'(t)\,dt }. $$

Integration with Respect to y

Area may also be built from horizontal strips.

Since:

$$ dy=y'(t)\,dt, $$

an expression involving:

$$ \int x\,dy $$

becomes:

$$ \boxed{ \int_\alpha^\beta x(t)y'(t)\,dt }. $$

The correct sign depends on which boundary is being traversed and in what orientation.

Closed Curves

Parametric equations are especially useful for closed curves, where a single parameter interval traces the entire boundary.

If a simple closed curve $C$ is traversed counterclockwise, one area formula is:

$$ \boxed{ A= \oint_C x\,dy }. $$

Another is:

$$ \boxed{ A= -\oint_C y\,dx }. $$

Symmetric Closed-Curve Formula

Combining these gives the orientation-aware formula:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \oint_C (x\,dy-y\,dx) } $$

for a positively oriented, counterclockwise simple closed curve.

Parametric Closed-Curve Formula

If:

$$ x=x(t), \qquad y=y(t), \qquad \alpha\le t\le\beta, $$

traces the curve once counterclockwise, then:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \int_\alpha^\beta \left[ x(t)y'(t) - y(t)x'(t) \right]dt }. $$

Clockwise Traversal

A clockwise traversal reverses the sign of the oriented integral.

Ordinary geometric area may therefore be written:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \left| \int_\alpha^\beta \left[ x(t)y'(t)-y(t)x'(t) \right]dt \right| }. $$

Circle Example

A circle of radius $r$ centered at the origin can be parametrized:

$$ x=r\cos t, \qquad y=r\sin t, \qquad 0\le t\le2\pi. $$

Then:

$$ x'(t)=-r\sin t, \qquad y'(t)=r\cos t. $$

Therefore:

$$ x(t)y'(t)-y(t)x'(t) = r^2\cos^2t+r^2\sin^2t = r^2. $$

Hence:

$$ A= \frac12 \int_0^{2\pi}r^2\,dt. $$

So:

$$ \boxed{ A=\pi r^2 }. $$

Ellipse Example

An ellipse may be parametrized:

$$ x=a\cos t, \qquad y=b\sin t, \qquad 0\le t\le2\pi. $$

Then:

$$ x'=-a\sin t, \qquad y'=b\cos t. $$

Therefore:

$$ xy'-yx' = ab(\cos^2t+\sin^2t) = ab. $$

Thus:

$$ A= \frac12 \int_0^{2\pi}ab\,dt = \boxed{ \pi ab }. $$

Area under the Upper Semicircle

The upper semicircle can be parametrized:

$$ x=r\cos t, \qquad y=r\sin t. $$

To move from left to right across the upper semicircle, use:

$$ \pi\ge t\ge0. $$

Equivalently:

$$ A= \int_\pi^0 y(t)x'(t)\,dt. $$

Since:

$$ x'(t)=-r\sin t, $$

we get:

$$ A= \int_\pi^0 -r^2\sin^2t\,dt = \boxed{ \frac12\pi r^2 }. $$

Parameterization Does Not Change Geometric Area

The same curve can often be parametrized in many different ways. If the reparameterization is regular and traces the same boundary once with the same orientation, the resulting geometric area is unchanged.

$$ \boxed{ \text{area depends on the traced region, not on the speed of traversal} }. $$

Speed Does Not Matter

One parametrization may travel rapidly through one part of the curve and slowly through another. The derivatives $x'(t)$ and $y'(t)$ automatically compensate for this change in parameter speed.

Repeated Traversal

If a closed curve is traversed twice, an oriented area integral generally counts the enclosed area twice:

$$ \boxed{ \text{repeated traversal can count area with multiplicity} }. $$

The parameter interval must therefore be chosen to trace the intended boundary the desired number of times.

Self-Intersecting Curves

For a self-intersecting parametrized curve, the closed-curve integral naturally produces an algebraic or oriented area. Regions traversed with opposite orientations may partially or completely cancel.

If total geometric area of all lobes is desired, the curve usually must be split into simpler non-self-intersecting pieces.

Connection to Green's Theorem

The closed-curve formulas are special cases of Green's theorem.

For a positively oriented simple closed boundary $C$ enclosing region $D$:

$$ \iint_D1\,dA = \frac12 \oint_C (x\,dy-y\,dx). $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Area}(D) = \frac12 \oint_C (x\,dy-y\,dx) }. $$

Connection to Polar Area

A polar curve:

$$ r=r(\theta) $$

can be regarded parametrically as:

$$ x(\theta)=r(\theta)\cos\theta, \qquad y(\theta)=r(\theta)\sin\theta. $$

Substituting this into the closed or sector-area machinery leads to the familiar polar formula:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \int_\alpha^\beta r(\theta)^2\,d\theta }. $$

Scaling

If the entire parametrized curve is scaled by $k$:

$$ x(t)\mapsto kx(t), \qquad y(t)\mapsto ky(t), $$

then:

$$ x'(t)\mapsto kx'(t), \qquad y'(t)\mapsto ky'(t). $$

The closed-curve integrand scales by:

$$ k^2. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ A\mapsto k^2A }. $$

Translation

Translating a closed curve does not change its geometric area.

If:

$$ x(t)\mapsto x(t)+a, \qquad y(t)\mapsto y(t)+b, $$

the additional terms in the closed integral contribute zero over a complete closed traversal.

Rotation

A rigid rotation also preserves area. Thus parametric area formulas are geometric rather than dependent on a particular axis orientation.

Units

In:

$$ y\,dx, $$

both $y$ and $dx$ have length units in a geometric coordinate plane. Their product therefore has square units.

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ is measured in units}^2 }. $$

Common Misconception

The parametric area formula is not:

$$ \int y(t)\,dt. $$

The horizontal width must also be accounted for:

$$ \boxed{ dx=x'(t)\,dt }. $$

Therefore the correct signed expression is:

$$ \boxed{ \int y(t)x'(t)\,dt }. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$x=x(t),\ y=y(t)$ "X and y are given parametrically as functions of t."
$dx=x'(t)\,dt$ "D x equals x prime of t d t."
$A=\int_\alpha^\beta y(t)x'(t)\,dt$ "Area is the integral from alpha to beta of y of t times x prime of t d t," with orientation understood.
$A=\frac12\oint_C(x\,dy-y\,dx)$ "Area equals one-half the closed line integral of x d y minus y d x."
$A=\frac12\int_\alpha^\beta[xy'-yx']\,dt$ "Area equals one-half the integral of x y prime minus y x prime."
$A=\frac12\int_\alpha^\beta r(\theta)^2\,d\theta$ "Polar area equals one-half the integral of r squared with respect to theta."

Parametric notation requires careful attention to what the limits represent. In:

$$ \int_\alpha^\beta y(t)x'(t)\,dt, $$

$\alpha$ and $\beta$ are parameter values, while $x(\alpha)$ and $x(\beta)$ are the corresponding horizontal coordinates.

Defense Notation Advice: Before integrating, determine the direction in which the parameter traces the curve. A reversed traversal reverses the sign of an oriented area integral even though the physical region itself has not changed.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Parametric Curve: $$ \boxed{ x=x(t), \qquad y=y(t) }. $$
  • Differential Substitution: $$ \boxed{ dx=x'(t)\,dt }. $$
  • Signed Area under a Parametric Curve: $$ \boxed{ A= \int_\alpha^\beta y(t)x'(t)\,dt }. $$
  • Standard Positive Case: If $y(t)\ge0$ and $x'(t)\ge0$, the signed integral agrees directly with geometric area.
  • Orientation: Reversing traversal reverses the sign of the signed area integral.
  • Horizontal Reversal: A sign change in $x'(t)$ indicates that the curve changes horizontal direction.
  • Axis Crossing: A sign change in $y(t)$ may require splitting the calculation when total geometric area is desired.
  • Horizontal-Line Boundary: $$ \boxed{ A= \int_\alpha^\beta [y(t)-c]x'(t)\,dt }. $$
  • Two Parametric Boundaries: $$ \boxed{ A= \int_\alpha^\beta [y_{\text{upper}}(t)-y_{\text{lower}}(t)] x'(t)\,dt } $$ when the curves share the same increasing horizontal parametrization.
  • Integration with Respect to y: $$ \boxed{ dy=y'(t)\,dt }. $$
  • Horizontal-Strip Integral: Expressions involving $\int x\,dy$ become: $$ \boxed{ \int x(t)y'(t)\,dt }. $$
  • Closed Curve: For counterclockwise orientation: $$ \boxed{ A= \oint_Cx\,dy }. $$
  • Alternative Closed-Curve Formula: $$ \boxed{ A= -\oint_Cy\,dx }. $$
  • Symmetric Closed-Curve Formula: $$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \oint_C (x\,dy-y\,dx) }. $$
  • Parametric Closed-Curve Formula: $$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \int_\alpha^\beta [x(t)y'(t)-y(t)x'(t)]\,dt } $$ for a single counterclockwise traversal.
  • Orientation-Free Geometric Form: $$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \left| \int_\alpha^\beta [xy'-yx']\,dt \right| } $$ for a simple closed curve traversed once.
  • Circle: $$ \boxed{ x=r\cos t,\quad y=r\sin t \Rightarrow A=\pi r^2 }. $$
  • Ellipse: $$ \boxed{ x=a\cos t,\quad y=b\sin t \Rightarrow A=\pi ab }. $$
  • Polar Connection: $$ \boxed{ x=r(\theta)\cos\theta, \qquad y=r(\theta)\sin\theta }. $$
  • Polar Area Formula: $$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \int_\alpha^\beta r(\theta)^2\,d\theta }. $$
  • Green's Theorem Connection: Closed parametric area formulas are line-integral forms of planar area.
  • Reparameterization: Changing the parameter without changing the traced curve or orientation does not change geometric area.
  • Traversal Speed: Faster or slower motion along the same curve does not alter area.
  • Repeated Traversal: Tracing the same closed boundary multiple times can count oriented area with multiplicity.
  • Self-Intersection: A self-intersecting closed curve may produce algebraic area rather than the sum of all positive lobe areas.
  • Scaling: $$ \boxed{ x,y\mapsto kx,ky \Rightarrow A\mapsto k^2A }. $$
  • Translation: Translation preserves geometric area.
  • Rotation: Rotation preserves geometric area.
  • Square Units: When $x$ and $y$ are length coordinates, area is measured in square units.
  • Common Error: $$ \boxed{ \int y(t)\,dt } $$ is generally not area under a parametric curve.
  • Correct Horizontal-Strip Conversion: $$ \boxed{ y\,dx = y(t)x'(t)\,dt }. $$
  • Common Error: Taking an absolute value only after a self-intersecting closed-curve integral does not necessarily produce total geometric area of all lobes.
  • Terminological Contrast: Parameter controls the traversal of the curve. Parametric equations give $x$ and $y$ separately as functions of that parameter. Signed area depends on traversal direction. Geometric area is nonnegative and may require splitting a parametrized curve when orientation, sign, or self-intersection changes.
