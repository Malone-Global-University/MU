Periodicity Identities

Subfield: Geometry & Topology

Etymology & Roots

Periodicity identities are equations expressing the fact that a periodic function repeats, possibly with a predictable sign change or other symmetry, after particular shifts of its input.

$$ \boxed{ f(x+T)=f(x) } $$

is the basic periodicity identity for a function having period $T$.

The word periodicity derives from period, whose roots carry the idea of a circuit, cycle, or return. An identity is an equation true for every value in its intended domain. Thus a periodicity identity records a repetition rule that is universally valid wherever the expressions are defined.

$$ \boxed{ \text{periodicity identity} = \text{identity generated by cyclic repetition} }. $$

The most familiar periodicity identities arise in trigonometry:

$$ \boxed{ \sin(x+2\pi)=\sin x } $$
$$ \boxed{ \cos(x+2\pi)=\cos x } $$
$$ \boxed{ \tan(x+\pi)=\tan x }. $$

These identities reflect the geometry of rotation on the unit circle. Rotating an angle by a complete turn returns to the same point, while certain half-turn shifts produce related coordinates and therefore predictable sign changes.

The term can therefore be used narrowly for standard trigonometric repetition identities or more broadly for identities expressing periodic behavior of functions, sequences, signals, or other cyclic mathematical objects.

Operational Nuance

General Periodicity Identity

If $T$ is a period of $f$, then:

$$ \boxed{ f(x+T)=f(x) }. $$

Repeated Period Shifts

Applying the identity repeatedly gives:

$$ \boxed{ f(x+nT)=f(x), \qquad n\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Negative Period Shifts

Periodicity works in both directions:

$$ \boxed{ f(x-T)=f(x) }. $$

Sine Periodicity

$$ \boxed{ \sin(x+2\pi)=\sin x }. $$

General Sine Periodicity

$$ \boxed{ \sin(x+2\pi n)=\sin x, \qquad n\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Cosine Periodicity

$$ \boxed{ \cos(x+2\pi)=\cos x }. $$

General Cosine Periodicity

$$ \boxed{ \cos(x+2\pi n)=\cos x, \qquad n\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Tangent Periodicity

$$ \boxed{ \tan(x+\pi)=\tan x }. $$

General Tangent Periodicity

$$ \boxed{ \tan(x+\pi n)=\tan x, \qquad n\in\mathbb{Z} } $$

wherever tangent is defined.

Cotangent Periodicity

$$ \boxed{ \cot(x+\pi)=\cot x }. $$

General Cotangent Periodicity

$$ \boxed{ \cot(x+\pi n)=\cot x, \qquad n\in\mathbb{Z} } $$

wherever cotangent is defined.

Secant Periodicity

$$ \boxed{ \sec(x+2\pi)=\sec x } $$

wherever both sides are defined.

Cosecant Periodicity

$$ \boxed{ \csc(x+2\pi)=\csc x } $$

wherever defined.

Basic Fundamental Periods

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \sin x &: 2\pi,\\ \cos x &: 2\pi,\\ \tan x &: \pi,\\ \cot x &: \pi,\\ \sec x &: 2\pi,\\ \csc x &: 2\pi. \end{aligned} } $$

Full-Turn Geometry

On the unit circle, adding $2\pi$ radians produces one complete revolution:

$$ \boxed{ \theta \mapsto \theta+2\pi }. $$

The terminal point is unchanged, so its cosine and sine coordinates are unchanged.

Unit-Circle Coordinate Identity

$$ \boxed{ \left( \cos(\theta+2\pi), \sin(\theta+2\pi) \right) = \left( \cos\theta, \sin\theta \right) }. $$

Degree Form

Since:

$$ \boxed{ 2\pi\text{ radians} = 360^\circ }, $$

equivalent identities in degrees include:

$$ \boxed{ \sin(\theta+360^\circ)=\sin\theta } $$
$$ \boxed{ \cos(\theta+360^\circ)=\cos\theta }. $$

Tangent in Degrees

$$ \boxed{ \tan(\theta+180^\circ)=\tan\theta }. $$

Half-Turn Sine Identity

$$ \boxed{ \sin(x+\pi) = -\sin x }. $$

Half-Turn Cosine Identity

$$ \boxed{ \cos(x+\pi) = -\cos x }. $$

Why Half-Turn Signs Change

Adding $\pi$ moves a unit-circle point to its antipodal point:

$$ \boxed{ (\cos x,\sin x) \mapsto (-\cos x,-\sin x) }. $$

Tangent after a Half Turn

Since tangent is:

$$ \tan x = \frac{\sin x}{\cos x}, $$

both numerator and denominator change sign after adding $\pi$:

$$ \tan(x+\pi) = \frac{-\sin x}{-\cos x} = \boxed{ \tan x }. $$

Cotangent after a Half Turn

$$ \boxed{ \cot(x+\pi) = \cot x }. $$

Secant after a Half Turn

Since:

$$ \cos(x+\pi)=-\cos x, $$

we obtain:

$$ \boxed{ \sec(x+\pi) = -\sec x }. $$

Cosecant after a Half Turn

$$ \boxed{ \csc(x+\pi) = -\csc x }. $$

Half-Period Sign Reversal

The sine and cosine half-turn equations are not full periodicity equations because the output changes sign.

$$ \boxed{ \sin(x+\pi)\neq\sin x } $$

in general.

Instead:

$$ \boxed{ \sin(x+\pi)=-\sin x }. $$

Antiperiodicity

A relation of the form:

$$ \boxed{ f(x+L)=-f(x) } $$

is often called antiperiodicity with antiperiod $L$.

Antiperiod Implies a Period

Applying the sign reversal twice:

$$ f(x+2L) = -f(x+L) = f(x). $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ 2L \text{ is a period} }. $$

Sine as Antiperiodic

$$ \boxed{ \sin(x+\pi) = -\sin x } $$

so $\pi$ is an antiperiod of sine and $2\pi$ is a period.

Cosine as Antiperiodic

$$ \boxed{ \cos(x+\pi) = -\cos x }. $$

Quarter-Turn Identities

Shifts by $\pi/2$ transform sine and cosine into each other:

$$ \boxed{ \sin \left( x+\frac{\pi}{2} \right) = \cos x }. $$
$$ \boxed{ \cos \left( x+\frac{\pi}{2} \right) = -\sin x }. $$

Negative Quarter-Turn Identities

$$ \boxed{ \sin \left( x-\frac{\pi}{2} \right) = -\cos x }. $$
$$ \boxed{ \cos \left( x-\frac{\pi}{2} \right) = \sin x }. $$

These Are Phase-Shift Identities

Quarter-turn identities are closely related to periodicity because they describe movement within the same cyclic structure, but they are not pure repetition identities of one unchanged function.

Periodicity vs. Cofunction Identity

An identity such as:

$$ \sin \left( \frac{\pi}{2}-x \right) = \cos x $$

is a cofunction identity rather than a pure periodicity identity, although both arise from unit-circle geometry.

Periodicity vs. Odd-Even Identity

The identities:

$$ \sin(-x)=-\sin x $$

and:

$$ \cos(-x)=\cos x $$

concern reflection symmetry, not translational periodicity.

$$ \boxed{ \text{periodicity} = \text{translation symmetry} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{even/odd identities} = \text{reflection symmetry} }. $$

Combining Periodicity with Odd-Even Symmetry

Periodicity and parity identities can be combined to reduce complicated angles.

Example

$$ \sin(-x+2\pi) = \sin(-x) = -\sin x. $$

Coterminal-Angle Interpretation

Angles differing by full revolutions are coterminal:

$$ \boxed{ \theta \text{ and } \theta+2\pi n } $$

have the same terminal side.

Sine and Cosine of Coterminal Angles

$$ \boxed{ \sin(\theta+2\pi n) = \sin\theta } $$
$$ \boxed{ \cos(\theta+2\pi n) = \cos\theta }. $$

Tangent's Smaller Period

Tangent repeats after only a half turn because opposite unit-circle points have the same coordinate ratio:

$$ \frac{-y}{-x} = \frac{y}{x}. $$

Angle Reduction

Periodicity identities allow large or negative angles to be reduced to simpler coterminal or equivalent arguments.

Example

$$ \sin \left( \frac{17\pi}{6} \right) = \sin \left( \frac{17\pi}{6} - 2\pi \right) = \sin \left( \frac{5\pi}{6} \right). $$

Continue Reduction if Useful

$$ \sin \left( \frac{5\pi}{6} \right) = \frac{1}{2}. $$

Tangent Reduction

Because tangent has period $\pi$:

$$ \tan \left( \frac{7\pi}{4} \right) = \tan \left( \frac{3\pi}{4} \right) = -1. $$

General Angle Reduction for Sine and Cosine

One may replace $\theta$ with:

$$ \boxed{ \theta-2\pi n } $$

without changing sine or cosine.

General Angle Reduction for Tangent and Cotangent

One may replace $\theta$ with:

$$ \boxed{ \theta-\pi n } $$

without changing tangent or cotangent, provided the expressions remain defined.

Secant and Cosecant

Since they are reciprocals of cosine and sine:

$$ \boxed{ \sec(x+2\pi n)=\sec x } $$
$$ \boxed{ \csc(x+2\pi n)=\csc x } $$

wherever denominators are nonzero.

Transformed Sine Periodicity

For:

$$ f(x) = A\sin(Bx+C)+D, \qquad B\neq0, $$

a period is:

$$ \boxed{ T = \frac{2\pi}{|B|} }. $$

Verification

Assuming $B>0$ for a direct display:

$$ \begin{aligned} f \left( x+\frac{2\pi}{B} \right) &= A\sin \left( Bx+2\pi+C \right)+D\\ &= A\sin(Bx+C)+D\\ &= f(x). \end{aligned} $$

Why Absolute Value Is Used

Period is reported as a positive length:

$$ \boxed{ T = \frac{2\pi}{|B|} }. $$

Transformed Tangent Periodicity

For:

$$ f(x)=A\tan(Bx+C)+D, $$

a fundamental period under ordinary nondegenerate conditions is:

$$ \boxed{ T = \frac{\pi}{|B|} }. $$

Amplitude Does Not Change Parent Period

Multiplication by a nonzero output scale $A$ does not change the input translation required for repetition.

Vertical Translation Does Not Change Period

Adding $D$ changes output location but not horizontal repetition.

Phase Shift Does Not Change Period

Adding $C$ inside the argument changes where the cycle begins but not the length of the cycle.

Horizontal Scaling Changes Period

The coefficient $B$ changes how rapidly the argument moves through a complete trigonometric cycle.

Absolute Value Can Shorten Fundamental Period

Since:

$$ \sin(x+\pi) = -\sin x, $$

taking absolute values gives:

$$ \boxed{ |\sin(x+\pi)| = |\sin x| }. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ |\sin x| \text{ has fundamental period }\pi }. $$

Squaring Can Shorten Fundamental Period

$$ \boxed{ \sin^2(x+\pi) = \sin^2x }. $$

Squared Cosine

$$ \boxed{ \cos^2(x+\pi) = \cos^2x }. $$

Fundamental Periods of Squared Sine and Cosine

$$ \boxed{ T_{\sin^2x} = T_{\cos^2x} = \pi }. $$

Power Reduction Connection

The identities:

$$ \sin^2x = \frac{1-\cos(2x)}{2} $$

and:

$$ \cos^2x = \frac{1+\cos(2x)}{2} $$

make the $\pi$ period visible because $\cos(2x)$ completes a cycle twice as rapidly as $\cos x$.

Odd Powers of Sine

For an odd positive integer $m$:

$$ \sin^m(x+\pi) = -\sin^m x. $$

Thus the half-turn remains an antiperiod, while $2\pi$ remains the fundamental period for the ordinary nonzero function.

Even Powers of Sine

For an even positive integer $m$:

$$ \boxed{ \sin^m(x+\pi) = \sin^m x }. $$

Even Powers of Cosine

$$ \boxed{ \cos^m(x+\pi) = \cos^m x } $$

for even positive integers $m$.

Products Can Create New Periodicity

Consider:

$$ f(x) = \sin x\cos x. $$

Using:

$$ \boxed{ \sin x\cos x = \frac{1}{2}\sin(2x) }, $$

the fundamental period is:

$$ \boxed{ \pi }. $$

Period of a Sum

If two periodic functions possess a common period $T$, then:

$$ \boxed{ (f+g)(x+T) = (f+g)(x) }. $$

Common Period Requirement

Individual periodicity does not automatically imply that a sum has a finite common period.

Example with Compatible Periods

$$ f(x) = \sin x+\cos(2x). $$

The first term has fundamental period $2\pi$ and the second has fundamental period $\pi$, so $2\pi$ is a common period.

Example with Incommensurable Periods

$$ \boxed{ f(x) = \sin x+\sin(\sqrt{2}x) } $$

is not periodic because the component periods are irrationally related and possess no common positive multiple.

Commensurability

If two fundamental periods $T_1$ and $T_2$ satisfy:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{T_1}{T_2} \in \mathbb{Q} }, $$

then they are commensurable and common periods exist.

Complex Exponential Periodicity

Euler's formula gives:

$$ e^{ix} = \cos x+i\sin x. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ e^{i(x+2\pi)} = e^{ix} }. $$

General Complex Exponential Shift

$$ \boxed{ e^{i(x+2\pi n)} = e^{ix}, \qquad n\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Roots of Unity Connection

Since:

$$ e^{2\pi i}=1, $$

multiplying a complex exponential by $e^{2\pi i n}$ leaves it unchanged.

Fourier Harmonics

For a base period $T$, the complex harmonic:

$$ \boxed{ e^{2\pi i n x/T} } $$

satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ e^{2\pi i n(x+T)/T} = e^{2\pi i n x/T} }. $$

Fundamental Angular Frequency

$$ \boxed{ \omega_0 = \frac{2\pi}{T} }. $$

Harmonic Periodicity

Terms such as:

$$ \cos(n\omega_0x), \qquad \sin(n\omega_0x) $$

all repeat after the common period $T$, although individual harmonics may have smaller fundamental periods.

Periodicity of Derivatives

If $f$ is differentiable and:

$$ f(x+T)=f(x), $$

then differentiating yields:

$$ \boxed{ f'(x+T)=f'(x) }. $$

Derivative Period Caveat

$T$ remains a period of $f'$, but $f'$ may possess a smaller fundamental period.

Periodic Integral over Shifted Intervals

For an integrable $T$-periodic function:

$$ \boxed{ \int_a^{a+T} f(x)\,dx = \int_b^{b+T} f(x)\,dx }. $$

Average over a Period

$$ \boxed{ \frac{1}{T} \int_a^{a+T} f(x)\,dx } $$

is independent of the starting point $a$ under suitable integrability conditions.

Periodic Sequences

For a sequence with period $p$:

$$ \boxed{ a_{n+p}=a_n }. $$

Repeated Sequence Period

$$ \boxed{ a_{n+kp}=a_n, \qquad k\in\mathbb{Z} } $$

where indices are valid.

Modular Arithmetic Connection

Congruence itself expresses a type of periodic equivalence:

$$ \boxed{ n+m \equiv n \pmod{m} }. $$

Residue-Class Periodicity

A function depending only on $n\bmod m$ satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ f(n+m)=f(n) }. $$

Periodic Geometry

In geometry, periodicity can describe repeating transformations, tessellations, or spatial patterns invariant under a translation.

Spatial Periodicity

A spatially periodic function may satisfy:

$$ \boxed{ f(x+\lambda)=f(x) }, $$

where $\lambda$ is a spatial period such as a wavelength.

Wave Periodicity

A traveling wave:

$$ y(x,t) = A\cos(kx-\omega t+\phi) $$

satisfies temporal and spatial periodicity identities.

Temporal Wave Identity

$$ \boxed{ y(x,t+T) = y(x,t) } $$

for:

$$ T = \frac{2\pi}{|\omega|}. $$

Spatial Wave Identity

$$ \boxed{ y(x+\lambda,t) = y(x,t) } $$

for:

$$ \lambda = \frac{2\pi}{|k|}. $$

Multivariable Periodicity

A function of several variables may satisfy:

$$ \boxed{ f(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{v}) = f(\mathbf{x}) }, $$

where $\mathbf{v}$ is a period vector.

Lattice Periodicity

Multiple independent period vectors can generate a translation lattice.

Crystallographic Interpretation

Repetition under lattice translations is central to mathematical descriptions of crystal structures.

Periodicity Identity as Translation Symmetry

The equation:

$$ f(x+T)=f(x) $$

is precisely a symmetry statement.

$$ \boxed{ \text{translation by }T \text{ leaves }f\text{ invariant} }. $$

Identity vs. Equation

A periodicity identity is intended to hold for every admissible input, not merely for selected solutions.

Example

The statement:

$$ \sin(x+2\pi)=\sin x $$

is an identity.

By contrast:

$$ \sin x=\frac{1}{2} $$

is an equation whose truth depends on particular values of $x$.

Using Periodicity to Solve Trigonometric Equations

If one solution of:

$$ \sin x=c $$

is known, periodicity generates infinitely many coterminal solutions by adding:

$$ \boxed{ 2\pi n, \qquad n\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Tangent Solution Families

Because tangent has period $\pi$, a solution family typically repeats through:

$$ \boxed{ x=x_0+\pi n, \qquad n\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Periodicity and Inverse Trigonometric Functions

Periodic trigonometric functions are not one-to-one on all of $\mathbb{R}$. Therefore their domains must be restricted to define inverse functions.

Why Periodicity Prevents Global Injectivity

If $T\neq0$ is a period:

$$ f(x+T)=f(x) $$

while:

$$ x+T\neq x. $$

Hence a nonconstant periodic function cannot be globally one-to-one.

Common Error: Treating $\pi$ as a Period of Sine

In general:

$$ \boxed{ \sin(x+\pi) = -\sin x } $$

rather than $\sin x$.

Common Error: Treating $\pi$ as a Period of Cosine

$$ \boxed{ \cos(x+\pi) = -\cos x }. $$

Common Error: Treating $2\pi$ as Tangent's Fundamental Period

$2\pi$ is a period of tangent, but its least positive period is:

$$ \boxed{ \pi }. $$

Common Error: Confusing Periodicity Identities with Reciprocal Identities

For example:

$$ \sec x=\frac{1}{\cos x} $$

is a reciprocal identity, not a periodicity identity.

Common Error: Confusing Periodicity Identities with Pythagorean Identities

$$ \sin^2x+\cos^2x=1 $$

is a Pythagorean identity.

Common Error: Confusing Periodicity with Parity

Relations involving $-x$ describe even or odd symmetry; relations involving $x+T$ describe translational periodicity.

Common Error: Confusing Periodicity with Phase-Shift Identities

An equation such as:

$$ \sin \left( x+\frac{\pi}{2} \right) = \cos x $$

relates two functions under a quarter-cycle shift rather than expressing exact repetition of one unchanged function.

Common Error: Forgetting Domain Restrictions

Tangent, cotangent, secant, and cosecant identities are understood only where the relevant expressions are defined.

Common Error: Using $360$ without Degree Symbols in a Radian Context

Angle units must remain explicit:

$$ \boxed{ 360^\circ=2\pi\text{ radians} }. $$

Common Error: Mixing Degrees and Radians

The identities:

$$ \sin(x+2\pi)=\sin x $$

and:

$$ \sin(\theta+360^\circ)=\sin\theta $$

express the same geometry using different units.

Common Error: Assuming Every Multiple of a Parent Period Is Fundamental

Multiples are periods but generally not least positive periods.

Common Error: Assuming Operations Preserve the Fundamental Period Exactly

Absolute value, squaring, or composition may produce additional symmetry and a smaller fundamental period.

Common Error: Assuming Sums of Periodic Functions Are Always Periodic

Their periods must be compatible enough to admit a common positive period.

Common Error: Treating Periodicity as Approximate Repetition

An identity is exact:

$$ \boxed{ f(x+T)=f(x) } $$

not merely approximately equal.

Common Error: Checking the Identity at Only One Input

A periodicity identity must hold for every admissible input, not just a selected example.

Common Error: Assuming a Half-Turn Sign Reversal Is Full Periodicity

An antiperiodic relation:

$$ f(x+L)=-f(x) $$

is distinct from:

$$ f(x+L)=f(x). $$

Common Error: Forgetting Two Antiperiods Produce a Full Period

If:

$$ f(x+L)=-f(x), $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ f(x+2L)=f(x) }. $$

Common Misconception: Periodicity identities are not simply a list of memorized trigonometric formulas. They express translation symmetry. The central structure is $f(x+T)=f(x)$, while related half-period and quarter-period identities describe predictable transformations occurring at fractions of the full cycle.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$f(x+T)=f(x)$ "F repeats when the input is shifted by T."
$\sin(x+2\pi)=\sin x$ "Sine repeats after a full turn of two pi radians."
$\cos(x+2\pi)=\cos x$ "Cosine has period two pi."
$\tan(x+\pi)=\tan x$ "Tangent repeats after pi radians."
$\sin(x+\pi)=-\sin x$ "A half-turn changes the sign of sine."
$\cos(x+\pi)=-\cos x$ "A half-turn changes the sign of cosine."
$f(x+nT)=f(x)$ "F repeats after any integer multiple of the period."

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"periodicity identity"} } $$

most directly refers to an identity expressing exact repetition after a period.

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"reduce the angle using periodicity"} } $$

means to add or subtract whole periods until the angle is replaced by a simpler equivalent argument.

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"half-period identity"} } $$

may refer to a predictable transformation after half of a full period, such as:

$$ \sin(x+\pi)=-\sin x. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish a genuine period identity $f(x+T)=f(x)$ from an antiperiodic sign-change identity $f(x+L)=-f(x)$. Keep degrees and radians separate, preserve domain restrictions for reciprocal and quotient trigonometric functions, and use integer parameters such as $n\in\mathbb{Z}$ when writing complete families of periodic shifts.
