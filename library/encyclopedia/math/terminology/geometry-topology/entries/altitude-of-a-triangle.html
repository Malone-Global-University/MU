Altitude of a Triangle

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

The altitude of a triangle is a perpendicular segment drawn from a vertex to the line containing the opposite side. The length of this segment is also commonly called the altitude or height corresponding to that side.

In triangle $\triangle ABC$, suppose the perpendicular from vertex $A$ meets the line containing $BC$ at $H$. Then:

$$ \boxed{ AH\perp BC }. $$

The segment $\overline{AH}$ is the altitude from $A$, while $H$ is called the foot of the altitude.

The definition refers to the line containing the opposite side rather than only to the finite side segment. This distinction is essential in obtuse triangles, where a perpendicular may meet an extension of the opposite side.

$$ \boxed{ \text{triangle altitude} = \text{perpendicular from a vertex to the opposite-side line} }. $$

Every nondegenerate triangle has three altitudes, one corresponding to each vertex and opposite side.

Operational Nuance

Three Altitudes

For a triangle with side lengths $a$, $b$, and $c$, the corresponding altitude lengths are commonly written:

$$ \boxed{ h_a,\qquad h_b,\qquad h_c }. $$

Here $h_a$ is the altitude perpendicular to side $a$, $h_b$ is perpendicular to side $b$, and $h_c$ is perpendicular to side $c$.

Altitude and Area

If a side of length $b$ is chosen as the base and its corresponding altitude is $h$, then:

$$ \boxed{ A=\frac12bh }. $$

Since any side may serve as the base:

$$ \boxed{ A = \frac12ah_a = \frac12bh_b = \frac12ch_c }. $$

Finding an Altitude from Area

Solving:

$$ A=\frac12bh $$

for $h$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ h=\frac{2A}{b} }. $$

Example

Suppose a triangle has area:

$$ A=42 $$

and a corresponding base of length:

$$ b=12. $$

Then:

$$ h = \frac{2(42)}{12} = \frac{84}{12} = \boxed{7}. $$

Orthocenter

The three altitude lines of a triangle are concurrent. Their common intersection point is called the orthocenter.

$$ \boxed{ \text{three altitude lines} \longrightarrow \text{orthocenter} }. $$

The orthocenter is commonly denoted by $H$, although the same letter may also be used locally for the foot of an altitude. Context determines the meaning.

Acute Triangle

In an acute triangle, all three altitudes meet the opposite side segments themselves. The orthocenter lies inside the triangle.

$$ \boxed{ \text{acute triangle} \Longrightarrow \text{orthocenter inside} }. $$

Right Triangle

In a right triangle, the two legs are perpendicular. Therefore each leg serves as an altitude corresponding to the other leg.

The orthocenter is the right-angle vertex:

$$ \boxed{ \text{right triangle} \Longrightarrow \text{orthocenter at right-angle vertex} }. $$

Obtuse Triangle

In an obtuse triangle, two of the altitudes meet extensions of the opposite sides rather than the side segments themselves. Consequently, the orthocenter lies outside the triangle.

$$ \boxed{ \text{obtuse triangle} \Longrightarrow \text{orthocenter outside} }. $$

Altitude vs. Median

A median runs from a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side. Its defining condition is midpoint division:

$$ \boxed{ \text{median} \Longrightarrow \text{opposite side is bisected} }. $$

An altitude instead satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ \text{altitude} \Longrightarrow \text{perpendicularity} }. $$

Altitude vs. Angle Bisector

An angle bisector divides a vertex angle into two congruent angles. An altitude need not bisect either an angle or the opposite side.

Isosceles Triangle

In an isosceles triangle, the altitude from the apex to the base is also a median and angle bisector.

$$ \boxed{ \text{isosceles apex altitude} = \text{median} = \text{angle bisector} }. $$

Equilateral Triangle

In an equilateral triangle, every altitude is simultaneously a median, angle bisector, and perpendicular bisector.

For side length $s$:

$$ \boxed{ h=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}s }. $$

Deriving the Equilateral Altitude

An altitude divides an equilateral triangle into two $30^\circ$-$60^\circ$-$90^\circ$ right triangles. Using the Pythagorean Theorem:

$$ h^2+\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)^2=s^2. $$

Therefore:

$$ h^2 = s^2-\frac{s^2}{4} = \frac{3s^2}{4}, $$

so:

$$ h=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}s. $$

Right-Triangle Altitude to the Hypotenuse

A particularly important altitude in a right triangle runs from the right-angle vertex to the hypotenuse.

If this altitude has length $h$ and divides the hypotenuse into segments $p$ and $q$, then:

$$ \boxed{ h^2=pq }. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ h=\sqrt{pq} }. $$

Altitude to the Hypotenuse from the Legs

If a right triangle has legs $a$ and $b$ and hypotenuse $c$, comparing its two area formulas gives:

$$ \frac12ab=\frac12ch. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ h=\frac{ab}{c} }. $$

Trigonometric Form

If side $c$ is used as a base and an adjacent side $b$ forms included angle $A$, then the corresponding altitude is:

$$ \boxed{ h_c=b\sin A }. $$

Likewise:

$$ h_c=a\sin B. $$

Connection to the Sine Area Formula

Substituting:

$$ h_c=b\sin A $$

into:

$$ A=\frac12ch_c $$

yields:

$$ \boxed{ A=\frac12bc\sin A }. $$

Altitude from Heron's Formula

If all three side lengths are known, first compute:

$$ s=\frac{a+b+c}{2} $$

and:

$$ A=\sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ h_a= \frac{2\sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}}{a} }. $$

Similar formulas hold for $h_b$ and $h_c$.

Common Misconception: An altitude is not required to remain inside the triangle. Its defining property is perpendicularity to the line containing the opposite side.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$AH\perp BC$ "A H is perpendicular to B C."
$h_a$ "The altitude corresponding to side a."
$A=\frac12ah_a$ "Area equals one-half a times h sub a."
$h=\frac{2A}{b}$ "Height equals twice the area divided by the base."
$h^2=pq$ "The altitude squared equals p times q."
$h=\frac{ab}{c}$ "The altitude to the hypotenuse equals the product of the legs divided by the hypotenuse."

The notation:

$$ \overline{AH} $$

refers to the altitude segment, while:

$$ AH $$

or:

$$ h_a $$

may be used for its length, depending on the author's notation.

Defense Notation Advice: When identifying an altitude, begin at a vertex and look for a perpendicular relationship with the opposite-side line. Do not rely on whether the segment appears vertical, bisects a side, or lies inside the triangle.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: An altitude of a triangle is the perpendicular segment from a vertex to the line containing the opposite side.
  • Three Altitudes: Every nondegenerate triangle has three altitude lines.
  • Common Symbols: $$ \boxed{ h_a,\qquad h_b,\qquad h_c }. $$
  • Foot of the Altitude: The perpendicular intersection point with the opposite-side line is called the foot of the altitude.
  • Supporting-Line Precision: The perpendicular is drawn to the line containing the opposite side, not necessarily to the finite side segment.
  • Perpendicular Requirement: $$ \boxed{ AH\perp BC }. $$
  • Triangle Area: $$ \boxed{ A=\frac12bh }. $$
  • Three Area Forms: $$ \boxed{ A = \frac12ah_a = \frac12bh_b = \frac12ch_c }. $$
  • Altitude from Area: $$ \boxed{ h_a=\frac{2A}{a} }. $$
  • Orthocenter: The three altitude lines intersect at the orthocenter.
  • Acute Triangle: All three feet lie on the opposite side segments, and the orthocenter lies inside the triangle.
  • Right Triangle: Two of the altitudes are the legs themselves.
  • Right-Triangle Orthocenter: The orthocenter is the right-angle vertex.
  • Obtuse Triangle: Two altitude feet lie on extensions of opposite sides, and the orthocenter lies outside the triangle.
  • Isosceles Triangle: The altitude from the apex to the base is also a median and angle bisector.
  • Equilateral Triangle: Every altitude is also a median, angle bisector, and perpendicular bisector.
  • Equilateral Altitude: For side length $s$: $$ \boxed{ h=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}s }. $$
  • Equilateral Area: Substituting that altitude into $A=\frac12sh$ gives: $$ \boxed{ A=\frac{\sqrt3}{4}s^2 }. $$
  • Right-Triangle Hypotenuse Altitude: If the altitude to the hypotenuse divides it into segments $p$ and $q$: $$ \boxed{ h^2=pq }. $$
  • Geometric Mean Form: $$ \boxed{ h=\sqrt{pq} }. $$
  • Right-Triangle Leg-Hypotenuse Formula: If the legs are $a,b$ and hypotenuse is $c$: $$ \boxed{ h=\frac{ab}{c} }. $$
  • Trigonometric Form: $$ \boxed{ h_c=b\sin A=a\sin B }. $$
  • Sine Area Formula: $$ \boxed{ A=\frac12bc\sin A }. $$
  • Heron's Formula Connection: If: $$ s=\frac{a+b+c}{2}, $$ then: $$ A=\sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}. $$
  • Altitude from Three Sides: $$ \boxed{ h_a= \frac{2\sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}}{a} }. $$
  • Median Contrast: A median is required to meet the midpoint of the opposite side; an altitude is required to meet the opposite-side line perpendicularly.
  • Angle-Bisector Contrast: An angle bisector divides the vertex angle into equal angles; an altitude need not.
  • Perpendicular-Bisector Contrast: A perpendicular bisector is perpendicular to a side at its midpoint and need not pass through the opposite vertex.
  • Coordinate Geometry: An altitude can be constructed as the line through a vertex perpendicular to the opposite-side line.
  • Slope Method: For finite nonzero slopes $m_1$ and $m_2$, perpendicular lines satisfy: $$ \boxed{ m_1m_2=-1 }. $$
  • Vertical-Horizontal Case: A vertical line is perpendicular to a horizontal line even though the vertical line has undefined slope, so the product-of-slopes rule should not be applied mechanically in that case.
  • Point-to-Line Distance: If the opposite side lies on: $$ Ax+By+C=0 $$ and the vertex is $(x_0,y_0)$, then the altitude length is: $$ \boxed{ h= \frac{|Ax_0+By_0+C|} {\sqrt{A^2+B^2}} }. $$
  • Coordinate Area Method: If the triangle's area and one side length are computed from coordinates, the corresponding altitude follows from: $$ \boxed{ h=\frac{2A}{b} }. $$
  • Vector Geometry: If vectors $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ form two sides from a common vertex, then: $$ A=\frac12\|\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}\|. $$
  • Vector Altitude: If $\|\mathbf{u}\|$ is used as the base: $$ \boxed{ h= \frac{\|\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}\|} {\|\mathbf{u}\|} }. $$
  • Dot-Product Test: An altitude direction $\mathbf{n}$ and base direction $\mathbf{v}$ satisfy: $$ \boxed{ \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{v}=0 }. $$
  • Scaling: Under dilation by factor $k$: $$ \boxed{ h\mapsto kh }. $$
  • Area Scaling: Under the same dilation: $$ \boxed{ A\mapsto k^2A }. $$
  • Similar Triangles: Corresponding altitudes are proportional to corresponding side lengths.
  • Altitude Ratio in Similar Triangles: If the similarity scale factor is $k$: $$ \boxed{ \frac{h_2}{h_1}=k }. $$
  • Area Ratio: For the same similar triangles: $$ \boxed{ \frac{A_2}{A_1}=k^2 }. $$
  • Units: Altitudes are measured in linear units such as centimeters, meters, or feet.
  • Orientation Independence: Rotating a triangle does not change its altitudes. The defining condition is perpendicularity, not page orientation.
  • Diagram Caution: A line from a vertex that merely appears vertical is not necessarily an altitude. A right-angle relationship with the opposite-side line must be established.
  • Terminological Contrast: An altitude runs from a vertex perpendicular to the opposite-side line. A median runs from a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side. An angle bisector divides the vertex angle into two equal angles. A perpendicular bisector cuts a side in half at a right angle. These segments coincide only in special triangles.
