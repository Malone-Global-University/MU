Bounded Set of Geometric Points

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

A bounded set of geometric points is a set whose points remain within some finite spatial distance of a fixed reference point.

In Euclidean space, a set $S$ is bounded if there exist a point $c$ and a finite number $R>0$ such that every point $p\in S$ satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ \|p-c\|\le R }. $$

Equivalently, the entire set lies inside some ball of finite radius:

$$ \boxed{ S\subseteq B(c,R) } $$

or, if the boundary sphere is included:

$$ \boxed{ S\subseteq \overline{B}(c,R) }. $$

The central idea is finite spatial extent. A bounded set does not continue arbitrarily far away in any direction.

Operational Nuance

Metric-Space Definition

In a metric space $(X,d)$, a subset $S\subseteq X$ is bounded if there exist $c\in X$ and $R<\infty$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ d(x,c)\le R \qquad \forall x\in S }. $$

Thus boundedness depends on the metric used to measure distance.

Euclidean Plane

In $\mathbb R^2$, if:

$$ p=(x,y), \qquad c=(a,b), $$

then boundedness means there is some finite $R$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt{(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2} \le R } $$

for every $(x,y)\in S$.

Euclidean Three-Space

In $\mathbb R^3$, a set is bounded if all of its points fit inside some finite sphere or ball:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt{ (x-a)^2+ (y-b)^2+ (z-c)^2 } \le R }. $$

Reference Point Is Not Unique

The center used to establish boundedness does not have to belong to the set and is not unique.

If one finite-radius ball contains the set, many larger balls with different centers may also contain it.

Example: Finite Point Set

Consider:

$$ S= \{(0,0),(2,1),(-1,3),(4,-2)\}. $$

Because the set contains only finitely many points, the distances of all points from the origin have a finite maximum. Therefore $S$ is bounded.

$$ \boxed{ \text{every finite subset of }\mathbb R^n \text{ is bounded} }. $$

Infinite Sets Can Also Be Bounded

Bounded does not mean finite.

The unit circle:

$$ \boxed{ x^2+y^2=1 } $$

contains infinitely many points, but every point lies exactly one unit from the origin.

Open Disk

The open unit disk:

$$ \boxed{ x^2+y^2<1 } $$

is bounded even though it does not contain its boundary circle.

Closed Disk

The closed unit disk:

$$ \boxed{ x^2+y^2\le1 } $$

is also bounded.

Bounded Does Not Mean Closed

The open disk is a standard counterexample:

$$ \boxed{ x^2+y^2<1 } $$

is bounded but not closed.

$$ \boxed{ \text{bounded} \not\Rightarrow \text{closed} }. $$

Closed Does Not Mean Bounded

The entire $x$-axis:

$$ \boxed{ \{(x,0):x\in\mathbb R\} } $$

is closed in $\mathbb R^2$ but unbounded.

$$ \boxed{ \text{closed} \not\Rightarrow \text{bounded} }. $$

Compactness in Euclidean Space

In finite-dimensional Euclidean space, the Heine-Borel theorem states that a set is compact exactly when it is both closed and bounded.

$$ \boxed{ K\subseteq\mathbb R^n \text{ compact} \iff K\text{ is closed and bounded} }. $$

Boundedness is therefore one half of the familiar Euclidean compactness condition.

Coordinate-Wise Characterization

A subset $S\subseteq\mathbb R^n$ is bounded if and only if every coordinate is bounded across the set.

In $\mathbb R^2$, this means there exist finite numbers $A$ and $B$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ |x|\le A, \qquad |y|\le B } $$

for every $(x,y)\in S$.

Bounding Rectangle

Thus every bounded subset of the plane fits inside some finite rectangle:

$$ \boxed{ S\subseteq [-A,A]\times[-B,B] }. $$

Bounding Box in Higher Dimensions

Likewise, a bounded subset of $\mathbb R^n$ fits inside some finite rectangular box:

$$ \boxed{ S\subseteq [-M,M]^n } $$

for a sufficiently large $M$.

Diameter

The diameter of a set $S$ in a metric space is:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{diam}(S) = \sup\{d(x,y):x,y\in S\} }. $$

It measures the greatest possible separation between points of the set, interpreted as a supremum.

Boundedness and Diameter

In a metric space, a nonempty set is bounded if and only if it has finite diameter:

$$ \boxed{ S\text{ bounded} \iff \operatorname{diam}(S)<\infty }. $$

Diameter Need Not Be Attained

The supremum defining the diameter need not correspond to an actual pair of points in the set.

For the open interval:

$$ S=(0,1), $$

the diameter is:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{diam}(S)=1 }, $$

even though no two points in the set are exactly one unit apart.

Line Segment

A finite line segment $\overline{AB}$ is bounded.

Its diameter is:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{diam}(\overline{AB})=AB }. $$

Ray

A ray extends indefinitely in one direction and is therefore unbounded.

$$ \boxed{ \text{ray} \Rightarrow \text{unbounded} }. $$

Line

A complete Euclidean line extends indefinitely in two opposite directions and is unbounded.

Polygon

Any ordinary polygon consisting of finitely many finite sides is bounded.

Its interior and boundary together fit inside some finite disk.

Circle

A circle of radius $r$ centered at $c$ is bounded because:

$$ \boxed{ \|p-c\|=r } $$

for every point $p$ on the circle.

Ellipse

Every ellipse with finite semiaxes is bounded.

In standard form:

$$ \frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1, $$

one has:

$$ |x|\le a, \qquad |y|\le b. $$

Parabola

A complete parabola is unbounded.

For:

$$ y=x^2, $$

points occur with arbitrarily large $|x|$ and $y$.

Hyperbola

A complete hyperbola is also unbounded because its branches extend indefinitely.

Half-Plane

A half-plane such as:

$$ y\ge0 $$

is unbounded.

Strip

The infinite strip:

$$ 0\le y\le1 $$

is bounded vertically but unbounded as a subset of $\mathbb R^2$ because $x$ may take arbitrarily large positive or negative values.

$$ \boxed{ \text{bounded in one coordinate} \ne \text{bounded as a point set} }. $$

First Quadrant

The set:

$$ \{(x,y):x\ge0,\ y\ge0\} $$

is unbounded because distances from the origin can become arbitrarily large.

Graph of a Bounded Function Need Not Be a Bounded Set

Consider:

$$ y=\sin x. $$

The function values are bounded:

$$ -1\le\sin x\le1. $$

But its graph:

$$ \{(x,\sin x):x\in\mathbb R\} $$

is an unbounded point set because its $x$-coordinates extend indefinitely.

$$ \boxed{ \text{bounded function values} \not\Rightarrow \text{bounded graph as a subset of }\mathbb R^2 }. $$

Graph on a Bounded Domain

If a function is bounded on a bounded interval, then its graph is a bounded subset of the plane.

For example:

$$ y=\sin x, \qquad 0\le x\le2\pi $$

has a bounded graph.

Bounded Does Not Mean Connected

The set:

$$ \boxed{ \{(-1,0),(1,0)\} } $$

is bounded but disconnected.

$$ \boxed{ \text{bounded} \not\Rightarrow \text{connected} }. $$

Bounded Does Not Mean Convex

A crescent-shaped region or two separated disks may be bounded without being convex.

$$ \boxed{ \text{bounded} \not\Rightarrow \text{convex} }. $$

Convex Does Not Mean Bounded

A line, half-plane, or infinite strip can be convex while remaining unbounded.

Subset Property

Every subset of a bounded set is bounded.

$$ \boxed{ A\subseteq B \text{ and }B\text{ bounded} \Rightarrow A\text{ bounded} }. $$

Finite Union

The union of finitely many bounded subsets of a metric space is bounded.

$$ \boxed{ A_1,\ldots,A_n\text{ bounded} \Rightarrow \bigcup_{k=1}^{n}A_k \text{ bounded} }. $$

Infinite Union Caution

An infinite union of bounded sets need not be bounded.

For example:

$$ A_n=[-n,n]. $$

Every $A_n$ is bounded, but:

$$ \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_n = \mathbb R, $$

which is unbounded.

Closure of a Bounded Set

In a metric space, the closure of a bounded set remains bounded.

$$ \boxed{ S\text{ bounded} \Rightarrow \overline S\text{ bounded} }. $$

Interior of a Bounded Set

The interior of a bounded set is also bounded because it is a subset of the original set.

Boundary of a Bounded Set

The topological boundary of a bounded set is bounded as well, since it lies within the closure of the set.

Translation Preserves Boundedness

Translating a set does not change whether it is bounded.

If:

$$ S+\mathbf v = \{x+\mathbf v:x\in S\}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ S\text{ bounded} \iff S+\mathbf v\text{ bounded} }. $$

Rotation Preserves Boundedness

Euclidean rotations preserve distances, so rotating a bounded point set produces another bounded point set.

Scaling

Multiplying all position vectors by a finite scalar $\lambda$ preserves boundedness:

$$ \boxed{ S\text{ bounded} \Rightarrow \lambda S\text{ bounded} }. $$

If $S$ lies within radius $R$ of the origin, then $\lambda S$ lies within radius:

$$ |\lambda|R. $$

Continuous Images of Compact Sets

If $K$ is compact and $f$ is continuous, then $f(K)$ is compact and therefore bounded in Euclidean space.

This principle frequently converts geometric compactness into numerical bounds.

Sequences of Points

A sequence of points $(p_n)$ is bounded if its set of terms:

$$ \{p_1,p_2,p_3,\ldots\} $$

is a bounded point set.

Equivalently, for some $c$ and $R$:

$$ \boxed{ d(p_n,c)\le R \qquad \forall n }. $$

Bolzano-Weierstrass Connection

Every bounded infinite subset of $\mathbb R^n$ has an accumulation point.

Equivalently, every bounded sequence in $\mathbb R^n$ has a convergent subsequence.

Boundedness and Accumulation

Boundedness alone does not force a set to contain its accumulation points. The open interval $(0,1)$ is bounded but does not contain its endpoint accumulation points $0$ and $1$.

Bounded versus Totally Bounded

In general metric spaces, totally bounded is stronger than merely bounded.

A set is totally bounded if, for every $\varepsilon>0$, it can be covered by finitely many balls of radius $\varepsilon$.

$$ \boxed{ \text{totally bounded} \Rightarrow \text{bounded} }. $$

The converse is not true in every metric space, although in finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces bounded sets are totally bounded.

Common Misconception: Bounded Means Finite

A bounded set may contain infinitely many or even uncountably many points.

A disk, circle, or line segment provides an immediate example.

Common Misconception: Bounded Means Closed

The open disk and open interval are bounded but not closed.

Common Misconception: Bounded Means Compact Everywhere

In $\mathbb R^n$, closed plus bounded is equivalent to compactness. Boundedness by itself is not enough, and the same characterization does not automatically extend unchanged to arbitrary metric spaces.

Common Misconception: Bounded Coordinates in One Direction Are Enough

Every spatial direction must be controlled. An infinite strip may be bounded vertically while remaining unbounded horizontally.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$S\subseteq B(c,R)$ "S is contained in a ball of finite radius R centered at c."
$d(x,c)\le R$ for all $x\in S$ "Every point of S lies within distance R of c."
$|x|\le A,\ |y|\le B$ "The x- and y-coordinates are uniformly bounded."
$\operatorname{diam}(S)<\infty$ "S has finite diameter."
$K\subseteq\mathbb R^n$ closed and bounded "K is compact by the Heine-Borel theorem."
$A\subseteq B$, $B$ bounded "Every subset of a bounded set is bounded."

In elementary geometry, one may say that a figure is bounded when it has finite spatial extent. In analysis and topology, the more precise formulation is that the underlying point set is contained in a ball of finite radius.

Defense Notation Advice: Do not confuse boundedness with closure or finiteness. When working in an abstract space, identify the metric or norm because boundedness is defined through distance. In Euclidean coordinate problems, checking that every coordinate remains within finite limits is often the simplest test.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: A point set is bounded if all of its points fit inside some finite-radius ball.
  • Metric Form: $$ \boxed{ d(x,c)\le R \qquad \forall x\in S }. $$
  • Euclidean Form: $$ \boxed{ \|x-c\|\le R }. $$
  • Ball Containment: $$ \boxed{ S\subseteq B(c,R) } $$ for some finite $R$.
  • Coordinate Criterion: In $\mathbb R^n$, boundedness is equivalent to every coordinate being uniformly bounded.
  • Diameter Criterion: $$ \boxed{ S\text{ bounded} \iff \operatorname{diam}(S)<\infty } $$ for nonempty metric-space subsets.
  • Finite Sets: Every finite Euclidean point set is bounded.
  • Infinite Sets: A set may be infinite and still bounded.
  • Circle: Bounded.
  • Ellipse: Bounded.
  • Finite Line Segment: Bounded.
  • Polygon: Bounded.
  • Ray: Unbounded.
  • Line: Unbounded.
  • Parabola: Unbounded as a complete curve.
  • Hyperbola: Unbounded as a complete curve.
  • Half-Plane: Unbounded.
  • Open Disk: Bounded but not closed.
  • Closed Disk: Bounded and closed.
  • Closed-Set Contrast: Closed sets need not be bounded.
  • Compactness in Euclidean Space: $$ \boxed{ \text{compact} \iff \text{closed and bounded} }. $$
  • Subset Property: Every subset of a bounded set is bounded.
  • Finite Union: A finite union of bounded sets is bounded.
  • Infinite Union Caution: An infinite union of bounded sets may be unbounded.
  • Closure: The closure of a bounded set is bounded.
  • Interior: The interior of a bounded set is bounded.
  • Boundary: The topological boundary of a bounded set is bounded.
  • Translation: Preserves boundedness.
  • Rotation: Preserves boundedness in Euclidean space.
  • Finite Scaling: Preserves boundedness.
  • Connectedness Contrast: A bounded set need not be connected.
  • Convexity Contrast: A bounded set need not be convex, and a convex set need not be bounded.
  • Graph Caution: A bounded real-valued function on an unbounded domain may have an unbounded graph as a subset of the plane.
  • Sequence Connection: A bounded sequence of points has all of its terms inside one finite-radius ball.
  • Bolzano-Weierstrass: Every bounded infinite subset of $\mathbb R^n$ has an accumulation point.
  • Total Boundedness: Stronger than boundedness in general metric spaces.
  • Common Error: Do not interpret bounded as meaning finite.
  • Common Error: Do not interpret bounded as meaning closed.
  • Common Error: Do not assume bounded alone implies compactness.
  • Common Error: Do not check only one coordinate direction in a multidimensional set.
  • Terminological Contrast: Bounded set fits inside a finite-radius ball. Unbounded set extends arbitrarily far in the metric. Diameter measures the supremum of pairwise distances. Closed set contains its limit points. Compact set is closed and bounded in finite-dimensional Euclidean space. Totally bounded set can be covered by finitely many arbitrarily small balls and is a stronger concept in general metric spaces.
