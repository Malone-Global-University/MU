Adjacent

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

In mathematics, adjacent means next to, neighboring, or sharing a specified boundary or structural connection. The exact meaning depends on the object being discussed.

In geometry, the word commonly describes angles, sides, faces, or vertices that are directly next to one another according to the structure of a figure. In graph theory, two vertices are adjacent when an edge connects them.

The mathematical use preserves the ordinary-language sense of "lying next to" while making the relationship precise.

A useful general interpretation is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{adjacent} \approx \text{directly neighboring according to the structure} }. $$

The word should not be treated as meaning merely "visually close." Two objects may appear near one another in a diagram without being mathematically adjacent.

Operational Nuance

Adjacent Angles

Two angles are adjacent angles when they share:

  • a common vertex,
  • a common side, and
  • no overlapping interior region.

For example, if rays $BA$, $BC$, and $BD$ extend from vertex $B$, then:

$$ \angle ABC $$

and:

$$ \angle CBD $$

are adjacent when they share ray $BC$ and lie on opposite sides of that shared ray.

Angle Addition

When adjacent angles combine to form a larger angle:

$$ \boxed{ m\angle ABD = m\angle ABC + m\angle CBD }. $$

This relationship is an instance of the Angle Addition Postulate.

Adjacent Sides of a Polygon

Two sides of a polygon are adjacent when they share a vertex.

In quadrilateral $ABCD$, for example:

$$ AB\text{ and }BC $$

are adjacent sides because they meet at $B$.

By contrast:

$$ AB\text{ and }CD $$

are opposite sides.

Adjacent Vertices

Two vertices of a polygon are adjacent when they are endpoints of the same side. Thus in quadrilateral $ABCD$:

$$ A\text{ and }B $$

are adjacent vertices, while:

$$ A\text{ and }C $$

are nonadjacent vertices.

Adjacent Side in Right-Triangle Trigonometry

In right-triangle trigonometry, the phrase adjacent side has a specialized meaning relative to a chosen acute angle.

For reference angle $\theta$, the adjacent side is the leg that touches $\theta$ but is not the hypotenuse.

$$ \boxed{ \cos\theta = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}} } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \tan\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}} }. $$

Relative Nature of Adjacent and Opposite

In a right triangle, the labels adjacent and opposite depend on which acute angle has been chosen as the reference angle.

A leg that is adjacent to one acute angle is opposite the other acute angle.

Graph Theory

In graph theory, two vertices $u$ and $v$ are adjacent when there is an edge joining them:

$$ \boxed{ uv\in E }. $$

Equivalently, one may say that $u$ and $v$ are neighbors.

Adjacent Edges in Graph Theory

Two edges are adjacent when they share a common endpoint.

Common Misconception: Adjacent does not simply mean "nearby." Mathematical adjacency requires a specific shared component or direct structural relationship.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\angle ABC$ and $\angle CBD$ "Angle A B C and angle C B D are adjacent angles."
$AB$ and $BC$ "A B and B C are adjacent sides."
$A$ and $B$ "Vertices A and B are adjacent."
$uv\in E$ "U and v are joined by an edge."
$\cos\theta=\frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}}$ "Cosine theta equals adjacent over hypotenuse."
$\tan\theta=\frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}$ "Tangent theta equals opposite over adjacent."

In geometry, the word is normally used adjectivally:

$$ \text{adjacent angles},\qquad \text{adjacent sides},\qquad \text{adjacent vertices}. $$

In graph theory, it may also appear predicatively:

$$ \boxed{ u\text{ is adjacent to }v }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: In right-triangle trigonometry, avoid saying simply "the adjacent side" before identifying the reference angle. Adjacency is relative to that angle.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Adjacent Angles: Two angles are adjacent when they share a common vertex and side without overlapping interiors.
  • Shared Vertex: Adjacent angles must have the same vertex.
  • Shared Side: Adjacent angles must share one ray.
  • No Interior Overlap: Two angles that overlap internally are not adjacent in the standard elementary sense.
  • Angle Addition: If adjacent angles form a larger angle: $$ \boxed{ m\angle ABD = m\angle ABC + m\angle CBD }. $$
  • Linear Pair: A linear pair consists of two adjacent angles whose noncommon sides are opposite rays.
  • Linear-Pair Sum: Angles in a linear pair satisfy: $$ \boxed{ m\angle1+m\angle2=180^\circ }. $$
  • Supplementary vs. Adjacent: Supplementary angles need not be adjacent. They need only have measures summing to $180^\circ$.
  • Complementary vs. Adjacent: Complementary angles need not be adjacent. They need only sum to $90^\circ$.
  • Vertical Angles: Vertical angles are not adjacent. They lie opposite one another at the intersection of two lines.
  • Adjacent Sides: Two polygon sides are adjacent when they share a common endpoint.
  • Opposite Sides: In a quadrilateral, opposite sides do not share a vertex.
  • Adjacent Vertices: Two polygon vertices are adjacent when a side directly connects them.
  • Nonadjacent Vertices: In a polygon, nonadjacent vertices may be connected by a diagonal.
  • Diagonal: A polygonal diagonal joins two nonadjacent vertices.
  • Polyhedra: Faces of a polyhedron are adjacent when they share an edge.
  • Adjacent Faces: Two faces sharing only a vertex but not an edge are generally not called adjacent faces in elementary polyhedral terminology.
  • Right-Triangle Trigonometry: Relative to an acute angle $\theta$, the adjacent side is the leg touching $\theta$ that is not the hypotenuse.
  • Cosine: $$ \boxed{ \cos\theta = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}} }. $$
  • Tangent: $$ \boxed{ \tan\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}} }. $$
  • SOHCAHTOA: The "A" in CAH and TOA refers to the side adjacent to the chosen reference angle.
  • Reference-Angle Dependence: In a right triangle, switching the reference acute angle generally swaps the labels "adjacent" and "opposite" for the two legs.
  • Hypotenuse Exception: Although the hypotenuse touches each acute angle, it is not called the adjacent side in the standard trigonometric side-labeling system.
  • Graph Theory: Vertices $u$ and $v$ are adjacent when an edge joins them.
  • Neighbor: In graph theory, neighbor is a common synonym for an adjacent vertex.
  • Neighborhood: The neighborhood of a vertex $v$ is commonly the set of vertices adjacent to $v$: $$ \boxed{ N(v)=\{u:uv\in E\} }. $$
  • Adjacent Edges: Two graph edges are adjacent when they share an endpoint.
  • Adjacency Matrix: For a simple graph, an adjacency matrix $A=[a_{ij}]$ commonly has: $$ \boxed{ a_{ij}= \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if vertices }i\text{ and }j\text{ are adjacent},\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} } $$
  • Grid Geometry: Cells in a grid may be called adjacent when they share a side. Some applications also define diagonal adjacency, so the convention must be stated.
  • Matrices and Arrays: Entries may be described informally as adjacent when they occupy neighboring positions, although the precise neighborhood convention depends on the application.
  • Topology: In discrete or combinatorial settings, adjacency may help define neighborhood structures, though general topology uses the term neighborhood in a broader and more abstract sense.
  • Computer Science: Adjacency relations appear in graphs, grids, meshes, networks, image processing, and pathfinding algorithms.
  • Visual Proximity Caution: Two objects drawn close together are not necessarily adjacent. Mathematical adjacency is determined by structure, not drawing distance.
  • Terminological Contrast: Adjacent means directly neighboring according to a defined structure. Opposite usually indicates separation across a figure, while nonadjacent means the relevant direct connection or shared boundary is absent.
