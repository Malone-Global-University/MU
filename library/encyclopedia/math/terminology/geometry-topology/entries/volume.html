Volume

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

Volume is the measure of the three-dimensional space occupied by a solid or enclosed by a three-dimensional region. In elementary geometry, volume answers the question:

$$ \boxed{ \text{How much three-dimensional space does the object occupy?} } $$

The word comes through Latin volumen, originally referring to something rolled or a roll, and later developing broader meanings involving bulk, size, and extent. In mathematics, the word came to denote spatial magnitude in three dimensions.

Volume is fundamentally different from length, which measures one-dimensional extent, and area, which measures two-dimensional extent.

$$ \boxed{ \begin{array}{ccl} \text{Length} & \longrightarrow & \text{linear units},\\[4pt] \text{Area} & \longrightarrow & \text{square units},\\[4pt] \text{Volume} & \longrightarrow & \text{cubic units}. \end{array} } $$

Thus a length might be measured in centimeters, an area in square centimeters, and a volume in cubic centimeters.

Operational Nuance

Cubic Units

The natural unit of volume is a cube whose side length is one unit. A unit cube has:

$$ 1\times1\times1=1 $$

cubic unit of volume.

For example:

$$ 1\text{ cm}^3 $$

is the volume of a cube with side length:

$$ 1\text{ cm}. $$

Rectangular Prism

For a rectangular prism with length $l$, width $w$, and height $h$:

$$ \boxed{ V=lwh }. $$

This can be understood as:

$$ \text{base area}\times\text{height}. $$

Since the base area is:

$$ B=lw, $$

the volume may also be written:

$$ \boxed{ V=Bh }. $$

General Prism

For any prism with constant cross-sectional base area $B$ and perpendicular height $h$:

$$ \boxed{ V=Bh }. $$

The same principle applies to cylinders:

$$ V=\pi r^2h. $$

Pyramids and Cones

A pyramid with base area $B$ and perpendicular height $h$ has:

$$ \boxed{ V=\frac13Bh }. $$

A cone follows the same structural formula:

$$ \boxed{ V=\frac13\pi r^2h }. $$

Sphere

A sphere of radius $r$ has volume:

$$ \boxed{ V=\frac43\pi r^3 }. $$

The cubic power reflects the three-dimensional nature of volume. If every linear dimension is multiplied by a scale factor $k$, the volume is multiplied by:

$$ \boxed{k^3}. $$

Similarity and Scaling

If two similar solids have corresponding lengths in the ratio:

$$ k:1, $$

then their surface areas are in the ratio:

$$ k^2:1, $$

while their volumes are in the ratio:

$$ \boxed{k^3:1}. $$

This distinction is essential. Doubling every dimension of a solid does not double its volume; it multiplies the volume by:

$$ 2^3=8. $$

Volume by Cross Sections

Calculus generalizes the elementary formula $V=Bh$. If the cross-sectional area at position $x$ is $A(x)$, then:

$$ \boxed{ V=\int_a^b A(x)\,dx }. $$

Conceptually, the solid is decomposed into very thin slices, and their infinitesimal volumes are accumulated.

Volume as a Triple Integral

For a three-dimensional region $D$, volume can be expressed as:

$$ \boxed{ V=\iiint_D 1\,dV }. $$

This formulation extends the geometric notion of volume into multivariable calculus and measure theory.

Common Misconception: Volume is not the amount of material on the exterior of a solid. That is related to surface area. Volume measures the three-dimensional region itself.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$V$ "V," commonly representing volume.
$V=lwh$ "Volume equals length times width times height."
$V=Bh$ "Volume equals base area times height."
$V=\frac13Bh$ "Volume equals one-third times the base area times the height."
$V=\frac43\pi r^3$ "The volume of a sphere equals four-thirds pi r cubed."
$\text{cm}^3$ "Cubic centimeters."

The exponent in a cubic unit belongs to the unit itself. Thus:

$$ 125\text{ cm}^3 $$

is read:

$$ \text{"one hundred twenty-five cubic centimeters."} $$

It should not be read as though only the numerical value were cubed.

Defense Notation Advice: In formulas such as:

$$ V=Bh, $$

the symbol $B$ generally represents the area of the base, not the base length itself. This distinction is especially important for prisms, cylinders, pyramids, and cones.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Cube: A cube with side length $s$ has: $$ \boxed{ V=s^3 }. $$
  • Rectangular Prism: $$ \boxed{ V=lwh }. $$
  • General Prism: $$ \boxed{ V=Bh }, $$ where $B$ is base area and $h$ is perpendicular height.
  • Cylinder: $$ \boxed{ V=\pi r^2h }. $$
  • Pyramid: $$ \boxed{ V=\frac13Bh }. $$
  • Cone: $$ \boxed{ V=\frac13\pi r^2h }. $$
  • Sphere: $$ \boxed{ V=\frac43\pi r^3 }. $$
  • Hemisphere: Half of a sphere has: $$ \boxed{ V=\frac23\pi r^3 }. $$
  • Frustum: A frustum of a pyramid with parallel base areas $B_1$ and $B_2$ and height $h$ has: $$ \boxed{ V= \frac{h}{3} \left( B_1+\sqrt{B_1B_2}+B_2 \right) }. $$
  • Conical Frustum: With radii $R$ and $r$: $$ \boxed{ V= \frac{\pi h}{3} \left( R^2+Rr+r^2 \right) }. $$
  • Composite Solids: The volume of a solid assembled from nonoverlapping components can be found by adding their individual volumes.
  • Removed Regions: Cavities or drilled portions are handled by subtraction: $$ \boxed{ V_{\text{remaining}} = V_{\text{whole}} - V_{\text{removed}} }. $$
  • Displacement: The volume of an irregular object can sometimes be determined by immersing it in a fluid and measuring the increase in displaced fluid volume.
  • Capacity: Capacity usually refers to how much a container can hold, while volume refers more generally to three-dimensional extent. In practical measurement, the numerical quantities are closely related.
  • Liter Conversion: One liter satisfies: $$ \boxed{ 1\text{ L} = 1\text{ dm}^3 }. $$
  • Milliliter Conversion: $$ \boxed{ 1\text{ mL} = 1\text{ cm}^3 }. $$
  • Unit Conversion: Length conversion factors must be cubed when converting volume. For example: $$ 1\text{ m}=100\text{ cm} $$ implies: $$ \boxed{ 1\text{ m}^3 = 100^3\text{ cm}^3 = 1{,}000{,}000\text{ cm}^3 }. $$
  • Scaling: If every length is multiplied by $k$, then: $$ \boxed{ V_{\text{new}}=k^3V_{\text{old}} }. $$
  • Cavalieri's Principle: Two solids with equal heights and equal cross-sectional areas at every corresponding height have equal volumes.
  • Cross-Section Method: If a solid has cross-sectional area $A(x)$: $$ \boxed{ V=\int_a^bA(x)\,dx }. $$
  • Disk Method: For a solid of revolution with radius $R(x)$: $$ \boxed{ V= \pi \int_a^b [R(x)]^2\,dx }. $$
  • Washer Method: With outer radius $R(x)$ and inner radius $r(x)$: $$ \boxed{ V= \pi \int_a^b \left( R(x)^2-r(x)^2 \right)dx }. $$
  • Cylindrical Shell Method: A shell with radius $x$ and height $f(x)$ contributes: $$ dV=2\pi x f(x)\,dx, $$ giving: $$ \boxed{ V= 2\pi \int_a^b x f(x)\,dx } $$ in the standard vertical-shell setup.
  • Double Integrals: If a solid lies below: $$ z=f(x,y) $$ and above a region $R$, then: $$ \boxed{ V= \iint_R f(x,y)\,dA } $$ when $f(x,y)\ge0$.
  • Triple Integrals: For a spatial region $D$: $$ \boxed{ V= \iiint_D1\,dV }. $$
  • Cylindrical Coordinates: The volume element is: $$ \boxed{ dV=r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz }. $$
  • Spherical Coordinates: Under the common convention: $$ (r,\theta,\phi), $$ the volume element is: $$ \boxed{ dV= r^2\sin\phi\, dr\,d\phi\,d\theta }. $$ Symbol conventions vary among textbooks and fields.
  • Signed Integrals: Ordinary geometric volume is nonnegative. An integral that subtracts regions or incorporates orientation may produce a signed quantity, which should not automatically be interpreted as physical volume.
  • Measure Theory: In higher mathematics, volume is generalized by measure. Three-dimensional Lebesgue measure extends ordinary geometric volume to much broader classes of subsets of $\mathbb{R}^3$.
  • Higher Dimensions: The word volume is sometimes generalized to $n$-dimensional measure. For example, a four-dimensional object may have a hypervolume even though it does not occupy ordinary three-dimensional space.
  • Determinants: For three vectors $\mathbf{a}$, $\mathbf{b}$, and $\mathbf{c}$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$, the volume of the parallelepiped they span is: $$ \boxed{ V= \left| \det \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{a} & \mathbf{b} & \mathbf{c} \end{pmatrix} \right| }. $$
  • Scalar Triple Product: The same volume can be written: $$ \boxed{ V= |\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c})| }. $$
  • Density: If mass density is constant: $$ \rho=\frac{m}{V}. $$ Thus: $$ \boxed{ m=\rho V }. $$
  • Variable Density: If density varies through a region: $$ \boxed{ m= \iiint_D \rho(x,y,z)\,dV }. $$
  • Surface Area Contrast: Surface area measures the two-dimensional boundary of a solid, while volume measures the three-dimensional region enclosed or occupied by it.
  • Dimensional Analysis: Volume has physical dimension: $$ \boxed{ L^3 }, $$ where $L$ represents length.
  • Terminological Contrast: Length measures one-dimensional extent, area measures two-dimensional extent, and volume measures three-dimensional extent. Capacity emphasizes how much a container can hold, while displacement is one practical method for determining the volume of an irregular object.
