Area under a Curve

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

Area under a curve commonly refers to the two-dimensional region lying between the graph of a function, the horizontal axis, and specified vertical boundaries.

If a function $f(x)$ is nonnegative on an interval $[a,b]$, then the ordinary geometric area beneath the graph and above the $x$-axis is:

$$ \boxed{ A=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx }. $$

The phrase becomes more subtle when $f(x)$ takes negative values. A definite integral measures signed accumulation, so portions below the $x$-axis contribute negatively.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \text{definite integral} \ne \text{geometric area in every case} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Nonnegative Function

If:

$$ f(x)\ge0 \qquad \text{for all }x\in[a,b], $$

then the area between the graph, the $x$-axis, and the lines $x=a$ and $x=b$ is:

$$ \boxed{ A=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx }. $$

Riemann-Sum Foundation

The definite integral arises from approximating the region with many thin rectangles.

Divide $[a,b]$ into subintervals of width $\Delta x_i$, and choose sample points $x_i^*$.

A rectangular approximation is:

$$ \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i^*)\Delta x_i. $$

In the limit:

$$ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \lim_{\max\Delta x_i\to0} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i^*)\Delta x_i }. $$

Differential Interpretation

A very thin vertical strip has approximate area:

$$ dA=f(x)\,dx. $$

Summing these infinitesimal contributions leads formally to:

$$ A= \int_a^b dA = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx. $$

Simple Example

For:

$$ f(x)=x, \qquad 0\le x\le2, $$

the area is:

$$ A= \int_0^2x\,dx. $$

Therefore:

$$ A= \left[ \frac{x^2}{2} \right]_0^2 = \boxed{2}. $$

Geometry confirms the result: the region is a right triangle with base $2$ and height $2$.

$$ \frac12(2)(2)=2. $$

Signed Area

If the graph lies below the $x$-axis, the definite integral is negative.

For example:

$$ \int_0^2(-x)\,dx=-2. $$

But the geometric area of the corresponding region is:

$$ \boxed{ 2 }. $$

Thus a definite integral records orientation relative to the axis rather than automatically returning a positive geometric area.

Net Area

The quantity:

$$ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx } $$

may be interpreted as net signed area:

$$ \boxed{ \text{area above axis} - \text{area below axis} }. $$

Total Geometric Area

To obtain total geometric area between a graph and the $x$-axis:

$$ \boxed{ A= \int_a^b|f(x)|\,dx }. $$

Splitting at Zeros

In practice, it is often clearer to split the interval at points where:

$$ f(x)=0. $$

Suppose $f(x)\ge0$ on $[a,c]$ and $f(x)\le0$ on $[c,b]$. Then:

$$ \boxed{ A= \int_a^c f(x)\,dx - \int_c^b f(x)\,dx }. $$

Cancellation Example

Consider:

$$ f(x)=x, \qquad -1\le x\le1. $$

The definite integral is:

$$ \int_{-1}^{1}x\,dx=0. $$

But there are two triangular geometric regions, each of area:

$$ \frac12. $$

Therefore total geometric area is:

$$ \boxed{ A=1 }. $$

Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

If $F'(x)=f(x)$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b)-F(a) }. $$

This converts the limiting process of area accumulation into an antiderivative calculation.

Accumulation Function

Define:

$$ \boxed{ A(x)= \int_a^x f(t)\,dt }. $$

Then, under the usual continuity conditions:

$$ \boxed{ A'(x)=f(x) }. $$

The function value $f(x)$ therefore describes the instantaneous rate at which signed area accumulates.

Why a Dummy Variable Is Useful

In:

$$ A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt, $$

the variable $t$ is used inside the integral so that $x$ can remain the variable upper limit.

Area between a Curve and a Horizontal Line

If the curve $y=f(x)$ lies above the horizontal line $y=c$ on $[a,b]$, then:

$$ \boxed{ A= \int_a^b[f(x)-c]\,dx }. $$

Area between Two Curves

If:

$$ f(x)\ge g(x) \qquad \text{on }[a,b], $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ A= \int_a^b [f(x)-g(x)]\,dx }. $$

The familiar phrase area under a curve is therefore a special case with:

$$ g(x)=0. $$

Horizontal Slicing

Some regions are easier to measure using horizontal strips. If:

$$ x=R(y) \qquad\text{and}\qquad x=L(y), $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ A= \int_c^d [R(y)-L(y)]\,dy }. $$

Thus the underlying concept is not inherently tied to vertical strips.

Improper Area

An unbounded region may still have finite area.

For example:

$$ \int_1^\infty\frac{1}{x^2}\,dx = \lim_{b\to\infty} \int_1^b\frac{1}{x^2}\,dx. $$

Evaluating:

$$ \boxed{ \int_1^\infty\frac{1}{x^2}\,dx=1 }. $$

So an infinitely extended region can have finite area.

Divergent Improper Area

By contrast:

$$ \int_1^\infty\frac1x\,dx $$

diverges.

$$ \boxed{ \int_1^\infty\frac1x\,dx=\infty }. $$

The region extends indefinitely and its accumulated area is unbounded.

Area under a Probability Density Function

If $f(x)$ is a probability density function, then:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)\ge0 } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx=1 }. $$

Probability over an interval is represented by area:

$$ \boxed{ P(a\le X\le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx }. $$

Velocity and Displacement

If $v(t)$ is velocity, then:

$$ \boxed{ \int_a^b v(t)\,dt = \text{displacement} }. $$

Negative velocity contributes negatively, just as portions of a graph below the axis contribute negative signed area.

Total Distance

Total distance traveled is instead:

$$ \boxed{ \int_a^b|v(t)|\,dt }. $$

This parallels the distinction between signed integral and total geometric area.

Rate Accumulation

More generally, if $r(t)$ represents a rate of change of some quantity, then:

$$ \boxed{ \int_a^b r(t)\,dt = \text{net accumulated change} }. $$

Thus the idea of "area under a curve" often represents accumulation rather than literal geometric area.

Numerical Approximation

When an antiderivative is unavailable or data are known only at discrete points, area may be approximated numerically.

Common methods include:

  • left and right Riemann sums,
  • midpoint sums,
  • the trapezoidal rule,
  • Simpson's rule.

Trapezoidal Rule

On a single interval $[a,b]$:

$$ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \approx \frac{b-a}{2} [f(a)+f(b)] }. $$

Midpoint Rule

$$ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \approx (b-a) f\left( \frac{a+b}{2} \right) }. $$

Simpson's Rule on One Pair of Subintervals

$$ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \approx \frac{b-a}{6} \left[ f(a) + 4f\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right) + f(b) \right] }. $$

Units

If $x$ is measured in units $U_x$ and $f(x)$ is measured in units $U_f$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \int f(x)\,dx \text{ has units } U_fU_x }. $$

These units are square units only when both axes represent compatible lengths.

For example, integrating velocity in meters per second over seconds produces meters, not square meters.

Area Need Not Literally Be Physical Area

The phrase area under a curve is often used informally even when the integral represents probability, displacement, work, accumulated mass, charge, or another quantity.

The geometric picture is a model for accumulation, but the dimensional meaning depends on the variables involved.

Common Misconception

It is inaccurate to say simply:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"the definite integral is always area"} } $$

without qualification.

A better statement is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{a definite integral measures signed accumulation} }. $$

When the integrand is nonnegative and the axes represent geometric lengths, this accumulation coincides directly with ordinary geometric area.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$A=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ "Area equals the integral from a to b of f of x with respect to x," when the function is nonnegative.
$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ "The definite integral from a to b of f of x d x."
$A=\int_a^b|f(x)|\,dx$ "Total geometric area equals the integral from a to b of the absolute value of f of x."
$A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt$ "A of x equals the integral from a to x of f of t d t."
$A'(x)=f(x)$ "The derivative of the accumulation function equals f of x."
$\int_a^b[f(x)-g(x)]\,dx$ "The integral from a to b of the upper function minus the lower function."

The phrase under the curve is usually interpreted relative to the horizontal axis or another specified lower boundary. It should not be treated as a complete mathematical description without identifying the interval and boundary.

Defense Notation Advice: Before calling a definite integral an area, determine whether the function changes sign. If it does, distinguish net signed area from total geometric area.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Nonnegative Function: If $f(x)\ge0$ on $[a,b]$: $$ \boxed{ A=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx }. $$
  • Definite Integral: $$ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx } $$ represents signed accumulation.
  • Total Geometric Area: $$ \boxed{ A= \int_a^b|f(x)|\,dx }. $$
  • Net Area: $$ \boxed{ \text{area above axis} - \text{area below axis} }. $$
  • Riemann Sum: $$ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \lim_{\max\Delta x_i\to0} \sum f(x_i^*)\Delta x_i }. $$
  • Fundamental Theorem of Calculus: If $F'=f$: $$ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b)-F(a) }. $$
  • Accumulation Function: $$ \boxed{ A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt }. $$
  • Accumulation Derivative: $$ \boxed{ A'(x)=f(x) }. $$
  • Area above a Horizontal Line: $$ \boxed{ A= \int_a^b[f(x)-c]\,dx }. $$
  • Area between Curves: If $f\ge g$: $$ \boxed{ A= \int_a^b[f(x)-g(x)]\,dx }. $$
  • Horizontal Slices: $$ \boxed{ A= \int_c^d [R(y)-L(y)]\,dy }. $$
  • Improper Integral: $$ \boxed{ \int_a^\infty f(x)\,dx = \lim_{b\to\infty} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx } $$ when the limit exists.
  • Finite Unbounded Region: $$ \boxed{ \int_1^\infty\frac1{x^2}\,dx=1 }. $$
  • Divergent Region: $$ \boxed{ \int_1^\infty\frac1x\,dx \text{ diverges} }. $$
  • Probability Density: $$ \boxed{ P(a\le X\le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx }. $$
  • Total Probability: $$ \boxed{ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx=1 }. $$
  • Velocity: $$ \boxed{ \int_a^b v(t)\,dt = \text{displacement} }. $$
  • Total Distance: $$ \boxed{ \int_a^b|v(t)|\,dt }. $$
  • Rate Accumulation: Integral of a rate over time gives net accumulated change.
  • Trapezoidal Approximation: $$ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \approx \frac{b-a}{2} [f(a)+f(b)] }. $$
  • Midpoint Approximation: $$ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \approx (b-a) f\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right) }. $$
  • Simpson Approximation: $$ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \approx \frac{b-a}{6} \left[ f(a) + 4f\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right) + f(b) \right] }. $$
  • Units: Integral units equal the product of the vertical-unit dimension and horizontal-unit dimension.
  • Geometric Square Units: Square units occur when both axes represent compatible lengths.
  • Sign Change: Zeros of $f$ are natural places to split an area calculation.
  • Cancellation: Positive and negative portions can cancel in a definite integral.
  • Common Error: A zero definite integral does not necessarily mean there is no geometric area.
  • Common Error: A negative definite integral does not mean geometric area is negative.
  • Common Error: Do not use $\int f(x)\,dx$ as total geometric area across sign changes without accounting for absolute value.
  • Terminological Contrast: Definite integral measures signed accumulation. Net area counts regions below the axis negatively. Total geometric area counts all enclosed regions positively. Area under a curve is therefore context-dependent and is most literal when the function remains nonnegative on the interval.
