Bearing

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

A bearing is a numerical or directional description of the direction from one point toward another relative to an agreed reference direction. In elementary mathematics and navigation, the usual reference is north.

Under the common whole-circle bearing convention, the direction is measured clockwise from north:

$$ \boxed{ \text{bearing} = \text{clockwise angle measured from north} }. $$

Bearings therefore differ from ordinary Cartesian direction angles, which are usually measured counterclockwise from the positive $x$-axis.

$$ \boxed{ \text{bearing reference} = \text{north}, \qquad \text{Cartesian angle reference} = +x\text{-axis} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Reference Direction

In the standard navigation convention, north is assigned:

$$ \boxed{ 000^\circ } $$

or equivalently $360^\circ$ when completing a full turn.

Cardinal Directions

The four principal compass directions correspond to:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{array}{c|c} \text{Direction} & \text{Bearing}\\ \hline N & 000^\circ\\ E & 090^\circ\\ S & 180^\circ\\ W & 270^\circ \end{array} }. $$

Intercardinal Directions

The diagonal compass directions are:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{array}{c|c} \text{Direction} & \text{Bearing}\\ \hline NE & 045^\circ\\ SE & 135^\circ\\ SW & 225^\circ\\ NW & 315^\circ \end{array} }. $$

Three-Figure Bearings

Bearings are commonly written with three digits so that every direction has a uniform format.

Thus:

$$ 45^\circ $$

is conventionally written:

$$ \boxed{ 045^\circ }. $$

Likewise:

$$ \boxed{ 5^\circ=005^\circ }. $$

Clockwise Measurement

Bearings increase clockwise:

$$ 000^\circ \rightarrow 090^\circ \rightarrow 180^\circ \rightarrow 270^\circ \rightarrow 360^\circ. $$

This orientation is opposite the usual positive angular direction in Cartesian trigonometry.

Example: Bearing 060°

A bearing of:

$$ \boxed{ 060^\circ } $$

means that the direction is $60^\circ$ clockwise from north.

It lies between north and east.

Example: Bearing 210°

A bearing of:

$$ \boxed{ 210^\circ } $$

is $210^\circ$ clockwise from north and lies in the southwest quadrant of the compass.

Direction from One Point to Another

The phrase:

the bearing of B from A

means the direction one must face or travel when starting at $A$ and heading toward $B$.

$$ \boxed{ \text{bearing of }B\text{ from }A = A\rightarrow B }. $$

Order Matters

The bearing of $B$ from $A$ is generally different from the bearing of $A$ from $B$.

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{bearing}(A\rightarrow B) \ne \operatorname{bearing}(B\rightarrow A) } $$

although the two are related by a half-turn.

Reverse Bearing

The bearing in the opposite direction is called the reverse bearing or back bearing.

If the original bearing is $\theta$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \theta_{\text{reverse}} = (\theta+180^\circ) \bmod 360^\circ }. $$

Practical Reverse-Bearing Rule

If:

$$ 0^\circ\le\theta<180^\circ, $$

add $180^\circ$.

If:

$$ 180^\circ\le\theta<360^\circ, $$

subtract $180^\circ$.

Reverse-Bearing Example

If the bearing of $B$ from $A$ is:

$$ 070^\circ, $$

then the bearing of $A$ from $B$ is:

$$ 070^\circ+180^\circ = \boxed{250^\circ}. $$

Another Reverse-Bearing Example

If the original bearing is:

$$ 225^\circ, $$

then:

$$ 225^\circ-180^\circ = \boxed{045^\circ}. $$

Quadrantal Bearings

Another system describes a direction by beginning from north or south and measuring an acute angle toward east or west.

Examples include:

$$ \boxed{ N30^\circ E, \qquad S20^\circ W }. $$

These are often called quadrantal bearings or reduced bearings.

Quadrantal-Bearing Structure

A quadrantal bearing has the general form:

$$ \boxed{ N\theta E,\quad N\theta W,\quad S\theta E,\quad S\theta W } $$

where:

$$ 0^\circ\le\theta\le90^\circ. $$

Converting NθE to Whole-Circle Bearing

For:

$$ N\theta E, $$

the whole-circle bearing is:

$$ \boxed{ \theta }. $$

Thus:

$$ N35^\circ E = \boxed{035^\circ}. $$

Converting SθE

For:

$$ S\theta E, $$

the whole-circle bearing is:

$$ \boxed{ 180^\circ-\theta }. $$

Example:

$$ S40^\circ E = 180^\circ-40^\circ = \boxed{140^\circ}. $$

Converting SθW

For:

$$ S\theta W, $$

the whole-circle bearing is:

$$ \boxed{ 180^\circ+\theta }. $$

Thus:

$$ S25^\circ W = \boxed{205^\circ}. $$

Converting NθW

For:

$$ N\theta W, $$

the whole-circle bearing is:

$$ \boxed{ 360^\circ-\theta }. $$

Hence:

$$ N50^\circ W = \boxed{310^\circ}. $$

Bearing and Coordinate Geometry

Suppose east is the positive $x$-direction and north is the positive $y$-direction. A displacement of distance $d$ at bearing $\theta$ has components:

$$ \boxed{ \Delta x=d\sin\theta, \qquad \Delta y=d\cos\theta }. $$

The sine and cosine positions may look reversed compared with ordinary Cartesian angle formulas because the bearing angle is measured from the positive $y$-direction rather than the positive $x$-direction.

Example of Bearing Components

A displacement of $10$ units on a bearing of $030^\circ$ gives:

$$ \Delta x = 10\sin30^\circ = 5, $$

and:

$$ \Delta y = 10\cos30^\circ = 5\sqrt3. $$

Thus the motion is $5$ units east and $5\sqrt3$ units north.

Finding Bearing from Coordinates

Suppose:

$$ A=(x_1,y_1), \qquad B=(x_2,y_2). $$

Define:

$$ \Delta x=x_2-x_1, \qquad \Delta y=y_2-y_1. $$

The bearing must be determined from the direction of the vector:

$$ \boxed{ \langle\Delta x,\Delta y\rangle }. $$

A quadrant-aware inverse-trigonometric calculation is preferable because an ordinary tangent value alone does not identify the correct quadrant.

Relationship to atan2

In computational settings, a bearing can be obtained from a two-argument arctangent adapted to east-north components:

$$ \boxed{ \theta = \operatorname{atan2}(\Delta x,\Delta y) } $$

with the resulting angle normalized into the interval:

$$ \boxed{ 0^\circ\le\theta<360^\circ }. $$

Bearing versus Standard Position Angle

A standard Cartesian angle $\alpha$ is typically measured counterclockwise from east, while a bearing $\theta$ is measured clockwise from north.

One useful conversion is:

$$ \boxed{ \alpha = 90^\circ-\theta \pmod{360^\circ} }. $$

Equivalently:

$$ \boxed{ \theta = 90^\circ-\alpha \pmod{360^\circ} }. $$

Example of Angle Conversion

A bearing of:

$$ 030^\circ $$

corresponds to a standard-position angle:

$$ 90^\circ-30^\circ = \boxed{60^\circ}. $$

Bearings and Triangle Problems

Bearing problems often create triangles involving:

  • known travel distances,
  • known bearings,
  • unknown separation distances,
  • unknown angles.

These problems may be solved using trigonometry, the Law of Sines, or the Law of Cosines.

Included Angle from Bearings

If two rays leave the same point with bearings $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$, the smaller angle between them is:

$$ \boxed{ \phi = \min\left( |\theta_1-\theta_2|, 360^\circ-|\theta_1-\theta_2| \right) }. $$

Example of Included Angle

For bearings:

$$ 040^\circ \qquad\text{and}\qquad 110^\circ, $$

the smaller included angle is:

$$ \boxed{ 70^\circ }. $$

Bearings from Different Points

When bearings are measured from different vertices of a triangle, they cannot always be subtracted directly. One may first need reverse bearings or parallel-line angle relationships to determine the triangle's interior angles.

North Lines Are Parallel in Elementary Diagrams

In elementary plane-bearing problems, north lines drawn at different points are treated as parallel:

$$ \boxed{ N_A\parallel N_B }. $$

This allows alternate, corresponding, and supplementary angle relationships to be used.

Bearing and Vectors

A bearing describes the direction of a displacement vector.

If the vector has magnitude $d$:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf v = \langle d\sin\theta, d\cos\theta \rangle }. $$

Unit Direction Vector

The unit vector pointing along bearing $\theta$ is:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf u = \langle \sin\theta, \cos\theta \rangle }. $$

Bearing and Distance

A bearing specifies direction but not distance.

$$ \boxed{ \text{bearing} = \text{direction only} }. $$

To determine a displacement completely in the plane, both magnitude and direction are needed.

Bearing versus Heading

In navigation, heading and bearing are related but are not always interchangeable.

A heading usually describes the direction in which a vehicle or person is pointed, while a bearing commonly describes the direction from one location toward another reference point.

Bearing versus Direction Angle

A bearing uses a specified navigation reference, commonly north and clockwise measurement. A direction angle in analytic geometry ordinarily uses the positive $x$-axis and counterclockwise measurement.

Bearing versus Azimuth

In many mathematical, surveying, and navigation contexts, a whole-circle bearing measured clockwise from north is closely related to what is called an azimuth.

Terminology varies by field, so the angular convention should always be stated explicitly.

Degrees and Minutes

More precise bearings may use degrees, minutes, and seconds:

$$ \boxed{ 072^\circ 15' 30'' }. $$

where:

$$ 1^\circ=60', \qquad 1'=60''. $$

Decimal Degrees

The same direction can be written in decimal degrees. For example:

$$ 72^\circ 15' = 72.25^\circ. $$

Normalization of Bearings

Bearings are commonly normalized to:

$$ \boxed{ 0^\circ\le\theta<360^\circ }. $$

Thus:

$$ 390^\circ \equiv 030^\circ \pmod{360^\circ}. $$

Negative Angular Results

If a calculation produces a negative bearing, add $360^\circ$ until the result lies in the standard interval.

For example:

$$ -40^\circ+360^\circ = \boxed{320^\circ}. $$

Common Misconception: Bearings Start at East

Standard Cartesian angles start at the positive $x$-axis, often drawn eastward. Whole-circle bearings instead ordinarily begin at north.

$$ \boxed{ \text{bearing }000^\circ = \text{north} }. $$

Common Misconception: Bearings Increase Counterclockwise

Under the standard navigation convention, bearings increase clockwise.

$$ \boxed{ N\rightarrow E\rightarrow S\rightarrow W } $$

corresponds to increasing bearing values.

Common Misconception: 90° and 090° Are Different Directions

Numerically they represent the same angle. The leading zero is a formatting convention used to make a three-figure bearing explicit.

Common Misconception: Reverse Bearing Means Negating the Angle

The reverse direction differs by $180^\circ$, not generally by multiplication by $-1$.

$$ \boxed{ \theta_{\text{reverse}} = \theta+180^\circ \pmod{360^\circ} }. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$045^\circ$ "A bearing of zero-four-five degrees."
$180^\circ$ "Due south."
$N30^\circ E$ "North thirty degrees east."
$S20^\circ W$ "South twenty degrees west."
$\theta_{\mathrm{rev}}=(\theta+180^\circ)\bmod360^\circ$ "The reverse bearing differs by one hundred eighty degrees."
$\Delta x=d\sin\theta,\ \Delta y=d\cos\theta$ "Resolve the displacement into east and north components."

Three-figure bearings are often read digit by digit when clarity matters: $007^\circ$ may be spoken as zero-zero-seven degrees. A phrase such as the bearing of B from A should be interpreted as the direction from $A$ toward $B$.

Defense Notation Advice: Before solving a bearing problem, identify the reference direction, the direction in which angles increase, and which point is the starting point. Many bearing errors come from reversing the phrase from A to B or accidentally using Cartesian angle conventions.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: A bearing specifies direction relative to a reference direction.
  • Common Reference: North.
  • Measurement Direction: Clockwise.
  • North: $$ \boxed{ 000^\circ }. $$
  • East: $$ \boxed{ 090^\circ }. $$
  • South: $$ \boxed{ 180^\circ }. $$
  • West: $$ \boxed{ 270^\circ }. $$
  • Three-Figure Convention: Angles below $100^\circ$ are commonly padded with leading zeros.
  • Direction Phrase: "Bearing of B from A" means the direction $A\rightarrow B$.
  • Reverse Bearing: $$ \boxed{ \theta_{\text{reverse}} = (\theta+180^\circ)\bmod360^\circ }. $$
  • Quadrantal Bearing: Directions may also be written as $N\theta E$, $N\theta W$, $S\theta E$, or $S\theta W$.
  • NθE Conversion: Whole-circle bearing $=\theta$.
  • SθE Conversion: Whole-circle bearing $=180^\circ-\theta$.
  • SθW Conversion: Whole-circle bearing $=180^\circ+\theta$.
  • NθW Conversion: Whole-circle bearing $=360^\circ-\theta$.
  • Coordinate Components: $$ \boxed{ \Delta x=d\sin\theta, \qquad \Delta y=d\cos\theta }. $$
  • Direction Vector: $$ \boxed{ \mathbf u= \langle\sin\theta,\cos\theta\rangle }. $$
  • Cartesian-Angle Conversion: $$ \boxed{ \alpha = 90^\circ-\theta \pmod{360^\circ} }. $$
  • Triangle Problems: Bearings commonly supply angles used with the Law of Sines and Law of Cosines.
  • Parallel North Lines: Elementary bearing diagrams treat north directions at different points as parallel.
  • Magnitude Contrast: A bearing gives direction; distance gives magnitude.
  • Heading Contrast: Heading commonly describes the direction an object is pointed; bearing commonly describes direction toward another location.
  • Direction-Angle Contrast: Bearings usually start at north and increase clockwise; standard Cartesian angles usually start at the positive $x$-axis and increase counterclockwise.
  • Azimuth Connection: Whole-circle bearings are closely related to azimuth measurements in many navigation and surveying conventions.
  • Normalization: Bearings are commonly expressed in: $$ \boxed{ 0^\circ\le\theta<360^\circ }. $$
  • Common Error: Do not measure a standard whole-circle bearing counterclockwise from north.
  • Common Error: Do not begin a bearing at the positive $x$-axis unless the problem explicitly defines that convention.
  • Common Error: Do not reverse "bearing of B from A"; the starting point is $A$.
  • Common Error: Do not obtain a reverse bearing by merely negating the original angle.
  • Common Error: When finding a bearing from coordinates, do not use a single inverse-tangent value without checking the direction's quadrant.
  • Terminological Contrast: Bearing specifies direction from a reference direction. Whole-circle bearing typically measures clockwise from north through $360^\circ$. Quadrantal bearing measures an acute angle from north or south toward east or west. Reverse bearing gives the opposite travel direction. Heading describes the direction an object faces or travels. Direction angle commonly uses Cartesian counterclockwise measurement from the positive $x$-axis.
