Parallelepiped

Subfield: Geometry & Topology

Etymology & Roots

A parallelepiped is a three-dimensional polyhedron whose six faces are parallelograms. Opposite faces are parallel and congruent.

$$ \boxed{ \text{parallelepiped} = \text{polyhedron with six parallelogram faces} } $$

The word is built from Greek roots associated with parallel and plane or surface. The name reflects the defining geometric structure: opposite faces occur in parallel pairs.

A parallelepiped may be viewed as the three-dimensional analogue of a parallelogram.

$$ \boxed{ \text{parallelogram} \longrightarrow \text{2-dimensional} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{parallelepiped} \longrightarrow \text{3-dimensional} }. $$

The term is broader than rectangular prism. Every rectangular prism is a parallelepiped, but a parallelepiped may be slanted and need not have right angles.

$$ \boxed{ \text{rectangular prism} \Longrightarrow \text{parallelepiped} } $$

but:

$$ \boxed{ \text{parallelepiped} \not\Longrightarrow \text{rectangular prism} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Basic Structure

A parallelepiped has:

  • $6$ faces;
  • $12$ edges;
  • $8$ vertices.
$$ \boxed{ F=6,\qquad E=12,\qquad V=8 }. $$

Euler Characteristic Check

As a convex polyhedron, it satisfies Euler's relation:

$$ V-E+F = 8-12+6 = \boxed{ 2 }. $$

Faces

Every face is a parallelogram.

$$ \boxed{ \text{all six faces are parallelograms} }. $$

Opposite Faces

The six faces occur in three pairs of opposite faces. Each pair consists of congruent parallel parallelograms.

Opposite Edges

Edges occur in parallel families. The $12$ edges are grouped into three directions, with four mutually parallel edges in each direction.

Vector Construction

A parallelepiped can be generated from three noncoplanar vectors:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a}, \qquad \mathbf{b}, \qquad \mathbf{c} }. $$

starting from a point $\mathbf{p}$.

Parametric Region

The filled parallelepiped is:

$$ \boxed{ \left\{ \mathbf{p} +s\mathbf{a} +t\mathbf{b} +u\mathbf{c} : 0\leq s,t,u\leq1 \right\} }. $$

Vertices

The eight vertices are obtained by independently choosing whether each generating vector is included:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{p} +\varepsilon_1\mathbf{a} +\varepsilon_2\mathbf{b} +\varepsilon_3\mathbf{c}, \qquad \varepsilon_i\in\{0,1\} }. $$

Vertex List from the Origin

If $\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{0}$, the vertices are:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{0}, \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c}, \mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}, \mathbf{a}+\mathbf{c}, \mathbf{b}+\mathbf{c}, \mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{c} }. $$

Noncoplanarity Requirement

For a genuine three-dimensional parallelepiped, the generating vectors must be linearly independent.

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c} \text{ linearly independent} }. $$

If they are linearly dependent, the solid collapses to lower dimension and has zero volume.

Volume by Base Area and Height

Like any prism-like solid:

$$ \boxed{ V = (\text{base area})(\text{perpendicular height}) }. $$

Base Parallelogram Area

If $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ generate a base face, then:

$$ \boxed{ A_{\text{base}} = \|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\| }. $$

Volume by Scalar Triple Product

The volume of the parallelepiped generated by $\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}$ is:

$$ \boxed{ V = \left| \mathbf{a} \cdot (\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c}) \right| }. $$

Equivalent Triple Product Orders

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c}) = \mathbf{b}\cdot(\mathbf{c}\times\mathbf{a}) = \mathbf{c}\cdot(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}) }. $$

Absolute Value

Volume is nonnegative, so the absolute value is used:

$$ \boxed{ V = |\text{scalar triple product}| }. $$

Signed Volume

Without the absolute value:

$$ \det[\mathbf{a}\ \mathbf{b}\ \mathbf{c}] $$

gives an oriented or signed volume whose sign records orientation.

Determinant Formula

If the generating vectors form the columns of:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & b_1 & c_1\\ a_2 & b_2 & c_2\\ a_3 & b_3 & c_3 \end{bmatrix}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ V = |\det A| }. $$

Determinant Zero

If:

$$ \det A=0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ V=0 }. $$

This means the generating vectors are coplanar and do not form a genuine three-dimensional parallelepiped.

Geometric Meaning of the Determinant

The determinant measures how a linear transformation scales oriented three-dimensional volume.

Unit Cube Connection

A matrix $A$ maps the unit cube to the parallelepiped generated by the columns of $A$.

$$ \boxed{ A([0,1]^3) = \text{parallelepiped generated by the columns of }A }. $$

Volume Scaling

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ |\det A| = \text{volume scaling factor} }. $$

Rectangular Parallelepiped

If the three generating edge directions are mutually perpendicular, the parallelepiped is rectangular.

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{c} = 0 }. $$

Rectangular Prism

A rectangular parallelepiped is the familiar rectangular prism or cuboid.

$$ \boxed{ \text{rectangular parallelepiped} = \text{rectangular prism} }. $$

Rectangular Volume

If side lengths are $a$, $b$, and $c$:

$$ \boxed{ V=abc }. $$

Cube

A cube is a special rectangular parallelepiped with all edge lengths equal:

$$ \boxed{ a=b=c }. $$

Hierarchy

$$ \boxed{ \text{cube} \Longrightarrow \text{rectangular parallelepiped} \Longrightarrow \text{parallelepiped} }. $$

Rhombohedron

A rhombohedron is a parallelepiped whose faces are rhombi.

Equivalently, its generating edge vectors have equal lengths:

$$ \boxed{ \|\mathbf{a}\| = \|\mathbf{b}\| = \|\mathbf{c}\| } $$

while the angles need not be right angles.

Cube as a Special Rhombohedron

A cube is a rhombohedron whose face angles are right angles.

Oblique Parallelepiped

A parallelepiped whose adjacent edges are not all mutually perpendicular is commonly described as oblique.

Slant Does Not Change the Base-Times-Height Rule

An oblique parallelepiped still satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ V = A_{\text{base}}h }. $$

Shearing

A shear can turn a rectangular parallelepiped into an oblique parallelepiped while preserving volume when the shear determinant is $1$.

Cavalieri-Type Interpretation

Slanting a prism-like solid without changing its base area or perpendicular height preserves volume.

Face Area

The three distinct face areas generated by vectors $\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}$ are:

$$ \boxed{ A_{ab} = \|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\| } $$
$$ \boxed{ A_{ac} = \|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{c}\| } $$
$$ \boxed{ A_{bc} = \|\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c}\| }. $$

Surface Area

Since opposite faces are congruent:

$$ \boxed{ S = 2\left( \|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\| + \|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{c}\| + \|\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c}\| \right) }. $$

Rectangular Surface Area

In the perpendicular case with side lengths $a,b,c$:

$$ \boxed{ S = 2(ab+ac+bc) }. $$

Face Diagonals

Each parallelogram face has two diagonals. For a face generated by $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$, these correspond to:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b} }. $$

Space Diagonals

A parallelepiped has four body or space diagonals joining pairs of opposite vertices.

Main Space Diagonal from a Chosen Vertex

From $\mathbf{p}$ to the opposite vertex:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} + \mathbf{c} }. $$

Length of That Space Diagonal

$$ \boxed{ d = \|\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{c}\| }. $$

Rectangular Space Diagonal

For a rectangular parallelepiped:

$$ \boxed{ d = \sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2} }. $$

Parallelepiped Diagonals Bisect One Another

The four body diagonals meet at one common midpoint.

$$ \boxed{ \text{all body diagonals bisect one another} }. $$

Center

If the parallelepiped starts at $\mathbf{p}$, its center is:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{p} + \frac{1}{2} \left( \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} + \mathbf{c} \right) }. $$

Central Symmetry

Every parallelepiped is centrally symmetric about its center.

Each vertex has an opposite vertex equally far from the center in the opposite direction.

Parallel Face Planes

Each pair of opposite faces lies in parallel planes.

Equal Opposite Face Areas

Since opposite faces are translates of one another:

$$ \boxed{ \text{opposite faces are congruent} }. $$

Translation Structure

One face is translated to its opposite face by one of the generating vectors.

Affine Image of a Cube

Every nondegenerate parallelepiped is an invertible affine image of a cube.

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x} \mapsto \mathbf{p} + A\mathbf{x} } $$

maps $[0,1]^3$ to a parallelepiped when $A$ is invertible.

Affine Geometry Significance

Parallelepipeds are natural fundamental shapes in affine geometry because affine transformations preserve parallelism.

Angles Are Not Affine Invariants

An affine transformation may change right angles, so a cube can become an oblique parallelepiped.

Parallelism Is Preserved

The parallel-face structure survives every invertible affine transformation.

Coordinate Transformation

Coordinates relative to the edge basis $\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}$ can be written:

$$ \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{p} + s\mathbf{a} + t\mathbf{b} + u\mathbf{c}. $$

Points inside the parallelepiped satisfy:

$$ \boxed{ 0\leq s,t,u\leq1 }. $$

Interior

Points strictly inside satisfy:

$$ \boxed{ 0<s,t,u<1 }. $$

Boundary

A point lies on the boundary when at least one of $s,t,u$ equals $0$ or $1$.

Face Equations in Parameter Coordinates

The six faces correspond to:

$$ \boxed{ s=0,\quad s=1,\quad t=0,\quad t=1,\quad u=0,\quad u=1 }. $$

Barycentric-Like Local Coordinates

The parameters $(s,t,u)$ serve as natural coordinates inside the parallelepiped relative to a chosen vertex and its three edge vectors.

Gram Matrix

The geometry of the generating vectors can also be encoded by the Gram matrix:

$$ G = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{a} & \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} & \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{c}\\ \mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{a} & \mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{b} & \mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{c}\\ \mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{a} & \mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{b} & \mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{c} \end{bmatrix}. $$

Volume from the Gram Determinant

$$ \boxed{ V = \sqrt{\det G} }. $$

for a nondegenerate Euclidean parallelepiped.

Why the Gram Formula Works

If $A$ has the generating vectors as columns, then:

$$ G=A^TA, $$

so:

$$ \det G = \det(A^TA) = (\det A)^2. $$

Therefore:

$$ \sqrt{\det G} = |\det A|. $$

Angle Information

The off-diagonal Gram entries encode the pairwise angles:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = \|\mathbf{a}\| \|\mathbf{b}\| \cos\theta_{ab} }. $$

Rectangular Criterion via Gram Matrix

The parallelepiped is rectangular exactly when the Gram matrix is diagonal.

$$ \boxed{ G\text{ diagonal} \iff \mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c} \text{ mutually orthogonal} }. $$

Unit Parallelepiped

The unit cube is the parallelepiped generated by the standard basis:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{e}_1, \mathbf{e}_2, \mathbf{e}_3 }. $$

Lattice Geometry

Three linearly independent lattice vectors generate a fundamental parallelepiped for a three-dimensional lattice.

Fundamental Parallelepiped

For lattice basis vectors $\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}$, a common half-open fundamental region is:

$$ \boxed{ \left\{ s\mathbf{a} +t\mathbf{b} +u\mathbf{c} : 0\leq s,t,u<1 \right\} }. $$

Why Half-Open Boundaries Are Often Used

Excluding selected opposite boundary faces prevents translated copies from overlapping on their boundaries when partitioning space.

Lattice Cell Volume

The volume of a fundamental parallelepiped is:

$$ \boxed{ |\det[\mathbf{a}\ \mathbf{b}\ \mathbf{c}]| }. $$

Crystallography

Parallelepiped-shaped unit cells arise naturally in crystallography because crystal lattices are generated by translation along independent basis directions.

Primitive Cell

A primitive lattice cell has volume equal to the fundamental lattice volume and contains one lattice point in the counting sense after boundary sharing is taken into account.

Computational Geometry

Parallelepipeds appear in bounding volumes, spatial coordinates, mesh cells, and geometric transformations.

Finite Elements

Hexahedral finite elements may be parallelepiped-shaped in simple meshes or be obtained from cube-like reference elements through more general mappings.

Computer Graphics

Transforming a unit cube by a linear or affine map gives a convenient model for scaling, shearing, rotating, and translating a parallelepiped.

Physics

Infinitesimal volume elements can be understood geometrically through small parallelepipeds generated by tangent or coordinate vectors.

Jacobian Connection

Under a differentiable coordinate transformation, a small cube is locally mapped to an approximately parallelepiped-shaped region whose volume scaling is governed by:

$$ \boxed{ |\det J| }. $$

Multivariable Calculus

The determinant in a change of variables measures the local volume of the parallelepiped generated by the transformed coordinate basis vectors.

Cross Product Interpretation

The vector:

$$ \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} $$

is perpendicular to the base face and has magnitude equal to its area.

Height from a Projection

The perpendicular height relative to the base generated by $\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}$ is:

$$ \boxed{ h = \frac{ |\mathbf{c}\cdot(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b})| }{ \|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\| } }. $$

Recovering Base-Times-Height

Multiplying by the base area:

$$ \|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\|h = \left| \mathbf{c} \cdot (\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}) \right|. $$

Permuting Generating Vectors

Reordering the edge vectors may reverse orientation but does not change ordinary volume.

Determinant Sign under Exchange

Swapping two generating vectors changes:

$$ \det A $$

to:

$$ -\det A, $$

while:

$$ |\det A| $$

remains unchanged.

Scaling One Edge

Multiplying one generating vector by a scalar $k$ multiplies the volume magnitude by $|k|$.

$$ \boxed{ V(k\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}) = |k|V(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}) }. $$

Scaling All Edges

Uniformly scaling every edge vector by $k$ scales volume by:

$$ \boxed{ |k|^3 }. $$

Translation Does Not Change Volume

Moving the entire parallelepiped without changing its edge vectors preserves all lengths, areas, angles, and volume.

Rotation Does Not Change Volume

A rigid rotation has determinant magnitude $1$, so volume is preserved.

Reflection

A reflection reverses orientation but preserves ordinary volume.

Shear

A determinant-$1$ shear changes angles and shape but preserves volume.

Parallelepiped vs. Prism

A parallelepiped is a type of prism whose bases and lateral faces are parallelograms.

A general prism need not have parallelogram bases.

Parallelepiped vs. Rectangular Prism

A rectangular prism requires rectangular faces and right angles. A general parallelepiped does not.

Parallelepiped vs. Cuboid

Cuboid usually means a rectangular parallelepiped, not an arbitrary slanted parallelepiped.

Parallelepiped vs. Cube

A cube additionally requires all edges to have equal length and all face angles to be right angles.

Parallelepiped vs. Rhombohedron

A rhombohedron requires all faces to be rhombi. A general parallelepiped may have parallelogram faces of unequal adjacent side lengths.

Parallelepiped vs. Parallelogram

A parallelogram is two-dimensional; a parallelepiped is three-dimensional.

Parallelepiped vs. Parallelotope

A parallelotope is the higher-dimensional generalization of a parallelepiped.

In three dimensions, the terms may overlap depending on convention.

Higher-Dimensional Analogue

In $n$ dimensions, vectors $\mathbf{v}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{v}_n$ generate:

$$ \boxed{ \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^{n} t_i\mathbf{v}_i : 0\leq t_i\leq1 \right\} } $$

called an $n$-dimensional parallelotope.

Higher-Dimensional Volume

If the vectors form a square matrix $A$:

$$ \boxed{ V_n = |\det A| }. $$

Topology

Every filled nondegenerate parallelepiped is topologically equivalent to a closed three-dimensional ball or cube.

$$ \boxed{ \text{parallelepiped} \cong [0,1]^3 } $$

through an affine homeomorphism.

Boundary Topology

The boundary of a convex parallelepiped is topologically equivalent to a sphere:

$$ \boxed{ \partial P \cong S^2 }. $$

Geometry vs. Topology

Topologically, a cube and a slanted parallelepiped are indistinguishable as solids. Geometrically, their angles and edge lengths may differ substantially.

Common Error: Assuming Every Parallelepiped Is Rectangular

Right angles are not required.

Common Error: Assuming Every Face Is a Rectangle

Every face is a parallelogram, but it need not be rectangular.

Common Error: Using $abc$ for Every Parallelepiped

The simple product of edge lengths gives volume only when those edge directions are mutually perpendicular.

Correct General Volume

$$ \boxed{ V = \left| \mathbf{a} \cdot (\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c}) \right| }. $$

Common Error: Omitting Absolute Value

A determinant may be negative because of orientation, but physical volume is nonnegative.

Common Error: Treating Zero Determinant as a Small Parallelepiped

A zero determinant means the generating vectors are linearly dependent and the three-dimensional solid has collapsed.

Common Error: Confusing Face Area with Volume

A cross product magnitude gives a parallelogram area; a scalar triple product gives parallelepiped volume.

Common Error: Confusing a Parallelepiped with a Parallelogram

They are dimensional analogues, not synonyms.

Common Error: Assuming Opposite Faces Merely Have Equal Area

They are stronger than equal-area faces: they are congruent, parallel parallelograms.

Common Error: Assuming Every Prism Is a Parallelepiped

A triangular prism, for example, is not a parallelepiped because two of its faces are triangles.

Common Error: Confusing Rectangular Parallelepiped and Rhombohedron

Rectangular describes right angles; rhombohedral describes equal edge lengths or rhombic faces.

Common Error: Ignoring the Choice of Perpendicular Height

In an oblique parallelepiped, the height used in $V=Bh$ is not generally the length of a slanted edge.

Common Error: Treating a Slanted Edge as Height

Height is the perpendicular distance between the base plane and its parallel opposite face.

Common Error: Assuming Shearing Changes Volume

A volume-preserving shear can strongly alter the appearance while leaving the determinant magnitude unchanged.

Common Error: Assuming the Four Body Diagonals Have Equal Length

They all share a common midpoint, but their lengths need not be equal in a general parallelepiped.

Rectangular Exception

In a rectangular parallelepiped, all four body diagonals have equal length.

Common Error: Assuming All Face Diagonals Are Equal

General parallelogram faces can have diagonals of different lengths.

Common Error: Confusing Determinant Sign with Negative Volume

The sign records orientation; volume uses the absolute value.

Common Error: Assuming the Generating Vectors Must Be Unit Vectors

They may have arbitrary nonzero lengths and arbitrary linearly independent directions.

Common Error: Assuming Any Three Vectors Form a Parallelepiped

Three vectors form a nondegenerate three-dimensional parallelepiped only when they are linearly independent.

Common Error: Ignoring the Starting Point

Edge vectors determine size and shape, while the starting point determines position.

Common Misconception: A parallelepiped is not merely another name for a rectangular box. It is the broader affine family of six-faced solids whose faces are parallelograms. Its general volume is controlled by the scalar triple product or determinant, which accounts automatically for edge lengths, angles, slant, and orientation.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\mathbf{p}+s\mathbf{a}+t\mathbf{b}+u\mathbf{c}$ "A point in the parallelepiped generated from p by the edge vectors a, b, and c."
$V=|\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c})|$ "The volume is the absolute value of the scalar triple product."
$V=|\det A|$ "The volume is the absolute value of the determinant of the edge-vector matrix."
$A_{\text{base}}=\|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\|$ "The base parallelogram area is the magnitude of a cross b."
$V=A_{\text{base}}h$ "Volume equals base area times perpendicular height."
$\det A=0$ "The generating vectors are linearly dependent, so the parallelepiped is degenerate."
$A([0,1]^3)$ "The linear image of the unit cube under A."

The word parallelepiped is commonly pronounced with emphasis that separates its parallel-related beginning from its final geometric root. In mathematical speech, the exact pronunciation varies somewhat by region, but the written term should not be shortened to parallel prism because that loses its established technical meaning.

In vector calculus, one often says:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"the parallelepiped spanned by } \mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}\text{"} } $$

or:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"the parallelepiped generated by the three vectors."} } $$

The word spanned here should be distinguished from the full vector-space span, which allows arbitrary scalar coefficients. The parallelepiped region uses bounded coefficients such as:

$$ 0\leq s,t,u\leq1. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Use $|\det A|$ or the absolute scalar triple product for ordinary volume. Reserve $\det A$ without absolute value for oriented volume. If using $V=Bh$, make clear that $h$ is perpendicular height, not generally the length of a slanted edge. Distinguish a general parallelepiped from the rectangular special case before applying formulas such as $V=abc$ or $d=\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}$.
