Area of an Ellipse

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

The area of an ellipse is the measure of the two-dimensional region enclosed by an ellipse.

If $a$ and $b$ are the lengths of the ellipse's two semiaxes, then:

$$ \boxed{ A=\pi ab }. $$

Conventionally, $a$ is often chosen as the semimajor axis and $b$ as the semiminor axis, so:

$$ a\ge b>0. $$

However, the area formula itself is symmetric:

$$ \pi ab=\pi ba. $$

Therefore the numerical area does not depend on which semiaxis is labeled $a$ and which is labeled $b$.

The formula may be viewed as a natural generalization of the area of a circle. When:

$$ a=b=r, $$

the ellipse becomes a circle and:

$$ A=\pi r^2. $$

Operational Nuance

Standard Formula

$$ \boxed{ A=\pi ab }. $$

Here:

  • $a$ is one semiaxis, usually the semimajor axis,
  • $b$ is the other semiaxis, usually the semiminor axis.

Major and Minor Axes

The full major axis has length:

$$ \boxed{ 2a }. $$

The full minor axis has length:

$$ \boxed{ 2b }. $$

The area formula uses the semiaxis lengths, not the full axis lengths.

Area from Full Axis Lengths

Suppose the full major and minor axis lengths are $M$ and $m$. Then:

$$ a=\frac{M}{2}, \qquad b=\frac{m}{2}. $$

Therefore:

$$ A = \pi \left(\frac{M}{2}\right) \left(\frac{m}{2}\right) = \boxed{ \frac{\pi Mm}{4} }. $$

Example

Suppose an ellipse has:

$$ a=5, \qquad b=3. $$

Then:

$$ A=\pi(5)(3). $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ A=15\pi }. $$

Numerically:

$$ A\approx47.12. $$

Standard Coordinate Equation

An axis-aligned ellipse centered at $(h,k)$ has equation:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{(x-h)^2}{a^2} + \frac{(y-k)^2}{b^2} = 1 }. $$

Its area is:

$$ \boxed{ A=\pi ab }. $$

The location of the center $(h,k)$ does not affect the area.

Horizontal Major Axis

If:

$$ a>b, $$

and $a^2$ appears beneath the $x$-term, the major axis is horizontal.

Vertical Major Axis

If the larger denominator lies beneath the $y$-term, the major axis is vertical. The area remains:

$$ \boxed{ \pi ab }. $$

Rotation Does Not Change Area

Rotating an ellipse changes its orientation but not its semiaxis lengths, so:

$$ \boxed{ \text{rotation preserves ellipse area} }. $$

Translation Does Not Change Area

Moving the ellipse to another location in the plane likewise preserves its area.

Ellipse as a Scaled Circle

Consider the unit circle:

$$ x^2+y^2\le1. $$

It has area:

$$ \pi. $$

Apply the scaling:

$$ x\mapsto ax, \qquad y\mapsto by. $$

Horizontal lengths are multiplied by $a$, while vertical lengths are multiplied by $b$. Area is therefore multiplied by:

$$ ab. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ A=\pi ab }. $$

Determinant Interpretation

The scaling transformation can be represented by:

$$ M= \begin{pmatrix} a & 0\\ 0 & b \end{pmatrix}. $$

Its determinant is:

$$ \det M=ab. $$

Since planar area scales by the absolute determinant:

$$ A_{\text{ellipse}} = |\det M| A_{\text{unit circle}}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ A=ab\pi }. $$

General Affine Image

More generally, an ellipse can be produced as an affine image of a circle. If a linear transformation $M$ maps the unit disk to an ellipse, then:

$$ \boxed{ A_{\text{ellipse}} = \pi|\det M| }. $$

Scaling Both Semiaxes

If:

$$ a\mapsto ka, \qquad b\mapsto kb, $$

then:

$$ A' = \pi(ka)(kb) = k^2\pi ab. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ A'=k^2A }. $$

Scaling Only One Semiaxis

If only $a$ is multiplied by $k$:

$$ a\mapsto ka, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ A'\!=kA }. $$

This is because only one independent linear direction has been scaled.

Area Ratio of Similar Ellipses

If two ellipses are similar with linear scale factor $k$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{A_2}{A_1}=k^2 }. $$

Calculus Derivation

For an ellipse centered at the origin:

$$ \frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1, $$

solve for the upper half:

$$ y = b\sqrt{ 1-\frac{x^2}{a^2} }. $$

By symmetry:

$$ A = 2 \int_{-a}^{a} b\sqrt{ 1-\frac{x^2}{a^2} }\,dx. $$

Equivalently:

$$ A = 4b \int_0^a \sqrt{ 1-\frac{x^2}{a^2} }\,dx. $$

Let:

$$ x=au. $$

Then:

$$ dx=a\,du. $$

Therefore:

$$ A = 4ab \int_0^1 \sqrt{1-u^2}\,du. $$

Since:

$$ \int_0^1\sqrt{1-u^2}\,du = \frac{\pi}{4}, $$

we obtain:

$$ \boxed{ A=\pi ab }. $$

Parametric Description

An ellipse may be parameterized by:

$$ \boxed{ x=a\cos t, \qquad y=b\sin t }. $$

with:

$$ 0\le t\le2\pi. $$

This makes clear that the ellipse is obtained by independently scaling the cosine and sine coordinates of a unit circle.

Area from a Line Integral

For a positively oriented simple closed curve:

$$ A = \frac12 \oint (x\,dy-y\,dx). $$

Substituting:

$$ x=a\cos t, \qquad y=b\sin t $$

gives:

$$ \boxed{ A=\pi ab }. $$

Relation to Eccentricity

For an ellipse with semimajor axis $a$ and semiminor axis $b$:

$$ e= \sqrt{ 1-\frac{b^2}{a^2} }. $$

Solving for $b$:

$$ b= a\sqrt{1-e^2}. $$

Therefore the area may be written:

$$ \boxed{ A= \pi a^2 \sqrt{1-e^2} }. $$

Relation to the Focal Distance

If $c$ is the distance from the center to either focus:

$$ c^2=a^2-b^2. $$

Hence:

$$ b= \sqrt{a^2-c^2}. $$

The area can therefore be expressed as:

$$ \boxed{ A= \pi a\sqrt{a^2-c^2} }. $$

Circle as a Special Case

If:

$$ a=b=r, $$

then:

$$ A = \pi rr = \boxed{ \pi r^2 }. $$

Thus the circle-area formula is contained within the ellipse-area formula.

Area and Perimeter Behave Differently

The area of an ellipse has the simple exact formula:

$$ \pi ab. $$

Its circumference, however, does not generally reduce to an equally simple elementary formula.

Therefore area and perimeter should not be assumed to have parallel formulas merely because both are geometric measurements of the same ellipse.

Units

If $a$ and $b$ are measured in centimeters:

$$ ab $$

has units:

$$ \text{cm}^2. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ is measured in square units} }. $$

Common Misconception: $a$ and $b$ in the standard ellipse formula are semiaxis lengths. If a problem gives the full major and minor axis lengths, each must first be divided by $2$ before using $A=\pi ab$.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$A=\pi ab$ "Area equals pi a b."
$2a$ "Two a, the full major-axis length."
$2b$ "Two b, the full minor-axis length."
$A=\frac{\pi Mm}{4}$ "Area equals pi times the major-axis length times the minor-axis length, divided by four."
$\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1$ "X squared over a squared plus y squared over b squared equals one."
$A=\pi a^2\sqrt{1-e^2}$ "Area equals pi a squared times the square root of one minus e squared."

The letters $a$ and $b$ are conventions, not universal meanings. Some sources may assign them differently, but in standard conic notation $a$ often denotes the semimajor axis and $b$ the semiminor axis.

Defense Notation Advice: Determine whether the given measurements are semiaxes or full axis lengths before substituting. If the equation is provided, remember that the denominators are $a^2$ and $b^2$, so the semiaxis lengths are obtained by taking square roots.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Formula: $$ \boxed{ A=\pi ab }. $$
  • Semimajor Axis: Conventionally: $$ \boxed{ a\ge b }. $$
  • Semiminor Axis: $b$ commonly denotes the shorter semiaxis.
  • Major-Axis Length: $$ \boxed{ 2a }. $$
  • Minor-Axis Length: $$ \boxed{ 2b }. $$
  • Full-Axis Formula: If the full axis lengths are $M$ and $m$: $$ \boxed{ A=\frac{\pi Mm}{4} }. $$
  • Standard Equation: $$ \boxed{ \frac{(x-h)^2}{a^2} + \frac{(y-k)^2}{b^2} = 1 }. $$
  • Center: Translation of the center does not change area.
  • Orientation: Rotation of the ellipse does not change area.
  • Circle Special Case: If: $$ a=b=r, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ A=\pi r^2 }. $$
  • Unit-Circle Scaling: Scaling the unit circle by $a$ horizontally and $b$ vertically multiplies its area by $ab$.
  • Determinant Interpretation: $$ \boxed{ A_{\text{new}} = |\det M|A_{\text{old}} }. $$
  • Diagonal Scaling Matrix: $$ \boxed{ M= \begin{pmatrix} a&0\\ 0&b \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det M=ab }. $$
  • General Affine Image: If an ellipse is the image of the unit disk under $M$: $$ \boxed{ A=\pi|\det M| }. $$
  • Uniform Scaling: $$ \boxed{ a\mapsto ka,\quad b\mapsto kb \Rightarrow A\mapsto k^2A }. $$
  • One-Direction Scaling: If only one semiaxis is multiplied by $k$, area is multiplied by $k$.
  • Area Ratio: Similar ellipses with linear scale factor $k$ have area ratio: $$ \boxed{ k^2 }. $$
  • Calculus Formula: $$ \boxed{ A= 4b \int_0^a \sqrt{ 1-\frac{x^2}{a^2} }\,dx }. $$
  • Parametric Form: $$ \boxed{ x=a\cos t, \qquad y=b\sin t }. $$
  • Line-Integral Form: $$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \oint (x\,dy-y\,dx) }. $$
  • Eccentricity: $$ \boxed{ e= \sqrt{ 1-\frac{b^2}{a^2} } }. $$
  • Area from Eccentricity: $$ \boxed{ A= \pi a^2\sqrt{1-e^2} }. $$
  • Focal Relation: $$ \boxed{ c^2=a^2-b^2 }. $$
  • Area from Focal Distance: $$ \boxed{ A= \pi a\sqrt{a^2-c^2} }. $$
  • Area vs. Circumference: Ellipse area has the simple exact formula $\pi ab$, while ellipse circumference is substantially more complicated.
  • Square Units: Area is measured in units such as $\text{cm}^2$, $\text{m}^2$, or $\text{ft}^2$.
  • Common Error: Using the full axis lengths directly in $\pi ab$ produces an area four times too large.
  • Common Error: In the standard equation, the denominators are the squares of the semiaxes, not the semiaxes themselves.
  • Terminological Contrast: Major axis and minor axis refer to full diameters of the ellipse. Semimajor axis and semiminor axis are half of those lengths. The area formula $A=\pi ab$ uses the semiaxes.
