Altitude of a Parallelogram

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

The altitude of a parallelogram is the perpendicular distance between one side chosen as a base and the opposite parallel side.

If side $AB$ is used as the base and a perpendicular from the opposite side meets the line containing $AB$ at $H$, then:

$$ \boxed{ DH\perp AB }. $$

The length $DH$ is the altitude corresponding to base $AB$.

As in other geometric uses of altitude, the essential idea is perpendicular height rather than visual verticality.

$$ \boxed{ \text{altitude} = \text{perpendicular distance between opposite parallel sides} }. $$

A parallelogram has two natural altitude measurements, one corresponding to each pair of opposite sides.

Operational Nuance

Choosing a Base

Any side of a parallelogram may be chosen as the base. Once a base is selected, the corresponding altitude is measured perpendicular to that base.

If sides have lengths $a$ and $b$, then the corresponding altitudes may be denoted:

$$ \boxed{ h_a,\qquad h_b }. $$

Two Different Altitudes

A nonrectangular parallelogram generally has two different altitude lengths.

$$ \boxed{ h_a\ne h_b } $$

in general.

Each is tied to its corresponding base.

Area Formula

The area of a parallelogram is:

$$ \boxed{ A=bh }. $$

More precisely:

$$ \boxed{ A=ah_a=bh_b }. $$

The base and altitude must correspond to the same orientation.

Altitude from Area

If area $A$ and base $b$ are known:

$$ \boxed{ h=\frac{A}{b} }. $$

Altitude vs. Side Length

In an oblique parallelogram, the slanted side is not the altitude because it is not perpendicular to the chosen base.

$$ \boxed{ \text{side length} \ne \text{altitude} } $$

in general.

Trigonometric Relationship

Suppose adjacent sides have lengths $a$ and $b$ and the included angle is $\theta$. If $a$ is chosen as the base, then:

$$ \boxed{ h_a=b\sin\theta }. $$

Therefore the area may also be written:

$$ \boxed{ A=ab\sin\theta }. $$

If $b$ is chosen as the base, then:

$$ \boxed{ h_b=a\sin\theta }. $$

Example

Suppose a parallelogram has a base of length:

$$ b=12 $$

and area:

$$ A=84. $$

Then:

$$ h = \frac{84}{12} = \boxed{7}. $$

Exterior Altitude Segment

In an oblique parallelogram, a perpendicular dropped from a vertex may meet an extension of the opposite side rather than the side segment itself.

This is still a valid altitude because the altitude is measured to the line containing the base.

Rectangle as a Special Case

A rectangle is a parallelogram with right angles. Therefore, when one side is chosen as the base, the adjacent side is already perpendicular to it and serves as the altitude.

$$ \boxed{ A=lw } $$

is therefore a special case of:

$$ A=bh. $$

Rhombus as a Special Case

A rhombus has four equal sides, but its altitude is generally shorter than a side unless the rhombus is a square.

If side length is $s$ and interior angle is $\theta$:

$$ \boxed{ h=s\sin\theta }. $$

Shearing Interpretation

A rectangle can be sheared sideways into an oblique parallelogram while preserving its base and perpendicular altitude.

Since:

$$ A=bh, $$

the area remains unchanged under such a shear.

Common Misconception: The altitude of a parallelogram is not automatically one of its sides. It is specifically the perpendicular distance between the chosen base and the opposite parallel side.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$h$ "The height," or "the altitude."
$DH\perp AB$ "D H is perpendicular to A B."
$A=bh$ "Area equals base times height."
$A=ah_a=bh_b$ "The area equals a times its corresponding height and also b times its corresponding height."
$h=b\sin\theta$ "The height equals b sine theta."
$A=ab\sin\theta$ "Area equals a b sine theta."

The phrase height of the parallelogram is incomplete unless the base is clear from context, because changing the chosen base changes the corresponding altitude.

More explicit notation such as:

$$ h_a,\qquad h_b $$

identifies which altitude belongs to which base.

Defense Notation Advice: Always pair the base with its corresponding perpendicular height. Using a side length that is not perpendicular to the chosen base in $A=bh$ gives an incorrect area.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: The altitude is the perpendicular distance between a chosen base and the opposite parallel side.
  • Base Dependence: A parallelogram has a different corresponding altitude for each pair of opposite sides.
  • Common Symbols: $$ \boxed{ h,\qquad h_a,\qquad h_b }. $$
  • Perpendicular Requirement: $$ \boxed{ \text{altitude}\perp\text{base} }. $$
  • Parallel-Side Distance: Because opposite sides are parallel, the perpendicular distance between them is constant.
  • Area Formula: $$ \boxed{ A=bh }. $$
  • Two Base Choices: $$ \boxed{ A=ah_a=bh_b }. $$
  • Altitude from Area: $$ \boxed{ h=\frac{A}{b} }. $$
  • Trigonometric Altitude: If adjacent side $b$ forms angle $\theta$ with base $a$: $$ \boxed{ h_a=b\sin\theta }. $$
  • Area with Two Sides and Included Angle: $$ \boxed{ A=ab\sin\theta }. $$
  • Exterior Foot: The foot of an altitude may lie on an extension of the base in a sufficiently oblique drawing.
  • Supporting Line: The altitude is measured to the line containing the chosen base, not necessarily to the finite side segment.
  • Side-Length Contrast: A slanted side is not an altitude unless it is perpendicular to the base.
  • Rectangle: The adjacent side serves as the altitude because adjacent sides are perpendicular.
  • Square: Either side adjacent to a chosen base is an altitude of equal length.
  • Rhombus: Equal side lengths do not imply that the altitude equals the side length.
  • Rhombus Altitude: If side length is $s$ and included angle is $\theta$: $$ \boxed{ h=s\sin\theta }. $$
  • Shearing: Sliding one base parallel to itself can change the side angle without changing base length or altitude.
  • Area Preservation Under Shear: If $b$ and $h$ remain fixed: $$ \boxed{ A=bh } $$ remains fixed.
  • Coordinate Geometry: The altitude can be found as the perpendicular distance between two parallel lines containing opposite sides.
  • Parallel-Line Form: Suppose opposite sides lie on: $$ Ax+By+C_1=0 $$ and: $$ Ax+By+C_2=0. $$ Their perpendicular separation is: $$ \boxed{ h= \frac{|C_2-C_1|} {\sqrt{A^2+B^2}} }. $$
  • Point-to-Line Method: If one base lies on: $$ Ax+By+C=0 $$ and $(x_0,y_0)$ lies on the opposite side, then: $$ \boxed{ h= \frac{|Ax_0+By_0+C|} {\sqrt{A^2+B^2}} }. $$
  • Vector Geometry: If adjacent side vectors are $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$, the area is: $$ \boxed{ A=\|\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}\| } $$ in three-dimensional notation.
  • Altitude from Vector Area: If $\|\mathbf{u}\|$ is the base: $$ \boxed{ h= \frac{\|\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}\|} {\|\mathbf{u}\|} }. $$
  • Two-Dimensional Determinant: For vectors: $$ \mathbf{u}=(u_1,u_2),\qquad \mathbf{v}=(v_1,v_2), $$ the parallelogram area is: $$ \boxed{ A= |u_1v_2-u_2v_1| }. $$
  • Scaling: Under dilation by factor $k$: $$ h\mapsto kh. $$
  • Area Scaling: Under the same dilation: $$ \boxed{ A\mapsto k^2A }. $$
  • Units: Altitude is measured in linear units.
  • Area Units: The product $bh$ is measured in square units.
  • Orientation Independence: Rotating the parallelogram does not change its altitude relative to a chosen base.
  • Diagram Caution: The segment drawn vertically on a page is not necessarily the altitude unless it is perpendicular to the base.
  • Terminological Contrast: The base is the selected side used for measurement. The altitude is the perpendicular distance to the opposite parallel side. A side length may be slanted and need not equal the altitude. The area formula $A=bh$ always requires a corresponding base-altitude pair.
