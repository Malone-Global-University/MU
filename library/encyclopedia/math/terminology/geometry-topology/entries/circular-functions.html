Circular Functions

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

Circular functions is a traditional name for the trigonometric functions when they are viewed through geometry on a circle rather than only through right-triangle ratios.

The principal circular functions are:

$$ \boxed{ \sin\theta,\quad \cos\theta,\quad \tan\theta,\quad \csc\theta,\quad \sec\theta,\quad \cot\theta }. $$

Their circular interpretation begins with an angle $\theta$ in standard position and a point:

$$ P=(x,y) $$

on a circle of radius $r$ centered at the origin.

$$ \boxed{ x^2+y^2=r^2 }. $$

The circle coordinates then define the trigonometric functions.

Operational Nuance

Basic Circular Definitions

If the terminal side of angle $\theta$ passes through:

$$ P=(x,y) $$

on a circle of radius $r>0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \sin\theta=\frac{y}{r} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \cos\theta=\frac{x}{r} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \tan\theta=\frac{y}{x} } $$

where tangent is defined only when $x\ne0$.

Reciprocal Circular Functions

$$ \boxed{ \csc\theta=\frac{r}{y} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \sec\theta=\frac{r}{x} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \cot\theta=\frac{x}{y} }. $$

Unit Circle

The simplest circular model uses radius:

$$ r=1. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ x^2+y^2=1 }. $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ P=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta) }. $$

Coordinate Meaning

On the unit circle:

$$ \boxed{ \cos\theta=x } $$
$$ \boxed{ \sin\theta=y }. $$

Thus cosine is the horizontal coordinate and sine is the vertical coordinate of a rotating point on the unit circle.

Tangent as Slope

When $x\ne0$, the terminal ray has slope:

$$ \boxed{ \tan\theta = \frac{y}{x} }. $$

This gives tangent a natural geometric interpretation as slope.

Why These Are Called Functions

Each permitted real angle $\theta$ is associated with a unique trigonometric value. For example:

$$ \boxed{ \theta \mapsto \sin\theta }. $$

Thus sine and cosine are genuine functions of a real variable.

Right-Triangle Connection

In Quadrant I, the circle point forms a right triangle with:

  • adjacent side $x$,
  • opposite side $y$,
  • hypotenuse $r$.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \sin\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \cos\theta = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \tan\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}} }. $$

Circle Model Extends Triangle Trigonometry

Right-triangle definitions naturally begin with acute angles. Circular functions extend the same ratios to general real angles.

$$ \boxed{ \theta\in\mathbb R } $$

subject to the domain restrictions of the individual functions.

Positive and Negative Angles

Under the standard convention:

$$ \boxed{ \text{counterclockwise} \Rightarrow \theta>0 } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{clockwise} \Rightarrow \theta<0 }. $$

Coterminal Angles

Angles differing by complete revolutions have the same terminal side:

$$ \boxed{ \theta+2\pi k, \qquad k\in\mathbb Z }. $$

Periodicity of Sine and Cosine

After one complete revolution, the terminal point returns to its previous location. Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \sin(\theta+2\pi) = \sin\theta } $$
$$ \boxed{ \cos(\theta+2\pi) = \cos\theta }. $$

Sine and cosine therefore have period:

$$ \boxed{ 2\pi }. $$

Periodicity of Tangent and Cotangent

Rotating by $\pi$ sends:

$$ (x,y)\mapsto(-x,-y). $$

Since:

$$ \frac{-y}{-x} = \frac{y}{x}, $$

tangent has period:

$$ \boxed{ \pi }. $$

Similarly:

$$ \boxed{ \cot(\theta+\pi) = \cot\theta }. $$

Periodicity of Secant and Cosecant

As reciprocals of cosine and sine, secant and cosecant have period:

$$ \boxed{ 2\pi }. $$

Domains

Sine and cosine are defined for every real angle:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(\sin) = \operatorname{Dom}(\cos) = \mathbb R }. $$

Tangent and Secant Domains

Since both involve division by $\cos\theta$:

$$ \boxed{ \theta \ne \frac{\pi}{2}+k\pi, \qquad k\in\mathbb Z }. $$

Cotangent and Cosecant Domains

Since both involve division by $\sin\theta$:

$$ \boxed{ \theta \ne k\pi, \qquad k\in\mathbb Z }. $$

Ranges of Sine and Cosine

Unit-circle coordinates satisfy:

$$ -1\le x\le1, \qquad -1\le y\le1. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ -1\le\sin\theta\le1 } $$
$$ \boxed{ -1\le\cos\theta\le1 }. $$

Range of Tangent

Tangent takes every real value:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Range}(\tan) = \mathbb R }. $$

Ranges of Secant and Cosecant

$$ \boxed{ \sec\theta \in (-\infty,-1]\cup[1,\infty) } $$
$$ \boxed{ \csc\theta \in (-\infty,-1]\cup[1,\infty) }. $$

Range of Cotangent

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Range}(\cot) = \mathbb R }. $$

Quadrant Signs

Circular geometry makes the signs of the trigonometric functions immediate.

Quadrant $\sin$ $\cos$ $\tan$
I $+$ $+$ $+$
II $+$ $-$ $-$
III $-$ $-$ $+$
IV $-$ $+$ $-$

Reference Angles

A reference angle gives the acute angular distance from the terminal side to the $x$-axis.

It determines the magnitude of many trigonometric values, while the quadrant determines their signs.

Pythagorean Identity

On the unit circle:

$$ x^2+y^2=1. $$

Since:

$$ x=\cos\theta, \qquad y=\sin\theta, $$

we obtain:

$$ \boxed{ \sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1 }. $$

Quotient Identities

$$ \boxed{ \tan\theta = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \cot\theta = \frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta} }. $$

Reciprocal Identities

$$ \boxed{ \csc\theta = \frac1{\sin\theta} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \sec\theta = \frac1{\cos\theta} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \cot\theta = \frac1{\tan\theta} } $$

wherever the relevant functions are defined.

Additional Pythagorean Identities

$$ \boxed{ 1+\tan^2\theta = \sec^2\theta } $$
$$ \boxed{ 1+\cot^2\theta = \csc^2\theta }. $$

Even and Odd Circular Functions

Reflection across the $x$-axis gives:

$$ \boxed{ \cos(-\theta)=\cos\theta } $$

so cosine is even.

Meanwhile:

$$ \boxed{ \sin(-\theta)=-\sin\theta } $$
$$ \boxed{ \tan(-\theta)=-\tan\theta }. $$

Thus sine and tangent are odd.

Secant, Cosecant, and Cotangent Symmetry

$$ \boxed{ \sec(-\theta)=\sec\theta } $$
$$ \boxed{ \csc(-\theta)=-\csc\theta } $$
$$ \boxed{ \cot(-\theta)=-\cot\theta }. $$

Radian Measure

Circular functions are especially natural when angles are measured in radians.

If $s$ is arc length on a circle of radius $r$:

$$ \boxed{ \theta=\frac{s}{r} }. $$

On the unit circle:

$$ \boxed{ s=\theta }. $$

Sine Graph from Circular Motion

As a point moves uniformly around the unit circle, its vertical coordinate traces:

$$ \boxed{ y=\sin\theta }. $$

Cosine Graph from Circular Motion

The horizontal coordinate traces:

$$ \boxed{ y=\cos\theta }. $$

after the horizontal coordinate is plotted against the changing angle.

Amplitude

The basic sine and cosine functions have amplitude:

$$ \boxed{ 1 }. $$

Period

For the basic sine and cosine functions:

$$ \boxed{ T=2\pi }. $$

Transformed Circular Functions

A sinusoidal function such as:

$$ y=A\sin(Bx+C)+D $$

has amplitude:

$$ \boxed{ |A| } $$

and period:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{2\pi}{|B|} }. $$

Phase and Vertical Translation

In:

$$ y=A\sin(Bx+C)+D, $$

$D$ translates the midline vertically, while the combination of $B$ and $C$ controls horizontal placement.

Rotational Interpretation

Rotating the vector $(1,0)$ by angle $\theta$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta\\ \sin\theta \end{bmatrix} }. $$

Circular functions are therefore built directly into planar rotation.

Complex Exponential Connection

$$ \boxed{ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta }. $$

Euler's formula links circular functions with complex exponentials and rotations in the complex plane.

Periodic Phenomena

Because circular functions repeat, they naturally model processes with repeating behavior, including:

  • rotation,
  • oscillation,
  • waves,
  • seasonal patterns,
  • alternating signals,
  • harmonic motion.

Simple Harmonic Motion

A typical model is:

$$ \boxed{ x(t)=A\cos(\omega t+\phi) }. $$

Circular motion projected onto one coordinate axis produces sinusoidal motion.

Circular Functions versus Trigonometric Functions

In standard usage, the terms substantially overlap.

$$ \boxed{ \text{circular functions} \approx \text{trigonometric functions} }. $$

The phrase circular functions emphasizes their circle-based interpretation, while trigonometric functions is now the more general and more common term.

Inverse Circular Functions

Older terminology sometimes calls inverse trigonometric functions inverse circular functions.

Examples include:

$$ \arcsin x,\qquad \arccos x,\qquad \arctan x. $$

These return selected angle values corresponding to given trigonometric values.

Inverse Does Not Mean Reciprocal

The inverse function:

$$ \sin^{-1}x $$

when used to mean $\arcsin x$, is not the same as:

$$ \frac1{\sin x} = \csc x. $$

Common Misconception: Circular Functions Are Different from Trigonometric Functions

They are generally the same functions viewed from a circle-based perspective.

Common Misconception: Circular Functions Apply Only from 0° to 360°

The functions accept arbitrary real angles, with periodicity accounting for repeated revolutions.

Common Misconception: Sine and Cosine Are Triangle-Side Ratios Only

Their circle definitions extend them beyond acute right-triangle geometry and reveal their behavior as real-valued periodic functions.

Common Misconception: All Six Circular Functions Are Defined Everywhere

Tangent, secant, cotangent, and cosecant have excluded angles caused by division by zero.

Common Misconception: Period Means the Function Is Constant

Periodicity means the function repeats after a fixed horizontal interval, not that it has the same value at every input.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$P=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ "The unit-circle point corresponding to theta has coordinates cosine theta, sine theta."
$\sin(\theta+2\pi)=\sin\theta$ "Sine has period two pi."
$\tan(\theta+\pi)=\tan\theta$ "Tangent has period pi."
$\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1$ "Sine squared theta plus cosine squared theta equals one."
$e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$ "e to the i theta equals cosine theta plus i sine theta."

The phrase circular functions is often encountered in older textbooks or treatments emphasizing the unit-circle origin of trigonometry. Modern texts more commonly use trigonometric functions.

Defense Notation Advice: Treat circular functions as functions of an angle or real variable, not merely as triangle ratios. State angle units when numerical values are involved, remember the domain exclusions of reciprocal and quotient functions, and distinguish inverse functions such as $\arcsin$ from reciprocals such as $\csc$.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: Circular functions are the trigonometric functions viewed through their definitions on a circle.
  • Primary Functions: $\sin$, $\cos$, and $\tan$.
  • Reciprocal Functions: $\csc$, $\sec$, and $\cot$.
  • Unit Circle: $$ \boxed{ P=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta) }. $$
  • Sine: Vertical coordinate on the unit circle.
  • Cosine: Horizontal coordinate on the unit circle.
  • Tangent: $$ \boxed{ \tan\theta = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} }. $$
  • Pythagorean Identity: $$ \boxed{ \sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1 }. $$
  • Sine and Cosine Period: $$ \boxed{ 2\pi }. $$
  • Tangent and Cotangent Period: $$ \boxed{ \pi }. $$
  • Sine and Cosine Range: $$ \boxed{ [-1,1] }. $$
  • Tangent Range: $\mathbb R$.
  • General-Angle Use: Circular functions extend right-triangle trigonometry to arbitrary real angles.
  • Radians: Circular functions are especially natural with radian angle measure.
  • Rotation: Sine and cosine are coordinate components of planar rotation.
  • Complex Numbers: $$ \boxed{ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta }. $$
  • Modeling: Circular functions describe periodic and oscillatory phenomena.
  • Modern Terminology: Trigonometric functions is more common than circular functions, though the meanings substantially overlap.
  • Common Error: Do not restrict circular functions to acute angles.
  • Common Error: Do not assume all six functions are defined for every real angle.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse inverse circular functions with reciprocal functions.
  • Terminological Contrast: Circular functions emphasize circle-based definitions of sine, cosine, tangent, secant, cosecant, and cotangent. Trigonometric functions is the modern general term for the same function family. Unit-circle definitions use radius $1$ so cosine and sine become the point coordinates directly. Right-triangle definitions describe the same functions through side ratios for acute angles. Inverse circular functions return selected angles from trigonometric values. Periodic function repeats its values after a fixed input interval.
