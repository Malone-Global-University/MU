Approximation by Differentials

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

Approximation by differentials is a calculus technique that uses the derivative of a function to estimate how much the function changes when its input changes by a small amount.

If:

$$ y=f(x), $$

and $x$ changes by a small amount $dx$, the corresponding differential in $y$ is defined by:

$$ \boxed{ dy=f'(x)\,dx }. $$

The actual change in the function is:

$$ \boxed{ \Delta y = f(x+\Delta x)-f(x) }. $$

When the input change is small and we identify:

$$ dx=\Delta x, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \Delta y\approx dy }. $$

Thus approximation by differentials replaces a small curved change in the function with the corresponding change predicted by its tangent line.

Operational Nuance

Actual Change vs. Differential

Suppose:

$$ y=f(x). $$

If $x$ changes from $x$ to $x+\Delta x$, the exact change in $y$ is:

$$ \boxed{ \Delta y=f(x+\Delta x)-f(x) }. $$

The differential predicts:

$$ \boxed{ dy=f'(x)\,dx }. $$

Setting:

$$ dx=\Delta x $$

gives the approximation:

$$ \boxed{ f(x+\Delta x)-f(x) \approx f'(x)\Delta x }. $$

Linear Approximation

The same principle can be written as:

$$ \boxed{ f(x+\Delta x) \approx f(x)+f'(x)\Delta x }. $$

More generally, near $x=a$:

$$ \boxed{ f(x) \approx f(a)+f'(a)(x-a) }. $$

This is the linearization of $f$ at $x=a$.

Tangent-Line Interpretation

The graph of:

$$ y=f(x) $$

is approximated near $x=a$ by its tangent line:

$$ \boxed{ L(x)=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a) }. $$

Approximation by differentials and tangent-line approximation are therefore two expressions of the same first-order idea.

Why the Approximation Works

Differentiability means that near $x$:

$$ \Delta y = f'(x)\Delta x + \text{smaller-order error}. $$

More precisely:

$$ \boxed{ \Delta y = f'(x)\Delta x + o(\Delta x) } $$

as $\Delta x\to0$.

The differential:

$$ dy=f'(x)\Delta x $$

captures the dominant first-order part of the change.

Example: Square Root

Estimate:

$$ \sqrt{4.1}. $$

Let:

$$ f(x)=\sqrt{x}. $$

Choose:

$$ x=4, \qquad dx=0.1. $$

Since:

$$ f'(x)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}, $$

we have:

$$ dy = \frac{1}{2\sqrt4}(0.1) = \frac14(0.1) = 0.025. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt{4.1} \approx 2+0.025 = 2.025 }. $$

Example: Cube

Let:

$$ y=x^3. $$

If:

$$ x=5, \qquad dx=0.02, $$

then:

$$ dy = 3x^2\,dx = 3(25)(0.02) = \boxed{1.5}. $$

Thus increasing $x$ from $5$ to $5.02$ is predicted to increase $x^3$ by approximately $1.5$.

Error in the Approximation

The difference between the actual change and the differential estimate is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{error} = \Delta y-dy }. $$

For sufficiently smooth functions, Taylor's Theorem shows that this error is typically of second order in the input change:

$$ \Delta y = f'(x)\Delta x + \frac12f''(\xi)(\Delta x)^2 $$

for an appropriate $\xi$ between the initial and final input values.

Thus, when $\Delta x$ becomes very small, the quadratic error usually decreases much faster than the first-order change.

Concavity and Overestimation

If:

$$ f''(x)>0, $$

the graph is concave upward, so the tangent line typically lies below the graph nearby. The linear approximation therefore tends to underestimate the function.

If:

$$ f''(x)<0, $$

the graph is concave downward, and the tangent line typically lies above the graph nearby, producing an overestimate.

Measurement Error

Differentials are especially useful for estimating how measurement error propagates through a formula.

If:

$$ y=f(x), $$

then a small input uncertainty $dx$ produces approximate output uncertainty:

$$ \boxed{ dy\approx f'(x)\,dx }. $$

Absolute Error

If $dx$ represents a small measurement error in $x$, then:

$$ \boxed{ |dy| \approx \text{absolute error in }y }. $$

Relative Error

Relative error compares an error with the size of the quantity:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{|dy|}{|y|} }. $$

Percentage Error

The approximate percentage error is:

$$ \boxed{ 100\% \frac{|dy|}{|y|} }. $$

Example: Area of a Circle

Let:

$$ A=\pi r^2. $$

Differentiating gives:

$$ \boxed{ dA=2\pi r\,dr }. $$

If the radius has a small measurement error $dr$, then $dA$ estimates the resulting error in calculated area.

Relative Error for Circle Area

Dividing by:

$$ A=\pi r^2 $$

gives:

$$ \frac{dA}{A} = \frac{2\pi r\,dr}{\pi r^2} = \boxed{ 2\frac{dr}{r} }. $$

So a small relative error in radius produces approximately twice that relative error in area.

Example: Volume of a Sphere

For:

$$ V=\frac43\pi r^3, $$

the differential is:

$$ \boxed{ dV=4\pi r^2\,dr }. $$

Dividing by $V$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dV}{V} = 3\frac{dr}{r} }. $$

Thus a small relative radius error produces approximately three times that relative error in volume.

Multiple Variables

For a differentiable function of several variables:

$$ z=f(x,y), $$

the total differential is:

$$ \boxed{ dz = f_x\,dx + f_y\,dy }. $$

For small input changes:

$$ \boxed{ \Delta z\approx dz }. $$

Higher Dimensions

More generally, for:

$$ f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}, $$

the first-order differential can be written:

$$ \boxed{ df = \nabla f\cdot d\mathbf{x} }. $$

This is the multivariable form of linear approximation.

Common Misconception: The notation $dy=f'(x)\,dx$ does not mean that $dy$ is always exactly equal to the actual finite change $\Delta y$. The equality defines the differential; the approximation is:

$$ \boxed{ \Delta y\approx dy }. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$dy=f'(x)\,dx$ "D y equals f prime of x times d x."
$\Delta y=f(x+\Delta x)-f(x)$ "Delta y is the actual change in the function."
$\Delta y\approx dy$ "Delta y is approximately equal to d y."
$f(x+\Delta x)\approx f(x)+f'(x)\Delta x$ "The new function value is approximately the old value plus the derivative times the input change."
$L(x)=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)$ "L of x is the linearization of f at a."
$\frac{|dy|}{|y|}$ "The approximate relative error in y."

The symbols $\Delta x$ and $dx$ are often set equal during elementary differential approximation:

$$ dx=\Delta x. $$

But the corresponding output quantities play different roles:

$$ \boxed{ \Delta y = \text{actual change}, \qquad dy = \text{linearized change} }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Use the equality sign in:

$$ dy=f'(x)\,dx, $$

because this defines the differential. Use the approximation sign in:

$$ \Delta y\approx dy, $$

because the actual finite change generally differs from the tangent-line estimate.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Principle: Approximation by differentials uses the derivative to estimate a small change in a function.
  • Differential: $$ \boxed{ dy=f'(x)\,dx }. $$
  • Actual Change: $$ \boxed{ \Delta y = f(x+\Delta x)-f(x) }. $$
  • Input Identification: In elementary applications: $$ \boxed{ dx=\Delta x }. $$
  • Approximation: $$ \boxed{ \Delta y\approx dy }. $$
  • Function-Value Approximation: $$ \boxed{ f(x+\Delta x) \approx f(x)+f'(x)\Delta x }. $$
  • Linearization: Near $x=a$: $$ \boxed{ L(x)=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a) }. $$
  • Tangent-Line Interpretation: The tangent line approximates the graph locally.
  • First-Order Approximation: Differentials retain the part of the change proportional to $\Delta x$.
  • Higher-Order Error: The omitted error is typically quadratic or smaller relative to the first-order term for sufficiently smooth functions.
  • Taylor Connection: $$ f(x+\Delta x) = f(x) + f'(x)\Delta x + \frac12f''(x)(\Delta x)^2 +\cdots. $$
  • Small-Change Requirement: The approximation is generally more accurate when $|\Delta x|$ is small.
  • Derivative Requirement: The method requires differentiability at or near the point of approximation.
  • Concave Up: A tangent-line estimate commonly underestimates a concave-up function locally.
  • Concave Down: A tangent-line estimate commonly overestimates a concave-down function locally.
  • Square Root Approximation: For: $$ f(x)=\sqrt{x}, $$ $$ \boxed{ dy=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\,dx }. $$
  • Power Function: For: $$ y=x^n, $$ $$ \boxed{ dy=nx^{n-1}\,dx }. $$
  • Reciprocal Function: For: $$ y=\frac1x, $$ $$ \boxed{ dy=-\frac{1}{x^2}\,dx }. $$
  • Exponential Function: For: $$ y=e^x, $$ $$ \boxed{ dy=e^x\,dx }. $$
  • Logarithm: For: $$ y=\ln x, $$ $$ \boxed{ dy=\frac{dx}{x} }. $$
  • Sine: $$ \boxed{ d(\sin x)=\cos x\,dx }. $$
  • Cosine: $$ \boxed{ d(\cos x)=-\sin x\,dx }. $$
  • Measurement Error: Small measurement uncertainty in an input can be propagated approximately through the derivative.
  • Absolute Error: $$ \boxed{ |dy| } $$ often estimates the absolute error in the output.
  • Relative Error: $$ \boxed{ \frac{|dy|}{|y|} }. $$
  • Percentage Error: $$ \boxed{ 100\%\frac{|dy|}{|y|} }. $$
  • Circle Area: If: $$ A=\pi r^2, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ dA=2\pi r\,dr }. $$
  • Circle-Area Relative Error: $$ \boxed{ \frac{dA}{A} = 2\frac{dr}{r} }. $$
  • Sphere Volume: If: $$ V=\frac43\pi r^3, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ dV=4\pi r^2\,dr }. $$
  • Sphere-Volume Relative Error: $$ \boxed{ \frac{dV}{V} = 3\frac{dr}{r} }. $$
  • Product Error Approximation: If: $$ z=xy, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ dz=y\,dx+x\,dy }. $$
  • Product Relative Differential: When $x,y,z\ne0$: $$ \boxed{ \frac{dz}{z} = \frac{dx}{x} + \frac{dy}{y} }. $$
  • Quotient Relative Differential: If: $$ z=\frac{x}{y}, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ \frac{dz}{z} = \frac{dx}{x} - \frac{dy}{y} }. $$
  • Power Relative Differential: If: $$ y=x^n, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ \frac{dy}{y} = n\frac{dx}{x} }. $$
  • Multivariable Differential: For: $$ z=f(x,y), $$ $$ \boxed{ dz=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy }. $$
  • Multivariable Approximation: $$ \boxed{ \Delta z\approx dz }. $$
  • Gradient Form: $$ \boxed{ df=\nabla f\cdot d\mathbf{x} }. $$
  • Total Differential: The multivariable differential is the linear part of the total change in the function.
  • Engineering Use: Differentials are useful for tolerance estimates and sensitivity calculations.
  • Scientific Measurement: They provide a first-order estimate of how uncertainty in measurements affects a calculated result.
  • Numerical Use: Differential approximations can provide fast mental or hand estimates near convenient reference values.
  • Local Nature: The method is fundamentally local; accuracy generally worsens as one moves farther from the point of linearization.
  • Not an Exact Identity: $$ \Delta y=dy $$ is generally false for a finite nonzero change.
  • Exact for Linear Functions: If: $$ f(x)=mx+b, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ \Delta y=dy=m\Delta x } $$ exactly.
  • Derivative Contrast: The derivative $f'(x)$ is a rate of change; the differential $dy$ is the derivative multiplied by an input differential $dx$.
  • Linearization Contrast: Linearization approximates the new function value, while the differential approximates the change in the function value.
  • Terminological Contrast: $\Delta y$ is the actual finite change in output. $dy$ is the first-order differential change predicted by the derivative. Linearization approximates the function itself by its tangent line. Approximation by differentials uses these ideas to estimate values, changes, and propagated errors.
