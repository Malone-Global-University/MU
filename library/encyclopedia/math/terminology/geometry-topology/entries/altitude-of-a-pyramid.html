Altitude of a Pyramid

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

The altitude of a pyramid is the perpendicular segment, or the length of that segment, from the pyramid's apex to the plane containing its base.

If $A$ is the apex and $H$ is the foot of the perpendicular on the base plane, then:

$$ \boxed{ AH\perp \text{base plane} }. $$

The altitude is commonly denoted by:

$$ \boxed{ h=AH }. $$

In elementary geometry, the words altitude and height are usually interchangeable when referring to this perpendicular distance.

The essential idea is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{altitude} = \text{perpendicular distance from apex to base plane} }. $$

It should not be confused with a lateral edge, a face altitude, or the slant height of a regular pyramid.

Operational Nuance

Apex and Base Plane

A pyramid consists of a polygonal base together with triangular lateral faces that meet at a common point called the apex.

The altitude begins at the apex and meets the plane of the base at a right angle.

$$ \boxed{ \text{apex} \longrightarrow \text{perpendicular} \longrightarrow \text{base plane} }. $$

Foot of the Altitude

The point where the altitude meets the base plane is the foot of the altitude.

The foot may lie inside the base polygon, on its boundary, or outside the polygon, depending on the shape and orientation of the pyramid.

Right and Regular Pyramids

In the familiar regular-pyramid setting, the base is a regular polygon and the apex lies directly above its center. The altitude therefore joins the apex to the center of the base.

$$ \boxed{ \text{regular pyramid} \Longrightarrow \text{altitude passes through base center} }. $$

Oblique Pyramid

In an oblique pyramid, the apex is displaced sideways relative to the base. The altitude remains perpendicular to the base plane but need not pass through the center of the base.

$$ \boxed{ \text{oblique pyramid} \Longrightarrow \text{altitude need not meet base center} }. $$

Altitude vs. Lateral Edge

A lateral edge joins the apex to a vertex of the base. It generally slopes away from the perpendicular direction.

$$ \boxed{ \text{lateral edge} \ne \text{altitude} } $$

in general.

Altitude vs. Slant Height

In a regular pyramid, the slant height is the altitude of a lateral triangular face, commonly drawn from the apex to the midpoint of a base edge.

If $\ell$ denotes slant height and $h$ denotes pyramid altitude, then:

$$ \boxed{ h\ne\ell } $$

for an ordinary nondegenerate regular pyramid.

Right-Triangle Relationship

In a regular pyramid, the altitude $h$, the base apothem $a$, and the slant height $\ell$ form a right triangle:

$$ \boxed{ \ell^2=h^2+a^2 }. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ h=\sqrt{\ell^2-a^2} }. $$

Lateral Edge Relationship

In a regular pyramid, let $R$ be the distance from the base center to a base vertex. If $e$ is a lateral edge, then:

$$ \boxed{ e^2=h^2+R^2 }. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ h=\sqrt{e^2-R^2} }. $$

Volume

The volume of any pyramid is:

$$ \boxed{ V=\frac13Bh }, $$

where $B$ is the area of the base and $h$ is the perpendicular altitude.

Altitude from Volume

Rearranging the volume formula gives:

$$ \boxed{ h=\frac{3V}{B} }. $$

Example

Suppose a pyramid has base area:

$$ B=48 $$

and volume:

$$ V=320. $$

Then:

$$ h = \frac{3(320)}{48} = \frac{960}{48} = \boxed{20}. $$

Square Pyramid

For a square pyramid with base side length $s$:

$$ B=s^2. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ V=\frac13s^2h }. $$

Regular Square-Pyramid Slant Height

In a regular square pyramid, the base apothem is:

$$ a=\frac{s}{2}. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \ell^2 = h^2+\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)^2 }. $$

Why Obliqueness Does Not Change the Volume Formula

The formula:

$$ V=\frac13Bh $$

applies to both right and oblique pyramids. What matters is the base area and perpendicular distance from the apex to the base plane.

Common Misconception: The altitude of a pyramid is not the sloping distance from the apex to a base edge or vertex. It is measured perpendicular to the entire base plane.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$h$ "The height," or "the altitude of the pyramid."
$AH\perp\text{ base plane}$ "A H is perpendicular to the base plane."
$V=\frac13Bh$ "Volume equals one-third base area times height."
$h=\frac{3V}{B}$ "Height equals three times the volume divided by the base area."
$\ell^2=h^2+a^2$ "Slant height squared equals height squared plus base apothem squared."
$e^2=h^2+R^2$ "The lateral edge squared equals the altitude squared plus the base circumradius squared."

The notation:

$$ h $$

normally refers to the perpendicular height of the entire pyramid.

By contrast:

$$ \ell $$

often denotes the slant height of a regular pyramid, while a symbol such as:

$$ e $$

may denote a lateral edge.

Defense Notation Advice: When a pyramid diagram contains several segments from the apex, identify the one carrying a right-angle relationship with the base plane. That segment represents the altitude.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: The altitude of a pyramid is the perpendicular distance from its apex to the plane containing its base.
  • Common Symbol: $$ \boxed{h}. $$
  • Perpendicular Requirement: $$ \boxed{ \text{altitude}\perp\text{base plane} }. $$
  • Foot of Altitude: The point where the perpendicular from the apex meets the base plane is the foot of the altitude.
  • Regular Pyramid: The altitude passes through the center of the regular polygonal base.
  • Oblique Pyramid: The altitude need not meet the center of the base.
  • Exterior Foot: For a sufficiently oblique pyramid, the foot of the altitude may lie outside the base polygon while remaining in the base plane.
  • Shortest Distance: The altitude is the shortest distance from the apex to the base plane.
  • Lateral Edge: A lateral edge joins the apex to a base vertex and is generally not perpendicular to the base.
  • Slant Height: In a regular pyramid, slant height is the altitude of a lateral triangular face.
  • Altitude vs. Slant Height: $$ \boxed{ h\ne\ell } $$ in a nondegenerate regular pyramid.
  • Regular-Pyramid Relation: If $a$ is the apothem of the base: $$ \boxed{ \ell^2=h^2+a^2 }. $$
  • Altitude from Slant Height: $$ \boxed{ h=\sqrt{\ell^2-a^2} }. $$
  • Lateral Edge Relation: If $R$ is the base circumradius: $$ \boxed{ e^2=h^2+R^2 }. $$
  • Altitude from Lateral Edge: $$ \boxed{ h=\sqrt{e^2-R^2} }. $$
  • General Volume Formula: $$ \boxed{ V=\frac13Bh }. $$
  • Altitude from Volume: $$ \boxed{ h=\frac{3V}{B} }. $$
  • Base Area from Volume: $$ \boxed{ B=\frac{3V}{h} }. $$
  • Square Pyramid: For base side length $s$: $$ \boxed{ V=\frac13s^2h }. $$
  • Rectangular Pyramid: For base dimensions $l$ and $w$: $$ \boxed{ V=\frac13lwh }. $$
  • Triangular Pyramid: If the triangular base has area $B_{\triangle}$: $$ \boxed{ V=\frac13B_{\triangle}h }. $$
  • Tetrahedron: A tetrahedron is a triangular pyramid, so any face may be chosen as the base and the corresponding altitude is measured from the opposite vertex to that face's plane.
  • Changing the Base: For a tetrahedron, choosing a different face as the base gives a different corresponding altitude while preserving the same volume.
  • Cavalieri Connection: Pyramids with equal base areas and equal perpendicular altitudes have equal volumes under the standard cross-sectional comparison.
  • One-Third Factor: A pyramid with base area $B$ and altitude $h$ has one-third the volume of a prism with the same base area and altitude: $$ \boxed{ V_{\text{pyramid}} = \frac13V_{\text{prism}} }. $$
  • Surface Area Caution: The familiar lateral-area formula for a regular pyramid uses slant height rather than vertical altitude.
  • Regular Pyramid Lateral Area: If $P$ is the perimeter of the base and $\ell$ the slant height: $$ \boxed{ L=\frac12P\ell }. $$
  • Total Surface Area: For a regular pyramid: $$ \boxed{ S=B+\frac12P\ell }. $$
  • Surface Area vs. Volume: Volume uses the perpendicular altitude $h$, whereas regular-pyramid lateral surface area uses the slant height $\ell$.
  • Coordinate Geometry: If the base lies in: $$ z=0 $$ and the apex is: $$ (a,b,c), $$ then: $$ \boxed{ h=|c| }. $$
  • General Point-to-Plane Distance: If the base plane is: $$ Ax+By+Cz+D=0 $$ and the apex is: $$ (x_0,y_0,z_0), $$ then: $$ \boxed{ h= \frac{|Ax_0+By_0+Cz_0+D|} {\sqrt{A^2+B^2+C^2}} }. $$
  • Vector Geometry: The altitude direction is parallel to a normal vector of the base plane.
  • Tetrahedron Vector Volume: For edge vectors $\mathbf{a}$, $\mathbf{b}$, and $\mathbf{c}$ from one vertex: $$ \boxed{ V= \frac16 |\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c})| }. $$
  • Altitude from Vector Volume: If a triangular base has area $B$: $$ \boxed{ h=\frac{3V}{B} }. $$
  • Scaling: Under dilation by factor $k$: $$ h\mapsto kh. $$
  • Base-Area Scaling: $$ B\mapsto k^2B. $$
  • Volume Scaling: $$ \boxed{ V\mapsto k^3V }. $$
  • Similar Pyramids: Corresponding altitudes, lateral edges, slant heights, and base dimensions scale by the same linear factor.
  • Volume Ratio: If similar pyramids have linear scale factor $k$: $$ \boxed{ \frac{V_2}{V_1}=k^3 }. $$
  • Frustum Connection: Cutting a pyramid with a plane parallel to its base creates a smaller similar pyramid and a frustum. Altitude ratios can then be used to determine the corresponding linear scale factor.
  • Units: Altitude is measured in linear units such as centimeters, meters, or feet.
  • Orientation Independence: A pyramid need not be drawn upright. Its altitude is determined by perpendicularity to the base plane, not visual direction.
  • Diagram Caution: A segment drawn from the apex into the base is not automatically an altitude unless it is perpendicular to the base plane.
  • Terminological Contrast: The altitude is the perpendicular distance from the apex to the base plane. A lateral edge joins the apex to a base vertex. In a regular pyramid, the slant height is the altitude of a lateral face. These are distinct measurements and serve different roles in volume and surface-area formulas.
