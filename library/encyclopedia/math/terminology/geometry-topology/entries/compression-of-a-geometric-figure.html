Compression of a Geometric Figure

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

A compression of a geometric figure is a transformation that reduces the figure's extent in one or more directions by multiplying selected distances or coordinates by scale factors whose magnitudes are less than $1$.

For example, the coordinate transformation:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto\left(\frac12x,y\right) } $$

compresses a plane figure horizontally toward the $y$-axis. Horizontal distances from the $y$-axis are halved while vertical coordinates remain unchanged.

The word compression comes from the ordinary idea of pressing something into a smaller extent. In geometry, however, the term does not imply a physical force. It describes a mathematical transformation of position and scale.

$$ \boxed{ \text{geometric compression} = \text{directional reduction of scale} }. $$

The terminology is often used informally for nonuniform scaling. When every direction is scaled by the same positive factor, the more precise geometric term is usually dilation, even when the scale factor is less than $1$ and the figure becomes smaller.

Operational Nuance

Horizontal Compression

A horizontal compression toward the $y$-axis can be written:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(kx,y), \qquad 0

Every horizontal coordinate is multiplied by $k$.

Example: Horizontal Compression

Let a triangle have vertices:

$$ A=(2,1),\qquad B=(6,1),\qquad C=(4,5). $$

Compress horizontally by factor $1/2$:

$$ (x,y)\mapsto\left(\frac{x}{2},y\right). $$

The image vertices are:

$$ \boxed{ A'=(1,1),\qquad B'=(3,1),\qquad C'=(2,5) }. $$

Horizontal separations are halved while vertical separations are preserved.

Vertical Compression

A vertical compression toward the $x$-axis is:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(x,ky), \qquad 0

Every vertical distance from the $x$-axis is multiplied by $k$.

Example: Vertical Compression

Applying:

$$ (x,y)\mapsto\left(x,\frac13y\right) $$

to the point:

$$ P=(6,9) $$

gives:

$$ \boxed{ P'=(6,3) }. $$

Compression in Both Coordinate Directions

A figure may be compressed both horizontally and vertically:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(ax,by), \qquad 0

If $a\ne b$, the scaling is nonuniform.

Uniform Compression

If:

$$ a=b=k, \qquad 0

then:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(kx,ky) }. $$

Every distance from the origin is multiplied by the same factor $k$.

This is more precisely a dilation centered at the origin with scale factor $k$.

Compression about a Point Other Than the Origin

Suppose the center is:

$$ C=(h,k). $$

Uniform compression toward $C$ by factor $s$, with $0

$$ \boxed{ (x,y) \mapsto \left( h+s(x-h), k+s(y-k) \right) }. $$

The displacement from the center is scaled:

$$ \boxed{ \overrightarrow{CP'} = s\,\overrightarrow{CP} }. $$

Horizontal Compression toward a Vertical Line

Compression need not be toward the $y$-axis. To compress horizontally toward:

$$ x=h, $$

use:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y) \mapsto \bigl(h+s(x-h),y\bigr), \qquad 0

The horizontal distance from $x=h$ is multiplied by $s$.

Vertical Compression toward a Horizontal Line

To compress vertically toward:

$$ y=k, $$

use:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y) \mapsto \bigl(x,k+s(y-k)\bigr), \qquad 0

Matrix Representation

Compression relative to the coordinate axes can be represented by:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{pmatrix} x'\\ y' \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a&0\\ 0&b \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x\\ y \end{pmatrix} }. $$

Thus:

$$ x'=ax, \qquad y'=by. $$

Values of $|a|<1$ or $|b|<1$ indicate compression in the corresponding coordinate direction.

Negative Scale Factors

A negative factor combines compression with reflection.

For example:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y) \mapsto \left(-\frac12x,y\right) } $$

compresses horizontally by factor $1/2$ and reflects across the $y$-axis.

Compression Is Usually Not a Rigid Motion

A rigid motion preserves all distances. Compression usually does not.

$$ \boxed{ \text{compression} \notin \{\text{translations, rotations, reflections}\} } $$

unless the alleged compression has scale magnitude $1$, in which case no actual compression occurs.

Effect on Length

Under uniform compression by factor $k$:

$$ \boxed{ L'=kL }. $$

Every segment length is multiplied by $k$.

Effect on Perimeter

For a uniform scale factor $k$:

$$ \boxed{ P'=kP }. $$

Effect on Area

Uniform compression by factor $k$ in the plane scales area by:

$$ \boxed{ A'=k^2A }. $$

Thus a compression by factor $1/2$ reduces area to:

$$ \boxed{ \frac14 } $$

of the original.

Nonuniform Area Scaling

Under:

$$ (x,y)\mapsto(ax,by), $$

area is multiplied by:

$$ \boxed{ |ab| }. $$

This is the absolute value of the determinant:

$$ \det \begin{pmatrix} a&0\\ 0&b \end{pmatrix} =ab. $$

Example: Area under Directional Compression

Suppose a rectangle has area $24$ and is compressed horizontally by $1/2$ while its vertical dimension is unchanged.

Then:

$$ \boxed{ A' = \frac12(1)(24) = 12 }. $$

Effect on Volume

In three dimensions, uniform compression by factor $k$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ V'=k^3V }. $$

More generally:

$$ (x,y,z)\mapsto(ax,by,cz) $$

scales volume by:

$$ \boxed{ |abc| }. $$

Uniform Compression Preserves Angles

A uniform positive dilation:

$$ (x,y)\mapsto(kx,ky) $$

preserves angles and shape.

The image figure is similar to the original:

$$ \boxed{ F'\sim F }. $$

Nonuniform Compression May Distort Angles

If horizontal and vertical scale factors differ:

$$ (x,y)\mapsto(ax,by), \qquad a\ne b, $$

angles generally are not preserved.

$$ \boxed{ a\ne b \Rightarrow \text{shape may be distorted} }. $$

A Circle May Become an Ellipse

Consider the unit circle:

$$ x^2+y^2=1. $$

Compress horizontally by factor $a$, where $0

$$ \boxed{ \frac{x'^2}{a^2}+y'^2=1 }. $$

Thus the circle becomes an ellipse.

A Rectangle under Compression

A rectangle whose sides are parallel to the coordinate axes remains a rectangle under axis-aligned compression, although its side lengths change.

If width is $w$ and height is $h$, then:

$$ \boxed{ w'=aw, \qquad h'=bh }. $$

A Square under Uniform Compression

Uniform compression preserves the square's shape:

$$ \boxed{ s'=ks }. $$

Its image is another square.

A Square under Nonuniform Compression

If horizontal and vertical scale factors differ, an axis-aligned square generally becomes a rectangle:

$$ \boxed{ s\times s \mapsto as\times bs }. $$

Similarity and Compression

Uniform compression produces similar figures because all corresponding lengths have the same ratio:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{L'}{L}=k }. $$

Nonuniform compression generally does not preserve similarity.

Compression as a Linear Transformation

Axis-centered compression of the form:

$$ T(x,y)=(ax,by) $$

is linear because:

$$ \boxed{ T(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}) = T(\mathbf{u})+T(\mathbf{v}) } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ T(c\mathbf{u}) = cT(\mathbf{u}) }. $$

Compression about a Non-Origin Center Is Affine

A compression or dilation centered away from the origin generally includes a translation component and is therefore affine rather than linear.

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x}' = \mathbf{c} + A(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{c}) }. $$

Inverse of a Nonzero Compression

If no scale factor is zero, the transformation is invertible.

For:

$$ T(x,y)=(ax,by), \qquad a,b\ne0, $$

the inverse is:

$$ \boxed{ T^{-1}(x,y) = \left( \frac{x}{a}, \frac{y}{b} \right) }. $$

The inverse stretches the compressed directions by reciprocal factors.

Zero Scale Factor Collapses Dimension

If:

$$ (x,y)\mapsto(0,y), $$

every point is sent to the $y$-axis.

This is not merely an ordinary reversible compression. It collapses an entire coordinate dimension.

$$ \boxed{ \det \begin{pmatrix} 0&0\\ 0&1 \end{pmatrix} =0 } $$

and the transformation is not invertible.

Compression versus Dilation

In elementary geometry, dilation is the standard transformation that multiplies every distance from a center by one common scale factor.

the dilation reduces the figure and may informally be called a compression.

Compression versus Reduction

Reduction often describes the smaller image produced by a dilation with scale factor less than $1$. Compression emphasizes the transformation process, especially when the reduction is directional or nonuniform.

Compression versus Contraction

A contraction has a precise metric-space meaning:

$$ \boxed{ d(TP,TQ) \le c\,d(P,Q), \qquad 0\le c<1 }. $$

A uniform geometric compression with factor $0

$$ (x,y)\mapsto\left(\frac12x,y\right) $$

is not a strict contraction of the whole Euclidean plane, because vertical distances can remain unchanged.

Compression versus Projection

A projection may collapse a dimension entirely, such as:

$$ (x,y)\mapsto(x,0). $$

Ordinary compression with nonzero scale factors reduces distances without fully eliminating the corresponding dimension.

Compression versus Shear

A shear displaces points by an amount depending on another coordinate:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(x+ky,y) }. $$

Compression instead primarily changes scale. The two transformations have different geometric effects.

Compression versus Translation

Translation:

$$ (x,y)\mapsto(x+h,y+k) $$

changes location but preserves size and shape.

Compression changes one or more dimensions of the figure.

Compression versus Reflection

Reflection preserves distance but reverses orientation relative to a line or plane. Compression changes scale. A negative scale factor can combine both effects.

Compression versus Rotation

A rotation preserves lengths and angles. Compression generally changes lengths and, under nonuniform scaling, may also change angles.

Composition with Other Transformations

Compression may be combined with translation, rotation, reflection, or shear.

For example:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x}' = R \begin{pmatrix} a&0\\ 0&b \end{pmatrix} \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{t} } $$

can represent compression followed by rotation and translation.

Order of Transformations Matters

Nonuniform compression and rotation generally do not commute:

$$ \boxed{ RS\ne SR \quad\text{in general} }. $$

Compressing along the coordinate axes and then rotating is generally different from rotating first and then compressing along the original coordinate axes.

Compression along Arbitrary Directions

A geometric figure can be compressed along directions not aligned with the coordinate axes. Such transformations can be represented by changing to a suitable basis, scaling in that basis, and transforming back.

$$ \boxed{ T = P \begin{pmatrix} a&0\\ 0&b \end{pmatrix} P^{-1} }. $$

Here the columns of $P$ may specify the directions along which the scaling is performed.

Singular Values and Directional Compression

In more advanced linear algebra, singular values describe how a linear transformation scales lengths along special orthogonal directions. Singular values less than $1$ correspond to compression in those directions.

$$ \boxed{ 0<\sigma_i<1 \Rightarrow \text{compression along the associated singular direction} }. $$

Common Misconception: Compression Always Preserves Shape

Only uniform scaling preserves all angles and produces a similar figure in the usual Euclidean sense.

Directional compression may distort the figure.

Common Misconception: Halving Length Halves Area

Under uniform compression by $1/2$, area becomes:

$$ \boxed{ \left(\frac12\right)^2 = \frac14 } $$

of the original, not one-half.

Common Misconception: Halving Length Halves Volume

Under uniform three-dimensional compression by $1/2$:

$$ \boxed{ \left(\frac12\right)^3 = \frac18 }. $$

Common Misconception: Compression Means Moving the Figure

Compression changes scale. Translation changes position. A transformation can do both, but the terms describe different effects.

Common Misconception: Every Smaller Figure Is a Compression

A figure may appear smaller because of projection, cropping, perspective, or another transformation. Mathematically, compression should refer to a specified scaling or distance-reducing transformation.

Common Misconception: A Scale Factor of 2 Is Compression

$(x,y)\mapsto(\frac12x,y)$ "Compress horizontally by a factor of one-half."
$(x,y)\mapsto(x,\frac13y)$ "Compress vertically by a factor of one-third."
$(x,y)\mapsto(kx,ky)$ "Dilate about the origin by scale factor k."
$0 "k is a scale factor between zero and one."
$A'=k^2A$ "The image area equals k squared times the original area."
$V'=k^3V$ "The image volume equals k cubed times the original volume."
$(x,y)\mapsto(ax,by)$ "Scale the x-coordinate by a and the y-coordinate by b."

The phrase compressed by a factor of $k$ should identify the direction or center whenever the transformation is not uniform. Saying merely that a figure is "compressed by one-half" may be ambiguous: it could mean all distances are halved, only horizontal distances are halved, or only vertical distances are halved.

In formal geometry, dilation with scale factor $k$ is preferable when all distances from a center are multiplied uniformly by the same factor. The word compression is especially useful when emphasizing directional reduction.

Defense Notation Advice: State the fixed center, axis, line, or coordinate directions relative to which compression occurs. Distinguish uniform dilation from nonuniform scaling. When using a matrix, interpret each scale factor geometrically and use the determinant to track area or volume scaling. If a scale factor is negative, identify the accompanying reflection separately. Do not describe a zero scale factor as an ordinary reversible compression, because it collapses a dimension.
