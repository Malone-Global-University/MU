Center of Rotation

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

The center of rotation is the fixed point around which a planar figure is rotated.

If a point $P$ rotates to an image point $P'$, and $C$ is the center of rotation, then:

$$ \boxed{ CP=CP' }. $$

The angle through which $P$ turns about $C$ is the same rotation angle applied to every point of the figure.

$$ \boxed{ \angle PCP'=\theta } $$

for the directed rotation angle $\theta$, interpreted according to the chosen orientation convention.

The word center refers to the fixed reference point of the turning motion, while rotation refers to the rigid turning transformation.

Operational Nuance

Rotation as a Transformation

A rotation of the plane is determined by:

  • a center of rotation $C$,
  • a rotation angle $\theta$,
  • a direction, usually counterclockwise for positive angles.
$$ \boxed{ R_{C,\theta} } $$

may be used to denote the rotation through angle $\theta$ about center $C$.

The Center Is Fixed

The defining point of the rotation does not move:

$$ \boxed{ R_{C,\theta}(C)=C }. $$

Distance from the Center Is Preserved

If:

$$ P'=R_{C,\theta}(P), $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ d(C,P)=d(C,P') }. $$

Thus $P$ and $P'$ lie on the same circle centered at $C$.

Same Angle for Every Point

Every point of the figure turns through the same directed angle:

$$ \boxed{ \angle PCP'=\theta }. $$

The radius $CP$ may differ from point to point, but the rotation angle is common to the entire figure.

Rigid Motion

A rotation is an isometry. It preserves distances between arbitrary points:

$$ \boxed{ d(P,Q)=d(P',Q') }. $$

Consequently, rotations preserve:

  • length,
  • angle measure,
  • area,
  • shape,
  • congruence.

Orientation Is Preserved

A rotation preserves orientation. A clockwise ordering of vertices remains clockwise after a rotation, and similarly for counterclockwise ordering.

Rotation about the Origin

When the center is the origin:

$$ C=(0,0), $$

a point $(x,y)$ rotated counterclockwise through angle $\theta$ becomes:

$$ \boxed{ x'=x\cos\theta-y\sin\theta } $$
$$ \boxed{ y'=x\sin\theta+y\cos\theta }. $$

Rotation Matrix

In matrix form:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} x'\\ y' \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x\\ y \end{bmatrix} }. $$

Rotation about an Arbitrary Center

Suppose the center is:

$$ C=(h,k). $$

To rotate a point $(x,y)$ about $C$, first translate the center to the origin:

$$ (x,y)\mapsto(x-h,y-k). $$

Apply the rotation, then translate back.

Coordinate Formula about (h,k)

$$ \boxed{ x' = h +(x-h)\cos\theta -(y-k)\sin\theta } $$
$$ \boxed{ y' = k +(x-h)\sin\theta +(y-k)\cos\theta }. $$

Translation-Rotation-Translation Structure

$$ \boxed{ \text{translate to origin} \rightarrow \text{rotate} \rightarrow \text{translate back} }. $$

90° Counterclockwise Rotation about the Origin

$$ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(-y,x) }. $$

180° Rotation about the Origin

$$ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(-x,-y) }. $$

270° Counterclockwise Rotation

$$ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(y,-x) }. $$

This is equivalent to a $90^\circ$ clockwise rotation.

360° Rotation

$$ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(x,y) }. $$

A full turn is the identity transformation.

Finding the Center from a Point and Its Image

If $P$ rotates to $P'$, then the center $C$ must satisfy:

$$ CP=CP'. $$

Therefore $C$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of segment $\overline{PP'}$.

$$ \boxed{ C \in \text{perpendicular bisector of }\overline{PP'} }. $$

Using Two Point-Image Pairs

Suppose:

$$ P\mapsto P' $$

and:

$$ Q\mapsto Q'. $$

For a nontrivial rotation, the center can be found by intersecting:

  • the perpendicular bisector of $\overline{PP'}$,
  • the perpendicular bisector of $\overline{QQ'}$.
$$ \boxed{ C = \operatorname{PB}(PP') \cap \operatorname{PB}(QQ') }. $$

Why the Perpendicular Bisector Works

Every point on a perpendicular bisector is equidistant from the endpoints of the corresponding segment.

Since the center must be equidistant from $P$ and $P'$, it must lie on that locus.

One Point Pair Is Usually Not Enough

Knowing only:

$$ P\mapsto P' $$

generally places the center somewhere on a perpendicular-bisector line rather than identifying one unique point.

Additional information, such as the angle of rotation or a second point-image pair, is usually required.

Finding the Rotation Angle

Once the center $C$ is known, the angle can be found from the directed angle:

$$ \boxed{ \theta=\angle PCP' }. $$

Central-Angle Interpretation

Because $P$ and $P'$ lie on a circle centered at $C$, the rotation angle is the central angle subtending the arc from $P$ to $P'$.

Clockwise and Counterclockwise Angles

A common convention takes counterclockwise angles as positive and clockwise angles as negative:

$$ \boxed{ \theta>0 \Rightarrow \text{counterclockwise} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \theta<0 \Rightarrow \text{clockwise} }. $$

Equivalent Rotation Angles

Rotation angles differing by whole turns produce the same transformation:

$$ \boxed{ \theta \sim \theta+2\pi k, \qquad k\in\mathbb Z }. $$

In degrees:

$$ \boxed{ \theta \sim \theta+360^\circ k }. $$

Composition of Rotations with the Same Center

Rotations about the same center add their angles:

$$ \boxed{ R_{C,\alpha} \circ R_{C,\beta} = R_{C,\alpha+\beta} }. $$

Inverse Rotation

The inverse of rotation by $\theta$ about $C$ is rotation by $-\theta$ about the same center:

$$ \boxed{ R_{C,\theta}^{-1} = R_{C,-\theta} }. $$

Fixed Points of a Nontrivial Plane Rotation

For a rotation through an angle not congruent to $0$ modulo $2\pi$, the center is the unique fixed point in the plane.

$$ \boxed{ R_{C,\theta}(P)=P \Rightarrow P=C } $$

for a nonidentity rotation.

Identity Rotation Is Exceptional

For a full-turn or zero-angle rotation, every point is fixed. In that case, the transformation itself does not determine a unique center.

Rotation Symmetry

A figure has rotational symmetry about a point $C$ if some nontrivial rotation about $C$ maps the figure onto itself.

$$ \boxed{ R_{C,\theta}(S)=S } $$

for some:

$$ 0<\theta<360^\circ. $$

Center of Rotational Symmetry

The point $C$ may then be called the center of rotational symmetry of the figure.

Example: Rectangle

A non-square rectangle has $180^\circ$ rotational symmetry about the intersection of its diagonals.

Example: Square

A square has rotational symmetry through:

$$ \boxed{ 90^\circ,\ 180^\circ,\ 270^\circ } $$

about the intersection of its diagonals.

Example: Equilateral Triangle

An equilateral triangle has rotational symmetry through:

$$ \boxed{ 120^\circ \text{ and } 240^\circ } $$

about its geometric center.

Regular n-Gon

A regular $n$-gon has rotational symmetry about its center through multiples of:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{360^\circ}{n} }. $$

Order of Rotational Symmetry

The order of rotational symmetry is the number of rotations through a full turn that map the figure onto itself, including the identity rotation.

A regular $n$-gon has order:

$$ \boxed{ n }. $$

Center of Rotation versus Center of a Circle

The center of a circle is a geometric point equidistant from all points on the circle.

A center of rotation is defined by a transformation.

$$ \boxed{ \text{circle center} \ne \text{center of rotation as concepts} }. $$

They may coincide in particular problems, especially rotations of a circle about its own center.

Center of Rotation versus Midpoint

For a $180^\circ$ rotation carrying $P$ to $P'$, the center is the midpoint of $\overline{PP'}$.

$$ \boxed{ \theta=180^\circ \Rightarrow C= \text{midpoint of }PP' }. $$

This special rule does not hold for arbitrary rotation angles.

Center of Rotation versus Reflection Line

A rotation is organized around a fixed point, whereas a reflection in the plane is organized around a fixed line.

$$ \boxed{ \text{rotation} \rightarrow \text{fixed center} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{reflection} \rightarrow \text{mirror line} }. $$

Center of Rotation versus Translation

A nonzero translation has no finite center of rotation and ordinarily has no fixed point.

A rotation instead has a fixed center.

Three-Dimensional Rotation

In three-dimensional Euclidean geometry, rotations are generally organized around an axis of rotation rather than a single center point.

Every point on the rotation axis remains fixed.

Plane Rotation versus Spatial Rotation

$$ \boxed{ \text{2D rotation} \rightarrow \text{center point} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{3D rotation} \rightarrow \text{axis line} }. $$

Complex-Number Representation

If the center is the origin, a planar rotation through angle $\theta$ can be written using complex numbers as:

$$ \boxed{ z' = e^{i\theta}z }. $$

Complex Rotation about an Arbitrary Center

If the center corresponds to complex number $c$, then:

$$ \boxed{ z' = c + e^{i\theta}(z-c) }. $$

This is the complex-number version of translate, rotate, and translate back.

Common Misconception: The Center Must Lie inside the Figure

The center of rotation can lie inside, on, or outside the figure being rotated.

Common Misconception: Every Point Has Its Own Center

In one rotation transformation, all points rotate around the same center.

Common Misconception: The Center Moves with the Figure

The defining center is fixed under the rotation.

Common Misconception: A Rotation Changes Size

A rotation preserves all distances and therefore cannot enlarge or shrink a figure.

Common Misconception: The Midpoint of P and P′ Is Always the Center

This is true for a $180^\circ$ rotation, but not for a general rotation angle.

Common Misconception: One Point-Image Pair Always Determines the Center

A single pair generally determines only a perpendicular-bisector locus. A second pair or additional angle information is needed to identify the center uniquely.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$R_{C,\theta}$ "The rotation through angle theta about center C."
$R_{C,\theta}(C)=C$ "The center of rotation is fixed."
$CP=CP'$ "P and its image P prime are equally distant from the center."
$\angle PCP'=\theta$ "The directed angle from P to P prime about C is theta."
$(x,y)\mapsto(-y,x)$ "Rotate ninety degrees counterclockwise about the origin."
$z'=c+e^{i\theta}(z-c)$ "Rotate the complex point z through theta about center c."

In planar geometry, center of rotation normally refers to the fixed point of a rotation. In three-dimensional settings, axis of rotation is usually the more appropriate term because a whole line of points remains fixed.

Defense Notation Advice: Always specify the center, angle, and direction when defining a rotation. When reconstructing an unknown center, use the perpendicular bisectors of at least two point-image segments unless special information, such as a $180^\circ$ angle, makes one pair sufficient.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: The center of rotation is the fixed point about which a planar rotation occurs.
  • Fixed-Point Property: $$ \boxed{ R_{C,\theta}(C)=C }. $$
  • Radial Distance: $$ \boxed{ CP=CP' }. $$
  • Rotation Angle: $$ \boxed{ \angle PCP'=\theta }. $$
  • Rigid Motion: Rotations preserve distance, angle, area, shape, and congruence.
  • Orientation: Rotations preserve orientation.
  • Origin Formula: $$ \boxed{ (x,y) \mapsto (x\cos\theta-y\sin\theta,\, x\sin\theta+y\cos\theta) }. $$
  • 90° Counterclockwise: $(x,y)\mapsto(-y,x)$.
  • 180°: $(x,y)\mapsto(-x,-y)$.
  • 270° Counterclockwise: $(x,y)\mapsto(y,-x)$.
  • Arbitrary Center: Translate to the origin, rotate, then translate back.
  • Center Construction: The center lies on the perpendicular bisector of every segment connecting a point to its rotated image.
  • Two-Pair Method: Intersect perpendicular bisectors of two point-image segments to locate a nontrivial rotation center.
  • 180° Special Case: The center is the midpoint of each point-image segment.
  • Rotational Symmetry: A figure has rotational symmetry when a nontrivial rotation about a center maps it onto itself.
  • Regular Polygon: A regular $n$-gon has basic rotational increment $360^\circ/n$ about its center.
  • Composition: Rotations about the same center add their angles.
  • Inverse: Rotation by $-\theta$ reverses rotation by $\theta$.
  • Unique Fixed Point: A nonidentity planar rotation has exactly one fixed point, its center.
  • 3D Contrast: Spatial rotations are ordinarily described by an axis of rotation rather than a single center.
  • Common Error: Do not assume the center must lie inside the rotated figure.
  • Common Error: Do not use the midpoint of a point-image pair as the center unless the rotation is $180^\circ$ or additional information justifies it.
  • Common Error: Do not infer a unique center from one point-image pair alone in the general case.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse a center of rotation with the center of a circle merely because both can serve as centers of circular motion.
  • Terminological Contrast: Center of rotation is the fixed point of a planar rotation. Angle of rotation is the directed amount of turning. Rotational symmetry occurs when a figure maps onto itself under a nontrivial rotation. Center of rotational symmetry is the point about which those symmetry rotations occur. Axis of rotation is the fixed line used for rotations in three-dimensional space. Reflection line is the fixed line associated with a reflection rather than a rotation.
