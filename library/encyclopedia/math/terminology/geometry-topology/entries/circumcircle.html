Circumcircle

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

A circumcircle of a triangle is the circle that passes through all three vertices of the triangle.

$$ \boxed{ A,B,C \in \text{circumcircle of }\triangle ABC }. $$

For every nondegenerate triangle, this circle is unique.

Its center is called the circumcenter, usually denoted $O$, and its radius is called the circumradius, usually denoted $R$.

$$ \boxed{ OA=OB=OC=R }. $$

The prefix circum- indicates something lying around or surrounding an object. A circumcircle surrounds the triangle in the sense that its vertices lie on the circle.

Operational Nuance

Existence and Uniqueness

Any three noncollinear points determine exactly one circle.

$$ \boxed{ \text{three noncollinear points} \longrightarrow \text{one unique circle} }. $$

Since the vertices of a nondegenerate triangle are noncollinear, every such triangle has exactly one circumcircle.

Circumcenter

The center of the circumcircle is the intersection of the three perpendicular bisectors of the triangle's sides.

$$ \boxed{ O = \operatorname{PB}(AB) \cap \operatorname{PB}(BC) \cap \operatorname{PB}(CA) }. $$

Why Perpendicular Bisectors Locate the Circumcircle

Every point on the perpendicular bisector of $\overline{AB}$ is equidistant from $A$ and $B$. At the intersection of two perpendicular bisectors:

$$ OA=OB $$

and:

$$ OA=OC. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ OA=OB=OC }. $$

A circle centered at $O$ with this common radius passes through all three vertices.

Circumradius

The radius of the circumcircle is:

$$ \boxed{ R=OA=OB=OC }. $$

Acute Triangle

For an acute triangle, the circumcenter lies inside the triangle, although the circumcircle itself necessarily extends outside the triangular region.

Right Triangle

For a right triangle, the hypotenuse is a diameter of the circumcircle.

$$ \boxed{ 2R=c } $$

where $c$ is the hypotenuse.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ R=\frac{c}{2} }. $$

The circumcenter is the midpoint of the hypotenuse.

Thales' Theorem

The right-triangle result is closely related to Thales' Theorem: an angle subtended by a diameter at a point on the circle is a right angle.

$$ \boxed{ AB \text{ diameter} \Rightarrow \angle ACB=90^\circ }. $$

Obtuse Triangle

For an obtuse triangle, the circumcenter lies outside the triangle. The three vertices still lie on the same circumcircle.

Circumcenter Location Summary

Triangle Type Circumcenter
Acute Inside the triangle
Right At the midpoint of the hypotenuse
Obtuse Outside the triangle

Standard Coordinate Equation

If the circumcenter is:

$$ O=(h,k) $$

and the circumradius is $R$, then the circumcircle has equation:

$$ \boxed{ (x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=R^2 }. $$

Finding a Circumcircle from Three Points

Given:

$$ A=(x_1,y_1),\qquad B=(x_2,y_2),\qquad C=(x_3,y_3), $$

one can:

  1. find the perpendicular bisectors of two sides,
  2. solve for their intersection $O=(h,k)$,
  3. compute $R=OA$,
  4. write $(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=R^2$.

General Circle Equation Method

Another method begins with:

$$ x^2+y^2+Dx+Ey+F=0. $$

Substitute the three triangle vertices to obtain three linear equations for $D$, $E$, and $F$.

Once those constants are known, the center is:

$$ \boxed{ O= \left( -\frac D2, -\frac E2 \right) }. $$

Example

Consider:

$$ A=(0,0),\qquad B=(6,0),\qquad C=(0,8). $$

This is a right triangle, so its circumcenter is the midpoint of the hypotenuse $\overline{BC}$:

$$ O=(3,4). $$

The circumradius is:

$$ R=5. $$

Therefore the circumcircle is:

$$ \boxed{ (x-3)^2+(y-4)^2=25 }. $$

Extended Law of Sines

If $a$, $b$, and $c$ are opposite angles $A$, $B$, and $C$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C} = 2R }. $$

This links the circumcircle directly to the ordinary Law of Sines.

Side as a Chord of the Circumcircle

Each side of the triangle is a chord of the circumcircle.

$$ \boxed{ a=2R\sin A } $$
$$ \boxed{ b=2R\sin B } $$
$$ \boxed{ c=2R\sin C }. $$

Circumradius from Area

If the triangle has area $K$, then:

$$ \boxed{ R= \frac{abc}{4K} }. $$

Equivalently:

$$ \boxed{ K= \frac{abc}{4R} }. $$

Deriving the Area-Circumradius Formula

Starting from:

$$ K=\frac12bc\sin A $$

and using:

$$ \sin A=\frac{a}{2R}, $$

gives:

$$ K= \frac12bc \left( \frac{a}{2R} \right) = \frac{abc}{4R}. $$

Inscribed Angles

Because the triangle vertices lie on the circumcircle, every triangle angle is an inscribed angle of the circumcircle.

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ m\angle A = \frac12 m\widehat{BC} }. $$

Corresponding Central Angle

If $O$ is the circumcenter, then the central angle subtending the same chord $\overline{BC}$ satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ m\angle BOC = 2m\angle BAC } $$

when the appropriate intercepted arc is understood.

Equal Chords and Equal Angles

If two sides of an inscribed triangle are equal, they are equal chords of the circumcircle and subtend equal arcs and equal corresponding inscribed angles.

Equilateral Triangle

In an equilateral triangle of side length $a$:

$$ \boxed{ R=\frac{a}{\sqrt3} }. $$

The circumcenter coincides with the centroid, incenter, and orthocenter.

Isosceles Triangle

For an isosceles triangle, the center of the circumcircle lies on the triangle's axis of symmetry.

Cyclic Polygon

A polygon whose vertices all lie on one circle is called a cyclic polygon.

The circle containing those vertices may be called the polygon's circumcircle.

Cyclic Quadrilateral

A quadrilateral has a circumcircle exactly when its opposite angles are supplementary:

$$ \boxed{ A+C=180^\circ } $$

and equivalently:

$$ \boxed{ B+D=180^\circ }. $$

Regular Polygons

Every regular polygon is cyclic.

Its circumcenter is the common center determined by its rotational symmetry.

Regular n-Gon and Circumradius

For a regular $n$-gon with side length $s$ and circumradius $R$:

$$ \boxed{ s= 2R\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{n}\right) }. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ R= \frac{s}{ 2\sin(\pi/n) } }. $$

Circumcircle and Euler Line

The center $O$ of the circumcircle lies on the Euler line together with the centroid $G$ and orthocenter $H$ of a non-equilateral triangle.

$$ \boxed{ O,\ G,\ H \text{ are collinear} }. $$

Nine-Point Circle Connection

The nine-point circle of a triangle has radius:

$$ \boxed{ \frac R2 }. $$

where $R$ is the circumradius.

Circumcircle versus Incircle

The circumcircle passes through the triangle's vertices.

The incircle lies inside the triangle and is tangent to its three sides.

$$ \boxed{ \text{circumcircle} \rightarrow \text{vertices} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{incircle} \rightarrow \text{sides} }. $$

Circumradius versus Inradius

The circumradius $R$ measures distance from the circumcenter to a vertex. The inradius $r$ measures perpendicular distance from the incenter to a side.

Euler's Circumradius-Inradius Inequality

For every nondegenerate triangle:

$$ \boxed{ R\ge2r }. $$

Equality occurs for an equilateral triangle.

Construction with Compass and Straightedge

To construct the circumcircle of a triangle:

  1. construct the perpendicular bisector of one side,
  2. construct the perpendicular bisector of a second side,
  3. mark their intersection as $O$,
  4. set a compass to $OA$,
  5. draw the circle centered at $O$.

Because:

$$ OA=OB=OC, $$

the resulting circle passes through all three vertices.

Degenerate Triangle

Three distinct collinear points do not lie on any finite ordinary circle. Therefore a degenerate triangle with collinear vertices has no finite circumcircle in ordinary Euclidean geometry.

Common Misconception: The Circumcircle Lies Entirely outside the Triangle

The triangle's vertices lie on the circumcircle. Parts of the circle may visually surround the triangle, but the relationship is not that the circle is disjoint from it.

Common Misconception: The Circumcircle Is Centered at the Centroid

Its center is the circumcenter. The centroid generally lies elsewhere.

Common Misconception: Every Polygon Has a Circumcircle

Every nondegenerate triangle is cyclic, but an arbitrary polygon need not have all vertices on one circle.

Common Misconception: Circumradius Is Half the Longest Side of Every Triangle

That formula is specifically true for right triangles, where the hypotenuse is a diameter.

Common Misconception: Circumcircle and Incircle Are the Same

The circumcircle passes through vertices; the incircle is tangent to sides.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$OA=OB=OC=R$ "The three vertices lie at circumradius R from the circumcenter O."
$(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=R^2$ "The circumcircle has center h comma k and radius R."
$\frac{a}{\sin A}=2R$ "a over sine A equals the diameter of the circumcircle."
$R=\frac{abc}{4K}$ "The circumradius equals a b c divided by four times the triangle's area."
$m\angle A=\frac12m\widehat{BC}$ "Angle A is half the measure of its intercepted arc B C."

The words circumcircle, circumcenter, and circumradius form a related terminology family. The circle is the geometric object, the circumcenter is its center, and the circumradius is its radius.

Defense Notation Advice: When working with a triangle's circumcircle, use $O$ for the circumcenter and $R$ for the circumradius unless a different convention is explicitly established. Distinguish carefully between the circumradius $R$ and the inradius $r$.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: The circumcircle of a triangle is the unique circle through its three vertices.
  • Circumcenter: Intersection of the three perpendicular bisectors.
  • Circumradius: $$ \boxed{ R=OA=OB=OC }. $$
  • Coordinate Equation: $$ \boxed{ (x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=R^2 }. $$
  • Right Triangle: The hypotenuse is a diameter of the circumcircle.
  • Right-Triangle Circumradius: $$ \boxed{ R=\frac c2 }. $$
  • Extended Law of Sines: $$ \boxed{ \frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C} = 2R }. $$
  • Area Relation: $$ \boxed{ K=\frac{abc}{4R} }. $$
  • Triangle Sides: Each side is a chord of the circumcircle.
  • Triangle Angles: Each vertex angle is an inscribed angle of the circumcircle.
  • Equilateral Triangle: $$ \boxed{ R=\frac{a}{\sqrt3} }. $$
  • Cyclic Polygon: A polygon whose vertices lie on a common circumcircle.
  • Cyclic Quadrilateral: Opposite angles are supplementary.
  • Regular Polygon: Every regular polygon has a circumcircle.
  • Regular n-Gon: $$ \boxed{ R= \frac{s}{2\sin(\pi/n)} }. $$
  • Construction: Two perpendicular bisectors locate the circumcenter; the radius to any vertex determines the circumcircle.
  • Incircle Contrast: Circumcircle passes through vertices; incircle is tangent to sides.
  • Common Error: Do not assume the circumcenter is always inside the triangle.
  • Common Error: Do not assume every polygon is cyclic.
  • Common Error: Do not use $R=c/2$ unless the triangle is right and $c$ is the hypotenuse.
  • Terminological Contrast: Circumcircle is the circle through a triangle's vertices. Circumcenter is the center of that circle. Circumradius is its radius. Incircle is tangent to the triangle's sides. Incenter is the center of the incircle. Cyclic polygon is any polygon whose vertices lie on one circle. Inscribed polygon is a polygon drawn with all vertices on a containing circle.
