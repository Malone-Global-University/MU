Absolute Maximum

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

An absolute maximum of a function is its greatest attained value over the entire domain or interval being considered. If $f$ has an absolute maximum at $x=c$, then:

$$ \boxed{ f(c)\ge f(x) \text{ for every }x\text{ in the domain under consideration} }. $$

The adjective absolute emphasizes that the comparison is global rather than restricted to nearby points. For this reason, global maximum is a common synonym.

The word maximum refers to the greatest member of a set of values. In function language, it is important to distinguish the maximum value from the point at which that value occurs.

If:

$$ f(c)=M $$

and:

$$ f(x)\le M $$

for every relevant $x$, then $M$ is the absolute maximum value and $x=c$ is a point where the absolute maximum is attained.

Operational Nuance

Formal Definition

Let $f$ be defined on a set $D$. The function has an absolute maximum at $c\in D$ if:

$$ \boxed{ f(c)\ge f(x) \qquad \text{for every }x\in D }. $$

The number:

$$ f(c) $$

is the absolute maximum value of $f$ on $D$.

Maximum Value vs. Maximum Point

Suppose:

$$ f(3)=10 $$

and no other function value exceeds $10$. Then:

$$ \boxed{ 10=\text{absolute maximum value} } $$

while:

$$ \boxed{ x=3=\text{a point where the absolute maximum occurs} }. $$

Confusing these two ideas is a common source of imprecise answers.

Absolute vs. Local Maximum

A local maximum only requires the function value to dominate nearby values. An absolute maximum must dominate all values in the domain under consideration.

A local maximum at $x=c$ satisfies:

$$ f(c)\ge f(x) $$

for all $x$ sufficiently close to $c$.

An absolute maximum satisfies:

$$ f(c)\ge f(x) $$

for every relevant $x$.

Example

Consider:

$$ f(x)=-x^2+4 $$

on:

$$ [-3,3]. $$

Since:

$$ f(0)=4 $$

and:

$$ -x^2+4\le4 $$

for every $x$, the function has absolute maximum value:

$$ \boxed{4} $$

attained at:

$$ \boxed{x=0}. $$

More Than One Maximum Point

A function may attain its absolute maximum at several points.

For example, on:

$$ [0,2\pi], $$

the function:

$$ f(x)=\cos x $$

has absolute maximum value:

$$ 1 $$

at both:

$$ x=0 $$

and:

$$ x=2\pi. $$

Critical Points

For a differentiable function, an interior absolute maximum often occurs at a critical point. If $c$ is an interior point and $f$ is differentiable there, then:

$$ \boxed{ f'(c)=0 } $$

is a necessary condition for a differentiable local extremum.

However, not every critical point is an absolute maximum.

Endpoints Matter

On a closed interval:

$$ [a,b], $$

an absolute maximum can occur at an endpoint. Therefore a calculus search for absolute extrema must compare endpoint values with values at relevant critical points.

Closed Interval Method

For a continuous function on $[a,b]$:

  1. Find critical numbers in $(a,b)$.
  2. Evaluate $f$ at each critical number.
  3. Evaluate $f(a)$ and $f(b)$.
  4. Compare all resulting values.
  5. The largest value is the absolute maximum.

Common Misconception: Solving:

$$ f'(x)=0 $$

does not by itself find the absolute maximum. Critical points are candidates, not automatic answers.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$f(c)\ge f(x)$ "F of c is greater than or equal to f of x."
$\max_{x\in D}f(x)$ "The maximum of f over x in D."
$\max_{a\le x\le b}f(x)$ "The maximum of f on the interval from a to b."
$f'(c)=0$ "F prime of c equals zero."
$x=c$ "The maximum is attained at x equals c."
$f(c)=M$ "The absolute maximum value is M."

The statement:

$$ \max_{x\in D}f(x)=M $$

means that $M$ is actually attained by the function. This differs from merely saying that $M$ is the least upper bound of the range.

Defense Notation Advice: When a problem asks for the absolute maximum, determine whether it wants the maximum value, the point where it occurs, or both. A complete answer often states both:

$$ \boxed{ \text{Absolute maximum value }M \text{ occurs at }x=c }. $$

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Formal Definition: $$ \boxed{ f(c)\ge f(x) \text{ for every }x\in D }. $$
  • Global Maximum: Global maximum is a standard synonym for absolute maximum.
  • Maximum Value: If: $$ M=f(c), $$ then $M$ is the maximum value.
  • Point of Attainment: The input: $$ c $$ is a point at which the maximum is attained.
  • Multiple Maximizers: Several different inputs may produce the same absolute maximum value.
  • Absolute vs. Local: Every absolute maximum at an interior point is also a local maximum under the usual neighborhood definition, but a local maximum need not be absolute.
  • Interior Differentiable Maximum: If $f$ is differentiable and has a local maximum at an interior point $c$, then: $$ \boxed{ f'(c)=0 }. $$ This is Fermat's theorem for stationary extrema.
  • Nondifferentiable Maximum: An absolute maximum can occur where: $$ f'(c) $$ does not exist. Cusps, corners, and other nondifferentiable points must therefore be considered when searching for extrema.
  • Critical Number: A critical number is usually a domain value $c$ for which: $$ f'(c)=0 $$ or: $$ f'(c) $$ does not exist.
  • Critical Point Caution: A critical point may be a maximum, minimum, or neither.
  • Endpoint Maximum: On a restricted interval, an endpoint may provide the greatest function value even though the derivative there is nonzero or is not considered in the same interior sense.
  • Extreme Value Theorem: If $f$ is continuous on a closed bounded interval: $$ [a,b], $$ then $f$ attains both an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum on that interval.
  • Closed Interval Requirement: The Extreme Value Theorem does not automatically apply to open intervals, unbounded intervals, or discontinuous functions.
  • Open Interval Failure: On: $$ (0,1), $$ the function: $$ f(x)=x $$ has no absolute maximum because values approach $1$ but never attain it.
  • Supremum vs. Maximum: A supremum need not be attained. A maximum must be.
  • Example: For: $$ S=(0,1), $$ one has: $$ \sup S=1, $$ but: $$ \max S $$ does not exist.
  • Constant Function: If: $$ f(x)=c $$ throughout the domain, then every point is both an absolute maximum point and an absolute minimum point.
  • Quadratic Functions: For: $$ f(x)=ax^2+bx+c $$ with: $$ a<0, $$ the parabola opens downward and its vertex gives the absolute maximum over $\mathbb{R}$.
  • Upward-Opening Quadratic: If: $$ a>0, $$ the function has no absolute maximum over all real numbers because: $$ f(x)\to\infty $$ as: $$ |x|\to\infty. $$
  • First Derivative Test: If: $$ f' $$ changes from positive to negative at $c$, then $c$ is a local maximum point. Additional comparison is needed to establish absolute maximality.
  • Second Derivative Test: If: $$ f'(c)=0 $$ and: $$ f''(c)<0, $$ then $c$ is a local maximum. This does not by itself prove that the maximum is absolute.
  • Concavity: If a function is concave on an interval, a critical point may be enough to identify a global maximum under appropriate conditions.
  • Optimization: Absolute maxima appear in optimization problems involving greatest profit, greatest area, greatest volume, maximum efficiency, maximum height, or other largest attainable quantities.
  • Constraints: In constrained optimization, the absolute maximum is taken only over the permitted feasible region, not over the entire unrestricted domain.
  • Multivariable Functions: For: $$ f:D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}, $$ an absolute maximum at $\mathbf{c}$ satisfies: $$ \boxed{ f(\mathbf{c})\ge f(\mathbf{x}) \text{ for every }\mathbf{x}\in D }. $$
  • Compact Domains: More generally, a continuous real-valued function on a compact set attains an absolute maximum and minimum.
  • Boundary Analysis: In multivariable optimization, maxima may occur in the interior or on the boundary of the domain.
  • Lagrange Multipliers: Under smooth equality constraints, Lagrange multipliers can generate candidates for constrained extrema, which must still be compared to determine the absolute maximum.
  • Discrete Sets: The notion of absolute maximum does not require calculus. A finite set of function values can be compared directly.
  • Sequence Maximum: A sequence: $$ \{a_n\} $$ has an absolute maximum if some term $a_k$ satisfies: $$ a_k\ge a_n $$ for every $n$.
  • Range Interpretation: The absolute maximum of a function, when it exists, is the largest element of its range over the specified domain.
  • Domain Dependence: The same function may have an absolute maximum on one domain but not on another. Domain restrictions are therefore part of the definition.
  • Terminological Contrast: An absolute maximum is the greatest attained value over the entire specified domain. A local maximum is greatest only relative to nearby values. A supremum is the least upper bound and need not be attained, while a maximum must actually occur.
