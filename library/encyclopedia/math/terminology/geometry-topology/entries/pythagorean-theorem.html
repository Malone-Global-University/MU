Pythagorean Theorem

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

The Pythagorean Theorem, also called Pythagoras's Theorem, is named after the ancient Greek philosopher and mathematician Pythagoras of Samos, who lived during the sixth century BCE.

The theorem states a relationship among the three side lengths of a right triangle. In modern notation, if $a$ and $b$ are the legs and $c$ is the hypotenuse, then:

$$ a^2+b^2=c^2. $$

The historical name should not be interpreted as proof that Pythagoras was the first person to know the relationship. Mathematical evidence from Old Babylonian tablets shows knowledge of right-triangle relationships and Pythagorean triples many centuries before Pythagoras. Related forms also appeared in ancient Indian and Chinese mathematical traditions.

What role Pythagoras or the early Pythagorean school played in the theorem's first proof remains historically uncertain. Ancient Greek tradition strongly associated the result with the Pythagoreans, and later mathematical culture attached Pythagoras's name to the theorem.

The word hypotenuse refers specifically to the side opposite the right angle. It is necessarily the longest side of a right triangle. The other two sides are commonly called the legs.

Operational Nuance

Let a right triangle have legs of lengths $a$ and $b$ and hypotenuse of length $c$. The Pythagorean Theorem states:

$$ \boxed{ a^2+b^2=c^2 }. $$

The location of $c$ matters. The variable $c$ must represent the side opposite the $90^\circ$ angle:

$$ c=\text{hypotenuse}. $$

For example, if the legs are:

$$ a=3, \qquad b=4, $$

then:

$$ c^2 = 3^2+4^2 $$ $$ =9+16 $$ $$ =25. $$

Therefore:

$$ c=5. $$

This produces the familiar:

$$ \boxed{ 3^2+4^2=5^2 }. $$

The theorem can also be rearranged to find a missing leg. If $c$ is the hypotenuse and $a$ is known, then:

$$ b^2=c^2-a^2 $$

and therefore:

$$ \boxed{ b=\sqrt{c^2-a^2} }. $$

For instance, if:

$$ c=13, \qquad a=5, $$

then:

$$ b^2 = 13^2-5^2 $$ $$ =169-25 $$ $$ =144, $$

so:

$$ b=12. $$

The theorem has a geometric area interpretation. If a square is constructed on each side of a right triangle, then:

$$ \boxed{ \text{area of square on leg }a + \text{area of square on leg }b = \text{area of square on hypotenuse} }. $$

Since the area of a square of side length $s$ is $s^2$, this geometric statement is exactly:

$$ a^2+b^2=c^2. $$

Common Misconception: The Pythagorean Theorem does not apply to every triangle. It applies specifically to a right triangle. For a general triangle, the broader Law of Cosines is required.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$a^2+b^2=c^2$ "A squared plus b squared equals c squared."
$c=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ "C equals the square root of a squared plus b squared."
$a=\sqrt{c^2-b^2}$ "A equals the square root of c squared minus b squared."
$\angle C=90^\circ$ "Angle C equals ninety degrees."
$c$ is the hypotenuse "C is the hypotenuse."
$(3,4,5)$ "Three, four, five is a Pythagorean triple."

The lettering:

$$ a^2+b^2=c^2 $$

is conventional but not mandatory. The theorem depends on the geometric roles of the sides, not their letters. If a triangle has sides $x$, $y$, and $z$, and $z$ is the hypotenuse, then:

$$ x^2+y^2=z^2. $$

A common shorthand is:

$$ \text{leg}^2+\text{leg}^2=\text{hypotenuse}^2. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Never identify the hypotenuse merely by looking for the side drawn diagonally or at the top of a diagram. The hypotenuse is defined by its relationship to the right angle:

$$ \boxed{ \text{hypotenuse} = \text{side opposite the }90^\circ\text{ angle} }. $$

The orientation of the drawing is irrelevant.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Finding a Hypotenuse: If the legs are known: $$ c=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}. $$ Since $c$ is a length, the positive square root is used.
  • Finding a Leg: If the hypotenuse and one leg are known: $$ a=\sqrt{c^2-b^2} $$ or: $$ b=\sqrt{c^2-a^2}. $$
  • Converse of the Pythagorean Theorem: If three positive side lengths satisfy: $$ a^2+b^2=c^2, $$ where $c$ is the longest side, then the triangle is a right triangle. Thus the equation can be used not only to calculate lengths but also to identify right triangles.
  • Acute & Obtuse Triangle Test: Let $c$ be the longest side. Then: $$ c^2a^2+b^2 $$ indicates an obtuse triangle. Equality gives a right triangle.
  • Pythagorean Triples: A Pythagorean triple consists of positive integers: $$ (a,b,c) $$ satisfying: $$ a^2+b^2=c^2. $$ Examples include: $$ (3,4,5), $$ $$ (5,12,13), $$ $$ (8,15,17). $$
  • Generating Pythagorean Triples: For integers $m>n>0$, Euclid's formula gives: $$ a=m^2-n^2, $$ $$ b=2mn, $$ $$ c=m^2+n^2. $$ Under additional number-theoretic conditions, these generate primitive Pythagorean triples.
  • Distance Formula: The distance between: $$ (x_1,y_1) $$ and: $$ (x_2,y_2) $$ follows directly from the Pythagorean Theorem: $$ d = \sqrt{ (x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2 }. $$
  • Three-Dimensional Distance: Applying the theorem repeatedly gives: $$ d = \sqrt{ (x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2 + (z_2-z_1)^2 }. $$
  • Equation of a Circle: A point $(x,y)$ lying a distance $r$ from the origin satisfies: $$ x^2+y^2=r^2. $$ Thus the standard equation of a circle is a direct coordinate-geometric application of the Pythagorean Theorem.
  • Vector Magnitude: For a vector: $$ \mathbf{v}=\langle x,y\rangle, $$ its Euclidean magnitude is: $$ \|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}. $$ In three dimensions: $$ \|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}. $$
  • Law of Cosines: For any triangle: $$ c^2 = a^2+b^2-2ab\cos C. $$ If: $$ C=90^\circ, $$ then: $$ \cos90^\circ=0, $$ so the Law of Cosines reduces to: $$ c^2=a^2+b^2. $$ The Pythagorean Theorem is therefore the right-angle special case of the Law of Cosines.
  • Trigonometry: Dividing: $$ x^2+y^2=r^2 $$ by $r^2$ gives: $$ \left(\frac{x}{r}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{y}{r}\right)^2 = 1. $$ Since: $$ \cos\theta=\frac{x}{r} $$ and: $$ \sin\theta=\frac{y}{r}, $$ this produces the Pythagorean identity: $$ \sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1. $$
  • Euclidean Norm: In higher-dimensional Euclidean space: $$ \|\mathbf{x}\| = \sqrt{x_1^2+x_2^2+\cdots+x_n^2}. $$ This can be understood as a higher-dimensional extension of repeated Pythagorean relationships among mutually perpendicular components.
  • Orthogonality in Inner Product Spaces: A generalized Pythagorean identity states that if vectors $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are orthogonal, then: $$ \|\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2 + \|\mathbf{v}\|^2. $$ The familiar triangle theorem is the two-dimensional geometric instance of this broader principle.
  • Many Proofs: The Pythagorean Theorem is famous for having a very large number of distinct proofs. These include similarity proofs, rearrangement proofs, area proofs, algebraic proofs, and proofs based on Euclidean geometry.
  • Similarity Proof: One classical proof drops an altitude from the right-angle vertex to the hypotenuse. The resulting two smaller triangles are each similar to the original triangle. The proportional relationships obtained from similarity lead directly to: $$ a^2+b^2=c^2. $$
  • Historical Babylonian Usage: Old Babylonian mathematical tablets from roughly the second millennium BCE show sophisticated knowledge of right-triangle numerical relationships and Pythagorean triples, demonstrating that the mathematical relationship predates the historical Pythagoras by many centuries.
  • Indian Tradition: Ancient Indian Śulba Sūtras contain geometric rules equivalent to the Pythagorean relationship and use it in constructions involving ritual altars.
  • Chinese Tradition: Chinese mathematics developed the gougu rule, corresponding to the relationship between the legs and hypotenuse of a right triangle. It appears in classical mathematical works such as the Zhoubi Suanjing.
  • Terminological Contrast: The Pythagorean Theorem states the side-length relation for a known right triangle. Its converse uses that relation to prove a triangle is right. A Pythagorean triple is an integer solution to the equation. The Law of Cosines generalizes the side-angle relationship to arbitrary triangles.
