Area Using Polar Coordinates

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

Area using polar coordinates refers to measuring planar regions described in terms of a distance from the origin and an angle rather than ordinary Cartesian $x$- and $y$-coordinates.

A polar point is written:

$$ \boxed{ (r,\theta) }. $$

Here $r$ is the radial coordinate and $\theta$ is the angular coordinate. A polar curve is commonly expressed as:

$$ \boxed{ r=f(\theta) }. $$

Instead of approximating area with thin rectangles, polar area is naturally approximated with thin circular sectors.

This produces the fundamental formula:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \int_\alpha^\beta r(\theta)^2\,d\theta }. $$

The factor $\frac12$ comes directly from the area formula for a circular sector.

Operational Nuance

Polar Area Formula

If a polar curve:

$$ r=f(\theta) $$

bounds a region as $\theta$ moves from $\alpha$ to $\beta$, then:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \int_\alpha^\beta [f(\theta)]^2\,d\theta }. $$

Why the Formula Works

A small change in angle:

$$ \Delta\theta $$

creates a thin sector whose radius is approximately $r$.

The area of a sector of radius $r$ and angle $\Delta\theta$ radians is:

$$ \Delta A \approx \frac12r^2\Delta\theta. $$

Summing many such sectors gives:

$$ \sum \frac12 r(\theta_i^*)^2 \Delta\theta_i. $$

Passing to the limit produces:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \int_\alpha^\beta r(\theta)^2\,d\theta }. $$

Riemann-Sum Form

$$ \boxed{ A= \lim_{\max\Delta\theta_i\to0} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac12 r(\theta_i^*)^2 \Delta\theta_i }. $$

This is the polar counterpart of approximating Cartesian area with rectangles.

Differential Area

The infinitesimal sector contribution is:

$$ \boxed{ dA= \frac12r^2\,d\theta }. $$

Integrating these contributions gives the total area.

Constant Radius: Recovering Sector Area

If:

$$ r(\theta)=R, $$

then:

$$ A= \frac12 \int_\alpha^\beta R^2\,d\theta. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12R^2(\beta-\alpha) }. $$

This is exactly the ordinary sector-area formula when the angle is measured in radians.

Full Circle

For a circle centered at the pole:

$$ r=R, \qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi. $$

Then:

$$ A= \frac12 \int_0^{2\pi}R^2\,d\theta. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ A=\pi R^2 }. $$

Example: r = 2cosθ

Consider:

$$ r=2\cos\theta. $$

This traces a circle of radius $1$ centered at $(1,0)$. One complete tracing occurs for:

$$ -\frac{\pi}{2} \le\theta\le \frac{\pi}{2}. $$

Its area is:

$$ A= \frac12 \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} (2\cos\theta)^2\,d\theta. $$

Simplifying:

$$ A= 2 \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} \cos^2\theta\,d\theta. $$

Using symmetry:

$$ A= 4 \int_0^{\pi/2} \cos^2\theta\,d\theta. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ A=\pi }. $$

Area between Two Polar Curves

Suppose:

$$ R(\theta) \ge r(\theta) \ge0 $$

over an angular interval $[\alpha,\beta]$.

The area between the curves is:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \int_\alpha^\beta \left[ R(\theta)^2-r(\theta)^2 \right]d\theta }. $$

Outer Squared Minus Inner Squared

The polar area-between-curves rule is analogous to the washer method:

$$ \boxed{ \text{outer radius}^2 - \text{inner radius}^2 }. $$

It is not:

$$ [R(\theta)-r(\theta)]^2. $$

In general:

$$ \boxed{ R^2-r^2 \ne (R-r)^2 }. $$

Annular Sector

If the outer and inner radii are constants $R$ and $r$, then:

$$ A= \frac12 \int_\alpha^\beta (R^2-r^2)\,d\theta. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 (R^2-r^2) (\beta-\alpha) }. $$

Finding Intersection Angles

When two polar curves bound a region, the angular bounds often come from solving:

$$ \boxed{ r_1(\theta)=r_2(\theta) }. $$

However, polar coordinates are not unique, so geometric intersections may also occur through equivalent representations such as:

$$ (r,\theta) = (-r,\theta+\pi). $$

Therefore intersection analysis sometimes requires more than simply setting the two formulas equal.

Negative Radius

Polar radius may be negative. The point:

$$ (-r,\theta) $$

represents the same Cartesian point as:

$$ \boxed{ (r,\theta+\pi) }. $$

In the area formula, the radius is squared:

$$ r(\theta)^2. $$

Thus a negative radius does not by itself produce negative area.

But Negative Radius Still Affects Geometry

Although squaring removes the sign numerically, a negative radius changes which direction from the pole the point lies. This affects:

  • which portion of the curve is being traced,
  • where loops occur,
  • which angular interval encloses the intended region,
  • which curve is geometrically inner or outer.

Therefore one should not ignore the sign of $r$ when identifying the region.

Tracing Interval Matters

A polar curve may trace itself more than once if the chosen angular interval is too long.

Since the integral accumulates area over the parameter interval, repeated tracing can count the same geometric region multiple times.

$$ \boxed{ \text{find an interval that traces the intended region once} }. $$

Rose Curves

For a rose:

$$ r=a\cos(n\theta) \qquad\text{or}\qquad r=a\sin(n\theta), $$

it is often easiest to find the area of one petal and multiply by the number of congruent petals.

One-Petal Area

If one petal is traced from $\theta=\alpha$ to $\theta=\beta$, then:

$$ \boxed{ A_{\text{petal}} = \frac12 \int_\alpha^\beta r(\theta)^2\,d\theta }. $$

Symmetry

Polar curves frequently possess rotational or reflection symmetry. If a region consists of $m$ congruent pieces:

$$ \boxed{ A_{\text{total}} = mA_{\text{one piece}} }. $$

Symmetry can dramatically simplify the required integration.

Symmetry about the Polar Axis

A common test is whether replacing $\theta$ by $-\theta$ leaves the equation unchanged.

If so, the graph is typically symmetric about the polar axis.

Symmetry about the Line θ = π/2

Replacing:

$$ \theta \quad\text{by}\quad \pi-\theta $$

can reveal symmetry about the vertical axis.

Rotational Symmetry about the Pole

Replacing:

$$ \theta \quad\text{by}\quad \theta+\pi $$

can reveal symmetry through the pole.

Area of a Cardioid

Consider:

$$ r=a(1+\cos\theta). $$

One complete tracing occurs over:

$$ 0\le\theta\le2\pi. $$

Therefore:

$$ A= \frac12 \int_0^{2\pi} a^2(1+\cos\theta)^2\,d\theta. $$

Expanding:

$$ A= \frac{a^2}{2} \int_0^{2\pi} \left( 1+2\cos\theta+\cos^2\theta \right)d\theta. $$

The cosine term integrates to zero over a full period, and:

$$ \int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta=\pi. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac{3\pi a^2}{2} }. $$

Area of a Lemniscate Loop

Consider:

$$ r^2=a^2\cos(2\theta). $$

One loop is traced where:

$$ -\frac{\pi}{4} \le\theta\le \frac{\pi}{4}. $$

Its area is:

$$ A_{\text{loop}} = \frac12 \int_{-\pi/4}^{\pi/4} a^2\cos(2\theta)\,d\theta. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ A_{\text{loop}} = \frac{a^2}{2} }. $$

Both loops together have area:

$$ \boxed{ A_{\text{total}}=a^2 }. $$

Polar-to-Cartesian Conversion

Polar and Cartesian coordinates are connected by:

$$ \boxed{ x=r\cos\theta, \qquad y=r\sin\theta }. $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ r^2=x^2+y^2 }. $$

Converting to Cartesian form can sometimes clarify the geometric region, although direct polar integration is often simpler.

Connection to Parametric Area

A polar curve:

$$ r=r(\theta) $$

can be parametrized by:

$$ x(\theta)=r(\theta)\cos\theta, \qquad y(\theta)=r(\theta)\sin\theta. $$

The symmetric parametric area formula is:

$$ A= \frac12 \int \left( x\frac{dy}{d\theta} - y\frac{dx}{d\theta} \right)d\theta. $$

For the polar parametrization:

$$ x\frac{dy}{d\theta} - y\frac{dx}{d\theta} = r^2. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \int r^2\,d\theta }. $$

Double-Integral Interpretation

In polar coordinates, the planar area element is:

$$ \boxed{ dA=r\,dr\,d\theta }. $$

If a region is:

$$ \alpha\le\theta\le\beta, \qquad 0\le r\le f(\theta), $$

then:

$$ A= \int_\alpha^\beta \int_0^{f(\theta)} r\,dr\,d\theta. $$

Evaluating the inner integral gives:

$$ A= \int_\alpha^\beta \left[ \frac12r^2 \right]_0^{f(\theta)} d\theta. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \int_\alpha^\beta f(\theta)^2\,d\theta }. $$

Why dA Is Not dr dθ

Polar coordinates stretch with distance from the pole. A small polar rectangle has one side approximately $dr$ and the other approximately:

$$ r\,d\theta. $$

Therefore:

$$ dA \approx dr(r\,d\theta) = \boxed{ r\,dr\,d\theta }. $$

The factor $r$ is the polar Jacobian.

Area between Polar Curves via Double Integrals

If:

$$ r_{\text{inner}}(\theta) \le r\le r_{\text{outer}}(\theta), $$

then:

$$ A= \int_\alpha^\beta \int_{r_{\text{inner}}(\theta)}^{r_{\text{outer}}(\theta)} r\,dr\,d\theta. $$

Evaluating:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \int_\alpha^\beta \left[ r_{\text{outer}}(\theta)^2 - r_{\text{inner}}(\theta)^2 \right]d\theta }. $$

Choosing the Correct Bounds

Before integrating, determine:

  • where the intended region begins and ends angularly,
  • where the curve passes through the pole,
  • where two curves intersect,
  • where the inner and outer curves exchange roles,
  • whether symmetry can reduce the interval,
  • whether the chosen interval traces the region more than once.

Much of the difficulty in polar area problems lies in identifying the region correctly rather than in performing the final integration.

Splitting the Integral

If one curve is outer on $[\alpha,c]$ but the other is outer on $[c,\beta]$, the calculation must be split:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \int_\alpha^c (R_1^2-r_1^2)\,d\theta + \frac12 \int_c^\beta (R_2^2-r_2^2)\,d\theta }. $$

Area Inside One Curve and Outside Another

The phrase inside one curve and outside another usually translates into:

$$ \boxed{ \frac12 \int \left[ (\text{outer radius})^2 - (\text{inner radius})^2 \right]d\theta }. $$

The geometric words inside and outside should be resolved before assigning formulas to the radii.

Scaling

Suppose:

$$ r(\theta)\mapsto kr(\theta). $$

Then:

$$ A' = \frac12 \int [kr(\theta)]^2\,d\theta. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ A'\,=\,k^2A }. $$

Doubling Every Radius

$$ \boxed{ r\mapsto2r \Rightarrow A\mapsto4A }. $$

Rotation

Replacing:

$$ r=f(\theta) $$

by:

$$ r=f(\theta-\phi) $$

rotates the curve without changing its area.

$$ \boxed{ \text{rotation preserves area} }. $$

Area Is Not Arc Length

Polar arc length uses:

$$ \boxed{ L= \int_\alpha^\beta \sqrt{ r^2+ \left( \frac{dr}{d\theta} \right)^2 }\,d\theta }. $$

Polar area instead uses:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \int_\alpha^\beta r^2\,d\theta }. $$

These formulas measure fundamentally different geometric quantities.

Area Is Not Simply ∫r dθ

The quantity:

$$ \int r\,d\theta $$

does not generally represent polar area.

The correct sector contribution depends on the square of the radius:

$$ \boxed{ dA= \frac12r^2\,d\theta }. $$

Radians Are Essential

The formula:

$$ \frac12r^2\,d\theta $$

is based on the radian sector formula. Therefore angular integration in standard calculus uses radians.

$$ \boxed{ \theta\text{ is interpreted in radians} }. $$

Units

Since $r^2$ has squared length units and radians are dimensionless in ordinary mathematical treatment:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ has square units} }. $$

Common Misconception

The most common structural error is forgetting either the square or the factor $\frac12$.

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \int r^2\,d\theta } $$

not:

$$ \int r\,d\theta \qquad\text{or}\qquad \int r^2\,d\theta. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$r=f(\theta)$ "R is a function of theta."
$A=\frac12\int_\alpha^\beta r^2\,d\theta$ "Area equals one-half the integral from alpha to beta of r squared with respect to theta."
$A=\frac12\int_\alpha^\beta[R^2-r^2]\,d\theta$ "Area equals one-half the integral of outer radius squared minus inner radius squared."
$dA=\frac12r^2\,d\theta$ "D A equals one-half r squared d theta."
$dA=r\,dr\,d\theta$ "The polar area element is r d r d theta."
$x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta$ "X equals r cosine theta, and y equals r sine theta."

The same letter $r$ may represent a fixed radius in one problem and a function $r(\theta)$ in another. In area-between-curves problems, uppercase $R$ and lowercase $r$ are often used informally for outer and inner radii, but the geometric roles matter more than the letter choice.

Defense Notation Advice: Determine the angular interval and trace the curve conceptually before integrating. When two curves are present, identify which is farther from the pole on each interval rather than assuming the first equation listed is always the outer curve.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Polar Curve: $$ \boxed{ r=f(\theta) }. $$
  • Core Polar Area Formula: $$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \int_\alpha^\beta r(\theta)^2\,d\theta }. $$
  • Differential Sector Area: $$ \boxed{ dA= \frac12r^2\,d\theta }. $$
  • Riemann-Sum Interpretation: $$ \boxed{ A= \lim \sum \frac12r_i^2\Delta\theta_i }. $$
  • Constant-Radius Sector: $$ \boxed{ A= \frac12R^2(\beta-\alpha) }. $$
  • Full Circle: $$ \boxed{ A=\pi R^2 }. $$
  • Area between Polar Curves: $$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \int_\alpha^\beta \left[ r_{\text{outer}}^2 - r_{\text{inner}}^2 \right]d\theta }. $$
  • Outer-Inner Rule: Use outer radius squared minus inner radius squared.
  • Difference-of-Squares Caution: $$ \boxed{ R^2-r^2\ne(R-r)^2 }. $$
  • Intersection Angles: Bounds often come from solving: $$ \boxed{ r_1(\theta)=r_2(\theta) }. $$
  • Polar Nonuniqueness: $$ \boxed{ (r,\theta) = (-r,\theta+\pi) }. $$
  • Negative Radius: Negative $r$ changes direction from the pole but does not make $r^2$ negative.
  • Tracing Caution: Choose bounds that trace the intended region exactly once unless multiplicity is deliberately intended.
  • Symmetry: Congruent polar lobes or petals may be integrated once and multiplied.
  • One-Petal Formula: $$ \boxed{ A_{\text{petal}} = \frac12 \int_\alpha^\beta r^2\,d\theta }. $$
  • Cardioid Example: For $r=a(1+\cos\theta)$: $$ \boxed{ A= \frac{3\pi a^2}{2} }. $$
  • Lemniscate Loop Example: For $r^2=a^2\cos2\theta$: $$ \boxed{ A_{\text{one loop}} = \frac{a^2}{2} }. $$
  • Polar-to-Cartesian Conversion: $$ \boxed{ x=r\cos\theta, \qquad y=r\sin\theta }. $$
  • Radial Identity: $$ \boxed{ r^2=x^2+y^2 }. $$
  • Parametric Connection: $$ \boxed{ x(\theta)=r(\theta)\cos\theta, \qquad y(\theta)=r(\theta)\sin\theta }. $$
  • Parametric Area Reduction: $$ \boxed{ x\frac{dy}{d\theta} - y\frac{dx}{d\theta} = r^2 }. $$
  • Polar Double-Integral Element: $$ \boxed{ dA=r\,dr\,d\theta }. $$
  • Region from Pole to Curve: $$ \boxed{ A= \int_\alpha^\beta \int_0^{f(\theta)} r\,dr\,d\theta }. $$
  • Region between Radial Boundaries: $$ \boxed{ A= \int_\alpha^\beta \int_{r_{\text{inner}}}^{r_{\text{outer}}} r\,dr\,d\theta }. $$
  • Jacobian: The factor $r$ in $dA=r\,dr\,d\theta$ accounts for the stretching of angular distance farther from the pole.
  • Splitting: Split the integral whenever the outer and inner curves exchange roles.
  • Scaling: $$ \boxed{ r\mapsto kr \Rightarrow A\mapsto k^2A }. $$
  • Rotation: Rotating a polar region preserves its area.
  • Arc-Length Contrast: $$ \boxed{ L= \int \sqrt{ r^2+ \left( \frac{dr}{d\theta} \right)^2 }\,d\theta } $$ measures length, not area.
  • Angle Unit: Standard polar area formulas assume radians.
  • Square Units: If $r$ is measured in centimeters, polar area is measured in square centimeters.
  • Common Error: $$ \boxed{ A\ne\int r\,d\theta }. $$
  • Common Error: $$ \boxed{ A\ne\int r^2\,d\theta } $$ because the factor $\frac12$ is required.
  • Common Error: Do not assume that integrating from $0$ to $2\pi$ is always necessary; many polar curves complete their intended region over a shorter interval and would otherwise be traced repeatedly.
  • Terminological Contrast: Radius gives distance from the pole. Angle gives direction. Polar area is accumulated from thin sectors. Area between polar curves compares their radial distances from the pole at the same angle and integrates outer radius squared minus inner radius squared.
