Circumcenter

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

The circumcenter of a triangle is the common intersection point of the perpendicular bisectors of its three sides.

$$ \boxed{ O = \text{intersection of the perpendicular bisectors} }. $$

The circumcenter is conventionally denoted by:

$$ \boxed{ O }. $$

It is equidistant from the three vertices:

$$ \boxed{ OA=OB=OC }. $$

Therefore the circumcenter is the center of the unique circle passing through the three vertices of any nondegenerate triangle. That circle is the circumcircle.

Operational Nuance

Perpendicular Bisector Definition

In triangle $ABC$, construct the perpendicular bisector of each side.

These three lines meet at a single point:

$$ \boxed{ O }. $$

Why Perpendicular Bisectors Matter

Every point on the perpendicular bisector of $\overline{AB}$ is equidistant from $A$ and $B$.

$$ \boxed{ P\in\operatorname{PB}(AB) \Rightarrow PA=PB }. $$

Likewise, every point on the perpendicular bisector of $\overline{AC}$ is equidistant from $A$ and $C$.

Therefore their intersection satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ OA=OB=OC }. $$

Only Two Perpendicular Bisectors Are Needed

Since the perpendicular bisectors of the three sides are concurrent, constructing any two of them determines the circumcenter. The third passes through the same point.

Circumcircle

The circle centered at $O$ with radius:

$$ R=OA=OB=OC $$

passes through all three vertices.

$$ \boxed{ \text{circumcircle} = \text{circle through }A,B,C }. $$

Circumradius

The common distance from the circumcenter to the vertices is the circumradius:

$$ \boxed{ R=OA=OB=OC }. $$

Location in an Acute Triangle

If triangle $ABC$ is acute, the circumcenter lies inside the triangle.

$$ \boxed{ \text{acute triangle} \Rightarrow O \text{ inside} }. $$

Location in a Right Triangle

If triangle $ABC$ is right, the circumcenter is the midpoint of the hypotenuse.

$$ \boxed{ \text{right triangle} \Rightarrow O = \text{midpoint of hypotenuse} }. $$

Right-Triangle Circumradius

If the hypotenuse has length $c$, then:

$$ \boxed{ R=\frac c2 }. $$

Connection to Thales' Theorem

A right triangle inscribed in a circle has its hypotenuse as a diameter.

$$ \boxed{ c=2R }. $$

Location in an Obtuse Triangle

If the triangle is obtuse, the circumcenter lies outside the triangle.

$$ \boxed{ \text{obtuse triangle} \Rightarrow O \text{ outside} }. $$

Location Summary

Triangle Type Circumcenter Location
Acute Inside the triangle
Right Midpoint of the hypotenuse
Obtuse Outside the triangle

Coordinate Construction

For triangle vertices:

$$ A=(x_1,y_1),\qquad B=(x_2,y_2),\qquad C=(x_3,y_3), $$

the circumcenter can be found by writing equations for two perpendicular bisectors and solving them simultaneously.

Midpoint Step

The midpoint of $\overline{AB}$ is:

$$ \boxed{ M_{AB} = \left( \frac{x_1+x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1+y_2}{2} \right) }. $$

Slope Step

If the slope of $\overline{AB}$ is:

$$ m_{AB} = \frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}, $$

then the perpendicular-bisector slope, when both are finite and nonzero, is:

$$ \boxed{ m_{\perp} = -\frac1{m_{AB}} }. $$

Distance-Equation Method

An alternative coordinate method uses equal-distance equations.

If:

$$ O=(h,k), $$

then:

$$ (h-x_1)^2+(k-y_1)^2 = (h-x_2)^2+(k-y_2)^2 $$

and:

$$ (h-x_1)^2+(k-y_1)^2 = (h-x_3)^2+(k-y_3)^2. $$

Expanding causes the quadratic terms $h^2$ and $k^2$ to cancel, leaving two linear equations in $h$ and $k$.

General Coordinate Formula

Let:

$$ D= 2\left[ x_1(y_2-y_3) + x_2(y_3-y_1) + x_3(y_1-y_2) \right]. $$

For noncollinear vertices, $D\ne0$, and the circumcenter coordinates are:

$$ \boxed{ h= \frac{ (x_1^2+y_1^2)(y_2-y_3) +(x_2^2+y_2^2)(y_3-y_1) +(x_3^2+y_3^2)(y_1-y_2) }{D} } $$
$$ \boxed{ k= \frac{ (x_1^2+y_1^2)(x_3-x_2) +(x_2^2+y_2^2)(x_1-x_3) +(x_3^2+y_3^2)(x_2-x_1) }{D} }. $$

Example: Right Triangle

Consider:

$$ A=(0,0),\qquad B=(6,0),\qquad C=(0,8). $$

The right angle is at $A$, so the hypotenuse is $\overline{BC}$.

Its midpoint is:

$$ \left( \frac{6+0}{2}, \frac{0+8}{2} \right) = (3,4). $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ O=(3,4) }. $$

The hypotenuse length is:

$$ \sqrt{6^2+8^2}=10. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ R=5 }. $$

Circumradius from Side Length and Area

If the side lengths are $a$, $b$, and $c$ and the triangle area is $K$, then:

$$ \boxed{ R= \frac{abc}{4K} }. $$

Extended Law of Sines

The circumradius is also related to the triangle sides and angles by:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C} = 2R }. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ R= \frac{a}{2\sin A} = \frac{b}{2\sin B} = \frac{c}{2\sin C} }. $$

Equilateral Triangle

In an equilateral triangle, all major classical centers coincide:

$$ \boxed{ O=G=I=H }. $$

If the side length is $a$, then:

$$ \boxed{ R=\frac{a}{\sqrt3} }. $$

Isosceles Triangle

In an isosceles triangle, the circumcenter lies on the axis of symmetry.

That same line contains the centroid, incenter, and orthocenter.

Euler Line

In every non-equilateral triangle, the circumcenter $O$, centroid $G$, and orthocenter $H$ lie on one line called the Euler line.

$$ \boxed{ O,\ G,\ H \text{ are collinear} }. $$

Euler-Line Ratio

$$ \boxed{ OG:GH=1:2 }. $$

Equivalently:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf H = 3\mathbf G-2\mathbf O }. $$

Nine-Point Center

The nine-point center $N$ is the midpoint of the circumcenter and orthocenter:

$$ \boxed{ N= \frac{O+H}{2} }. $$

Nine-Point Radius

The nine-point circle has radius:

$$ \boxed{ \frac R2 }. $$

Circumcenter versus Centroid

The circumcenter is determined by perpendicular bisectors and is equidistant from the vertices.

The centroid is determined by medians and is the geometric average position of the vertices.

Circumcenter versus Incenter

The circumcenter is equidistant from the vertices:

$$ OA=OB=OC. $$

The incenter is equidistant from the side lines.

Circumcenter versus Orthocenter

The circumcenter is the intersection of perpendicular bisectors. The orthocenter is the intersection of altitude lines.

Perpendicular Bisector versus Altitude

A perpendicular bisector passes through the midpoint of a side and is perpendicular to that side.

An altitude passes through a vertex and is perpendicular to the opposite side or its extension.

Circumcenter as a Nearest Equal-Distance Point

The circumcenter is the unique point in the plane that has equal distance to the three noncollinear vertices.

$$ \boxed{ OA=OB=OC }. $$

Three Noncollinear Points

More generally, any three noncollinear points determine a unique circle. Its center is the intersection of the perpendicular bisectors of the segments joining the points.

Collinear Points

Three distinct collinear points do not determine a finite circumcircle, so there is no ordinary finite circumcenter for the degenerate triangle they form.

Compass-and-Straightedge Construction

A classical construction is:

  1. construct the perpendicular bisector of one side,
  2. construct the perpendicular bisector of another side,
  3. mark their intersection $O$,
  4. draw the circle centered at $O$ through any vertex.

Why the Third Vertex Lies on the Circle

Since $O$ is equidistant from all three vertices:

$$ OA=OB=OC, $$

a circle centered at $O$ through one vertex automatically passes through the other two.

Common Misconception: The Circumcenter Is Always inside the Triangle

It lies inside only for acute triangles. It lies on the triangle in the right case and outside in the obtuse case.

Common Misconception: The Circumcenter Is the Intersection of Medians

Medians meet at the centroid. Perpendicular bisectors meet at the circumcenter.

Common Misconception: The Circumcenter Is Equidistant from the Sides

Equal distance from the sides characterizes the incenter, not the circumcenter.

Common Misconception: Perpendicular Bisectors Must Pass through Vertices

A perpendicular bisector passes through the midpoint of a side. It generally does not pass through any triangle vertex.

Common Misconception: Every Triangle Center Is the Same Point

The classical centers generally differ. They coincide only in special cases, most notably the equilateral triangle.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$O$ "The circumcenter."
$OA=OB=OC$ "The circumcenter is equidistant from all three vertices."
$R=OA$ "R is the circumradius."
$R=\frac{abc}{4K}$ "The circumradius equals a b c divided by four times the area."
$\frac{a}{\sin A}=2R$ "a over sine A equals twice the circumradius."
$O,G,H$ collinear "The circumcenter, centroid, and orthocenter lie on the Euler line."

The prefix circum- signals something lying around or surrounding another object. Thus the circumcenter is the center of the circle surrounding the triangle through its vertices, while the circumradius is that circle's radius.

Defense Notation Advice: Associate circumcenter with perpendicular bisectors and equal distance to vertices. This separates it cleanly from the centroid, incenter, and orthocenter, which arise from medians, angle bisectors, and altitudes respectively.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: The circumcenter is the intersection of a triangle's perpendicular bisectors.
  • Common Symbol: $$ \boxed{ O }. $$
  • Equal-Distance Property: $$ \boxed{ OA=OB=OC }. $$
  • Circumcircle: The circumcenter is the center of the circle through the three triangle vertices.
  • Circumradius: $$ \boxed{ R=OA=OB=OC }. $$
  • Acute Triangle: Circumcenter lies inside.
  • Right Triangle: Circumcenter is the midpoint of the hypotenuse.
  • Right-Triangle Radius: $$ \boxed{ R=\frac c2 }. $$
  • Obtuse Triangle: Circumcenter lies outside.
  • Area Formula: $$ \boxed{ R=\frac{abc}{4K} }. $$
  • Extended Law of Sines: $$ \boxed{ \frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C} = 2R }. $$
  • Euler Line: $O$, $G$, and $H$ are collinear in a non-equilateral triangle.
  • Euler-Line Ratio: $$ \boxed{ OG:GH=1:2 }. $$
  • Nine-Point Center: The midpoint of the circumcenter and orthocenter.
  • Equilateral Triangle: $$ \boxed{ O=G=I=H }. $$
  • Coordinate Geometry: Solve two perpendicular-bisector equations or two equal-distance equations.
  • Construction: Two perpendicular bisectors are sufficient to locate the circumcenter.
  • Common Error: Do not assume the circumcenter always lies inside the triangle.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse perpendicular bisectors with medians or altitudes.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse equal distance from vertices with equal distance from sides.
  • Terminological Contrast: Circumcenter is the intersection of perpendicular bisectors and is equidistant from the vertices. Circumcircle is the circle through the triangle's vertices. Circumradius is the radius of the circumcircle. Centroid is the intersection of medians. Incenter is the intersection of internal angle bisectors and is equidistant from the sides. Orthocenter is the intersection of altitude lines.
