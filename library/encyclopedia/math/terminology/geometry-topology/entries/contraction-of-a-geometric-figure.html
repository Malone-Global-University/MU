Contraction of a Geometric Figure

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

A contraction of a geometric figure is a transformation that reduces the size of a figure while preserving its shape under the usual dilation model. In elementary geometry, this is typically a dilation with scale factor $k$ satisfying:

$$ \boxed{ 0

The word contraction comes from the idea of drawing inward or making something smaller. Geometrically, corresponding points of the image lie closer to the center of dilation than the corresponding points of the original figure.

$$ \boxed{ CP'=k\,CP, \qquad 0

where $C$ is the center of dilation, $P$ is a point of the original figure, and $P'$ is its image.

The term is closely related to reduction, shrinking dilation, and dilation with scale factor less than one. Usage varies by textbook and field, so the intended transformation should be identified explicitly when precision matters.

Operational Nuance

Contraction as a Dilation

In elementary Euclidean geometry, a contraction is usually treated as a special case of dilation.

$$ \boxed{ \text{contraction} = \text{dilation with }0

Each point moves along the ray determined by the center of dilation and the original point, but to a position nearer the center.

Center of Dilation

The center of dilation is the fixed point from which distances are scaled.

$$ \boxed{ C\mapsto C }. $$

The center remains unchanged under the transformation.

If the center is:

$$ C=(a,b), $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y) \mapsto \bigl( a+k(x-a), b+k(y-b) \bigr) }. $$

Vector Form

Using vectors:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf p' = \mathbf c + k(\mathbf p-\mathbf c) }. $$

This form works naturally in higher-dimensional Euclidean spaces as well.

Distance from the Center

Every distance from the center is multiplied by $k$:

$$ \boxed{ CP'=k\,CP }. $$

Side Lengths

Corresponding side lengths are multiplied by the same scale factor:

$$ \boxed{ L'=kL }. $$

Similarity

A geometric figure and its image under an ordinary positive dilation are similar:

$$ \boxed{ F\sim F' }. $$

Corresponding angles are congruent and corresponding lengths are proportional.

Angles

A contraction preserves angle measure:

$$ \boxed{ m\angle A = m\angle A' }. $$

Thus the figure becomes smaller without changing its angular shape.

Perimeter Scaling

Because every length is multiplied by $k$, perimeter is also multiplied by $k$:

$$ \boxed{ P'=kP }. $$

Area Scaling

In two dimensions, area is multiplied by the square of the scale factor:

$$ \boxed{ A'=k^2A }. $$

Volume Scaling

In three dimensions, volume is multiplied by:

$$ \boxed{ V'=k^3V }. $$

General Dimensional Scaling

In $n$ dimensions, an $n$-dimensional measure scales by:

$$ \boxed{ k^n }. $$

Example: Triangle

Suppose a triangle has side lengths:

$$ 6,\ 8,\ 10 $$

and is contracted by:

$$ k=\frac12. $$

The image has side lengths:

$$ \boxed{ 3,\ 4,\ 5 }. $$

Perimeter Example

The original perimeter is:

$$ 6+8+10=24. $$

The contracted perimeter is:

$$ \boxed{ \frac12(24)=12 }. $$

Area Example

If the original area is $24$, then under $k=1/2$:

$$ A' = \left(\frac12\right)^2(24) = 6. $$

Parallel Lines

Under a dilation, a line not passing through the center maps to a parallel line.

$$ \boxed{ \ell'\parallel\ell } $$

when $\ell$ does not pass through the center.

Lines through the Center

A line passing through the center of dilation maps onto itself as a set.

Collinearity

Contractions preserve collinearity. Points lying on one line before the transformation remain collinear afterward.

Betweenness

For a positive scale factor, the ordering of collinear points is preserved.

Orientation

A contraction with positive scale factor preserves orientation.

Clockwise vertex order remains clockwise, and counterclockwise order remains counterclockwise.

Shape versus Size

Under ordinary Euclidean dilation, shape is preserved in the sense of similarity, but size changes.

$$ \boxed{ \text{same shape} + \text{smaller scale} }. $$

Inverse Transformation

A contraction with factor $k$ can be reversed by a dilation with reciprocal factor:

$$ \boxed{ \frac1k>1 }. $$

Thus:

$$ D_{1/k}\circ D_k = I. $$

The two are opposite cases of dilation:

The Case k = 1

If:

$$ k=1, $$

every point remains where it started:

$$ \boxed{ P'=P }. $$

This is the identity dilation, not a contraction.

The Case k = 0

If the coordinate rule is extended formally to $k=0$, every point is sent to the center:

$$ \boxed{ P'\!=C }. $$

This collapses the entire figure to a single point and is ordinarily excluded from the standard definition of a geometric dilation because the transformation is no longer invertible and does not preserve similarity in the usual nondegenerate sense.

Negative Scale Factors

Some advanced or coordinate treatments allow a dilation factor $k<0$. Then the magnitude $|k|$ controls size while the sign places the image on the opposite side of the center.

However, when elementary geometry uses the term contraction, it usually means:

Fixed Point

For a nontrivial contraction centered at $C$, the center is the unique fixed point:

$$ \boxed{ D_k(C)=C }. $$

Transformation Matrix about the Origin

In two dimensions, contraction about the origin can be represented by:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} k&0\\ 0&k \end{bmatrix} }. $$

Acting on:

$$ \begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix} $$

produces:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} kx\\ky \end{bmatrix}. $$

Determinant

The determinant of the two-dimensional contraction matrix is:

$$ \boxed{ k^2 }. $$

This agrees with the area scale factor.

Three-Dimensional Matrix

Uniform contraction about the origin in three dimensions uses:

$$ \boxed{ kI_3 }. $$

Its determinant is:

$$ \boxed{ k^3 }, $$

matching the volume scale factor.

Uniform versus Nonuniform Scaling

A geometric dilation multiplies all directions by the same scale factor.

A transformation such as:

$$ (x,y)\mapsto \left( \frac12x, \frac13y \right) $$

reduces the figure in both coordinate directions but does not generally preserve shape.

It is better described as nonuniform scaling or anisotropic compression, not as an ordinary Euclidean dilation.

Contraction versus Compression

The words are sometimes used informally as synonyms, but they can imply different transformations.

A geometric contraction as dilation uses one scale factor in every direction:

$$ (x,y)\mapsto(kx,ky). $$

A compression may act in only one direction:

$$ (x,y)\mapsto(x,ky). $$

The latter generally changes shape rather than producing a similar figure.

Contraction versus Translation

A translation moves all points by the same vector and preserves size:

$$ \mathbf x\mapsto\mathbf x+\mathbf v. $$

A contraction changes distances according to a scale factor.

Contraction versus Rotation

A rotation changes direction about a center but preserves distance from that center. A contraction preserves ray direction from the center but decreases distance.

Contraction versus Reflection

A reflection reverses orientation and preserves distance. A positive contraction preserves orientation and reduces distance.

Contraction versus Similarity Transformation

A contraction is one example of a similarity transformation. Similarity transformations more generally may include dilation together with rigid motions.

Homothety

In some geometry literature, a dilation centered at a fixed point is called a homothety or homothetic transformation.

Coordinate Geometry

In coordinate geometry, contractions provide a direct way to construct similar figures numerically.

If:

$$ A=(2,4),\quad B=(6,4),\quad C=(4,8), $$

then a contraction about the origin with $k=1/2$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ A'=(1,2),\quad B'=(3,2),\quad C'=(2,4) }. $$

Scale Drawings

Contractions are used in scale drawings when a large object is represented by a smaller similar figure.

If the drawing scale is:

$$ 1:100, $$

then drawing lengths are:

$$ \boxed{ \frac1{100} } $$

of corresponding actual lengths.

Maps and Models

Maps, architectural drawings, miniature models, and diagrams commonly use uniform geometric reduction to preserve proportional relationships while decreasing size.

Computer Graphics

Uniform scaling by a factor less than one is the computational analogue of contracting a geometric figure.

In homogeneous coordinates, scaling can be incorporated into transformation matrices and combined with translations and rotations.

Fractal Geometry

Contractive similarity maps play a major role in iterated function systems used to construct self-similar fractals.

Repeatedly applying smaller-scale copies can generate structures such as certain Cantor-type sets and self-similar geometric patterns.

Metric-Space Meaning of Contraction

In analysis, a contraction mapping has a related but more general meaning. A map $T$ is contractive if there is a constant $0\le q<1$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ d(Tx,Ty) \le q\,d(x,y) }. $$

This notion is broader than contracting a geometric figure by a Euclidean dilation.

Banach Contraction Principle Connection

In a complete metric space, an appropriate contraction mapping has a unique fixed point and repeated iteration converges toward it.

This resembles repeated geometric contraction toward a center, but the theorem belongs to analysis rather than elementary transformation geometry.

Common Misconception: A Contraction Changes Shape

Under the ordinary dilation meaning, a contraction preserves shape through similarity. Nonuniform squeezing is a different transformation.

Common Misconception: Every Coordinate Reduction Is a Dilation

Multiplying $x$ and $y$ by different factors generally creates a nonuniform scaling, not a dilation.

Common Misconception: Scale Factor One-Half Halves Area

A scale factor of $1/2$ halves lengths but quarters areas:

$$ \boxed{ \left(\frac12\right)^2 = \frac14 }. $$

Common Misconception: Scale Factor One-Half Halves Volume

Three-dimensional volume scales by the cube:

$$ \boxed{ \left(\frac12\right)^3 = \frac18 }. $$

Common Misconception: The Center Moves

The center of dilation is fixed under the transformation.

Common Misconception: Contraction Is a Rigid Motion

Contractions do not preserve distances unless $k=1$, which is not a nontrivial contraction.

Common Misconception: k = 0 Is an Ordinary Contraction Dilation

The zero-factor map collapses the figure to the center and is usually excluded from standard dilation terminology.

Common Misconception: A Smaller Figure Is Automatically a Contraction Image

Merely being smaller is insufficient. Under dilation, corresponding lengths must all share the same scale factor and corresponding angles must agree.

Common Misconception: Contraction and Compression Always Mean the Same Thing

Some elementary sources use the words loosely, but geometric contraction by dilation is uniform, while compression may refer to directional scaling.

In elementary geometry, one may hear “contract the figure by a factor of one-half,” “dilate the figure by a scale factor of one-half,” or “reduce the figure to half size.” The second wording is usually the most mathematically explicit because it identifies both the transformation and its scale factor.

The phrase half size should be used cautiously. A scale factor of $1/2$ means every corresponding length is halved; it does not mean that area or volume is halved.
