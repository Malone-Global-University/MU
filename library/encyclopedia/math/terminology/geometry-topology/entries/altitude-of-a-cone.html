Altitude of a Cone

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

The altitude of a cone is the perpendicular segment, or the length of that segment, from the cone's apex to the plane containing its base.

If $A$ is the apex and $H$ is the foot of the perpendicular on the base plane, then:

$$ \boxed{ AH\perp \text{base plane} }. $$

The length:

$$ \boxed{ h=AH } $$

is commonly called the cone's height or altitude.

The word altitude emphasizes perpendicular height. It does not mean the visually longest segment from the apex to the base, nor does it mean a segment following the cone's sloping surface.

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \text{altitude} = \text{perpendicular distance from apex to base plane} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Perpendicular Height

The defining feature of a cone's altitude is perpendicularity to the base plane. The altitude therefore represents the shortest distance from the apex to that plane.

$$ \boxed{ h=d(\text{apex},\text{base plane}) }. $$

Right Circular Cone

In a right circular cone, the foot of the altitude lies at the center of the circular base.

If the center of the base is $O$ and the apex is $A$, then:

$$ \boxed{ AO=h } $$

and:

$$ AO\perp \text{base plane}. $$

In this special case, the altitude lies along the axis of the cone.

Oblique Cone

In an oblique cone, the apex is not positioned directly above the center of the base.

The altitude is still the perpendicular from the apex to the base plane, but its foot need not be the center of the base.

$$ \boxed{ \text{oblique cone} \Longrightarrow \text{altitude need not pass through base center} }. $$

Altitude vs. Slant Height

The slant height of a right circular cone runs from the apex to a point on the circumference of the base along the lateral surface.

It is commonly written:

$$ \ell. $$

The altitude is commonly written:

$$ h. $$

These are different quantities.

$$ \boxed{ h\ne \ell } $$

for an ordinary nondegenerate right circular cone.

Right-Triangle Relationship

In a right circular cone, the radius $r$, altitude $h$, and slant height $\ell$ form a right triangle.

$$ \boxed{ \ell^2=r^2+h^2 }. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ h=\sqrt{\ell^2-r^2} } $$

when $\ell$ and $r$ are known.

Example

Suppose a right circular cone has radius:

$$ r=5 $$

and slant height:

$$ \ell=13. $$

Then:

$$ h^2 = 13^2-5^2 = 169-25 = 144, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ h=12 }. $$

Volume

The volume of a cone is:

$$ \boxed{ V=\frac13Bh }, $$

where $B$ is the area of the base and $h$ is the perpendicular altitude.

For a circular base:

$$ B=\pi r^2, $$

giving:

$$ \boxed{ V=\frac13\pi r^2h }. $$

Altitude from Volume

Rearranging the volume formula gives:

$$ \boxed{ h=\frac{3V}{B} }. $$

For a circular cone:

$$ \boxed{ h=\frac{3V}{\pi r^2} }. $$

Oblique Cone Volume

The same volume formula applies to an oblique cone:

$$ \boxed{ V=\frac13Bh }. $$

The value $h$ must still be the perpendicular distance from the apex to the base plane, not the length of a sloping segment.

Surface Area Does Not Use the Altitude Directly

For a right circular cone, the lateral surface area is:

$$ \boxed{ L=\pi r\ell }. $$

The total surface area is:

$$ \boxed{ S=\pi r^2+\pi r\ell }. $$

These formulas use the slant height $\ell$, not the altitude $h$, although one may compute $\ell$ from $r$ and $h$ using the Pythagorean Theorem.

Common Misconception: The altitude and slant height of a cone are not interchangeable. The altitude is perpendicular to the base plane; the slant height follows the cone's side.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$h$ "The height," or "the altitude of the cone."
$AH\perp\text{ base plane}$ "A H is perpendicular to the base plane."
$V=\frac13Bh$ "Volume equals one-third base area times height."
$V=\frac13\pi r^2h$ "Volume equals one-third pi r squared h."
$\ell^2=r^2+h^2$ "Slant height squared equals radius squared plus height squared."
$h=\sqrt{\ell^2-r^2}$ "Height equals the square root of slant height squared minus radius squared."

In geometry problems, the words height and altitude are commonly used interchangeably for the perpendicular distance:

$$ \boxed{ h=\text{perpendicular height} }. $$

The phrase slant height should always be treated separately:

$$ \boxed{ \ell=\text{sloping lateral length} }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: If a diagram labels both $h$ and $\ell$, identify the right-angle marker at the base. The segment meeting the base plane perpendicularly is the altitude.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: The altitude of a cone is the perpendicular distance from the apex to the base plane.
  • Common Symbol: $$ \boxed{h}. $$
  • Right Circular Cone: The altitude joins the apex to the center of the circular base.
  • Axis of a Right Cone: In a right circular cone, the altitude coincides with the cone's axis.
  • Oblique Cone: The altitude need not pass through the center of the base.
  • Perpendicular Requirement: $$ \boxed{ \text{altitude}\perp\text{base plane} }. $$
  • Shortest Distance: The altitude gives the shortest distance from the apex to the base plane.
  • Slant Height Contrast: Slant height follows the lateral surface and is generally longer than the altitude in a right circular cone.
  • Right-Triangle Relation: $$ \boxed{ \ell^2=r^2+h^2 }. $$
  • Altitude from Slant Height: $$ \boxed{ h=\sqrt{\ell^2-r^2} }. $$
  • Slant Height from Altitude: $$ \boxed{ \ell=\sqrt{r^2+h^2} }. $$
  • Radius from Altitude and Slant Height: $$ \boxed{ r=\sqrt{\ell^2-h^2} }. $$
  • Volume: $$ \boxed{ V=\frac13Bh }. $$
  • Circular Cone Volume: $$ \boxed{ V=\frac13\pi r^2h }. $$
  • Altitude from Volume: $$ \boxed{ h=\frac{3V}{B} }. $$
  • Circular Altitude from Volume: $$ \boxed{ h=\frac{3V}{\pi r^2} }. $$
  • Oblique Cone Volume: The same volume formula applies, provided $h$ is measured perpendicular to the base plane.
  • Cavalieri Principle: Right and oblique cones with equal base areas and equal perpendicular heights have equal volumes when their corresponding cross-sectional areas agree in the required way.
  • Lateral Surface Area: For a right circular cone: $$ \boxed{ L=\pi r\ell }. $$
  • Total Surface Area: For a right circular cone: $$ \boxed{ S=\pi r^2+\pi r\ell }. $$
  • Surface Area Caution: Surface-area formulas use slant height rather than altitude directly.
  • Cross-Section Through the Axis: A plane through the axis of a right circular cone produces an isosceles triangle whose altitude is the cone's altitude.
  • Axial Triangle: In that cross-section, the base of the isosceles triangle has length: $$ 2r, $$ the equal sides have length: $$ \ell, $$ and its altitude has length: $$ h. $$
  • Similar Cross-Sections: Cross-sections parallel to the base of a right circular cone are circles whose radii scale linearly with distance from the apex.
  • Coordinate Geometry: If the base lies in the plane: $$ z=0 $$ and the apex is: $$ (a,b,h), $$ then the perpendicular distance to the base plane is: $$ \boxed{|h|}. $$
  • General Point-to-Plane Distance: For a base plane: $$ Ax+By+Cz+D=0 $$ and apex: $$ (x_0,y_0,z_0), $$ the altitude length is: $$ \boxed{ h= \frac{|Ax_0+By_0+Cz_0+D|} {\sqrt{A^2+B^2+C^2}} }. $$
  • Vector Geometry: The altitude direction is parallel to a normal vector of the base plane.
  • Normal Vector: If $\mathbf{n}$ is normal to the base plane, the altitude line from the apex has direction parallel to $\mathbf{n}$.
  • Scaling: If every linear dimension of a cone is multiplied by $k$, then: $$ h\mapsto kh. $$
  • Volume Scaling: Under the same dilation: $$ \boxed{ V\mapsto k^3V }. $$
  • Similarity: Similar cones have proportional altitudes, radii, and slant heights.
  • Similar-Cone Ratio: If two similar cones have scale factor $k$, then: $$ \frac{h_2}{h_1} = \frac{r_2}{r_1} = \frac{\ell_2}{\ell_1} = k. $$
  • Volume Ratio: For similar cones: $$ \boxed{ \frac{V_2}{V_1}=k^3 }. $$
  • Frustum Connection: When the top of a cone is removed by a plane parallel to its base, the heights of the original cone and removed smaller cone are often used to establish similarity ratios.
  • Dimension: Altitude is a length quantity and is therefore measured in linear units such as centimeters, meters, or feet.
  • Not an Area: The altitude itself is not measured in square units.
  • Not a Volume: The altitude itself is not measured in cubic units.
  • Diagram Orientation: The cone need not be drawn upright. The altitude is determined by perpendicularity, not by the vertical direction of the page.
  • Terminological Contrast: The altitude $h$ is the perpendicular distance from the apex to the base plane. The slant height $\ell$ runs along the side of a right circular cone. The radius $r$ runs from the center of the circular base to its circumference. In a right circular cone these three lengths form a right triangle satisfying $\ell^2=r^2+h^2$.
