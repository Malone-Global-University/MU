Box and Whisker Plot

Subfield: Probability & Statistical Inference

Etymology & Roots

A box and whisker plot, commonly shortened to box plot, is a statistical display that summarizes the location and spread of numerical data using quartiles, a median, a rectangular box, and lines called whiskers.

The box usually extends from the first quartile $Q_1$ to the third quartile $Q_3$:

$$ \boxed{ Q_1\longrightarrow Q_3 }. $$

A line inside the box marks the median:

$$ \boxed{ Q_2=\text{median} }. $$

The whiskers extend beyond the box to show additional spread in the data. The precise endpoints of the whiskers depend on the convention being used.

$$ \boxed{ \text{box} = \text{middle 50\% of the data} } $$

The display is especially useful for comparing distributions because it compresses information about center, spread, asymmetry, and possible outliers into a compact graphical form.

Operational Nuance

Ordered Data

Before quartiles are determined, numerical observations are arranged from least to greatest:

$$ \boxed{ x_{(1)}\le x_{(2)}\le\cdots\le x_{(n)} }. $$

The parenthesized subscripts indicate positions in the ordered sample rather than original observation labels.

Five-Number Summary

In many elementary treatments, a box and whisker plot is described using the five-number summary:

$$ \boxed{ \text{minimum}, \quad Q_1, \quad \text{median}, \quad Q_3, \quad \text{maximum} }. $$

First Quartile

The first quartile $Q_1$ marks approximately the 25th percentile of the ordered data.

$$ \boxed{ Q_1\approx P_{25} }. $$

Median

The median, also called the second quartile $Q_2$, divides the ordered observations into lower and upper halves.

$$ \boxed{ Q_2=\operatorname{median} }. $$

Third Quartile

The third quartile $Q_3$ marks approximately the 75th percentile.

$$ \boxed{ Q_3\approx P_{75} }. $$

The Box

The rectangular box extends from $Q_1$ to $Q_3$. Its length represents the interquartile range.

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{IQR}=Q_3-Q_1 }. $$

Middle 50 Percent

Approximately one-half of the observations lie between $Q_1$ and $Q_3$.

$$ \boxed{ Q_1\le x\le Q_3 \quad\text{contains the central 50\% of the distribution} }. $$

The Median Line

A line is drawn through the box at the median. Its location within the box helps reveal whether the middle half of the distribution is approximately symmetric or stretched more strongly in one direction.

The Whiskers

Whiskers extend outward from the box. Their exact meaning must be determined from the plotting convention.

Elementary Min-Max Convention

In many introductory treatments, the lower whisker extends from $Q_1$ to the minimum and the upper whisker extends from $Q_3$ to the maximum:

$$ \boxed{ \min \longrightarrow Q_1 \longrightarrow Q_2 \longrightarrow Q_3 \longrightarrow \max }. $$

Under this convention, the box plot directly represents the five-number summary.

Tukey-Style Convention

A commonly used modern box-plot convention treats sufficiently distant observations as possible outliers rather than extending the whiskers all the way to the minimum and maximum.

First compute:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{IQR}=Q_3-Q_1 }. $$

Then define the usual inner fences:

$$ \boxed{ L=Q_1-1.5(\operatorname{IQR}) } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ U=Q_3+1.5(\operatorname{IQR}) }. $$

Whiskers under the 1.5 IQR Rule

Under the common Tukey-style rule, the whiskers extend to the most extreme observed values that remain within the fences.

The lower whisker therefore ends at the smallest observed value satisfying:

$$ x\ge Q_1-1.5\operatorname{IQR}, $$

while the upper whisker ends at the largest observed value satisfying:

$$ x\le Q_3+1.5\operatorname{IQR}. $$

Outliers

Observations beyond those fences are commonly plotted individually as possible outliers.

$$ \boxed{ xQ_3+1.5\operatorname{IQR} } $$

Important Outlier Caution

The $1.5\operatorname{IQR}$ rule is a screening convention, not proof that an observation is erroneous, impossible, or generated by a different population.

$$ \boxed{ \text{flagged as an outlier} \ne \text{proven bad data} }. $$

Whisker Convention Must Be Known

Because software packages and textbooks can define whiskers differently, a box plot should be interpreted together with its stated convention whenever precise endpoint meaning matters.

$$ \boxed{ \text{whisker} \not\equiv \text{minimum or maximum in every box plot} }. $$

Example Data

Consider the ordered data:

$$ 2,\ 4,\ 5,\ 7,\ 8,\ 10,\ 12,\ 14,\ 15. $$

There are $9$ observations, so the median is the fifth value:

$$ \boxed{ Q_2=8 }. $$

Finding Q₁ and Q₃

Under the common convention of excluding the overall median before finding the medians of the two halves, the lower half is:

$$ 2,\ 4,\ 5,\ 7, $$

giving:

$$ Q_1=\frac{4+5}{2} = \boxed{4.5}. $$

The upper half is:

$$ 10,\ 12,\ 14,\ 15, $$

giving:

$$ Q_3=\frac{12+14}{2} = \boxed{13}. $$

Interquartile Range

$$ \operatorname{IQR} = 13-4.5 = \boxed{8.5}. $$

Five-Number Summary

Under that quartile convention:

$$ \boxed{ 2,\ 4.5,\ 8,\ 13,\ 15 }. $$

Quartile Conventions Vary

There is more than one accepted method for calculating sample quartiles, particularly for small data sets or when interpolation is involved.

As a result, different calculators or statistical software may report slightly different values of $Q_1$ and $Q_3$ for the same finite data set.

$$ \boxed{ \text{state or recognize the quartile convention when exact values matter} }. $$

Range

In a min-max box plot, the total numerical span is:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{range} = \max-\min }. $$

In an outlier-aware box plot, the visual distance between whisker ends is not necessarily the full range because observations may be plotted beyond the whiskers.

IQR versus Range

The range uses the two most extreme observations and is therefore highly sensitive to extreme values.

The IQR depends only on $Q_1$ and $Q_3$ and is more resistant to extreme observations.

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{IQR}=Q_3-Q_1 } $$

Robustness

The median and IQR are called relatively resistant or robust summaries because a small number of extremely large or small observations usually affect them less than they affect the mean and range.

Center

The median line provides the primary measure of center shown by an ordinary box plot.

The arithmetic mean is not ordinarily displayed unless an additional marker is deliberately added.

Spread

The width or length of the box along the data scale represents the IQR.

A larger box therefore indicates greater spread among the central half of the data.

Comparing Two Box Plots

Side-by-side box plots make it possible to compare:

  • medians,
  • interquartile ranges,
  • overall or non-outlier spread,
  • possible asymmetry,
  • possible outliers.

Reading the Median

A median farther to the right on a horizontal common scale indicates a larger median value.

When comparing groups, all plots must use the same numerical scale for visual distances to be directly meaningful.

Reading the IQR

If one box is longer than another along the measurement axis, the corresponding group has a larger IQR.

Possible Asymmetry

If the median lies near the center of the box and the two sides of the plot have similar lengths, the distribution may appear approximately symmetric.

If one side of the box or one whisker is noticeably longer, the data may have more spread in that direction.

Skewness Caution

A box plot can suggest asymmetry, but it does not display the full shape of a distribution. Declaring a distribution definitively skewed from a box plot alone can therefore oversimplify the data.

Right-Skewed Pattern

A distribution with a longer upper side or upper whisker and high-side outliers may suggest a longer right tail.

Left-Skewed Pattern

A longer lower side or lower whisker and low-side outliers may suggest a longer left tail.

What a Box Plot Does Not Show

An ordinary box plot generally does not reveal:

  • every individual observation,
  • the exact number of repeated values,
  • gaps inside quartile regions,
  • multiple peaks or modes clearly,
  • the precise density shape.

Different Data Can Produce the Same Box Plot

Two very different data distributions can share the same quartiles and whisker endpoints.

$$ \boxed{ \text{same box plot summary} \not\Rightarrow \text{same underlying data} }. $$

Horizontal Box Plot

A box plot may be drawn horizontally, with values increasing from left to right.

Vertical Box Plot

It may instead be drawn vertically, with values increasing from bottom to top.

Orientation changes the presentation but not the statistical meaning.

Notched Box Plot

Some box plots place a narrowing or notch around the median to display an approximate indication of uncertainty surrounding the median.

A notch is an additional convention and is not part of the basic box-and-whisker definition.

Variable-Width Box Plot

Some statistical displays vary the physical width of each box to encode another quantity, often sample size. Again, this is an extension rather than a required feature of a standard box plot.

Box Plot versus Histogram

A histogram divides numerical data into intervals and displays frequencies or densities.

A box plot instead emphasizes quartiles and robust spread.

$$ \boxed{ \text{histogram} \rightarrow \text{distribution shape}, \qquad \text{box plot} \rightarrow \text{compact numerical summary} }. $$

Box Plot versus Dot Plot

A dot plot can preserve individual observations and repetitions, whereas a box plot deliberately compresses the data into summary statistics.

Box Plot versus Stem-and-Leaf Plot

A stem-and-leaf display retains much of the original numerical information. A box plot does not; it prioritizes rapid comparison of center and spread.

Box Plot versus Bar Chart

A bar chart ordinarily compares values or frequencies across categories. A box plot summarizes the distribution of numerical observations within one or more groups.

Quartiles as Position Measures

The three quartiles partition ordered data conceptually into four sections:

$$ \boxed{ \min \;|\; Q_1 \;|\; Q_2 \;|\; Q_3 \;|\; \max }. $$

Each quartile interval contains approximately one-quarter of the observations, but equal numbers of observations do not imply equal numerical interval lengths.

Equal Percentages, Unequal Distances

The interval from $Q_1$ to the median may be much shorter than the interval from the median to $Q_3$ even though each contains approximately 25% of the data.

$$ \boxed{ \text{equal proportions of observations} \not\Rightarrow \text{equal numerical widths} }. $$

Constructing a Basic Box Plot

A common elementary procedure is:

  1. sort the data;
  2. find the minimum;
  3. find $Q_1$;
  4. find the median;
  5. find $Q_3$;
  6. find the maximum;
  7. draw the box from $Q_1$ to $Q_3$;
  8. draw the median line;
  9. draw whiskers according to the chosen convention.

Constructing an Outlier-Aware Box Plot

If the $1.5\operatorname{IQR}$ convention is being used, the procedure additionally requires:

  1. compute the IQR;
  2. compute the lower and upper fences;
  3. identify observations outside the fences;
  4. draw whiskers to the most extreme non-outlier observations;
  5. plot flagged observations separately.

Lower Fence

$$ \boxed{ Q_1-1.5(Q_3-Q_1) }. $$

Upper Fence

$$ \boxed{ Q_3+1.5(Q_3-Q_1) }. $$

Fences Are Not Whisker Endpoints

Under the common Tukey-style convention, the fence values themselves are usually not drawn as whisker endpoints unless an actual observation occurs there.

The whisker ends at the most extreme observation still inside the corresponding fence.

$$ \boxed{ \text{fence} \ne \text{whisker endpoint in general} }. $$

Extreme Outlier Conventions

Some statistical systems further distinguish observations more than $3\operatorname{IQR}$ below $Q_1$ or above $Q_3$. Symbol choices vary, so such markings should be interpreted using the accompanying legend or software documentation.

Effect of Adding an Extreme Value

Adding one very large observation can change the maximum dramatically while leaving the median and quartiles relatively stable.

This illustrates why box plots are useful when robust summaries are desired.

Effect of Translation

If a constant $c$ is added to every observation, then every quartile and whisker endpoint shifts by $c$:

$$ \boxed{ Q_1'=Q_1+c,\quad Q_2'=Q_2+c,\quad Q_3'=Q_3+c }. $$

The IQR remains unchanged:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{IQR}'=\operatorname{IQR} }. $$

Effect of Positive Scaling

If every observation is multiplied by $c>0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ Q_1'=cQ_1,\quad Q_2'=cQ_2,\quad Q_3'=cQ_3 } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{IQR}'=c\operatorname{IQR} }. $$

Units

Quartiles, medians, whisker endpoints, and the IQR all use the same physical units as the original data.

If observations are measured in centimeters, the IQR is also measured in centimeters.

Common Misconception: The Box Contains All the Data

The box itself represents only the interval from $Q_1$ to $Q_3$, corresponding to the central half of the ordered data.

Common Misconception: Whiskers Always Reach the Minimum and Maximum

That is true for one common elementary convention but not for all box plots. Outlier-aware box plots commonly stop the whiskers at the most extreme non-outlying observations.

Common Misconception: Each Section Has Equal Width

Quartiles divide observations by position, not the numerical scale into equal lengths.

Common Misconception: Every Point Beyond a Whisker Is an Error

Such points may be genuine observations. They deserve investigation, not automatic deletion.

Common Misconception: A Long Box Means More Data

The box length represents numerical spread, not sample size. Two groups may contain very different numbers of observations but display boxes of similar length.

Common Misconception: The Median Is the Center of the Box

The median lies somewhere between $Q_1$ and $Q_3$, but it need not lie halfway between them numerically.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$Q_1$ "The first quartile."
$Q_2$ "The second quartile, or median."
$Q_3$ "The third quartile."
$\operatorname{IQR}=Q_3-Q_1$ "The interquartile range equals Q three minus Q one."
$Q_1-1.5\operatorname{IQR}$ "The lower one-point-five-IQR fence."
$Q_3+1.5\operatorname{IQR}$ "The upper one-point-five-IQR fence."

Box plot and box-and-whisker plot are commonly used for the same family of statistical displays. The shorter term box plot is especially common in statistical writing and software.

Defense Notation Advice: Before interpreting the whiskers, identify the convention used. Also determine how the quartiles were calculated if exact numerical replication matters. The box, median, IQR, whiskers, and outlier markers describe different features and should not be treated as interchangeable.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: A box and whisker plot summarizes numerical data using quartiles, a median, a box, and whiskers.
  • Common Synonym: Box plot.
  • First Quartile: $Q_1$ marks approximately the 25th percentile.
  • Median: $$ \boxed{ Q_2=\operatorname{median} }. $$
  • Third Quartile: $Q_3$ marks approximately the 75th percentile.
  • Box: Extends from $Q_1$ to $Q_3$.
  • Middle Half: The box represents the central 50% of the ordered distribution.
  • Interquartile Range: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{IQR}=Q_3-Q_1 }. $$
  • Five-Number Summary: Minimum, $Q_1$, median, $Q_3$, maximum.
  • Elementary Whisker Convention: Whiskers may extend to the minimum and maximum.
  • Tukey-Style Convention: Whiskers commonly extend to the most extreme observed values within the $1.5\operatorname{IQR}$ fences.
  • Lower Fence: $$ \boxed{ Q_1-1.5\operatorname{IQR} }. $$
  • Upper Fence: $$ \boxed{ Q_3+1.5\operatorname{IQR} }. $$
  • Possible Outliers: Observations outside the chosen fences are commonly plotted individually.
  • Outlier Caution: A flagged observation is not automatically erroneous data.
  • Whisker Caution: Whisker definitions vary by convention.
  • Quartile Caution: Different accepted quartile algorithms can produce different values for small samples.
  • Range: $$ \boxed{ \max-\min } $$ when the actual extreme observations are being compared.
  • Robust Spread: The IQR is less sensitive to extreme observations than the range.
  • Center: The ordinary box plot emphasizes the median rather than the mean.
  • Comparison: Side-by-side box plots are useful for comparing centers, spreads, asymmetry, and possible outliers across groups.
  • Symmetry: Similar box halves and whisker lengths may suggest approximate symmetry.
  • Skewness Caution: A box plot may suggest tail asymmetry but does not reveal the complete distribution shape.
  • Horizontal or Vertical: Either orientation preserves the statistical meaning.
  • Histogram Contrast: A histogram emphasizes frequency distribution and shape; a box plot emphasizes quartiles and robust spread.
  • Dot-Plot Contrast: A dot plot preserves individual observations more directly.
  • Bar-Chart Contrast: A bar chart compares categorical values or counts; a box plot summarizes a numerical distribution.
  • Translation: Adding a constant shifts all quartiles by that constant but leaves the IQR unchanged.
  • Positive Scaling: Multiplying all observations by $c>0$ multiplies quartiles and the IQR by $c$.
  • Common Error: Do not assume the box contains every observation.
  • Common Error: Do not assume whiskers always represent the sample minimum and maximum.
  • Common Error: Do not assume each quartile occupies an equal numerical width on the scale.
  • Common Error: Do not automatically delete observations marked as outliers.
  • Common Error: Do not interpret a longer box as meaning a larger sample size.
  • Terminological Contrast: Box plot is the compact statistical display. Quartiles divide ordered data by position. Median is the central quartile $Q_2$. Interquartile range measures the width of the middle 50%. Whiskers show additional spread according to a stated convention. Fence is a calculated threshold used in one common outlier rule and is not necessarily a whisker endpoint. Outlier is an observation unusually distant according to a chosen criterion, not necessarily an error.
