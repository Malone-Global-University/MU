Conic Sections

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

Conic sections, often shortened to conics, are curves obtained by intersecting a plane with a double circular cone.

$$ \boxed{ \text{plane} \cap \text{double cone} \longrightarrow \text{conic section} }. $$

The four principal nondegenerate conic sections are:

$$ \boxed{ \text{circle,\quad ellipse,\quad parabola,\quad hyperbola} }. $$

The word conic derives from cone, while section refers to the cross-section created by slicing the cone with a plane.

Historically, the conic sections became central objects in Greek geometry and later became foundational in analytic geometry, astronomy, mechanics, optics, and mathematical physics.

Operational Nuance

The Double Cone

The classical geometric construction uses a double cone, consisting of two opposite nappes meeting at a common vertex.

$$ \boxed{ \text{double cone} = \text{upper nappe} + \text{lower nappe} }. $$

How the Cutting Plane Determines the Conic

The type of conic depends on the inclination and position of the cutting plane relative to the cone.

  • A plane perpendicular to the cone axis produces a circle.
  • A tilted plane cutting one nappe and not parallel to a generator produces an ellipse.
  • A plane parallel to a generator produces a parabola.
  • A plane cutting both nappes produces a hyperbola.

Circle

A circle is the set of points in a plane at a fixed distance from a fixed point called the center.

$$ \boxed{ (x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=r^2 }. $$

Here $(h,k)$ is the center and $r>0$ is the radius.

Ellipse

An ellipse is the set of points for which the sum of the distances to two fixed points, called foci, is constant.

$$ \boxed{ PF_1+PF_2=\text{constant} }. $$

A standard horizontal ellipse has equation:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{(x-h)^2}{a^2} + \frac{(y-k)^2}{b^2} =1, \qquad a>b>0 }. $$

Parabola

A parabola is the set of points equidistant from a fixed point, called the focus, and a fixed line, called the directrix.

$$ \boxed{ PF=d(P,\text{directrix}) }. $$

A standard vertical parabola has equation:

$$ \boxed{ (x-h)^2=4p(y-k) }. $$

Its focus is:

$$ (h,k+p), $$

and its directrix is:

$$ y=k-p. $$

Hyperbola

A hyperbola is the set of points for which the absolute difference of distances to two fixed foci is constant.

$$ \boxed{ |PF_1-PF_2|=\text{constant} }. $$

A standard horizontal hyperbola has equation:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{(x-h)^2}{a^2} - \frac{(y-k)^2}{b^2} =1 }. $$

Circle as a Special Ellipse

Analytically, a circle may be regarded as a special ellipse whose two semiaxes are equal:

$$ \boxed{ a=b=r }. $$

Its two foci then coincide at the center.

Eccentricity

A unified way to classify nondegenerate conics uses eccentricity $e$.

In a focus-directrix formulation:

$$ \boxed{ PF=e\,d(P,\ell) } $$

where $F$ is a focus and $\ell$ is the corresponding directrix.

Eccentricity Classification

Conic Eccentricity
Circle $e=0$ in the standard limiting/special-case classification
Ellipse $0<e<1$
Parabola $e=1$
Hyperbola $e>1$

Ellipse Parameters

For:

$$ \frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1, \qquad a>b>0, $$

the focal distance $c$ satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ c^2=a^2-b^2 }. $$

The eccentricity is:

$$ \boxed{ e=\frac{c}{a} }. $$

Hyperbola Parameters

For:

$$ \frac{x^2}{a^2}-\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1, $$

the focal distance satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ c^2=a^2+b^2 }. $$

Again:

$$ \boxed{ e=\frac{c}{a}>1 }. $$

Parabola Eccentricity

For every parabola:

$$ \boxed{e=1}. $$

This reflects the equality of the distance to the focus and distance to the directrix.

Standard Forms

Conic Typical Standard Form
Circle $(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=r^2$
Ellipse $\frac{(x-h)^2}{a^2}+\frac{(y-k)^2}{b^2}=1$
Parabola $(x-h)^2=4p(y-k)$
Hyperbola $\frac{(x-h)^2}{a^2}-\frac{(y-k)^2}{b^2}=1$

General Second-Degree Equation

A general conic in Cartesian coordinates can be written:

$$ \boxed{ Ax^2+Bxy+Cy^2+Dx+Ey+F=0 }. $$

The quadratic terms determine much of the conic's basic classification.

Discriminant Classification

For the general quadratic equation, the quantity:

$$ \boxed{ \Delta=B^2-4AC } $$

helps classify the conic, subject to nondegeneracy and other coefficient conditions.

Condition Typical Conic Type
$B^2-4AC<0$ Ellipse-type, including circles
$B^2-4AC=0$ Parabola-type
$B^2-4AC>0$ Hyperbola-type

Rotation of Axes

The mixed term $Bxy$ often indicates that the principal axes of the conic are rotated relative to the coordinate axes.

A suitable rotation of coordinates can eliminate the $xy$ term in the nondegenerate cases.

Translation of Axes

Linear terms can often be simplified by translating the coordinate system:

$$ x=X+h,\qquad y=Y+k. $$

Completing the square is the elementary algebraic version of this process.

Completing the Square

Consider:

$$ x^2+y^2-6x+4y-12=0. $$

Grouping and completing squares:

$$ (x-3)^2+(y+2)^2=25. $$

Thus the conic is a circle centered at:

$$ \boxed{(3,-2)} $$

with radius:

$$ \boxed{5}. $$

Symmetry

Standard conics possess characteristic symmetries.

  • A circle has infinitely many reflection axes through its center.
  • An ellipse has two principal reflection axes.
  • A standard parabola has one axis of symmetry.
  • A hyperbola has two principal reflection axes and central symmetry.

Centers

Circles, ellipses, and hyperbolas have centers in the ordinary affine sense. A parabola does not.

$$ \boxed{ \text{circle, ellipse, hyperbola: central conics} } $$

while:

$$ \boxed{ \text{parabola: noncentral conic} }. $$

Vertices

Conics have distinguished points called vertices.

  • An ellipse has vertices at the ends of its principal axes.
  • A parabola has one vertex.
  • A hyperbola has two principal vertices.

Foci

Ellipses and hyperbolas have two foci. A parabola has one focus. A circle can be treated as an ellipse whose two foci coincide at the center.

Directrices

Ellipses, parabolas, and hyperbolas can all be described by focus-directrix relationships. The directrix is especially visible in the standard definition of a parabola.

Major and Minor Axes of an Ellipse

The longest diameter of an ellipse is the major axis, and the perpendicular shorter diameter through the center is the minor axis.

Their lengths are:

$$ \boxed{ 2a \qquad\text{and}\qquad 2b }. $$

Transverse and Conjugate Axes of a Hyperbola

A hyperbola has a transverse axis passing through its vertices and foci, and a perpendicular conjugate axis.

Hyperbola Asymptotes

For:

$$ \frac{x^2}{a^2}-\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1, $$

the asymptotes are:

$$ \boxed{ y=\pm\frac{b}{a}x }. $$

Translated Hyperbola Asymptotes

For:

$$ \frac{(x-h)^2}{a^2}-\frac{(y-k)^2}{b^2}=1, $$

the asymptotes are:

$$ \boxed{ y-k = \pm\frac{b}{a}(x-h) }. $$

Latus Rectum

A latus rectum is a chord of a conic passing through a focus and perpendicular to the principal axis associated with that focus.

For a parabola:

$$ y^2=4px, $$

the latus rectum has length:

$$ \boxed{4|p|}. $$

Reflective Properties

Conic sections have important reflective properties.

  • Parabolic reflectors send rays parallel to the axis through the focus.
  • Elliptical reflection relates the two foci.
  • Hyperbolic reflection also has a focal relationship involving its two foci.

Parabolas in Projectile Models

Under idealized uniform gravity with no air resistance, the trajectory of a projectile can be represented by a parabola when horizontal position is used as the independent coordinate.

Ellipses in Orbital Mechanics

In the ideal two-body gravitational model, bound Keplerian orbits are ellipses with the central attracting body at one focus.

Parabolic and Hyperbolic Orbits

In the same idealized orbital framework, parabolic and hyperbolic trajectories represent escape-type paths at different energies.

Circle and Ellipse in Scaling

A nonuniform scaling of a circle along perpendicular coordinate directions produces an ellipse.

$$ x^2+y^2=1 \quad\longrightarrow\quad \frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1. $$

Parametric Form of a Circle

$$ \boxed{ x=h+r\cos t, \qquad y=k+r\sin t }. $$

Parametric Form of an Ellipse

$$ \boxed{ x=h+a\cos t, \qquad y=k+b\sin t }. $$

Parametric Form of a Parabola

One convenient parameterization of:

$$ y^2=4px $$

is:

$$ \boxed{ x=pt^2, \qquad y=2pt }. $$

Parametric Form of a Hyperbola

One useful parameterization of:

$$ \frac{x^2}{a^2}-\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1 $$

is:

$$ \boxed{ x=a\cosh t, \qquad y=b\sinh t }. $$

Polar Form of a Conic

With a focus placed at the pole, a conic can be written in polar form as:

$$ \boxed{ r = \frac{\ell}{1+e\cos\theta} } $$

for an appropriate orientation and parameter $\ell>0$.

Variants using $1-e\cos\theta$, $1+e\sin\theta$, or $1-e\sin\theta$ arise from different orientations.

Unified Polar Classification

The same polar formula accommodates:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} 0<e<1&\Rightarrow\text{ellipse},\\ e=1&\Rightarrow\text{parabola},\\ e>1&\Rightarrow\text{hyperbola}. \end{aligned} } $$

Degenerate Conics

Some plane intersections or quadratic equations produce degenerate conics rather than the ordinary nondegenerate curves.

Possible degenerate forms include:

  • a point,
  • a single line,
  • a pair of intersecting lines,
  • a pair of parallel lines, or
  • the empty set, depending on the real-coordinate equation and convention.

Point as a Degenerate Ellipse-Type Conic

For example:

$$ x^2+y^2=0 $$

has only:

$$ \boxed{(0,0)}. $$

Pair of Intersecting Lines

The equation:

$$ x^2-y^2=0 $$

factors as:

$$ (x-y)(x+y)=0, $$

giving:

$$ \boxed{ y=x \quad\text{or}\quad y=-x }. $$

Pair of Parallel Lines

The equation:

$$ x^2=1 $$

gives:

$$ \boxed{ x=1 \quad\text{or}\quad x=-1 }. $$

Empty Real Locus

The equation:

$$ x^2+y^2=-1 $$

has no real points:

$$ \boxed{ S=\varnothing }. $$

Conic versus Cone

A cone is a three-dimensional object or surface. A conic section is a planar curve obtained from a suitable section of that cone.

$$ \boxed{ \text{cone} \ne \text{conic section} }. $$

Conic versus Quadratic Function

Not every conic is the graph of a function $y=f(x)$.

For example, a circle:

$$ x^2+y^2=1 $$

fails the vertical-line test when treated as a relation between $x$ and $y$.

A parabola such as:

$$ y=x^2 $$

is a function of $x$, but:

$$ x=y^2 $$

is not a single-valued function of $x$ in the form $y=f(x)$.

Conic versus Quadratic Equation

A conic can often be represented by a quadratic equation in two variables, but the geometric object is the locus of points satisfying the equation, not the symbolic equation itself.

Euclidean and Projective Views

In Euclidean geometry, circles, ellipses, parabolas, and hyperbolas are often treated as visibly different classes.

In projective geometry, nondegenerate conics are more unified: projective transformations can transform one nondegenerate conic into another.

Affine Distinction

Under affine transformations, ellipses, parabolas, and hyperbolas remain separated by their broad affine types, while circles need not remain circles because unequal scaling can turn a circle into an ellipse.

Conics as Loci

The cone-slicing description is geometrically historical and intuitive, but conics can also be defined directly as loci satisfying distance conditions.

$$ \boxed{ \text{cone-section definition} \longleftrightarrow \text{focus/directrix or focal-distance definition} }. $$

Dandelin Spheres

Dandelin spheres provide a geometric bridge between the cone-section definition and the focus-based definitions of conics. Spheres tangent to both the cone and the cutting plane identify the focal points and help explain the focal properties of the resulting curves.

Common Misconception: A Conic Section Must Be Drawn from a Physical Cone

Once defined, conics can be studied through equations, loci, transformations, focus-directrix properties, parametric forms, or projective geometry without explicitly constructing a cone.

Common Misconception: Every Quadratic Equation Is a Parabola

A quadratic equation in two variables may represent an ellipse, circle, parabola, hyperbola, or degenerate conic.

Common Misconception: Every Conic Is a Function

A conic is fundamentally a set of points. Whether it defines a function depends on the chosen coordinates and orientation.

Common Misconception: A Circle Is Not an Ellipse

In many analytic classifications, a circle is treated as a special ellipse with equal semiaxes and coincident foci.

Common Misconception: Parabolas Have Two Foci

A standard parabola has one focus and one corresponding directrix.

Common Misconception: Hyperbola Asymptotes Are Part of the Hyperbola

The asymptotes guide the branches and describe limiting behavior, but they are not themselves points of the nondegenerate hyperbola.

Common Misconception: The Two Hyperbola Branches Are Separate Conics

Both branches together form one hyperbola.

Common Misconception: Rotated Conics Are Different Species

A rotated ellipse remains an ellipse, a rotated parabola remains a parabola, and a rotated hyperbola remains a hyperbola. The coordinate equation changes, but the geometric class does not.

Common Misconception: B² − 4AC Alone Handles Every Detail

The discriminant $B^2-4AC$ distinguishes broad quadratic types, but a full classification must also check degeneracy, reality, and other coefficient conditions.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=r^2$ "A circle centered at h comma k with radius r."
$\frac{(x-h)^2}{a^2}+\frac{(y-k)^2}{b^2}=1$ "An ellipse in standard form."
$(x-h)^2=4p(y-k)$ "A vertical parabola in standard focus form."
$\frac{(x-h)^2}{a^2}-\frac{(y-k)^2}{b^2}=1$ "A horizontal hyperbola in standard form."
$Ax^2+Bxy+Cy^2+Dx+Ey+F=0$ "The general second-degree equation in two variables."
$B^2-4AC$ "The quadratic discriminant used in broad conic classification."
$PF=e\,d(P,\ell)$ "The distance from P to the focus equals eccentricity times the distance from P to the directrix."

The plural phrase conic sections usually refers collectively to circles, ellipses, parabolas, and hyperbolas. The singular conic refers to one member of this family.

Standard-form equations should be interpreted geometrically rather than memorized as isolated symbol patterns. Signs, denominators, translations, rotations, foci, axes, and eccentricity all carry structural information.

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish the geometric conic from the equation used to represent it. When classifying a general quadratic, check $B^2-4AC$ but also test for degeneracy and real solutions. Preserve the orientation information encoded in the squared terms and signs. When using eccentricity, state the focus and directrix convention. Do not assume a conic is the graph of a single-valued function merely because it has a Cartesian equation.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: Conic sections are planar curves obtained by intersecting a plane with a double cone.
  • Principal Types: Circle, ellipse, parabola, and hyperbola.
  • Circle: Fixed distance from one center point.
  • Ellipse: Constant sum of distances to two foci.
  • Parabola: Equal distance from a focus and directrix.
  • Hyperbola: Constant absolute difference of distances to two foci.
  • Circle Standard Form: $$ \boxed{ (x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=r^2 }. $$
  • Ellipse Standard Form: $$ \boxed{ \frac{(x-h)^2}{a^2} + \frac{(y-k)^2}{b^2} =1 }. $$
  • Parabola Standard Form: $$ \boxed{ (x-h)^2=4p(y-k) }. $$
  • Hyperbola Standard Form: $$ \boxed{ \frac{(x-h)^2}{a^2} - \frac{(y-k)^2}{b^2} =1 }. $$
  • Eccentricity: Provides a unified numerical classification of nondegenerate conics.
  • Ellipse Eccentricity: $0<e<1$.
  • Parabola Eccentricity: $e=1$.
  • Hyperbola Eccentricity: $e>1$.
  • Circle Special Case: Often treated as an ellipse with eccentricity $0$.
  • General Quadratic: $$ \boxed{ Ax^2+Bxy+Cy^2+Dx+Ey+F=0 }. $$
  • Discriminant: $B^2-4AC$ distinguishes ellipse-, parabola-, and hyperbola-type quadratics, subject to degeneracy checks.
  • Rotation: A nonzero $xy$ term may indicate rotated principal axes.
  • Translation: Completing the square often reveals the conic's center or vertex.
  • Central Conics: Circles, ellipses, and hyperbolas possess centers.
  • Noncentral Conic: A parabola has no center of symmetry.
  • Hyperbola Asymptotes: Describe the limiting directions of its branches.
  • Polar Form: $$ \boxed{ r=\frac{\ell}{1+e\cos\theta} } $$ is one standard focus-based representation.
  • Parametric Forms: Conics can be represented through one-parameter coordinate formulas.
  • Degenerate Conics: May include points, lines, pairs of lines, or empty real loci.
  • Dandelin Spheres: Connect the cone-slicing construction with focal definitions.
  • Orbital Mechanics: Ideal Keplerian trajectories are conic sections.
  • Optics: Parabolic, elliptical, and hyperbolic reflection properties have practical applications.
  • Projective Geometry: Nondegenerate conics form a highly unified class under projective transformations.
  • Function Contrast: A conic is a locus and need not define $y$ as a single-valued function of $x$.
  • Cone Contrast: The cone is the three-dimensional generating object; the conic section is the planar intersection.
  • Quadratic-Equation Contrast: The equation is a symbolic representation; the conic is the geometric solution set.
  • Common Error: Do not assume every quadratic relation in two variables is a parabola.
  • Common Error: Do not assume every conic is a function.
  • Common Error: Do not exclude the circle from the broader ellipse family when using the standard analytic special-case convention.
  • Common Error: Do not treat hyperbola asymptotes as part of the hyperbola itself.
  • Common Error: Do not treat the two branches of a hyperbola as separate conics.
  • Common Error: Do not assume rotation changes the species of a conic.
  • Common Error: Do not use $B^2-4AC$ as the sole test when degeneracy or real existence is at issue.
  • Terminological Contrast: A conic section is a curve obtained from a planar section of a cone. A conic is the abbreviated term for such a curve. A circle has constant distance from one center. An ellipse has constant sum of distances to two foci. A parabola has equal distance from a focus and a directrix. A hyperbola has constant absolute difference of distances to two foci. A degenerate conic arises when the quadratic relation collapses into a simpler locus such as a point or lines. A cone is the three-dimensional object from which the classical sections are taken.
