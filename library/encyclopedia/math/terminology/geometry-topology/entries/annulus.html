Annulus

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

The mathematical term annulus comes directly from the Latin annulus, meaning "ring." The word refers to the ring-shaped region formed between two concentric circles. Its linguistic origin is therefore unusually close to its geometric meaning: an annulus literally resembles a flat ring.

The plural traditionally used in mathematics is annuli, pronounced approximately "AN-yuh-lie," although the regularized English plural annuluses may also appear outside technical writing. In mathematical literature, annuli remains the conventional plural.

Operational Nuance

In elementary geometry, an annulus is the region lying between two concentric circles of different radii. If the outer circle has radius $R$ and the inner circle has radius $r$, with $R > r \ge 0$, the annular region consists of the points that lie outside or on the inner circle and inside or on the outer circle.

$$ r \le \rho \le R $$

Here, $\rho$ represents the distance from a point in the plane to the common center of the circles. In Cartesian coordinates, an annulus centered at the origin can be described by:

$$ r^2 \le x^2 + y^2 \le R^2 $$

The area of an annulus is found by subtracting the area of the inner disk from the area of the outer disk:

$$ A = \pi R^2 - \pi r^2 $$ $$ A = \pi(R^2-r^2) $$

Because $R^2-r^2$ is a difference of squares, the same formula may also be written:

$$ A = \pi(R-r)(R+r) $$

This form can be useful when the width of the annulus, $R-r$, is known directly.

Common Misconception: An annulus is the region between two concentric circles, not merely the pair of circular boundaries themselves. It should also not be confused with a disk. A disk contains all points inside one circle, including its interior, whereas a nondegenerate annulus has a central region removed. In elementary geometry, the two boundary circles share the same center; two nonconcentric circles do not form an annulus in the standard sense.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$r \le \rho \le R$ "Rho lies between the inner radius r and the outer radius capital R."
$r^2 \le x^2+y^2 \le R^2$ "x squared plus y squared lies between r squared and capital R squared."
$A=\pi(R^2-r^2)$ "The area of the annulus is pi times the difference of the squared radii."
$A=\pi(R-r)(R+r)$ "The area is pi times the difference of the radii times their sum."
$r<|z| "The modulus of z is greater than r and less than capital R."

The notation used for an annulus depends strongly on context. In elementary geometry, the region is usually described using inner and outer radii. In coordinate geometry, inequalities involving $x^2+y^2$ are common. In complex analysis, the same geometric idea is naturally expressed using the modulus $|z|$ of a complex number.

Defense Notation Advice: When describing an annulus, explicitly identify which radius is inner and which is outer, and maintain a consistent convention such as $0 \le r < R$. Also state whether the boundary circles are included. The inequalities $r \le |z| \le R$, $r < |z| < R$, and $r < |z| \le R$ describe related but technically different annular sets.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Euclidean Geometry: An annulus is treated as the planar region between two concentric circles. Problems commonly ask for its area, width, inner radius, or outer radius. Real-world examples include washers, circular tracks, rings, and cross-sections of hollow cylindrical objects.
  • Coordinate Geometry: An annulus centered at $(h,k)$ can be represented by an inequality of the form: $$ r^2 \le (x-h)^2+(y-k)^2 \le R^2. $$ This formulation describes every point whose distance from the center lies between the two specified radii.
  • Complex Analysis: An annulus is commonly written as: $$ \{z \in \mathbb{C} : r < |z-z_0| < R\}. $$ Here, $z_0$ is the center, while $r$ and $R$ are the inner and outer radii. Annular domains play an important role in Laurent series, because functions may have different series representations in different annular regions surrounding a singularity.
  • Topology: An open annulus is topologically equivalent to a cylinder without its boundary circles and, in an appropriate sense, behaves like a circle with radial thickness. Unlike a disk, an annulus contains a central "hole," making it a basic example used to illustrate properties such as connectedness and non-simply-connected regions.
  • Calculus & Polar Coordinates: Annular regions arise naturally in double integrals because polar coordinates express them efficiently. A typical annulus centered at the origin may be written: $$ r \le \rho \le R, \qquad 0 \le \theta \le 2\pi. $$ This avoids the more cumbersome Cartesian inequalities required to describe the same circular region.
  • Applied Mathematics & Engineering: Annular shapes occur in pipe cross-sections, bearings, seals, washers, circular ducts, electromagnetic systems, and fluid-flow models. In these applications, quantities such as cross-sectional area often depend directly on the difference between the squares of the outer and inner radii.
