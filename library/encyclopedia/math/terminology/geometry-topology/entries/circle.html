Circle

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

A circle is the set of all points in a plane that are the same fixed distance from a fixed point called the center.

If $O$ is the center and $r>0$ is the fixed distance, then:

$$ \boxed{ \text{Circle} = \{P:d(P,O)=r\} }. $$

The fixed distance $r$ is the radius.

$$ \boxed{ OP=r } $$

for every point $P$ on the circle.

A crucial terminology distinction is that the circle is the boundary curve itself. The region consisting of the circle together with all points inside it is a closed disk.

$$ \boxed{ \text{circle} \ne \text{filled disk} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Center and Radius

A circle is determined by:

  • a center point,
  • a positive radius.
$$ \boxed{ (O,r) \longrightarrow \text{one circle} }. $$

Standard Coordinate Equation

A circle with center:

$$ (h,k) $$

and radius $r$ has equation:

$$ \boxed{ (x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=r^2 }. $$

Why the Equation Works

The distance from $(x,y)$ to the center $(h,k)$ is:

$$ \sqrt{(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2}. $$

Requiring that distance to equal $r$ gives:

$$ \sqrt{(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2}=r. $$

Squaring both sides produces the standard equation.

Circle Centered at the Origin

If:

$$ (h,k)=(0,0), $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ x^2+y^2=r^2 }. $$

Unit Circle

The circle centered at the origin with radius $1$ is the unit circle:

$$ \boxed{ x^2+y^2=1 }. $$

It is fundamental in trigonometry, complex numbers, rotations, and periodic mathematics.

General Cartesian Form

Expanding the standard equation gives:

$$ \boxed{ x^2+y^2+Dx+Ey+F=0 }. $$

For this equation to describe a real nondegenerate circle, the resulting radius squared must be positive.

Recovering Center and Radius

From:

$$ x^2+y^2+Dx+Ey+F=0, $$

the center is:

$$ \boxed{ \left(-\frac D2,-\frac E2\right) } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ r^2= \frac{D^2+E^2}{4}-F }. $$

Completing the Square

A general-form circle equation can be converted to standard form by completing the square in both $x$ and $y$.

For example:

$$ x^2+y^2-6x+4y-12=0. $$

Regroup:

$$ (x^2-6x)+(y^2+4y)=12. $$

Complete the squares:

$$ (x-3)^2+(y+2)^2=25. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \text{center}=(3,-2), \qquad r=5 }. $$

Radius

A radius is a segment from the center to a point on the circle.

$$ \boxed{ OA=r }. $$

Diameter

A diameter is a chord passing through the center.

$$ \boxed{ d=2r }. $$

Every diameter is a chord, and every circle has infinitely many diameters.

Chord

A chord is a segment whose endpoints lie on the circle.

$$ \boxed{ A,B\in\text{circle} \Rightarrow \overline{AB} \text{ is a chord} }. $$

Longest Chord

Every diameter has length $2r$, and no chord is longer.

$$ \boxed{ c\le2r }. $$

Chord Length from Central Angle

A chord subtending central angle $\theta$ has length:

$$ \boxed{ c= 2r\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) }. $$

Arc

An arc is a connected portion of the circumference between points on the circle.

Unlike a chord, an arc follows the curved boundary.

Minor and Major Arcs

Two distinct non-antipodal points divide a circle into:

  • a minor arc,
  • a major arc.

Their angular measures add to:

$$ \boxed{ 360^\circ }. $$

Semicircle

The endpoints of a diameter divide the circle into two semicircular arcs.

$$ \boxed{ 180^\circ=\pi\text{ radians} }. $$

Central Angle

A central angle has its vertex at the center and its sides along radii.

Its degree measure equals the degree measure of its intercepted arc:

$$ \boxed{ m\angle AOB = m\widehat{AB} }. $$

Arc Length

If $\theta$ is measured in radians:

$$ \boxed{ s=r\theta }. $$

Circumference

The length of the entire circle is its circumference:

$$ \boxed{ C=2\pi r }. $$

Since $d=2r$, this may also be written:

$$ \boxed{ C=\pi d }. $$

Meaning of π

For every Euclidean circle:

$$ \boxed{ \pi= \frac{C}{d} }. $$

Thus $\pi$ is the constant ratio of circumference to diameter.

Area Enclosed by a Circle

The area of the disk bounded by a circle of radius $r$ is:

$$ \boxed{ A=\pi r^2 }. $$

In everyday language this is often called the "area of the circle," although strictly speaking the two-dimensional area belongs to the enclosed disk.

Sector

A sector is the region bounded by two radii and the arc joining their endpoints.

Sector Area

If $\theta$ is measured in radians:

$$ \boxed{ A_{\text{sector}} = \frac12r^2\theta }. $$

In degrees:

$$ \boxed{ A_{\text{sector}} = \frac{\theta}{360^\circ} \pi r^2 }. $$

Segment of a Circle

A circular segment is the region bounded by a chord and its corresponding arc.

It should not be confused with a sector, which uses two radii as boundaries.

Tangent

A tangent line to a circle meets the circle at exactly one point in elementary Euclidean geometry.

If the tangent touches at $T$, then:

$$ \boxed{ OT\perp\text{tangent at }T }. $$

Tangent Equation

For:

$$ x^2+y^2=r^2, $$

the tangent at $(x_0,y_0)$ on the circle can be written:

$$ \boxed{ xx_0+yy_0=r^2 }. $$

Secant

A secant line intersects the circle at two points.

$$ \boxed{ \text{secant} \rightarrow \text{two circle intersections} }. $$

The segment between those points is a chord.

Interior, Circle, and Exterior

For a circle centered at $(h,k)$ with radius $r$, a point $(x,y)$ lies:

  • inside if $(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2<r^2$,
  • on the circle if $(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=r^2$,
  • outside if $(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2>r^2$.

Open Disk

$$ \boxed{ (x-h)^2+(y-k)^2

describes the interior without the boundary.

Closed Disk

$$ \boxed{ (x-h)^2+(y-k)^2\le r^2 } $$

describes the interior together with the circle.

Parametric Form

A circle centered at $(h,k)$ can be parametrized by:

$$ \boxed{ x=h+r\cos t, \qquad y=k+r\sin t }. $$

As $t$ runs through an interval of length $2\pi$, the circle is traversed once.

Unit-Circle Parametrization

$$ \boxed{ (\cos t,\sin t) }. $$

This gives the standard connection between circular geometry and trigonometric functions.

Polar Form

A circle centered at the origin with radius $R$ has the polar equation:

$$ \boxed{ r=R }. $$

Circle through the Polar Origin

Certain translated circles have especially simple polar equations. For example:

$$ \boxed{ r=2a\cos\theta } $$

represents a circle centered at $(a,0)$ with radius $|a|$ when interpreted in the standard Cartesian-polar plane.

Complex-Number Form

In the complex plane, a circle with center $z_0$ and radius $r$ is:

$$ \boxed{ |z-z_0|=r }. $$

Rotation Symmetry

A circle is invariant under every rotation about its center.

$$ \boxed{ R_{O,\theta}(C)=C \text{ for every }\theta }. $$

Reflection Symmetry

Every line through the center is an axis of symmetry of the circle.

Thus a circle has infinitely many reflection axes.

Translation

Translating a circle changes its center but not its radius.

$$ (h,k)\mapsto(h+a,k+b). $$

Uniform Scaling

Scaling a circle uniformly about its center by factor $s$ changes the radius to:

$$ \boxed{ r'=|s|r }. $$

Circumference scales by $|s|$, while enclosed area scales by $s^2$.

Nonuniform Scaling

Unequal horizontal and vertical scaling generally turns a circle into an ellipse.

$$ \boxed{ \text{circle} \xrightarrow{\text{nonuniform scaling}} \text{ellipse} }. $$

Circle as a Conic Section

A circle is a special conic section and may be viewed as an ellipse whose two semiaxes are equal.

$$ \boxed{ a=b=r }. $$

Eccentricity

In the conic-section classification, a circle has eccentricity:

$$ \boxed{ e=0 }. $$

Circle through Three Points

Any three noncollinear points determine exactly one circle.

Its center is the intersection of the perpendicular bisectors of any two of the chords formed by the three points.

Circumcircle of a Triangle

The unique circle through the three vertices of a nondegenerate triangle is its circumcircle.

Its center is the circumcenter.

Incircle

A triangle's incircle is the circle tangent to all three sides.

Its center is the incenter.

Inscribed Polygon

A polygon is inscribed in a circle when all of its vertices lie on the circle.

Cyclic Polygon

A polygon whose vertices all lie on one circle is called cyclic.

Inscribed Angle

An inscribed angle has its vertex on the circle and its sides along chords.

Its measure is half the measure of its intercepted arc:

$$ \boxed{ m\angle ACB = \frac12m\widehat{AB} }. $$

Thales' Theorem

An angle inscribed in a semicircle is a right angle.

$$ \boxed{ AB \text{ diameter} \Rightarrow \angle ACB=90^\circ }. $$

Intersecting Chords

If chords $AB$ and $CD$ intersect at interior point $P$, then:

$$ \boxed{ PA\cdot PB = PC\cdot PD }. $$

Power of a Point

For a circle centered at $O$ with radius $r$, the power of a point $P$ can be expressed as:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Pow}(P) = OP^2-r^2 }. $$

This quantity underlies secant, chord, and tangent product relations.

Topology of a Circle

In topology, the standard circle is commonly denoted:

$$ \boxed{ S^1 }. $$

One standard realization is:

$$ \boxed{ S^1= \{(x,y)\in\mathbb R^2:x^2+y^2=1\} }. $$

Circle versus Disk in Topology

The circle $S^1$ is one-dimensional as a manifold, even though it is commonly embedded in the two-dimensional plane.

The disk:

$$ D^2= \{(x,y):x^2+y^2\le1\} $$

is two-dimensional and includes the interior.

Circle Is Closed and Bounded

As a subset of the Euclidean plane, a circle is both closed and bounded.

Consequently it is compact.

Every Point Looks Locally Like a Line

Although globally closed into a loop, sufficiently small neighborhoods of points on a circle resemble intervals. This is why $S^1$ is a one-dimensional manifold.

No Endpoints

Unlike a line segment or arc segment, a complete circle has no endpoints.

Common Misconception: A Circle Includes Its Interior

Strictly, the circle is the boundary. The filled region is a disk.

Common Misconception: Radius and Diameter Are Interchangeable

The diameter is twice the radius:

$$ \boxed{ d=2r }. $$

Common Misconception: Area Belongs to the Boundary Curve

The expression $\pi r^2$ measures the area enclosed by the circle—the disk—not the one-dimensional circumference itself.

Common Misconception: Every Oval Is a Circle

A circle requires every boundary point to be exactly the same distance from one center. General ellipses and other oval curves do not satisfy this condition.

Common Misconception: x²+y²+Dx+Ey+F=0 Always Gives a Real Circle

The radius squared obtained after completing the square must be positive for a real nondegenerate circle.

Degenerate Radius

In elementary Euclidean geometry, a circle normally has $r>0$. An equation with $r=0$ reduces to a single point and is sometimes called a degenerate circle in algebraic contexts.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=r^2$ "The circle with center h comma k and radius r."
$x^2+y^2=r^2$ "A circle of radius r centered at the origin."
$x^2+y^2=1$ "The unit circle."
$C=2\pi r$ "The circumference equals two pi r."
$A=\pi r^2$ "The area enclosed by the circle is pi r squared."
$S^1$ "S one," commonly denoting the topological circle.

In elementary speech, people often use circle for both the circumference and the filled interior. Mathematical writing benefits from distinguishing circle from disk whenever the distinction affects the statement.

Defense Notation Advice: Identify whether a problem concerns the boundary, the enclosed disk, or both. In coordinate equations, read the center from the signs inside $(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2$, and remember that the right-hand side is $r^2$, not $r$.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: A circle is the set of points in a plane at a fixed positive distance from a fixed center.
  • Standard Equation: $$ \boxed{ (x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=r^2 }. $$
  • Origin-Centered Circle: $$ \boxed{ x^2+y^2=r^2 }. $$
  • Unit Circle: $$ \boxed{ x^2+y^2=1 }. $$
  • Radius: Fixed distance from the center to the circle.
  • Diameter: $$ \boxed{ d=2r }. $$
  • Circumference: $$ \boxed{ C=2\pi r=\pi d }. $$
  • Enclosed Area: $$ \boxed{ A=\pi r^2 }. $$
  • Chord: Segment whose endpoints lie on the circle.
  • Longest Chord: A diameter.
  • Arc Length: $$ \boxed{ s=r\theta } $$ for $\theta$ in radians.
  • Sector Area: $$ \boxed{ A_{\text{sector}} = \frac12r^2\theta } $$ for $\theta$ in radians.
  • Tangent: Perpendicular to the radius at the point of tangency.
  • Secant: Line intersecting the circle at two points.
  • Parametric Form: $$ \boxed{ x=h+r\cos t, \qquad y=k+r\sin t }. $$
  • Complex Form: $$ \boxed{ |z-z_0|=r }. $$
  • Polar Form at Origin: $$ \boxed{ r=R }. $$
  • Conic Classification: A circle is a special ellipse with equal semiaxes and eccentricity $0$.
  • Triangle Geometry: Circumcircles pass through triangle vertices; incircles are tangent to triangle sides.
  • Topology: The standard topological circle is $S^1$.
  • Circle versus Disk: The circle is the boundary; the disk includes interior points.
  • Symmetry: A circle has rotational symmetry through every angle about its center and reflection symmetry across every line through its center.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse radius with diameter.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse circumference with enclosed area.
  • Common Error: Do not treat the filled disk and its boundary circle as identical mathematical sets.
  • Common Error: Do not assume every equation of the form $x^2+y^2+Dx+Ey+F=0$ produces a real nondegenerate circle.
  • Terminological Contrast: Circle is the locus of points at fixed distance from a center. Disk is the planar region inside a circle, with or without the boundary depending on whether it is open or closed. Radius is the center-to-circle distance. Diameter is a chord through the center. Chord joins two points on the circle. Arc is a curved portion of the circumference. Sector is bounded by two radii and an arc. Segment is bounded by a chord and an arc. Tangent touches the circle at one point. Secant crosses the circle at two points. Circumference is the total length around the circle.
