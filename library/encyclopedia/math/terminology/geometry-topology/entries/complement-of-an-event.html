Complement of an Event

Subfield: Probability & Statistical Inference

Etymology & Roots

In probability, the complement of an event is the event consisting of every outcome in the sample space that is not contained in the original event.

If the sample space is $\Omega$ and $A$ is an event, then the complement of $A$ is:

$$ \boxed{ A^c=\Omega\setminus A }. $$

The complement may also be written as $A'$, $\overline{A}$, or occasionally $\neg A$, depending on the field and notation convention.

$$ \boxed{ A^c = \{\omega\in\Omega:\omega\notin A\} }. $$

The word complement comes from the idea of completing a whole. The event $A$ and its complement together account for every outcome in the sample space.

$$ \boxed{ A\cup A^c=\Omega }. $$

At the same time, they share no outcomes:

$$ \boxed{ A\cap A^c=\varnothing }. $$

Thus an event and its complement divide the sample space into exactly two mutually exclusive possibilities: the event occurs, or it does not occur.

Operational Nuance

Basic Set Definition

Let:

$$ A\subseteq\Omega. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ A^c = \Omega\setminus A }. $$

Every outcome in $\Omega$ belongs to exactly one of $A$ or $A^c$.

Event and Complement Form a Partition

The pair:

$$ A,\ A^c $$

partitions the sample space because:

$$ \boxed{ A\cup A^c=\Omega \qquad\text{and}\qquad A\cap A^c=\varnothing }. $$

Probability Complement Rule

Because the event and its complement exhaust the sample space:

$$ P(A)+P(A^c)=1. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ P(A^c)=1-P(A) }. $$

Equivalently:

$$ \boxed{ P(A)=1-P(A^c) }. $$

Example: Coin Toss

Suppose:

$$ \Omega=\{H,T\}. $$

Let:

$$ A=\{H\}. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ A^c=\{T\} }. $$

For a fair coin:

$$ P(A)=\frac12, \qquad P(A^c)=\frac12. $$

Example: Die Roll

Suppose a standard six-sided die is rolled:

$$ \Omega=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}. $$

Let $A$ be the event of rolling an even number:

$$ A=\{2,4,6\}. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ A^c=\{1,3,5\} }. $$

Thus:

$$ P(A^c) = 1-\frac36 = \frac12. $$

The Complement Depends on the Sample Space

A complement is always defined relative to a specified universal set or sample space.

For example, if:

$$ \Omega=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\} $$

and:

$$ A=\{2,4,6\}, $$

then:

$$ A^c=\{1,3,5\}. $$

If the sample space were changed, the complement could change as well.

$$ \boxed{ \text{complement is relative to }\Omega }. $$

Complement of the Empty Event

The impossible event contains no outcomes:

$$ \varnothing. $$

Its complement is the entire sample space:

$$ \boxed{ \varnothing^c=\Omega }. $$

Consequently:

$$ P(\varnothing)=0, \qquad P(\Omega)=1. $$

Complement of the Sure Event

The complement of the entire sample space is the empty event:

$$ \boxed{ \Omega^c=\varnothing }. $$

Double Complement

Taking the complement twice returns the original event:

$$ \boxed{ (A^c)^c=A }. $$

The complement operation is therefore an involution.

Complement and Mutual Exclusivity

An event and its complement are always mutually exclusive:

$$ \boxed{ A\cap A^c=\varnothing }. $$

They cannot occur simultaneously in the same trial.

But Not Every Mutually Exclusive Event Is a Complement

Two events may be disjoint without exhausting the entire sample space.

For example, in a die roll:

$$ A=\{1\}, \qquad B=\{2\}. $$

Then:

$$ A\cap B=\varnothing, $$

but:

$$ A\cup B\ne\Omega. $$

Therefore $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive but are not complements.

Complement and Exhaustiveness

Complements have two simultaneous properties:

$$ \boxed{ \text{mutually exclusive} + \text{collectively exhaustive} }. $$

They do not overlap, and together they contain all outcomes.

At Least One Problems

Complementary reasoning is especially useful for probabilities involving phrases such as at least one.

The complement of:

$$ \text{at least one success} $$

is:

$$ \text{no successes}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ P(\text{at least one success}) = 1-P(\text{no successes}) }. $$

Example: At Least One Six

Suppose a fair die is rolled four times. Let $A$ be the event that at least one six appears.

The complement is:

$$ A^c= \text{no six appears in four rolls}. $$

The probability of no six on one roll is:

$$ \frac56. $$

For four independent rolls:

$$ P(A^c)=\left(\frac56\right)^4. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ P(A) = 1-\left(\frac56\right)^4 }. $$

At Least One Is Not the Complement of Exactly One

The complement of at least one is none, not exactly one.

$$ \boxed{ (\text{at least one})^c = \text{none} }. $$

At Most and Greater Than

Complement relationships frequently translate verbal probability conditions.

For a random variable $X$:

$$ \boxed{ \{X\le k\}^c = \{X>k\} }. $$

Similarly:

$$ \boxed{ \{X

Greater Than versus At Most

$$ \boxed{ P(X>k) = 1-P(X\le k) }. $$

This identity is frequently useful when probability tables or cumulative distribution functions provide $P(X\le k)$ directly.

Cumulative Distribution Functions

For a random variable $X$, the cumulative distribution function is:

$$ F(x)=P(X\le x). $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ P(X>x)=1-F(x) }. $$

This is a direct application of event complementation.

Survival Functions

In reliability and survival analysis, the survival function is commonly expressed through a complementary event:

$$ \boxed{ S(t)=P(T>t)=1-F(t) }. $$

Here $F(t)=P(T\le t)$ is the cumulative distribution function of a lifetime or waiting-time variable.

De Morgan's Law for a Union

The complement of a union is the intersection of the complements:

$$ \boxed{ (A\cup B)^c = A^c\cap B^c }. $$

In words, neither A nor B means that A does not occur and B does not occur.

De Morgan's Law for an Intersection

The complement of an intersection is the union of the complements:

$$ \boxed{ (A\cap B)^c = A^c\cup B^c }. $$

In words, not both A and B means that at least one of them fails to occur.

Probability Form of De Morgan's Law

Since:

$$ (A\cup B)^c=A^c\cap B^c, $$

we obtain:

$$ \boxed{ P(A\cup B) = 1-P(A^c\cap B^c) }. $$

This is another common form of the at-least-one strategy.

Neither Event

The phrase neither A nor B corresponds to:

$$ \boxed{ A^c\cap B^c }. $$

By De Morgan's law:

$$ A^c\cap B^c = (A\cup B)^c. $$

Not Both

The phrase not both A and B corresponds to:

$$ \boxed{ (A\cap B)^c = A^c\cup B^c }. $$

This includes outcomes in which only $A$ occurs, only $B$ occurs, or neither occurs.

Exactly One Is Different

The event that exactly one of $A$ and $B$ occurs is:

$$ \boxed{ (A\cap B^c) \cup (A^c\cap B) }. $$

It should not be confused with a simple complement.

Complement in Conditional Probability

Within a fixed conditioning event $B$ with $P(B)>0$:

$$ \boxed{ P(A^c\mid B) = 1-P(A\mid B) }. $$

The complement is still taken with respect to the original sample space, while the conditional probability restricts attention to outcomes within $B$.

Conditional Complement Viewed inside B

Within the conditioned universe $B$, the outcomes corresponding to the failure of $A$ are:

$$ A^c\cap B. $$

Thus:

$$ P(A^c\mid B) = \frac{P(A^c\cap B)}{P(B)}. $$

Independence and Complements

If $A$ and $B$ are independent, then their complements preserve related independence relationships.

In particular:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ independent of }B \Rightarrow A^c\text{ independent of }B }. $$

Likewise, $A^c$ and $B^c$ are independent.

Complement of a Continuous Event

Suppose a continuous random variable $X$ has event:

$$ A=\{X<5\}. $$

Its exact set-theoretic complement is:

$$ \boxed{ A^c=\{X\ge5\} }. $$

For a continuous distribution one may often have:

$$ P(X=5)=0, $$

so $P(X\ge5)=P(X>5)$, but the events themselves are not identical.

Probability Equality Does Not Mean Event Equality

Two distinct events can have the same probability.

Consequently, even if:

$$ P(A)=P(B), $$

this does not imply:

$$ A=B. $$

Likewise, an event having probability $1-P(A)$ is not automatically the complement of $A$ unless the set relationship is also correct.

Complement of an Event versus Set Complement

An event is itself a subset of the sample space. Thus the probability concept is fundamentally an application of set complement:

$$ \boxed{ A^c = \Omega\setminus A }. $$

Probability then assigns the measure:

$$ P(A^c)=1-P(A). $$

Complement Reasoning Can Simplify Counting

Sometimes directly counting the desired event is difficult while counting its complement is easy.

$$ \boxed{ P(A) = 1-P(A^c) }. $$

This is especially effective when $A$ contains many overlapping possibilities but $A^c$ has a simple description.

Example: At Least One Birthday Match

In birthday-type problems, directly counting all possible ways that at least two people share a birthday is cumbersome.

The complementary event:

$$ \text{all birthdays are different} $$

is usually easier to calculate.

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ P(\text{at least one match}) = 1-P(\text{all different}) }. $$

Complement Rule Requires the Same Sample Space

The identity:

$$ P(A^c)=1-P(A) $$

assumes that $A^c$ is defined with respect to the same probability space as $A$.

$$ \boxed{ A\subseteq\Omega \Rightarrow A^c=\Omega\setminus A }. $$

Common Misconception: The Complement Is the Least Likely Alternative

The complement is not selected according to likelihood. It consists of all outcomes outside the event.

If:

$$ P(A)=0.9, $$

then:

$$ P(A^c)=0.1. $$

If instead $P(A)=0.2$, then $P(A^c)=0.8$.

Common Misconception: Any Mutually Exclusive Event Is the Complement

A complement must be both disjoint from $A$ and contain every outcome not in $A$.

$$ \boxed{ A^c \text{ requires } A\cap A^c=\varnothing \text{ and } A\cup A^c=\Omega }. $$

Common Misconception: The Complement of At Least One Is Exactly One

The correct complement of at least one occurrence is zero occurrences.

$$ \boxed{ (\text{at least one})^c = \text{none} }. $$

Common Misconception: 1 − P(A) Defines the Event Itself

The quantity:

$$ 1-P(A) $$

gives the probability of the complement. The complement event itself is a set of outcomes:

$$ \boxed{ A^c=\Omega\setminus A }. $$

Common Misconception: Complements Are Independent

An event and its own complement are generally not independent.

Since:

$$ A\cap A^c=\varnothing, $$

we have:

$$ P(A\cap A^c)=0. $$

Independence would require:

$$ 0=P(A)P(A^c), $$

which occurs only in degenerate cases where $P(A)=0$ or $P(A)=1$.

Common Misconception: Complement Means Opposite Numerical Value

The complement of an event is not obtained by negating numerical outcomes. It is obtained by selecting the outcomes outside the event.

$$ \boxed{ \text{event complement} = \text{set-theoretic exclusion}, \text{ not arithmetic negation} }. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$A^c$ "A complement" or "the complement of A."
$A'$ "A prime," when the notation is being used for the complement.
$\overline{A}$ "A bar," when the notation is defined as the complement.
$A^c=\Omega\setminus A$ "The complement of A is the sample space minus A."
$P(A^c)=1-P(A)$ "The probability of A complement equals one minus the probability of A."
$A\cup A^c=\Omega$ "A union A complement equals the sample space."
$A\cap A^c=\varnothing$ "A intersect A complement is the empty set."

$P(X>k)=1-P(X\le k)$ "The probability that X is greater than k equals one minus the probability that X is at most k."

In probability, the phrase A complement is commonly used for $A^c$. One may also say not A when translating the event into ordinary language, provided that the negation refers to every outcome outside $A$.

Notation varies substantially between textbooks. $A^c$ is explicit and common in probability and set theory, while $A'$, $\overline A$, and other forms may also occur. The bar notation can have other meanings in mathematics, so local definitions matter.

Defense Notation Advice: Define the sample space before identifying an event complement, because the complement is relative to that space. Prefer $A^c$ when there is any risk that $A'$ could be mistaken for a derivative or that $\overline A$ could be mistaken for closure, conjugation, or another bar notation. Distinguish the event $A^c$ from its probability $P(A^c)$: the first is a set of outcomes, while the second is a number between $0$ and $1$.

An event and its complement are both mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive. The complement rule refers to the probability identity $P(A^c)=1-P(A)$.
