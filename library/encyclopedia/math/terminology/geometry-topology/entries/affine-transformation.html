Affine Transformation

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

An affine transformation is a geometric transformation built from a linear transformation together with a translation. In vector notation, its characteristic form is:

$$ \boxed{ T(\mathbf{x}) = A\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b} }. $$

Here $A$ is a matrix representing the linear part of the transformation, while $\mathbf{b}$ is a translation vector.

The word affine comes through New Latin from Latin affinis, with senses connected to bordering, relationship, or connection. In mathematics, the word became attached to affine geometry, where relationships such as collinearity, parallelism, and ratios along a line matter even when ordinary Euclidean distances and angles are allowed to change.

Affine geometry can therefore be viewed as less rigid than Euclidean geometry. A figure may be stretched, compressed, sheared, reflected, rotated, or translated and still retain its affine structure.

A terminology distinction is useful. Some authors use affine map for any function:

$$ T(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}, $$

including cases where $A$ is singular. In geometric usage, affine transformation often implies that $A$ is invertible, so the transformation is a one-to-one correspondence of the affine space with itself.

Operational Nuance

Linear Part Plus Translation

The defining structure:

$$ T(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b} $$

separates an affine transformation into two components:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x} \xrightarrow{\ A\ } A\mathbf{x} \xrightarrow{\ +\mathbf{b}\ } A\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b} }. $$

The matrix $A$ controls stretching, rotation, reflection, shear, and other linear effects. The vector $\mathbf{b}$ shifts the result through space.

Two-Dimensional Form

In the plane:

$$ \begin{pmatrix} x'\\ y' \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a & b\\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x\\ y \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} e\\ f \end{pmatrix}. $$

Equivalently:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} x'&=ax+by+e,\\ y'&=cx+dy+f. \end{aligned} } $$

One-Dimensional Form

On the real line, every affine function has the form:

$$ \boxed{ T(x)=ax+b }. $$

If $a\ne0$, this gives an invertible affine transformation of the real line.

Preservation of Lines

An invertible affine transformation maps every line to another line. If:

$$ \mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{p} + t\mathbf{v}, $$

then:

$$ T(\mathbf{x}(t)) = A\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{b} + tA\mathbf{v}. $$

This again has the parametric form of a line when $A\mathbf{v}\ne\mathbf{0}$.

Collinearity

Points lying on a common line remain collinear under an affine transformation.

$$ \boxed{ \text{collinear points} \longrightarrow \text{collinear images} }. $$

Parallelism

Under an invertible affine transformation, parallel lines remain parallel.

$$ \boxed{ \ell_1\parallel\ell_2 \Longrightarrow T(\ell_1)\parallel T(\ell_2) }. $$

A singular affine map requires more care because a line or higher-dimensional object can collapse into a lower-dimensional set.

Affine Combinations

Affine transformations preserve combinations whose coefficients sum to one. For example:

$$ \boxed{ T\bigl((1-t)\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{y}\bigr) = (1-t)T(\mathbf{x}) + tT(\mathbf{y}) }. $$

This identity is one of the most useful ways to recognize the affine structure.

Midpoints

Setting:

$$ t=\frac12 $$

gives:

$$ \boxed{ T\left( \frac{\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}}{2} \right) = \frac{T(\mathbf{x})+T(\mathbf{y})}{2} }. $$

Therefore midpoints remain midpoints.

Ratios Along a Line

More generally, affine transformations preserve ratios in which a point divides a line segment. If $P$ divides segment $AB$ in a particular ratio, then $T(P)$ divides $T(A)T(B)$ in the same ratio.

What Is Not Generally Preserved

Affine transformations need not preserve:

  • length,
  • distance,
  • angle measure,
  • perpendicularity,
  • circle shape, or
  • ordinary area and volume.

A circle, for example, can become an ellipse under an invertible affine transformation.

Common Misconception: An affine transformation is not necessarily a rigid motion. Rotations and translations are affine, but so are shears and nonuniform scalings, which can dramatically alter lengths and angles.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$T(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}$ "T of x equals A x plus b."
$A$ "The linear part of the affine transformation."
$\mathbf{b}$ "The translation vector."
$\det(A)\ne0$ "A is invertible."
$T^{-1}(\mathbf{y})=A^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{b})$ "The inverse transformation subtracts b and then applies A inverse."
$T((1-t)x+ty)=(1-t)T(x)+tT(y)$ "T preserves affine combinations."

In elementary algebra, an expression such as:

$$ f(x)=mx+b $$

is often called a linear function. In linear algebra, however, it is technically affine unless:

$$ \boxed{b=0}. $$

A genuinely linear transformation must preserve the origin:

$$ L(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0}. $$

An affine transformation satisfies:

$$ T(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{b}, $$

which need not equal zero.

Defense Notation Advice: Do not assume that every expression $A\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}$ is linear. It is affine; it becomes linear precisely when the translation term $\mathbf{b}$ is zero.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • General Form: $$ \boxed{ T(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b} }. $$
  • Linear Transformation: Every linear transformation is affine by taking: $$ \mathbf{b}=\mathbf{0}. $$
  • Translation: $$ \boxed{ T(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b} } $$ is affine with $A=I$.
  • Rotation: A rotation about the origin is linear and therefore affine.
  • Rotation About Another Point: A rotation about an arbitrary center generally combines a linear rotation with a translation and is therefore affine.
  • Reflection: Reflections across lines or hyperplanes are affine transformations.
  • Uniform Scaling: $$ T(\mathbf{x})=s\mathbf{x} $$ is affine.
  • Nonuniform Scaling: Different coordinate directions may be scaled by different factors, and the resulting transformation is still affine.
  • Shear: For example: $$ \boxed{ \begin{pmatrix} x'\\ y' \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & k\\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x\\ y \end{pmatrix} } $$ is an affine shear.
  • Composition: The composition of affine maps is affine. If: $$ T_1(x)=A_1x+b_1 $$ and: $$ T_2(x)=A_2x+b_2, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ (T_2\circ T_1)(x) = A_2A_1x + A_2b_1+b_2 }. $$
  • Invertibility: An affine map: $$ T(x)=Ax+b $$ is invertible precisely when $A$ is invertible.
  • Determinant Test: For a square matrix $A$: $$ \boxed{ \det(A)\ne0 } $$ guarantees an invertible affine transformation.
  • Inverse: If $A$ is invertible: $$ \boxed{ T^{-1}(y) = A^{-1}(y-b) }. $$
  • Collinearity: Affine transformations preserve collinear point relationships.
  • Parallelism: Invertible affine transformations preserve parallel lines and parallel hyperplanes.
  • Midpoints: $$ \boxed{ T\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right) = \frac{T(x)+T(y)}{2} }. $$
  • Centroids: Because centroids are affine combinations of vertices, affine transformations preserve centroid relationships.
  • Barycentric Coordinates: If: $$ p=\lambda_1p_1+\cdots+\lambda_np_n $$ with: $$ \lambda_1+\cdots+\lambda_n=1, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ T(p) = \lambda_1T(p_1)+\cdots+\lambda_nT(p_n) }. $$
  • Ratios on a Line: Division ratios along a common line are preserved.
  • Convex Combinations: Affine transformations preserve convex combinations and therefore map convex sets to convex sets.
  • Triangles: An invertible affine transformation maps a nondegenerate triangle to another nondegenerate triangle.
  • Parallelograms: Parallelograms remain parallelograms because parallelism is preserved.
  • Rectangles: A rectangle need not remain a rectangle because right angles need not be preserved. It may become a general parallelogram.
  • Squares: A square may become a parallelogram under a general affine transformation.
  • Circles: A circle may become an ellipse, so circularity is not an affine invariant.
  • Length: General affine transformations do not preserve length.
  • Angles: General affine transformations do not preserve angle measure.
  • Perpendicularity: Perpendicular lines may cease to be perpendicular after a shear or nonuniform scaling.
  • Area Scaling: In two dimensions, an affine transformation with linear part $A$ multiplies area by: $$ \boxed{ |\det(A)| }. $$
  • Volume Scaling: In $\mathbb{R}^n$, $n$-dimensional volume is multiplied by: $$ \boxed{ |\det(A)| }. $$
  • Orientation: For an invertible real affine transformation: $$ \det(A)>0 $$ preserves orientation, while: $$ \det(A)<0 $$ reverses orientation.
  • Singular Affine Map: If: $$ \det(A)=0, $$ the map is not invertible and may collapse dimensions. A plane, for example, may be mapped onto a line.
  • Homogeneous Coordinates: An affine transformation can be represented as a single matrix multiplication: $$ \boxed{ \begin{pmatrix} x'\\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} A & b\\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x\\ 1 \end{pmatrix} } $$ in block notation.
  • Computer Graphics: Homogeneous-coordinate matrices allow translations, rotations, scalings, and shears to be combined efficiently into transformation pipelines.
  • Image Processing: Affine transformations are used for image translation, scaling, rotation, skewing, and geometric registration.
  • Coordinate Changes: Affine coordinate systems need not use perpendicular axes or equal unit scales.
  • Affine Function: A scalar-valued affine function has the form: $$ \boxed{ f(x)=a^Tx+b }. $$
  • Linear vs. Affine: A linear map necessarily sends the origin to the origin. An affine map may translate the origin elsewhere.
  • Euclidean Transformation Contrast: Rigid motions preserve lengths and angles. General affine transformations need not.
  • Similarity Transformation Contrast: Similarities preserve angles and scale all lengths by one common factor. A general affine transformation can use different scales in different directions and may shear the figure.
  • Projective Transformation Contrast: Affine transformations form a more restrictive class than general projective transformations. Affine geometry retains parallelism, whereas projective geometry allows parallel lines to meet at points at infinity.
  • Terminological Contrast: A linear transformation has the form $Ax$ and fixes the origin. An affine transformation has the form $Ax+b$ and may include translation. A rigid transformation preserves distances, while a general affine transformation preserves incidence, collinearity, parallelism, and affine ratios but may alter lengths and angles.
