Area below a Curve

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

Area below a curve refers to the two-dimensional region lying vertically between a graph and a lower boundary, commonly the $x$-axis, over a specified interval.

In elementary calculus, the closely related expression area under a curve is more common. Both phrases usually describe a region bounded by:

  • a function $y=f(x)$,
  • two vertical lines $x=a$ and $x=b$, and
  • the $x$-axis or another lower curve.

If $f(x)\ge0$ throughout $[a,b]$, the geometric area below the curve and above the $x$-axis is:

$$ \boxed{ A=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx }. $$

The word area is important: geometric area is nonnegative. A definite integral, however, is a signed quantity, so the two ideas must be distinguished when the graph passes below the axis.

Operational Nuance

Basic Case: Curve Above the x-Axis

Suppose:

$$ f(x)\ge0 \qquad \text{for }a\le x\le b. $$

Then the area bounded by the graph, the $x$-axis, and the vertical lines $x=a$ and $x=b$ is:

$$ \boxed{ A=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx }. $$

Riemann-Sum Foundation

The integral arises by approximating the region with many thin rectangles.

Divide $[a,b]$ into subintervals with widths $\Delta x_i$. A typical rectangle has approximate area:

$$ f(x_i^*)\Delta x_i. $$

Adding these rectangles gives:

$$ \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i^*)\Delta x_i. $$

In the limit:

$$ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \lim_{\|P\|\to0} \sum_i f(x_i^*)\Delta x_i }. $$

Width Times Height

The differential element of area is:

$$ \boxed{ dA=f(x)\,dx } $$

when the lower boundary is the $x$-axis and $f(x)\ge0$.

Conceptually:

$$ \boxed{ \text{area} = \int \text{height}\times\text{width} }. $$

Definite Integral as Net Area

A definite integral does not always equal geometric area.

If:

$$ f(x)<0 $$

on part of the interval, that portion contributes negatively to:

$$ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \text{signed or net area} }. $$

Area Above the Axis

Regions above the $x$-axis contribute positively:

$$ f(x)>0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{positive contribution}. $$

Area Below the Axis

Regions below the $x$-axis contribute negatively to the definite integral:

$$ f(x)<0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{negative contribution}. $$

But their geometric area remains positive.

Total Geometric Area

The total geometric area between the graph and the $x$-axis is:

$$ \boxed{ A= \int_a^b |f(x)|\,dx } $$

whenever the integral exists.

Splitting at x-Intercepts

In practice, geometric area is often computed by locating where:

$$ f(x)=0 $$

and splitting the integral at those points.

If $f$ is positive on $[a,c]$ and negative on $[c,b]$, then:

$$ \boxed{ A= \int_a^c f(x)\,dx - \int_c^b f(x)\,dx }. $$

The minus sign converts the negative integral on $[c,b]$ into positive geometric area.

Example: y = x

Consider:

$$ y=x, \qquad 0\le x\le2. $$

Since the graph is above the $x$-axis:

$$ A = \int_0^2 x\,dx. $$

Therefore:

$$ A = \left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^2 = \boxed{2}. $$

Geometric Check

The same region is a right triangle with base $2$ and height $2$:

$$ A = \frac12(2)(2) = \boxed{2}. $$

The geometric formula and the definite integral agree.

Example with Positive and Negative Portions

Consider:

$$ f(x)=x, \qquad -1\le x\le1. $$

The definite integral is:

$$ \int_{-1}^{1}x\,dx=0. $$

But the geometric area is not zero. There are two triangular regions, each having area $1/2$:

$$ \boxed{ A=1 }. $$

Equivalently:

$$ \boxed{ A= \int_{-1}^{1}|x|\,dx = 1 }. $$

Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

If $F'(x)=f(x)$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b)-F(a) }. $$

This allows the limiting area process to be evaluated through antiderivatives.

Accumulated Area Function

One can define:

$$ A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt. $$

Under appropriate continuity conditions:

$$ \boxed{ A'(x)=f(x) }. $$

Thus the original function gives the rate at which signed area accumulates.

Area between Two Curves

Suppose the upper curve is:

$$ y=f(x) $$

and the lower curve is:

$$ y=g(x), $$

with:

$$ f(x)\ge g(x). $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ A= \int_a^b \bigl(f(x)-g(x)\bigr)\,dx }. $$

Top Minus Bottom

The vertical height of a representative rectangle is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{height} = \text{top}-\text{bottom} }. $$

Therefore:

$$ dA= \bigl(f(x)-g(x)\bigr)\,dx. $$

Changing Which Curve Is on Top

If the curves cross, one formula may not work across the entire interval. Intersection points should be found and the integral split where the upper and lower functions exchange roles.

Integration with Respect to y

Sometimes horizontal slices are simpler.

If:

$$ x=x_{\text{right}}(y) $$

and:

$$ x=x_{\text{left}}(y), $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ A= \int_c^d \left( x_{\text{right}}(y) - x_{\text{left}}(y) \right)\,dy }. $$

Right Minus Left

With horizontal slices:

$$ \boxed{ \text{width} = \text{right}-\text{left} }. $$

Improper Areas

Some regions extend indefinitely yet may have finite area.

For example:

$$ \int_1^\infty \frac1{x^2}\,dx = \lim_{b\to\infty} \int_1^b\frac1{x^2}\,dx = \boxed{1}. $$

The region extends infinitely far to the right but has finite total area.

Infinite Area

Other improper regions do not have finite area. For example:

$$ \int_1^\infty\frac1x\,dx $$

diverges, so the corresponding unbounded region has infinite area.

Units

Area is measured in square units:

$$ \boxed{ \text{units}^2 }. $$

If $x$ is measured in meters and $f(x)$ is also measured in meters, then:

$$ f(x)\,dx $$

has units of square meters.

Common Misconception: The statement "$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ is the area under the curve" is only literally correct when $f(x)\ge0$ on the interval. In general the definite integral gives signed area.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ "The definite integral of f of x from a to b."
$A=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ "Area equals the integral of f from a to b," when f is nonnegative.
$\int_a^b |f(x)|\,dx$ "The integral of the absolute value of f from a to b."
$\int_a^b(f-g)\,dx$ "The integral of the upper function minus the lower function."
$dA=f(x)\,dx$ "D A equals f of x d x."
$A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt$ "A of x is the accumulated signed area from a to x."

The phrases area below a curve and area under a curve are often used informally as synonyms. In precise work, however, the boundaries of the region should be explicitly identified.

For example:

$$ \boxed{ \text{area below }y=f(x) \text{ and above the }x\text{-axis} } $$

is more precise than merely saying "the area below the curve."

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish definite integral, signed area, and geometric area. Use absolute values or split the interval whenever a region below the $x$-axis must count positively.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: Area below a curve measures a two-dimensional region bounded in part by a graph.
  • Common Synonym: Area under a curve is the more common elementary-calculus phrasing.
  • Nonnegative Function: If $f(x)\ge0$ on $[a,b]$: $$ \boxed{ A=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx }. $$
  • Signed Area: $$ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx } $$ counts regions below the $x$-axis negatively.
  • Geometric Area: $$ \boxed{ A=\int_a^b|f(x)|\,dx } $$ gives total area between the graph and the $x$-axis when applicable.
  • Area Element: $$ \boxed{ dA=f(x)\,dx } $$ for a nonnegative function above the $x$-axis.
  • Rectangle Interpretation: $$ \boxed{ dA= \text{height}\times\text{width} }. $$
  • Riemann Sum: $$ \boxed{ \sum_i f(x_i^*)\Delta x_i } $$ approximates the integral.
  • Limit Definition: $$ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \lim_{\|P\|\to0} \sum_i f(x_i^*)\Delta x_i }. $$
  • Fundamental Theorem: If $F'=f$: $$ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b)-F(a) }. $$
  • Area Accumulation Function: $$ \boxed{ A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt }. $$
  • Derivative of Accumulated Area: Under suitable continuity: $$ \boxed{ A'(x)=f(x) }. $$
  • Crossing the x-Axis: Split the integral wherever $f(x)=0$ if geometric area is required.
  • Cancellation: Positive and negative signed areas can cancel in a definite integral.
  • Zero Integral Does Not Imply Zero Area: A function can enclose substantial geometric area while its net integral equals zero.
  • Odd Symmetry: For an integrable odd function: $$ \boxed{ \int_{-a}^{a}f(x)\,dx=0 }. $$
  • Even Symmetry: For an integrable even function: $$ \boxed{ \int_{-a}^{a}f(x)\,dx = 2\int_0^a f(x)\,dx }. $$
  • Area between Curves: If $f(x)\ge g(x)$: $$ \boxed{ A= \int_a^b [f(x)-g(x)]\,dx }. $$
  • Vertical Slices: $$ \boxed{ \text{top}-\text{bottom} }. $$
  • Horizontal Slices: $$ \boxed{ \text{right}-\text{left} }. $$
  • Area with respect to y: $$ \boxed{ A= \int_c^d [x_{\text{right}}(y)-x_{\text{left}}(y)]\,dy }. $$
  • Intersection Points: Curves should be split at points where the upper and lower boundaries exchange positions.
  • Piecewise Functions: Area may need to be computed separately over each piece.
  • Improper Area: Unbounded regions are evaluated using limits.
  • Finite Unbounded Region: $$ \boxed{ \int_1^\infty\frac1{x^2}\,dx=1 }. $$
  • Divergent Area: $$ \boxed{ \int_1^\infty\frac1x\,dx \text{ diverges} }. $$
  • Probability: For a probability density function $f(x)$: $$ \boxed{ P(a\le X\le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx }. $$
  • Probability Density Requirement: A probability density satisfies: $$ \boxed{ f(x)\ge0 } $$ and: $$ \boxed{ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx=1 }. $$
  • Motion: The integral of velocity gives displacement, not necessarily total distance. This mirrors the distinction between signed area and geometric area.
  • Total Distance Analogy: $$ \boxed{ \text{total distance} = \int |v(t)|\,dt } $$ parallels: $$ \boxed{ \text{geometric area} = \int |f(x)|\,dx }. $$
  • Economics: Areas under marginal functions can represent accumulated changes in total quantities.
  • Physics: Areas under rate-versus-variable graphs frequently represent accumulated quantities, though dimensional interpretation depends on the axes.
  • Units: If both axes measure length, geometric area is measured in square units.
  • Dimensional Caution: An integral represented visually as an area need not physically represent geometric area if the axes carry different units.
  • Graphical Estimate: Area may be approximated with rectangles, trapezoids, or numerical integration when an antiderivative is unavailable.
  • Numerical Integration: Methods include midpoint, trapezoidal, and Simpson-type approximations.
  • Not Always Elementary: A definite integral may represent an exact area even when no elementary antiderivative exists.
  • Terminological Contrast: Area below a curve describes a geometric region relative to specified boundaries. A definite integral measures signed accumulation. Net area allows positive and negative contributions to cancel, while total geometric area counts every bounded region positively.
