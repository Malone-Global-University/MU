Collinear

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

Points are collinear when they all lie on the same straight line.

$$ \boxed{ A,B,C\text{ are collinear} \iff \text{one line contains }A,B,C }. $$

The idea is fundamentally geometric: the points need not be equally spaced, and their order along the line does not matter. What matters is that a single line contains all of them.

The corresponding noun is collinearity.

Operational Nuance

Basic Geometric Definition

Any two distinct points determine exactly one line. A third point is collinear with them if it lies on that same line.

$$ \boxed{ C\in\overleftrightarrow{AB} \Rightarrow A,B,C\text{ are collinear} }. $$

Two Points Are Automatically Collinear

Any pair of points is collinear because at least one line contains both.

The idea becomes a meaningful condition primarily when three or more points are involved.

Three Distinct Collinear Points

If $A$, $B$, and $C$ lie on one line, one of the points may lie between the other two.

For example, if $B$ lies between $A$ and $C$, then:

$$ \boxed{ AB+BC=AC }. $$

Distance Test

For three points, a distance relation can sometimes establish collinearity. If one point lies between the other two, then:

$$ \boxed{ AB+BC=AC }. $$

More generally, the largest of the three pairwise distances equals the sum of the other two exactly when the three points are collinear and one lies between the other two.

Slope Test

For points:

$$ A=(x_1,y_1),\qquad B=(x_2,y_2),\qquad C=(x_3,y_3), $$

if the relevant denominators are nonzero, collinearity can be tested by comparing slopes:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1} = \frac{y_3-y_1}{x_3-x_1} }. $$

Example Using Slopes

Consider:

$$ A=(1,2),\qquad B=(3,6),\qquad C=(5,10). $$

Then:

$$ m_{AB} = \frac{6-2}{3-1} = 2 $$

and:

$$ m_{AC} = \frac{10-2}{5-1} = 2. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ A,B,C\text{ are collinear} }. $$

Problem with Vertical Lines

A direct slope test can become awkward when the line is vertical because the usual slope formula has denominator zero.

For example:

$$ (4,1),\qquad (4,5),\qquad (4,-2) $$

are clearly collinear on:

$$ \boxed{ x=4 }. $$

even though the ordinary slope is undefined.

Cross-Multiplied Coordinate Test

To avoid division, use:

$$ \boxed{ (y_2-y_1)(x_3-x_1) = (y_3-y_1)(x_2-x_1) }. $$

This works for vertical and nonvertical configurations alike.

Determinant Test

Three planar points are collinear exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{vmatrix} x_1&y_1&1\\ x_2&y_2&1\\ x_3&y_3&1 \end{vmatrix} = 0 }. $$

Expanded Determinant Form

$$ \boxed{ x_1(y_2-y_3) + x_2(y_3-y_1) + x_3(y_1-y_2) = 0 }. $$

Area Test

Three points are collinear exactly when the triangle they would form has area zero.

$$ \boxed{ K_{\triangle ABC}=0 }. $$

Using coordinates:

$$ K = \frac12 \left| x_1(y_2-y_3) + x_2(y_3-y_1) + x_3(y_1-y_2) \right|. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ K=0 \iff A,B,C\text{ are collinear} }. $$

Vector Test

Let:

$$ \overrightarrow{AB} = B-A $$

and:

$$ \overrightarrow{AC} = C-A. $$

Then $A$, $B$, and $C$ are collinear exactly when these two displacement vectors are scalar multiples:

$$ \boxed{ \overrightarrow{AC} = \lambda\overrightarrow{AB} } $$

for some scalar $\lambda$.

Meaning of the Scalar λ

In:

$$ C=A+\lambda(B-A), $$

the value of $\lambda$ indicates where $C$ lies relative to $A$ and $B$.

Value of $\lambda$ Geometric Position
$\lambda=0$ $C=A$
$0<\lambda<1$ $C$ lies between $A$ and $B$
$\lambda=1$ $C=B$
$\lambda>1$ $C$ lies beyond $B$
$\lambda<0$ $C$ lies on the opposite side of $A$ from $B$

Parametric Equation of a Line

The line through points $A$ and $B$ can be written:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf r = \mathbf A+t(\mathbf B-\mathbf A), \qquad t\in\mathbb R }. $$

A point $C$ is collinear with $A$ and $B$ exactly when its position vector satisfies this equation for some $t$.

Affine Combination Interpretation

A point on the line through $A$ and $B$ can also be written:

$$ \boxed{ C=(1-t)A+tB }. $$

Because the coefficients add to $1$, this is an affine combination of $A$ and $B$.

Collinearity in Three Dimensions

Points in $\mathbb R^3$ are collinear when their displacement vectors are parallel.

For:

$$ A,B,C\in\mathbb R^3, $$

collinearity is equivalent to:

$$ \boxed{ \overrightarrow{AB} \times \overrightarrow{AC} = \mathbf0 }. $$

Why the Cross Product Works

The magnitude:

$$ \left| \overrightarrow{AB} \times \overrightarrow{AC} \right| $$

equals the area of the parallelogram determined by the two vectors.

Zero area means the vectors are parallel, so the three points lie on one line.

More Than Three Points

Points $P_1,P_2,\ldots,P_n$ are collinear if one line contains every one of them.

Once two distinct points determine the candidate line, every remaining point must lie on that same line.

Collinearity and Linear Dependence

For three points, collinearity can be expressed through linear dependence of displacement vectors:

$$ \boxed{ B-A \text{ and } C-A \text{ are linearly dependent} }. $$

This connects elementary geometry directly to linear algebra.

Collinearity and Rank

In the plane, the matrix:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} x_2-x_1&y_2-y_1\\ x_3-x_1&y_3-y_1 \end{bmatrix} $$

has rank at most $1$ when the three points are collinear.

Collinear Points and Triangle Degeneracy

Three collinear points do not form a nondegenerate triangle.

$$ \boxed{ A,B,C\text{ collinear} \Rightarrow \text{degenerate triangle} }. $$

Its area is zero.

Circumcircle Consequence

Three distinct collinear points do not determine a finite ordinary circumcircle.

This contrasts with three noncollinear points, which determine exactly one circle.

Collinear versus Coplanar

Collinear points all lie on one line.

Coplanar points all lie in one plane.

$$ \boxed{ \text{collinear} \Rightarrow \text{coplanar} } $$

but the converse is generally false.

Why Collinear Points Are Coplanar

Any line lies in infinitely many planes, so any collection of points on one line is automatically contained in a plane.

Collinear versus Parallel

Collinear normally describes points lying on one line.

Parallel normally describes lines, segments, vectors, or directions that share direction without necessarily occupying the same line.

Collinear versus Coincident

Collinear points may occupy different locations along the same line.

Coincident points occupy the exact same location.

$$ \boxed{ \text{coincident points} \Rightarrow \text{collinear with any third point on the line} } $$

but distinct collinear points are not coincident.

Geometry Theorems and Collinearity

Many classical geometric theorems assert that specially constructed points are collinear. Examples include configurations involving triangle centers, projective geometry, and transversals.

In such contexts, proving collinearity means proving that several independently defined points actually lie on a single line.

Common Misconception: Collinear Means Equally Spaced

Equal spacing is not required. The points only need to lie on the same line.

Common Misconception: Three Points with Equal x-Coordinates Are Not Testable by Slope

They may still be collinear on a vertical line. Use the common $x$-coordinate, cross-multiplication, determinant, or vector method instead of ordinary finite slope division.

Common Misconception: Zero Triangle Area Is Impossible

If the three proposed vertices are collinear, the triangle is degenerate and has area zero.

Common Misconception: Coplanar Means Collinear

Many points can lie in the same plane without lying on one line.

Common Misconception: Same Slope Alone Is Always Enough

Equal slopes from unrelated pairs may describe parallel directions rather than one common line. The compared slopes must be formed from the candidate points in a way that shares a reference point or otherwise confirms they lie on the same line.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$A,B,C$ collinear "A, B, and C are collinear."
$C\in\overleftrightarrow{AB}$ "C lies on the line through A and B."
$\overrightarrow{AC}=\lambda\overrightarrow{AB}$ "A C is a scalar multiple of A B, so the three points are collinear."
$K_{\triangle ABC}=0$ "The three points form a degenerate triangle of area zero."
$\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}=\mathbf0$ "The displacement vectors are parallel, so the points are collinear."

The most natural spoken form is simply "the points are collinear" or "the points lie on the same line." In coordinate work, it is often clearer to state the test being used, such as "the slopes are equal" or "the determinant is zero."

Defense Notation Advice: For coordinate proofs, prefer a determinant, cross-multiplied slope relation, or vector test when vertical lines are possible. These avoid undefined-slope complications and make the condition symmetric and algebraically robust.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: Collinear points lie on one common straight line.
  • Two-Point Case: Any two points are collinear.
  • Slope Test: $$ \boxed{ \frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1} = \frac{y_3-y_1}{x_3-x_1} } $$ when the denominators are nonzero.
  • Division-Free Test: $$ \boxed{ (y_2-y_1)(x_3-x_1) = (y_3-y_1)(x_2-x_1) }. $$
  • Determinant Test: $$ \boxed{ \begin{vmatrix} x_1&y_1&1\\ x_2&y_2&1\\ x_3&y_3&1 \end{vmatrix} =0 }. $$
  • Area Test: $$ \boxed{ K_{\triangle ABC}=0 }. $$
  • Vector Test: $$ \boxed{ \overrightarrow{AC} = \lambda\overrightarrow{AB} }. $$
  • Three-Dimensional Test: $$ \boxed{ \overrightarrow{AB} \times \overrightarrow{AC} = \mathbf0 }. $$
  • Affine Form: $$ \boxed{ C=(1-t)A+tB }. $$
  • Degenerate Triangle: Three collinear proposed vertices produce zero area.
  • Circle Geometry: Three distinct collinear points do not determine a finite ordinary circumcircle.
  • Coplanarity: Every collinear collection of points is coplanar.
  • Common Error: Do not assume collinear points must be equally spaced.
  • Common Error: Do not reject a vertical configuration merely because slope is undefined.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse collinear with coincident.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse collinear with merely coplanar.
  • Terminological Contrast: Collinear means lying on one line. Collinearity is the property of being collinear. Coincident means occupying the same geometric position. Coplanar means lying in one plane. Parallel describes matching directions rather than common point membership. Degenerate triangle results when three proposed vertices are collinear.
