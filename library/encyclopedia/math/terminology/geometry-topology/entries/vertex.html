Vertex

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

A vertex is a distinguished point in a mathematical structure. In elementary geometry, it is commonly the point where two sides of an angle meet, where consecutive sides of a polygon meet, or where edges of a polyhedron meet. In other contexts, such as conic sections and graph theory, the word has related but more specialized meanings.

The word comes from Latin vertex, meaning a turning point, summit, or highest point, ultimately related to the Latin verb vertere, meaning "to turn." This linguistic history helps explain why vertex became associated with corners, turning points, and distinguished extreme points.

The normal mathematical plural is:

$$ \boxed{\text{vertices}} $$

pronounced approximately "VER-tuh-seez." The plural vertexes also exists in English, but vertices is overwhelmingly preferred in mathematical writing.

The term has a long history in English mathematical vocabulary. It appears in John Dee's preface to Henry Billingsley's 1570 English translation of Euclid.

A vertex should not automatically be interpreted as the physically highest point of a diagram. Mathematical figures may be rotated without changing which points are vertices. The term identifies a structural role, not merely visual height.

Operational Nuance

Vertex of an Angle

An angle is formed by two rays with a common endpoint. That common endpoint is the vertex of the angle.

In:

$$ \angle ABC, $$

the vertex is:

$$ \boxed{B}. $$

The middle letter identifies the vertex.

Vertex of a Polygon

In a polygon, a vertex is a point where two adjacent sides meet. A triangle:

$$ \triangle ABC $$

has three vertices:

$$ A,\ B,\ C. $$

A quadrilateral has four vertices, a pentagon has five, and in general an $n$-gon has:

$$ \boxed{n\text{ vertices}}. $$

Vertex of a Polyhedron

In a three-dimensional polyhedron, a vertex is a point where several edges meet. For example, a cube has:

$$ 8\text{ vertices}, $$

together with:

$$ 12\text{ edges} $$

and:

$$ 6\text{ faces}. $$

Vertex of a Parabola

For a parabola, the vertex is the point where the curve reaches its local minimum or maximum along its axis of symmetry.

In vertex form:

$$ \boxed{ y=a(x-h)^2+k }, $$

the vertex is:

$$ \boxed{(h,k)}. $$

If:

$$ a>0, $$

the parabola opens upward and the vertex is a minimum point. If:

$$ a<0, $$

the parabola opens downward and the vertex is a maximum point.

Finding the Vertex of a Quadratic

For:

$$ y=ax^2+bx+c, \qquad a\ne0, $$

the $x$-coordinate of the vertex is:

$$ \boxed{ x_v=-\frac{b}{2a} }. $$

The corresponding $y$-coordinate is:

$$ y_v = f\left(-\frac{b}{2a}\right). $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ V= \left( -\frac{b}{2a}, f\left(-\frac{b}{2a}\right) \right) }. $$

Vertices of Other Conic Sections

Ellipses and hyperbolas also have vertices. In these contexts, the vertices are distinguished points where the principal axis intersects the conic.

For the ellipse:

$$ \frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1, \qquad a>b>0, $$

the major-axis vertices are:

$$ \boxed{(\pm a,0)}. $$

The points:

$$ (0,\pm b) $$

are commonly called co-vertices.

Vertex in Graph Theory

In graph theory, a vertex is an abstract object representing a node of a graph. A graph is often written:

$$ \boxed{ G=(V,E) }, $$

where:

$$ V=\text{set of vertices} $$

and:

$$ E=\text{set of edges}. $$

Here a vertex need not represent a geometric corner at all. It may represent a city, computer, person, state, webpage, or any other object participating in a network.

Common Misconception: A vertex does not always mean "the top point." Its meaning is determined by the mathematical structure in which it appears.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\angle ABC$ "Angle A B C," with B as the vertex.
$V=(h,k)$ "The vertex is h comma k."
$x_v=-\frac{b}{2a}$ "The x-coordinate of the vertex equals negative b over two a."
$G=(V,E)$ "G is a graph with vertex set V and edge set E."
$|V|$ "The number of vertices," or "the cardinality of V."
$V-E+F=2$ "Vertices minus edges plus faces equals two."

The singular and plural forms are:

$$ \boxed{ \text{vertex} \longrightarrow \text{vertices} }. $$

In geometric notation, the vertex of an angle written with three letters is normally the middle letter:

$$ \angle XYZ \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{Y\text{ is the vertex}}. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Do not confuse a vertex with a vertical direction. Although the words share linguistic ancestry, their mathematical meanings are distinct.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Angle: The common endpoint of the two rays forming an angle is its vertex.
  • Triangle: A triangle has: $$ \boxed{3\text{ vertices}}. $$ Each vertex is the meeting point of two sides.
  • Polygon: An $n$-gon has: $$ \boxed{n\text{ vertices}}. $$ The vertices occur where consecutive sides meet.
  • Regular Polygon: In a regular polygon, all vertices lie on a common circumscribed circle.
  • Interior Angle: Each interior angle of a polygon is located at one of its vertices.
  • Diagonal: A polygonal diagonal joins two nonadjacent vertices.
  • Polyhedron: A vertex is a zero-dimensional feature where edges meet. Faces are two-dimensional features, and edges are one-dimensional features.
  • Cube: A cube has: $$ V=8,\qquad E=12,\qquad F=6. $$
  • Euler's Polyhedron Formula: For a convex polyhedron: $$ \boxed{ V-E+F=2 }, $$ where $V$ is the number of vertices, $E$ the number of edges, and $F$ the number of faces.
  • Pyramid: The distinguished vertex opposite the base is commonly called the apex. It is still a vertex, but not every vertex of a pyramid is an apex.
  • Vertex vs. Apex: Vertex is the broader structural term. Apex usually identifies a particular distinguished vertex opposite a base.
  • Parabola: For: $$ y=a(x-h)^2+k, $$ the vertex is: $$ \boxed{(h,k)}. $$
  • Axis of Symmetry: A vertical parabola with vertex $(h,k)$ has axis: $$ \boxed{x=h}. $$
  • Quadratic Standard Form: For: $$ f(x)=ax^2+bx+c, $$ the vertex occurs at: $$ \boxed{ x=-\frac{b}{2a} }. $$
  • Completing the Square: Rewriting a quadratic from: $$ ax^2+bx+c $$ into: $$ a(x-h)^2+k $$ makes the vertex immediately visible as: $$ (h,k). $$
  • Optimization: For a quadratic function, the vertex determines the global minimum when $a>0$ and the global maximum when $a<0$.
  • Horizontal Parabola: For: $$ x=a(y-k)^2+h, $$ the vertex is again: $$ \boxed{(h,k)}. $$
  • Ellipse: For a horizontally oriented ellipse: $$ \frac{(x-h)^2}{a^2} + \frac{(y-k)^2}{b^2} = 1, \qquad a>b, $$ its principal vertices are: $$ \boxed{(h\pm a,k)}. $$
  • Ellipse Co-Vertices: Under the same orientation, the endpoints of the minor axis are: $$ \boxed{(h,k\pm b)}, $$ commonly called co-vertices.
  • Hyperbola: For: $$ \frac{(x-h)^2}{a^2} - \frac{(y-k)^2}{b^2} = 1, $$ the vertices are: $$ \boxed{(h\pm a,k)}. $$
  • Conic Interpretation: In a parabola, ellipse, or hyperbola, a vertex is a distinguished point where a principal axis meets the curve.
  • Graph Theory: A graph: $$ G=(V,E) $$ consists of a vertex set $V$ and an edge set $E$.
  • Node: In computing and network language, node is frequently used as a synonym for graph-theoretic vertex.
  • Vertex Degree: The degree of a vertex in an undirected graph is the number of edges incident to it, with loops counted according to the graph-theoretic convention being used.
  • Adjacent Vertices: Two graph vertices are adjacent when an edge connects them.
  • Path: A graph-theoretic path is a sequence of vertices connected successively by edges.
  • Tree: A finite tree with: $$ n\text{ vertices} $$ has: $$ \boxed{n-1\text{ edges}}. $$
  • Convex Geometry: For a convex polygon or polytope, the word vertex is closely connected with the idea of an extreme point.
  • Coordinate Geometry: Vertices may be represented by ordered pairs or tuples. For example, the vertices of a planar triangle might be: $$ A=(x_1,y_1),\quad B=(x_2,y_2),\quad C=(x_3,y_3). $$
  • Transformations: Translation, rotation, reflection, and dilation move polygonal vertices while preserving or predictably changing geometric relationships among them.
  • Not Necessarily a Sharp Corner: In conic-section terminology, the vertex of a parabola is a smooth point of the curve. A vertex therefore does not universally mean a visually sharp corner.
  • Not Necessarily an Intersection: A graph-theoretic vertex is an abstract element of a vertex set and need not arise from intersecting geometric lines.
  • Terminological Contrast: A vertex is a distinguished point of a geometric or combinatorial structure. An edge joins vertices in polygons, polyhedra, or graphs. A face is a two-dimensional boundary component of a polyhedron. An apex is a specially designated vertex, while a focus is a different distinguished point used in the definition of conic sections.
