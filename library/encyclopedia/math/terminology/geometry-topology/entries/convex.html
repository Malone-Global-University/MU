Convex

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

In mathematics, convex describes objects whose interior structure does not bend inward in the relevant sense. The exact definition depends on the object being discussed.

For a set $C$ in a vector space, convexity means that whenever two points belong to the set, the entire line segment joining them also belongs to the set.

$$ \boxed{ x,y\in C,\quad 0\le t\le1 \Rightarrow (1-t)x+ty\in C }. $$

For a real-valued function $f$, convexity means that the graph lies at or below the straight chord connecting any two points on the graph:

$$ \boxed{ f((1-t)x+ty) \le (1-t)f(x)+tf(y), \qquad 0\le t\le1 }. $$

Thus the word convex carries one unifying idea across geometry and analysis: interpolation between two admissible points remains controlled by the object rather than passing through an inward indentation.

Operational Nuance

Convex Set

A set $C$ is convex if every line segment joining two points of $C$ lies entirely inside $C$.

$$ \boxed{ [x,y]\subseteq C \quad\text{for all }x,y\in C }. $$

The line segment from $x$ to $y$ can be parameterized as:

$$ \boxed{ (1-t)x+ty, \qquad 0\le t\le1 }. $$

Geometric Interpretation

Pick any two points in a convex region. If a straight line segment connecting them never leaves the region, the region satisfies the geometric convexity condition.

Examples of Convex Sets

Common convex sets include:

  • intervals on the real number line,
  • disks and balls,
  • half-planes and half-spaces,
  • rectangles and boxes,
  • triangles,
  • convex polygons and polyhedra,
  • affine subspaces, and
  • the empty set and single-point sets under standard conventions.

Nonconvex Set

A set is nonconvex if there exist two points in the set whose connecting line segment leaves the set.

$$ \boxed{ \exists x,y\in C,\; \exists t\in(0,1): (1-t)x+ty\notin C }. $$

Convex Polygon

A polygon is convex if its entire interior lies on the same side of each edge line and every segment joining two points of the polygon lies within the polygon.

Equivalently, for an ordinary simple polygon, every interior angle is strictly less than:

$$ \boxed{ 180^\circ } $$

when collinear degeneracies are excluded.

Concave Polygon Contrast

A concave polygon contains at least one inward indentation. At least one interior angle exceeds:

$$ 180^\circ. $$

Diagonal Test for Polygons

In a convex polygon, every diagonal lies inside the polygon, apart from its endpoints on the boundary.

A concave polygon has at least one diagonal that passes partly outside the polygon.

Convex Combination

A convex combination of points $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ is an expression:

$$ \boxed{ \lambda_1x_1+\cdots+\lambda_nx_n } $$

where:

$$ \boxed{ \lambda_i\ge0, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_i=1 }. $$

Convexity of a set is equivalent to closure under all finite convex combinations.

Two-Point Convex Combination

For two points:

$$ (1-t)x+ty, \qquad 0\le t\le1, $$

the coefficients are nonnegative and add to $1$.

Midpoint Convexity

A set is midpoint convex if:

$$ x,y\in C \Rightarrow \frac{x+y}{2}\in C. $$

Ordinary convexity implies midpoint convexity. Under additional regularity assumptions, midpoint conditions can sometimes be strengthened to full convexity.

Convex Hull

The convex hull of a set $S$ is the smallest convex set containing $S$.

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{conv}(S) }. $$

It can be described as the set of all finite convex combinations of points of $S$.

Finite-Point Convex Hull

For points $x_1,\ldots,x_n$:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{conv}\{x_1,\ldots,x_n\} = \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_i x_i: \lambda_i\ge0,\; \sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_i=1 \right\} }. $$

Intersection of Convex Sets

Any intersection of convex sets is convex.

$$ \boxed{ \{C_\alpha\}_{\alpha\in A} \text{ convex} \Rightarrow \bigcap_{\alpha\in A}C_\alpha \text{ convex} }. $$

Union Need Not Be Convex

The union of two convex sets need not be convex.

For example, two disjoint disks are individually convex, but their union is not.

Affine Images of Convex Sets

If $C$ is convex and:

$$ T(x)=Ax+b $$

is affine, then:

$$ \boxed{ T(C) \text{ is convex} }. $$

Preimages under Affine Maps

The inverse image of a convex set under an affine map is also convex.

Convex Cone

A convex cone is a set closed under nonnegative linear combinations:

$$ x,y\in C,\quad \alpha,\beta\ge0 \Rightarrow \alpha x+\beta y\in C. $$

A convex cone need not be bounded and generally differs from an ordinary convex set because the coefficients need not sum to $1$.

Convex Function

Let $C$ be a convex subset of a vector space. A function:

$$ f:C\to\mathbb R $$

is convex if:

$$ \boxed{ f((1-t)x+ty) \le (1-t)f(x)+tf(y) } $$

for all $x,y\in C$ and $0\le t\le1$.

Geometric Interpretation of a Convex Function

Between any two points on the graph, the graph of a convex function lies at or below the chord joining those points.

Example: Quadratic Function

The function:

$$ f(x)=x^2 $$

is convex on $\mathbb R$.

Affine Functions

Every affine function:

$$ f(x)=mx+b $$

is both convex and concave because equality holds in the defining inequality:

$$ f((1-t)x+ty) = (1-t)f(x)+tf(y). $$

Strictly Convex Function

A function is strictly convex if:

$$ \boxed{ f((1-t)x+ty) < (1-t)f(x)+tf(y) } $$

whenever $x\ne y$ and $0

Strict versus Ordinary Convexity

Ordinary convexity permits equality along a chord. Strict convexity requires the graph to lie strictly below the chord between distinct points.

Convexity and Second Derivatives

If $f$ is twice differentiable on an interval and:

$$ \boxed{ f''(x)\ge0 } $$

throughout the interval, then $f$ is convex there.

Strict Second-Derivative Criterion

If:

$$ \boxed{ f''(x)>0 } $$

throughout an interval, then $f$ is strictly convex there.

The converse need not require strict positivity everywhere.

First-Derivative Criterion

For differentiable functions of one variable, convexity is equivalent to the derivative being nondecreasing.

$$ \boxed{ f\text{ convex} \iff f'\text{ nondecreasing} } $$

under the standard differentiability hypotheses on an interval.

Tangent-Line Property

If $f$ is differentiable and convex, every tangent line lies below or on the graph:

$$ \boxed{ f(y) \ge f(x)+f'(x)(y-x) }. $$

This inequality is fundamental in optimization.

Multivariable Convex Function

For:

$$ f:C\subseteq\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R, $$

convexity has the same line-segment definition:

$$ f((1-t)x+ty) \le (1-t)f(x)+tf(y). $$

Gradient Characterization

If $f$ is differentiable on a convex domain, then $f$ is convex exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ f(y) \ge f(x)+\nabla f(x)\cdot(y-x) } $$

for all $x,y$ in the domain.

Hessian Criterion

If $f$ is twice continuously differentiable on a convex open set, then $f$ is convex when its Hessian matrix is positive semidefinite everywhere:

$$ \boxed{ \nabla^2f(x)\succeq0 }. $$

Positive Definite Hessian

A positive definite Hessian throughout a convex region is a common sufficient condition for strict convexity.

Epigraph

The epigraph of $f$ is:

$$ \operatorname{epi}(f) = \{(x,r):r\ge f(x)\}. $$

A function is convex exactly when its epigraph is a convex set.

$$ \boxed{ f\text{ convex} \iff \operatorname{epi}(f)\text{ convex} }. $$

Sublevel Sets

If $f$ is convex, every sublevel set:

$$ \{x:f(x)\le c\} $$

is convex.

The converse does not characterize ordinary convexity; functions with convex sublevel sets are called quasiconvex.

Convexity and Optimization

Convexity is central to optimization because local minima of convex functions over convex feasible sets are global minima.

$$ \boxed{ \text{local minimum} \Rightarrow \text{global minimum} } $$

for convex optimization problems under the standard hypotheses.

Strict Convexity and Uniqueness

A strictly convex objective has at most one minimizer on a convex domain.

$$ \boxed{ \text{strict convexity} \Rightarrow \text{at most one minimizer} }. $$

Convex Feasible Region

An optimization problem has a convex feasible region when all feasible points remain feasible under convex combinations.

Jensen's Inequality

If $f$ is convex and:

$$ \lambda_i\ge0, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_i=1, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ f\left( \sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_i x_i \right) \le \sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_i f(x_i) }. $$

Probabilistic Form of Jensen's Inequality

If $f$ is convex and the relevant expectations exist, then:

$$ \boxed{ f(E[X]) \le E[f(X)] }. $$

Convexity and Averages

Jensen's inequality formalizes the idea that applying a convex function after taking an average produces no larger value than averaging the function values in the reversed comparison:

$$ f(\text{average input}) \le \text{average output}. $$

Weighted Average Interpretation

A convex combination is mathematically a weighted average of points, provided the coefficients are nonnegative and total $1$.

Convex Hull and Barycentric Coordinates

Points in the convex hull of finitely many vertices can be represented by nonnegative barycentric-type coordinates whose coefficients sum to $1$.

Carathéodory's Theorem

In $\mathbb R^d$, any point in the convex hull of a set can be represented as a convex combination of at most $d+1$ points from that set.

Convex Polytope

A convex polytope may be described as the convex hull of finitely many points.

$$ \boxed{ P= \operatorname{conv}\{v_1,\ldots,v_m\} }. $$

Half-Space Description

Convex polyhedra can also be represented as intersections of affine half-spaces:

$$ Ax\le b. $$

Since intersections of convex sets are convex, such feasible regions are convex.

Linear Programming

The feasible region of a linear program is convex because it is formed from linear equalities and inequalities.

Its objective function is affine and therefore both convex and concave.

Convex Norm Balls

In a normed vector space, every norm ball:

$$ \{x:\|x-c\|\le r\} $$

is convex.

Why Norm Balls Are Convex

This follows from the triangle inequality and homogeneity of the norm.

Intervals Are Convex

Every interval in $\mathbb R$ is convex because every point between two points of an interval also lies in the interval.

Disconnected Convex Sets

A nonempty convex subset of a real vector space is path-connected and therefore connected.

$$ \boxed{ \text{convex} \Rightarrow \text{path-connected} }. $$

Thus a nonempty convex set cannot consist of separated components.

Star-Convex Sets

A set is star-convex if there is at least one point from which every other point can be connected by a segment remaining in the set.

Every convex set is star-convex, but a star-convex set need not be convex.

Convexity versus Simply Connected

Convex sets in Euclidean space are simply connected when the usual topological conditions apply, but simply connected sets need not be convex.

Convexity versus Connectedness

Convexity is much stronger than connectedness. A curved or winding region may be connected while failing the line-segment test.

Convexity versus Concavity for Functions

A function $f$ is concave when:

$$ \boxed{ f((1-t)x+ty) \ge (1-t)f(x)+tf(y) }. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ f\text{ convex} \iff -f\text{ concave} }. $$

Convex Up versus Convex Function

Some older or region-dependent terminology uses phrases such as convex upward or convex downward. These expressions can be ambiguous because different traditions reverse their descriptive orientation.

The inequality definition is therefore more reliable than relying on visual wording alone.

Second Derivative and Concavity Language

Under the common calculus convention:

$$ f''(x)>0 $$

means the graph is concave up and the function is locally convex.

Convex Boundary versus Convex Set

Saying that a curve or surface looks outwardly curved is not itself the formal definition of a convex set. Convexity concerns the entire set and its line segments.

Convex Lens or Surface Usage

In elementary geometry and physical contexts, convex may describe a surface bulging outward, as in a convex lens or convex side of a curve.

This intuitive usage is related to, but not identical with, the formal definitions used in convex geometry and analysis.

Convex Angle

In some geometric terminology, an angle with measure less than $180^\circ$ may be called a convex angle.

$$ \boxed{ 0^\circ<\theta<180^\circ } $$

depending on the convention and whether zero or straight angles are included in the surrounding classification.

Convex Combination Is Not a General Linear Combination

A linear combination permits arbitrary scalar coefficients.

$$ \alpha_1x_1+\cdots+\alpha_nx_n. $$

A convex combination imposes:

$$ \alpha_i\ge0, \qquad \sum\alpha_i=1. $$

Affine Combination versus Convex Combination

An affine combination requires only:

$$ \sum\alpha_i=1, $$

while a convex combination additionally requires all coefficients to be nonnegative.

Conic Combination versus Convex Combination

A conic combination requires:

$$ \alpha_i\ge0 $$

but does not require the coefficients to sum to $1$.

Convex Optimization

A standard convex optimization problem has:

  • a convex objective function for minimization, and
  • a convex feasible set.

Such problems have unusually strong global structure compared with general nonconvex optimization.

Local versus Global Minima

For a convex function on a convex domain, any local minimizer is a global minimizer.

This property is one of the major reasons convexity is so important computationally.

Stationary Point of a Differentiable Convex Function

If $f$ is differentiable and convex and:

$$ \nabla f(x^\ast)=0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ x^\ast \text{ is a global minimizer} }. $$

Strict Convexity and Equality Cases

Strict convexity eliminates nontrivial line segments along which the function acts affinely.

Strong Convexity

A differentiable function is strongly convex with parameter $\mu>0$ if, under a standard Euclidean formulation:

$$ \boxed{ f(y) \ge f(x) + \nabla f(x)\cdot(y-x) + \frac{\mu}{2}\|y-x\|^2 }. $$

Strong convexity is stronger than strict convexity and provides quantitative curvature.

Strongly Convex Does Not Mean Geometrically Convex in the Elementary Shape Sense

Here strongly convex is a technical analytic property of a function, not a statement that a geometric figure looks especially rounded.

Common Misconception: Convex Means Circular

Convexity does not require roundness. Triangles, rectangles, and half-spaces are convex.

Common Misconception: Every Connected Set Is Convex

Connectedness does not guarantee that every line segment remains in the set.

Common Misconception: A Convex Function Must Be Increasing

Convexity describes the behavior of slopes, not their sign.

For example:

$$ f(x)=x^2 $$

decreases on $(-\infty,0)$ and increases on $(0,\infty)$ while remaining convex everywhere.

Common Misconception: Convex Means f'' > 0 Everywhere

The condition $f''>0$ is a convenient sufficient condition for strict convexity, but convexity itself permits $f''=0$ at some or all points when the second derivative exists.

An affine function is convex despite having:

$$ f''(x)=0. $$

Common Misconception: Strict Convexity Guarantees a Minimizer Exists

Strict convexity guarantees at most one minimizer, not existence. Existence requires additional conditions.

Common Misconception: Convex Hull Is the Boundary around a Set

The convex hull includes the entire smallest convex region containing the set, not merely its outer boundary.

Common Misconception: Union of Convex Sets Is Convex

Convexity is preserved under intersections but not under arbitrary unions.

Common Misconception: Convex and Concave Are Always Mutually Exclusive

For functions, affine functions are both convex and concave.

Common Misconception: Visual Curvature Alone Determines Convexity

Formal convexity should be checked by line segments, convex combinations, derivative criteria, Hessians, or equivalent field-specific definitions rather than visual intuition alone.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$(1-t)x+ty\in C$ for $0\le t\le1$ "C is convex."
$\operatorname{conv}(S)$ "The convex hull of S."
$\sum_i\lambda_i x_i$, $\lambda_i\ge0$, $\sum_i\lambda_i=1$ "A convex combination of the points x sub i."
$f((1-t)x+ty)\le(1-t)f(x)+tf(y)$ "f is convex."
$f''(x)\ge0$ "The second derivative is nonnegative, supporting convexity."
$\nabla^2f(x)\succeq0$ "The Hessian is positive semidefinite."
$f(E[X])\le E[f(X)]$ "Jensen's inequality for a convex function."

The adjective convex should normally be attached to the mathematical object it modifies: convex set, convex polygon, convex function, convex cone, convex hull, or convex optimization problem.

The definitions are related but not interchangeable. For a set, convexity is a line-segment property. For a function, convexity is an inequality comparing the function at an interpolated input with the corresponding interpolation of function values.

Defense Notation Advice: Always identify what is convex. For sets, use the line-segment or convex-combination definition. For functions, state the inequality on a convex domain. Do not infer convexity merely from a graph that visually bends upward. When derivatives are available, distinguish $f''\ge0$ from the stronger $f''>0$. In multivariable work, use positive semidefiniteness of the Hessian under the appropriate smoothness hypotheses. Distinguish convex combinations from arbitrary linear, affine, and conic combinations.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Geometric Meaning: A set is convex when every segment joining two of its points remains inside the set.
  • Set Criterion: $$ \boxed{ x,y\in C,\; 0\le t\le1 \Rightarrow (1-t)x+ty\in C }. $$
  • Polygon Usage: A convex polygon has no inward indentation; under ordinary nondegenerate conventions every interior angle is less than $180^\circ$.
  • Convex Combination: A weighted combination with nonnegative coefficients summing to $1$.
  • Convex Hull: The smallest convex set containing a given set.
  • Intersection: Arbitrary intersections of convex sets are convex.
  • Union Warning: Arbitrary unions of convex sets need not be convex.
  • Affine Mapping: Affine images of convex sets remain convex.
  • Function Criterion: $$ \boxed{ f((1-t)x+ty) \le (1-t)f(x)+tf(y) }. $$
  • One-Variable Differential Criterion: $f''\ge0$ on an interval is a standard sufficient criterion for convexity when the second derivative exists.
  • Gradient Criterion: A differentiable convex function lies above all of its tangent hyperplanes.
  • Hessian Criterion: A positive-semidefinite Hessian gives convexity under standard $C^2$ hypotheses.
  • Strict Convexity: Replaces the defining inequality by strict inequality for distinct points and interior interpolation parameters.
  • Strong Convexity: Adds a quantitative quadratic lower bound.
  • Optimization: Local minima of convex functions on convex domains are global minima.
  • Uniqueness: Strict convexity gives at most one minimizer.
  • Jensen's Inequality: Convex functions satisfy $f(\sum\lambda_i x_i)\le\sum\lambda_i f(x_i)$.
  • Probability: Jensen's inequality gives $f(E[X])\le E[f(X)]$ under suitable hypotheses.
  • Linear Programming: Feasible regions formed by linear constraints are convex.
  • Normed Spaces: Norm balls are convex.
  • Topology: Nonempty convex subsets of real vector spaces are path-connected.
  • Concavity Contrast: $f$ is convex exactly when $-f$ is concave.
  • Affine-Function Case: Affine functions are both convex and concave.
  • Star-Convex Contrast: Every convex set is star-convex, but not every star-convex set is convex.
  • Connectedness Contrast: Convexity implies connectedness, but connectedness does not imply convexity.
  • Linear-Combination Contrast: Linear combinations permit arbitrary coefficients.
  • Affine-Combination Contrast: Affine combinations require coefficients summing to $1$ but permit negative coefficients.
  • Conic-Combination Contrast: Conic combinations require nonnegative coefficients but not a sum of $1$.
  • Common Error: Do not assume convex means circular or smoothly rounded.
  • Common Error: Do not assume every connected region is convex.
  • Common Error: Do not assume every union of convex sets is convex.
  • Common Error: Do not assume a convex function must be increasing.
  • Common Error: Do not require $f''>0$ everywhere merely to establish ordinary convexity.
  • Common Error: Do not assume strict convexity guarantees existence of a minimizer.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse the convex hull with only the boundary of the enclosing region.
  • Terminological Contrast: A convex set contains every line segment between its points. A convex polygon has no inward indentation and satisfies the corresponding segment condition. A convex combination is a weighted average with nonnegative coefficients summing to $1$. The convex hull is the smallest convex set containing a given set. A convex function lies below its secant chords in the defining inequality sense. A strictly convex function satisfies a strict version of that inequality. A strongly convex function satisfies an additional quantitative curvature bound. A concave function reverses the convexity inequality. A nonconvex object fails the relevant convexity criterion.
