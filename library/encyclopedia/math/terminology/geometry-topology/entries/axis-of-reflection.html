Axis of Reflection

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

The axis of reflection is the line across which a geometric figure, point, or set is reflected. It acts as a mirror line: points on the axis remain fixed, while points away from the axis are mapped to corresponding points on the opposite side.

If a reflection maps a point $P$ to $P'$, and the reflection axis is the line $\ell$, then $\ell$ is the perpendicular bisector of the segment $\overline{PP'}$.

$$ \boxed{ \ell \perp \overline{PP'} \quad\text{and}\quad d(P,\ell)=d(P',\ell) }. $$

The terms axis of reflection, line of reflection, and mirror line are closely related. In elementary transformation geometry, line of reflection is especially common.

$$ \boxed{ \text{axis of reflection} \approx \text{line of reflection} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Reflection as a Transformation

A reflection is a rigid transformation that sends every point to a mirror image across a fixed line.

If the reflection across $\ell$ is denoted $R_\ell$, then:

$$ \boxed{ R_\ell(P)=P' }. $$

Points on the Axis Stay Fixed

Every point on the reflection axis is unchanged.

$$ \boxed{ P\in\ell \Rightarrow R_\ell(P)=P }. $$

Such points are called fixed points of the reflection.

The Axis Is a Perpendicular Bisector

If $P$ is not on $\ell$, and $P'$ is its reflected image, then the axis:

  • passes through the midpoint of $\overline{PP'}$, and
  • is perpendicular to $\overline{PP'}$.
$$ \boxed{ \ell = \text{perpendicular bisector of } \overline{PP'} }. $$

Equal Distance from the Axis

A point and its image are the same perpendicular distance from the axis:

$$ \boxed{ d(P,\ell) = d(P',\ell) }. $$

Opposite Sides of the Axis

Unless $P$ lies on the axis, $P$ and $P'$ lie on opposite sides of $\ell$.

Their joining segment crosses the axis at a right angle.

Reflection across the x-Axis

Reflection across the $x$-axis follows:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y) \mapsto (x,-y) }. $$

The axis of reflection is:

$$ \boxed{ y=0 }. $$

Reflection across the y-Axis

$$ \boxed{ (x,y) \mapsto (-x,y) }. $$

The axis is:

$$ \boxed{ x=0 }. $$

Reflection across y = x

Reflecting across:

$$ y=x $$

interchanges the coordinates:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y) \mapsto (y,x) }. $$

Reflection across y = -x

$$ \boxed{ (x,y) \mapsto (-y,-x) }. $$

Reflection across a Vertical Line

Reflection across:

$$ x=h $$

sends:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y) \mapsto (2h-x,\ y) }. $$

The horizontal distance from $x=h$ is reversed while the $y$-coordinate remains unchanged.

Reflection across a Horizontal Line

Reflection across:

$$ y=k $$

sends:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y) \mapsto (x,\ 2k-y) }. $$

Finding the Axis from a Point and Its Image

Suppose:

$$ P=(x_1,y_1), \qquad P'=(x_2,y_2). $$

The midpoint is:

$$ \boxed{ M= \left( \frac{x_1+x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1+y_2}{2} \right) }. $$

The reflection axis is the line through $M$ perpendicular to $\overline{PP'}$.

Example: Finding a Reflection Axis

Let:

$$ P=(2,1), \qquad P'=(8,1). $$

Their midpoint is:

$$ M=(5,1). $$

Since $\overline{PP'}$ is horizontal, its perpendicular bisector is vertical:

$$ \boxed{ x=5 }. $$

Reflection Is Distance-Preserving

A reflection preserves distances:

$$ \boxed{ d(P,Q) = d(R_\ell(P),R_\ell(Q)) }. $$

Therefore reflections are isometries.

Reflection Preserves Angle Measure

Reflections preserve angle size:

$$ \boxed{ m\angle ABC = m\angle A'B'C' }. $$

Reflection Preserves Length

$$ \boxed{ AB=A'B' }. $$

Reflection Preserves Area

Because reflections are rigid motions, planar figures retain area:

$$ \boxed{ A(F)=A(R_\ell(F)) }. $$

Orientation Is Reversed

Although size and shape are preserved, orientation is reversed.

A triangle whose vertices run counterclockwise before reflection will run clockwise afterward.

$$ \boxed{ \text{reflection reverses orientation} }. $$

Reflection Is Its Own Inverse

Reflecting twice across the same axis returns every point to its original position:

$$ \boxed{ R_\ell\circ R_\ell = I }. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ R_\ell^{-1}=R_\ell }. $$

Axis of Reflection and Symmetry

If reflecting an entire figure across a line leaves the figure unchanged, then that line is an axis of symmetry.

$$ \boxed{ R_\ell(F)=F }. $$

Thus an axis of symmetry is a special case of a reflection axis for which the reflected image coincides with the original figure.

Axis of Reflection versus Axis of Symmetry

A line can be used as the axis for reflecting any figure, even when the image does not coincide with the original.

It is an axis of symmetry only when:

$$ \boxed{ R_\ell(F)=F }. $$

Square

A square has four reflection symmetries:

  • two diagonals,
  • the vertical midline,
  • the horizontal midline.

Each is an axis of reflection that maps the square onto itself.

Rectangle

A nonsquare rectangle has two axes of reflection:

  • the line through the midpoints of the left and right sides,
  • the line through the midpoints of the top and bottom sides.

Equilateral Triangle

An equilateral triangle has three axes of reflection. Each passes through one vertex and the midpoint of the opposite side.

Isosceles Triangle

A non-equilateral isosceles triangle has one axis of reflection. It passes through the apex and the midpoint of the base.

Scalene Triangle

A scalene triangle has no axis of reflectional symmetry.

Circle

Every line through the center of a circle is an axis of reflection.

Therefore a circle has infinitely many reflection axes.

Parabola

A parabola has one axis of reflectional symmetry.

For:

$$ y=a(x-h)^2+k, $$

the axis is:

$$ \boxed{ x=h }. $$

Absolute-Value Graph

For:

$$ y=|x|, $$

the $y$-axis is an axis of reflectional symmetry:

$$ \boxed{ x=0 }. $$

Even Functions

A function is even when:

$$ \boxed{ f(-x)=f(x) }. $$

Its graph is symmetric about the $y$-axis.

Reflection of a Function across the x-Axis

If:

$$ y=f(x), $$

then reflection across the $x$-axis gives:

$$ \boxed{ y=-f(x) }. $$

Reflection of a Function across the y-Axis

Reflection across the $y$-axis gives:

$$ \boxed{ y=f(-x) }. $$

Reflection across y = x and Inverse Functions

The graphs of a one-to-one function and its inverse are reflections across:

$$ \boxed{ y=x }. $$

Thus:

$$ y=f(x) $$

and:

$$ y=f^{-1}(x) $$

are mirror images across $y=x$ when the inverse is a function.

Matrix for Reflection across the x-Axis

$$ \boxed{ \begin{pmatrix} 1&0\\ 0&-1 \end{pmatrix} }. $$

Matrix for Reflection across the y-Axis

$$ \boxed{ \begin{pmatrix} -1&0\\ 0&1 \end{pmatrix} }. $$

Matrix for Reflection across y = x

$$ \boxed{ \begin{pmatrix} 0&1\\ 1&0 \end{pmatrix} }. $$

Reflection across a Line through the Origin

If the reflection axis passes through the origin and makes angle $\theta$ with the positive $x$-axis, one standard reflection matrix is:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{pmatrix} \cos 2\theta & \sin 2\theta\\ \sin 2\theta & -\cos 2\theta \end{pmatrix} }. $$

Determinant of a Reflection Matrix

In two-dimensional Euclidean space, a reflection matrix has determinant:

$$ \boxed{ -1 }. $$

This algebraically reflects the reversal of orientation.

Eigenvectors and the Reflection Axis

For a linear reflection through a line passing through the origin, every vector along the reflection axis is unchanged:

$$ \boxed{ R\mathbf v=\mathbf v }. $$

Such vectors have eigenvalue $1$.

Perpendicular Direction

Vectors perpendicular to the reflection axis reverse direction:

$$ \boxed{ R\mathbf n=-\mathbf n }. $$

They have eigenvalue $-1$.

Reflection Using Parallel and Perpendicular Components

If a vector is decomposed as:

$$ \mathbf v = \mathbf v_{\parallel} + \mathbf v_{\perp}, $$

then reflection across the axis gives:

$$ \boxed{ R(\mathbf v) = \mathbf v_{\parallel} - \mathbf v_{\perp} }. $$

The component parallel to the mirror line stays unchanged, while the perpendicular component reverses.

Composition of Two Reflections across Intersecting Lines

Reflecting across one line and then another intersecting line produces a rotation.

If the angle from the first axis to the second is $\theta$, the resulting rotation has angle:

$$ \boxed{ 2\theta }. $$

Composition of Two Reflections across Parallel Lines

Reflecting successively across two distinct parallel lines produces a translation perpendicular to them.

If the lines are distance $d$ apart, the translation distance is:

$$ \boxed{ 2d }. $$

Reflection in Three Dimensions

In three-dimensional geometry, reflection is usually performed across a plane rather than a line.

Thus the phrase axis of reflection is most natural in two-dimensional geometry, whereas plane of reflection is more precise in three dimensions.

Axis versus Plane of Reflection

$$ \boxed{ \text{2D reflection: line} \qquad \text{3D reflection: plane} }. $$

Axis of Reflection versus Axis of Rotation

An axis of reflection is a mirror line across which points are flipped.

An axis of rotation is a line about which points rotate.

$$ \boxed{ \text{reflection} \ne \text{rotation} }. $$

Axis of Reflection versus Perpendicular Bisector

A reflection axis is the perpendicular bisector of every segment joining a point to its own reflected image.

A general perpendicular bisector, however, need not be serving as a reflection axis unless a reflection transformation is being considered.

Axis of Reflection versus Line of Symmetry

A line of symmetry is an axis of reflection that maps a figure onto itself. A reflection axis can be used to map a figure to a different location.

$$ \boxed{ \text{line of symmetry} \subset \text{possible reflection axes} }. $$

Recovering the Mirror Line from Multiple Point Pairs

If several corresponding pairs:

$$ P_1\leftrightarrow P_1', \qquad P_2\leftrightarrow P_2', \qquad \ldots $$

all result from one reflection, then the perpendicular bisectors of:

$$ \overline{P_1P_1'}, \quad \overline{P_2P_2'}, \quad \ldots $$

must coincide in the same line.

Common Misconception

The reflection axis is not a line halfway between two figures merely by visual appearance. It must satisfy the exact perpendicular-bisector condition for every corresponding point pair.

$$ \boxed{ \text{reflection axis} = \text{common perpendicular bisector of corresponding point-image segments} }. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$R_\ell(P)=P'$ "Reflection across line ell maps P to P prime."
$(x,y)\mapsto(x,-y)$ "Reflect the point across the x-axis."
$(x,y)\mapsto(-x,y)$ "Reflect the point across the y-axis."
$(x,y)\mapsto(y,x)$ "Reflect the point across the line y equals x."
$R_\ell^2=I$ "Reflecting twice across the same line gives the identity transformation."
$R_\ell(F)=F$ "The figure is symmetric about line ell."

Axis of reflection, line of reflection, and mirror line may all appear in geometric discussion. In formal transformation notation, naming the line explicitly as $\ell$ often removes ambiguity.

Defense Notation Advice: To verify a proposed reflection axis, check both defining conditions: each point-image segment must be perpendicular to the axis, and its midpoint must lie on the axis. Equal visual spacing alone is not sufficient.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: The axis of reflection is the fixed mirror line across which a planar reflection occurs.
  • Fixed Points: $$ \boxed{ P\in\ell \Rightarrow R_\ell(P)=P }. $$
  • Perpendicular-Bisector Property: The axis is the perpendicular bisector of every segment joining a point to its reflected image.
  • Equal Distance: $$ \boxed{ d(P,\ell)=d(P',\ell) }. $$
  • x-Axis Reflection: $$ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(x,-y) }. $$
  • y-Axis Reflection: $$ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(-x,y) }. $$
  • y = x Reflection: $$ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(y,x) }. $$
  • y = -x Reflection: $$ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(-y,-x) }. $$
  • Vertical Mirror Line: Reflection across $x=h$ gives: $$ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(2h-x,y) }. $$
  • Horizontal Mirror Line: Reflection across $y=k$ gives: $$ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(x,2k-y) }. $$
  • Rigid Motion: Reflection preserves distances and angle measures.
  • Area: Reflection preserves area.
  • Orientation: Reflection reverses orientation.
  • Inverse: $$ \boxed{ R_\ell^{-1}=R_\ell }. $$
  • Double Reflection: $$ \boxed{ R_\ell\circ R_\ell=I }. $$
  • Axis of Symmetry: A reflection axis is an axis of symmetry when it maps the entire figure onto itself.
  • Square: A square has four reflection-symmetry axes.
  • Rectangle: A nonsquare rectangle has two.
  • Equilateral Triangle: It has three.
  • Isosceles Triangle: A non-equilateral isosceles triangle has one.
  • Scalene Triangle: It has none.
  • Circle: Every line through its center is an axis of reflectional symmetry.
  • Parabola: For $y=a(x-h)^2+k$, the axis is: $$ \boxed{ x=h }. $$
  • Even Function: $f(-x)=f(x)$ gives y-axis symmetry.
  • Inverse-Function Connection: A function and its inverse are reflected across $y=x$ when the inverse is a function.
  • Linear Algebra: A planar reflection through a line through the origin has eigenvalue $1$ along the axis and $-1$ in the perpendicular direction.
  • Determinant: A two-dimensional reflection matrix has determinant $-1$.
  • Intersecting Reflection Axes: Two successive reflections across intersecting lines produce a rotation by twice the angle between them.
  • Parallel Reflection Axes: Two successive reflections across parallel lines produce a translation.
  • Three-Dimensional Contrast: In 3D, reflection ordinarily occurs across a plane rather than a line.
  • Rotation-Axis Contrast: A reflection axis is a mirror line; a rotation axis is a line about which turning occurs.
  • Symmetry-Axis Contrast: Every line of reflectional symmetry is a reflection axis, but a chosen reflection axis need not map a figure onto itself.
  • Common Error: Do not identify a reflection axis merely by visual midpoint placement; verify perpendicularity and equal distance.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse reflection across the x-axis with reflection across the y-axis; the sign change occurs in different coordinates.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse reflection with rotation; reflection reverses orientation, whereas ordinary planar rotation preserves it.
  • Terminological Contrast: Axis of reflection is the fixed mirror line of a reflection. Line of reflection is a common synonym. Axis of symmetry is a reflection axis that maps a figure onto itself. Perpendicular bisector describes the geometric relation between the axis and each point-image segment. Plane of reflection is the corresponding three-dimensional concept.
