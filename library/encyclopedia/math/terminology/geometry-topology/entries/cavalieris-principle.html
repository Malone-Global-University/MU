Cavalieri’s Principle

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

Cavalieri’s Principle is named after the Italian mathematician Bonaventura Cavalieri (1598–1647), who developed a systematic geometric method for comparing areas and volumes through families of parallel cross-sections.

Cavalieri called his approach a method of indivisibles. In his seventeenth-century geometry, plane figures were compared through parallel line sections and solids through parallel plane sections. The method anticipated an important idea of integral calculus: a geometric quantity can be understood by accumulating lower-dimensional slices.

Cavalieri presented his theory of indivisibles in Geometria indivisibilibus continuorum nova quadam ratione promota, published in 1635. His work provided a systematic framework for calculating and comparing areas and volumes without relying solely on the classical Greek method of exhaustion.

The underlying idea was not historically unique to Cavalieri. Related slicing and comparison arguments appeared much earlier in the work of Archimedes, and comparable reasoning was developed independently in ancient Chinese mathematics. Johannes Kepler also used infinitesimal-style geometric arguments before Cavalieri. The modern name nevertheless honors Cavalieri because of his extensive and influential development of the method.

Operational Nuance

Three-Dimensional Form

Consider two solids having the same height $h$. Suppose that for every height $y$ between their bases and tops, a plane parallel to the bases cuts the two solids in cross-sections having equal areas.

If:

$$ A_1(y)=A_2(y) $$

for every:

$$ 0\le y\le h, $$

then Cavalieri’s Principle states:

$$ \boxed{ V_1=V_2 }. $$

In words:

$$ \boxed{ \text{equal corresponding cross-sectional areas} \Longrightarrow \text{equal volumes} }. $$

The solids do not need to have the same shape. Their sides may be vertical, slanted, curved, or otherwise different. What matters is that corresponding parallel slices have equal area at every height.

Integral Interpretation

Modern calculus expresses the volume of a solid whose cross-sectional area is $A(y)$ as:

$$ V=\int_0^h A(y)\,dy. $$

Therefore, if:

$$ A_1(y)=A_2(y) $$

throughout the interval, then:

$$ \int_0^h A_1(y)\,dy = \int_0^h A_2(y)\,dy, $$

and hence:

$$ V_1=V_2. $$

Two-Dimensional Analogue

There is also a planar version. Suppose two plane regions lie between the same pair of parallel lines. If every line parallel to those boundaries intersects the two regions in segments of equal length, then the two regions have equal area.

If:

$$ \ell_1(y)=\ell_2(y) $$

at every corresponding height, then:

$$ \boxed{ A_1=A_2 }. $$

Example: Right and Oblique Prisms

Consider a right prism and an oblique prism having the same base area $B$ and the same perpendicular height $h$.

Every cross-section parallel to the base has area:

$$ B. $$

Therefore corresponding cross-sections have equal area at every height. By Cavalieri’s Principle:

$$ V_{\text{right}} = V_{\text{oblique}}. $$

Since the right prism has volume:

$$ Bh, $$

the oblique prism must also have:

$$ \boxed{ V=Bh }. $$

This explains geometrically why slanting a prism without changing its perpendicular height or base area does not change its volume.

Common Misconception: Cavalieri’s Principle does not say that two solids with equal base areas and equal heights must always have equal volumes. Equal base area and height alone are insufficient for arbitrary solids. The required condition is equality of corresponding cross-sectional areas at every height.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$A_1(y)=A_2(y)$ "The corresponding cross-sectional areas are equal at height y."
$V_1=V_2$ "The two solids have equal volume."
$V=\int_0^h A(y)\,dy$ "The volume equals the integral from zero to h of the cross-sectional area with respect to y."
$\ell_1(y)=\ell_2(y)$ "The corresponding planar cross-sections have equal length."
$0\le y\le h$ "For every height y from zero through h."
$A_1=A_2$ "The two plane regions have equal area."

The phrase corresponding cross-sections means sections taken at the same relative height using parallel cutting planes.

Thus one should not compare an arbitrary slice through one solid with an unrelated slice through another. The slices must correspond through a shared direction and height measurement.

Defense Notation Advice: Height refers to perpendicular distance between the chosen parallel base planes. For an oblique solid, a slanted edge is not generally the height.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Equal-Volume Form: If two solids have equal heights and: $$ A_1(y)=A_2(y) $$ for every corresponding height $y$, then: $$ \boxed{V_1=V_2}. $$
  • Equal-Area Form: If two plane regions have equal widths in every corresponding parallel slice, then they have equal area.
  • Proportional Version: A broader form compares proportional sections. If: $$ A_1(y)=kA_2(y) $$ for every corresponding height, then: $$ \boxed{ V_1=kV_2 }. $$ Equality is the special case $k=1$.
  • Prisms: Any two prisms having equal base areas and equal perpendicular heights have equal volume because every section parallel to the base has the same area as the base.
  • Oblique Prisms: Cavalieri’s Principle explains why: $$ V=Bh $$ remains valid when a right prism is sheared into an oblique prism without changing base area or perpendicular height.
  • Cylinders: The same reasoning applies to cylinders. A right cylinder and an oblique cylinder with equal base areas and equal perpendicular heights have equal volumes: $$ V=Bh. $$
  • Shearing: A shear transformation can move horizontal slices sideways while preserving their areas. Cavalieri’s Principle shows why such a deformation can preserve volume.
  • Sphere Volume: One famous application compares cross-sections of a sphere with cross-sections of other solids whose volumes are already known. Slice-by-slice equality or subtraction can then establish: $$ V_{\text{sphere}} = \frac43\pi r^3. $$
  • Cross-Section Method in Calculus: The modern formula: $$ V=\int_a^b A(x)\,dx $$ is a continuous accumulation of cross-sectional areas. Cavalieri’s Principle can therefore be viewed as a geometric precursor to this method.
  • Area by Slices: Likewise: $$ A=\int_a^b \ell(x)\,dx $$ accumulates one-dimensional slice lengths to obtain two-dimensional area.
  • Dimension-Raising Pattern: Cavalieri-type reasoning follows the pattern: $$ \text{lengths} \longrightarrow \text{areas} $$ and: $$ \text{areas} \longrightarrow \text{volumes}. $$ Lower-dimensional measurements determine higher-dimensional quantities through accumulation.
  • Relation to the Method of Exhaustion: Classical Greek mathematicians, especially Archimedes, established areas and volumes through exhaustion arguments. Cavalieri’s indivisibles provided a different and often more direct comparison method, although early formulations did not possess the modern foundations of integral calculus.
  • Relation to Integral Calculus: In contemporary notation: $$ A_1(y)=A_2(y) \Longrightarrow \int A_1(y)\,dy = \int A_2(y)\,dy. $$ Integral calculus supplies a modern rigorous framework for the accumulation idea behind the principle.
  • Historical Precursors: Cavalieri did not originate every instance of slice-based geometric reasoning. Archimedes used related comparison ideas in antiquity, and comparable principles appeared independently in Chinese mathematics. Cavalieri’s importance lies in developing the method systematically and making it a major tool of seventeenth-century geometry.
  • Not Merely Equal Bases: Two arbitrary solids can have equal base area and equal height while possessing different cross-sectional profiles and therefore different volumes. The slice-by-slice condition is the crucial hypothesis.
  • Not Congruence: Equal volume does not imply that the solids are congruent. Cavalieri’s Principle can establish equal volume for solids having visibly different shapes.
  • Not Similarity: The two solids need not be similar either. The theorem compares measured slices, not corresponding angles or proportional side lengths.
  • Terminological Contrast: A cross-section is the figure obtained by intersecting a solid with a plane. Cavalieri’s Principle compares corresponding cross-sections to infer equality or proportionality of total area or volume. The cross-sectional integration method is the modern calculus formulation of accumulating those sections directly.
