Axis of Symmetry

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

An axis of symmetry is a line across which a figure can be reflected so that the reflected image coincides exactly with the original figure. It is therefore a line of reflectional symmetry.

If $F$ is a geometric figure and $R_\ell$ denotes reflection across a line $\ell$, then $\ell$ is an axis of symmetry of $F$ when:

$$ \boxed{ R_\ell(F)=F }. $$

Informally, the axis divides the figure into mirror-image portions. However, the precise mathematical condition is not merely visual resemblance: every reflected point of the figure must land on another point of the same figure.

$$ \boxed{ \text{axis of symmetry} = \text{line that leaves a figure unchanged under reflection} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Reflectional Symmetry

A figure has reflectional symmetry if there exists at least one line $\ell$ such that reflection across $\ell$ maps the figure onto itself.

$$ \boxed{ R_\ell(F)=F }. $$

Points on the Axis Are Fixed

Any point lying on the symmetry axis remains unchanged under the reflection:

$$ \boxed{ P\in\ell \Rightarrow R_\ell(P)=P }. $$

Paired Points Away from the Axis

If $P$ is not on the axis and $P'$ is its mirror image, then the symmetry axis is the perpendicular bisector of $\overline{PP'}$.

$$ \boxed{ \ell \perp \overline{PP'} } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ d(P,\ell) = d(P',\ell) }. $$

Mirror-Half Interpretation

For many elementary figures, the axis visually divides the object into two halves that correspond by reflection.

This is useful intuition, but the formal definition remains:

$$ \boxed{ \text{reflection across the axis preserves the entire figure} }. $$

Vertical Axis of Symmetry

A vertical symmetry axis has equation:

$$ \boxed{ x=h }. $$

Points equidistant horizontally from $x=h$ correspond under reflection.

Horizontal Axis of Symmetry

A horizontal symmetry axis has equation:

$$ \boxed{ y=k }. $$

Reflection across x = h

Reflection across a vertical line $x=h$ follows:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y) \mapsto (2h-x,y) }. $$

Reflection across y = k

$$ \boxed{ (x,y) \mapsto (x,2k-y) }. $$

Symmetry about the y-Axis

The $y$-axis has equation:

$$ x=0. $$

Reflection across it sends:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(-x,y) }. $$

Even Functions

A function $f$ has y-axis symmetry exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ f(-x)=f(x) } $$

for every $x$ in its domain for which both sides are defined.

Such functions are called even functions.

Example: y = x²

For:

$$ f(x)=x^2, $$

we have:

$$ f(-x)=(-x)^2=x^2=f(x). $$

Therefore the graph has symmetry axis:

$$ \boxed{ x=0 }. $$

Parabola in Vertex Form

For:

$$ y=a(x-h)^2+k, $$

the axis of symmetry is:

$$ \boxed{ x=h }. $$

It passes through the vertex:

$$ \boxed{ (h,k) }. $$

Quadratic in Standard Form

For:

$$ y=ax^2+bx+c, \qquad a\ne0, $$

the vertical axis of symmetry is:

$$ \boxed{ x=-\frac{b}{2a} }. $$

Deriving the Quadratic Axis

Completing the square converts:

$$ ax^2+bx+c $$

into vertex form:

$$ a(x-h)^2+k, $$

where:

$$ \boxed{ h=-\frac{b}{2a} }. $$

This explains the standard axis formula.

Axis Passes through the Vertex

For an ordinary parabola, the axis of symmetry passes through the vertex and focus.

It is perpendicular to the directrix.

Horizontal Parabola

For:

$$ x=a(y-k)^2+h, $$

the symmetry axis is horizontal:

$$ \boxed{ y=k }. $$

Isosceles Triangle

A non-equilateral isosceles triangle has one axis of symmetry.

It passes through:

  • the apex,
  • the midpoint of the base.

In that triangle, the symmetry axis is simultaneously:

  • an altitude,
  • a median,
  • an angle bisector,
  • the perpendicular bisector of the base.

Equilateral Triangle

An equilateral triangle has three axes of symmetry.

Each connects a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side.

Scalene Triangle

A scalene triangle has:

$$ \boxed{ 0 } $$

axes of reflectional symmetry.

Rectangle

A nonsquare rectangle has two axes of symmetry:

  • a horizontal midline,
  • a vertical midline.

Its diagonals are not symmetry axes unless the rectangle is a square.

Square

A square has four axes of symmetry:

  • the two diagonals,
  • the horizontal midline,
  • the vertical midline.

Rhombus

A non-square rhombus has two axes of symmetry: its diagonals.

Kite

An ordinary non-rhombic kite has one axis of symmetry, running through the vertices where the pairs of congruent adjacent sides meet.

Regular Polygons

A regular $n$-gon has:

$$ \boxed{ n } $$

axes of symmetry.

Regular Polygon with Odd n

If $n$ is odd, each symmetry axis passes through:

  • one vertex, and
  • the midpoint of the opposite side.

Regular Polygon with Even n

If $n$ is even, half of the axes pass through pairs of opposite vertices and half pass through midpoints of opposite sides.

Circle

A circle has infinitely many axes of symmetry.

Every line through its center is a symmetry axis.

$$ \boxed{ \text{every diameter lies on an axis of symmetry} }. $$

Ellipse

A noncircular ellipse has two axes of reflectional symmetry:

  • the major axis,
  • the minor axis.

Both pass through the center.

Standard Ellipse

For:

$$ \frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1, $$

the $x$-axis and $y$-axis are axes of symmetry.

Hyperbola

Standard hyperbolas also possess reflectional symmetry.

For:

$$ \frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1, $$

both coordinate axes are axes of symmetry.

Graph Symmetry Test about the y-Axis

For an equation involving $x$ and $y$, replace $x$ by $-x$.

If the equation remains equivalent, the graph is symmetric about the $y$-axis.

$$ \boxed{ x\mapsto -x }. $$

Graph Symmetry Test about the x-Axis

Replace:

$$ y\mapsto -y. $$

If the equation remains unchanged, the graph is symmetric about the $x$-axis.

Symmetry about y = x

A relation is symmetric about:

$$ y=x $$

when interchanging $x$ and $y$ leaves its equation equivalent.

$$ \boxed{ x\leftrightarrow y }. $$

Inverse Relations

A relation and its inverse are mirror images across:

$$ \boxed{ y=x }. $$

If a relation is equal to its own inverse, then its graph may possess symmetry about $y=x$.

Axis of Symmetry versus Axis of Reflection

An axis of reflection is any line across which a reflection is performed.

An axis of symmetry is a reflection axis for which the entire figure maps onto itself.

$$ \boxed{ \text{axis of symmetry} = \text{self-mapping reflection axis} }. $$

Axis of Symmetry versus Axis of Rotation

An axis of symmetry in planar geometry concerns reflection.

Rotational symmetry instead concerns turning a figure about a point or, in three dimensions, about a line.

These are different transformations.

Reflectional versus Rotational Symmetry

A figure may have:

  • reflectional symmetry but limited rotational symmetry,
  • rotational symmetry without reflectional symmetry,
  • both,
  • neither.

Therefore one kind of symmetry should not automatically be inferred from the other.

Three-Dimensional Symmetry

In three dimensions, the corresponding reflectional object is usually a plane of symmetry rather than an axis.

$$ \boxed{ \text{2D: symmetry axis} \qquad \text{3D: symmetry plane} }. $$

Sphere

A sphere has infinitely many planes of symmetry. Every plane through its center divides it into mirror-image halves.

Right Circular Cylinder

A right circular cylinder has multiple planes of reflectional symmetry, including every plane containing its central axis and the plane midway between its bases.

Its central line is an axis of rotational symmetry, while reflectional symmetry is described more precisely using planes.

Symmetry as Invariance

More generally, symmetry means that an object remains unchanged under a specified transformation.

For reflectional symmetry:

$$ \boxed{ R_\ell(F)=F }. $$

This is an example of invariance.

Symmetry and Transformations

The study of symmetry can be framed through transformations that preserve an object. The collection of such transformations can itself have algebraic structure.

This forms an important bridge between geometry and abstract algebra.

Finding an Axis from Corresponding Points

If known pairs:

$$ P\leftrightarrow P' $$

belong to a reflected configuration, the axis must be the perpendicular bisector of $\overline{PP'}$.

If several pairs are available, their perpendicular bisectors must agree on the same axis.

Finding the Axis of a Parabola from Two Equal Outputs

If a parabola has equal outputs at:

$$ x=x_1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad x=x_2, $$

then these inputs lie symmetrically about the axis.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ x_{\text{axis}} = \frac{x_1+x_2}{2} }. $$

Quadratic Roots and the Axis

If:

$$ ax^2+bx+c=0 $$

has real roots $r_1$ and $r_2$, the parabola's symmetry axis lies midway between them:

$$ \boxed{ x= \frac{r_1+r_2}{2} }. $$

Since:

$$ r_1+r_2=-\frac ba, $$

this agrees with:

$$ \boxed{ x=-\frac{b}{2a} }. $$

Common Misconception

A line that merely passes through the center of a figure is not automatically an axis of symmetry.

It must satisfy the reflection condition:

$$ \boxed{ R_\ell(F)=F }. $$

For example, every line through the center of a circle works, but only two central lines work for a nonsquare rectangle.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$R_\ell(F)=F$ "The figure is invariant under reflection across line ell."
$x=h$ "The vertical line x equals h is the axis of symmetry."
$x=-\frac{b}{2a}$ "The axis of symmetry of the quadratic is x equals negative b over two a."
$f(-x)=f(x)$ "The function is even, so its graph is symmetric about the y-axis."
$(x,y)\mapsto(2h-x,y)$ "Reflect the point across the vertical line x equals h."
$x_{\text{axis}}=\frac{x_1+x_2}{2}$ "The symmetry axis lies halfway between the two symmetric x-values."

The phrases axis of symmetry, line of symmetry, and reflectional symmetry axis are often used for the same planar concept. In coordinate problems, the equation of the axis should be stated explicitly when possible.

Defense Notation Advice: Do not identify an axis of symmetry by visual appearance alone. Verify that reflection across the proposed line maps the complete figure or graph onto itself.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: An axis of symmetry is a line across which reflection leaves a figure unchanged.
  • Transformation Form: $$ \boxed{ R_\ell(F)=F }. $$
  • Fixed Points: Points on the axis remain fixed under the reflection.
  • Paired Points: The axis is the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining each point to its reflected counterpart.
  • Vertical Axis: $$ \boxed{ x=h }. $$
  • Horizontal Axis: $$ \boxed{ y=k }. $$
  • y-Axis Symmetry: $$ \boxed{ f(-x)=f(x) } $$ for an even function.
  • Quadratic Formula: For $y=ax^2+bx+c$: $$ \boxed{ x=-\frac{b}{2a} }. $$
  • Vertex Form: For $y=a(x-h)^2+k$, the axis is $x=h$.
  • Horizontal Parabola: For $x=a(y-k)^2+h$, the axis is $y=k$.
  • Root Midpoint: If a parabola has real roots $r_1,r_2$, its axis is: $$ \boxed{ x=\frac{r_1+r_2}{2} }. $$
  • Isosceles Triangle: A non-equilateral isosceles triangle has one axis of symmetry.
  • Equilateral Triangle: It has three axes of symmetry.
  • Scalene Triangle: It has none.
  • Nonsquare Rectangle: It has two axes of symmetry.
  • Square: It has four axes of symmetry.
  • Non-square Rhombus: It has two axes of symmetry, along its diagonals.
  • Kite: A typical non-rhombic kite has one axis of symmetry.
  • Regular Polygon: A regular $n$-gon has exactly $n$ axes of symmetry.
  • Circle: Every line through the center is an axis of symmetry.
  • Ellipse: A noncircular ellipse has two axes of symmetry.
  • Hyperbola: Standard centered hyperbolas possess two reflection axes.
  • Coordinate Test: Replacing $x$ by $-x$ tests y-axis symmetry.
  • x-Axis Test: Replacing $y$ by $-y$ tests x-axis symmetry.
  • y = x Test: Interchanging $x$ and $y$ tests symmetry about $y=x$.
  • Axis-of-Reflection Contrast: Any line may serve as a reflection axis for a transformation; it is an axis of symmetry only when the figure maps onto itself.
  • Rotational-Symmetry Contrast: Reflectional symmetry uses a mirror transformation; rotational symmetry uses turning.
  • Three-Dimensional Contrast: Reflectional symmetry in 3D is commonly described using a plane of symmetry.
  • Invariance: Symmetry is fundamentally the property of remaining unchanged under an allowed transformation.
  • Common Error: A line through the center of a figure is not automatically a symmetry axis.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse the axis of a parabola with the x-axis or y-axis unless the equation specifically places it there.
  • Common Error: A figure may possess rotational symmetry without possessing a reflection axis.
  • Terminological Contrast: Axis of symmetry is a line that leaves a figure invariant under reflection. Line of symmetry is a common synonym. Axis of reflection is the mirror line used in a reflection, whether or not the original figure maps onto itself. Axis of rotation refers to rotational rather than reflectional symmetry. Plane of symmetry is the three-dimensional analogue of a planar symmetry axis.
