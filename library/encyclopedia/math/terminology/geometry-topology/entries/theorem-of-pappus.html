Theorem of Pappus

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

The Theorem of Pappus, usually called Pappus's Hexagon Theorem, is named after Pappus of Alexandria, a Greek mathematician who worked in Alexandria during the fourth century CE. Pappus is often regarded as one of the last major mathematicians of the classical Greek geometric tradition.

Pappus's principal surviving mathematical work is the Collection (Synagoge), an eight-book compilation preserving, explaining, and extending a large body of earlier Greek mathematics. One result contained in this work later became especially important because it expresses a purely incidence-based geometric relationship involving points and lines.

That result is now regarded as one of the foundational theorems of projective geometry. Unlike many familiar Euclidean theorems, it does not depend fundamentally on distances, angle measures, perpendicularity, or circles. Instead, it concerns which points lie on which lines and which constructed points become collinear.

The phrase Pappus's Theorem is potentially ambiguous. Several results are associated with Pappus, including the Pappus Centroid Theorems concerning surfaces and volumes of revolution. In projective geometry, however, Theorem of Pappus most commonly refers to the hexagon theorem discussed on this page.

Operational Nuance

The theorem begins with six points arranged on two lines. Let:

$$ A,\ B,\ C $$

lie on one straight line, and let:

$$ A',\ B',\ C' $$

lie on another straight line.

Construct three new points by intersecting opposite cross-connections:

$$ X = \overleftrightarrow{AB'} \cap \overleftrightarrow{A'B}, $$
$$ Y = \overleftrightarrow{AC'} \cap \overleftrightarrow{A'C}, $$
$$ Z = \overleftrightarrow{BC'} \cap \overleftrightarrow{B'C}. $$

Pappus's Theorem states that the three resulting intersection points:

$$ X,\ Y,\ Z $$

are collinear.

$$ \boxed{ X,\ Y,\ Z \text{ lie on a single straight line} } $$

The line containing $X$, $Y$, and $Z$ is commonly called the Pappus line.

The theorem is often described using a hexagon because the six original points may be viewed as the vertices of a special hexagonal configuration:

$$ A,\ B',\ C,\ A',\ B,\ C'. $$

The remarkable feature is that the intersections of three pairs of opposite sides of this configuration lie on one straight line.

The conclusion is not visually obvious. The six original points can be moved along their two supporting lines, producing many differently shaped configurations, yet the three constructed intersection points remain collinear whenever the construction is nondegenerate.

This makes the theorem an example of an incidence theorem. Its essential information concerns only:

  • which points are collinear;
  • which lines pass through which points;
  • where pairs of lines intersect.

Distances and angles do not appear in the statement.

Common Misconception: Pappus's Theorem is not a theorem saying that any six points determine three collinear intersections. The six original points must be arranged as two triples of collinear points, and the three intersection points must be constructed from the specific cross-pairs prescribed by the theorem.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$A,B,C\in\ell$ "A, B, and C lie on line ell."
$A',B',C'\in m$ "A-prime, B-prime, and C-prime lie on line m."
$X=AB'\cap A'B$ "X is the intersection of line A B-prime and line A-prime B."
$Y=AC'\cap A'C$ "Y is the intersection of line A C-prime and line A-prime C."
$Z=BC'\cap B'C$ "Z is the intersection of line B C-prime and line B-prime C."
$X,Y,Z\text{ are collinear}$ "X, Y, and Z lie on the same straight line."

In projective geometry, line notation is often shortened because the unique line through two distinct points $A$ and $B$ may simply be denoted:

$$ AB. $$

Thus:

$$ AB'\cap A'B $$

means "the point of intersection of the line through $A$ and $B'$ with the line through $A'$ and $B$."

The prime marks in:

$$ A',\ B',\ C' $$

do not indicate derivatives. They simply distinguish the three points on the second line from the corresponding labels $A$, $B$, and $C$ on the first.

Other textbooks may instead use lowercase letters:

$$ A,B,C \qquad\text{and}\qquad a,b,c. $$

Under that convention, one commonly writes:

$$ X=Ab\cap aB, $$ $$ Y=Ac\cap aC, $$ $$ Z=Bc\cap bC. $$

The theorem is unchanged; only the labels differ.

Defense Notation Advice: The construction is much easier to verify if the two original collinear triples are clearly distinguished. Prime notation or uppercase/lowercase pairs help prevent accidentally connecting points belonging to the wrong pair. The important structure is the pattern of incidences, not the choice of letters.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Projective Geometry: Pappus's Hexagon Theorem is one of the classical results underlying projective geometry. It depends on incidence and collinearity rather than metric concepts such as distance or angle.
  • Pappus Line: The three constructed points: $$ X,\ Y,\ Z $$ lie on a common line. This line is called the Pappus line of the configuration.
  • Collinearity: The theorem provides a powerful method for proving that three complicated intersection points are collinear. Rather than calculating coordinates directly, one may recognize that the points form a Pappus configuration.
  • Affine Geometry: Many finite-looking versions of Pappus's configuration can be studied in affine geometry. Projective completion makes the result even more natural because parallel lines may be regarded as meeting at points at infinity.
  • Points at Infinity: In Euclidean drawings, one of the required pairs of lines may happen to be parallel. Projective geometry handles this uniformly by saying that parallel lines intersect at a point at infinity. This eliminates several exceptional cases that would otherwise require separate treatment.
  • Projective Invariance: Because Pappus's Theorem concerns only incidence relations, the configuration is preserved under projective transformations. A projective distortion may radically alter lengths and angles while preserving the theorem's collinearities.
  • Coordinate Geometry: The theorem can be proved analytically by assigning coordinates to the two original lines, calculating the three cross-intersections, and verifying that their coordinates satisfy a single linear equation. Such a proof translates an incidence theorem into algebra.
  • Determinant Test: In coordinate geometry, three planar points: $$ (x_1,y_1),\quad (x_2,y_2),\quad (x_3,y_3) $$ are collinear precisely when: $$ \det \begin{pmatrix} x_1 & y_1 & 1 \\ x_2 & y_2 & 1 \\ x_3 & y_3 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = 0. $$ Coordinate proofs of Pappus's Theorem may ultimately reduce its geometric conclusion to an identity of this kind.
  • Incidence Geometry: Pappus's Theorem is important in the axiomatic study of points and lines. A projective plane in which the theorem always holds is called a Pappian projective plane.
  • Algebraic Significance: In the theory of projective planes, validity of Pappus's Theorem is closely connected with the commutativity of the coordinate field. This reveals a deep link between a seemingly visual configuration of points and lines and the algebraic structure used to coordinate the geometry.
  • Relationship to Desargues' Theorem: Pappus's and Desargues' theorems are both fundamental incidence results in projective geometry. Desargues' Theorem relates perspective triangles and collinearity, while Pappus's Theorem begins with two triples of collinear points and produces a new collinear triple.
  • Pappus's Centroid Theorems: The name Pappus's Theorem is also used for two entirely different results concerning solids and surfaces of revolution. In those theorems, the path traveled by a centroid determines surface area or volume. They belong to geometry and calculus rather than projective incidence geometry.
  • First Centroid Theorem: If a plane curve of length $L$ is revolved around an external axis in its plane, under the usual conditions the surface area generated is: $$ S=Ld, $$ where $d$ is the distance traveled by the curve's centroid. If the centroid lies a distance $r$ from the axis: $$ d=2\pi r, $$ so: $$ S=2\pi rL. $$
  • Second Centroid Theorem: If a plane region of area $A$ is revolved around an external axis in its plane, the volume generated is: $$ V=Ad, $$ where $d$ is the distance traveled by the region's centroid. Thus: $$ V=2\pi rA $$ when the centroid is a distance $r$ from the axis.
  • Terminological Contrast: Pappus's Hexagon Theorem concerns six points on two lines and guarantees the collinearity of three constructed intersections. The Pappus Centroid Theorems concern surfaces and volumes generated by rotation. Because all may be shortened to Pappus's Theorem, a precise mathematical reference should specify which result is intended.
