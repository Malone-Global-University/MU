Periodic Motion

Subfield: Geometry & Topology

Etymology & Roots

Periodic motion is motion that repeats after a fixed interval of time. After one complete cycle, the system returns to the same phase or state of its motion.

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x}(t+T) = \mathbf{x}(t) } $$

for some positive period $T$, where $\mathbf{x}(t)$ represents the collection of variables needed to describe the motion.

The adjective periodic comes from the word period, whose Greek roots carry the idea of a circuit, cycle, or going around. The word therefore fits repeating physical and geometric motion naturally: after one period, the motion completes a cycle and begins the same pattern again.

$$ \boxed{ \text{periodic motion} = \text{motion with exact temporal repetition} }. $$

Common examples include idealized circular motion, oscillation of an ideal mass-spring system, small-angle pendulum motion, rotating mechanisms, orbital motion, and repeated wave motion.

The word motion matters because periodicity can apply to more than a scalar function. A moving system may require position, velocity, orientation, or other state variables to repeat before the complete motion has returned.

$$ \boxed{ \text{return to same position} \neq \text{necessarily return to same motion state} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Core Definition

A motion is periodic when there exists a positive time $T$ such that the relevant state repeats:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x}(t+T) = \mathbf{x}(t) }. $$

Period

The positive time $T$ is called a period of the motion.

$$ \boxed{ T = \text{time required for one complete cycle} }. $$

Fundamental Period

If there is a smallest positive period, it is the fundamental period:

$$ \boxed{ T_0 = \text{least positive complete-state repetition time} }. $$

Multiple Periods

If $T$ is a period, then:

$$ \boxed{ 2T,\ 3T,\ 4T,\ldots } $$

are also repetition times.

Cycle

A cycle is one complete instance of the repeated motion.

$$ \boxed{ \text{one cycle} \longleftrightarrow \text{elapsed time }T }. $$

Frequency

Frequency counts cycles per unit time:

$$ \boxed{ f = \frac{1}{T} }. $$

Period from Frequency

$$ \boxed{ T = \frac{1}{f} }. $$

Period-Frequency Product

$$ \boxed{ Tf=1 }. $$

Units of Period

Period is measured in units of time, such as:

  • seconds;
  • minutes;
  • hours;
  • days;
  • years.

Frequency Units

When period is measured in seconds, frequency has units:

$$ \boxed{ \text{s}^{-1} = \text{Hz} }. $$

Hertz

$$ \boxed{ 1\text{ Hz} = 1\text{ cycle per second} }. $$

Angular Frequency

Periodic motion is often described with angular frequency $\omega$:

$$ \boxed{ \omega = 2\pi f }. $$

Angular Frequency and Period

$$ \boxed{ \omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} }. $$

Period from Angular Frequency

$$ \boxed{ T = \frac{2\pi}{|\omega|} }. $$

Why $2\pi$ Appears

One complete angular cycle corresponds to:

$$ \boxed{ 2\pi\text{ radians} }. $$

Phase

Phase identifies a system's location within one repeating cycle.

Full Phase Advance

Advancing by one period changes the phase by one full cycle:

$$ \boxed{ \Delta\theta = 2\pi }. $$

Phase Evolution

Under constant angular frequency:

$$ \boxed{ \theta(t) = \omega t+\phi }. $$

Here $\phi$ is the initial phase.

Full-Cycle Condition

$$ \boxed{ |\omega|T = 2\pi }. $$

Periodic Position Function

A one-dimensional position can be periodic:

$$ \boxed{ x(t+T)=x(t) }. $$

Periodic Vector Position

In several dimensions:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{r}(t+T) = \mathbf{r}(t) }. $$

Complete State

In mechanics, position alone may not completely specify the state. A state vector may include both position and velocity:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x}(t) = \bigl( \mathbf{r}(t), \mathbf{v}(t) \bigr) }. $$

Complete-State Periodicity

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{r}(t+T) = \mathbf{r}(t), \qquad \mathbf{v}(t+T) = \mathbf{v}(t) }. $$

Same Position Can Occur Twice Per Cycle

An oscillator can pass through the same location while moving in opposite directions.

Example

A mass moving back and forth through equilibrium reaches:

$$ x=0 $$

twice per cycle.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \text{same position} \not\Longrightarrow \text{same phase} }. $$

Uniform Circular Motion

A point moving around a circle at constant angular speed is a basic example of periodic motion.

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{r}(t) = \left\langle R\cos(\omega t+\phi), R\sin(\omega t+\phi) \right\rangle }. $$

Circular Period

$$ \boxed{ T = \frac{2\pi}{|\omega|} }. $$

Linear Speed in Uniform Circular Motion

$$ \boxed{ v=R|\omega| }. $$

Period from Circumference

One revolution covers:

$$ 2\pi R. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ T = \frac{2\pi R}{v} }. $$

Circular Speed from Period

$$ \boxed{ v = \frac{2\pi R}{T} }. $$

Centripetal Acceleration from Period

Since:

$$ a_c = \omega^2R, $$

substituting $\omega=2\pi/T$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ a_c = \frac{4\pi^2R}{T^2} }. $$

Rotational Frequency

If $N$ revolutions occur during elapsed time $\Delta t$:

$$ \boxed{ f = \frac{N}{\Delta t} }. $$

Average Period from Revolutions

$$ \boxed{ T = \frac{\Delta t}{N} }. $$

Revolutions per Minute

If a wheel rotates at $r$ revolutions per minute, its period in seconds is:

$$ \boxed{ T = \frac{60}{r} }. $$

Oscillatory Motion

Oscillatory motion repeatedly moves back and forth around an equilibrium or reference state.

Periodic vs. Oscillatory

Many oscillations are periodic, but the terms are not identical.

$$ \boxed{ \text{oscillatory} \not\Longrightarrow \text{exactly periodic} }. $$

Simple Harmonic Motion

A fundamental model of periodic oscillation is:

$$ \boxed{ x(t) = A\cos(\omega t+\phi) }. $$

SHM Period

$$ \boxed{ T = \frac{2\pi}{|\omega|} }. $$

Amplitude

The quantity $|A|$ measures maximum displacement from equilibrium.

Amplitude vs. Period

In ideal simple harmonic motion, changing $A$ does not change the period.

Phase Constant

The parameter $\phi$ determines the starting point within the cycle.

Phase vs. Period

Changing phase shifts the timing of the motion without changing its cycle length.

Velocity in Simple Harmonic Motion

$$ \boxed{ v(t) = -A\omega \sin(\omega t+\phi) }. $$

Acceleration in Simple Harmonic Motion

$$ \boxed{ a(t) = -A\omega^2 \cos(\omega t+\phi) }. $$

SHM Acceleration Law

$$ \boxed{ a(t) = -\omega^2x(t) }. $$

Shared Period

In nondegenerate simple harmonic motion, position, velocity, and acceleration all have the same fundamental period:

$$ \boxed{ T_x=T_v=T_a = \frac{2\pi}{|\omega|} }. $$

Half-Period Symmetry

$$ \boxed{ x \left( t+\frac{T}{2} \right) = -x(t) }. $$

Quarter Period

In ideal SHM, motion from one extreme to equilibrium takes:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{T}{4} }. $$

Extreme to Opposite Extreme

$$ \boxed{ \frac{T}{2} }. $$

Extreme Back to Same Extreme

$$ \boxed{ T }. $$

Mass-Spring Motion

For an ideal mass $m$ attached to a spring with constant $k$:

$$ \boxed{ \omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} }. $$

Mass-Spring Period

$$ \boxed{ T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} }. $$

Mass Effect

$$ \boxed{ T\propto\sqrt{m} }. $$

Spring-Stiffness Effect

$$ \boxed{ T\propto\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}} }. $$

Simple Pendulum

Under the small-angle approximation, a pendulum of length $L$ has:

$$ \boxed{ T \approx 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{L}{g}} }. $$

Small-Angle Caveat

This familiar pendulum formula is approximate. For larger amplitudes, the period depends on amplitude and requires a more advanced expression.

Pendulum Length Dependence

$$ \boxed{ T\propto\sqrt{L} }. $$

Gravity Dependence

$$ \boxed{ T\propto\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}} }. $$

Periodic Motion Need Not Have Constant Speed

A pendulum, for example, moves rapidly near equilibrium and slowly near its turning points while still repeating its motion.

$$ \boxed{ \text{periodic} \not\Longrightarrow \text{uniform speed} }. $$

Uniform Circular Motion Is Special

Uniform circular motion is periodic and has constant speed, but constant speed is an additional condition rather than part of the definition of periodic motion.

Periodic Motion Need Not Be Simple Harmonic

Simple harmonic motion is a particular sinusoidal form of periodic motion.

$$ \boxed{ \text{simple harmonic motion} \Longrightarrow \text{periodic motion} } $$

but:

$$ \boxed{ \text{periodic motion} \not\Longrightarrow \text{simple harmonic motion} }. $$

Periodic Motion Need Not Be Circular

A mass moving along a straight line can be periodic.

Circular Motion Need Not Be Periodic without Repetition

If a trajectory does not continue in a manner that returns to the same complete state after a fixed time, merely having curved or circular geometry over part of the motion does not establish periodicity.

Orbital Motion

In an ideal closed orbit, the orbital period is the time required to complete one orbit.

$$ \boxed{ T = \text{time for one complete orbit} }. $$

Kepler-Type Relation

For an ideal two-body orbit with semimajor axis $a$:

$$ \boxed{ T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM} a^3 } $$

under the standard central-mass approximation.

Wave Motion

A wave can be periodic in time, space, or both.

Traveling-Wave Form

$$ \boxed{ y(x,t) = A\cos(kx-\omega t+\phi) }. $$

Temporal Period

Holding $x$ fixed:

$$ \boxed{ T = \frac{2\pi}{|\omega|} }. $$

Spatial Period

Holding $t$ fixed:

$$ \boxed{ \lambda = \frac{2\pi}{|k|} }. $$

Wave-Speed Relation

$$ \boxed{ v = \lambda f = \frac{\lambda}{T} }. $$

Period vs. Wavelength

$$ \boxed{ T = \text{time per cycle} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \lambda = \text{distance per spatial cycle} }. $$

Trigonometric Motion Model

A motion coordinate of the form:

$$ x(t) = A\sin(Bt+C)+D $$

with $A\neq0$ and $B\neq0$ is periodic with:

$$ \boxed{ T = \frac{2\pi}{|B|} }. $$

Role of $A$

$A$ controls the vertical or positional scale of the oscillation.

Role of $B$

$B$ controls how quickly the phase changes and therefore controls the period.

Role of $C$

$C$ changes the starting phase.

Role of $D$

$D$ shifts the equilibrium or center position.

Cycle Count

If a system has period $T$, then the number of cycles completed during elapsed time $\Delta t$ is:

$$ \boxed{ N = \frac{\Delta t}{T} }. $$

Using Frequency

$$ \boxed{ N = f\Delta t }. $$

Elapsed Time for $N$ Cycles

$$ \boxed{ \Delta t = NT }. $$

Fractional Cycles

The number $N$ need not be an integer if the observed time does not contain a whole number of complete cycles.

Graphical Detection

Periodic motion can often be recognized in a position-versus-time graph by repeated patterns at regular horizontal intervals.

Peak-to-Peak Period

$$ \boxed{ T = t_{\text{next peak}} - t_{\text{peak}} }. $$

Trough-to-Trough Period

Consecutive equivalent troughs give the same full-cycle interval.

Zero Crossings

Zero crossings require care because adjacent crossings can correspond to opposite directions of motion.

Sinusoidal Zero-Crossing Separation

Adjacent zero crossings of a sine wave are separated by:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{T}{2} }. $$

Equivalent Zero Crossings

Zero crossings with the same direction of motion are separated by one full period.

Experimental Period Measurement

Period can be estimated by timing several cycles:

$$ \boxed{ T \approx \frac{ t_{\mathrm{final}} - t_{\mathrm{initial}} }{N} }. $$

Why Measure Multiple Cycles

Timing several cycles can reduce the relative effect of fixed reaction-time or measurement errors.

Exact vs. Approximate Periodicity

Mathematical periodicity requires exact repetition. Real physical systems often exhibit only approximate repetition.

Damped Motion

A common damped oscillation model is:

$$ \boxed{ x(t) = Ae^{-\gamma t} \cos(\omega_dt+\phi) }. $$

Damped Motion Is Not Strictly Periodic

If $\gamma>0$, the amplitude changes:

$$ e^{-\gamma(t+T)} \neq e^{-\gamma t}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ x(t+T) \neq x(t) } $$

in general.

Damped Oscillation Period

The spacing between oscillatory cycles may nevertheless be described by:

$$ \boxed{ T_d = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_d} }. $$

Terminological Nuance

In physics, one may speak informally of the period of a damped oscillation even though the complete decaying signal is not mathematically periodic.

Driven Periodic Motion

A system subjected to periodic forcing can develop repeated steady-state behavior at the driving frequency under appropriate conditions.

Natural Frequency

A system may have an intrinsic frequency:

$$ \boxed{ \omega_0 }. $$

Natural Period

$$ \boxed{ T_0 = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_0} }. $$

Driving Frequency

An external force can have another frequency:

$$ \boxed{ \omega_d }. $$

Driving Period

$$ \boxed{ T_d = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_d} }. $$

Natural and Driving Periods Are Distinct Concepts

They coincide only under particular circumstances.

Resonance

Resonance occurs when periodic forcing interacts strongly with a system's natural response, often when driving frequency is near an appropriate natural frequency.

Quasiperiodic Motion

Motion containing independent frequencies with irrational ratios may never repeat exactly after one finite time.

Example

$$ \boxed{ x(t) = \cos t + \cos(\sqrt{2}t) }. $$

The component frequencies are incommensurable, so no common finite period exists.

Periodic vs. Quasiperiodic

$$ \boxed{ \text{periodic} = \text{exact finite-time repetition} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{quasiperiodic} = \text{multiple recurrent frequencies without one common period} }. $$

Periodic Coordinate vs. Periodic Trajectory

Repetition of one coordinate does not guarantee repetition of the complete motion.

Helical Example

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{r}(t) = \langle \cos t, \sin t, t \rangle }. $$

The $x$- and $y$-coordinates repeat every $2\pi$, but:

$$ z(t+2\pi) = t+2\pi \neq t. $$

Therefore the full position is not periodic.

Same Geometric Path, Different Motion

The same closed path can be traversed with different time parameterizations and therefore different periods.

Example

$$ \mathbf{r}_1(t) = \langle \cos t, \sin t \rangle $$

has period $2\pi$, while:

$$ \mathbf{r}_2(t) = \langle \cos(2t), \sin(2t) \rangle $$

has period:

$$ \boxed{ \pi }. $$

Path vs. Timing

Both parameterizations trace the same unit circle, but the second completes each revolution twice as quickly.

$$ \boxed{ \text{same path} \not\Longrightarrow \text{same period} }. $$

Period Does Not Determine Path

Two completely different trajectories can share the same period.

State-Space Interpretation

In dynamical systems, periodic motion corresponds to a trajectory that closes on itself in state space.

Periodic Orbit

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x}(t+T) = \mathbf{x}(t) } $$

describes a periodic orbit when the evolving state follows a closed trajectory.

Closed Geometric Curve vs. Periodic Orbit

A closed geometric curve describes shape. A periodic orbit includes both the curve in state space and repeated evolution along it.

Time-Translation Symmetry

Periodic motion has a discrete time-translation symmetry:

$$ \boxed{ t \mapsto t+T } $$

leaves the motion state unchanged.

Repeated Time Translation

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x}(t+nT) = \mathbf{x}(t), \qquad n\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Periodicity as Symmetry

This makes periodic motion an important bridge between motion, functions, transformations, and symmetry.

Common Error: Confusing Periodic with Constant-Speed Motion

A motion may repeat while speeding up and slowing down within every cycle.

Common Error: Confusing Periodic with Circular Motion

Circular motion is one example, but linear oscillation can also be periodic.

Common Error: Assuming Every Circular-Looking Motion Is Periodic

The complete state must repeat after a fixed time.

Common Error: Confusing Periodic Motion with Simple Harmonic Motion

Simple harmonic motion is a special sinusoidal type of periodic motion.

Common Error: Assuming Every Oscillation Is Periodic

Oscillatory behavior can change in amplitude, frequency, or phase so that exact repetition never occurs.

Common Error: Assuming the Same Position Means the Same State

Velocity, direction, orientation, and other state variables can differ.

Common Error: Calling Half a Cycle a Full Period

An oscillator may reach the opposite extreme after $T/2$, but the complete state has not yet returned to its starting phase.

Common Error: Treating Adjacent Equilibrium Crossings as a Full Period

In sinusoidal motion, adjacent crossings with opposite directions are half a period apart.

Common Error: Confusing Period with Frequency

Period is time per cycle; frequency is cycles per time.

Common Error: Confusing Frequency with Angular Frequency

$$ \boxed{ \omega = 2\pi f } $$

rather than $\omega=f$.

Common Error: Treating $2\pi$ as a Time Period

$2\pi$ is one full angular phase measured in radians. The corresponding elapsed time is:

$$ \boxed{ T=\frac{2\pi}{|\omega|} }. $$

Common Error: Forgetting Units

A period should carry units of time.

Common Error: Treating rpm as Hertz

Revolutions per minute must be converted to revolutions per second before being interpreted numerically in hertz.

Common Error: Confusing One Revolution with One Radian

$$ \boxed{ 1\text{ revolution} = 2\pi\text{ radians} }. $$

Common Error: Assuming Amplitude Determines Period

In ideal SHM the period is independent of amplitude.

Common Error: Extending the Ideal SHM Result to Every Oscillator

Nonlinear oscillators can have amplitude-dependent periods.

Common Error: Applying the Small-Angle Pendulum Formula Universally

The standard expression:

$$ T \approx 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}} $$

assumes sufficiently small angular displacement.

Common Error: Calling Damped Motion Exactly Periodic

A changing envelope prevents the full motion from repeating exactly.

Common Error: Confusing Oscillation Period with Exact Function Periodicity

A damped signal may have nearly regular peak spacing without satisfying $x(t+T)=x(t)$.

Common Error: Confusing Natural Period with Driving Period

These describe different physical sources of repetition.

Common Error: Assuming Multiple Frequencies Always Produce Periodic Motion

Incommensurable frequencies can produce quasiperiodic behavior.

Common Error: Assuming Repeating Coordinates Guarantee Repeating Full Motion

Every component needed to specify the state must repeat appropriately.

Common Error: Assuming a Closed Path Automatically Determines the Period

The timing along the path must also be specified.

Common Error: Assuming Same Path Means Same Motion

Different parameterizations can trace the same path at different speeds and with different periods.

Common Error: Assuming Same Period Means Same Motion

Entirely different motions may share identical cycle durations.

Common Error: Reporting a Negative Physical Period

The period is conventionally a positive duration even though negative time shifts may preserve the mathematical state relation.

Common Error: Calling Any Repetition Time the Fundamental Period

The fundamental period must be the least positive full-state repetition time.

Common Error: Confusing Period with Wavelength

Period is temporal repetition; wavelength is spatial repetition.

Common Misconception: Periodic motion does not simply mean that an object repeatedly visits the same place. The complete motion must repeat after a fixed time. In an oscillator, for example, the object can occupy the same position while moving in opposite directions, so position repetition alone does not establish completion of a period.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\mathbf{x}(t+T)=\mathbf{x}(t)$ "The state repeats after time T."
$T$ "The period," meaning the duration of one complete cycle.
$f=1/T$ "Frequency is the reciprocal of the period."
$\omega=2\pi f$ "Angular frequency equals two pi times the ordinary frequency."
$T=2\pi/|\omega|$ "The period is two pi divided by the magnitude of the angular frequency."
$x(t)=A\cos(\omega t+\phi)$ "A simple harmonic position function with amplitude A, angular frequency omega, and phase phi."
$N=\Delta t/T$ "The number of cycles equals elapsed time divided by the period."

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"the motion is periodic"} } $$

means that the relevant state repeats after a fixed positive time interval.

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"one full cycle"} } $$

means that the system has returned to the same phase of its repeated motion.

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"period }T\text{"} } $$

means that $T$ is a valid complete repetition time.

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"fundamental period"} } $$

means the least positive complete-state repetition time.

Defense Notation Advice: Use $T$ for a positive time interval and include units. Distinguish ordinary frequency $f$ from angular frequency $\omega$ through $\omega=2\pi f$. When deciding whether a physical motion has completed a cycle, compare equivalent states rather than position alone. If damping, nonlinearity, forcing, or multiple frequencies are present, do not assume exact periodicity without checking the full time dependence.

  • Core Interpretation: Periodic motion is motion whose relevant state repeats after a fixed positive time: $$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x}(t+T)=\mathbf{x}(t) }. $$ Its period $T$ is the duration of one complete cycle and is related to frequency and angular frequency by: $$ \boxed{ f=\frac{1}{T}, \qquad \omega=\frac{2\pi}{T} }. $$ Circular motion, simple harmonic motion, orbital motion, rotating systems, and waves provide major examples, but periodicity itself requires only exact complete-state repetition. The path may be circular, linear, curved, or multidimensional, and the speed need not remain constant within the cycle.
