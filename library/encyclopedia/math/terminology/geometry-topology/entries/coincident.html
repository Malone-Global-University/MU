Coincident

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

In mathematics, two objects are coincident when they occupy the same location or represent the same geometric set in the context being considered.

$$ \boxed{ \text{coincident} \approx \text{same position or exact overlap} }. $$

The word is commonly used for points, lines, rays, segments, curves, planes, circles, and other geometric objects.

Coincidence is stronger than merely touching or intersecting. Two coincident objects overlap in the relevant geometric sense rather than sharing only isolated points.

Operational Nuance

Coincident Points

Two points are coincident if they occupy exactly the same location.

In Cartesian coordinates:

$$ \boxed{ P=(x_1,y_1),\quad Q=(x_2,y_2),\quad P=Q \iff x_1=x_2,\ y_1=y_2 }. $$

Example

$$ P=(3,-2), \qquad Q=(3,-2). $$

Then $P$ and $Q$ are coincident points.

Coincident Lines

Two lines are coincident if they are actually the same line.

For example:

$$ 2x+4y=6 $$

and:

$$ x+2y=3 $$

describe the same line because the first equation is twice the second.

$$ \boxed{ 2x+4y=6 \iff x+2y=3 }. $$

Coincident versus Parallel Lines

Distinct parallel lines never meet.

Coincident lines, however, share every point.

$$ \boxed{ \text{coincident lines} \Rightarrow \text{infinitely many common points} }. $$

Linear-System Interpretation

Consider:

$$ \begin{aligned} x+2y&=5,\\ 2x+4y&=10. \end{aligned} $$

The two equations describe coincident lines.

Therefore the system has infinitely many solutions:

$$ \boxed{ \text{infinitely many solutions} }. $$

Row-Reduction Signal

When one equation is a scalar multiple of another, row reduction may produce a zero row:

$$ \left[ \begin{array}{cc|c} 1&2&5\\ 2&4&10 \end{array} \right] \longrightarrow \left[ \begin{array}{cc|c} 1&2&5\\ 0&0&0 \end{array} \right]. $$

This indicates dependence rather than contradiction.

Coincident Segments

Two line segments are coincident if they have the same endpoints and occupy the same set of points.

$$ \boxed{ \overline{AB}=\overline{CD} } $$

as geometric sets, even if the endpoint labels differ.

Partial Overlap Is Not Full Coincidence

Two segments may overlap along only part of their lengths.

In that case they are overlapping, but not fully coincident as segments.

Coincident Rays

Two rays are coincident if they have the same endpoint and extend in the same direction along the same line.

Opposite Rays Are Not Coincident

Rays sharing an endpoint but extending in opposite directions lie on the same line but represent different sets of points.

Therefore they are not coincident rays.

Coincident Circles

Two circles are coincident if they have the same center and the same radius.

$$ \boxed{ C_1=C_2 \iff O_1=O_2 \text{ and } r_1=r_2 }. $$

Coordinate Form for Coincident Circles

The circles:

$$ (x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=r^2 $$

and:

$$ (x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=r^2 $$

are the same geometric circle and therefore coincident.

Concentric Is Not Coincident

Two circles with the same center but different radii are concentric, not coincident.

$$ \boxed{ O_1=O_2,\quad r_1\ne r_2 \Rightarrow \text{concentric but not coincident} }. $$

Coincident Curves

Two equations may appear different algebraically yet define exactly the same curve.

For example:

$$ y=x^2 $$

and:

$$ 2y=2x^2 $$

define coincident parabolas.

Equivalent Equations

Coincident geometric loci often arise from equivalent equations.

$$ \boxed{ F(x,y)=0 \quad\text{and}\quad cF(x,y)=0 } $$

define the same set when $c\ne0$.

Caution with Algebraic Manipulation

Not every transformation of an equation preserves exactly the same solution set.

Squaring both sides, multiplying by an expression that may be zero, or introducing domain restrictions can produce equations that are not equivalent.

Therefore identical-looking algebraic forms are not the only way to establish coincidence; the actual solution sets must agree.

Coincident Planes

Two planes in three-dimensional space are coincident when they represent the same plane.

For example:

$$ x+2y-z=4 $$

and:

$$ 3x+6y-3z=12 $$

are coincident planes.

Normal-Vector Criterion

For planes:

$$ a_1x+b_1y+c_1z=d_1 $$

and:

$$ a_2x+b_2y+c_2z=d_2, $$

coincidence occurs when all coefficients, including the constant term, are proportional by the same nonzero factor.

$$ \boxed{ (a_2,b_2,c_2,d_2) = \lambda(a_1,b_1,c_1,d_1), \qquad \lambda\ne0 }. $$

Coincident Geometric Transformations

Under a transformation, an image can coincide with its original figure.

For example, rotating a square through $90^\circ$ about its center maps the square onto itself as a set.

The transformed figure is then coincident with the original figure, although individual labeled vertices may have moved.

Coincidence versus Fixed Points

A transformed figure may coincide with itself even when not every point is fixed.

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \text{figure mapped onto itself} \not\Rightarrow \text{every point fixed} }. $$

Symmetry Connection

A symmetry of a geometric object is a transformation that maps the object onto a coincident copy of itself.

$$ \boxed{ T(S)=S }. $$

Coincident Angles

In some geometric contexts, two angles may be described as coincident when their vertices and corresponding rays occupy the same positions.

Merely having equal angle measure does not make two angles coincident.

Congruent versus Coincident

Congruent objects have the same size and shape.

Coincident objects additionally occupy the same geometric position.

$$ \boxed{ \text{coincident} \Rightarrow \text{congruent} } $$

for ordinary geometric figures of the same type, but congruent figures need not be coincident.

Example

Two unit circles centered at different points are congruent but not coincident.

Coincident versus Intersecting

Intersecting objects share one or more points.

Coincident objects share all points of the relevant object.

$$ \boxed{ \text{coincident} \text{ is stronger than merely intersecting} }. $$

Coincident versus Tangent

Tangent curves typically share a point and a common tangent direction there. Coincident curves overlap as the same curve over the region being considered.

Coincident Solutions

In algebra or geometry, multiple symbolic descriptions may yield the same solution point or object. Those solutions may be called coincident when their geometric locations are identical.

Repeated Roots and Coincidence

In graphing contexts, repeated roots can cause intersection points to merge.

For example:

$$ y=(x-2)^2 $$

meets the $x$-axis at:

$$ \boxed{ (2,0) }. $$

The repeated algebraic root corresponds to a single geometric point of contact, rather than two distinct intersection points.

Coincidence in Parametric Curves

Different parameter values may describe the same geometric point.

For a unit circle:

$$ (x,y)=(\cos t,\sin t), $$

the parameter values:

$$ t \quad\text{and}\quad t+2\pi $$

produce coincident points.

Coincidence as Equality of Sets

At a more abstract level, geometric coincidence is often simply equality of the point sets representing the figures.

$$ \boxed{ S_1=S_2 }. $$

The two descriptions may be different, but the underlying geometric set is the same.

Common Misconception: Coincident Means Close Together

Coincident means occupying the same position, not merely being near one another.

Common Misconception: Parallel Lines Are Coincident

Distinct parallel lines have no common points. Coincident lines have every point in common.

Common Misconception: Equal Equations Must Look Identical

Different equations can define the same geometric set.

For example:

$$ x+y=1 $$

and:

$$ 5x+5y=5 $$

define coincident lines.

Common Misconception: Congruent Means Coincident

Congruent figures may occupy different positions. Coincidence requires the same location as well.

Common Misconception: Sharing One Point Means Coincident

Objects that share only one point are merely intersecting or tangent, depending on the context. Coincident objects overlap fully in the relevant sense.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$P=Q$ "The points P and Q are coincident."
$L_1=L_2$ "The two lines are coincident."
$2x+4y=6$ and $x+2y=3$ "The equations represent the same, or coincident, line."
$C_1=C_2$ "The circles coincide."
$T(S)=S$ "The transformation maps the figure onto itself."
$S_1=S_2$ "The two geometric sets coincide."

The adjective coincident and the verb coincide are both common. One may say "the lines are coincident" or "the lines coincide."

Defense Notation Advice: Use coincident only when the relevant geometric sets occupy the same position. Distinguish it from parallel, intersecting, tangent, and congruent, each of which describes a weaker or different relationship.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: Coincident objects occupy the same geometric location or represent the same point set.
  • Points: Coincident points have identical coordinates.
  • Lines: Coincident lines are the same line and have infinitely many common points.
  • Linear Systems: Two equations representing coincident lines produce dependent equations and infinitely many solutions.
  • Segments: Fully coincident segments occupy exactly the same segment.
  • Rays: Coincident rays share endpoint, line, and direction.
  • Circles: Coincident circles have the same center and radius.
  • Planes: Coincident planes represent the same plane in space.
  • Curves: Algebraically different equations may define coincident curves.
  • Transformations: A symmetry maps a figure onto a coincident copy of itself.
  • Set Interpretation: $$ \boxed{ S_1=S_2 } $$ expresses exact geometric coincidence.
  • Parallel Contrast: Distinct parallel lines have no common points; coincident lines share all points.
  • Congruence Contrast: Congruent objects have equal size and shape but need not occupy the same position.
  • Intersection Contrast: Intersecting objects may share only a few points; coincident objects overlap completely in the relevant sense.
  • Tangency Contrast: Tangent objects share a contact point without being the same object.
  • Common Error: Do not use coincident merely to mean nearby.
  • Common Error: Do not call distinct parallel lines coincident.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse equal measure with coincidence of position.
  • Terminological Contrast: Coincident means occupying the same geometric position. Congruent means having the same size and shape. Parallel means lying in the same direction without meeting when the lines are distinct. Intersecting means sharing one or more points. Tangent means touching under a specified local contact condition. Concentric means sharing a center but not necessarily a radius. Equivalent equations may produce coincident geometric loci when they have the same solution set.
