Apothem

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

The mathematical term apothem entered English through the French apothème. Its deeper origin is somewhat uncertain, but the term was probably formed from Greek elements associated with placing or setting something aside. Unlike many classical geometric terms, apothem does not appear to have been an established technical term in ancient Greek geometry; its mathematical use developed considerably later.

In modern geometry, the word has acquired a very specific meaning. For a regular polygon, the apothem is the perpendicular segment from the polygon's center to one of its sides, or the length of that segment. The word is pronounced approximately "AP-uh-them," with the primary stress on the first syllable.

Operational Nuance

For a regular polygon, an apothem is the perpendicular segment drawn from the center of the polygon to one of its sides. Because the polygon is regular, this perpendicular meets the side at its midpoint, and every side has an apothem of the same length.

$$ OM \perp AB $$ $$ AM = MB $$

Here, $O$ is the center of the regular polygon, $AB$ is one of its sides, and $M$ is the midpoint of that side. The segment $\overline{OM}$ is an apothem.

The apothem is also the radius of the circle inscribed in a regular polygon, often called the incircle. Consequently, the apothem may also be described as the polygon's inradius.

$$ a = r_{\text{in}} $$

If a regular polygon has $n$ sides of length $s$, its apothem $a$ can be calculated by dividing the polygon into congruent isosceles triangles and then splitting one of those triangles into two right triangles:

$$ a = \frac{s}{2\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{n}\right)} $$

If the polygon's circumradius $R$—the distance from the center to a vertex—is known instead, the apothem can be found from:

$$ a = R\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{n}\right) $$

The apothem is especially important in the area formula for a regular polygon. If $P$ is the perimeter and $a$ is the apothem, then:

$$ A = \frac{1}{2}Pa $$

This formula works because the polygon can be partitioned into triangles whose combined bases equal the perimeter and whose common height is the apothem.

Common Misconception: The apothem is not the same as the radius from the center of a regular polygon to one of its vertices. The apothem reaches a side at a right angle, while the circumradius reaches a vertex. Except in limiting or degenerate situations, these lengths are different, with the apothem shorter than the circumradius.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$OM \perp AB$ "O-M is perpendicular to side A-B."
$a = \frac{s}{2\tan(\pi/n)}$ "The apothem equals the side length divided by twice the tangent of pi over n."
$a = R\cos(\pi/n)$ "The apothem equals the circumradius times the cosine of pi over n."
$A = \frac{1}{2}Pa$ "The area equals one-half the perimeter times the apothem."
$a = r_{\text{in}}$ "The apothem is equal to the inradius."

The word apothem can refer either to the geometric segment itself or to its numerical length. Thus, both "draw the apothem from the center to the side" and "the apothem is 8 centimeters" are acceptable mathematical statements. Context normally determines whether the segment or its measure is intended.

Defense Notation Advice: When discussing an apothem, specify that the polygon is regular and distinguish the apothem from the circumradius. A precise statement is: "The apothem is the perpendicular distance from the center of the regular polygon to a side." When using $A=\frac{1}{2}Pa$, also identify $P$ as the entire perimeter rather than the length of a single side.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Regular Polygons: The standard use of apothem is the perpendicular distance from the center of a regular polygon to any side. Because all sides and central angles are equal, all of the polygon's apothems have the same length.
  • Inscribed Circles: The apothem of a regular polygon is equal to the radius of its inscribed circle. The incircle is tangent to every side of the polygon, and each apothem extends from the center to a point of tangency.
  • Area of Regular Polygons: The apothem acts as the common height of the congruent triangles formed by connecting the center to the polygon's vertices. This leads directly to: $$ A = \frac{1}{2}Pa. $$ The formula is particularly convenient for regular polygons with many sides, where decomposing the figure into other familiar shapes would be cumbersome.
  • Trigonometry: Apothem calculations naturally involve right-triangle trigonometry. For a regular $n$-gon, half of one central angle is $\frac{\pi}{n}$ radians, producing relationships among the side length, apothem, and circumradius through tangent and cosine.
  • Regular Pyramids: In solid geometry, apothem of a regular pyramid is also used for the altitude of a lateral face—the segment running from the apex to the midpoint of a base edge. This quantity is commonly called the pyramid's slant height. If $h$ is the perpendicular height of the pyramid and $a$ is the apothem of its regular polygonal base, then the lateral apothem $\ell$ satisfies: $$ \ell^2 = h^2 + a^2. $$
  • Terminological Contrast: In a regular polygon, the apothem runs from the center to a side, while the circumradius runs from the center to a vertex. In a regular pyramid, the term may instead refer to the slant height of a lateral face. The surrounding geometric context must therefore be identified before interpreting the term.
