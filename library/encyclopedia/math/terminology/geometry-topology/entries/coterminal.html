Coterminal

Subfield: Geometry & Topology

Etymology & Roots

In trigonometry and angular geometry, coterminal describes angles that share the same initial side and the same terminal side when placed in standard position.

The word combines the prefix co-, meaning "together" or "jointly," with terminal, referring to an endpoint or final position. Coterminal angles therefore literally have a common terminal side.

If two angles differ by one or more complete rotations, they terminate in the same geometric direction and are coterminal.

One complete counterclockwise rotation measures:

$$ \boxed{360^\circ} $$

or, in radians:

$$ \boxed{2\pi}. $$

Thus adding or subtracting any integer number of complete rotations preserves the terminal side of an angle.

Operational Nuance

Standard Position

An angle is in standard position when its vertex is at the origin and its initial side lies along the positive $x$-axis.

The angle is formed by rotating the initial side to a terminal side.

Coterminal angles make different amounts of rotation but finish with the terminal side in exactly the same position.

General Formula in Degrees

If $\theta$ is an angle measured in degrees, every angle coterminal with $\theta$ has the form:

$$ \boxed{ \theta+360^\circ k, \qquad k\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Here $k$ may be positive, negative, or zero.

General Formula in Radians

If $\theta$ is measured in radians, every coterminal angle has the form:

$$ \boxed{ \theta+2\pi k, \qquad k\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Example in Degrees

Consider:

$$ 45^\circ. $$

Adding one complete rotation gives:

$$ 45^\circ+360^\circ=405^\circ. $$

Subtracting one complete rotation gives:

$$ 45^\circ-360^\circ=-315^\circ. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ 45^\circ,\quad405^\circ,\quad-315^\circ } $$

are coterminal.

Example in Radians

Consider:

$$ \frac{\pi}{3}. $$

Adding $2\pi$ gives:

$$ \frac{\pi}{3}+2\pi = \frac{7\pi}{3}. $$

Subtracting $2\pi$ gives:

$$ \frac{\pi}{3}-2\pi = -\frac{5\pi}{3}. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\pi}{3}, \quad \frac{7\pi}{3}, \quad -\frac{5\pi}{3} } $$

are coterminal angles.

Positive and Negative Rotation

By standard convention, counterclockwise rotation represents positive angles, while clockwise rotation represents negative angles.

For example:

$$ 90^\circ $$

and:

$$ -270^\circ $$

are coterminal because:

$$ 90^\circ-360^\circ=-270^\circ. $$

Infinitely Many Coterminal Angles

Every angle has infinitely many coterminal angles because an arbitrary integer number of complete rotations may be added or subtracted.

$$ \boxed{ \{\theta+2\pi k:k\in\mathbb{Z}\} } $$

describes the entire family of coterminal angles in radians.

Finding a Coterminal Angle in a Standard Interval

Angles are often reduced to a representative angle in the interval:

$$ 0^\circ\le\theta<360^\circ $$

or:

$$ 0\le\theta<2\pi. $$

For example:

$$ 750^\circ-720^\circ=30^\circ. $$

Therefore $750^\circ$ is coterminal with:

$$ \boxed{30^\circ}. $$

Negative Example

To reduce:

$$ -520^\circ, $$

add complete rotations:

$$ -520^\circ+360^\circ=-160^\circ, $$
$$ -160^\circ+360^\circ=200^\circ. $$

Thus the coterminal representative in:

$$ 0^\circ\le\theta<360^\circ $$

is:

$$ \boxed{200^\circ}. $$

Coterminal Angles and Trigonometric Values

Because coterminal angles terminate at the same point on the unit circle, they have the same sine and cosine values:

$$ \boxed{ \sin(\theta+2\pi k)=\sin\theta } $$
$$ \boxed{ \cos(\theta+2\pi k)=\cos\theta }. $$

Likewise:

$$ \tan(\theta+\pi k)=\tan\theta $$

wherever tangent is defined. Tangent has period $\pi$, so some angles sharing tangent values are not coterminal under the full-angle definition.

Coterminal vs. Equal Angles

Coterminal angles need not represent the same amount of rotation.

For example:

$$ 30^\circ\neq390^\circ $$

as numerical angle measures, but:

$$ \boxed{ 30^\circ \text{ and } 390^\circ \text{ are coterminal} }. $$

The second angle includes one additional full revolution.

Common Misconception: Coterminal angles are not necessarily equal as real numbers. They are equivalent with respect to terminal direction because they differ by an integer number of complete rotations.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\theta+360^\circ k$ "Theta plus three hundred sixty degrees times k."
$\theta+2\pi k$ "Theta plus two pi k."
$k\in\mathbb{Z}$ "K is an integer."
$45^\circ\sim405^\circ$ "Forty-five degrees and four hundred five degrees are coterminal."
$\frac{\pi}{3}+2\pi$ "Pi over three plus two pi."
$\theta\equiv\phi\pmod{2\pi}$ "Theta is congruent to phi modulo two pi."

Coterminality is usually expressed verbally rather than with one universal special symbol.

A typical statement is:

$$ 30^\circ \text{ and } 390^\circ \text{ are coterminal}. $$

More formally, two radian measures $\theta$ and $\phi$ are coterminal when:

$$ \boxed{ \theta-\phi=2\pi k, \qquad k\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$

This may also be written using modular notation:

$$ \boxed{ \theta\equiv\phi\pmod{2\pi} }. $$

In degrees, the corresponding modular statement is:

$$ \boxed{ \theta\equiv\phi\pmod{360^\circ} }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Do not use an ordinary equality sign to claim that distinct angle measures such as $30^\circ$ and $390^\circ$ are numerically equal. State that they are coterminal or use an appropriate congruence relation modulo a full rotation.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Degree Form: Angles coterminal with $\theta$ are: $$ \boxed{ \theta+360^\circ k, \qquad k\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$
  • Radian Form: Angles coterminal with $\theta$ are: $$ \boxed{ \theta+2\pi k, \qquad k\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$
  • Criterion in Degrees: Two angles $\theta$ and $\phi$ are coterminal exactly when: $$ \boxed{ \theta-\phi=360^\circ k } $$ for some integer $k$.
  • Criterion in Radians: Two angles $\theta$ and $\phi$ are coterminal exactly when: $$ \boxed{ \theta-\phi=2\pi k } $$ for some integer $k$.
  • Modular Interpretation: Coterminality in radians can be expressed as: $$ \boxed{ \theta\equiv\phi\pmod{2\pi} }. $$
  • Full Rotation: One complete revolution is: $$ 360^\circ=2\pi\text{ radians}. $$
  • Positive Coterminal Angle: A positive coterminal angle can be found by repeatedly adding a full rotation until the result is positive.
  • Negative Coterminal Angle: A negative coterminal angle can be found by repeatedly subtracting a full rotation.
  • Principal Representative: A common convention chooses a coterminal representative in: $$ \boxed{ 0\le\theta<2\pi } $$ or: $$ \boxed{ 0^\circ\le\theta<360^\circ }. $$
  • Alternative Principal Interval: Some contexts instead use: $$ -\pi<\theta\le\pi $$ or: $$ -180^\circ<\theta\le180^\circ. $$ The convention should therefore be identified when a unique representative is required.
  • Unit Circle: Coterminal angles identify the same point: $$ \boxed{ (\cos(\theta+2\pi k),\sin(\theta+2\pi k)) = (\cos\theta,\sin\theta) }. $$
  • Sine: $$ \boxed{ \sin(\theta+2\pi k)=\sin\theta }. $$
  • Cosine: $$ \boxed{ \cos(\theta+2\pi k)=\cos\theta }. $$
  • Secant: Wherever defined: $$ \sec(\theta+2\pi k)=\sec\theta. $$
  • Cosecant: Wherever defined: $$ \csc(\theta+2\pi k)=\csc\theta. $$
  • Tangent: Tangent satisfies: $$ \tan(\theta+\pi k)=\tan\theta. $$ Its fundamental period is $\pi$, which is smaller than a complete rotation.
  • Cotangent: Likewise: $$ \cot(\theta+\pi k)=\cot\theta. $$
  • Periodic Functions: Coterminal angles help explain why trigonometric functions repeat their values as rotation continues around the unit circle.
  • Reference Angles: A coterminal angle may first be reduced to a standard interval before a reference angle is determined. Coterminal angles and reference angles are related concepts but are not the same.
  • Reference Angle Contrast: A reference angle is the acute angle between an angle's terminal side and the $x$-axis. A coterminal angle has the same terminal side as the original angle.
  • Quadrants: Coterminal angles always terminate in the same quadrant or on the same coordinate axis.
  • Rotation Geometry: Rotations differing by full turns produce the same final orientation. Thus: $$ R_{\theta+2\pi k}=R_\theta $$ in planar rotation.
  • Rotation Matrices: The planar rotation matrix: $$ R(\theta)= \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix} $$ satisfies: $$ \boxed{ R(\theta+2\pi k)=R(\theta) }. $$
  • Polar Coordinates: In polar coordinates: $$ (r,\theta) $$ and: $$ (r,\theta+2\pi k) $$ represent the same point for every integer $k$.
  • Complex Numbers: Euler's formula: $$ e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta $$ gives: $$ \boxed{ e^{i(\theta+2\pi k)}=e^{i\theta} }. $$
  • Argument of a Complex Number: If $\theta$ is one argument of a nonzero complex number, then all its arguments have the form: $$ \boxed{ \theta+2\pi k, \qquad k\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$
  • Angular Position vs. Angular Displacement: Coterminal angles may indicate the same final orientation while describing different total angular displacements. For example, $30^\circ$ and $750^\circ$ have the same terminal direction, but the second includes two additional complete rotations.
  • Navigation and Bearings: Directions differing by complete rotations are geometrically equivalent. Angular normalization is commonly used to express headings within a chosen interval such as $0^\circ$ through $360^\circ$.
  • Computer Graphics: Rotation angles may be normalized modulo $2\pi$ or $360^\circ$ because coterminal angles produce the same planar orientation.
  • Terminological Contrast: Coterminal refers to angles sharing initial and terminal sides. Complementary angles have measures summing to $90^\circ$. Supplementary angles have measures summing to $180^\circ$. These terms describe different relationships.
  • Core Interpretation: Coterminality identifies angular measures that differ by complete rotations: $$ \boxed{ \theta \sim \theta+2\pi k, \qquad k\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$ They represent different numerical measures of the same terminal direction.
