Recursive Formula of a Sequence

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

A recursive formula of a sequence defines one or more terms of a sequence in relation to earlier terms. Instead of giving $a_n$ directly as a function of $n$, the formula describes how to generate the sequence step by step.

$$ \boxed{ a_n = F(a_{n-1},a_{n-2},\ldots) } $$

A recursive definition normally includes at least one initial condition, such as:

$$ \boxed{ a_1=c }. $$

The word recursive reflects repetition or return: later terms are computed by referring back to previously established terms. The closely related term recurrence relation is often used for the equation that specifies this dependence.

A complete recursive sequence definition therefore typically has two parts:

$$ \boxed{ \text{initial condition(s)} + \text{recurrence rule} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Basic First-Order Form

$$ \boxed{ a_1=c, \qquad a_n=F(a_{n-1}), \quad n\geq2 }. $$

Each new term depends on the immediately preceding term.

Arithmetic Sequence

An arithmetic sequence with common difference $d$ may be defined recursively by:

$$ \boxed{ a_1=c, \qquad a_n=a_{n-1}+d, \quad n\geq2 }. $$

Example:

$$ a_1=4, \qquad a_n=a_{n-1}+3. $$

This produces:

$$ \boxed{ 4,\ 7,\ 10,\ 13,\ldots }. $$

Geometric Sequence

A geometric sequence with common ratio $r$ may be defined by:

$$ \boxed{ a_1=c, \qquad a_n=ra_{n-1}, \quad n\geq2 }. $$

Example:

$$ a_1=2, \qquad a_n=3a_{n-1} $$

gives:

$$ \boxed{ 2,\ 6,\ 18,\ 54,\ldots }. $$

Second-Order Recursion

A recursive formula may depend on more than one previous term:

$$ \boxed{ a_n = F(a_{n-1},a_{n-2}) }. $$

In that case, enough initial values must be supplied to begin the process.

Fibonacci Example

$$ \boxed{ F_1=1, \qquad F_2=1, \qquad F_n=F_{n-1}+F_{n-2}, \quad n\geq3 }. $$

This generates:

$$ \boxed{ 1,\ 1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 5,\ 8,\ldots }. $$

Order of a Recurrence

The order is commonly the greatest backward step needed to compute a new term.

For:

$$ a_n = 2a_{n-1}-a_{n-3}, $$

the recurrence is third order because the largest lag is $3$.

Initial Conditions

A recurrence relation alone may not determine a unique sequence. Initial conditions select the specific sequence.

For example:

$$ a_n=a_{n-1}+2 $$

describes infinitely many arithmetic sequences until an initial value is specified.

Index Restrictions

A recursive formula should specify where the recurrence begins:

$$ \boxed{ n\geq2 } $$

or:

$$ \boxed{ n\geq3 }, $$

depending on how many earlier terms are required.

Recursive vs. Explicit Formula

A recursive formula computes terms from previous terms. An explicit formula gives $a_n$ directly from $n$.

For an arithmetic sequence:

$$ \boxed{ a_n = a_1+(n-1)d } $$

is explicit, while:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=a_{n-1}+d } $$

is recursive.

Geometric Explicit Form

$$ \boxed{ a_n = a_1r^{n-1} }. $$

Iteration

Applying the same recursive rule repeatedly is an iterative process:

$$ \boxed{ a_1 \to a_2 \to a_3 \to \cdots }. $$

State-Transition View

A first-order recursion:

$$ a_{n+1}=F(a_n) $$

may be viewed as repeatedly applying a state-transition function:

$$ \boxed{ a \mapsto F(a) }. $$

Linear Recurrence

A common class has the form:

$$ \boxed{ a_n = c_1a_{n-1} + c_2a_{n-2} + \cdots + c_ka_{n-k} + g(n) }. $$

When $g(n)=0$, the recurrence is homogeneous.

Nonlinear Recurrence

Recursive formulas need not be linear. For example:

$$ \boxed{ a_{n+1} = a_n^2-1 }. $$

Recursion and Algorithms

Recursive sequence formulas translate naturally into algorithms because each term may be computed from stored earlier values.

Computational Cost

Direct recursive evaluation can be efficient when terms are generated in order, but naive repeated recomputation may be inefficient if earlier terms are calculated many times. Memoization or iterative storage can avoid such duplication.

Recursive Definitions beyond Numeric Sequences

The same structural idea extends to recursively defined strings, trees, sets, functions, and combinatorial objects. For a sequence, however, the recursion specifically defines indexed terms.

Common Misconception

A recursive formula is not complete merely because it tells how to move from one term to the next. Unless enough starting information is already understood, initial conditions are required to determine a unique sequence.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$a_n=a_{n-1}+d$ "A sub n equals the previous term plus d."
$a_n=ra_{n-1}$ "A sub n equals r times the preceding term."
$F_n=F_{n-1}+F_{n-2}$ "The nth Fibonacci term equals the two preceding Fibonacci terms added together."
$a_1=c$ "The first term is c," an initial condition.
$n\geq2$ "The recursive rule applies for n greater than or equal to two."
$a_{n+1}=F(a_n)$ "The next term is obtained by applying F to the current term."

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"write a recursive formula"} } $$

generally means provide both the starting value or values and a rule that generates each later term from earlier terms.

The notation:

$$ a_{n-1} $$

means the term immediately before $a_n$, while $a_{n-2}$ means the term two positions earlier.

Defense Notation Advice: Include enough initial conditions for the recurrence order, state the valid index range, and distinguish $a_n$ from $a_{n-1}$ carefully. A recursive rule should make clear which previously defined terms are required before the next term can be computed.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: Defines sequence terms using earlier sequence terms.
  • Initial Condition: Supplies the starting term or terms.
  • Recurrence Relation: Specifies dependence of later terms on earlier ones.
  • First-Order Recurrence: Depends on the immediately preceding term.
  • Higher-Order Recurrence: Depends on terms farther back in the sequence.
  • Arithmetic Sequence: $a_n=a_{n-1}+d$.
  • Geometric Sequence: $a_n=ra_{n-1}$.
  • Fibonacci-Type Sequence: A term may depend on two preceding terms.
  • Index Condition: States when the recursive rule begins.
  • Explicit-Formula Contrast: Explicit formulas compute $a_n$ directly from $n$.
  • Iteration: Recursive formulas generate terms sequentially.
  • State Transition: A recurrence may describe repeated movement from one state to the next.
  • Linear Recurrence: Previous terms appear linearly.
  • Nonlinear Recurrence: Previous terms may be squared, multiplied, or otherwise transformed nonlinearly.
  • Homogeneous Recurrence: Contains no independent forcing term.
  • Algorithmic Use: Naturally supports stepwise computation.
  • Dynamic Programming: Stored previous terms can prevent repeated computation.
  • Discrete Modeling: Recurrences model populations, balances, inventories, states, and repeated processes.
  • Difference Equations: Recursive formulas are closely related to discrete-time dynamical equations.
  • Common Error: Omitting initial conditions.
  • Common Error: Providing too few initial values for a higher-order recurrence.
  • Common Error: Failing to state the valid index range.
  • Common Error: Confusing a recursive formula with an explicit formula.
  • Common Error: Using a term before it has been defined.
  • Common Error: Treating the recurrence relation alone as necessarily defining a unique sequence.
  • Core Interpretation: A recursive formula defines a sequence by combining initial information with a rule for generating later terms: $$ \boxed{ \text{initial condition(s)} + \text{recurrence relation} }. $$ A typical first-order form is: $$ \boxed{ a_1=c, \qquad a_n=F(a_{n-1}), \quad n\geq2 }. $$ Recursive formulas provide the natural language of stepwise change, sequence generation, recurrence relations, discrete dynamical systems, and many algorithmic processes.
