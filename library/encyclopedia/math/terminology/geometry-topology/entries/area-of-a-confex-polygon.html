Area of a Convex Polygon

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

The area of a convex polygon is the measure of the two-dimensional region enclosed by a polygon whose interior contains every line segment joining any two of its points.

A polygon is convex when it has no inward dents. Equivalently, for any two points inside or on the polygon, the entire segment joining them remains inside or on the polygon.

This property makes area computation especially straightforward because diagonals drawn from one vertex to nonadjacent vertices remain inside the polygon and divide it into nonoverlapping triangles.

$$ \boxed{ \text{area of polygon} = \text{sum of areas of a triangulation} }. $$

There is no single universal area formula depending only on the number of sides. The required data may instead include side lengths, heights, coordinates, diagonals, angles, or—when the polygon is regular—its apothem and perimeter.

Operational Nuance

Convex Polygon

A polygon is convex if every interior angle is less than $180^\circ$ and every diagonal lies entirely inside the polygon, apart from its endpoints.

Convexity permits a simple fan triangulation from any vertex.

Triangulation

An $n$-sided convex polygon can be divided into:

$$ \boxed{ n-2 } $$

triangles by drawing diagonals from one vertex to all nonadjacent vertices.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ A_{\text{polygon}} = A_1+A_2+\cdots+A_{n-2} }. $$

Triangle-Area Method

If each triangle has known base and height:

$$ A_i=\frac12b_ih_i. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ A_{\text{polygon}} = \frac12 \sum_{i=1}^{n-2} b_ih_i }. $$

Example: Convex Quadrilateral

A convex quadrilateral can be split by one diagonal into two triangles:

$$ \boxed{ A_{\text{quad}} = A_{\triangle 1} + A_{\triangle 2} }. $$

If both triangle areas can be found, the quadrilateral area follows immediately.

Example: Convex Pentagon

A convex pentagon can be triangulated into:

$$ 5-2=3 $$

triangles.

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ A=A_1+A_2+A_3 }. $$

Coordinate Method

When the vertices are known as coordinates:

$$ (x_1,y_1), (x_2,y_2), \ldots, (x_n,y_n), $$

ordered consecutively around the polygon, the area can be found with the shoelace formula.

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \left| \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( x_i y_{i+1} - y_i x_{i+1} \right) \right| } $$

where:

$$ (x_{n+1},y_{n+1}) = (x_1,y_1). $$

Expanded Shoelace Form

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \left| x_1y_2+x_2y_3+\cdots+x_ny_1 - (y_1x_2+y_2x_3+\cdots+y_nx_1) \right| }. $$

Vertex Order Matters

The vertices must be listed in cyclic order around the polygon:

$$ \boxed{ \text{clockwise or counterclockwise} }. $$

Arbitrarily scrambling the vertices may describe crossing edges rather than the intended polygon and can produce an incorrect area.

Signed Area

Before taking the absolute value, the shoelace expression gives an oriented or signed area:

$$ A_{\text{signed}} = \frac12 \sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i y_{i+1}-y_i x_{i+1}). $$

Reversing the order of the vertices changes the sign but not the geometric area.

Determinant Form

The same formula may be expressed using two-dimensional determinants:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \left| \sum_{i=1}^{n} \det \begin{pmatrix} x_i & y_i\\ x_{i+1} & y_{i+1} \end{pmatrix} \right| }. $$

Since:

$$ \det \begin{pmatrix} x_i & y_i\\ x_{i+1} & y_{i+1} \end{pmatrix} = x_i y_{i+1}-y_i x_{i+1}, $$

this is precisely the shoelace formula in determinant notation.

Vector Cross-Product Interpretation

Treating planar points as vectors in three dimensions with zero $z$-coordinate, the signed contribution of adjacent position vectors can be written using:

$$ \mathbf{r}_i\times\mathbf{r}_{i+1}. $$

The polygon area becomes:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \left| \sum_i (\mathbf{r}_i\times\mathbf{r}_{i+1})_z \right| }. $$

Area of a Regular Convex Polygon

A regular polygon has equal side lengths and equal interior angles.

If its perimeter is $P$ and apothem is $a$, then:

$$ \boxed{ A=\frac12Pa }. $$

Why the Regular-Polygon Formula Works

Joining the center to each vertex divides a regular $n$-gon into $n$ congruent triangles.

Each triangle has height $a$, the apothem, and the combined bases equal the perimeter $P$.

Therefore:

$$ A = \frac12a (s+s+\cdots+s) = \boxed{ \frac12Pa }. $$

Regular Polygon from Side Length

For a regular $n$-gon with side length $s$:

$$ P=ns. $$

Its apothem is:

$$ a= \frac{s} {2\tan(\pi/n)}. $$

Substitution gives:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac{ns^2} {4\tan(\pi/n)} }. $$

Equivalent Cotangent Form

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac14ns^2 \cot\left(\frac{\pi}{n}\right) }. $$

Regular Polygon from Circumradius

If $R$ is the circumradius:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12nR^2 \sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{n}\right) }. $$

Regular Polygon from Apothem

Since:

$$ s= 2a\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{n}\right), $$

one may also write:

$$ \boxed{ A= na^2 \tan\left(\frac{\pi}{n}\right) }. $$

Area from Triangles with Two Sides and Included Angle

If a triangulation produces triangles for which two sides and the included angle are known, use:

$$ \boxed{ A_{\triangle} = \frac12ab\sin C }. $$

The polygon area is then the sum of the resulting triangle areas.

Area from Three Sides

If a triangle in the decomposition has side lengths $a$, $b$, and $c$, its area can be found using Heron's formula:

$$ \boxed{ K= \sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)} } $$

where:

$$ s=\frac{a+b+c}{2}. $$

This can be applied separately to each triangle in the polygon.

Scaling

If every length in a polygon is multiplied by $k$, then:

$$ \boxed{ A\mapsto k^2A }. $$

Thus a scale factor of $2$ multiplies area by $4$, and a scale factor of $3$ multiplies area by $9$.

Affine Transformations

If a polygon is transformed by:

$$ T(\mathbf{x})=M\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}, $$

then translation by $\mathbf{b}$ does not change area, while the linear part scales area by:

$$ \boxed{ |\det M| }. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ A_{\text{new}} = |\det M|A_{\text{old}} }. $$

Translation Invariance

Moving a polygon without resizing or deforming it preserves its area.

Rotation Invariance

Rotating a polygon preserves area.

Reflection Invariance

Reflection changes orientation but not geometric area.

Convexity and Triangulation

For a convex polygon, a fan of diagonals from any vertex always lies within the polygon. This makes triangulation especially simple.

Concave polygons can also be triangulated, but arbitrary diagonals may pass outside the region, so greater care is required.

Interior Angle Sum Does Not Determine Area

Every convex $n$-gon has interior angle sum:

$$ \boxed{ (n-2)180^\circ }. $$

But this tells nothing by itself about area. Polygons with the same number of sides and the same angle sum can have vastly different sizes.

Perimeter Does Not Determine Area

Two convex polygons may have the same perimeter but different areas.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \text{perimeter alone does not determine polygon area} }. $$

Units

Polygon area is measured in square units:

$$ \boxed{ \text{units}^2 }. $$

For coordinates measured in meters, the resulting area is measured in square meters.

Common Misconception: There is no general formula of the form "number of sides times side length" for the area of an arbitrary convex polygon. Additional geometric information is required unless the polygon belongs to a more specialized class, such as a regular polygon.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$A=A_1+\cdots+A_{n-2}$ "The polygon area is the sum of the areas of its triangulation."
$A=\frac12Pa$ "Area equals one-half the perimeter times the apothem."
$A=\frac{ns^2}{4\tan(\pi/n)}$ "Area equals n s squared divided by four tangent pi over n."
$A=\frac12\left|\sum(x_iy_{i+1}-y_ix_{i+1})\right|$ "Area equals one-half the absolute value of the shoelace sum."
$A\mapsto k^2A$ "Area scales by the square of the linear scale factor."
$A'=|\det M|A$ "The transformed area equals the absolute determinant times the original area."

The phrase area of a convex polygon does not identify a single formula. It identifies a geometric quantity that may be computed by different methods depending on the information supplied.

Defense Notation Advice: When using coordinate formulas, list the vertices in consecutive order around the boundary. When using triangulation, make clear that triangle interiors do not overlap and together cover the entire polygon.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: The area of a convex polygon is the two-dimensional measure of the region enclosed by its sides.
  • Convexity: Every segment joining two points of the polygon remains inside or on the polygon.
  • Diagonal Property: Every diagonal of a convex polygon lies inside the polygon.
  • Triangulation Count: An $n$-gon can be triangulated into: $$ \boxed{ n-2 } $$ triangles.
  • Triangulation Area: $$ \boxed{ A= \sum_{i=1}^{n-2}A_i }. $$
  • Triangle Base-Height Formula: $$ \boxed{ A_i=\frac12b_ih_i }. $$
  • Triangle Trigonometric Formula: $$ \boxed{ A_i=\frac12ab\sin C }. $$
  • Triangle Heron Formula: $$ \boxed{ A_i= \sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)} }. $$
  • Coordinate Method: Vertices must be arranged consecutively clockwise or counterclockwise.
  • Shoelace Formula: $$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \left| \sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i y_{i+1}-y_i x_{i+1}) \right| }. $$
  • Closing Convention: $$ \boxed{ (x_{n+1},y_{n+1})=(x_1,y_1) }. $$
  • Signed Area: The shoelace sum before taking absolute value records orientation as well as magnitude.
  • Orientation Reversal: Reversing vertex order changes the sign of signed area but not geometric area.
  • Determinant Form: $$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \left| \sum_i \det \begin{pmatrix} x_i & y_i\\ x_{i+1} & y_{i+1} \end{pmatrix} \right| }. $$
  • Regular Polygon: $$ \boxed{ A=\frac12Pa }. $$
  • Regular n-gon Perimeter: $$ \boxed{ P=ns }. $$
  • Regular n-gon Apothem: $$ \boxed{ a= \frac{s} {2\tan(\pi/n)} }. $$
  • Regular n-gon from Side Length: $$ \boxed{ A= \frac{ns^2} {4\tan(\pi/n)} }. $$
  • Cotangent Form: $$ \boxed{ A= \frac14ns^2 \cot\left(\frac{\pi}{n}\right) }. $$
  • Regular n-gon from Circumradius: $$ \boxed{ A= \frac12nR^2 \sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{n}\right) }. $$
  • Regular n-gon from Apothem: $$ \boxed{ A= na^2 \tan\left(\frac{\pi}{n}\right) }. $$
  • Scaling: $$ \boxed{ \text{linear scale }k \Rightarrow \text{area scale }k^2 }. $$
  • Double Scale: $$ \boxed{ k=2 \Rightarrow A'=4A }. $$
  • Affine Transformation: $$ \boxed{ A'=|\det M|A }. $$
  • Translation: Translation preserves polygon area.
  • Rotation: Rotation preserves polygon area.
  • Reflection: Reflection preserves geometric area while reversing orientation.
  • Shear: A shear with determinant $1$ preserves area even though it changes shape.
  • Interior Angle Sum: $$ \boxed{ (n-2)180^\circ } $$ depends only on the number of sides and does not determine area.
  • Perimeter Caution: Equal perimeter does not imply equal area.
  • Side Count Caution: Equal numbers of sides do not imply equal area.
  • Shape Dependence: Area depends on the spatial arrangement and metric dimensions of the vertices, not merely the polygon's combinatorial type.
  • Convex vs. Concave: Both can have area, but convex polygons permit especially simple triangulations because their diagonals remain inside.
  • Computational Geometry: The shoelace formula is widely useful for finding polygon area from ordered vertex coordinates.
  • Coordinate Units: If coordinates are measured in meters, the resulting area is in square meters.
  • Nonnegative Measure: Geometric area satisfies: $$ \boxed{ A\ge0 }. $$
  • Terminological Contrast: Perimeter measures boundary length. Area measures the enclosed region. Convexity describes shape structure and makes triangulation especially straightforward, but does not by itself determine numerical area.
