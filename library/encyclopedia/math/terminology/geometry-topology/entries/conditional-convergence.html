Conditional Convergence

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

A series is said to exhibit conditional convergence when the series itself converges, but the corresponding series formed from the absolute values of its terms diverges.

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n \text{ converges conditionally} \iff \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n \text{ converges and } \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|a_n| \text{ diverges} }. $$

The adjective conditional signals that the convergence is weaker than absolute convergence. The series converges because of the particular interaction of its positive and negative terms rather than because the total magnitudes of those terms form a convergent series.

This meaning of conditional differs from the logical use of the word in an if-then statement. Here it describes a type of convergence whose stability depends on the arrangement and cancellation of signed terms.

$$ \boxed{ \text{conditional convergence} = \text{convergence without absolute convergence} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Definition

Let:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n $$

be an infinite series. It is conditionally convergent if:

$$ \boxed{ \sum a_n \text{ converges} } $$

but:

$$ \boxed{ \sum |a_n| \text{ diverges} }. $$

Absolute Convergence

By contrast, a series is absolutely convergent if:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|a_n| \text{ converges} }. $$

Absolute convergence automatically implies ordinary convergence:

$$ \boxed{ \sum|a_n|\text{ converges} \Rightarrow \sum a_n\text{ converges} }. $$

The converse is false, and conditionally convergent series provide the standard counterexamples.

Conditional, Absolute, and Divergent

Classification $\sum a_n$ $\sum|a_n|$
Absolutely convergent Converges Converges
Conditionally convergent Converges Diverges
Divergent Diverges May converge or diverge? No: if $\sum|a_n|$ converges, then $\sum a_n$ must converge

The Alternating Harmonic Series

The standard example of conditional convergence is:

$$ \boxed{ 1-\frac12+\frac13-\frac14+\frac15-\cdots } $$

written as:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n} }. $$

This series converges by the alternating series test.

Absolute Values of the Alternating Harmonic Series

Taking absolute values gives:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left| \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n} \right| = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1n. $$

This is the harmonic series, which diverges.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n} \text{ converges conditionally} }. $$

Its Sum

In its usual order:

$$ \boxed{ 1-\frac12+\frac13-\frac14+\cdots = \ln2 }. $$

The fact that the series has a definite sum in its given order does not make it absolutely convergent.

Alternating Series Test

Suppose:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}b_n $$

or:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n+1}b_n $$

has positive terms $b_n$ satisfying:

$$ \boxed{ b_{n+1}\le b_n \text{ eventually} } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{n\to\infty}b_n=0 }. $$

Then the alternating series converges.

The Alternating Series Test Does Not Establish Absolute Convergence

Passing the alternating series test proves convergence of the signed series. To determine whether that convergence is absolute or conditional, one must separately examine:

$$ \boxed{ \sum b_n }. $$

If $\sum b_n$ diverges, then the alternating series is conditionally convergent.

Example: Alternating p-Series

Consider:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n^p}. $$

For $p>0$, the terms:

$$ \frac1{n^p} $$

decrease to zero, so the alternating series converges.

Its absolute-value series is:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{n^p}. $$

The $p$-series test therefore gives:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{cases} 01 &\Rightarrow \text{absolute convergence}. \end{cases} } $$

Example with p = 1/2

The series:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{\sqrt n} $$

converges by the alternating series test.

However:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{\sqrt n} $$

is a divergent $p$-series with $p=1/2$.

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{\sqrt n} \text{ converges conditionally} }. $$

Why Sign Cancellation Matters

A conditionally convergent series contains enough cancellation between positive and negative contributions for its partial sums to approach a finite limit even though the total magnitudes of its terms are too large to form a convergent series.

$$ \boxed{ \text{signed cancellation} \text{ can produce convergence even when } \sum|a_n| \text{ diverges} }. $$

Partial Sums

A series:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n $$

converges when its sequence of partial sums:

$$ S_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_n $$

approaches a finite limit:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{N\to\infty}S_N=S }. $$

Conditional convergence is therefore still genuine convergence of the partial-sum sequence.

Positive and Negative Parts

Every real term can be decomposed as:

$$ a_n=a_n^+-a_n^- $$

where:

$$ a_n^+=\max(a_n,0), \qquad a_n^-=\max(-a_n,0). $$

Also:

$$ |a_n|=a_n^++a_n^-. $$

Behavior of the Positive and Negative Parts

For a conditionally convergent real series:

$$ \boxed{ \sum a_n^+=+\infty } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \sum a_n^- =+\infty }. $$

Thus the positive contributions have infinite total magnitude and the negative contributions also have infinite total magnitude, yet their interleaving can produce a finite sum.

Why Rearrangement Matters

Finite sums can be reordered freely:

$$ a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n $$

has the same value under every permutation of its terms.

Infinite conditionally convergent series behave differently. Their sums can depend on the order of the terms.

Riemann Rearrangement Theorem

A conditionally convergent real series has an extraordinary property: its terms can be rearranged to converge to any prescribed real number.

$$ \boxed{ \text{conditionally convergent series} \xrightarrow{\text{appropriate rearrangement}} \text{any chosen real sum} }. $$

Rearrangements can also be constructed that diverge.

Why the Riemann Rearrangement Theorem Works

Since the positive terms have unbounded total sum and the negative terms have unbounded total magnitude, one may alternately add enough positive terms to cross a target value and enough negative terms to fall below it.

Because:

$$ a_n\to0, $$

these overshoots become progressively smaller, allowing convergence to the selected target.

Absolute Convergence Is Rearrangement-Stable

Absolutely convergent series do not have this instability. Every rearrangement of an absolutely convergent series converges to the same sum.

$$ \boxed{ \text{absolute convergence} \Rightarrow \text{sum invariant under rearrangement} }. $$

Conditional Convergence Is Order-Sensitive

Thus one major conceptual distinction is:

Absolute Convergence Conditional Convergence
Converges after taking absolute values Diverges after taking absolute values
Stable under arbitrary rearrangement May change sum under rearrangement
Cancellation is not required for convergence Cancellation is essential to convergence

Conditional Convergence Does Not Mean Tentative Convergence

The word conditional does not mean that mathematicians are uncertain whether the series converges.

Once proven:

$$ \boxed{ \sum a_n=S } $$

in its stated order, the convergence is mathematically definite. The adjective identifies the weaker mode of convergence.

The nth-Term Test Comes First

Every convergent series must satisfy:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=0 }. $$

Therefore a series whose terms fail to approach zero cannot be conditionally convergent.

However:

$$ a_n\to0 $$

does not by itself imply convergence.

Testing Strategy

A practical classification procedure is:

  1. Analyze $\sum|a_n|$.
  2. If it converges, the original series converges absolutely.
  3. If it diverges, analyze $\sum a_n$ itself.
  4. If the original series converges, the convergence is conditional.
  5. If the original series also diverges, the series is divergent.

Absolute Convergence Test First

For many signed series, testing absolute convergence first is efficient because a successful absolute-convergence test immediately settles ordinary convergence.

$$ \boxed{ \sum|a_n|\text{ converges} \Rightarrow \text{stop: absolutely convergent} }. $$

Ratio Test

The ratio test examines:

$$ L= \lim_{n\to\infty} \left| \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} \right|. $$

If:

$$ \boxed{L<1}, $$

the series converges absolutely.

Therefore the ratio test does not yield conditional convergence when it succeeds with $L<1$.

Root Test

The root test uses:

$$ L= \limsup_{n\to\infty}|a_n|^{1/n}. $$

If:

$$ L<1, $$

the series converges absolutely.

Comparison Tests

Comparison or limit-comparison tests can often establish divergence of:

$$ \sum|a_n|, $$

after which a separate test can determine whether the signed series itself still converges.

Leibniz-Type Alternation

A common pattern for conditional convergence is:

$$ \boxed{ \sum(-1)^n b_n } $$

where $b_n\to0$ and decreases sufficiently for the alternating series test, while:

$$ \sum b_n $$

diverges.

Not Every Alternating Series Is Conditionally Convergent

Alternation alone does not determine the classification.

For example:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{n^2} $$

is absolutely convergent because:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{n^2} $$

converges.

Not Every Alternating Series Converges

Consider:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^n. $$

Its terms do not approach zero:

$$ (-1)^n\not\to0. $$

Therefore the series diverges.

Alternating Series Error Estimate

When an alternating series satisfies the standard hypotheses and has sum $S$, the remainder after $N$ terms satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ |S-S_N| \le b_{N+1} }. $$

This gives a useful numerical estimate even for many conditionally convergent series.

Example of Error Control

For the alternating harmonic series:

$$ S_N= \sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}n, $$

the error satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ |\ln2-S_N| \le \frac1{N+1} }. $$

Conditional Convergence in Power Series

A power series:

$$ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_n(x-a)^n $$

often converges absolutely inside its radius of convergence and diverges outside it. At boundary points, the series must be tested separately.

Boundary points may exhibit:

  • absolute convergence,
  • conditional convergence, or
  • divergence.

Example: Endpoint Conditional Convergence

Consider:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{x^n}{n}. $$

At $x=-1$:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{n} $$

converges conditionally.

At $x=1$:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1n $$

diverges.

Power-Series Interval Notation Must Preserve Endpoint Behavior

If one endpoint converges conditionally while the other diverges, interval notation should reflect that distinction exactly.

Improper Integrals: Related but Distinct Terminology

Improper integrals also have notions analogous to absolute and conditional convergence.

An improper integral may converge while:

$$ \int |f(x)|\,dx $$

diverges. This is often called conditional convergence of the improper integral.

$$ \boxed{ \int f(x)\,dx \text{ converges but } \int|f(x)|\,dx \text{ diverges} }. $$

Example: Oscillatory Improper Integral

The integral:

$$ \int_1^\infty\frac{\sin x}{x}\,dx $$

converges, while:

$$ \int_1^\infty\frac{|\sin x|}{x}\,dx $$

diverges. This is a standard integral analogue of conditional convergence.

Conditional Convergence and Cancellation

The key structural theme is cancellation:

$$ \boxed{ \text{positive contributions} + \text{negative contributions} \longrightarrow \text{finite limiting total} } $$

even though the sum of their magnitudes diverges.

Conditional Convergence Is Not Absolute Smallness

Individual terms approaching zero does not mean their total absolute contribution is finite.

For the alternating harmonic series:

$$ \frac1n\to0, $$

yet:

$$ \sum\frac1n=\infty. $$

Conditional Convergence and Commutativity

Addition of finitely many real numbers is commutative, but an infinite series is defined through the limit of an ordered sequence of partial sums.

Thus arbitrary infinite rearrangement is an additional operation whose validity must be justified.

$$ \boxed{ \text{finite commutativity} \not\Rightarrow \text{unrestricted rearrangement of every convergent series} }. $$

Grouping versus Rearrangement

Grouping consecutive terms without changing their order is different from rearranging terms.

For a convergent series, grouping finitely many consecutive terms at a time preserves the sum, provided the order is unchanged and the grouping is well formed.

Rearrangement permutes the order and is precisely where conditional convergence becomes delicate.

Conditional Convergence in Complex Series

For a complex series:

$$ \sum z_n, $$

absolute convergence means:

$$ \sum|z_n|<\infty. $$

If the complex series converges but the absolute-value series diverges, one may also describe the convergence as conditional, although rearrangement behavior is naturally interpreted in the vector geometry of $\mathbb C\cong\mathbb R^2$.

Conditional Convergence in Normed Spaces

In more advanced analysis, series of vectors may be compared with:

$$ \sum\|x_n\|. $$

Convergence of the norm series is a strong absolute-type condition. The precise relationships among convergence, unconditional convergence, and absolute convergence depend on the space under study.

Conditional versus Unconditional Convergence

Unconditional convergence means, roughly, that a series converges to the same value regardless of how its terms are rearranged.

For real and complex scalar series:

$$ \boxed{ \text{unconditional convergence} \iff \text{absolute convergence} }. $$

Conditional convergence therefore represents the failure of unconditional convergence in the scalar setting.

Conditional Convergence and Series Manipulation

Operations involving infinite series must respect their convergence class. Rearrangement, multiplication, termwise integration, termwise differentiation, and exchange of limits can require stronger hypotheses than ordinary convergence alone.

Absolute or uniform convergence often provides the stability needed for such operations.

Common Misconception: Conditional Convergence Means the Series Might or Might Not Converge

A conditionally convergent series does converge. The condition concerns its failure to converge absolutely.

Common Misconception: Alternating Means Conditionally Convergent

An alternating series can be absolutely convergent, conditionally convergent, or divergent.

$$ \boxed{ \text{alternating} \not\Rightarrow \text{conditionally convergent} }. $$

Common Misconception: Absolute Values Preserve the Sum

Replacing $a_n$ with $|a_n|$ creates a different series. The purpose is to test the total magnitude of the terms, not to preserve the original sum.

Common Misconception: If a_n → 0, the Series Converges

The condition:

$$ a_n\to0 $$

is necessary but not sufficient.

The harmonic series provides the classical counterexample:

$$ \frac1n\to0 \qquad\text{but}\qquad \sum\frac1n \text{ diverges}. $$

Common Misconception: Every Rearrangement Has the Same Sum

This is guaranteed for absolutely convergent scalar series, not for conditionally convergent ones.

Common Misconception: Rearrangement Means Regrouping

Rearrangement changes the order of terms. Regrouping consecutive terms can leave the order untouched. These are distinct operations.

Common Misconception: Conditional Convergence Is Numerical Error

Conditional convergence is an exact mathematical property of an infinite series. It is not caused by rounding or computational approximation.

Common Misconception: A Conditionally Convergent Series Is Less “True”

The limiting sum is fully valid in the stated order. The difference is structural: the convergence lacks the stronger stability supplied by absolute convergence.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\sum a_n$ converges, $\sum|a_n|$ diverges "The series converges conditionally."
$\sum|a_n|<\infty$ "The series converges absolutely."
$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}n$ "The alternating harmonic series."
$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1n=\infty$ "The harmonic series diverges."
$S_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_n$ "S sub N is the Nth partial sum."
$|S-S_N|\le b_{N+1}$ "The alternating-series remainder is at most the magnitude of the next term."

The standard phrase is the series converges conditionally or the series is conditionally convergent. The noun form is conditional convergence.

A complete classification should usually distinguish among absolute convergence, conditional convergence, and divergence. Saying only that a series "converges" may omit mathematically important information when rearrangement or later manipulation is relevant.

Defense Notation Advice: To establish conditional convergence, prove both required claims: the signed series converges and the absolute-value series diverges. Do not label a series conditionally convergent merely because it alternates. Preserve the distinction between regrouping and rearrangement, and do not rearrange a conditionally convergent series as though finite-sum commutativity automatically extended to arbitrary infinite permutations.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: $$ \boxed{ \sum a_n\text{ converges conditionally} \iff \sum a_n\text{ converges and }\sum|a_n|\text{ diverges} }. $$
  • Absolute-Convergence Contrast: Absolute convergence requires $\sum|a_n|$ to converge.
  • Implication: Absolute convergence implies ordinary convergence.
  • Converse: Ordinary convergence does not imply absolute convergence.
  • Canonical Example: $$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}n } $$ converges conditionally.
  • Alternating Harmonic Sum: $$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}n=\ln2 }. $$
  • Absolute-Value Series: The corresponding harmonic series $\sum1/n$ diverges.
  • Alternating-Series Test: Decreasing positive magnitudes tending to zero guarantee convergence of the associated alternating series.
  • Classification Step: After establishing alternating convergence, test the absolute-value series to determine whether convergence is absolute or conditional.
  • Alternating p-Series: $$ \boxed{ \sum\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n^p} } $$ is conditionally convergent for $01$.
  • Cancellation: Positive and negative contributions cancel sufficiently to produce a finite partial-sum limit.
  • Positive Part: For a conditionally convergent real series, the sum of positive terms diverges to $+\infty$.
  • Negative Part: The total magnitude of the negative terms also diverges to $+\infty$.
  • Rearrangement Sensitivity: Conditional convergence is not stable under arbitrary rearrangement.
  • Riemann Rearrangement Theorem: A conditionally convergent real series can be rearranged to converge to any prescribed real number and can also be rearranged to diverge.
  • Absolute Rearrangement Stability: Rearrangements of absolutely convergent scalar series preserve the sum.
  • nth-Term Requirement: Every convergent series must satisfy $a_n\to0$.
  • Necessary versus Sufficient: $a_n\to0$ is necessary for convergence but not sufficient.
  • Ratio Test: A successful ratio test with limit below $1$ establishes absolute, not merely conditional, convergence.
  • Root Test: A root-test value below $1$ likewise establishes absolute convergence.
  • Power Series: Conditional convergence frequently appears at endpoints of intervals of convergence.
  • Improper Integrals: An analogous term is used when $\int f$ converges while $\int|f|$ diverges.
  • Unconditional Convergence: For scalar real and complex series, unconditional convergence is equivalent to absolute convergence.
  • Finite-Sum Contrast: Finite sums may be arbitrarily reordered; conditionally convergent infinite series generally may not.
  • Grouping Contrast: Grouping consecutive terms without changing their order is distinct from rearranging terms.
  • Common Error: Do not interpret conditional as meaning uncertain or tentative.
  • Common Error: Do not assume every alternating series is conditionally convergent.
  • Common Error: Do not assume $a_n\to0$ proves convergence.
  • Common Error: Do not assume every rearrangement of a convergent series preserves its sum.
  • Common Error: Do not establish conditional convergence without separately showing divergence of the absolute-value series.
  • Terminological Contrast: Conditional convergence means that a series converges while its absolute-value series diverges. Absolute convergence means the series of absolute values converges. Ordinary convergence refers only to convergence of the original partial-sum sequence without specifying its stronger classification. Divergence means the partial sums do not approach a finite limit. Unconditional convergence means convergence is preserved under rearrangement; for scalar series this is equivalent to absolute convergence.
