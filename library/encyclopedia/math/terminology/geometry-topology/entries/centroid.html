Centroid

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

A centroid is a geometric center defined as an average position of points, lengths, areas, or volumes, depending on the object being studied.

$$ \boxed{ \text{centroid} = \text{geometric average position} }. $$

For a triangle, the centroid is the point where the three medians intersect.

$$ \boxed{ \text{three medians} \longrightarrow G }. $$

The centroid is commonly denoted by:

$$ \boxed{ G }. $$

More broadly, the centroid of a region is the point obtained by averaging its coordinates with respect to geometric measure. For a body of uniform density, this coincides with its center of mass.

Operational Nuance

Centroid of a Triangle

A median of a triangle is a segment joining a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side.

If $M$ is the midpoint of $\overline{BC}$, then:

$$ \boxed{ AM \text{ is a median} }. $$

Concurrency of the Medians

Every triangle has three medians, and they intersect at one common point:

$$ \boxed{ G= \text{intersection of the three medians} }. $$

2:1 Division Property

The centroid divides every median in a $2:1$ ratio, with the longer portion adjacent to the vertex.

$$ \boxed{ AG:GM=2:1 }. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ AG=\frac23AM } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ GM=\frac13AM }. $$

Coordinate Formula for a Triangle

If the vertices are:

$$ A=(x_1,y_1),\qquad B=(x_2,y_2),\qquad C=(x_3,y_3), $$

then the centroid is:

$$ \boxed{ G= \left( \frac{x_1+x_2+x_3}{3}, \frac{y_1+y_2+y_3}{3} \right) }. $$

Coordinate Averaging

The formula shows that the centroid is found by separately averaging the corresponding coordinates.

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{x_1+x_2+x_3}{3}, \qquad \bar{y} = \frac{y_1+y_2+y_3}{3} }. $$

Vector Form

If the triangle vertices have position vectors $\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf g = \frac{\mathbf a+\mathbf b+\mathbf c}{3} }. $$

Example

Suppose:

$$ A=(0,0),\qquad B=(6,0),\qquad C=(0,3). $$

Then:

$$ G= \left( \frac{0+6+0}{3}, \frac{0+0+3}{3} \right). $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ G=(2,1) }. $$

Centroid Always Lies Inside a Triangle

For every nondegenerate triangle, the centroid lies strictly inside the triangle.

$$ \boxed{ G\in\operatorname{interior}(\triangle ABC) }. $$

Centroid as Balance Point

If a triangular lamina has uniform density, its centroid is its center of mass.

$$ \boxed{ \text{uniform triangle} \Rightarrow \text{centroid}= \text{center of mass} }. $$

Equal-Area Partition by Medians

The three medians divide a triangle into six smaller triangles of equal area.

$$ \boxed{ A_1=A_2=\cdots=A_6 }. $$

This area symmetry is closely related to the balancing role of the centroid.

One Median Divides Area in Half

Every median divides a triangle into two smaller triangles of equal area because those triangles have equal base lengths and the same altitude.

$$ \boxed{ [\triangle ABM] = [\triangle ACM] }. $$

Barycentric Coordinates

Relative to triangle $ABC$, the centroid has barycentric coordinates:

$$ \boxed{ G=(1:1:1) }. $$

This expresses equal weighting of the three vertices.

Affine Nature of the Centroid

The centroid behaves naturally under affine transformations. If an affine map sends the vertices of a triangle to new vertices, it sends the original centroid to the new centroid.

$$ \boxed{ T(G) = \text{centroid of } T(A),T(B),T(C) }. $$

Translation

If every vertex is translated by vector $\mathbf v$, then the centroid is translated by the same vector.

$$ \boxed{ G\mapsto G+\mathbf v }. $$

Scaling

Scaling all position vectors by $k$ scales the centroid by $k$ relative to the same origin.

Centroid of a Finite Point Set

For points:

$$ P_1,\ldots,P_n $$

with position vectors $\mathbf p_1,\ldots,\mathbf p_n$, their equal-weight centroid is:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{\bar p} = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf p_i }. $$

This generalizes the triangle coordinate-average formula.

Centroid of a Polygon

The centroid of a polygonal region is the geometric center of its area, not simply the average of its vertices in general.

$$ \boxed{ \text{polygon area centroid} \ne \text{vertex average in general} }. $$

The triangle is a special case in which the two agree.

Polygon Centroid Formula

For a simple polygon with vertices $(x_i,y_i)$ listed cyclically and $(x_{n+1},y_{n+1})=(x_1,y_1)$, define the signed area:

$$ A= \frac12 \sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i y_{i+1}-x_{i+1}y_i). $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac1{6A} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i+x_{i+1}) (x_i y_{i+1}-x_{i+1}y_i) } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{y} = \frac1{6A} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i+y_{i+1}) (x_i y_{i+1}-x_{i+1}y_i) }. $$

Centroid of a Planar Region

For a region $R$ of area $A$ and uniform density:

$$ \boxed{ A= \iint_R dA }. $$

The centroid coordinates are:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac1A \iint_R x\,dA } $$
$$ \boxed{ \bar{y} = \frac1A \iint_R y\,dA }. $$

Region between Two Curves

Suppose:

$$ R= \{(x,y): a\le x\le b,\, g(x)\le y\le f(x)\}. $$

Then:

$$ A= \int_a^b [f(x)-g(x)]\,dx. $$

The centroid's $x$-coordinate is:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{ \int_a^b x[f(x)-g(x)]\,dx }{ A } }. $$

The $y$-coordinate is:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{y} = \frac{ \frac12 \int_a^b [f(x)^2-g(x)^2]\,dx }{ A } }. $$

Centroid of a Line Segment

For a uniform line segment with endpoints $A$ and $B$, the centroid is the midpoint:

$$ \boxed{ G= \frac{A+B}{2} }. $$

Centroid of a Rectangle

The centroid of a rectangle lies at the intersection of its diagonals.

For:

$$ [a,b]\times[c,d], $$

the centroid is:

$$ \boxed{ \left( \frac{a+b}{2}, \frac{c+d}{2} \right) }. $$

Centroid of a Circle

A uniform circular disk has its centroid at the circle's center.

Centroid of a Semicircular Area

For a semicircular region of radius $R$ whose diameter lies on the $x$-axis and whose curved portion lies above it, symmetry gives:

$$ \bar{x}=0. $$

Its centroid lies a distance:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{y} = \frac{4R}{3\pi} } $$

above the diameter.

Centroid of a Solid

For a three-dimensional region $V$ of uniform density and volume $\operatorname{Vol}(V)$:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{ \iiint_V x\,dV }{ \iiint_V dV } } $$
$$ \boxed{ \bar{y} = \frac{ \iiint_V y\,dV }{ \iiint_V dV } } $$
$$ \boxed{ \bar{z} = \frac{ \iiint_V z\,dV }{ \iiint_V dV } }. $$

Centroid of a Uniform Solid

The centroid of a uniform solid is its center of mass.

Composite Regions

A complex region can often be decomposed into simpler pieces whose centroids are known.

If areas $A_i$ have centroids $(x_i,y_i)$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{\sum A_i x_i}{\sum A_i} } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{y} = \frac{\sum A_i y_i}{\sum A_i} }. $$

Holes as Negative Area

In composite-area calculations, a removed region may be represented using negative area:

$$ \boxed{ A_{\text{hole}}<0 }. $$

This permits the same weighted-average formulas to handle cutouts.

Symmetry

If a uniform region is symmetric about a line, its centroid lies on that line.

$$ \boxed{ \text{axis of symmetry} \Rightarrow G \text{ lies on that axis} }. $$

Two Symmetry Axes

If a region has two intersecting symmetry axes, their intersection determines the centroid.

Point Symmetry

If a bounded uniform region has central symmetry about a point $C$, then its centroid is $C$.

Centroid versus Center of Mass

The centroid depends on geometry alone. The center of mass depends on how mass is distributed.

$$ \boxed{ \text{uniform density} \Rightarrow \text{centroid} = \text{center of mass} }. $$

For nonuniform density, the two can differ.

Centroid versus Circumcenter

In a triangle, the centroid is the intersection of medians, while the circumcenter is the intersection of perpendicular bisectors.

They generally do not coincide.

Centroid versus Incenter

The centroid is based on side midpoints. The incenter is based on angle bisectors and equal distance to sides.

Centroid versus Orthocenter

The centroid is formed by medians; the orthocenter is formed by altitudes.

Euler Line

In a non-equilateral triangle, the centroid lies on the Euler line with the circumcenter and orthocenter.

$$ \boxed{ O,\ G,\ H \text{ are collinear} }. $$

Euler-Line Ratio

$$ \boxed{ OG:GH=1:2 }. $$

Equilateral Triangle

In an equilateral triangle:

$$ \boxed{ G=O=I=H }. $$

Thus the centroid coincides with the circumcenter, incenter, and orthocenter.

Centroid and Optimization

For finitely many points with equal weights, the centroid minimizes the sum of squared Euclidean distances:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_i \|\mathbf x-\mathbf p_i\|^2 } $$

over $\mathbf x$.

This connects the geometric centroid with least-squares ideas.

Centroid Need Not Lie Inside an Arbitrary Shape

Although a triangle's centroid always lies inside the triangle, the centroid of a nonconvex region can lie outside the material region itself.

Convex Regions

For a bounded convex body of positive uniform measure, its centroid lies within the body.

Common Misconception: The Centroid Is Always the Center of a Circumscribed Circle

That role belongs to the circumcenter, not the centroid.

Common Misconception: The Centroid Is the Intersection of Angle Bisectors

The angle bisectors meet at the incenter. The medians meet at the centroid.

Common Misconception: The Centroid Bisects Each Median

The centroid does not divide a median into two equal lengths. It divides it in a $2:1$ ratio.

Common Misconception: Averaging Polygon Vertices Always Gives the Area Centroid

This works for triangles and certain highly symmetric polygons, but not for an arbitrary polygonal region.

Common Misconception: Centroid and Center of Mass Are Always Identical

They coincide only when the relevant density distribution is uniform or otherwise arranged so that the two happen to agree.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$G$ "The centroid."
$G=\left(\frac{x_1+x_2+x_3}{3},\frac{y_1+y_2+y_3}{3}\right)$ "The centroid coordinates are the averages of the corresponding vertex coordinates."
$AG:GM=2:1$ "The centroid divides the median in a two-to-one ratio from the vertex."
$\bar{x}=\frac1A\iint_R x\,dA$ "x bar is the area-average x-coordinate of the region."
$\bar{y}=\frac1A\iint_R y\,dA$ "y bar is the area-average y-coordinate of the region."
$G=(1:1:1)$ "The centroid has equal barycentric weights at the three vertices."

The word centroid is used in several dimensions and contexts. For a finite set of equally weighted points it means their coordinate average; for a planar region it means the area centroid; for a solid it means the volume centroid.

Defense Notation Advice: State what object is being averaged. Averaging triangle vertices gives the triangle centroid, but averaging the vertices of an arbitrary polygon does not generally give the centroid of the polygonal area. Distinguish geometric centroid formulas from center-of-mass formulas when density varies.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: A centroid is a geometric average position.
  • Triangle Centroid: Intersection of the three medians.
  • Common Symbol: $G$.
  • Median Ratio: $$ \boxed{ AG:GM=2:1 }. $$
  • Triangle Coordinate Formula: $$ \boxed{ G= \left( \frac{x_1+x_2+x_3}{3}, \frac{y_1+y_2+y_3}{3} \right) }. $$
  • Vector Formula: $$ \boxed{ \mathbf g= \frac{\mathbf a+\mathbf b+\mathbf c}{3} }. $$
  • Triangle Location: The centroid always lies inside a nondegenerate triangle.
  • Uniform Triangle: The centroid is the center of mass.
  • Median Area Property: Each median divides a triangle into two equal-area triangles.
  • Three-Median Area Property: The medians divide a triangle into six equal-area triangles.
  • Barycentric Coordinates: $$ \boxed{ G=(1:1:1) }. $$
  • Finite Point Set: $$ \boxed{ \bar{\mathbf p} = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf p_i }. $$
  • Planar Region: $$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac1A\iint_R x\,dA, \qquad \bar{y} = \frac1A\iint_R y\,dA }. $$
  • Solid Region: Centroid coordinates are volume-weighted averages obtained by triple integration.
  • Composite Area: $$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{\sum A_i x_i}{\sum A_i}, \qquad \bar{y} = \frac{\sum A_i y_i}{\sum A_i} }. $$
  • Symmetry: A centroid lies on every symmetry axis of a uniform region.
  • Affine Geometry: Affine transformations preserve centroid relationships.
  • Center of Mass Contrast: Centroid is geometric; center of mass incorporates density.
  • Circumcenter Contrast: Circumcenter is equidistant from triangle vertices.
  • Incenter Contrast: Incenter is equidistant from triangle sides.
  • Orthocenter Contrast: Orthocenter is the intersection of triangle altitudes.
  • Euler Line: The centroid lies on the Euler line with the circumcenter and orthocenter.
  • Equilateral Triangle: $$ \boxed{ G=O=I=H }. $$
  • Optimization: The equal-weight centroid minimizes the sum of squared distances to a finite set of points.
  • Common Error: Do not say the centroid bisects a median; it divides it in a $2:1$ ratio.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse medians with angle bisectors, perpendicular bisectors, or altitudes.
  • Common Error: Do not assume the average of arbitrary polygon vertices is the area centroid.
  • Common Error: Do not assume centroid and center of mass coincide under nonuniform density.
  • Terminological Contrast: Centroid is the geometric average position. Median joins a triangle vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side. Center of mass is the mass-weighted average position. Circumcenter is the center of the circumcircle. Incenter is the center of the incircle. Orthocenter is the intersection of the altitudes. Barycentric coordinates express points as weighted combinations of triangle vertices.
