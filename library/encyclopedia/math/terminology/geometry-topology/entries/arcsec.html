arcsec

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

arcsec, read arc secant, is the inverse function associated with the secant function after secant has been restricted to a domain on which it is one-to-one.

It answers the question:

$$ \boxed{ \text{What principal angle has secant }x? }. $$

Under a common principal-value convention:

$$ \boxed{ \theta=\operatorname{arcsec}(x) \iff \sec\theta=x, \qquad 0\le\theta\le\pi, \quad \theta\ne\frac{\pi}{2} }. $$

Another notation sometimes used is:

$$ \boxed{ \sec^{-1}(x) }. $$

Here the superscript $-1$ means inverse function, not reciprocal. The reciprocal of secant is cosine:

$$ \boxed{ \frac1{\sec x}=\cos x }. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{arcsec}x \ne \cos x }. $$

Since:

$$ \sec\theta=\frac1{\cos\theta}, $$

inverse secant is closely connected to inverse cosine:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{arcsec}(x) = \arccos\left(\frac1x\right) } $$

for real $x$ in the domain under the standard principal-value convention.

Operational Nuance

Secant

Secant is the reciprocal of cosine:

$$ \boxed{ \sec\theta = \frac1{\cos\theta} }. $$

Since:

$$ -1\le\cos\theta\le1, $$

every real secant value satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ |\sec\theta|\ge1 }. $$

Real Domain

Consequently, the real domain of arcsec is:

$$ \boxed{ (-\infty,-1]\cup[1,\infty) }. $$

Inputs strictly between $-1$ and $1$ have no real inverse-secant value.

Principal Range

A common principal range is:

$$ \boxed{ [0,\pi] \setminus \left\{\frac{\pi}{2}\right\} }. $$

Equivalently:

$$ \boxed{ \left[0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right) \cup \left(\frac{\pi}{2},\pi\right] }. $$

In degrees:

$$ \boxed{ [0^\circ,90^\circ) \cup (90^\circ,180^\circ] }. $$

The angle $\pi/2$ is excluded because:

$$ \cos\frac{\pi}{2}=0, $$

so:

$$ \sec\frac{\pi}{2} $$

is undefined.

Function Mapping

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{arcsec}: (-\infty,-1]\cup[1,\infty) \to [0,\pi]\setminus \left\{\frac{\pi}{2}\right\} }. $$

Branch Convention Caution

Principal-value conventions for inverse secant are not completely uniform across all textbooks and software systems. The range above is common because it makes:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{arcsec}x = \arccos\left(\frac1x\right) } $$

valid directly throughout the real domain.

Important Values

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{arcsec}(1)=0 }. $$
$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{arcsec}(-1)=\pi }. $$
$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{arcsec}(2) = \frac{\pi}{3} }. $$
$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{arcsec}(-2) = \frac{2\pi}{3} }. $$
$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{arcsec}(\sqrt2) = \frac{\pi}{4} }. $$

Relationship with arccos

If:

$$ \sec\theta=x, $$

then:

$$ \cos\theta=\frac1x. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{arcsec}x = \arccos\left(\frac1x\right) }. $$

Calculator Evaluation

Many calculators do not provide a dedicated arcsec key. Instead use:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{arcsec}x = \arccos\left(\frac1x\right) }. $$

For example:

$$ \operatorname{arcsec}(4) = \arccos\left(\frac14\right). $$

Right-Triangle Interpretation

For an acute angle in a right triangle:

$$ \boxed{ \sec\theta = \frac{\text{hypotenuse}} {\text{adjacent}} }. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \theta = \operatorname{arcsec} \left( \frac{\text{hypotenuse}} {\text{adjacent}} \right) }. $$

Inverse Composition

For every real $x$ in the domain:

$$ \boxed{ \sec(\operatorname{arcsec}x)=x }. $$

Reverse Composition

The reverse relation:

$$ \operatorname{arcsec}(\sec\theta)=\theta $$

holds directly only when $\theta$ lies in the chosen principal range.

Outside that range, arcsec returns the principal angle having the same secant value.

Symmetry

Under the standard range convention:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{arcsec}(-x) = \pi-\operatorname{arcsec}(x) } $$

for $|x|\ge1$.

Derivative

For:

$$ |x|>1, $$

the standard real derivative is:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx} \operatorname{arcsec}x = \frac1{|x|\sqrt{x^2-1}} }. $$

Why the Absolute Value Appears

Begin with:

$$ y= \arccos\left(\frac1x\right). $$

Differentiating:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{-1/x^2} {\sqrt{1-1/x^2}}. $$

Since:

$$ \sqrt{1-\frac1{x^2}} = \frac{\sqrt{x^2-1}}{|x|}, $$

we obtain:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac1{|x|\sqrt{x^2-1}} }. $$

Chain Rule

For differentiable $u(x)$ satisfying $|u(x)|>1$:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx} \operatorname{arcsec}(u) = \frac{u'} {|u|\sqrt{u^2-1}} }. $$

Graph

The graph of:

$$ y=\operatorname{arcsec}x $$

consists of two branches because its real domain is:

$$ x\le-1 \qquad\text{or}\qquad x\ge1. $$

It passes through:

$$ \boxed{ (1,0) } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ (-1,\pi) }. $$

Behavior at Positive Infinity

Since:

$$ \frac1x\to0^+ $$

as $x\to\infty$:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to\infty} \operatorname{arcsec}x = \frac{\pi}{2}^{-} }. $$

Behavior at Negative Infinity

Since:

$$ \frac1x\to0^- $$

as $x\to-\infty$:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to-\infty} \operatorname{arcsec}x = \frac{\pi}{2}^{+} }. $$

Thus the graph approaches $y=\pi/2$ from opposite sides but never reaches it.

Solving Secant Equations

Suppose:

$$ \sec\theta=c. $$

A real solution can exist only when:

$$ \boxed{ |c|\ge1 }. $$

Rewrite:

$$ \boxed{ \cos\theta=\frac1c }. $$

A principal solution is:

$$ \alpha = \operatorname{arcsec}(c) = \arccos\left(\frac1c\right). $$

Because cosine and secant are periodic, this principal value does not by itself represent every solution.

Complete Secant Equation

If:

$$ \alpha=\operatorname{arcsec}(c), $$

then the real solutions may be expressed as:

$$ \boxed{ \theta = 2\pi k\pm\alpha, \qquad k\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Inverse vs. Reciprocal

The most important notation distinction is:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{arcsec}x \ne \frac1{\sec x} }. $$

Instead:

$$ \boxed{ \frac1{\sec x} = \cos x }. $$

Common Misconception: $\sec^{-1}x$ can denote inverse secant when used as a function name, while $(\sec x)^{-1}$ means the reciprocal of a secant value. Writing $\operatorname{arcsec}$ avoids this ambiguity.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\operatorname{arcsec}x$ "Arc secant of x."
$\sec^{-1}x$ "Inverse secant of x."
$\operatorname{arcsec}(2)=\frac{\pi}{3}$ "The arc secant of two is pi over three."
$\operatorname{arcsec}x=\arccos(1/x)$ "Arc secant x equals arc cosine of one over x."
$\sec(\operatorname{arcsec}x)=x$ "Secant of arc secant x equals x."
$\frac{d}{dx}\operatorname{arcsec}x$ "The derivative of arc secant x."

The notation:

$$ \sec^{-1}x $$

should be interpreted from context.

As inverse-function notation:

$$ \boxed{ \sec^{-1}(x) = \operatorname{arcsec}(x) }. $$

But as an ordinary reciprocal:

$$ \boxed{ (\sec x)^{-1} = \cos x }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Prefer $\operatorname{arcsec}$ when inverse and reciprocal notation might be confused, and state the principal range whenever branch conventions are important.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: Arcsec returns a principal angle whose secant equals the given input.
  • Common Notation: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{arcsec}x }. $$
  • Alternative Notation: $$ \boxed{ \sec^{-1}x }. $$
  • Real Domain: $$ \boxed{ (-\infty,-1]\cup[1,\infty) }. $$
  • Common Principal Range: $$ \boxed{ [0,\pi]\setminus \left\{\frac{\pi}{2}\right\} }. $$
  • Equivalent Range: $$ \boxed{ \left[0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right) \cup \left(\frac{\pi}{2},\pi\right] }. $$
  • 90 Degrees Excluded: $\pi/2$ is not in the range because secant is undefined there.
  • Branch Caution: Some sources adopt a different principal range for inverse secant.
  • arccos Identity: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{arcsec}x = \arccos\left(\frac1x\right) }. $$
  • Positive Endpoint: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{arcsec}(1)=0 }. $$
  • Negative Endpoint: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{arcsec}(-1)=\pi }. $$
  • Special Value: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{arcsec}(2)=\frac{\pi}{3} }. $$
  • Special Negative Value: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{arcsec}(-2)=\frac{2\pi}{3} }. $$
  • Special Value: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{arcsec}(\sqrt2)=\frac{\pi}{4} }. $$
  • Inverse Composition: $$ \boxed{ \sec(\operatorname{arcsec}x)=x }. $$
  • Restricted Reverse Composition: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{arcsec}(\sec\theta)=\theta } $$ when $\theta$ lies in the chosen principal range.
  • Negative-Argument Identity: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{arcsec}(-x) = \pi-\operatorname{arcsec}(x) }. $$
  • Derivative: $$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx} \operatorname{arcsec}x = \frac1{|x|\sqrt{x^2-1}} }. $$
  • Derivative Domain: $$ \boxed{ |x|>1 }. $$
  • Absolute-Value Requirement: The standard real derivative contains $|x|$, not simply $x$.
  • Chain Rule: $$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx} \operatorname{arcsec}(u) = \frac{u'} {|u|\sqrt{u^2-1}} }. $$
  • Right-Triangle Interpretation: $$ \boxed{ \theta = \operatorname{arcsec} \left( \frac{\text{hypotenuse}} {\text{adjacent}} \right) }. $$
  • Calculator Workaround: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{arcsec}x = \arccos(1/x) }. $$
  • Secant Equation: $$ \sec\theta=c $$ is equivalent to: $$ \boxed{ \cos\theta=\frac1c }. $$
  • Real-Solution Condition: $$ \boxed{ |c|\ge1 }. $$
  • Principal Solution: $$ \boxed{ \alpha=\operatorname{arcsec}c }. $$
  • General Secant Solution: $$ \boxed{ \theta = 2\pi k\pm\operatorname{arcsec}c, \qquad k\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$
  • Graph Domain: The graph exists only for: $$ x\le-1 \qquad\text{or}\qquad x\ge1. $$
  • Positive-Infinity Limit: $$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to\infty} \operatorname{arcsec}x = \frac{\pi}{2}^{-} }. $$
  • Negative-Infinity Limit: $$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to-\infty} \operatorname{arcsec}x = \frac{\pi}{2}^{+} }. $$
  • Horizontal Asymptotic Level: The graph approaches: $$ \boxed{ y=\frac{\pi}{2} } $$ but does not contain that value.
  • Calculator Mode: Numerical output may be returned in radians or degrees depending on the selected angle mode.
  • Calculus Convention: Standard derivative formulas assume radian measure.
  • Reciprocal of Secant: $$ \boxed{ \frac1{\sec x}=\cos x }. $$
  • Inverse vs. Reciprocal: Arcsec reverses secant on a selected branch; cosine reciprocates secant.
  • Complex Extension: In complex analysis, inverse secant can be extended beyond the real domain using complex inverse-trigonometric formulas together with logarithmic and square-root branch choices.
  • Terminological Contrast: Secant maps an angle to $1/\cos\theta$. Arcsec maps a permissible secant value back to a principal angle. Cosine is the reciprocal of secant, not its inverse function.
