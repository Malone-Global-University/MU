Altitude of a Trapezoid

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

The altitude of a trapezoid is the perpendicular distance between the two parallel sides of the trapezoid. Those parallel sides are conventionally called the bases.

If $AB$ and $CD$ are the parallel bases and a perpendicular segment $DH$ joins their supporting lines, then:

$$ \boxed{ AB\parallel CD \qquad\text{and}\qquad DH\perp AB }. $$

Because $AB$ and $CD$ are parallel, the same segment is also perpendicular to $CD$. Its length is the altitude:

$$ \boxed{ h=DH }. $$

The word altitude emphasizes perpendicular height. In elementary geometry, height and altitude are commonly used interchangeably in this context.

$$ \boxed{ \text{altitude} = \text{perpendicular distance between the parallel bases} }. $$

The altitude is therefore determined by the spacing between the bases rather than by the lengths or slopes of the trapezoid's nonparallel sides.

Operational Nuance

The Parallel Sides Are the Bases

In the standard trapezoid setup, the parallel sides are called the bases:

$$ \boxed{ b_1\parallel b_2 }. $$

The altitude is measured perpendicular to these bases.

The Nonparallel Sides Are the Legs

The remaining two sides are usually called the legs. A leg may be slanted, vertical, or perpendicular to the bases depending on the type and orientation of the trapezoid.

In general:

$$ \boxed{ \text{leg length}\ne h }. $$

Constant Distance Between Parallel Lines

Since the bases lie on parallel lines, every perpendicular segment connecting those lines has the same length.

$$ \boxed{ h=d(\ell_1,\ell_2) } $$

where $\ell_1$ and $\ell_2$ are the lines containing the bases.

Area Formula

If the parallel bases have lengths $b_1$ and $b_2$ and the altitude is $h$, the area of the trapezoid is:

$$ \boxed{ A=\frac12(b_1+b_2)h }. $$

This can also be written:

$$ \boxed{ A= \left( \frac{b_1+b_2}{2} \right)h }. $$

Thus the area equals the average of the base lengths multiplied by the altitude.

Altitude from Area

Solving the area formula for $h$ gives:

$$ 2A=(b_1+b_2)h, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ h=\frac{2A}{b_1+b_2} }. $$

Example

Suppose a trapezoid has bases:

$$ b_1=8,\qquad b_2=14 $$

and area:

$$ A=66. $$

Then:

$$ h = \frac{2(66)}{8+14} = \frac{132}{22} = \boxed{6}. $$

Right Trapezoid

A right trapezoid has a leg perpendicular to its bases. That leg therefore serves directly as the altitude.

$$ \boxed{ \text{right trapezoid} \Longrightarrow \text{one leg has length }h }. $$

This is a special case. In a general trapezoid, neither leg need equal the altitude.

Isosceles Trapezoid

An isosceles trapezoid has congruent legs. Dropping perpendiculars from the endpoints of the shorter base to the longer base often divides the figure into a central rectangle and two congruent right triangles.

If the bases have lengths $B$ and $b$ with $B>b$, then the horizontal offset on each side is:

$$ \boxed{ x=\frac{B-b}{2} }. $$

Altitude from Leg Length in an Isosceles Trapezoid

If each leg has length $\ell$, then the resulting right triangle gives:

$$ \ell^2=h^2+x^2. $$

Since:

$$ x=\frac{B-b}{2}, $$

the altitude is:

$$ \boxed{ h= \sqrt{ \ell^2- \left(\frac{B-b}{2}\right)^2 } }. $$

Trigonometric Form

If a leg of length $\ell$ forms an acute angle $\theta$ with a base, its perpendicular component is:

$$ \boxed{ h=\ell\sin\theta }. $$

The horizontal component is:

$$ \ell\cos\theta. $$

Midsegment Connection

The midsegment of a trapezoid joins the midpoints of its legs and is parallel to the bases. Its length is:

$$ \boxed{ m=\frac{b_1+b_2}{2} }. $$

Therefore the area formula may be written elegantly as:

$$ \boxed{ A=mh }. $$

This makes the trapezoid area formula resemble the parallelogram formula: an effective base length multiplied by perpendicular height.

Orientation Does Not Matter

A trapezoid may be rotated so that its bases appear vertical or diagonal on the page. Its altitude is still measured perpendicular to the parallel bases.

$$ \boxed{ \text{altitude is geometric, not page-vertical} }. $$

Common Misconception: The height of a trapezoid is not generally a slanted leg. It is specifically the perpendicular separation of the two parallel bases.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$h$ "The height," or "the altitude of the trapezoid."
$b_1\parallel b_2$ "The two bases are parallel."
$DH\perp AB$ "D H is perpendicular to A B."
$A=\frac12(b_1+b_2)h$ "Area equals one-half the sum of the bases times the height."
$h=\frac{2A}{b_1+b_2}$ "Height equals twice the area divided by the sum of the bases."
$m=\frac{b_1+b_2}{2}$ "The midsegment equals the average of the base lengths."

In a trapezoid, the word base has a more specialized meaning than in many other polygons: it conventionally refers to the parallel sides.

The altitude is therefore:

$$ \boxed{ h=\text{perpendicular distance between the bases} }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: When using the trapezoid area formula, identify the two parallel sides as $b_1$ and $b_2$. Then use the segment perpendicular to those bases as $h$. Do not substitute a slanted leg merely because it appears to run from one base to the other.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: The altitude of a trapezoid is the perpendicular distance between its two parallel bases.
  • Common Symbol: $$ \boxed{h}. $$
  • Parallel Bases: $$ \boxed{ b_1\parallel b_2 }. $$
  • Perpendicular Requirement: $$ \boxed{ h\perp b_1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad h\perp b_2 }. $$
  • Constant Separation: The perpendicular distance between the two parallel base lines is constant.
  • Leg Contrast: The legs are the nonparallel sides and are not generally equal to the altitude.
  • Area Formula: $$ \boxed{ A=\frac12(b_1+b_2)h }. $$
  • Average-Base Form: $$ \boxed{ A= \left( \frac{b_1+b_2}{2} \right)h }. $$
  • Altitude from Area: $$ \boxed{ h= \frac{2A}{b_1+b_2} }. $$
  • Base-Sum from Area: $$ \boxed{ b_1+b_2=\frac{2A}{h} }. $$
  • Unknown Base: If $b_1$, $A$, and $h$ are known: $$ \boxed{ b_2=\frac{2A}{h}-b_1 }. $$
  • Midsegment: The segment joining the midpoints of the legs has length: $$ \boxed{ m=\frac{b_1+b_2}{2} }. $$
  • Area Using Midsegment: $$ \boxed{ A=mh }. $$
  • Right Trapezoid: A leg perpendicular to both bases has length equal to the altitude.
  • Right-Trapezoid Special Case: $$ \boxed{ \text{perpendicular leg}=h }. $$
  • Isosceles Trapezoid: The two legs are congruent and perpendicular drops from the shorter base create congruent right triangles.
  • Isosceles Horizontal Offset: If $B>b$: $$ \boxed{ x=\frac{B-b}{2} }. $$
  • Isosceles Altitude from Leg: If each leg has length $\ell$: $$ \boxed{ h= \sqrt{ \ell^2- \left(\frac{B-b}{2}\right)^2 } }. $$
  • Trigonometric Altitude: If a leg of length $\ell$ makes angle $\theta$ with a base: $$ \boxed{ h=\ell\sin\theta }. $$
  • Horizontal Projection: The same leg contributes horizontal displacement: $$ \boxed{ x=\ell\cos\theta }. $$
  • Pythagorean Relation: For a right triangle formed by a leg, altitude, and horizontal offset: $$ \boxed{ \ell^2=h^2+x^2 }. $$
  • Rectangle Connection: A rectangle may be viewed, under an inclusive definition of trapezoid, as a special trapezoid with two pairs of parallel sides. Its familiar area formula $A=bh$ agrees with the trapezoid formula when the two selected bases have equal length.
  • Convention Caution: Definitions of trapezoid vary. Some sources require at least one pair of parallel sides, while others require exactly one pair. The definition of altitude relative to the designated parallel bases remains the perpendicular distance between them.
  • Coordinate Geometry: If the bases lie on horizontal lines: $$ y=c_1 $$ and: $$ y=c_2, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ h=|c_2-c_1| }. $$
  • Vertical Bases: If the parallel bases lie on: $$ x=c_1 $$ and: $$ x=c_2, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ h=|c_2-c_1| }. $$
  • General Parallel Lines: If the base lines are: $$ Ax+By+C_1=0 $$ and: $$ Ax+By+C_2=0, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ h= \frac{|C_2-C_1|} {\sqrt{A^2+B^2}} }. $$
  • Point-to-Line Method: If one base lies on: $$ Ax+By+C=0 $$ and $(x_0,y_0)$ lies on the other base, then: $$ \boxed{ h= \frac{|Ax_0+By_0+C|} {\sqrt{A^2+B^2}} }. $$
  • Vector Geometry: The altitude direction is perpendicular to a direction vector of either base.
  • Scaling: Under a dilation of scale factor $k$: $$ \boxed{ h\mapsto kh }. $$
  • Area Scaling: Under the same dilation: $$ \boxed{ A\mapsto k^2A }. $$
  • Similar Trapezoids: Corresponding altitudes and base lengths have the same linear scale factor.
  • Area Ratio: If similar trapezoids have scale factor $k$: $$ \boxed{ \frac{A_2}{A_1}=k^2 }. $$
  • Units: Altitude is measured in linear units such as centimeters, meters, or feet.
  • Area Units: The quantity: $$ \frac12(b_1+b_2)h $$ is measured in square units.
  • Orientation Independence: Rotating the trapezoid does not change its altitude. The height remains perpendicular to its parallel bases.
  • Diagram Caution: A vertical-looking segment is not necessarily the altitude unless it is perpendicular to the parallel bases.
  • Terminological Contrast: The bases are the parallel sides of the trapezoid. The legs are the remaining sides. The altitude is the perpendicular distance between the bases. In a right trapezoid one leg may equal the altitude, but in a general trapezoid the leg lengths and altitude are distinct measurements.
