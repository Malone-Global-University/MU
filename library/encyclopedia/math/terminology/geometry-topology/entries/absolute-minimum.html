Absolute Minimum

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

An absolute minimum of a function is its least attained value over the entire domain or interval being considered. If $f$ has an absolute minimum at $x=c$, then:

$$ \boxed{ f(c)\le f(x) \text{ for every }x\text{ in the domain under consideration} }. $$

The adjective absolute emphasizes that every relevant function value is being compared, not merely values near $c$. For this reason, global minimum is a standard synonym.

The term minimum refers to the least member of a collection of values. In function language, it is useful to distinguish between the minimum value and the input at which that value is attained.

If:

$$ f(c)=m $$

and:

$$ f(x)\ge m $$

for every relevant $x$, then $m$ is the absolute minimum value and $x=c$ is a point where the absolute minimum is attained.

Operational Nuance

Formal Definition

Let $f$ be defined on a set $D$. The function has an absolute minimum at $c\in D$ if:

$$ \boxed{ f(c)\le f(x) \qquad \text{for every }x\in D }. $$

The number:

$$ f(c) $$

is the absolute minimum value of $f$ on $D$.

Minimum Value vs. Minimum Point

Suppose:

$$ f(2)=-5 $$

and no other function value is smaller than $-5$. Then:

$$ \boxed{ -5=\text{absolute minimum value} } $$

while:

$$ \boxed{ x=2=\text{a point where the absolute minimum occurs} }. $$

Absolute vs. Local Minimum

A local minimum only requires a function value to be no greater than nearby values. An absolute minimum must be no greater than every value in the entire specified domain.

A local minimum at $x=c$ satisfies:

$$ f(c)\le f(x) $$

for all $x$ sufficiently close to $c$.

An absolute minimum satisfies:

$$ f(c)\le f(x) $$

for every relevant $x$.

Example

Consider:

$$ f(x)=x^2-4 $$

on:

$$ [-3,3]. $$

Since:

$$ f(0)=-4 $$

and:

$$ x^2-4\ge-4 $$

for every $x$, the function has absolute minimum value:

$$ \boxed{-4} $$

attained at:

$$ \boxed{x=0}. $$

More Than One Minimum Point

A function may attain the same absolute minimum value at several points.

For example, on:

$$ [0,2\pi], $$

the function:

$$ f(x)=\sin^2x $$

has absolute minimum value:

$$ 0 $$

at:

$$ x=0,\ \pi,\ 2\pi. $$

Critical Points

If an absolute minimum occurs at an interior point where the function is differentiable, then that point is a local minimum and therefore satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ f'(c)=0 }. $$

Such points are important candidates for absolute minima, but a critical point need not actually be a minimum.

Endpoints Matter

On a closed interval:

$$ [a,b], $$

the smallest value may occur at either endpoint. Therefore both endpoints must be compared with values at relevant critical points.

Closed Interval Method

For a continuous function on $[a,b]$:

  1. Find critical numbers in $(a,b)$.
  2. Evaluate $f$ at each critical number.
  3. Evaluate $f(a)$ and $f(b)$.
  4. Compare all resulting values.
  5. The smallest value is the absolute minimum.

Common Misconception: Solving:

$$ f'(x)=0 $$

does not automatically identify the absolute minimum. It only produces some of the possible candidates.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$f(c)\le f(x)$ "F of c is less than or equal to f of x."
$\min_{x\in D}f(x)$ "The minimum of f over x in D."
$\min_{a\le x\le b}f(x)$ "The minimum of f on the interval from a to b."
$f'(c)=0$ "F prime of c equals zero."
$x=c$ "The minimum is attained at x equals c."
$f(c)=m$ "The absolute minimum value is m."

The notation:

$$ \min_{x\in D}f(x)=m $$

asserts that the value $m$ is actually attained by the function somewhere in $D$.

This differs from writing:

$$ \inf_{x\in D}f(x)=m, $$

which only states that $m$ is the greatest lower bound of the function values. The infimum need not be attained.

Defense Notation Advice: When a problem asks for the absolute minimum, it may expect the minimum value, the point where it occurs, or both. A complete response often states:

$$ \boxed{ \text{Absolute minimum value }m \text{ occurs at }x=c }. $$

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Formal Definition: $$ \boxed{ f(c)\le f(x) \text{ for every }x\in D }. $$
  • Global Minimum: Global minimum is a standard synonym for absolute minimum.
  • Minimum Value: If: $$ m=f(c), $$ then $m$ is the minimum value.
  • Point of Attainment: The input $c$ is a point at which the minimum is attained.
  • Multiple Minimizers: More than one input may attain the same absolute minimum value.
  • Absolute vs. Local: An interior absolute minimum is also a local minimum under the usual neighborhood definition, but a local minimum need not be absolute.
  • Interior Differentiable Minimum: If $f$ is differentiable and has a local minimum at an interior point $c$, then: $$ \boxed{ f'(c)=0 }. $$
  • Nondifferentiable Minimum: An absolute minimum can occur at a point where: $$ f'(c) $$ does not exist. Corners and cusps must therefore be included among possible extrema.
  • Critical Number: A critical number is generally a domain value where: $$ f'(c)=0 $$ or: $$ f'(c) $$ does not exist.
  • Critical Point Caution: A critical point can be a local minimum, local maximum, or neither.
  • Endpoint Minimum: On a restricted interval, an endpoint may provide the smallest function value.
  • Extreme Value Theorem: If $f$ is continuous on a closed bounded interval: $$ [a,b], $$ then $f$ attains both an absolute minimum and an absolute maximum.
  • Compact Generalization: More generally, a continuous real-valued function on a compact set attains its absolute minimum and maximum.
  • Open Interval Failure: On: $$ (0,1), $$ the function: $$ f(x)=x $$ has no absolute minimum. Its values approach $0$, but $0$ is never attained.
  • Infimum vs. Minimum: A minimum must belong to the set of attained values. An infimum need not.
  • Example: For: $$ S=(0,1), $$ one has: $$ \inf S=0, $$ but: $$ \min S $$ does not exist.
  • Constant Function: If: $$ f(x)=c $$ everywhere on its domain, then every point is both an absolute minimum point and an absolute maximum point.
  • Quadratic Functions: For: $$ f(x)=ax^2+bx+c $$ with: $$ a>0, $$ the parabola opens upward and its vertex gives the absolute minimum over $\mathbb{R}$.
  • Downward-Opening Quadratic: If: $$ a<0, $$ then over all real numbers: $$ f(x)\to-\infty $$ as: $$ |x|\to\infty, $$ so no absolute minimum exists.
  • First Derivative Test: If: $$ f' $$ changes from negative to positive at $c$, then $c$ is a local minimum. Additional comparison is required to determine whether it is absolute.
  • Second Derivative Test: If: $$ f'(c)=0 $$ and: $$ f''(c)>0, $$ then $c$ is a local minimum. This alone does not prove global minimality.
  • Convexity: For a convex function, any local minimum is global. Under strict convexity, the global minimizer is unique when it exists.
  • Optimization: Absolute minima appear in problems involving least cost, shortest distance, minimum surface area, minimum energy, minimum error, and other smallest attainable quantities.
  • Constraints: In constrained optimization, the absolute minimum is taken only over the feasible set specified by the constraints.
  • Multivariable Functions: For: $$ f:D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}, $$ an absolute minimum at $\mathbf{c}$ satisfies: $$ \boxed{ f(\mathbf{c})\le f(\mathbf{x}) \text{ for every }\mathbf{x}\in D }. $$
  • Boundary Analysis: In several variables, an absolute minimum may occur at an interior critical point or along the boundary of the domain.
  • Lagrange Multipliers: Under suitable smooth equality constraints, Lagrange multipliers identify candidates for constrained extrema. Candidate values must still be compared to establish the absolute minimum.
  • Distance Problems: Because distances are nonnegative, nearest-point problems are natural absolute minimum problems.
  • Squared Distance: Minimizing: $$ d $$ is often equivalent to minimizing: $$ d^2, $$ which can simplify derivatives by avoiding square roots.
  • Discrete Sets: Calculus is not required for absolute minima. On a finite set, simply compare all function values.
  • Sequence Minimum: A sequence $\{a_n\}$ has an absolute minimum if some term $a_k$ satisfies: $$ a_k\le a_n $$ for every $n$.
  • Range Interpretation: The absolute minimum of a function, when it exists, is the smallest element of the range over the specified domain.
  • Domain Dependence: The same function may have an absolute minimum on one domain but not on another. For example: $$ f(x)=x $$ has a minimum on: $$ [0,1] $$ but not on: $$ (0,1). $$

  • Terminological Contrast: An absolute minimum is the least attained value over the entire specified domain. A local minimum is least only relative to nearby values. An infimum is the greatest lower bound and need not be attained, while a minimum must actually occur.
