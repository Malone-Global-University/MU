Cone Angle

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

The cone angle is most commonly the full angle between opposite generators of a right circular cone when the cone is viewed in a longitudinal cross-section through its axis.

$$ \boxed{ \theta = \text{full included angle at the apex} }. $$

Such a cross-section produces an isosceles triangle. The angle at the apex of that triangle is the cone angle.

If $\alpha$ is the angle between the cone's axis and one generator, then $\alpha$ is the semi-vertical angle, half-angle, or cone semi-angle, and:

$$ \boxed{ \theta=2\alpha }. $$

Mathematical and technical sources are not perfectly uniform in their informal use of cone angle. Some contexts use the phrase for the full included angle, while others quote the half-angle from the axis. For precision, the quantity should therefore be identified explicitly.

A third angle also appears when a cone is developed into a flat sector. That sector angle belongs to the cone's net and should not automatically be confused with the three-dimensional apex angle.

Operational Nuance

Axial Cross-Section

Passing a plane through the axis of a right circular cone produces an isosceles triangle whose:

  • half-base is the cone radius $r$,
  • altitude is the cone height $h$,
  • equal sides have length equal to the slant height $\ell$, and
  • apex angle is the full cone angle $\theta$.

Bisecting this triangle along the cone's axis produces a right triangle with apex angle $\alpha=\theta/2$.

Full Cone Angle and Half-Angle

$$ \boxed{ \alpha=\frac{\theta}{2} } $$

and therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \theta=2\alpha }. $$

Radius and Height Formula

From the right triangle in an axial cross-section:

$$ \tan\alpha=\frac{r}{h}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \alpha = \arctan\left(\frac{r}{h}\right) } $$

and the full cone angle is:

$$ \boxed{ \theta = 2\arctan\left(\frac{r}{h}\right) }. $$

Using Diameter and Height

Since:

$$ D=2r, $$

we may write:

$$ \tan\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) = \frac{D}{2h}. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ \theta = 2\arctan\left(\frac{D}{2h}\right) }. $$

Using Radius and Slant Height

Since:

$$ \sin\alpha=\frac{r}{\ell}, $$

the half-angle is:

$$ \boxed{ \alpha = \arcsin\left(\frac{r}{\ell}\right) }. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \theta = 2\arcsin\left(\frac{r}{\ell}\right) }. $$

Using Height and Slant Height

Similarly:

$$ \cos\alpha=\frac{h}{\ell}. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ \theta = 2\arccos\left(\frac{h}{\ell}\right) }. $$

Slant Height Relation

The cone dimensions satisfy:

$$ \boxed{ \ell^2=r^2+h^2 }. $$

Consequently, any two of $r$, $h$, and $\ell$ determine the third and therefore determine the cone angle.

Example

Suppose a right circular cone has:

$$ r=3, \qquad h=4. $$

Then:

$$ \alpha = \arctan\left(\frac34\right) \approx36.87^\circ. $$

The full cone angle is:

$$ \boxed{ \theta \approx 73.74^\circ }. $$

Narrow Cones

A small half-angle produces a narrow cone.

$$ \boxed{ \alpha\text{ small} \Rightarrow \frac{r}{h}\text{ small} }. $$

The radius grows slowly relative to axial distance.

Wide Cones

A larger half-angle produces a wider cone:

$$ \boxed{ \alpha\text{ larger} \Rightarrow \frac{r}{h}\text{ larger} }. $$

Limiting Behavior

For an ordinary pointed right circular cone:

$$ 0^\circ<\alpha<90^\circ $$

and therefore:

$$ \boxed{ 0^\circ<\theta<180^\circ }. $$

As $\alpha\to0^\circ$, the cone becomes increasingly narrow. As $\alpha\to90^\circ$, the idealized cone becomes increasingly flat.

Angle between Axis and Generator

The semi-vertical angle $\alpha$ is specifically the angle between the axis and a generator:

$$ \boxed{ \alpha = \angle(\text{axis},\text{generator}) }. $$

This is one-half of the included cone angle.

Angle between Two Opposite Generators

In an axial cross-section, the full cone angle is:

$$ \boxed{ \theta = \angle(\text{generator},\text{opposite generator}) }. $$

Opening Angle

The phrase opening angle is often used for the angular width of a cone, but it can be ambiguous. In many settings it means the full angle $\theta$; in others an author may specify a half-opening angle.

The safest notation is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{full opening angle }=\theta, \qquad \text{half-opening angle }=\alpha=\theta/2 }. $$

Semi-Vertical Angle

Semi-vertical angle is especially useful because it explicitly communicates that the angle is measured from the cone axis to one side of the cone.

$$ \boxed{ \text{semi-vertical angle} = \frac12(\text{full apex angle}) }. $$

Apex Angle

The apex angle or vertex angle of the axial isosceles triangle is normally the full included angle:

$$ \boxed{ \theta=2\alpha }. $$

Cone Angle and Taper

In engineering geometry, a cone can also be described by its rate of taper.

For a frustum whose diameters change from $d$ to $D$ over axial length $L$:

$$ \boxed{ C=\frac{D-d}{L} }. $$

If $\theta$ is the full cone angle, then:

$$ \boxed{ C = 2\tan\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) }. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \theta = 2\arctan\left(\frac{C}{2}\right) }. $$

Taper per Side

The radial change per unit axial length is:

$$ \frac{R-r}{L}. $$

This equals:

$$ \boxed{ \tan\alpha }. $$

Since diameter changes twice as fast as radius:

$$ \frac{D-d}{L} = 2\tan\alpha. $$

Frustum Cone Angle

A frustum retains the same cone angle as the complete cone from which it is cut. Removing the apex does not alter the inclination of its generators.

Similarity and Cone Angle

All right circular cones having the same cone angle are similar.

If:

$$ \frac{r_1}{h_1} = \frac{r_2}{h_2}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \theta_1=\theta_2 }. $$

Scaling Does Not Change Cone Angle

If every linear dimension is multiplied by $k>0$:

$$ r\mapsto kr, \qquad h\mapsto kh, $$

then:

$$ \frac{kr}{kh} = \frac{r}{h}. $$

Therefore the cone angle is unchanged.

$$ \boxed{ \text{uniform scaling preserves cone angle} }. $$

Coordinate Equation and Cone Angle

A double right circular cone with axis along the $z$-axis may be written:

$$ x^2+y^2=k^2z^2. $$

At height $|z|$:

$$ r=k|z|. $$

Hence:

$$ \tan\alpha=k. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \alpha=\arctan k, \qquad \theta=2\arctan k }. $$

Equation from a Specified Cone Angle

If a cone has half-angle $\alpha$ and axis along the $z$-axis:

$$ \boxed{ x^2+y^2 = z^2\tan^2\alpha }. $$

In terms of the full cone angle $\theta$:

$$ \boxed{ x^2+y^2 = z^2\tan^2\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) }. $$

Cone Angle and Conic Sections

The inclination of a cutting plane relative to the cone's generators helps determine which conic section is produced.

In particular, a plane parallel to a generator produces a parabola in the nondegenerate classical construction.

The cone's semi-vertical angle therefore provides a natural angular reference for describing plane orientation.

Angle of a Cone Net

When the lateral surface of a right circular cone is cut and developed into a plane, it forms a circular sector. Let $\beta$ denote the central angle of this sector.

The sector radius is the slant height $\ell$, while its arc length equals the base circumference $2\pi r$.

$$ \ell\beta=2\pi r $$

when $\beta$ is measured in radians.

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \beta=\frac{2\pi r}{\ell} }. $$

Sector Angle in Degrees

In degrees:

$$ \boxed{ \beta = 360^\circ\frac{r}{\ell} }. $$

Sector Angle versus Apex Cone Angle

The sector angle $\beta$ of the flattened lateral surface is not generally equal to the three-dimensional apex angle $\theta$.

$$ \boxed{ \beta \ne \theta \quad\text{in general} }. $$

They describe different geometries:

  • $\theta$ measures the opening of the cone in an axial cross-section,
  • $\alpha=\theta/2$ measures one side's inclination from the axis, and
  • $\beta$ measures the central angle of the cone's planar sector net.

Relation between Sector Angle and Semi-Vertical Angle

Since:

$$ \sin\alpha=\frac{r}{\ell}, $$

the net-sector angle satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ \beta=2\pi\sin\alpha } $$

in radians.

Since $\alpha=\theta/2$:

$$ \boxed{ \beta = 2\pi \sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) }. $$

Solid Angle of a Circular Cone

A cone also determines a solid angle, which measures the amount of directional space subtended by the cone rather than an ordinary planar angle.

For a right circular cone with half-angle $\alpha$:

$$ \boxed{ \Omega = 2\pi(1-\cos\alpha) } $$

steradians.

In terms of the full cone angle:

$$ \boxed{ \Omega = 2\pi \left( 1-\cos\frac{\theta}{2} \right) }. $$

Plane Angle versus Solid Angle

The cone angle $\theta$ is an ordinary plane angle measured in radians or degrees. A solid angle $\Omega$ is measured in steradians.

Quantity Meaning Typical Unit
$\theta$ Full included apex angle degrees or radians
$\alpha$ Semi-vertical or half-angle degrees or radians
$\beta$ Central angle of developed sector degrees or radians
$\Omega$ Solid angle subtended by cone steradians

Volume in Terms of Cone Angle

Since:

$$ r=h\tan\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right), $$

the volume:

$$ V=\frac13\pi r^2h $$

can be written:

$$ \boxed{ V = \frac13\pi h^3 \tan^2\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) }. $$

Radius in Terms of Cone Angle

$$ \boxed{ r = h\tan\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) }. $$

Height in Terms of Cone Angle

$$ \boxed{ h = \frac{r}{ \tan(\theta/2) } }. $$

Slant Height in Terms of Half-Angle

Since:

$$ \cos\alpha=\frac{h}{\ell}, $$

we obtain:

$$ \boxed{ \ell=\frac{h}{\cos\alpha} }. $$

Likewise:

$$ \boxed{ \ell=\frac{r}{\sin\alpha} }. $$

Surface Area in Terms of Cone Angle

For a right circular cone:

$$ A_L=\pi r\ell. $$

Using $\ell=r/\sin\alpha$:

$$ \boxed{ A_L = \frac{\pi r^2}{\sin\alpha} } $$

or:

$$ \boxed{ A_L = \frac{\pi r^2}{ \sin(\theta/2) } }. $$

Engineering Drawings

In technical drawing and manufacturing, a cone may be specified by dimensions, taper, or included angle. Because half-angle and full-angle conventions coexist in technical communication, identifying the reference lines of the angle prevents costly ambiguity.

A statement such as:

$$ \boxed{ \text{included cone angle }=60^\circ } $$

unambiguously gives:

$$ \alpha=30^\circ. $$

Optics and Fields

Cone angles also appear in descriptions of beams, viewing regions, fields of view, numerical apertures, radiation patterns, and directional acceptance regions. These fields may use either full angular width or half-angle conventions.

The underlying geometry remains the same, but terminology must be checked within the specific field.

Common Misconception: Cone Angle Always Means Half-Angle

It does not. In many geometric and engineering contexts, cone angle means the full included angle.

$$ \boxed{ \theta=2\alpha } $$

should be stated whenever ambiguity is possible.

Common Misconception: Cone Angle Always Means Full Angle

Some disciplines and authors informally use cone angle when referring to the angle between the axis and a generator. This is why full included angle and semi-vertical angle are preferable when precision matters.

Common Misconception: Cone Angle Equals the Sector Angle of the Net

The apex angle belongs to an axial cross-section of the three-dimensional cone. The sector angle belongs to the flattened lateral surface.

$$ \boxed{ \theta \ne \beta \text{ in general} }. $$

Common Misconception: Cone Angle Is a Solid Angle

A cone's ordinary opening angle and its solid angle are related but have different definitions and units.

Common Misconception: Changing Size Changes Cone Angle

Uniformly scaling a cone changes its dimensions but not its shape or angle.

Common Misconception: Height and Slant Height Give the Same Angle Formula

The correct trigonometric ratio depends on which lengths are known:

$$ \boxed{ \tan\alpha=\frac{r}{h}, \qquad \sin\alpha=\frac{r}{\ell}, \qquad \cos\alpha=\frac{h}{\ell} }. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\theta=2\alpha$ "The full cone angle is twice the semi-vertical angle."
$\alpha=\theta/2$ "Alpha is the half-angle of the cone."
$\tan\alpha=r/h$ "The tangent of the semi-vertical angle equals radius over height."
$\theta=2\arctan(r/h)$ "The included cone angle is twice the arctangent of radius divided by height."
$C=2\tan(\theta/2)$ "The rate of taper equals two times the tangent of half the included cone angle."
$\beta=2\pi r/\ell$ "The developed sector angle equals two pi r divided by the slant height."
$\Omega=2\pi(1-\cos\alpha)$ "The solid angle equals two pi times one minus the cosine of the half-angle."

The phrase cone angle should ideally be accompanied by enough language or notation to show exactly which angle is intended. Phrases such as included cone angle, full apex angle, semi-vertical angle, and half-opening angle remove ambiguity.

If an engineering drawing states only a numerical cone angle, the applicable drawing convention or standard should be checked before assuming that the number is a full angle or half-angle.

Defense Notation Advice: Reserve $\theta$ for the full included angle and $\alpha=\theta/2$ for the semi-vertical angle when both appear in the same discussion. State explicitly whether an angle is measured between two generators or between the axis and one generator. Keep the cone-net sector angle $\beta$ distinct from the three-dimensional apex angle, and keep the solid angle $\Omega$ distinct from all ordinary plane angles.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Primary Geometric Meaning: The full cone angle is the included angle between opposite generators in an axial cross-section.
  • Full Angle: $$ \boxed{\theta=2\alpha}. $$
  • Semi-Vertical Angle: The angle $\alpha$ between the cone axis and one generator.
  • Radius-Height Relation: $$ \boxed{ \tan\alpha=\frac{r}{h} }. $$
  • Full-Angle Formula: $$ \boxed{ \theta = 2\arctan\left(\frac{r}{h}\right) }. $$
  • Diameter Form: $$ \boxed{ \theta = 2\arctan\left(\frac{D}{2h}\right) }. $$
  • Radius-Slant Relation: $$ \boxed{ \sin\alpha=\frac{r}{\ell} }. $$
  • Height-Slant Relation: $$ \boxed{ \cos\alpha=\frac{h}{\ell} }. $$
  • Slant Height: $$ \boxed{ \ell=\sqrt{r^2+h^2} }. $$
  • Narrow Cone: Smaller $\alpha$ means a smaller radius-to-height ratio.
  • Wide Cone: Larger $\alpha$ means a larger radius-to-height ratio.
  • Similarity: Right circular cones with the same cone angle are similar.
  • Scaling: Uniform scaling preserves cone angle.
  • Taper: $$ \boxed{ C=\frac{D-d}{L} = 2\tan\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) }. $$
  • Frustum: A frustum retains the angle of the original complete cone.
  • Coordinate Cone: $$ \boxed{ x^2+y^2 = z^2\tan^2\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) }. $$
  • Conic Sections: The semi-vertical angle provides a reference for comparing a cutting plane with the cone's generators.
  • Cone-Net Sector Angle: $$ \boxed{ \beta=\frac{2\pi r}{\ell} } $$ in radians.
  • Sector Angle in Degrees: $$ \boxed{ \beta=360^\circ\frac{r}{\ell} }. $$
  • Sector-Angle Contrast: The planar net angle $\beta$ is not generally the same as the apex cone angle $\theta$.
  • Solid Angle: $$ \boxed{ \Omega=2\pi(1-\cos\alpha) } $$ steradians.
  • Plane-Angle Contrast: Cone angle is measured in degrees or radians; solid angle is measured in steradians.
  • Engineering Usage: Cone dimensions may be specified by included angle, semi-angle, or rate of taper, so the convention should be made explicit.
  • Opening-Angle Usage: Opening angle may mean either the full angular width or a specified half-opening angle depending on field and author.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse the full cone angle with the semi-vertical angle.
  • Common Error: Do not assume every source uses cone angle for the same angular convention.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse the apex angle with the sector angle of the flattened cone.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse ordinary cone angle with solid angle.
  • Common Error: Do not expect uniform scaling to alter the cone angle.
  • Terminological Contrast: Cone angle commonly means the full included angle between two generators in an axial cross-section. Apex angle or vertex angle likewise normally refers to this full included angle. Semi-vertical angle, cone semi-angle, or half-angle means the angle between the axis and one generator and is one-half of the full angle. Opening angle can be ambiguous and should be qualified as full or half. The sector angle of a cone net is the central angle of the flattened lateral surface. A solid angle measures three-dimensional angular extent and is expressed in steradians. Taper measures dimensional change per unit axial length and is related trigonometrically to the cone angle.
