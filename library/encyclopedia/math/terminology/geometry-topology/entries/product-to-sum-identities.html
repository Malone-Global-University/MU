Product to Sum Identities

Subfield: Geometry & Topology

Etymology & Roots

Product-to-sum identities are trigonometric identities that rewrite a product of sine or cosine expressions as a sum or difference of trigonometric expressions.

$$ \boxed{ \text{trigonometric product} \longrightarrow \text{trigonometric sum or difference} } $$

The terminology is literal. The starting expression contains a product, such as:

$$ \sin A\cos B, $$

and the identity converts it into an additive combination, such as:

$$ \frac{1}{2} \left[ \sin(A+B)+\sin(A-B) \right]. $$

The four standard identities are:

$$ \boxed{ \sin A\sin B = \frac{1}{2} \left[ \cos(A-B)-\cos(A+B) \right] } $$
$$ \boxed{ \cos A\cos B = \frac{1}{2} \left[ \cos(A-B)+\cos(A+B) \right] } $$
$$ \boxed{ \sin A\cos B = \frac{1}{2} \left[ \sin(A+B)+\sin(A-B) \right] } $$
$$ \boxed{ \cos A\sin B = \frac{1}{2} \left[ \sin(A+B)-\sin(A-B) \right] }. $$

These identities arise naturally from the sine and cosine sum-and-difference identities. Their algebraic form reveals a deeper geometric fact: multiplying two oscillatory quantities creates components whose angular arguments are the sum and difference of the original arguments.

Product-to-sum identities are closely paired with sum-to-product identities. The two families transform trigonometric expressions in opposite structural directions.

$$ \boxed{ \text{product-to-sum} \quad\leftrightarrow\quad \text{sum-to-product} } $$

Operational Nuance

Sine Times Sine

$$ \boxed{ \sin A\sin B = \frac{ \cos(A-B)-\cos(A+B) }{2} }. $$

Cosine Times Cosine

$$ \boxed{ \cos A\cos B = \frac{ \cos(A-B)+\cos(A+B) }{2} }. $$

Sine Times Cosine

$$ \boxed{ \sin A\cos B = \frac{ \sin(A+B)+\sin(A-B) }{2} }. $$

Cosine Times Sine

$$ \boxed{ \cos A\sin B = \frac{ \sin(A+B)-\sin(A-B) }{2} }. $$

Why There Are Four Standard Forms

The two factors may be:

  • sine and sine;
  • cosine and cosine;
  • sine and cosine;
  • cosine and sine.

Because multiplication of ordinary real-valued functions is commutative:

$$ \sin A\cos B = \cos B\sin A, $$

the two mixed cases are algebraically related, but keeping both forms visible makes argument order and sign behavior easier to track.

Derivation from Cosine Sum

$$ \cos(A+B) = \cos A\cos B - \sin A\sin B. $$

Derivation from Cosine Difference

$$ \cos(A-B) = \cos A\cos B + \sin A\sin B. $$

Add the Cosine Identities

Adding gives:

$$ \cos(A-B)+\cos(A+B) = 2\cos A\cos B. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \cos A\cos B = \frac{ \cos(A-B)+\cos(A+B) }{2} }. $$

Subtract the Cosine Identities

Subtracting:

$$ \cos(A-B)-\cos(A+B) = 2\sin A\sin B. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \sin A\sin B = \frac{ \cos(A-B)-\cos(A+B) }{2} }. $$

Derivation from Sine Sum

$$ \sin(A+B) = \sin A\cos B + \cos A\sin B. $$

Derivation from Sine Difference

$$ \sin(A-B) = \sin A\cos B - \cos A\sin B. $$

Add the Sine Identities

$$ \sin(A+B)+\sin(A-B) = 2\sin A\cos B. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ \sin A\cos B = \frac{ \sin(A+B)+\sin(A-B) }{2} }. $$

Subtract the Sine Identities

$$ \sin(A+B)-\sin(A-B) = 2\cos A\sin B. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \cos A\sin B = \frac{ \sin(A+B)-\sin(A-B) }{2} }. $$

Memory Pattern: Same Functions

Products involving two sines or two cosines become cosine expressions.

$$ \boxed{ \sin\sin \longrightarrow \cos } $$
$$ \boxed{ \cos\cos \longrightarrow \cos }. $$

Memory Pattern: Mixed Functions

Products involving one sine and one cosine become sine expressions.

$$ \boxed{ \sin\cos \longrightarrow \sin }. $$

Memory Pattern: Half Factor

Every standard product-to-sum identity contains:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{1}{2} }. $$

Argument Pattern

The resulting angles are always:

$$ \boxed{ A-B \quad\text{and}\quad A+B }. $$

Sign Pattern for Sine-Sine

$$ \boxed{ \sin A\sin B = \frac{ \cos(A-B) - \cos(A+B) }{2} }. $$

Difference cosine first, then minus sum cosine.

Sign Pattern for Cosine-Cosine

$$ \boxed{ \cos A\cos B = \frac{ \cos(A-B) + \cos(A+B) }{2} }. $$

Difference cosine first, then plus sum cosine.

Sign Pattern for Sine-Cosine

$$ \boxed{ \sin A\cos B = \frac{ \sin(A+B) + \sin(A-B) }{2} }. $$

Sign Pattern for Cosine-Sine

$$ \boxed{ \cos A\sin B = \frac{ \sin(A+B) - \sin(A-B) }{2} }. $$

Argument Order Matters in Sine Differences

Since sine is odd:

$$ \boxed{ \sin(B-A) = -\sin(A-B) }, $$

reversing a difference can change a sign.

Argument Order Does Not Change Cosine Difference

Since cosine is even:

$$ \boxed{ \cos(B-A) = \cos(A-B) }. $$

Example: Rewrite $\sin(5x)\sin(2x)$

Use the sine-sine identity:

$$ \sin(5x)\sin(2x) = \frac{1}{2} \left[ \cos(3x)-\cos(7x) \right]. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \sin(5x)\sin(2x) = \frac{ \cos(3x)-\cos(7x) }{2} }. $$

Example: Rewrite $\cos(4x)\cos x$

$$ \cos(4x)\cos x = \frac{1}{2} \left[ \cos(3x)+\cos(5x) \right]. $$

Example: Rewrite $\sin(3x)\cos x$

$$ \boxed{ \sin(3x)\cos x = \frac{ \sin(4x)+\sin(2x) }{2} }. $$

Example: Rewrite $\cos(3x)\sin x$

$$ \cos(3x)\sin x = \frac{1}{2} \left[ \sin(4x)-\sin(2x) \right]. $$

Same Frequency Case

If $A=B$, the identities reduce to familiar power-reduction formulas.

Sine Squared

Set $A=B=x$:

$$ \sin^2x = \frac{ \cos0-\cos(2x) }{2}. $$

Since $\cos0=1$:

$$ \boxed{ \sin^2x = \frac{ 1-\cos(2x) }{2} }. $$

Cosine Squared

Set $A=B=x$ in the cosine-cosine identity:

$$ \boxed{ \cos^2x = \frac{ 1+\cos(2x) }{2} }. $$

Sine-Cosine with Equal Arguments

$$ \sin x\cos x = \frac{ \sin(2x)+\sin0 }{2}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \sin x\cos x = \frac{1}{2}\sin(2x) }. $$

Double-Angle Connection

Rearranging the preceding identity yields:

$$ \boxed{ \sin(2x) = 2\sin x\cos x }. $$

Power-Reduction Connection

Product-to-sum identities therefore include the standard power-reduction identities as equal-angle special cases.

Integration Use

Products such as:

$$ \sin(mx)\sin(nx) $$

can be converted into sums of simpler trigonometric functions before integration.

Example Integral

$$ \int \sin(5x)\sin(2x)\,dx $$

becomes:

$$ \frac{1}{2} \int \left[ \cos(3x)-\cos(7x) \right]dx. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ \int \sin(5x)\sin(2x)\,dx = \frac{1}{6}\sin(3x) - \frac{1}{14}\sin(7x) + C }. $$

Definite Integrals and Orthogonality

Product-to-sum identities are useful for evaluating integrals such as:

$$ \int_0^{2\pi} \sin(mx)\sin(nx)\,dx. $$

Distinct Integer Frequencies

For positive integers $m\neq n$:

$$ \boxed{ \int_0^{2\pi} \sin(mx)\sin(nx)\,dx = 0 }. $$

Why Orthogonality Appears

Product-to-sum gives:

$$ \sin(mx)\sin(nx) = \frac{1}{2} \left[ \cos((m-n)x) - \cos((m+n)x) \right]. $$

Each nonconstant cosine completes an integer number of cycles over $[0,2\pi]$, so its integral is zero.

Sine-Cosine Orthogonality

For positive integers $m,n$:

$$ \boxed{ \int_0^{2\pi} \sin(mx)\cos(nx)\,dx = 0 }. $$

Cosine Orthogonality

For distinct positive integers $m\neq n$:

$$ \boxed{ \int_0^{2\pi} \cos(mx)\cos(nx)\,dx = 0 }. $$

Same-Frequency Norm

$$ \boxed{ \int_0^{2\pi} \sin^2(nx)\,dx = \pi } $$

for positive integer $n$.

Cosine Same-Frequency Norm

$$ \boxed{ \int_0^{2\pi} \cos^2(nx)\,dx = \pi }. $$

Fourier Analysis

These orthogonality properties are foundational in Fourier series, where coefficients are extracted by integrating products of periodic basis functions.

Frequency Mixing

If:

$$ \cos(\omega_1t)\cos(\omega_2t) $$

is formed, then:

$$ \boxed{ \cos(\omega_1t)\cos(\omega_2t) = \frac{1}{2} \left[ \cos((\omega_1-\omega_2)t) + \cos((\omega_1+\omega_2)t) \right] }. $$

Sum Frequency

$$ \boxed{ \omega_{\text{sum}} = \omega_1+\omega_2 }. $$

Difference Frequency

$$ \boxed{ \omega_{\text{difference}} = |\omega_1-\omega_2| }. $$

Physical Interpretation

Multiplying sinusoidal signals can generate components at the sum and difference of the original frequencies.

Signal Mixing

This phenomenon is fundamental in communications, modulation, heterodyning, and frequency conversion.

Beat-Frequency Connection

Difference frequencies also appear in descriptions of beating and interference, although beat identities are often expressed using sum-to-product transformations rather than product-to-sum transformations.

Example: Product of Two Nearby Cosines

$$ \cos(101t)\cos(100t) = \frac{1}{2} \left[ \cos t+\cos(201t) \right]. $$

The product contains a low-frequency difference component and a high-frequency sum component.

Complex Exponential Interpretation

Euler's formulas:

$$ \cos\theta = \frac{ e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta} }{2} $$

and:

$$ \sin\theta = \frac{ e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta} }{2i} $$

make sum-and-difference frequencies natural consequences of multiplying complex exponentials.

Exponential Frequency Addition

$$ \boxed{ e^{iAt}e^{iBt} = e^{i(A+B)t} }. $$

Opposite Exponential Signs

$$ \boxed{ e^{iAt}e^{-iBt} = e^{i(A-B)t} }. $$

These two combinations explain why both sum and difference arguments arise.

Geometry of Angular Combination

The original sum-and-difference identities come from rotational geometry. The product-to-sum identities inherit that geometric structure after algebraic rearrangement.

Product-to-Sum vs. Sum-to-Product

Product-to-sum starts from multiplication:

$$ \boxed{ \sin A\sin B } $$

and produces addition or subtraction.

Sum-to-product starts from:

$$ \boxed{ \sin A+\sin B } $$

and produces multiplication.

Inverse Relationship

The two families are algebraically interconvertible through substitutions:

$$ u = \frac{A+B}{2}, \qquad v = \frac{A-B}{2}. $$

Recovering the Original Angles

$$ \boxed{ A=u+v, \qquad B=u-v }. $$

Sum-to-Product Example

$$ \boxed{ \cos A+\cos B = 2 \cos \left( \frac{A+B}{2} \right) \cos \left( \frac{A-B}{2} \right) }. $$

Why the Terminology Direction Matters

Naming the transformation direction helps identify which formula family is needed.

Product-to-Sum and Trigonometric Simplification

A product may be less convenient than a sum when integrating, comparing frequencies, or combining Fourier terms.

When Product Form Is Better

Product form may instead be preferable when solving for zeros or analyzing amplitude envelopes. Thus product-to-sum is a transformation tool, not a claim that sum form is universally superior.

Period of Resulting Terms

If $A=\omega_1x$ and $B=\omega_2x$, the resulting frequencies are $\omega_1+\omega_2$ and $\omega_1-\omega_2$.

Equal Frequencies

If $\omega_1=\omega_2=\omega$, the difference frequency is $0$:

$$ \cos0=1. $$

This is why products such as $\cos^2(\omega x)$ contain a constant term.

Cosine Square Frequency Content

$$ \boxed{ \cos^2(\omega x) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\cos(2\omega x) }. $$

Sine Square Frequency Content

$$ \boxed{ \sin^2(\omega x) = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2}\cos(2\omega x) }. $$

Average Value of Squared Sinusoids

Over a whole number of periods:

$$ \boxed{ \text{average of }\sin^2 = \text{average of }\cos^2 = \frac{1}{2} }. $$

RMS Connection

For a sinusoid $A\cos(\omega t)$, the mean square over a full cycle is:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{A^2}{2} }. $$

Thus the root-mean-square magnitude is:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{|A|}{\sqrt{2}} }. $$

Acoustics

Products and sums of trigonometric signals help describe interference, modulation, beating, and frequency components in sound.

Electrical Engineering

Products of sinusoidal voltage and current signals can be transformed into constant and oscillating components.

Instantaneous Power Example

Suppose:

$$ v(t) = V\cos(\omega t) $$

and:

$$ i(t) = I\cos(\omega t-\phi). $$

Then instantaneous power is:

$$ p(t) = VI \cos(\omega t) \cos(\omega t-\phi). $$

Apply Product-to-Sum

$$ \boxed{ p(t) = \frac{VI}{2} \left[ \cos\phi + \cos(2\omega t-\phi) \right] }. $$

Constant and Oscillatory Components

The identity separates the product into a constant average-power component and a double-frequency oscillatory component.

Quantum and Wave Mathematics

Products of oscillatory functions occur in normalization, interference, mode coupling, and orthogonality calculations.

Fourier Coefficient Calculations

Product-to-sum identities convert inner products of trigonometric basis functions into integrals that vanish or simplify over complete periods.

Trigonometric Polynomial Products

Multiplying finite sums of sine and cosine terms creates new frequency combinations. Product-to-sum identities reorganize those products into trigonometric polynomial form.

Frequency Convolution View

At an advanced level, multiplication in the time or spatial domain corresponds to convolution of spectral content. The appearance of sum and difference frequencies in elementary product-to-sum identities is a simple finite-frequency instance of this broader principle.

Common Error: Forgetting the Factor of One-Half

Every standard product-to-sum formula has:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{1}{2} }. $$

Common Error: Using Sum-to-Product Backwards without Adjusting Arguments

The two formula families are related, but their arguments are organized differently. Product-to-sum directly produces $A+B$ and $A-B$.

Common Error: Using Sine Outputs for Sine-Sine

A sine-sine product transforms into cosines:

$$ \boxed{ \sin A\sin B \longrightarrow \cos(A-B)-\cos(A+B) }. $$

Common Error: Using Sine Outputs for Cosine-Cosine

Cosine-cosine also transforms into cosine terms.

Common Error: Using Cosine Outputs for Mixed Products

Sine-cosine products transform into sine terms.

Common Error: Reversing the Sign in Sine-Sine

The correct identity is:

$$ \boxed{ \sin A\sin B = \frac{ \cos(A-B)-\cos(A+B) }{2} }. $$

Common Error: Using a Minus in Cosine-Cosine

$$ \boxed{ \cos A\cos B = \frac{ \cos(A-B)+\cos(A+B) }{2} }. $$

Common Error: Reversing $A-B$ in a Sine Term without Changing Sign

Because:

$$ \sin(B-A) = -\sin(A-B), $$

argument reversal can alter a mixed-product formula.

Common Error: Worrying about Reversal in Cosine Difference

Since cosine is even:

$$ \cos(B-A) = \cos(A-B). $$

Common Error: Adding the Angles but Forgetting the Difference Angle

Product-to-sum identities generate both sum and difference arguments.

Common Error: Doubling Instead of Halving

The isolated product contains one-half of the resulting sum or difference.

Common Error: Treating the Formula as Approximate

Product-to-sum relations are exact identities wherever the trigonometric expressions are defined.

Common Error: Applying the Identities Only to Numerical Angles

$A$ and $B$ may be expressions such as $3x$, $\omega t+\phi$, or more complicated angles.

Common Error: Multiplying the Arguments

The transformation produces:

$$ A+B \quad\text{and}\quad A-B, $$

not $AB$.

Common Error: Confusing Product-to-Sum with Angle-Sum Identities

Angle-sum identities start from expressions such as:

$$ \sin(A+B), $$

whereas product-to-sum starts from:

$$ \sin A\cos B. $$

Common Error: Confusing Product-to-Sum with Power-Reduction Identities

Power-reduction identities are equal-angle special cases of product-to-sum, not a completely unrelated family.

Common Error: Assuming the Resulting Sum Has the Same Individual Frequencies

The transformed terms use sum and difference frequencies rather than simply repeating the original frequencies.

Common Error: Assuming Difference Frequency Must Be Negative

A negative cosine frequency produces the same cosine because cosine is even:

$$ \cos(-\theta)=\cos\theta. $$

For sine terms, a negative argument introduces a sign change.

Common Error: Assuming Orthogonality without Checking the Interval

Standard integral orthogonality statements depend on the frequencies and the integration interval.

Common Error: Assuming Any Two Different Sinusoids Have Zero Product Integral

The result depends on frequency relationships and the interval of integration.

Common Error: Treating Signal Multiplication as Mere Amplitude Scaling

Multiplying sinusoids can create entirely new frequency components.

Common Error: Assuming Product-to-Sum Is Useful Only in Trigonometry Exercises

The identities are important in calculus, Fourier analysis, wave physics, electrical engineering, acoustics, communications, and spectral analysis.

Common Misconception: Product-to-sum identities do not simply "remove multiplication." They reveal the frequency structure hidden inside a trigonometric product. A product at frequencies $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ produces terms involving $\omega_1+\omega_2$ and $\omega_1-\omega_2$, which is why the same identities appear naturally in integration, Fourier orthogonality, waves, and signal mixing.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\sin A\sin B=\frac{1}{2}[\cos(A-B)-\cos(A+B)]$ "Sine A times sine B equals one-half the cosine of the difference minus the cosine of the sum."
$\cos A\cos B=\frac{1}{2}[\cos(A-B)+\cos(A+B)]$ "Cosine A times cosine B equals one-half the cosine of the difference plus the cosine of the sum."
$\sin A\cos B=\frac{1}{2}[\sin(A+B)+\sin(A-B)]$ "Sine A times cosine B equals one-half the sine of the sum plus the sine of the difference."
$\cos A\sin B=\frac{1}{2}[\sin(A+B)-\sin(A-B)]$ "Cosine A times sine B equals one-half the sine of the sum minus the sine of the difference."
$\cos((\omega_1-\omega_2)t)$ "The cosine component at the difference frequency."
$\cos((\omega_1+\omega_2)t)$ "The cosine component at the sum frequency."

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"convert the product to a sum"} } $$

means to replace a trigonometric multiplication with an equivalent sum or difference using the appropriate identity.

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"difference frequency"} } $$

refers to the frequency produced from subtraction of the original angular frequencies.

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"sum frequency"} } $$

refers to the frequency produced by adding them.

Defense Notation Advice: Preserve the factor of $1/2$, distinguish the sum argument $A+B$ from the difference argument $A-B$, and remember that reversing a sine difference changes its sign while reversing a cosine difference does not. When the arguments represent frequency, carry the full argument, including coefficients and phase constants, before simplifying.
