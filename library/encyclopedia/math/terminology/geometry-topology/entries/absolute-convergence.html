Absolute Convergence

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

Absolute convergence describes a particularly strong form of convergence for an infinite series. A series:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n $$

is said to converge absolutely when the series obtained by replacing every term with its absolute value also converges:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|a_n|<\infty }. $$

The adjective absolute refers to the use of absolute values. Signs are removed before convergence is tested, so positive and negative terms cannot rely on cancellation to make the series converge.

This distinction became central in mathematical analysis because some series converge only through cancellation between positive and negative terms. Such series behave differently from absolutely convergent series, especially under rearrangement.

Absolute convergence should therefore be understood not merely as "the series converges strongly," but through the precise condition:

$$ \boxed{ \sum |a_n|\text{ converges} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Definition

The series:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n $$

converges absolutely if:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|a_n| \text{ converges} }. $$

A fundamental theorem states:

$$ \boxed{ \text{absolute convergence} \Longrightarrow \text{convergence} }. $$

The converse is false.

Example of Absolute Convergence

Consider:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{n^2}. $$

Taking absolute values gives:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left| \frac{(-1)^n}{n^2} \right| = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{n^2}. $$

This is a $p$-series with:

$$ p=2>1, $$

so it converges. Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{n^2} \text{ converges absolutely} }. $$

Conditional Convergence

A series is conditionally convergent when the original series converges but the absolute-value series diverges.

The standard example is the alternating harmonic series:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}. $$

The alternating series converges, but:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left| \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n} \right| = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1n $$

is the harmonic series, which diverges.

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n} \text{ converges conditionally} }. $$

Why Absolute Convergence Is Stronger

If:

$$ \sum |a_n| $$

converges, then the positive magnitudes of all the terms already have a finite total. The original series does not need cancellation between positive and negative terms in order to settle to a finite sum.

By contrast, a conditionally convergent series may converge precisely because positive and negative terms offset one another.

Positive and Negative Parts

Every real number $a_n$ may be written as:

$$ a_n=a_n^+-a_n^-, $$

where:

$$ a_n^+=\max(a_n,0) $$

and:

$$ a_n^-=\max(-a_n,0). $$

Then:

$$ |a_n|=a_n^++a_n^-. $$

Absolute convergence means both:

$$ \sum a_n^+ $$

and:

$$ \sum a_n^- $$

converge to finite values.

Common Misconception: A series containing absolute-value symbols is not automatically absolutely convergent. One must actually determine whether the resulting nonnegative series converges.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\sum a_n$ "The series sum of a sub n."
$\sum |a_n|$ "The series of the absolute values of a sub n."
$\sum |a_n|<\infty$ "The absolute-value series converges."
$\sum a_n$ converges absolutely "The series converges absolutely."
$\sum a_n$ converges conditionally "The series converges conditionally."
$\sum |a_n|=\infty$ "The absolute-value series diverges."

The phrase:

$$ \sum a_n\text{ converges absolutely} $$

is shorthand for:

$$ \sum |a_n|\text{ converges}. $$

One should not say that "the absolute value of the series converges" when the actual operation being performed is term-by-term:

$$ \sum |a_n|. $$

This is distinct from:

$$ \left|\sum a_n\right|, $$

which takes the absolute value only after the original series has been summed.

Defense Notation Advice: Keep the distinction:

$$ \boxed{ \sum |a_n| \neq \left|\sum a_n\right| \text{ as operations} }. $$

Even when both expressions are defined, they serve different purposes.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Definition: $$ \boxed{ \sum a_n \text{ converges absolutely} \iff \sum |a_n| \text{ converges} }. $$
  • Absolute Implies Ordinary Convergence: $$ \boxed{ \sum |a_n|\text{ converges} \Longrightarrow \sum a_n\text{ converges} }. $$
  • Converse Failure: Ordinary convergence does not imply absolute convergence.
  • Conditional Convergence: $$ \boxed{ \sum a_n\text{ converges} \quad\text{and}\quad \sum|a_n|\text{ diverges} } $$ defines conditional convergence.
  • Alternating Harmonic Series: $$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}n $$ is conditionally convergent because the harmonic series: $$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1n $$ diverges.
  • Alternating p-Series: $$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{n^p} $$ converges absolutely when: $$ \boxed{p>1}. $$
  • When 0 < p ≤ 1: The corresponding absolute-value series: $$ \sum\frac1{n^p} $$ diverges. Some alternating versions still converge conditionally when the alternating-series hypotheses are satisfied.
  • Comparison Test: If: $$ 0\le |a_n|\le b_n $$ eventually and: $$ \sum b_n $$ converges, then: $$ \boxed{ \sum a_n \text{ converges absolutely} }. $$
  • Limit Comparison: To establish absolute convergence, one may compare: $$ |a_n| $$ with a familiar positive-term benchmark such as a $p$-series or geometric series.
  • Ratio Test: If: $$ L= \lim_{n\to\infty} \left| \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} \right|, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ L<1 \Longrightarrow \text{absolute convergence} }. $$
  • Ratio-Test Divergence: If: $$ L>1, $$ the series diverges. If: $$ L=1, $$ the test is inconclusive.
  • Root Test: If: $$ L= \limsup_{n\to\infty} \sqrt[n]{|a_n|}, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ L<1 \Longrightarrow \text{absolute convergence} }. $$
  • Power Series: Inside its radius of convergence, a power series: $$ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_n(x-c)^n $$ converges absolutely.
  • Boundary Points: At endpoints of an interval of convergence, absolute convergence, conditional convergence, or divergence may occur. Endpoints must generally be tested separately.
  • Geometric Series: $$ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}r^n $$ converges absolutely precisely when: $$ \boxed{|r|<1}. $$
  • Cauchy Criterion: Absolute convergence of: $$ \sum a_n $$ means that tails of: $$ \sum |a_n| $$ can be made arbitrarily small.
  • Triangle Inequality: For finite partial sums: $$ \left| \sum_{k=m}^{n}a_k \right| \le \sum_{k=m}^{n}|a_k|. $$ This inequality is fundamental in proving that absolute convergence implies convergence.
  • Bound on the Sum: If: $$ \sum a_n $$ converges absolutely, then: $$ \boxed{ \left| \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n \right| \le \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|a_n| }. $$
  • Rearrangements: An absolutely convergent series may be rearranged without changing its sum.
  • Conditional Rearrangements: A conditionally convergent real series can behave very differently under rearrangement. Rearrangement may change its sum or even cause divergence.
  • Riemann Rearrangement Theorem: For a conditionally convergent real series, suitable rearrangements can produce dramatically different limiting behavior. This is one of the major structural differences between conditional and absolute convergence.
  • Finite Changes: Adding, deleting, or changing finitely many terms does not affect whether an infinite series converges absolutely.
  • Scalar Multiplication: If: $$ \sum a_n $$ converges absolutely and $c$ is a constant, then: $$ \sum ca_n $$ also converges absolutely.
  • Addition of Series: If: $$ \sum a_n $$ and: $$ \sum b_n $$ converge absolutely, then: $$ \sum(a_n+b_n) $$ converges absolutely.
  • Cauchy Product: Absolute convergence provides strong conditions under which products of series behave well and Cauchy products can be manipulated safely.
  • Complex Series: For complex terms $z_n$, absolute convergence means: $$ \boxed{ \sum |z_n|<\infty }, $$ where $|z_n|$ denotes the complex modulus.
  • Vector-Valued Series: In normed spaces, the analogous condition is: $$ \sum \|x_n\|<\infty. $$ In complete normed spaces, this guarantees convergence of the vector series.
  • Improper Integrals: An improper integral: $$ \int_a^\infty f(x)\,dx $$ is said to converge absolutely when: $$ \boxed{ \int_a^\infty |f(x)|\,dx<\infty }. $$
  • Absolute Integral Convergence: Absolute convergence of an improper integral implies convergence of the original improper integral.
  • Conditional Improper Integral: An improper integral may converge through cancellation while: $$ \int |f(x)|\,dx $$ diverges. Such convergence is conditional.
  • Fourier Analysis: Absolute convergence of Fourier coefficients or Fourier series provides stronger control than mere pointwise convergence and often permits safer interchange of sums, integrals, and limits.
  • Numerical Stability of Truncation: For an absolutely convergent series, tail magnitudes can often be bounded using corresponding positive-term estimates, making approximation error easier to control.
  • Not the Absolute Convergence Test: There is no single theorem universally called the "absolute convergence test." Rather, one tests the positive-term series: $$ \sum |a_n| $$ using an appropriate convergence method.
  • Terminological Contrast: Convergence means the sequence of partial sums approaches a finite limit. Absolute convergence means the series of term magnitudes converges. Conditional convergence means the original series converges but its absolute-value series does not.
