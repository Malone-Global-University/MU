Arm of a Right Triangle

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

An arm of a right triangle refers descriptively to one of the two sides that meet to form the triangle's right angle. In standard modern geometry, these two sides are more commonly called the legs of the right triangle.

If $\triangle ABC$ has a right angle at $C$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \angle C=90^\circ } $$

and the sides:

$$ \boxed{ \overline{AC} \quad\text{and}\quad \overline{BC} } $$

are the two legs, or equivalently the two finite sides corresponding to the arms of the right angle.

The remaining side:

$$ \boxed{ \overline{AB} } $$

lies opposite the right angle and is called the hypotenuse.

Operational Nuance

The Two Legs Form the Right Angle

A right triangle is distinguished by exactly one interior angle measuring:

$$ \boxed{ 90^\circ=\frac{\pi}{2} }. $$

The two sides meeting at that angle are the legs.

If the legs have lengths $a$ and $b$, then they are perpendicular:

$$ \boxed{ a\perp b }. $$

Leg versus Hypotenuse

A right triangle has:

  • two legs, which form the right angle, and
  • one hypotenuse, which lies opposite the right angle.

The hypotenuse is always the longest side of a nondegenerate right triangle.

Pythagorean Relationship

If the leg lengths are $a$ and $b$ and the hypotenuse length is $c$, then:

$$ \boxed{ a^2+b^2=c^2 }. $$

This relationship uniquely connects the three side lengths of a right triangle.

Finding the Hypotenuse

If both legs are known:

$$ \boxed{ c=\sqrt{a^2+b^2} }. $$

For example, if:

$$ a=3, \qquad b=4, $$

then:

$$ c= \sqrt{3^2+4^2} = \boxed{5}. $$

Finding a Missing Leg

If the hypotenuse $c$ and one leg $a$ are known:

$$ b^2=c^2-a^2. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ b=\sqrt{c^2-a^2} }. $$

Side lengths are taken as nonnegative, so the positive square root is used for an ordinary triangle length.

Area from the Two Legs

Because the legs are perpendicular, either leg may serve as the base while the other serves as the corresponding height.

$$ \boxed{ A=\frac12ab }. $$

This is one reason the legs play a particularly important role in right-triangle geometry.

Perimeter

If the legs are $a$ and $b$ and the hypotenuse is $c$, then:

$$ \boxed{ P=a+b+c }. $$

Using the Pythagorean theorem:

$$ \boxed{ P= a+b+\sqrt{a^2+b^2} } $$

when the two legs are known.

Adjacent and Opposite Legs

Relative to one of the two acute angles, the two legs receive different trigonometric names.

For an acute reference angle $\theta$:

  • the leg touching $\theta$ is the adjacent leg,
  • the leg across from $\theta$ is the opposite leg.

The hypotenuse remains the hypotenuse regardless of which acute angle is selected.

Relative Terminology

The terms adjacent and opposite are not permanent names for the two legs.

If the reference angle changes from one acute angle to the other:

$$ \boxed{ \text{adjacent leg} \longleftrightarrow \text{opposite leg} }. $$

The two legs exchange trigonometric roles.

Sine

Relative to acute angle $\theta$:

$$ \boxed{ \sin\theta = \frac{\text{opposite leg}}{\text{hypotenuse}} }. $$

Cosine

$$ \boxed{ \cos\theta = \frac{\text{adjacent leg}}{\text{hypotenuse}} }. $$

Tangent

$$ \boxed{ \tan\theta = \frac{\text{opposite leg}}{\text{adjacent leg}} }. $$

SOHCAHTOA Connection

The familiar mnemonic:

$$ \boxed{ \text{SOH-CAH-TOA} } $$

organizes the relationships among the opposite leg, adjacent leg, and hypotenuse.

Legs as Coordinate Components

A right triangle naturally appears when a vector:

$$ \mathbf{v}= \langle x,y\rangle $$

is decomposed into horizontal and vertical components.

The component magnitudes can act as perpendicular legs:

$$ \boxed{ |x|, \qquad |y| }. $$

while the vector magnitude corresponds to the hypotenuse:

$$ \boxed{ \|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{x^2+y^2} }. $$

Coordinate Distance

For points:

$$ P=(x_1,y_1), \qquad Q=(x_2,y_2), $$

horizontal and vertical changes form perpendicular legs:

$$ \Delta x=x_2-x_1, \qquad \Delta y=y_2-y_1. $$

The distance between the points is the corresponding hypotenuse:

$$ \boxed{ PQ= \sqrt{ (\Delta x)^2+ (\Delta y)^2 } }. $$

Slope Connection

For a nonvertical line segment, the horizontal and vertical legs of its associated right triangle provide:

$$ \boxed{ m= \frac{\text{vertical change}} {\text{horizontal change}} = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} }. $$

Thus right-triangle legs underlie the geometric interpretation of slope.

45-45-90 Triangle

In an isosceles right triangle, the two legs are equal:

$$ \boxed{ a=b }. $$

If each leg has length $a$, the hypotenuse is:

$$ \boxed{ c=a\sqrt2 }. $$

30-60-90 Triangle

A $30^\circ$-$60^\circ$-$90^\circ$ triangle has side ratio:

$$ \boxed{ 1:\sqrt3:2 }. $$

The shorter leg lies opposite $30^\circ$, the longer leg lies opposite $60^\circ$, and the hypotenuse lies opposite $90^\circ$.

Altitude to the Hypotenuse

Drawing the altitude from the right-angle vertex to the hypotenuse divides the original right triangle into two smaller right triangles.

If the hypotenuse is split into segments $p$ and $q$, and the altitude is $h$, then:

$$ \boxed{ h^2=pq }. $$

Leg Theorem

In the same configuration, each leg is the geometric mean of the hypotenuse and the projection of that leg onto the hypotenuse.

If $c$ is the hypotenuse and its two projections are $p$ and $q$, then:

$$ \boxed{ a^2=cp, \qquad b^2=cq }. $$

Similarity Connection

The altitude to the hypotenuse creates three mutually similar right triangles. Consequently, ratios involving the legs recur throughout right-triangle geometry.

Inradius Connection

For a right triangle with legs $a,b$ and hypotenuse $c$, the inradius is:

$$ \boxed{ r= \frac{a+b-c}{2} }. $$

Circumradius Connection

The midpoint of the hypotenuse is the circumcenter of a right triangle. Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ R=\frac c2 }. $$

Leg versus Arm of an Angle

There is an important object-level distinction.

An arm of an angle is properly a ray extending indefinitely from a vertex:

$$ \overrightarrow{CA}. $$

A leg of a right triangle is the finite line segment:

$$ \overline{CA}. $$

Thus when one informally says that the legs are the "arms of the right angle," the directions coincide, but the mathematical objects are not literally identical:

$$ \boxed{ \text{angle arm}=\text{ray}, \qquad \text{triangle leg}=\text{segment} }. $$

Why Leg Is the More Precise Triangle Term

A triangle is composed of three finite line segments. Therefore, when referring specifically to one of the two sides forming a right triangle's $90^\circ$ angle, leg is the more precise standard term.

Arm is better reserved for the rays forming the angle itself.

Common Misconception

The hypotenuse is never one of the two arms or legs forming the right angle. It lies opposite the right angle.

$$ \boxed{ \text{legs meet at }90^\circ; \qquad \text{hypotenuse is opposite }90^\circ }. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\angle C=90^\circ$ "Angle C is a right angle."
$a^2+b^2=c^2$ "A squared plus b squared equals c squared."
$A=\frac12ab$ "The area is one-half the product of the two legs."
$\sin\theta=\frac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}}$ "Sine theta equals opposite over hypotenuse."
$\cos\theta=\frac{\text{adj}}{\text{hyp}}$ "Cosine theta equals adjacent over hypotenuse."
$\tan\theta=\frac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}}$ "Tangent theta equals opposite over adjacent."

In ordinary geometry, say leg of the right triangle when referring to the finite side segment. The phrase arm of the right angle is appropriate when emphasizing the two rays or directions that form the $90^\circ$ angle.

Defense Notation Advice: Do not label a leg permanently adjacent or opposite without specifying a reference acute angle. Those names change when the reference angle changes; leg and hypotenuse do not.
