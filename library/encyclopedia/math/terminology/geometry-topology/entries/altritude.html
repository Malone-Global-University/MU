Altitude

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

In geometry, an altitude is a perpendicular segment or perpendicular distance measured from a vertex to the opposite side, base, or supporting line.

In a triangle, the altitude from vertex $A$ is drawn perpendicular to the line containing side $BC$.

$$ \boxed{ AH\perp BC } $$

where $H$ is the foot of the perpendicular.

The word is closely associated with the ordinary idea of height, but in geometry the direction of an altitude is determined by perpendicularity rather than by what appears visually "up" or "down" on the page.

Thus a useful conceptual distinction is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{altitude} = \text{perpendicular height relative to a chosen base} }. $$

The term may refer either to the perpendicular segment itself or, depending on context, to the numerical length of that segment.

Operational Nuance

Altitude of a Triangle

In triangle $\triangle ABC$, the altitude from vertex $A$ is the perpendicular from $A$ to the line containing the opposite side $BC$.

$$ \boxed{ AH\perp BC }. $$

The point $H$ is called the foot of the altitude.

There Are Three Altitudes

Every nondegenerate triangle has three altitudes, one from each vertex:

$$ \boxed{ h_a,\qquad h_b,\qquad h_c }. $$

Here $h_a$ commonly denotes the altitude corresponding to side $a$, and similarly for $h_b$ and $h_c$.

Orthocenter

The three altitude lines of a triangle are concurrent. Their common point is the orthocenter, usually denoted:

$$ \boxed{H}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \text{three altitudes} \longrightarrow \text{one orthocenter} }. $$

Acute Triangle

In an acute triangle, all three feet of the altitudes lie on the sides of the triangle, and the orthocenter lies inside the triangle.

$$ \boxed{ \text{acute triangle} \Longrightarrow H\text{ inside} }. $$

Right Triangle

In a right triangle, the two legs are themselves altitudes because they are perpendicular to one another.

Consequently, the orthocenter is the right-angle vertex.

$$ \boxed{ \text{right triangle} \Longrightarrow H=\text{right-angle vertex} }. $$

Obtuse Triangle

In an obtuse triangle, two altitude segments must be drawn to extensions of the opposite sides. The orthocenter therefore lies outside the triangle.

$$ \boxed{ \text{obtuse triangle} \Longrightarrow H\text{ outside} }. $$

Altitude vs. Median

A median connects a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side:

$$ \boxed{ \text{median} \Longrightarrow \text{midpoint condition} }. $$

An altitude instead requires:

$$ \boxed{ \text{altitude} \Longrightarrow \text{perpendicular condition} }. $$

These are different requirements, although the same segment may satisfy both in special triangles.

Altitude vs. Angle Bisector

An angle bisector divides an angle into two congruent angles. An altitude is defined by perpendicularity to the opposite side or its supporting line.

The same segment can be both in an isosceles or equilateral triangle, but not in general.

Altitude and Area

If side $b$ is chosen as the base and $h$ is the corresponding altitude, the area of a triangle is:

$$ \boxed{ A=\frac12 bh }. $$

Each of the three sides may serve as a base:

$$ \boxed{ A = \frac12 ah_a = \frac12 bh_b = \frac12 ch_c }. $$

Finding an Altitude from Area

Rearranging the area formula gives:

$$ \boxed{ h=\frac{2A}{b} }. $$

This is useful when the area and corresponding base are known.

Trigonometric Form

If sides $b$ and $c$ include angle $A$, then the altitude relative to side $c$ can be expressed through sine:

$$ \boxed{ h=b\sin A } $$

in the appropriate geometric configuration.

Altitude in a Parallelogram

The altitude of a parallelogram is the perpendicular distance between a chosen base and the opposite parallel side.

$$ \boxed{ A=bh }. $$

Altitude in a Trapezoid

The altitude of a trapezoid is the perpendicular distance between its parallel bases.

$$ \boxed{ A=\frac12(b_1+b_2)h }. $$

Altitude in Three-Dimensional Geometry

For prisms, cylinders, pyramids, and cones, the altitude is the perpendicular distance between the base plane and the opposite base, vertex, or parallel plane as appropriate.

For a pyramid or cone:

$$ \boxed{ V=\frac13Bh }. $$

For a prism or cylinder:

$$ \boxed{ V=Bh }. $$

In each formula, $h$ is the perpendicular altitude, not a slanted edge.

Common Misconception: An altitude does not have to lie inside the figure. In an obtuse triangle, an altitude may meet an extension of the opposite side outside the triangle.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$AH\perp BC$ "A H is perpendicular to B C."
$h_a$ "The altitude corresponding to side a."
$A=\frac12 bh$ "Area equals one-half base times height."
$h=\frac{2A}{b}$ "The altitude equals twice the area divided by the base."
$H$ "The orthocenter," when used in the standard triangle-center context.
$h=b\sin A$ "The altitude equals b sine A."

The word altitude may refer to the segment:

$$ \overline{AH}, $$

while the symbol:

$$ h $$

often refers to its numerical length.

This distinction is similar to the difference between a side of a triangle and the length of that side.

Defense Notation Advice: Do not assume the altitude is vertical on the page. Its defining property is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{altitude}\perp\text{base line} }, $$

regardless of the figure's orientation.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Triangle Definition: An altitude is drawn from a vertex perpendicular to the line containing the opposite side.
  • Foot of Altitude: The point where the perpendicular meets the opposite side or its extension is the foot of the altitude.
  • Three Altitudes: Every nondegenerate triangle has three altitude lines.
  • Orthocenter: The three altitude lines meet at one point called the orthocenter.
  • Acute Triangle: The orthocenter lies inside the triangle.
  • Right Triangle: The orthocenter is the right-angle vertex.
  • Obtuse Triangle: The orthocenter lies outside the triangle.
  • Exterior Foot: In an obtuse triangle, the foot of an altitude may lie on an extension of the opposite side.
  • Area of a Triangle: $$ \boxed{ A=\frac12 bh }. $$
  • Three Equivalent Area Forms: $$ \boxed{ A = \frac12 ah_a = \frac12 bh_b = \frac12 ch_c }. $$
  • Altitude from Area: $$ \boxed{ h=\frac{2A}{b} }. $$
  • Right Triangle: Each leg serves as an altitude when the other leg is chosen as the base.
  • Altitude to the Hypotenuse: A right triangle also has a third altitude drawn from the right-angle vertex to the hypotenuse.
  • Right-Triangle Hypotenuse Altitude: If the altitude to the hypotenuse has length $h$ and divides the hypotenuse into segments $p$ and $q$, then: $$ \boxed{ h^2=pq }. $$
  • Isosceles Triangle: The altitude from the apex to the base is also a median and angle bisector.
  • Equilateral Triangle: Every altitude is simultaneously a median, angle bisector, and perpendicular bisector.
  • Equilateral Altitude: For side length $s$: $$ \boxed{ h=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}s }. $$
  • Median Contrast: A median is defined by a midpoint; an altitude is defined by perpendicularity.
  • Angle Bisector Contrast: An angle bisector divides an angle into two congruent angles; an altitude need not do so.
  • Perpendicular Bisector Contrast: A perpendicular bisector is perpendicular to a segment at its midpoint. An altitude need not meet the opposite side at its midpoint.
  • Parallelogram: The altitude is the perpendicular distance between opposite parallel sides.
  • Rectangle: A side perpendicular to the chosen base can serve directly as the altitude.
  • Trapezoid: The altitude is the perpendicular distance between the two parallel bases.
  • Prism: The altitude is the perpendicular distance between the two base planes.
  • Cylinder: In a right cylinder, the axis length equals the altitude; in an oblique cylinder, the slanted generator does not.
  • Pyramid: The altitude is perpendicular from the apex to the base plane.
  • Cone: The altitude is perpendicular from the apex to the plane of the base.
  • Slant Height Contrast: In cones and pyramids, slant height follows a sloping face or surface and is not the same as the altitude.
  • Volume of Prism or Cylinder: $$ \boxed{ V=Bh }. $$
  • Volume of Pyramid or Cone: $$ \boxed{ V=\frac13Bh }. $$
  • Coordinate Geometry: An altitude may be found by constructing a line through a vertex perpendicular to the opposite side.
  • Slope Method: For nonvertical and nonhorizontal lines with slopes $m_1$ and $m_2$: $$ \boxed{ m_1m_2=-1 } $$ is the standard perpendicular-slope condition.
  • Distance Formula Method: The altitude length may also be obtained as the perpendicular distance from a point to a line.
  • Point-to-Line Distance: For line: $$ Ax+By+C=0 $$ and point $(x_0,y_0)$: $$ \boxed{ d= \frac{|Ax_0+By_0+C|} {\sqrt{A^2+B^2}} }. $$
  • Vector Geometry: An altitude direction is orthogonal to the direction vector of the corresponding base.
  • Dot Product Test: If $\mathbf{u}$ is an altitude direction and $\mathbf{v}$ is a base direction: $$ \boxed{ \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=0 }. $$
  • Orientation Independence: Rotating or reflecting a figure does not change which segment is an altitude, because perpendicularity is preserved.
  • Diagram Caution: A segment that appears vertical is not necessarily an altitude unless it is perpendicular to the chosen base or supporting line.
  • Terminological Contrast: An altitude is a perpendicular height. A median goes to a midpoint. An angle bisector divides an angle in half. A perpendicular bisector cuts a segment in half at a right angle. These segments coincide only in special geometric configurations.
