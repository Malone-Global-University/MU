Area of a Trapezoid

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

The area of a trapezoid is the measure of the two-dimensional region enclosed by a quadrilateral having a pair of parallel sides.

The parallel sides are called the bases. If their lengths are $b_1$ and $b_2$, and $h$ is the perpendicular distance between them, then:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12(b_1+b_2)h }. $$

The formula can also be written:

$$ \boxed{ A= \left( \frac{b_1+b_2}{2} \right)h }. $$

This form reveals the central idea: the area equals the average length of the two parallel bases times the height.

Operational Nuance

Standard Formula

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12(b_1+b_2)h }. $$

Here:

  • $b_1$ and $b_2$ are the lengths of the parallel sides,
  • $h$ is the perpendicular distance between their supporting lines.

The Bases Must Be Parallel

The word base has a more specific role here than it sometimes does in triangle formulas. In the trapezoid formula, $b_1$ and $b_2$ refer specifically to the pair of parallel sides.

The remaining two sides are commonly called the legs.

The Height Is Perpendicular

A slanted leg is not generally the height. The height is:

$$ \boxed{ h= \text{perpendicular distance between the bases} }. $$

Average-Base Interpretation

Rewrite the area formula as:

$$ A= \left( \frac{b_1+b_2}{2} \right)h. $$

The quantity:

$$ \frac{b_1+b_2}{2} $$

is the arithmetic mean of the base lengths.

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ A= (\text{average base})(\text{height}) }. $$

Midsegment Formula

The segment joining the midpoints of the two legs is the midsegment or median of the trapezoid.

Its length is:

$$ \boxed{ m= \frac{b_1+b_2}{2} }. $$

Therefore the area formula becomes:

$$ \boxed{ A=mh }. $$

Example

Suppose:

$$ b_1=8, \qquad b_2=14, \qquad h=5. $$

Then:

$$ A= \frac12(8+14)(5). $$

Therefore:

$$ A= \frac12(22)(5) = \boxed{ 55 }. $$

Midsegment Check

The midsegment is:

$$ m= \frac{8+14}{2} = 11. $$

Then:

$$ A=mh=(11)(5)=\boxed{55}. $$

Derivation by Combining Two Congruent Trapezoids

Take two congruent copies of a trapezoid and rotate one so that they fit together to form a parallelogram.

The resulting parallelogram has base:

$$ b_1+b_2 $$

and height:

$$ h. $$

Its area is:

$$ (b_1+b_2)h. $$

Since this area consists of two congruent trapezoids, one trapezoid has half that area:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12(b_1+b_2)h }. $$

Derivation by Triangle Decomposition

A diagonal divides a convex trapezoid into two triangles. Using the two parallel sides as their respective bases, both triangles have the same perpendicular height $h$.

Therefore:

$$ A= \frac12b_1h + \frac12b_2h. $$

Factor:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12(b_1+b_2)h }. $$

Solving for Height

Starting with:

$$ A= \frac12(b_1+b_2)h, $$

multiply by $2$:

$$ 2A=(b_1+b_2)h. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ h= \frac{2A}{b_1+b_2} }. $$

Solving for a Missing Base

If $b_1$ is unknown:

$$ \frac{2A}{h}=b_1+b_2. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ b_1= \frac{2A}{h}-b_2 }. $$

Solving for the Midsegment

From:

$$ A=mh, $$

we obtain:

$$ \boxed{ m=\frac{A}{h} }. $$

Right Trapezoid

A right trapezoid has a leg perpendicular to the bases. In that case, the perpendicular leg itself is the height.

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12(b_1+b_2)h }. $$

The formula does not change; only the height is especially easy to identify.

Isosceles Trapezoid

In an isosceles trapezoid, the legs are congruent. Dropping perpendiculars from the endpoints of the shorter base creates two congruent right triangles.

If the leg length is $\ell$ and:

$$ x= \frac{|b_2-b_1|}{2}, $$

then:

$$ h^2+x^2=\ell^2. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ h= \sqrt{ \ell^2- \left( \frac{b_2-b_1}{2} \right)^2 } }. $$

Area of an Isosceles Trapezoid from Bases and Leg

Substituting this height gives:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac{b_1+b_2}{2} \sqrt{ \ell^2- \left( \frac{b_2-b_1}{2} \right)^2 } }. $$

Rectangle as a Limiting Special Case

Under definitions that allow a trapezoid to have at least one pair of parallel sides, a rectangle is a special trapezoid.

If:

$$ b_1=b_2=b, $$

then:

$$ A= \frac12(b+b)h = \boxed{ bh }. $$

Thus the trapezoid formula reduces naturally to the parallelogram and rectangle formula when the two bases are equal.

Definition Convention

Two common conventions exist. Under an inclusive definition, a trapezoid has at least one pair of parallel sides, so parallelograms count as trapezoids. Under an exclusive definition, it has exactly one pair of parallel sides.

The area formula:

$$ \frac12(b_1+b_2)h $$

remains valid whenever $b_1$ and $b_2$ are the selected parallel sides.

Coordinate Geometry

Consider an axis-aligned trapezoid with vertices:

$$ A=(x_1,0), \qquad B=(x_2,0), \qquad C=(x_3,h), \qquad D=(x_4,h). $$

The base lengths are:

$$ b_1=|x_2-x_1| $$

and:

$$ b_2=|x_3-x_4|. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \left( |x_2-x_1| + |x_3-x_4| \right)h }. $$

Shoelace Formula

For arbitrary vertex coordinates listed in cyclic order:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \left| \sum_{i=1}^{4} (x_i y_{i+1}-y_i x_{i+1}) \right| } $$

with:

$$ (x_5,y_5)=(x_1,y_1). $$

This coordinate formula works without first rotating the trapezoid so its bases are horizontal.

Integral Interpretation

Suppose the horizontal width of a trapezoidal region changes linearly from $b_1$ at one level to $b_2$ at another level a distance $h$ away.

Let:

$$ w(y)= b_1+ \frac{b_2-b_1}{h}y, \qquad 0\le y\le h. $$

Then:

$$ A= \int_0^h w(y)\,dy. $$

Evaluating:

$$ A= b_1h+ \frac12(b_2-b_1)h. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12(b_1+b_2)h }. $$

Connection to the Trapezoidal Rule

The numerical-integration trapezoidal rule approximates the area under a curve by replacing curved portions with straight-line segments, thereby forming trapezoids.

Over an interval of width $\Delta x$ with endpoint heights $f(x_0)$ and $f(x_1)$:

$$ \boxed{ A_{\text{trap}} = \frac{\Delta x}{2} \left[ f(x_0)+f(x_1) \right] }. $$

This is the same geometric formula with the two parallel sides interpreted as the endpoint heights.

Scaling

If every linear dimension is multiplied by $k$:

$$ b_1\mapsto kb_1, \qquad b_2\mapsto kb_2, \qquad h\mapsto kh, $$

then:

$$ A' = \frac12 (kb_1+kb_2)(kh) = k^2A. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ A\mapsto k^2A }. $$

Doubling All Dimensions

$$ \boxed{ k=2 \Rightarrow A\mapsto4A }. $$

Doubling the Height Only

If the bases stay fixed:

$$ \boxed{ h\mapsto2h \Rightarrow A\mapsto2A }. $$

Changing One Base

With $b_2$ and $h$ fixed:

$$ A(b_1) = \frac h2(b_1+b_2). $$

Thus area changes linearly with either base when the other measurements remain fixed.

Perimeter Does Not Determine Area

Trapezoids with the same perimeter may have different base lengths, heights, and therefore different areas.

$$ \boxed{ \text{perimeter alone does not determine trapezoid area} }. $$

Base Sum and Height Determine Area

Notice that the individual base lengths appear only through their sum:

$$ b_1+b_2. $$

Therefore any trapezoids having the same height and the same sum of parallel base lengths have the same area:

$$ \boxed{ b_1+b_2=\text{constant}, \quad h=\text{constant} \Rightarrow A=\text{constant} }. $$

Units

Since a length is multiplied by another length:

$$ \left( \frac{b_1+b_2}{2} \right)h, $$

the result is measured in square units.

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ is measured in units}^2 }. $$

Common Misconception: The formula does not use the average of all four side lengths. It uses only the average of the two parallel bases, multiplied by their perpendicular separation.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$A=\frac12(b_1+b_2)h$ "Area equals one-half the sum of the bases times the height."
$A=\frac{b_1+b_2}{2}h$ "Area equals the average of the two bases times the height."
$m=\frac{b_1+b_2}{2}$ "The midsegment equals the average of the base lengths."
$A=mh$ "Area equals midsegment times height."
$h=\frac{2A}{b_1+b_2}$ "Height equals two A divided by the sum of the bases."
$b_1=\frac{2A}{h}-b_2$ "Base one equals two A over h minus base two."

The labels $b_1$ and $b_2$ may be interchanged without changing the formula:

$$ b_1+b_2=b_2+b_1. $$

Defense Notation Advice: First identify the pair of parallel sides. Then measure the perpendicular distance between them. Do not substitute a slanted leg for $h$ unless that leg is known to be perpendicular to the bases.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Formula: $$ \boxed{ A= \frac12(b_1+b_2)h }. $$
  • Average-Base Form: $$ \boxed{ A= \left( \frac{b_1+b_2}{2} \right)h }. $$
  • Bases: $b_1$ and $b_2$ are the parallel sides.
  • Legs: The nonparallel sides are commonly called legs.
  • Height: $h$ is the perpendicular distance between the two base lines.
  • Midsegment: $$ \boxed{ m= \frac{b_1+b_2}{2} }. $$
  • Midsegment Area Formula: $$ \boxed{ A=mh }. $$
  • Height from Area: $$ \boxed{ h= \frac{2A}{b_1+b_2} }. $$
  • Missing Base: $$ \boxed{ b_1= \frac{2A}{h}-b_2 }. $$
  • Diagonal Decomposition: A diagonal divides a convex trapezoid into two triangles whose areas add to: $$ \boxed{ \frac12b_1h+\frac12b_2h }. $$
  • Two-Copy Derivation: Two congruent trapezoids can form a parallelogram with base $b_1+b_2$.
  • Right Trapezoid: A perpendicular leg may serve directly as $h$.
  • Isosceles Trapezoid: If the legs have length $\ell$: $$ \boxed{ h= \sqrt{ \ell^2- \left( \frac{b_2-b_1}{2} \right)^2 } }. $$
  • Isosceles-Trapezoid Area: $$ \boxed{ A= \frac{b_1+b_2}{2} \sqrt{ \ell^2- \left( \frac{b_2-b_1}{2} \right)^2 } }. $$
  • Equal-Base Case: If $b_1=b_2=b$: $$ \boxed{ A=bh }. $$
  • Definition Variation: Some conventions define a trapezoid as having at least one pair of parallel sides; others require exactly one pair.
  • Coordinate Method: When the bases are horizontal, their coordinate lengths and vertical separation can be substituted directly into the standard formula.
  • Shoelace Formula: $$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \left| \sum_{i=1}^{4} (x_i y_{i+1}-y_i x_{i+1}) \right| }. $$
  • Integral Interpretation: If width changes linearly from $b_1$ to $b_2$ across height $h$: $$ \boxed{ A= \int_0^h \left[ b_1+ \frac{b_2-b_1}{h}y \right]dy }. $$
  • Trapezoidal-Rule Connection: $$ \boxed{ A_{\text{trap}} = \frac{\Delta x}{2} [f(x_0)+f(x_1)] } $$ uses the same average-of-parallel-sides principle.
  • Uniform Scaling: $$ \boxed{ \text{linear scale }k \Rightarrow \text{area scale }k^2 }. $$
  • Doubling All Dimensions: $$ \boxed{ A\mapsto4A }. $$
  • Doubling Height Only: $$ \boxed{ A\mapsto2A } $$ when both bases remain fixed.
  • Constant Base Sum: Equal height and equal $b_1+b_2$ imply equal area.
  • Perimeter Caution: Equal perimeter does not imply equal area.
  • Square Units: If lengths are measured in meters, area is measured in square meters.
  • Common Error: Do not average all four sides; average only the two parallel bases.
  • Common Error: A slanted leg is not generally the perpendicular height.
  • Terminological Contrast: Bases are the parallel sides. Legs are the remaining sides. Height is the perpendicular separation of the bases. Midsegment has length equal to the average of the bases, which is why the compact formula $A=mh$ works.
