Catenary

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

A catenary is the curve assumed by an ideal flexible chain or cable hanging freely under its own weight when supported at its ends and subjected to uniform gravity.

$$ \boxed{ \text{catenary} = \text{equilibrium curve of a freely hanging uniform chain} }. $$

In its simplest centered form, the catenary is described by the hyperbolic cosine:

$$ \boxed{ y=a\cosh\left(\frac{x}{a}\right) }. $$

The parameter $a>0$ controls the scale and curvature of the curve.

A translated catenary can be written:

$$ \boxed{ y= a\cosh\left( \frac{x-h}{a} \right)+k }. $$

where $(h,a+k)$ is the lowest point.

The visual resemblance between a catenary and a parabola can be strong near the lowest point, but the two curves arise from different equations and different physical conditions.

Operational Nuance

Standard Equation

The standard catenary is:

$$ \boxed{ y=a\cosh\left(\frac{x}{a}\right) }. $$

Using:

$$ \cosh u = \frac{e^u+e^{-u}}{2}, $$

the catenary can also be written:

$$ \boxed{ y= \frac{a}{2} \left( e^{x/a}+e^{-x/a} \right) }. $$

Lowest Point

Since:

$$ \cosh u\ge1, $$

the standard catenary satisfies:

$$ y\ge a. $$

Its minimum occurs at:

$$ \boxed{ x=0, \qquad y=a }. $$

Translated Catenary

For:

$$ y= a\cosh\left( \frac{x-h}{a} \right)+k, $$

the minimum occurs at:

$$ \boxed{ (h,a+k) }. $$

Symmetry

Because hyperbolic cosine is even:

$$ \cosh(-u)=\cosh u, $$

the centered catenary is symmetric about the $y$-axis.

$$ \boxed{ y(x)=y(-x) }. $$

First Derivative

For:

$$ y=a\cosh(x/a), $$

differentiation gives:

$$ \boxed{ y'=\sinh(x/a) }. $$

Horizontal Tangent at the Minimum

Since:

$$ \sinh(0)=0, $$

the tangent at the lowest point is horizontal:

$$ \boxed{ y'(0)=0 }. $$

Second Derivative

$$ \boxed{ y'' = \frac1a \cosh(x/a) }. $$

Since $\cosh(x/a)>0$:

$$ \boxed{ y''>0 } $$

everywhere.

Thus the standard catenary is strictly concave upward.

Differential Equation of the Catenary

The identity:

$$ 1+\sinh^2u=\cosh^2u $$

yields:

$$ \sqrt{1+(y')^2} = \cosh(x/a). $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ y'' = \frac1a \sqrt{1+(y')^2} }. $$

This differential relation is one characterization of the catenary.

Arc-Length Element

For a graph $y(x)$:

$$ ds = \sqrt{1+(y')^2}\,dx. $$

For a catenary:

$$ \boxed{ ds = \cosh(x/a)\,dx }. $$

Arc Length from the Lowest Point

Measuring arc length from $x=0$:

$$ s(x) = \int_0^x \cosh(u/a)\,du. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ s(x) = a\sinh(x/a) }. $$

Arc Length and Slope

Since:

$$ y'=\sinh(x/a), $$

we obtain the useful relation:

$$ \boxed{ s=ay' } $$

when arc length is measured from the lowest point of the centered catenary.

Arc Length between Two Coordinates

From $x=x_1$ to $x=x_2$:

$$ \boxed{ L = a \left[ \sinh(x_2/a) - \sinh(x_1/a) \right] }. $$

Physical Derivation from Tension

Suppose a flexible uniform chain hangs in static equilibrium. Let:

  • $H$ be the constant horizontal component of tension,
  • $w$ be the chain's weight per unit arc length.

The catenary scale parameter is:

$$ \boxed{ a=\frac{H}{w} }. $$

Why the Horizontal Tension Is Constant

Gravity acts vertically. In static equilibrium there is therefore no distributed horizontal load on the ideal chain, so the horizontal component of tension remains constant along the chain.

Vertical Tension Component

If $s$ is arc length from the lowest point, the vertical component of tension must support the weight of that portion of chain:

$$ \boxed{ V=ws }. $$

Since:

$$ \tan\phi=y', $$

where $\phi$ is the tangent angle, equilibrium gives:

$$ \frac{V}{H}=y'. $$

Therefore:

$$ ws=Hy'. $$

Using $a=H/w$:

$$ \boxed{ s=ay' }, $$

consistent with the geometric arc-length relation.

Total Tension

The magnitude of the tension is:

$$ T= \sqrt{H^2+V^2}. $$

Using:

$$ V=H\sinh(x/a), $$

gives:

$$ \boxed{ T = H\cosh(x/a) }. $$

Minimum Tension

At the lowest point:

$$ x=0, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ T_{\min}=H }. $$

Tension increases away from the lowest point.

Curvature

For a graph $y(x)$, curvature is:

$$ \kappa = \frac{|y''|} {(1+(y')^2)^{3/2}}. $$

For the catenary:

$$ \boxed{ \kappa(x) = \frac{1} {a\cosh^2(x/a)} }. $$

Maximum Curvature

Curvature is greatest at the lowest point:

$$ \boxed{ \kappa_{\max} = \frac1a }. $$

Radius of Curvature

The radius of curvature is:

$$ \boxed{ \rho(x) = a\cosh^2(x/a) }. $$

Parameter a and Shape

A smaller $a$ produces greater curvature near the lowest point and a more sharply hanging shape.

A larger $a$ produces a flatter catenary over a comparable horizontal span.

Scaling

The family:

$$ y=a\cosh(x/a) $$

consists of geometrically similar curves under uniform scaling of both coordinate directions.

Catenary versus Parabola

A parabola may be written:

$$ y=Ax^2+Bx+C. $$

A catenary instead has hyperbolic-cosine form:

$$ \boxed{ y=a\cosh(x/a) }. $$

The two should not be identified simply because their graphs can look similar near their lowest points.

Why the Catenary Looks Parabolic Near Its Minimum

The hyperbolic cosine has expansion:

$$ \cosh u = 1+\frac{u^2}{2!} +\frac{u^4}{4!} +\cdots. $$

Therefore:

$$ a\cosh(x/a) = a + \frac{x^2}{2a} + \frac{x^4}{24a^3} +\cdots. $$

Near $x=0$:

$$ \boxed{ y \approx a+\frac{x^2}{2a} }. $$

This quadratic approximation explains the local resemblance to a parabola.

Uniform Load along the Cable versus Horizontal Load

A flexible cable carrying only its own uniformly distributed weight per unit cable length takes a catenary shape.

A cable under a load distributed uniformly with respect to horizontal distance is modeled by a parabola instead.

$$ \boxed{ \text{uniform weight per cable length} \rightarrow \text{catenary} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{uniform load per horizontal distance} \rightarrow \text{parabola} }. $$

Suspension-Bridge Caution

A freely hanging cable under predominantly its own weight is catenary-shaped. A suspension-bridge main cable supporting a roadway through many hangers is often modeled approximately by a parabola when the roadway load is treated as uniform horizontally.

Inverted Catenary

Turning a catenary upside down produces an inverted catenary.

$$ \boxed{ y= -a\cosh(x/a) } $$

up to translations.

Catenary Arch

An inverted catenary is important in structural geometry because an ideal arch with an appropriate self-weight distribution can carry compression along the curve with minimal bending.

Catenoid

Rotating a catenary around its axis of progression produces a surface called a catenoid.

For example, revolving:

$$ y=a\cosh(x/a) $$

about the $x$-axis gives a catenoid-type surface.

Minimal Surface Connection

The catenoid is a classical minimal surface: locally, it is a stationary surface for area under suitable boundary conditions.

This connects the catenary to the calculus of variations and surface geometry.

Hyperbolic Functions

The catenary provides one of the most natural geometric appearances of hyperbolic functions:

$$ \boxed{ \cosh x = \frac{e^x+e^{-x}}2, \qquad \sinh x = \frac{e^x-e^{-x}}2 }. $$

Hyperbolic Identity

The identity:

$$ \boxed{ \cosh^2u-\sinh^2u=1 } $$

plays a central role in simplifying catenary derivatives, arc length, and tension relations.

Endpoint Conditions

In practical hanging-chain problems, the parameter $a$ and translations $h,k$ must be chosen so that the curve passes through the specified supports.

For supports:

$$ (x_1,y_1), \qquad (x_2,y_2), $$

one solves:

$$ y_1 = a\cosh\left( \frac{x_1-h}{a} \right)+k $$

and:

$$ y_2 = a\cosh\left( \frac{x_2-h}{a} \right)+k. $$

Chain length may provide an additional condition needed to determine the physical catenary.

Equal-Height Supports

If the supports are at equal height and the chain is otherwise symmetric, the lowest point lies halfway between them horizontally.

Unequal-Height Supports

If the supports have unequal heights, the catenary remains a translated hyperbolic cosine curve, but its lowest point is generally not midway between the supports.

Common Misconception: Every Hanging Cable Is a Parabola

A freely hanging uniform cable under its own weight follows a catenary, not an exact parabola.

Common Misconception: Every Cable Is a Catenary

The resulting shape depends on how loads are distributed. Additional loads can produce other equilibrium curves.

Common Misconception: cosh Is cos

Hyperbolic cosine and ordinary cosine are different functions:

$$ \cosh x = \frac{e^x+e^{-x}}2, $$

whereas $\cos x$ is periodic and bounded.

In particular:

$$ \boxed{ \cosh x\ge1 } $$

for every real $x$.

Common Misconception: Parameter a Is the Lowest y-Coordinate in Every Form

In the untranslated equation $y=a\cosh(x/a)$, the minimum value is $a$. In:

$$ y= a\cosh((x-h)/a)+k, $$

the minimum is instead:

$$ \boxed{ a+k }. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$y=a\cosh(x/a)$ "y equals a times the hyperbolic cosine of x over a."
$y'=\sinh(x/a)$ "The slope is the hyperbolic sine of x over a."
$s=a\sinh(x/a)$ "Arc length from the lowest point is a times hyperbolic sine of x over a."
$a=H/w$ "The catenary parameter a equals horizontal tension divided by weight per unit length."
$T=H\cosh(x/a)$ "The tension magnitude equals H times hyperbolic cosine of x over a."
$y''=\frac1a\sqrt{1+(y')^2}$ "The catenary satisfies this nonlinear differential equation."

The word catenary usually names the mathematical curve itself. Phrases such as catenary cable, catenary arch, and catenoid describe physical or geometric constructions derived from the same underlying curve.

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish carefully between a catenary and a parabola by identifying how the load is distributed. In the standard formula, remember that $\cosh$ is the hyperbolic cosine rather than the ordinary cosine, and keep track of translations when locating the actual lowest point.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: A catenary is the equilibrium curve of an ideal flexible uniform chain hanging under its own weight.
  • Standard Equation: $$ \boxed{ y=a\cosh(x/a) }. $$
  • Translated Form: $$ \boxed{ y= a\cosh((x-h)/a)+k }. $$
  • Lowest Point: For the translated form, $(h,a+k)$.
  • Symmetry: The centered catenary is symmetric about the $y$-axis.
  • First Derivative: $$ \boxed{ y'=\sinh(x/a) }. $$
  • Second Derivative: $$ \boxed{ y''= \frac1a\cosh(x/a) }. $$
  • Differential Equation: $$ \boxed{ y'' = \frac1a \sqrt{1+(y')^2} }. $$
  • Arc Length from the Minimum: $$ \boxed{ s=a\sinh(x/a) }. $$
  • Curvature: $$ \boxed{ \kappa= \frac1{ a\cosh^2(x/a) } }. $$
  • Maximum Curvature: $\kappa_{\max}=1/a$ at the lowest point.
  • Physical Parameter: $$ \boxed{ a=\frac{H}{w} } $$ when $H$ is horizontal tension and $w$ is weight per unit cable length.
  • Total Tension: $$ \boxed{ T=H\cosh(x/a) }. $$
  • Parabola Contrast: A catenary arises from uniform self-weight per cable length; a parabola arises in the ideal cable model with uniform load per horizontal distance.
  • Local Parabolic Approximation: $$ \boxed{ a\cosh(x/a) \approx a+\frac{x^2}{2a} } $$ near the minimum.
  • Inverted Catenary: Used as an important idealized arch shape.
  • Catenoid: A surface obtained by revolving a catenary around an appropriate axis.
  • Minimal-Surface Connection: The catenoid is a classical minimal surface.
  • Hyperbolic Function Connection: Catenary geometry naturally involves $\cosh$ and $\sinh$.
  • Engineering: Catenary models appear in suspended chains, cables, wires, and related structural problems when self-weight dominates.
  • Common Error: Do not call every hanging cable shape a parabola.
  • Common Error: Do not assume every loaded cable remains an exact catenary.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse $\cosh x$ with $\cos x$.
  • Common Error: Do not assume $a$ is the vertical coordinate of the lowest point after arbitrary translations.
  • Terminological Contrast: Catenary is the hyperbolic-cosine curve of a freely hanging uniform chain. Parabola is a quadratic conic and arises from a different ideal cable loading model. Inverted catenary is the upside-down form used in arch geometry. Catenoid is the surface generated by revolving a catenary. Hyperbolic cosine is the function governing the standard catenary equation.
