Coordinates

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

Coordinates are numerical or symbolic values used together to specify the position of a point or the components of an object relative to a chosen coordinate system, frame, or basis.

In the standard two-dimensional Cartesian plane, a point is represented by two coordinates:

$$ \boxed{(x,y)}. $$

In three-dimensional Cartesian space, three coordinates are used:

$$ \boxed{(x,y,z)}. $$

More generally, a point or vector in an $n$-dimensional coordinate space may be represented by:

$$ \boxed{ (x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n) }. $$

The underlying idea is relational rather than absolute: coordinates describe an object relative to a specified reference structure. Change the origin, axes, basis, orientation, or coordinate system, and the coordinates may change while the underlying geometric object remains the same.

Operational Nuance

Coordinates as Position Data

In elementary geometry, coordinates locate points.

For:

$$ P=(3,-2), $$

the coordinates $3$ and $-2$ indicate the point's position relative to the chosen coordinate axes.

First and Second Coordinates

In the ordered pair:

$$ (x,y), $$

$x$ is the first coordinate and $y$ is the second.

In the standard Cartesian plane:

  • $x$ gives horizontal position,
  • $y$ gives vertical position.

Coordinate Order Matters

Coordinates form an ordered tuple. Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b)\ne(b,a) } $$

in general.

Equality occurs only when the corresponding coordinates match.

Equality of Ordered Pairs

$$ \boxed{ (a,b)=(c,d) \iff a=c \text{ and } b=d }. $$

Cartesian Coordinates

In a Cartesian coordinate system, coordinates describe signed displacement or components along coordinate axes.

In two dimensions:

$$ \boxed{ P=(x,y) }. $$

x-Coordinate

The $x$-coordinate measures signed horizontal displacement from the $y$-axis in the standard coordinate plane.

y-Coordinate

The $y$-coordinate measures signed vertical displacement from the $x$-axis.

Abscissa and Ordinate

Traditional terminology identifies:

$$ \boxed{ x=\text{abscissa}, \qquad y=\text{ordinate} }. $$

Origin

In a Cartesian system, coordinates are measured relative to an origin.

In the standard plane:

$$ \boxed{O=(0,0)}. $$

Coordinate Axes

The axes provide the directional reference for the coordinates.

In the standard plane:

$$ x\text{-axis}:y=0, \qquad y\text{-axis}:x=0. $$

Signs of Coordinates

The sign of a Cartesian coordinate indicates direction relative to the origin and corresponding axis orientation.

In the standard plane:

  • $x>0$ means right of the $y$-axis,
  • $x<0$ means left of the $y$-axis,
  • $y>0$ means above the $x$-axis,
  • $y<0$ means below the $x$-axis.

Coordinates and Quadrants

Quadrant Sign Pattern
I $(+,+)$
II $(-,+)$
III $(-,-)$
IV $(+,-)$

Zero Coordinates

If one coordinate is zero, the point lies on a coordinate axis.

$$ (a,0) \quad\text{lies on the }x\text{-axis}, $$
$$ (0,b) \quad\text{lies on the }y\text{-axis}. $$

Coordinates in Three Dimensions

In three-dimensional Cartesian space:

$$ \boxed{ P=(x,y,z) }. $$

The three coordinates measure position along three independent coordinate directions.

Three Coordinate Planes

The standard three-dimensional system contains:

  • the $xy$-plane, where $z=0$,
  • the $xz$-plane, where $y=0$,
  • the $yz$-plane, where $x=0$.

Higher-Dimensional Coordinates

In $\mathbb R^n$, an object may be represented by:

$$ \boxed{ (x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n) }. $$

Each coordinate corresponds to one independent direction in the chosen basis.

Coordinates and Cartesian Products

A Cartesian coordinate pair in the real plane belongs to:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y)\in\mathbb R\times\mathbb R = \mathbb R^2 }. $$

Coordinates of Vectors

Coordinates may describe vector components rather than point locations.

For example:

$$ \mathbf v = \langle 3,-2\rangle. $$

These coordinates describe the vector's components relative to the chosen basis.

Point Coordinates versus Vector Coordinates

The numerical pair:

$$ (3,-2) $$

may describe the location of a point, whereas:

$$ \langle3,-2\rangle $$

commonly describes a vector.

The numbers may be identical while the mathematical roles differ.

Position Vector

A point:

$$ P=(x,y) $$

can be associated with the position vector:

$$ \boxed{ \overrightarrow{OP} = \langle x,y\rangle }. $$

Coordinates Relative to a Basis

Suppose:

$$ \mathcal B= \{\mathbf b_1,\ldots,\mathbf b_n\} $$

is a basis. If:

$$ \mathbf v = c_1\mathbf b_1 + \cdots + c_n\mathbf b_n, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ [\mathbf v]_{\mathcal B} = \begin{pmatrix} c_1\\ \vdots\\ c_n \end{pmatrix} } $$

is the coordinate vector of $\mathbf v$ relative to $\mathcal B$.

Standard Basis

In $\mathbb R^2$:

$$ \mathbf e_1=(1,0), \qquad \mathbf e_2=(0,1). $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y) = x\mathbf e_1+y\mathbf e_2 }. $$

Coordinates Depend on the Basis

The same vector generally has different coordinates relative to different bases.

$$ \boxed{ [\mathbf v]_{\mathcal B} \ne [\mathbf v]_{\mathcal C} } $$

in general.

Change of Coordinates

A change of basis transforms one coordinate representation into another.

If $P$ is an appropriate change-of-basis matrix, then coordinate vectors may be related by:

$$ \boxed{ [\mathbf v]_{\mathcal C} = P[\mathbf v]_{\mathcal B} }. $$

Coordinates Are Representation-Dependent

A central conceptual distinction is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{object} \ne \text{its coordinates} }. $$

Coordinates are a representation of an object relative to a chosen system.

Changing the Origin

Suppose a new origin is located at:

$$ (h,k). $$

Then old Cartesian coordinates may be converted to translated coordinates:

$$ \boxed{ x'=x-h, \qquad y'=y-k }. $$

Coordinate Translation versus Point Translation

Changing the coordinate origin and physically translating a point can produce similar algebraic formulas but represent different conceptual operations.

One changes the reference system; the other changes the geometric object.

Rotating Coordinate Axes

Coordinates can also change if the coordinate axes are rotated while the underlying geometric point remains fixed.

Such changes are important in analytic geometry, linear algebra, mechanics, and tensor analysis.

Coordinate Transformations

More generally:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y) \longleftrightarrow (u,v) } $$

can describe a change between two coordinate systems.

Polar Coordinates

A point in the plane may be described by polar coordinates:

$$ \boxed{ (r,\theta) }. $$

Here:

  • $r$ is radial distance,
  • $\theta$ is angular direction.

Cartesian and Polar Conversion

$$ \boxed{ x=r\cos\theta, \qquad y=r\sin\theta }. $$

Also:

$$ \boxed{ r^2=x^2+y^2 }. $$

Polar Coordinates Need Not Be Unique

Unlike ordinary Cartesian coordinates, polar coordinates can represent the same point in multiple ways.

$$ \boxed{ (r,\theta) \sim (r,\theta+2\pi k) } $$

for any integer $k$.

Negative radial coordinates can create additional equivalent representations under standard conventions.

Cylindrical Coordinates

In three dimensions, cylindrical coordinates are commonly written:

$$ \boxed{ (r,\theta,z) }. $$

Spherical Coordinates

Spherical coordinates commonly use three quantities such as:

$$ \boxed{ (\rho,\theta,\phi) }, $$

though the interpretation and order of the angular coordinates vary by convention.

Coordinate Conventions Matter

Different disciplines may reverse the meanings of $\theta$ and $\phi$ in spherical coordinates. Coordinate symbols should therefore never be interpreted without checking the stated convention.

Homogeneous Coordinates

In projective geometry and computer graphics, a point in the plane may be represented using homogeneous coordinates:

$$ \boxed{ [x:y:w] }. $$

Equivalent triples represent the same projective point when multiplied by a common nonzero scalar.

$$ [x:y:w] = [\lambda x:\lambda y:\lambda w], \qquad \lambda\ne0. $$

Geographic Coordinates

Latitude and longitude are coordinates used to locate positions on or near the Earth's surface.

These are angular coordinates rather than ordinary Cartesian $x$- and $y$ coordinates.

Coordinates in Complex Numbers

A complex number:

$$ z=a+bi $$

can be represented in the complex plane by:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b) }. $$

Here $a$ is the real coordinate and $b$ is the imaginary coordinate.

Coordinates and Graphs

A graph in a coordinate system consists of points whose coordinates satisfy an equation, inequality, parametrization, or other condition.

For:

$$ y=f(x), $$

the graph consists of:

$$ \boxed{ (x,f(x)) }. $$

Coordinate Geometry

Coordinates allow geometric properties to be translated into algebra.

Examples include:

  • distance,
  • midpoints,
  • slope,
  • collinearity,
  • parallelism,
  • perpendicularity,
  • circles, and
  • conic sections.

Distance from Coordinates

In the Euclidean plane:

$$ \boxed{ d= \sqrt{ (x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2 } }. $$

Midpoint from Coordinates

$$ \boxed{ M= \left( \frac{x_1+x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1+y_2}{2} \right) }. $$

Slope from Coordinates

$$ \boxed{ m= \frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1} } $$

when $x_1\ne x_2$.

Coordinates and Transformations

Transformations can be described by rules acting directly on coordinates.

Translation

$$ \boxed{ (x,y) \mapsto (x+a,y+b) }. $$

Reflection across the x-Axis

$$ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(x,-y) }. $$

Reflection across the y-Axis

$$ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(-x,y) }. $$

Rotation by 90 Degrees Counterclockwise

$$ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(-y,x) }. $$

Dilation about the Origin

$$ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(kx,ky) }. $$

Coordinates and Matrices

Linear transformations act naturally on coordinate vectors:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf x' = A\mathbf x }. $$

Affine transformations may include translation:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf x' = A\mathbf x+\mathbf b }. $$

Coordinates and Data

Coordinates need not represent physical space. A data pair:

$$ (x_i,y_i) $$

may represent two measured variables plotted on perpendicular axes.

Coordinate Values May Have Units

Coordinates in applications may carry units.

For example:

$$ (t,d)=(5\text{ s},20\text{ m}) $$

describes a time-distance data pair, not a literal point in physical Euclidean space.

Dimension and Number of Coordinates

In a standard coordinate representation, the number of independent coordinates often corresponds to the dimension of the space.

$$ \boxed{ \dim(\mathbb R^n)=n }. $$

Local Coordinates

In differential geometry, curved spaces can be described locally by coordinate charts.

A chart may assign coordinates:

$$ (x^1,x^2,\ldots,x^n) $$

to points in a region of a manifold.

Coordinates on a Manifold

A manifold need not possess one global coordinate system. Different local coordinate charts may be required to cover the entire space.

Coordinate Chart

A chart consists of a region $U$ and a coordinate map:

$$ \boxed{ \varphi:U\to\mathbb R^n }. $$

The numbers assigned by $\varphi$ serve as local coordinates.

Coordinate Singularities

A coordinate system can become degenerate or ambiguous at particular locations even when the underlying geometric space itself is perfectly regular.

For example, the polar angle is undefined at:

$$ r=0. $$

This is a coordinate issue rather than necessarily a geometric singularity.

Coordinates and Tensors

Tensor components change when coordinates change, while the underlying tensor is interpreted as the coordinate-independent geometric or algebraic object.

This distinction between an object and its components is fundamental in differential geometry and physics.

Covariant and Contravariant Coordinates

In advanced geometry, vector and covector components transform differently under a change of coordinates. This leads to the distinction between contravariant and covariant components.

Coordinates versus Components

Coordinates most naturally locate points, while components most naturally describe vectors, tensors, or other objects relative to a basis.

In practice, the terminology overlaps considerably, especially in linear algebra and applied mathematics.

Coordinates versus Parameters

Parameters may generate coordinates without themselves being coordinates.

For a parametric curve:

$$ x=x(t), \qquad y=y(t), $$

$t$ is the parameter, while $x(t)$ and $y(t)$ are the Cartesian coordinates of the resulting point.

Coordinates versus Variables

Symbols such as $x$ and $y$ may serve as coordinate variables, but not every variable is a coordinate.

Coordinate-Free Mathematics

Many advanced mathematical statements are formulated without choosing coordinates. Coordinates can then be introduced later to compute or represent the abstract objects concretely.

This distinction separates intrinsic properties from artifacts of a particular coordinate system.

Intrinsic versus Coordinate-Dependent Properties

Distances, angles, curvature, vector values, and other geometric quantities may be intrinsic under suitable structures, while their numerical coordinate expressions depend on representation.

Common Misconception: Coordinates Are the Point

Coordinates describe a point relative to a chosen system; they are not necessarily the abstract point itself.

Common Misconception: Coordinates Are Unique in Every Coordinate System

Cartesian coordinates in a fixed ordinary coordinate system are unique, but other coordinate systems such as polar or homogeneous coordinates may allow multiple representations of the same point.

Common Misconception: Swapping Coordinates Does Not Matter

Coordinate order is part of the representation. Swapping components usually changes the represented point or vector.

Common Misconception: Coordinates Must Be x and y

Coordinate symbols may be $x,y,z$, $r,\theta$, $u,v$, $x^1,x^2$, or many other choices.

Common Misconception: All Coordinates Measure Distance

Cartesian coordinates may be interpreted as signed components along axes, but angular coordinates such as $\theta$ do not directly measure linear distance.

Common Misconception: Changing Coordinates Moves the Object

A change of coordinates can alter numerical descriptions while leaving the underlying geometric object fixed.

Common Misconception: Point Coordinates and Vector Components Are Always Identical Concepts

They may use identical numerical tuples, but a point denotes location and a vector denotes a directed quantity.

Common Misconception: Coordinate Singularities Are Always Physical Singularities

A coordinate description may fail or become ambiguous even when the underlying space remains regular.

Defense Notation Advice: Preserve coordinate order and identify the coordinate system or basis when it is not obvious. Distinguish point coordinates from vector components when conceptual precision matters. Do not assume a tuple of coordinates is invariant under a change of origin, basis, or coordinates. In polar, spherical, homogeneous, and manifold coordinates, state conventions explicitly because representation may be nonunique or symbol order may vary between fields.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: Coordinates are ordered numerical or symbolic values used to locate or represent an object relative to a chosen reference system.
  • Two-Dimensional Cartesian Coordinates: $(x,y)$.
  • Three-Dimensional Cartesian Coordinates: $(x,y,z)$.
  • Higher Dimensions: $(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$.
  • Coordinate Order: Coordinates form ordered tuples; their order generally cannot be exchanged.
  • x-Coordinate: First coordinate in the standard Cartesian plane.
  • y-Coordinate: Second coordinate in the standard Cartesian plane.
  • Abscissa and Ordinate: Traditional names for the first and second Cartesian coordinates.
  • Origin: The reference point whose standard Cartesian coordinates are $(0,0)$.
  • Linear Algebra: Coordinates describe vectors relative to a basis.
  • Change of Basis: A vector's coordinate components generally change when the basis changes.
  • Polar Coordinates: $(r,\theta)$ use radial distance and angular direction.
  • Cylindrical Coordinates: Commonly $(r,\theta,z)$.
  • Spherical Coordinates: Commonly use one radial and two angular coordinates, with notation varying by convention.
  • Projective Geometry: Homogeneous coordinates represent projective points through equivalence classes of proportional tuples.
  • Complex Plane: $a+bi$ corresponds to coordinates $(a,b)$.
  • Differential Geometry: Local coordinate charts assign numerical coordinates to points on manifolds.
  • Tensor Analysis: Coordinate components transform when the coordinate system changes.
  • Coordinate Geometry: Uses coordinates to convert geometric relationships into algebraic form.
  • Transformations: Coordinate rules encode translations, reflections, rotations, dilations, and more general transformations.
  • Data Analysis: Coordinates may represent paired measurements rather than physical location.
  • Parameter Contrast: A parameter may determine coordinates without itself being a coordinate.
  • Variable Contrast: Coordinate symbols are variables, but variables need not be coordinates.
  • Point Contrast: A point is the geometric object; its coordinates are a representation relative to a chosen system.
  • Vector Contrast: A vector's components resemble coordinates but describe a directed quantity relative to a basis.
  • Representation Dependence: Coordinates may change while the underlying object remains unchanged.
  • Coordinate Singularity: Failure of a coordinate description does not necessarily imply failure of the underlying geometric space.
  • Common Error: Do not reverse coordinate order.
  • Common Error: Do not assume coordinates are intrinsic properties of the point independent of the reference system.
  • Common Error: Do not assume all coordinate systems give unique representations.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse changing coordinates with physically moving an object.
  • Common Error: Do not assume spherical-coordinate symbol conventions are universal.
  • Terminological Contrast: Coordinates are ordered values used to locate or represent an object relative to a coordinate system. A coordinate system is the reference framework that gives those values meaning. A point is the geometric location represented by coordinates. A coordinate vector records the components of a vector relative to a chosen basis. Cartesian coordinates use components along coordinate axes. Polar coordinates use radial and angular information. Homogeneous coordinates use proportional tuples in projective geometry. Local coordinates describe points within charts on manifolds. A parameter generates or indexes objects and should not automatically be confused with a coordinate.
