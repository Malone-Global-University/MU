Cube Root

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

The cube root of a number is a number that, when multiplied by itself three times, produces the original number.

If:

$$ x^3=a, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{x=\sqrt[3]{a}}. $$

The term cube comes from the geometric cube. A cube with side length $x$ has volume:

$$ V=x^3. $$

Therefore, finding the side length of a cube from its volume requires taking a cube root:

$$ \boxed{ x=\sqrt[3]{V} }. $$

The word root reflects the inverse relationship between powers and radicals. Just as squaring and taking a square root are closely related, cubing and taking a cube root undo one another over the real numbers.

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[3]{x^3}=x \qquad \text{for every real }x }. $$

Operational Nuance

Basic Definition

The real cube root of $a$ is the unique real number $x$ satisfying:

$$ \boxed{x^3=a}. $$

It is written:

$$ \boxed{\sqrt[3]{a}}. $$

Examples

$$ \boxed{\sqrt[3]{8}=2} $$

because:

$$ 2^3=8. $$
$$ \boxed{\sqrt[3]{27}=3} $$

because:

$$ 3^3=27. $$
$$ \boxed{\sqrt[3]{125}=5} $$

because:

$$ 5^3=125. $$

Cube Roots of Negative Numbers

Unlike the real square root, the real cube root is defined for negative numbers.

For example:

$$ \boxed{\sqrt[3]{-8}=-2} $$

because:

$$ (-2)^3=-8. $$

More generally:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[3]{-a} = -\sqrt[3]{a} } $$

for every real $a$.

Why Negative Cube Roots Are Real

Odd powers preserve sign:

$$ x<0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x^3<0. $$

Therefore every negative real number has a negative real cube root.

Zero

Since:

$$ 0^3=0, $$

one has:

$$ \boxed{\sqrt[3]{0}=0}. $$

Cube Root as an Inverse Function

Consider:

$$ f(x)=x^3. $$

This function is one-to-one on all of $\mathbb{R}$, so it has an inverse:

$$ \boxed{ f^{-1}(x)=\sqrt[3]{x} }. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[3]{x^3}=x } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \left(\sqrt[3]{x}\right)^3=x }. $$

Contrast with Square Roots

For real numbers:

$$ \sqrt{x^2}=|x|, $$

but:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[3]{x^3}=x }. $$

No absolute value is needed for cube roots because cubing is one-to-one on the real numbers.

Radical Notation

In:

$$ \sqrt[3]{a}, $$

the $3$ is called the index, and $a$ is called the radicand.

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[\text{index}]{\text{radicand}} }. $$

Exponent Notation

Cube roots may also be written using rational exponents:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[3]{a}=a^{1/3} } $$

for real $a$, with the real cube root intended.

General Rational Exponents

For appropriate real values:

$$ \boxed{ a^{m/3} = \left(\sqrt[3]{a}\right)^m }. $$

For example:

$$ 8^{2/3} = \left(\sqrt[3]{8}\right)^2 = 2^2 = \boxed{4}. $$

Product Property

For real numbers $a$ and $b$:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[3]{ab} = \sqrt[3]{a}\sqrt[3]{b} }. $$

Because cube roots are real for all real radicands, this identity has fewer real-domain restrictions than the analogous square-root identity.

Quotient Property

For real $a$ and nonzero real $b$:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[3]{\frac{a}{b}} = \frac{\sqrt[3]{a}}{\sqrt[3]{b}} }. $$

Simplifying Cube Roots

Perfect cube factors may be extracted from a radical.

For example:

$$ \sqrt[3]{54} = \sqrt[3]{27\cdot2}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[3]{54} = 3\sqrt[3]{2} }. $$

Variable Example

Consider:

$$ \sqrt[3]{x^7}. $$

Since:

$$ x^7=x^6x=(x^2)^3x, $$

one obtains:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[3]{x^7} = x^2\sqrt[3]{x} }. $$

No absolute value is required when extracting a perfect cube from an odd-index radical.

Solving Cubic Equations of the Form $x^3=a$

If:

$$ x^3=64, $$

applying the cube root to both sides gives:

$$ x=\sqrt[3]{64}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{x=4}. $$

No Plus-or-Minus in the Real Cube Root

Solving:

$$ x^3=8 $$

over the real numbers gives:

$$ \boxed{x=2}, $$

not:

$$ x=\pm2. $$

This differs from:

$$ x^2=4, $$

which has the two real solutions:

$$ x=\pm2. $$

Cube Root Function

The function:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=\sqrt[3]{x} } $$

has domain:

$$ \boxed{\mathbb{R}} $$

and range:

$$ \boxed{\mathbb{R}}. $$

Odd Symmetry

The cube-root function is odd:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[3]{-x} = -\sqrt[3]{x} }. $$

Therefore its graph has rotational symmetry about the origin.

Monotonicity

The cube-root function is strictly increasing on all real numbers:

$$ a<b \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sqrt[3]{a}<\sqrt[3]{b}. $$

Complex Cube Roots

Over the complex numbers, a nonzero complex number has three distinct cube roots.

For example, solving:

$$ z^3=1 $$

gives:

$$ \boxed{ z=1,\qquad z=-\frac12+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i,\qquad z=-\frac12-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i }. $$

Thus the real cube-root function selects the unique real root, while complex algebra recognizes all three solutions of a cubic equation such as $z^3=a$ when $a\neq0$.

Common Misconception: The radical $\sqrt[3]{a}$ denotes one value—the real cube root when $a$ is real. It should not be confused with the complete set of three complex solutions to $z^3=a$.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\sqrt[3]{8}$ "The cube root of eight."
$\sqrt[3]{x}$ "The cube root of x."
$x^{1/3}$ "X to the one-third power."
$\sqrt[3]{-27}=-3$ "The cube root of negative twenty-seven is negative three."
$(\sqrt[3]{x})^3=x$ "The cube of the cube root of x equals x."
$\sqrt[3]{x^3}=x$ "The cube root of x cubed equals x."
$\sqrt[3]{ab}$ "The cube root of a b."

The small $3$ in:

$$ \sqrt[3]{x} $$

is the index of the radical. It specifies that a cube root, rather than a square root or another root, is intended.

Unlike square-root notation, the index cannot ordinarily be omitted:

$$ \sqrt{x} = \sqrt[2]{x}, $$

but:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[3]{x} \neq \sqrt{x} } $$

in general.

The expression:

$$ \sqrt[3]{x^3} $$

simplifies to:

$$ \boxed{x}, $$

not $|x|$.

Defense Notation Advice: Do not attach a $\pm$ sign to the radical symbol itself. The notation:

$$ \sqrt[3]{8} $$

denotes the single real number $2$. If solving a polynomial equation over the complex numbers, list all roots separately.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Definition: $$ \boxed{ \sqrt[3]{a}=x \iff x^3=a } $$ for real $a$ and $x$.
  • Inverse Relationship: $$ \boxed{ \sqrt[3]{x^3}=x }. $$
  • Reverse Composition: $$ \boxed{ \left(\sqrt[3]{x}\right)^3=x }. $$
  • Rational Exponent Form: $$ \boxed{ \sqrt[3]{x}=x^{1/3} }. $$
  • Cube Root of One: $$ \boxed{\sqrt[3]{1}=1}. $$
  • Cube Root of Zero: $$ \boxed{\sqrt[3]{0}=0}. $$
  • Cube Root of Eight: $$ \boxed{\sqrt[3]{8}=2}. $$
  • Cube Root of Twenty-Seven: $$ \boxed{\sqrt[3]{27}=3}. $$
  • Cube Root of Sixty-Four: $$ \boxed{\sqrt[3]{64}=4}. $$
  • Cube Root of One Hundred Twenty-Five: $$ \boxed{\sqrt[3]{125}=5}. $$
  • Negative Radicand: $$ \boxed{ \sqrt[3]{-a} = -\sqrt[3]{a} }. $$
  • Example with Negative Number: $$ \boxed{ \sqrt[3]{-125}=-5 }. $$
  • Perfect Cube: A number is a perfect cube when it can be written: $$ \boxed{n^3} $$ for an integer $n$.
  • Positive Perfect Cubes: $$ 1,\;8,\;27,\;64,\;125,\;216,\ldots $$
  • Integer Perfect Cubes: Including negative values: $$ \ldots,-27,-8,-1,0,1,8,27,\ldots $$
  • Product Property: For real $a,b$: $$ \boxed{ \sqrt[3]{ab} = \sqrt[3]{a}\sqrt[3]{b} }. $$
  • Quotient Property: For real $a$ and nonzero real $b$: $$ \boxed{ \sqrt[3]{\frac{a}{b}} = \frac{\sqrt[3]{a}}{\sqrt[3]{b}} }. $$
  • Simplifying Radicals: $$ \sqrt[3]{16} = \sqrt[3]{8\cdot2} = \boxed{2\sqrt[3]{2}}. $$
  • Another Simplification: $$ \sqrt[3]{250} = \sqrt[3]{125\cdot2} = \boxed{5\sqrt[3]{2}}. $$
  • Variable Simplification: $$ \boxed{ \sqrt[3]{x^4} = x\sqrt[3]{x} }. $$
  • No Absolute Value: $$ \boxed{ \sqrt[3]{x^3}=x } $$ for all real $x$.
  • Square-Root Contrast: $$ \sqrt{x^2}=|x| $$ but: $$ \sqrt[3]{x^3}=x. $$
  • Domain of Cube-Root Function: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f)=\mathbb{R} } $$ for: $$ f(x)=\sqrt[3]{x}. $$
  • Range of Cube-Root Function: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Range}(f)=\mathbb{R} }. $$
  • Odd Function: $$ \boxed{ f(-x)=-f(x) }. $$
  • Increasing Function: The real cube-root function is strictly increasing on: $$ \boxed{\mathbb{R}}. $$
  • Inverse Function: $$ f(x)=x^3 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f^{-1}(x)=\sqrt[3]{x}. $$
  • Graph Reflection: The graphs of: $$ y=x^3 $$ and: $$ y=\sqrt[3]{x} $$ are reflections of one another across: $$ \boxed{y=x}. $$
  • Equation Solving: If: $$ x^3=a, $$ the unique real solution is: $$ \boxed{x=\sqrt[3]{a}}. $$
  • Translated Cubic Equation: If: $$ (x-h)^3=k, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ x=h+\sqrt[3]{k} }. $$
  • Geometry: For a cube of volume $V$, the side length is: $$ \boxed{ s=\sqrt[3]{V} }. $$
  • Measurement: If: $$ V=216\text{ cm}^3, $$ then: $$ s=\sqrt[3]{216}=6\text{ cm}. $$
  • Scaling: If the volume of a cube is multiplied by $k$, its side length is multiplied by: $$ \boxed{\sqrt[3]{k}}. $$
  • Dimensional Analysis: Taking a cube root of a cubic unit returns the corresponding linear unit: $$ \boxed{ \sqrt[3]{\text{cm}^3}=\text{cm} }. $$
  • Calculator Notation: Cube roots may be entered as: $$ \sqrt[3]{x} $$ or: $$ x^{1/3}, $$ although care is needed with negative inputs because some software interprets fractional powers through complex principal-value conventions.
  • Complex Algebra: Every nonzero complex number has exactly three distinct complex cube roots.
  • Cube Roots of Unity: The solutions of: $$ z^3=1 $$ are: $$ \boxed{ 1,\; -\frac12+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i,\; -\frac12-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i }. $$
  • Polar Form: If: $$ z=re^{i\theta}, $$ then its complex cube roots are: $$ \boxed{ \sqrt[3]{r}\, e^{i(\theta+2\pi k)/3}, \qquad k=0,1,2 }. $$
  • Equally Spaced Complex Roots: The three complex cube roots of a nonzero complex number are separated by: $$ \boxed{\frac{2\pi}{3}=120^\circ}. $$
  • Calculus: For: $$ f(x)=x^{1/3}, $$ $$ f'(x)=\frac{1}{3x^{2/3}} $$ for $x\neq0$.
  • Vertical Tangent: The graph: $$ y=\sqrt[3]{x} $$ has a vertical tangent at the origin.
  • Terminological Contrast: A cube is the third power $x^3$ or the corresponding geometric solid. A cube root reverses cubing: $$ \boxed{ x^3\longleftrightarrow\sqrt[3]{x} }. $$
  • Core Interpretation: The real cube root is the unique real inverse of the cubing operation: $$ \boxed{ \sqrt[3]{a}=x \iff x^3=a }. $$ Because cubing preserves order and sign on the real numbers, every real number has exactly one real cube root.
