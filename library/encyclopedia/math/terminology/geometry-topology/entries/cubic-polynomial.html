Cubic Polynomial

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

A cubic polynomial is a polynomial of degree exactly $3$. Its standard form is:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=ax^3+bx^2+cx+d, \qquad a\neq0 }. $$

The adjective cubic comes from cube, reflecting the highest power:

$$ \boxed{x^3}. $$

Just as a quadratic polynomial has highest exponent $2$, a cubic polynomial has highest exponent $3$.

The requirement:

$$ a\neq0 $$

is essential. If $a=0$, the cubic term disappears and the polynomial has degree at most $2$.

Cubic polynomials occupy an important position in algebra because they are the first polynomial family capable of having two distinct turning points and the first odd-degree family beyond linear polynomials.

Over the complex numbers, every cubic polynomial has exactly three roots counted with multiplicity, by the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra.

Operational Nuance

Standard Form

The general cubic polynomial is:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=ax^3+bx^2+cx+d, \qquad a\neq0 }. $$

The coefficients are:

  • $a$: leading coefficient,
  • $b$: quadratic coefficient,
  • $c$: linear coefficient,
  • $d$: constant term.

Degree

The degree is determined by the highest power having a nonzero coefficient:

$$ \boxed{\deg f=3}. $$

Example

$$ f(x)=2x^3-5x^2+7x-4 $$

is cubic because its highest nonzero power is:

$$ x^3. $$

Not Every Expression Containing $x^3$ Is Cubic

Consider:

$$ x^4+x^3+1. $$

This expression contains an $x^3$ term but is not cubic because its highest power is $4$.

Likewise:

$$ 0x^3+2x^2+1 $$

is not cubic because the coefficient of $x^3$ is zero.

Factored Form

If a cubic has roots $r_1,r_2,r_3$ over a field containing those roots, then:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=a(x-r_1)(x-r_2)(x-r_3) }. $$

Repeated roots are included according to multiplicity.

Example of Factoring

Consider:

$$ f(x)=x^3-6x^2+11x-6. $$

Factoring gives:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=(x-1)(x-2)(x-3) }. $$

Therefore the roots are:

$$ \boxed{1,\;2,\;3}. $$

Roots and Zeros

A number $r$ is a root or zero when:

$$ \boxed{f(r)=0}. $$

Equivalently:

$$ \boxed{ (x-r)\text{ is a factor of }f(x) }. $$

Number of Roots

Over the complex numbers, every cubic polynomial has exactly three roots counted with multiplicity:

$$ \boxed{ 3\text{ complex roots counted with multiplicity} }. $$

Over the real numbers, a cubic has either:

  • three real roots counted with multiplicity, or
  • one real root and one nonreal complex-conjugate pair.

At Least One Real Root

Every cubic polynomial with real coefficients has at least one real root.

This follows from its odd degree and opposite end behavior:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to\infty}f(x) \text{ and } \lim_{x\to-\infty}f(x) \text{ have opposite signs} }. $$

Multiplicity

A cubic may have repeated roots. For example:

$$ f(x)=(x-2)^2(x+1). $$

Here:

$$ x=2 $$

has multiplicity $2$, while:

$$ x=-1 $$

has multiplicity $1$.

Triple Root

A cubic can have one root of multiplicity $3$:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=a(x-r)^3 }. $$

Graph Behavior at Roots

At a simple root, the graph normally crosses the $x$-axis.

At a double root, it touches the axis and reverses direction.

At a triple root, it crosses the axis while flattening.

End Behavior

The leading term:

$$ ax^3 $$

determines the end behavior.

If:

$$ a>0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ x\to-\infty \Rightarrow f(x)\to-\infty } $$
$$ \boxed{ x\to\infty \Rightarrow f(x)\to\infty }. $$

If:

$$ a<0, $$

the directions reverse.

Turning Points

A degree-$3$ polynomial has at most:

$$ \boxed{2} $$

turning points.

This follows because:

$$ f'(x) $$

is quadratic and therefore has at most two real zeros.

Derivative

If:

$$ f(x)=ax^3+bx^2+cx+d, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ f'(x)=3ax^2+2bx+c }. $$

Second Derivative

Differentiating again:

$$ \boxed{ f''(x)=6ax+2b }. $$

Inflection Point

Since $a\neq0$, the equation:

$$ f''(x)=0 $$

has the unique solution:

$$ \boxed{ x=-\frac{b}{3a} }. $$

Every real cubic polynomial therefore has one inflection point.

Symmetry About the Inflection Point

The graph of every cubic polynomial is centrally symmetric about its inflection point.

After translating the inflection point to the origin, a cubic can be written in the depressed form:

$$ \boxed{ y=Au^3+Bu }. $$

This is an odd function of $u$, giving $180^\circ$ rotational symmetry about the translated origin.

Depressed Cubic

A general cubic equation:

$$ ax^3+bx^2+cx+d=0 $$

can be transformed by:

$$ x=t-\frac{b}{3a} $$

into a depressed cubic:

$$ \boxed{ t^3+pt+q=0 }. $$

This removes the quadratic term and forms the standard starting point for Cardano's formula.

Rational Root Theorem

For a cubic with integer coefficients:

$$ ax^3+bx^2+cx+d, $$

any rational root:

$$ \frac{p}{q} $$

in lowest terms must satisfy:

$$ \boxed{ p\mid d, \qquad q\mid a }. $$

Reducing a Cubic After Finding One Root

If one root $r$ is known, the Factor Theorem gives:

$$ f(x)=(x-r)Q(x), $$

where $Q(x)$ is quadratic.

The remaining roots can then be found using factoring, completing the square, or the quadratic formula.

Cubic Discriminant

For:

$$ f(x)=ax^3+bx^2+cx+d, $$

the discriminant is:

$$ \boxed{ \Delta = b^2c^2 -4ac^3 -4b^3d -27a^2d^2 +18abcd }. $$

For real coefficients:

  • $\Delta>0$: three distinct real roots,
  • $\Delta<0$: one real root and two nonreal conjugate roots,
  • $\Delta=0$: at least one repeated root.

Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

A cubic polynomial over $\mathbb{C}$ factors completely into three linear factors:

$$ \boxed{ f(x) = a(x-r_1)(x-r_2)(x-r_3) }. $$

The roots may coincide.

Complex-Conjugate Roots

If a cubic has real coefficients and a nonreal root:

$$ u+vi, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{u-vi} $$

is also a root.

Consequently a real cubic cannot have exactly two nonreal roots without also having one real root.

Common Misconception: "Cubic" means degree exactly $3$, not merely that an expression contains a cube somewhere. The coefficient of the $x^3$ term must be nonzero, and no higher-degree term may be present.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$ax^3+bx^2+cx+d$ "A x cubed plus b x squared plus c x plus d."
$a\neq0$ "A is not equal to zero."
$\deg f=3$ "The degree of f is three."
$f(r)=0$ "F of r equals zero."
$(x-r)^2$ "X minus r squared," indicating a root of multiplicity two.
$f'(x)=3ax^2+2bx+c$ "F prime of x equals three a x squared plus two b x plus c."
$\Delta$ "Delta," denoting the discriminant.

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{\text{"cubic polynomial"}} $$

refers to the polynomial expression or polynomial function of degree $3$.

The related phrase:

$$ \boxed{\text{"cubic equation"}} $$

usually refers to an equation formed by setting a cubic polynomial equal to zero or another expression.

For example:

$$ x^3-6x^2+11x-6 $$

is a cubic polynomial, while:

$$ x^3-6x^2+11x-6=0 $$

is a cubic equation.

Defense Notation Advice: Always retain the condition $a\neq0$ when presenting:

$$ ax^3+bx^2+cx+d $$

as the general cubic form. Without it, the expression may collapse to a quadratic, linear, or constant polynomial.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • General Form: $$ \boxed{ f(x)=ax^3+bx^2+cx+d, \qquad a\neq0 }. $$
  • Degree: $$ \boxed{\deg f=3}. $$
  • Leading Term: $$ \boxed{ax^3}. $$
  • Leading Coefficient: $$ \boxed{a}. $$
  • Constant Term: $$ \boxed{d}. $$
  • $y$-Intercept: Since: $$ f(0)=d, $$ the graph intersects the $y$-axis at: $$ \boxed{(0,d)}. $$
  • Root Criterion: $$ \boxed{ f(r)=0 } $$ means $r$ is a root.
  • Factor Theorem: $$ \boxed{ f(r)=0 \iff (x-r)\mid f(x) }. $$
  • Three Distinct Real Roots: A cubic may factor: $$ a(x-r_1)(x-r_2)(x-r_3) $$ with: $$ r_1,r_2,r_3\in\mathbb{R} $$ distinct.
  • One Real Root: A real cubic may have one real root and a pair of nonreal complex-conjugate roots.
  • Double Root: A factor: $$ (x-r)^2 $$ gives a root of multiplicity $2$.
  • Triple Root: A cubic of the form: $$ a(x-r)^3 $$ has one root of multiplicity $3$.
  • Fundamental Theorem of Algebra: Every cubic polynomial has exactly: $$ \boxed{3} $$ complex roots counted with multiplicity.
  • Real-Root Guarantee: Every cubic polynomial with real coefficients has at least one real root.
  • Complex-Conjugate Pair: For real coefficients: $$ u+vi\text{ root} \Longrightarrow u-vi\text{ root}. $$
  • Positive Leading Coefficient: If: $$ a>0, $$ then: $$ f(x)\to-\infty \text{ as }x\to-\infty $$ and: $$ f(x)\to\infty \text{ as }x\to\infty. $$
  • Negative Leading Coefficient: If: $$ a<0, $$ the end behavior is reversed.
  • Maximum Turning Points: A cubic has at most: $$ \boxed{2} $$ turning points.
  • Derivative: $$ \boxed{ f'(x)=3ax^2+2bx+c }. $$
  • Critical Numbers: Smooth cubic polynomials have critical numbers where: $$ 3ax^2+2bx+c=0. $$
  • Zero Critical Numbers: A cubic may have no real critical numbers, in which case it is strictly monotonic.
  • One Repeated Critical Number: The derivative may have one repeated real root, producing a stationary inflection point.
  • Two Critical Numbers: The derivative may have two distinct real roots, corresponding to one local maximum and one local minimum.
  • Second Derivative: $$ \boxed{ f''(x)=6ax+2b }. $$
  • Inflection Input: $$ \boxed{ x=-\frac{b}{3a} }. $$
  • Inflection Point: $$ \boxed{ \left( -\frac{b}{3a}, f\left(-\frac{b}{3a}\right) \right) }. $$
  • Central Symmetry: The graph of a cubic polynomial has $180^\circ$ rotational symmetry about its inflection point.
  • Depressed Cubic: The substitution: $$ x=t-\frac{b}{3a} $$ converts a general cubic equation into: $$ \boxed{ t^3+pt+q=0 }. $$
  • Rational Root Theorem: For integer coefficients, a rational root: $$ \frac{p}{q} $$ in lowest terms must satisfy: $$ p\mid d,\qquad q\mid a. $$
  • Synthetic Division: Once a root is found, synthetic division can reduce the cubic to a quadratic polynomial.
  • Polynomial Division: If: $$ f(r)=0, $$ then dividing by: $$ x-r $$ leaves a quadratic quotient and zero remainder.
  • Cubic Formula: General cubic equations can be solved algebraically using formulas developed in the sixteenth century and commonly associated with Cardano.
  • Casus Irreducibilis: Some cubics with three real roots require complex intermediate expressions when solved using Cardano's radical formula, even though all final roots are real.
  • Discriminant: For: $$ ax^3+bx^2+cx+d, $$ $$ \boxed{ \Delta= b^2c^2-4ac^3-4b^3d-27a^2d^2+18abcd }. $$
  • Positive Discriminant: For real coefficients: $$ \Delta>0 $$ means three distinct real roots.
  • Negative Discriminant: $$ \Delta<0 $$ means one real root and two nonreal complex-conjugate roots.
  • Zero Discriminant: $$ \Delta=0 $$ means the cubic has a repeated root.
  • Graph Intercepts: A real cubic graph crosses or touches the $x$-axis at its real roots.
  • Multiplicity One: At a simple real root, the graph crosses the $x$-axis.
  • Multiplicity Two: At a double real root, the graph touches the $x$-axis and turns.
  • Multiplicity Three: At a triple root, the graph crosses the axis with a flattened tangent.
  • Parent Function: The basic cubic parent function is: $$ \boxed{f(x)=x^3}. $$
  • Parent Function Symmetry: $$ f(-x)=-f(x) $$ for: $$ f(x)=x^3, $$ so the parent cubic is odd.
  • General Cubic Symmetry: A translated general cubic need not be odd about the origin, but it is centrally symmetric about its inflection point.
  • Transformation Form: A transformed simple cubic may be written: $$ \boxed{ f(x)=a(x-h)^3+k }, $$ whose inflection point is: $$ (h,k). $$
  • Translation: In: $$ a(x-h)^3+k, $$ $h$ shifts the parent graph horizontally and $k$ shifts it vertically.
  • Vertical Scaling: The coefficient $a$ vertically scales the graph and reflects it across the $x$-axis when: $$ a<0. $$
  • Algebraic Modeling: Cubic polynomials can model relationships whose rates of change are quadratic.
  • Geometry: Volume relationships can naturally produce cubic expressions, such as: $$ V=s^3 $$ for a cube.
  • Optimization: Cubic objective or constraint functions can produce local maxima and minima found through the quadratic derivative.
  • Calculus: Differentiating a cubic reduces it to a quadratic, while integrating a quadratic produces a cubic plus a constant.
  • Interpolation: A cubic polynomial can be determined uniquely by four independent interpolation conditions under appropriate circumstances.
  • Cubic Interpolation: Piecewise cubic polynomials are widely used in spline interpolation because they allow smooth curve construction with manageable derivatives.
  • Terminological Contrast: A cubic polynomial is an expression of degree $3$. A cubic function is the function defined by such a polynomial. A cubic equation is an equation involving a degree-$3$ polynomial.
  • Core Interpretation: A cubic polynomial is the degree-$3$ polynomial family: $$ \boxed{ ax^3+bx^2+cx+d, \qquad a\neq0 }. $$ Its odd degree guarantees at least one real zero for real coefficients, while its quadratic derivative permits at most two turning points.
