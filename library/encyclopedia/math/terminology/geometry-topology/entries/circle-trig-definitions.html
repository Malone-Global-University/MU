Circle Trig Definitions

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

Circle trigonometric definitions define the trigonometric functions using the coordinates of a point reached by rotating a radius through an angle $\theta$.

Suppose a circle of radius $r>0$ is centered at the origin and the terminal side of angle $\theta$ meets the circle at:

$$ P=(x,y). $$

Because $P$ lies on the circle:

$$ \boxed{ x^2+y^2=r^2 }. $$

The basic circular definitions are:

$$ \boxed{ \cos\theta=\frac{x}{r}, \qquad \sin\theta=\frac{y}{r} }. $$

From these, the remaining standard trigonometric functions are obtained by quotients and reciprocals.

On the unit circle, where $r=1$, the formulas simplify to:

$$ \boxed{ P=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta) }. $$

Operational Nuance

Standard-Position Angle

In the coordinate-plane formulation, an angle is commonly placed in standard position:

  • its vertex is at the origin,
  • its initial side lies on the positive $x$-axis,
  • its terminal side is reached by rotation through $\theta$.

Terminal Point

If the terminal side intersects a circle of radius $r$ at $P=(x,y)$, then the coordinates of $P$ determine the trigonometric values of $\theta$.

Sine

$$ \boxed{ \sin\theta=\frac{y}{r} }. $$

Sine is the terminal point's vertical coordinate divided by the radius.

Cosine

$$ \boxed{ \cos\theta=\frac{x}{r} }. $$

Cosine is the terminal point's horizontal coordinate divided by the radius.

Tangent

When $x\ne0$:

$$ \boxed{ \tan\theta = \frac{y}{x} = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} }. $$

Cosecant

When $y\ne0$:

$$ \boxed{ \csc\theta = \frac{r}{y} = \frac1{\sin\theta} }. $$

Secant

When $x\ne0$:

$$ \boxed{ \sec\theta = \frac{r}{x} = \frac1{\cos\theta} }. $$

Cotangent

When $y\ne0$:

$$ \boxed{ \cot\theta = \frac{x}{y} = \frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta} }. $$

The Six Circle Definitions Together

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \sin\theta&=\frac{y}{r},& \cos\theta&=\frac{x}{r},& \tan\theta&=\frac{y}{x},\\[4pt] \csc\theta&=\frac{r}{y},& \sec\theta&=\frac{r}{x},& \cot\theta&=\frac{x}{y}. \end{aligned} } $$

Unit-Circle Definitions

When:

$$ r=1, $$

the circle equation becomes:

$$ \boxed{ x^2+y^2=1 }. $$

The terminal point is:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y) = (\cos\theta,\sin\theta) }. $$

Hence:

$$ x=\cos\theta, \qquad y=\sin\theta. $$

Unit-Circle Tangent

On the unit circle:

$$ \boxed{ \tan\theta=\frac{y}{x} } $$

whenever $x\ne0$.

Why the Radius Cancels

Suppose the same terminal ray intersects circles of radii $r_1$ and $r_2$. The resulting right triangles are similar, so the coordinate-to-radius ratios are unchanged.

$$ \boxed{ \frac{x_1}{r_1} = \frac{x_2}{r_2}, \qquad \frac{y_1}{r_1} = \frac{y_2}{r_2} }. $$

Therefore sine and cosine depend on the angle, not on which circle radius is chosen.

Connection to Right-Triangle Trigonometry

In the first quadrant, the point $(x,y)$ and radius $r$ form a right triangle. Relative to angle $\theta$:

  • $x$ is the adjacent leg,
  • $y$ is the opposite leg,
  • $r$ is the hypotenuse.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \sin\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \cos\theta = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \tan\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}} }. $$

Why Circle Definitions Are More General

Right-triangle definitions naturally describe acute angles. Circle definitions extend the trigonometric functions to:

  • right angles,
  • obtuse angles,
  • reflex angles,
  • negative angles,
  • angles greater than one revolution.
$$ \boxed{ \theta\in\mathbb R } $$

can therefore be treated within one unified framework, subject to the domains of the individual trigonometric functions.

Positive and Negative Rotation

Under the usual convention:

$$ \boxed{ \theta>0 \Rightarrow \text{counterclockwise rotation} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \theta<0 \Rightarrow \text{clockwise rotation} }. $$

Quadrant Signs

Since sine and cosine are coordinate ratios, their signs follow the signs of $y$ and $x$.

Quadrant $x$ $y$ $\cos\theta$ $\sin\theta$ $\tan\theta$
I $+$ $+$ $+$ $+$ $+$
II $-$ $+$ $-$ $+$ $-$
III $-$ $-$ $-$ $-$ $+$
IV $+$ $-$ $+$ $-$ $-$

Reciprocal Signs

Since secant is the reciprocal of cosine, it has the same sign as cosine. Cosecant has the same sign as sine, and cotangent has the same sign as tangent.

Reference Angle

A reference angle is the acute angle between the terminal side and the $x$-axis.

It can be used to determine the magnitude of trigonometric values while the quadrant determines their signs.

Example in Quadrant II

If an angle in Quadrant II has reference angle $\alpha$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \sin\theta=\sin\alpha, \qquad \cos\theta=-\cos\alpha, \qquad \tan\theta=-\tan\alpha }. $$

Axis Angles

The circle definitions also make trigonometric values at quadrantal angles immediate.

Angle Unit-Circle Point $\cos\theta$ $\sin\theta$ $\tan\theta$
$0$ $(1,0)$ $1$ $0$ $0$
$\frac{\pi}{2}$ $(0,1)$ $0$ $1$ undefined
$\pi$ $(-1,0)$ $-1$ $0$ $0$
$\frac{3\pi}{2}$ $(0,-1)$ $0$ $-1$ undefined
$2\pi$ $(1,0)$ $1$ $0$ $0$

Undefined Values

The quotient definitions immediately reveal where some functions are undefined.

Tangent and secant require:

$$ \boxed{ x\ne0 } $$

or equivalently:

$$ \boxed{ \cos\theta\ne0 }. $$

Cotangent and cosecant require:

$$ \boxed{ y\ne0 } $$

or:

$$ \boxed{ \sin\theta\ne0 }. $$

Domain of Tangent and Secant

$$ \boxed{ \theta \ne \frac{\pi}{2}+k\pi, \qquad k\in\mathbb Z }. $$

Domain of Cotangent and Cosecant

$$ \boxed{ \theta \ne k\pi, \qquad k\in\mathbb Z }. $$

Pythagorean Identity

From:

$$ x^2+y^2=r^2, $$

divide by $r^2$:

$$ \frac{x^2}{r^2} + \frac{y^2}{r^2} = 1. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1 }. $$

Other Pythagorean Identities

Dividing the basic identity by $\cos^2\theta$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ 1+\tan^2\theta=\sec^2\theta }. $$

Dividing by $\sin^2\theta$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ 1+\cot^2\theta=\csc^2\theta }. $$

Periodicity from Circular Motion

Rotating by one full revolution returns to the same terminal point:

$$ \boxed{ \theta \text{ and } \theta+2\pi \text{ have the same terminal point} }. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ \sin(\theta+2\pi)=\sin\theta } $$
$$ \boxed{ \cos(\theta+2\pi)=\cos\theta }. $$

Tangent Period

Opposite points on the circle reverse both $x$ and $y$, leaving their ratio unchanged:

$$ \frac{-y}{-x}=\frac{y}{x}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \tan(\theta+\pi)=\tan\theta }. $$

Even and Odd Symmetry

Reflection across the $x$-axis sends:

$$ (x,y)\mapsto(x,-y). $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \cos(-\theta)=\cos\theta } $$
$$ \boxed{ \sin(-\theta)=-\sin\theta } $$
$$ \boxed{ \tan(-\theta)=-\tan\theta }. $$

Cosine as Horizontal Projection

On the unit circle, $\cos\theta$ is the horizontal coordinate of the terminal point.

Sine as Vertical Projection

On the unit circle, $\sin\theta$ is the vertical coordinate of the terminal point.

Tangent as Slope

When $x\ne0$, the slope of the terminal ray is:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{y}{x}=\tan\theta }. $$

Thus tangent naturally describes the slope associated with an angle in standard position.

Connection to the Tangent Line x = 1

In a classical unit-circle construction, extend the terminal ray until it meets the vertical tangent line $x=1$. The resulting vertical coordinate is $\tan\theta$ whenever the construction is defined.

Radian Measure

Circle trigonometry naturally pairs with radian measure.

If an angle $\theta$ subtends arc length $s$ on a circle of radius $r$:

$$ \boxed{ \theta=\frac{s}{r} }. $$

On the unit circle:

$$ \boxed{ s=\theta } $$

when $\theta$ is measured in radians.

Degrees versus Radians

$$ \boxed{ 180^\circ=\pi \text{ radians} } $$
$$ \boxed{ 360^\circ=2\pi \text{ radians} }. $$

Special-Angle Coordinates

Familiar right triangles can be placed inside the unit circle to obtain exact coordinates such as:

$$ \boxed{ \left( \frac{\sqrt3}{2}, \frac12 \right) } $$

for $\theta=\pi/6$, and:

$$ \boxed{ \left( \frac{\sqrt2}{2}, \frac{\sqrt2}{2} \right) } $$

for $\theta=\pi/4$.

Exact Values across the Circle

Reference angles and quadrant signs extend first-quadrant special-angle values to the rest of the circle.

Example: 150°

The reference angle is:

$$ 30^\circ. $$

Since $150^\circ$ lies in Quadrant II:

$$ \boxed{ \sin150^\circ=\frac12, \qquad \cos150^\circ=-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}, \qquad \tan150^\circ=-\frac1{\sqrt3} }. $$

Coterminal Angles

Angles that differ by whole revolutions have the same terminal side:

$$ \boxed{ \theta+2\pi k, \qquad k\in\mathbb Z }. $$

They therefore have the same sine and cosine values.

Circle Definitions and Function Graphs

As $\theta$ continuously rotates around the circle, the $y$-coordinate traces the sine function while the $x$-coordinate traces the cosine function.

$$ \boxed{ y=\sin\theta } $$
$$ \boxed{ x=\cos\theta }. $$

Circle Definitions and Rotation Matrices

Rotating the unit vector $(1,0)$ through angle $\theta$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta\\ \sin\theta \end{bmatrix} }. $$

This reveals sine and cosine as fundamental coordinates of planar rotation.

Complex-Number Connection

On the complex unit circle:

$$ \boxed{ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta }. $$

Thus circle trigonometry also connects directly to complex multiplication and rotation.

Common Misconception: Sine Is Always y

On a circle of general radius $r$:

$$ \sin\theta=\frac{y}{r}. $$

Only on the unit circle does:

$$ \boxed{ \sin\theta=y }. $$

Common Misconception: Cosine Is Always x

In general:

$$ \cos\theta=\frac{x}{r}. $$

Only for $r=1$ does $\cos\theta=x$.

Common Misconception: Trig Ratios Must Be Positive

Circle definitions include signed coordinates, so trigonometric values can be positive, negative, or zero according to the angle.

Common Misconception: Tangent Exists for Every Angle

Tangent is undefined when $x=0$, equivalently when $\cos\theta=0$.

Common Misconception: Circle Trig and Triangle Trig Are Different Functions

They are the same trigonometric functions. The circle definitions extend the right-triangle ratios to a larger angle domain.

Common Misconception: A Larger Angle Must Give a Larger Sine or Cosine

Sine and cosine are periodic coordinates on a circle, not globally increasing measures of angle.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\sin\theta=\frac{y}{r}$ "Sine theta equals the y-coordinate divided by the radius."
$\cos\theta=\frac{x}{r}$ "Cosine theta equals the x-coordinate divided by the radius."
$\tan\theta=\frac{y}{x}$ "Tangent theta equals y over x."
$(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ "The unit-circle point corresponding to theta."
$\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1$ "Sine squared theta plus cosine squared theta equals one."
$\theta+2\pi k$ "Angles coterminal with theta after adding whole revolutions."

The phrase circle trig definitions commonly refers to defining trigonometric functions through a terminal point on a circle, especially the unit circle. The phrase unit-circle definitions is more specific because it assumes radius $1$.

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish the general circle formulas $\cos\theta=x/r$ and $\sin\theta=y/r$ from their unit-circle simplifications $\cos\theta=x$ and $\sin\theta=y$. State whether angles are measured in degrees or radians when numerical angle values are involved, and check denominators before using tangent, secant, cotangent, or cosecant.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Setup: A terminal point $P=(x,y)$ lies on a circle of radius $r$ centered at the origin.
  • Circle Equation: $$ \boxed{ x^2+y^2=r^2 }. $$
  • Sine: $$ \boxed{ \sin\theta=\frac{y}{r} }. $$
  • Cosine: $$ \boxed{ \cos\theta=\frac{x}{r} }. $$
  • Tangent: $$ \boxed{ \tan\theta=\frac{y}{x} } $$ when $x\ne0$.
  • Cosecant: $$ \boxed{ \csc\theta=\frac{r}{y} } $$ when $y\ne0$.
  • Secant: $$ \boxed{ \sec\theta=\frac{r}{x} } $$ when $x\ne0$.
  • Cotangent: $$ \boxed{ \cot\theta=\frac{x}{y} } $$ when $y\ne0$.
  • Unit Circle: $$ \boxed{ (\cos\theta,\sin\theta) } $$ is the terminal point.
  • Right-Triangle Connection: In Quadrant I, the circle definitions reduce to the familiar opposite, adjacent, and hypotenuse ratios.
  • Extended Angle Domain: Circle definitions allow trigonometric functions to be evaluated for arbitrary real angles, subject to function-specific domain restrictions.
  • Quadrant Signs: Sine follows the sign of $y$, cosine follows the sign of $x$, and tangent follows the sign of $y/x$.
  • Pythagorean Identity: $$ \boxed{ \sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1 }. $$
  • Reciprocal Identities: $\csc\theta=1/\sin\theta$ and $\sec\theta=1/\cos\theta$ where defined.
  • Quotient Identities: $$ \boxed{ \tan\theta=\frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}, \qquad \cot\theta=\frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta} }. $$
  • Periodicity: Sine and cosine repeat every $2\pi$ radians.
  • Tangent Period: Tangent repeats every $\pi$ radians.
  • Radian Connection: On the unit circle, radian angle measure equals intercepted arc length numerically.
  • Rotation Connection: Sine and cosine are the coordinate components of a rotated unit vector.
  • Complex Connection: $$ \boxed{ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta }. $$
  • Common Error: Do not write $\sin\theta=y$ or $\cos\theta=x$ unless the circle has radius $1$.
  • Common Error: Do not assume trigonometric values remain positive outside the first quadrant.
  • Common Error: Do not divide by $x$ or $y$ when that coordinate is zero.
  • Common Error: Do not treat right-triangle and circle definitions as competing definitions; the circle model extends the same ratios to general angles.
  • Terminological Contrast: Circle trig definitions define trigonometric functions from the coordinates of a terminal point on a circle. Unit-circle definitions are the radius-$1$ specialization. Right-triangle definitions express sine, cosine, and tangent using side ratios for acute angles. Terminal side is the rotated ray determining the angle's final direction. Terminal point is where that ray meets the chosen circle. Reference angle is the acute angle between the terminal side and the $x$-axis. Coterminal angles share the same terminal side after differing by whole revolutions.
