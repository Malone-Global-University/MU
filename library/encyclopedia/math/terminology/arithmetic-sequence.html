Arithmetic Sequence

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

An arithmetic sequence is an ordered sequence in which the difference between each term and the preceding term is constant.

If:

$$ a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots $$

is an arithmetic sequence, then there is a constant $d$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ a_{n+1}-a_n=d }. $$

The constant $d$ is called the common difference.

The word arithmetic emphasizes the additive nature of the pattern: successive terms are produced by repeatedly adding the same amount.

In contemporary usage, arithmetic sequence and arithmetic progression are ordinarily synonymous. The word sequence emphasizes the ordered mathematical object, while progression emphasizes the step-by-step development of its terms.

Operational Nuance

Defining Property

The defining condition is:

$$ \boxed{ a_{n+1}-a_n=d }. $$

Every pair of consecutive terms must have the same difference.

Example

Consider:

$$ 3,\ 8,\ 13,\ 18,\ 23,\ldots $$

The consecutive differences are:

$$ 8-3=5, $$
$$ 13-8=5, $$
$$ 18-13=5. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ d=5 }. $$

Recursive Formula

An arithmetic sequence can be defined from one term to the next:

$$ \boxed{ a_{n+1}=a_n+d }. $$

Together with an initial value such as $a_1$, this determines all later terms.

Explicit Formula

The $n$th term is:

$$ \boxed{ a_n= a_1+(n-1)d }. $$

This formula allows a term to be found directly without calculating all preceding terms.

Why n - 1 Appears

The first term requires no additions of the common difference:

$$ a_1=a_1+0d. $$

The second term requires one:

$$ a_2=a_1+d. $$

The third requires two:

$$ a_3=a_1+2d. $$

Therefore reaching term $n$ requires $n-1$ equal steps.

Zero-Based Indexing

Some contexts begin with $a_0$. Then:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=a_0+nd }. $$

The difference between this and $a_n=a_1+(n-1)d$ is purely an indexing convention.

Finding an nth Term

Suppose:

$$ a_1=12, \qquad d=-4. $$

Then:

$$ a_7= 12+(7-1)(-4). $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ a_7=-12 }. $$

Finding the Common Difference

If two consecutive terms are known:

$$ \boxed{ d=a_{n+1}-a_n }. $$

If two nonconsecutive terms $a_m$ and $a_n$ are known:

$$ a_n-a_m=(n-m)d. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ d= \frac{a_n-a_m}{n-m} } $$

when $n\ne m$.

Finding the First Term

From:

$$ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d, $$

solve for $a_1$:

$$ \boxed{ a_1= a_n-(n-1)d }. $$

Finding the Number of Terms

For $d\ne0$:

$$ a_n-a_1=(n-1)d. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ n= 1+ \frac{a_n-a_1}{d} }. $$

In a finite sequence problem, the result must be a positive integer consistent with the stated indexing.

Increasing Arithmetic Sequence

If:

$$ d>0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a_{n+1}>a_n }. $$

Decreasing Arithmetic Sequence

If:

$$ d<0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a_{n+1}

Constant Arithmetic Sequence

If:

$$ d=0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a_1=a_2=a_3=\cdots }. $$

Constant sequences are therefore included among arithmetic sequences.

Arithmetic Mean Property

If:

$$ a_{n-1},a_n,a_{n+1} $$

are consecutive terms, then:

$$ a_n-a_{n-1} = a_{n+1}-a_n. $$

Rearranging:

$$ 2a_n= a_{n-1}+a_{n+1}. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ a_n= \frac{ a_{n-1}+a_{n+1} }{2} }. $$

Every interior term is the arithmetic mean of its immediate neighbors.

Inserting One Arithmetic Mean

To make:

$$ a,\ m,\ b $$

arithmetic, choose:

$$ \boxed{ m= \frac{a+b}{2} }. $$

Inserting Several Arithmetic Means

If $k$ arithmetic means are inserted between $a$ and $b$, there are $k+1$ equal steps between the endpoints.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ d= \frac{b-a}{k+1} }. $$

Terms Equally Spaced in Index

Arithmetic sequences have a more general averaging property. If indices are equally spaced:

$$ m-r,\quad m,\quad m+r, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a_m= \frac{ a_{m-r}+a_{m+r} }{2} }. $$

Symmetric Terms in a Finite Sequence

For:

$$ a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n, $$

terms equally far from the two ends have the same sum:

$$ \boxed{ a_1+a_n = a_2+a_{n-1} = a_3+a_{n-2} = \cdots }. $$

Finite Arithmetic Sequence

A finite arithmetic sequence has a final indexed term:

$$ \boxed{ a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n }. $$

Infinite Arithmetic Sequence

An infinite arithmetic sequence continues indefinitely:

$$ \boxed{ a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots }. $$

Limit of an Arithmetic Sequence

Since:

$$ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d, $$

if $d>0$:

$$ a_n\to+\infty, $$

while if $d<0$:

$$ a_n\to-\infty. $$

The sequence has a finite real limit only when:

$$ \boxed{ d=0 }. $$

Graph of an Arithmetic Sequence

Represent the sequence by plotting:

$$ \boxed{ (n,a_n) }. $$

Using:

$$ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d, $$

rewrite:

$$ a_n= dn+(a_1-d). $$

Therefore all sequence points lie on a straight line whose slope is:

$$ \boxed{ d }. $$

Discrete Linear Relationship

An arithmetic sequence is therefore naturally interpreted as a linear rule on a discrete domain:

$$ \boxed{ a(n)=dn+c, \qquad n\in\mathbb N }. $$

The expression is linear, but only selected integer inputs belong to the sequence's index set.

Constant First Difference

Define the forward difference:

$$ \Delta a_n= a_{n+1}-a_n. $$

For an arithmetic sequence:

$$ \boxed{ \Delta a_n=d }. $$

Zero Second Difference

Since the first differences are constant:

$$ \boxed{ \Delta^2a_n=0 }. $$

This provides another test for arithmetic structure.

Discrete Calculus Connection

Constant first difference is the discrete counterpart of constant derivative.

Compare:

$$ a_n=dn+c \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \Delta a_n=d $$

with:

$$ f(x)=dx+c \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad f'(x)=d. $$

Partial Sums

The sum of the first $n$ terms is:

$$ S_n= \sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k. $$

For an arithmetic sequence:

$$ \boxed{ S_n= \frac n2(a_1+a_n) }. $$

Alternative Partial-Sum Formula

Substituting:

$$ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d $$

gives:

$$ \boxed{ S_n= \frac n2 [2a_1+(n-1)d] }. $$

Partial Sums Are Quadratic

Expanding:

$$ S_n= \frac d2n^2 + \left( a_1-\frac d2 \right)n. $$

Thus when $d\ne0$:

$$ \boxed{ S_n \text{ is quadratic in }n }. $$

Average of the Terms

For a finite arithmetic sequence:

$$ \frac{S_n}{n} = \frac{a_1+a_n}{2}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \text{mean of all terms} = \frac{ \text{first term}+\text{last term} }{2} }. $$

Odd Number of Terms

When there are an odd number of terms, the middle term is also the mean of the entire sequence:

$$ \boxed{ a_{(n+1)/2} = \frac{a_1+a_n}{2} }. $$

Arithmetic Sequence versus Arithmetic Series

The sequence:

$$ 2,\ 5,\ 8,\ 11,\ldots $$

is an ordered list.

The corresponding series:

$$ 2+5+8+11+\cdots $$

is a sum.

$$ \boxed{ \text{sequence lists terms;} \qquad \text{series adds terms} }. $$

Arithmetic Sequence versus Geometric Sequence

Arithmetic sequences have constant difference:

$$ \boxed{ a_{n+1}-a_n=d }. $$

Geometric sequences have constant ratio:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}=r } $$

whenever the quotient is defined.

Additive versus Multiplicative Pattern

Arithmetic:

$$ a,\ a+d,\ a+2d,\ a+3d,\ldots $$

Geometric:

$$ a,\ ar,\ ar^2,\ ar^3,\ldots $$

Arithmetic sequences model constant additive change; geometric sequences model constant multiplicative change.

Arithmetic Sequence versus Linear Function

Both have a constant rate of change, but a sequence is normally indexed by discrete values:

$$ n=1,2,3,\ldots $$

whereas a linear function may be defined for every real input.

Arithmetic Sequence from a Linear Function

If:

$$ f(x)=mx+b, $$

then evaluating $f$ at consecutive integers gives:

$$ f(1),f(2),f(3),\ldots $$

with common difference:

$$ \boxed{ m }. $$

Translation of a Sequence

If:

$$ b_n=a_n+c, $$

then:

$$ b_{n+1}-b_n = a_{n+1}-a_n = d. $$

Therefore adding a constant to every term preserves the common difference.

Scaling a Sequence

If:

$$ b_n=ca_n, $$

then:

$$ b_{n+1}-b_n = cd. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ d_{\text{new}}=cd }. $$

Affine Transformation

More generally:

$$ b_n=ca_n+k $$

is arithmetic whenever $a_n$ is arithmetic.

$$ \boxed{ d_b=cd_a }. $$

Termwise Sum

If $a_n$ and $b_n$ are arithmetic with differences $d_a$ and $d_b$, then:

$$ c_n=a_n+b_n $$

is arithmetic with:

$$ \boxed{ d_c=d_a+d_b }. $$

Termwise Difference

$$ \boxed{ d_{a-b}=d_a-d_b }. $$

Real-World Interpretation

Arithmetic sequences model quantities that change by the same absolute amount over equal discrete steps.

Typical examples include:

  • rows containing a fixed additional number of seats,
  • payments increasing by a fixed dollar amount each year,
  • fixed-rate depreciation measured at equal time intervals,
  • equally spaced positions on a number line,
  • regular step patterns in algorithms.

Example: Seating Rows

Suppose the first row has $20$ seats and every later row has $3$ more seats than the preceding row.

Then:

$$ a_1=20, \qquad d=3. $$

The number of seats in row $n$ is:

$$ \boxed{ a_n= 20+3(n-1) }. $$

Common Misconception

A sequence is not arithmetic merely because it changes regularly in some informal sense.

For example:

$$ 2,\ 4,\ 8,\ 16,\ldots $$

follows a clear pattern, but its first differences are:

$$ 2,\ 4,\ 8,\ldots $$

and are not constant.

The defining test is:

$$ \boxed{ a_{n+1}-a_n = \text{constant} }. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$a_{n+1}-a_n=d$ "The difference between consecutive terms is d."
$a_{n+1}=a_n+d$ "The next term equals the current term plus the common difference."
$a_n=a_1+(n-1)d$ "The nth term equals the first term plus n minus one times the common difference."
$d=\frac{a_n-a_m}{n-m}$ "The common difference equals the change in term value divided by the change in index."
$\Delta a_n=d$ "The first difference of the sequence is constant and equal to d."
$S_n=\frac n2(a_1+a_n)$ "The sum of the first n terms equals n over two times the first term plus the nth term."

The notation $a_n$ is read a sub n or the nth term of the sequence. It does not mean multiplication of $a$ and $n$.

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish carefully between $a_n$, the value of one term, and $S_n$, the sum of the first $n$ terms. Also check whether the sequence begins at index $0$ or $1$ before selecting an explicit formula.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: $$ \boxed{ a_{n+1}-a_n=d }. $$
  • Common Difference: $d$ is the constant additive change from one term to the next.
  • Recursive Formula: $$ \boxed{ a_{n+1}=a_n+d }. $$
  • Explicit Formula: $$ \boxed{ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d }. $$
  • Zero-Based Explicit Formula: $$ \boxed{ a_n=a_0+nd }. $$
  • Difference from Two Terms: $$ \boxed{ d= \frac{a_n-a_m}{n-m} }. $$
  • First Term: $$ \boxed{ a_1= a_n-(n-1)d }. $$
  • Increasing Sequence: $$ \boxed{ d>0 }. $$
  • Decreasing Sequence: $$ \boxed{ d<0 }. $$
  • Constant Sequence: $$ \boxed{ d=0 }. $$
  • Arithmetic Mean Property: $$ \boxed{ a_n= \frac{ a_{n-1}+a_{n+1} }{2} }. $$
  • Equally Spaced Index Property: $$ \boxed{ a_m= \frac{ a_{m-r}+a_{m+r} }{2} }. $$
  • Inserted Mean: $$ \boxed{ m= \frac{a+b}{2} }. $$
  • k Inserted Means: $$ \boxed{ d= \frac{b-a}{k+1} }. $$
  • Symmetric-Term Property: $$ \boxed{ a_1+a_n = a_2+a_{n-1} = \cdots }. $$
  • First Difference: $$ \boxed{ \Delta a_n=d }. $$
  • Second Difference: $$ \boxed{ \Delta^2a_n=0 }. $$
  • Graphical Form: The points $(n,a_n)$ lie on a straight line.
  • Discrete Slope: $$ \boxed{ \frac{\Delta a}{\Delta n}=d }. $$
  • Linear-Function Form: $$ \boxed{ a_n=dn+c } $$ on a discrete index set.
  • Finite Sum: $$ \boxed{ S_n= \frac n2(a_1+a_n) }. $$
  • Alternative Sum: $$ \boxed{ S_n= \frac n2 [2a_1+(n-1)d] }. $$
  • Mean of a Finite Arithmetic Sequence: $$ \boxed{ \frac{S_n}{n} = \frac{a_1+a_n}{2} }. $$
  • Partial-Sum Degree: The partial sums form a quadratic sequence when $d\ne0$.
  • Infinite-Sequence Limit: A finite real limit exists only in the constant case $d=0$.
  • Arithmetic Progression: Usually synonymous with arithmetic sequence.
  • Arithmetic Series: A series sums the terms of an arithmetic sequence.
  • Geometric-Sequence Contrast: Arithmetic sequences have constant difference; geometric sequences have constant ratio.
  • Linear-Function Contrast: Arithmetic sequences usually have discrete domains; ordinary linear functions may have continuous real domains.
  • Translation: Adding the same constant to every term preserves the common difference.
  • Scaling: Multiplying every term by $c$ multiplies the common difference by $c$.
  • Affine Transformation: $$ \boxed{ b_n=ca_n+k } $$ preserves arithmetic-sequence form.
  • Termwise Addition: The termwise sum of arithmetic sequences is arithmetic.
  • Termwise Subtraction: The termwise difference of arithmetic sequences is arithmetic.
  • Real-World Use: Arithmetic sequences model constant additive change over equal discrete steps.
  • Common Error: A sequence is not arithmetic merely because it is increasing or decreasing.
  • Common Error: Constant percentage or constant ratio change is geometric rather than arithmetic.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse the nth-term formula $a_n$ with the partial-sum formula $S_n$.
  • Terminological Contrast: Arithmetic sequence is an ordered list with constant difference. Arithmetic progression is usually another name for the same object. Common difference is its fixed additive step. Arithmetic series is the sum of its terms. An arithmetic sequence is the discrete analogue of a linear relationship.
