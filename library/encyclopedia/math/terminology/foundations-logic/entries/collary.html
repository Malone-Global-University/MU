Corollary

Foundations & Mathematical Logic

Baseline Definition

A corollary is a mathematical result that follows readily from a theorem, proposition, or other previously established result. A corollary normally requires little additional argument because most of the necessary reasoning has already been supplied by the result from which it follows.

Suppose a mathematical theory \(T\) establishes a statement \(\phi\), and \(\phi\) readily implies another statement \(\psi\). Then \(\psi\) may be presented as a corollary of \(\phi\):

\[ T\vdash\phi, \qquad T\vdash(\phi\rightarrow\psi) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad T\vdash\psi. \]

The word corollary does not normally indicate a different level of logical truth. Once proved, a corollary is just as valid as a theorem. The label describes its relationship to an earlier result and its role in mathematical exposition.

1. Etymology & Roots

Corollary comes from the Latin corollarium, meaning an additional gift, gratuity, or reward. The word is related to corolla, meaning a small crown or garland.

The mathematical usage preserves the idea of receiving something extra. After the principal work of proving a theorem has been completed, another useful result may emerge with comparatively little additional effort. That supplementary consequence is called a corollary.

This historical metaphor remains unusually close to the modern mathematical meaning: the theorem performs the major logical work, while the corollary is an additional result obtained from it.

2. Operational Nuance

A Corollary Is a Proven Result

A corollary is not a guess, observation, or informal consequence. It is a mathematically justified conclusion. What distinguishes it from a theorem is primarily how the result is positioned relative to previously established mathematics.

The Proof Is Usually Short

A corollary frequently follows by direct substitution, specialization, simplification, application of a definition, or one or two additional deductions.

For example, the Pythagorean theorem states that a right triangle with legs \(a\) and \(b\) and hypotenuse \(c\) satisfies

\[ a^2+b^2=c^2. \]

If the triangle is also isosceles, then \(a=b\). Substitution gives

\[ a^2+a^2=c^2, \] \[ 2a^2=c^2, \] \[ c=a\sqrt{2}. \]

Therefore the statement “the hypotenuse of an isosceles right triangle is \(\sqrt{2}\) times the length of either leg” can be presented as a corollary of the Pythagorean theorem.

“Immediate” Does Not Necessarily Mean Zero Work

Corollaries are often described as results that follow immediately from a theorem. In mathematical writing, however, immediately usually means that no substantial new idea is required. A few algebraic steps or a short deduction may still be necessary.

Corollary vs. Theorem

A theorem is generally presented as a principal result deserving independent emphasis. A corollary is emphasized because it follows readily from another established result.

This distinction is largely organizational rather than logical. The same mathematical statement could be labeled a theorem in one exposition and a corollary in another depending on what has already been proved.

Corollary vs. Lemma

A lemma is usually proved in preparation for another result. A corollary usually appears after a result and records something that follows from it.

\[ \text{Lemma} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{Theorem} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{Corollary} \]

This diagram represents a common expository pattern rather than a mandatory hierarchy. Mathematics does not assign different truth values to lemmas, theorems, and corollaries.

Corollary vs. Proposition

A proposition is a proved statement that an author chooses not to designate as a major theorem. Unlike corollary, the word proposition does not necessarily indicate that the statement follows directly from a particular preceding result.

Common Misconception

A corollary is not necessarily unimportant. Some corollaries become more frequently used than the more general theorem from which they were derived. The label describes the logical and expository dependency of the result, not its practical importance.

3. Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Standard Written Form

In formal mathematical writing, a corollary is commonly displayed in the same manner as a theorem:

Corollary. The diagonal of a square with side length \(s\) is \(s\sqrt{2}\).

A proof may then be extremely brief:

Proof. Apply the Pythagorean theorem to the two perpendicular sides of the square: \[ d^2=s^2+s^2=2s^2, \] so \[ d=s\sqrt{2}. \] \(\square\)

Reference to a Numbered Result

Corollaries are frequently numbered and referenced explicitly:

Corollary 4.2. Every square is a rectangle.

This follows directly from Theorem 4.1.

Spoken Form

The word is pronounced approximately “KOR-uh-lair-ee.” In spoken mathematics, the dependency should normally be stated explicitly.

For example:

  • “This is a corollary of the Pythagorean theorem.”
  • “As an immediate corollary of Theorem four point one, every square is a rectangle.”
  • “The following corollary is obtained by setting \(x=0\) in the preceding theorem.”

When reading a labeled statement aloud, Corollary 3.7 is normally spoken as “Corollary three point seven.”

4. Case Usage & Field Variations

Geometry

Geometry frequently uses corollaries to extract specialized facts from broad theorems. Results concerning special triangles, quadrilaterals, circles, parallel lines, and transformations often arise by imposing additional conditions on a more general theorem.

Algebra

In algebra, a general theorem about equations, polynomials, groups, rings, vector spaces, or other structures may immediately yield corollaries for important special cases.

If a theorem establishes a result for every object in a broad class, restricting the theorem to a subclass can often produce a corollary.

Number Theory

Number theory commonly develops chains of consequences in which a major divisibility or congruence theorem produces several short corollaries. These may convert an abstract theorem into practical tests or computational rules.

Calculus and Analysis

In analysis, a general theorem may imply simpler results when stronger assumptions are imposed. A theorem about differentiable functions, for example, may produce a corollary for polynomials or other particularly well-behaved function classes.

Linear Algebra and Abstract Algebra

Structural theorems often generate numerous corollaries by restricting dimensions, imposing invertibility, assuming finite generation, or specializing a general algebraic object.

Logic and Proof Theory

In formal logic, the underlying idea is simply deductive consequence: once established premises or theorems entail another statement, that statement may be derived within the system.

The term corollary, however, remains largely an expository classification. Formal proof systems generally track whether a statement is derivable, not whether an author chooses to label the resulting theorem a lemma, proposition, or corollary.

General Pattern

\[ \boxed{ \text{Established result} + \text{short deduction or specialization} \longrightarrow \text{corollary} } \]

The essential idea is therefore one of logical inheritance: a corollary receives most of its justification from a result that has already been proved.
