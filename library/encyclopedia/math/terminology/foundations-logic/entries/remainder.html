Remainder

Subfield: Foundations & Logic

Etymology & Roots

A remainder is the amount left after one quantity has been divided into as many complete groups of another quantity as a specified division convention allows.

In Euclidean division, if an integer $a$ is divided by a positive integer $b$, then there exist unique integers $q$ and $r$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ a=bq+r, \qquad 0\leq r<b }. $$

Here $q$ is the quotient and $r$ is the remainder.

The ordinary word remainder means what remains or is left over. Its mathematical use preserves exactly that idea while making the allowable leftover precise.

Operational Nuance

Basic Example

Dividing $17$ by $5$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ 17=5(3)+2 }. $$

Therefore the quotient is $3$ and the remainder is $2$.

Remainder Restriction

In standard Euclidean division by positive $b$:

$$ \boxed{ 0\leq r<b }. $$

A proposed remainder equal to or larger than the divisor means another complete group can still be removed.

Exact Division

If the remainder is zero:

$$ a=bq, $$

then $b$ divides $a$ exactly:

$$ \boxed{ b\mid a \Longleftrightarrow r=0 }. $$

Remainders and Congruence

If $a$ has remainder $r$ when divided by $n$, then:

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv r\pmod n }. $$

Thus remainder arithmetic is the computational foundation of modular arithmetic.

Same Remainder

Two integers have the same remainder modulo $n$ exactly when their difference is divisible by $n$:

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv b\pmod n \Longleftrightarrow n\mid(a-b) }. $$

Possible Remainders

When dividing by a positive integer $n$, the Euclidean remainder must be one of:

$$ \boxed{ 0,1,2,\ldots,n-1 }. $$

Parity as a Remainder Statement

Every integer has remainder $0$ or $1$ when divided by $2$:

$$ \boxed{ n=2q \quad\text{or}\quad n=2q+1 }. $$

These cases correspond to even and odd integers.

Remainder after Addition

If:

$$ a\equiv r\pmod n, \qquad b\equiv s\pmod n, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a+b\equiv r+s\pmod n }. $$

The result may then be reduced to its standard remainder modulo $n$.

Remainder after Multiplication

$$ \boxed{ ab\equiv rs\pmod n }. $$

This allows large calculations to be simplified by replacing numbers with their remainders.

Negative Integers

Under the standard Euclidean convention with positive divisor $b$, the remainder is still chosen so that:

$$ 0\leq r<b. $$

For example:

$$ \boxed{ -7=5(-2)+3 }. $$

so the Euclidean remainder of $-7$ upon division by $5$ is $3$.

Programming Convention Caveat

Programming languages do not all use identical conventions for remainder or modulo when negative operands occur. Mathematical Euclidean remainder conventions should therefore not be assumed automatically in software.

Polynomial Division

Remainders also occur when dividing polynomials. If $P(x)$ is divided by a nonzero polynomial $D(x)$, then:

$$ \boxed{ P(x) = D(x)Q(x)+R(x) } $$

where:

$$ \boxed{ R(x)=0 \quad\text{or}\quad \deg R<\deg D }. $$

Remainder Theorem

When $P(x)$ is divided by $x-c$, the remainder is:

$$ \boxed{ P(c) }. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ P(x) = (x-c)Q(x)+P(c) }. $$

Factor Theorem Connection

The divisor $x-c$ is a factor of $P(x)$ exactly when the remainder is zero:

$$ \boxed{ P(c)=0 \Longleftrightarrow x-c\text{ is a factor of }P(x) }. $$

Remainder vs. Decimal Part

A remainder is not the same thing as the decimal portion of a quotient. For example:

$$ 17\div5 = 3\text{ remainder }2 = 3.4. $$

The remainder is $2$, while the fractional part of the decimal quotient is $0.4$.

Common Misconception

The remainder is not arbitrary leftover notation. In Euclidean division it is uniquely constrained by:

$$ \boxed{ 0\leq r<b }. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$17=5(3)+2$ "Seventeen divided by five has quotient three and remainder two."
$a=bq+r$ "A equals b times the quotient q plus the remainder r."
$0\leq r<b$ "The remainder is at least zero and less than the divisor b."
$17\equiv2\pmod5$ "Seventeen is congruent to two modulo five."
$P(x)=D(x)Q(x)+R(x)$ "P equals divisor times quotient plus remainder."
$P(c)$ "The remainder when P of x is divided by x minus c."

The notation:

$$ a\bmod n $$

is often used computationally for a remainder operation, while:

$$ a\equiv r\pmod n $$

is a congruence statement. These related notations should not be treated as interchangeable in every context.

Defense Notation Advice: When using integer division, state the divisor and the remainder convention if negative values could create ambiguity. For Euclidean division with positive divisor $b$, enforce $0\leq r<b$. In polynomial division, remember that the remainder must have degree strictly less than the divisor unless it is zero.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: Quantity left after division under a specified quotient-and-remainder convention.
  • Euclidean Division: $a=bq+r$ with $0\leq r<b$ for positive $b$.
  • Quotient: Counts complete divisor-sized groups.
  • Exact Division: Remainder $0$ means the divisor divides the dividend.
  • Possible Remainders: Modulo $n$, standard Euclidean remainders are $0$ through $n-1$.
  • Parity: Division by $2$ produces remainder $0$ or $1$.
  • Congruence: $a\equiv r\pmod n$ records a remainder class.
  • Modular Arithmetic: Addition and multiplication can be performed using remainders.
  • Negative Integers: Euclidean remainder remains nonnegative for positive divisors.
  • Programming: Remainder and modulo conventions may differ for negative operands.
  • Polynomial Division: $P=DQ+R$ with $\deg R<\deg D$ unless $R=0$.
  • Remainder Theorem: Division by $x-c$ leaves remainder $P(c)$.
  • Factor Theorem: Zero remainder means $x-c$ is a factor.
  • Number Theory: Remainders organize integers into residue classes.
  • Algorithms: The Euclidean algorithm repeatedly uses remainders to compute greatest common divisors.
  • Decimal Contrast: Remainder is not the same as the fractional part of a decimal quotient.
  • Common Error: Giving a remainder greater than or equal to the positive divisor.
  • Common Error: Treating remainder and quotient as interchangeable.
  • Common Error: Assuming a negative-dividend programming result always matches Euclidean remainder.
  • Common Error: Confusing $a\bmod n$ with the relational statement $a\equiv b\pmod n$.
  • Common Error: Allowing a polynomial remainder whose degree is at least the divisor's degree.
  • Core Interpretation: A remainder records what is left after complete divisor-sized groups are removed: $$ \boxed{ a=bq+r, \qquad 0\leq r<b }. $$ In integer arithmetic this leads directly to divisibility and congruence; in polynomial algebra the same structural pattern becomes: $$ \boxed{ P(x)=D(x)Q(x)+R(x), \qquad \deg R<\deg D }, $$ unless the polynomial remainder is zero.
