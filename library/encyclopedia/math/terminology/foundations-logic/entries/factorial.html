Factorial

Subfield: Foundations & Mathematical Logic

Etymology & Roots

A factorial is the product of a nonnegative integer and all of the positive integers below it. For a positive integer $n$:

$$ \boxed{ n!=n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots3\cdot2\cdot1 }. $$

The exclamation mark in:

$$ n! $$

is called factorial notation. It does not indicate surprise, emphasis, or exponentiation; it represents a specific product.

The terminology developed from earlier language for products of systematically changing factors. Louis François Antoine Arbogast used the term corresponding to factorial, and Christian Kramp adopted it. Kramp introduced the now-standard notation $n!$ in his 1808 work Éléments d'arithmétique universelle.

Factorials are especially important in combinatorics because $n!$ counts the number of ways to arrange $n$ distinct objects in order. The concept also appears throughout probability, algebra, number theory, calculus, and mathematical analysis.

Operational Nuance

For a positive integer $n$:

$$ n! = n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots2\cdot1. $$

For example:

$$ 5! = 5\cdot4\cdot3\cdot2\cdot1 $$ $$ =120. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{5!=120}. $$

The first several factorial values are:

$$ 0!=1, $$ $$ 1!=1, $$ $$ 2!=2, $$ $$ 3!=6, $$ $$ 4!=24, $$ $$ 5!=120, $$ $$ 6!=720. $$

Why 0! = 1

The special value:

$$ \boxed{0!=1} $$

is not arbitrary. It is required for the recursive pattern of factorials to remain consistent. Since:

$$ n!=n(n-1)!, $$

setting $n=1$ gives:

$$ 1!=1\cdot0!. $$

Because:

$$ 1!=1, $$

it follows that:

$$ 0!=1. $$

The same value also has a combinatorial interpretation: there is exactly one way to arrange zero objects—the empty arrangement.

Recursive Definition

The factorial can therefore be defined recursively by:

$$ \boxed{ 0!=1 } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ n!=n(n-1)! \qquad(n\ge1) }. $$

For example:

$$ 6!=6(5!) $$ $$ =6(120) $$ $$ =720. $$

Combinatorial Interpretation

Suppose $n$ distinct objects must be arranged in $n$ ordered positions. There are:

$$ n $$

choices for the first position:

$$ n-1 $$

for the second:

$$ n-2 $$

for the third, and so on. By the Counting Principle:

$$ n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots2\cdot1=n!. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ n! = \text{number of permutations of }n\text{ distinct objects} }. $$

Common Misconception: Factorial notation is not exponentiation. For example:

$$ 4!=24, $$

whereas:

$$ 4^2=16. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$n!$ "N factorial."
$5!$ "Five factorial."
$0!=1$ "Zero factorial equals one."
$n!=n(n-1)!$ "N factorial equals n times n minus one factorial."
$\frac{n!}{(n-r)!}$ "N factorial divided by n minus r factorial."
$\binom{n}{r}=\frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}$ "N choose r equals n factorial over r factorial times n minus r factorial."

Factorial notation has very high binding strength in ordinary mathematical writing. Thus:

$$ 2n! $$

normally means:

$$ 2(n!), $$

not:

$$ (2n)!. $$

Similarly:

$$ n!^2 $$

is conventionally interpreted as:

$$ (n!)^2. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Parentheses should be used when the factorial applies to an entire expression:

$$ (n+1)! $$

is different from:

$$ n+1!. $$

Since:

$$ 1!=1, $$

the latter simplifies merely to:

$$ n+1. $$

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Basic Definition: For $n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}$: $$ \boxed{ n!=\prod_{k=1}^{n}k }. $$ The empty-product convention gives: $$ 0!=1. $$
  • Recursive Identity: Factorials satisfy: $$ \boxed{ n!=n(n-1)! }. $$ This identity is frequently used to simplify expressions involving neighboring factorials.
  • Cancellation: For example: $$ \frac{8!}{6!} = \frac{8\cdot7\cdot6!}{6!} = 8\cdot7 = 56. $$ Expanding every factor is usually unnecessary.
  • Permutations: The number of arrangements of $n$ distinct objects is: $$ \boxed{n!}. $$
  • Partial Permutations: The number of ways to choose and arrange $r$ objects from $n$ distinct objects is: $$ \boxed{ P(n,r) = \frac{n!}{(n-r)!} }. $$
  • Combinations: The number of unordered selections of $r$ objects from $n$ distinct objects is: $$ \boxed{ \binom{n}{r} = \frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!} }. $$
  • Binomial Theorem: Factorials occur in the binomial coefficients: $$ \binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}, $$ which appear in: $$ (a+b)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} a^{n-k}b^k. $$
  • Multinomial Coefficients: If: $$ n_1+n_2+\cdots+n_k=n, $$ then the number of arrangements divided into groups of those sizes is: $$ \frac{n!}{n_1!n_2!\cdots n_k!}. $$
  • Repeated Objects: If $n$ objects contain indistinguishable repetitions with multiplicities: $$ n_1,n_2,\ldots,n_k, $$ then the number of distinct arrangements is: $$ \boxed{ \frac{n!}{n_1!n_2!\cdots n_k!} }. $$
  • Probability: Factorials occur throughout classical probability when sample spaces consist of arrangements or selections, including card hands, rankings, random permutations, and occupancy problems.
  • Taylor Series: Factorials appear in Taylor coefficients: $$ f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x-a)^n. $$ The factorial denominator compensates for repeated differentiation of powers.
  • Exponential Series: One of the most important appearances is: $$ \boxed{ e^x = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^n}{n!} }. $$
  • Sine and Cosine Series: Factorials also occur in: $$ \sin x = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (-1)^n \frac{x^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!} $$ and: $$ \cos x = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (-1)^n \frac{x^{2n}}{(2n)!}. $$
  • Rapid Growth: Factorials grow extremely quickly: $$ 10!=3{,}628{,}800 $$ and: $$ 20!=2{,}432{,}902{,}008{,}176{,}640{,}000. $$ Their growth eventually exceeds that of any fixed exponential $c^n$.
  • Stirling's Approximation: For large $n$: $$ \boxed{ n! \sim \sqrt{2\pi n} \left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^n }. $$ This provides an extremely useful approximation in probability, statistics, combinatorics, and asymptotic analysis.
  • Trailing Zeros: The number of trailing zeros in $n!$ is determined by the number of factors of $10=2\cdot5$. Because factors of $2$ are more abundant, one counts factors of $5$: $$ \boxed{ \left\lfloor\frac n5\right\rfloor + \left\lfloor\frac n{25}\right\rfloor + \left\lfloor\frac n{125}\right\rfloor +\cdots }. $$
  • Prime Exponents: More generally, the exponent of a prime $p$ in the prime factorization of $n!$ is given by Legendre's Formula: $$ \boxed{ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \left\lfloor\frac{n}{p^k}\right\rfloor }. $$
  • Divisibility: For integers: $$ 0\le m\le n, $$ one has: $$ m!\mid n!, $$ because all factors of $m!$ occur within the product defining $n!$.
  • Gamma Function: The factorial extends naturally beyond nonnegative integers through the Gamma function: $$ \boxed{ n!=\Gamma(n+1) } $$ for nonnegative integers $n$.
  • Noninteger Example: Since: $$ \Gamma\left(\frac12\right)=\sqrt{\pi}, $$ one obtains: $$ \left(-\frac12\right)! = \Gamma\left(\frac12\right) = \sqrt{\pi} $$ when factorial notation is extended through the Gamma function.
  • Negative Integers: Ordinary factorials are not defined for negative integers. The Gamma function also has poles at: $$ 0,-1,-2,-3,\ldots, $$ so it does not supply finite factorial values there.
  • Double Factorial: The notation: $$ n!! $$ does not mean: $$ (n!)!. $$ Instead, the double factorial multiplies every second integer: $$ n!!=n(n-2)(n-4)\cdots. $$
  • Examples of Double Factorials: $$ 7!! = 7\cdot5\cdot3\cdot1 = 105, $$ while: $$ 8!! = 8\cdot6\cdot4\cdot2 = 384. $$
  • Subfactorial: The notation: $$ !n $$ is sometimes used for the number of derangements of $n$ objects. This is called a subfactorial and should not be confused with the ordinary factorial $n!$.
  • Counting Principle Connection: The factorial formula: $$ n!=n(n-1)\cdots1 $$ is a direct application of the Fundamental Counting Principle: each selected object reduces the number of remaining choices by one.
  • Terminological Contrast: A factorial $n!$ multiplies every positive integer from $n$ down to $1$. A double factorial $n!!$ skips every other integer. A subfactorial $!n$ counts derangements, while the Gamma function provides a continuous extension satisfying $\Gamma(n+1)=n!$ at nonnegative integers.
