Parameter

Subfield: Foundations & Logic

Etymology & Roots

A parameter is a quantity used to specify, control, or distinguish members of a mathematical family, model, equation, or process. Its role is contextual: a parameter may vary from one instance of a problem to another while remaining fixed during a particular calculation.

$$ \boxed{ \text{parameter} = \text{quantity that selects or controls a mathematical instance} } $$

The term comes from Greek roots associated with measuring beside or alongside something. In mathematics, the word developed into a way of describing quantities that sit alongside the primary variables and help determine the form of an equation, function, curve, distribution, or model.

For example, in:

$$ y=mx+b, $$

the symbols $m$ and $b$ can be treated as parameters identifying a particular member of the family of affine functions.

$$ \boxed{ f_{m,b}(x)=mx+b } $$

Here $x$ is the input variable, while $m$ and $b$ determine which line in the family is under consideration.

This distinction between variable and parameter is not absolute. The same symbol can play different roles in different contexts.

$$ \boxed{ \text{parameterhood is a role, not an intrinsic property of a symbol} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Parameter vs. Variable

A variable is typically allowed to range through values within the immediate mathematical relationship. A parameter typically selects or controls the relationship itself.

In:

$$ y=ax^2+bx+c, $$

one common interpretation is:

  • $x$ is the independent variable;
  • $y$ is the dependent variable;
  • $a,b,c$ are parameters.

Fixed During One Instance

A parameter often remains fixed while solving a particular instance.

For example:

$$ f_a(x)=x^2+a $$

defines a family of functions. For $a=3$:

$$ f_3(x)=x^2+3. $$

During work with $f_3$, the value $3$ is fixed even though the family allows $a$ to vary across other members.

Parameter vs. Constant

A parameter may behave like a constant within a particular calculation, but the terms are not identical.

$$ \boxed{ \text{parameter} = \text{fixed within one instance, variable across a family} } $$

By contrast, a universal mathematical constant such as:

$$ \pi $$

ordinarily has a fixed value independent of which model or family member is chosen.

Parameter vs. Coefficient

A coefficient is a multiplier of a term. A parameter may be a coefficient, but a parameter can play other roles as well.

In:

$$ y=A\sin(\omega x+\phi)+C, $$

the quantities $A$, $\omega$, $\phi$, and $C$ are parameters, but not all are best described merely as coefficients.

Parameter as Family Index

A parameter can index a family:

$$ \boxed{ \{f_a:a\in S\} }. $$

Choosing a particular $a$ selects one member of the family.

One-Parameter Family

A family controlled by one parameter may be written:

$$ \boxed{ f_a(x) }. $$

Multi-Parameter Family

A family may depend on several parameters:

$$ \boxed{ f_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}(x) }. $$

Parameter Space

The set of all permitted parameter values is called the parameter space.

$$ \boxed{ \Theta = \{\text{allowed parameter values}\} }. $$

Example of a Parameter Space

For the family:

$$ f_{a,b}(x)=ax+b, $$

one natural parameter space is:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^2 }. $$

Restrictions on Parameters

Parameters often have domain restrictions.

For example, in:

$$ f(x)=a^x, $$

the standard real exponential-function parameter satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ a>0, \qquad a\neq1 } $$

when one specifically wants a nonconstant real exponential function.

Parameters in Equations

Parameters can control the number or type of solutions to an equation.

Consider:

$$ x^2+kx+1=0. $$

Here $k$ is a parameter.

Solution Behavior Depends on the Parameter

The discriminant is:

$$ \Delta = k^2-4. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ |k|>2 \Longrightarrow \text{two distinct real roots} } $$
$$ \boxed{ |k|=2 \Longrightarrow \text{one repeated real root} } $$
$$ \boxed{ |k|<2 \Longrightarrow \text{no real roots} }. $$

Parameter-Dependent Systems

A system of equations may contain parameters whose values determine whether the system is consistent, inconsistent, or has infinitely many solutions.

For example:

$$ \begin{aligned} x+y &= 2,\\ 2x+2y &= a. \end{aligned} $$

If:

$$ a=4, $$

the equations are dependent.

If:

$$ a\neq4, $$

they are inconsistent.

Parameter in Geometry

Geometric objects can be described by parameters.

The family of circles:

$$ \boxed{ (x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=r^2 } $$

contains parameters $h$, $k$, and $r$.

They control:

  • horizontal center coordinate $h$;
  • vertical center coordinate $k$;
  • radius $r$.

Parameter vs. Parametric Variable

In a parametric curve:

$$ x=x(t), \qquad y=y(t), $$

the symbol $t$ is also called a parameter.

Here its role differs somewhat from a family parameter. It moves through the curve rather than selecting a different curve from a family.

Parametric Representation

A curve may be represented as:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{r}(t) = \langle x(t),y(t)\rangle }. $$

Circle Parameterization

$$ \boxed{ x=r\cos t, \qquad y=r\sin t }. $$

Here $t$ traces points around the circle.

Two Uses of Parameter

It is useful to distinguish:

  • family parameters, which choose a mathematical object;
  • curve or process parameters, which locate a point or state within an object or evolution.

Parameterization

A parameterization is a representation of an object using one or more parameters.

Same Curve, Different Parameterizations

The same geometric curve can have multiple parameterizations.

For the unit circle:

$$ \mathbf{r}_1(t) = \langle\cos t,\sin t\rangle $$

and:

$$ \mathbf{r}_2(s) = \langle\cos(2s),\sin(2s)\rangle $$

trace the same circle, though at different parameter rates.

Parameter Is Not Necessarily Time

The symbol $t$ often suggests time, but a curve parameter need not represent physical time.

Time as a Parameter

In motion problems:

$$ \mathbf{r}(t) $$

may describe position as a function of actual time, making the parameter physically meaningful.

Parameters in Differential Equations

Differential equations often contain parameters that control system behavior.

For example:

$$ \frac{dy}{dt} = ky $$

contains parameter $k$.

Growth and Decay Parameter

The solution:

$$ y(t)=Ce^{kt} $$

behaves differently according to the sign of $k$:

$$ \boxed{ k>0 \Longrightarrow \text{growth} } $$
$$ \boxed{ k<0 \Longrightarrow \text{decay} }. $$

Initial-Condition Constants

Constants such as $C$ may themselves be treated as parameters of a solution family.

General Solution Family

A differential equation can produce:

$$ \boxed{ y_C(x) } $$

where $C$ indexes individual solutions.

Parameter in Trigonometric Models

In:

$$ y=A\sin(\omega x+\phi)+D, $$

parameters control distinct features.

Amplitude Parameter

$$ \boxed{ |A| = \text{amplitude} }. $$

Frequency Parameter

$\omega$ controls angular frequency and period:

$$ \boxed{ T = \frac{2\pi}{|\omega|} } $$

when $\omega\neq0$.

Phase Parameter

$\phi$ controls phase shift together with $\omega$.

Vertical-Shift Parameter

$D$ shifts the graph vertically.

Parameter in Probability

Probability distributions are commonly indexed by parameters.

For example, a normal distribution may be written:

$$ \boxed{ X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2) }. $$

Here $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ are parameters of the distribution.

Population Parameter

In statistics, a parameter often means a numerical characteristic of a population or probability model.

Examples include:

$$ \boxed{ \mu,\qquad \sigma^2,\qquad p }. $$

Parameter vs. Statistic

A parameter describes the population or model, while a statistic is computed from sample data.

$$ \boxed{ \text{parameter} \longleftrightarrow \text{population/model} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{statistic} \longleftrightarrow \text{sample} }. $$

Example

Population mean:

$$ \mu $$

is a parameter, while sample mean:

$$ \bar{x} $$

is a statistic.

Unknown Parameter

A parameter may be conceptually fixed but numerically unknown.

This distinction is fundamental in statistics:

$$ \boxed{ \text{fixed} \neq \text{known} }. $$

Parameter Estimation

Data may be used to estimate unknown model parameters.

$$ \boxed{ \widehat{\theta} \approx \theta } $$

where $\theta$ is the parameter and $\widehat{\theta}$ is an estimator or estimate, depending on context.

Parameter Vector

Multiple parameters are often collected into:

$$ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\theta} = \begin{bmatrix} \theta_1\\ \vdots\\ \theta_p \end{bmatrix} }. $$

Optimization Parameters

Optimization models may distinguish decision variables from fixed model parameters.

For example:

$$ \min_x \left( ax^2+bx \right) $$

may treat $x$ as the decision variable and $a,b$ as parameters.

Parametric Optimization

If a model depends on $\theta$:

$$ \boxed{ \min_x f(x;\theta) } $$

then the optimal solution may itself depend on the parameter:

$$ \boxed{ x^\ast=x^\ast(\theta) }. $$

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analysis studies how outputs or optimal solutions change as parameters change.

Model Parameters

In mathematical modeling, parameters often represent fixed characteristics of a system, such as:

  • growth rates;
  • material constants;
  • reaction rates;
  • probabilities;
  • initial quantities;
  • geometric dimensions.

State Variables vs. Parameters

A dynamical model may distinguish:

$$ \boxed{ \text{state variables} = \text{quantities evolving within the model} } $$

from:

$$ \boxed{ \text{parameters} = \text{quantities controlling the evolution law} }. $$

Parameter Dependence

A mathematical object may be written:

$$ \boxed{ F(x;\theta) } $$

where the semicolon visually distinguishes ordinary variables from parameters.

Semicolon Convention

In:

$$ f(x;\theta), $$

$x$ is typically the main argument and $\theta$ is a parameter.

This notation is conventional rather than compulsory.

Subscript Convention

Another common notation is:

$$ \boxed{ f_\theta(x) }. $$

The subscript emphasizes that different values of $\theta$ select different functions.

Parameter in Linear Algebra

Solution sets containing free variables are often expressed using parameters.

For example:

$$ x+y=4 $$

may be written:

$$ \boxed{ x=4-t, \qquad y=t } $$

where $t\in\mathbb{R}$ parameterizes the solution set.

Free Variable as Parameter

A free variable can serve as a parameter for infinitely many solutions.

Vector Form of a Parameterized Solution

$$ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} x\\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4\\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + t \begin{bmatrix} -1\\ 1 \end{bmatrix} }. $$

Parameters in Lines and Planes

A line in space may be parameterized by:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{p} + t\mathbf{v} }. $$

Plane Parameterization

A plane may require two parameters:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{r}(s,t) = \mathbf{p} + s\mathbf{u} + t\mathbf{v} }. $$

Number of Parameters and Dimension

In regular parameterizations, the number of independent parameters often reflects the dimension of the object.

$$ \boxed{ \text{curve} \longrightarrow 1\text{ parameter} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{surface} \longrightarrow 2\text{ parameters} }. $$

Parameter Does Not Necessarily Equal Dimension

Redundant parameterizations can use more parameters than necessary, so parameter count alone does not universally determine intrinsic dimension.

Implicit vs. Parametric Description

A circle can be described implicitly:

$$ x^2+y^2=r^2 $$

or parametrically:

$$ x=r\cos t, \qquad y=r\sin t. $$

Eliminating a Parameter

In some parameterizations, the parameter can be eliminated to obtain a direct relation between the coordinates.

For:

$$ x=2t+1, \qquad y=3t-4, $$

solve:

$$ t = \frac{x-1}{2} $$

and substitute:

$$ \boxed{ y = \frac{3}{2}(x-1)-4 }. $$

Reparameterization

A parameter can sometimes be replaced by a new one without changing the underlying geometric object.

Orientation under Parameterization

Replacing $t$ by $-t$ may reverse the direction in which a curve is traced.

Speed under Parameterization

A different parameterization can trace the same curve at a different speed.

Arc-Length Parameter

A regular curve can sometimes be parameterized by arc length $s$, giving:

$$ \boxed{ \left\| \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{ds} \right\| = 1 }. $$

Parameter in Calculus

Parameters may be treated as constants when differentiating with respect to another variable.

If:

$$ f(x;a)=ax^2, $$

then differentiation with respect to $x$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2ax }. $$

Here $a$ is held fixed.

Differentiation with Respect to a Parameter

The same expression may also be differentiated with respect to $a$:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\partial f}{\partial a} = x^2 }. $$

Thus parameter status depends on which variables are currently being varied.

Partial Derivatives and Parameters

Multivariable notation makes role changes especially explicit: one quantity is varied while others are temporarily held fixed.

Parameter-Dependent Integrals

An integral may define a function of a parameter:

$$ \boxed{ F(a) = \int_0^1 f(x,a)\,dx }. $$

Differentiation under the Integral Sign

Under suitable hypotheses:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{da} \int f(x,a)\,dx = \int \frac{\partial f}{\partial a}(x,a)\,dx }. $$

This illustrates a parameter becoming an active differentiation variable.

Eigenvalue Problems with Parameters

Matrices can depend on a parameter:

$$ A(\lambda). $$

In:

$$ \det(A-\lambda I)=0, $$

$\lambda$ is traditionally called an eigenvalue variable during the equation-solving process, and later a specific eigenvalue once chosen. Terminological roles can shift during a calculation.

Bifurcation Parameters

In dynamical systems, changing a parameter can qualitatively alter system behavior.

Such a controlling quantity is often called a bifurcation parameter.

Example

For a parameterized differential equation:

$$ \frac{dx}{dt} = f(x;\mu), $$

different values of $\mu$ may create or destroy equilibria or alter their stability.

Dimensionless Parameters

Applied mathematics frequently combines physical quantities into dimensionless parameters that organize system behavior.

Parameter as Control Knob

A useful conceptual model is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{variable} = \text{quantity moving inside a model} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{parameter} = \text{control knob selecting or shaping the model} }. $$

This analogy is useful but should not be mistaken for a formal universal definition.

Parameter Can Become a Variable

If one studies how a solution changes as a parameter changes, that parameter becomes an active variable of the higher-level analysis.

For example:

$$ x^\ast(\theta) $$

treats $\theta$ as the independent argument of the solution map.

Parameter Can Be Unknown

Parameters do not have to be given numerically.

In:

$$ y=mx+b, $$

$m$ and $b$ may be unknown parameters to be estimated from data.

Parameter Can Be Known

Once values are specified, parameters may become ordinary constants in the current problem.

Parameter Can Be Discrete

Parameters need not vary continuously.

A family:

$$ \boxed{ f_n(x)=x^n, \qquad n\in\mathbb{N} } $$

is indexed by the discrete parameter $n$.

Parameter Can Be Categorical

In broader modeling contexts, a parameter may select among finitely many structural cases, though numerical mathematics often reserves the term for quantitative values.

Parameter Can Be a Vector or Matrix

A parameter need not be scalar.

$$ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^p } $$

or even a parameter matrix may determine a model.

Hyperparameter Distinction

In statistics and machine learning, a hyperparameter is typically a quantity controlling the fitting procedure or model structure rather than being estimated in the same way as ordinary model parameters.

Parameter Identifiability

A parameter is identifiable when distinct permitted parameter values produce distinguishable model behavior in the relevant observational sense.

Nonidentifiable Parameters

If:

$$ f_{\theta_1}(x) = f_{\theta_2}(x) $$

for different parameter values $\theta_1\neq\theta_2$, the parameterization may fail to be identifiable.

Redundant Parameterization

A model can contain more parameters than are independently needed to specify the underlying object.

Parameter Constraints

Constraints may be imposed to guarantee uniqueness, validity, or physical meaning.

Examples include:

$$ \sigma>0, \qquad 0\leq p\leq1, \qquad r>0. $$

Parameter Values and Boundary Cases

Special parameter values may produce degenerate cases.

For example:

$$ y=ax^2+bx+c $$

is quadratic only when:

$$ \boxed{ a\neq0 }. $$

At $a=0$, the family drops to a linear or constant equation.

Parameter Thresholds

Critical parameter values can mark transitions between qualitatively different cases.

Piecewise Parameter Analysis

Problems often require separating cases such as:

$$ \boxed{ a<0,\qquad a=0,\qquad a>0 }. $$

Parameter Substitution

Once a parameter value is chosen, it can be substituted just like any other fixed quantity.

Symbol Choice

Parameters are commonly denoted by letters such as:

$$ \boxed{ a,b,c,k,\lambda,\mu,\theta,\alpha,\beta }. $$

No letter is inherently a parameter.

Common Error: Treating Parameter as a Permanent Type of Symbol

A symbol is a parameter because of its role in a particular context, not because of the letter chosen.

Common Error: Saying a Parameter Cannot Vary

A parameter is often fixed within one instance but may vary across the family being studied.

Common Error: Saying a Parameter Must Be Known

Parameters can be fixed but unknown, especially in statistics and modeling.

Common Error: Treating Parameter and Constant as Exact Synonyms

A parameter can function as a constant locally while remaining variable across instances.

Common Error: Treating Parameter and Coefficient as Exact Synonyms

Some parameters are coefficients, but parameters can also occur inside exponents, arguments, limits, boundaries, matrices, and nonlinear expressions.

Common Error: Assuming Every Letter Other Than $x$ Is a Parameter

Mathematical roles are established by definitions and context, not by letter names.

Common Error: Confusing a Parameter with a Free Variable

A free variable can be used as a parameter for a solution set, but the terms are not universally interchangeable.

Common Error: Confusing Parameterization with Family Parameters

In $\mathbf{r}(t)$, $t$ may locate points along one curve. In $f_a(x)$, $a$ may select different functions. Both are called parameters, but their operational roles differ.

Common Error: Assuming the Parameter Must Represent Time

The traditional symbol $t$ does not force a temporal interpretation.

Common Error: Assuming Different Parameterizations Give Different Curves

Distinct parameterizations can trace the same geometric set.

Common Error: Assuming One Parameter Value Gives One Point in Every Context

A family parameter may select an entire function, equation, distribution, or model, not merely one point.

Common Error: Ignoring Parameter Restrictions

Parameter values outside the permitted parameter space may invalidate the model or change its mathematical type.

Common Error: Confusing a Population Parameter with a Sample Statistic

In statistics, $\mu$ and $\bar{x}$ have fundamentally different roles even if one is used to estimate the other.

Common Error: Assuming Estimated Parameters Are Exact

An estimate such as $\widehat{\theta}$ is inferred from data and generally carries uncertainty.

Common Error: Assuming More Parameters Always Produce a Better Model

Additional parameters can increase flexibility but may also create redundancy, overfitting, or identifiability problems.

Common Error: Forgetting Degenerate Parameter Values

Special values can reduce degree, dimension, rank, or other structural properties.

Common Misconception: A parameter is not simply "a constant" and not simply "another variable." It is a contextual role: a parameter typically controls or selects a mathematical object while remaining fixed during one instance of analysis. Across the larger family, however, the parameter may vary, and in higher-level analysis that same parameter may itself become the variable of interest.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$f_a(x)$ "F sub a of x," indicating a family indexed by parameter a.
$f(x;a)$ "F of x with parameter a."
$(a,b)\in\Theta$ "A and b belong to the parameter space theta."
$\mathbf{r}(t)$ "R of t, where t parameterizes the curve or motion."
$X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ "X is normally distributed with parameters mu and sigma squared."
$\widehat{\theta}$ "Theta hat," usually denoting an estimate or estimator of parameter theta.
$x^\ast(\theta)$ "The optimal solution x star as a function of the parameter theta."

In introductory mathematics, the phrase "treat $a$ as a parameter" usually means that $a$ should be held fixed while another variable is manipulated.

For example:

$$ y=ax^2 $$

may be described as:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"a family of parabolas parameterized by }a\text{."} } $$

The notation:

$$ f(x;\theta) $$

is especially useful when one wants to distinguish an ordinary input from a model parameter without introducing a different mathematical object.

Defense Notation Advice: State what is being held fixed and what is allowed to vary. Use notation such as $f_\theta(x)$ or $f(x;\theta)$ when the distinction matters. Do not infer parameter status from the letter alone. In statistical work, distinguish an unknown parameter $\theta$ from an estimate $\widehat{\theta}$. In parametric geometry, distinguish the parameter that traces an object from parameters that choose different members of a family.

  • Family Selection: A parameter can choose one member from a family of objects.
  • One-Parameter Family: Often written $f_a(x)$.
  • Multi-Parameter Family: May be written $f_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}(x)$.
  • Parameter Space: Set of all permitted parameter values.
  • Parameter Restriction: Values may be limited by algebraic, geometric, probabilistic, or physical conditions.
  • Variable Contrast: Variable usually ranges within the immediate relation; parameter controls the relation.
  • Constant Contrast: Parameter may be fixed locally but variable across a family.
  • Coefficient Contrast: Some parameters are coefficients, but not all parameters are coefficients.
  • Affine Function: $m$ and $b$ may parameterize the family $y=mx+b$.
  • Quadratic Family: $a,b,c$ parameterize $y=ax^2+bx+c$.
  • Degenerate Quadratic Case: $a=0$ changes the equation's degree.
  • Equation Classification: Parameters may determine the number or type of solutions.
  • Discriminant Analysis: Parameter values can divide a problem into solution cases.
  • Systems of Equations: Parameters may determine rank, consistency, and multiplicity of solutions.
  • Geometry: Parameters may control position, scale, orientation, or shape.
  • Circle Family: $h,k,r$ parameterize center and radius.
  • Parametric Curve: A parameter may locate points along a curve.
  • Curve Parameter: Often written $t$.
  • Time Parameter: May represent physical time, but need not.

  • Reparameterization: Changes the parameter rule without necessarily changing the geometric object.
  • Orientation: Parameter direction can determine traversal direction.
  • Speed: Different parameterizations may trace the same curve at different rates.
  • Arc-Length Parameter: Gives unit-speed parameterization when available.
  • Line Parameterization: $\mathbf{r}(t)=\mathbf{p}+t\mathbf{v}$.
  • Plane Parameterization: $\mathbf{r}(s,t)=\mathbf{p}+s\mathbf{u}+t\mathbf{v}$.
  • Dimension Heuristic: Regular curves often use one parameter and surfaces two.
  • Redundant Parameterization: May use more parameters than intrinsic dimension requires.

  • General Solution: Integration constants can parameterize solution families.
  • Trigonometric Models: Amplitude, frequency, phase, and offset are commonly parameters.

  • Dynamical Systems: Parameters control evolution laws.
  • Bifurcation Parameter: Parameter whose variation changes qualitative dynamics.
  • State Variable Contrast: States evolve; parameters commonly control the governing rule.
  • Dimensionless Parameter: Combines quantities into unit-free control values.
  • Calculus: Parameters may be held fixed during differentiation with respect to another variable.
  • Partial Derivative: A parameter can itself become the differentiation variable.
  • Parameter-Dependent Integral: Integral may define a function of a parameter.
  • Linear Algebra: Free variables can parameterize solution sets.
  • Vector Solution Form: Parameters multiply direction vectors in affine solution sets.
  • Discrete Parameter: May range over integers or another discrete set.
  • Vector Parameter: Parameter need not be scalar.
  • Matrix Parameter: Entire matrices can function as parameters.
  • Semicolon Notation: $f(x;\theta)$ distinguishes primary arguments from parameters.
  • Subscript Notation: $f_\theta(x)$ emphasizes a parameter-indexed family.
  • Hyperparameter: Often controls model structure or fitting procedure rather than functioning as an ordinary fitted parameter.
  • Identifiability: Distinct parameter values should produce distinguishable model behavior when identification is required.
  • Nonidentifiability: Different parameter values may encode the same model behavior.

  • Core Interpretation: A parameter is best understood by its role: $$ \boxed{ \text{parameter} = \text{quantity that selects, controls, or describes a mathematical instance} }. $$ In a family such as: $$ \boxed{ f_a(x)=x^2+a } $$ $x$ varies within one function while $a$ selects which function is being used. In a parametric curve: $$ \boxed{ \mathbf{r}(t) } $$ the parameter instead locates a point or state along the object. In statistics, parameters such as: $$ \boxed{ \mu,\sigma^2,p } $$ characterize an underlying population or model. The unifying idea is not that a parameter is permanently fixed, but that it occupies a different logical level from the primary variables in the current mathematical description.
