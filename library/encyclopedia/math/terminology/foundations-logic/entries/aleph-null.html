Aleph Null

Subfield: Foundations & Mathematical Logic

Etymology & Roots

Aleph null, written $\aleph_0$, is the cardinal number representing the size of every countably infinite set.

$$ \boxed{ \aleph_0=|\mathbb{N}| }. $$

The symbol $\aleph$ is the Hebrew letter aleph, the first letter of the Hebrew alphabet. Georg Cantor adopted aleph notation for infinite cardinal numbers during the development of modern set theory in the late nineteenth century.

The subscript zero indicates that $\aleph_0$ is the first and smallest aleph:

$$ \boxed{ \aleph_0<\aleph_1<\aleph_2<\cdots }. $$

The name is pronounced most commonly as aleph null, though aleph zero and aleph naught are also encountered.

The essential idea is not that $\aleph_0$ is merely "a very large number." Rather, it measures the cardinality of a particular kind of infinite set:

$$ \boxed{ \text{countably infinite} \Longleftrightarrow \text{cardinality }\aleph_0 }. $$

Operational Nuance

Cardinality

The cardinality of a set describes how many elements the set has. For finite sets this is ordinary counting. For infinite sets, cardinality is compared through one-to-one correspondences called bijections.

Two sets $A$ and $B$ have the same cardinality when there exists a bijection:

$$ \boxed{ f:A\to B }. $$

Definition of Aleph Null

A set $S$ has cardinality $\aleph_0$ precisely when its elements can be put into one-to-one correspondence with the natural numbers:

$$ \boxed{ |S|=\aleph_0 \iff S\sim\mathbb{N} }. $$

Informally, this means the members of $S$ can be arranged into an infinite list:

$$ s_1,s_2,s_3,\ldots $$

with every element appearing somewhere on the list.

The Natural Numbers

The canonical example is:

$$ \mathbb{N} = \{0,1,2,3,\ldots\}. $$

Whether a convention begins $\mathbb{N}$ with $0$ or $1$ does not affect its cardinality:

$$ \boxed{ |\mathbb{N}|=\aleph_0 }. $$

The Even Natural Numbers

Consider:

$$ E=\{0,2,4,6,\ldots\}. $$

At first glance $E$ appears to contain only "half" the natural numbers. Nevertheless:

$$ f(n)=2n $$

defines a bijection:

$$ \mathbb{N}\longleftrightarrow E. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ |E|=\aleph_0 }. $$

This illustrates one of the central differences between finite and infinite cardinality: an infinite set can have the same cardinality as a proper subset of itself.

The Integers

The integers:

$$ \mathbb{Z} = \{\ldots,-3,-2,-1,0,1,2,3,\ldots\} $$

can be listed, for example, as:

$$ 0,1,-1,2,-2,3,-3,\ldots $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ |\mathbb{Z}|=\aleph_0 }. $$

The Rational Numbers

The rational numbers are also countably infinite:

$$ \boxed{ |\mathbb{Q}|=\aleph_0 }. $$

This may initially seem surprising because between any two distinct rational numbers there are infinitely many additional rational numbers. Density on the number line does not imply uncountability.

Algebraic Numbers

The set of algebraic numbers is also countably infinite:

$$ \boxed{ |\overline{\mathbb{Q}}|=\aleph_0 } $$

when $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ denotes the algebraic numbers in $\mathbb{C}$. Thus even the collection of all roots of all nonzero integer-coefficient polynomials remains countable.

The Real Numbers Are Larger

Cantor's diagonal argument shows that the real numbers cannot be placed in one-to-one correspondence with $\mathbb{N}$.

$$ \boxed{ |\mathbb{R}|>\aleph_0 }. $$

The cardinality of the real numbers is called the cardinality of the continuum and is commonly denoted:

$$ \boxed{ \mathfrak{c} = |\mathbb{R}| = 2^{\aleph_0} }. $$

Aleph Null Is the Smallest Infinite Cardinal

There is no infinite cardinal smaller than $\aleph_0$:

$$ \boxed{ n<\aleph_0 \qquad \text{for every finite cardinal }n }. $$

The next aleph, $\aleph_1$, is by definition the next larger well-ordered cardinal after $\aleph_0$.

Cardinal Addition

Combining a countably infinite set with a finite set does not increase its cardinality:

$$ \boxed{ \aleph_0+n=\aleph_0 } $$

for every finite cardinal $n$.

Likewise:

$$ \boxed{ \aleph_0+\aleph_0=\aleph_0 }. $$

Two countably infinite collections can still be combined into one countably infinite collection.

Cardinal Multiplication

A Cartesian product of two countably infinite sets remains countably infinite:

$$ \boxed{ \aleph_0\cdot\aleph_0 = \aleph_0 }. $$

For example:

$$ |\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}|=\aleph_0. $$

Exponentiation Is Different

Cardinal exponentiation behaves differently. The set of all infinite binary sequences has cardinality:

$$ \boxed{ 2^{\aleph_0} = \mathfrak{c} > \aleph_0 }. $$

Thus exponentiation can move from countable infinity to an uncountable cardinality.

Common Misconception: All infinite sets are not the same size. $\mathbb{N}$ and $\mathbb{R}$ are both infinite, but:

$$ \boxed{ |\mathbb{N}|=\aleph_0 < |\mathbb{R}|=\mathfrak{c} }. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\aleph_0$ "Aleph null," "aleph zero," or "aleph naught."
$|\mathbb{N}|=\aleph_0$ "The natural numbers have cardinality aleph null."
$|S|=\aleph_0$ "S is countably infinite."
$|\mathbb{Z}|=|\mathbb{Q}|=\aleph_0$ "The integers and rationals are both countably infinite."
$\mathfrak{c}=2^{\aleph_0}$ "The cardinality of the continuum equals two to the aleph-null power."
$\aleph_0<\aleph_1$ "Aleph null is less than aleph one."

The word countable requires some contextual care. In some textbooks it means:

$$ \boxed{ \text{finite or countably infinite} }, $$

while other authors reserve countable for sets of cardinality exactly $\aleph_0$ and use at most countable for the broader category.

The phrase countably infinite removes this ambiguity:

$$ \boxed{ \text{countably infinite} = \text{cardinality exactly }\aleph_0 }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: The symbol $\aleph_0$ represents a cardinality, not a particular infinite set. The sets $\mathbb{N}$, $\mathbb{Z}$, and $\mathbb{Q}$ are different sets, but all have cardinality $\aleph_0$.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Definition: $$ \boxed{ \aleph_0 = |\mathbb{N}| }. $$
  • Countably Infinite: A set has cardinality $\aleph_0$ exactly when it can be put in bijection with $\mathbb{N}$.
  • Smallest Infinite Cardinal: $\aleph_0$ is the least infinite cardinal number.
  • Natural Numbers: $$ \boxed{ |\mathbb{N}|=\aleph_0 }. $$
  • Positive Integers: $$ |\{1,2,3,\ldots\}|=\aleph_0. $$
  • Even Integers: $$ |\{0,2,4,6,\ldots\}|=\aleph_0. $$
  • Odd Integers: $$ |\{1,3,5,7,\ldots\}|=\aleph_0. $$
  • Integers: $$ \boxed{ |\mathbb{Z}|=\aleph_0 }. $$
  • Rational Numbers: $$ \boxed{ |\mathbb{Q}|=\aleph_0 }. $$
  • Algebraic Numbers: The set of algebraic numbers is countably infinite.
  • Finite Strings: Over any finite or countable alphabet, the collection of all finite strings is at most countable and is countably infinite when arbitrarily long strings exist.
  • Finite Sequences of Natural Numbers: The set of all finite sequences of natural numbers is countably infinite.
  • Ordered Pairs: $$ \boxed{ |\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}| = \aleph_0 }. $$
  • Finite Cartesian Powers: For every positive integer $n$: $$ \boxed{ |\mathbb{N}^n| = \aleph_0 }. $$
  • Finite Union: A finite union of countable sets is countable.
  • Countable Union: In standard set theory, a countable union of countable sets is countable, with the usual choice assumptions relevant in the most general formulation.
  • Proper Subsets: A countably infinite set may have a proper subset of the same cardinality.
  • Infinite-Set Behavior: This contrasts with finite sets, where a proper subset always has strictly smaller cardinality.
  • Finite Addition: For finite $n$: $$ \boxed{ \aleph_0+n=\aleph_0 }. $$
  • Countable Addition: $$ \boxed{ \aleph_0+\aleph_0=\aleph_0 }. $$
  • Finite Multiplication: For every positive finite $n$: $$ \boxed{ n\aleph_0=\aleph_0 }. $$
  • Countable Multiplication: $$ \boxed{ \aleph_0\cdot\aleph_0 = \aleph_0 }. $$
  • Finite Positive Powers: For every positive integer $n$: $$ \boxed{ \aleph_0^n=\aleph_0 }. $$
  • Countable Sequences: The set of all sequences of natural numbers has cardinality: $$ \boxed{ \aleph_0^{\aleph_0} = 2^{\aleph_0} = \mathfrak{c} }. $$
  • Power Set: Cantor's theorem gives: $$ \boxed{ |\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})| = 2^{\aleph_0} > \aleph_0 }. $$
  • Real Numbers: $$ \boxed{ |\mathbb{R}| = \mathfrak{c} = 2^{\aleph_0} }. $$
  • Irrational Numbers: The irrational numbers are uncountable and have cardinality $\mathfrak{c}$.
  • Transcendental Numbers: The transcendental real numbers are uncountable and have cardinality $\mathfrak{c}$.
  • Complex Numbers: $$ |\mathbb{C}|=\mathfrak{c}. $$
  • Aleph One: $\aleph_1$ denotes the next larger aleph after $\aleph_0$.
  • Continuum Hypothesis: The Continuum Hypothesis asserts: $$ \boxed{ 2^{\aleph_0}=\aleph_1 }. $$ It states that no cardinal lies strictly between the cardinality of the natural numbers and the cardinality of the real numbers.
  • Independence of the Continuum Hypothesis: Within the standard axiomatic framework ZFC, the Continuum Hypothesis can neither be proved nor disproved, assuming ZFC itself is consistent.
  • Cardinal vs. Ordinal: $\aleph_0$ is a cardinal number describing size. The ordinal $\omega$ represents the order type of the natural numbers. They are closely related but conceptually distinct.
  • Cardinality vs. Arrangement: Cardinality ignores the order in which elements are arranged. Two sets may have different structures or orderings but the same cardinality.
  • Bijection Criterion: To prove: $$ |A|=\aleph_0, $$ one standard strategy is to construct a bijection: $$ f:\mathbb{N}\to A. $$
  • Enumeration: Another common method is to explicitly list the elements: $$ a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots $$ without omission.
  • Density Is Irrelevant: A set can be dense in $\mathbb{R}$ and still be countable. The rational numbers provide the standard example.
  • Infinity Is Not a Single Cardinality: Mathematical set theory recognizes multiple sizes of infinity.
  • Terminological Contrast: Aleph null $\aleph_0$ is the cardinality of the countably infinite sets. Aleph one $\aleph_1$ is the next larger aleph. The continuum $\mathfrak{c}=2^{\aleph_0}$ is the cardinality of $\mathbb{R}$ and is strictly larger than $\aleph_0$.
