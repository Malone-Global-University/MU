Restricted Function

Subfield: Foundations & Logic

Etymology & Roots

A restricted function is a function obtained by keeping the same output rule while limiting the original domain to a specified subset.

If:

$$ f:X\to Y $$

and $A\subseteq X$, then the restriction of $f$ to $A$ is written:

$$ \boxed{ f|_A:A\to Y } $$

and satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ f|_A(x)=f(x) \qquad \text{for every }x\in A }. $$

The word restricted refers to narrowing where the function is allowed to receive inputs. The function rule itself is not changed on the retained inputs.

Operational Nuance

Restriction Changes the Function

In modern function terminology, a function includes its domain as part of its definition. Therefore changing the domain produces a different function, even when the formula remains identical.

For example:

$$ f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}, \qquad f(x)=x^2 $$

and:

$$ g:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}, \qquad g(x)=x^2 $$

use the same formula but are not the same function because their domains differ.

Restriction Notation

$$ \boxed{ g=f|_A } $$

means that $g$ agrees with $f$ on $A$ and is defined only for inputs in $A$.

Restriction and Graphs

Graphically, restricting a function means retaining only those points whose horizontal coordinates lie in the restricted domain.

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Graph}(f|_A) = \{ (x,f(x)):x\in A \} }. $$

Making a Function One-to-One

A major use of restriction is to remove repeated output behavior. The squaring function is not one-to-one on $\mathbb{R}$ because:

$$ f(-x)=f(x). $$

Restricting to:

$$ \boxed{ [0,\infty) } $$

makes the function one-to-one.

Restricted Functions and Inverses

For:

$$ f(x)=x^2, \qquad x\geq0, $$

the inverse is:

$$ \boxed{ f^{-1}(x)=\sqrt{x} }. $$

If instead the squaring function is restricted to $x\leq0$, its inverse is:

$$ \boxed{ f^{-1}(x)=-\sqrt{x} }. $$

Thus different restrictions may produce different inverse branches.

Trigonometric Restrictions

Periodic trigonometric functions are commonly restricted to intervals on which they become one-to-one. For example, the standard inverse sine function is built from:

$$ \boxed{ \sin|_{[-\pi/2,\pi/2]} }. $$

Similarly, inverse cosine uses the standard restriction:

$$ \boxed{ \cos|_{[0,\pi]} }. $$

Restriction and Range

Restricting the domain may shrink the range because fewer inputs remain available.

For:

$$ f(x)=x^2 $$

restricted to $[-2,1]$, the range becomes:

$$ \boxed{ [0,4] }. $$

Restriction and Codomain

Restricting a function's domain does not automatically change its declared codomain. If:

$$ f:X\to Y, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ f|_A:A\to Y } $$

ordinarily retains $Y$ unless a new codomain is explicitly declared.

Restriction vs. Corestriction

A restriction changes the domain. A corestriction, when that terminology is used, changes the declared codomain while preserving the same input-output assignments.

$$ \boxed{ \text{restriction} \Rightarrow \text{domain change} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{corestriction} \Rightarrow \text{codomain change} }. $$

Restriction vs. Formula Domain

A function may also have inputs excluded because its defining expression is not meaningful there. Such exclusions determine the formula's natural domain rather than constituting an optional restriction.

For example:

$$ f(x)=\frac{1}{x-2} $$

necessarily excludes $x=2$. Restricting it further to $x>2$ is an additional chosen restriction.

Restriction in Piecewise Definitions

Each branch of a piecewise function may be viewed informally as a formula applied on a restricted part of the total domain:

$$ f(x) = \begin{cases} x^2, & x<0,\\ x+1, & x\geq0. \end{cases} $$

Restriction in Modeling

An applied model may be restricted to the physically meaningful inputs. A population model, for example, might be considered only for:

$$ \boxed{ t\geq0 }. $$

The restriction may reflect interpretation rather than algebraic necessity.

Restriction Preserves Existing Values

A restriction does not alter the original function value for any retained input:

$$ \boxed{ x\in A \Longrightarrow f|_A(x)=f(x) }. $$

Nested Restrictions

If $B\subseteq A\subseteq X$, then:

$$ \boxed{ (f|_A)|_B=f|_B }. $$

Common Misconception

Restricting a function does not mean changing its algebraic formula. It means narrowing the set of inputs on which the same original assignment is being used.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$f|_A$ "The restriction of f to A."
$f|_A:A\to Y$ "F restricted to A is a function from A to Y."
$f|_A(x)=f(x)$ "The restricted function agrees with f on A."
$\sin|_{[-\pi/2,\pi/2]}$ "Sine restricted to the interval from negative pi over two to pi over two."
$A\subseteq\operatorname{Dom}(f)$ "A is a subset of the domain of f."
$(f|_A)|_B=f|_B$ "Restricting first to A and then to B is the same as restricting directly to B," when B is contained in A.

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"restrict }f\text{ to }A } $$

means keep the same values $f(x)$ but allow only inputs $x\in A$.

Defense Notation Advice: Treat the domain as part of the function, not merely as a side condition. When writing $f|_A$, verify that $A\subseteq\operatorname{Dom}(f)$. Do not confuse restriction of the domain with changing the codomain, changing the formula, or merely excluding points where the original formula was already undefined.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: Same function rule considered on a smaller domain.
  • Standard Notation: $f|_A$.
  • Domain Requirement: $A\subseteq\operatorname{Dom}(f)$.
  • Value Preservation: $f|_A(x)=f(x)$ for every $x\in A$.
  • Function Identity: Changing the domain produces a different function in formal function theory.
  • Graph Interpretation: Retains only graph points with inputs in the restricted set.
  • Range: May become smaller after restriction.
  • Codomain: Usually remains unchanged unless explicitly redefined.
  • Inverse Functions: Restriction is often used to obtain one-to-one behavior.
  • Squaring Function: Restricting to $[0,\infty)$ yields the principal square-root inverse.
  • Trigonometry: Principal inverse functions depend on standard restricted intervals.
  • Piecewise Functions: Individual formulas operate on restricted portions of the domain.
  • Modeling: Restrictions may represent physical, temporal, or contextual limits.
  • Natural-Domain Contrast: Natural exclusions arise from where a formula fails to be defined.
  • Corestriction Contrast: Restriction changes domain; corestriction changes codomain.
  • Nested Restriction: $(f|_A)|_B=f|_B$ when $B\subseteq A$.
  • Analysis: Functions are frequently restricted to intervals, neighborhoods, or subsets for local study.
  • Topology: Continuous maps remain continuous when restricted to subspaces of their domains.
  • Linear Algebra: Linear transformations may be restricted to vector subspaces of their domains.
  • Common Error: Thinking restriction changes the function formula.
  • Common Error: Confusing restriction with deletion of naturally undefined inputs.
  • Common Error: Assuming the range stays unchanged after restricting the domain.
  • Common Error: Confusing domain restriction with codomain restriction.
  • Common Error: Forgetting that a changed domain formally creates a different function.
  • Core Interpretation: If $f:X\to Y$ and $A\subseteq X$, then the restriction: $$ \boxed{ f|_A:A\to Y } $$ preserves the original assignments: $$ \boxed{ f|_A(x)=f(x) \qquad \text{for }x\in A }. $$ Restricted functions are fundamental for defining inverse branches, studying local behavior, interpreting piecewise rules, and adapting functions to structural or applied domains.
