Base

Subfield: Foundations & Mathematical Logic

Etymology & Roots

Base is a highly context-dependent mathematical term. Across mathematics, it generally identifies a foundational, reference, repeated, or supporting quantity or object from which another mathematical structure is built.

Common uses include:

  • the repeated factor in exponentiation,
  • the reference number in a logarithm,
  • the radix of a numeral system,
  • a selected side of a triangle or polygon,
  • the congruent parallel face of a prism or cylinder,
  • the distinguished face opposite the apex of a cone or pyramid.
$$ \boxed{ \text{meaning of base} = \text{meaning determined by mathematical context} }. $$

The unifying idea is not a single formula but a structural role: the base is the element relative to which some larger construction, measurement, or operation is organized.

Operational Nuance

Base in Exponentiation

In an exponential expression:

$$ \boxed{ a^n }, $$

$a$ is the base and $n$ is the exponent.

For a positive integer exponent:

$$ a^n = \underbrace{ a\cdot a\cdot\ldots\cdot a }_{n\text{ factors}}. $$

Thus the base is the quantity repeatedly multiplied.

Example of an Exponential Base

$$ 5^3=5\cdot5\cdot5=125. $$

Here:

$$ \boxed{ \text{base}=5, \qquad \text{exponent}=3 }. $$

Base May Be an Expression

The base need not be a single numeral.

In:

$$ (x+2)^4, $$

the entire expression:

$$ \boxed{ x+2 } $$

is the base.

Parentheses Matter

Compare:

$$ (-3)^2=9 $$

with:

$$ -3^2=-9. $$

In the first expression, the base is $-3$. In the second, the exponent applies to $3$ before the leading negative sign is applied.

Base in Exponential Functions

In:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=a^x }, $$

$a$ is the exponential base.

For real-valued elementary exponential functions, one typically takes:

$$ \boxed{ a>0, \qquad a\ne1 }. $$

Growth and Decay

For:

$$ f(x)=a^x, $$

if:

$$ a>1, $$

the function exhibits exponential growth.

If:

$$ 0

the function exhibits exponential decay.

The Natural Base e

The constant:

$$ \boxed{ e\approx2.71828 } $$

is especially important in calculus because:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}e^x=e^x }. $$

Base in a Logarithm

In:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b x }, $$

$b$ is the base of the logarithm.

The logarithm answers:

$$ \boxed{ b^y=x \iff \log_b x=y }. $$

Valid Real Logarithm Bases

For the ordinary real logarithm:

$$ \boxed{ b>0, \qquad b\ne1 }. $$

Common Logarithm

The common logarithm uses base $10$:

$$ \boxed{ \log_{10}x }. $$

Natural Logarithm

The natural logarithm uses base $e$:

$$ \boxed{ \ln x=\log_e x }. $$

Binary Logarithm

Base $2$ is especially common in computer science:

$$ \boxed{ \log_2 x }. $$

Change of Base

A logarithm can be rewritten using another logarithmic base:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b x = \frac{\log_c x}{\log_c b} }. $$

Base in Numeral Systems

A positional numeral system has a base, also called its radix.

In base $b$, place values are powers of $b$:

$$ \boxed{ \ldots, b^3, b^2, b^1, b^0, b^{-1}, b^{-2}, \ldots }. $$

Decimal System

The ordinary decimal system is base $10$.

For example:

$$ 472 = 4(10^2)+7(10^1)+2(10^0). $$

Binary System

Binary is base $2$ and uses the digits:

$$ \boxed{ 0,\ 1 }. $$

For example:

$$ (1011)_2 = 1(2^3)+0(2^2)+1(2^1)+1(2^0) = 11. $$

Hexadecimal System

Hexadecimal uses base $16$.

Its digit symbols typically represent values:

$$ 0,1,\ldots,9,A,B,C,D,E,F. $$

Digits and the Base

In an integer positional system of base $b$, the ordinary digit values are:

$$ \boxed{ 0,1,2,\ldots,b-1 }. $$

General Positional Expansion

A numeral:

$$ (d_nd_{n-1}\ldots d_1d_0)_b $$

represents:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{k=0}^{n}d_kb^k }. $$

Base in Geometry

In geometry, a base is often a distinguished side, face, or region relative to which height or other measurements are defined.

Unlike ordinary physical language, the base need not actually appear at the bottom of a drawing.

$$ \boxed{ \text{geometric base} \ne \text{necessarily visual bottom} }. $$

Base of a Triangle

Any side of a triangle may be selected as the base.

Once a base of length $b$ is selected, the corresponding height $h$ is measured perpendicular to the line containing that base.

The area is:

$$ \boxed{ A=\frac12bh }. $$

A Triangle Has No Permanent Bottom Side

For a general triangle, any of its three sides may serve as the base.

Changing the selected base changes the corresponding altitude, but not the area of the triangle.

Isosceles Triangle

In an isosceles triangle, the term base commonly refers specifically to the side that is not one of the two congruent sides.

The two angles adjacent to that side are called the base angles.

Base of a Parallelogram

Any side of a parallelogram may be selected as a base.

Its area is:

$$ \boxed{ A=bh }. $$

where $h$ is perpendicular to the selected base.

Base of a Trapezoid

In a trapezoid, the two parallel sides are commonly called the bases:

$$ \boxed{ b_1,\ b_2 }. $$

The area is:

$$ \boxed{ A = \frac12(b_1+b_2)h }. $$

Base of a Prism

A prism has two congruent parallel polygonal faces called its bases.

If the area of one base is $B$ and the perpendicular height is $h$, then:

$$ \boxed{ V=Bh }. $$

Base of a Cylinder

A circular cylinder has two congruent parallel circular bases.

Their area is:

$$ \boxed{ B=\pi r^2 }. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ V=\pi r^2h }. $$

Base of a Pyramid

A pyramid has one polygonal base and triangular lateral faces meeting at an apex.

If its base area is $B$ and perpendicular height is $h$:

$$ \boxed{ V=\frac13Bh }. $$

Base of a Cone

A circular cone has one circular base and an apex.

Its volume is:

$$ \boxed{ V=\frac13\pi r^2h }. $$

Base versus Base Area

In solid geometry, the symbol $B$ often denotes the area of the base, not the base object itself.

$$ \boxed{ B=\text{base area} }. $$

This is important when interpreting:

$$ V=Bh. $$

Base versus Height

A base establishes the reference from which a corresponding perpendicular height is measured.

In area and volume formulas, the base and height therefore occur as paired quantities.

Base Need Not Be Horizontal

Rotating a triangle, prism, or other figure does not change which side or face has been designated as its base.

Orientation on the page is irrelevant.

Base in Similar Figures

In similar triangles and other similar figures, corresponding bases scale by the same linear scale factor as corresponding sides.

If the scale factor is $k$, then:

$$ \boxed{ b_2=kb_1 }. $$

Base and Logarithm Are Inverse-Operation Partners

The same base connects an exponential and its corresponding logarithm:

$$ \boxed{ b^y=x \iff \log_b x=y }. $$

Changing the base changes both the exponential function and the logarithmic function.

Base 1 in Exponentials

Although:

$$ 1^x=1, $$

base $1$ is excluded from ordinary logarithmic bases because:

$$ 1^x=1 $$

never produces values other than $1$, so there is no one-to-one logarithmic inverse on the positive reals.

Base 0 in Exponential Expressions

Expressions involving base $0$ require care.

For positive integers $n$:

$$ 0^n=0. $$

But negative exponents would require division by zero, and $0^0$ requires context-specific treatment rather than a universal elementary assignment.

Negative Bases

Negative bases are perfectly meaningful for integer exponents:

$$ (-2)^3=-8, \qquad (-2)^4=16. $$

Extending negative-base exponentiation to arbitrary real exponents requires more care because not every resulting expression is real.

Base versus Coefficient

In:

$$ 3(2^x), $$

$3$ is a coefficient and $2$ is the exponential base.

$$ \boxed{ 3=\text{coefficient}, \qquad 2=\text{base} }. $$

Base versus Exponent

In:

$$ a^n, $$

the base identifies what is being raised, while the exponent determines the power.

Base versus Radix

In numeral systems, radix is a more specialized synonym for base.

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \text{binary has radix }2 = \text{binary has base }2 }. $$

Base versus Bottom

In geometry, base is a mathematical designation, not a statement about physical orientation.

A selected base may appear on the top, side, or at an angle in a diagram.

Context Determines Meaning

Compare:

$$ 2^5, \qquad \log_2 32, \qquad (101)_2, \qquad A=\frac12bh. $$

The word base refers respectively to:

  1. the repeated factor $2$,
  2. the logarithmic reference number $2$,
  3. the numeral-system radix $2$,
  4. a geometric side represented by $b$.

Common Misconception

There is no single definition of base that can simply be substituted into every mathematical context.

$$ \boxed{ \text{identify the domain first, then interpret the base} }. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$a^n$ "a to the nth power," where a is the base.
$\log_b x$ "Log base b of x."
$(1011)_2$ "One-zero-one-one base two."
$A=\frac12bh$ "The area of a triangle is one-half base times height."
$V=Bh$ "Volume equals base area times height."
$V=\frac13Bh$ "Volume equals one-third the base area times the perpendicular height."

In spoken mathematics, the surrounding expression usually makes the intended meaning of base clear. The phrase base two, for example, may refer either to binary notation or to an exponential or logarithmic base, depending on context.

Defense Notation Advice: When ambiguity is possible, qualify the term explicitly: say exponential base, logarithm base, numeral-system base, triangle base, or base area.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • General Meaning: Base is a context-dependent term identifying a foundational or reference quantity or object.
  • Exponentiation: In $a^n$, $a$ is the base.
  • Expression Base: In $(x+1)^4$, the entire expression $x+1$ is the base.
  • Exponential Function: In $f(x)=a^x$, $a$ is the exponential base.
  • Real Exponential Convention: Common elementary treatment uses: $$ \boxed{ a>0,\quad a\ne1 }. $$
  • Logarithm: In $\log_b x$, $b$ is the logarithmic base.
  • Logarithmic Inverse Relation: $$ \boxed{ b^y=x \iff \log_bx=y }. $$
  • Common Logarithm: Base $10$.
  • Natural Logarithm: Base $e$.
  • Binary Logarithm: Base $2$.
  • Change of Base: $$ \boxed{ \log_bx = \frac{\log_cx}{\log_cb} }. $$
  • Numeral Systems: Base and radix are near-synonyms.
  • Decimal: Base $10$.
  • Binary: Base $2$.
  • Hexadecimal: Base $16$.
  • Place Value: Positions in base $b$ correspond to powers of $b$.
  • Triangle: Any side may ordinarily be chosen as a base.
  • Triangle Area: $$ \boxed{ A=\frac12bh }. $$
  • Isosceles Triangle: The base usually means the side opposite the vertex where the congruent sides meet.
  • Parallelogram: Any side may be chosen as a base, with corresponding perpendicular height.
  • Trapezoid: The parallel sides are commonly called the bases.
  • Prism: The congruent parallel faces are the bases.
  • Cylinder: The two congruent parallel circular regions are the bases.
  • Pyramid: The polygonal face opposite the apex is the base.
  • Cone: The circular region opposite the apex is the base.
  • Base Area: In formulas such as $V=Bh$, $B$ often means the area of the base.
  • Height Relationship: Geometric height is measured perpendicular to the chosen base or base plane.
  • Orientation Caution: A geometric base need not appear physically below the rest of the figure.
  • Negative Exponential Base: Negative bases work routinely with integer exponents but require care for arbitrary real exponents.
  • Base Zero: Some powers are defined, but zero cannot serve as an ordinary logarithmic base.
  • Base One: Base $1$ is excluded from ordinary logarithms because $1^x$ is constant.
  • Coefficient Contrast: In $3(2^x)$, $3$ is the coefficient and $2$ is the base.
  • Exponent Contrast: In $a^n$, $a$ is the base and $n$ is the exponent.
  • Radix Contrast: Radix specifically refers to the base of a positional numeral system.
  • Common Error: Do not assume the visible bottom side of a geometric figure must be its base.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse a geometric base with its numerical base length or base area.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse the logarithm base with the logarithm's argument.
  • Common Error: In exponentiation, parentheses determine whether a sign or larger expression belongs to the base.
  • Terminological Contrast: Exponential base is the quantity being raised to a power. Logarithm base specifies the inverse exponential system. Numeral-system base determines positional place values. Geometric base is a selected side, face, or region used as a reference for dimensions such as height, area, or volume. Base area is the measure of that geometric region rather than the region itself.
