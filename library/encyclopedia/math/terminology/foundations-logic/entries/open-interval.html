Open Interval

Subfield: Foundations & Logic

Etymology & Roots

An open interval is a set of real numbers lying strictly between two endpoints, with neither endpoint included.

$$ \boxed{ (a,b) = \{x\in\mathbb{R}:a<x<b\} } $$

The word interval comes from Latin roots associated with a space or gap between things. In mathematics, an interval is a continuous stretch of the real number line with no missing points between its included values.

The adjective open indicates that the boundary endpoints are excluded. Thus:

$$ a\notin(a,b) \qquad\text{and}\qquad b\notin(a,b). $$

For example:

$$ \boxed{ (2,5) = \{x\in\mathbb{R}:2<x<5\} }. $$

The numbers $2$ and $5$ mark the boundaries of the interval, but neither belongs to the interval itself.

The term open later acquires a broader topological meaning. On the real line, open intervals are among the basic examples from which the general concept of an open set is built.

Operational Nuance

Basic Definition

For real numbers $a<b$, the open interval from $a$ to $b$ is:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b) = \{x\in\mathbb{R}:a<x<b\} }. $$

Endpoint Exclusion

The defining inequalities are strict:

$$ \boxed{ a<x<b }. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ a\notin(a,b), \qquad b\notin(a,b) }. $$

Interval Notation

Parentheses indicate endpoint exclusion:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b) }. $$

Inequality Notation

The same set may be written:

$$ \boxed{ a<x<b }. $$

Set-Builder Notation

$$ \boxed{ \{x\in\mathbb{R}:a<x<b\} }. $$

Example

$$ (-3,4) = \{x\in\mathbb{R}:-3<x<4\}. $$

Values such as $-2$, $0$, and $3.999$ belong to the interval, but $-3$ and $4$ do not.

Number-Line Representation

On a number line, an open interval is conventionally shown with open circles at both endpoints and the segment between them highlighted.

Open Circle Meaning

An open circle indicates:

$$ \boxed{ \text{endpoint excluded} }. $$

Closed Interval Contrast

A closed interval includes both endpoints:

$$ \boxed{ [a,b] = \{x\in\mathbb{R}:a\leq x\leq b\} }. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b) \neq [a,b] }. $$

Half-Open Interval Contrast

An interval may include exactly one endpoint:

$$ \boxed{ [a,b) } $$

or:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b] }. $$

These are commonly called half-open or half-closed intervals.

Bounded Open Interval

A finite open interval $(a,b)$ with real $a<b$ is bounded above and below.

$$ \boxed{ a<x<b \text{ for every }x\in(a,b) }. $$

Lower Bound

The endpoint $a$ is a lower bound of $(a,b)$.

Upper Bound

The endpoint $b$ is an upper bound of $(a,b)$.

Infimum

Although $a$ is not in the interval:

$$ \boxed{ \inf(a,b)=a }. $$

Supremum

Likewise:

$$ \boxed{ \sup(a,b)=b }. $$

No Minimum

The interval $(a,b)$ has no minimum element.

Given any:

$$ x\in(a,b), $$

there is another point in the interval closer to $a$, for example:

$$ \frac{a+x}{2}. $$

No Maximum

Similarly, $(a,b)$ has no maximum element.

Endpoints Are Boundary Points

Both $a$ and $b$ are boundary points of the interval even though they are excluded.

$$ \boxed{ \partial(a,b) = \{a,b\} }. $$

Interior

The interior of an open interval is the interval itself:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{int}(a,b) = (a,b) }. $$

Closure

The closure adds both missing endpoints:

$$ \boxed{ \overline{(a,b)} = [a,b] }. $$

Why the Closure Includes the Endpoints

Every neighborhood of $a$ contains points of $(a,b)$, and every neighborhood of $b$ contains points of $(a,b)$. Thus both are limit points of the interval.

Every Point Has Room Around It

If:

$$ x\in(a,b), $$

then there exists some $\varepsilon>0$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ (x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon) \subseteq (a,b) }. $$

Constructing Such an $\varepsilon$

A convenient choice is:

$$ \boxed{ \varepsilon = \frac{1}{2} \min\{x-a,b-x\} }. $$

Since $a<x<b$, both distances are positive.

Topological Openness

This neighborhood property is exactly why $(a,b)$ is an open set in the usual topology on $\mathbb{R}$.

$$ \boxed{ (a,b)\text{ is open in }\mathbb{R} }. $$

Open Sets as Unions of Open Intervals

Every open subset of $\mathbb{R}$ can be written as a union of open intervals.

Basis for the Real-Line Topology

Open intervals form a basis for the usual topology on $\mathbb{R}$.

Neighborhood Interpretation

A common open neighborhood of a point $c$ is:

$$ \boxed{ (c-\varepsilon,c+\varepsilon) } $$

for some $\varepsilon>0$.

Absolute-Value Form

The interval:

$$ (c-r,c+r) $$

is equivalently:

$$ \boxed{ |x-c|<r }. $$

Open Ball in $\mathbb{R}$

In metric-space language:

$$ \boxed{ B(c,r) = \{x\in\mathbb{R}:|x-c|<r\} = (c-r,c+r) }. $$

Center and Radius Form

Any finite open interval $(a,b)$ can be rewritten using its midpoint:

$$ c = \frac{a+b}{2} $$

and radius:

$$ r = \frac{b-a}{2}. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b) = (c-r,c+r) }. $$

Absolute-Value Conversion

$$ \boxed{ a<x<b \iff \left| x-\frac{a+b}{2} \right| < \frac{b-a}{2} }. $$

Example

The interval:

$$ (2,8) $$

has midpoint $5$ and radius $3$, so:

$$ \boxed{ 2<x<8 \iff |x-5|<3 }. $$

Solving Compound Inequalities

A compound inequality:

$$ -2<3x+1<10 $$

can be solved simultaneously.

Subtracting $1$:

$$ -3<3x<9. $$

Dividing by $3$:

$$ -1<x<3. $$

Therefore the solution set is:

$$ \boxed{ (-1,3) }. $$

Open Interval as a Domain

Functions are often restricted to open intervals:

$$ \boxed{ f:(a,b)\to\mathbb{R} }. $$

This means only inputs strictly between $a$ and $b$ are allowed.

Open Interval in Calculus

Derivative statements often naturally apply on open intervals because differentiability is a two-sided local property at interior points.

Example

A theorem may assume:

$$ f\text{ is differentiable on }(a,b) $$

while separately requiring continuity on:

$$ [a,b]. $$

Mean Value Theorem Pattern

The classical Mean Value Theorem assumes continuity on the closed interval and differentiability on the open interval:

$$ \boxed{ f\text{ continuous on }[a,b] \text{ and differentiable on }(a,b) }. $$

Why Endpoints Differ

At an endpoint, a two-sided derivative may not be available because inputs exist only on one side within the interval.

Intersection of Open Intervals

The intersection of two open intervals is either empty or another open interval.

For example:

$$ (1,5)\cap(3,8) = \boxed{ (3,5) }. $$

Disjoint Open Intervals

$$ (1,2)\cap(3,4) = \boxed{ \varnothing }. $$

Finite Intersections of Open Sets

More generally, finite intersections of open sets are open.

Arbitrary Unions of Open Intervals

Any union of open intervals is open.

Union Need Not Be One Interval

For example:

$$ (1,2)\cup(4,5) $$

is open but is not itself a single interval because values between $2$ and $4$ are missing.

Overlapping Union

If open intervals overlap sufficiently, their union may be another open interval:

$$ (1,5)\cup(4,8) = \boxed{ (1,8) }. $$

Touching Open Intervals

The union:

$$ (1,3)\cup(3,5) $$

is not $(1,5)$ because $3$ is missing.

Infinite Intervals

Intervals extending indefinitely are written with infinity symbols:

$$ \boxed{ (a,\infty) } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ (-\infty,b) }. $$

Infinity Is Not an Endpoint Number

The symbols $\infty$ and $-\infty$ are not real numbers and are never included as ordinary endpoints.

Thus parentheses are used:

$$ \boxed{ (a,\infty), \qquad (-\infty,b) }. $$

Entire Real Line

$$ \boxed{ (-\infty,\infty) = \mathbb{R} }. $$

Are Infinite Intervals Open?

In the usual topology on $\mathbb{R}$:

$$ \boxed{ (a,\infty) \text{ and } (-\infty,b) \text{ are open} }. $$

Degenerate Endpoint Case

If the same endpoint is used twice:

$$ (a,a), $$

no real number satisfies:

$$ a<x<a. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ (a,a)=\varnothing }. $$

Reversed Endpoint Order

Under the standard set definition:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b)=\varnothing \text{ when }a\geq b } $$

unless a different specialized convention is explicitly introduced.

Density of the Real Numbers

If $a<b$, then $(a,b)$ always contains infinitely many real numbers.

Midpoint Witness

At least one point between $a$ and $b$ is:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{a+b}{2} }. $$

Since:

$$ a < \frac{a+b}{2} < b. $$

Infinitely Many Rational Numbers

Every nonempty open interval contains infinitely many rational numbers.

Infinitely Many Irrational Numbers

Every nonempty open interval also contains infinitely many irrational numbers.

Uncountably Many Real Numbers

Every nonempty open interval contains uncountably many real numbers.

Cardinality

Any nonempty finite open interval has the same cardinality as $\mathbb{R}$.

$$ \boxed{ |(a,b)| = |\mathbb{R}| } $$

for $a<b$.

Affine Mapping between Open Intervals

The map:

$$ x \mapsto c+\frac{d-c}{b-a}(x-a) $$

gives a bijection:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b)\to(c,d) } $$

whenever $a<b$ and $c<d$.

Connectedness

Every interval in $\mathbb{R}$ is connected. Thus an open interval cannot be split into two separated nonempty open pieces relative to itself.

Convexity

Every real interval is convex:

$$ x,y\in(a,b), \quad 0<t<1 $$

imply:

$$ \boxed{ tx+(1-t)y\in(a,b) }. $$

Open Interval as a Convex Set

The entire line segment between any two points of the interval remains inside the interval.

Length

The geometric length of $(a,b)$ is:

$$ \boxed{ b-a }. $$

Endpoint Inclusion Does Not Change Length

The intervals:

$$ (a,b), \qquad [a,b], \qquad [a,b), \qquad (a,b] $$

all have the same ordinary length:

$$ \boxed{ b-a }. $$

Measure-Theoretic View

Adding or removing finitely many endpoints does not change the Lebesgue measure of an interval.

Open Interval in Probability

For a continuous random variable, endpoint inclusion often does not affect probability:

$$ P(a<X<b) = P(a\leq X\leq b) $$

when single points have probability zero.

Probability Caveat

This endpoint-indifference is specific to distributions with zero point masses at the endpoints and is not a general set-theoretic identity.

Open Interval in Optimization

If a feasible variable is restricted to:

$$ x\in(a,b), $$

the boundary values cannot themselves be chosen.

Supremum without Maximum

For example, maximizing:

$$ f(x)=x $$

over:

$$ x\in(0,1) $$

gives supremum $1$, but no maximum because $1$ is excluded.

Infimum without Minimum

Similarly, the infimum is $0$, but the interval has no minimum.

Open Interval in Continuity

A function may be said to be continuous on $(a,b)$ when it is continuous at every point of that interval.

Local Property

Since every point of an open interval has a smaller neighborhood contained inside it, open intervals are especially natural domains for local analysis.

Sequence Convergence to an Excluded Endpoint

A sequence can remain entirely inside an open interval while converging to an endpoint outside it.

For example:

$$ x_n = \frac{1}{n} $$

lies in $(0,1)$ for $n>1$, but:

$$ \boxed{ x_n\to0 } $$

and $0\notin(0,1)$.

Open Sets Need Not Contain Their Limit Points

This illustrates a fundamental distinction between open and closed sets.

Open Interval Is Not Closed in $\mathbb{R}$

A nonempty bounded open interval fails to contain its two boundary points, so it is not closed in the usual real topology.

Clopen Exceptions

In $\mathbb{R}$ itself, the empty set and $\mathbb{R}$ are both open and closed. A proper nonempty finite open interval is not closed in $\mathbb{R}$.

Relative Topology

Openness depends on the ambient space.

For example:

$$ [0,1) $$

is not open in $\mathbb{R}$, but it is open relative to the subspace:

$$ [0,2). $$

Open Interval as an Ordered Set

In order theory, $(a,b)$ denotes the set of elements strictly between $a$ and $b$ when the underlying order is understood.

Integer Interval Caveat

If the ambient set is $\mathbb{Z}$ rather than $\mathbb{R}$, the notation:

$$ (2,6)\cap\mathbb{Z} $$

yields:

$$ \boxed{ \{3,4,5\} }. $$

The continuous real interval and its integer points should not be confused.

Open Interval vs. Open Set

Every open interval is an open set in $\mathbb{R}$, but not every open set is a single interval.

For example:

$$ (0,1)\cup(2,3) $$

is open but is not an interval.

Open Interval vs. Punctured Interval

A punctured interval removes an interior point:

$$ (a,c)\cup(c,b) $$

and therefore is not itself an interval.

Punctured Neighborhood

A punctured neighborhood of $c$ may be written:

$$ \boxed{ 0<|x-c|<\delta } $$

which corresponds to:

$$ (c-\delta,c)\cup(c,c+\delta). $$

Open Interval vs. Range

A function's range may happen to be an open interval, but the terms describe different concepts. Open interval describes a type of set; range describes outputs actually attained.

Open Interval vs. Domain

A domain may be an open interval, but not every domain is one.

Open Interval vs. Inequality

The interval:

$$ (a,b) $$

is a set, while:

$$ a<x<b $$

is a condition describing membership in that set.

Common Error: Including the Endpoints

An open interval excludes both endpoints.

Common Error: Using Brackets for an Open Interval

Brackets ordinarily indicate inclusion, so:

$$ [a,b] $$

is not an open interval.

Common Error: Using $\leq$ in the Inequality Form

Open intervals correspond to strict inequalities:

$$ \boxed{ a<x<b }. $$

Common Error: Treating Infinity as an Included Endpoint

Infinity is not a real number. Expressions such as:

$$ [a,\infty] $$

are not standard real interval notation.

Common Error: Assuming Open Means Unbounded

The interval $(2,5)$ is open but bounded.

Common Error: Assuming Open Means Infinite Length

A bounded open interval has finite length $b-a$.

Common Error: Assuming Open Means Empty at the Ends

Although endpoints are excluded, points can lie arbitrarily close to either endpoint.

Common Error: Saying the Endpoints Are Not Related to the Interval

Excluded endpoints remain its infimum, supremum, and boundary points.

Common Error: Assuming an Open Interval Has a Minimum or Maximum

A nonempty finite open interval has neither.

Common Error: Confusing Minimum with Infimum

For $(a,b)$:

$$ \inf(a,b)=a, $$

but $a$ is not a minimum because $a\notin(a,b)$.

Common Error: Confusing Maximum with Supremum

Likewise:

$$ \sup(a,b)=b, $$

but $b$ is not a maximum.

Common Error: Treating $(a,a)$ as a One-Point Set

Since no real number is strictly between $a$ and itself:

$$ \boxed{ (a,a)=\varnothing }. $$

Common Error: Assuming Every Open Set Is an Interval

Disconnected unions of open intervals are open sets but not intervals.

Common Error: Assuming Every Interval Is Open

Closed and half-open intervals are also intervals.

Common Error: Assuming Endpoint Inclusion Changes Interval Length

Adding or removing finitely many endpoints does not alter ordinary interval length.

Common Error: Confusing Open Interval with Punctured Interval

Removing an interior point destroys the interval property because the set no longer contains every real number between two of its points.

Common Error: Ignoring the Ambient Number System

The notation $(a,b)$ usually refers to real numbers in elementary analysis, but a different ambient ordered set may change the actual elements present.

Common Error: Assuming Openness Is Absolute

A set can be open relative to one ambient space and not open in another.

Common Misconception: An open interval is not "open-ended" in the sense of being unbounded. Its defining feature is endpoint exclusion. The bounded set $(a,b)$ is open because every point inside it has a smaller neighborhood still contained in the interval. Its endpoints are excluded but remain mathematically important as boundary points, infimum and supremum.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$(a,b)$ "The open interval from a to b."
$a<x<b$ "X is strictly between a and b."
$\{x\in\mathbb{R}:a<x<b\}$ "The set of real x such that a is less than x and x is less than b."
$(a,\infty)$ "The open interval of all real numbers greater than a," or "from a to infinity, excluding a."
$(-\infty,b)$ "All real numbers less than b."
$|x-c|<r$ "X lies within distance r of c."
$\operatorname{int}(a,b)=(a,b)$ "The interior of the open interval is the interval itself."

The word open should be tied to endpoint exclusion in elementary interval notation, not to physical length or whether the interval extends to infinity.

In:

$$ (2,7), $$

one may say:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"All real numbers strictly between two and seven."} } $$

In topology, the same notation can be described more structurally:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"An open subset of the real line."} } $$

Defense Notation Advice: Use parentheses for excluded finite endpoints and strict inequalities in the corresponding inequality form. Never treat $\infty$ or $-\infty$ as ordinary included real endpoints. Distinguish the set $(a,b)$ from the condition $a<x<b$, and distinguish excluded endpoints from irrelevant endpoints: they remain boundary points and determine the interval's infimum, supremum, midpoint, and length.
