Mathematical Model

Subfield: Foundations & Logic

Etymology & Roots

A mathematical model is a mathematical representation of selected features, relationships, or behaviors of a real, conceptual, or hypothetical system. The model may use equations, functions, inequalities, geometric objects, probability distributions, algorithms, graphs, matrices, differential equations, simulations, or combinations of these structures.

A model does not reproduce every feature of the thing being studied. It deliberately selects certain quantities and relationships while ignoring or simplifying others.

$$ \boxed{ \text{system} \longrightarrow \text{assumptions} \longrightarrow \text{mathematical representation} } $$

For example, a simple constant-speed motion model may represent position as:

$$ \boxed{ x(t)=x_0+vt }. $$

This model keeps track of initial position $x_0$, speed or velocity $v$, and time $t$, while ignoring effects such as acceleration, changing terrain, measurement error, or external forces unless they are added explicitly.

The noun model comes from a long linguistic tradition meaning a pattern, representation, or simplified form used to understand something else. In mathematics, the word emphasizes correspondence: a mathematical structure is used to stand for selected aspects of another structure or phenomenon.

The adjective mathematical distinguishes such a model from a physical scale model, verbal analogy, conceptual diagram, or purely qualitative description.

Operational Nuance

Core Idea

A mathematical model translates a situation into mathematical objects and relationships that can be analyzed.

$$ \boxed{ \text{quantities} + \text{relationships} + \text{assumptions} = \text{model structure} }. $$

Models Are Selective

A model contains only the features judged relevant to its purpose. This selectivity is not automatically a defect; simplification is often what makes a problem analyzable.

Purpose Matters

Two models of the same system can both be useful if they serve different purposes. One model may emphasize prediction, another explanation, another optimization, and another qualitative behavior.

Variables

A model usually contains variables representing quantities that can change.

$$ \boxed{ y=f(x) } $$

may represent a response $y$ depending on an input $x$.

Independent Variable

An independent variable commonly represents an input, control, time, location, or other quantity against which change is studied.

Dependent Variable

A dependent variable represents an output or quantity whose value is determined or modeled in relation to other quantities.

Parameters

Parameters are quantities treated as fixed within a particular model instance but allowed to vary across scenarios or model calibrations.

In:

$$ y=mx+b, $$

$m$ and $b$ may act as parameters while $x$ varies.

Constants

A model may contain universal constants, physical constants, empirically estimated constants, or merely fixed numerical values.

Assumptions

Every model rests on assumptions, whether stated explicitly or hidden in the chosen equations.

Typical assumptions may include:

  • constant rates;
  • independent observations;
  • negligible friction;
  • fixed population size;
  • continuous change;
  • linear response;
  • normal error distributions;
  • perfect mixing;
  • constant environmental conditions.

Model Assumptions Are Part of the Model

The equation alone does not completely specify a model if its variables, parameters, units, domains, and assumptions are unknown.

Domain

A model's domain specifies which input values are meaningful.

For example, if $t$ represents elapsed time:

$$ \boxed{ t\geq0 } $$

may be part of the model.

Range and Feasible Outputs

A model may likewise impose restrictions on outputs.

If $P(t)$ represents a probability:

$$ \boxed{ 0\leq P(t)\leq1 }. $$

Units

Variables and parameters in an applied model often carry units.

If:

$$ d=rt, $$

and $d$ is measured in kilometers while $t$ is measured in hours, then $r$ must carry units:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\text{kilometers}}{\text{hour}} }. $$

Dimensional Consistency

A physically meaningful equation generally requires compatible dimensions on both sides.

$$ \boxed{ [\text{left side}] = [\text{right side}] }. $$

Linear Model

One of the simplest model forms is:

$$ \boxed{ y=mx+b }. $$

It describes a constant rate of change.

Direct-Proportion Model

$$ \boxed{ y=kx }. $$

Here $y$ is proportional to $x$.

Quadratic Model

$$ \boxed{ y=ax^2+bx+c }. $$

Such models can represent curved relationships, including projectile height under simplified constant-gravity assumptions.

Exponential Model

$$ \boxed{ y=y_0e^{kt} }. $$

This model describes continuous proportional growth or decay.

Logistic Model

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dP}{dt} = rP\left(1-\frac{P}{K}\right) }. $$

Logistic modeling adds a limiting capacity to proportional growth.

Power-Law Model

$$ \boxed{ y=ax^p }. $$

Power laws often appear in scaling relationships.

Polynomial Model

A polynomial model may take the form:

$$ \boxed{ y = a_nx^n+\cdots+a_1x+a_0 }. $$

Rational Model

$$ \boxed{ y = \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} }. $$

Rational models can describe saturation, rates, and relationships containing asymptotes or excluded inputs.

Trigonometric Model

Periodic behavior may be modeled with:

$$ \boxed{ y = A\sin(\omega t+\phi)+C }. $$

Difference-Equation Model

Discrete-time systems may be modeled recursively:

$$ \boxed{ x_{n+1}=F(x_n) }. $$

Differential-Equation Model

Continuous dynamic systems often relate a quantity to its derivatives:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dy}{dt} = F(t,y) }. $$

System of Equations

Multiple interacting quantities may require several equations.

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} x' &= f(x,y),\\ y' &= g(x,y) \end{aligned} } $$

Matrix Model

Linear systems can be modeled as:

$$ \boxed{ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b} }. $$

State-Space Model

A dynamic linear system may be written:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x}'(t) = A\mathbf{x}(t)+B\mathbf{u}(t) }. $$

Probability Model

A probability model specifies possible outcomes together with a probability law.

$$ \boxed{ (\Omega,\mathcal{F},P) } $$

is a formal probability-space representation.

Statistical Model

A statistical model describes a family of probability distributions indexed by parameters.

$$ \boxed{ \{P_\theta:\theta\in\Theta\} }. $$

Regression Model

A simple statistical model may be:

$$ \boxed{ Y = \beta_0+\beta_1X+\varepsilon }. $$

where $\varepsilon$ represents unexplained variation or error.

Deterministic Model

In a deterministic model, specified inputs and parameters determine the model output uniquely.

Stochastic Model

A stochastic model includes random variables or probabilistic mechanisms.

Deterministic Example

$$ \boxed{ d=vt } $$

under fixed $v$ and $t$ gives a determined value of $d$.

Stochastic Example

A random-walk model may use:

$$ \boxed{ X_{n+1}=X_n+\varepsilon_n } $$

where $\varepsilon_n$ is random.

Discrete Model

Discrete models evolve through countable stages or states.

Continuous Model

Continuous models allow quantities to vary over continua such as real-valued time.

Static Model

A static model represents relationships without explicitly modeling evolution in time.

Dynamic Model

A dynamic model represents change over time or iterative stages.

Mechanistic Model

A mechanistic model is built from hypothesized processes, laws, interactions, or causal mechanisms.

Empirical Model

An empirical model is fitted primarily to observed data, sometimes without claiming that its mathematical form represents the true underlying mechanism.

Phenomenological Model

A phenomenological model describes observed behavior effectively while abstracting away some deeper mechanism.

Descriptive Model

A descriptive model organizes or summarizes observed structure.

Predictive Model

A predictive model aims to estimate future or unobserved outcomes.

Explanatory Model

An explanatory model emphasizes interpretation of relationships or mechanisms.

Prescriptive Model

A prescriptive model recommends choices, often through optimization.

Optimization Model

An optimization model contains:

  • decision variables;
  • an objective function;
  • constraints.
$$ \boxed{ \text{maximize or minimize }f(\mathbf{x}) } $$

subject to:

$$ \boxed{ g_i(\mathbf{x})\leq0 }. $$

Constraint

A constraint restricts permissible values of model variables.

Feasible Region

The set of values satisfying every model constraint is the feasible region.

Objective Function

An objective function measures the quantity to be optimized.

Model Construction

A typical modeling process includes:

  1. identify the question or goal;
  2. choose relevant quantities;
  3. define variables and parameters;
  4. state assumptions;
  5. construct mathematical relationships;
  6. analyze or solve the model;
  7. compare results with reality or data;
  8. revise if needed.

Problem Formulation

Modeling begins before algebra. The first major task is deciding what should be represented mathematically.

Variable Selection

Good variables make relevant relationships visible while avoiding unnecessary complexity.

Parameter Selection

Parameters should have interpretable roles where possible.

Calibration

Calibration means choosing parameter values so the model aligns with measurements, observations, or specified conditions.

Parameter Estimation

Statistical techniques may estimate unknown parameters from data.

Initial Conditions

Dynamic models often require initial values.

$$ \boxed{ y(0)=y_0 }. $$

Boundary Conditions

Models involving spatial or differential equations may require conditions imposed at boundaries.

Solution of a Model

A model solution is a mathematical result consistent with the model's equations and constraints. It is not automatically a true statement about reality.

Interpretation

The mathematical answer must be translated back into the language of the modeled system.

Validation

Validation assesses whether a model is sufficiently accurate or useful for its intended purpose.

Verification vs. Validation

In computational modeling, a useful distinction is:

  • verification: was the mathematical or computational model implemented correctly?
  • validation: does the model adequately represent the target system for the intended use?

Model Fit

A fitted model can be compared with observations using residuals, error measures, likelihoods, or other criteria.

Residual

In a simple predictive setting:

$$ \boxed{ \text{residual} = \text{observed} - \text{predicted} }. $$

Model Error

Model error may arise from imperfect assumptions, omitted variables, inaccurate parameters, measurement error, or numerical approximation.

Measurement Error vs. Model Error

Measurement error belongs to observed data. Model error belongs to inadequacy in the mathematical representation. The two should not be conflated.

Uncertainty

Models may contain uncertainty in inputs, parameters, structure, or observations.

Parameter Uncertainty

Estimated parameters may not be known exactly.

Structural Uncertainty

Several plausible model forms may fit the same phenomenon.

Input Uncertainty

Model inputs may themselves be measured or forecast imperfectly.

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analysis studies how model outputs respond to changes in inputs or parameters.

$$ \boxed{ \text{sensitivity} \sim \frac{\text{change in output}} {\text{change in input}} }. $$

Local Sensitivity

If:

$$ y=f(p), $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\partial y}{\partial p} } $$

measures local sensitivity to parameter $p$.

Robustness

A model or conclusion is robust when reasonable changes in assumptions or inputs do not drastically change the relevant conclusion.

Identifiability

A parameter is identifiable when the available model structure and observations can, in principle, distinguish its value from competing values.

Overfitting

A model may fit observed data extremely closely while performing poorly on new data because it captures noise rather than stable structure.

Underfitting

A model may be too simple to capture important structure in the system.

Model Complexity

More complicated models can represent more detail but may require more data, computation, assumptions, and parameter estimation.

Parsimony

A parsimonious model uses no more complexity than is justified by the modeling purpose and available evidence.

Approximation

Many mathematical models are approximations rather than exact descriptions.

Linear Approximation

A nonlinear relationship may be approximated locally by:

$$ \boxed{ f(x) \approx f(a)+f'(a)(x-a) }. $$

Idealization

Idealization intentionally replaces complicated reality with a simpler conceptual system.

Examples include:

  • point masses;
  • frictionless surfaces;
  • perfectly rigid bodies;
  • perfect competition;
  • infinite populations;
  • continuous media.

Abstraction

A model may remove specific physical interpretation and retain only mathematical structure.

Simulation

When a model cannot be solved conveniently in closed form, numerical simulation may approximate its behavior.

Numerical Model

A numerical model may represent a continuous system through discretized equations evaluated computationally.

Closed-Form Solution

Some models permit explicit formulas.

$$ \boxed{ y(t)=y_0e^{kt} } $$

is a closed-form solution of $y'=ky$ with $y(0)=y_0$.

Numerical Solution

Other models may require approximate numerical values at discrete points.

Model Prediction

A prediction is an output generated by a model for an input or condition of interest.

Interpolation

Prediction within the range of observed or calibrated inputs is interpolation.

Extrapolation

Prediction outside the range used to construct or calibrate the model is extrapolation.

Extrapolation Risk

Model assumptions that are reasonable within a calibrated range may fail outside it.

Scenario Analysis

Scenario analysis studies model outcomes under alternative assumptions or input choices.

Counterfactual Modeling

Some models ask what would happen under conditions different from those actually observed.

Optimization and Decision Making

A model can be used not only to predict what will happen but to compare choices.

Constraint Models

Resource limitations may be represented by inequalities:

$$ \boxed{ a_1x_1+\cdots+a_nx_n\leq b }. $$

Network Model

Systems of locations and connections may be represented by graphs:

$$ \boxed{ G=(V,E) }. $$

Flow Model

Network edges may carry capacities, costs, or flows subject to conservation rules.

Geometric Model

Shape, distance, orientation, and spatial relationships can be represented by geometric objects.

Coordinate Model

Geometry may be translated into equations using coordinates.

Physical Model

Mathematical physics uses equations to represent laws of motion, fields, waves, energy, and other physical relationships.

Population Model

Population change may be represented with exponential, logistic, age-structured, stochastic, or interacting-species models depending on the question.

Economic Model

Economic models may represent supply, demand, utility, production, markets, incentives, or dynamic systems.

Financial Model

Financial models may represent interest, cash flows, asset values, uncertainty, risk, or portfolio relationships.

Epidemiological Model

Compartment models divide populations into states and describe flows between them.

SIR Example

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} S' &= -\beta\frac{SI}{N},\\ I' &= \beta\frac{SI}{N}-\gamma I,\\ R' &= \gamma I \end{aligned} } $$

Engineering Model

Engineering models may represent loads, circuits, control systems, heat transfer, fluids, materials, or signal behavior.

Control Model

State-space systems can represent inputs, outputs, and evolving internal states.

Machine-Learning Model

In machine learning, model often refers to a parameterized function or probabilistic system learned from data.

$$ \boxed{ \widehat{y} = f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}) }. $$

Training

Training selects parameters $\theta$ according to a loss or objective function.

Loss Function

$$ \boxed{ L(\theta) = \sum_i \ell \left( y_i, f_\theta(x_i) \right) }. $$

Model vs. Algorithm

A model describes a mathematical representation or relationship. An algorithm is a procedure for carrying out a computation.

An algorithm may fit, solve, simulate, or evaluate a model without being the model itself.

Model vs. Formula

A formula can be part of a model, but a model includes interpretation, assumptions, domains, variable meanings, and often parameter choices as well.

Model vs. Equation

An equation is a mathematical statement. It becomes part of a model when its symbols are interpreted as representing aspects of a target system.

Model vs. Theory

A theory may contain broader explanatory principles. A mathematical model can be a specific formal representation derived from or used within a theory.

Model vs. Simulation

A model specifies mathematical relationships; a simulation executes or approximates the model under chosen inputs and conditions.

Model vs. Data

Data are observations or measurements. A model is a structured representation used to describe, explain, predict, or organize those observations.

Model vs. Reality

A mathematical model is not the system itself.

$$ \boxed{ \text{model} \neq \text{thing modeled} }. $$

Model Scope

The scope of a model identifies where its assumptions and conclusions are intended to apply.

Validity Range

A useful model can be accurate only over a restricted range of inputs or conditions.

Example of Limited Scope

A linear approximation:

$$ f(x) \approx f(a)+f'(a)(x-a) $$

may be excellent near $x=a$ and poor far away.

Model Comparison

Competing models may be compared using accuracy, interpretability, simplicity, computational cost, predictive performance, robustness, or consistency with known mechanisms.

Model Selection

Model selection chooses among candidate model structures or parameterizations.

Model Revision

If predictions systematically disagree with observation, the model may require revised parameters, new variables, altered assumptions, or an entirely different structure.

Model Hierarchy

A family of models can progress from simple to complex.

For population growth:

$$ \boxed{ \text{linear} \rightarrow \text{exponential} \rightarrow \text{logistic} \rightarrow \text{structured stochastic models} }. $$

Model Reduction

Complex models may be simplified while retaining the dominant behavior relevant to a particular question.

Dimensionless Models

Rescaling variables can reduce the number of parameters and reveal common structure.

Nondimensionalization Example

If:

$$ P'=rP\left(1-\frac{P}{K}\right), $$

define:

$$ p=\frac{P}{K}, \qquad \tau=rt. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dp}{d\tau} = p(1-p) }. $$

The scaled equation reveals a universal form independent of the original units.

Model Identifiers

A complete model statement should often specify:

  • what is being modeled;
  • the variables;
  • the parameters;
  • the equations or rules;
  • initial or boundary conditions;
  • units;
  • assumptions;
  • domain and scope.

Model Output

A model output may be a number, function, distribution, graph, trajectory, classification, decision, or range of possible outcomes.

Exact Model vs. Approximate Solution

A model equation can be exact within its assumptions even if its numerical solution is approximate.

Approximate Model vs. Exact Solution

Conversely, one can solve an approximate model exactly. Exact algebra does not make the model itself exact as a representation of reality.

Common Error: Treating a Model as Reality

Mathematical conclusions are conditional on the assumptions and structure of the model.

Common Error: Assuming More Detail Always Means a Better Model

Additional detail may increase complexity without improving usefulness for the intended question.

Common Error: Ignoring Assumptions

A formula without its assumptions can be applied outside its legitimate scope.

Common Error: Ignoring Units

Dimensional inconsistency can reveal a flawed equation or interpretation.

Common Error: Confusing Variables and Parameters

Variables change within a modeled relationship; parameters often specify which member of a family of relationships is being used.

Common Error: Assuming Every Model Is Deterministic

Many models are explicitly stochastic.

Common Error: Assuming Every Model Has a Closed-Form Formula

Models may require numerical computation or simulation.

Common Error: Equating Good Fit with Correct Mechanism

Different model structures can fit the same data, and empirical agreement alone may not establish causal correctness.

Common Error: Equating Prediction with Explanation

A model can predict well without identifying the true underlying mechanism.

Common Error: Ignoring Extrapolation Risk

Accurate interpolation does not guarantee reliable prediction far outside the calibration range.

Common Error: Ignoring Uncertainty

A single predicted value can hide uncertainty in data, parameters, assumptions, or model structure.

Common Error: Treating Parameter Estimates as Exact Constants

Estimated parameters often carry sampling or measurement uncertainty.

Common Error: Confusing Model Validation with Proof

Empirical validation supports usefulness within a tested domain; it does not prove that a model is universally true.

Common Error: Confusing a Model with the Algorithm Used to Fit It

Gradient descent, least squares, or another procedure may estimate model parameters, but the fitting procedure is conceptually distinct from the model structure.

Common Error: Confusing Simulation with the Model

A simulation is one computational realization of a model under specified inputs and numerical choices.

Common Misconception: A mathematical model is not merely an equation and it is not a claim that reality literally obeys that equation in every circumstance. A model is an interpreted mathematical representation built for a purpose. Its variables, parameters, assumptions, domains, units, and scope are essential parts of its meaning, and its conclusions are only as applicable as those choices allow.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$y=mx+b$ "A linear model relating y to x."
$y=y_0e^{kt}$ "An exponential growth or decay model."
$P'=rP(1-P/K)$ "A logistic growth model with intrinsic rate r and carrying capacity K."
$A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ "A linear system model in matrix form."
$Y=\beta_0+\beta_1X+\varepsilon$ "A simple linear regression model."
$\widehat{y}=f_\theta(\mathbf{x})$ "A parameterized predictive model."
$\min f(\mathbf{x})$ subject to $g_i(\mathbf{x})\leq0$ "An optimization model with an objective and constraints."

In mathematical writing, the word model often refers not only to the displayed equation but to the interpreted system surrounding it.

For example:

$$ y=3x+5 $$

is merely an equation until the symbols are assigned meanings such as cost, quantity, time, distance, or another application.

The phrase model predicts is usually preferable to saying "mathematics proves" when the conclusion depends on empirical assumptions.

Likewise, phrases such as under the model, assuming the model, within the model's scope, and according to the model make the conditional nature of a modeling conclusion explicit.

Defense Notation Advice: Define every variable and parameter, attach units where relevant, state domain restrictions and assumptions, and distinguish observed quantities from predicted quantities. Use notation such as $\widehat{y}$ when helpful to distinguish a model prediction from an observed value $y$. Do not present an equation detached from the conditions that give it meaning.
