Graph

Subfield: Foundations & Logic

Etymology & Roots

In mathematics, the word graph has several closely related but distinct meanings. Most commonly in elementary algebra and calculus, a graph is a geometric representation of ordered pairs, relations, functions, equations, or data. In discrete mathematics, a graph is instead an abstract structure made of vertices connected by edges.

The word derives through forms related to the Greek graphein, meaning to write, to draw, or to describe. The same root appears in words such as graphic, geography, and graphical.

In coordinate mathematics, if a relation $R$ consists of ordered pairs $(x,y)$, its graph is the corresponding set of points in a coordinate space:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Graph}(R) = \{(x,y):(x,y)\in R\} }. $$

For a real-valued function $f$, the graph is:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Graph}(f) = \{(x,f(x)):x\in\operatorname{Dom}(f)\} }. $$

In graph theory, a graph is commonly represented abstractly as:

$$ \boxed{ G=(V,E) } $$

where $V$ is a set of vertices and $E$ is a collection of edges connecting specified vertices.

The central linguistic caution is therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"graph" is context-dependent} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Coordinate Graph

In coordinate geometry, a graph represents mathematical objects as points, curves, lines, surfaces, or other subsets of coordinate space.

In two dimensions, an ordered pair:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y) } $$

identifies a point in the Cartesian plane.

Graph of a Function

If:

$$ y=f(x), $$

then the graph consists of all points:

$$ \boxed{ (x,f(x)) } $$

for admissible inputs $x$.

Example: Linear Function

For:

$$ f(x)=2x+1, $$

the graph is:

$$ \boxed{ \{(x,2x+1):x\in\mathbb{R}\} }. $$

In the Cartesian plane this set forms a straight line.

Example: Quadratic Function

For:

$$ f(x)=x^2, $$

the graph is:

$$ \boxed{ \{(x,x^2):x\in\mathbb{R}\} }. $$

Its familiar geometric shape is a parabola.

Graph of a Relation

A graph need not represent a function.

For example:

$$ x^2+y^2=1 $$

defines the unit circle:

$$ \boxed{ \{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^2:x^2+y^2=1\} }. $$

The circle is the graph of a relation, but it is not the graph of a single real-valued function $y=f(x)$ over the entire interval $[-1,1]$.

Equation Graph

To graph an equation means to display all coordinate points satisfying that equation.

$$ \boxed{ \text{graph of }F(x,y)=0 = \{(x,y):F(x,y)=0\} }. $$

Solution Set Interpretation

A coordinate graph can therefore be viewed as a geometric representation of a solution set.

For:

$$ y=3x-4, $$

every point on the graph represents a solution pair satisfying the equation.

Graph as a Set

Formally, the graph is not merely the visible curve drawn on paper or a screen. It is a set of ordered pairs.

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Graph}(f) \subseteq X\times Y }. $$

The drawing is a representation of that set.

Function as a Set of Ordered Pairs

In a set-theoretic treatment, a function itself may be identified with its graph, together with suitable domain or codomain conventions.

A function $f:X\to Y$ can be represented by:

$$ \boxed{ \{(x,f(x)):x\in X\} }. $$

Domain from a Graph

In a conventional graph of $y=f(x)$, the domain consists of the horizontal input values represented:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f) = \{x:\exists y\text{ with }(x,y)\in\operatorname{Graph}(f)\} }. $$

Range from a Graph

The range consists of the vertical output values attained:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Range}(f) = \{y:\exists x\text{ with }(x,y)\in\operatorname{Graph}(f)\} }. $$

Vertical-Line Test

A subset of the coordinate plane represents $y$ as a function of $x$ precisely when every vertical line meets the graph in at most one point.

$$ \boxed{ \text{one input }x \Longrightarrow \text{at most one output }y }. $$

Why the Vertical-Line Test Works

If a vertical line $x=c$ intersects the graph twice, then the same input $c$ would correspond to two distinct outputs. That violates the defining uniqueness condition for a function.

Horizontal-Line Test

The horizontal-line test determines whether a function is one-to-one on the displayed domain.

If every horizontal line intersects the graph in at most one point, then:

$$ \boxed{ f\text{ is injective} }. $$

Intercepts

Intercepts are points where a graph meets a coordinate axis.

$x$-Intercept

An $x$-intercept of $y=f(x)$ occurs where:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=0 }. $$

Thus $x$-intercepts correspond to real zeros or roots of the function.

$y$-Intercept

If $0$ lies in the domain, the $y$-intercept is:

$$ \boxed{ (0,f(0)) }. $$

Zeros and Graphs

For a real function:

$$ \boxed{ f(c)=0 \iff (c,0) \text{ lies on the graph} }. $$

Slope

For a line through distinct points $(x_1,y_1)$ and $(x_2,y_2)$:

$$ \boxed{ m = \frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1} } $$

when $x_1\neq x_2$.

Increasing Graph

Informally, a graph is increasing over an interval when function values rise as inputs increase.

More precisely:

$$ x_1<x_2 \Longrightarrow f(x_1)\leq f(x_2) $$

for a nondecreasing function on the interval.

Decreasing Graph

A function is nonincreasing when:

$$ x_1<x_2 \Longrightarrow f(x_1)\geq f(x_2). $$

Maximum and Minimum

Graphs help visualize local and absolute extrema.

If:

$$ f(c)\geq f(x) $$

for all relevant $x$, then $(c,f(c))$ represents an absolute maximum point.

Continuity

A continuous graph is often informally described as drawable without lifting the pencil, but the formal mathematical definition of continuity is expressed using limits rather than drawing behavior.

Discontinuity

A graph may display holes, jumps, vertical asymptotes, or other features associated with discontinuity.

Hole

An open circle is often used to indicate a point excluded from the graph.

Closed Point

A filled point conventionally indicates that the represented endpoint or value is included.

Inequality Graph

Graphing an inequality generally represents a region of points satisfying the inequality rather than only a curve.

For example:

$$ y\geq x^2 $$

represents the parabola $y=x^2$ together with the region on or above it.

Boundary Inclusion

In graphical conventions, a solid boundary commonly represents inclusion:

$$ \leq,\quad\geq, $$

while a dashed boundary commonly represents strict inequality:

$$ <,\quad>. $$

Number-Line Graph

Inequalities and intervals can be graphed on a one-dimensional number line.

For:

$$ x<8, $$

the graph consists of every point to the left of $8$, excluding $8$ itself.

Cartesian Graph

A Cartesian graph uses perpendicular coordinate axes, commonly the $x$- and $y$-axes in two dimensions.

Three-Dimensional Graph

A function of two variables:

$$ z=f(x,y) $$

has graph:

$$ \boxed{ \{(x,y,f(x,y)):(x,y)\in\operatorname{Dom}(f)\} } $$

in three-dimensional space.

Higher-Dimensional Graph

More generally, if:

$$ f:X\to Y, $$

its graph is a subset of:

$$ \boxed{ X\times Y }. $$

The concept therefore extends beyond objects that can literally be drawn.

Parametric Graph

A curve can be specified parametrically:

$$ \boxed{ x=x(t), \qquad y=y(t) }. $$

The parameter $t$ determines points on the graph.

Polar Graph

In polar coordinates a curve may be specified by:

$$ \boxed{ r=f(\theta) }. $$

Here the symbol $r$ represents radial distance rather than a Cartesian output variable.

Implicit Graph

A relation:

$$ F(x,y)=0 $$

defines its graph implicitly without first solving for $y$.

Discrete Graph

Sequences and discrete functions produce isolated graph points rather than a continuous curve.

A sequence $(a_n)$ is often graphed as:

$$ \boxed{ (n,a_n) }. $$

Scatter Plot

A scatter plot displays observed data pairs. It may suggest a mathematical relationship, but the plotted observations do not automatically define a continuous function between the points.

Graph vs. Plot

In ordinary mathematical language, graph and plot often overlap. Plot frequently emphasizes the act or visual product of placing data or function values on axes, while graph may refer more formally to the mathematical set represented.

Graphing

Graphing is the process of constructing or displaying a graph from symbolic, numerical, or relational information.

Graph from a Table

A table of ordered pairs can be graphed by plotting each pair:

$$ (x_1,y_1),\ (x_2,y_2),\ldots. $$

Graph from an Equation

One may choose admissible $x$-values, calculate corresponding $y$-values, and plot the resulting ordered pairs.

Graph from Structural Features

More efficient graphing often uses intercepts, symmetry, asymptotes, extrema, transformations, and end behavior instead of producing a large table.

Graph Transformations

Starting from $y=f(x)$, common transformations include:

$$ \boxed{ y=f(x)+k } $$

for vertical translation and:

$$ \boxed{ y=f(x-h) } $$

for horizontal translation.

Vertical Reflection

$$ \boxed{ y=-f(x) } $$

reflects a graph across the $x$-axis.

Horizontal Reflection

$$ \boxed{ y=f(-x) } $$

reflects a graph across the $y$-axis.

Vertical Scaling

$$ \boxed{ y=af(x) } $$

multiplies all output coordinates by $a$.

Horizontal Scaling

$$ \boxed{ y=f(bx) } $$

changes the horizontal scale by a reciprocal factor relative to $b$.

Graph of an Inverse Function

The graph of an invertible function $f^{-1}$ is obtained by reflecting the graph of $f$ across:

$$ \boxed{ y=x }. $$

This corresponds to exchanging ordered-pair coordinates:

$$ (x,y)\mapsto(y,x). $$

Even Function Graph

If:

$$ f(-x)=f(x), $$

then the graph is symmetric about the $y$-axis.

Odd Function Graph

If:

$$ f(-x)=-f(x), $$

then the graph has $180^\circ$ rotational symmetry about the origin.

Periodic Graph

If:

$$ f(x+T)=f(x), $$

the graph repeats horizontally with period $T$.

Asymptote

An asymptote describes a line or other comparison object that a graph approaches in a specified limiting sense.

Vertical Asymptote

A vertical asymptote can occur where function values become unbounded as $x$ approaches a finite value.

Horizontal Asymptote

A horizontal asymptote:

$$ y=L $$

can occur when:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to\pm\infty}f(x)=L }. $$

End Behavior

The graph's behavior for large positive or negative inputs can reveal dominant algebraic or asymptotic structure.

Graphical Solution of Equations

To solve:

$$ f(x)=g(x), $$

graph both functions and locate their intersection points.

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=g(x) \iff (x,f(x)) \text{ lies on both graphs} }. $$

Graphical Solution of $f(x)=0$

Solve graphically by finding the $x$-intercepts of $y=f(x)$.

Approximate Nature of Visual Reading

A graph may suggest a numerical solution, but unless the graph is supplemented by exact symbolic information, visually read coordinates are generally approximate.

Scale Matters

Changing axis scales can dramatically alter visual appearance without changing the underlying mathematical relation.

Equal Aspect Ratio

If one unit on the horizontal axis is drawn with the same physical length as one unit on the vertical axis, geometric angles and shapes are represented without directional scale distortion.

Distorted Graph

Unequal scales may make slopes appear steeper or flatter and circles appear as ellipses.

Window Selection

Graphing technology displays only a selected viewing window. Important behavior can occur outside that window.

Graphing Technology

Calculators, spreadsheets, computer algebra systems, and mathematical software can sample functions numerically and render graphical approximations.

Sampling Artifacts

Digital graphers typically evaluate finitely many points. Discontinuities or rapid oscillations can therefore be rendered misleadingly if sampling is too coarse.

A Drawn Curve Is Not the Definition

A graph displayed by software is an approximation to a mathematical set. The underlying function or relation is defined independently of pixel resolution.

Graph Theory Meaning

In discrete mathematics, a graph is an abstract structure used to model connections.

$$ \boxed{ G=(V,E) }. $$

Here $V$ is the vertex set and $E$ is the edge set.

Vertex

A vertex, also called a node in many applied contexts, represents an object or position.

Edge

An edge represents a connection between vertices.

Simple Graph

A simple undirected graph is often formalized with edges as two-element subsets of $V$:

$$ \boxed{ E\subseteq \bigl\{\{u,v\}:u,v\in V,\ u\neq v\bigr\} }. $$

Directed Graph

In a directed graph, or digraph, edges have orientation and may be represented as ordered pairs:

$$ \boxed{ E\subseteq V\times V }. $$

Weighted Graph

A weighted graph assigns a numerical quantity such as distance, cost, capacity, or probability to edges or vertices.

Graph Drawing vs. Abstract Graph

In graph theory, the physical drawing is not the graph itself. Moving vertices on the page without changing their incidence relationships does not change the abstract graph.

Graph-Theory Isomorphism

Two graphs can look different geometrically but represent the same abstract connection structure if they are isomorphic.

Network

In applied settings, graphs model transportation systems, communication systems, social connections, dependency structures, and flows.

Path

A path is a sequence of adjacent vertices or edges describing a route through a graph.

Cycle

A cycle is a closed route satisfying specified nonrepetition conditions.

Degree of a Vertex

In an undirected graph, the degree of a vertex counts incident edges, with conventions adjusted when loops are allowed.

Connected Graph

A graph is connected when every pair of vertices is joined by a path.

Tree

A tree is a connected graph containing no cycles.

Graph of a Function vs. Graph-Theory Graph

These are different mathematical meanings of the same word.

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Graph}(f) = \{(x,f(x))\} } $$

describes ordered pairs, while:

$$ \boxed{ G=(V,E) } $$

describes vertices and edges.

Graph vs. Chart

In everyday language, graph and chart are sometimes used interchangeably. Mathematically, a chart may include bars, sectors, tables, or other displays that are not graphs of functions or relations.

Bar Graph

A bar graph is a statistical or categorical display using rectangular bars. It does not ordinarily mean the graph of a function in the formal set-theoretic sense.

Line Graph in Statistics

In elementary data analysis, a line graph connects successive data points to emphasize change over an ordered variable, often time.

Line Graph in Graph Theory

In graph theory, a line graph has a specialized meaning: its vertices correspond to edges of another graph, with adjacency determined by shared endpoints.

This is another example of context-sensitive mathematical terminology.

Graph vs. Diagram

A diagram may be a broad explanatory drawing. A graph usually follows a defined coordinate, relational, data, or network structure.

Graph vs. Model

A graph can represent a model, but the graph is not necessarily the model itself. The model may consist of equations, assumptions, parameters, and relationships whose consequences are visualized graphically.

Common Error: Assuming Every Graph Is a Function

Circles, sideways parabolas, and many relations fail the vertical-line test and therefore are not graphs of functions $y=f(x)$, even though they are perfectly valid graphs.

Common Error: Assuming a Graph Is Only a Drawing

Formally, the graph of a function or relation is a set of ordered pairs. A displayed curve is a visual representation of that set.

Common Error: Connecting Discrete Points Automatically

If a sequence is defined only for integer indices, connecting the plotted points may falsely suggest values at noninteger inputs.

Common Error: Reading Exact Values from a Sketch

A hand-drawn or digitally sampled graph may provide estimates rather than exact coordinates.

Common Error: Ignoring Scale

Apparent steepness, curvature, and relative distances can change when the axis scales change.

Common Error: Assuming a Graphing Window Shows All Behavior

Zeros, extrema, asymptotes, or oscillations may lie outside the displayed window.

Common Error: Confusing an Open Circle with the Number Zero

An open circle is a graphical convention indicating exclusion; it does not mean that the coordinate value itself is zero.

Common Error: Confusing Graph-Theory Edges with Coordinate Axes

In graph theory, edges connect vertices. They are unrelated to the $x$- and $y$-axes of a Cartesian coordinate graph unless a particular application makes such a connection.

Common Error: Treating Graph-Theory Drawings Geometrically

Unless the graph is explicitly geometric or weighted, ordinary edge lengths and angles in a drawing usually carry no mathematical meaning.

Common Misconception: The word graph does not have one universal visual meaning. In algebra and analysis, a graph is typically a set of ordered pairs such as $(x,f(x))$. In graph theory, a graph is an abstract vertex-edge structure $G=(V,E)$. In data analysis, the word may refer more broadly to a visual display. Mathematical context determines which meaning is intended.

The verb graph means to construct or display a graph:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"Graph }y=x^2\text{"} } $$

means to represent the solution points of $y=x^2$ in the appropriate coordinate system.

The noun and verb must therefore be distinguished:

$$ \boxed{ \text{a graph} \qquad\text{vs.}\qquad \text{to graph} }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: When the word graph could be ambiguous, specify graph of the function, coordinate graph, data graph, or graph $G=(V,E)$. Treat a function graph as the set of ordered pairs it represents rather than merely its drawn curve, and do not assign geometric meaning to a graph-theory drawing unless that structure is explicitly part of the problem.

  • Solution-Set Interpretation: Every point on an equation's graph is a solution.

  • Number-Line Graph: Represents one-dimensional sets, inequalities, or intervals.
  • Three-Dimensional Graph: May represent $z=f(x,y)$ as a surface.
  • Higher-Dimensional Graph: The graph of $f:X\to Y$ is a subset of $X\times Y$ even when it cannot be visualized directly.
  • Parametric Graph: Defined by coordinates depending on a parameter.
  • Polar Graph: Represented using radial and angular coordinates.
  • Implicit Graph: Defined by a relation such as $F(x,y)=0$.

  • Sequence Graph: Commonly represented by points $(n,a_n)$.

  • Vertical-Line Test: Determines whether a plane relation represents $y$ as a function of $x$.
  • Horizontal-Line Test: Tests one-to-one behavior of a function.
  • $x$-Intercept: Corresponds to a real zero of $f$.
  • $y$-Intercept: Usually $(0,f(0))$.
  • Slope: Measures rate of vertical change relative to horizontal change.
  • Increasing Behavior: Function values rise as inputs increase over the relevant interval.
  • Decreasing Behavior: Function values fall as inputs increase over the relevant interval.
  • Extrema: Maximum and minimum values can often be identified graphically.
  • Continuity: Graphs help visualize but do not formally define continuity.
  • Discontinuity: May appear as holes, jumps, asymptotes, or other breaks in behavior.
  • Open Circle: Common convention for an excluded point.
  • Closed Point: Common convention for an included point.
  • Inequality Graph: Represents a region or set satisfying an inequality.

  • Aspect Ratio: Affects apparent geometric shape and slope.
  • Viewing Window: Determines which portion of a graph is displayed.

  • Graphing Calculator: Automates numerical evaluation and display.
  • Computer Algebra System: Can combine symbolic analysis with graph generation.

  • Bar Graph: Categorical or numerical data display using bars.
