Conclusion

Subfield: Foundations & Mathematical Logic

Etymology & Roots

In mathematics and logic, a conclusion is a statement that is asserted to follow from one or more premises, assumptions, hypotheses, or previously established results.

$$ \boxed{ \text{premises} \longrightarrow \text{reasoning} \longrightarrow \text{conclusion} }. $$

The word comes from roots associated with bringing something to a close. In mathematical reasoning, the conclusion is the statement reached at the end of an argument or proof.

If premises $P_1,P_2,\ldots,P_n$ logically support a statement $Q$, an argument may be represented schematically as:

$$ \boxed{ P_1,\ P_2,\ldots,P_n \therefore Q }. $$

Here $Q$ is the conclusion.

The term also appears in conditional statements, theorem statements, proofs, mathematical induction, contradiction arguments, statistical inference, and ordinary problem solving. Its exact role depends on the surrounding logical structure.

Operational Nuance

Conclusion of an Argument

An argument consists of one or more premises together with a statement presented as following from them.

For example:

$$ \begin{aligned} &\text{All multiples of }4\text{ are even.}\\ &20\text{ is a multiple of }4.\\ &\therefore 20\text{ is even.} \end{aligned} $$

The statement:

$$ \boxed{ 20\text{ is even} } $$

is the conclusion.

Premises versus Conclusion

Premises Conclusion
Statements accepted or assumed for the argument Statement claimed to follow from them
Starting information Derived endpoint
Often appear before $\therefore$ Often appears after $\therefore$

The Therefore Symbol

The symbol:

$$ \boxed{ \therefore } $$

is read therefore and is often used to introduce a conclusion.

For example:

$$ x=4 \quad\therefore\quad x^2=16. $$

Logical Consequence

In formal logic, a conclusion $Q$ is a logical consequence of premises $P_1,\ldots,P_n$ if every interpretation that makes all the premises true also makes $Q$ true.

This may be written:

$$ \boxed{ P_1,\ldots,P_n\models Q }. $$

The symbol $\models$ expresses semantic entailment.

Formal Derivability

In a formal proof system, one may instead write:

$$ \boxed{ P_1,\ldots,P_n\vdash Q }. $$

This means that $Q$ can be formally derived from the premises according to the rules of the proof system.

Semantic versus Syntactic Consequence

Notation Meaning
$\Gamma\models Q$ $Q$ is true in every model in which all statements in $\Gamma$ are true
$\Gamma\vdash Q$ $Q$ can be derived from $\Gamma$ using formal inference rules

Validity Concerns the Connection

An argument is valid when it is impossible for all of its premises to be true while its conclusion is false.

$$ \boxed{ \text{true premises} \Rightarrow \text{true conclusion} } $$

describes what validity guarantees.

A Valid Argument Can Have False Premises

Validity concerns the logical relationship between premises and conclusion, not whether the premises happen to be factually true.

For example:

$$ \begin{aligned} &\text{All squares are circles.}\\ &\text{Every circle is blue.}\\ &\therefore\text{ every square is blue.} \end{aligned} $$

The form of the argument is valid even though the premises are false in ordinary Euclidean geometry.

Soundness

A deductive argument is sound when it is valid and its premises are true.

$$ \boxed{ \text{sound} = \text{valid} + \text{true premises} }. $$

A sound argument therefore has a true conclusion.

Conclusion of a Conditional Statement

In elementary mathematics, a conditional statement has the form:

$$ \boxed{ P\Rightarrow Q }. $$

It is read:

$$ \text{“If }P,\text{ then }Q\text{.”} $$

The statement $P$ is the hypothesis or antecedent, and $Q$ is often called the conclusion or consequent.

Example of a Conditional Conclusion

Consider:

$$ \boxed{ \text{If }n\text{ is divisible by }4, \text{ then }n\text{ is even.} } $$

The hypothesis is:

$$ n\text{ is divisible by }4, $$

and the conclusion is:

$$ \boxed{ n\text{ is even} }. $$

Conclusion versus Consequent

In a conditional statement:

$$ P\Rightarrow Q, $$

the term consequent refers specifically to $Q$ as the second part of the conditional.

Conclusion is broader and may refer to the endpoint of any argument or proof, even when the reasoning is not expressed as one conditional statement.

Conclusion of a Theorem

Many theorems can be expressed schematically as:

$$ \boxed{ \text{hypotheses} \Rightarrow \text{conclusion} }. $$

For example, the statement:

$$ \text{If }a,b\text{ are even integers, then }a+b\text{ is even} $$

has assumptions about $a$ and $b$ followed by the conclusion that $a+b$ is even.

A Proof Establishes the Conclusion

A proof provides a logically justified route from accepted starting information to the statement being established.

$$ \boxed{ \text{hypotheses} \overset{\text{proof}}{\Longrightarrow} \text{conclusion} }. $$

Direct Proof

In a direct proof of:

$$ P\Rightarrow Q, $$

one assumes $P$ and derives $Q$.

$$ \boxed{ P \to R_1 \to R_2 \to \cdots \to Q }. $$

The final statement $Q$ is the desired conclusion.

Proof by Contrapositive

Instead of proving:

$$ P\Rightarrow Q, $$

one may prove the logically equivalent contrapositive:

$$ \boxed{ \neg Q\Rightarrow\neg P }. $$

This still establishes the original conclusion conditionally upon $P$.

Proof by Contradiction

To prove a proposition $Q$ by contradiction, one assumes:

$$ \neg Q $$

and derives an impossibility:

$$ \boxed{ \bot }. $$

The contradiction forces rejection of $\neg Q$, thereby establishing $Q$.

Mathematical Induction

In an induction proof, the conclusion is typically a universally quantified statement:

$$ \boxed{ P(n)\text{ holds for all }n\ge n_0 }. $$

The proof establishes a base case and an inductive implication:

$$ P(k)\Rightarrow P(k+1). $$

Together, these justify the final universal conclusion.

Conclusion of a Worked Problem

In applied mathematics, the conclusion is often the interpreted result rather than merely the last numerical expression.

If calculation gives:

$$ t=3.5, $$

the complete conclusion might be:

$$ \boxed{ \text{The process requires }3.5\text{ hours.} } $$

Units and context are part of the final mathematical interpretation.

Conclusion versus Answer

An answer responds to a question. A conclusion emphasizes that the result has been reached from evidence, premises, calculations, or reasoning.

In many routine exercises they coincide, but their meanings are not identical.

Conclusion versus Result

A result may be any mathematical output obtained during a calculation or argument.

A conclusion is specifically the proposition ultimately supported or asserted.

$$ \boxed{ \text{intermediate result} \ne \text{necessarily the final conclusion} }. $$

Conclusion versus Hypothesis

In:

$$ P\Rightarrow Q, $$

$P$ is the hypothesis and $Q$ is the conclusion.

$$ \boxed{ \text{hypothesis} \longrightarrow \text{conclusion} }. $$

Conclusion versus Assumption

An assumption is temporarily or permanently accepted as a starting point. A conclusion is reached from assumptions and other justified statements.

Conclusion versus Conjecture

A conjecture is a proposed mathematical statement believed to be true but not yet proved.

A theorem's conclusion is established within a proof; a conjecture may be the prospective conclusion one hopes eventually to prove.

Conclusion versus Theorem

A theorem is a complete proved mathematical proposition. Its conclusion is the part asserted to follow from its hypotheses.

Therefore a theorem and its conclusion are related but not necessarily identical grammatical objects.

Inference Rules Produce Conclusions

Formal reasoning uses inference rules to move from accepted statements to new conclusions.

One important example is modus ponens:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{P\Rightarrow Q,\qquad P}{Q} }. $$

From the conditional and its antecedent, one concludes $Q$.

Modus Tollens

Another valid form is:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{P\Rightarrow Q,\qquad\neg Q}{\neg P} }. $$

The conclusion is $\neg P$.

Hypothetical Syllogism

$$ \boxed{ \frac{P\Rightarrow Q,\qquad Q\Rightarrow R} {P\Rightarrow R} }. $$

Here the conclusion combines two implications into a new implication.

Disjunctive Syllogism

$$ \boxed{ \frac{P\lor Q,\qquad\neg P}{Q} }. $$

The conclusion follows by eliminating one disjunct.

Invalid Conclusion: Affirming the Consequent

From:

$$ P\Rightarrow Q $$

and:

$$ Q, $$

one cannot generally conclude:

$$ P. $$

This invalid pattern is called affirming the consequent.

$$ \boxed{ P\Rightarrow Q,\ Q \not\Rightarrow P }. $$

Invalid Conclusion: Denying the Antecedent

From:

$$ P\Rightarrow Q $$

and:

$$ \neg P, $$

one cannot generally conclude:

$$ \neg Q. $$
$$ \boxed{ P\Rightarrow Q,\ \neg P \not\Rightarrow \neg Q }. $$

Necessary and Sufficient Conditions

If:

$$ P\Rightarrow Q, $$

then $P$ is sufficient for $Q$, while $Q$ is necessary for $P$.

Thus the conclusion of an implication is often a necessary consequence of the hypothesis.

Conclusions from Equivalence

If:

$$ P\iff Q, $$

then both directions are valid:

$$ P\Rightarrow Q \qquad\text{and}\qquad Q\Rightarrow P. $$

Either proposition may therefore serve as a conclusion when the other is assumed.

Universal Conclusions

A proof may conclude:

$$ \boxed{ \forall x\in S,\ P(x) }. $$

Such a conclusion is much stronger than verifying $P(x)$ for several individual examples.

Existential Conclusions

A conclusion may instead assert existence:

$$ \boxed{ \exists x\in S \text{ such that }P(x) }. $$

A constructive proof may establish this by explicitly producing such an $x$.

Counterexamples Defeat Universal Conclusions

To disprove:

$$ \forall x\in S,\ P(x), $$

it is enough to exhibit one $c\in S$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ \neg P(c) }. $$

Thus a single valid counterexample blocks the proposed universal conclusion.

Statistical Conclusions

In statistics, a conclusion is often inferential rather than deductively certain. Data may support a conclusion about a population, model, parameter, or hypothesis, but the conclusion is typically qualified by probability, uncertainty, significance level, confidence level, or model assumptions.

This differs from deductive mathematical proof, where a valid derivation from true premises establishes the conclusion necessarily.

Conclusion in Hypothesis Testing

A statistical test may lead to language such as:

$$ \boxed{ \text{reject }H_0 \quad\text{or}\quad \text{fail to reject }H_0 }. $$

The second phrase does not ordinarily mean that the null hypothesis has been proved true.

Conclusion in Mathematical Modeling

A model-based conclusion is conditional on the assumptions of the model.

$$ \boxed{ \text{model assumptions} + \text{analysis} \Rightarrow \text{model-dependent conclusion} }. $$

If assumptions change, the conclusion may change as well.

Conclusion Should Match the Scope of the Premises

A valid conclusion cannot legitimately claim more than the premises and reasoning support.

For example, evidence concerning all integers in a specified class may support a conclusion about that class, but not automatically about all real numbers.

Conclusion Should Preserve Conditions

If a result is valid only under a restriction:

$$ x\ne0, $$

the conclusion should preserve that restriction whenever it remains relevant.

Extraneous Solutions and Conclusions

Algebraic manipulation can sometimes produce candidate values that do not satisfy the original equation.

Therefore the final conclusion should contain only verified solutions.

$$ \boxed{ \text{candidate solutions} \xrightarrow{\text{check}} \text{valid conclusion} }. $$

Example: Squaring Can Introduce an Extraneous Candidate

Suppose:

$$ \sqrt{x}=x-2. $$

Squaring produces:

$$ x=(x-2)^2. $$

The resulting candidates must be substituted back into the original equation before stating the solution-set conclusion.

Conclusion in Geometry

A geometric proof may conclude that two angles are congruent, two lines are parallel, triangles are similar, or a particular incidence relation holds.

For example:

$$ \boxed{ \triangle ABC\cong\triangle DEF \therefore \angle A\cong\angle D }. $$

The conclusion follows from corresponding parts of congruent triangles.

Conclusion in Algebra

An algebraic argument may conclude with a solution set:

$$ x^2=9 \quad\therefore\quad \boxed{x\in\{-3,3\}}. $$

Writing only one root would give an incomplete conclusion.

Conclusion in Calculus

After derivative analysis, one might conclude:

$$ \boxed{ f\text{ has a local maximum at }x=c }. $$

This conclusion should be supported by the appropriate derivative or comparison test.

Conclusion in Probability

If $A$ and $B$ are independent:

$$ P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B). $$

A calculation may then support a numerical probability conclusion, but the independence assumption remains part of the justification.

Conclusion Indicators in Prose

Words and phrases often introducing conclusions include:

  • therefore,
  • thus,
  • hence,
  • consequently,
  • it follows that,
  • we conclude that, and
  • so.

These are linguistic indicators rather than proofs by themselves. The preceding reasoning must actually justify the conclusion.

Q.E.D.

The abbreviation:

$$ \boxed{ \mathrm{Q.E.D.} } $$

traditionally marks the end of a proof after the desired conclusion has been established. It derives from the Latin phrase quod erat demonstrandum, meaning approximately "which was to be demonstrated."

The symbol:

$$ \boxed{\square} $$

is also commonly used to mark the end of a proof.

Common Misconception: A Conclusion Is Whatever Comes Last

The final sentence of an argument is not automatically a justified conclusion. It must be supported by the preceding reasoning.

Common Misconception: A True Conclusion Makes an Argument Valid

An invalid argument can accidentally have a true conclusion.

Validity concerns whether the conclusion follows from the premises, not merely whether the conclusion happens to be true.

$$ \boxed{ \text{true conclusion} \not\Rightarrow \text{valid argument} }. $$

Common Misconception: Validity Means Every Statement Is True

A valid argument can contain false premises. Soundness, not validity alone, requires true premises.

Common Misconception: The Conclusion of P → Q Is P

In the conditional:

$$ P\Rightarrow Q, $$

$P$ is the hypothesis and $Q$ is the conclusion.

Common Misconception: A Conclusion Must Be Numerical

Conclusions may be equations, inequalities, classifications, existence claims, universal statements, geometric relations, logical propositions, or verbal interpretations.

Common Misconception: Evidence and Proof Produce the Same Kind of Conclusion

Statistical or empirical evidence may strongly support a claim without establishing it deductively. A mathematical proof aims at logical necessity under its stated assumptions.

Common Misconception: Failing to Reject Means Accepting as Proved

In hypothesis testing, failing to reject a null hypothesis generally means the data did not provide sufficient evidence for rejection under the chosen procedure. It does not ordinarily prove the null hypothesis.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$P\therefore Q$ "P; therefore, Q."
$P\Rightarrow Q$ "If P, then Q."
$P_1,\ldots,P_n\models Q$ "Q is a logical consequence of the premises."
$P_1,\ldots,P_n\vdash Q$ "Q is derivable from the premises."
$\frac{P\Rightarrow Q,\ P}{Q}$ "From P implies Q and P, conclude Q."
$\therefore x=5$ "Therefore, x equals five."
$\square$ "End of proof."

In ordinary mathematical prose, the conclusion is often introduced by words such as therefore, thus, hence, or it follows that. These words signal the logical role of the statement but do not themselves supply justification.

When discussing a conditional statement, one may say: the hypothesis is $P$ and the conclusion is $Q$. In formal logic, antecedent and consequent are also common.

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish premises from conclusions and distinguish the consequent of a conditional from the broader notion of a conclusion of an argument. Use $\therefore$ only as a marker of inference, not as a substitute for justification. Preserve restrictions, assumptions, units, and domain conditions in the final conclusion. Distinguish semantic consequence $\models$ from formal derivability $\vdash$ when working in logic, and do not treat a true conclusion alone as evidence that an argument is valid.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: A conclusion is the statement asserted to follow from premises, assumptions, hypotheses, calculations, or previously established results.
  • Argument Structure: $$ \boxed{ P_1,\ldots,P_n \therefore Q }. $$
  • Conditional Structure: In $P\Rightarrow Q$, $P$ is the hypothesis and $Q$ is the conclusion.
  • Consequent: The consequent is specifically the $Q$ portion of a conditional statement.
  • Logical Consequence: $$ \boxed{ \Gamma\models Q }. $$
  • Formal Derivation: $$ \boxed{ \Gamma\vdash Q }. $$
  • Validity: A valid argument cannot have true premises and a false conclusion.
  • Soundness: A sound argument is valid and has true premises.
  • Theorem Structure: The conclusion is the proposition established under the theorem's hypotheses.
  • Direct Proof: Begins from hypotheses and derives the desired conclusion.
  • Contrapositive: Proving $\neg Q\Rightarrow\neg P$ establishes $P\Rightarrow Q$.
  • Contradiction: Assuming the negation of the desired conclusion and deriving $\bot$ establishes the original claim.
  • Induction: Base and inductive steps support a universal conclusion.
  • Modus Ponens: $$ \boxed{ P\Rightarrow Q,\ P \therefore Q }. $$
  • Modus Tollens: $$ \boxed{ P\Rightarrow Q,\ \neg Q \therefore\neg P }. $$
  • Invalid Form: Affirming the consequent does not justify concluding $P$ from $P\Rightarrow Q$ and $Q$.
  • Invalid Form: Denying the antecedent does not justify concluding $\neg Q$ from $P\Rightarrow Q$ and $\neg P$.
  • Problem Solving: A conclusion should interpret the mathematical output in the context of the original problem.
  • Units: Relevant units belong in a quantitative conclusion.
  • Domain Restrictions: Conditions that remain necessary should be preserved in the final conclusion.
  • Extraneous Solutions: Candidate values should be checked before entering the final solution-set conclusion.
  • Geometry: Conclusions may state congruence, similarity, parallelism, incidence, or angle relationships.
  • Algebra: A conclusion may be a solution set, identity, inequality, or classification.
  • Calculus: Conclusions may concern limits, extrema, monotonicity, concavity, convergence, or accumulated quantities.
  • Statistics: Conclusions are generally inferential and qualified by uncertainty rather than deductively certain.
  • Modeling: A conclusion is conditional on the assumptions and scope of the mathematical model.
  • Conclusion versus Result: A result may be intermediate; a conclusion is the statement ultimately asserted or supported.
  • Conclusion versus Answer: An answer responds to a question, while a conclusion emphasizes what follows from reasoning or evidence.
  • Conclusion versus Hypothesis: A hypothesis is starting information; a conclusion is what follows from it.
  • Conclusion versus Assumption: An assumption is accepted as a starting point; a conclusion is derived.
  • Conclusion versus Conjecture: A conjecture is proposed but unproved; a proved conclusion has been logically established within the argument.
  • Proof Ending: $\square$ or Q.E.D. may mark that the intended conclusion has been demonstrated.
  • Common Error: Do not assume a statement is justified merely because it occurs at the end of an argument.
  • Common Error: Do not infer validity merely because the conclusion is true.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse validity with soundness.
  • Common Error: Do not reverse $P\Rightarrow Q$ and infer $P$ merely from $Q$.
  • Common Error: Do not state a conclusion more broadly than the premises and reasoning justify.
  • Common Error: Do not omit relevant restrictions, units, or assumptions from the final interpretation.
  • Terminological Contrast: A conclusion is the statement claimed to follow from reasoning. A premise is a starting statement used in an argument. A hypothesis is an assumed condition, especially in a conditional or theorem. A consequent is the second component $Q$ of $P\Rightarrow Q$. An assumption is accepted temporarily or axiomatically as a starting point. A result is any obtained mathematical output. A conjecture is an unproved proposed statement. A theorem is a proposition established by proof.
