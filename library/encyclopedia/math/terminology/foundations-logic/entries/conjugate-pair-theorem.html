Conjugate Pair Theorem

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

The Conjugate Pair Theorem states that nonreal complex roots of a polynomial with real coefficients occur in complex-conjugate pairs.

$$ \boxed{ p(a+bi)=0 \Rightarrow p(a-bi)=0, \qquad p(x)\in\mathbb R[x] }. $$

Thus, if $a+bi$ with $b\ne0$ is a zero of a real-coefficient polynomial, then $a-bi$ must also be a zero.

The theorem is also commonly called the Complex Conjugate Root Theorem, Conjugate Root Theorem, or Complex Conjugate Zeros Theorem. The names emphasize the same structural fact: real polynomial coefficients force nonreal roots to appear together with their reflections across the real axis.

$$ \boxed{ a+bi \longleftrightarrow a-bi }. $$

Operational Nuance

Formal Statement

Let:

$$ p(x) = a_nx^n+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_1x+a_0 $$

where:

$$ a_0,a_1,\ldots,a_n\in\mathbb R. $$

If:

$$ p(z)=0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ p(\overline z)=0 }. $$

When $z=a+bi$, its conjugate is $\overline z=a-bi$.

The Real-Coefficient Condition

The requirement that the coefficients be real is essential.

$$ \boxed{ p(x)\in\mathbb R[x] } $$

ensures that conjugation leaves every coefficient unchanged:

$$ \overline{a_k}=a_k. $$

Proof

Suppose:

$$ p(z) = a_nz^n+\cdots+a_1z+a_0 = 0. $$

Taking complex conjugates:

$$ \overline{p(z)} = \overline0 = 0. $$

Since conjugation preserves sums and products:

$$ \overline{p(z)} = \overline{a_n}\,\overline z^{\,n} +\cdots+ \overline{a_1}\,\overline z + \overline{a_0}. $$

Because every coefficient is real:

$$ \overline{a_k}=a_k, $$

so:

$$ \overline{p(z)} = p(\overline z). $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ p(z)=0 \Rightarrow p(\overline z)=0 }. $$

Example

Consider:

$$ p(x)=x^2-6x+13. $$

The quadratic formula gives:

$$ x = \frac{6\pm\sqrt{36-52}}{2} = \frac{6\pm4i}{2}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ x=3+2i \quad\text{and}\quad x=3-2i }. $$

The two roots form a conjugate pair.

Factor Form

If $a+bi$ and $a-bi$ are roots, then the corresponding factors are:

$$ x-(a+bi) \qquad\text{and}\qquad x-(a-bi). $$

Multiplying:

$$ [x-(a+bi)][x-(a-bi)]. $$

This simplifies to:

$$ \boxed{ (x-a)^2+b^2 }. $$

Expanding:

$$ \boxed{ x^2-2ax+(a^2+b^2) }. $$

Every coefficient is real.

Why the Pair Produces Real Coefficients

The imaginary terms cancel when conjugate factors are multiplied:

$$ (x-a-bi)(x-a+bi) = (x-a)^2-(bi)^2. $$

Since $i^2=-1$:

$$ \boxed{ (x-a)^2+b^2 }. $$

Constructing a Polynomial from a Conjugate Pair

Suppose a polynomial with real coefficients has root:

$$ 2+3i. $$

The theorem guarantees the root:

$$ 2-3i. $$

Their factors give:

$$ [x-(2+3i)][x-(2-3i)]. $$

Therefore:

$$ (x-2)^2+9 = \boxed{x^2-4x+13}. $$

Recovering the Missing Root

If a problem states that a real-coefficient polynomial has a zero:

$$ -4+7i, $$

no further calculation is necessary to identify another zero:

$$ \boxed{-4-7i}. $$

Roots Occur with Matching Multiplicity

The theorem extends to multiplicity. If a nonreal root $z$ occurs with multiplicity $m$, then $\overline z$ occurs with the same multiplicity.

$$ \boxed{ m(z)=m(\overline z) } $$

for a polynomial with real coefficients.

Factorization with Multiplicity

If $(x-z)^m$ divides $p(x)$ and $p$ has real coefficients, then:

$$ \boxed{ (x-\overline z)^m \text{ also divides }p(x) }. $$

Odd-Degree Real Polynomials

Since nonreal roots occur in pairs, a real polynomial of odd degree must have at least one real root.

$$ \boxed{ \deg p\text{ odd},\quad p\in\mathbb R[x] \Rightarrow p\text{ has at least one real zero} }. $$

This conclusion is also consistent with the Intermediate Value Theorem for real polynomials of odd degree.

Even-Degree Polynomials

A real polynomial of even degree may have no real roots because all of its roots can occur in nonreal conjugate pairs.

For example:

$$ x^2+1=0 $$

has roots:

$$ \boxed{i,\,-i}. $$

Counting Nonreal Roots

A real-coefficient polynomial has an even number of nonreal roots when roots are counted with multiplicity.

$$ \boxed{ \#(\text{nonreal roots}) \text{ is even} } $$

Fundamental Theorem of Algebra Connection

The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra states that a degree-$n$ polynomial over $\mathbb C$ has exactly $n$ complex roots when counted with multiplicity.

For real-coefficient polynomials, the Conjugate Pair Theorem adds structure to that root set:

$$ \boxed{ \text{real roots} + \text{nonreal conjugate pairs} = n\text{ roots counting multiplicity} }. $$

Cubic Polynomial Consequence

A cubic polynomial with real coefficients has one of two broad root patterns:

  • three real roots, counting multiplicity, or
  • one real root and one nonreal conjugate pair.

Quartic Polynomial Consequence

A quartic with real coefficients may have:

  • four real roots,
  • two real roots and one nonreal conjugate pair, or
  • two nonreal conjugate pairs,

with multiplicities included in the count.

Example with Four Complex Roots

Consider:

$$ p(x) = (x^2+1)(x^2+4). $$

The roots are:

$$ \boxed{ i,\,-i,\, 2i,\,-2i }. $$

They form two conjugate pairs.

Real Roots Are Their Own Conjugates

If $r\in\mathbb R$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \overline r=r }. $$

Thus the theorem does not require distinct paired roots for real zeros. Real roots are fixed points of complex conjugation.

Geometric Interpretation

In the complex plane:

$$ a+bi \leftrightarrow (a,b), $$

while:

$$ a-bi \leftrightarrow (a,-b). $$

Therefore the roots of any real-coefficient polynomial are symmetric with respect to the real axis.

$$ \boxed{ \text{real coefficients} \Rightarrow \text{root set symmetric across the real axis} }. $$

Polynomial Graphs and Complex Roots

A real polynomial graph displays only real input-output pairs. Nonreal zeros do not appear as ordinary $x$-intercepts of the real graph.

Thus a quadratic such as:

$$ x^2+4x+13 $$

can have no real $x$-intercepts while still possessing the complex roots:

$$ \boxed{ -2+3i,\quad -2-3i }. $$

Quadratic Formula Connection

For:

$$ ax^2+bx+c=0, \qquad a,b,c\in\mathbb R, $$

the quadratic formula gives:

$$ x = \frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}. $$

If:

$$ b^2-4ac<0, $$

then the two roots automatically have the form:

$$ \boxed{ u+vi \quad\text{and}\quad u-vi }. $$

Discriminant Connection

For a real quadratic:

Discriminant Roots
$b^2-4ac>0$ Two distinct real roots
$b^2-4ac=0$ One repeated real root
$b^2-4ac<0$ One nonreal complex-conjugate pair

Vieta's Formulas

For a quadratic with roots $r_1$ and $r_2$:

$$ r_1+r_2=-\frac ba, \qquad r_1r_2=\frac ca. $$

If:

$$ r_1=u+vi, \qquad r_2=u-vi, $$

then:

$$ r_1+r_2=2u $$

and:

$$ r_1r_2=u^2+v^2, $$

both of which are real.

Using Known Roots to Factor a Polynomial

Suppose a real polynomial is known to have roots:

$$ 1+2i \qquad\text{and}\qquad -3. $$

The theorem supplies:

$$ 1-2i. $$

A polynomial having exactly these roots, each once, can be constructed as:

$$ [x-(1+2i)][x-(1-2i)](x+3). $$

The conjugate factors combine:

$$ [x-(1+2i)][x-(1-2i)] = (x-1)^2+4 = x^2-2x+5. $$

Hence:

$$ p(x) = (x^2-2x+5)(x+3). $$

The Theorem Does Not Require Integer Coefficients

The coefficients need only be real.

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb Z[x] \subset \mathbb Q[x] \subset \mathbb R[x] } $$

so the theorem applies to integer and rational coefficients as special cases.

The Theorem Can Fail with Complex Coefficients

Consider:

$$ p(x)=x-(1+i). $$

Its only root is:

$$ 1+i. $$

But:

$$ 1-i $$

is not a root.

The polynomial has a nonreal coefficient, so the Conjugate Pair Theorem does not apply.

Why Realness Is the Decisive Property

The key identity:

$$ \overline{p(z)} = p(\overline z) $$

works because conjugation fixes every coefficient.

If the coefficients are complex, then generally:

$$ \overline{p(z)} = \overline p(\overline z), $$

where $\overline p$ is the polynomial obtained by conjugating the coefficients.

Polynomial Invariance under Conjugation

For a real-coefficient polynomial:

$$ \boxed{ p(\overline z) = \overline{p(z)} }. $$

This commutation relation is the underlying algebraic reason for the theorem.

Symmetry of the Root Multiset

Counting multiplicities, the complete root multiset of a real polynomial is invariant under:

$$ \boxed{ z\mapsto\overline z }. $$

Relation to Algebraic Conjugates

Complex-conjugate roots are often also algebraic conjugates in familiar examples, but the concepts are not identical.

Complex conjugation specifically maps:

$$ a+bi \mapsto a-bi. $$

Algebraic conjugates are related through roots or embeddings associated with a minimal polynomial over a specified base field.

Example of Algebraic Conjugates Beyond a Pair

The polynomial:

$$ x^3-2 $$

has three algebraic roots:

$$ \sqrt[3]{2}, \qquad \omega\sqrt[3]{2}, \qquad \omega^2\sqrt[3]{2}. $$

The latter two are a complex-conjugate pair, while all three are algebraically related as roots of the same irreducible polynomial over $\mathbb Q$.

Relation to the Factor Theorem

The Factor Theorem states:

$$ \boxed{ p(c)=0 \iff (x-c)\mid p(x) }. $$

Combining this with the Conjugate Pair Theorem:

$$ p(a+bi)=0 \Rightarrow (x-a-bi)(x-a+bi)\mid p(x). $$

Irreducible Quadratic Factors over the Reals

Each nonreal conjugate pair corresponds to a real quadratic factor:

$$ \boxed{ (x-a)^2+b^2, \qquad b\ne0 }. $$

Its discriminant is:

$$ (-2a)^2 - 4(a^2+b^2) = -4b^2<0, $$

so it has no real roots and is irreducible over $\mathbb R$.

Real Factorization Structure

Consequently, every nonconstant real polynomial factors over $\mathbb R$ into linear factors and irreducible quadratic factors.

$$ \boxed{ p(x) = c \prod_i(x-r_i) \prod_j \left[ (x-a_j)^2+b_j^2 \right] } $$

with real $r_i$ and nonzero real $b_j$, counting multiplicities appropriately.

Common Misconception: Every Complex Root Must Have a Different Partner

Real roots are complex numbers too, but each real number is its own conjugate. The distinct-pair conclusion concerns nonreal roots.

Common Misconception: The Theorem Applies to Every Polynomial

It does not. Real coefficients are required.

Common Misconception: Only Quadratics Have Conjugate Roots

The theorem applies to real polynomials of every positive degree.

Common Misconception: Conjugate Roots Need Different Multiplicities

They occur with equal multiplicity.

Common Misconception: A Nonreal Root Can Appear Alone in a Real Cubic

It cannot. Any nonreal root forces its conjugate, so the remaining third root must be real.

Common Misconception: The Conjugate Root Has the Opposite Real Part

Complex conjugation preserves the real part and reverses only the imaginary part:

$$ \boxed{ a+bi \mapsto a-bi }. $$

Common Misconception: The Theorem Says Roots Are Additive Inverses

The additive inverse of:

$$ a+bi $$

is:

$$ -a-bi, $$

not $a-bi$.

Common Misconception: The Pair Theorem Is the Same as the Quadratic Formula

The quadratic formula visibly produces conjugate pairs for real quadratics with negative discriminant, but the Conjugate Pair Theorem is a general structural result for real polynomials of any degree.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$p(a+bi)=0\Rightarrow p(a-bi)=0$ "If a plus b i is a root, then a minus b i is also a root."
$p(z)=0\Rightarrow p(\overline z)=0$ "If z is a zero of the real-coefficient polynomial p, then z bar is also a zero."
$z=a+bi,\ \overline z=a-bi$ "z and z bar form a complex-conjugate pair."
$(x-a)^2+b^2$ "The real quadratic factor associated with the conjugate pair a plus or minus b i."
$m(z)=m(\overline z)$ "A nonreal root and its conjugate occur with equal multiplicity."

The theorem may be stated using either roots or zeros. Thus “$a+bi$ is a root of $p$” and “$a+bi$ is a zero of $p$” express the same condition:

$$ p(a+bi)=0. $$

The term pair refers specifically to the two nonreal numbers $a+bi$ and $a-bi$. When $b=0$, the root is real and equals its own conjugate.

Defense Notation Advice: State explicitly that the polynomial has real coefficients before invoking the theorem. Write the conjugate of $a+bi$ as $a-bi$, changing only the sign of the imaginary part. Preserve multiplicity when reconstructing a polynomial from known roots. Distinguish complex-conjugate roots from broader algebraic conjugates, and do not assume that a polynomial with arbitrary complex coefficients must contain both members of a conjugate pair.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Statement: $$ \boxed{ p(a+bi)=0 \Rightarrow p(a-bi)=0, \qquad p\in\mathbb R[x] }. $$
  • Alternative Names: Complex Conjugate Root Theorem, Conjugate Root Theorem, and Complex Conjugate Zeros Theorem.
  • Required Condition: The polynomial coefficients must be real.
  • Complex Conjugate: $$ \boxed{ \overline{a+bi}=a-bi }. $$
  • Geometric Meaning: Nonreal roots occur symmetrically across the real axis in the complex plane.
  • Factor Pair: $$ \boxed{ [x-(a+bi)][x-(a-bi)] = (x-a)^2+b^2 }. $$
  • Real Quadratic Factor: A conjugate pair combines into a quadratic with real coefficients.
  • Multiplicity: A root and its nonreal conjugate occur with equal multiplicity.
  • Odd Degree: Every odd-degree real polynomial has at least one real root.
  • Nonreal Root Count: A real polynomial has an even number of nonreal roots when multiplicity is counted.
  • Cubic Case: A real cubic with nonreal roots has one real root and one conjugate pair.
  • Quadratic Formula: A negative discriminant in a real quadratic produces a conjugate pair.
  • Fundamental Theorem of Algebra: The theorem structures the complex roots guaranteed by the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra.
  • Factor Theorem: Each root supplies a linear factor, and conjugate linear factors combine into a real quadratic.
  • Real Factorization: Real polynomials factor over $\mathbb R$ into linear and irreducible quadratic factors.
  • Vieta Connection: The sum and product of a conjugate pair are real.
  • Real Root Nuance: Every real root is its own complex conjugate.
  • Complex-Coefficient Warning: A polynomial with nonreal coefficients need not contain the conjugate of each root.
  • Algebraic-Conjugate Contrast: Complex conjugation is one specific transformation; algebraic conjugacy is defined relative to polynomial and field structure.
  • Common Error: Do not change the sign of the real part when forming the conjugate.
  • Common Error: Do not invoke the theorem without checking that all coefficients are real.
  • Common Error: Do not assume the theorem is restricted to quadratic polynomials.
  • Common Error: Do not treat conjugate roots as additive inverses.
  • Common Error: Do not ignore multiplicity when reconstructing a polynomial from its roots.
  • Terminological Contrast: The Conjugate Pair Theorem states that nonreal roots of a polynomial with real coefficients occur with their complex conjugates. A complex conjugate of $a+bi$ is $a-bi$. A root or zero is a value $z$ satisfying $p(z)=0$. The Factor Theorem connects such a root with the factor $(x-z)$. An algebraic conjugate is a broader field-dependent concept and should not automatically be identified with complex conjugation.
