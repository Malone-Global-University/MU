Factoring Rules

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

Factoring rules are algebraic patterns, identities, and procedures used to rewrite an expression as a product of simpler factors. Factoring reverses multiplication or expansion.

If:

$$ A(B+C)=AB+AC, $$

then factoring reverses the distributive step:

$$ \boxed{ AB+AC=A(B+C) }. $$

For polynomials, factoring seeks a representation such as:

$$ \boxed{ P(x)=A(x)B(x) }. $$

The word factor refers to a quantity participating in multiplication. Thus factoring changes the visible structure of an expression from additive or expanded form into multiplicative form without changing its mathematical value.

The term rules does not refer to one universal algorithm. It refers to a collection of reusable patterns, including common-factor extraction, grouping, special-product identities, quadratic methods, and root-based factorization.

Operational Nuance

Factoring as Reverse Distribution

The most fundamental factoring rule is the distributive law used backward:

$$ \boxed{ ab+ac=a(b+c) }. $$

Likewise:

$$ \boxed{ ab-ac=a(b-c) }. $$

Greatest Common Factor First

A standard first step is to identify the greatest common factor of all terms.

For example:

$$ 12x^3+18x^2 = \boxed{ 6x^2(2x+3) }. $$

Numerical Greatest Common Factor

Factor the greatest numerical divisor shared by all coefficients.

For:

$$ 15x+25, $$

the numerical GCF is $5$:

$$ \boxed{ 15x+25=5(3x+5) }. $$

Variable Greatest Common Factor

For variable factors, use the smallest exponent present in every term.

For example:

$$ x^5+x^3 = \boxed{ x^3(x^2+1) }. $$

Multiple Variables

Consider:

$$ 18x^4y^3+24x^2y^5. $$

The GCF is:

$$ 6x^2y^3. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ 18x^4y^3+24x^2y^5 = 6x^2y^3(3x^2+4y^2) }. $$

Factoring Out a Negative

A negative common factor may be extracted to make the leading term inside parentheses positive:

$$ -x^2+5x-6 = \boxed{ -(x^2-5x+6) }. $$

Difference of Squares

One of the most important special factoring identities is:

$$ \boxed{ a^2-b^2=(a-b)(a+b) }. $$

Example

$$ x^2-49 = \boxed{ (x-7)(x+7) }. $$

Recognizing a Difference of Squares

Three features are required:

  • two terms,
  • subtraction between them, and
  • both terms are perfect squares in the coefficient system being used.

Sum of Squares

Over the real numbers:

$$ \boxed{ a^2+b^2 } $$

does not generally factor into real linear factors.

Over the complex numbers:

$$ \boxed{ a^2+b^2 = (a+bi)(a-bi) }. $$

Perfect-Square Trinomial

$$ \boxed{ a^2+2ab+b^2=(a+b)^2 }. $$

Likewise:

$$ \boxed{ a^2-2ab+b^2=(a-b)^2 }. $$

Example

$$ x^2+10x+25 = \boxed{ (x+5)^2 }. $$

Recognizing a Perfect-Square Trinomial

The first and last terms must be squares, while the middle term must equal twice the product of their square roots.

Factoring a Monic Quadratic

For:

$$ x^2+bx+c, $$

seek numbers $m$ and $n$ satisfying:

$$ \boxed{ m+n=b \qquad\text{and}\qquad mn=c }. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ x^2+bx+c=(x+m)(x+n) }. $$

Monic Quadratic Example

Factor:

$$ x^2+7x+12. $$

Since:

$$ 3+4=7, \qquad 3\cdot4=12, $$

one obtains:

$$ \boxed{ x^2+7x+12 = (x+3)(x+4) }. $$

Signs in a Monic Quadratic

If $c>0$, the two factor constants have the same sign.

If $c<0$, the two factor constants have opposite signs.

Their sum must still equal the coefficient of $x$.

Nonmonic Quadratic

For:

$$ ax^2+bx+c, \qquad a\neq1, $$

one common method is to find numbers whose product is:

$$ \boxed{ac} $$

and whose sum is:

$$ \boxed{b}. $$

AC Method Example

Factor:

$$ 6x^2+11x+3. $$

Since:

$$ ac=18, $$

seek numbers with product $18$ and sum $11$:

$$ 9+2=11. $$

Split the middle term:

$$ 6x^2+9x+2x+3. $$

Group:

$$ 3x(2x+3)+1(2x+3). $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ 6x^2+11x+3 = (3x+1)(2x+3) }. $$

Factoring by Grouping

Expressions with four or more terms may sometimes be grouped so that a common binomial factor appears.

For example:

$$ x^3+3x^2+2x+6. $$

Group:

$$ (x^3+3x^2)+(2x+6). $$

Factor each group:

$$ x^2(x+3)+2(x+3). $$

Then factor the common binomial:

$$ \boxed{ (x+3)(x^2+2) }. $$

Grouping Requires a Common Factor

Merely placing terms in parentheses does not factor an expression. Grouping is useful only when the grouped pieces reveal a shared factor.

Difference of Cubes

$$ \boxed{ a^3-b^3 = (a-b)(a^2+ab+b^2) }. $$

Sum of Cubes

$$ \boxed{ a^3+b^3 = (a+b)(a^2-ab+b^2) }. $$

Example: Difference of Cubes

$$ x^3-27 = \boxed{ (x-3)(x^2+3x+9) }. $$

Example: Sum of Cubes

$$ x^3+8 = \boxed{ (x+2)(x^2-2x+4) }. $$

General Difference of Powers

For positive integer $n$:

$$ \boxed{ a^n-b^n = (a-b) \left( a^{n-1}+a^{n-2}b+\cdots+ab^{n-2}+b^{n-1} \right) }. $$

Immediate Consequence

For every positive integer $n$:

$$ \boxed{ a-b \mid a^n-b^n }. $$

Sum of Odd Powers

If $n$ is odd:

$$ \boxed{ a+b \mid a^n+b^n }. $$

For example:

$$ a^5+b^5 = (a+b) \left( a^4-a^3b+a^2b^2-ab^3+b^4 \right). $$

Sum of Even Powers

A sum such as:

$$ a^4+b^4 $$

does not have $a+b$ as a factor in general.

Quadratic in Form

Some higher-degree polynomials can be factored by substitution.

For example:

$$ x^4-5x^2+4. $$

Let:

$$ u=x^2. $$

Then:

$$ u^2-5u+4 = (u-1)(u-4). $$

Substitute back:

$$ (x^2-1)(x^2-4). $$

Factor further:

$$ \boxed{ (x-1)(x+1)(x-2)(x+2) }. $$

Always Check for Further Factoring

A factorization may be mathematically correct but incomplete relative to the intended coefficient system.

For example:

$$ x^4-16 = (x^2-4)(x^2+4). $$

Over $\mathbb{R}$, continue:

$$ \boxed{ x^4-16 = (x-2)(x+2)(x^2+4) }. $$

Over $\mathbb{C}$, $x^2+4$ can also be factored.

Factor Theorem

A linear factor may be identified from a root:

$$ \boxed{ P(c)=0 \iff x-c\mid P(x) }. $$

Root-Based Factoring

If a root $c$ is known, divide $P(x)$ by $x-c$ to obtain a lower-degree polynomial and continue factoring.

Rational Root Theorem

For integer-coefficient polynomials, the Rational Root Theorem provides candidate rational roots that can be tested using the Factor Theorem.

Multiplicity

If:

$$ P(x)=(x-c)^mQ(x), \qquad Q(c)\neq0, $$

then $x-c$ occurs as a factor with multiplicity $m$.

Repeated Factor Example

$$ x^3-3x^2+3x-1 = \boxed{ (x-1)^3 }. $$

Factoring and the Zero-Product Property

Factoring is especially useful for solving equations because:

$$ AB=0 \Longrightarrow A=0 \text{ or } B=0. $$

Thus:

$$ (x-2)(x+5)=0 $$

gives:

$$ \boxed{ x=2 \quad\text{or}\quad x=-5 }. $$

Zero-Product Property Requires Zero

From:

$$ (x-2)(x+5)=12, $$

one cannot conclude that either factor equals $12$ or $0$. The zero-product property applies only when the entire product equals zero.

Factoring Does Not Change an Expression's Value

A correct factorization is an identity:

$$ \boxed{ x^2-9=(x-3)(x+3) }. $$

Both expressions produce the same value for every input in their common domain.

Verification by Expansion

The safest general check is to multiply the factors:

$$ (x+2)(x+5) = x^2+7x+10. $$

If expansion reproduces the original expression, the factorization is verified.

Factoring vs. Simplifying

Factoring is a particular type of rewriting into products. Simplifying is a broader goal and need not produce factored form.

Factoring vs. Expanding

$$ \boxed{ \text{factoring: sum}\longrightarrow\text{product} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{expanding: product}\longrightarrow\text{sum} }. $$

Factoring vs. Solving

Factoring rewrites an expression. Solving seeks values of variables satisfying an equation.

Factoring is often a method used within solving, but the two operations are not synonymous.

Factoring vs. Dividing

Polynomial division may reveal a factor, but factoring seeks a multiplicative decomposition of the entire expression.

Factoring Rational Expressions

Numerators and denominators are often factored before simplifying:

$$ \frac{x^2-9}{x^2+x-6} = \frac{(x-3)(x+3)} {(x+3)(x-2)}. $$

Where defined:

$$ \frac{x^2-9}{x^2+x-6} = \frac{x-3}{x-2}. $$

Cancellation Is of Factors, Not Terms

The common factor $x+3$ may cancel because it multiplies the entire numerator and denominator.

By contrast:

$$ \frac{x+3}{x+5} $$

contains no common multiplicative factor $x$ or $3$ that can simply be canceled.

Domain Restrictions Survive Cancellation

In the example:

$$ \frac{(x-3)(x+3)} {(x+3)(x-2)}, $$

the original restrictions are:

$$ \boxed{ x\neq-3, \qquad x\neq2 }. $$

Canceling $x+3$ does not restore $x=-3$ to the original domain.

Coefficient Domain Matters

Whether an expression is "fully factored" depends on the numbers allowed in its factors.

Example over the Integers

The polynomial:

$$ x^2-2 $$

does not factor into nonconstant polynomials with integer coefficients.

Example over the Reals

$$ \boxed{ x^2-2 = (x-\sqrt2)(x+\sqrt2) }. $$

Example over the Complex Numbers

$$ \boxed{ x^2+1 = (x-i)(x+i) }. $$

Irreducible Factor

A polynomial factor is irreducible over a specified field or ring if it cannot be decomposed there into lower-degree nonconstant factors.

Complete Factorization

To factor completely means to continue until all nonconstant factors are irreducible over the intended coefficient system.

Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

Over the complex numbers, every nonconstant polynomial factors into linear factors:

$$ \boxed{ P(x) = a \prod_{k=1}^{n}(x-r_k) }. $$

Real Polynomial Factorization

Over $\mathbb{R}$, every polynomial factors into real linear factors and irreducible real quadratic factors.

Factoring Multivariable Expressions

The same principles apply with several variables:

$$ x^2-y^2 = \boxed{ (x-y)(x+y) }. $$

Common Monomial Factor in Several Variables

$$ 8x^3y^2+12x^2y^4 = \boxed{ 4x^2y^2(2x+3y^2) }. $$

Factoring by Substitution

Repeated algebraic structures can sometimes be treated as a single temporary variable.

For example:

$$ (x+1)^2-5(x+1)+6. $$

Let:

$$ u=x+1. $$

Then:

$$ u^2-5u+6 = (u-2)(u-3). $$

Substituting back:

$$ \boxed{ (x-1)(x-2) }. $$

Pattern Recognition Is Central

Many factoring rules depend on recognizing structural forms rather than merely following one fixed sequence of steps.

Recommended Factoring Order

A useful general strategy is:

  1. factor out the greatest common factor,
  2. count the remaining terms,
  3. look for special identities,
  4. try quadratic methods or grouping when appropriate,
  5. use root-based methods for higher-degree polynomials, and
  6. check whether any resulting factors can be factored further.

Two Terms

After removing a GCF, check especially for:

  • difference of squares,
  • sum of cubes,
  • difference of cubes, and
  • more general sums or differences of powers.

Three Terms

After removing a GCF, check especially for:

  • perfect-square trinomials,
  • monic quadratics, and
  • nonmonic quadratics.

Four Terms

Factoring by grouping is frequently useful, although not every four-term polynomial factors by grouping.

Higher-Degree Expressions

Look for substitution patterns, special powers, known roots, or polynomial division.

Not Every Polynomial Factors Nicely

Over a specified coefficient system, a polynomial may be irreducible.

Failure to find simple integer factors does not imply an algebraic mistake.

Common Error: Forgetting the GCF

For:

$$ 2x^2-8, $$

one should first write:

$$ 2(x^2-4), $$

then continue:

$$ \boxed{ 2(x-2)(x+2) }. $$

Common Error: Sum of Squares

The identity:

$$ a^2-b^2=(a-b)(a+b) $$

does not become:

$$ a^2+b^2=(a+b)(a-b). $$

The latter expands to $a^2-b^2$, not $a^2+b^2$.

Common Error: Incorrect Signs in Cube Formulas

The standard patterns are:

$$ a^3+b^3 = (a+b)(a^2-ab+b^2), $$
$$ a^3-b^3 = (a-b)(a^2+ab+b^2). $$

The signs in the quadratic factors are not arbitrary.

Common Error: Stopping Too Early

A factorization such as:

$$ x^4-81=(x^2-9)(x^2+9) $$

is incomplete over $\mathbb{R}$ because:

$$ x^2-9=(x-3)(x+3). $$

Common Error: Factoring Across Addition Incorrectly

A common factor must divide every term involved.

For example:

$$ 6x+9 $$

may be factored as:

$$ \boxed{ 3(2x+3) }, $$

but not as $6(x+9)$.

Common Error: Canceling Terms Rather Than Factors

Cancellation requires common multiplicative factors. It cannot be performed across addition or subtraction without first factoring.

Common Misconception: Factoring rules are not isolated tricks. They are consequences of multiplication, distributivity, polynomial identities, divisibility, and root-factor relationships. A correct factorization must multiply back to the original expression, and what counts as "fully factored" depends on the coefficient domain being used.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$ab+ac=a(b+c)$ "Factor out the common factor a."
$a^2-b^2=(a-b)(a+b)$ "A difference of squares factors into the difference times the sum."
$a^2+2ab+b^2=(a+b)^2$ "This is a perfect-square trinomial."
$a^3-b^3=(a-b)(a^2+ab+b^2)$ "Factor the difference of cubes."
$a^3+b^3=(a+b)(a^2-ab+b^2)$ "Factor the sum of cubes."
$P(c)=0\iff x-c\mid P(x)$ "C is a root if and only if x minus c is a factor."
$P(x)=A(x)B(x)$ "P of x is written in factored form."

Common instructions include:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"factor"} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{"factor completely"} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{"factor out the greatest common factor"} }. $$

The phrase factor completely requires an understood coefficient domain. For example, a polynomial may be completely factored over $\mathbb{Q}$ but factor further over $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$.

Defense Notation Advice: Keep multiplication visually clear. Use parentheses around polynomial factors, preserve extracted numerical factors, and verify by expansion. When a root $c$ is used, remember that its corresponding factor is $x-c$, not $x+c$ unless the root itself is negative.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Definition: Factoring rules rewrite expressions as products of simpler factors.
  • Reverse Distribution: $$ ab+ac=a(b+c). $$
  • GCF Rule: Factor out the greatest factor common to every term.
  • Numerical GCF: Use the greatest common divisor of the coefficients.
  • Variable GCF: Use the smallest exponent of each variable appearing in every term.
  • Negative GCF: A negative factor may be extracted to improve sign structure.
  • Difference of Squares: $$ \boxed{ a^2-b^2=(a-b)(a+b) }. $$
  • Sum of Squares: Does not generally factor into real linear factors.
  • Perfect-Square Trinomial: $$ a^2+2ab+b^2=(a+b)^2. $$
  • Negative Perfect-Square Trinomial: $$ a^2-2ab+b^2=(a-b)^2. $$
  • Monic Quadratic: Find two numbers whose product is the constant term and whose sum is the linear coefficient.
  • Monic Quadratic Form: $$ x^2+bx+c=(x+m)(x+n) $$ when $m+n=b$ and $mn=c$.
  • Nonmonic Quadratic: The AC method is one common approach.
  • AC Method: Seek numbers with product $ac$ and sum $b$.
  • Grouping: Rearrange or group terms to reveal a common polynomial factor.
  • Four-Term Polynomial: Grouping is often useful but is not guaranteed to work.
  • Difference of Cubes: $$ a^3-b^3=(a-b)(a^2+ab+b^2). $$
  • Sum of Cubes: $$ a^3+b^3=(a+b)(a^2-ab+b^2). $$
  • Difference of Powers: $a-b$ divides $a^n-b^n$ for every positive integer $n$.
  • Odd Sum of Powers: $a+b$ divides $a^n+b^n$ when $n$ is odd.
  • Quadratic in Form: Higher-degree expressions may become quadratics after substitution.
  • Substitution: Repeated expressions can be temporarily represented by one variable.
  • Factor Theorem: $$ P(c)=0\iff x-c\mid P(x). $$
  • Rational Root Theorem: Provides rational-root candidates for integer-coefficient polynomials.
  • Synthetic Division: Efficiently removes a verified linear factor.
  • Polynomial Long Division: Can divide out known polynomial factors.
  • Repeated Factor: A factor may occur with multiplicity greater than one.
  • Multiplicity: $(x-c)^m$ records repeated occurrence of the factor $x-c$.
  • Zero-Product Property: Factoring helps solve polynomial equations set equal to zero.
  • Equation Requirement: Factoring an expression alone does not solve for a variable.
  • Expansion Check: Multiply the factors to recover the original expression.
  • Identity: A correct factorization is equal to the original expression throughout its domain.
  • Factoring vs. Expanding: Factoring converts sums to products; expansion converts products to sums.
  • Factoring vs. Simplifying: Factoring is one type of simplification or structural rewriting.
  • Factoring vs. Solving: Factoring rewrites; solving finds values satisfying an equation.
  • Factoring vs. Division: Division can identify quotients, while factoring decomposes the entire expression multiplicatively.
  • Rational Expressions: Factor numerators and denominators before canceling common factors.
  • Cancellation Rule: Only common multiplicative factors may cancel.
  • Terms Do Not Cancel: Addition and subtraction must first be factored if cancellation is desired.
  • Domain Restriction: Canceled denominator factors still determine exclusions from the original domain.
  • Integer Coefficients: "Factor completely" may mean irreducible over $\mathbb{Z}$ or $\mathbb{Q}$ in elementary contexts.
  • Real Coefficients: Some rationally irreducible factors split over $\mathbb{R}$.
  • Complex Coefficients: Every nonconstant polynomial factors into linear factors over $\mathbb{C}$.
  • Irreducibility: Always depends on the specified coefficient system.
  • Fundamental Theorem of Algebra: Guarantees complete linear factorization over $\mathbb{C}$.
  • Real Polynomial Rule: Real polynomials factor into real linear and irreducible quadratic factors.
  • Multivariable Factoring: Common factors and identities extend naturally to several variables.
  • Polynomial GCF: Entire polynomial expressions may serve as common factors.
  • Pattern Recognition: Effective factoring depends heavily on recognizing structural forms.
  • Two-Term Strategy: Check for a GCF and special differences or sums of powers.
  • Three-Term Strategy: Check for a GCF, perfect-square trinomial, or quadratic pattern.
  • Four-Term Strategy: Check whether grouping reveals a common factor.
  • Higher-Degree Strategy: Look for substitutions, known identities, roots, or lower-degree factors.
  • First Step: Check for a greatest common factor before using more specialized methods.
  • Last Step: Examine every resulting factor to determine whether it factors further.
  • Difference-of-Squares Error: The rule does not apply to $a^2+b^2$ over the reals.
  • Cube-Sign Error: Sum and difference of cubes have different sign patterns in their quadratic factors.
  • GCF Error: Forgetting a common factor leaves an incomplete factorization.
  • Stopping Error: A correct intermediate factorization may still need further factoring.
  • Sign Error: Expanding the proposed factors is an effective way to detect incorrect signs.
  • Root-Factor Sign: Root $c$ corresponds to factor $x-c$.
  • Graphical Use: Linear factors reveal real polynomial zeros and $x$-intercepts.
  • Polynomial Inequalities: Factored form exposes boundary points and sign changes.
  • Calculus: Factoring can simplify derivatives, limits, critical-point equations, and rational expressions.
  • Number Theory: Polynomial factor identities mirror broader divisibility relationships such as $a-b\mid a^n-b^n$.
  • Computer Algebra: Symbolic systems use sophisticated algorithms beyond classroom pattern recognition to factor large or multivariable polynomials.
  • Verification Principle: A proposed factorization is valid only if its product equals the original expression.
  • Core Interpretation: Factoring rules are systematic ways of reversing multiplication and exposing multiplicative structure: $$ \boxed{ P(x) \longrightarrow A(x)B(x)\cdots }. $$ The most important tools include greatest-common-factor extraction, distributivity, differences of squares, perfect-square trinomials, sums and differences of cubes, quadratic methods, grouping, substitution, and the Factor Theorem. The process is complete only when no factor can be reduced further within the intended coefficient system, and expansion provides the universal check.
