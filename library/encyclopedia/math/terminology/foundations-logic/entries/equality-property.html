Equivalence Properties of Equality

Subfield: Foundations & Logic

Etymology & Roots

The equivalence properties of equality are the structural properties that make ordinary equality an equivalence relation. The three central properties are:

$$ \boxed{ \text{reflexive},\qquad \text{symmetric},\qquad \text{transitive} }. $$

Equality is reflexive because every object equals itself:

$$ \boxed{a=a}. $$

It is symmetric because:

$$ \boxed{ a=b\Longrightarrow b=a }. $$

It is transitive because:

$$ \boxed{ a=b \text{ and } b=c \Longrightarrow a=c }. $$

The word equivalence refers to a relationship under which objects are treated as equivalent according to specified criteria. Equality is the strongest and most familiar example: equal objects are not merely similar under a chosen rule; they are the same mathematical object or value in the relevant sense.

The phrase equivalence properties of equality therefore emphasizes that equality possesses precisely the structural properties used to define the broader concept of an equivalence relation.

Operational Nuance

Equality as a Relation

Equality can be regarded as a binary relation on a set $S$:

$$ \boxed{ a=b } $$

for $a,b\in S$.

As a relation, equality satisfies the defining conditions for an equivalence relation.

Reflexive Property

A relation is reflexive when every object is related to itself.

For equality:

$$ \boxed{ \forall a,\quad a=a }. $$

No object can fail to equal itself.

Reflexivity in Examples

$$ \boxed{ 5=5,\qquad x=x,\qquad A=A,\qquad \mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v} }. $$

The objects may be numbers, sets, functions, matrices, vectors, geometric objects, or other mathematical entities.

Symmetric Property

A relation is symmetric when reversing the order of related objects preserves the relation.

Equality satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ a=b \Longrightarrow b=a }. $$

Symmetry Example

If:

$$ 2+3=5, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ 5=2+3 }. $$

The two sides of an equality have no intrinsic directional priority.

Transitive Property

A relation is transitive when two linked relations can be chained.

For equality:

$$ \boxed{ a=b,\quad b=c \Longrightarrow a=c }. $$

Transitivity Example

If:

$$ x=7 \qquad\text{and}\qquad 7=y, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{x=y}. $$

Equality Is an Equivalence Relation

Since equality is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive:

$$ \boxed{ =\ \text{is an equivalence relation} }. $$

Equivalence Relation Definition

More generally, a relation $\sim$ on a set $S$ is an equivalence relation if:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} &a\sim a &&\text{for every }a\in S,\\ &a\sim b\Longrightarrow b\sim a,\\ &a\sim b,\ b\sim c\Longrightarrow a\sim c. \end{aligned} } $$

Equality vs. General Equivalence

Equality is stronger than many other equivalence relations.

For example:

$$ 1\equiv4\pmod3 $$

means $1$ and $4$ are congruent modulo $3$, but:

$$ \boxed{1\neq4}. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \text{equivalent} \not\Rightarrow \text{equal} } $$

unless the equivalence relation itself is equality.

Equality Implies Any Equality-Based Equivalence

If two objects are literally equal, then any property depending only on the object must agree for both.

This is closely connected to substitution.

Substitution or Indiscernibility of Equals

If:

$$ a=b, $$

then one may replace $a$ by $b$ in any well-defined context without changing truth or value.

$$ \boxed{ a=b \Longrightarrow F(a)=F(b) } $$

whenever $F$ is defined.

Substitutability is often treated alongside the equivalence properties because it captures the operational force of equality.

Example of Substitution

If:

$$ a=b, $$

then:

$$ a^2+1=b^2+1. $$

The expressions remain equal because equal quantities have been placed into the same expression.

Equality Chains

Transitivity makes chains of equalities possible:

$$ \boxed{ a=b=c=d }. $$

This compact notation asserts:

$$ a=b,\qquad b=c,\qquad c=d, $$

and therefore, by transitivity, every quantity in the chain is equal to every other quantity.

Proof by Chained Equalities

Mathematical proofs commonly establish:

$$ A=B, \qquad B=C, \qquad C=D, $$

then conclude:

$$ \boxed{A=D}. $$

Symmetry and Equation Reversal

Because equality is symmetric, any equation may be written with its sides reversed:

$$ \boxed{ x=5 \iff 5=x }. $$

The reversed equation asserts exactly the same equality.

Reflexivity and Verification

Algebraic simplification often ends in an identity such as:

$$ 7=7. $$

Such a statement is automatically true by reflexivity.

Reflexivity in Identity Equations

Suppose:

$$ 2(x+3)=2x+6. $$

Simplifying the left side gives:

$$ 2x+6=2x+6. $$

The result is reflexively true for every admissible $x$.

Equivalence Classes

Every equivalence relation partitions a set into equivalence classes.

For equality, the equivalence class of $a$ is:

$$ \boxed{ [a]_{=} = \{x:x=a\} = \{a\} }. $$

Thus equality produces singleton equivalence classes.

Contrast with Modular Congruence Classes

Under congruence modulo $3$:

$$ [1] = \{\ldots,-5,-2,1,4,7,10,\ldots\}. $$

Many distinct integers belong to the same equivalence class.

Under equality, by contrast:

$$ [1]_{=}=\boxed{\{1\}}. $$

Equality of Numbers

Numeric equality means the expressions represent the same number.

For example:

$$ \boxed{ \frac12=0.5=\frac{50}{100} }. $$

These are different symbolic representations of the same number.

Representation vs. Object

Equality does not require expressions to look the same.

The expressions:

$$ 2+3 \qquad\text{and}\qquad 10/2 $$

are syntactically different but numerically equal:

$$ \boxed{ 2+3=\frac{10}{2} }. $$

Equality of Ordered Pairs

Equality is componentwise:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b)=(c,d) \iff a=c \text{ and } b=d }. $$

Equality of Tuples

More generally:

$$ \boxed{ (a_1,\ldots,a_n) = (b_1,\ldots,b_n) \iff a_i=b_i \text{ for all }i }. $$

Equality of Sets

Set equality is extensional:

$$ \boxed{ A=B \iff \forall x\, (x\in A\iff x\in B) }. $$

The order in which elements are written does not matter.

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \{1,2,3\}=\{3,1,2\} }. $$

Equality of Functions

Two functions are equal when they have compatible function data and agree at every input in their stated domain.

$$ \boxed{ f=g \Longrightarrow f(x)=g(x) \text{ for every }x }. $$

Equality of Matrices

Matrices are equal exactly when they have the same dimensions and equal corresponding entries:

$$ \boxed{ A=B \iff a_{ij}=b_{ij} \text{ for every }i,j }. $$

Equality of Vectors

Coordinate vectors are equal componentwise:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{u}=\mathbf{v} \iff u_i=v_i \text{ for every }i }. $$

Equality of Complex Numbers

For real $a,b,c,d$:

$$ \boxed{ a+bi=c+di \iff a=c \text{ and } b=d }. $$

Equality of Polynomials

Two polynomials over a fixed coefficient system are equal when corresponding coefficients agree after like powers have been collected.

For example:

$$ ax^2+bx+c = px^2+qx+r $$

as polynomials exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ a=p,\qquad b=q,\qquad c=r }. $$

Equality vs. Approximate Equality

Exact equality:

$$ a=b $$

must be distinguished from approximate equality:

$$ \boxed{ a\approx b }. $$

For example:

$$ \pi\approx3.14159 $$

but:

$$ \pi\neq3.14159. $$

Equality vs. Congruence

Geometric congruence is generally an equivalence relation but need not mean literal equality of geometric objects as sets of points.

Two triangles may be congruent while occupying different positions in the plane.

Equality vs. Similarity

Similarity is also an equivalence relation in many geometric settings, but similar figures need not be equal or congruent.

Equality vs. Isomorphism

In abstract mathematics, two structures may be isomorphic without being literally equal.

$$ \boxed{ A\cong B } $$

may indicate that $A$ and $B$ have the same structural form while remaining distinct objects.

Equality and Definitions

A definition may use a specialized symbol such as:

$$ \boxed{:=} $$

to indicate that one expression is being defined to mean another.

Once defined, ordinary equality can then be used according to the usual equivalence properties.

Equality and Logical Biconditionals

Equality between mathematical objects is distinct from logical equivalence between propositions:

$$ P\Longleftrightarrow Q. $$

Both ideas involve equivalence-like structure, but they operate on different kinds of objects.

Logical Equivalence as an Equivalence Relation

Logical equivalence of propositions is itself reflexive, symmetric, and transitive.

This illustrates how the same three structural properties recur throughout mathematics.

Equality and Quotient Construction

General equivalence relations allow mathematicians to treat all members of an equivalence class as one new abstract object.

Equality represents the limiting case in which each equivalence class already contains only one object.

Failure of Reflexivity

A relation that is not reflexive cannot be an equivalence relation.

For example, the strict relation:

$$ a<b $$

is not reflexive because:

$$ a<a $$

is always false.

Failure of Symmetry

The relation $\leq$ is not symmetric:

$$ 2\leq3 $$

does not imply:

$$ 3\leq2. $$

Failure of Transitivity

A relation may be reflexive and symmetric yet fail transitivity, in which case it still is not an equivalence relation.

The three properties must all hold.

Common Misconception: The phrase equivalence properties of equality does not mean that equality and every equivalence relation are the same thing. Equality is one particular equivalence relation. Other equivalence relations may group distinct objects together according to a chosen criterion.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$a=a$ "A equals itself; equality is reflexive."
$a=b\Rightarrow b=a$ "If a equals b, then b equals a; equality is symmetric."
$a=b,\ b=c\Rightarrow a=c$ "If a equals b and b equals c, then a equals c; equality is transitive."
$a=b=c$ "A, b, and c are all equal."
$a\sim b$ "A is equivalent to b under the specified relation."
$a\equiv b\pmod n$ "A is congruent to b modulo n."
$a\approx b$ "A is approximately equal to b."

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"equality is an equivalence relation"} } $$

means specifically that equality is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive.

It does not mean that the symbols:

$$ =,\qquad \sim,\qquad \equiv,\qquad \cong $$

are interchangeable. Each notation may express a different relation.

Defense Notation Advice: Use the ordinary equality sign:

$$ \boxed{=} $$

only when literal mathematical equality is intended. If the relationship is congruence, similarity, isomorphism, approximation, or another equivalence, use notation appropriate to that relation rather than weakening the meaning of equality.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Equivalence Properties: Equality is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive.
  • Reflexive Property: $$ \boxed{a=a}. $$
  • Symmetric Property: $$ \boxed{ a=b\Rightarrow b=a }. $$
  • Transitive Property: $$ \boxed{ a=b,\ b=c\Rightarrow a=c }. $$
  • Equivalence Relation: A relation possessing all three properties is an equivalence relation.
  • Equality Classification: $$ \boxed{ =\ \text{is an equivalence relation} }. $$
  • Substitution: Equal objects may replace one another in well-defined contexts.
  • Substitution Form: $$ \boxed{ a=b\Rightarrow F(a)=F(b) }. $$
  • Equality Chain: $$ a=b=c $$ uses transitivity to assert that all three quantities are equal.
  • Equation Reversal: Symmetry permits: $$ a=b\iff b=a. $$
  • Identity Verification: A terminal statement such as $5=5$ is true by reflexivity.
  • Proof Chaining: Transitivity supports multi-step chains of equal expressions.
  • Representation Independence: Equal objects may have different symbolic representations.
  • Numeric Example: $$ \boxed{ 0.5=\frac12 }. $$
  • Arithmetic Example: $$ \boxed{ 2+3=10/2 }. $$
  • Set Equality: $$ \boxed{ A=B \iff \forall x\,(x\in A\iff x\in B) }. $$
  • Set Ordering: $\{1,2,3\}=\{3,2,1\}$ because sets do not depend on listing order.
  • Ordered Pairs: $$ \boxed{ (a,b)=(c,d) \iff a=c,\ b=d }. $$
  • Tuples: Tuple equality is componentwise.
  • Vectors: Coordinate vectors are equal exactly when corresponding components are equal.
  • Matrices: Equal matrices have equal dimensions and equal corresponding entries.
  • Functions: Equal functions agree at each input in the relevant domain and share the required function structure.
  • Complex Numbers: $$ a+bi=c+di $$ implies equality of corresponding real and imaginary parts.
  • Polynomials: Polynomial equality is coefficientwise after like terms are collected.
  • Equality Classes: Under ordinary equality: $$ \boxed{ [a]_{=}=\{a\} }. $$
  • Singleton Classes: Equality partitions a set into singleton equivalence classes.
  • General Equivalence Classes: Other equivalence relations may contain many distinct objects in one class.
  • Modular Congruence: $$ a\equiv b\pmod n $$ is an equivalence relation on integers.
  • Modular Contrast: Congruent integers need not be equal.
  • Geometric Congruence: Congruence commonly forms an equivalence relation among geometric figures.
  • Congruence Contrast: Two congruent figures can occupy different locations and therefore need not be the same set of points.
  • Geometric Similarity: Similarity is an equivalence relation under standard geometric definitions.
  • Similarity Contrast: Similar figures may differ in size and therefore need not be congruent or equal.
  • Isomorphism: Mathematical structures may be structurally equivalent without being literally equal.
  • Approximation: $$ a\approx b $$ does not assert exact equality.
  • Approximation Example: $$ \pi\approx3.14 $$ but: $$ \pi\neq3.14. $$
  • Logical Equivalence: $$ P\Longleftrightarrow Q $$ expresses equivalence of propositions rather than equality of ordinary mathematical objects.
  • Logical Reflexivity: Every proposition is logically equivalent to itself.
  • Logical Symmetry: If $P$ is logically equivalent to $Q$, then $Q$ is logically equivalent to $P$.
  • Logical Transitivity: Logical equivalence can be chained.
  • Reflexivity Failure Example: Strict inequality $<$ is not reflexive.
  • Symmetry Failure Example: $\leq$ is not symmetric.
  • All Three Required: A relation is not an equivalence relation unless reflexivity, symmetry, and transitivity all hold.
  • Equality vs. Equivalence: Equality is a particular equivalence relation, not a synonym for every equivalence relation.
  • Equality vs. Identity: An identity is a statement of equality true throughout a specified domain; equality itself is the relation expressed by $=$.
  • Equality vs. Equation: Equality is a relation; an equation is a statement asserting that the relation holds between two expressions.
  • Equality vs. Definition: Definition notation may establish a convention, whereas ordinary equality states sameness of mathematical value or object.
  • Substitution in Algebra: Established equalities justify replacement of expressions during simplification.
  • Substitution in Proof: Equality lets one replace one representation by another without changing the mathematical object under discussion.
  • Equivalence Relations in Classification: Reflexive, symmetric, and transitive relations naturally group objects into equivalence classes.
  • Partition Connection: Every equivalence relation on a set determines a partition of that set, and every partition determines an equivalence relation.
  • Equality Partition: The partition associated with equality consists entirely of singleton subsets.
  • Quotient Sets: General equivalence classes can be treated as elements of a quotient set.
  • Equality Quotient: Quotienting by literal equality produces no further identification beyond the objects already present.
  • Foundational Role: Equality provides the model example from which the abstraction of an equivalence relation is naturally understood.
  • Terminological Precision: Equivalence properties of equality most directly refers to reflexivity, symmetry, and transitivity, although substitutability is often discussed alongside them because it expresses the operational force of equality.
  • Core Interpretation: Equality behaves as an equivalence relation because: $$ \boxed{ a=a,\qquad a=b\Rightarrow b=a,\qquad a=b,\ b=c\Rightarrow a=c }. $$ These reflexive, symmetric, and transitive properties allow equality to be reversed, chained, and used consistently throughout mathematical reasoning, while substitution permits equal objects to replace one another in valid contexts.
