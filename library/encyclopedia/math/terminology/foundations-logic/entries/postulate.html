Postulate

Subfield: Foundations & Mathematical Logic

Etymology & Roots

A postulate is a statement accepted as a starting assumption within a mathematical system rather than proved from earlier statements in that system. Postulates establish part of the framework from which definitions, propositions, lemmas, theorems, and other consequences may be developed deductively.

The term is especially associated with classical geometry. Euclid's Elements begins its geometric development with definitions, postulates, and common notions before proving propositions from them.

The words postulate and axiom have historically carried somewhat different shades of meaning. In a traditional distinction, a postulate is an assumption adopted for a particular mathematical subject, especially geometry, while an axiom is regarded as a more general logical or mathematical principle.

Modern mathematics does not consistently preserve that distinction. In many contemporary texts, axiom and postulate are effectively interchangeable terms for an assumed foundational statement. Consequently, the intended distinction should be inferred from the author's terminology rather than treated as a universal rule.

Operational Nuance

A postulate functions as a permitted starting point in a deductive system. If a system has postulates:

$$ P_1,P_2,\ldots,P_n, $$

then mathematical consequences may be derived from those assumptions by valid rules of inference.

Schematically:

$$ \boxed{ \text{Postulates} \longrightarrow \text{Deductive Reasoning} \longrightarrow \text{Theorems} }. $$

A theorem is therefore fundamentally different from a postulate. A theorem requires proof within the system; a postulate does not.

Example: Euclidean Geometry

One of Euclid's geometric postulates permits the construction of a straight line joining two given points. In modernized language:

$$ \boxed{ \text{A straight line may be drawn from any point to any point.} } $$

The statement is not first established by proving it from earlier geometric theorems. Instead, it is part of the assumed framework in which those theorems are subsequently proved.

The Parallel Postulate

Euclid's fifth postulate concerns the behavior of lines and became known as the parallel postulate. A familiar equivalent formulation in Euclidean geometry is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{Through a point not on a given line, exactly one parallel to the line exists.} } $$

This statement is particularly important because altering the parallel assumption produces different geometric systems.

In Euclidean geometry:

$$ \text{exactly one parallel}. $$

In hyperbolic geometry, the corresponding structure permits:

$$ \text{more than one nonintersecting line}. $$

In elliptic geometry:

$$ \text{no parallel lines}. $$

The development of non-Euclidean geometry demonstrated that a postulate does not need to be a self-evident description of physical reality. It can instead function as an assumption that specifies which mathematical system is being studied.

Postulates as Conditional Foundations

Modern mathematics often treats an axiomatic system conditionally:

$$ \boxed{ \text{If these assumptions hold, then these consequences follow.} } $$

This viewpoint shifts attention away from asking whether a postulate is universally "true" and toward examining the structures that satisfy it.

Common Misconception: A postulate is not a theorem whose proof has merely been omitted. Its role is different: it is deliberately accepted as part of the starting framework.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Written Expression Professional Articulation
Postulate "An assumed foundational statement."
Parallel Postulate "The postulate governing parallel lines in the geometric system."
$P_1,P_2,\ldots,P_n$ "The postulates P one through P n."
$P_1,\ldots,P_n\vdash T$ "T is derivable from postulates P one through P n."
$\Gamma\vdash\varphi$ "Phi is provable from the assumptions in Gamma."
$\Gamma\models\varphi$ "Gamma semantically entails phi."

In ordinary mathematical prose, common constructions include:

  • "Assume the following postulates."
  • "By the parallel postulate..."
  • "The theorem follows from the postulates."
  • "Replace the Euclidean postulate with an alternative assumption."

The verb postulate may also be used:

$$ \text{"We postulate that }P\text{ holds."} $$

In that construction, postulate means to adopt a proposition as an assumption for the purposes of the theory or argument.

Defense Notation Advice: Mathematical writing should avoid calling a postulate "proved." One may motivate, justify, interpret, compare, or adopt a postulate, but once it has been proved from earlier assumptions it is functioning as a derived result rather than as a primitive postulate of that particular system.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Postulate vs. Theorem: A postulate is assumed: $$ \boxed{ \text{postulate} \longrightarrow \text{no proof required within the system} }. $$ A theorem is derived: $$ \boxed{ \text{theorem} \longrightarrow \text{proof required} }. $$
  • Postulate vs. Axiom: Historically, postulate was often used for assumptions specific to a mathematical domain, while axiom could refer to more general principles. In modern practice, the two terms frequently overlap.
  • Postulate vs. Definition: A definition assigns meaning to terminology or notation. A postulate asserts or assumes a relationship within the mathematical structure. Defining a triangle does not establish a theorem about triangles.
  • Postulate vs. Conjecture: A conjecture is proposed as a statement expected to be true and normally awaits proof or disproof. A postulate is intentionally assumed rather than awaiting proof within the system.
  • Postulate vs. Hypothesis: A hypothesis is usually a temporary assumption attached to a particular theorem, problem, experiment, or argument. A postulate generally occupies a more foundational role in defining an entire mathematical framework.
  • Postulate vs. Premise: A premise is any statement used as a starting point in an argument. Every postulate may function as a premise, but not every premise is a foundational postulate.
  • Euclid's Postulates: Euclid's Book I is traditionally presented with five geometric postulates, including assumptions concerning straight-line construction, extension of line segments, construction of circles, equality of right angles, and the behavior associated with parallel lines.
  • Common Notions: Euclid distinguished his geometric postulates from common notions such as the principle that quantities equal to the same quantity are equal to one another. This historical distinction contributed to the later vocabulary of postulates and axioms.
  • Parallel Postulate: The fifth Euclidean postulate is historically the most famous because sustained attempts to derive it from other assumptions eventually contributed to the development of non-Euclidean geometry.
  • Equivalent Postulates: Two statements may look very different yet be equivalent relative to the remaining assumptions of a system. The Euclidean parallel postulate has numerous equivalent formulations.
  • Independence: A postulate is independent of the others when it cannot be derived from them. Showing independence generally requires more than simply failing to find a proof.
  • Models and Independence: One major method of establishing independence is to construct a model satisfying the other axioms while the disputed statement fails. This demonstrates that the statement is not forced by those assumptions.
  • Consistency: A system of postulates is consistent when it does not permit derivation of a contradiction. Consistency is a fundamental question in mathematical logic and foundations.
  • Relative Consistency: Sometimes one proves that if one mathematical system is consistent, then another system built from different postulates is also consistent. Such a result is called a relative consistency result.
  • Redundancy: If one supposed postulate can actually be proved from the others, it is redundant as an independent foundational assumption.
  • Axiomatic Systems: Modern theories are frequently presented as collections of primitive concepts and axioms or postulates from which theorems are deduced.
  • Group Theory: A group is characterized by assumptions concerning closure through an operation, associativity, an identity element, and inverses. These are normally called group axioms, illustrating the modern preference for axiom outside classical geometry.
  • Probability: Modern probability theory begins from axioms governing probability measures. These foundational assumptions play structurally the same kind of role that postulates play in an axiomatic geometry.
  • Geometry: Different choices of geometric postulates can produce Euclidean, hyperbolic, elliptic, projective, affine, and other geometric theories.
  • Truth Within a Model: Once a mathematical structure satisfies a collection of axioms, theorems derived from those axioms hold within that structure. This permits the same deductive language to describe many different mathematical models.
  • Physical Interpretation: Whether a mathematical postulate accurately models physical space is a separate empirical question. Logical consequences within a mathematical theory should not be confused with claims that the theory must describe the physical universe.
  • Foundational Role: Postulates stop an infinite regress of proof. If every statement required proof from earlier statements, no proof could ever begin. Deductive systems therefore require some starting assumptions or equivalent foundational framework.
  • Terminological Contrast: A postulate is assumed as part of a framework. An axiom often serves the same modern role. A definition establishes meaning, a conjecture awaits proof or refutation, and a theorem is a proposition established by proof from accepted assumptions and earlier results.
