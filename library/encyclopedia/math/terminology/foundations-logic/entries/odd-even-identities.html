Odd/Even Identities

Subfield: Foundations & Logic

Etymology & Roots

Odd/even identities are algebraic statements that encode the parity behavior of integers or, in a separate functional context, the symmetry behavior of even and odd functions.

For integers, parity begins with the standard forms:

$$ \boxed{ \text{even integer} = 2k } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{odd integer} = 2k+1 } $$

where:

$$ k\in\mathbb{Z}. $$

These forms generate a family of reliable parity identities for addition, subtraction, multiplication, powers, and modular arithmetic.

The word even reflects the idea of being divisible into equal pairs, while odd historically reflects something left unmatched after pairing.

The word identity means a statement that holds for every permitted value of its variables.

Thus an odd/even identity is not merely a numerical example such as:

$$ 3+5=8, $$

but a general structural claim such as:

$$ \boxed{ \text{odd}+\text{odd} = \text{even} }. $$

A second common use of the phrase occurs with even and odd functions, where the identities:

$$ \boxed{ f(-x)=f(x) } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ f(-x)=-f(x) } $$

characterize even and odd symmetry respectively. The two uses are related through the language of parity but should not be confused.

Operational Nuance

Fundamental Even Form

Every even integer can be written:

$$ \boxed{ n=2k, \qquad k\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Fundamental Odd Form

Every odd integer can be written:

$$ \boxed{ n=2k+1, \qquad k\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Modulo $2$ Forms

$$ \boxed{ n\text{ even} \iff n\equiv0\pmod2 } $$
$$ \boxed{ n\text{ odd} \iff n\equiv1\pmod2 }. $$

Parity Addition Identities

$$ \boxed{ \text{even}+\text{even} = \text{even} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{even}+\text{odd} = \text{odd} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{odd}+\text{even} = \text{odd} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{odd}+\text{odd} = \text{even} }. $$

Even Plus Even Proof

Let:

$$ a=2m, \qquad b=2n. $$

Then:

$$ a+b = 2m+2n = 2(m+n). $$

Since $m+n\in\mathbb{Z}$:

$$ \boxed{ a+b\text{ is even} }. $$

Even Plus Odd Proof

Let:

$$ a=2m, \qquad b=2n+1. $$

Then:

$$ a+b = 2m+2n+1 = 2(m+n)+1. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ a+b\text{ is odd} }. $$

Odd Plus Odd Proof

Let:

$$ a=2m+1, \qquad b=2n+1. $$

Then:

$$ a+b = 2m+2n+2 = 2(m+n+1). $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ \text{odd}+\text{odd} = \text{even} }. $$

Parity Subtraction Identities

$$ \boxed{ \text{even}-\text{even} = \text{even} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{even}-\text{odd} = \text{odd} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{odd}-\text{even} = \text{odd} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{odd}-\text{odd} = \text{even} }. $$

Why Addition and Subtraction Have the Same Parity Table

Modulo $2$:

$$ -1\equiv1\pmod2. $$

Therefore subtraction and addition behave identically with respect to parity.

Parity Multiplication Identities

$$ \boxed{ \text{even}\cdot\text{even} = \text{even} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{even}\cdot\text{odd} = \text{even} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{odd}\cdot\text{even} = \text{even} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{odd}\cdot\text{odd} = \text{odd} }. $$

Even Factor Rule

If an integer product contains at least one even factor, then the product is even.

$$ \boxed{ a\text{ even} \Longrightarrow ab\text{ even} } $$

for every integer $b$.

Odd Product Criterion

A product of integers is odd exactly when every factor is odd.

$$ \boxed{ a_1a_2\cdots a_n\text{ odd} \iff a_1,\ldots,a_n\text{ are all odd} }. $$

Power Identities

Positive integer powers preserve parity.

$$ \boxed{ n\text{ even} \Longrightarrow n^k\text{ even} } $$
$$ \boxed{ n\text{ odd} \Longrightarrow n^k\text{ odd} } $$

for positive integer $k$.

Square Parity Identity

$$ \boxed{ n^2\text{ even} \iff n\text{ even} } $$
$$ \boxed{ n^2\text{ odd} \iff n\text{ odd} }. $$

Odd Square Modulo $8$

If $n$ is odd:

$$ \boxed{ n^2\equiv1\pmod8 }. $$

Proof

Let:

$$ n=2k+1. $$

Then:

$$ n^2 = 4k^2+4k+1 = 4k(k+1)+1. $$

Since one of $k$ and $k+1$ is even, $k(k+1)$ is even. Therefore:

$$ 4k(k+1) $$

is divisible by $8$, giving:

$$ n^2\equiv1\pmod8. $$

Even Square Modulo $4$

If $n$ is even:

$$ \boxed{ n^2\equiv0\pmod4 }. $$

Square Modulo $4$ Identity

Every integer square satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ n^2\equiv0 \text{ or } 1\pmod4 }. $$

Consecutive Integers

Two consecutive integers always have opposite parity.

$$ \boxed{ n \text{ and } n+1 \text{ have opposite parity} }. $$

Consecutive Product Identity

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ n(n+1)\text{ is always even} }. $$

Three Consecutive Integers

Among:

$$ n,\quad n+1,\quad n+2, $$

at least one is even.

Consecutive Odd Integers

$$ \boxed{ 2k+1,\quad 2k+3,\quad 2k+5,\ldots }. $$

Consecutive Even Integers

$$ \boxed{ 2k,\quad 2k+2,\quad 2k+4,\ldots }. $$

Sum of Consecutive Odd Numbers

The first $n$ positive odd integers satisfy:

$$ \boxed{ 1+3+5+\cdots+(2n-1) = n^2 }. $$

Difference of Consecutive Squares

$$ (n+1)^2-n^2 = \boxed{ 2n+1 }. $$

Thus consecutive squares differ by consecutive odd numbers.

Finite-Sum Parity Rule

A finite sum of integers is odd exactly when an odd number of the summands are odd.

$$ \boxed{ \text{sum is odd} \iff \text{number of odd summands is odd} }. $$

Finite-Sum Even Rule

A finite sum is even exactly when it contains an even number of odd summands.

Modulo $2$ Arithmetic

Odd/even identities can be compressed into arithmetic in:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Associate:

$$ \boxed{ \text{even}\leftrightarrow0, \qquad \text{odd}\leftrightarrow1 }. $$

Addition Table Modulo $2$

$$ \begin{aligned} 0+0 &\equiv0\pmod2,\\ 0+1 &\equiv1\pmod2,\\ 1+0 &\equiv1\pmod2,\\ 1+1 &\equiv0\pmod2. \end{aligned} $$

Multiplication Table Modulo $2$

$$ \begin{aligned} 0\cdot0 &\equiv0\pmod2,\\ 0\cdot1 &\equiv0\pmod2,\\ 1\cdot0 &\equiv0\pmod2,\\ 1\cdot1 &\equiv1\pmod2. \end{aligned} $$

Parity as Boolean-Like Arithmetic

Modulo $2$, addition behaves like exclusive-or on parity classes:

$$ \boxed{ 1+1\equiv0\pmod2 }. $$

Negation Does Not Change Parity

If $n$ is even, $-n$ is even. If $n$ is odd, $-n$ is odd.

$$ \boxed{ n\text{ and }-n \text{ have the same parity} }. $$

Absolute Value Does Not Change Parity

$$ \boxed{ n\text{ and }|n| \text{ have the same parity} }. $$

Adding $2$ Preserves Parity

$$ \boxed{ n \text{ and } n+2k \text{ have the same parity} } $$

for every integer $k$.

Adding $1$ Reverses Parity

$$ \boxed{ n \text{ and } n+1 \text{ have opposite parity} }. $$

Difference Has Same Parity Criterion

Two integers have the same parity exactly when their difference is even.

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv b\pmod2 \iff a-b\text{ is even} }. $$

Opposite Parity Criterion

Two integers have opposite parity exactly when their sum or difference is odd.

$$ \boxed{ a,b\text{ opposite parity} \iff a+b\text{ is odd} }. $$

Same Parity and Sum

If $a$ and $b$ have the same parity, then:

$$ \boxed{ a+b\text{ is even} }. $$

Opposite Parity and Product

If one integer is odd and the other even, their product is even.

Polynomial Parity

For integer inputs, polynomial expressions can often be classified by reducing coefficients and powers modulo $2$.

Example: $n^2+n$

$$ n^2+n = n(n+1). $$

Since one of two consecutive integers is even:

$$ \boxed{ n^2+n\text{ is always even} }. $$

Example: $n^2-n$

$$ n^2-n = n(n-1). $$

Again:

$$ \boxed{ n^2-n\text{ is always even} }. $$

Example: $n^2+n+1$

Since $n^2+n$ is even:

$$ \boxed{ n^2+n+1\text{ is always odd} }. $$

Parity of $n^k$

For positive integer $k$:

$$ \boxed{ n^k\equiv n\pmod2 }. $$

Polynomial Reduction Modulo $2$

Since:

$$ n^k\equiv n\pmod2 $$

for every positive integer $k$, parity questions for polynomial expressions can often simplify dramatically.

Example

Consider:

$$ n^5+3n^2+7n. $$

Modulo $2$:

$$ n^5+3n^2+7n \equiv n+n+n \equiv n \pmod2. $$

Therefore the expression has the same parity as $n$.

Even Coefficients Vanish Modulo $2$

Any term with an even integer coefficient contributes:

$$ \boxed{ 0\pmod2 }. $$

Odd Coefficients Reduce to $1$ Modulo $2$

$$ \boxed{ 2k+1 \equiv1\pmod2 }. $$

Parity Proof Strategy

A direct parity proof typically follows one of two methods:

  • substitute $2k$ or $2k+1$;
  • work modulo $2$.

Direct Even Proof

To prove an expression is even, rewrite it as:

$$ \boxed{ 2K } $$

for some integer $K$.

Direct Odd Proof

To prove an expression is odd, rewrite it as:

$$ \boxed{ 2K+1 } $$

for some integer $K$.

Contrapositive Use

Parity identities often make contrapositives easy.

For example:

$$ n\text{ even} \Longrightarrow n^2\text{ even} $$

gives the contrapositive:

$$ \boxed{ n^2\text{ odd} \Longrightarrow n\text{ odd} }. $$

Parity Contradictions

If a proof forces the same integer to be both odd and even, a contradiction has been reached because parity classes are disjoint.

Divisibility by $2$

Many parity identities are simply divisibility statements written in compressed form.

Prime Factor Interpretation

An integer is even exactly when its prime factorization contains at least one factor $2$.

A nonzero integer is odd exactly when its prime factorization contains no factor $2$.

Product Interpretation

This immediately explains why one even factor makes an entire integer product even.

Odd Divisors

Every divisor of an odd integer is odd.

Divisors of Even Integers

An even integer may have both even and odd divisors.

Only Even Prime

$$ \boxed{ 2\text{ is the only even prime} }. $$

All Other Primes

$$ \boxed{ p>2\text{ prime} \Longrightarrow p\text{ odd} }. $$

Function-Parity Identities

In function theory, the adjective even is defined by:

$$ \boxed{ f(-x)=f(x) }. $$

The adjective odd is defined by:

$$ \boxed{ f(-x)=-f(x) }. $$

Even-Function Symmetry

An even function has symmetry about the $y$-axis.

Odd-Function Symmetry

An odd function has rotational symmetry by $180^\circ$ about the origin.

Basic Even Function Examples

$$ \boxed{ x^2,\quad x^4,\quad \cos x } $$

Basic Odd Function Examples

$$ \boxed{ x,\quad x^3,\quad \sin x }. $$

Function Sum Identities

If $f$ and $g$ are even, then:

$$ \boxed{ f+g\text{ is even} }. $$

If $f$ and $g$ are odd, then:

$$ \boxed{ f+g\text{ is odd} }. $$

Even Plus Odd Function

The sum of a nonzero even function and a nonzero odd function is generally neither even nor odd.

Function Product Identities

$$ \boxed{ \text{even}\cdot\text{even} = \text{even} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{odd}\cdot\text{odd} = \text{even} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{even}\cdot\text{odd} = \text{odd} }. $$

Important Context Difference

For integer parity:

$$ \text{odd}\cdot\text{odd} = \text{odd}. $$

For function parity:

$$ \text{odd function}\cdot\text{odd function} = \text{even function}. $$

These statements are both correct because they refer to different meanings of odd.

Function Quotient Identities

Where defined:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\text{even}}{\text{even}} = \text{even} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \frac{\text{odd}}{\text{odd}} = \text{even} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \frac{\text{odd}}{\text{even}} = \text{odd} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \frac{\text{even}}{\text{odd}} = \text{odd} }. $$

These require domains symmetric about zero and denominators nonzero where the quotient is evaluated.

Composition Identities

If $f$ is even, then:

$$ \boxed{ f\circ g \text{ is even whenever }g\text{ is odd} } $$

under suitable symmetric-domain conditions.

Odd after Odd

If both $f$ and $g$ are odd:

$$ \boxed{ f\circ g \text{ is odd} }. $$

Even after Even

If $f$ is even, then $f\circ g$ can be even for many standard symmetric cases, but composition parity should be checked from the defining identity rather than memorized without domain conditions.

Derivative of an Even Function

If $f$ is differentiable and even:

$$ \boxed{ f'\text{ is odd} }. $$

Derivative of an Odd Function

If $f$ is differentiable and odd:

$$ \boxed{ f'\text{ is even} }. $$

Integral Symmetry for Odd Functions

If $f$ is integrable and odd on $[-a,a]$:

$$ \boxed{ \int_{-a}^{a}f(x)\,dx=0 }. $$

Integral Symmetry for Even Functions

If $f$ is integrable and even:

$$ \boxed{ \int_{-a}^{a}f(x)\,dx = 2\int_{0}^{a}f(x)\,dx }. $$

Even/Odd Function Decomposition

Any function defined on a domain symmetric about zero can be decomposed, where the expressions are meaningful, into even and odd parts:

$$ \boxed{ f_{\text{even}}(x) = \frac{f(x)+f(-x)}{2} } $$
$$ \boxed{ f_{\text{odd}}(x) = \frac{f(x)-f(-x)}{2} }. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ f(x) = f_{\text{even}}(x) + f_{\text{odd}}(x) }. $$

Parity and Trigonometric Identities

$$ \boxed{ \cos(-x)=\cos x } $$
$$ \boxed{ \sin(-x)=-\sin x } $$
$$ \boxed{ \tan(-x)=-\tan x }. $$

Thus cosine is even, while sine and tangent are odd.

Even Powers of $x$

$$ \boxed{ (-x)^{2n}=x^{2n} }. $$

Hence even powers generate even monomial functions.

Odd Powers of $x$

$$ \boxed{ (-x)^{2n+1} = -x^{2n+1} }. $$

Hence odd powers generate odd monomial functions.

Polynomial Function Parity

A polynomial containing only even powers is an even function.

A polynomial containing only odd powers and no constant term is an odd function.

Example: Even Polynomial

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=3x^6-2x^4+7x^2+1 } $$

is even.

Example: Odd Polynomial

$$ \boxed{ g(x)=5x^7-3x^3+x } $$

is odd.

Mixed Powers

A polynomial containing both nonzero even-power and odd-power terms is generally neither even nor odd.

Zero Function

The zero function:

$$ f(x)=0 $$

satisfies both:

$$ f(-x)=f(x) $$

and:

$$ f(-x)=-f(x). $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \text{the zero function is both even and odd} }. $$

Nonzero Function Cannot Be Both

If a function is both even and odd, then:

$$ f(x)=-f(x), $$

which implies:

$$ 2f(x)=0. $$

Over ordinary real or complex scalars:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=0 }. $$

Domain Symmetry Requirement

To classify a function as even or odd in the standard sense, its domain must be symmetric about zero:

$$ \boxed{ x\in D \Longrightarrow -x\in D }. $$

Common Error: Confusing Integer-Parity Rules with Function-Parity Rules

The identity:

$$ \text{odd integer}\cdot\text{odd integer} = \text{odd integer} $$

differs from:

$$ \text{odd function}\cdot\text{odd function} = \text{even function}. $$

Common Error: Treating Examples as Proof

Checking several odd or even numbers suggests a pattern but does not prove an identity for all integers.

Common Error: Forgetting Integer Conditions

Forms such as $2k$ and $2k+1$ establish parity only when:

$$ \boxed{ k\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Common Error: Assuming Odd Plus Odd Is Odd

The correct identity is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{odd}+\text{odd} = \text{even} }. $$

Common Error: Assuming Odd Times Odd Is Even

For integer parity:

$$ \boxed{ \text{odd}\cdot\text{odd} = \text{odd} }. $$

Common Error: Assuming Even Plus Odd Is Even

$$ \boxed{ \text{even}+\text{odd} = \text{odd} }. $$

Common Error: Assuming an Even Product Requires Every Factor to Be Even

One even factor is sufficient to make an integer product even.

Common Error: Assuming an Odd Product Can Contain an Even Factor

An odd integer product must consist entirely of odd factors.

Common Error: Calling Nonintegers Odd or Even

Integer parity ordinarily applies only to integers. Rational or irrational numbers such as:

$$ \frac{1}{2}, \qquad \sqrt{2} $$

are not classified as odd or even in the elementary integer sense.

Common Error: Thinking Zero Is Neither Odd nor Even

Since:

$$ 0=2(0), $$

zero is even.

Common Error: Assuming Negative Integers Have Different Parity Rules

The same identities apply to positive, negative, and zero integers.

Common Error: Forgetting Modulo $2$ Simplification

Many lengthy parity calculations reduce immediately to arithmetic with only:

$$ \boxed{ 0 \quad\text{and}\quad 1 }. $$

Common Error: Calling a Function Odd Because It Has Odd Outputs

An odd function is defined by:

$$ f(-x)=-f(x), $$

not by whether its outputs are odd integers.

Common Error: Calling a Function Even Because It Has Even Exponents Somewhere

The entire function must satisfy $f(-x)=f(x)$. A mixed polynomial can contain even powers without being an even function.

Common Error: Ignoring Function Domain Symmetry

Standard odd/even function classification requires both $x$ and $-x$ to lie in the domain whenever either does.

Common Error: Assuming the Zero Function Is Only Even

The zero function satisfies both even and odd function identities.

Common Misconception: Odd/even identities are not one single table applied indiscriminately to every context. For integers, parity is arithmetic modulo $2$: odd times odd is odd, while odd plus odd is even. For functions, odd and even describe symmetry instead, so the product of two odd functions is even. The notation and vocabulary overlap, but the underlying structures are different and must be identified before applying a rule.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$n=2k$ "N is even because it is two times an integer."
$n=2k+1$ "N is odd because it is two times an integer plus one."
$n\equiv0\pmod2$ "N is congruent to zero modulo two."
$n\equiv1\pmod2$ "N is congruent to one modulo two."
$\text{odd}+\text{odd}=\text{even}$ "The sum of two odd integers is even."
$\text{odd}\cdot\text{odd}=\text{odd}$ "The product of two odd integers is odd."
$f(-x)=f(x)$ "F is an even function."
$f(-x)=-f(x)$ "F is an odd function."

In integer arithmetic, phrases such as "odd plus odd is even" are compressed statements about parity classes rather than literal equations between words.

More formally:

$$ a\equiv1\pmod2, \qquad b\equiv1\pmod2 $$

imply:

$$ a+b \equiv 1+1 \equiv 0 \pmod2. $$

In function language, the phrases "even identity" and "odd identity" may refer to substitution under $x\mapsto -x$, especially in trigonometry:

$$ \cos(-x)=\cos x $$

and:

$$ \sin(-x)=-\sin x. $$

Defense Notation Advice: State whether parity refers to integers or functions before using compressed odd/even rules. For integer proofs, use $2k$, $2k+1$, or congruences modulo $2$. For functions, verify $f(-x)=f(x)$ or $f(-x)=-f(x)$ and confirm that the domain is symmetric about zero. Do not transfer the integer rule "odd times odd is odd" into function-parity calculations, where odd times odd is even.
