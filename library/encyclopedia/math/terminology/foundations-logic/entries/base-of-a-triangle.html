Base of a Triangle

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

The base of a triangle is a side chosen as a reference for a particular geometric measurement or construction. Unlike certain specialized figures, a general triangle does not possess one permanently designated base.

If triangle $ABC$ is being considered and side $BC$ is selected as the base, then:

$$ \boxed{ \overline{BC} = \text{chosen base} }. $$

The opposite vertex is $A$, and the corresponding height is measured perpendicularly from $A$ to the line containing $BC$.

$$ \boxed{ \text{chosen base} \longleftrightarrow \text{corresponding altitude} }. $$

Thus the word base identifies a relational role rather than necessarily a visually lower side.

Operational Nuance

Any Side May Be Chosen

A triangle has three sides, and in a general triangle any one of them may be designated as the base.

If the side lengths are:

$$ a,\quad b,\quad c, $$

then each can serve as a base when paired with its corresponding altitude.

Base and Opposite Vertex

If $\overline{BC}$ is chosen as the base, then $A$ is the opposite vertex.

If $\overline{AC}$ is chosen as the base, then $B$ is the opposite vertex.

If $\overline{AB}$ is chosen as the base, then $C$ is the opposite vertex.

Corresponding Altitude

For base $\overline{BC}$, let $D$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $A$ to the line containing $BC$.

$$ AD\perp BC. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ AD=h_{BC} } $$

is the height corresponding to base $BC$.

Area Formula

If a chosen base has length $b$ and its corresponding height is $h$, the area is:

$$ \boxed{ A=\frac12bh }. $$

Why Base and Height Must Correspond

The height used in:

$$ A=\frac12bh $$

must be perpendicular to the line containing the selected base.

A height associated with a different side cannot simply be paired with the chosen base.

$$ \boxed{ b \text{ and } h \text{ must be a corresponding pair} }. $$

Three Equivalent Area Expressions

If $a,b,c$ are side lengths and $h_a,h_b,h_c$ are their corresponding altitudes, then:

$$ \boxed{ A = \frac12ah_a = \frac12bh_b = \frac12ch_c }. $$

Changing the base changes the corresponding altitude but not the triangle's area.

The Base Need Not Be Horizontal

A base is frequently drawn horizontally for convenience, but horizontal position is not part of its mathematical definition.

$$ \boxed{ \text{base} \ne \text{necessarily bottom side} }. $$

Rotating the Triangle

Rotating a diagram changes its appearance but not its geometric relationships. A side already designated as the base remains the base unless a different side is deliberately selected.

Height May Lie Inside the Triangle

In an acute triangle, the altitude corresponding to each side falls inside the triangle.

Height May Coincide with a Side

In a right triangle, if one leg is selected as the base, the other leg is perpendicular to it and therefore serves as the corresponding height.

For legs $a$ and $b$:

$$ \boxed{ A=\frac12ab }. $$

Hypotenuse as a Base

The hypotenuse of a right triangle may also be selected as the base.

If its length is $c$ and the perpendicular altitude to the hypotenuse is $h_c$, then:

$$ \boxed{ A=\frac12ch_c }. $$

Height May Fall Outside the Triangle

In an obtuse triangle, the perpendicular from an opposite vertex to the line containing certain sides may meet an extension of the side rather than the side segment itself.

The height is still the perpendicular distance to the line containing the base.

$$ \boxed{ h = \text{distance from opposite vertex to base line} }. $$

Base Segment versus Base Line

The base itself is usually the side segment. For altitude construction, however, one often works with the infinite line containing that side.

$$ \boxed{ \overline{BC} = \text{base segment} } $$

while:

$$ \boxed{ \overleftrightarrow{BC} = \text{line containing the base} }. $$

Base versus Base Length

The geometric base and its numerical length are related but distinct.

If $\overline{BC}$ is the base, then:

$$ \boxed{ \overline{BC} = \text{segment}, \qquad BC=b = \text{length} }. $$

Base and Included Angle Area Formula

Suppose two sides of lengths $a$ and $b$ meet at included angle $C$. Then:

$$ \boxed{ A=\frac12ab\sin C }. $$

This formula can be understood by treating one side as a base and recognizing that the corresponding height is the perpendicular component of the other side.

Deriving the Sine Area Formula

If $a$ is chosen as the base and side $b$ forms angle $C$ with it, then:

$$ h=b\sin C. $$

Therefore:

$$ A = \frac12a(b\sin C) = \boxed{ \frac12ab\sin C }. $$

Base in Coordinate Geometry

Suppose:

$$ B=(x_1,y_1), \qquad C=(x_2,y_2). $$

The base length $BC$ is:

$$ \boxed{ BC = \sqrt{ (x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2 } }. $$

Convenient Horizontal Base

If a triangle has coordinates:

$$ A=(p,h), \qquad B=(0,0), \qquad C=(b,0), $$

then $\overline{BC}$ is horizontal with base length:

$$ \boxed{ b }. $$

The perpendicular height is $|h|$, so:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12b|h| }. $$

Determinant Area Formula

Coordinate geometry can compute triangle area without first choosing a visibly horizontal base:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \left| x_1(y_2-y_3) + x_2(y_3-y_1) + x_3(y_1-y_2) \right| }. $$

Even here, the geometric base-height interpretation remains valid.

Vector Cross-Product Interpretation

If two side vectors from one vertex are $\mathbf u$ and $\mathbf v$, then:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \|\mathbf u\times\mathbf v\| } $$

in three-dimensional vector notation.

The cross product contains the same geometric information as base times perpendicular height.

Isosceles Triangle

In a non-equilateral isosceles triangle, the base has a more specialized meaning: it is the side opposite the vertex where the two congruent sides meet.

Thus if:

$$ AB=AC, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ BC=\text{isosceles base} }. $$

Base of an Isosceles Triangle and Symmetry

The altitude from the apex to the base of an isosceles triangle is simultaneously:

  • an altitude,
  • a median,
  • an angle bisector,
  • a perpendicular bisector of the base,
  • an axis of reflectional symmetry.

Equilateral Triangle

In an equilateral triangle all three sides are congruent. No side is inherently a unique base.

Any side may be selected as the base:

$$ \boxed{ \text{equilateral triangle: any side may serve as base} }. $$

Equilateral Height

If an equilateral triangle has side length $s$, choosing any side as the base gives height:

$$ \boxed{ h= \frac{\sqrt3}{2}s }. $$

Therefore its area is:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac{\sqrt3}{4}s^2 }. $$

Base of a Scalene Triangle

A scalene triangle has no naturally distinguished side from congruence alone. Any side can be chosen as the base according to convenience.

Choosing a Convenient Base

In problem solving, one often chooses the base that makes the corresponding height easiest to determine.

For example, a horizontal or vertical side in coordinate geometry may be especially convenient.

Changing the Base Does Not Change Area

If one side is longer, its corresponding altitude is proportionally shorter so that:

$$ ah_a = bh_b = ch_c = 2A. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ h_a:h_b:h_c = \frac1a:\frac1b:\frac1c }. $$

Longest Side and Shortest Altitude

Because:

$$ h_a=\frac{2A}{a}, $$

the longest side of a fixed triangle corresponds to the shortest altitude.

Likewise, the shortest side corresponds to the longest altitude.

Base and Heron's Formula

Heron's formula computes area from the three side lengths without selecting a base-height pair explicitly:

$$ \boxed{ A= \sqrt{ s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c) } }, $$

where:

$$ s= \frac{a+b+c}{2}. $$

Once the area is known, any corresponding altitude can be recovered. For example:

$$ \boxed{ h_a=\frac{2A}{a} }. $$

Base and Median

A median drawn to a selected base connects the opposite vertex to the midpoint of that base.

A median is not generally perpendicular to the base.

$$ \boxed{ \text{median} \ne \text{altitude} } $$

in a general triangle.

Base and Perpendicular Bisector

The perpendicular bisector of a base passes through the base midpoint and is perpendicular to the base, but it need not pass through the opposite vertex.

Therefore it is not generally the corresponding altitude.

Base and Angle Bisector

An angle bisector from the opposite vertex divides the angle, not necessarily the base into equal lengths and not necessarily at a right angle.

These constructions coincide in special symmetric triangles but differ in general.

Base in Similar Triangles

If two triangles are similar with scale factor $k$, corresponding base lengths scale by $k$:

$$ \boxed{ b_2=kb_1 }. $$

Corresponding heights also scale by $k$, so areas scale by:

$$ \boxed{ k^2 }. $$

Base in Congruent Triangles

Corresponding bases in congruent triangles have equal length because all corresponding sides are congruent.

Common Misconception: A Triangle Has One Permanent Base

A general triangle does not have a single permanent base. Base designation depends on the problem or construction.

$$ \boxed{ \text{any side can be a base} }. $$

Common Misconception: Height Is Always Inside

The height is a perpendicular distance. In an obtuse triangle, the altitude may extend outside the triangle to meet the extended base line.

Common Misconception: The Longest Side Must Be the Base

Side length does not determine base status in a general triangle. The longest, shortest, or intermediate side may all serve as the base.

Common Misconception: Base and Height Are Both Sides

The base is a side segment. The corresponding height is a perpendicular distance from the opposite vertex to the base line and need not itself be a side of the triangle.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$BC=b$ "Take B C as the base, with base length b."
$AD\perp BC$ "A D is the altitude corresponding to base B C."
$A=\frac12bh$ "The area is one-half the base times the corresponding height."
$A=\frac12ah_a=\frac12bh_b=\frac12ch_c$ "The same area can be computed using any side with its corresponding altitude."
$A=\frac12ab\sin C$ "The area is one-half the product of two sides times the sine of their included angle."
$h_a=\frac{2A}{a}$ "The altitude to side a equals twice the area divided by a."

In elementary geometry, phrases such as let BC be the base signal that a side is being selected as the reference side. If the word base appears without qualification in an isosceles-triangle context, it may instead refer to the distinguished side opposite the vertex angle.

Defense Notation Advice: State both the selected base and its corresponding height when using $A=\frac12bh$. This prevents accidentally pairing a side with the wrong altitude.
