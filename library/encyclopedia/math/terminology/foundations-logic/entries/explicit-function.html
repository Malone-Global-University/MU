Explicit Function

Subfield: Foundations & Logic

Etymology & Roots

An explicit function is a function whose output or dependent variable is given directly in terms of its input or independent variable.

The standard form is:

$$ \boxed{ y=f(x) }. $$

For example:

$$ \boxed{ y=3x^2-5x+1 } $$

defines $y$ explicitly as a function of $x$. Once a valid value of $x$ is supplied, the right-hand side directly determines the corresponding value of $y$.

The word explicit comes from the idea of something being stated directly or made fully visible. In an explicit functional rule, the dependent quantity has already been isolated.

This contrasts with an implicit relation such as:

$$ x^2+y^2=25, $$

where $y$ is not initially isolated as a single expression in $x$.

Solving for $y$ gives:

$$ y=\pm\sqrt{25-x^2}. $$

The appearance of two possible values shows that the entire circle is not the graph of one explicit real-valued function of $x$, although its upper and lower semicircles can each be expressed explicitly.

Operational Nuance

Canonical Form

An explicit function is typically written:

$$ \boxed{ y=f(x) } $$

or:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=\text{expression involving }x }. $$

Direct Evaluation

If:

$$ f(x)=2x^2+3, $$

then:

$$ f(4) = 2(4)^2+3 = 35. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{f(4)=35}. $$

Dependent and Independent Variables

In:

$$ y=f(x), $$

$x$ is ordinarily the independent variable and $y$ is the dependent variable. The explicit formula directly specifies how $y$ depends on $x$.

Explicit Polynomial Function

The rule:

$$ \boxed{ y=x^3-4x+7 } $$

is explicit because the dependent variable appears alone on one side.

Explicit Rational Function

The rule:

$$ \boxed{ y=\frac{x+1}{x-2} } $$

is explicit, subject to the domain restriction:

$$ \boxed{x\neq2}. $$

Explicit Radical Function

The rule:

$$ \boxed{ y=\sqrt{x-3} } $$

is explicit over the real numbers with:

$$ \boxed{x\geq3}. $$

Explicit Exponential Function

$$ \boxed{ y=5e^{2x} }. $$

Explicit Logarithmic Function

$$ \boxed{ y=\ln(x+4) } $$

with:

$$ \boxed{x>-4}. $$

Explicit Trigonometric Function

$$ \boxed{ y=2\sin x+1 }. $$

Explicit vs. Implicit Form

Compare:

$$ 2x+3y=12 $$

with:

$$ \boxed{ y=4-\frac23x }. $$

The first is an implicit equation in $x$ and $y$; the second explicitly gives $y$ as a function of $x$.

Converting an Implicit Equation to Explicit Form

Begin with:

$$ 3x+2y=10. $$

Subtract $3x$:

$$ 2y=10-3x. $$

Divide by $2$:

$$ \boxed{ y=5-\frac32x }. $$

The relationship is now in explicit form with respect to $y$.

Explicit with Respect to a Chosen Variable

Explicitness is relative to the variable being isolated.

From:

$$ y=3x+6, $$

one can solve for $x$:

$$ \boxed{ x=\frac{y-6}{3} }. $$

The same relation is then explicit with respect to $x$ as a function of $y$.

Not Every Implicit Relation Can Be Globally Made Explicit

Consider:

$$ x^2+y^2=1. $$

Solving for $y$ gives:

$$ y=\pm\sqrt{1-x^2}. $$

Since most $x$ values in $(-1,1)$ correspond to two $y$ values, the complete circle cannot be represented by a single explicit function $y=f(x)$.

Branches

The circle can instead be divided into two explicit branches:

$$ \boxed{ y=\sqrt{1-x^2} } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ y=-\sqrt{1-x^2} }. $$

Function Requirement

A formula is explicit as a function of $x$ only if each permitted input $x$ determines exactly one output under the stated rule.

Vertical-Line Test

Graphically, an explicit real-valued function $y=f(x)$ must pass the vertical line test:

$$ \boxed{ \text{each permitted }x \text{ corresponds to at most one }y }. $$

Explicit Form Does Not Remove Domain Restrictions

Rewriting a relation explicitly does not mean that every real input becomes valid.

For:

$$ y=\frac1{x-4}, $$

one still requires:

$$ \boxed{x\neq4}. $$

Original Restrictions Must Be Preserved

Consider:

$$ y=\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}. $$

Algebraically:

$$ \frac{x^2-1}{x-1}=x+1 $$

for $x\neq1$.

Therefore the original function is:

$$ \boxed{ y=x+1, \qquad x\neq1 }. $$

Cancellation does not restore the excluded input.

Piecewise Explicit Functions

An explicit function can be defined by cases:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)= \begin{cases} x^2, & x<0,\\ 2x+1, & x\geq0. \end{cases} } $$

The function remains explicit because each permitted input directly determines the applicable expression and output.

Absolute Value as Explicit Function

The rule:

$$ \boxed{ y=|x| } $$

is explicit, even though it can also be written piecewise:

$$ |x|= \begin{cases} -x, & x<0,\\ x, & x\geq0. \end{cases} $$

Explicit Formula vs. Function Notation

The expressions:

$$ y=2x+3 $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=2x+3 } $$

can describe the same rule, but function notation more explicitly identifies the function as an object.

Explicit Function vs. Formula

A formula and a function are not always identical concepts. A function includes a domain and an assignment rule, and sometimes a codomain as part of its formal specification.

Thus:

$$ f(x)=x^2 $$

can define different functions depending on whether the declared domain is $\mathbb{R}$, $[0,\infty)$, $\mathbb{Z}$, or another set.

Explicit Function vs. Explicit Formula of a Sequence

For a sequence:

$$ a_n=f(n), $$

the explicit formula directly computes a term from its discrete index.

An explicit function is the broader concept:

$$ \boxed{ y=f(x) }. $$

Sequences are special functions with discrete domains.

Parametric Contrast

A curve may be given parametrically:

$$ x=g(t), \qquad y=h(t). $$

This gives both coordinates explicitly in terms of the parameter $t$, but it does not necessarily give $y$ explicitly as a function of $x$.

Example: Parametric Circle

$$ x=\cos t, \qquad y=\sin t. $$

Both coordinates are explicit functions of $t$, while the full circle is not a single explicit function $y=f(x)$.

Inverse Function Contrast

If $f$ is one-to-one, an equation:

$$ y=f(x) $$

may be rearranged to:

$$ \boxed{ x=f^{-1}(y) }. $$

This makes the former independent variable explicit in terms of the former dependent variable.

Example of Inversion

From:

$$ y=2x+5, $$

solve:

$$ x=\frac{y-5}{2}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ f^{-1}(x)=\frac{x-5}{2} }. $$

Explicit Differentiation

If:

$$ y=f(x), $$

then differentiation can often be performed directly:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dy}{dx}=f'(x) }. $$

Example

If:

$$ y=x^3+2x, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dy}{dx}=3x^2+2 }. $$

Implicit Differentiation Contrast

If a relation is left in implicit form:

$$ x^2+y^2=25, $$

differentiating gives:

$$ 2x+2y\frac{dy}{dx}=0, $$

hence:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{x}{y} } $$

wherever $y\neq0$.

One need not first solve the relation explicitly.

Implicit Function Theorem

In advanced analysis, an equation:

$$ F(x,y)=0 $$

may locally determine $y$ as an explicit function:

$$ \boxed{ y=f(x) } $$

even when no convenient algebraic formula for $f$ can be written.

Under suitable differentiability conditions, if:

$$ F(x_0,y_0)=0 $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\partial F}{\partial y}(x_0,y_0)\neq0 }, $$

then the implicit function theorem provides a local function $y=f(x)$ near the point.

Explicit Solutions of Differential Equations

In differential equations, the term explicit solution may refer to a solution written directly as:

$$ \boxed{ y=f(x) }. $$

This contrasts with an implicit solution such as:

$$ F(x,y)=C. $$

Example

An implicit solution:

$$ y^2=x^2+C $$

may produce explicit branches:

$$ \boxed{ y=\pm\sqrt{x^2+C} }. $$

Multivariable Explicit Functions

Explicitness is not restricted to one input variable.

For example:

$$ \boxed{ z=f(x,y)=x^2+xy-y^2 } $$

explicitly defines $z$ in terms of $x$ and $y$.

Higher-Dimensional Example

$$ \boxed{ w=f(x,y,z)=x+y^2-e^z }. $$

Vector-Valued Explicit Function

A function can explicitly assign a vector:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{r}(t) = \langle\cos t,\sin t,t\rangle }. $$

Each component is given directly in terms of $t$.

Explicit Does Not Mean Elementary

An explicit function may contain complicated operations or special functions. What matters is direct dependence on the input, not elementary simplicity.

Explicit Does Not Mean Invertible

The explicit function:

$$ y=x^2 $$

is not one-to-one on $\mathbb{R}$ and therefore does not possess an inverse function on all of $\mathbb{R}$.

Explicit Does Not Mean Continuous

A piecewise or discontinuous function may still be explicit.

For example:

$$ f(x)= \begin{cases} 0, & x<0,\\ 1, & x\geq0 \end{cases} $$

is explicit but discontinuous at $x=0$.

Explicit Does Not Mean Differentiable

The absolute-value function:

$$ y=|x| $$

is explicit but not differentiable at $x=0$.

Explicit Does Not Mean One Formula Without Cases

Piecewise rules are legitimate explicit functions because the output remains directly determined from the input and its applicable condition.

Common Misconception: An explicit function is not simply any equation containing $x$ and $y$. In the usual sense, the dependent variable has been isolated and each permitted input directly determines one output. Also, writing a relation explicitly does not erase domain restrictions or guarantee invertibility, continuity, or differentiability.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$y=f(x)$ "Y is given explicitly as a function of x."
$f(x)=2x+3$ "F of x equals two x plus three."
$2x+3y=12$ "A relation written implicitly in x and y."
$y=4-\frac23x$ "The same linear relation solved explicitly for y."
$F(x,y)=0$ "F of x comma y equals zero; an implicit relation."
$z=f(x,y)$ "Z is defined explicitly as a function of x and y."
$\mathbf{r}(t)=\langle x(t),y(t),z(t)\rangle$ "R of t is a vector-valued function given explicitly in the parameter t."

Typical instructions include:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"solve explicitly for }y" } $$

meaning that the equation should be rearranged into a form such as:

$$ \boxed{ y=f(x) }. $$

Similarly:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"write }x\text{ explicitly in terms of }y" } $$

asks for a form:

$$ \boxed{ x=g(y) }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: State which variable is being made explicit. A relation can be explicit with respect to $y$, explicit with respect to $x$, or fail to define a single global function in one of those directions. Preserve all domain restrictions introduced by denominators, radicals, logarithms, inverse functions, or the original relation.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Basic Definition: An explicit function gives the output directly in terms of the input.
  • Standard Form: $$ \boxed{ y=f(x) }. $$
  • Function Notation: $$ \boxed{ f(x)=\text{rule in }x }. $$
  • Independent Variable: $x$ commonly serves as the input.
  • Dependent Variable: $y$ commonly serves as the output.
  • Evaluation: Substitute an input into the explicit rule to determine its output.
  • Polynomial Example: $$ y=x^3-2x+1. $$
  • Rational Example: $$ y=\frac{x+2}{x-1}, \qquad x\neq1. $$
  • Radical Example: $$ y=\sqrt{x+5}, \qquad x\geq-5. $$
  • Exponential Example: $$ y=e^{3x}. $$
  • Logarithmic Example: $$ y=\ln x, \qquad x>0. $$
  • Trigonometric Example: $$ y=\cos x. $$
  • Absolute-Value Example: $$ y=|x|. $$
  • Piecewise Example: A function can be explicit even when defined by several cases.
  • Implicit Contrast: $$ F(x,y)=0 $$ may define a relation without isolating either variable.
  • Linear Conversion: $$ ax+by=c $$ can be written: $$ y=\frac{c-ax}{b} $$ when $b\neq0$.
  • Variable Dependence: Explicitness is relative to which variable is selected as dependent.
  • Circle Example: $$ x^2+y^2=r^2 $$ is not globally a single explicit function $y=f(x)$.
  • Upper Circle Branch: $$ \boxed{ y=\sqrt{r^2-x^2} }. $$
  • Lower Circle Branch: $$ \boxed{ y=-\sqrt{r^2-x^2} }. $$
  • Multiple Branches: An implicit relation may require several explicit functions to represent all of its points.
  • Vertical-Line Test: A graph of $y=f(x)$ cannot assign two distinct outputs to the same input.
  • Domain: The explicit formula must be interpreted together with its permitted input set.
  • Natural Domain: If no domain is stated, the largest standard domain where the formula makes sense is often inferred.
  • Denominator Restriction: Denominators cannot equal zero.
  • Even-Radical Restriction: Real even roots require nonnegative radicands.
  • Logarithmic Restriction: Real logarithm arguments must be positive.
  • Original Restriction: Algebraic simplification does not automatically restore excluded inputs.
  • Removable Hole: An explicitly simplified formula may need an accompanying excluded value.
  • Formula vs. Function: A formula alone does not always specify the full function without domain information.
  • Function Equality: Identical formulas on different domains can define different functions.
  • Explicit Sequence: $$ a_n=f(n) $$ is a discrete special case of explicit functional definition.
  • Sequence Domain: A sequence commonly uses integer indices rather than a continuous real domain.
  • Explicit Formula of a Sequence: Directly determines $a_n$ from $n$ without requiring preceding terms.
  • Recursive Contrast: Recursive definitions depend on previous values rather than only the current input index.
  • Parametric Form: $$ x=g(t),\qquad y=h(t) $$ explicitly defines both coordinates in terms of $t$.
  • Parametric Caution: Parametric equations do not necessarily produce a single explicit relation $y=f(x)$.
  • Inverse Function: A one-to-one explicit function may be inverted to express $x$ in terms of $y$.
  • Inverse Restriction: A non-one-to-one function may require a restricted domain before an inverse function exists.
  • Example: $y=x^2$ is explicit but not invertible on all of $\mathbb{R}$.
  • Restricted Inversion: On $x\geq0$, $y=x^2$ has inverse: $$ x=\sqrt y. $$
  • Explicit Differentiation: If $y=f(x)$, differentiation usually proceeds directly from the formula.
  • Derivative: $$ \boxed{ \frac{dy}{dx}=f'(x) }. $$
  • Implicit Differentiation: A relation need not first be solved explicitly before differentiation.
  • Implicit Function Theorem: Under appropriate conditions, an implicit relation can locally define one variable explicitly as a function of another.
  • Local vs. Global: Local explicit solvability does not guarantee one global explicit branch.
  • Differential Equations: An explicit solution is often written directly as $y=f(x)$.
  • Implicit Differential-Equation Solution: A solution may instead remain in the form $F(x,y)=C$.
  • Multivariable Function: $$ z=f(x,y) $$ explicitly gives one output from two inputs.
  • Higher Dimensions: Explicit functions may depend on any finite or infinite collection of appropriate input variables.
  • Vector-Valued Function: A vector can be given explicitly component by component.
  • Transformation: $$ T(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{y} $$ may explicitly define an output vector in terms of an input vector.
  • Modeling: Explicit functions give direct formulas for dependent quantities in terms of model inputs.
  • Physics: Position may be given explicitly as $s=s(t)$.
  • Economics: Cost may be written explicitly as $C=C(q)$ in terms of quantity $q$.
  • Probability: Probability mass and density functions are commonly given explicitly by formulas over specified domains.
  • Computer Science: Explicit functional rules often support direct computation from input data.
  • Numerical Methods: Some equations cannot conveniently be solved into elementary explicit form and instead require numerical approximation.
  • Transcendental Example: An equation containing both algebraic and transcendental dependence may resist elementary isolation of a variable.
  • Explicit Does Not Mean Simple: The direct formula may involve special functions or complicated expressions.
  • Explicit Does Not Mean Polynomial: Rational, radical, logarithmic, exponential, trigonometric, piecewise, and special-function rules can all be explicit.
  • Explicit Does Not Mean Continuous: A discontinuous piecewise function can be explicit.
  • Explicit Does Not Mean Differentiable: $|x|$ is explicit but not differentiable at $0$.
  • Explicit Does Not Mean Invertible: $x^2$ on $\mathbb{R}$ is explicit but not one-to-one.
  • Explicit Does Not Mean Unrestricted: Formula domains may still exclude many inputs.
  • Notation: $y=f(x)$ is the conventional explicit-function form.
  • Terminological Contrast: Explicit refers to direct specification of the output; implicit refers to a relation in which the variables remain linked by an equation.
  • Core Interpretation: An explicit function directly states how an output depends on its input: $$ \boxed{ y=f(x) }. $$ The dependent variable is isolated, evaluation can proceed directly, and the rule must still be interpreted with its domain. Implicit equations, parametric representations, recursive definitions, and multibranch relations provide important contrasting ways to describe mathematical dependence.
