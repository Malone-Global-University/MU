Modular Numbers

Subfield: Foundations & Logic

Etymology & Roots

Modular numbers is an informal term for numbers considered through their residue classes relative to a fixed modulus. More precisely, modular arithmetic works with congruence classes of integers rather than treating every integer as distinct.

For a positive integer $n$, two integers belong to the same modular class when:

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv b\pmod n } $$

which means:

$$ \boxed{ n\mid(a-b) }. $$

Thus modulo $5$, the integers:

$$ \ldots,-8,-3,2,7,12,17,\ldots $$

all represent the same modular class because each is congruent to $2$ modulo $5$.

The word modular comes from modulus, the fixed number that determines the repeating equivalence pattern. The phrase modular number is useful informally, but more precise mathematical language usually refers to residue classes, congruence classes, or elements of:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Fixed Modulus

Modular numbers make sense only relative to a chosen modulus:

$$ \boxed{ n>0 }. $$

Changing the modulus changes the modular number system.

Residue Class

The class represented by an integer $a$ modulo $n$ is:

$$ \boxed{ [a]_n = \{a+kn:k\in\mathbb{Z}\} }. $$

Example

$$ [3]_7 = \{\ldots,-11,-4,3,10,17,24,\ldots\}. $$

Representative

Any integer in a residue class can serve as a representative of that modular number.

$$ \boxed{ [3]_7 = [10]_7 = [-4]_7 }. $$

Least Nonnegative Representative

A standard representative is chosen from:

$$ \boxed{ 0,1,2,\ldots,n-1 }. $$

These are the least nonnegative residues modulo $n$.

Exactly $n$ Modular Classes

Modulo $n$, there are exactly $n$ distinct residue classes:

$$ \boxed{ [0]_n,[1]_n,\ldots,[n-1]_n }. $$

Integers Modulo $n$

The collection of these classes is written:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Some texts also write:

$$ \mathbb{Z}_n, $$

although this notation can have other meanings in advanced mathematics.

Modulo $5$ Example

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z} = \{[0],[1],[2],[3],[4]\} }. $$

Equality of Modular Numbers

Two residue classes are equal exactly when their representatives are congruent:

$$ \boxed{ [a]_n=[b]_n \iff a\equiv b\pmod n }. $$

Ordinary Equality vs. Modular Equality

As ordinary integers:

$$ 17\neq2. $$

But modulo $5$:

$$ \boxed{ [17]_5=[2]_5 }. $$

Addition of Modular Numbers

Addition is defined by:

$$ \boxed{ [a]_n+[b]_n = [a+b]_n }. $$

Addition Example

Modulo $5$:

$$ [3]+[4] = [7] = \boxed{[2]}. $$

Additive Identity

$$ \boxed{ [0]_n } $$

satisfies:

$$ [a]_n+[0]_n=[a]_n. $$

Additive Inverse

The additive inverse of $[a]_n$ is:

$$ \boxed{ [-a]_n }. $$

because:

$$ [a]_n+[-a]_n=[0]_n. $$

Additive-Inverse Example

Modulo $7$:

$$ [3]+[4]=[7]=[0]. $$

Therefore $[4]$ is the additive inverse of $[3]$.

Multiplication of Modular Numbers

$$ \boxed{ [a]_n[b]_n = [ab]_n }. $$

Multiplication Example

Modulo $5$:

$$ [3][4] = [12] = \boxed{[2]}. $$

Multiplicative Identity

$$ \boxed{ [1]_n } $$

satisfies:

$$ [a]_n[1]_n=[a]_n. $$

Multiplicative Inverse

A modular number $[a]_n$ has a multiplicative inverse when there exists $[b]_n$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ [a]_n[b]_n=[1]_n }. $$

Equivalently:

$$ \boxed{ ab\equiv1\pmod n }. $$

Inverse Criterion

$$ \boxed{ [a]_n \text{ is invertible} \iff \gcd(a,n)=1 }. $$

Example of an Invertible Modular Number

Modulo $7$:

$$ 3\cdot5=15\equiv1\pmod7. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ [3]_7^{-1}=[5]_7 }. $$

Example of a Noninvertible Modular Number

Modulo $6$, $[2]_6$ has no multiplicative inverse because:

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(2,6)=2 }. $$

Zero Divisor

A nonzero modular number can sometimes multiply another nonzero modular number to produce zero.

Modulo $6$:

$$ \boxed{ [2]_6[3]_6=[0]_6 }. $$

Thus $[2]_6$ and $[3]_6$ are zero divisors.

Prime Modulus

If $p$ is prime, every nonzero class modulo $p$ is invertible.

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} \text{ is a field} }. $$

Composite Modulus

If $n$ is composite, $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ contains nonzero noninvertible elements and usually zero divisors.

Ring Structure

Under modular addition and multiplication:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} } $$

forms a commutative ring.

Additive Group

Under addition alone:

$$ \boxed{ (\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z},+) } $$

is a cyclic group.

Generator

The class $[1]_n$ generates every additive residue class:

$$ \boxed{ [k]_n = k[1]_n }. $$

Cyclic Addition

Repeated addition of $[1]_n$ cycles:

$$ [0] \to [1] \to [2] \to \cdots \to [n-1] \to [0]. $$

Clock Interpretation

Modular numbers are often visualized as positions on a clock.

Modulo $12$:

$$ [15]_{12} = \boxed{[3]_{12}}. $$

Parity as Modular Numbers

Modulo $2$, there are only two classes:

$$ \boxed{ [0]_2,\ [1]_2 }. $$

They represent even and odd integers.

Even Class

$$ \boxed{ [0]_2 = \{\ldots,-4,-2,0,2,4,\ldots\} }. $$

Odd Class

$$ \boxed{ [1]_2 = \{\ldots,-3,-1,1,3,5,\ldots\} }. $$

Modulo $1$

Modulo $1$, there is only one residue class:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{Z}/1\mathbb{Z} = \{[0]\} }. $$

Negative Representatives

Negative representatives can be useful:

$$ \boxed{ [6]_7=[-1]_7 }. $$

Thus:

$$ [6]_7^4 = [-1]_7^4 = \boxed{[1]_7}. $$

Balanced Residues

Instead of using only $0$ through $n-1$, one may choose representatives centered around zero when convenient.

Modulo $7$, for example:

$$ \boxed{ -3,-2,-1,0,1,2,3 } $$

form another complete residue system.

Powers of Modular Numbers

Positive integer powers are computed through repeated modular multiplication:

$$ \boxed{ [a]_n^k=[a^k]_n }. $$

Example

Modulo $5$:

$$ [2]^4 = [16] = \boxed{[1]}. $$

Power Cycles

Powers often repeat because the modular number system is finite.

Modulo $5$:

$$ [2], [4], [3], [1], [2], \ldots $$

Multiplicative Order

If $[a]_n$ is invertible, its multiplicative order is the least positive integer $k$ satisfying:

$$ \boxed{ [a]_n^k=[1]_n }. $$

Units Modulo $n$

The invertible modular numbers form:

$$ \boxed{ (\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times }. $$

Euler Totient

The number of invertible classes modulo $n$ is:

$$ \boxed{ \varphi(n) }. $$

Euler's Theorem

For an invertible modular number:

$$ \boxed{ [a]_n^{\varphi(n)} = [1]_n }. $$

Fermat's Little Theorem

For prime $p$ and nonzero class $[a]_p$:

$$ \boxed{ [a]_p^{p-1} = [1]_p }. $$

Polynomial Evaluation

Polynomials can be evaluated at modular numbers:

$$ \boxed{ P([a]_n) = [P(a)]_n } $$

for integer-coefficient polynomials.

Linear Equations with Modular Numbers

Solving:

$$ [a]_n[x]_n=[b]_n $$

is equivalent to solving:

$$ \boxed{ ax\equiv b\pmod n }. $$

Unique Solution for an Invertible Coefficient

If:

$$ \gcd(a,n)=1, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ [x]_n = [a]_n^{-1}[b]_n }. $$

Example

Modulo $7$:

$$ [3][x]=[5]. $$

Since:

$$ [3]^{-1}=[5], $$

then:

$$ [x] = [5][5] = [25] = \boxed{[4]}. $$

Division Is Conditional

Not every modular number can be divided by another. Division is defined through multiplication by an inverse.

Prime-Modulus Advantage

Modulo a prime, every nonzero modular number can serve as a divisor because every nonzero class is invertible.

Composite-Modulus Limitation

Modulo a composite integer, some nonzero classes cannot be used as divisors.

Quotient Interpretation

The notation:

$$ \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} $$

describes a quotient of the integers by the equivalence relation of congruence modulo $n$.

Coset Form

Each modular number can be written:

$$ \boxed{ [a]_n = a+n\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Zero Class

$$ \boxed{ [0]_n = n\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Canonical Projection

The map:

$$ \boxed{ \pi_n:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}, \qquad \pi_n(a)=[a]_n } $$

sends each integer to its modular number.

Projection Preserves Addition

$$ \boxed{ \pi_n(a+b) = \pi_n(a)+\pi_n(b) }. $$

Projection Preserves Multiplication

$$ \boxed{ \pi_n(ab) = \pi_n(a)\pi_n(b) }. $$

Representative Independence

Arithmetic on modular numbers is well-defined because choosing different representatives of the same classes gives the same resulting class.

Example

Since:

$$ [2]_5=[7]_5 $$

and:

$$ [3]_5=[8]_5, $$

both:

$$ [2]+[3] $$

and:

$$ [7]+[8] $$

give:

$$ \boxed{ [0]_5 }. $$

Modular Numbers and Finite Fields

When the modulus is prime, modular numbers form one of the most basic examples of a finite field.

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{F}_p \cong \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} } $$

for prime $p$.

Cryptography

Cryptographic algorithms commonly work with modular numbers, especially modular powers and inverses.

RSA-Type Expression

$$ \boxed{ [c]_n = [m]_n^e }. $$

Computer Science

Modular numbers model cyclic indexing, wraparound counters, finite-state positions, and periodic data structures.

Circular Index Example

For an array of length $8$:

$$ [10]_8=[2]_8. $$

Thus positions differing by multiples of $8$ correspond under cyclic indexing.

Calendars

Weekday positions can be modeled as classes modulo $7$.

Pitch Classes

In mathematical music theory, chromatic pitch classes may be modeled by:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Rotational Positions

Discrete rotational systems can be modeled by modular classes representing positions after repeated turns.

Modular Numbers vs. Remainders

A remainder is one chosen representative. A modular number, interpreted precisely as a residue class, contains every congruent representative.

Modular Numbers vs. Integers

Ordinary integers distinguish $2$, $7$, and $12$. Modulo $5$, they represent the same class:

$$ \boxed{ [2]_5=[7]_5=[12]_5 }. $$

Modular Numbers vs. Fractions

A notation such as:

$$ [3]_7^{-1} $$

means the multiplicative inverse of a residue class, not the ordinary rational number $1/3$.

Example

$$ [3]_7^{-1} = [5]_7 $$

because $3\cdot5\equiv1\pmod7$.

Common Error: Treating a Modular Number as a Single Integer

A residue class represents infinitely many congruent integers.

Common Error: Assuming the Least Nonnegative Residue Is the Class Itself

The residue $3$ is a representative; $[3]_7$ is the entire class.

Common Error: Forgetting the Modulus

The modular class represented by an integer depends on the modulus.

Common Error: Assuming $[a]_n=[b]_n$ Means $a=b$

It means only:

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv b\pmod n }. $$

Common Error: Assuming Every Nonzero Modular Number Has an Inverse

This fails for composite moduli.

Common Error: Dividing by Any Nonzero Modular Number

A divisor must be invertible modulo the chosen modulus.

Common Error: Assuming There Are Infinitely Many Modular Numbers Modulo $n$

There are infinitely many integer representatives but only $n$ residue classes.

Common Error: Confusing a Modular Number with the Remainder Operation

$a\bmod n$ often produces one representative, while $[a]_n$ denotes the entire equivalence class.

Common Error: Assuming Negative Representatives Are Invalid

Negative representatives are fully valid and often convenient.

Common Error: Treating $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ as Ordinary Division

The notation denotes a quotient structure of equivalence classes, not a numerical quotient.

Common Error: Assuming Composite Moduli Behave Like Prime Moduli

Composite moduli can produce zero divisors and noninvertible nonzero classes.

Common Error: Calling Modular Numbers a New Kind of Ordinary Integer

Modular numbers are more precisely equivalence classes of integers under a chosen congruence relation.

Common Misconception: A modular number is not merely the remainder left after division. The remainder is a convenient representative of a residue class. Modulo $5$, the numbers $2$, $7$, $12$, and $-3$ all represent the same modular object: $[2]_5$. Arithmetic is performed on these classes, producing the finite algebraic system $\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$[a]_n$ "The residue class of a modulo n."
$[2]_5=[7]_5$ "Two and seven represent the same residue class modulo five."
$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ "The integers modulo n," or "Z mod n Z."
$[a]_n+[b]_n=[a+b]_n$ "Add residue classes by adding representatives and reducing modulo n."
$[a]_n[b]_n=[ab]_n$ "Multiply residue classes by multiplying representatives and reducing modulo n."
$[a]_n^{-1}$ "The multiplicative inverse of the class of a modulo n."
$(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$ "The group of units modulo n."

The phrase modular number is understandable but less formal than residue class or congruence class.

In elementary discussion, one may say:

$$ \text{"the numbers modulo }5 $$

and mean the five classes represented by:

$$ \boxed{ 0,1,2,3,4 }. $$

In more precise algebraic language, those objects are:

$$ \boxed{ [0]_5,[1]_5,[2]_5,[3]_5,[4]_5 }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish a representative $a$ from the modular class $[a]_n$. Use $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ when referring to the complete modular number system, and use $\equiv$ when comparing integer representatives. Do not assume that every nonzero class is invertible unless the modulus is prime or the representative is relatively prime to the modulus.
