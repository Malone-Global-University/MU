Alternating Series

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

An alternating series is an infinite series whose terms alternate in sign, typically switching between positive and negative values.

A standard form is:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n-1}b_n } $$

where:

$$ b_n\ge0. $$

Written out, this becomes:

$$ b_1-b_2+b_3-b_4+\cdots. $$

The word alternating describes the repeated switching of signs. The word series indicates that the terms of a sequence are being added rather than merely listed.

Thus the distinction between an alternating sequence and an alternating series is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{sequence: }a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots } $$

versus:

$$ \boxed{ \text{series: }a_1+a_2+a_3+\cdots }. $$

The sign pattern alone does not guarantee convergence. Alternation can help a series converge because positive and negative contributions partially cancel, but the term magnitudes must behave appropriately.

Operational Nuance

Standard Forms

Two equivalent conventional forms are:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n-1}b_n } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^n b_n }. $$

The first begins with a positive term, while the second begins with a negative term. This starting sign does not fundamentally affect the convergence analysis.

Alternating Series Test

Suppose:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n-1}b_n $$

has nonnegative magnitudes $b_n$. The Alternating Series Test states that the series converges if:

$$ \boxed{ b_{n+1}\le b_n } $$

eventually, and:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{n\to\infty}b_n=0 }. $$

In words, the magnitudes must eventually decrease and approach zero.

Why the Test Works

Consider the partial sums:

$$ S_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}(-1)^{n-1}b_n. $$

When the magnitudes decrease, the positive and negative corrections become progressively smaller. The even and odd partial sums approach the same limiting value from opposite sides.

Informally:

$$ S_1>S_3>S_5>\cdots $$

and:

$$ S_2

under the usual positive-first convention, with both subsequences approaching the same limit.

Classic Example

The alternating harmonic series is:

$$ \boxed{ 1-\frac12+\frac13-\frac14+\frac15-\cdots }. $$

Here:

$$ b_n=\frac1n. $$

Since:

$$ \frac{1}{n+1}<\frac1n $$

and:

$$ \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1n=0, $$

the series converges by the Alternating Series Test.

Terms Must Approach Zero

The condition:

$$ \lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=0 $$

is necessary for every convergent infinite series.

Therefore:

$$ 1-1+1-1+1-\cdots $$

does not converge in the ordinary sense because its terms do not approach zero.

Approaching Zero Is Not Enough

Even for a general series, the condition:

$$ a_n\to0 $$

does not by itself prove convergence. It is merely a necessary condition.

For alternating series, the additional decreasing-magnitude condition allows the Alternating Series Test to establish convergence.

Absolute Convergence

An alternating series:

$$ \sum a_n $$

converges absolutely when:

$$ \boxed{ \sum |a_n| \text{ converges} }. $$

For:

$$ a_n=(-1)^{n-1}b_n, $$

this means:

$$ \sum b_n $$

converges.

Conditional Convergence

A series converges conditionally when:

$$ \boxed{ \sum a_n \text{ converges} \quad\text{but}\quad \sum |a_n| \text{ diverges} }. $$

The alternating harmonic series is the standard example:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n} $$

converges, but:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1n $$

diverges.

Alternating Series Error Estimate

One of the most useful features of the Alternating Series Test is its built-in approximation error bound.

If:

$$ S=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n-1}b_n $$

satisfies the test and:

$$ S_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}(-1)^{n-1}b_n, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ |S-S_N|\le b_{N+1} }. $$

The error after stopping at $N$ terms is therefore no greater than the magnitude of the first omitted term.

Example of the Error Bound

For the alternating harmonic series, if the first $100$ terms are used:

$$ \boxed{ |S-S_{100}| \le \frac1{101} }. $$

No exact value of the infinite sum is required to obtain this guarantee.

Common Misconception: A series does not converge merely because its signs alternate. The magnitudes must also become sufficiently small; under the standard Alternating Series Test, they must tend to zero and eventually decrease.

The factors:

$$ (-1)^n $$

and:

$$ (-1)^{n-1} $$

are common devices for encoding alternating signs.

For positive integer $n$:

$$ (-1)^n= \begin{cases} -1, & n\text{ odd},\\ 1, & n\text{ even}. \end{cases} $$

while:

$$ (-1)^{n-1}= \begin{cases} 1, & n\text{ odd},\\ -1, & n\text{ even}. \end{cases} $$

Defense Notation Advice: When applying the Alternating Series Test, isolate the nonnegative magnitude $b_n$. The alternating factor $(-1)^n$ or $(-1)^{n-1}$ controls the sign; the convergence conditions are checked on $b_n$.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Basic Form: $$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n-1}b_n }. $$
  • Opposite Starting Sign: $$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^n b_n $$ is also alternating.
  • Alternating Sign Pattern: A typical series has the form: $$ b_1-b_2+b_3-b_4+\cdots. $$
  • Alternating Series Test: Convergence follows when $b_n\ge0$, the $b_n$ are eventually nonincreasing, and: $$ \boxed{ \lim_{n\to\infty}b_n=0 }. $$
  • Eventually Decreasing: The magnitudes do not need to decrease from the very first term. It is enough that they become nonincreasing from some point onward.
  • Necessary Term Test: If: $$ \lim_{n\to\infty}a_n\ne0, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ \sum a_n \text{ diverges} }. $$
  • Divergence Example: $$ 1-1+1-1+\cdots $$ diverges in the ordinary sense because its terms fail to approach zero.
  • Alternating Harmonic Series: $$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n} } $$ converges.
  • Harmonic Contrast: $$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1n $$ diverges, showing that alternating signs can change the convergence behavior.
  • Absolute Convergence: $$ \sum a_n $$ converges absolutely if: $$ \boxed{ \sum |a_n| \text{ converges} }. $$
  • Absolute Implies Ordinary: Absolute convergence guarantees convergence of the original series.
  • Conditional Convergence: $$ \boxed{ \sum a_n\text{ converges} \quad\text{and}\quad \sum|a_n|\text{ diverges} }. $$
  • Conditional Example: The alternating harmonic series converges conditionally.
  • p-Series Example: $$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n^p} $$ converges by the Alternating Series Test for every: $$ p>0. $$
  • Absolute p-Series Case: The same alternating $p$-series converges absolutely when: $$ \boxed{p>1}. $$
  • Conditional p-Series Case: It converges conditionally when: $$ \boxed{ 0
  • Error Estimate: If the Alternating Series Test applies: $$ \boxed{ |R_N| = |S-S_N| \le b_{N+1} }. $$
  • First Omitted Term: The error is bounded by the magnitude of the first term not included in the partial sum.
  • Approximation Strategy: To guarantee error below $\varepsilon$, choose $N$ so that: $$ \boxed{ b_{N+1}<\varepsilon }. $$
  • Partial Sums: The finite approximations: $$ S_N=a_1+\cdots+a_N $$ determine convergence through: $$ \lim_{N\to\infty}S_N. $$
  • Oscillating Partial Sums: For a standard decreasing alternating series, odd and even partial sums bracket the limiting value.
  • Upper and Lower Bounds: Consecutive partial sums often provide natural bounds for the true sum.
  • Power Series: Substituting negative values into a power series can produce an alternating series.
  • Taylor Series: Several important Taylor series alternate, including: $$ \sin x = x-\frac{x^3}{3!}+\frac{x^5}{5!}-\cdots. $$
  • Cosine Series: $$ \cos x = 1-\frac{x^2}{2!}+\frac{x^4}{4!}-\cdots. $$
  • Logarithm Series: At $x=1$, the expansion: $$ \ln(1+x) = x-\frac{x^2}{2}+\frac{x^3}{3}-\cdots $$ yields the alternating harmonic series: $$ \ln2 = 1-\frac12+\frac13-\frac14+\cdots. $$
  • Geometric Series Connection: A geometric series with negative common ratio alternates: $$ 1-r+r^2-r^3+\cdots $$ when $r>0$.
  • Example: $$ 1-\frac12+\frac14-\frac18+\cdots $$ is an alternating geometric series with ratio: $$ -\frac12. $$
  • Geometric Sum: Therefore: $$ \boxed{ 1-\frac12+\frac14-\frac18+\cdots = \frac{1}{1+\frac12} = \frac23 }. $$
  • Comparison with Positive Series: Cancellation allows some alternating series to converge even when the corresponding positive-term series diverges.
  • Rearrangement: Absolutely convergent series may be rearranged without changing their sum. Conditionally convergent series require much greater care.
  • Riemann Rearrangement Phenomenon: A conditionally convergent real series can be rearranged so that its sum changes or so that the rearranged series fails to converge.
  • Integral Comparison: Alternating-series questions may be combined with the Integral Test, Comparison Test, Ratio Test, or Root Test when absolute convergence is being investigated.
  • Ratio Test: If the absolute values satisfy the Ratio Test with limit less than $1$, then the alternating series converges absolutely.
  • Root Test: Likewise, the Root Test may establish absolute convergence before the Alternating Series Test is needed.
  • Test Hierarchy: When absolute convergence is easy to establish, it gives a stronger conclusion than the Alternating Series Test alone.
  • Terminological Contrast: An alternating series changes sign from term to term. Absolute convergence means the series of magnitudes converges. Conditional convergence means the alternating series converges only after sign cancellation, while its absolute-value series diverges.
