Conjecture

Subfield: Foundations & Mathematical Logic

Etymology & Roots

A conjecture is a mathematical statement believed to be true on the basis of evidence, patterns, examples, computation, analogy, or partial reasoning, but which has not yet been established by a valid proof.

$$ \boxed{ \text{conjecture} = \text{plausible mathematical claim not yet proved} }. $$

The word comes from Latin roots associated with forming a judgment by putting information together. This linguistic history fits mathematical practice well: mathematicians often form conjectures by collecting observations and detecting a pattern before a proof is known.

A conjecture is neither a random guess nor a theorem. It normally has some mathematical motivation, but the available evidence does not yet constitute a proof.

$$ \boxed{ \text{evidence} \longrightarrow \text{conjecture} \longrightarrow \text{proof or counterexample} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Conjecture as a Mathematical Claim

A conjecture is ordinarily stated as a proposition capable of being true or false. For example:

$$ \boxed{ \text{Every even integer greater than }2 \text{ is the sum of two primes} }. $$

This is the famous Goldbach conjecture. It has been verified for extremely many cases, but finite verification alone is not the same as a proof for all integers.

Evidence Is Not Proof

A conjecture may be supported by thousands, millions, or billions of examples and still remain unproved.

$$ \boxed{ \text{many confirming examples} \not\Rightarrow \text{universal proof} }. $$

A universal statement concerns every object in its domain, including cases that have not been directly checked.

Example-Based Discovery

Conjectures often emerge from repeated calculations.

Suppose one computes:

$$ 1+3=4, $$
$$ 1+3+5=9, $$
$$ 1+3+5+7=16. $$

The pattern suggests:

$$ \boxed{ 1+3+5+\cdots+(2n-1)=n^2 }. $$

At the moment of discovery, this may begin as a conjecture. A proof then elevates the statement from plausible pattern to theorem.

Proof Converts a Conjecture into an Established Result

Once a conjecture has been proved, it is no longer merely conjectural.

$$ \boxed{ \text{conjecture} + \text{valid proof} \longrightarrow \text{theorem} }. $$

Historical names may remain attached even after proof, however.

Historical Names Can Persist

A statement may continue to be called a conjecture because that name became historically established before the proof was found.

For example, a result long known as a conjecture may continue to appear under its traditional name even after becoming a theorem.

Counterexample Disproves a Universal Conjecture

A universal conjecture:

$$ \forall x\in D,\quad P(x) $$

is disproved by a single counterexample $c\in D$ satisfying:

$$ \boxed{ \neg P(c) }. $$

Thus one counterexample can outweigh arbitrarily many confirming examples.

Example: A False Pattern

Consider the claim:

$$ n^2+n+41 \text{ is prime for every nonnegative integer }n. $$

It produces primes for many initial values. But at:

$$ n=40, $$

one obtains:

$$ 40^2+40+41 = 1681 = 41^2. $$

Therefore the universal claim is false.

Conjecture and Inductive Reasoning

Conjectures are frequently formed through inductive reasoning: observing particular cases and proposing a general pattern.

$$ \boxed{ \text{examples} \longrightarrow \text{pattern} \longrightarrow \text{conjecture} }. $$

This is different from mathematical induction, which is a deductive proof method.

Inductive Reasoning versus Mathematical Induction

Process Role
Inductive reasoning Suggests a general claim from observed cases
Mathematical induction Proves a statement for an infinite family of cases

Conjecture and Deduction

Once a conjecture has been formulated, deductive reasoning is ordinarily required to prove it.

$$ \boxed{ \text{conjecture formation may be inductive;} \qquad \text{proof must be deductively valid} }. $$

Computation as Evidence

Computers can test a conjecture over enormous finite ranges.

Such computation can:

  • discover patterns,
  • find counterexamples,
  • eliminate candidate conjectures,
  • suggest stronger or weaker formulations, and
  • provide evidence supporting further investigation.

Unless the domain itself is finite and exhaustively checked, numerical verification ordinarily does not replace proof.

Finite Domains Are Different

If a conjecture concerns a genuinely finite set of possibilities, complete exhaustive verification can constitute a proof.

$$ \boxed{ \text{finite exhaustive verification} = \text{proof when every case is covered} }. $$

Computer-Assisted Proof

Computer use does not automatically mean that a result remains conjectural. A computer-assisted argument can be a proof when the computation is incorporated into a logically complete, verifiable argument covering all required cases.

Conjecture and Pattern Recognition

Sequences provide a common setting for conjecture formation.

From:

$$ 2,\ 4,\ 8,\ 16,\ 32,\ldots $$

one might conjecture:

$$ a_n=2^n $$

under an appropriate indexing convention.

But a finite initial sequence does not determine a unique infinite continuation.

Finite Data Can Fit Many Rules

Given any finite list of values, multiple formulas can often reproduce the same observations but diverge later.

$$ \boxed{ \text{finite pattern} \not\Rightarrow \text{unique general law} }. $$

This is one reason conjecture must be followed by proof.

Geometric Conjectures

Geometry frequently produces conjectures through diagrams, measurement, or dynamic geometry software.

For example, measuring the medians of many triangles may suggest that all three medians are concurrent.

That observation becomes a theorem only after a valid argument establishes the result for every triangle.

A Diagram Is Evidence, Not Proof

A geometric diagram represents particular configurations and may contain accidental visual features.

$$ \boxed{ \text{looks true in diagram} \not\Rightarrow \text{proved} }. $$

Numerical Conjectures

Number theory is especially rich in conjectures because simple arithmetic patterns can generate questions whose proofs are extremely difficult.

Examples include conjectures about:

  • prime numbers,
  • Diophantine equations,
  • integer sequences,
  • divisibility, and
  • distribution of arithmetic objects.

Analytic Conjectures

Analysis produces conjectures about convergence, regularity, approximation, differential equations, inequalities, and the behavior of functions.

Combinatorial Conjectures

In combinatorics, computation can reveal patterns in finite structures that suggest general extremal bounds, counting formulas, or structural theorems.

Probabilistic Conjectures

Simulations may suggest limiting distributions, expected values, threshold behavior, or asymptotic laws. Simulation can motivate a conjecture but usually does not prove it.

Conjecture and Experimental Mathematics

Experimental mathematics uses computation, numerical experiments, visualization, symbolic calculation, and pattern exploration to discover and test mathematical ideas.

Conjecture formation is one of its central activities.

Strengthening a Conjecture

Evidence may suggest that a proposed statement can be made stronger.

For example, one may initially conjecture:

$$ P(x)\Rightarrow Q(x), $$

and later discover evidence for:

$$ \boxed{ P(x)\iff Q(x) }. $$

Weakening a False Conjecture

A counterexample does not always end the investigation. It may reveal that the conjecture needs an additional hypothesis.

A false claim:

$$ P\Rightarrow Q $$

might become valid after refinement:

$$ \boxed{ P\land R \Rightarrow Q }. $$

Counterexamples Improve Conjectures

In mathematical research, a counterexample can be productive because it identifies exactly where the original claim fails.

$$ \boxed{ \text{counterexample} \longrightarrow \text{refined hypotheses} \longrightarrow \text{better conjecture} }. $$

Minimal Counterexample

When a conjecture is false, the smallest or structurally simplest counterexample can be especially informative.

It may expose the mechanism responsible for failure.

Proof by Contradiction of a Conjecture

A conjecture $P$ may be proved by assuming its negation:

$$ \neg P $$

and deriving a contradiction:

$$ \boxed{ \neg P \Rightarrow \bot \Rightarrow P } $$

in classical mathematical reasoning.

Proof by Contrapositive

If a conjecture has conditional form:

$$ P\Rightarrow Q, $$

one may instead prove:

$$ \boxed{ \neg Q\Rightarrow\neg P }. $$

Proof by Mathematical Induction

A conjecture about every positive integer:

$$ P(n) $$

may sometimes be proved using:

$$ \boxed{ P(1) \quad\text{and}\quad P(k)\Rightarrow P(k+1) }. $$

Conjecture versus Theorem

The distinction is epistemic rather than grammatical:

Conjecture Theorem
Believed true Proved true within the stated framework
Supported by evidence or reasoning Supported by a valid proof
May later be disproved Follows deductively from accepted assumptions

Conjecture versus Hypothesis

A hypothesis can mean an assumption under which a theorem or conditional statement is considered:

$$ P\Rightarrow Q, $$

where $P$ is the hypothesis.

A conjecture, by contrast, is itself a proposed mathematical claim.

Conjecture versus Assumption

An assumption is adopted temporarily or as part of a framework. A conjecture is proposed as a statement expected to be true.

$$ \boxed{ \text{assume }P \ne \text{conjecture that }P }. $$

Conjecture versus Axiom

An axiom is accepted as a starting principle within a formal system rather than proved from earlier statements in that system.

A conjecture is not normally accepted as a foundational truth merely because it is plausible.

Conjecture versus Prediction

A prediction usually concerns what will happen or what data will be observed. A conjecture ordinarily asserts a mathematical relationship or property.

In applied mathematics the language can overlap, but the roles remain conceptually distinct.

Conjecture versus Guess

A conjecture may begin as a guess, but mathematical usage usually implies some supporting reasoning, evidence, structure, or motivation.

Conjecture versus Heuristic

A heuristic is a method or line of reasoning useful for discovery but not necessarily rigorous.

A heuristic may motivate a conjecture:

$$ \boxed{ \text{heuristic reasoning} \longrightarrow \text{conjecture} }. $$

Conjecture versus Lemma

A lemma is a proved result used primarily to establish another result. An unproved supporting claim is not a lemma merely because it would be useful.

Conjecture versus Open Problem

An open problem is a mathematical question that remains unresolved. A conjecture is a proposed answer or assertion associated with such a question.

$$ \boxed{ \text{open problem} = \text{unresolved question}, \qquad \text{conjecture} = \text{proposed mathematical claim} }. $$

A Conjecture Can Be True or False

The word conjecture describes the current status of our knowledge, not the underlying truth value.

$$ \boxed{ \text{conjecture} \not\Rightarrow \text{actually true} }. $$

A conjecture may eventually be proved true or disproved by counterexample.

A True Conjecture Can Remain Unproved

If a statement is in fact true but no proof is known, it remains conjectural from the standpoint of established mathematics.

A False Conjecture Can Have Enormous Supporting Evidence

A false statement may agree with all tested examples up to an extraordinarily large bound before eventually failing.

This demonstrates why empirical evidence and proof have different logical roles.

Conditional Conjectures

Some conjectures assert that one statement implies another:

$$ \boxed{ P\Rightarrow Q }. $$

Such conjectures must preserve their hypotheses carefully. A counterexample must satisfy $P$ while violating $Q$.

Existential Conjectures

A conjecture can assert existence:

$$ \boxed{ \exists x\in D \text{ such that }P(x) }. $$

One valid example proves an existential claim.

Universal Conjectures

A universal conjecture has form:

$$ \boxed{ \forall x\in D,\quad P(x) }. $$

It requires a general proof, while one counterexample disproves it.

Universal versus Existential Evidence

Claim Type What Proves It? What Disproves It?
$\forall x\,P(x)$ General proof One counterexample
$\exists x\,P(x)$ One valid example Proof that no such example exists

Conjecture and Definitions

Definitions are not ordinarily conjectured as true or false in the same sense. A definition establishes how a term will be used.

Conjectures concern propositions formed using defined concepts.

Conjecture and Models

In applied mathematics, one may conjecture that a model has a certain property or predicts a certain mathematical relationship.

The distinction between proving a statement about the model and validating the model against reality should be preserved.

Refinement by Domain

A conjecture that is false over one domain may become true over a restricted domain.

For example:

$$ P(x) \text{ for all real }x $$

may fail, while:

$$ \boxed{ P(x) \text{ for all }x\in D } $$

is provable for a suitable subset $D$.

Conjecture and Generalization

A proved special case may suggest a broader conjecture.

$$ \boxed{ \text{proved special case} \longrightarrow \text{possible general conjecture} }. $$

Conjecture and Analogy

Similar structures in different branches of mathematics often motivate analogous conjectures.

Analogy is a powerful discovery tool, but analogous statements still require their own proofs.

Conjecture and Research Programs

Important conjectures can organize entire areas of research. Even before resolution, they may generate:

  • partial results,
  • equivalent reformulations,
  • special cases,
  • new methods,
  • counterexample searches, and
  • connections among previously separate topics.

Common Misconception: Conjecture Means Wild Guess

A mathematical conjecture is usually motivated by evidence or mathematical structure, though the amount of evidence can vary.

Common Misconception: Many Examples Prove a Conjecture

Finite verification cannot prove an unrestricted universal statement over an infinite domain unless some additional argument reduces the problem to those cases.

Common Misconception: One Failed Example Only Weakens a Conjecture

For a universal conjecture, one genuine counterexample disproves the statement completely as written.

Common Misconception: A Famous Conjecture Is Probably a Theorem

Fame, longevity, and numerical verification do not establish proof.

Common Misconception: A Disproved Conjecture Was Useless

False conjectures can lead to new definitions, refined hypotheses, counterexamples, and deeper structural understanding.

Common Misconception: Mathematical Induction Is Conjecture Formation

Mathematical induction is a proof technique. Inductive reasoning from examples is a method of discovery.

Common Misconception: Computer Verification Can Never Be Proof

A finite exhaustive or formally verified computer-assisted argument can be part of a valid proof. The issue is logical completeness, not whether a computer was used.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Written Form Professional Articulation
$\forall n\in\mathbb N,\ P(n)$ "We conjecture that P of n holds for every natural number n."
$P\Rightarrow Q$ "We conjecture that P implies Q."
$\exists x\in D:P(x)$ "We conjecture that there exists an x in D satisfying P."
$\neg P(c)$ "c is a counterexample to the conjecture that P holds universally."
$P(n)$ verified for $n\le N$ "The conjecture has been verified computationally through N, but this does not by itself prove the general statement."

Mathematical writing commonly uses phrases such as: we conjecture that, this suggests the conjecture, the following conjecture remains open, a counterexample disproves the conjecture, and the conjecture was proved.

A careful author should not write therefore after a collection of examples when only a conjecture has been formed. Phrases such as suggests, appears to indicate, or motivates the conjecture preserve the distinction between evidence and deduction.

Defense Notation Advice: State the quantifiers, domain, and hypotheses of a conjecture explicitly. Distinguish computational verification from proof, and distinguish a counterexample from an example that merely fails to support the pattern. When a universal statement is disproved, identify the exact value violating it. If a false conjecture is repaired by adding conditions, state the revised domain or hypotheses rather than continuing to use the original claim informally.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: A conjecture is a mathematically motivated claim believed to be true but not yet proved.
  • Evidence: Examples, computation, diagrams, analogy, and partial results may support a conjecture.
  • Proof: A valid proof converts an unproved conjectural claim into an established result.
  • Counterexample: One counterexample disproves a universal conjecture.
  • Universal Form: $$ \boxed{ \forall x\in D,\ P(x) }. $$
  • Existential Form: $$ \boxed{ \exists x\in D:P(x) }. $$
  • Conditional Form: $$ \boxed{ P\Rightarrow Q }. $$
  • Inductive Reasoning: Particular examples can suggest a conjecture.
  • Mathematical Induction: A deductive proof method and not the same as pattern-based conjecture formation.
  • Computation: Useful for testing, discovering, refining, and disproving conjectures.
  • Finite Verification: Can constitute proof when every case in a finite domain is rigorously exhausted.
  • Infinite Domain: Arbitrarily extensive finite testing does not by itself prove a universal claim over infinitely many cases.
  • Geometry: Diagrams and measurement frequently motivate conjectures but do not by themselves prove them.
  • Number Theory: Many important conjectures concern primes, divisibility, integer sequences, and Diophantine equations.
  • Analysis: Conjectures may concern convergence, inequalities, regularity, differential equations, and asymptotic behavior.
  • Combinatorics: Finite experimentation often suggests general structural or counting results.
  • Experimental Mathematics: Uses computation and exploration systematically to generate and investigate conjectures.
  • Refinement: Counterexamples can reveal missing hypotheses and lead to a corrected conjecture.
  • Generalization: A theorem in a special case may motivate a broader conjecture.
  • Historical Naming: The word conjecture may remain in a result's traditional name even after proof.
  • Theorem Contrast: A theorem has been proved; a conjecture has not.
  • Hypothesis Contrast: A hypothesis is often an assumed condition in a theorem or implication; a conjecture is itself the proposed claim.
  • Assumption Contrast: An assumption is provisionally adopted, while a conjecture is asserted as a plausible mathematical truth awaiting proof.
  • Axiom Contrast: An axiom is accepted as foundational within a formal system rather than treated as an unproved research claim.
  • Prediction Contrast: A prediction usually concerns an expected outcome; a conjecture concerns a proposed mathematical statement.
  • Heuristic Contrast: A heuristic is a discovery method; a conjecture is the claim such reasoning may suggest.
  • Open-Problem Contrast: An open problem is an unresolved question; a conjecture is a proposed assertion relevant to such a question.
  • Truth-Status Nuance: Calling something a conjecture describes its proof status, not whether it is ultimately true or false.
  • Common Error: Do not treat a conjecture as a theorem merely because it has strong empirical support.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse inductive reasoning with mathematical induction.
  • Common Error: Do not assume a finite pattern determines a unique infinite rule.
  • Common Error: Do not disregard a valid counterexample to a universal claim.
  • Common Error: Do not assume a disproved conjecture has no mathematical value.
  • Terminological Contrast: A conjecture is a proposed mathematical statement believed true but not yet proved. A theorem is a statement established by proof. A hypothesis is commonly an assumed condition within an argument or conditional theorem. An assumption is adopted for the purposes of reasoning or modeling. An axiom is accepted as a foundational premise within a formal system. A heuristic is a nonrigorous discovery strategy. A counterexample is an instance that falsifies a universal claim. An open problem is an unresolved mathematical question, while a conjecture is a proposed answer or assertion that may form part of that problem.
