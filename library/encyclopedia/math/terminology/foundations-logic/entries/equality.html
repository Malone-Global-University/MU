Equality, Properties of

Subfield: Foundations & Logic

Etymology & Roots

The properties of equality are logical and algebraic rules that describe how equality behaves and which operations preserve an existing equality.

The equality symbol:

$$ \boxed{=} $$

states that two expressions denote the same mathematical object or value within the context being used.

If:

$$ a=b, $$

then any legitimate transformation that treats both equal quantities in the same way preserves equality, subject to the operation being defined.

The word equality derives from the idea of being equal, alike in value, or identical with respect to the relevant mathematical object. The properties of equality formalize this idea and make equation solving logically defensible rather than a collection of arbitrary symbol-moving rules.

At the foundational level, equality satisfies the reflexive, symmetric, and transitive properties:

$$ \boxed{ a=a } $$
$$ \boxed{ a=b\Longrightarrow b=a } $$
$$ \boxed{ a=b\text{ and }b=c \Longrightarrow a=c }. $$

These are structural properties of equality itself. Addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and substitution properties describe how equality is preserved under operations or replacement.

Operational Nuance

Reflexive Property of Equality

Every mathematical object is equal to itself:

$$ \boxed{ a=a }. $$

This is the reflexive property of equality.

Symmetric Property of Equality

If:

$$ a=b, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ b=a }. $$

Equality can therefore be read in either direction.

Transitive Property of Equality

If:

$$ a=b \qquad\text{and}\qquad b=c, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a=c }. $$

This property allows chains of equal expressions to be connected.

Substitution Property of Equality

If:

$$ a=b, $$

then one may replace $a$ by $b$, or $b$ by $a$, in an expression where the replacement is meaningful.

$$ \boxed{ a=b \Longrightarrow F(a)=F(b) } $$

for any well-defined function or operation $F$ applied to both sides.

Example of Substitution

If:

$$ x=4, $$

then:

$$ x^2+1 = 4^2+1 = \boxed{17}. $$

Addition Property of Equality

If:

$$ a=b, $$

then adding the same quantity $c$ to both sides preserves equality:

$$ \boxed{ a+c=b+c }. $$

Subtraction Property of Equality

If:

$$ a=b, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a-c=b-c }. $$

This can also be viewed as addition of the additive inverse $-c$.

Multiplication Property of Equality

If:

$$ a=b, $$

then multiplying both sides by the same quantity $c$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ ac=bc }. $$

Unlike division, multiplication by $0$ still produces a true equality, although it may destroy information and is not reversible.

Division Property of Equality

If:

$$ a=b, $$

and:

$$ c\neq0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{a}{c} = \frac{b}{c} }. $$

The condition $c\neq0$ is essential because division by zero is undefined.

Function-Preserving Principle

The elementary properties can be unified by a broader statement:

$$ \boxed{ a=b \Longrightarrow f(a)=f(b) } $$

whenever $f$ is defined at the relevant inputs.

Addition by $c$, multiplication by $c$, squaring, taking sine, and many other operations are examples of applying the same function to equal quantities.

Applying a Function Preserves Truth of Equality

If:

$$ a=b, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a^2=b^2 } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \sin a=\sin b }. $$

But the Converse Need Not Hold

From:

$$ a^2=b^2, $$

one cannot conclude:

$$ a=b $$

because:

$$ \boxed{ a=\pm b } $$

may occur.

For example:

$$ (-3)^2=3^2 $$

even though:

$$ -3\neq3. $$

Reversible vs. Nonreversible Transformations

A transformation is especially useful in equation solving when it preserves equality in both directions.

For example, if $c\neq0$:

$$ \boxed{ a=b \iff ac=bc }. $$

Multiplication by nonzero $c$ is reversible by division by $c$.

But:

$$ a=b \Longrightarrow a^2=b^2 $$

is not reversible without additional restrictions.

Equation Solving as Preservation of Equality

Consider:

$$ x+5=12. $$

Subtracting $5$ from both sides gives:

$$ x+5-5=12-5, $$

hence:

$$ \boxed{x=7}. $$

The step is justified by the subtraction property of equality.

Solving a Multiplicative Equation

For:

$$ 4x=20, $$

divide both sides by $4$:

$$ \frac{4x}{4} = \frac{20}{4}, $$

giving:

$$ \boxed{x=5}. $$

Same Operation on Both Sides

The common classroom phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"Do the same thing to both sides"} } $$

summarizes an important principle but should be interpreted carefully. The operation must be defined, and if the goal is an equivalent equation, the transformation should also be reversible on the relevant domain.

Adding Different Representations of the Same Quantity

It is not necessary that the added expressions look identical. If:

$$ c=d, $$

then from $a=b$ one also obtains:

$$ \boxed{ a+c=b+d }. $$

because equal quantities may replace one another.

Cancellation and Equality

If:

$$ a+c=b+c, $$

then subtracting $c$ from both sides gives:

$$ \boxed{a=b}. $$

This is sometimes called an additive cancellation property.

Multiplicative Cancellation

If:

$$ ac=bc $$

and:

$$ c\neq0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{a=b}. $$

The nonzero condition is essential.

Why Zero Cannot Be Canceled

From:

$$ 3(0)=7(0), $$

we obtain the true statement:

$$ 0=0, $$

but we cannot conclude:

$$ 3=7. $$

Multiplication by zero destroys the information required for reversal.

Equality and Identity

An equation may be true for particular values, while an identity is true for every value in its stated domain.

For example:

$$ x+2=5 $$

is true only when:

$$ x=3, $$

whereas:

$$ \boxed{ (x+1)^2=x^2+2x+1 } $$

is an identity over the usual real or complex domain.

Equality vs. Equivalence

Equality and equivalence are related but not always identical concepts. Equality usually means the same mathematical object or value, while an equivalence relation may identify distinct objects as equivalent under a chosen criterion.

For example:

$$ 1\equiv4\pmod3 $$

expresses congruence modulo $3$, not literal equality:

$$ 1\neq4. $$

Equality as an Equivalence Relation

Equality itself satisfies the three defining properties of an equivalence relation:

$$ \boxed{ \text{reflexive, symmetric, transitive} }. $$

Substitution in Proof

If a proof establishes:

$$ a=b, $$

then $a$ may be replaced by $b$ in later expressions whenever the replacement is legitimate.

This makes equality fundamental to chains of mathematical reasoning.

Equality in Functions

If two functions are equal:

$$ f=g, $$

then, on their common stated domain:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=g(x) \text{ for every relevant }x }. $$

Equality of Ordered Pairs

$$ \boxed{ (a,b)=(c,d) \iff a=c \text{ and } b=d }. $$

Equality of Vectors

Two coordinate vectors are equal exactly when corresponding components are equal:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{u}=\mathbf{v} \iff u_i=v_i \text{ for every }i }. $$

Equality of Matrices

Two matrices are equal exactly when they have the same dimensions and all corresponding entries are equal:

$$ \boxed{ A=B \iff a_{ij}=b_{ij} \text{ for all }i,j }. $$

Equality of Sets

Two sets are equal exactly when they contain precisely the same elements:

$$ \boxed{ A=B \iff \forall x\, (x\in A\iff x\in B) }. $$

Applying Square Roots

If:

$$ a=b $$

and both sides lie in the real domain of the principal square root, then:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt a=\sqrt b }. $$

Domain conditions must be respected.

Taking Reciprocals

If:

$$ a=b $$

and:

$$ a\neq0,\qquad b\neq0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac1a=\frac1b }. $$

Taking Logarithms

If:

$$ a=b>0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \ln a=\ln b }. $$

Positivity is required in the ordinary real logarithmic setting.

Injective Functions and Reversibility

If $f$ is injective, then:

$$ \boxed{ f(a)=f(b) \Longrightarrow a=b }. $$

Thus applying an injective function preserves equality in both directions.

Noninjective Functions

If $f$ is not injective, then:

$$ f(a)=f(b) $$

need not imply:

$$ a=b. $$

Squaring on $\mathbb{R}$ is a standard example.

Extraneous Solutions

Operations that preserve an implication but not an equivalence can create additional candidate solutions.

For example, from:

$$ x=2 $$

squaring gives:

$$ x^2=4, $$

but the latter equation also has:

$$ x=-2. $$

Therefore squaring is not generally an equivalence-preserving operation unless additional restrictions are imposed.

Common Misconception: The properties of equality do not mean that any operation performed on both sides automatically produces an equivalent equation. A valid operation preserves a true equality when it is defined, but reversibility may fail. Squaring, multiplying by zero, and other noninjective transformations can lose information or introduce extra candidate solutions.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$a=a$ "A equals itself; reflexive property of equality."
$a=b\Rightarrow b=a$ "If a equals b, then b equals a."
$a=b,\ b=c\Rightarrow a=c$ "If a equals b and b equals c, then a equals c."
$a=b\Rightarrow a+c=b+c$ "Add the same quantity to both sides."
$a=b\Rightarrow ac=bc$ "Multiply both sides by the same quantity."
$a=b,\ c\neq0\Rightarrow a/c=b/c$ "Divide both sides by the same nonzero quantity."
$a=b\Rightarrow f(a)=f(b)$ "Equal quantities have equal images under the same function."

Classroom phrases such as:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"do the same thing to both sides"} } $$

are shorthand for applying an equality-preserving operation.

More precise statements include:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"subtract 5 from both sides"} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{"divide both sides by the nonzero coefficient 3"} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{"substitute equals for equals"} }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Use:

$$ \boxed{\Longleftrightarrow} $$

only when a transformation is reversible and the two statements are genuinely equivalent. Use:

$$ \boxed{\Longrightarrow} $$

when only one direction has been justified. This distinction is especially important when squaring, taking roots, clearing variable denominators, or applying other potentially nonreversible transformations.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Equality Symbol: $$ \boxed{=} $$ states equality of the two expressions or objects.
  • Reflexive Property: $$ \boxed{a=a}. $$
  • Symmetric Property: $$ \boxed{ a=b\Rightarrow b=a }. $$
  • Transitive Property: $$ \boxed{ a=b,\ b=c\Rightarrow a=c }. $$
  • Substitution Property: Equal quantities may replace one another in legitimate expressions.
  • General Substitution Form: $$ \boxed{ a=b\Rightarrow f(a)=f(b) } $$ whenever $f$ is defined.
  • Addition Property: $$ \boxed{ a=b\Rightarrow a+c=b+c }. $$
  • Subtraction Property: $$ \boxed{ a=b\Rightarrow a-c=b-c }. $$
  • Multiplication Property: $$ \boxed{ a=b\Rightarrow ac=bc }. $$
  • Division Property: For $c\neq0$: $$ \boxed{ a=b\Rightarrow\frac ac=\frac bc }. $$
  • Additive Cancellation: $$ \boxed{ a+c=b+c\Rightarrow a=b }. $$
  • Multiplicative Cancellation: For $c\neq0$: $$ \boxed{ ac=bc\Rightarrow a=b }. $$
  • Zero-Factor Caution: Multiplicative cancellation is invalid when the common factor is zero.
  • Equation Solving: Addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division properties justify standard isolation of variables.
  • Balance Interpretation: An equation is often pictured as a balance: equal changes made to both sides preserve the balance.
  • Balance Caution: The balance metaphor is useful but does not replace domain restrictions or reversibility analysis.
  • Substitution Example: If $x=5$, then: $$ x+3=8. $$
  • Squaring: $$ a=b\Rightarrow a^2=b^2. $$
  • Squaring Converse: $$ a^2=b^2 $$ does not generally imply $a=b$.
  • Square-Root Operation: Equal nonnegative real quantities have equal principal square roots.
  • Reciprocal Operation: Equal nonzero quantities have equal reciprocals.
  • Logarithms: Equal positive quantities have equal real logarithms.
  • Exponentials: If $a=b$, then: $$ e^a=e^b. $$
  • Injective Exponential: Since $e^x$ is injective on $\mathbb{R}$: $$ \boxed{ e^a=e^b\Rightarrow a=b }. $$
  • Natural Logarithm: Since $\ln x$ is injective on $(0,\infty)$: $$ \boxed{ \ln a=\ln b\Rightarrow a=b } $$ for positive $a,b$.
  • Trigonometric Caution: $$ \sin a=\sin b $$ does not generally imply $a=b$ because sine is not injective on $\mathbb{R}$.
  • Absolute Value Caution: $$ |a|=|b| $$ does not generally imply $a=b$.
  • Identity: An identity is an equality true for every value in its stated domain.
  • Conditional Equation: A conditional equation is true only for certain values of its variables.
  • Contradiction: An equation such as: $$ 0=1 $$ signals an impossible condition in ordinary arithmetic.
  • Equivalent Equations: Equations with exactly the same solution set are equivalent.
  • Reversible Step: A reversible equality-preserving step produces an equivalent equation.
  • Nonreversible Step: A one-way transformation may enlarge or otherwise alter the candidate solution set.
  • Extraneous Solutions: Nonreversible transformations can generate candidate values that fail the original equation.
  • Verification: Substitution into the original equation checks whether a candidate actually satisfies the initial equality.
  • Fractions: Clearing denominators requires attention to values that make the original denominator zero.
  • Multiplication by an Expression: Multiplying both sides by an expression that may equal zero can make the reverse step invalid.
  • Division by an Expression: Division requires proving or assuming that the divisor is nonzero.
  • Radical Equations: Squaring both sides may create extraneous solutions and therefore requires verification.
  • Absolute-Value Equations: Equality involving absolute values often represents multiple sign cases.
  • Function Equality: Functions are equal when they have the same relevant domain and agree at every input in that domain.
  • Matrix Equality: Matrices must have the same dimensions and equal corresponding entries.
  • Vector Equality: Corresponding vector components must be equal.
  • Ordered-Pair Equality: $$ \boxed{ (a,b)=(c,d) \iff a=c\text{ and }b=d }. $$
  • Set Equality: $$ \boxed{ A=B \iff \forall x\,(x\in A\iff x\in B) }. $$
  • Complex Numbers: $$ a+bi=c+di $$ implies: $$ \boxed{ a=c,\qquad b=d } $$ for real $a,b,c,d$.
  • Polynomial Equality: Two polynomials over a fixed coefficient system are equal when corresponding coefficients agree after like terms are collected.
  • Coordinate Equality: Equality of coordinate tuples is componentwise.
  • Proof Writing: Transitivity permits chains such as: $$ a=b=c=d. $$
  • Proof Substitution: Established equalities may be substituted into later expressions.
  • Equivalence Relation: Equality is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive.
  • Congruence Contrast: $$ a\equiv b\pmod n $$ expresses modular equivalence, not necessarily literal equality.
  • Approximation Contrast: $$ a\approx b $$ means approximately equal, not exactly equal.
  • Definition Symbol Contrast: Notations such as: $$ := $$ or: $$ \equiv $$ may be used for definitions or identities depending on author convention; they should not automatically be treated as ordinary equality notation.
  • Inequality Contrast: Equality-preserving multiplication by a negative number does not reverse the equality symbol; the reversal phenomenon belongs to inequalities.
  • Negative Multiplication: If: $$ a=b, $$ then: $$ \boxed{-a=-b}. $$
  • Equality Has No Direction: Because of symmetry, the left and right sides of an equality do not have inherent greater or lesser status.
  • Expression Replacement: If two expressions are equal, one may replace the other where the equality is valid.
  • Domain Dependence: Equality involving functions or expressions may be valid only on a stated domain.
  • Example of Domain Dependence: $$ \frac{x^2-1}{x-1}=x+1 $$ is valid for: $$ \boxed{x\neq1}, $$ but not as an unrestricted equality of the two original functions on all real numbers.
  • Logical Role: Equality allows mathematical statements to transfer information from one representation to another.
  • Algebraic Role: Properties of equality justify the transformations used to solve equations.
  • Foundational Role: Reflexivity, symmetry, transitivity, and substitutability make equality one of the central relations of mathematics.
  • Terminological Contrast: The properties of equality include both structural properties of the equality relation and operational rules that preserve equality.
  • Core Interpretation: The properties of equality formalize the principle that equal quantities may be treated identically: $$ \boxed{ a=b \Longrightarrow f(a)=f(b) } $$ whenever $f$ is defined. Reflexivity, symmetry, transitivity, substitution, and the arithmetic properties of equality provide the logical foundation for equation solving, algebraic transformation, and proof.
