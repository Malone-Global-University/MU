Improper Rational Expression

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

An improper rational expression is a rational expression whose numerator polynomial has degree greater than or equal to the degree of its denominator polynomial.

$$ \boxed{ \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} \text{ is improper when } \deg P\geq\deg Q } $$

where $P(x)$ and $Q(x)$ are polynomials and:

$$ \boxed{ Q(x)\neq0 } $$

on the domain of the expression.

The term improper parallels the older language of improper numerical fractions. For example, the numerical fraction:

$$ \frac{7}{3} $$

has numerator larger than denominator, while the rational expression:

$$ \frac{x^3+1}{x+2} $$

has numerator degree greater than denominator degree.

As with improper fractions, the word improper does not mean invalid or incorrect. It simply identifies a form that can be decomposed into a polynomial part plus a proper rational remainder.

$$ \boxed{ \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} = S(x)+\frac{R(x)}{Q(x)} } $$

where:

$$ \boxed{ \deg R<\deg Q }. $$

Operational Nuance

Rational Expression

A rational expression is a quotient of polynomials:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} } $$

with $Q(x)$ not identically zero.

Domain Restriction

Every value satisfying:

$$ Q(x)=0 $$

must be excluded from the domain.

Proper Rational Expression

A rational expression is proper when:

$$ \boxed{ \deg P<\deg Q }. $$

Improper Rational Expression

It is improper when:

$$ \boxed{ \deg P\geq\deg Q }. $$

Example: Greater Numerator Degree

$$ \boxed{ \frac{x^3+2x}{x^2+1} } $$

is improper because:

$$ 3>2. $$

Example: Equal Degrees

$$ \boxed{ \frac{2x^2+3}{x^2-4} } $$

is also improper because:

$$ 2=2. $$

Example: Proper Rational Expression

$$ \boxed{ \frac{x+1}{x^2+4} } $$

is proper because:

$$ 1<2. $$

Degree Comparison Comes Before Division

To classify a rational expression, compare the polynomial degrees before performing division:

$$ \boxed{ \deg P \quad\text{versus}\quad \deg Q }. $$

Polynomial Division

If $\deg P\geq\deg Q$, polynomial division gives:

$$ \boxed{ P(x) = Q(x)S(x)+R(x) } $$

where:

$$ \boxed{ R(x)=0 \quad\text{or}\quad \deg R<\deg Q }. $$

Quotient-Remainder Form

Dividing by $Q(x)$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} = S(x) + \frac{R(x)}{Q(x)} }. $$

The remaining rational term is proper.

Numerical-Fraction Analogy

The structure parallels:

$$ 11=4(2)+3, $$

which gives:

$$ \frac{11}{4} = 2+\frac{3}{4}. $$

Polynomial division gives the analogous decomposition:

$$ \boxed{ \text{improper rational expression} = \text{polynomial} + \text{proper rational expression} }. $$

Example of Polynomial Division

Consider:

$$ \frac{x^2+3x+5}{x+1}. $$

Polynomial division gives:

$$ x^2+3x+5 = (x+1)(x+2)+3. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{x^2+3x+5}{x+1} = x+2+\frac{3}{x+1} }. $$

Checking the Division

Multiply back:

$$ (x+1)(x+2)+3 = x^2+3x+5. $$

This verifies the decomposition.

Exact Division

Sometimes the remainder is zero.

For example:

$$ \frac{x^2-1}{x-1}. $$

Since:

$$ x^2-1=(x-1)(x+1), $$

one obtains:

$$ \frac{x^2-1}{x-1} = x+1 $$

for:

$$ \boxed{ x\neq1 }. $$

Domain Restriction Survives Simplification

Although the simplified algebraic expression is $x+1$, the original rational expression is undefined at $x=1$.

Removable Discontinuity

The missing input $x=1$ produces a hole in the graph of the original rational function.

Improper Classification after Cancellation

Classification may be discussed either before or after algebraic simplification, depending on textbook convention. For analytic behavior, simplification and domain restrictions should both be preserved.

Equal-Degree Case

If:

$$ \deg P=\deg Q, $$

the quotient from polynomial division is a nonzero constant.

Example

$$ \frac{2x^2+1}{x^2-3} $$

can be rewritten:

$$ 2x^2+1 = 2(x^2-3)+7. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{2x^2+1}{x^2-3} = 2+\frac{7}{x^2-3} }. $$

Horizontal Asymptote in Equal-Degree Case

Since the proper remainder tends to zero for large $|x|$, the graph approaches:

$$ \boxed{ y=2 }. $$

Leading-Coefficient Rule

More generally, if numerator and denominator have equal degree:

$$ \boxed{ y= \frac{\text{leading coefficient of }P} {\text{leading coefficient of }Q} } $$

is the horizontal asymptote, provided the function is considered in its usual rational-function setting.

Numerator Degree One Higher

If:

$$ \deg P=\deg Q+1, $$

polynomial division produces a linear quotient.

Slant Asymptote

In that case, the quotient line often gives a slant or oblique asymptote.

Example

Consider:

$$ \frac{x^2+1}{x-1}. $$

Division gives:

$$ x^2+1 = (x-1)(x+1)+2. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{x^2+1}{x-1} = x+1+\frac{2}{x-1} }. $$

As $|x|$ becomes large:

$$ \frac{2}{x-1}\to0, $$

so the graph approaches:

$$ \boxed{ y=x+1 }. $$

Polynomial Asymptote

If the numerator degree exceeds the denominator degree by more than one, the polynomial quotient may have degree greater than one.

Then the rational function may approach a polynomial curve:

$$ \boxed{ y=S(x) } $$

because the proper remainder tends to zero under the usual degree conditions.

Why Division Helps Graphing

The form:

$$ S(x)+\frac{R(x)}{Q(x)} $$

separates large-scale polynomial behavior from the smaller rational correction.

Vertical Asymptotes

Vertical asymptotes arise from uncancelled zeros of the denominator.

If:

$$ Q(a)=0 $$

and the corresponding factor does not cancel completely, then $x=a$ may be a vertical asymptote.

Cancellation before Asymptote Classification

A cancelled denominator factor usually produces a removable discontinuity rather than a vertical asymptote.

Example

$$ \frac{(x-2)(x+3)}{x-2} $$

simplifies to:

$$ x+3 $$

but retains the restriction:

$$ \boxed{ x\neq2 }. $$

The graph has a hole at $x=2$, not a vertical asymptote there.

Partial Fraction Decomposition

Before applying ordinary partial-fraction decomposition, an improper rational expression is usually divided first.

$$ \boxed{ \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} = S(x)+\frac{R(x)}{Q(x)} } $$

with $\deg R<\deg Q$.

Why Division Comes First

Standard partial-fraction decomposition is formulated for the proper rational part, not for an unreduced improper quotient.

Partial Fraction Example Structure

After polynomial division, one might obtain:

$$ x+2+\frac{3x+1}{(x-1)(x+4)}. $$

The proper part can then be decomposed:

$$ \frac{3x+1}{(x-1)(x+4)} = \frac{A}{x-1} + \frac{B}{x+4}. $$

Integration

Polynomial division is often an important first step when integrating an improper rational function.

For example:

$$ \int \frac{x^2+1}{x-1}\,dx $$

becomes:

$$ \int \left( x+1+\frac{2}{x-1} \right)\,dx. $$

Result of the Integration Example

$$ \boxed{ \frac{x^2}{2} +x +2\ln|x-1| +C }. $$

Long Division

Polynomial long division works for any polynomial denominator of positive degree.

Synthetic Division

Synthetic division provides a shortcut when dividing by a linear divisor of the form:

$$ \boxed{ x-c }. $$

Missing Polynomial Terms

When using long or synthetic division, omitted powers should be represented by zero coefficients.

For example:

$$ x^3+2x-1 = x^3+0x^2+2x-1. $$

Degree of Zero Polynomial Caveat

Degree comparisons assume ordinary nonzero numerator and denominator polynomials. The zero polynomial requires separate convention because its degree is not treated uniformly across all elementary texts.

Constant Denominator

If $Q(x)$ is a nonzero constant, then:

$$ \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} $$

is simply a constant multiple of a polynomial and is improper whenever $P$ is nonzero under the usual degree convention.

Constant Numerator

A nonzero constant numerator over a positive-degree denominator is proper:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{c}{Q(x)} } $$

because:

$$ 0<\deg Q. $$

Improper Form Does Not Determine Simplification

A rational expression can be improper and irreducible, improper and reducible, or exactly divisible.

Improper and Reducible

$$ \frac{x^2-1}{x-1} $$

is improper and reducible.

Improper and Irreducible

$$ \boxed{ \frac{x^2+1}{x-1} } $$

is improper but has no common polynomial factor to cancel over the real polynomial ring.

Proper Does Not Mean Simplified

The expression:

$$ \frac{x^2-1}{x^3-x^2-x+1} $$

may be proper by degree while still possessing common factors that should be simplified.

Classification and Reduction Are Different Questions

$$ \boxed{ \text{proper/improper} \neq \text{reduced/not reduced} }. $$

Rational Function

A rational function is a function represented by a rational expression together with an appropriate domain.

Improper rational expressions therefore commonly define rational functions with polynomial-like end behavior.

End Behavior

Polynomial division makes end behavior transparent:

$$ \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} = S(x)+\frac{R(x)}{Q(x)}. $$

When:

$$ \frac{R(x)}{Q(x)}\to0 $$

as $|x|\to\infty$, the rational function behaves increasingly like $S(x)$.

Degree Difference

The quantity:

$$ \boxed{ \deg P-\deg Q } $$

helps predict the degree of the polynomial quotient.

Equal Degrees

Degree difference $0$ gives a constant quotient.

Difference of One

Degree difference $1$ gives a linear quotient.

Difference of Two

Degree difference $2$ generally gives a quadratic quotient.

Equation Solving

Rational equations containing improper rational expressions can often be simplified by polynomial division, although clearing denominators may also be useful.

Domain Must Be Recorded before Clearing Denominators

If:

$$ Q(x)=0, $$

those values are excluded even if later algebraic manipulation removes the denominator.

Extraneous Candidates

Candidate solutions obtained after clearing denominators must be checked against the original domain restrictions.

Improper Rational Expressions in Sequences

Rational expressions in an index $n$ may likewise have numerator degree at least denominator degree and can often be decomposed by polynomial division to clarify asymptotic behavior.

Asymptotic Simplification

For large $|x|$, leading-degree terms dominate:

$$ \frac{a_mx^m+\cdots}{b_nx^n+\cdots} $$

and polynomial division gives a more precise version of this comparison when $m\geq n$.

Common Error: Calling Improper Rational Expressions Invalid

Improper rational expressions are legitimate algebraic expressions. The label merely indicates that polynomial division is available.

Common Error: Comparing Coefficient Sizes Instead of Degrees

Proper or improper classification depends on polynomial degree, not on whether the numerator's coefficients are numerically larger.

Common Error: Comparing Number of Terms

A polynomial with fewer written terms can have higher degree than one with more terms. Classification depends on the highest nonzero power.

Common Error: Treating Equal Degrees as Proper

Equal degrees are classified as improper under the standard convention:

$$ \boxed{ \deg P=\deg Q \Longrightarrow \text{improper} }. $$

Common Error: Skipping Polynomial Division before Partial Fractions

Standard partial-fraction decomposition should be applied to the proper remainder after division.

Common Error: Forgetting the Remainder

Polynomial division gives:

$$ \frac{P}{Q} = S+\frac{R}{Q}, $$

not merely $P/Q=S$ unless $R=0$.

Common Error: Forgetting Original Domain Restrictions

Cancellation or division may simplify the formula without restoring values that were excluded from the original denominator.

Common Error: Confusing a Hole with a Vertical Asymptote

Cancelled denominator factors normally create removable discontinuities; uncancelled denominator zeros may create vertical asymptotes.

Common Error: Assuming Numerator Degree Determines All End Behavior

End behavior depends on the relationship between numerator and denominator degrees and their leading coefficients.

Common Misconception: An improper rational expression is not a malformed rational expression. It is simply one whose numerator degree is at least as large as its denominator degree. Polynomial division converts it into a polynomial quotient plus a proper rational remainder, often revealing its algebraic structure, asymptotes, and end behavior much more clearly.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}$ "P of x divided by Q of x; a rational expression."
$\deg P\geq\deg Q$ "The numerator degree is greater than or equal to the denominator degree, so the rational expression is improper."
$P=QS+R$ "P equals Q times the quotient S plus remainder R."
$\frac{P}{Q}=S+\frac{R}{Q}$ "The improper rational expression equals a polynomial quotient plus a proper rational remainder."
$\deg R<\deg Q$ "The remainder has lower degree than the divisor."
$\frac{x^2+1}{x-1}=x+1+\frac{2}{x-1}$ "Polynomial division gives x plus one plus two over x minus one."
$x\neq1$ "X equals one is excluded from the domain."

The phrase improper rational expression should not be confused with improper fraction, although the terminology is analogous. Numerical fractions compare numerator and denominator magnitudes, whereas rational expressions compare polynomial degrees.

The terms polynomial division, polynomial long division, and quotient-remainder decomposition commonly appear when rewriting improper rational expressions.

Defense Notation Advice: Compare polynomial degrees, not coefficient sizes or numbers of terms. Record denominator restrictions before simplifying. When $\deg P\geq\deg Q$, divide first and preserve the remainder: $P/Q=S+R/Q$. Apply partial fractions only to the resulting proper rational part, and distinguish cancelled denominator factors from uncancelled asymptotic factors.
