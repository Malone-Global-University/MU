Modular Equivalence Rules

Subfield: Foundations & Logic

Etymology & Roots

Modular equivalence rules are the operational laws governing congruence modulo a fixed positive integer. They describe which ordinary arithmetic transformations preserve modular equivalence and which require additional conditions.

The foundational relation is:

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv b\pmod n \iff n\mid(a-b) }. $$

Once two integers are known to be congruent modulo $n$, they can often replace one another inside addition, subtraction, multiplication, integer powers, and integer-coefficient polynomial expressions.

The phrase equivalence rules emphasizes that congruence is not merely a remainder shortcut. It is an equivalence relation compatible with important algebraic operations.

The word modular comes from modulus, the fixed number determining the equivalence classes. The word equivalence reflects the fact that congruence modulo $n$ is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive.

$$ \boxed{ \text{congruence} + \text{operation compatibility} = \text{modular equivalence rules} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Foundational Definition

For integers $a,b$ and positive integer $n$:

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv b\pmod n \iff a-b=kn \text{ for some }k\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Replacement Principle

If:

$$ a\equiv b\pmod n, $$

then $a$ may be replaced by $b$ inside many arithmetic expressions when working modulo $n$.

Reflexive Rule

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv a\pmod n }. $$

Every integer is congruent to itself because:

$$ a-a=0 $$

and $n\mid0$.

Symmetric Rule

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv b\pmod n \Longrightarrow b\equiv a\pmod n }. $$

Transitive Rule

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv b\pmod n \text{ and } b\equiv c\pmod n \Longrightarrow a\equiv c\pmod n }. $$

Addition Rule

If:

$$ a\equiv b\pmod n $$

and:

$$ c\equiv d\pmod n, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a+c\equiv b+d\pmod n }. $$

Why Addition Works

If:

$$ a-b=kn $$

and:

$$ c-d=\ell n, $$

then:

$$ (a+c)-(b+d) = (k+\ell)n. $$

Add the Same Quantity Rule

A common special case is:

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv b\pmod n \Longrightarrow a+c\equiv b+c\pmod n }. $$

Subtraction Rule

$$ \boxed{ a-c\equiv b-d\pmod n } $$

whenever:

$$ a\equiv b\pmod n $$

and:

$$ c\equiv d\pmod n. $$

Subtract the Same Quantity Rule

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv b\pmod n \Longrightarrow a-c\equiv b-c\pmod n }. $$

Negation Rule

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv b\pmod n \Longrightarrow -a\equiv-b\pmod n }. $$

Multiplication Rule

If:

$$ a\equiv b\pmod n $$

and:

$$ c\equiv d\pmod n, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ ac\equiv bd\pmod n }. $$

Multiply by the Same Integer Rule

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv b\pmod n \Longrightarrow ac\equiv bc\pmod n }. $$

Why Multiplication Works

If $a=b+kn$, then:

$$ ac = bc+kcn, $$

so:

$$ ac-bc=kcn $$

is divisible by $n$.

Power Rule

For every nonnegative integer $r$:

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv b\pmod n \Longrightarrow a^r\equiv b^r\pmod n }. $$

Repeated Multiplication Interpretation

The power rule follows by repeatedly applying the multiplication rule.

Polynomial Rule

If $P(x)$ has integer coefficients, then:

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv b\pmod n \Longrightarrow P(a)\equiv P(b)\pmod n }. $$

Example

If:

$$ x\equiv2\pmod5, $$

then:

$$ x^3+4x+1 \equiv 2^3+4(2)+1 = 17 \equiv \boxed{2} \pmod5. $$

Reduction Rule

Any integer can be replaced by a congruent residue:

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv a\bmod n\pmod n } $$

under the usual least-nonnegative-remainder convention.

Reduce Before Adding

$$ \boxed{ (a+b)\bmod n = ((a\bmod n)+(b\bmod n))\bmod n }. $$

Reduce Before Multiplying

$$ \boxed{ (ab)\bmod n = ((a\bmod n)(b\bmod n))\bmod n }. $$

Reduce Before Exponentiation

If:

$$ a\equiv r\pmod n, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a^k\equiv r^k\pmod n }. $$

Example of Early Reduction

Compute:

$$ 98^3\pmod7. $$

Since:

$$ 98\equiv0\pmod7, $$

immediately:

$$ \boxed{ 98^3\equiv0\pmod7 }. $$

Equivalent Representatives Rule

If:

$$ a\equiv b\pmod n, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ [a]_n=[b]_n }. $$

Class Addition Rule

$$ \boxed{ [a]_n+[b]_n = [a+b]_n }. $$

Class Multiplication Rule

$$ \boxed{ [a]_n[b]_n = [ab]_n }. $$

Well-Definedness Rule

If:

$$ a\equiv a'\pmod n $$

and:

$$ b\equiv b'\pmod n, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a+b\equiv a'+b'\pmod n } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ ab\equiv a'b'\pmod n }. $$

This ensures that arithmetic on residue classes does not depend on the chosen representatives.

Equality of Classes Rule

$$ \boxed{ [a]_n=[b]_n \iff a\equiv b\pmod n }. $$

Congruent-to-Zero Rule

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv0\pmod n \iff n\mid a }. $$

Congruent-to-One Rule

The statement:

$$ a\equiv1\pmod n $$

means:

$$ \boxed{ n\mid(a-1) }. $$

Parity Rules

Modulo $2$:

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv0\pmod2 \iff a\text{ is even} } $$
$$ \boxed{ a\equiv1\pmod2 \iff a\text{ is odd} }. $$

Even-Plus-Even Rule

$$ 0+0\equiv0\pmod2. $$

Odd-Plus-Odd Rule

$$ 1+1\equiv0\pmod2. $$

Odd-Times-Odd Rule

$$ 1\cdot1\equiv1\pmod2. $$

Change-of-Modulus Rule: Passing to a Divisor

If:

$$ a\equiv b\pmod n $$

and:

$$ d\mid n, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv b\pmod d }. $$

Reason

Since $n\mid(a-b)$ and $d\mid n$, the difference $a-b$ is also divisible by $d$.

Converse Warning

Congruence modulo a divisor does not generally imply congruence modulo the larger modulus.

For example:

$$ 8\equiv2\pmod3 $$

but:

$$ 8\not\equiv2\pmod6. $$

Combining Moduli

If:

$$ a\equiv b\pmod m $$

and:

$$ a\equiv b\pmod n, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv b \pmod{\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)} }. $$

Why Least Common Multiple Appears

The difference $a-b$ is divisible by both $m$ and $n$, so it is divisible by their least common multiple.

Coprime Special Case

If:

$$ \gcd(m,n)=1, $$

then:

$$ \operatorname{lcm}(m,n)=mn. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv b\pmod m \text{ and } a\equiv b\pmod n \Longrightarrow a\equiv b\pmod{mn} }. $$

Cancellation Is Not Automatic

From:

$$ ac\equiv bc\pmod n, $$

it does not always follow that:

$$ a\equiv b\pmod n. $$

Cancellation Counterexample

Modulo $6$:

$$ 2\cdot1 \equiv 2\cdot4 \pmod6 $$

because:

$$ 2\equiv8\pmod6. $$

But:

$$ \boxed{ 1\not\equiv4\pmod6 }. $$

Safe Cancellation Rule

If:

$$ ac\equiv bc\pmod n $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(c,n)=1 }, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv b\pmod n }. $$

Why Coprimality Allows Cancellation

If $\gcd(c,n)=1$, then $c$ has a multiplicative inverse modulo $n$. Multiplying both sides by that inverse removes $c$.

General Cancellation Rule

If:

$$ ac\equiv bc\pmod n $$

and:

$$ d=\gcd(c,n), $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv b \pmod{\frac{n}{d}} }. $$

Division Rule Requires an Inverse

Modular arithmetic has no unrestricted division rule. To divide by $c$ modulo $n$, the class of $c$ must be invertible.

Inverse Criterion

$$ \boxed{ c^{-1}\pmod n \text{ exists} \iff \gcd(c,n)=1 }. $$

Inverse Definition

$$ \boxed{ cc^{-1}\equiv1\pmod n }. $$

Division by an Invertible Factor

If:

$$ cx\equiv b\pmod n $$

and $\gcd(c,n)=1$, then:

$$ \boxed{ x\equiv c^{-1}b\pmod n }. $$

Example

Solve:

$$ 3x\equiv5\pmod7. $$

Since:

$$ 3^{-1}\equiv5\pmod7, $$

multiply both sides by $5$:

$$ x \equiv 25 \equiv \boxed{4} \pmod7. $$

Linear Congruence Solvability Rule

For:

$$ ax\equiv b\pmod n, $$

let:

$$ d=\gcd(a,n). $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ ax\equiv b\pmod n \text{ is solvable} \iff d\mid b }. $$

Number of Solutions Rule

If $d=\gcd(a,n)$ divides $b$, then the linear congruence has exactly $d$ distinct solution classes modulo $n$.

Unique-Solution Rule

If:

$$ \gcd(a,n)=1, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ ax\equiv b\pmod n } $$

has exactly one solution class modulo $n$.

Prime-Modulus Cancellation

If $p$ is prime and:

$$ c\not\equiv0\pmod p, $$

then $c$ is invertible modulo $p$, so:

$$ \boxed{ ac\equiv bc\pmod p \Longrightarrow a\equiv b\pmod p }. $$

Zero-Product Rule under Prime Modulus

If $p$ is prime:

$$ \boxed{ ab\equiv0\pmod p \Longrightarrow a\equiv0\pmod p \text{ or } b\equiv0\pmod p }. $$

Composite-Modulus Warning

The preceding zero-product implication can fail for composite moduli.

Modulo $6$:

$$ 2\cdot3 \equiv0\pmod6, $$

even though neither factor is congruent to $0$ modulo $6$.

Exponent Reduction via Cycles

Modular powers often repeat. If:

$$ a^r\equiv1\pmod n, $$

then exponents may often be reduced modulo the cycle length in expressions involving powers of $a$.

Euler's Theorem Rule

If:

$$ \gcd(a,n)=1, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a^{\varphi(n)} \equiv1\pmod n }. $$

Fermat's Little Theorem Rule

If $p$ is prime and $p\nmid a$, then:

$$ \boxed{ a^{p-1} \equiv1\pmod p }. $$

Universal Fermat Form

For every integer $a$ and prime $p$:

$$ \boxed{ a^p\equiv a\pmod p }. $$

Congruence of Products

A useful chained form is:

$$ a_1\equiv b_1,\ldots,a_r\equiv b_r\pmod n $$

implies:

$$ \boxed{ a_1a_2\cdots a_r \equiv b_1b_2\cdots b_r \pmod n }. $$

Congruence of Sums

Likewise:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_i a_i \equiv \sum_i b_i \pmod n } $$

whenever each $a_i\equiv b_i\pmod n$.

Integer Linear Combination Rule

If:

$$ a\equiv b\pmod n $$

and $r,s\in\mathbb{Z}$, then:

$$ \boxed{ ra+s \equiv rb+s \pmod n }. $$

Two-Congruence Linear Combination Rule

If:

$$ a\equiv b\pmod n $$

and:

$$ c\equiv d\pmod n, $$

then for integers $r,s$:

$$ \boxed{ ra+sc \equiv rb+sd \pmod n }. $$

Substitution into Identities

Since integer polynomial expressions preserve congruence, standard algebraic identities may be used modulo $n$.

For example:

$$ (a+b)^2 = a^2+2ab+b^2 $$

remains valid before reduction modulo $n$.

Modular Equality Chains

Calculations commonly use chains such as:

$$ 7^3 \equiv 2^3 \equiv 8 \equiv \boxed{3} \pmod5. $$

All Links Must Use the Same Modulus

A congruence chain is only meaningful when each comparison is made under the stated modulus unless a modulus change is explicitly announced.

Solving by Replacement

A complicated coefficient can be replaced by a simpler congruent one:

$$ 17x\equiv4\pmod5 $$

becomes:

$$ \boxed{ 2x\equiv4\pmod5 }. $$

Then Use an Inverse

Since:

$$ 2^{-1}\equiv3\pmod5, $$

one gets:

$$ x \equiv 12 \equiv \boxed{2} \pmod5. $$

Congruence and Divisibility Rules

Divisibility statements can be translated directly:

$$ \boxed{ n\mid a \iff a\equiv0\pmod n }. $$

Divisibility of a Difference

$$ \boxed{ n\mid(a-b) \iff a\equiv b\pmod n }. $$

Digit-Sum Rule Modulo $9$

Since:

$$ 10\equiv1\pmod9, $$

every decimal integer is congruent modulo $9$ to the sum of its digits.

Digit-Sum Rule Modulo $3$

Likewise:

$$ \boxed{ 10\equiv1\pmod3 }. $$

Alternating-Digit Rule Modulo $11$

Since:

$$ \boxed{ 10\equiv-1\pmod{11} }, $$

decimal place values alternate between $1$ and $-1$ modulo $11$.

Square-Residue Rule Modulo $4$

$$ \boxed{ x^2\equiv0 \text{ or } 1 \pmod4 }. $$

Odd-Square Rule Modulo $8$

$$ \boxed{ x\text{ odd} \Longrightarrow x^2\equiv1\pmod8 }. $$

Congruence Preservation through Functions

Not every arbitrary function preserves modular equivalence. The safe general elementary rule applies to functions built from integer addition and multiplication, such as integer-coefficient polynomials.

Division Is the Main Danger

Addition, subtraction, and multiplication are always compatible with congruence. Division is conditional because not every residue class has a multiplicative inverse.

Square Roots Are Not Automatically Reversible

From:

$$ a^2\equiv b^2\pmod n $$

one cannot generally conclude:

$$ a\equiv b\pmod n. $$

Example

Modulo $5$:

$$ 2^2 \equiv 3^2 \equiv 4 \pmod5, $$

but:

$$ 2\not\equiv3\pmod5. $$

Exponentiation Is Forward-Safe but Not Generally Reversible

Congruent numbers have congruent powers, but congruent powers do not necessarily imply congruent bases.

Multiplication Is Forward-Safe but Cancellation Is Conditional

Multiplying both sides by the same integer always preserves congruence. Reversing that operation requires an invertibility condition.

Addition and Subtraction Are Fully Reversible

Since additive inverses always exist modulo $n$, adding or subtracting the same quantity can always be reversed.

Modular Equation Analogy

Congruences resemble equations, but their transformation rules are those of a quotient ring rather than unrestricted ordinary rational arithmetic.

Common Error: Using Equality Instead of Congruence

Incorrect:

$$ 17=2\pmod5. $$

Preferred:

$$ \boxed{ 17\equiv2\pmod5 }. $$

Common Error: Treating $\equiv$ as Approximation

Modular congruence is an exact divisibility relation, not an approximation symbol.

Common Error: Changing the Modulus without Justification

A congruence statement is tied to its modulus. Passing to another modulus requires a valid divisibility relationship or theorem.

Common Error: Cancelling Any Common Factor

Cancellation modulo $n$ is only unrestricted for factors relatively prime to $n$.

Common Error: Dividing as in Ordinary Algebra

Division means multiplication by a modular inverse, and that inverse may not exist.

Common Error: Assuming Every Nonzero Class Is Invertible

This is true modulo a prime but false in general modulo a composite integer.

Common Error: Assuming Products Can Be Cancelled from Zero

For composite moduli:

$$ ab\equiv0\pmod n $$

need not force either factor to be equivalent to zero.

Common Error: Taking Square Roots of Both Sides

Squaring is not generally injective modulo $n$, so "taking square roots" can lose or multiply solution possibilities.

Common Error: Reversing the Power Rule

From $a\equiv b$ one may infer $a^k\equiv b^k$, but the converse can fail.

Common Error: Confusing Residue Reduction with Equality

Replacing $17$ by $2$ modulo $5$ is valid within the congruence context, not as an ordinary equality of integers.

Common Error: Forgetting Negative Representatives

Modulo $7$:

$$ \boxed{ 6\equiv-1\pmod7 }. $$

The negative representative may simplify powers and products.

Common Error: Assuming Reduction Must Wait until the End

Congruence rules allow reduction throughout addition, multiplication, and exponentiation.

Common Error: Treating Representative Choice as Meaningful

Different representatives of the same residue class describe the same modular object.

Common Error: Confusing a Congruence Rule with a Remainder Formula

Congruence rules concern equivalence classes; a remainder operation chooses a representative.

Common Misconception: Modular equivalence rules are not identical to ordinary equation rules. Addition, subtraction, and multiplication preserve congruence freely, and integer powers preserve it in the forward direction. Division and cancellation, however, require special care because some nonzero residue classes have no multiplicative inverse. The safest question is not simply "Can I do this to both sides?" but "Does this operation preserve or reverse congruence under the current modulus?"

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$a\equiv b\pmod n$ "A is congruent to b modulo n."
$a+c\equiv b+c\pmod n$ "Adding the same integer preserves congruence modulo n."
$ac\equiv bc\pmod n$ "Multiplying by the same integer preserves congruence modulo n."
$a^k\equiv b^k\pmod n$ "Congruent integers have congruent k-th powers modulo n."
$\gcd(c,n)=1$ "C is relatively prime to n," allowing cancellation of c modulo n.
$c^{-1}\pmod n$ "The multiplicative inverse of c modulo n."
$a\equiv b\pmod n\Rightarrow P(a)\equiv P(b)\pmod n$ "Integer-coefficient polynomial substitution preserves congruence."

The most important linguistic distinction is between a rule that always preserves congruence and a rule that requires a condition.

Statements such as:

$$ a\equiv b\pmod n \Longrightarrow a+c\equiv b+c\pmod n $$

are unconditional.

By contrast:

$$ ac\equiv bc\pmod n \Longrightarrow a\equiv b\pmod n $$

requires an additional hypothesis such as:

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(c,n)=1 }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Keep the modulus visible throughout a congruence calculation, use $\equiv$ rather than $=$, and distinguish forward rules from reversible rules. Addition and subtraction can always be reversed; multiplication can always be performed but cannot always be cancelled; division requires a modular inverse. When changing the modulus, state the divisibility relationship that justifies the change.

  • Core Interpretation: Modular equivalence rules describe which operations preserve the relation: $$ \boxed{ a\equiv b\pmod n \iff n\mid(a-b) }. $$ Addition, subtraction, and multiplication behave cleanly: $$ \boxed{ a\equiv b,\ c\equiv d \pmod n \Longrightarrow a+c\equiv b+d,\ ac\equiv bd \pmod n }. $$ Integer powers and polynomial substitution also preserve congruence: $$ \boxed{ a\equiv b\pmod n \Longrightarrow P(a)\equiv P(b)\pmod n }. $$ The major restriction concerns reversing multiplication: $$ \boxed{ ac\equiv bc\pmod n \text{ and } \gcd(c,n)=1 \Longrightarrow a\equiv b\pmod n }. $$ Thus modular arithmetic resembles ordinary algebra in many forward operations, but cancellation and division depend on invertibility within the chosen modulus.
