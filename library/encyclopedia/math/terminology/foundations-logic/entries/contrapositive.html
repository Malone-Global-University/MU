Contrapositive

Subfield: Foundations & Mathematical Logic

Etymology & Roots

The contrapositive of a conditional statement is formed by reversing the order of the hypothesis and conclusion and negating both.

Starting with:

$$ P\rightarrow Q, $$

the contrapositive is:

$$ \boxed{ \neg Q\rightarrow\neg P }. $$

In words:

$$ \boxed{ \text{If }P\text{, then }Q \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad \text{If not }Q\text{, then not }P }. $$

The prefix contra- conveys opposition, while positive in the historical linguistic formation relates to the proposition being transformed. Operationally, the important rule is simple: switch and negate.

The contrapositive is especially important because a conditional statement and its contrapositive are logically equivalent.

$$ \boxed{ P\rightarrow Q \equiv \neg Q\rightarrow\neg P }. $$

Operational Nuance

Conditional Statement

A conditional statement has the form:

$$ \boxed{ P\rightarrow Q }. $$

$P$ is the hypothesis or antecedent, and $Q$ is the conclusion or consequent.

Forming the Contrapositive

To form the contrapositive:

  1. reverse the hypothesis and conclusion,
  2. negate the new hypothesis, and
  3. negate the new conclusion.
$$ P\rightarrow Q \quad\longrightarrow\quad \neg Q\rightarrow\neg P. $$

Example

Consider the statement:

“If an integer is divisible by $4$, then it is even.”

Let:

$$ P: \text{the integer is divisible by }4 $$

and:

$$ Q: \text{the integer is even}. $$

The contrapositive is:

“If an integer is not even, then it is not divisible by $4$.”

$$ \boxed{ \neg Q\rightarrow\neg P }. $$

Logical Equivalence

A statement and its contrapositive always have the same truth value.

$$ \boxed{ P\rightarrow Q \equiv \neg Q\rightarrow\neg P }. $$

This equivalence is one of the most useful transformations in mathematical logic and proof.

Truth Table

$P$ $Q$ $P\rightarrow Q$ $\neg Q\rightarrow\neg P$
T T T T
T F F F
F T T T
F F T T

Since the final two columns match in every row, the statements are logically equivalent.

Why the Contrapositive Works

The implication:

$$ P\rightarrow Q $$

fails only in the case where $P$ is true and $Q$ is false.

The contrapositive:

$$ \neg Q\rightarrow\neg P $$

fails under exactly the same assignment: $Q$ false and $P$ true.

Therefore their truth conditions are identical.

Implication as a Disjunction

A conditional is equivalent to:

$$ P\rightarrow Q \equiv \neg P\lor Q. $$

The contrapositive becomes:

$$ \neg Q\rightarrow\neg P \equiv Q\lor\neg P. $$

By commutativity of disjunction:

$$ Q\lor\neg P \equiv \neg P\lor Q. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ P\rightarrow Q \equiv \neg Q\rightarrow\neg P }. $$

Converse

The converse of:

$$ P\rightarrow Q $$

is:

$$ \boxed{ Q\rightarrow P }. $$

The converse reverses the statements but does not negate them.

Inverse

The inverse is:

$$ \boxed{ \neg P\rightarrow\neg Q }. $$

The inverse negates both statements but does not reverse their order.

Four Related Conditionals

Name Form
Conditional $P\rightarrow Q$
Converse $Q\rightarrow P$
Inverse $\neg P\rightarrow\neg Q$
Contrapositive $\neg Q\rightarrow\neg P$

Equivalence Pairings

The conditional is equivalent to its contrapositive:

$$ \boxed{ P\rightarrow Q \equiv \neg Q\rightarrow\neg P }. $$

The converse is equivalent to the inverse:

$$ \boxed{ Q\rightarrow P \equiv \neg P\rightarrow\neg Q }. $$

But the original conditional is not generally equivalent to its converse or inverse.

Example Showing Converse Failure

Original statement:

“If an integer is divisible by $4$, then it is even.”

Converse:

“If an integer is even, then it is divisible by $4$.”

This is false. For example:

$$ 6 $$

is even but not divisible by $4$.

Contrapositive of the Same Statement

The contrapositive:

“If an integer is not even, then it is not divisible by $4$”

is logically equivalent to the original and therefore true whenever the original is true.

Proof by Contraposition

Because a conditional and its contrapositive are equivalent, one may prove:

$$ P\rightarrow Q $$

by proving instead:

$$ \boxed{ \neg Q\rightarrow\neg P }. $$

This method is called proof by contraposition or a contrapositive proof.

Typical Proof Pattern

To prove:

$$ P\rightarrow Q, $$

begin by assuming:

$$ \neg Q. $$

Then derive:

$$ \neg P. $$

This establishes the contrapositive and therefore the original implication.

Example: Square of an Integer

Suppose we want to prove:

“If $n^2$ is even, then $n$ is even.”

The contrapositive is:

“If $n$ is odd, then $n^2$ is odd.”

Let:

$$ n=2k+1. $$

Then:

$$ n^2 = (2k+1)^2 = 4k^2+4k+1 = 2(2k^2+2k)+1. $$

Therefore $n^2$ is odd. Hence the contrapositive is true, so the original implication is true.

Why Contraposition Can Be Easier

Sometimes the negation of the conclusion gives a more usable algebraic or structural condition than the original hypothesis.

This is particularly common when the conclusion states that an object has a broad property while its negation gives a concrete classification.

Direct Proof versus Contrapositive Proof

A direct proof begins with $P$ and derives $Q$:

$$ P\Rightarrow\cdots\Rightarrow Q. $$

A contrapositive proof begins with $\neg Q$ and derives $\neg P$:

$$ \boxed{ \neg Q \Rightarrow \cdots \Rightarrow \neg P }. $$

Both methods establish the same implication.

Contrapositive versus Proof by Contradiction

Contraposition and contradiction are related but distinct proof strategies.

In contraposition, one explicitly proves:

$$ \neg Q\rightarrow\neg P. $$

In proof by contradiction, one assumes the negation of the desired claim and derives an impossibility.

Contrapositive and Necessary Conditions

If:

$$ P\rightarrow Q, $$

then $Q$ is necessary for $P$.

The contrapositive says:

$$ \neg Q\rightarrow\neg P, $$

meaning that failure of the necessary condition rules out $P$.

Contrapositive and Sufficient Conditions

If $P$ is sufficient for $Q$, then:

$$ P\rightarrow Q. $$

The contrapositive states that failure of $Q$ is sufficient to conclude failure of $P$.

Set Inclusion Interpretation

Suppose:

$$ A\subseteq B. $$

This means:

$$ x\in A\rightarrow x\in B. $$

The contrapositive is:

$$ \boxed{ x\notin B\rightarrow x\notin A }. $$

Complement Interpretation

The same set relation can be expressed using complements:

$$ \boxed{ A\subseteq B \iff B^c\subseteq A^c }. $$

This reversal of inclusion under complementation mirrors contraposition.

Quantified Statements

A theorem often has form:

$$ \forall x\, [P(x)\rightarrow Q(x)]. $$

Its contrapositive form is:

$$ \boxed{ \forall x\, [\neg Q(x)\rightarrow\neg P(x)] }. $$

Example with Real Numbers

Consider:

“For every real number $x$, if $x>2$, then $x^2>4$.”

Its contrapositive is:

“For every real number $x$, if $x^2\le4$, then $x\le2$.”

Care must be taken when negating inequalities correctly.

Negating Inequalities

Common negations include:

Statement Negation
$x>a$ $x\le a$
$x\ge a$ $x<a$
$x<a$ $x\ge a$
$x\le a$ $x>a$
$x=a$ $x\ne a$

Negating Compound Conditions

If the conclusion is:

$$ Q\land R, $$

its negation is:

$$ \boxed{ \neg Q\lor\neg R }. $$

by De Morgan's law.

Negating a Disjunction

If the conclusion is:

$$ Q\lor R, $$

its negation is:

$$ \boxed{ \neg Q\land\neg R }. $$

Contrapositive of a Compound Conditional

If:

$$ P\rightarrow(Q\land R), $$

then the contrapositive is:

$$ \boxed{ (\neg Q\lor\neg R)\rightarrow\neg P }. $$

Contrapositive in Geometry

Suppose a theorem states:

“If two lines are parallel, then a certain pair of corresponding angles is congruent.”

Its contrapositive is:

“If that corresponding-angle pair is not congruent, then the lines are not parallel.”

Contrapositive in Number Theory

Statements about divisibility, parity, primality conditions, and congruences often become easier to prove in contrapositive form.

Example:

$$ n^2\text{ even}\rightarrow n\text{ even} $$

becomes:

$$ \boxed{ n\text{ odd}\rightarrow n^2\text{ odd} }. $$

Contrapositive in Analysis

Suppose a theorem states:

$$ f\text{ differentiable at }a \rightarrow f\text{ continuous at }a. $$

Its contrapositive is:

$$ \boxed{ f\text{ not continuous at }a \rightarrow f\text{ not differentiable at }a }. $$

This is a useful diagnostic consequence.

Contrapositive in Linear Algebra

If a theorem says:

$$ A\text{ invertible} \rightarrow \det(A)\ne0, $$

then its contrapositive is:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A)=0 \rightarrow A\text{ not invertible} }. $$

Contrapositive in Set Theory

From:

$$ x\in A\rightarrow x\in B, $$

we obtain:

$$ \boxed{ x\notin B\rightarrow x\notin A }. $$

Biconditionals

A biconditional:

$$ P\leftrightarrow Q $$

consists of two implications:

$$ (P\rightarrow Q) \land (Q\rightarrow P). $$

Each direction has its own contrapositive.

Biconditional under Negation

Since both directions can be contraposed:

$$ \boxed{ P\leftrightarrow Q \equiv \neg P\leftrightarrow\neg Q }. $$

Contrapositive and Modus Tollens

Modus tollens has the form:

$$ P\rightarrow Q,\qquad \neg Q \quad\therefore\quad \neg P. $$

This rule of inference reflects the same logical structure captured by the contrapositive.

Modus Ponens Contrast

Modus ponens uses:

$$ P\rightarrow Q,\qquad P \quad\therefore\quad Q. $$

Modus tollens instead reasons backward through negation.

Affirming the Consequent

From:

$$ P\rightarrow Q $$

and:

$$ Q, $$

one may not generally conclude $P$.

This invalid inference is called affirming the consequent.

Denying the Antecedent

From:

$$ P\rightarrow Q $$

and:

$$ \neg P, $$

one may not generally conclude $\neg Q$.

This invalid inference is called denying the antecedent.

Why the Contrapositive Is Not an Invalid Reversal

The contrapositive does not merely reverse:

$$ P\rightarrow Q. $$

It reverses and negates:

$$ \boxed{ \neg Q\rightarrow\neg P }. $$

Both operations are essential.

Algorithmic Memory Aid

A useful procedural description is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{contrapositive} = \text{reverse} + \text{negate both} }. $$

Negation Must Be Exact

A common source of error is replacing a statement with something merely different rather than with its logical negation.

For example, the negation of:

$$ x>5 $$

is not:

$$ x<5, $$

but:

$$ \boxed{x\le5}. $$

Negating Quantifiers

Quantified propositions require additional care:

$$ \boxed{ \neg(\forall x\,P(x)) \equiv \exists x\,\neg P(x) } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \neg(\exists x\,P(x)) \equiv \forall x\,\neg P(x) }. $$

Contrapositive of a Universal Implication

For:

$$ \forall x\, [P(x)\rightarrow Q(x)], $$

one may rewrite it as:

$$ \boxed{ \forall x\, [\neg Q(x)\rightarrow\neg P(x)] }. $$

The universal quantifier itself remains in place because contraposition occurs inside the conditional.

Contrapositive Is Not the Negation of a Conditional

The negation of:

$$ P\rightarrow Q $$

is:

$$ \boxed{ P\land\neg Q }. $$

This is very different from the contrapositive:

$$ \neg Q\rightarrow\neg P. $$

Common Misconception: Contrapositive Means Converse

The converse is $Q\rightarrow P$. The contrapositive is $\neg Q\rightarrow\neg P$.

Common Misconception: Contrapositive Means Inverse

The inverse is $\neg P\rightarrow\neg Q$. It is logically equivalent to the converse, not generally to the original conditional.

Common Misconception: Reversing Alone Preserves Truth

Reversing a conditional produces the converse, which may be false even when the original is true.

Common Misconception: Negating Alone Produces the Contrapositive

Negating both parts without reversing them produces the inverse.

Common Misconception: A Contrapositive Is a Different Theorem

Logically, a theorem and its contrapositive express equivalent truth conditions. They may look very different linguistically, but proving one proves the other.

Common Misconception: Contrapositive Proof Is Proof by Contradiction

Contrapositive proof establishes $\neg Q\rightarrow\neg P$ directly. Proof by contradiction assumes the negation of the target conclusion and derives an impossibility.

Common Misconception: Negation Means Adding the Word “Not” Anywhere

Logical negation must reverse the complete truth condition. Compound statements, inequalities, and quantified statements often require De Morgan's laws or quantifier negation rules.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$P\rightarrow Q$ "If P, then Q."
$\neg Q\rightarrow\neg P$ "If not Q, then not P."
$P\rightarrow Q\equiv\neg Q\rightarrow\neg P$ "A conditional is logically equivalent to its contrapositive."
$Q\rightarrow P$ "The converse: if Q, then P."
$\neg P\rightarrow\neg Q$ "The inverse: if not P, then not Q."
$A\subseteq B\iff B^c\subseteq A^c$ "Set inclusion reverses under complementation."

The phrase take the contrapositive means to transform a conditional by reversing its hypothesis and conclusion and negating both. In proofs, one may say “we prove the contrapositive” when the argument establishes $\neg Q\rightarrow\neg P$ instead of directly proving $P\rightarrow Q$.

The terms converse, inverse, and contrapositive should not be used interchangeably. Each corresponds to a distinct transformation of the original implication.

Defense Notation Advice: Begin from a clearly identified conditional $P\rightarrow Q$. To form the contrapositive, reverse the order and negate both complete propositions. Check negations carefully, especially for inequalities, conjunctions, disjunctions, and quantified statements. Do not mistake the converse $Q\rightarrow P$ or inverse $\neg P\rightarrow\neg Q$ for the contrapositive. When using contraposition as a proof method, state explicitly that proving the contrapositive is sufficient because it is logically equivalent to the original conditional.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: The contrapositive of $P\rightarrow Q$ is $\neg Q\rightarrow\neg P$.
  • Formation Rule: Reverse the propositions and negate both.
  • Logical Equivalence: $$ \boxed{ P\rightarrow Q \equiv \neg Q\rightarrow\neg P }. $$
  • Truth Table: The original conditional and its contrapositive have identical truth values in every case.
  • Converse: $Q\rightarrow P$.
  • Inverse: $\neg P\rightarrow\neg Q$.
  • Equivalence Pair: The converse and inverse are logically equivalent to each other.
  • Proof Method: To prove $P\rightarrow Q$, one may instead prove $\neg Q\rightarrow\neg P$.
  • Number Theory: Contraposition is often useful for divisibility and parity arguments.
  • Geometry: Failure of a theorem's conclusion can be used to rule out its hypothesis through the contrapositive.
  • Analysis: From differentiability implying continuity, one obtains that discontinuity implies nondifferentiability.
  • Linear Algebra: Statements about invertibility and determinants frequently admit useful contrapositive forms.
  • Set Theory: $A\subseteq B$ is equivalent to $B^c\subseteq A^c$.
  • Quantified Logic: Universal implications can be contraposed pointwise.
  • Necessary Conditions: If $Q$ is necessary for $P$, failure of $Q$ rules out $P$.
  • Sufficient Conditions: If $P$ is sufficient for $Q$, then $\neg Q$ is sufficient for $\neg P$.
  • Modus Tollens: The inference from $P\rightarrow Q$ and $\neg Q$ to $\neg P$ reflects contrapositive reasoning.
  • Negation of Conditional Contrast: The negation of $P\rightarrow Q$ is $P\land\neg Q$, not the contrapositive.
  • Converse Contrast: Reversing without negating gives the converse.
  • Inverse Contrast: Negating without reversing gives the inverse.
  • Contradiction Contrast: Proof by contraposition directly proves an equivalent implication; proof by contradiction derives an impossibility from a contrary assumption.
  • Common Error: Do not reverse the statement without also negating both parts.
  • Common Error: Do not negate both parts without reversing them.
  • Common Error: Do not assume the converse is true because the original conditional is true.
  • Common Error: Do not assume the inverse is equivalent to the original conditional.
  • Common Error: Do not form an incomplete negation such as replacing $x>a$ with $x<a$; the exact negation is $x\le a$.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse contraposition with affirming the consequent.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse contraposition with denying the antecedent.
  • Terminological Contrast: A conditional has form $P\rightarrow Q$. Its converse is $Q\rightarrow P$. Its inverse is $\neg P\rightarrow\neg Q$. Its contrapositive is $\neg Q\rightarrow\neg P$ and is logically equivalent to the original conditional. Proof by contraposition proves the contrapositive instead of the original statement directly. Modus tollens is the inference rule that uses a conditional together with the negation of its conclusion to infer the negation of its hypothesis.
