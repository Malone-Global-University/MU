Greatest Integer Function

Subfield: Foundations & Logic

Etymology & Roots

The greatest integer function assigns to each real number the greatest integer less than or equal to that number. In modern notation, this is the floor function.

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor = \max\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:n\leq x\} }. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor\leq x<\lfloor x\rfloor+1 }. $$

The phrase greatest integer function comes directly from this defining rule: among all integers that do not exceed $x$, choose the greatest one.

The modern term floor emphasizes the idea of moving downward to the nearest integer at or below the given value. The corresponding ceiling function moves upward to the least integer at or above the value.

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor = \text{greatest integer }\leq x }. $$

Older textbooks sometimes used notation such as $[x]$ for the greatest integer function. That notation is now potentially ambiguous because square brackets have many other mathematical meanings. The symbols $\lfloor x\rfloor$ are generally preferred.

Operational Nuance

Basic Definition

For every real number $x$, $\lfloor x\rfloor$ is the unique integer $n$ satisfying:

$$ \boxed{ n\leq x<n+1 }. $$

Positive Example

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor 4.7\rfloor=4 }. $$

The integer $4$ is the greatest integer not exceeding $4.7$.

Integer Input

If $n$ is already an integer:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor n\rfloor=n }. $$

Negative Example

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor -2.3\rfloor=-3 }. $$

This is a crucial case. The result is not $-2$, because $-2$ is greater than $-2.3$. The greatest integer that is still less than or equal to $-2.3$ is $-3$.

Negative Integer Input

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor -5\rfloor=-5 }. $$

Interval Characterization

For any integer $n$:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor=n \iff n\leq x<n+1 }. $$

Step-Function Structure

The greatest integer function is constant on every half-open interval:

$$ \boxed{ [n,n+1) }. $$

On that interval:

$$ \lfloor x\rfloor=n. $$

Graph

The graph is a staircase consisting of horizontal segments. Each step includes its left endpoint and excludes its right endpoint.

Closed and Open Endpoints

On the interval $[n,n+1)$:

  • the point $(n,n)$ is included;
  • the point $(n+1,n)$ is excluded;
  • the next step begins at $(n+1,n+1)$.

Domain

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(\lfloor x\rfloor)=\mathbb{R} }. $$

Range

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Range}(\lfloor x\rfloor)=\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Integer-Valued Function

Although the input may be any real number, the output is always an integer.

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor\cdot\rfloor:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Monotonicity

The floor function is nondecreasing:

$$ \boxed{ x\leq y \Longrightarrow \lfloor x\rfloor\leq\lfloor y\rfloor }. $$

Not Strictly Increasing

Different real inputs can have the same floor:

$$ \lfloor 2.1\rfloor = \lfloor 2.9\rfloor = 2. $$

Bounding Inequality

$$ \boxed{ x-1<\lfloor x\rfloor\leq x }. $$

Equivalently:

$$ \boxed{ 0\leq x-\lfloor x\rfloor<1 }. $$

Fractional Part

The fractional part of $x$ is defined by:

$$ \boxed{ \{x\} = x-\lfloor x\rfloor }. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ 0\leq\{x\}<1 }. $$

Integer Plus Fractional Part

Every real number can be decomposed as:

$$ \boxed{ x=\lfloor x\rfloor+\{x\} }. $$

Example of Fractional Part

For $x=5.37$:

$$ \lfloor5.37\rfloor=5 $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \{5.37\}=0.37 }. $$

Negative Fractional Part Example

For $x=-2.3$:

$$ \lfloor-2.3\rfloor=-3, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ \{-2.3\} = -2.3-(-3) = 0.7 }. $$

The standard fractional part is therefore nonnegative even for negative inputs.

Translation by an Integer

For every integer $n$:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x+n\rfloor = \lfloor x\rfloor+n }. $$

Example

$$ \lfloor3.6+4\rfloor = \lfloor3.6\rfloor+4 = \boxed{7}. $$

Floor of a Sum

In general:

$$ \lfloor x+y\rfloor $$

is not always equal to:

$$ \lfloor x\rfloor+\lfloor y\rfloor. $$

Counterexample

Let:

$$ x=0.7, \qquad y=0.6. $$

Then:

$$ \lfloor x\rfloor+\lfloor y\rfloor = 0, $$

while:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x+y\rfloor = \lfloor1.3\rfloor = 1 }. $$

Sum Bounds

For real $x$ and $y$:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor+\lfloor y\rfloor \leq \lfloor x+y\rfloor \leq \lfloor x\rfloor+\lfloor y\rfloor+1 }. $$

Superadditivity

The left inequality gives:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x+y\rfloor \geq \lfloor x\rfloor+\lfloor y\rfloor }. $$

Multiplication by a Positive Integer

There is no general identity:

$$ \lfloor nx\rfloor=n\lfloor x\rfloor. $$

The fractional part can create additional whole units.

Example

$$ \lfloor2(1.7)\rfloor = \lfloor3.4\rfloor = 3, $$

while:

$$ 2\lfloor1.7\rfloor = 2. $$

Negation

The floor of a negative number is related to the ceiling function:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor-x\rfloor = -\lceil x\rceil }. $$

Floor Is Not an Odd Function

In general:

$$ \lfloor-x\rfloor \neq -\lfloor x\rfloor. $$

For example:

$$ \lfloor-2.4\rfloor=-3, \qquad -\lfloor2.4\rfloor=-2. $$

Integer Detection

A real number $x$ is an integer exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor=x }. $$

Equivalent Integer Criterion

$$ \boxed{ \{x\}=0 \iff x\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Floor and Ceiling

The ceiling function is:

$$ \boxed{ \lceil x\rceil = \min\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:n\geq x\} }. $$

Thus floor moves downward and ceiling moves upward in the order structure of the integers.

Floor-Ceiling Relation

$$ \boxed{ \lceil x\rceil = -\lfloor-x\rfloor }. $$

When Floor Equals Ceiling

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor=\lceil x\rceil \iff x\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$

When $x$ Is Not an Integer

If $x\notin\mathbb{Z}$:

$$ \boxed{ \lceil x\rceil = \lfloor x\rfloor+1 }. $$

Floor vs. Truncation

Truncating a decimal toward zero is not the same as taking the floor for negative numbers.

For example:

$$ \operatorname{trunc}(-3.8)=-3, $$

but:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor-3.8\rfloor=-4 }. $$

Floor vs. Rounding

Rounding chooses an integer according to proximity. Floor always chooses the greatest integer not exceeding the input.

For example:

$$ \lfloor7.9\rfloor=7, $$

even though ordinary rounding to the nearest integer gives $8$.

Floor vs. Integer Part

The phrase integer part is potentially ambiguous. In some contexts it means truncation toward zero; in others it is used for the floor. Explicit floor notation avoids this ambiguity.

Continuity

The floor function is continuous at every noninteger real number.

Discontinuities

It is discontinuous at every integer.

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Disc}(\lfloor x\rfloor)=\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Left-Hand Limit at an Integer

For integer $n$:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to n^-}\lfloor x\rfloor=n-1 }. $$

Right-Hand Limit at an Integer

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to n^+}\lfloor x\rfloor=n }. $$

Function Value at an Integer

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor n\rfloor=n }. $$

Right Continuity

The greatest integer function is right-continuous at every integer:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to n^+}\lfloor x\rfloor = \lfloor n\rfloor }. $$

Jump Discontinuities

At each integer, the function jumps upward by exactly $1$.

Differentiability

On every open interval $(n,n+1)$:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}\lfloor x\rfloor=0 }. $$

At integers, the function is not differentiable because it is discontinuous.

Piecewise Representation

The function may be expressed conceptually as:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor=n \quad\text{for}\quad n\leq x<n+1, \quad n\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Solving Floor Equations

To solve:

$$ \lfloor x\rfloor=5, $$

use the interval definition:

$$ \boxed{ 5\leq x<6 }. $$

General Floor Equation

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor f(x)\rfloor=n \iff n\leq f(x)<n+1 }. $$

Example

Solve:

$$ \lfloor2x\rfloor=3. $$

This means:

$$ 3\leq2x<4. $$

Dividing by $2$:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{3}{2}\leq x<2 }. $$

Solving Floor Inequalities

For an integer $n$:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor\geq n \iff x\geq n }. $$

Upper Floor Inequality

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor<n \iff x<n } $$

for integer $n$.

Floor Less Than or Equal to an Integer

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor\leq n \iff x<n+1 }. $$

Floor Greater Than an Integer

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor>n \iff x\geq n+1 }. $$

Counting Integers in an Interval

Floor functions are useful for counting integers subject to upper bounds.

The number of positive integers $k$ satisfying:

$$ k\leq x $$

for $x\geq0$ is:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor }. $$

Multiples up to a Bound

The number of positive multiples of $m$ not exceeding positive $N$ is:

$$ \boxed{ \left\lfloor \frac{N}{m} \right\rfloor }. $$

Example

The number of multiples of $7$ not exceeding $100$ is:

$$ \left\lfloor \frac{100}{7} \right\rfloor = \boxed{14}. $$

Integer Quotient

For positive integers $a$ and $b$:

$$ \boxed{ \left\lfloor \frac{a}{b} \right\rfloor } $$

is the quotient appearing in Euclidean division:

$$ a=bq+r, \qquad 0\leq r<b. $$

Euclidean Division

For $b>0$:

$$ \boxed{ q= \left\lfloor \frac{a}{b} \right\rfloor }. $$

Remainder Formula

The corresponding remainder is:

$$ \boxed{ r = a-b \left\lfloor \frac{a}{b} \right\rfloor }. $$

Number Theory

Floor functions appear throughout number theory because they convert continuous ratios into exact integer counts.

Counting Divisibility Events

The number of multiples of $p$ among:

$$ 1,2,\ldots,n $$

is:

$$ \boxed{ \left\lfloor \frac{n}{p} \right\rfloor }. $$

Legendre-Type Counting

The exponent of a prime $p$ in $n!$ is:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \left\lfloor \frac{n}{p^k} \right\rfloor }. $$

Only finitely many terms are nonzero.

Computer Science

Floor-like operations arise when mapping continuous or real-valued quantities into discrete array indices, bins, pages, blocks, or integer coordinates.

Pagination Example

If objects are grouped in blocks of size $m$, expressions involving:

$$ \left\lfloor \frac{n}{m} \right\rfloor $$

count complete blocks.

Histogram Binning

A value can be assigned to a fixed-width bin using a floor expression such as:

$$ \boxed{ k = \left\lfloor \frac{x-a}{h} \right\rfloor }. $$

Here $h$ is the bin width and $a$ is an offset.

Coordinate Grids

Floor functions can map continuous coordinates into unit cells:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y) \mapsto (\lfloor x\rfloor,\lfloor y\rfloor) }. $$

Digital Quantization

Quantization schemes often replace continuous values by discrete levels. Floor-like operations are one basic way to perform such discretization.

Step Models

Pricing, taxation, shipping, billing, and capacity models may change only when an input crosses integer thresholds, producing floor or ceiling functions.

Periodic Fractional Part

Since:

$$ \{x\} = x-\lfloor x\rfloor, $$

one has:

$$ \boxed{ \{x+n\} = \{x\} } $$

for every integer $n$.

Sawtooth Structure

The fractional-part function rises linearly from $0$ toward $1$ on each interval $[n,n+1)$ and then resets to $0$ at the next integer.

Floor as Projection onto Integers

The floor map selects the integer immediately below or equal to a real value in the usual order:

$$ \boxed{ x \mapsto \max(\mathbb{Z}\cap(-\infty,x]) }. $$

Order-Theoretic Interpretation

Floor is fundamentally an order operation rather than a proximity operation. It depends on which integers lie below the input, not which is closest.

Generalized Floors

In ordered structures, the idea of a floor can be generalized whenever one can meaningfully select the greatest admissible element below a given object.

Common Error: Rounding toward Zero

For positive inputs, truncation and floor may agree. For negative nonintegers, they differ.

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor-4.2\rfloor=-5 } $$

rather than $-4$.

Common Error: Choosing the Nearest Integer

The floor of $6.9$ is:

$$ \boxed{6}, $$

not $7$.

Common Error: Thinking "Greatest" Means Greatest in Magnitude

For negative numbers, the relevant ordering is the ordinary number-line order. Since:

$$ -4<-3<-2, $$

the greatest integer not exceeding $-2.6$ is $-3$.

Common Error: Assuming Floor Distributes over Addition

In general:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x+y\rfloor \neq \lfloor x\rfloor+\lfloor y\rfloor } $$

without additional conditions.

Common Error: Assuming Floor Distributes over Multiplication

Similarly:

$$ \lfloor xy\rfloor $$

cannot generally be computed from $\lfloor x\rfloor$ and $\lfloor y\rfloor$ alone.

Common Error: Treating $[x]$ as Unambiguous

Square brackets may denote matrices, intervals, equivalence classes, grouping, or other structures. Modern floor brackets are clearer:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor }. $$

Common Error: Confusing Floor and Ceiling

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor = \text{greatest integer }\leq x } $$
$$ \boxed{ \lceil x\rceil = \text{least integer }\geq x }. $$

Common Error: Assuming the Function Is Continuous

The staircase graph has a jump discontinuity at every integer.

Common Misconception: The greatest integer function does not round to the nearest integer and does not simply remove the decimal part. It chooses the greatest integer that is less than or equal to the input. This distinction is especially important for negative numbers, where $\lfloor-2.3\rfloor=-3$ rather than $-2$.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\lfloor x\rfloor$ "The floor of x," or "the greatest integer less than or equal to x."
$\lfloor4.8\rfloor=4$ "The floor of four point eight is four."
$\lfloor-2.3\rfloor=-3$ "The floor of negative two point three is negative three."
$\lfloor x\rfloor=n$ "The floor of x equals n."
$n\leq x<n+1$ "X is at least n and less than n plus one."
$\{x\}=x-\lfloor x\rfloor$ "The fractional part of x equals x minus the floor of x."
$\lceil x\rceil=-\lfloor-x\rfloor$ "The ceiling of x equals the negative of the floor of negative x."

Older notation may use:

$$ [x] $$

for the greatest integer function, but this should be used cautiously because square brackets are heavily overloaded in mathematics.

The modern symbols:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor } $$

visually resemble a pair of brackets with only their lower horizontal strokes, reinforcing the metaphor of a floor.

Defense Notation Advice: Prefer $\lfloor x\rfloor$ over $[x]$ unless a source has explicitly defined square-bracket notation. When solving an equation such as $\lfloor f(x)\rfloor=n$, immediately translate it into $n\leq f(x)<n+1$. For negative inputs, reason from the inequality definition rather than from decimal truncation.
