Ratio

Subfield: Foundations & Logic

Etymology & Roots

A ratio compares two quantities by division. If quantities $a$ and $b$ are compared, with $b\neq0$, their ratio may be written:

$$ \boxed{ a:b } $$

or:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{a}{b} }. $$

The ratio answers a comparison question such as how many times as large, how much per unit, or what relative amount.

For example, if a class contains $12$ students wearing blue and $8$ wearing red, the ratio of blue to red is:

$$ \boxed{ 12:8 = 3:2 }. $$

The word ratio is historically associated with reckoning, comparison, and proportional reasoning. In modern mathematics, the central idea remains a multiplicative comparison rather than an additive difference.

$$ \boxed{ \text{ratio} = \text{comparison by division} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Order Matters

The ratio of $a$ to $b$ is:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{a}{b} } $$

while the ratio of $b$ to $a$ is:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{b}{a} }. $$

These are generally different.

Example

$$ \frac{6}{3}=2, \qquad \frac{3}{6}=\frac{1}{2}. $$

Equivalent Ratios

Multiplying or dividing both parts of a ratio by the same nonzero quantity preserves its value:

$$ \boxed{ a:b = ka:kb } $$

for $k\neq0$.

Example

$$ 6:9 = 2:3. $$

Ratio as Fraction

The colon notation:

$$ a:b $$

corresponds to:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{a}{b} }. $$

This makes fraction simplification available for ratio simplification.

Part-to-Part Ratio

A part-to-part ratio compares two components of a whole.

If a mixture contains $3$ cups of water and $2$ cups of concentrate:

$$ \boxed{ \text{water}:\text{concentrate} = 3:2 }. $$

Part-to-Whole Ratio

The total mixture is $5$ cups, so:

$$ \boxed{ \text{concentrate}:\text{total} = 2:5 }. $$

Ratio vs. Fraction of a Whole

A part-to-whole ratio can be interpreted as a fraction of the total, but a part-to-part ratio generally cannot be interpreted that way without first forming the whole.

Unit Ratio

A ratio can be normalized so that the second quantity is $1$:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{a}{b} = \frac{a/b}{1} }. $$

This is closely related to a unit rate.

Rate

A ratio involving quantities with different units is often called a rate.

$$ \boxed{ \frac{180\text{ miles}}{3\text{ hours}} = 60\frac{\text{miles}}{\text{hour}} }. $$

Same-Unit Ratio

When numerator and denominator have the same units, the units often cancel:

$$ \frac{6\text{ cm}}{3\text{ cm}} = \boxed{ 2 }. $$

The resulting ratio is dimensionless.

Different-Unit Ratio

When units differ, they do not cancel:

$$ \frac{120\text{ km}}{2\text{ h}} = \boxed{ 60\text{ km/h} }. $$

Proportion

A proportion is an equality of two ratios:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d} }. $$

Cross-Product Property

When $b\neq0$ and $d\neq0$:

$$ \frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \boxed{ ad=bc }. $$

Constant Ratio and Direct Proportionality

If:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{y}{x}=k } $$

for nonzero $x$, then:

$$ \boxed{ y=kx }. $$

The constant $k$ is the constant of proportionality.

Scaling

Ratios describe multiplicative scale. If two corresponding lengths satisfy:

$$ \frac{L_2}{L_1}=3, $$

then the second length is three times the first.

Similarity

Similar geometric figures have equal ratios of corresponding lengths.

$$ \boxed{ \frac{a_1}{a_2} = \frac{b_1}{b_2} = \frac{c_1}{c_2} }. $$

Scale Factor

A scale factor is itself a ratio:

$$ \boxed{ k = \frac{ \text{new length} }{ \text{original length} } }. $$

Maps and Models

A map scale such as:

$$ \boxed{ 1:50{,}000 } $$

means one unit on the map corresponds to $50{,}000$ of the same units in reality.

Percent as Ratio

A percent is a ratio to $100$:

$$ \boxed{ p\% = \frac{p}{100} }. $$

Probability Ratio

In a finite equally likely model:

$$ \boxed{ P(E) = \frac{ \text{number of favorable outcomes} }{ \text{number of possible outcomes} } }. $$

This is a part-to-whole ratio under the equally likely assumption.

Odds

Odds compare favorable outcomes to unfavorable outcomes rather than favorable outcomes to the total.

If an event has $3$ favorable and $2$ unfavorable equally likely outcomes:

$$ \boxed{ \text{odds in favor} = 3:2 }. $$

The corresponding probability is:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{3}{5} }. $$

Aspect Ratio

Width and height are commonly compared by a ratio:

$$ \boxed{ 16:9 }. $$

Gear Ratio

Mechanical systems use ratios to compare rotational counts, tooth counts, angular speeds, or torques under specific conventions.

Financial Ratios

Finance and accounting use ratios such as:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{ \text{debt} }{ \text{equity} } } $$

or:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{ \text{current assets} }{ \text{current liabilities} } }. $$

Scientific Ratios

Dimensionless ratios often reveal structural similarity across systems, while dimensional ratios define quantities such as velocity, density, or concentration.

Ratio of Consecutive Sequence Terms

In a geometric sequence:

$$ a_n=a_1r^{n-1}, $$

the common ratio is:

$$ \boxed{ r = \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} } $$

whenever $a_n\neq0$.

Trigonometric Ratios

In right-triangle trigonometry:

$$ \boxed{ \sin\theta = \frac{ \text{opposite} }{ \text{hypotenuse} } } $$
$$ \boxed{ \cos\theta = \frac{ \text{adjacent} }{ \text{hypotenuse} } } $$
$$ \boxed{ \tan\theta = \frac{ \text{opposite} }{ \text{adjacent} } }. $$

Ratio Test in Analysis

In infinite-series analysis, ratios of successive terms appear in the ratio test:

$$ \boxed{ L = \lim_{n\to\infty} \left| \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} \right| }. $$

This is a specialized use of quotient comparison, not the elementary definition of ratio itself.

Ratio vs. Difference

Difference compares additively:

$$ \boxed{ a-b }, $$

while ratio compares multiplicatively:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{a}{b} }. $$

Example

Between $10$ and $5$, the difference is:

$$ 10-5=5, $$

while the ratio is:

$$ \frac{10}{5}=2. $$

Ratio vs. Quotient

A ratio emphasizes a comparison between quantities. A quotient emphasizes the result of division. Evaluating a ratio usually produces a quotient.

Ratio vs. Fraction

Fraction notation can represent a ratio, but fractions also serve broader roles as numbers, operators, and parts of wholes.

Ratio vs. Rate

A rate is typically a ratio between quantities measured in different units.

Ratio vs. Proportion

A ratio is one comparison:

$$ a:b, $$

whereas a proportion asserts equality between ratios:

$$ \boxed{ a:b=c:d }. $$

Zero in the First Position

The ratio:

$$ \boxed{ 0:b=0 } $$

is valid when $b\neq0$.

Zero in the Second Position

A quotient interpretation of:

$$ a:0 $$

would require division by zero and is therefore undefined in ordinary arithmetic.

Negative Ratios

Ratios can be negative in algebraic settings:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{-6}{3}=-2 }. $$

In some applied contexts involving physical magnitudes or counts, only nonnegative ratios are meaningful.

Common Error: Reversing the Order

"The ratio of $a$ to $b$" means:

$$ \boxed{ a:b = \frac{a}{b} }, $$

not $b/a$.

Common Error: Comparing by Subtraction

A ratio is not the difference between the two quantities.

Common Error: Adding Both Parts to Simplify

Equivalent ratios arise from multiplying or dividing both parts by the same nonzero factor, not by adding the same quantity.

In general:

$$ \frac{a+k}{b+k} \neq \frac{a}{b}. $$

Common Error: Treating $2:3$ as $2+3$

Colon notation represents a comparison, not addition.

Common Error: Confusing Part-to-Part and Part-to-Whole

If red:blue is $2:3$, then red is not automatically $2/3$ of the whole. The whole has $2+3=5$ parts, so red is:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{2}{5} } $$

of the total.

Common Error: Assuming Every Ratio Is Dimensionless

Units cancel only when they are compatible and identical in dimensional structure.

Common Error: Ignoring Unit Conversion

Before simplifying a same-kind ratio, units should usually be converted to a common unit.

For example:

$$ 1\text{ m}:50\text{ cm} = 100\text{ cm}:50\text{ cm} = \boxed{ 2:1 }. $$

Common Error: Confusing Ratio with Percentage

A percentage is specifically a ratio expressed per $100$.

Common Error: Confusing Odds with Probability

Odds compare favorable to unfavorable outcomes; probability compares favorable to total outcomes in the elementary equally likely model.

Common Error: Assuming Equal Differences Mean Equal Ratios

The pairs $(2,4)$ and $(4,6)$ both differ by $2$, but:

$$ \frac{4}{2}=2, \qquad \frac{6}{4}=\frac{3}{2}. $$

Common Error: Assuming Equal Ratios Mean Equal Differences

The pairs $(2,4)$ and $(5,10)$ both have ratio $2$, but their differences are $2$ and $5$.

Common Error: Simplifying Only One Side of a Ratio

Equivalent-ratio transformations must apply the same nonzero multiplicative factor to both quantities.

Common Error: Treating a Ratio as Meaningful without Context

The order, units, and quantities being compared should be stated whenever ambiguity is possible.

Common Misconception: A ratio is not merely a fraction written with a colon. Its primary meaning is multiplicative comparison. The expression $3:2$ says the first quantity is $3/2$ times the second, while its interpretation as part-to-part, part-to-whole, rate, scale factor, odds, or proportional constant depends on context.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$3:2$ "Three to two."
$\frac{3}{2}$ "The ratio of three to two," or "three divided by two."
$a:b$ "The ratio of a to b."
$a:b=c:d$ "A is to b as c is to d."
$\frac{y}{x}=k$ "The ratio of y to x is the constant k."
$16:9$ "Sixteen to nine," often an aspect ratio.
$1:50{,}000$ "One to fifty thousand," often a map or scale ratio.

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"the ratio of }a\text{ to }b\text{"} } $$

means:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{a}{b} }, $$

so spoken order should be preserved carefully.

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"simplify the ratio"} } $$

generally means divide both terms by a common nonzero factor and, when appropriate, convert comparable measurements to common units first.

Defense Notation Advice: Preserve the stated order of comparison, identify whether the ratio is part-to-part or part-to-whole, and keep units visible until legitimate cancellation or conversion occurs. Treat $a:b$ as $a/b$ only when $b\neq0$, and distinguish one ratio from a proportion, which is an equality of two ratios.
