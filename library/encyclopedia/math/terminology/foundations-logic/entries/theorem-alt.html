Theorem

Subfield: Foundations & Mathematical Logic

Etymology & Roots

The mathematical word theorem comes through French and Late Latin from the Greek theōrēma, meaning something viewed, contemplated, or considered. It is related to the Greek verb theōrein, meaning "to look at," "to observe," or "to contemplate." The same linguistic family ultimately contributes to English words such as theory.

In ancient Greek mathematics, particularly in the tradition represented by Euclid, a theorem was a proposition whose truth was to be established by demonstration. This meaning became central to the deductive structure of mathematics: rather than accepting a mathematical claim merely because examples suggest it is true, a theorem is supported by a logical proof.

The English mathematical word was in use by the sixteenth century. Robert Recorde's 1551 The Pathwaie to Knowledge described theorems as established truths used in geometrical reasoning. The first complete English translation of Euclid's Elements, published by Henry Billingsley in 1570, likewise used theoreme for a proposition requiring demonstration.

Operational Nuance

A theorem is a mathematical statement that has been logically proved from accepted assumptions, definitions, axioms, and previously established results. In schematic form:

$$ \text{axioms + definitions + earlier results} \Longrightarrow \text{proof} \Longrightarrow \text{theorem}. $$

The theorem is the statement being established. The proof is the logical argument establishing it. These are related but distinct objects.

For example, the Pythagorean Theorem states that in a right triangle with legs $a$ and $b$ and hypotenuse $c$:

$$ a^2+b^2=c^2. $$

The equation is part of the theorem's statement. A geometric, algebraic, or other rigorous argument showing why the equation must hold constitutes a proof of the theorem.

Many theorems are conditional statements. They do not assert that their conclusion is true under every imaginable circumstance. Instead, they say that if specified hypotheses are satisfied, then a particular conclusion follows:

$$ P\Longrightarrow Q. $$

Here, $P$ represents the hypotheses and $Q$ represents the conclusion.

Rolle's Theorem provides a typical example. Its hypotheses include continuity, differentiability, and equal endpoint values. Only under those hypotheses does it guarantee:

$$ \exists c\in(a,b) \text{ such that } f'(c)=0. $$

A theorem therefore includes not only what is concluded but also the conditions under which the conclusion has been proved.

A theorem need not be a complicated or famous statement. In formal mathematics, any statement proved from the relevant assumptions may technically qualify as a theorem. In ordinary mathematical writing, however, authors often reserve the label theorem for results considered sufficiently important and use terms such as proposition, lemma, or corollary for other proved results.

Common Misconception: A theorem is not something that mathematicians merely believe to be true because it has worked in many examples. Observing thousands or even millions of successful cases can provide evidence for a conjecture, but it does not by itself prove a universal mathematical statement. A theorem requires a logically valid proof within its mathematical framework.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Written Form Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$P\Rightarrow Q$ "P implies Q," or "if P, then Q."
$P\Longleftrightarrow Q$ "P if and only if Q."
$\forall x\in S$ "For every x in S."
$\exists x\in S$ "There exists an x in S."
$\therefore Q$ "Therefore Q."
Theorem 3.2 "Theorem three point two."

A theorem is often presented using a recognizable written structure:

$$ \textbf{Theorem.} \quad \text{If the hypotheses }H\text{ hold, then conclusion }C\text{ follows.} $$

It is then followed by a proof:

$$ \textbf{Proof.} \quad \text{Logical argument establishing the conclusion.} \qquad \square $$

The square symbol:

$$ \square $$

is commonly used to mark the end of a proof. It is sometimes called a tombstone, Halmos symbol, or QED symbol. The traditional abbreviation Q.E.D. comes from Latin quod erat demonstrandum, meaning approximately "which was to be demonstrated."

The word theorem may also form part of a proper mathematical name, such as:

  • Pythagorean Theorem;
  • Fundamental Theorem of Calculus;
  • Remainder Theorem;
  • Rolle's Theorem;
  • Green's Theorem;
  • Stewart's Theorem.

Defense Notation Advice: When using a theorem, state or verify its hypotheses before applying its conclusion. Quoting the conclusion alone can produce an invalid argument if the conditions of the theorem have not been satisfied.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Axiom or Postulate: An axiom is a foundational assumption adopted without proof within a mathematical system. A theorem, by contrast, is derived from axioms, definitions, and other established results: $$ \text{axioms} \Longrightarrow \text{theorems}. $$ The distinction concerns logical role, not necessarily how intuitively obvious a statement appears.
  • Definition: A definition specifies what a mathematical term means. It is not normally proved. For example, defining an even integer as one expressible in the form: $$ n=2k, \qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}, $$ establishes terminology rather than asserting a theorem.
  • Conjecture: A conjecture is a mathematical statement believed or suspected to be true but not yet proved. Once a valid proof is established and accepted, the statement may become a theorem. A conjecture can also be disproved by finding a counterexample.
  • Lemma: A lemma is a proved result used primarily to help prove another result. Logically, a lemma is no less proved than a theorem. The difference is principally one of function and presentation: $$ \text{Lemma} \Longrightarrow \text{Theorem}. $$ Some lemmas later become famous and important in their own right.
  • Proposition: A proposition is also a proved mathematical statement. Authors often use the term for results considered less central than the principal theorems of a discussion. There is no universal logical boundary separating a proposition from a theorem.
  • Corollary: A corollary is a result that follows relatively directly from a theorem or another established statement. For example: $$ \text{Theorem} \Longrightarrow \text{Corollary}. $$ A corollary still requires logical justification, even when that justification is immediate.
  • Proof: A proof is the logical demonstration that establishes a theorem. The theorem is the claim; the proof is the argument supporting the claim. A single theorem may have many different proofs.
  • Counterexample: A universal claim can often be disproved by a single valid counterexample. If a claim states: $$ \forall x,\ P(x), $$ finding one $x$ for which: $$ \neg P(x) $$ is sufficient to show that the proposed theorem is false as stated.
  • Formal Logic: In a formal deductive system, a theorem is a formula or statement derivable from the system's axioms by permitted rules of inference. If $\Gamma$ represents a collection of assumptions and $\varphi$ is derivable from them, one may write: $$ \Gamma\vdash\varphi. $$ This is read as "Gamma proves phi" or "phi is derivable from Gamma."
  • Geometry: Classical geometry contains theorems concerning triangles, circles, parallel lines, similarity, congruence, and other spatial structures. The Euclidean tradition strongly influenced the familiar pattern: definitions and postulates followed by propositions and proofs.
  • Algebra: Algebraic theorems establish relationships among numbers, equations, polynomials, groups, rings, fields, vector spaces, and other structures. Examples range from the elementary Remainder Theorem to major structural results such as the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra.
  • Calculus & Analysis: Results such as Rolle's Theorem, the Mean Value Theorem, Squeeze Theorem, and Fundamental Theorem of Calculus establish rigorous relationships among limits, derivatives, integrals, and functions.
  • Named Theorems: Many theorems are named after mathematicians, but such names should not always be interpreted as exact historical claims of first discovery. Mathematical eponyms can reflect publication, popularization, later attribution, or historical convention rather than uncontested priority.
  • Theorem vs. Mathematical Truth: Calling a statement a theorem normally means that it follows from a specified mathematical framework. In foundational mathematics, changing the axioms can change which statements are provable. Thus, "theorem" describes a statement's deductive status within a system rather than simply labeling it an isolated fact.
  • Terminological Contrast: An axiom is assumed, a definition assigns meaning, a conjecture remains unproved, a theorem has been proved, a lemma is typically a supporting proved result, a proposition is another proved statement, a corollary follows comparatively directly from an established result, and a proof is the argument establishing the result.
