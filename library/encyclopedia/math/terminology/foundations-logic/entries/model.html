Model

Subfield: Foundations & Logic

Etymology & Roots

A model is a representation used to describe, organize, approximate, explain, predict, simulate, or reason about another object, system, relationship, or phenomenon. In mathematics, a model often uses mathematical structures to represent selected features of something being studied.

$$ \boxed{ \text{target system} \longrightarrow \text{selected features} \longrightarrow \text{representation} } $$

The term is intentionally broad. A model may be an equation, a function, a graph, a geometric construction, a probability distribution, a matrix, an algorithmic system, a network, a simulation, or an abstract mathematical structure.

For example, the equation:

$$ \boxed{ d=rt } $$

can serve as a model of constant-rate motion when $d$ represents distance, $r$ represents rate, and $t$ represents time.

The word model comes through French and Italian forms from Latin modulus, meaning a measure, pattern, or standard. Its mathematical use retains the idea of a structured representation that stands in for something else.

The central linguistic distinction is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{model} \neq \text{thing modeled} }. $$

A model is a purposeful representation, not a duplicate of reality.

Operational Nuance

Representation

A model represents selected aspects of a target object or system.

$$ \boxed{ \text{model} = \text{structured representation} }. $$

Selection

A model does not normally include every property of its target. It selects features relevant to a particular purpose.

Abstraction

Modeling often removes detail so that underlying relationships become easier to analyze.

Idealization

A model may deliberately assume simplified conditions such as:

  • constant speed;
  • perfectly rigid objects;
  • no friction;
  • independent observations;
  • uniform populations;
  • exact measurements;
  • continuous change.

Model Purpose

Models may be designed for different purposes:

  • description;
  • explanation;
  • prediction;
  • classification;
  • simulation;
  • optimization;
  • decision support.

Mathematical Model

A mathematical model represents a target using mathematical objects and relations.

$$ \boxed{ \text{mathematical model} = \text{mathematical structure} + \text{interpretation} }. $$

Equation as a Model

An equation becomes a model when its symbols are assigned contextual meaning.

$$ y=mx+b $$

may model cost, motion, temperature trend, revenue, or another linear relationship.

Function as a Model

A function:

$$ \boxed{ y=f(x) } $$

can model how an output depends on an input.

Variables

Variables represent quantities allowed to change within the model.

Parameters

Parameters select a particular member of a family of models.

In:

$$ y=mx+b, $$

$m$ and $b$ may act as parameters.

Constants

Constants are fixed quantities within the specified model.

Assumptions

Assumptions specify conditions under which the model is intended to operate.

Domain

A model's domain identifies allowable inputs.

$$ \boxed{ x\in D }. $$

Output

A model may output a number, function, classification, probability, trajectory, graph, decision, or distribution.

Linear Model

$$ \boxed{ y=mx+b }. $$

A linear model represents a constant rate of change in the elementary one-variable setting.

Direct-Variation Model

$$ \boxed{ y=kx }. $$

Quadratic Model

$$ \boxed{ y=ax^2+bx+c }. $$

Exponential Model

$$ \boxed{ y=y_0e^{kt} }. $$

Logistic Model

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dP}{dt} = rP\left( 1-\frac{P}{K} \right) }. $$

Trigonometric Model

$$ \boxed{ y = A\sin(\omega t+\phi)+C }. $$

Probability Model

A probability model assigns mathematical probabilities to possible outcomes.

$$ \boxed{ (\Omega,\mathcal{F},P) } $$

is a formal probability-space model.

Statistical Model

A statistical model is commonly a family of probability distributions indexed by parameters:

$$ \boxed{ \{P_\theta:\theta\in\Theta\} }. $$

Regression Model

$$ \boxed{ Y = \beta_0+\beta_1X+\varepsilon }. $$

Geometric Model

A geometric model may represent spatial structure through points, lines, planes, coordinates, transformations, or higher-dimensional objects.

Matrix Model

$$ \boxed{ A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} }. $$

can model a system of simultaneous linear relationships.

Network Model

A network may be represented by a graph:

$$ \boxed{ G=(V,E) }. $$

Differential-Equation Model

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dy}{dt} = F(t,y) }. $$

Such a model describes how a continuously varying state changes.

Difference-Equation Model

$$ \boxed{ x_{n+1}=F(x_n) }. $$

This form models discrete evolution.

Deterministic Model

A deterministic model assigns the same output whenever its inputs and parameters are the same.

Stochastic Model

A stochastic model includes randomness explicitly.

$$ \boxed{ X_{n+1} = F(X_n,\varepsilon_n) }. $$

Static Model

A static model represents relationships without explicitly representing evolution in time.

Dynamic Model

A dynamic model represents change through time or iterative stages.

Discrete Model

A discrete model uses countable states, stages, or quantities.

Continuous Model

A continuous model allows variables to range over continua such as intervals of real numbers.

Mechanistic Model

A mechanistic model attempts to represent underlying processes or causal relationships.

Empirical Model

An empirical model is built primarily from observed data patterns.

Phenomenological Model

A phenomenological model reproduces observed behavior without necessarily claiming to represent the deepest underlying mechanism.

Conceptual Model

A conceptual model organizes important entities and relationships, sometimes before they are translated into equations.

Physical Model

A physical model may be a tangible scaled or simplified representation rather than a mathematical one.

Scale Model

A scale model preserves selected proportional relationships while changing overall size.

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\text{model length}} {\text{actual length}} = k }. $$

Model as an Example Structure

In logic, the word model has a more specialized meaning: a structure in which a collection of statements is true.

Model in Mathematical Logic

If $T$ is a theory and $\mathcal{M}$ satisfies all sentences of $T$, one writes:

$$ \boxed{ \mathcal{M}\models T }. $$

meaning "$\mathcal{M}$ is a model of $T$."

Satisfaction Relation

The symbol:

$$ \boxed{ \models } $$

is read "models" or "satisfies" in logic.

Model of a Formula

If a structure $\mathcal{M}$ makes a formula $\varphi$ true under the relevant interpretation:

$$ \boxed{ \mathcal{M}\models\varphi }. $$

Model Theory

Model theory studies relationships between formal languages, theories, and the mathematical structures in which their statements are true.

Model in Applied Mathematics vs. Logic

These meanings are related through representation but are not identical.

In applied mathematics, a model represents a phenomenon. In mathematical logic, a model is a structure satisfying specified formal statements.

Model vs. Formula

A formula is symbolic syntax. A model includes interpretation.

$$ \boxed{ \text{formula} + \text{meaning} + \text{conditions} \longrightarrow \text{model use} }. $$

Model vs. Equation

An equation states a mathematical relationship. It becomes a model when its symbols are linked to a target situation or interpreted structure.

Model vs. Function

A function can serve as a model, but the mathematical function itself and its contextual interpretation should be distinguished.

Model vs. Theory

A theory is typically broader than one model. A theory may motivate, constrain, or contain many possible models.

Model vs. Law

A mathematical law expresses a relationship intended to have general validity within a scientific framework. A model may combine laws with assumptions, approximations, and specific parameters.

Model vs. Algorithm

An algorithm is a procedure. A model is a representation.

An algorithm may:

  • fit a model;
  • solve a model;
  • simulate a model;
  • optimize a model.

Model vs. Simulation

A model specifies relationships; a simulation evaluates or evolves those relationships computationally under specified conditions.

Model vs. Data

Data are observations. A model is a representation used to organize or interpret observations.

Model vs. Diagram

A diagram can be part of a model, but many models include relationships that are not purely visual.

Model vs. Representation

Representation is the broader category. A model is a representation deliberately used to stand for or reason about a target.

Model Construction

A common modeling sequence is:

  1. identify the target and question;
  2. select relevant quantities;
  3. define variables and parameters;
  4. state assumptions;
  5. construct relationships;
  6. analyze or compute;
  7. interpret results;
  8. compare with observations or requirements;
  9. revise if necessary.

Model Calibration

Calibration chooses parameter values so the model better corresponds to specified observations or conditions.

Parameter Estimation

Statistical methods may estimate model parameters from data.

Model Fitting

Fitting selects model parameters or structure to reduce discrepancy between model output and observations.

Residual

In a prediction setting:

$$ \boxed{ \text{residual} = \text{observed} - \text{predicted} }. $$

Model Error

Model error is discrepancy caused by limitations of the representation itself, rather than merely measurement noise.

Validation

Validation asks whether a model is sufficiently accurate or appropriate for its intended use.

Verification

In computational modeling, verification asks whether the mathematical model has been implemented or solved correctly.

Verification vs. Validation

$$ \boxed{ \text{verification} \longrightarrow \text{implementation correctness} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{validation} \longrightarrow \text{adequacy for intended use} }. $$

Prediction

A predictive model produces an output for an unobserved or future input condition.

Interpolation

Interpolation predicts within the range of data or calibrated conditions.

Extrapolation

Extrapolation predicts outside that range and may be substantially less reliable.

Model Scope

Scope specifies the range of situations for which the model is intended.

Validity Range

A model can be useful over one region and inaccurate elsewhere.

Example of Local Validity

A tangent-line approximation:

$$ \boxed{ f(x) \approx f(a)+f'(a)(x-a) } $$

can be excellent near $x=a$ but poor far from $a$.

Uncertainty

A model may contain uncertainty in:

  • parameters;
  • measurements;
  • inputs;
  • future conditions;
  • model structure.

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analysis studies how model output changes when inputs or parameters change.

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\partial y}{\partial p} } $$

may represent local sensitivity to a parameter $p$.

Robustness

A result is robust when reasonable changes in assumptions, parameters, or inputs do not substantially change the conclusion.

Model Complexity

More complicated models may capture more detail but can demand more data, computation, assumptions, and parameter estimation.

Parsimony

Parsimony favors a model that is no more complex than necessary for its intended purpose.

Underfitting

A model underfits when it is too simple to capture important structure.

Overfitting

A model overfits when it captures sample-specific noise rather than stable relationships.

Model Selection

Model selection compares alternative candidate models.

Model Comparison

Models may be compared by:

  • accuracy;
  • simplicity;
  • interpretability;
  • predictive performance;
  • robustness;
  • computational cost;
  • consistency with theory.

Model Revision

Models may be revised when assumptions fail, new evidence appears, or the purpose changes.

Model Hierarchy

The same target may be represented at different levels of complexity.

$$ \boxed{ \text{simple model} \longrightarrow \text{refined model} \longrightarrow \text{high-detail model} }. $$

Model Reduction

Model reduction simplifies a complex system while attempting to preserve behavior important to the problem.

Dimensionless Model

Rescaling variables can reveal common structure and reduce parameter counts.

Example

Logistic growth:

$$ P' = rP \left( 1-\frac{P}{K} \right) $$

becomes:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dp}{d\tau} = p(1-p) } $$

after setting:

$$ p=\frac{P}{K}, \qquad \tau=rt. $$

Machine-Learning Model

In machine learning, a model often means a parameterized mapping:

$$ \boxed{ \widehat{y} = f_\theta(\mathbf{x}) }. $$

The parameters $\theta$ are learned from data.

Training

Training adjusts model parameters according to an objective such as a loss function.

$$ \boxed{ \widehat{\theta} \in \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\theta} L(\theta) }. $$

Fitted Model

A fitted model is a model whose parameters have been estimated or selected from data.

Model Prediction vs. Observation

Notation such as:

$$ \boxed{ \widehat{y} } $$

can help distinguish predicted values from observed values $y$.

Model in Computer Science

The term can also refer to an abstract representation of computation, data, systems, or behavior.

State Model

A system may be represented by states and transitions.

$$ \boxed{ s_{n+1} = F(s_n,u_n) }. $$

Model in Geometry

A geometric model may realize an abstract geometry in a concrete space.

Model of Non-Euclidean Geometry

For example, hyperbolic geometry admits models such as the Poincaré disk model. The geometric model provides a representation in which the axioms and relations can be interpreted.

Model in Set Theory and Logic

A model may be a mathematical universe or structure satisfying a chosen axiom system.

Consistency Connection

If a formal theory has a model, then under ordinary classical first-order semantics the theory is semantically satisfiable: its axioms can all hold together in at least one structure.

Countermodel

A countermodel is a model in which premises are true but a proposed conclusion is false, thereby showing that the conclusion is not a semantic consequence of those premises.

Semantic Consequence

$$ \boxed{ \Gamma\models\varphi } $$

means every model of $\Gamma$ is also a model of $\varphi$.

Model Is Context-Dependent

The word can refer to:

  • a practical representation of a real system;
  • a mathematical structure satisfying axioms;
  • a fitted predictive function;
  • a geometric realization;
  • a computational abstraction.

Common Error: Treating the Model as Reality

A model represents selected features of its target and may omit important details.

Common Error: Assuming Every Model Is an Equation

Models can be graphs, simulations, probability spaces, matrices, algorithms, geometric structures, or systems of many equations.

Common Error: Assuming Every Equation Is Automatically a Model

An equation becomes a model only when it is interpreted relative to a target or formal structure.

Common Error: Ignoring Assumptions

Model conclusions depend on assumptions.

Common Error: Ignoring Scope

A model useful in one domain may fail outside it.

Common Error: Treating Simplification as Automatically Bad

Simplification is often necessary for a model to be useful.

Common Error: Assuming More Complex Means More Accurate

Increased complexity can produce overfitting, instability, or unnecessary computational burden.

Common Error: Confusing Model with Algorithm

A model represents; an algorithm performs a procedure.

Common Error: Confusing Model with Simulation

A simulation is an execution or numerical realization of a model.

Common Error: Confusing Model with Data

Data are observations; a model is a structure used to describe or interpret them.

Common Error: Confusing Fit with Truth

A model can fit observations well without being a complete or uniquely correct description of the underlying mechanism.

Common Error: Confusing Prediction with Explanation

A predictive model can perform well without explaining why the observed pattern occurs.

Common Error: Treating Parameters as Universal Constants

Parameters may depend on data, context, population, units, or calibration.

Common Error: Ignoring Units

In applied models, units are part of the interpretation and can expose invalid relationships.

Common Error: Assuming Validation Is Proof

Validation supports adequacy for a specified purpose and domain; it does not prove universal truth.

Common Error: Ignoring the Specialized Logical Meaning

In logic, a model is not merely an approximate representation. It is a structure in which specified statements are true.

Common Misconception: A model is not simply a formula and is not a miniature copy of reality. It is a structured representation chosen for a purpose. In applied mathematics it represents selected features of a system; in logic it can mean a structure satisfying a theory. In every use, understanding what the model represents, what assumptions it uses, and where it applies is essential.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$y=mx+b$ "A linear model relating y to x."
$y=y_0e^{kt}$ "An exponential model."
$A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ "A matrix model of a linear system."
$\widehat{y}=f_\theta(\mathbf{x})$ "A parameterized predictive model."
$\mathcal{M}\models T$ "M is a model of T," or "M satisfies T."
$\Gamma\models\varphi$ "Gamma semantically entails phi."
$s_{n+1}=F(s_n)$ "A discrete dynamic model."

In applied mathematics, phrases such as the model predicts, under the model, and according to the model emphasize that a conclusion depends on the chosen representation.

In logic, model becomes a technical verb in expressions such as:

$$ \mathcal{M}\models\varphi. $$

This is read "M models phi" or more naturally "M satisfies phi."

The phrase model of therefore has two common mathematical uses:

  • a representation of a target system;
  • a structure satisfying a formal theory or sentence.

Defense Notation Advice: State what the model represents, define its variables and parameters, and specify assumptions, domains, and units when relevant. In logic, use $\models$ carefully and distinguish semantic satisfaction from ordinary applied modeling. Avoid treating the word model as a synonym for equation, algorithm, simulation, or reality itself.
