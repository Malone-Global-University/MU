Floor Function

Subfield: Foundations & Logic

Etymology & Roots

The floor function assigns to a real number $x$ the greatest integer that is less than or equal to $x$.

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor = \max\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:n\leq x\} }. $$

For example:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor3.7\rfloor=3 }. $$

because $3$ is the greatest integer not exceeding $3.7$.

The word floor uses a geometric metaphor. The value $\lfloor x\rfloor$ is the integer immediately at or below $x$, as though $x$ were resting on an integer-valued floor.

The corresponding upward operation is the ceiling function:

$$ \boxed{ \lceil x\rceil = \min\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:n\geq x\} }. $$

Historically, the floor function has also been called the greatest integer function or greatest integer function of $x$, although modern notation with floor brackets is generally clearer.

Operational Nuance

Defining Inequality

The most important operational characterization is:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor \leq x < \lfloor x\rfloor+1 }. $$

Equivalently, for an integer $n$:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor=n \iff n\leq x<n+1 }. $$

Positive Example

$$ \lfloor5.92\rfloor=\boxed5. $$

Integer Input

If $x$ is already an integer:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor=x \qquad (x\in\mathbb{Z}) }. $$

Therefore:

$$ \lfloor7\rfloor=7. $$

Negative Inputs

Negative numbers reveal why the floor function is not the same as merely deleting decimal digits.

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor-2.3\rfloor=-3 }. $$

The integer $-3$ is less than $-2.3$, while $-2$ is greater than $-2.3$. Therefore $-3$ is the greatest integer satisfying the required less-than-or-equal condition.

Floor Is Not Truncation

For positive numbers, truncation toward zero and flooring happen to agree:

$$ \lfloor3.8\rfloor=3. $$

For negative numbers they differ:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor-3.8\rfloor=-4 } $$

while truncation toward zero would produce $-3$.

Domain and Range

The standard floor function is:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor\cdot\rfloor: \mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Its domain is all real numbers and its range is the integers.

Step-Function Behavior

The function is constant on every half-open interval:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor=n \quad \text{for } x\in[n,n+1) }. $$

Therefore its graph consists of horizontal steps.

Endpoint Convention

On each step:

$$ [n,n+1), $$

the left endpoint is included and the right endpoint is excluded.

Monotonicity

The floor function is nondecreasing:

$$ \boxed{ x\leq y \Longrightarrow \lfloor x\rfloor\leq\lfloor y\rfloor }. $$

It is not strictly increasing because many inputs have the same floor.

Translation by an Integer

For every integer $n$:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x+n\rfloor = \lfloor x\rfloor+n }. $$

Example

$$ \lfloor2.6+4\rfloor = \lfloor6.6\rfloor = 6 = \lfloor2.6\rfloor+4. $$

Idempotence on Floor Values

Since $\lfloor x\rfloor$ is an integer:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor\lfloor x\rfloor\rfloor = \lfloor x\rfloor }. $$

Fractional Part

The fractional part of $x$ is defined by:

$$ \boxed{ \{x\} = x-\lfloor x\rfloor }. $$

It always satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ 0\leq\{x\}<1 }. $$

Integer and Fractional Decomposition

Every real number can therefore be written:

$$ \boxed{ x = \lfloor x\rfloor+\{x\} }. $$

Negative Fractional Part Example

For $x=-2.3$:

$$ \lfloor-2.3\rfloor=-3, $$

so:

$$ \{-2.3\} = -2.3-(-3) = \boxed{0.7}. $$

Under this standard definition, the fractional part is always nonnegative.

Relationship with Ceiling

Floor and ceiling are connected by negation:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor = -\lceil-x\rceil }. $$

Equivalently:

$$ \boxed{ \lceil x\rceil = -\lfloor-x\rfloor }. $$

Floor and Ceiling at Nonintegers

If $x\notin\mathbb{Z}$:

$$ \boxed{ \lceil x\rceil = \lfloor x\rfloor+1 }. $$

Floor and Ceiling at Integers

If $x\in\mathbb{Z}$:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor = \lceil x\rceil = x }. $$

Negation Identity

If $x$ is not an integer:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor+\lfloor-x\rfloor=-1 }. $$

If $x$ is an integer:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor+\lfloor-x\rfloor=0 }. $$

Addition Inequality

In general:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor+\lfloor y\rfloor \leq \lfloor x+y\rfloor \leq \lfloor x\rfloor+\lfloor y\rfloor+1 }. $$

Floor Does Not Simply Distribute over Addition

It is generally false that:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x+y\rfloor = \lfloor x\rfloor+\lfloor y\rfloor } $$

for all real $x$ and $y$.

For example:

$$ \lfloor0.6+0.6\rfloor = 1, $$

while:

$$ \lfloor0.6\rfloor+\lfloor0.6\rfloor = 0. $$

Integer Translation Is Special

The distributive-looking rule does work when one addend is an integer:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x+n\rfloor = \lfloor x\rfloor+n \qquad (n\in\mathbb{Z}) }. $$

Multiplication Caution

In general:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor xy\rfloor \neq \lfloor x\rfloor\lfloor y\rfloor } $$

and no simple multiplicative distribution rule exists.

Nested Floor Identity

For positive integer $n$:

$$ \boxed{ \left\lfloor \frac{\lfloor x\rfloor}{n} \right\rfloor = \left\lfloor \frac{x}{n} \right\rfloor }. $$

Floor of a Floor Shift

For integer $m$:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor\lfloor x\rfloor+m\rfloor = \lfloor x\rfloor+m }. $$

Integer-Counting Interpretation

For $x\geq0$, $\lfloor x\rfloor$ counts how many positive integer thresholds $1,2,\ldots$ have been reached by $x$.

More generally, floor expressions often convert continuous quantities into counts of complete units.

Complete Groups

If $n$ objects are grouped into packages of size $k>0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \left\lfloor\frac nk\right\rfloor } $$

gives the number of complete packages.

Quotient and Remainder

For integers $n$ and positive $d$, the quotient in Euclidean division is:

$$ \boxed{ q=\left\lfloor\frac nd\right\rfloor }. $$

The remainder is:

$$ \boxed{ r=n-d\left\lfloor\frac nd\right\rfloor }. $$

Then:

$$ n=dq+r, \qquad 0\leq r<d. $$

Modular Arithmetic Connection

For positive integer $d$:

$$ \boxed{ n\bmod d = n-d \left\lfloor\frac nd\right\rfloor } $$

under the standard least-nonnegative-remainder convention.

Counting Multiples

The number of positive multiples of $d$ not exceeding a positive real number $x$ is:

$$ \boxed{ \left\lfloor\frac xd\right\rfloor }. $$

Example

The number of multiples of $7$ not exceeding $100$ is:

$$ \left\lfloor\frac{100}{7}\right\rfloor = \boxed{14}. $$

Counting Integers in an Interval

The number of integers in the closed interval $[a,b]$ is:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor b\rfloor-\lceil a\rceil+1 } $$

when the interval contains at least one integer.

Discrete Indexing

Floor functions are frequently used to map continuous values into discrete bins:

$$ \boxed{ k=\left\lfloor\frac{x-a}{h}\right\rfloor }. $$

Here $h$ may represent the bin width.

Piecewise Representation

The floor function may be described piecewise as:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor=n \quad \text{whenever } n\leq x<n+1, \qquad n\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Continuity

The floor function is continuous at every noninteger real number.

It is discontinuous at every integer.

Jump Discontinuity

At an integer $n$:

$$ \lim_{x\to n^-}\lfloor x\rfloor=n-1, $$

while:

$$ \lim_{x\to n^+}\lfloor x\rfloor=n. $$

The function therefore has a jump of size $1$ at every integer.

Right Continuity

At every real $x$:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{h\to0^+} \lfloor x+h\rfloor = \lfloor x\rfloor }. $$

Thus the floor function is right-continuous.

Differentiability

On every open interval:

$$ (n,n+1), $$

the floor function is constant, so:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}\lfloor x\rfloor=0 } $$

at every noninteger $x$.

It is not differentiable at integers because it is discontinuous there.

Integral Example

The step structure can make integrals into finite sums. For example:

$$ \int_0^n\lfloor x\rfloor\,dx = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}k $$

for positive integer $n$.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \int_0^n\lfloor x\rfloor\,dx = \frac{n(n-1)}{2} }. $$

Summation Applications

Floor functions frequently appear in number-theoretic sums such as:

$$ \sum_{k=1}^{n} \left\lfloor\frac nk\right\rfloor. $$

Such expressions often count lattice points, divisors, or integer solutions.

Legendre's Formula

The exponent of a prime $p$ in $n!$ is:

$$ \boxed{ v_p(n!) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \left\lfloor \frac{n}{p^k} \right\rfloor }. $$

Only finitely many terms are nonzero.

Lattice-Point Counting

Suppose $y=f(x)$ and one needs to count integer values satisfying an upper bound. The floor function converts a real bound into the greatest admissible integer.

Computer Science

Programming languages and numerical libraries commonly provide a floor operation.

It rounds toward negative infinity, not toward zero.

Programming Example

Mathematically:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{floor}(-2.8)=-3 }. $$

A language's integer-conversion operation may instead truncate toward zero, so the two operations should not automatically be treated as identical.

Data Binning

Floor is useful for assigning continuous values to equally sized bins:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{bin}(x) = \left\lfloor\frac{x-a}{h}\right\rfloor }. $$

Time Conversion

If $t$ is measured in minutes, the number of complete hours elapsed is:

$$ \boxed{ \left\lfloor\frac{t}{60}\right\rfloor }. $$

Periodic Fractional Part

Since:

$$ \{x\}=x-\lfloor x\rfloor, $$

one has:

$$ \boxed{ \{x+n\}=\{x\} \qquad (n\in\mathbb{Z}) }. $$

Thus the fractional-part function is periodic with period $1$.

Floor as Integer Part

Some older literature informally calls $\lfloor x\rfloor$ the integer part of $x$.

This terminology can be ambiguous because integer part is sometimes used for truncation toward zero. The floor notation is therefore preferable when negative numbers are possible.

Greatest Integer Function

The older name greatest integer function means:

$$ \boxed{ \text{greatest integer }\leq x }. $$

The phrase should not be interpreted as the largest integer in existence.

Iverson-Style Ambiguity

Older notation occasionally used brackets such as $[x]$ for the greatest integer function. This can conflict with interval notation, matrices, equivalence classes, or indicator conventions.

Modern floor brackets:

$$ \boxed{\lfloor x\rfloor} $$

remove much of that ambiguity.

Floor vs. Rounding to Nearest

Flooring always moves to the greatest integer not exceeding the input.

Thus:

$$ \lfloor4.9\rfloor=4, $$

even though ordinary rounding to the nearest integer gives $5$.

Floor vs. Ceiling

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor = \text{greatest integer }\leq x } $$
$$ \boxed{ \lceil x\rceil = \text{least integer }\geq x }. $$

Floor vs. Truncation

Floor rounds downward toward $-\infty$. Truncation moves toward zero. They coincide for nonnegative inputs but can differ for negative inputs.

Floor vs. Nearest-Integer Function

A nearest-integer function minimizes distance to an integer, while the floor function is determined by order.

Floor and Inequality Solving

An equation:

$$ \lfloor x\rfloor=4 $$

is equivalent to:

$$ \boxed{ 4\leq x<5 }. $$

Floor Inequality

For integer $n$:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor\geq n \iff x\geq n }. $$

Another Floor Inequality

For integer $n$:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor<n \iff x<n }. $$

Floor Upper-Bound Condition

For integer $n$:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor\leq n \iff x<n+1 }. $$

Floor Lower-Bound Condition

For integer $n$:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor>n \iff x\geq n+1 }. $$

Common Misconception: The floor function does not simply "remove the decimal part." That description fails for negative numbers. The correct definition is always the greatest integer less than or equal to the input. Therefore $\lfloor-2.3\rfloor=-3$, not $-2$.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\lfloor x\rfloor$ "The floor of x."
$\lfloor3.7\rfloor=3$ "The floor of three point seven is three."
$\lfloor-2.3\rfloor=-3$ "The floor of negative two point three is negative three."
$\lfloor x\rfloor=n$ "The floor of x equals n."
$n\leq x<n+1$ "X lies from n inclusive to n plus one exclusive."
$\{x\}=x-\lfloor x\rfloor$ "The fractional part of x equals x minus its floor."
$\lceil x\rceil=-\lfloor-x\rfloor$ "The ceiling of x equals the negative of the floor of negative x."

The symbols:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor\quad\rfloor } $$

are called floor brackets. Their lower horizontal strokes help visually distinguish them from ceiling brackets:

$$ \boxed{ \lceil\quad\rceil }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Do not replace floor brackets with ordinary parentheses or square brackets when precision matters. When evaluating negative inputs, return to the defining inequality:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor\leq x<\lfloor x\rfloor+1 }. $$

This prevents the common mistake of treating floor as truncation toward zero.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Definition: The floor of $x$ is the greatest integer less than or equal to $x$.
  • Notation: $$ \boxed{\lfloor x\rfloor}. $$
  • Formal Definition: $$ \lfloor x\rfloor=\max\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:n\leq x\}. $$
  • Defining Inequality: $$ \lfloor x\rfloor\leq x<\lfloor x\rfloor+1. $$
  • Integer Characterization: $$ \lfloor x\rfloor=n\iff n\leq x<n+1. $$
  • Domain: $\mathbb{R}$.
  • Range: $\mathbb{Z}$.
  • Positive Example: $\lfloor8.6\rfloor=8$.
  • Negative Example: $\lfloor-8.6\rfloor=-9$.
  • Integer Input: $\lfloor n\rfloor=n$ for every integer $n$.
  • Step Function: Constant on each interval $[n,n+1)$.
  • Left Endpoint: Included in each step.
  • Right Endpoint: Excluded from each step.
  • Nondecreasing: $x\leq y$ implies $\lfloor x\rfloor\leq\lfloor y\rfloor$.
  • Not Strictly Increasing: Entire intervals share one floor value.
  • Integer Translation: $$ \lfloor x+n\rfloor=\lfloor x\rfloor+n. $$
  • Idempotence: $$ \lfloor\lfloor x\rfloor\rfloor=\lfloor x\rfloor. $$
  • Fractional Part: $$ \{x\}=x-\lfloor x\rfloor. $$
  • Fractional-Part Range: $$ 0\leq\{x\}<1. $$
  • Decomposition: $$ x=\lfloor x\rfloor+\{x\}. $$
  • Ceiling Relation: $$ \lfloor x\rfloor=-\lceil-x\rceil. $$
  • Noninteger Ceiling: If $x\notin\mathbb{Z}$, then $\lceil x\rceil=\lfloor x\rfloor+1$.
  • Integer Floor and Ceiling: If $x\in\mathbb{Z}$, then $\lfloor x\rfloor=\lceil x\rceil=x$.
  • Negation: If $x\notin\mathbb{Z}$, then $\lfloor x\rfloor+\lfloor-x\rfloor=-1$.
  • Addition Bound: $$ \lfloor x\rfloor+\lfloor y\rfloor \leq\lfloor x+y\rfloor \leq\lfloor x\rfloor+\lfloor y\rfloor+1. $$
  • No General Additive Distribution: $\lfloor x+y\rfloor$ need not equal $\lfloor x\rfloor+\lfloor y\rfloor$.
  • No General Multiplicative Distribution: $\lfloor xy\rfloor$ need not equal $\lfloor x\rfloor\lfloor y\rfloor$.
  • Floor vs. Truncation: Floor moves toward negative infinity; truncation moves toward zero.
  • Floor vs. Rounding: Flooring is order-based rather than nearest-distance based.
  • Floor vs. Ceiling: Floor moves to the integer at or below; ceiling moves to the integer at or above.
  • Greatest Integer Function: Older synonym for floor function.
  • Integer Part: Sometimes used for floor, but potentially ambiguous.
  • Equation Solving: $\lfloor x\rfloor=n$ converts to $n\leq x<n+1$.
  • Lower Inequality: For integer $n$, $\lfloor x\rfloor\geq n\iff x\geq n$.
  • Upper Inequality: For integer $n$, $\lfloor x\rfloor\leq n\iff x<n+1$.
  • Complete Groups: $\lfloor n/k\rfloor$ counts complete groups of size $k$.
  • Euclidean Division: $$ q=\left\lfloor\frac nd\right\rfloor $$ for positive divisor $d$.
  • Remainder: $$ r=n-d\left\lfloor\frac nd\right\rfloor. $$
  • Modular Arithmetic: Floor provides a formula for the least nonnegative remainder.
  • Counting Multiples: $\lfloor x/d\rfloor$ counts positive multiples of $d$ not exceeding $x$.
  • Interval Counting: Floor and ceiling count integers contained in real intervals.
  • Discrete Binning: Floor converts continuous coordinates into integer bin indices.
  • Computer Graphics: Used in grid-cell and pixel-index calculations.
  • Computer Science: Common in indexing, pagination, buckets, hashing, and discrete simulation.
  • Programming: floor should not be confused with integer truncation.
  • Time Conversion: Floor counts complete elapsed units such as hours, days, or periods.
  • Number Theory: Floor expressions count multiples, divisors, lattice points, and prime-power contributions.
  • Legendre's Formula: $$ v_p(n!) = \sum_{k\geq1} \left\lfloor\frac{n}{p^k}\right\rfloor. $$
  • Combinatorics: Floor appears when counts are constrained by divisibility or packing limits.
  • Discrete Mathematics: Converts continuous or rational expressions into integer-valued counts.
  • Analysis: Provides a standard example of a discontinuous step function.
  • Continuity: Continuous at every noninteger.
  • Discontinuity: Discontinuous at every integer.
  • Jump Size: Each integer produces a jump of $1$.
  • Right Continuity: The floor function is right-continuous.
  • Derivative: Equal to $0$ at every noninteger input.
  • Nondifferentiability: Not differentiable at integers.
  • Integration: Integrals of floor functions frequently reduce to sums over unit intervals.
  • Graph: Consists of horizontal half-open steps.
  • Open and Closed Points: Graphs conventionally show a closed left endpoint and open right endpoint on each step.
  • Fractional-Part Periodicity: $\{x+n\}=\{x\}$ for integers $n$.
  • Nested Floors: Some floor expressions simplify through integer-valued inner terms.
  • Lattice Problems: Floor functions convert real bounds into admissible integer coordinates.
  • Geometry: May count complete grid cells or lattice positions below a boundary.
  • Probability: Can discretize continuous measurements into integer categories.
  • Algorithms: Often used when a real quantity must determine a discrete index.
  • Terminological Precision: "Round down" is useful intuition only if down means toward negative infinity.
  • Negative-Input Defense: Always use the order definition when signs make intuition uncertain.
  • Core Interpretation: The floor function converts a real number into the greatest integer not exceeding it: $$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor = \max\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:n\leq x\} }. $$ Its defining inequality $\lfloor x\rfloor\leq x<\lfloor x\rfloor+1$ governs its algebraic behavior and explains its uses in integer counting, quotient-and-remainder calculations, discrete indexing, number theory, step functions, and the decomposition of a real number into integer and fractional parts.
