Base of an Isosceles Triangle

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

In a non-equilateral isosceles triangle, the base is the side opposite the vertex at which the two congruent sides meet.

Suppose:

$$ AB=AC. $$

Then $AB$ and $AC$ are the congruent sides, while:

$$ \boxed{ BC=\text{base} }. $$

The common vertex of the congruent sides, $A$, is the apex or vertex of the isosceles triangle. The angle there:

$$ \angle BAC $$

is the vertex angle.

The two angles touching the base:

$$ \angle ABC \qquad\text{and}\qquad \angle BCA $$

are called the base angles.

$$ \boxed{ AB=AC \Rightarrow \angle ABC=\angle BCA }. $$

Operational Nuance

Identifying the Base

In an ordinary non-equilateral isosceles triangle, first identify the two congruent sides. The remaining side is the base.

$$ \boxed{ \text{two congruent sides} \longrightarrow \text{remaining side is the base} }. $$

The Base Need Not Be at the Bottom

The term base describes the side's structural role, not its orientation in a drawing.

A triangle may be rotated without changing which side is its designated isosceles base.

$$ \boxed{ \text{base} \ne \text{necessarily the visually lowest side} }. $$

Congruent Sides as Legs

The two congruent sides of an isosceles triangle are often called its legs.

If:

$$ AB=AC=s, $$

and:

$$ BC=b, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ s=\text{leg length}, \qquad b=\text{base length} }. $$

Base Angles

The angles at the endpoints of the base are congruent:

$$ \boxed{ \angle B=\angle C }. $$

This is the Isosceles Triangle Theorem.

Converse Isosceles Triangle Theorem

The converse also holds: if two angles of a triangle are congruent, the sides opposite those angles are congruent.

$$ \boxed{ \angle B=\angle C \Rightarrow AB=AC }. $$

The side connecting the two congruent-angle vertices is then the base.

Vertex Angle and Base Angles

If each base angle has measure $\alpha$ and the vertex angle has measure $\theta$, then:

$$ \theta+2\alpha=180^\circ. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \alpha = \frac{180^\circ-\theta}{2} }. $$

Equivalently:

$$ \boxed{ \theta = 180^\circ-2\alpha }. $$

Altitude to the Base

Draw a segment from the apex $A$ perpendicular to the base $BC$, meeting it at $D$:

$$ AD\perp BC. $$

In an isosceles triangle, this altitude has several roles simultaneously.

The Altitude Bisects the Base

$$ \boxed{ BD=DC }. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ BD=DC=\frac b2 }. $$

The Altitude Is Also a Median

Because $D$ is the midpoint of the base, $\overline{AD}$ is a median.

$$ \boxed{ \text{apex altitude} = \text{median to the base} }. $$

The Altitude Is Also an Angle Bisector

The same segment bisects the vertex angle:

$$ \boxed{ \angle BAD = \angle DAC }. $$

The Altitude Is Also the Perpendicular Bisector

Since:

$$ AD\perp BC $$

and:

$$ BD=DC, $$

the line containing $AD$ is the perpendicular bisector of the base.

$$ \boxed{ \text{altitude} = \text{median} = \text{angle bisector} = \text{perpendicular bisector} } $$

for the segment drawn from the apex to the base of an isosceles triangle.

Axis of Symmetry

The line through the apex and midpoint of the base is also the triangle's axis of reflectional symmetry.

$$ \boxed{ R_\ell(\triangle ABC) = \triangle ABC }. $$

Reflection across this axis exchanges $B$ and $C$ while leaving $A$ fixed.

Splitting the Triangle into Right Triangles

The altitude to the base divides the isosceles triangle into two congruent right triangles:

$$ \boxed{ \triangle ABD \cong \triangle ACD }. $$

This provides a useful route to length, angle, trigonometric, and area formulas.

Pythagorean Relationship

Let each congruent leg have length $s$, the base have length $b$, and the altitude to the base have length $h$.

Each half forms a right triangle with legs:

$$ h \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac b2, $$

and hypotenuse $s$.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ s^2 = h^2+\left(\frac b2\right)^2 }. $$

Finding the Height

Solving for $h$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ h = \sqrt{ s^2-\frac{b^2}{4} } }. $$

Finding the Base

If the congruent side length $s$ and height $h$ are known:

$$ \left(\frac b2\right)^2 = s^2-h^2. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ b = 2\sqrt{s^2-h^2} }. $$

Area Using the Base

As with any triangle:

$$ \boxed{ A=\frac12bh }. $$

Here $b$ is the isosceles base and $h$ is the perpendicular distance from the apex to the line containing the base.

Area from Base and Equal Side

Substituting:

$$ h= \sqrt{s^2-\frac{b^2}{4}} $$

into the area formula gives:

$$ \boxed{ A = \frac b4 \sqrt{4s^2-b^2} }. $$

Perimeter

For congruent side length $s$ and base length $b$:

$$ \boxed{ P=2s+b }. $$

Base from Perimeter

If $P$ and $s$ are known:

$$ \boxed{ b=P-2s }. $$

Base from the Vertex Angle

Suppose each congruent side has length $s$ and the vertex angle is $\theta$. The altitude bisects the vertex angle, so in either right triangle:

$$ \sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) = \frac{b/2}{s}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ b = 2s \sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) }. $$

Height from the Vertex Angle

Similarly:

$$ \cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) = \frac hs, $$

giving:

$$ \boxed{ h = s \cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) }. $$

Base from a Base Angle

If each equal side has length $s$ and each base angle is $\alpha$, then:

$$ \boxed{ b=2s\cos\alpha }. $$

This follows because:

$$ \frac{\theta}{2} = 90^\circ-\alpha. $$

Law of Cosines

The base can also be related to the equal sides and vertex angle using the Law of Cosines:

$$ b^2 = s^2+s^2-2s^2\cos\theta. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ b^2 = 2s^2(1-\cos\theta) }. $$

Coordinate Representation

An isosceles triangle can be positioned symmetrically about the $y$-axis with base endpoints:

$$ B=(-a,0), \qquad C=(a,0), $$

and apex:

$$ A=(0,h). $$

Its base length is:

$$ \boxed{ BC=2a }. $$

The y-axis is the perpendicular bisector of the base and the triangle's axis of symmetry.

Base Midpoint

If the base endpoints are:

$$ B=(x_1,y_1), \qquad C=(x_2,y_2), $$

then its midpoint is:

$$ \boxed{ M= \left( \frac{x_1+x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1+y_2}{2} \right) }. $$

The symmetry axis passes through $M$ perpendicular to the base.

Base Length from Coordinates

The base length is found by the distance formula:

$$ \boxed{ b = \sqrt{ (x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2 } }. $$

The Equilateral Special Case

There are two common conventions for defining an isosceles triangle.

Under the inclusive definition, an isosceles triangle has at least two congruent sides. An equilateral triangle is therefore a special isosceles triangle.

Under the exclusive definition, an isosceles triangle has exactly two congruent sides, so an equilateral triangle is treated as a separate category.

Base Ambiguity in an Equilateral Triangle

If the inclusive definition is used, an equilateral triangle has no uniquely distinguished isosceles base because all three sides are congruent.

Any side may be designated as the base relative to the chosen apex.

$$ \boxed{ \text{equilateral case: base depends on chosen vertex} }. $$

Base of an Isosceles Right Triangle

In an isosceles right triangle, the two legs forming the right angle are congruent. Under the ordinary isosceles terminology, the side opposite their common vertex is the base.

That side is also the hypotenuse.

$$ \boxed{ \text{isosceles right triangle base} = \text{hypotenuse} }. $$

45°-45°-90° Relationship

If each congruent leg has length $s$, the base has length:

$$ \boxed{ b=s\sqrt2 }. $$

Base versus Leg

For a non-equilateral isosceles triangle:

$$ \boxed{ \text{base} = \text{unequal side} } $$

while the two congruent sides are usually called the legs.

Base versus Vertex

The base is a side. The apex or vertex angle vertex is the point opposite that side.

If $BC$ is the base, then $A$ is the apex.

Base versus Base Angles

The base is the segment itself:

$$ \overline{BC}. $$

The base angles are:

$$ \angle ABC \qquad\text{and}\qquad \angle BCA. $$

These are related terms but different geometric objects.

Base versus Base Length

The base is a geometric segment. Its length is a numerical measurement.

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \overline{BC} = \text{base segment}, \qquad BC=b = \text{base length} }. $$

Common Misconception

The base of an isosceles triangle is not identified merely by looking for the horizontal side in a diagram. It is identified from the triangle's congruent-side structure.

$$ \boxed{ \text{identify the equal sides first} \rightarrow \text{the opposite remaining side is the base} }. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$AB=AC$ "A B and A C are the congruent sides of the isosceles triangle."
$BC=b$ "B C is the base, with base length b."
$\angle B=\angle C$ "The base angles are congruent."
$BD=DC=\frac b2$ "The altitude from the apex bisects the base."
$A=\frac12bh$ "The area is one-half the base times the corresponding height."
$b=2s\sin(\theta/2)$ "The base equals twice the equal side times sine of half the vertex angle."

The words base, legs, base angles, and vertex angle form a coordinated vocabulary for isosceles triangles. Once the congruent sides are identified, these terms determine one another naturally.

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish the geometric base segment from its numerical length. If $\overline{BC}$ is the base, write $BC=b$ when referring specifically to its length.
