Expansion by Cofactors

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

Expansion by cofactors is a method for computing the determinant of a square matrix by expressing that determinant as a sum of matrix entries multiplied by their corresponding cofactors.

The method is also called cofactor expansion or Laplace expansion.

For an $n\times n$ matrix:

$$ A=(a_{ij}), $$

the cofactor associated with the entry $a_{ij}$ is:

$$ \boxed{ C_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j}M_{ij} }, $$

where $M_{ij}$ is the minor obtained by deleting row $i$ and column $j$ from $A$ and taking the determinant of the remaining matrix.

Expansion along row $i$ is:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A) = \sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}C_{ij} }. $$

Expansion along column $j$ is:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ij}C_{ij} }. $$

The word expansion reflects the fact that one determinant is unfolded into a sum of smaller determinants. The word cofactor refers to the signed minor paired with each selected matrix entry.

Operational Nuance

Minor of an Entry

The minor $M_{ij}$ of the entry $a_{ij}$ is the determinant remaining after deleting row $i$ and column $j$.

For:

$$ A= \begin{pmatrix} a & b & c\\ d & e & f\\ g & h & i \end{pmatrix}, $$

the minor of $a_{12}=b$ is:

$$ M_{12} = \begin{vmatrix} d & f\\ g & i \end{vmatrix} = di-fg. $$

Cofactor

The cofactor adds the alternating sign factor:

$$ \boxed{ C_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j}M_{ij} }. $$

Therefore:

$$ C_{12} = (-1)^{1+2}M_{12} = -M_{12}. $$

Cofactor Sign Pattern

The signs alternate in a checkerboard pattern beginning with positive in the upper-left corner:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{pmatrix} + & - & + & - & \cdots\\ - & + & - & + & \cdots\\ + & - & + & - & \cdots\\ - & + & - & + & \cdots\\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots \end{pmatrix} }. $$

Sign Rule

The cofactor is positive when $i+j$ is even and negative when $i+j$ is odd:

$$ \boxed{ (-1)^{i+j} = \begin{cases} +1, & i+j\text{ even},\\ -1, & i+j\text{ odd}. \end{cases} } $$

Expansion Along the First Row

For:

$$ A= \begin{pmatrix} a & b & c\\ d & e & f\\ g & h & i \end{pmatrix}, $$

expansion along the first row gives:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A) = aC_{11}+bC_{12}+cC_{13} }. $$

Substituting minors and signs:

$$ \det(A) = a \begin{vmatrix} e & f\\ h & i \end{vmatrix} - b \begin{vmatrix} d & f\\ g & i \end{vmatrix} + c \begin{vmatrix} d & e\\ g & h \end{vmatrix}. $$

Expanded $3\times3$ Formula

$$ \boxed{ \det(A) = a(ei-fh) - b(di-fg) + c(dh-eg) }. $$

Numerical Example

Let:

$$ A= \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 3\\ 0 & 4 & 5\\ 1 & 0 & 6 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Expanding along the first row:

$$ \det(A) = 2 \begin{vmatrix} 4 & 5\\ 0 & 6 \end{vmatrix} - 1 \begin{vmatrix} 0 & 5\\ 1 & 6 \end{vmatrix} + 3 \begin{vmatrix} 0 & 4\\ 1 & 0 \end{vmatrix}. $$

Compute the $2\times2$ determinants:

$$ \det(A) = 2(24) - 1(-5) + 3(-4). $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A)=41 }. $$

Expansion Along Any Row

One need not use the first row. For any fixed row $i$:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A) = a_{i1}C_{i1} + a_{i2}C_{i2} + \cdots + a_{in}C_{in} }. $$

Expansion Along Any Column

Likewise, for any fixed column $j$:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A) = a_{1j}C_{1j} + a_{2j}C_{2j} + \cdots + a_{nj}C_{nj} }. $$

The Result Is Independent of the Chosen Row or Column

Every valid cofactor expansion of the same square matrix produces the same determinant:

$$ \boxed{ \text{expand along any row or any column} \Longrightarrow \text{same }\det(A) }. $$

Choosing a Row or Column Strategically

Since terms containing zero vanish:

$$ 0\cdot C_{ij}=0, $$

the most efficient hand calculation usually expands along a row or column containing many zeros.

Example with Zeros

Consider:

$$ A= \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 0 & 0\\ 2 & 3 & 4\\ 1 & 6 & 7 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Expanding along the first row gives only one nonzero term:

$$ \det(A) = 5 \begin{vmatrix} 3 & 4\\ 6 & 7 \end{vmatrix}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \det(A) = 5(21-24) = \boxed{-15}. $$

Recursive Structure

Cofactor expansion reduces an $n\times n$ determinant to determinants of size:

$$ \boxed{ (n-1)\times(n-1) }. $$

Those determinants can themselves be expanded by cofactors until $2\times2$ or $1\times1$ determinants remain.

Base Case

For a $1\times1$ matrix:

$$ A=(a), $$

the determinant is:

$$ \boxed{\det(A)=a}. $$

$2\times2$ Determinant

For:

$$ A= \begin{pmatrix} a & b\\ c & d \end{pmatrix}, $$

cofactor expansion along the first row gives:

$$ a(d)-b(c), $$

hence:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A)=ad-bc }. $$

Minor vs. Cofactor

The minor is:

$$ \boxed{M_{ij}} $$

while the cofactor is:

$$ \boxed{ C_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j}M_{ij} }. $$

The sign factor is the essential difference.

Deleting the Correct Row and Column

To form $M_{ij}$:

$$ \boxed{ \text{delete row }i \text{ and column }j }. $$

The indices belong to the selected entry $a_{ij}$.

Cofactor Matrix

The matrix of all cofactors is:

$$ \boxed{ C=(C_{ij}) }. $$

Adjugate Connection

The adjugate of a square matrix is the transpose of its cofactor matrix:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{adj}(A) = C^T }. $$

Inverse Formula

If:

$$ \det(A)\neq0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ A^{-1} = \frac1{\det(A)} \operatorname{adj}(A) }. $$

Thus cofactors also appear in the classical formula for the matrix inverse.

Cramer's Rule Connection

Cramer's Rule uses determinants to solve:

$$ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b} $$

when $A$ is square and invertible. Cofactor expansion may be used to compute the determinants appearing in:

$$ \boxed{ x_i = \frac{\det(A_i)}{\det(A)} }. $$

Determinant Linearity in a Row

Cofactor expansion makes visible that the determinant is linear in each row when all other rows are held fixed.

Expanding along row $i$:

$$ \det(A) = \sum_j a_{ij}C_{ij}, $$

and the cofactors $C_{ij}$ do not depend on entries from row $i$ itself.

Determinant Linearity in a Column

The same reasoning applies to any fixed column.

Repeated Rows

If a square matrix has two identical rows, its determinant is zero. Cofactor expansion is compatible with this alternating property of the determinant.

Triangular Matrices

Cofactor expansion can prove recursively that the determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal entries:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A) = a_{11}a_{22}\cdots a_{nn} }. $$

Identity Matrix

Repeated cofactor expansion gives:

$$ \boxed{ \det(I_n)=1 }. $$

Diagonal Matrices

For:

$$ D= \operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_n), $$

cofactor expansion yields:

$$ \boxed{ \det(D)=d_1d_2\cdots d_n }. $$

Symbolic Determinants

Cofactor expansion is especially useful when a determinant contains many symbolic entries and a row or column has a simple structure.

Computational Cost

Although conceptually important, naive recursive cofactor expansion becomes computationally expensive for large dense matrices because it creates many smaller determinant calculations.

For large numerical matrices, elimination-based methods are usually much more efficient.

When Cofactor Expansion Is Efficient

It remains practical when:

  • a matrix is small,
  • a row or column contains many zeros,
  • symbolic structure makes minors simple, or
  • a theoretical proof requires recursive determinant structure.

Not Restricted to the First Row

The memorized $3\times3$ pattern:

$$ +\quad-\quad+ $$

along the first row is only one instance of the general checkerboard sign rule.

Expansion Is of the Determinant, Not the Matrix

One does not "expand the matrix" into smaller matrices in the ordinary sense. Rather, one expands its determinant as a sum involving entries and cofactors.

Common Misconception: A cofactor is not the same as a minor. The minor $M_{ij}$ is the determinant of the submatrix formed by deleting row $i$ and column $j$. The cofactor includes the sign: $C_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j}M_{ij}$. Forgetting this sign pattern is one of the most common errors in cofactor expansion.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$M_{ij}$ "The i-j minor."
$C_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j}M_{ij}$ "The i-j cofactor equals negative one to the i plus j times the i-j minor."
$\det(A)=\sum_j a_{ij}C_{ij}$ "Expand the determinant along row i."
$\det(A)=\sum_i a_{ij}C_{ij}$ "Expand the determinant along column j."
$C=(C_{ij})$ "C is the cofactor matrix."
$\operatorname{adj}(A)=C^T$ "The adjugate of A is the transpose of the cofactor matrix."
$A^{-1}=\frac1{\det(A)}\operatorname{adj}(A)$ "A inverse equals one over the determinant of A times the adjugate of A."

Common verbal instructions include:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"expand along the first row"} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{"expand along the third column"} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{"use cofactor expansion"} }. $$

All mean that the determinant should be written as the sum of selected entries multiplied by their signed minors.

Defense Notation Advice: Keep the distinction:

$$ \boxed{ M_{ij} = \text{minor}, \qquad C_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j}M_{ij} = \text{cofactor} }. $$

When working by hand, writing the checkerboard sign pattern before beginning a larger expansion can prevent sign errors.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Primary Meaning: Expansion by cofactors computes a square-matrix determinant from entries and signed minors.
  • Synonym: Cofactor expansion.
  • Historical Synonym: Laplace expansion.
  • Minor: $$ \boxed{M_{ij}} $$ is formed by deleting row $i$ and column $j$ and taking the determinant.
  • Cofactor: $$ \boxed{ C_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j}M_{ij} }. $$
  • Row Expansion: $$ \boxed{ \det(A)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}C_{ij} }. $$
  • Column Expansion: $$ \boxed{ \det(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ij}C_{ij} }. $$
  • Choice of Row: Any row may be used.
  • Choice of Column: Any column may be used.
  • Invariant Result: Every valid row or column expansion gives the same determinant.
  • Sign Pattern: $$ \begin{pmatrix} + & - & +\\ - & + & -\\ + & - & + \end{pmatrix}. $$
  • Sign Criterion: $C_{ij}$ has positive minor sign when $i+j$ is even.
  • Negative Cofactor Sign: $C_{ij}$ has negative minor sign when $i+j$ is odd.
  • First Position: $C_{11}$ carries a positive sign.
  • Second Position: $C_{12}$ carries a negative sign.
  • Third Position: $C_{13}$ carries a positive sign.
  • Strategic Choice: Prefer a row or column containing many zeros.
  • Zero Terms: If $a_{ij}=0$, then: $$ a_{ij}C_{ij}=0. $$
  • Recursive Reduction: An $n\times n$ determinant reduces to $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ determinants.
  • Base Case: A $1\times1$ determinant equals its single entry.
  • $2\times2$ Formula: $$ \boxed{ \begin{vmatrix} a & b\\ c & d \end{vmatrix} = ad-bc }. $$
  • $3\times3$ First-Row Expansion: $$ \boxed{ \det(A) = a_{11}C_{11} + a_{12}C_{12} + a_{13}C_{13} }. $$
  • Minor vs. Cofactor: The cofactor is the signed minor, not merely the minor.
  • Submatrix Construction: The minor deletes exactly one row and one column.
  • Square-Matrix Requirement: Ordinary determinants and cofactor expansions apply to square matrices.
  • Rectangular Matrix Caution: A general rectangular matrix does not have an ordinary determinant to expand.
  • Cofactor Matrix: $$ \boxed{ C=(C_{ij}) }. $$
  • Adjugate: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{adj}(A)=C^T }. $$
  • Inverse Connection: $$ \boxed{ A^{-1} = \frac1{\det(A)} \operatorname{adj}(A) } $$ when $\det(A)\neq0$.
  • Invertibility: If a cofactor expansion gives $\det(A)=0$, the square matrix is singular.
  • Nonsingular Matrix: $$ \det(A)\neq0 $$ implies $A$ is invertible.
  • Cramer's Rule: Cofactor expansion may be used to evaluate determinants appearing in Cramer's Rule.
  • Characteristic Polynomial: Cofactor expansion can sometimes simplify evaluation of: $$ \det(A-\lambda I). $$
  • Eigenvalue Calculations: Structured matrices may make cofactor expansion useful when computing characteristic polynomials.
  • Triangular Matrices: Cofactor reasoning helps establish that the determinant is the product of diagonal entries.
  • Diagonal Matrices: $$ \boxed{ \det(D)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}d_i }. $$
  • Identity Matrix: $$ \boxed{ \det(I_n)=1 }. $$
  • Zero Row: A matrix with a zero row has determinant zero; expansion along that row makes this immediate.
  • Zero Column: A matrix with a zero column also has determinant zero.
  • Repeated Rows: A square matrix with repeated rows has determinant zero.
  • Repeated Columns: The same holds for repeated columns.
  • Symbolic Matrices: Cofactor expansion is often useful when entries are symbolic rather than numerical.
  • Sparse Matrices: Matrices with many zero entries can be especially convenient for cofactor expansion.
  • Dense Large Matrices: Naive cofactor expansion is usually inefficient computationally.
  • Numerical Linear Algebra: Elimination or factorization methods are generally preferred for large numerical determinants.
  • Pedagogical Role: Cofactor expansion makes the recursive structure of the determinant explicit.
  • Theoretical Role: It is useful for proving determinant properties by induction on matrix size.
  • Linearity: Cofactor expansion displays the determinant's linear dependence on a fixed row or column.
  • Alternating Structure: The checkerboard signs encode the determinant's alternating behavior.
  • Entry Dependence: Each term in a row expansion has the form: $$ a_{ij}C_{ij}. $$
  • Cofactor Independence: When expanding along row $i$, $C_{ij}$ is computed from a matrix with row $i$ removed.
  • Sign Error Warning: The sign is determined by the location $(i,j)$, not by the numerical sign of the entry itself.
  • Negative Entry: A negative matrix entry and a negative cofactor sign may combine to produce a positive term.
  • Row vs. Column: One must keep all selected entries in the same row or the same column for a standard cofactor expansion.
  • Expansion Terminology: The determinant is expanded; the matrix itself is not being algebraically expanded in the polynomial sense.
  • Algebraic Expansion Contrast: Polynomial expansion uses distributivity, whereas cofactor expansion uses determinants, minors, and alternating signs.
  • Minor Terminology: A minor is itself a determinant, not merely the smaller matrix left after deleting a row and column.
  • Submatrix Terminology: The smaller matrix from which $M_{ij}$ is computed is often called the associated minor submatrix.
  • Laplace Expansion: The name emphasizes the general determinant-expansion theorem rather than a particular row.
  • Hand Calculation: Count zeros before choosing the expansion row or column.
  • Verification: A determinant calculated by one cofactor expansion may be checked by expanding along a different row or column.
  • Core Interpretation: Expansion by cofactors expresses a determinant recursively as a weighted sum of smaller determinants: $$ \boxed{ \det(A) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} a_{ij}(-1)^{i+j}M_{ij} }. $$ Each matrix entry is paired with a signed minor, any row or column may be chosen, and strategic use of zeros can greatly simplify hand computation.
