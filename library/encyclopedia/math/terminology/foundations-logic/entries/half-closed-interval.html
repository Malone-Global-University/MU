Half-Closed Interval

Subfield: Foundations & Logic

Etymology & Roots

A half-closed interval is an interval that contains exactly one of its two finite endpoints. The same object is also commonly called a half-open interval.

For real numbers $a<b$, there are two standard forms:

$$ \boxed{ [a,b) = \{x\in\mathbb{R}:a\leq x<b\} } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b] = \{x\in\mathbb{R}:a<x\leq b\} }. $$

The term half-closed reflects the fact that one endpoint is treated as closed, meaning included, while the other endpoint is treated as open, meaning excluded.

The alternative term half-open describes exactly the same structural fact from the opposite linguistic perspective. Neither term means that half of the numerical length is open or closed.

$$ \boxed{ \text{half-closed} = \text{half-open} = \text{exactly one endpoint included} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Left-Closed, Right-Open Interval

The interval:

$$ \boxed{ [a,b) } $$

includes $a$ and excludes $b$.

$$ \boxed{ a\leq x<b }. $$

Left-Open, Right-Closed Interval

The interval:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b] } $$

excludes $a$ and includes $b$.

$$ \boxed{ a<x\leq b }. $$

Example: $[2,5)$

The interval:

$$ [2,5) $$

contains every real number $x$ satisfying:

$$ \boxed{ 2\leq x<5 }. $$

Thus $2$ belongs to the interval, while $5$ does not.

Example: $(-3,4]$

$$ \boxed{ (-3,4] = \{x\in\mathbb{R}:-3<x\leq4\} }. $$

Here $-3$ is excluded and $4$ is included.

Bracket Meaning

A square bracket adjacent to a finite endpoint conventionally means the endpoint is included:

$$ \boxed{ [ \quad\text{or}\quad ] \Longrightarrow \text{included endpoint} }. $$

Parenthesis Meaning

A parenthesis adjacent to a finite endpoint conventionally means the endpoint is excluded:

$$ \boxed{ ( \quad\text{or}\quad ) \Longrightarrow \text{excluded endpoint} }. $$

Number-Line Representation

On a number line, an included endpoint is conventionally shown with a filled or closed point, while an excluded endpoint is shown with an open point.

Half-Closed vs. Open Interval

An open interval excludes both endpoints:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b) = \{x:a<x<b\} }. $$

Half-Closed vs. Closed Interval

A closed interval includes both endpoints:

$$ \boxed{ [a,b] = \{x:a\leq x\leq b\} }. $$

Four Standard Finite Endpoint Patterns

$$ \boxed{ (a,b),\qquad [a,b],\qquad [a,b),\qquad (a,b] }. $$

These correspond respectively to:

  • neither endpoint included;
  • both endpoints included;
  • left endpoint included only;
  • right endpoint included only.

Set-Builder Form

Interval notation can always be translated into inequalities.

$$ \boxed{ [a,b) = \{x\in\mathbb{R}:a\leq x<b\} }. $$
$$ \boxed{ (a,b] = \{x\in\mathbb{R}:a<x\leq b\} }. $$

Membership Test

To test whether a number belongs to a half-closed interval, check both boundary inequalities.

For example:

$$ 3.5\in[2,5) $$

because:

$$ 2\leq3.5<5. $$

Endpoint Membership

$$ \boxed{ a\in[a,b) } $$

while:

$$ \boxed{ b\notin[a,b) }. $$

Opposite Orientation

$$ \boxed{ a\notin(a,b] } $$

while:

$$ \boxed{ b\in(a,b] }. $$

Length

For finite $a<b$, all four intervals:

$$ (a,b),\qquad [a,b],\qquad [a,b),\qquad (a,b] $$

have the same geometric length:

$$ \boxed{ b-a }. $$

Including or excluding finitely many endpoints does not change ordinary interval length.

Cardinality

For $a<b$, each nondegenerate real interval above contains uncountably many points.

Including or excluding one or two endpoints does not change its cardinality.

Degenerate Case $a=b$

If the endpoints coincide:

$$ [a,a) $$

is empty because the conditions require:

$$ a\leq x<a, $$

which no real number satisfies.

$$ \boxed{ [a,a)=\varnothing }. $$

Other Half-Closed Degenerate Case

$$ \boxed{ (a,a]=\varnothing }. $$

Contrast with Closed Degenerate Interval

$$ \boxed{ [a,a]=\{a\} }. $$

Contrast with Open Degenerate Interval

$$ \boxed{ (a,a)=\varnothing }. $$

Intervals with Infinity

An infinite endpoint is never included because $\infty$ and $-\infty$ are not ordinary real numbers.

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ [a,\infty) = \{x\in\mathbb{R}:x\geq a\} }. $$

This interval is closed at the finite endpoint $a$ and open toward infinity.

Negative Infinity Example

$$ \boxed{ (-\infty,b] = \{x\in\mathbb{R}:x\leq b\} }. $$

Do Not Write a Closed Infinity Endpoint

In ordinary real interval notation, expressions such as:

$$ [a,\infty] $$

are not standard because $\infty$ is not a real endpoint that can belong to the set.

Solving Compound Inequalities

The inequality:

$$ 2\leq x<7 $$

is represented compactly by:

$$ \boxed{ x\in[2,7) }. $$

Reverse Translation

The interval:

$$ (-4,9] $$

translates to:

$$ \boxed{ -4<x\leq9 }. $$

Intersection Example

Consider:

$$ [1,6) \cap (3,8]. $$

Both conditions hold when:

$$ 3<x<6. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ [1,6)\cap(3,8] = (3,6) }. $$

Union Example

The adjacent intervals:

$$ [0,1) \qquad\text{and}\qquad [1,2) $$

combine without overlap at $1$:

$$ \boxed{ [0,1)\cup[1,2) = [0,2) }. $$

Why Half-Closed Intervals Are Useful in Partitions

Half-closed intervals can divide a larger interval into adjacent pieces without double-counting shared boundary points.

For example:

$$ [0,1),\ [1,2),\ [2,3),\ldots $$

are pairwise disjoint and tile the real line when extended over all integers:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{R} = \bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}} [n,n+1) }. $$

Unique Cell Assignment

Every real number $x$ belongs to exactly one interval:

$$ \boxed{ [n,n+1) } $$

where:

$$ n=\lfloor x\rfloor. $$

Floor Function Connection

The defining relation:

$$ \lfloor x\rfloor=n $$

is equivalent to:

$$ \boxed{ x\in[n,n+1) }. $$

Step Functions

Step functions often use half-closed intervals so that every boundary input is assigned exactly one output.

Piecewise Functions

A piecewise definition may use:

$$ f(x) = \begin{cases} f_1(x), & a\leq x<b,\\ f_2(x), & b\leq x<c. \end{cases} $$

The shared boundary $b$ belongs to exactly one branch.

Probability Bins

Continuous data are often grouped into bins such as:

$$ [0,10),\quad [10,20),\quad [20,30). $$

Half-closed bins prevent a value such as $10$ from belonging to two categories.

Histogram Classes

Half-open or half-closed class intervals are common in histograms and frequency tables because shared boundaries need a unique assignment rule.

Time Intervals

A time window may be represented as:

$$ [t_0,t_1) $$

to mean the start time is included while the ending instant belongs to the next interval.

Scheduling

Consecutive time blocks:

$$ [9,10),\quad [10,11),\quad [11,12) $$

meet without overlapping.

Computer Science

Half-closed integer index ranges are common because they interact naturally with counts.

The integer indices in:

$$ [0,n) $$

are:

$$ \boxed{ 0,1,\ldots,n-1 }. $$

There are exactly $n$ such integers.

Array Slices

Programming languages frequently use start-inclusive, end-exclusive ranges because the interval $[a,b)$ contains:

$$ \boxed{ b-a } $$

integer indices when $a$ and $b$ are integers with $a\leq b$.

Integer Count in $[a,b)$

If $a,b\in\mathbb{Z}$ and $a\leq b$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \#\bigl([a,b)\cap\mathbb{Z}\bigr) = b-a }. $$

Integer Count in $(a,b]$

Similarly:

$$ \boxed{ \#\bigl((a,b]\cap\mathbb{Z}\bigr) = b-a } $$

for integer $a<b$.

Modular Representatives

A half-closed interval such as:

$$ [0,n) $$

provides a natural real interval corresponding to the standard integer representatives:

$$ \boxed{ 0,1,\ldots,n-1 } $$

modulo $n$.

Fundamental Domains

In periodic settings, a half-closed interval can serve as a fundamental domain so that equivalent boundary points are not counted twice.

For a period $T>0$, one often chooses:

$$ \boxed{ [0,T) }. $$

Angles Modulo a Full Turn

Angles may be represented uniquely in a half-closed range such as:

$$ \boxed{ [0,2\pi) } $$

because $0$ and $2\pi$ represent the same direction modulo a full revolution.

Alternative Angular Convention

Another common choice is:

$$ \boxed{ (-\pi,\pi] }. $$

This includes $\pi$ while excluding $-\pi$.

Principal Arguments

Complex-number argument conventions often use a half-closed interval such as $(-\pi,\pi]$ or $[0,2\pi)$ to choose one representative angle from infinitely many coterminal possibilities.

Topology of $[a,b)$ in $\mathbb{R}$

Under the usual topology on the real line, a nondegenerate finite half-closed interval:

$$ [a,b) $$

is generally neither open nor closed in $\mathbb{R}$.

Why It Is Not Open

The included endpoint $a$ has no sufficiently small ordinary open interval around it contained entirely in $[a,b)$.

Why It Is Not Closed

The excluded endpoint $b$ is a limit point of $[a,b)$ but does not belong to the set.

Closure

$$ \boxed{ \overline{[a,b)} = [a,b] }. $$

Interior

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{int}([a,b)) = (a,b) }. $$

Boundary

$$ \boxed{ \partial[a,b) = \{a,b\} }. $$

Analogous Results for $(a,b]$

$$ \boxed{ \overline{(a,b]} = [a,b], \qquad \operatorname{int}((a,b]) = (a,b) }. $$

Relative Topology

A half-closed interval can become open or closed relative to a smaller ambient space. Openness and closedness therefore depend on the topology and ambient set being used.

Connectedness

Every interval in $\mathbb{R}$, including a half-closed interval, is connected.

Convexity

Half-closed intervals are convex subsets of $\mathbb{R}$: if two points lie in the interval, every point between them also lies in the interval.

Boundedness

A finite interval $[a,b)$ or $(a,b]$ is bounded above and below.

Maximum and Minimum of $[a,b)$

For:

$$ [a,b), $$

the minimum exists:

$$ \boxed{ \min[a,b)=a }, $$

but there is no maximum when $a<b$ because $b$ is excluded.

Supremum of $[a,b)$

$$ \boxed{ \sup[a,b)=b }. $$

Infimum of $[a,b)$

$$ \boxed{ \inf[a,b)=a }. $$

Maximum and Minimum of $(a,b]$

For:

$$ (a,b], $$

the maximum is:

$$ \boxed{ \max(a,b]=b }, $$

while there is no minimum when $a<b$.

Infimum of $(a,b]$

$$ \boxed{ \inf(a,b]=a }. $$

Supremum of $(a,b]$

$$ \boxed{ \sup(a,b]=b }. $$

Compactness in $\mathbb{R}$

A bounded half-closed interval such as $[a,b)$ is not compact in the usual real topology because it is not closed.

Heine-Borel Contrast

In $\mathbb{R}$, a set is compact exactly when it is closed and bounded. Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ [a,b] \text{ is compact} } $$

while:

$$ \boxed{ [a,b) \text{ is not compact} } $$

for $a<b$.

Measure

In standard length measure:

$$ \boxed{ m([a,b)) = b-a }. $$

Endpoints have measure zero, so their inclusion or exclusion does not alter the interval's length.

Integration

For ordinary Riemann or Lebesgue integration of a suitable function, changing inclusion of a finite endpoint does not change the value of the integral:

$$ \boxed{ \int_{[a,b)}f = \int_{[a,b]}f } $$

under the usual conditions and interpretation, because a single endpoint has measure zero.

Probability

For a continuous random variable, endpoint inclusion often does not affect probability because single points commonly have probability zero.

For example, in a continuous distribution:

$$ P(a\leq X<b) = P(a<X\leq b) $$

when $P(X=a)=P(X=b)=0$.

Discrete Probability Caution

For discrete random variables, endpoint inclusion can change probability because a single endpoint may carry positive probability.

Domains and Ranges

Functions may naturally have half-closed domains or ranges.

For example, a periodic parameter may be restricted to:

$$ \boxed{ 0\leq\theta<2\pi }. $$

Inverse Trigonometric Conventions

Principal-value ranges are selected to make inverse relations into functions. Some such conventions use intervals whose endpoint choices are essential for uniqueness.

Partitions in Analysis

Half-closed intervals are useful when decomposing a domain into disjoint pieces whose union reconstructs the original set.

Measure-Theoretic Partitions

Collections such as:

$$ [a_0,a_1),\quad [a_1,a_2),\quad \ldots $$

avoid overlap at internal boundary points.

Probability Class Intervals

Half-closed bins establish a deterministic convention for assigning observations lying exactly on class boundaries.

Common Error: Including Both Endpoints

The interval:

$$ [a,b) $$

does not include $b$.

Common Error: Excluding Both Endpoints

A half-closed interval includes exactly one finite endpoint. If both are excluded, the interval is open:

$$ (a,b). $$

Common Error: Thinking Half-Closed Means Half the Length Is Closed

"Half" refers to the two endpoint conditions, not to any geometric subdivision of the interval.

Common Error: Treating Half-Open and Half-Closed as Different Objects

The phrases usually describe the same interval type.

Common Error: Using a Bracket at Infinity

Since infinity is not a real number, it is not included as an endpoint in ordinary real interval notation.

Common Error: Confusing Supremum with Maximum

For:

$$ [a,b), $$

$b$ is the supremum but not a maximum because:

$$ b\notin[a,b). $$

Common Error: Assuming $[a,b)$ Is Closed Topologically

The word closed in half-closed describes one endpoint convention. It does not mean the set is a closed subset of $\mathbb{R}$.

Terminological Caution: Interval "Closedness"

In elementary interval notation, closed endpoint means included. In topology, a closed set is defined through complements or limit points. These meanings are related historically but should not be conflated.

Common Misconception: A half-closed interval is not "partly closed" in the topological sense. It simply includes one finite endpoint and excludes the other. Thus $[a,b)$ and $(a,b]$ are half-closed or half-open intervals, while in the ordinary topology of $\mathbb{R}$ they are generally neither open nor closed when $a<b$.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$[a,b)$ "The interval from a inclusive to b exclusive."
$(a,b]$ "The interval from a exclusive to b inclusive."
$a\leq x<b$ "X is at least a and less than b."
$a<x\leq b$ "X is greater than a and at most b."
$x\in[a,b)$ "X belongs to the half-closed interval from a to b, including a."
$[0,2\pi)$ "Zero inclusive to two pi exclusive."
$(-\pi,\pi]$ "Negative pi exclusive to pi inclusive."

Common spoken alternatives include:

  • "left-closed, right-open";
  • "closed on the left and open on the right";
  • "left-inclusive, right-exclusive";
  • "half-open";
  • "half-closed."

For $(a,b]$, the corresponding language reverses left and right.

Defense Notation Advice: Read each endpoint independently. Square brackets mean inclusion for finite endpoints; parentheses mean exclusion. Translate to inequalities whenever there is doubt. Use parentheses with $\pm\infty$, and do not infer topological closedness merely from the phrase half-closed interval.
