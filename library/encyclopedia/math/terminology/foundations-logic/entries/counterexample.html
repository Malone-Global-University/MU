Counterexample

Subfield: Foundations & Logic

Etymology & Roots

A counterexample is a specific example that demonstrates that a proposed universal statement, conjecture, implication, or general claim is false.

The word combines counter-, meaning "against," "opposite," or "contrary to," with example. A counterexample is therefore an example that stands against a claim by showing that the claim fails in at least one case.

Counterexamples are especially powerful in mathematics because a universal statement asserts that something holds for every object in a specified domain. To disprove such a statement, it is enough to exhibit one object for which the conclusion fails.

$$ \boxed{ \text{one valid counterexample} \Longrightarrow \text{universal claim is false} }. $$

Counterexamples play a central role in logic, proof, conjecture testing, definition refinement, theorem development, and mathematical problem solving.

Operational Nuance

Universal Statements

A universal statement has the logical form:

$$ \boxed{ \forall x\in D,\;P(x) }. $$

This means:

$$ \text{"For every }x\text{ in the domain }D,\;P(x)\text{ is true."} $$

To disprove the statement, it is enough to find some:

$$ a\in D $$

such that:

$$ \boxed{ \neg P(a) }. $$

That value $a$ is a counterexample.

Logical Negation

The negation of:

$$ \forall x\in D,\;P(x) $$

is:

$$ \boxed{ \exists x\in D \text{ such that } \neg P(x) }. $$

This explains why one counterexample is sufficient: the logical negation of a universal statement requires only one case in which the property fails.

Simple Numerical Example

Consider the false statement:

$$ \text{"Every prime number is odd."} $$

The number:

$$ \boxed{2} $$

is prime but not odd. Therefore $2$ is a counterexample, and the universal statement is false.

Algebraic Example

Consider the claim:

$$ (a+b)^2=a^2+b^2 $$

for all real numbers $a$ and $b$.

Choose:

$$ a=1,\qquad b=1. $$

Then:

$$ (1+1)^2=4, $$

while:

$$ 1^2+1^2=2. $$

Since:

$$ 4\neq2, $$

the pair:

$$ \boxed{(a,b)=(1,1)} $$

is a counterexample.

Counterexample to an Implication

A conditional statement:

$$ P\rightarrow Q $$

is false precisely when:

$$ \boxed{ P\text{ is true and }Q\text{ is false} }. $$

Therefore, a counterexample to an implication must satisfy the hypothesis while violating the conclusion.

Example: Implication

Consider:

$$ \text{"If an integer is divisible by }4,\text{ then it is divisible by }8." $$

Choose:

$$ n=4. $$

Then $4$ is divisible by $4$, so the hypothesis is true, but $4$ is not divisible by $8$, so the conclusion is false.

Thus:

$$ \boxed{4} $$

is a counterexample.

A Failed Hypothesis Is Not a Counterexample

Suppose the claim is:

$$ x>5\Longrightarrow x^2>25. $$

Choosing:

$$ x=2 $$

does not provide a counterexample, because the hypothesis:

$$ x>5 $$

is already false.

A genuine counterexample to a conditional statement must make the hypothesis true and the conclusion false.

Counterexamples and Conjectures

A conjecture is a mathematical statement believed to be true but not yet proved. Testing examples can provide evidence for a conjecture, but no finite collection of successful examples proves an unrestricted universal claim.

A single counterexample, however, disproves it.

$$ \boxed{ \text{many confirming examples} \not\Rightarrow \text{proof} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{one counterexample} \Rightarrow \text{disproof} }. $$

Counterexamples and Definitions

Counterexamples are often used to test whether a proposed definition is too broad or too narrow. If a definition unintentionally includes an object that should be excluded, that object functions as a counterexample to the proposed formulation.

This process frequently leads mathematicians to refine hypotheses, add restrictions, or distinguish related concepts more carefully.

Boundary Cases

Effective counterexamples are often found at boundaries, special values, or degenerate cases such as:

$$ 0,\qquad1,\qquad-1, $$

empty sets, constant functions, repeated roots, vertical lines, or other structurally unusual objects.

Counterexample vs. Exception

An exception may simply be a case excluded from a stated rule. A counterexample specifically disproves a claim as it was stated.

If a theorem already says:

$$ P(x)\text{ for all }x\neq0, $$

then $x=0$ is not a counterexample because it lies outside the stated domain.

Common Misconception: A value that violates the conclusion is not automatically a counterexample. It must also satisfy every relevant hypothesis and belong to the domain under consideration.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\forall x\in D,\;P(x)$ "For every x in D, P of x holds."
$\exists x\in D:\neg P(x)$ "There exists an x in D for which P of x is false."
$P\rightarrow Q$ "If P, then Q."
$P\land\neg Q$ "P is true and Q is false."
$a\in D$ "A is an element of D."
$P(a)=\text{false}$ "P of a is false."

A counterexample is usually stated explicitly in prose:

$$ \text{"The statement is false; }x=2\text{ is a counterexample."} $$

For an implication, a rigorous counterexample should make clear both parts:

$$ \boxed{ P(a)\text{ is true} \quad\text{and}\quad Q(a)\text{ is false} }. $$

For example:

$$ \text{"Take }n=4. \text{ Then }4\mid4 \text{ but }8\nmid4. \text{ Hence }4\text{ is a counterexample."} $$

Defense Notation Advice: A counterexample should identify the object, verify that it satisfies the hypotheses, and show precisely where the claimed conclusion fails. Merely asserting that a statement is false is not the same as supplying a counterexample.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Universal Statement: To disprove: $$ \forall x\in D,\;P(x), $$ it is enough to find: $$ \boxed{ a\in D \text{ such that } \neg P(a) }. $$
  • Existential Negation: $$ \boxed{ \neg(\forall x\,P(x)) \equiv \exists x\,\neg P(x) }. $$ This logical equivalence explains the force of a counterexample.
  • Conditional Statement: A counterexample to: $$ P\rightarrow Q $$ requires: $$ \boxed{ P\land\neg Q }. $$
  • Number Theory: The statement: $$ \text{"Every prime is odd"} $$ is false because: $$ \boxed{2} $$ is prime and even.
  • Algebra: The claim: $$ (a+b)^2=a^2+b^2 $$ is disproved by: $$ a=b=1. $$
  • Absolute Value: The claim: $$ |a+b|=|a|+|b| $$ for all real $a,b$ is false. Choose: $$ a=1,\qquad b=-1. $$ Then: $$ |a+b|=0 $$ but: $$ |a|+|b|=2. $$
  • Square Roots: The claim: $$ \sqrt{a+b} = \sqrt a+\sqrt b $$ for all nonnegative $a,b$ is false. For example: $$ a=b=1 $$ gives: $$ \sqrt2\neq2. $$
  • Division: Claims involving division should be tested at values where denominators may become zero. Such cases often reveal missing domain restrictions.
  • Geometry: A single geometric configuration violating a claimed universal property can serve as a counterexample.
  • Quadrilaterals: The statement: $$ \text{"Every rectangle is a square"} $$ is false. A $2$-by-$3$ rectangle is a counterexample.
  • Triangles: The claim: $$ \text{"Every triangle is acute"} $$ is disproved by any right or obtuse triangle.
  • Functions: To disprove a claim about all functions, one may test especially simple functions such as: $$ f(x)=0,\qquad f(x)=x,\qquad f(x)=x^2. $$
  • Continuity: A discontinuous function can disprove a statement that mistakenly assumes a property holds for all functions rather than only continuous ones.
  • Differentiability: The function: $$ f(x)=|x| $$ is a standard counterexample to the converse of: $$ \text{"Differentiable implies continuous."} $$ It is continuous at $0$ but not differentiable there.
  • Sequences: A carefully chosen sequence may disprove a proposed implication involving convergence, boundedness, or monotonicity.
  • Bounded Sequences: The sequence: $$ a_n=(-1)^n $$ is bounded but not convergent, providing a counterexample to: $$ \text{"Every bounded sequence converges."} $$
  • Series: The harmonic series: $$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1n $$ is a standard counterexample to the claim that terms approaching zero are sufficient for series convergence.
  • Set Theory: Small finite sets are often useful for testing statements involving unions, intersections, complements, subsets, functions, and cardinality.
  • Relations: A relation satisfying some but not all required properties can provide a counterexample to a proposed classification.
  • Functions and Inverses: A non-injective function can serve as a counterexample to the claim that every function has an inverse function on its entire codomain.
  • Linear Algebra: Small matrices, especially $2\times2$ matrices, are frequently used to construct counterexamples.
  • Matrix Multiplication: The claim: $$ AB=BA $$ for all matrices is false. For example: $$ A= \begin{bmatrix} 1&1\\ 0&0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad B= \begin{bmatrix} 0&0\\ 1&0 \end{bmatrix} $$ satisfy: $$ AB\neq BA. $$
  • Abstract Algebra: Counterexamples distinguish properties such as commutativity, associativity, invertibility, normality, and distributivity by exhibiting structures in which a proposed implication fails.
  • Topology: Counterexamples are especially important in topology because many properties that appear similar under familiar Euclidean examples become distinct in more general spaces.
  • Probability: A probability distribution or dependence structure may serve as a counterexample to an overly broad claim about independence, expectation, variance, or convergence.
  • Uncorrelated vs. Independent: There exist random variables that are uncorrelated but dependent, providing counterexamples to the false converse: $$ \text{"Uncorrelated implies independent."} $$
  • Computer-Assisted Search: Computers may be used to search finite domains for counterexamples to conjectures. Finding one proves the conjecture false; failing to find one does not generally prove it true.
  • Minimal Counterexample: A minimal counterexample is a counterexample chosen to be smallest according to some ordering or measure. Assuming such a counterexample exists can itself form the basis of a proof by contradiction.
  • Counterexample-Guided Refinement: When a statement fails, mathematicians often examine the counterexample to identify a missing hypothesis and formulate a corrected theorem.
  • Special Cases: Useful places to search for counterexamples include: $$ 0,\quad1,\quad-1, $$ empty sets, singleton sets, constant functions, zero matrices, identity matrices, repeated roots, and boundary values.
  • Proof vs. Disproof: A proof of a universal statement must account for every admissible case. A disproof by counterexample needs only one admissible failing case.
  • Examples Do Not Prove Universality: Testing: $$ 1,2,3,\ldots,1000 $$ successfully does not by itself prove a claim for all natural numbers. The next value could still fail.
  • Domain Requirement: An alleged counterexample must belong to the domain specified by the statement. Objects outside that domain are irrelevant to the claim.
  • Hypothesis Requirement: For: $$ P(x)\rightarrow Q(x), $$ an object for which $P(x)$ is false cannot disprove the implication.
  • Terminological Contrast: An example illustrates a concept. A nonexample shows an object that does not satisfy a definition. A counterexample specifically falsifies a mathematical claim.
  • Core Interpretation: Counterexamples exploit the logical asymmetry between proof and disproof: $$ \boxed{ \forall x\,P(x) \text{ is defeated by one } a \text{ with } \neg P(a) }. $$
