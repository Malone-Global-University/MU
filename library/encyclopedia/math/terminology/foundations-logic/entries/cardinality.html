Cardinality

Subfield: Foundations & Mathematical Logic

Etymology & Roots

Cardinality is the mathematical measure of the size of a set. For a finite set, its cardinality is simply the number of distinct elements it contains.

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{cardinality} = \text{number of elements in a set} } $$

If:

$$ A=\{2,4,6,8\}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ |A|=4 }. $$

The notation $|A|$ is read "the cardinality of $A$" or "the size of $A$."

In set theory, cardinality extends this idea to infinite collections. Infinite sets are compared by determining whether their elements can be placed in one-to-one correspondence.

Operational Nuance

Finite Cardinality

A finite set $A$ has cardinality $n$ if it can be placed in one-to-one correspondence with:

$$ \boxed{ \{1,2,\ldots,n\} }. $$

Thus:

$$ |A|=n. $$

Empty Set

The empty set has no elements:

$$ \varnothing=\{\}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ |\varnothing|=0 }. $$

Singleton

A set containing exactly one element is a singleton:

$$ A=\{x\}. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ |A|=1 }. $$

Distinct Elements Matter

Cardinality counts distinct set elements, not repeated written entries.

$$ \{1,1,2,2,3\} = \{1,2,3\}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ |\{1,1,2,2,3\}|=3 }. $$

Order Does Not Matter

Since ordinary sets are unordered:

$$ \{a,b,c\} = \{c,b,a\}, $$

and both have cardinality $3$.

Cardinality Is Independent of Element Magnitude

The numerical size of elements does not affect set size.

$$ |\{1,2,3\}| = |\{100,1000,1000000\}| = 3. $$

Equal Cardinality

Two sets $A$ and $B$ have the same cardinality precisely when there exists a bijection:

$$ \boxed{ |A|=|B| \iff \exists\text{ bijection }f:A\to B }. $$

Why Bijections Matter

A bijection pairs every element of $A$ with exactly one element of $B$, with no element left unmatched on either side.

Thus bijection provides a notion of "same number of elements" that works even when ordinary counting cannot terminate.

Finite Example

Let:

$$ A=\{p,q,r\}, \qquad B=\{10,20,30\}. $$

The map:

$$ p\mapsto10,\qquad q\mapsto20,\qquad r\mapsto30 $$

is a bijection, so:

$$ \boxed{ |A|=|B|=3 }. $$

Comparing Cardinalities with Injections

One writes:

$$ \boxed{ |A|\le|B| } $$

when there exists an injective function:

$$ f:A\to B. $$

Intuitively, each element of $A$ can be assigned a distinct element of $B$.

Strictly Smaller Cardinality

One writes:

$$ \boxed{ |A|<|B| } $$

when $|A|\le|B|$ but $A$ and $B$ do not have equal cardinality.

Finite Proper Subsets

If $B$ is a proper subset of a finite set $A$:

$$ B\subsetneq A, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ |B|<|A| }. $$

Infinite Proper Subsets Behave Differently

For infinite sets, a proper subset may have the same cardinality as the whole set.

Let:

$$ \mathbb N=\{1,2,3,\ldots\} $$

and:

$$ E=\{2,4,6,\ldots\}. $$

Although:

$$ E\subsetneq\mathbb N, $$

the map:

$$ \boxed{ f(n)=2n } $$

is a bijection, so:

$$ \boxed{ |E|=|\mathbb N| }. $$

Countably Infinite Cardinality

A set has countably infinite cardinality when it is in bijection with the natural numbers.

$$ \boxed{ |A|=\aleph_0 }. $$

The symbol $\aleph_0$ denotes the cardinality of $\mathbb N$.

Natural Numbers

$$ \boxed{ |\mathbb N|=\aleph_0 }. $$

Integers

The integers can be listed:

$$ 0,1,-1,2,-2,3,-3,\ldots $$

and therefore:

$$ \boxed{ |\mathbb Z|=\aleph_0 }. $$

Rational Numbers

Although the rationals are densely distributed on the number line, they are also countably infinite:

$$ \boxed{ |\mathbb Q|=\aleph_0 }. $$

Density Is Not Cardinality

A set may be dense in an interval without having the same cardinality as the real numbers.

The rationals are dense in $\mathbb R$, yet:

$$ \boxed{ |\mathbb Q| < |\mathbb R| }. $$

Uncountable Cardinality

A set is uncountable when it cannot be placed in bijection with $\mathbb N$.

The real numbers are uncountable:

$$ \boxed{ |\mathbb R|>\aleph_0 }. $$

Cardinality of the Continuum

The cardinality of $\mathbb R$ is commonly denoted:

$$ \boxed{ \mathfrak c=|\mathbb R| }. $$

This is called the cardinality of the continuum.

Intervals and the Continuum

Any nondegenerate real interval has the same cardinality as $\mathbb R$.

$$ \boxed{ |(0,1)| = |[0,1]| = |\mathbb R| = \mathfrak c }. $$

Thus restricting the real line to a finite interval does not reduce its cardinality.

Power Sets

The power set $\mathcal P(A)$ consists of every subset of $A$.

If:

$$ |A|=n $$

for finite $n$, then:

$$ \boxed{ |\mathcal P(A)|=2^n }. $$

Example of a Power Set

If:

$$ A=\{a,b\}, $$

then:

$$ \mathcal P(A) = \{\varnothing,\{a\},\{b\},\{a,b\}\}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ |\mathcal P(A)|=4 }. $$

Cantor's Theorem

For every set $A$:

$$ \boxed{ |A| < |\mathcal P(A)| }. $$

Thus the power set always has strictly greater cardinality than the original set.

No Largest Cardinality

Since $\mathcal P(A)$ always has greater cardinality than $A$, there can be no largest cardinal number.

$$ \boxed{ \kappa < 2^\kappa } $$

in the corresponding cardinal-arithmetic notation.

Cardinality of a Cartesian Product

For finite sets:

$$ \boxed{ |A\times B| = |A||B| }. $$

For example, if $|A|=3$ and $|B|=4$, then:

$$ |A\times B|=12. $$

Number of Functions between Finite Sets

If:

$$ |A|=m, \qquad |B|=n, $$

then the set of all functions from $B$ to $A$ has cardinality:

$$ \boxed{ m^n }. $$

Finite Unions

If finite sets $A$ and $B$ are disjoint:

$$ A\cap B=\varnothing, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ |A\cup B| = |A|+|B| }. $$

Inclusion-Exclusion

If $A$ and $B$ overlap:

$$ \boxed{ |A\cup B| = |A| + |B| - |A\cap B| }. $$

Infinite Cardinal Addition

Infinite cardinal arithmetic can differ dramatically from finite arithmetic.

$$ \boxed{ \aleph_0+\aleph_0 = \aleph_0 }. $$

Two disjoint countably infinite sets together remain countably infinite.

Infinite Cardinal Multiplication

$$ \boxed{ \aleph_0\cdot\aleph_0 = \aleph_0 }. $$

This helps explain why $\mathbb N\times\mathbb N$ is still countable.

Cardinality of Sequences

The set of all infinite binary sequences:

$$ \{0,1\}^{\mathbb N} $$

has cardinality:

$$ \boxed{ 2^{\aleph_0} }. $$

This cardinality is equal to the cardinality of the continuum:

$$ \boxed{ 2^{\aleph_0} = \mathfrak c }. $$

Cardinality versus Measure

Cardinality and geometric measure are different concepts.

For example:

$$ [0,1] $$

and:

$$ [0,100] $$

have different lengths but the same cardinality:

$$ \boxed{ |[0,1]| = |[0,100]| = \mathfrak c }. $$

Cardinality versus Density

Density describes how a set is distributed in a space, while cardinality describes only how many elements it has in the set-theoretic sense.

Cardinality versus Dimension

Dimension and cardinality are also different.

The plane and the real line have the same cardinality:

$$ \boxed{ |\mathbb R^2| = |\mathbb R| }. $$

yet they have different Euclidean dimensions.

Cardinality of Finite Sequences

For a finite sequence:

$$ (a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n), $$

the number of positions is $n$, but this should not automatically be confused with the cardinality of the set of distinct values:

$$ \{a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n\}. $$

Example with Repetition

The sequence:

$$ (1,1,2,3,3) $$

has five terms, while its value set:

$$ \{1,2,3\} $$

has cardinality:

$$ \boxed{ 3 }. $$

Cardinality versus Cardinal Number

Cardinality refers to the size property of a set. A cardinal number is an abstract number used to represent such a size.

$$ \boxed{ \text{cardinality of }A = |A| } $$

Thus the two terms are closely related but are not grammatically identical.

Cardinality versus Ordinality

Cardinality disregards order and asks how many elements there are.

Ordinal concepts describe ordering or position.

$$ \boxed{ \text{cardinality} \rightarrow \text{size} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{ordinality} \rightarrow \text{order or position} }. $$

Common Misconception: Infinite Means One Size

Infinite sets do not all have equal cardinality.

$$ \boxed{ |\mathbb N| < |\mathbb R| }. $$

Common Misconception: Proper Subset Means Smaller Cardinality

This is guaranteed for finite sets but not for infinite sets.

Common Misconception: More Spread Means More Cardinality

A set can occupy a larger region geometrically without having a larger cardinality.

Common Misconception: Equal Cardinality Means Same Structure

Two sets can have equal cardinality while differing completely in geometry, algebraic structure, topology, order, or meaning.

$$ \boxed{ |A|=|B| \not\Rightarrow A\text{ and }B\text{ are otherwise structurally identical} }. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$|A|$ "The cardinality of A," or "the size of A."
$|A|=5$ "A has cardinality five."
$|A|=|B|$ "A and B have the same cardinality."
$|\mathbb N|=\aleph_0$ "The natural numbers have cardinality aleph-null."
$|\mathbb R|=\mathfrak c$ "The real numbers have cardinality continuum."
$|A|<|B|$ "A has strictly smaller cardinality than B."

The vertical bars in $|A|$ denote cardinality when $A$ is a set. The same symbols may denote absolute value, determinant, or norm in other contexts, so the type of object inside the bars is important.

Defense Notation Advice: For finite sets, count distinct elements. For infinite sets, use mappings—especially injections and bijections—to compare size. Do not infer cardinality from spatial extent, density, order, or the numerical magnitude of the elements.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: Cardinality is the size of a set.
  • Primary Notation: $$ \boxed{ |A| }. $$
  • Alternative Notation: $\operatorname{card}(A)$.
  • Empty Set: $$ \boxed{ |\varnothing|=0 }. $$
  • Finite Set: Cardinality equals the number of distinct elements.
  • Repeated Entries: Do not increase cardinality in ordinary set notation.
  • Equal Cardinality: Established by a bijection.
  • Cardinal Comparison: An injection $A\to B$ establishes $|A|\le|B|$.
  • Countably Infinite: $$ \boxed{ |A|=\aleph_0 } $$ when $A$ is in bijection with $\mathbb N$.
  • Natural Numbers: $|\mathbb N|=\aleph_0$.
  • Integers: $|\mathbb Z|=\aleph_0$.
  • Rationals: $|\mathbb Q|=\aleph_0$.
  • Real Numbers: $|\mathbb R|>\aleph_0$.
  • Continuum: $$ \boxed{ |\mathbb R|=\mathfrak c }. $$
  • Intervals: Every nondegenerate real interval has cardinality $\mathfrak c$.
  • Power Set: $\mathcal P(A)$ always has strictly greater cardinality than $A$.
  • Cantor's Theorem: $$ \boxed{ |A|<|\mathcal P(A)| }. $$
  • No Largest Cardinal: The power-set operation always produces a larger cardinality.
  • Finite Cartesian Product: $$ \boxed{ |A\times B| = |A||B| }. $$
  • Finite Union: For disjoint finite sets, $|A\cup B|=|A|+|B|$.
  • Inclusion-Exclusion: $$ \boxed{ |A\cup B| = |A|+|B|-|A\cap B| }. $$
  • Infinite Arithmetic: $\aleph_0+\aleph_0=\aleph_0$ and $\aleph_0\cdot\aleph_0=\aleph_0$.
  • Cardinality versus Measure: Equal cardinality does not imply equal length, area, volume, or measure.
  • Cardinality versus Density: Density in a space does not determine cardinality.
  • Cardinality versus Dimension: Sets of different geometric dimensions may have equal cardinality.
  • Cardinality versus Sequence Length: The number of sequence positions and the number of distinct values may differ.
  • Cardinal Number Contrast: Cardinality is the size property; a cardinal number represents that size.
  • Ordinal Contrast: Cardinality concerns how many; ordinality concerns order or position.
  • Common Error: Do not assume all infinite sets have the same cardinality.
  • Common Error: Do not assume every proper subset has strictly smaller cardinality.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse cardinality with physical or geometric size.
  • Common Error: Do not infer equal mathematical structure from equal cardinality.
  • Terminological Contrast: Cardinality is the size of a set. Cardinal number is an abstract number representing that size. Finite cardinality is represented by a nonnegative integer. Countably infinite means cardinality $\aleph_0$. Uncountable means not in bijection with $\mathbb N$. Continuum cardinality is the cardinality $\mathfrak c$ of the real numbers. Bijection establishes equal cardinality. Injection establishes a cardinal inequality.
