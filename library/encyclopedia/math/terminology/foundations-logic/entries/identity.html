Identity

Subfield: Foundations & Logic

Etymology & Roots

In mathematics, identity expresses some form of sameness that remains valid under the relevant structure. Depending on context, the word may refer to an equation that is always true, an element that leaves other elements unchanged under an operation, a function that returns every input unchanged, or a transformation that does nothing.

The word ultimately derives from Latin roots associated with sameness or being the same. This linguistic idea appears directly in several mathematical meanings.

$$ \boxed{ \text{identity} \longrightarrow \text{preservation of sameness} }. $$

One of the most common meanings is an identity equation: an equality true for every permitted value of its variables.

$$ \boxed{ (x+1)^2 = x^2+2x+1 } $$

is an identity because both sides are equal for every real or complex value of $x$.

The same word appears in structural mathematics through identity elements, identity functions, identity matrices, and identity transformations. Although these meanings differ operationally, each preserves an object unchanged.

Operational Nuance

Identity as a Universally Valid Equation

An equation in a variable is an identity when it holds for every value in the stated domain for which both sides are defined.

$$ \boxed{ L(x)=R(x) \text{ for all admissible }x }. $$

Example

$$ \boxed{ (a+b)^2 = a^2+2ab+b^2 }. $$

This holds for all values of $a$ and $b$ in ordinary commutative number systems such as $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}$.

Identity vs. Conditional Equation

Compare:

$$ 2(x+3)=2x+6 $$

with:

$$ 2x+3=11. $$

The first is an identity. The second is true only when:

$$ \boxed{ x=4 }. $$

Identity vs. Contradiction

An identity is true throughout its permitted domain. A contradiction is false for every permitted value.

For example:

$$ x+1=x+2 $$

simplifies to:

$$ 1=2, $$

so it has no solutions.

Identity after Simplification

An equation such as:

$$ 3(x-2)+6=3x $$

simplifies to:

$$ \boxed{ 3x=3x }. $$

This signals an identity.

Domain Restrictions Still Matter

An identity may be valid only where both sides are defined.

For example:

$$ \frac{x^2-1}{x-1} = x+1 $$

is valid only when:

$$ \boxed{ x\neq1 }. $$

It is therefore an identity on the restricted domain $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{1\}$, but not an equality of functions if the left side is treated as undefined at $x=1$ while the right side is defined there.

Algebraic Identity

An algebraic identity is an equality that follows from algebraic laws and remains true throughout the relevant domain.

Difference of Squares

$$ \boxed{ a^2-b^2 = (a-b)(a+b) }. $$

Square of a Sum

$$ \boxed{ (a+b)^2 = a^2+2ab+b^2 }. $$

Square of a Difference

$$ \boxed{ (a-b)^2 = a^2-2ab+b^2 }. $$

Sum of Cubes

$$ \boxed{ a^3+b^3 = (a+b)(a^2-ab+b^2) }. $$

Difference of Cubes

$$ \boxed{ a^3-b^3 = (a-b)(a^2+ab+b^2) }. $$

Rational Identity

Identities involving rational expressions require attention to denominator restrictions.

$$ \frac{1}{x} + \frac{1}{y} = \frac{x+y}{xy} $$

provided:

$$ \boxed{ x\neq0,\qquad y\neq0 }. $$

Exponent Identity

Under ordinary exponent-law conditions:

$$ \boxed{ a^m a^n = a^{m+n} }. $$

Logarithmic Identity

For positive real $x$ and $y$:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(xy) = \log_bx+\log_by } $$

where:

$$ b>0,\qquad b\neq1. $$

Trigonometric Identity

A trigonometric identity is an equation involving trigonometric functions that holds throughout its common domain.

Pythagorean Identity

$$ \boxed{ \sin^2x+\cos^2x=1 }. $$

Reciprocal Identities

$$ \boxed{ \sec x=\frac{1}{\cos x}, \qquad \csc x=\frac{1}{\sin x}, \qquad \cot x=\frac{\cos x}{\sin x} } $$

wherever the denominators are nonzero.

Quotient Identity

$$ \boxed{ \tan x = \frac{\sin x}{\cos x} } $$

wherever $\cos x\neq0$.

Double-Angle Identity

$$ \boxed{ \sin(2x)=2\sin x\cos x }. $$

Identity Verification

To verify an identity, one normally transforms one side using valid algebraic or trigonometric rules until it matches the other side.

Do Not "Solve" an Identity in the Ordinary Sense

An identity is not usually asking for isolated variable values. The aim is to prove equality throughout a domain.

Identity Element

In an algebraic structure with operation $\ast$, an identity element is an element $e$ satisfying:

$$ \boxed{ e\ast a = a\ast e = a } $$

for every element $a$ in the structure.

Additive Identity

For ordinary addition:

$$ \boxed{ a+0 = 0+a = a }. $$

Therefore $0$ is the additive identity.

Multiplicative Identity

For ordinary multiplication:

$$ \boxed{ a\cdot1 = 1\cdot a = a }. $$

Therefore $1$ is the multiplicative identity.

Identity Element Is Operation-Dependent

The same set may have different identity elements for different operations.

$$ \boxed{ 0 \text{ is the additive identity} } $$
$$ \boxed{ 1 \text{ is the multiplicative identity} }. $$

Uniqueness of Identity Element

If an identity element exists for a given binary operation, it is unique.

Suppose $e$ and $f$ are both identities. Then:

$$ e=e\ast f=f. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ e=f }. $$

One-Sided Identity

In more general algebraic systems, one may distinguish a left identity:

$$ e\ast a=a, $$

from a right identity:

$$ a\ast e=a. $$

A two-sided identity satisfies both.

Identity Function

The identity function on a set $S$ is:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{id}_S(x)=x } $$

for every $x\in S$.

Identity Function Leaves Inputs Unchanged

$$ \boxed{ x\mapsto x }. $$

Composition with the Identity Function

If $f:A\to B$, then:

$$ \boxed{ f\circ\operatorname{id}_A = f } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{id}_B\circ f = f }. $$

Composition Identity

Thus the identity function plays the role of identity element for function composition.

Inverse Function Relation

If $f$ is invertible:

$$ \boxed{ f^{-1}\circ f = \operatorname{id} } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ f\circ f^{-1} = \operatorname{id} }. $$

Identity Matrix

The $n\times n$ identity matrix is:

$$ \boxed{ I_n = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0\\ 0 & 1 & \cdots & 0\\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 \end{pmatrix} }. $$

Matrix Multiplicative Identity

For a compatible matrix $A$:

$$ \boxed{ I_mA=A } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ AI_n=A }. $$

Identity Matrix on Vectors

$$ \boxed{ I_n\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x} }. $$

Inverse Matrix Relation

A square matrix $A$ is invertible when there exists $A^{-1}$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I }. $$

Identity Transformation

The identity transformation on a vector space $V$ sends every vector to itself:

$$ \boxed{ I_V(\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{v} }. $$

Geometric Identity Transformation

In transformation geometry, the identity transformation moves no point:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(x,y) }. $$

Identity Transformation as Neutral Motion

Composing any transformation $T$ with the identity transformation leaves $T$ unchanged:

$$ \boxed{ T\circ I = I\circ T = T }. $$

Identity Permutation

In permutation groups, the identity permutation fixes every element:

$$ \boxed{ e(x)=x }. $$

Identity in Group Theory

Every group contains a unique identity element $e$ satisfying:

$$ \boxed{ eg=ge=g } $$

for every group element $g$.

Inverse Relative to Identity

For a group element $g$, its inverse $g^{-1}$ satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ gg^{-1} = g^{-1}g = e }. $$

Identity in Rings

A ring may have a multiplicative identity, often denoted $1$, satisfying:

$$ \boxed{ 1a=a1=a }. $$

Definitions of ring vary by textbook: some require a multiplicative identity and some do not.

Identity in Boolean Algebra

Boolean operations possess identity laws such as:

$$ \boxed{ x\lor0=x } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ x\land1=x }. $$

Logical Identity

In logic, an identity law may describe equivalences that preserve the truth value of a proposition.

$$ \boxed{ p\land\text{True} \equiv p } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ p\lor\text{False} \equiv p }. $$

Set-Theoretic Identity Laws

Under union:

$$ \boxed{ A\cup\varnothing=A }. $$

Thus $\varnothing$ acts as the identity element for union.

Relative to a universal set $U$, under intersection:

$$ \boxed{ A\cap U=A }. $$

Thus $U$ acts as the identity for intersection.

Identity Relation

On a set $S$, the identity relation is:

$$ \boxed{ I_S = \{(x,x):x\in S\} }. $$

It relates each element only to itself.

Equality Relation

The identity relation corresponds naturally to equality on a set.

Kronecker Delta as Identity Components

The identity matrix may be described by:

$$ \boxed{ (I_n)_{ij} = \delta_{ij} } $$

where:

$$ \delta_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1, & i=j,\\ 0, & i\neq j. \end{cases} $$

Identity Operator

In functional analysis and operator theory, the identity operator satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ Ix=x }. $$

Eigenvalues of the Identity Matrix

Every nonzero vector is an eigenvector of $I$ with eigenvalue $1$:

$$ \boxed{ I\mathbf{v} = 1\mathbf{v} }. $$

Determinant of the Identity Matrix

$$ \boxed{ \det(I_n)=1 }. $$

Trace of the Identity Matrix

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{tr}(I_n)=n }. $$

Powers of the Identity Matrix

$$ \boxed{ I_n^k=I_n } $$

for every positive integer $k$.

Identity under Exponentiation

In multiplicative structures:

$$ \boxed{ a^0=1 } $$

for nonzero $a$ in familiar number systems, because exponent zero returns the multiplicative identity.

Identity and Inverse

An inverse undoes an operation by returning the identity element.

$$ \boxed{ a+(-a)=0 } $$

uses the additive identity, while:

$$ \boxed{ a\cdot a^{-1}=1 } $$

uses the multiplicative identity.

Identity and Cancellation

Many cancellation laws can be understood as applying inverse operations until the relevant identity remains.

Identity in Category Theory

Every object $A$ has an identity morphism:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{id}_A:A\to A } $$

satisfying:

$$ f\circ\operatorname{id}_A=f $$

and:

$$ \operatorname{id}_B\circ f=f $$

for every appropriate morphism $f:A\to B$.

Identity as Structural Neutrality

Across these settings, the identity is the neutral object for an operation:

$$ \boxed{ \text{apply identity} \longrightarrow \text{no change} }. $$

Identity vs. Equality

Equality is a relation asserting that two expressions or objects are equal in a given statement. An identity is a stronger contextual claim that the equality persists universally across the relevant domain.

Identity vs. Equivalence

Equivalence may express sameness according to a specified relation without requiring literal equality. Identity often expresses exact preservation or equality within the relevant structure.

Identity vs. Definition

A definition establishes meaning. An identity asserts or records a universally valid relationship.

For example:

$$ \tan x = \frac{\sin x}{\cos x} $$

may be introduced definitionally in one treatment and then used as a trigonometric identity on its valid domain.

Identity vs. Formula

A formula may describe a relationship that applies under particular conditions. An identity specifically emphasizes universal validity over a stated domain.

Identity vs. Theorem

An identity can be a theorem once proved, but the term identity emphasizes the form and universal equality rather than the logical status of the result.

Testing an Alleged Identity Numerically

Substituting examples can disprove a false identity but cannot by itself prove a universal identity.

Counterexample Principle

To show that:

$$ L(x)=R(x) $$

is not an identity, it is enough to find one admissible value $x=c$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ L(c)\neq R(c) }. $$

Proof of an Identity

To establish an identity, one must show the equality follows from valid rules throughout the stated domain.

Common Error: Calling Every Equation an Identity

An equation may have only a few solutions. An identity is true for every admissible value.

Common Error: Ignoring Domain Restrictions

Simplification may produce an expression that appears universally equal while the original expression excludes certain inputs.

Common Error: Proving an Identity by Testing Examples

Numerical examples provide evidence but not a universal proof.

Common Error: Confusing Identity Element with Inverse

The identity leaves an element unchanged. An inverse combines with an element to produce the identity.

$$ \boxed{ a\ast e=a } $$

versus:

$$ \boxed{ a\ast a^{-1}=e }. $$

Common Error: Assuming the Identity Element Is Always $1$

For addition, the identity is $0$. For multiplication, it is $1$. Other operations may have other identity elements.

Common Error: Confusing Identity Function with Constant Function

The identity function is:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=x }, $$

not a constant function.

Common Error: Confusing Identity Matrix with a Matrix of All Ones

The identity matrix has ones only on the main diagonal and zeros elsewhere.

Common Misconception: The word identity has several mathematical meanings, but they share a common theme. An identity equation preserves equality for every admissible input; an identity element leaves an operand unchanged; an identity function returns its input unchanged; and an identity matrix or transformation leaves vectors or points unchanged. Context determines which meaning is intended.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$(a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2$ "The square-of-a-sum identity."
$\sin^2x+\cos^2x=1$ "The Pythagorean trigonometric identity."
$a+0=a$ "Zero is the additive identity."
$a\cdot1=a$ "One is the multiplicative identity."
$\operatorname{id}_S(x)=x$ "The identity function on S maps x to itself."
$I_n\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}$ "The identity matrix leaves the vector unchanged."
$f^{-1}\circ f=\operatorname{id}$ "Composing a function with its inverse gives the identity."

The word identity should usually be qualified when ambiguity is possible: algebraic identity, trigonometric identity, identity element, identity function, identity matrix, or identity transformation.

The identity function is commonly written:

$$ \operatorname{id}, \qquad \operatorname{id}_S, \qquad I $$

depending on field and context.

The identity matrix is commonly written:

$$ \boxed{ I_n } $$

when its size needs to be explicit.

Defense Notation Advice: Specify which kind of identity you mean. For identity equations, state domain restrictions and distinguish universal truth from a conditional equation. For identity elements, name the operation. For functions, transformations, and matrices, make the underlying set or dimension explicit when ambiguity could arise.

  • Conditional Equation: True only for selected values.

  • Domain Restriction: Identity validity is always interpreted on the common domain.
  • Algebraic Identity: Universal equality derived from algebraic laws.

  • Rational Identity: Requires denominator restrictions.
  • Exponent Identity: Laws such as $a^ma^n=a^{m+n}$ under appropriate conditions.
  • Logarithmic Identity: Laws relating products, quotients, and powers to logarithms.
  • Trigonometric Identity: Universal trigonometric equality on its common domain.
  • Pythagorean Identity: $\sin^2x+\cos^2x=1$.
  • Quotient Identity: $\tan x=\sin x/\cos x$ where defined.
  • Reciprocal Identity: $\sec x=1/\cos x$ and analogous relations.

  • Multiplicative Identity: $1$.

  • Ring Theory: Multiplicative identity is often written $1$.

  • Set Intersection: The universal set is the identity for intersection.

  • Boolean AND: $1$ or True acts as identity.

  • Equality: The identity relation is closely connected with equality on a set.
