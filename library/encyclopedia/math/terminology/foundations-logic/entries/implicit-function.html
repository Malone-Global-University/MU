Implicit Function or Relation

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

An implicit function or relation is a mathematical relationship in which the variables are constrained together by an equation rather than having one variable already isolated as an explicit formula in terms of another.

A typical implicit relation in two variables has the form:

$$ \boxed{ F(x,y)=0 }. $$

By contrast, an explicit function is commonly written:

$$ \boxed{ y=f(x) }. $$

The word implicit comes from roots associated with something being entangled, folded into, or contained within something else. In an implicit equation, the dependence of one variable on another is contained within the equation rather than displayed directly.

The word relation is important because an implicit equation need not define a function globally. It may describe a curve or set for which one input corresponds to several possible outputs.

$$ \boxed{ \text{implicit equation} \not\Longrightarrow \text{global function} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Basic Implicit Relation

Consider:

$$ \boxed{ x^2+y^2=25 }. $$

This equation defines a circle of radius $5$ centered at the origin.

Relation Rather than Global Function

Solving for $y$ gives:

$$ y^2=25-x^2, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ y=\pm\sqrt{25-x^2} }. $$

For most $x$ in $(-5,5)$, there are two corresponding $y$-values. Therefore the whole circle is not the graph of a single-valued function $y=f(x)$.

Branches

The circle can nevertheless be separated into two explicit branches:

$$ \boxed{ y=\sqrt{25-x^2} } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ y=-\sqrt{25-x^2} }. $$

Implicit Function

An implicit relation may define $y$ as a function of $x$ on part of its domain, even if no convenient explicit formula has been written.

For example:

$$ \boxed{ x^3+y^3=6xy } $$

relates $x$ and $y$ implicitly.

Standard Form

Any equation:

$$ G(x,y)=H(x,y) $$

can be rewritten as:

$$ \boxed{ F(x,y) = G(x,y)-H(x,y) = 0 }. $$

Explicit Form

An equation is explicit in $y$ when written:

$$ \boxed{ y=f(x) }. $$

Implicit Form

An equation is implicit in $x$ and $y$ when the variables remain intermingled:

$$ \boxed{ F(x,y)=0 }. $$

Example: Explicit to Implicit

Starting with:

$$ y=2x+3, $$

move all terms to one side:

$$ \boxed{ 2x-y+3=0 }. $$

This is an implicit representation of the same line.

Example: Implicit to Explicit

Starting with:

$$ 3x+2y=8, $$

solve for $y$:

$$ 2y=8-3x, $$
$$ \boxed{ y=4-\frac{3}{2}x }. $$

Not Every Implicit Relation Can Be Solved Easily

Some implicit equations can be rearranged into explicit form only with difficulty, while others have no useful elementary explicit form.

Implicit Does Not Mean Unsolvable

The term describes how the relationship is represented, not whether the equation can be solved.

Solution Set Interpretation

An implicit relation:

$$ F(x,y)=0 $$

represents the set:

$$ \boxed{ \{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^2:F(x,y)=0\} }. $$

Geometric Locus

Thus an implicit equation naturally defines a geometric locus: the collection of points satisfying the stated condition.

Line Example

$$ \boxed{ ax+by+c=0 } $$

is an implicit equation of a line when $a$ and $b$ are not both zero.

Circle Example

$$ \boxed{ (x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=r^2 } $$

is the standard implicit form of a circle.

Ellipse Example

$$ \boxed{ \frac{(x-h)^2}{a^2} + \frac{(y-k)^2}{b^2} = 1 }. $$

Hyperbola Example

$$ \boxed{ \frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 }. $$

General Conic

$$ \boxed{ Ax^2+Bxy+Cy^2+Dx+Ey+F=0 }. $$

This is naturally an implicit relation between $x$ and $y$.

Vertical-Line Test

To determine whether an implicit relation defines $y$ as a global function of $x$, examine whether each permitted $x$ corresponds to at most one $y$.

Geometrically, this is the vertical-line test.

Implicit Function without Explicit Isolation

A relation may satisfy the vertical-line test over a region even when solving symbolically for $y$ is inconvenient.

Local Function Behavior

An implicit relation may fail to define one global function but still define a function locally around a particular point.

Implicit Function Theorem Idea

Suppose:

$$ F(x,y)=0 $$

and $(a,b)$ satisfies:

$$ F(a,b)=0. $$

If:

$$ \boxed{ F_y(a,b)\neq0 }, $$

then, under suitable differentiability conditions, the relation locally defines $y$ as a differentiable function of $x$ near $(a,b)$.

Local Explicit Representation

Under those conditions, one can locally write:

$$ \boxed{ y=f(x) } $$

even if no simple global formula is available.

Why $F_y\neq0$ Matters

A nonzero partial derivative with respect to $y$ indicates that the relation changes sufficiently in the $y$-direction to solve locally for $y$ in terms of $x$.

Solving Locally for $x$

Similarly, if:

$$ \boxed{ F_x(a,b)\neq0 }, $$

then the relation may locally define:

$$ \boxed{ x=g(y) }. $$

Implicit Differentiation

If $y$ depends on $x$ implicitly through:

$$ F(x,y)=0, $$

differentiate both sides with respect to $x$:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}F(x,y)=0. $$

Chain Rule Form

Applying the multivariable chain rule:

$$ F_x + F_y\frac{dy}{dx} = 0. $$

Therefore, when $F_y\neq0$:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{F_x}{F_y} }. $$

Circle Derivative Example

For:

$$ x^2+y^2=25, $$

differentiate:

$$ 2x+2y\frac{dy}{dx}=0. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{x}{y} } $$

wherever $y\neq0$.

Vertical Tangent

In the derivative formula:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{F_x}{F_y}, $$

a zero value of $F_y$ may signal failure of the ordinary slope formula and can be associated with a vertical tangent or a more singular feature.

Horizontal Tangent

If:

$$ F_x=0 $$

while $F_y\neq0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dy}{dx}=0 }. $$

Singular Point

A point may be singular when:

$$ \boxed{ F_x=0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad F_y=0 } $$

simultaneously on the curve.

Example of a Singular Relation

Consider:

$$ \boxed{ y^2=x^3 }. $$

The origin is a special point where both partial derivatives of:

$$ F(x,y)=y^2-x^3 $$

vanish.

Implicit Curves Can Self-Intersect

Relations may describe curves with crossings, loops, cusps, or multiple branches that are awkward or impossible to describe globally as one function $y=f(x)$.

Implicit Representation Can Be More Natural

A circle is much simpler as:

$$ x^2+y^2=r^2 $$

than as two separate explicit functions.

Symmetry Is Often Clearer Implicitly

In:

$$ x^2+y^2=25, $$

replacing $x$ by $-x$ or $y$ by $-y$ leaves the equation unchanged, immediately revealing symmetry about both coordinate axes.

Relation vs. Function

A relation is any set of ordered pairs. A function is a relation in which each allowed input corresponds to exactly one output.

$$ \boxed{ \text{function} \subset \text{relation} }. $$

Implicit Relation as Ordered Pairs

The equation:

$$ F(x,y)=0 $$

determines the relation:

$$ \boxed{ R = \{(x,y):F(x,y)=0\} }. $$

Implicit Function as Restricted Relation

If a suitable region or branch is chosen so that each $x$ has exactly one $y$, that restricted relation may be treated as a function.

Domain of an Implicit Relation

The domain consists of all $x$ for which at least one corresponding $y$ exists:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(R) = \{x:\exists y,\ F(x,y)=0\} }. $$

Range of an Implicit Relation

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Range}(R) = \{y:\exists x,\ F(x,y)=0\} }. $$

Circle Domain and Range

For:

$$ x^2+y^2=25, $$

both domain and range are:

$$ \boxed{ [-5,5] }. $$

Inverse Relation

Swapping $x$ and $y$ in a relation produces its inverse relation.

If:

$$ F(x,y)=0, $$

the inverse relation can be expressed through:

$$ \boxed{ F(y,x)=0 }. $$

Reflection across $y=x$

Geometrically, the inverse relation is obtained by reflecting the original relation across the line:

$$ \boxed{ y=x }. $$

Implicit Equations and Level Sets

More generally:

$$ \boxed{ F(x,y)=c } $$

defines a level curve of the function $F$.

Zero Level Set

The relation:

$$ F(x,y)=0 $$

is the zero level set of $F$.

Three Variables

An implicit equation:

$$ \boxed{ F(x,y,z)=0 } $$

often describes a surface in three-dimensional space.

Sphere Example

$$ \boxed{ x^2+y^2+z^2=r^2 }. $$

This is an implicit representation of a sphere.

Surface as a Level Set

Writing:

$$ F(x,y,z) = x^2+y^2+z^2-r^2, $$

the sphere is:

$$ \boxed{ F(x,y,z)=0 }. $$

Gradient

For a regular level surface:

$$ F(x,y,z)=c, $$

the gradient:

$$ \boxed{ \nabla F } $$

is normal to the surface when $\nabla F\neq\mathbf{0}$.

Tangent-Line Normal Vector in Two Variables

For a regular implicit curve:

$$ F(x,y)=0, $$

the vector:

$$ \boxed{ \nabla F = (F_x,F_y) } $$

is perpendicular to the tangent line.

Tangent-Line Equation

At a regular point $(a,b)$:

$$ \boxed{ F_x(a,b)(x-a) + F_y(a,b)(y-b) = 0 }. $$

Multiple Variables

An implicit relation can involve many variables:

$$ \boxed{ F(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n)=0 }. $$

Depending on the context, one or more variables may be determined locally in terms of the others.

Systems of Implicit Equations

Several relations may be imposed simultaneously:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} F_1(x_1,\ldots,x_n) &=0,\\ F_2(x_1,\ldots,x_n) &=0,\\ &\vdots \end{aligned} } $$

Their common solution set is an intersection of level sets.

Implicit Algebraic Curve

When $F(x,y)$ is a polynomial, the equation:

$$ \boxed{ F(x,y)=0 } $$

defines an algebraic curve.

Polynomial Relations

Polynomial implicit equations are central in algebraic geometry because they describe geometric objects through algebraic constraints.

Parametric vs. Implicit Representation

A curve may instead be described parametrically:

$$ \boxed{ x=x(t), \qquad y=y(t) }. $$

Parametric and implicit forms describe geometry in different ways.

Circle Parametrization

The implicit circle:

$$ x^2+y^2=r^2 $$

can be parametrized by:

$$ \boxed{ x=r\cos t, \qquad y=r\sin t }. $$

Implicitization

Converting a parametric or explicit representation into an implicit equation is called implicitization.

Example of Implicitization

From:

$$ x=r\cos t, \qquad y=r\sin t, $$

use:

$$ \cos^2t+\sin^2t=1 $$

to obtain:

$$ \boxed{ x^2+y^2=r^2 }. $$

Explicitization

Attempting to solve an implicit relation for one variable in terms of another may be called explicitization.

Numerical Solution

When an explicit formula is unavailable, numerical methods can approximate points satisfying:

$$ F(x,y)=0. $$

Newton-Type Methods

Numerical root-finding methods can solve for one variable after fixing another or can solve systems of implicit equations directly.

Computer Graphics

Implicit surfaces are useful in geometric modeling because membership can be tested by evaluating the sign or value of a function $F$.

Inside, Outside, and Boundary

A surface may be represented by:

$$ F(x,y,z)=0, $$

with regions classified by:

$$ F<0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad F>0. $$

Constraint Modeling

In applied mathematics, an implicit relation often represents a constraint:

$$ \boxed{ F(x_1,\ldots,x_n)=0 }. $$

Feasible solutions must satisfy the constraint even when no variable is isolated.

Optimization Constraints

Constrained optimization frequently uses equations such as:

$$ g(x,y)=c $$

as implicit restrictions on the variables.

Lagrange Multiplier Connection

For an optimization problem constrained by:

$$ g(x,y)=c, $$

a standard condition is:

$$ \boxed{ \nabla f = \lambda\nabla g }. $$

Differential Equations

An implicit solution to a differential equation may be written as a relation:

$$ \boxed{ F(x,y)=C } $$

rather than explicitly as $y=f(x)$.

Implicit Solution Example

A differential equation may lead naturally to:

$$ x^2+y^2=C, $$

which describes a family of solution curves without explicitly isolating $y$.

Economics and Constraint Equations

Relationships among economic variables may be given implicitly when equilibrium conditions determine variables jointly rather than through one direct formula.

Physics

Conservation laws and geometric constraints often produce implicit equations relating several quantities.

Inverse Functions

The equation:

$$ y=f(x) $$

can be viewed implicitly as:

$$ \boxed{ y-f(x)=0 }. $$

Swapping variables produces the inverse relation.

Branch Restrictions for Inverses

An inverse relation becomes an inverse function only after suitable restrictions ensure one output for each input.

Example with $y=x^2$

The relation:

$$ x=y^2 $$

gives:

$$ y=\pm\sqrt{x}, $$

so a branch restriction is needed to define a single inverse function.

Implicit Form Can Avoid Artificial Branch Choices

Keeping:

$$ x=y^2 $$

retains the entire relation without prematurely selecting one branch.

Common Error: Calling Every Implicit Equation a Function

An implicit equation defines a relation first. It defines a function only when the function condition is satisfied globally or on a specified branch.

Common Error: Thinking Implicit Means Nonlinear

Linear equations can be implicit:

$$ 2x+3y-6=0. $$

Common Error: Thinking Explicit Means Linear

Explicit functions may be highly nonlinear:

$$ y=e^{x^2+\sin x}. $$

Common Error: Ignoring Multiple Branches

Solving:

$$ y^2=1-x^2 $$

requires:

$$ \boxed{ y=\pm\sqrt{1-x^2} }, $$

not only the positive branch.

Common Error: Treating $\pm$ as One Function Value

The notation $\pm$ represents two possible values. A function must still assign exactly one output to each input.

Common Error: Dividing during Implicit Manipulation without Checking Zero Cases

Algebraic operations that divide by expressions can remove valid portions of an implicit relation if the divisor may equal zero.

Common Error: Assuming Implicit Differentiation Makes $y$ Independent

During implicit differentiation, $y$ is treated as a function of $x$, so the chain rule produces factors of $dy/dx$.

Common Error: Forgetting Domain Restrictions after Explicitization

Solving an implicit equation for one variable may introduce radicals, denominators, or branch restrictions that constrain the explicit formula.

Common Error: Assuming Local Solvability Means Global Solvability

A relation may define a smooth function near one point while failing to define a single function over the entire curve.

Common Misconception: An implicit function or relation is not merely an equation that has not yet been solved. It is a representation in which variables are constrained jointly, often in the form $F(x,y)=0$. Some implicit relations can be converted into a single explicit function, some split into several branches, and some are best understood directly as geometric relations or level sets.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$F(x,y)=0$ "F of x and y equals zero; an implicit relation between x and y."
$y=f(x)$ "Y is given explicitly as a function of x."
$x^2+y^2=25$ "X squared plus y squared equals twenty-five; the implicit equation of a circle."
$y=\pm\sqrt{25-x^2}$ "The circle separates into upper and lower explicit branches."
$\frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{F_x}{F_y}$ "The implicit derivative is negative F sub x over F sub y."
$F(x,y)=c$ "A level curve of F at level c."
$F(x,y,z)=0$ "An implicitly defined surface in three variables."

The phrase implicit function should be used carefully. Strictly speaking, the equation itself first defines a relation. It becomes appropriate to speak of an implicit function when the relation determines one variable uniquely, either globally or locally on a chosen branch.

Common related expressions include:

  • implicit equation;
  • implicit relation;
  • implicitly defined function;
  • implicit curve;
  • implicit surface;
  • level set;
  • implicit differentiation.

Defense Notation Advice: Use $F(x,y)=0$ when emphasizing the relation itself. Do not call the entire relation a function unless uniqueness of the dependent variable has been established. When differentiating implicitly, state that $y=y(x)$ and retain every chain-rule factor. When solving for $y$, preserve all branches and domain restrictions.
