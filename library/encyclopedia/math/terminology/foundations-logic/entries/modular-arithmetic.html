Modular Arithmetic

Subfield: Foundations & Logic

Etymology & Roots

Modular arithmetic is arithmetic in which integers are compared according to their remainders after division by a fixed positive integer called the modulus. Numbers that differ by an integer multiple of the modulus are treated as congruent.

The central notation is:

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv b\pmod n } $$

which means that $a$ and $b$ are congruent modulo $n$.

Formally:

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv b\pmod n \iff n\mid(a-b) }. $$

Equivalently, $a$ and $b$ leave the same remainder when divided by $n$.

The word modular comes from modulus, a Latin-derived word meaning a measure or standard. In modular arithmetic, the modulus determines the repeating cycle by which integers are grouped.

The systematic theory of congruences was developed prominently by Carl Friedrich Gauss, who introduced modern congruence notation in his work on number theory.

The central conceptual shift is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{ordinary integer value} \longrightarrow \text{equivalence by remainder class} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Modulus

The modulus is the positive integer that determines the arithmetic cycle.

$$ \boxed{ n>0 }. $$

Congruence

Two integers are congruent modulo $n$ when their difference is divisible by $n$:

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv b\pmod n \iff n\mid(a-b) }. $$

Example

$$ 17\equiv5\pmod{12} $$

because:

$$ 17-5=12 $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ 12\mid12 }. $$

Same-Remainder Interpretation

Dividing both $17$ and $5$ by $12$ produces remainder $5$:

$$ 17=12(1)+5, \qquad 5=12(0)+5. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ 17\equiv5\pmod{12} }. $$

Congruence Is Not Equality

The statement:

$$ 17\equiv5\pmod{12} $$

does not mean:

$$ 17=5. $$

It means they belong to the same residue class modulo $12$.

General Congruence Form

If:

$$ a\equiv b\pmod n, $$

then for some integer $k$:

$$ \boxed{ a=b+kn }. $$

Residue

A residue modulo $n$ is a representative of a congruence class. Standard representatives are often chosen from:

$$ \boxed{ 0,1,2,\ldots,n-1 }. $$

Complete Residue System

The integers:

$$ \boxed{ 0,1,\ldots,n-1 } $$

form a complete residue system modulo $n$.

Residue Class

The residue class of $a$ modulo $n$ is:

$$ \boxed{ [a]_n = \{a+kn:k\in\mathbb{Z}\} }. $$

Example of a Residue Class

Modulo $5$:

$$ [2]_5 = \{\ldots,-8,-3,2,7,12,17,\ldots\}. $$

Integers Modulo $n$

The collection of residue classes modulo $n$ is commonly written:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} } $$

or informally:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{Z}_n }. $$

The notation $\mathbb{Z}_n$ can be ambiguous in some advanced contexts, so $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is often more precise.

Reduction Modulo $n$

To reduce modulo $n$ means to replace an integer by a congruent representative, often its least nonnegative residue.

For example:

$$ 38\equiv3\pmod5. $$

Least Nonnegative Residue

Every integer has exactly one representative $r$ satisfying:

$$ \boxed{ 0\leq r<n }. $$

Division Algorithm Connection

For every integer $a$ and positive integer $n$, there exist unique integers $q$ and $r$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ a=nq+r, \qquad 0\leq r<n }. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv r\pmod n }. $$

Negative Integers

Negative integers also reduce normally modulo $n$.

For example:

$$ -3\equiv2\pmod5 $$

because:

$$ -3-2=-5. $$

Alternative Representatives

A residue class has infinitely many representatives:

$$ \boxed{ a, a+n, a-n, a+2n, a-2n, \ldots }. $$

Congruence Is an Equivalence Relation

Congruence modulo $n$ is reflexive:

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv a\pmod n }. $$

It is symmetric:

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv b\pmod n \Longrightarrow b\equiv a\pmod n }. $$

It is transitive:

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv b\pmod n \text{ and } b\equiv c\pmod n \Longrightarrow a\equiv c\pmod n }. $$

Addition Rule

If:

$$ a\equiv b\pmod n, \qquad c\equiv d\pmod n, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a+c\equiv b+d\pmod n }. $$

Subtraction Rule

$$ \boxed{ a-c\equiv b-d\pmod n }. $$

Multiplication Rule

$$ \boxed{ ac\equiv bd\pmod n }. $$

Power Rule

If:

$$ a\equiv b\pmod n, $$

then for every nonnegative integer $k$:

$$ \boxed{ a^k\equiv b^k\pmod n }. $$

Polynomial Substitution

If $a\equiv b\pmod n$, then for any polynomial $P(x)$ with integer coefficients:

$$ \boxed{ P(a)\equiv P(b)\pmod n }. $$

Reduce Early

One major practical advantage of modular arithmetic is that intermediate values can often be reduced before completing a calculation.

For example:

$$ 73\cdot48\pmod5. $$

Since:

$$ 73\equiv3\pmod5, \qquad 48\equiv3\pmod5, $$

one obtains:

$$ 73\cdot48 \equiv 3\cdot3 = 9 \equiv \boxed{4} \pmod5. $$

Repeated Reduction

Reduction may occur at every step:

$$ \boxed{ (a+b)\bmod n = ((a\bmod n)+(b\bmod n))\bmod n } $$

under the ordinary least-nonnegative-remainder convention.

Multiplication Reduction

$$ \boxed{ (ab)\bmod n = ((a\bmod n)(b\bmod n))\bmod n }. $$

Remainder Notation vs. Congruence Notation

The expression:

$$ a\bmod n $$

commonly denotes a remainder or residue representative, while:

$$ a\equiv b\pmod n $$

denotes a congruence relation.

Example of Remainder Form

$$ \boxed{ 17\bmod5=2 }. $$

Equivalent Congruence Form

$$ \boxed{ 17\equiv2\pmod5 }. $$

Programming Caveat

Programming-language remainder operators may treat negative inputs differently. Mathematical modular arithmetic should therefore be distinguished from a language-specific remainder implementation.

Clock Arithmetic

A standard intuitive model is a clock.

On a $12$-hour clock:

$$ \boxed{ 15\equiv3\pmod{12} }. $$

Advancing $15$ hours has the same clock-position effect as advancing $3$ hours.

Weekly Cycles

Days of the week naturally behave modulo $7$.

$$ \boxed{ 10\equiv3\pmod7 }. $$

Ten days after a starting day lands on the same weekday as three days after it.

Parity as Modulo $2$

Even and odd integers are the two residue classes modulo $2$.

$$ \boxed{ n\equiv0\pmod2 \Longleftrightarrow n\text{ is even} } $$
$$ \boxed{ n\equiv1\pmod2 \Longleftrightarrow n\text{ is odd} }. $$

Divisibility Test through Congruence

The statement:

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv0\pmod n } $$

is equivalent to:

$$ \boxed{ n\mid a }. $$

Last Digit

The last decimal digit of an integer is determined modulo $10$.

$$ \boxed{ N\equiv r\pmod{10} } $$

where $r$ is the last digit for nonnegative $N$.

Last Two Digits

The last two decimal digits are determined modulo $100$.

Digit-Sum Test for $9$

Since:

$$ 10\equiv1\pmod9, $$

every power of $10$ satisfies:

$$ 10^k\equiv1\pmod9. $$

Therefore a base-$10$ integer is congruent modulo $9$ to the sum of its digits.

Digit-Sum Test for $3$

The same reasoning works modulo $3$ because:

$$ \boxed{ 10\equiv1\pmod3 }. $$

Alternating-Sum Test for $11$

Since:

$$ 10\equiv-1\pmod{11}, $$

powers of $10$ alternate between $1$ and $-1$ modulo $11$, producing the familiar alternating-digit-sum divisibility test.

Cancellation Warning

From:

$$ ac\equiv bc\pmod n, $$

one cannot always conclude:

$$ a\equiv b\pmod n. $$

Counterexample

Modulo $6$:

$$ 2\cdot1 \equiv 2\cdot4 \pmod6 $$

because:

$$ 2\equiv8\pmod6. $$

Yet:

$$ 1\not\equiv4\pmod6. $$

Safe Cancellation Condition

If:

$$ ac\equiv bc\pmod n $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(c,n)=1 }, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv b\pmod n }. $$

Division Is Not Ordinary Division

Modular division requires a multiplicative inverse. One cannot simply divide both sides of a congruence by an arbitrary integer.

Multiplicative Inverse

An integer $a$ has a multiplicative inverse modulo $n$ if some integer $b$ satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ ab\equiv1\pmod n }. $$

Inverse Criterion

An integer $a$ has an inverse modulo $n$ exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(a,n)=1 }. $$

Example of a Modular Inverse

Modulo $7$:

$$ 3\cdot5=15\equiv1\pmod7. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ 3^{-1}\equiv5\pmod7 }. $$

No Inverse Example

Modulo $6$, the class of $2$ has no multiplicative inverse because:

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(2,6)=2\neq1 }. $$

Linear Congruence

A congruence of the form:

$$ \boxed{ ax\equiv b\pmod n } $$

is called a linear congruence.

Solvability Criterion

Let:

$$ d=\gcd(a,n). $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ ax\equiv b\pmod n \text{ is solvable} \iff d\mid b }. $$

Unique Solution When Coefficient Is Invertible

If $\gcd(a,n)=1$, then:

$$ ax\equiv b\pmod n $$

has exactly one residue-class solution modulo $n$:

$$ \boxed{ x\equiv a^{-1}b\pmod n }. $$

Example

Solve:

$$ 3x\equiv4\pmod7. $$

Since:

$$ 3^{-1}\equiv5\pmod7, $$

multiply by $5$:

$$ x \equiv 20 \equiv \boxed{6} \pmod7. $$

Zero Divisors

In $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ with composite $n$, nonzero classes can multiply to zero.

Modulo $6$:

$$ \boxed{ 2\cdot3 \equiv0\pmod6 }. $$

The nonzero classes $[2]$ and $[3]$ are therefore zero divisors.

Prime Modulus

If $p$ is prime, every nonzero residue class modulo $p$ has a multiplicative inverse.

Field Structure

For prime $p$:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} \text{ is a field} }. $$

Composite Modulus

For composite $n$, $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ generally contains zero divisors and is not a field.

Units Modulo $n$

The invertible residue classes modulo $n$ form the multiplicative group:

$$ \boxed{ (\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times }. $$

Euler's Totient Function

The number of invertible residue classes modulo $n$ is:

$$ \boxed{ \varphi(n) }. $$

Equivalently, $\varphi(n)$ counts integers in:

$$ 1,2,\ldots,n $$

that are relatively prime to $n$.

Euler's Theorem

If:

$$ \gcd(a,n)=1, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a^{\varphi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n }. $$

Fermat's Little Theorem

If $p$ is prime and $p\nmid a$, then:

$$ \boxed{ a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p }. $$

Equivalent Fermat Form

For every integer $a$ and prime $p$:

$$ \boxed{ a^p\equiv a\pmod p }. $$

Large Powers

Modular arithmetic is especially useful for computing large powers without expanding enormous integers.

For example, to find:

$$ 2^{100}\pmod7, $$

note:

$$ 2^3=8\equiv1\pmod7. $$

Since:

$$ 100=3(33)+1, $$

one gets:

$$ 2^{100} = (2^3)^{33}2 \equiv 1^{33}2 = \boxed{2} \pmod7. $$

Cyclic Patterns

Powers modulo $n$ often repeat because only finitely many residue classes are available.

Example of a Power Cycle

Powers of $2$ modulo $5$ give:

$$ 2,4,3,1,2,4,3,1,\ldots $$

producing a cycle of length $4$.

Multiplicative Order

If $\gcd(a,n)=1$, the multiplicative order of $a$ modulo $n$ is the least positive integer $k$ satisfying:

$$ \boxed{ a^k\equiv1\pmod n }. $$

Chinese Remainder Theorem

If moduli are pairwise coprime, simultaneous congruences can be combined into a unique solution modulo their product.

For example:

$$ \begin{aligned} x &\equiv2\pmod3,\\ x &\equiv3\pmod5 \end{aligned} $$

has a unique solution modulo $15$.

Testing gives:

$$ \boxed{ x\equiv8\pmod{15} }. $$

Simultaneous Congruences

Modular systems can encode multiple periodic restrictions at once.

Modular Arithmetic and Rings

The residue classes modulo $n$ form a commutative ring:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Additive Identity

$$ \boxed{ [0] }. $$

Multiplicative Identity

$$ \boxed{ [1] }. $$

Additive Inverse

The additive inverse of $[a]$ is:

$$ \boxed{ [-a] }. $$

Example of Additive Inverse

Modulo $7$, the additive inverse of $3$ is $4$ because:

$$ 3+4=7\equiv0\pmod7. $$

Congruence and Equivalence Classes

Modular arithmetic is a concrete example of quotienting a set by an equivalence relation.

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{Z} \longrightarrow \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Canonical Projection

The map:

$$ \boxed{ \pi:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}, \qquad \pi(a)=[a] } $$

sends each integer to its residue class.

Homomorphism Property

The projection respects addition and multiplication:

$$ \boxed{ [a+b]=[a]+[b] } $$
$$ \boxed{ [ab]=[a][b] }. $$

Congruence in Proofs

Congruences are useful for proving impossibility statements.

Square Residues Modulo $4$

Every integer is congruent to $0$, $1$, $2$, or $3$ modulo $4$. Squaring gives:

$$ 0^2\equiv0, \qquad 1^2\equiv1, \qquad 2^2\equiv0, \qquad 3^2\equiv1 \pmod4. $$

Therefore every integer square satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ x^2\equiv0 \text{ or } 1 \pmod4 }. $$

Impossibility Example

No integer square can satisfy:

$$ x^2\equiv2\pmod4. $$

Quadratic Residue

A residue $a$ is a quadratic residue modulo $n$ if:

$$ \boxed{ x^2\equiv a\pmod n } $$

has an integer solution.

Checksums

Modular arithmetic is used in checksum systems that detect certain transcription or transmission errors.

Cryptography

Modern public-key cryptography makes extensive use of modular exponentiation, inverses, prime moduli, and related number-theoretic structures.

RSA-Style Arithmetic

Modular exponentiation of the form:

$$ \boxed{ c\equiv m^e\pmod n } $$

is central to RSA-type constructions.

Discrete Logarithm Context

In some finite cyclic groups, one studies equations:

$$ \boxed{ g^x\equiv h\pmod p } $$

where recovering $x$ is a discrete logarithm problem.

Computer Science

Modular arithmetic is used for cyclic indexing, hashing, pseudorandom generators, circular buffers, finite-state systems, and overflow-style computations.

Circular Indexing

An index can be wrapped into a range of size $n$ using modular reduction.

$$ \boxed{ i_{\text{wrapped}} = i\bmod n }. $$

Calendars

Weekday calculations naturally use arithmetic modulo $7$.

Music

Pitch-class theory commonly treats the twelve chromatic pitch classes modulo $12$.

Rotational Symmetry

Repeated rotations by fixed angles can often be described with cyclic modular structure.

Modular Addition Table

Addition modulo $4$ includes:

$$ \boxed{ 3+2\equiv1\pmod4 }. $$

Modular Multiplication Table

Multiplication modulo $5$ includes:

$$ \boxed{ 3\cdot4 = 12 \equiv2\pmod5 }. $$

Wrapping Around

The common phrase "wrap around" is an intuitive description of reducing a result back into a chosen complete residue system.

Modulo Is Not Merely Truncation

Modular reduction groups infinitely many integers into the same equivalence class. It is not simply discarding digits or cutting off a quotient.

Common Error: Using an Equal Sign Instead of Congruence

Writing:

$$ 17=5\pmod{12} $$

is nonstandard and misleading. Prefer:

$$ \boxed{ 17\equiv5\pmod{12} }. $$

Common Error: Treating Congruence as Approximate Equality

The symbol $\equiv$ here expresses an exact divisibility relation, not numerical approximation.

Common Error: Forgetting the Modulus

Congruence depends on the chosen modulus.

$$ 8\equiv2\pmod6 $$

but:

$$ 8\not\equiv2\pmod5. $$

Common Error: Assuming the Remainder Is the Only Representative

A residue class contains infinitely many integers. The least nonnegative residue is merely a preferred representative.

Common Error: Mishandling Negative Residues

For example:

$$ -1\equiv4\pmod5. $$

Both $-1$ and $4$ represent the same class modulo $5$.

Common Error: Dividing Congruences Freely

Modular division requires an inverse or an appropriate cancellation theorem.

Common Error: Cancelling a Noninvertible Factor

A common factor cannot always be cancelled modulo a composite modulus.

Common Error: Assuming Every Nonzero Residue Has an Inverse

This is true modulo a prime but false in general modulo a composite integer.

Common Error: Confusing Remainder with Congruence Class

A remainder is one representative; a congruence class contains every integer differing by a multiple of the modulus.

Common Error: Assuming Modulo Arithmetic Destroys Ordinary Arithmetic Laws

Addition, subtraction, and multiplication remain compatible with congruence. What changes is the interpretation through residue classes.

Common Error: Assuming Ordinary Ordering Survives Modulo $n$

The residue classes modulo $n$ do not inherit an ordinary linear order compatible with modular addition in the same way the integers do.

Common Error: Calling $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ a Field for Every $n$

It is a field exactly when $n$ is prime.

Common Error: Confusing Modular Arithmetic with Floating-Point Remainders

Mathematical modular arithmetic is fundamentally about integer congruence and quotient structures, even though related remainder operators may be defined for other numerical types in software.

Common Misconception: Modular arithmetic does not say that distinct integers become literally equal. It groups integers into equivalence classes according to divisibility by a fixed modulus. Thus $17\equiv5\pmod{12}$ means that $17$ and $5$ differ by a multiple of $12$, not that $17=5$. This distinction makes modular arithmetic precise enough to support algebraic operations, divisibility arguments, number theory, cyclic systems, and cryptography.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$a\equiv b\pmod n$ "A is congruent to b modulo n."
$n\mid(a-b)$ "N divides a minus b."
$17\bmod5=2$ "Seventeen mod five equals two," when referring to the least nonnegative remainder.
$[a]_n$ "The residue class of a modulo n."
$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ "The integers modulo n," or "Z mod n Z."
$a^{-1}\pmod n$ "The multiplicative inverse of a modulo n."
$ax\equiv b\pmod n$ "A x is congruent to b modulo n."

The words mod, modulo, and modulus are related but should not be treated as interchangeable in every sentence.

In:

$$ a\equiv b\pmod n, $$

$n$ is the modulus, while "modulo $n$" describes the congruence context.

The notation:

$$ a\bmod n $$

often functions as an operation returning a chosen remainder, especially in computing contexts.

By contrast:

$$ a\equiv b\pmod n $$

is a relation between integers.

Defense Notation Advice: Use $\equiv$ rather than $=$ for congruence statements, always state the modulus, and distinguish the relation $a\equiv b\pmod n$ from the remainder expression $a\bmod n$. When dividing or cancelling in a congruence, first verify that the relevant factor is invertible modulo the modulus, usually by checking a greatest common divisor condition.
