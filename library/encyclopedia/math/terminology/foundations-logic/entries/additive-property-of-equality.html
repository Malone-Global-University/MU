Additive Property of Equality

Subfield: Foundations & Mathematical Logic

Etymology & Roots

The Additive Property of Equality states that adding the same quantity to both sides of an equation preserves equality.

Symbolically:

$$ \boxed{ a=b \Longrightarrow a+c=b+c }. $$

The word additive identifies the operation being applied, while equality indicates that the relation between the two expressions remains unchanged.

This property formalizes one of the most basic principles of equation solving: whatever quantity is added to one side of an equation must also be added to the other if the equality is to remain true.

In elementary algebra, the property often appears implicitly in statements such as "add $5$ to both sides." More formally, that step is justified by the Additive Property of Equality.

Operational Nuance

Basic Form

If:

$$ a=b, $$

then for any compatible quantity $c$:

$$ \boxed{ a+c=b+c }. $$

The same quantity must be added to both sides.

Example

Suppose:

$$ x-7=12. $$

Add $7$ to both sides:

$$ x-7+7=12+7. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{x=19}. $$

The equality remains valid because both sides were changed in the same additive way.

Subtraction as Addition

What is often called the Subtraction Property of Equality can be viewed as a direct consequence of the additive property because subtraction is addition of an additive inverse.

If:

$$ a=b, $$

then adding $-c$ to both sides gives:

$$ a+(-c)=b+(-c), $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ a-c=b-c }. $$

Preservation of Solutions

When the same quantity is added to both sides of an equation, the transformed equation has exactly the same solutions as the original equation.

For example:

$$ x+3=8 $$

and:

$$ x=5 $$

are equivalent equations because subtracting $3$, or equivalently adding $-3$, to both sides preserves the solution set.

Logical Reversibility

The transformation is reversible. If:

$$ a+c=b+c, $$

then adding $-c$ to both sides yields:

$$ a=b. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ a=b \iff a+c=b+c }. $$

This stronger biconditional statement is especially useful in algebra because it shows that adding the same quantity to both sides preserves and reflects equality.

Equation-Balancing Interpretation

An equation may be thought of as a balance. If two expressions are equal, applying the same additive change to each maintains the balance:

$$ \boxed{ \text{equal quantities} + \text{equal additions} = \text{equal results} }. $$

Common Misconception: It is not enough to "move a term to the other side and change its sign." That shortcut describes the result of adding an additive inverse to both sides, but the actual algebraic justification is the Additive Property of Equality.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$a=b$ "A equals b."
$a+c=b+c$ "Add c to both sides."
$a-c=b-c$ "Subtract c from both sides."
$x-7+7=12+7$ "Add seven to both sides."
$a=b\iff a+c=b+c$ "A equals b if and only if a plus c equals b plus c."
$a+c=b+c\Rightarrow a=b$ "Cancel c from both sides," or more formally, "add negative c to both sides."

Informal algebra often uses phrases such as:

$$ \text{"move the }3\text{ to the other side."} $$

A more precise statement is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{add }-3\text{ to both sides} }. $$

This wording makes the underlying property explicit.

Defense Notation Advice: The equality sign means the two expressions have the same value. Any legal transformation must preserve that relation. Adding the same quantity to both sides is one such transformation.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Basic Property: $$ \boxed{ a=b \Longrightarrow a+c=b+c }. $$
  • Reversible Form: $$ \boxed{ a=b \iff a+c=b+c }. $$
  • Subtraction Consequence: $$ \boxed{ a=b \Longrightarrow a-c=b-c }. $$
  • Additive Inverse Connection: Subtracting $c$ is equivalent to adding $-c$: $$ a-c=a+(-c). $$
  • Solving Linear Equations: If: $$ x+a=b, $$ then adding $-a$ to both sides gives: $$ \boxed{ x=b-a }. $$
  • Cancellation Law: If: $$ a+c=b+c, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ a=b }. $$
  • Equal Additions: More generally, if: $$ a=b $$ and: $$ c=d, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ a+c=b+d }. $$
  • Expressions: The property applies to expressions, not just single numbers: $$ f(x)=g(x) \Longrightarrow f(x)+h(x)=g(x)+h(x). $$
  • Functions: If two functions satisfy: $$ f=g, $$ then adding the same function $h$ to both gives: $$ \boxed{ f+h=g+h }. $$
  • Vectors: If: $$ \mathbf{u}=\mathbf{v}, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ \mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w} }. $$
  • Matrices: If: $$ A=B, $$ then for any same-sized matrix $C$: $$ \boxed{ A+C=B+C }. $$
  • Polynomials: If: $$ p(x)=q(x), $$ then: $$ \boxed{ p(x)+r(x)=q(x)+r(x) }. $$
  • Congruences: Modular arithmetic has a related preservation rule: $$ a\equiv b\pmod n \Longrightarrow a+c\equiv b+c\pmod n. $$

  • No Sign Reversal: Unlike multiplication by a negative number in an inequality, adding a quantity to both sides never reverses the equality or inequality relation.
  • Equation Equivalence: Adding the same quantity to both sides produces an equivalent equation with the same solution set.
  • Systems of Equations: Within an individual equation of a system, adding the same expression to both sides preserves that equation's solution set.
  • Row Operations: Elementary row operations on augmented matrices reflect the same broader principle of performing transformations that preserve the solution set of a system.
  • Group-Theoretic Basis: The property follows from the existence of additive inverses and the additive identity in algebraic structures such as groups and vector spaces.
  • Additive Identity: Zero satisfies: $$ a+0=a. $$ Additive inverses are defined relative to this identity.
  • Additive Inverse: For every $a$: $$ a+(-a)=0. $$ This allows additive terms to be canceled from both sides.
  • Transposition Shortcut: The classroom phrase: $$ x+5=12 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x=12-5 $$ is a compressed way of applying the additive property with $-5$.
  • Not Literal Movement: Terms do not physically "move across the equals sign." The equation is transformed by applying the same operation to both sides.
  • Multiplicative Property Contrast: The Multiplicative Property of Equality states: $$ a=b \Longrightarrow ac=bc. $$ The additive property uses addition instead.
  • Division Property Contrast: Division by a nonzero common quantity preserves equality: $$ a=b,\ c\ne0 \Longrightarrow \frac ac=\frac bc. $$
  • Substitution Contrast: The Substitution Property of Equality permits one equal expression to replace another. The Additive Property instead applies the same additive operation to both sides.
  • Logical Interpretation: Equality is preserved because addition by a fixed quantity is a reversible transformation: $$ x\mapsto x+c. $$
  • Inverse Transformation: The inverse of: $$ x\mapsto x+c $$ is: $$ x\mapsto x-c. $$ This reversibility explains why no solutions are lost or introduced.
  • Terminological Contrast: The Additive Property of Equality preserves equality by adding the same quantity to both sides. The Subtraction Property of Equality is the corresponding subtraction form and can be derived by adding an additive inverse. The Multiplicative Property of Equality instead multiplies both sides by the same quantity.
