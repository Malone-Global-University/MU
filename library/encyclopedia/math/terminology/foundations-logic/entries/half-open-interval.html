Half-Open Interval

Subfield: Foundations & Logic

Etymology & Roots

A half-open interval is an interval that includes exactly one of its two finite endpoints and excludes the other. The same kind of interval is also commonly called a half-closed interval.

For real numbers $a<b$, the two standard forms are:

$$ \boxed{ [a,b) = \{x\in\mathbb{R}:a\leq x<b\} } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b] = \{x\in\mathbb{R}:a<x\leq b\} }. $$

The word half-open refers to the endpoint conditions: one endpoint is open, meaning excluded, while the other is closed, meaning included. It does not mean that one-half of the interval's geometric length is open.

The synonymous phrase half-closed describes the same structure from the opposite perspective.

$$ \boxed{ \text{half-open} = \text{half-closed} = \text{exactly one finite endpoint included} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Left-Closed, Right-Open Form

The interval:

$$ \boxed{ [a,b) } $$

includes $a$ and excludes $b$.

$$ \boxed{ a\leq x<b }. $$

Left-Open, Right-Closed Form

The interval:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b] } $$

excludes $a$ and includes $b$.

$$ \boxed{ a<x\leq b }. $$

Example: $[3,7)$

The interval:

$$ [3,7) $$

contains every real number satisfying:

$$ \boxed{ 3\leq x<7 }. $$

Therefore $3$ is included and $7$ is excluded.

Example: $(-2,5]$

$$ \boxed{ (-2,5] = \{x\in\mathbb{R}:-2<x\leq5\} }. $$

Here $-2$ is excluded and $5$ is included.

Bracket Meaning

A square bracket at a finite endpoint indicates inclusion.

$$ \boxed{ [ \quad\text{or}\quad ] \Longrightarrow \text{endpoint included} }. $$

Parenthesis Meaning

A parenthesis at a finite endpoint indicates exclusion.

$$ \boxed{ ( \quad\text{or}\quad ) \Longrightarrow \text{endpoint excluded} }. $$

Number-Line Representation

On a number line, the included endpoint is usually shown with a filled point, while the excluded endpoint is usually shown with an open point.

Open Interval Contrast

An open interval excludes both endpoints:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b) = \{x:a<x<b\} }. $$

Closed Interval Contrast

A closed interval includes both endpoints:

$$ \boxed{ [a,b] = \{x:a\leq x\leq b\} }. $$

Four Basic Finite Interval Types

$$ \boxed{ (a,b),\qquad [a,b],\qquad [a,b),\qquad (a,b] }. $$

These differ only in whether each endpoint is included.

Set-Builder Translation

$$ \boxed{ [a,b) = \{x\in\mathbb{R}:a\leq x<b\} }. $$
$$ \boxed{ (a,b] = \{x\in\mathbb{R}:a<x\leq b\} }. $$

Membership

For:

$$ x\in[a,b), $$

both conditions:

$$ a\leq x \qquad\text{and}\qquad x<b $$

must hold.

Endpoint Membership in $[a,b)$

$$ \boxed{ a\in[a,b) } $$

while:

$$ \boxed{ b\notin[a,b) }. $$

Endpoint Membership in $(a,b]$

$$ \boxed{ a\notin(a,b] } $$

while:

$$ \boxed{ b\in(a,b] }. $$

Interval Length

If $a<b$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \text{length}([a,b)) = b-a }. $$

The same length applies to $(a,b]$, $(a,b)$, and $[a,b]$.

Why Endpoint Inclusion Does Not Change Length

A finite collection of individual points has zero ordinary length, so adding or removing endpoints does not alter interval length.

Cardinality

Every nondegenerate real interval contains uncountably many points. Therefore open, closed, and half-open versions have the same cardinality when $a<b$.

Degenerate Half-Open Interval

If $a=b$:

$$ \boxed{ [a,a)=\varnothing }. $$

The conditions $a\leq x<a$ cannot both hold.

Opposite Degenerate Form

$$ \boxed{ (a,a]=\varnothing }. $$

Closed Degenerate Contrast

$$ \boxed{ [a,a]=\{a\} }. $$

Infinite Endpoints

Infinity is never included as an ordinary real endpoint.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ [a,\infty) } $$

is standard, while a square bracket at $\infty$ is not used in ordinary real interval notation.

Negative Infinity

$$ \boxed{ (-\infty,b] = \{x\in\mathbb{R}:x\leq b\} }. $$

Compound Inequality

The inequality:

$$ 4\leq x<9 $$

is equivalent to:

$$ \boxed{ x\in[4,9) }. $$

Reverse Translation

The interval:

$$ (-6,2] $$

means:

$$ \boxed{ -6<x\leq2 }. $$

Intersection Example

Consider:

$$ [0,5) \cap (2,7]. $$

The shared values satisfy:

$$ 2<x<5. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ [0,5)\cap(2,7] = (2,5) }. $$

Adjacent Half-Open Intervals

The intervals:

$$ [0,1) \qquad\text{and}\qquad [1,2) $$

are disjoint because $1$ belongs only to the second interval.

Union of Adjacent Intervals

$$ \boxed{ [0,1)\cup[1,2) = [0,2) }. $$

Partitioning the Real Line

The collection:

$$ \boxed{ \{[n,n+1):n\in\mathbb{Z}\} } $$

partitions the real line.

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{R} = \bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}} [n,n+1) }. $$

No Boundary Double-Counting

Every integer boundary belongs to exactly one interval in this partition.

Floor Function Connection

The floor function satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor=n \iff x\in[n,n+1) }. $$

Natural Cell Decomposition

The interval $[n,n+1)$ can be regarded as the real-number cell associated with the integer $n$ under the floor function.

Piecewise Functions

Half-open intervals are useful for defining adjacent pieces without overlapping boundary values:

$$ f(x) = \begin{cases} f_1(x), & a\leq x<b,\\ f_2(x), & b\leq x<c. \end{cases} $$

Step Functions

Step functions often use intervals of the form $[n,n+1)$ so that every input receives exactly one output.

Histogram Bins

Data bins may be defined as:

$$ [0,10),\quad [10,20),\quad [20,30). $$

A boundary value such as $10$ then belongs to only one bin.

Frequency Tables

Half-open class intervals eliminate ambiguity when observations fall exactly on class boundaries.

Time Intervals

A time block:

$$ [t_0,t_1) $$

begins at $t_0$ and runs up to, but not including, $t_1$.

Consecutive Time Blocks

The intervals:

$$ [8,9),\quad [9,10),\quad [10,11) $$

cover consecutive time spans without overlap.

Computer Science

Start-inclusive, end-exclusive ranges are extremely common in computing.

Integer Indices in $[0,n)$

$$ \boxed{ [0,n)\cap\mathbb{Z} = \{0,1,\ldots,n-1\} }. $$

This set contains exactly $n$ integers.

Index Count

If $a,b\in\mathbb{Z}$ and $a\leq b$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \#\bigl([a,b)\cap\mathbb{Z}\bigr) = b-a }. $$

Why End-Exclusive Ranges Are Convenient

The number of integer indices is simply:

$$ \boxed{ \text{end}-\text{start} }. $$

This makes half-open ranges especially convenient for arrays, slices, loops, and memory blocks.

Modular Representatives

The interval:

$$ [0,n) $$

naturally corresponds to integer representatives:

$$ \boxed{ 0,1,\ldots,n-1 } $$

modulo $n$.

Periodic Quantities

When values repeat every period $T$, the half-open interval:

$$ \boxed{ [0,T) } $$

provides one representative from each equivalence class modulo $T$.

Angles

Direction angles are often represented uniquely in:

$$ \boxed{ [0,2\pi) }. $$

The angle $2\pi$ is excluded because it represents the same direction as $0$.

Alternative Angle Range

$$ \boxed{ (-\pi,\pi] }. $$

This convention chooses $\pi$ rather than $-\pi$ at the shared direction.

Principal Argument

Complex analysis commonly chooses a half-open interval for a principal argument so that each nonzero complex number receives one selected angle.

Fundamental Domain

More generally, half-open intervals are natural fundamental domains for periodic equivalence relations because equivalent endpoints need not both be retained.

Topology of $[a,b)$

In the usual topology of $\mathbb{R}$, for $a<b$, the set:

$$ [a,b) $$

is generally neither open nor closed.

Why $[a,b)$ Is Not Open

The included endpoint $a$ has no ordinary open neighborhood contained entirely in $[a,b)$.

Why $[a,b)$ Is Not Closed

The excluded endpoint $b$ is a limit point of the interval but does not belong to it.

Interior

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{int}([a,b)) = (a,b) }. $$

Closure

$$ \boxed{ \overline{[a,b)} = [a,b] }. $$

Boundary

$$ \boxed{ \partial[a,b) = \{a,b\} }. $$

Topology of $(a,b]$

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{int}((a,b]) = (a,b) } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \overline{(a,b]} = [a,b] }. $$

Relative Topology

Whether a set is open or closed can depend on the ambient space. A half-open interval in $\mathbb{R}$ may have a different open or closed status when treated as a subset of a smaller space.

Connectedness

Every real interval, including every half-open interval, is connected.

Convexity

If $x$ and $y$ lie in a half-open interval, every point between them also lies in the interval.

Boundedness

A finite half-open interval is bounded above and below.

Minimum of $[a,b)$

$$ \boxed{ \min[a,b)=a }. $$

Maximum of $[a,b)$

If $a<b$, no maximum exists because $b$ is excluded and points can be chosen arbitrarily close to $b$ from below.

Supremum of $[a,b)$

$$ \boxed{ \sup[a,b)=b }. $$

Infimum of $[a,b)$

$$ \boxed{ \inf[a,b)=a }. $$

Maximum of $(a,b]$

$$ \boxed{ \max(a,b]=b }. $$

Minimum of $(a,b]$

If $a<b$, no minimum exists because $a$ is excluded.

Infimum of $(a,b]$

$$ \boxed{ \inf(a,b]=a }. $$

Supremum of $(a,b]$

$$ \boxed{ \sup(a,b]=b }. $$

Compactness

A bounded half-open interval such as $[a,b)$ is not compact in the usual topology of $\mathbb{R}$ because it is not closed.

Heine-Borel Contrast

$$ \boxed{ [a,b] \text{ is compact} } $$

while for $a<b$:

$$ \boxed{ [a,b) \text{ is not compact} }. $$

Measure

Under ordinary length measure:

$$ \boxed{ m([a,b)) = b-a }. $$

Endpoint Measure

A finite endpoint has measure zero, so changing its inclusion does not alter the measure of the interval.

Integration

For a suitable function, inclusion or exclusion of an individual endpoint does not alter the ordinary definite integral.

Continuous Probability

If a continuous random variable assigns probability zero to individual points, then endpoint choices do not affect probabilities such as:

$$ P(a\leq X<b) \qquad\text{and}\qquad P(a<X\leq b). $$

Discrete Probability

For a discrete random variable, endpoint inclusion can matter because individual points may have positive probability.

Domains

A function can naturally have a half-open domain, especially when periodic or piecewise conventions require unique boundary assignments.

Ranges

Half-open ranges occur when one limiting value is attained and the other is not.

Common Error: Including the Parenthesized Endpoint

In:

$$ [a,b), $$

$b$ is excluded.

Common Error: Excluding the Bracketed Endpoint

In:

$$ [a,b), $$

$a$ is included.

Common Error: Thinking "Half-Open" Refers to Half the Length

The term concerns endpoint inclusion only.

Common Error: Treating Half-Open and Half-Closed as Opposites

They are usually synonyms for the same interval type.

Common Error: Writing a Square Bracket at Infinity

Infinity is not an ordinary real number and cannot be included as an endpoint in standard real interval notation.

Common Error: Confusing Supremum and Maximum

For $[a,b)$:

$$ \boxed{ \sup[a,b)=b } $$

even though $b$ is not a maximum because $b\notin[a,b)$.

Common Error: Assuming "Half-Open" Is a Topological Classification

The phrase describes endpoint notation. In the usual topology of $\mathbb{R}$, a nondegenerate finite half-open interval is generally neither open nor closed.

Common Misconception: A half-open interval is not partially open in the topological sense. It simply includes one finite endpoint and excludes the other. Thus $[a,b)$ and $(a,b]$ are half-open intervals, yet in the ordinary topology of the real line they are generally neither open nor closed when $a<b$.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$[a,b)$ "The interval from a inclusive to b exclusive."
$(a,b]$ "The interval from a exclusive to b inclusive."
$a\leq x<b$ "X is at least a and less than b."
$a<x\leq b$ "X is greater than a and at most b."
$x\in[a,b)$ "X belongs to the left-closed, right-open interval from a to b."
$[0,2\pi)$ "Zero inclusive to two pi exclusive."
$(-\pi,\pi]$ "Negative pi exclusive to pi inclusive."

Common spoken descriptions include:

  • "left-closed, right-open";
  • "left-open, right-closed";
  • "start-inclusive, end-exclusive";
  • "half-open";
  • "half-closed."

In computing, start-inclusive, end-exclusive is especially common for ranges of the form $[a,b)$.

Defense Notation Advice: Read each endpoint separately. Square brackets include finite endpoints; parentheses exclude them. Translate interval notation into inequalities whenever there is uncertainty. Use parentheses next to $\pm\infty$, and do not confuse the descriptive phrase half-open with the formal topological property of being an open set.
