Newton's Method

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

Newton's Method is an iterative numerical procedure for approximating solutions of equations of the form:

$$ f(x)=0. $$

Beginning with an initial estimate, the method repeatedly replaces that estimate with the $x$-intercept of the tangent line to the graph of $f$ at the current point.

The method is named after Isaac Newton, who developed an early root-approximation procedure during the seventeenth century. The English mathematician Joseph Raphson later published a related and simplified iterative procedure. For this reason the modern method is also widely called the Newton-Raphson Method.

The word method is important: Newton's Method is not a formula that produces an exact root in a single operation. It generates a sequence of approximations:

$$ x_0,\ x_1,\ x_2,\ x_3,\ldots $$

that may converge toward a root.

It is also called the method of tangents because its familiar calculus interpretation is built directly from tangent-line equations.

Operational Nuance

Suppose the goal is to solve:

$$ f(x)=0. $$

Choose an initial approximation:

$$ x_0. $$

At the point:

$$ (x_n,f(x_n)), $$

the tangent line has equation:

$$ y-f(x_n) = f'(x_n)(x-x_n). $$

Newton's Method uses the $x$-intercept of this tangent line as the next approximation. At an $x$-intercept:

$$ y=0. $$

Therefore:

$$ -f(x_n) = f'(x_n)(x_{n+1}-x_n). $$

Solving for $x_{n+1}$ gives the Newton iteration:

$$ \boxed{ x_{n+1} = x_n-\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)} }. $$

Geometric Interpretation

The basic geometric procedure is:

  1. Choose a starting value $x_0$.
  2. Locate the point $(x_0,f(x_0))$ on the curve.
  3. Draw the tangent line there.
  4. Find where that tangent line crosses the $x$-axis.
  5. Use that intercept as $x_1$.
  6. Repeat.

If the process behaves well, the tangent-line intercepts move progressively closer to an actual root.

Example: Approximating $\sqrt2$

To approximate:

$$ \sqrt2, $$

solve:

$$ x^2-2=0. $$

Set:

$$ f(x)=x^2-2 $$

so:

$$ f'(x)=2x. $$

Newton's formula becomes:

$$ x_{n+1} = x_n-\frac{x_n^2-2}{2x_n}. $$

Simplifying:

$$ \boxed{ x_{n+1} = \frac12 \left( x_n+\frac{2}{x_n} \right) }. $$

Choose:

$$ x_0=1.5. $$

Then:

$$ x_1 = \frac12 \left( 1.5+\frac{2}{1.5} \right) \approx1.4166667. $$

A second iteration gives:

$$ x_2\approx1.4142157, $$

and another gives:

$$ x_3\approx1.4142136. $$

Thus:

$$ \sqrt2 \approx1.4142136. $$

Fixed-Point Interpretation

Define the Newton iteration function:

$$ N(x) = x-\frac{f(x)}{f'(x)}. $$

Newton's Method then has the recursive form:

$$ x_{n+1}=N(x_n). $$

If $r$ is a simple root of $f$, then:

$$ f(r)=0 $$

implies:

$$ N(r)=r. $$

Thus a root is a fixed point of the Newton iteration.

Quadratic Convergence

When the root $r$ is simple, the function is sufficiently smooth, and the starting approximation is sufficiently close to the root, Newton's Method usually has quadratic convergence.

Roughly speaking, if:

$$ e_n=x_n-r $$

denotes the error, then near the root:

$$ |e_{n+1}| \approx C|e_n|^2 $$

for some constant $C$. Squaring a small error can cause the number of correct digits to increase very rapidly.

Common Misconception: Newton's Method does not guarantee convergence merely because a root exists. The result depends on the function, its derivative, and the choice of starting value.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$f(x)=0$ "Solve f of x equals zero."
$x_0$ "X sub zero," or "the initial approximation."
$x_n$ "X sub n," or "the current approximation."
$x_{n+1}$ "X sub n plus one," or "the next approximation."
$f'(x_n)$ "F prime evaluated at x sub n."
$x_{n+1}=x_n-\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}$ "The next approximation equals the current approximation minus f of the current approximation divided by f prime of the current approximation."

The phrases Newton's Method and Newton-Raphson Method ordinarily refer to the same familiar numerical root-finding iteration:

$$ x_{n+1} = x_n-\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}. $$

The word iterate means to repeat the same computational rule using the previous output as the next input.

Defense Notation Advice: The subscript $n$ is an iteration index, not an exponent. Thus:

$$ x_3 $$

means the third indexed approximation, whereas:

$$ x^3 $$

means the cube of $x$.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Standard Iteration: The defining computational rule is: $$ \boxed{ x_{n+1} = x_n-\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)} }. $$
  • Root-Finding Purpose: Newton's Method seeks values $r$ satisfying: $$ f(r)=0. $$ It generally produces numerical approximations rather than symbolic exact solutions.
  • Tangent-Line Basis: Each new approximation is the $x$-intercept of the tangent line at: $$ (x_n,f(x_n)). $$
  • Initial Guess: The process requires a starting value: $$ x_0. $$ Different starting values can lead to different roots or entirely different behavior.
  • Simple Root: A root $r$ is simple when: $$ f(r)=0 $$ and: $$ f'(r)\neq0. $$ This is the standard setting in which Newton's Method exhibits its characteristic rapid local convergence.
  • Quadratic Convergence: For a sufficiently smooth function and a starting value sufficiently close to a simple root, Newton's Method converges quadratically: $$ |x_{n+1}-r| \le C|x_n-r|^2 $$ locally for an appropriate constant $C$.
  • Multiple Roots: If $r$ has multiplicity $m>1$, then: $$ f'(r)=0, $$ and ordinary Newton iteration generally loses quadratic convergence and becomes only linearly convergent near the root.
  • Modified Newton Method for Known Multiplicity: If a root has known multiplicity $m$, convergence can be improved using: $$ \boxed{ x_{n+1} = x_n - m\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)} }. $$
  • Derivative Equal to Zero: If: $$ f'(x_n)=0, $$ the standard formula requires division by zero and cannot produce the next iterate.
  • Small Derivatives: Even when: $$ f'(x_n)\neq0, $$ a very small derivative can make: $$ \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)} $$ extremely large, causing the iteration to jump far from the desired root.
  • Divergence: Some starting values generate iterates that move away from every desired root. Newton's Method is therefore a local convergence method rather than an unconditional global solver.
  • Oscillation: An iteration may cycle between two or more values instead of approaching a root. Thus repeated computation alone does not guarantee convergence.
  • Different Roots: For a function with several roots, different starting values may converge to different roots.
  • No Real Root: A real-valued Newton iteration need not converge when the equation has no real solution. For example, attempting to solve: $$ x^2+1=0 $$ using only real iterates cannot produce a real root.
  • Complex Newton Method: Newton's Method can also be applied over the complex numbers: $$ z_{n+1} = z_n-\frac{f(z_n)}{f'(z_n)}. $$ Different initial complex values may converge to different complex roots.
  • Newton Fractals: For complex polynomials with multiple roots, coloring each starting point according to the root to which Newton iteration converges produces intricate fractal boundaries known as Newton fractals.
  • Stopping Criterion: Numerical implementations require a decision about when to stop. One common criterion is: $$ |x_{n+1}-x_n|<\varepsilon $$ for a chosen tolerance $\varepsilon$.
  • Residual Criterion: Another possibility is: $$ |f(x_n)|<\varepsilon. $$ A small residual means the current approximation nearly satisfies the equation, although residual size and root error are not always equivalent.
  • Maximum Iterations: Practical software generally imposes a maximum number of iterations so that a nonconvergent process does not continue indefinitely.
  • Square Roots: Applying Newton's Method to: $$ f(x)=x^2-A $$ gives: $$ \boxed{ x_{n+1} = \frac12 \left( x_n+\frac{A}{x_n} \right) }. $$ This is a classical and highly efficient square-root iteration.
  • Reciprocal Computation: Newton-style iterations can be constructed for reciprocals and other elementary operations, making the idea important in numerical computing and numerical hardware algorithms.
  • Comparison with the Bisection Method: Bisection converges more slowly but, when its hypotheses are satisfied, provides strong convergence guarantees through interval bracketing. Newton's Method is usually much faster near a simple root but can fail from a poor starting value.
  • Comparison with the Secant Method: The secant method approximates the derivative using two previous points rather than evaluating: $$ f'(x_n). $$ It therefore avoids explicit differentiation but typically converges more slowly than Newton's Method near a simple root.
  • Optimization: To locate stationary points of a function $g$, one may apply Newton's Method to: $$ g'(x)=0. $$ The resulting iteration is: $$ \boxed{ x_{n+1} = x_n-\frac{g'(x_n)}{g''(x_n)} }. $$
  • Several Variables: For a system: $$ F(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{0}, $$ Newton's Method generalizes using the Jacobian matrix: $$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x}_{n+1} = \mathbf{x}_n - J_F(\mathbf{x}_n)^{-1} F(\mathbf{x}_n) }, $$ when the inverse exists.
  • Linear-System Form: In numerical practice, one normally does not compute the Jacobian inverse explicitly. Instead, solve: $$ J_F(\mathbf{x}_n)\mathbf{s}_n = -F(\mathbf{x}_n) $$ and update: $$ \mathbf{x}_{n+1} = \mathbf{x}_n+\mathbf{s}_n. $$
  • Numerical Analysis: Newton's Method is a central example for studying convergence rate, local error, conditioning, stopping conditions, and the interaction between analytic formulas and finite-precision computation.
  • Historical Name: Newton's Method and Newton-Raphson Method are both standard. The second name better signals Joseph Raphson's important independent contribution to the development and publication history of the method.
  • Terminological Contrast: Newton's Method is a derivative-based iterative root solver. The bisection method narrows a bracketing interval, while the secant method replaces the derivative with a finite secant slope. All three seek roots, but their information requirements, convergence rates, and reliability differ.
