GLB

Subfield: Calculus & Analysis

Etymology & Roots

GLB abbreviates greatest lower bound. For a set of ordered values, the GLB is the largest number that is less than or equal to every element of the set.

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{GLB}(S)=\inf S } $$

The standard modern term is infimum. The phrase greatest lower bound emphasizes that the value must first be a lower bound and then must be greater than or equal to every other lower bound.

Operational Nuance

Lower Bound

A number $L$ is a lower bound of a set $S$ if:

$$ \boxed{ L\leq x \quad\text{for every }x\in S }. $$

Greatest Lower Bound

A number $g$ is the greatest lower bound of $S$ when:

$$ \boxed{ g\leq x \text{ for every }x\in S, \qquad L\leq g \text{ for every lower bound }L }. $$

Infimum Notation

$$ \boxed{ g=\inf S }. $$

The notation $\inf S$ is generally preferred in analysis, although GLB remains common in instructional language.

GLB vs. Minimum

A minimum must belong to the set. A greatest lower bound need not.

$$ S=(0,1) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \inf S=0, $$

but $S$ has no minimum because $0\notin S$.

When GLB Is a Minimum

If $\inf S$ is itself an element of $S$, then it is also the minimum:

$$ \boxed{ \inf S\in S \Longrightarrow \min S=\inf S }. $$

Approximation Property

If $g=\inf S$ and $g$ is finite, then elements of $S$ can be found arbitrarily close above $g$. Equivalently, for every $\varepsilon>0$, there exists $x\in S$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ g\leq x<g+\varepsilon }. $$

Completeness of the Real Numbers

A central completeness property of $\mathbb{R}$ states that every nonempty subset of $\mathbb{R}$ that is bounded below has a real greatest lower bound.

Common Misconception

The greatest lower bound is not the "smallest number in the set" unless that bound actually belongs to the set. GLB and minimum coincide only under that additional condition.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\inf S$ "The infimum of S."
$\operatorname{GLB}(S)$ "The greatest lower bound of S."
$L\leq x$ for all $x\in S$ "L is a lower bound for S."
$g=\inf S$ "G is the greatest lower bound of S."
$\min S$ "The minimum element of S."
$\inf S\notin S$ "The infimum is not attained by the set."

GLB is an abbreviation rather than a universal operator notation. Professional analysis texts generally favor $\inf S$.

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish three statements: "bounded below," "has infimum $g$," and "has minimum $g$." They describe related but different properties.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: GLB means the largest of all lower bounds of an ordered set.
  • Standard Synonym: Greatest lower bound and infimum denote the same concept.
  • Minimum Distinction: The GLB need not belong to the set.
  • Attained GLB: If the greatest lower bound belongs to the set, it is the minimum.
  • Open Intervals: $(a,b)$ has GLB $a$ even though $a$ is excluded.
  • Closed Intervals: $[a,b]$ has GLB $a$, and here the GLB is also the minimum.
  • Functions: The infimum of the range of a function may exist even when the function never attains that value.
  • Calculus: GLB language appears in discussions of bounded functions, extrema, limits, and integration.
  • Real Analysis: The greatest-lower-bound property is one formulation of completeness of the real numbers.
  • Order Theory: Infima generalize beyond real numbers to partially ordered sets.
  • Dual Concept: The corresponding upper-bound concept is the least upper bound, or supremum.
  • Common Error: Assuming the GLB must be an element of the set.
  • Common Error: Choosing any lower bound instead of the greatest one.
  • Common Error: Confusing $\inf S$ with $\min S$ when the boundary value is not attained.
  • Core Interpretation: The GLB is the sharpest possible lower boundary: $$ \boxed{ \text{all elements of }S \text{ lie at or above }g, \quad \text{and no larger lower bound exists} }. $$ It captures the lower edge of a set even when that edge is not itself contained in the set.
