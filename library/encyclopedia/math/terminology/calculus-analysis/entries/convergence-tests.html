Convergence Tests

Subfield: Calculus & Analysis

Etymology & Roots

Convergence tests are criteria used to determine whether an infinite sequence or series approaches a finite limiting behavior. In introductory calculus, the term most often refers to tests for infinite series.

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n \text{ converges} \iff \lim_{N\to\infty} \sum_{n=1}^{N}a_n \text{ exists and is finite} } $$

The word convergence describes values drawing toward a limit, while a test supplies conditions that establish convergence or divergence without necessarily computing the exact sum.

Operational Nuance

Term Test for Divergence

A necessary condition for $\sum a_n$ to converge is:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=0 }. $$

Therefore, if the terms do not approach zero, the series diverges. The converse is false: terms approaching zero do not by themselves guarantee convergence.

Direct Comparison Test

For eventually nonnegative terms, comparison with a known benchmark can establish convergence or divergence:

$$ 0\leq a_n\leq b_n, \qquad \sum b_n\text{ converges} \Longrightarrow \sum a_n\text{ converges}. $$

Limit Comparison Test

If $a_n,b_n>0$ eventually and:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_n}{b_n}=L, \qquad 0<L<\infty } $$

then $\sum a_n$ and $\sum b_n$ have the same convergence behavior.

Ratio Test

$$ \boxed{ L= \lim_{n\to\infty} \left| \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} \right| } $$

When the limit exists, $L<1$ gives absolute convergence and $L>1$ gives divergence. The case $L=1$ is inconclusive.

Root Test

$$ \boxed{ L= \limsup_{n\to\infty} \sqrt[n]{|a_n|} } $$

If $L<1$, the series converges absolutely; if $L>1$, it diverges. Again, $L=1$ is inconclusive.

Integral Test

If $a_n=f(n)$ where $f$ is positive, continuous, and decreasing eventually, then:

$$ \boxed{ \sum a_n \text{ and } \int f(x)\,dx \text{ have the same convergence behavior} }. $$

Alternating Series Test

If $b_n\geq0$, $b_n$ is eventually nonincreasing, and $b_n\to0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n-1}b_n \text{ converges} }. $$

Absolute Convergence

If:

$$ \boxed{ \sum |a_n| \text{ converges} } $$

then $\sum a_n$ converges. This is called absolute convergence.

Benchmark Series

Many convergence tests depend on recognizing standard examples such as geometric and $p$-series:

$$ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}ar^n \text{ converges when }|r|<1, $$
$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^p} \text{ converges when }p>1. $$

Inconclusive Does Not Mean Divergent

A convergence test can fail to decide the question. For example, the ratio test yielding $L=1$ says only that another method is needed.

Common Misconception

Convergence tests generally determine whether a series converges; they usually do not determine the exact value of its sum.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\sum a_n$ "The infinite series with terms a sub n."
$a_n\to0$ "The terms approach zero."
$0\leq a_n\leq b_n$ "Compare a sub n with the larger benchmark b sub n."
$\left|a_{n+1}/a_n\right|$ "The absolute ratio of consecutive terms."
$\sqrt[n]{|a_n|}$ "The nth root of the absolute value of the nth term."
$\sum|a_n|$ "The corresponding absolute-value series."

Authors may say that a test establishes convergence, proves divergence, or is inconclusive. These outcomes should not be conflated.

Defense Notation Advice: State the hypotheses of the test before applying it. Positivity, monotonicity, absolute values, or existence of a relevant limit may be essential to the conclusion.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Term Test: If $a_n\nrightarrow0$, then $\sum a_n$ diverges.
  • Comparison Test: Useful for positive-term series resembling known benchmarks.
  • Limit Comparison: Useful when two positive-term series have asymptotically proportional terms.
  • Ratio Test: Especially effective with factorials, exponentials, and power-series terms.
  • Root Test: Especially natural when the nth term contains an nth power.
  • Integral Test: Connects positive decreasing series with improper integrals.
  • Alternating Series Test: Handles many sign-alternating series that may fail absolute convergence.
  • Absolute Convergence: Stronger than ordinary convergence and implies it.
  • Conditional Convergence: Occurs when $\sum a_n$ converges but $\sum|a_n|$ diverges.
  • Power Series: Ratio and root tests are commonly used to determine radii and intervals of convergence.
  • Common Error: Assuming $a_n\to0$ proves that $\sum a_n$ converges.
  • Common Error: Treating an inconclusive test result as proof of divergence.
  • Common Error: Applying a comparison or integral test without checking its hypotheses.
  • Common Error: Confusing convergence of the sequence $a_n$ with convergence of the series $\sum a_n$.
  • Core Interpretation: Convergence tests are decision tools for infinite processes: $$ \boxed{ \text{recognize structure} \longrightarrow \text{choose an appropriate test} \longrightarrow \text{establish convergence or divergence} }. $$ They allow infinite series to be classified without requiring an exact closed-form sum.
