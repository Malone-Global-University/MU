Instantaneous Acceleration

Subfield: Calculus & Analysis

Etymology & Roots

Instantaneous acceleration is the rate at which velocity is changing at a particular instant. The word instantaneous distinguishes this pointwise rate from an average change measured across a finite time interval.

$$ \boxed{ a(t)=\frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{d^2s}{dt^2} } $$

Thus instantaneous acceleration is the first derivative of velocity and, when position $s(t)$ is twice differentiable, the second derivative of position.

Operational Nuance

Limit Definition

Instantaneous acceleration at time $t$ is obtained by shrinking the time interval used for average acceleration:

$$ \boxed{ a(t) = \lim_{\Delta t\to0} \frac{v(t+\Delta t)-v(t)}{\Delta t} } $$

Average vs. Instantaneous Acceleration

Over a finite interval from $t_1$ to $t_2$, average acceleration is:

$$ a_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{v(t_2)-v(t_1)}{t_2-t_1}. $$

Instantaneous acceleration instead describes the local rate of velocity change at one time.

From Position

If $s(t)$ represents one-dimensional position, then:

$$ \boxed{ v(t)=s'(t), \qquad a(t)=v'(t)=s''(t) }. $$

Example

If:

$$ s(t)=t^3-3t^2+2t, $$

then:

$$ v(t)=3t^2-6t+2, \qquad \boxed{a(t)=6t-6}. $$

Units

Acceleration has units of velocity divided by time. If position is measured in meters and time in seconds, then:

$$ \boxed{ [a]=\mathrm{m/s^2} }. $$

Sign in One Dimension

Positive acceleration means velocity is changing in the positive coordinate direction; negative acceleration means it is changing in the negative direction. Neither statement alone determines whether the object is speeding up.

Speeding Up and Slowing Down

In one-dimensional motion, velocity and acceleration having the same sign means speed is increasing; opposite signs mean speed is decreasing:

$$ \boxed{ va>0 \Rightarrow \text{speed increasing}, \qquad va<0 \Rightarrow \text{speed decreasing} } $$

at times where the relevant quantities are nonzero.

Vector Acceleration

For position vector $\mathbf{r}(t)$:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a}(t) = \mathbf{v}'(t) = \mathbf{r}''(t) }. $$

A velocity vector can change because its magnitude changes, its direction changes, or both. An object therefore may accelerate even while moving at constant speed.

Common Misconception

Negative acceleration does not automatically mean slowing down. Acceleration describes change in velocity, so its effect on speed depends on the direction of the velocity as well.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$a(t)=v'(t)$ "Acceleration is the derivative of velocity with respect to time."
$a(t)=s''(t)$ "Acceleration is the second derivative of position."
$a(t_0)$ "The instantaneous acceleration at time t sub zero."
$a_{\mathrm{avg}}=\Delta v/\Delta t$ "Average acceleration is change in velocity divided by change in time."
$\mathbf{a}(t)=\mathbf{r}''(t)$ "The acceleration vector is the second derivative of the position vector."
$a=0$ "The instantaneous velocity is not changing at that instant."

In elementary one-dimensional calculus, $a(t)$ is often treated as a signed scalar. In multidimensional motion, acceleration is naturally a vector quantity.

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish acceleration from velocity and speed. A statement about the sign of acceleration should always be interpreted relative to the chosen coordinate orientation and the current velocity.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: Instantaneous acceleration is the instantaneous rate of change of velocity.
  • Derivative of Velocity: $a(t)=v'(t)$ whenever velocity is differentiable.
  • Second Derivative of Position: $a(t)=s''(t)$ whenever position is twice differentiable.
  • Average-Rate Connection: Instantaneous acceleration is obtained as the limiting value of average acceleration over shrinking time intervals.
  • Constant Acceleration: If $a(t)=a_0$, velocity changes linearly with time.
  • Variable Acceleration: If $a$ depends on time or state, velocity generally changes nonlinearly.
  • Free Fall: Near Earth's surface and neglecting air resistance, vertical acceleration is often approximated as constant and downward.
  • One-Dimensional Motion: The signs of velocity and acceleration together determine whether speed is increasing or decreasing.
  • Curved Motion: Acceleration can arise solely from a change in velocity direction even when speed remains constant.
  • Vector Calculus: Acceleration is the derivative of the velocity vector and the second derivative of the position vector.
  • Differential Equations: Laws prescribing acceleration often generate second-order equations of motion.
  • Mechanics: Newton's second law connects acceleration with net force through $\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{net}}=m\mathbf{a}$ for constant mass.
  • Common Error: Treating negative acceleration as synonymous with deceleration.
  • Common Error: Confusing instantaneous acceleration with average acceleration over a finite interval.
  • Common Error: Assuming zero acceleration means zero velocity; it means velocity is locally not changing.
  • Core Interpretation: Instantaneous acceleration measures change of motion at a particular moment: $$ \boxed{ \text{position} \xrightarrow{\ d/dt\ } \text{velocity} \xrightarrow{\ d/dt\ } \text{acceleration} }. $$ It is therefore a second-order description of position and a first-order description of how velocity evolves.
