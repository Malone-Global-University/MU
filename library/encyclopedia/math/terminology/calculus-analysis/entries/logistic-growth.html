Logistic Growth

Subfield: Calculus & Analysis

Etymology & Roots

Logistic growth is a growth pattern in which a quantity initially grows approximately exponentially but gradually slows as it approaches a limiting level called the carrying capacity.

Its standard continuous model is the logistic differential equation:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dP}{dt} = rP\left(1-\frac{P}{K}\right) } $$

where:

  • $P=P(t)$ is the quantity being modeled;
  • $t$ is the independent variable, commonly time;
  • $r$ is the intrinsic growth-rate parameter;
  • $K$ is the carrying capacity.

The word growth describes increase in the modeled quantity. The adjective logistic identifies the particular self-limiting mathematical law rather than merely any process involving a maximum value.

The logistic function became associated with nineteenth-century population modeling, especially the work of Pierre François Verhulst. The model was developed as an alternative to unrestricted exponential growth by incorporating the idea that growth should weaken as population size approaches environmental limits.

Its central conceptual contrast is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{exponential growth} \longrightarrow \text{unrestricted proportional growth} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{logistic growth} \longrightarrow \text{proportional growth moderated by capacity} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Core Differential Equation

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dP}{dt} = rP\left(1-\frac{P}{K}\right) }. $$

The factor $rP$ resembles exponential growth, while:

$$ \boxed{ 1-\frac{P}{K} } $$

reduces the growth rate as $P$ approaches $K$.

Intrinsic Growth Rate

The parameter $r$ controls the underlying proportional tendency toward growth. When the population is small relative to $K$:

$$ \frac{P}{K}\approx0, $$

so:

$$ 1-\frac{P}{K}\approx1. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dP}{dt} \approx rP }. $$

Early logistic growth therefore resembles exponential growth.

Carrying Capacity

The parameter $K$ is the equilibrium level toward which a standard logistic solution with $0<P_0<K$ approaches.

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{t\to\infty}P(t)=K }. $$

Meaning of Carrying Capacity

In population applications, $K$ represents the population level the modeled environment can sustain under the assumptions of the model.

In other applications, it can represent a saturation level, maximum adoption level, system capacity, or another limiting quantity.

Equilibria

Equilibria occur where:

$$ \frac{dP}{dt}=0. $$

From:

$$ rP\left(1-\frac{P}{K}\right)=0, $$

the equilibria are:

$$ \boxed{ P=0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad P=K }. $$

Growth below Carrying Capacity

If:

$$ 0<P<K $$

and $r>0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dP}{dt}>0 }. $$

The quantity increases.

Behavior above Carrying Capacity

If:

$$ P>K, $$

then:

$$ 1-\frac{P}{K}<0, $$

so for $r>0$:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dP}{dt}<0 }. $$

The standard model therefore drives values above $K$ downward toward $K$.

Stability of $K$

For $r>0$, the equilibrium $P=K$ is stable: nearby positive solutions tend toward it.

Instability of Zero

For $r>0$, the equilibrium $P=0$ is unstable in the standard continuous logistic model because a small positive population grows away from zero.

Closed-Form Logistic Function

The standard solution can be written:

$$ \boxed{ P(t) = \frac{K} {1+Ae^{-rt}} } $$

where $A$ is determined by the initial condition.

Initial Population

If:

$$ P(0)=P_0, $$

then:

$$ P_0 = \frac{K}{1+A}. $$

Solving for $A$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ A = \frac{K-P_0}{P_0} }. $$

Initial-Value Form

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ P(t) = \frac{K} {1+ \left( \frac{K-P_0}{P_0} \right)e^{-rt}} } $$

for a positive initial value $P_0$.

Equivalent Form

Multiplying numerator and denominator by $P_0e^{rt}$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ P(t) = \frac{KP_0e^{rt}} {K-P_0+P_0e^{rt}} }. $$

S-Shaped Curve

For $0<P_0<K$, the graph has a characteristic sigmoid or S-shaped form:

  • slow absolute growth when the population is very small;
  • rapid growth at intermediate population levels;
  • slowing growth near carrying capacity.

Growth Rate as a Function of Population

Define:

$$ G(P) = rP\left(1-\frac{P}{K}\right). $$

Expanding:

$$ \boxed{ G(P) = rP - \frac{r}{K}P^2 }. $$

Thus the instantaneous growth rate is a downward-opening quadratic function of population size.

Maximum Growth Rate

The quadratic $G(P)$ reaches its maximum at:

$$ \boxed{ P=\frac{K}{2} }. $$

Maximum Instantaneous Growth

Substituting $P=K/2$:

$$ G\left(\frac{K}{2}\right) = r\frac{K}{2} \left( 1-\frac{1}{2} \right). $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ \left(\frac{dP}{dt}\right)_{\max} = \frac{rK}{4} }. $$

Inflection Point

For the standard increasing logistic curve with $0<P_0<K$, the inflection point occurs when:

$$ \boxed{ P=\frac{K}{2} }. $$

Concavity before the Inflection Point

When:

$$ 0<P<\frac{K}{2}, $$

the population curve is concave upward.

Concavity after the Inflection Point

When:

$$ \frac{K}{2}<P<K, $$

the curve is concave downward.

Second Derivative

Starting from:

$$ P' = rP\left(1-\frac{P}{K}\right), $$

differentiating gives:

$$ \boxed{ P'' = rP' \left( 1-\frac{2P}{K} \right) }. $$

For an increasing solution with $P'>0$, the sign of $P''$ changes when $P=K/2$.

Inflection Time

For:

$$ P(t) = \frac{K}{1+Ae^{-rt}}, $$

the inflection condition $P=K/2$ gives:

$$ Ae^{-rt}=1. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ t_{\mathrm{inf}} = \frac{\ln A}{r} } $$

when $A>0$.

Inflection Time from Initial Value

If $0<P_0<K$:

$$ \boxed{ t_{\mathrm{inf}} = \frac{1}{r} \ln\left( \frac{K-P_0}{P_0} \right) }. $$

This value may be negative if the initial population is already above $K/2$.

Per-Capita Growth Rate

Divide the logistic equation by $P>0$:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{1}{P}\frac{dP}{dt} = r\left(1-\frac{P}{K}\right) }. $$

The per-capita growth rate decreases linearly as population increases.

At Very Small Population

$$ \frac{1}{P}\frac{dP}{dt} \approx r. $$

At Carrying Capacity

$$ \boxed{ \frac{1}{P}\frac{dP}{dt}=0 } $$

when $P=K$.

Exponential Approximation

When:

$$ P\ll K, $$

the logistic equation approximates:

$$ \boxed{ P'\approx rP }. $$

Thus:

$$ P(t)\approx P_0e^{rt} $$

during sufficiently early growth.

Departure from Exponential Growth

As $P/K$ ceases to be negligible, the limiting factor:

$$ 1-\frac{P}{K} $$

reduces growth below the exponential prediction.

Asymptotic Approach

In the standard continuous model, a solution beginning below $K$ approaches the carrying capacity asymptotically rather than reaching it in finite time.

$$ \boxed{ P(t)<K \text{ for finite }t \quad\text{when}\quad 0<P_0<K }. $$

Horizontal Asymptote

The upper horizontal asymptote is:

$$ \boxed{ P=K }. $$

Lower Asymptotic Behavior

For the standard form with $A>0$:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{t\to-\infty}P(t)=0 }. $$

Logistic Curve Symmetry

In its standard centered form, the logistic curve has point symmetry about its inflection point.

Centered Logistic Form

Writing the inflection time as $t_0$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ P(t) = \frac{K} {1+e^{-r(t-t_0)}} }. $$

At $t=t_0$:

$$ \boxed{ P(t_0)=\frac{K}{2} }. $$

Normalized Logistic Function

Setting $K=1$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ L(x) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-x}} }. $$

This normalized form maps the real line into the interval $(0,1)$.

Complement Identity

The normalized logistic function satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ L(-x)=1-L(x) }. $$

Derivative of the Normalized Logistic Function

$$ \boxed{ L'(x) = L(x)\bigl(1-L(x)\bigr) }. $$

This compact identity is a normalized version of the logistic growth law.

Solving for Time

From:

$$ P = \frac{K}{1+Ae^{-rt}}, $$

rearrange:

$$ \frac{K}{P}-1 = Ae^{-rt}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ t = -\frac{1}{r} \ln\left( \frac{K/P-1}{A} \right) } $$

where all logarithmic arguments must be positive.

Odds Form

Rearranging the logistic solution gives:

$$ \frac{P}{K-P} = \frac{1}{A}e^{rt}. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \ln\left( \frac{P}{K-P} \right) = rt-\ln A }. $$

Logit Transformation

If:

$$ p=\frac{P}{K}, \qquad 0<p<1, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \ln\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right) } $$

is the logit transformation.

Linearized Logistic Relation

For the standard logistic curve:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{logit}(p) = rt-\ln A }. $$

The nonlinear S-curve becomes linear after the log-odds transformation.

Separation of Variables

The logistic differential equation is separable:

$$ \frac{dP}{dt} = rP\left(1-\frac{P}{K}\right). $$

Rearranging:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dP} {P(1-P/K)} = r\,dt }. $$

Partial-Fraction Form

Since:

$$ P\left(1-\frac{P}{K}\right) = \frac{P(K-P)}{K}, $$

one obtains:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{K}{P(K-P)} = \frac{1}{P} + \frac{1}{K-P} }. $$

Integration

Integrating leads to:

$$ \ln|P| - \ln|K-P| = rt+C. $$

Equivalently:

$$ \boxed{ \ln\left| \frac{P}{K-P} \right| = rt+C }. $$

Recovering the Logistic Formula

Exponentiating and solving for $P$ yields the standard logistic form:

$$ \boxed{ P(t) = \frac{K} {1+Ae^{-rt}} }. $$

Units of $r$

Because $rt$ must be dimensionless in the exponential:

$$ \boxed{ [r] = \frac{1}{[\text{time}]} }. $$

Units of $K$

$K$ has the same units as $P$.

$$ \boxed{ [K]=[P] }. $$

Dimensionless Capacity Ratio

The ratio:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{P}{K} } $$

is dimensionless.

Normalized Population

Let:

$$ p=\frac{P}{K}. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dp}{dt} = rp(1-p) }. $$

Dimensionless Time

Let:

$$ \tau=rt. $$

Then the model becomes:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dp}{d\tau} = p(1-p) }. $$

This normalized equation reveals the universal shape of the basic logistic model.

Exponential Growth Comparison

Exponential growth obeys:

$$ \boxed{ P'=rP }. $$

Logistic growth obeys:

$$ \boxed{ P' = rP \left( 1-\frac{P}{K} \right) }. $$

The logistic model adds density-dependent limitation.

Constant Per-Capita Rate in Exponential Growth

$$ \boxed{ \frac{P'}{P}=r }. $$

Declining Per-Capita Rate in Logistic Growth

$$ \boxed{ \frac{P'}{P} = r\left(1-\frac{P}{K}\right) }. $$

Logistic Growth Is Not Linear Growth

Linear growth has constant absolute rate:

$$ \boxed{ P'=c }. $$

Logistic growth has a rate depending nonlinearly on $P$.

Logistic Equation Is Nonlinear

Expanding:

$$ P' = rP-\frac{r}{K}P^2. $$

The $P^2$ term makes the differential equation nonlinear.

Logistic Growth vs. Logistic Regression

Logistic growth models a quantity evolving toward a carrying capacity. Logistic regression models probabilities through a logistic response function. The same sigmoid function appears, but the modeling goals are different.

Logistic Regression Form

A common probability model is:

$$ \boxed{ p = \frac{1} {1+e^{-(\beta_0+\beta_1x)}} }. $$

This is not, by itself, a population-growth differential equation.

Population Biology

Logistic growth is commonly introduced as a model of population expansion under finite environmental resources.

Ecological Interpretation

The factor:

$$ 1-\frac{P}{K} $$

represents an idealized reduction in per-capita growth as population density increases.

Epidemiology and Adoption Models

Logistic curves may approximate cumulative processes that begin slowly, accelerate, and then saturate. The interpretation of $K$ depends on the application and need not represent ecological carrying capacity literally.

Technology Adoption

A logistic curve can model adoption approaching a finite market or saturation level under simplified assumptions.

Diffusion of Innovations

Logistic-like S-curves are used as simplified models of cumulative adoption, though other sigmoid models may fit particular diffusion processes better.

Chemical and Biological Saturation

Logistic-shaped curves may appear in systems with limiting resources or finite capacity, but similar visual shape alone does not prove that the logistic differential equation is the correct mechanism.

Neural Networks

The normalized logistic function:

$$ \frac{1}{1+e^{-x}} $$

has historically been used as a sigmoid activation function.

Logistic Map

The logistic map is a discrete dynamical system:

$$ \boxed{ x_{n+1} = rx_n(1-x_n) }. $$

It is related algebraically to the normalized logistic growth term but is not the same model as the continuous logistic differential equation.

Continuous vs. Discrete Logistic Models

The differential equation:

$$ P' = rP\left(1-\frac{P}{K}\right) $$

evolves continuously in time, whereas a recurrence such as:

$$ x_{n+1} = rx_n(1-x_n) $$

updates at discrete steps.

Qualitative Difference from the Logistic Map

The basic continuous logistic equation with positive parameters approaches equilibrium smoothly. The discrete logistic map can display oscillations, period-doubling, and chaotic behavior for certain parameter values.

Parameter Estimation

In applied work, $K$, $r$, and timing parameters may be estimated from observed data rather than assumed known.

Carrying Capacity Need Not Be Truly Constant

Real environments may change over time. If resources, technology, climate, or system constraints change, a fixed $K$ may be an inadequate assumption.

Time-Varying Capacity

More advanced models may replace constant $K$ by:

$$ \boxed{ K=K(t) }. $$

Such models are no longer the simplest autonomous logistic equation.

Delayed Logistic Growth

Some systems respond to crowding or resource limitation with a delay, leading to delay differential equations rather than the elementary logistic equation.

Harvesting Models

A harvesting term may modify logistic growth:

$$ \boxed{ P' = rP\left(1-\frac{P}{K}\right)-H }. $$

This changes the equilibrium structure and may introduce extinction thresholds.

Proportional Harvesting

Another model uses:

$$ \boxed{ P' = rP\left(1-\frac{P}{K}\right)-hP }. $$

Model Assumptions

The basic logistic model typically assumes:

  • a single aggregated population or quantity;
  • a fixed carrying capacity;
  • a fixed intrinsic growth parameter;
  • instantaneous density dependence;
  • continuous change;
  • no age, spatial, or subgroup structure unless absorbed into parameters.

Logistic Growth Is a Model, Not a Universal Law

Real populations can overshoot, collapse, oscillate, migrate, experience changing environments, or respond to interactions not represented by the elementary logistic equation.

Carrying Capacity Is Model-Dependent

$K$ is not necessarily an immutable physical maximum. It is a parameter describing the limiting level under a particular model and set of conditions.

Common Error: Calling Every S-Curve Logistic

Many functions are sigmoid. A visually S-shaped graph is not sufficient evidence that the data obey the logistic differential equation.

Common Error: Treating $K$ as a Hard Ceiling Reached in Finite Time

In the standard continuous solution beginning below $K$, the population approaches $K$ asymptotically.

Common Error: Assuming Growth Rate Is Greatest near Zero

The per-capita growth rate is greatest near zero, but the absolute growth rate $P'$ is greatest at:

$$ \boxed{ P=\frac{K}{2} }. $$

Common Error: Confusing Absolute and Per-Capita Growth

Absolute growth is:

$$ P' = rP\left(1-\frac{P}{K}\right), $$

whereas per-capita growth is:

$$ \frac{P'}{P} = r\left(1-\frac{P}{K}\right). $$

Common Error: Confusing Logistic and Exponential Growth

Logistic growth resembles exponential growth only while $P$ is small relative to $K$.

Common Error: Treating $r$ as the Actual Growth Rate at All Times

$r$ is the intrinsic per-capita growth parameter. The actual per-capita rate is:

$$ \boxed{ r\left(1-\frac{P}{K}\right) }. $$

Common Error: Assuming Growth Stops Because $r$ Becomes Zero

In the basic model, $r$ remains constant. Growth slows because the capacity factor $1-P/K$ approaches zero.

Common Error: Confusing the Inflection Point with the Carrying Capacity

The inflection point occurs at $P=K/2$, not $P=K$.

Common Error: Assuming the Curve Is Concave Down Everywhere

The standard increasing logistic curve is concave up before $K/2$ and concave down after $K/2$.

Common Error: Confusing Logistic Growth with Logistic Regression

Both use the logistic function, but one models dynamical growth and the other commonly models probabilities or categorical outcomes.

Common Error: Confusing Logistic Growth with the Logistic Map

The continuous differential equation and discrete recurrence have substantially different dynamical behavior.

Common Error: Assuming Carrying Capacity Is Always Population

$K$ can represent any modeled saturation level with the same units as the response variable.

Common Error: Ignoring Units

$P$ and $K$ must have compatible units, while $r$ has reciprocal-time units in the standard temporal model.

Common Error: Treating the Model as Mechanistically Exact

The logistic equation is an idealized approximation whose validity depends on how well its assumptions match the system.

Common Misconception: Logistic growth is not merely exponential growth with a maximum pasted onto the end. Its growth rate changes continuously with the current population through the factor $1-P/K$. The model therefore predicts early near-exponential growth, maximum absolute growth at half the carrying capacity, and progressively slower growth as the limiting level is approached.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$P'=rP(1-P/K)$ "The rate of change of P equals r times P times one minus P over K."
$K$ "The carrying capacity," or more generally "the limiting capacity."
$r$ "The intrinsic growth-rate parameter."
$P(t)=K/(1+Ae^{-rt})$ "P of t follows a logistic curve with carrying capacity K."
$P=K/2$ "The population is at half the carrying capacity."
$(dP/dt)_{\max}=rK/4$ "The maximum instantaneous growth rate is r K over four."
$\lim_{t\to\infty}P(t)=K$ "The population approaches the carrying capacity as time tends to infinity."

The phrase logistic growth rate can be ambiguous. It may refer to the instantaneous absolute rate:

$$ \frac{dP}{dt}, $$

the per-capita rate:

$$ \frac{1}{P}\frac{dP}{dt}, $$

or the parameter $r$. Precise writing should identify which meaning is intended.

Likewise, carrying capacity is most natural in ecological language, while saturation level, limiting value, or capacity may be preferable in nonpopulation applications.

Defense Notation Advice: Define $P$, $r$, and $K$ before using the logistic equation, and distinguish the parameter $r$ from the actual growth rate $P'$. State whether the model is continuous or discrete. Do not use the phrase "logistic" merely because a graph is S-shaped; identify the actual logistic equation or function being assumed.
