Absolutely Convergent

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

Absolutely convergent is an adjectival phrase used to describe an infinite series, improper integral, or related object whose convergence remains valid after taking absolute values or magnitudes.

For an infinite series:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n, $$

saying that the series is absolutely convergent means:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|a_n| \text{ converges} }. $$

The adjective absolute refers to the use of absolute value, while convergent means that the relevant sequence of partial sums approaches a finite limit.

The noun phrase is:

$$ \boxed{\text{absolute convergence}}, $$

while the corresponding adjective phrase is:

$$ \boxed{\text{absolutely convergent}}. $$

Thus one says that a series has absolute convergence or, more naturally, that the series is absolutely convergent.

Operational Nuance

Series Definition

A real or complex series:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n $$

is absolutely convergent if:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|a_n| <\infty }. $$

Equivalently:

$$ \boxed{ \sum a_n \text{ is absolutely convergent} \iff \sum|a_n| \text{ converges} }. $$

Absolute Convergence Implies Convergence

A fundamental result of analysis states:

$$ \boxed{ \sum|a_n|\text{ converges} \Longrightarrow \sum a_n\text{ converges} }. $$

Therefore every absolutely convergent series is convergent.

The converse is false.

Example

Consider:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{n^2}. $$

Taking absolute values gives:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left| \frac{(-1)^n}{n^2} \right| = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^2}. $$

Since this is a $p$-series with:

$$ p=2>1, $$

it converges. Therefore the original series is:

$$ \boxed{\text{absolutely convergent}}. $$

Conditionally Convergent Contrast

A series may converge without being absolutely convergent.

For example:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n} $$

converges, but:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1n $$

diverges.

Therefore the alternating harmonic series is:

$$ \boxed{\text{conditionally convergent}}, $$

not absolutely convergent.

Why the Distinction Matters

An absolutely convergent series does not depend on cancellation between positive and negative terms in order to converge. The total magnitude:

$$ \sum|a_n| $$

is already finite.

This gives absolutely convergent series stronger structural properties than merely convergent ones.

Improper Integrals

The same adjective is used for improper integrals. An improper integral:

$$ \int_a^\infty f(x)\,dx $$

is absolutely convergent if:

$$ \boxed{ \int_a^\infty|f(x)|\,dx \text{ converges} }. $$

As with series, absolute convergence implies convergence of the original improper integral.

Common Misconception: Absolutely convergent does not mean "certainly convergent" or "very convergent." It has a precise mathematical meaning: convergence after replacing terms or integrands by their absolute values.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\sum a_n$ is absolutely convergent "The series is absolutely convergent."
$\sum|a_n|<\infty$ "The series of absolute values converges."
$\sum a_n$ converges absolutely "The series converges absolutely."
$\sum a_n$ is conditionally convergent "The series is conditionally convergent."
$\int_a^\infty|f(x)|\,dx<\infty$ "The improper integral is absolutely convergent."
$|a_n|$ "The absolute value of a sub n."

Two equivalent grammatical constructions are common:

$$ \boxed{ \text{The series is absolutely convergent} } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \text{The series converges absolutely} }. $$

The corresponding noun construction is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{The series converges by absolute convergence} }. $$

In ordinary mathematical prose, however, is absolutely convergent and converges absolutely are usually more natural.

Defense Notation Advice: Do not confuse:

$$ \sum|a_n| $$

with:

$$ \left|\sum a_n\right|. $$

Absolute convergence concerns the first expression, where absolute value is applied term by term.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Series: $$ \boxed{ \sum a_n \text{ is absolutely convergent} \iff \sum|a_n| \text{ converges} }. $$
  • Absolute Convergence: Absolute convergence is the noun naming the property; absolutely convergent describes an object possessing that property.
  • Ordinary Convergence: Every absolutely convergent series is convergent.
  • Converse Failure: A convergent series need not be absolutely convergent.
  • Conditional Convergence: A convergent series for which: $$ \sum|a_n| $$ diverges is called conditionally convergent.
  • Alternating Harmonic Series: $$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}n $$ is conditionally convergent, not absolutely convergent.
  • Alternating p-Series: $$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{n^p} $$ is absolutely convergent when: $$ \boxed{p>1}. $$
  • Geometric Series: $$ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}r^n $$ is absolutely convergent when: $$ \boxed{|r|<1}. $$
  • Comparison Test: If: $$ |a_n|\le b_n $$ eventually and: $$ \sum b_n $$ converges, then: $$ \sum a_n $$ is absolutely convergent.
  • Limit Comparison Test: Positive-term comparison may be applied to: $$ |a_n| $$ to determine whether the original series is absolutely convergent.
  • Ratio Test: If: $$ \lim_{n\to\infty} \left| \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} \right| =L<1, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ \sum a_n \text{ is absolutely convergent} }. $$
  • Root Test: If: $$ \limsup_{n\to\infty}\sqrt[n]{|a_n|}<1, $$ then the series is absolutely convergent.
  • Power Series: A power series is absolutely convergent at every point strictly inside its radius of convergence.
  • Endpoints of Power Series: At an endpoint, a power series may be absolutely convergent, conditionally convergent, or divergent.
  • Rearrangement: Rearranging the terms of an absolutely convergent real or complex series does not change its sum.
  • Conditional Contrast: Conditionally convergent real series may change sum under rearrangement.
  • Triangle Inequality: The estimate: $$ \left| \sum_{k=m}^{n}a_k \right| \le \sum_{k=m}^{n}|a_k| $$ is central to proving that absolute convergence implies convergence.
  • Complex Series: For complex terms $z_n$: $$ \sum z_n $$ is absolutely convergent when: $$ \boxed{ \sum|z_n|<\infty }. $$
  • Improper Integral: $$ \int_a^\infty f(x)\,dx $$ is absolutely convergent when: $$ \boxed{ \int_a^\infty|f(x)|\,dx<\infty }. $$
  • Two-Sided Improper Integral: For: $$ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx, $$ absolute convergence means: $$ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f(x)|\,dx<\infty. $$
  • Normed Spaces: For a series of vectors or functions, the analogous condition often takes the form: $$ \sum\|x_n\|<\infty. $$ In a Banach space, this guarantees convergence of the series.
  • Finite Changes: Changing finitely many terms does not affect whether a series is absolutely convergent.
  • Scalar Multiplication: If: $$ \sum a_n $$ is absolutely convergent, then: $$ \sum ca_n $$ is absolutely convergent for every fixed scalar $c$.
  • Addition: The sum of two absolutely convergent series is absolutely convergent.
  • Cauchy Product: Absolute convergence supplies strong hypotheses under which products of infinite series behave well.
  • Positive-Term Series: If every: $$ a_n\ge0, $$ then ordinary convergence and absolute convergence are the same because: $$ |a_n|=a_n. $$
  • Sign-Changing Series: The distinction becomes important when positive and negative, or complex, terms can cancel.
  • Terminological Contrast: Convergent means the relevant partial sums or improper integral approach a finite value. Absolutely convergent means convergence persists after taking termwise absolute values or magnitudes. Conditionally convergent means convergence occurs without absolute convergence.
