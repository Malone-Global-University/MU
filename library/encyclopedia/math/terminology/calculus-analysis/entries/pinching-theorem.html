Pinching Theorem

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

The Pinching Theorem is an alternate name for the result more commonly called the Squeeze Theorem. It is also known as the Sandwich Theorem or Squeezing Theorem.

The word pinching describes the visual idea behind the theorem. A middle function is constrained between a lower function and an upper function. As the two outer functions approach the same limiting value, the allowable space for the middle function becomes progressively narrower until its limiting behavior is forced.

Nothing is literally compressed. The "pinching" is produced by inequalities. The theorem is therefore an order-based argument: knowing that one quantity cannot fall below a lower bound or rise above an upper bound can be enough to determine its limit without evaluating it directly.

In contemporary English-language calculus texts, Squeeze Theorem is usually the most familiar name. Pinching Theorem is a legitimate synonym, but a terminology reference should make the equivalence explicit because a student may otherwise mistake them for separate results.

Operational Nuance

Suppose three functions satisfy:

$$ f(x)\le g(x)\le h(x) $$

for all $x$ sufficiently close to $a$, possibly excluding $x=a$ itself.

If:

$$ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L $$

and:

$$ \lim_{x\to a}h(x)=L, $$

then the Pinching Theorem guarantees:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a}g(x)=L }. $$

The lower and upper functions are sometimes called bounding functions. Their role is to restrict the possible values of the middle function.

The critical condition is that both bounding functions approach the same limit. If:

$$ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L_1 $$

and:

$$ \lim_{x\to a}h(x)=L_2, $$

with:

$$ L_1\neq L_2, $$

then the inequalities alone do not force $g(x)$ toward a unique limiting value.

For example, consider:

$$ g(x)=x^2\sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right). $$

Since:

$$ -1\le \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) \le1, $$

and:

$$ x^2\ge0, $$

multiplying through by $x^2$ gives:

$$ -x^2 \le x^2\sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) \le x^2. $$

Now:

$$ \lim_{x\to0}(-x^2)=0 $$

and:

$$ \lim_{x\to0}x^2=0. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to0} x^2\sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) = 0 }. $$

The oscillating sine factor has no limit as $x\to0$, but its amplitude is being pinched toward zero by the factor $x^2$.

A particularly useful equivalent form is:

$$ |g(x)-L|\le h(x) $$

together with:

$$ h(x)\to0. $$

The absolute-value inequality implies:

$$ -h(x) \le g(x)-L \le h(x). $$

Since both outer expressions approach $0$:

$$ g(x)-L\to0, $$

and therefore:

$$ g(x)\to L. $$

Common Misconception: The function being pinched does not need to move smoothly or monotonically toward the limit. It may oscillate infinitely often. The theorem requires only that it remain between bounds that collapse toward the same limiting value.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$f(x)\le g(x)\le h(x)$ "G of x is pinched between f of x and h of x."
$\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L$ "The limit of f of x as x approaches a equals L."
$\lim_{x\to a}h(x)=L$ "The limit of h of x as x approaches a equals L."
$\lim_{x\to a}g(x)=L$ "Therefore, the limit of g of x as x approaches a equals L."
$|g(x)|\le h(x)$ "The absolute value of g of x is bounded above by h of x."
$a_n\le b_n\le c_n$ "B sub n is pinched between a sub n and c sub n."

A compact symbolic statement is:

$$ f(x)\le g(x)\le h(x), $$ $$ \lim_{x\to a}f(x) = \lim_{x\to a}h(x) = L $$ $$ \Longrightarrow $$ $$ \lim_{x\to a}g(x)=L. $$

In a written proof, the inequalities and the two bounding limits should both be shown explicitly. Merely writing "by the Pinching Theorem" without identifying the quantities doing the pinching leaves the essential argument unstated.

Defense Notation Advice: The inequalities need only hold sufficiently close to the point of interest. They do not need to hold throughout the entire domain, and the three functions need not even be defined at the limiting point itself.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Squeeze Theorem: In standard calculus terminology: $$ \boxed{ \text{Pinching Theorem} = \text{Squeeze Theorem} }. $$ They are alternate names for the same limit principle.
  • Sandwich Theorem: The name Sandwich Theorem uses the same imagery: the middle function is placed between two bounding functions. Thus: $$ \text{Pinching} = \text{Squeezing} = \text{Sandwiching}. $$
  • Oscillatory Limits: The theorem is especially useful when a bounded oscillatory factor such as: $$ \sin\left(\frac1x\right) $$ or: $$ \cos\left(\frac1x\right) $$ is multiplied by a factor tending to zero.
  • Absolute-Value Form: If: $$ |f(x)-L|\le g(x) $$ and: $$ g(x)\to0, $$ then: $$ f(x)\to L. $$ This form interprets $g(x)$ as an upper bound on the error between $f(x)$ and its proposed limit.
  • Sequences: The theorem has a sequence version. If: $$ a_n\le b_n\le c_n $$ for all sufficiently large $n$, and: $$ \lim_{n\to\infty}a_n = \lim_{n\to\infty}c_n = L, $$ then: $$ \lim_{n\to\infty}b_n=L. $$
  • Trigonometric Limits: A classical use of the theorem is proving: $$ \lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x}=1. $$ Geometric inequalities produce upper and lower bounds that both approach $1$.
  • Error Bounds: If an approximation error $E_n$ satisfies: $$ |E_n|\le M_n $$ and: $$ M_n\to0, $$ then: $$ E_n\to0. $$ The approximation therefore converges to the desired value.
  • Comparison Reasoning: The Pinching Theorem is part of a broader analytical strategy: instead of calculating a difficult quantity directly, compare it with simpler quantities whose behavior is already known.
  • One-Sided Limits: The same logic applies to one-sided limits. If: $$ f(x)\le g(x)\le h(x) $$ for $x$ sufficiently close to $a$ from one side, and both bounds approach $L$ from that side, then $g$ has the same one-sided limit.
  • Infinite Limits: Related comparison principles apply when bounds diverge to $+\infty$ or $-\infty$. These are sometimes discussed as extensions of squeezing, although many texts state them separately as comparison theorems.
  • Proof in Real Analysis: The theorem follows naturally from the $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ definition of a limit. Once both outer functions lie within an arbitrarily small neighborhood of $L$, every value trapped between them must also lie within that neighborhood.
  • Order Structure: The theorem depends fundamentally on the ordered nature of the real numbers. Inequalities can constrain the middle function because real values can be meaningfully compared as less than, equal to, or greater than one another.
  • Terminological Contrast: Pinching Theorem, Squeeze Theorem, Squeezing Theorem, and Sandwich Theorem are alternate names for the same standard calculus result. The Ham Sandwich Theorem is an entirely different theorem in geometry and topology concerning simultaneous bisection of measurable sets.
