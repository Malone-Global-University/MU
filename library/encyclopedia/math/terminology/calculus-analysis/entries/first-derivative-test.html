First Derivative Test

Subfield: Calculus & Analysis

Etymology & Roots

The First Derivative Test classifies local extrema by examining the sign of the first derivative on either side of a critical number. The word test indicates a criterion for deciding whether the original function changes from increasing to decreasing, decreasing to increasing, or continues in the same direction.

$$ \boxed{ \text{sign change of }f' \Longrightarrow \text{local behavior of }f } $$

It is called the first derivative test because it relies on $f'$ rather than on the second derivative $f''$.

Operational Nuance

Critical-Number Starting Point

A typical candidate $c$ satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ f'(c)=0 \quad\text{or}\quad f'(c)\text{ does not exist}, \qquad f(c)\text{ defined} } $$

The test then examines the sign of $f'$ on intervals immediately to the left and right of $c$.

Local Maximum

$$ \boxed{ f':+\to- \Longrightarrow f(c)\text{ is a local maximum} } $$

The function increases before $c$ and decreases after $c$.

Local Minimum

$$ \boxed{ f':-\to+ \Longrightarrow f(c)\text{ is a local minimum} } $$

The function decreases before $c$ and increases after $c$.

No Local Extremum

$$ \boxed{ +\to+ \text{ or } -\to- \Longrightarrow \text{no local extremum at }c } $$

A stationary point can therefore fail to be a maximum or minimum.

Example

For $f(x)=x^2$, we have:

$$ f'(x)=2x. $$

Since $f'(x)<0$ when $x<0$ and $f'(x)>0$ when $x>0$, the sign change $-\to+$ shows that $x=0$ is a local minimum.

Sign Charts

A derivative sign chart organizes the test efficiently:

$$ \boxed{ \text{critical numbers} \longrightarrow \text{test intervals} \longrightarrow \operatorname{sign}(f') \longrightarrow \text{classification} } $$

Why the Test Works

Where $f'(x)>0$, the function is increasing; where $f'(x)<0$, it is decreasing. The First Derivative Test therefore detects changes in the direction of the original function.

Undefined Derivatives

The test can sometimes classify extrema at corners or cusps where $f'(c)$ does not exist, provided $f(c)$ exists and the neighboring derivative signs can be analyzed.

Common Misconception

Solving $f'(x)=0$ only identifies possible extrema. Classification requires information about the derivative on both sides of each candidate.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$f'(x)>0$ "F prime is positive, so f is increasing."
$f'(x)<0$ "F prime is negative, so f is decreasing."
$+\to-$ "The derivative changes from positive to negative."
$-\to+$ "The derivative changes from negative to positive."
$f'(c)=0$ "C is a stationary critical number."
$f'$ has no sign change at $c$ "The critical number is not a local extremum."

The phrase First Derivative Test refers to sign analysis of $f'$ around a critical number. It should not be reduced to the equation $f'(x)=0$ alone.

Defense Notation Advice: Mark each critical number on a sign chart and record the sign of $f'$ on the intervals between them. The transition, not the isolated derivative value, determines the classification.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Positive-to-Negative: Indicates a local maximum.
  • Negative-to-Positive: Indicates a local minimum.
  • Positive-to-Positive: The function continues increasing, so no local extremum occurs.
  • Negative-to-Negative: The function continues decreasing, so no local extremum occurs.
  • Critical Numbers: Supply candidate locations where monotonic behavior may change.
  • Stationary Points: Points where $f'(c)=0$ may be extrema or may have no extremal behavior.
  • Nondifferentiable Candidates: Corners and cusps can sometimes be classified using neighboring derivative signs.
  • Monotonicity: The same derivative sign chart identifies increasing and decreasing intervals.
  • Optimization: The test classifies local candidate solutions in one-variable optimization problems.
  • Polynomial Analysis: Real zeros of $p'$ divide the real line into intervals for sign testing.
  • Comparison with the Second Derivative Test: The First Derivative Test uses sign changes of $f'$ and remains useful when $f''(c)=0$ or the second-derivative test is inconclusive.
  • Common Error: Declaring every solution of $f'(x)=0$ a maximum or minimum.
  • Common Error: Testing the sign of $f$ instead of the sign of $f'$.
  • Common Error: Checking only one side of a critical number.
  • Common Error: Ignoring a point where $f'$ is undefined even though $f$ itself is defined.
  • Core Interpretation: The First Derivative Test converts derivative signs into a local shape classification: $$ \boxed{ f' \longrightarrow \text{increase/decrease} \longrightarrow \text{maximum, minimum, or neither} }. $$ Its central idea is that extrema are identified by changes in the direction of the original function, not merely by isolated zeros of its derivative.
