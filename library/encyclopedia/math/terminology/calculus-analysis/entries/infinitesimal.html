Infinitesimal

Subfield: Calculus & Analysis

Etymology & Roots

An infinitesimal is, informally, a quantity regarded as smaller in magnitude than every ordinary positive quantity while still representing a nonzero change. The word derives from Latin roots associated with the idea of something indefinitely small.

$$ \boxed{ \text{infinitesimal} \approx \text{vanishingly small quantity or change} } $$

Infinitesimal language played a central historical role in the development of calculus. Modern analysis usually formalizes the same limiting ideas through limits, while nonstandard analysis provides a rigorous number system containing actual nonzero infinitesimals.

Operational Nuance

Limit-Based Interpretation

In standard calculus, symbols such as $\Delta x$ may be allowed to approach zero:

$$ \boxed{ \Delta x\to0 } $$

The quantity is not treated as a fixed nonzero real number smaller than every positive real number. Instead, its smallness is expressed through a limiting process.

Differential Language

Leibniz notation uses symbols such as $dx$ and $dy$, historically associated with infinitesimal changes:

$$ \boxed{ dy=f'(x)\,dx } $$

In modern differential calculus, this relation can be understood rigorously through linear approximation and differentials.

Orders of Smallness

Some quantities become negligible relative to others. For example:

$$ \boxed{ x^2=o(x) \quad\text{as }x\to0 } $$

because $x^2/x=x\to0$. Thus $x^2$ is of smaller asymptotic order than $x$ near zero.

Nonstandard Analysis

In the hyperreal number system, a nonzero number $\varepsilon$ is infinitesimal if:

$$ \boxed{ |\varepsilon|<r \quad\text{for every positive real }r }. $$

Such infinitesimals are not ordinary real numbers.

Derivative Interpretation

Informally, the derivative may be viewed as the ratio of corresponding infinitesimal changes:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx}=f'(x). $$

In standard real analysis, the rigorous foundation is the limit of difference quotients rather than literal division of ordinary nonzero real infinitesimals.

Integration

The notation $f(x)\,dx$ evokes accumulation from extremely thin contributions. Riemann integration formalizes this idea through finite partitions whose widths tend toward zero.

Common Misconception

In the ordinary real-number system, there is no positive nonzero real number smaller than every positive real number. Genuine nonzero infinitesimals require an extended framework such as the hyperreal numbers.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$dx$ "The differential of x."
$dy=f'(x)\,dx$ "The differential dy equals f prime of x times dx."
$\Delta x\to0$ "Delta x approaches zero."
$x^2=o(x)$ "X squared is little-o of x near the stated limit."
$|\varepsilon|<r$ "Epsilon is smaller in magnitude than every positive real r."
$\varepsilon\neq0$ "The infinitesimal epsilon is nonzero."

The word infinitesimal may be used informally in elementary calculus, asymptotically in analysis, or literally within nonstandard analysis. These uses should not be conflated.

Defense Notation Advice: State the framework. In standard calculus, describe quantities as approaching zero unless a genuine infinitesimal number system has been introduced.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Historical Calculus: Infinitesimals were central to early formulations of differentiation and integration.
  • Standard Analysis: Limit definitions replace literal infinitesimal real quantities.
  • Differentials: $dx$ and $dy$ encode first-order linear changes in modern calculus.
  • Asymptotic Analysis: Little-o notation compares quantities that become negligible relative to others.
  • Nonstandard Analysis: Hyperreal systems contain rigorous nonzero infinitesimal numbers.
  • Derivative: Infinitesimal language provides an intuitive interpretation of instantaneous rate of change.
  • Integration: Infinitesimal-width language motivates the accumulation of continuously distributed quantities.
  • Linear Approximation: Higher-order terms may become negligible compared with first-order changes.
  • Physics: Infinitesimal displacement, time, mass, and volume elements commonly appear in differential models.
  • Geometry: Differential arc, area, and volume elements support local geometric approximation.
  • Common Error: Treating an infinitesimal as merely a very small fixed real number.
  • Common Error: Treating $dx$ as automatically identical to an ordinary finite change $\Delta x$.
  • Common Error: Assuming nonzero real infinitesimals exist inside the ordinary real-number system.
  • Common Error: Mixing informal infinitesimal reasoning with formal limit notation without specifying the interpretation.
  • Core Interpretation: An infinitesimal captures the mathematical idea of change at vanishing scale: $$ \boxed{ \text{finite change} \longrightarrow \text{smaller and smaller change} \longrightarrow \text{local limiting behavior} }. $$ Its precise meaning depends on whether the surrounding framework uses standard limits, differentials, asymptotics, or genuine nonstandard infinitesimal numbers.
