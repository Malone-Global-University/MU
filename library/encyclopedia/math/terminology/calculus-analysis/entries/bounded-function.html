Bounded Function

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

A bounded function is a function whose output values remain confined within fixed numerical limits on the domain or subset of the domain under consideration.

For a real-valued function:

$$ f:D\to\mathbb R, $$

the function is bounded on $D$ if there exist real numbers $m$ and $M$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ m\le f(x)\le M \qquad \text{for every }x\in D }. $$

The numbers $m$ and $M$ are respectively a lower bound and an upper bound for the values of the function.

$$ \boxed{ \text{bounded} = \text{bounded above and bounded below} }. $$

The word bounded therefore describes the range of possible function values, not necessarily the size or shape of the function's domain.

Operational Nuance

Bounded Above

A function $f:D\to\mathbb R$ is bounded above if there exists some real number $M$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)\le M \qquad \text{for all }x\in D }. $$

Any such $M$ is called an upper bound.

Bounded Below

A function is bounded below if there exists some real number $m$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ m\le f(x) \qquad \text{for all }x\in D }. $$

Any such $m$ is called a lower bound.

Bounded on Both Sides

A function is bounded when both conditions hold:

$$ \boxed{ \exists\,m,M\in\mathbb R \text{ such that } m\le f(x)\le M \quad \forall x\in D }. $$

Absolute-Value Form

For real-valued functions, boundedness can equivalently be expressed by saying that there exists some $K\ge0$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ |f(x)|\le K \qquad \forall x\in D }. $$

This single inequality simultaneously provides upper and lower bounds:

$$ -K\le f(x)\le K. $$

Why the Domain Matters

Boundedness is always relative to the set on which the function is being considered.

For example:

$$ f(x)=x $$

is unbounded on $\mathbb R$, but bounded on $[0,1]$ because:

$$ \boxed{ 0\le x\le1 }. $$

Bounded on a Set

It is therefore often more precise to say:

$$ \boxed{ f\text{ is bounded on }A } $$

where $A$ is a specified subset of the domain.

Example: Sine

The sine function satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ -1\le\sin x\le1 \qquad \forall x\in\mathbb R }. $$

Thus $\sin x$ is bounded on the entire real line.

Example: Cosine

$$ \boxed{ -1\le\cos x\le1 }. $$

Therefore cosine is also globally bounded on $\mathbb R$.

Example: x²

Consider:

$$ f(x)=x^2. $$

Since:

$$ x^2\ge0, $$

the function is bounded below by $0$.

However, it has no real upper bound on $\mathbb R$ because $x^2$ can become arbitrarily large.

$$ \boxed{ x^2 \text{ is bounded below but not bounded above on }\mathbb R }. $$

Example: −x²

Similarly:

$$ f(x)=-x^2 $$

satisfies:

$$ -x^2\le0. $$

It is bounded above by $0$ but unbounded below.

Example: 1/x

The function:

$$ f(x)=\frac1x $$

illustrates how changing the domain changes boundedness.

On:

$$ [1,\infty), $$

we have:

$$ \boxed{ 0

so the function is bounded.

On:

$$ (0,1], $$

however, $1/x$ becomes arbitrarily large as $x\to0^+$, so it is unbounded above.

Bounded Does Not Mean Constant

A bounded function may vary substantially while remaining confined between fixed bounds.

For example:

$$ f(x)=\sin x $$

oscillates indefinitely but never leaves the interval $[-1,1]$.

Range Interpretation

A real-valued function is bounded exactly when its range is contained in some bounded interval:

$$ \boxed{ f(D)\subseteq[m,M] } $$

for suitable finite $m$ and $M$.

Upper Bound Need Not Be Best Possible

If:

$$ f(x)\le5, $$

then every larger number is also an upper bound:

$$ 6,\ 10,\ 100,\ldots $$

may all be valid upper bounds.

Least Upper Bound

The smallest upper bound is the supremum:

$$ \boxed{ \sup_{x\in D}f(x) }. $$

Greatest Lower Bound

The greatest lower bound is the infimum:

$$ \boxed{ \inf_{x\in D}f(x) }. $$

Bound versus Maximum

An upper bound does not need to be attained by the function.

Consider:

$$ f(x)=x, \qquad 0

The function is bounded above by $1$, and:

$$ \boxed{ \sup f=1 }. $$

But there is no $x\in(0,1)$ for which $f(x)=1$.

Therefore $1$ is the supremum but not a maximum.

Bound versus Minimum

For the same function:

$$ \inf f=0, $$

but $0$ is not attained. Thus the function has no minimum on $(0,1)$.

Bounded Functions Need Not Attain Their Bounds

$$ \boxed{ \text{bounded} \not\Rightarrow \text{has maximum and minimum} }. $$

Extreme Value Theorem

If $f$ is continuous on a closed and bounded interval $[a,b]$, then $f$ attains both an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum.

$$ \boxed{ f\text{ continuous on }[a,b] \Rightarrow f\text{ is bounded and attains its extrema} }. $$

Why Closed and Bounded Matters

The function:

$$ f(x)=\frac1x $$

is continuous on $(0,1]$ but unbounded there, showing that continuity alone on an interval is insufficient.

Continuity Does Not Imply Global Boundedness

The function:

$$ f(x)=x $$

is continuous everywhere on $\mathbb R$ but unbounded.

$$ \boxed{ \text{continuous} \not\Rightarrow \text{globally bounded} }. $$

Bounded Does Not Imply Continuous

A function may be bounded while highly discontinuous.

For example, define:

$$ f(x)= \begin{cases} 1,&x\in\mathbb Q,\\ 0,&x\notin\mathbb Q. \end{cases} $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ 0\le f(x)\le1 } $$

everywhere, even though the function is discontinuous at every real number.

Local Boundedness

A function is locally bounded at a point $a$ if there is some neighborhood of $a$ on which the function is bounded.

$$ \boxed{ \exists\,\delta>0,\ M>0 \text{ such that } |f(x)|\le M \text{ whenever }|x-a|<\delta }. $$

Locally Bounded versus Globally Bounded

The identity function $f(x)=x$ is locally bounded at every real number but is not globally bounded on $\mathbb R$.

$$ \boxed{ \text{locally bounded} \not\Rightarrow \text{globally bounded} }. $$

Continuous Functions Are Locally Bounded

Every real-valued function that is continuous at a point is locally bounded near that point.

Bounded Sequence as a Function

A sequence can be viewed as a function:

$$ a:\mathbb N\to\mathbb R. $$

The sequence $(a_n)$ is bounded if:

$$ \boxed{ |a_n|\le M \qquad \forall n\in\mathbb N }. $$

Example of a Bounded Sequence

$$ a_n=(-1)^n. $$

Since:

$$ |a_n|=1, $$

the sequence is bounded.

Convergent Sequences Are Bounded

Every convergent real sequence is bounded:

$$ \boxed{ a_n\to L \Rightarrow (a_n)\text{ is bounded} }. $$

The converse is false; a bounded sequence need not converge.

For example:

$$ (-1)^n $$

is bounded but does not converge.

Operations on Bounded Functions

If $f$ and $g$ are bounded real-valued functions on the same set, then their sum is bounded.

If:

$$ |f(x)|\le M, \qquad |g(x)|\le N, $$

then:

$$ |f(x)+g(x)| \le |f(x)|+|g(x)| \le M+N. $$

Scalar Multiples

If $f$ is bounded and $c$ is a scalar, then $cf$ is bounded:

$$ \boxed{ |cf(x)| \le |c|M }. $$

Products

If $f$ and $g$ are bounded, then $fg$ is bounded:

$$ \boxed{ |f(x)g(x)| \le MN }. $$

Quotients Require More

If $f$ and $g$ are bounded, $f/g$ need not be bounded. The denominator must also remain sufficiently far from zero.

If:

$$ |f(x)|\le M $$

and:

$$ |g(x)|\ge c>0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \left| \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} \right| \le \frac Mc }. $$

Bounded Functions Form a Vector Space

On a fixed domain $D$, the collection of bounded real-valued functions is closed under addition and scalar multiplication.

This collection is commonly denoted:

$$ \boxed{ B(D) } $$

or similar notation, depending on context.

Supremum Norm

For a bounded function, one may define:

$$ \boxed{ \|f\|_\infty = \sup_{x\in D}|f(x)| }. $$

This is called the supremum norm or uniform norm.

Boundedness through the Supremum Norm

A real- or complex-valued function is bounded exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ \|f\|_\infty<\infty }. $$

Vector-Valued Functions

If:

$$ f:D\to\mathbb R^n, $$

boundedness is naturally expressed using a norm:

$$ \boxed{ \|f(x)\|\le M \qquad \forall x\in D }. $$

Metric-Space Interpretation

More generally, if the codomain is a metric space $(Y,d)$, the image $f(D)$ is bounded if it fits inside a finite-radius ball.

That is, for some $y_0\in Y$ and $M<\infty$:

$$ \boxed{ d(f(x),y_0)\le M \qquad \forall x\in D }. $$

Bounded Polynomial Functions

On the entire real line, a nonconstant real polynomial cannot be bounded both above and below.

For example, even-degree polynomials may be bounded on one side:

$$ x^2\ge0, $$

while odd-degree polynomials are unbounded both above and below.

A real polynomial that is globally bounded on all of $\mathbb R$ must therefore be constant.

Boundedness and Asymptotes

A horizontal asymptote may suggest bounded behavior for sufficiently large $|x|$, but it does not by itself guarantee global boundedness.

For example:

$$ f(x)=\frac1x $$

approaches $0$ as $|x|\to\infty$ but is unbounded near $x=0$ on its full domain.

Boundedness and Limits

If a finite limit:

$$ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L $$

exists, then $f$ is bounded in some punctured neighborhood of $a$.

By contrast:

$$ f(x)\to\infty $$

indicates unbounded growth rather than convergence to a finite real number.

Boundedness versus Bounded Variation

A bounded function and a function of bounded variation are different concepts.

Boundedness controls how far function values can move away numerically. Bounded variation controls the total accumulated variation of the function across an interval.

$$ \boxed{ \text{bounded} \ne \text{bounded variation} }. $$

Boundedness versus Monotonicity

A bounded function need not be increasing or decreasing.

For example, $\sin x$ is bounded but oscillatory.

Common Misconception: Bounded Domain Means Bounded Function

A bounded domain does not by itself force the function values to be bounded.

For example:

$$ f(x)=\frac1x, \qquad x\in(0,1], $$

has a bounded domain but is unbounded above.

Common Misconception: Unbounded Domain Means Unbounded Function

An unbounded domain does not force the function to be unbounded.

For example:

$$ \sin x $$

is bounded on the unbounded domain $\mathbb R$.

Common Misconception: Upper Bound Means Maximum

An upper bound need not be attained. A maximum is an actual function value; an upper bound merely limits the function from above.

Common Misconception: Bounded Means Positive

A bounded function may take positive, zero, and negative values. Boundedness concerns finite limits on magnitude or range, not sign.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$f(x)\le M$ for all $x\in D$ "f is bounded above on D by M."
$m\le f(x)$ for all $x\in D$ "f is bounded below on D by m."
$m\le f(x)\le M$ "f is bounded on D."
$|f(x)|\le K$ "The absolute value of f is bounded by K."
$\sup_{x\in D}f(x)$ "The supremum, or least upper bound, of f on D."
$\|f\|_\infty=\sup_{x\in D}|f(x)|$ "The supremum norm of f."

The phrase bounded function often means bounded both above and below. When only one direction is intended, mathematical writing normally says explicitly bounded above or bounded below.

Defense Notation Advice: Always state the domain or set on which boundedness is being asserted. The same formula can be bounded on one domain and unbounded on another. Also distinguish an arbitrary bound from a supremum or infimum, and distinguish those from a maximum or minimum that must actually be attained.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: A real-valued function is bounded when its values lie between two finite real numbers.
  • Two-Sided Form: $$ \boxed{ m\le f(x)\le M \qquad \forall x\in D }. $$
  • Absolute-Value Form: $$ \boxed{ |f(x)|\le K \qquad \forall x\in D }. $$
  • Bounded Above: There exists $M$ such that $f(x)\le M$ for every $x$ in the specified domain.
  • Bounded Below: There exists $m$ such that $m\le f(x)$ for every $x$ in the specified domain.
  • Domain Dependence: Boundedness must be stated relative to a domain or subset.
  • Range Interpretation: $$ \boxed{ f(D)\subseteq[m,M] }. $$
  • Supremum: The least upper bound of the function's values.
  • Infimum: The greatest lower bound of the function's values.
  • Maximum Contrast: A supremum need not be attained; a maximum must be an actual function value.
  • Minimum Contrast: An infimum need not be attained; a minimum must be an actual function value.
  • Extreme Value Theorem: A continuous function on a closed bounded interval is bounded and attains its maximum and minimum.
  • Continuity Caution: A continuous function on all of $\mathbb R$ need not be bounded.
  • Reverse Continuity Caution: A bounded function need not be continuous.
  • Local Boundedness: Requires boundedness only on some neighborhood of each point under consideration.
  • Sequence Connection: A sequence is a function on $\mathbb N$ and is bounded when $|a_n|\le M$ for all $n$.
  • Convergent Sequence: Every convergent real sequence is bounded.
  • Nonconverse: A bounded sequence need not converge.
  • Addition: The sum of two bounded functions on the same domain is bounded.
  • Scalar Multiplication: A scalar multiple of a bounded function is bounded.
  • Products: The product of two bounded functions is bounded.
  • Quotient Caution: A quotient additionally requires the denominator to remain bounded away from zero.
  • Supremum Norm: $$ \boxed{ \|f\|_\infty = \sup_{x\in D}|f(x)| }. $$
  • Vector-Valued Form: $$ \boxed{ \|f(x)\|\le M }. $$
  • Metric-Space Form: The image of the function lies within a finite-radius ball.
  • Polynomial Example: $x^2$ is bounded below but not above on $\mathbb R$.
  • Trigonometric Example: $\sin x$ and $\cos x$ are globally bounded.
  • Domain Example: $1/x$ is bounded on $[1,\infty)$ but unbounded on $(0,1]$.
  • Bounded Variation Contrast: Boundedness of values is distinct from bounded total variation.
  • Common Error: Do not assume a bounded domain guarantees a bounded function.
  • Common Error: Do not assume an unbounded domain forces the function to be unbounded.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse an upper bound with a maximum.
  • Common Error: Do not assume boundedness implies continuity or convergence.
  • Terminological Contrast: Upper bound limits function values from above. Lower bound limits them from below. Bounded function has both kinds of finite bounds. Supremum is the least upper bound. Infimum is the greatest lower bound. Maximum and minimum are attained extremal values. Locally bounded means bounded on suitable neighborhoods. Bounded variation is a different analytic condition measuring total accumulated change.
