Bounded Sequence

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

A bounded sequence is a sequence whose terms remain confined between fixed finite limits.

If $(a_n)$ is a real sequence, then it is bounded when there exist real numbers $m$ and $M$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ m\le a_n\le M \qquad \text{for every }n }. $$

Equivalently, there exists a finite number $K>0$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ |a_n|\le K \qquad \forall n }. $$

The term bounded refers to the values of the terms, not to the number of terms. An infinite sequence may be bounded even though it continues indefinitely.

Operational Nuance

Sequence as a Function

A sequence may be viewed as a function:

$$ a:\mathbb N\to\mathbb R, \qquad n\mapsto a_n. $$

Thus a bounded sequence is a special case of a bounded function whose domain is discrete.

Bounded Above

A sequence $(a_n)$ is bounded above if there exists a real number $M$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ a_n\le M \qquad \forall n }. $$

Any such $M$ is an upper bound of the sequence.

Bounded Below

A sequence is bounded below if there exists a real number $m$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ m\le a_n \qquad \forall n }. $$

Bounded on Both Sides

The sequence is bounded when it is both bounded above and bounded below:

$$ \boxed{ \exists\,m,M\in\mathbb R \text{ such that } m\le a_n\le M \quad \forall n }. $$

Absolute-Value Criterion

The two-sided condition is equivalent to:

$$ \boxed{ \exists\,K>0 \text{ such that } |a_n|\le K \quad \forall n }. $$

Example: Alternating ±1

Consider:

$$ a_n=(-1)^n. $$

Every term equals either $1$ or $-1$, so:

$$ \boxed{ -1\le a_n\le1 }. $$

Therefore the sequence is bounded.

Bounded Does Not Mean Convergent

The sequence:

$$ (-1)^n $$

is bounded but oscillates between $-1$ and $1$ and therefore does not converge.

$$ \boxed{ \text{bounded} \not\Rightarrow \text{convergent} }. $$

Example: 1/n

The sequence:

$$ a_n=\frac1n $$

satisfies:

$$ 0

It is therefore bounded and also converges:

$$ \boxed{ \frac1n\to0 }. $$

Example: n

The sequence:

$$ a_n=n $$

has no finite upper bound because its terms can exceed any prescribed real number.

$$ \boxed{ a_n=n \text{ is unbounded above} }. $$

Example: −n

Similarly:

$$ a_n=-n $$

is bounded above but unbounded below.

Example: n²

$$ a_n=n^2 $$

is bounded below by $1$ for $n\ge1$ but is unbounded above.

Convergent Sequences Are Bounded

One of the fundamental results of elementary analysis is:

$$ \boxed{ a_n\to L \Rightarrow (a_n)\text{ is bounded} }. $$

Why Convergence Implies Boundedness

If $a_n\to L$, then eventually the terms lie within, for example, distance $1$ of $L$:

$$ |a_n-L|<1. $$

Hence sufficiently late terms satisfy:

$$ |a_n|\le |L|+1. $$

Only finitely many earlier terms remain, and a finite set of real numbers is bounded. Combining the two groups gives a global bound.

Finite Changes Do Not Affect Boundedness Essentially

If all but finitely many terms of a sequence lie in a bounded interval, then the entire sequence is still bounded because the finitely many exceptional terms can be included by enlarging the bounds.

Subsequences of Bounded Sequences

Every subsequence of a bounded sequence is bounded by the same bounds.

$$ \boxed{ |a_n|\le M \Rightarrow |a_{n_k}|\le M }. $$

Bolzano-Weierstrass Theorem

Every bounded sequence of real numbers has a convergent subsequence.

$$ \boxed{ \text{bounded real sequence} \Rightarrow \text{has a convergent subsequence} }. $$

This is much weaker than saying the original sequence itself converges.

Example of Bolzano-Weierstrass

For:

$$ a_n=(-1)^n, $$

the even-indexed subsequence is:

$$ a_{2k}=1, $$

while the odd-indexed subsequence is:

$$ a_{2k+1}=-1. $$

Both subsequences converge even though the original sequence does not.

Monotone Bounded Sequences

A monotone sequence has much stronger convergence behavior.

If $(a_n)$ is increasing and bounded above, then it converges.

$$ \boxed{ a_n\le a_{n+1} \text{ and bounded above} \Rightarrow a_n\text{ converges} }. $$

If $(a_n)$ is decreasing and bounded below, then it also converges.

Monotone Convergence Theorem for Sequences

$$ \boxed{ \text{monotone} + \text{bounded} \Rightarrow \text{convergent} }. $$

Increasing Sequence and Supremum

If $(a_n)$ is increasing and bounded above, then:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{n\to\infty}a_n = \sup\{a_n:n\in\mathbb N\} }. $$

Decreasing Sequence and Infimum

If $(a_n)$ is decreasing and bounded below, then:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{n\to\infty}a_n = \inf\{a_n:n\in\mathbb N\} }. $$

Supremum of a Sequence

If a sequence is bounded above, its set of values has a least upper bound:

$$ \boxed{ \sup\{a_n:n\in\mathbb N\} }. $$

Infimum of a Sequence

If a sequence is bounded below, its set of values has a greatest lower bound:

$$ \boxed{ \inf\{a_n:n\in\mathbb N\} }. $$

Supremum Need Not Be a Term

Consider:

$$ a_n=1-\frac1n. $$

Then:

$$ a_n<1 $$

for every finite $n$, but:

$$ \boxed{ \sup\{a_n\}=1 }. $$

The supremum need not occur as one of the terms.

Maximum versus Supremum

A sequence has a maximum if some actual term is greater than or equal to every other term.

A supremum is merely the least upper bound and need not be attained.

$$ \boxed{ \text{maximum} \Rightarrow \text{supremum attained} }. $$

Minimum versus Infimum

Likewise, an infimum need not be an actual term unless the sequence attains its greatest lower bound.

Bounded Oscillatory Sequence

Consider:

$$ a_n=\sin n. $$

Since:

$$ -1\le\sin n\le1, $$

the sequence is bounded even though its values do not settle to a single limit.

Bounded Geometric Sequence

Consider:

$$ a_n=ar^n. $$

If:

$$ |r|<1, $$

then:

$$ a_n\to0, $$

so the sequence is bounded.

If $|r|>1$ and $a\ne0$, the sequence is generally unbounded in magnitude.

Case r = −1

If:

$$ a_n=a(-1)^n, $$

the sequence is bounded by $|a|$ but generally does not converge unless $a=0$.

Boundedness under Addition

If $(a_n)$ and $(b_n)$ are bounded:

$$ |a_n|\le M, \qquad |b_n|\le N, $$

then:

$$ |a_n+b_n| \le M+N. $$

Therefore their sum is bounded.

Scalar Multiplication

If $(a_n)$ is bounded and $c$ is a scalar:

$$ \boxed{ |ca_n| \le |c|M }. $$

Products of Bounded Sequences

If $(a_n)$ and $(b_n)$ are bounded, then:

$$ \boxed{ |a_nb_n| \le MN }. $$

Hence their product is bounded.

Quotients Require a Lower Bound Away from Zero

Even if $(a_n)$ and $(b_n)$ are bounded, the quotient $a_n/b_n$ need not be bounded if $b_n$ approaches $0$.

If:

$$ |a_n|\le M $$

and:

$$ |b_n|\ge c>0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \left| \frac{a_n}{b_n} \right| \le \frac Mc }. $$

Cauchy Sequences Are Bounded

Every Cauchy sequence of real or complex numbers is bounded.

$$ \boxed{ \text{Cauchy} \Rightarrow \text{bounded} }. $$

Over $\mathbb R$ and $\mathbb C$, every Cauchy sequence also converges because these spaces are complete.

Limsup and Liminf

For a bounded real sequence, the limiting upper and lower behavior can be described using:

$$ \boxed{ \limsup_{n\to\infty}a_n } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \liminf_{n\to\infty}a_n }. $$

Tail Suprema and Infima

Define:

$$ s_n=\sup\{a_k:k\ge n\}, $$

and:

$$ i_n=\inf\{a_k:k\ge n\}. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ \limsup a_n=\lim s_n, \qquad \liminf a_n=\lim i_n }. $$

Convergence through Limsup and Liminf

A bounded real sequence converges exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ \limsup_{n\to\infty}a_n = \liminf_{n\to\infty}a_n }. $$

Their common value is then the ordinary limit.

Example: Alternating Sequence

For:

$$ a_n=(-1)^n, $$

we have:

$$ \boxed{ \limsup a_n=1, \qquad \liminf a_n=-1 }. $$

Since these are unequal, the sequence does not converge.

Vector-Valued Sequences

For a sequence:

$$ \mathbf a_n\in\mathbb R^m, $$

boundedness is expressed using a norm:

$$ \boxed{ \|\mathbf a_n\|\le M \qquad \forall n }. $$

Bolzano-Weierstrass in Rᵐ

Every bounded sequence in finite-dimensional Euclidean space $\mathbb R^m$ has a convergent subsequence.

Bounded versus Eventually Bounded

A sequence is eventually bounded if there exists some index $N$ and bound $M$ such that:

$$ |a_n|\le M \qquad \text{for all }n\ge N. $$

For ordinary real sequences, eventual boundedness implies full boundedness because only finitely many earlier terms remain.

Boundedness and Infinite Length

The word bounded does not mean the sequence has finitely many terms. For example:

$$ 1,\frac12,\frac13,\frac14,\ldots $$

is infinite but bounded.

Common Misconception: Bounded Means Convergent

Boundedness prevents escape to arbitrarily large magnitude, but it does not prevent oscillation.

$$ \boxed{ (-1)^n \text{ is bounded but divergent} }. $$

Common Misconception: Increasing Means Unbounded

An increasing sequence may still approach a finite ceiling.

For example:

$$ a_n=1-\frac1n $$

is increasing and bounded above by $1$.

Common Misconception: Upper Bound Must Be a Term

A bound need not occur as an actual term. In:

$$ a_n=1-\frac1n, $$

$1$ is an upper bound and the supremum, but no term equals $1$.

Common Misconception: Bounded Means All Terms Are Positive

Boundedness concerns finite upper and lower limits, not sign. A bounded sequence may contain positive, negative, and zero terms.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$a_n\le M$ for all $n$ "The sequence is bounded above by M."
$m\le a_n$ for all $n$ "The sequence is bounded below by m."
$m\le a_n\le M$ "The sequence is bounded."
$|a_n|\le K$ "The magnitude of every term is bounded by K."
$a_n\to L$ "The sequence converges to L, and therefore is bounded."
$\limsup a_n$ and $\liminf a_n$ "The limit superior and limit inferior of the sequence."

The phrase bounded sequence normally means bounded both above and below. If only one side is controlled, the more precise terms are bounded above or bounded below.

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish a bound from a limit. A number can bound every term without being approached by the sequence, and a bounded sequence may have several subsequential limits rather than one ordinary limit.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: A bounded sequence has all terms between fixed finite lower and upper bounds.
  • Two-Sided Form: $$ \boxed{ m\le a_n\le M \qquad \forall n }. $$
  • Absolute-Value Form: $$ \boxed{ |a_n|\le K \qquad \forall n }. $$
  • Bounded Above: There exists $M$ with $a_n\le M$ for every $n$.
  • Bounded Below: There exists $m$ with $m\le a_n$ for every $n$.
  • Convergence: Every convergent real or complex sequence is bounded.
  • Nonconverse: A bounded sequence need not converge.
  • Classic Counterexample: $$ \boxed{ a_n=(-1)^n } $$ is bounded but divergent.
  • Subsequences: Every subsequence of a bounded sequence is bounded.
  • Bolzano-Weierstrass: Every bounded real sequence has a convergent subsequence.
  • Monotone Convergence: A bounded monotone real sequence converges.
  • Increasing Case: An increasing sequence bounded above converges to its supremum.
  • Decreasing Case: A decreasing sequence bounded below converges to its infimum.
  • Supremum: The least upper bound of the set of sequence values.
  • Infimum: The greatest lower bound of the set of sequence values.
  • Maximum Caution: The supremum need not occur as an actual term.
  • Geometric Sequence: $ar^n$ is bounded when $|r|\le1$ under the usual fixed starting-index conventions, while $|r|>1$ with $a\ne0$ produces unbounded magnitude.
  • Addition: The sum of two bounded sequences is bounded.
  • Scalar Multiplication: A scalar multiple of a bounded sequence is bounded.
  • Product: The product of two bounded sequences is bounded.
  • Quotient Caution: A quotient is bounded only with additional control preventing the denominator from approaching zero.
  • Cauchy Sequence: Every Cauchy sequence is bounded.
  • Limsup: Describes the asymptotic upper accumulation level.
  • Liminf: Describes the asymptotic lower accumulation level.
  • Convergence Test: For a bounded real sequence: $$ \boxed{ \limsup a_n=\liminf a_n } $$ exactly when the sequence converges.
  • Vector-Valued Sequences: Boundedness is expressed by $\|\mathbf a_n\|\le M$.
  • Eventually Bounded: If all sufficiently late terms are bounded, then an ordinary real sequence is fully bounded after enlarging the bound to include the finitely many early terms.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse boundedness with convergence.
  • Common Error: Do not assume an increasing sequence must be unbounded.
  • Common Error: Do not assume an upper or lower bound must occur among the terms.
  • Common Error: Do not interpret "bounded" as meaning the sequence has finitely many terms.
  • Terminological Contrast: Bounded above means all terms lie below some finite upper limit. Bounded below means all terms lie above some finite lower limit. Bounded sequence satisfies both. Supremum is the least upper bound. Infimum is the greatest lower bound. Convergent sequence approaches a single limit and is therefore bounded. Monotone bounded sequence is guaranteed to converge. Bolzano-Weierstrass guarantees a convergent subsequence for every bounded real sequence.
