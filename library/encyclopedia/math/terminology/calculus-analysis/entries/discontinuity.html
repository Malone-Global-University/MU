Discontinuity

Subfield: Calculus & Analysis

Etymology & Roots

A discontinuity is a point at which a function fails to be continuous. The word combines the prefix dis-, indicating separation or failure, with continuity, the property of local agreement between function values and limiting behavior.

$$ \boxed{ f\text{ is discontinuous at }a \iff f\text{ is not continuous at }a } $$

For a real-valued function, continuity at $a$ requires that $f(a)$ be defined, that $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)$ exist, and that the two values agree.

Operational Nuance

Continuity Test

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a) } $$

A failure of any required part of this statement produces a discontinuity at $a$.

Removable Discontinuity

A removable discontinuity occurs when the limit exists and is finite, but the function value is missing or does not equal the limit:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L, \qquad f(a)\neq L \text{ or }f(a)\text{ undefined} }. $$

Such a discontinuity can be removed by redefining $f(a)=L$.

Jump Discontinuity

A jump discontinuity occurs when the one-sided limits exist as finite values but are unequal:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a^-}f(x) \neq \lim_{x\to a^+}f(x) }. $$

Infinite Discontinuity

An infinite discontinuity occurs when the function grows without bound near the point from at least one side:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a^\pm}f(x)=\pm\infty } $$

Vertical asymptotes commonly produce this type of discontinuity.

Oscillatory Discontinuity

A limit can also fail because the function oscillates without settling toward a single value. A standard example is:

$$ f(x)=\sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) $$

near $x=0$.

Domain Matters

A discontinuity is discussed at a point relative to the function's domain and the relevant notion of approach. An endpoint may use one-sided continuity rather than a two-sided limit.

Piecewise Functions

For piecewise-defined functions, possible discontinuities often occur where the defining formula changes. One-sided limits are especially useful there.

Common Misconception

A discontinuity is not simply any visually unusual point. Corners and cusps can be continuous even though they are not differentiable.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\lim_{x\to a}f(x)\neq f(a)$ "F is not continuous at a."
$\lim_{x\to a^-}f(x)$ "The left-hand limit of f at a."
$\lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)$ "The right-hand limit of f at a."
$f(a)$ undefined "The function has no assigned value at a."
$\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L$ "The limiting value exists and equals L."
$x=a$ vertical asymptote "The function has infinite behavior near x equals a."

The classifications removable, jump, infinite, and oscillatory are common in introductory calculus, although terminology and grouping conventions vary somewhat among texts.

Defense Notation Advice: Check one-sided limits before declaring a two-sided limit nonexistent, and distinguish failure of continuity from failure of differentiability.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: A discontinuity is a point where continuity fails.
  • Removable Case: The nearby limit exists, but the point value is missing or mismatched.
  • Jump Case: Finite left- and right-hand limits exist but differ.
  • Infinite Case: At least one one-sided behavior becomes unbounded.
  • Oscillatory Case: The function fails to settle toward a unique limiting value.
  • Rational Functions: Denominator zeros may produce removable discontinuities or vertical asymptotes.
  • Piecewise Functions: Formula-change points require one-sided analysis.
  • Continuity vs. Differentiability: A function can be continuous at a corner or cusp while failing to be differentiable there.
  • Topology: Continuity is generalized through open sets and neighborhoods, so discontinuity likewise has a broader topological meaning.
  • Applied Models: Discontinuities can represent abrupt changes, switches, shocks, thresholds, or idealized breaks in physical or economic systems.
  • Common Error: Calling every undefined point a removable discontinuity.
  • Common Error: Assuming a jump discontinuity has an infinite limit.
  • Common Error: Confusing nondifferentiability with discontinuity.
  • Common Error: Ignoring one-sided behavior when classifying a discontinuity.
  • Core Interpretation: Discontinuity is best understood as failure of local agreement: $$ \boxed{ \text{function value} \quad\text{and}\quad \text{nearby limiting behavior} \text{ do not fit the continuity condition} }. $$ Different failure modes lead to removable, jump, infinite, or oscillatory behavior and form a central part of limit and continuity analysis.
