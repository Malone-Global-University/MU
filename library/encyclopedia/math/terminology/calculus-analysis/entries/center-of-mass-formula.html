Center of Mass Formula

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

The center of mass of a system is the point whose position is the mass-weighted average of all the mass in that system.

$$ \boxed{ \text{center of mass} = \text{mass-weighted average position} }. $$

For discrete particles with masses $m_1,m_2,\ldots,m_n$ located at positions $x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n$ on a line, the center of mass is:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{ \sum_{i=1}^{n}m_i x_i }{ \sum_{i=1}^{n}m_i } }. $$

The numerator is a weighted sum of positions, while the denominator is the total mass.

In continuous systems, the same idea remains valid, but summation is replaced by integration:

$$ \boxed{ \text{weighted sum} \longrightarrow \text{weighted integral} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Discrete Masses on a Line

Suppose point masses $m_1,\ldots,m_n$ lie at coordinates $x_1,\ldots,x_n$.

The total mass is:

$$ \boxed{ M= \sum_{i=1}^{n}m_i }. $$

The first moment about the origin is:

$$ \boxed{ M_0= \sum_{i=1}^{n}m_i x_i }. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{M_0}{M} = \frac{\sum m_i x_i}{\sum m_i} }. $$

Two-Mass Example

Suppose a $2$ kg mass is at $x=0$ and a $6$ kg mass is at $x=4$.

Then:

$$ \bar{x} = \frac{ 2(0)+6(4) }{ 2+6 } = \frac{24}{8} = \boxed{3}. $$

The center of mass lies closer to the heavier mass.

Weighted Average Interpretation

The center of mass is an ordinary weighted mean in which the weights are masses.

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{ m_1x_1+\cdots+m_nx_n }{ m_1+\cdots+m_n } }. $$

Discrete Masses in the Plane

If masses $m_i$ are located at points $(x_i,y_i)$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{\sum m_i x_i}{\sum m_i} } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{y} = \frac{\sum m_i y_i}{\sum m_i} }. $$

The center of mass is:

$$ \boxed{ (\bar{x},\bar{y}) }. $$

Discrete Masses in Three Dimensions

For masses at $(x_i,y_i,z_i)$:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{\sum m_i x_i}{M}, \qquad \bar{y} = \frac{\sum m_i y_i}{M}, \qquad \bar{z} = \frac{\sum m_i z_i}{M} } $$

where:

$$ M=\sum m_i. $$

Vector Form

If $\mathbf r_i$ is the position vector of mass $m_i$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf r_{\text{cm}} = \frac1M \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i\mathbf r_i }. $$

This is the coordinate-independent weighted-average form.

Continuous Mass Distribution

For a continuous body, small pieces of mass are represented by $dm$.

The total mass is:

$$ \boxed{ M=\int dm }. $$

The vector center of mass is:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf r_{\text{cm}} = \frac1M \int \mathbf r\,dm }. $$

One-Dimensional Rod

Suppose a rod occupies:

$$ a\le x\le b $$

and has linear density $\lambda(x)$.

Then:

$$ dm=\lambda(x)\,dx. $$

Its total mass is:

$$ \boxed{ M= \int_a^b \lambda(x)\,dx }. $$

Its center of mass is:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{ \int_a^b x\lambda(x)\,dx }{ \int_a^b\lambda(x)\,dx } }. $$

Uniform Rod

If $\lambda(x)=\lambda_0$ is constant:

$$ \bar{x} = \frac{ \int_a^b x\lambda_0\,dx }{ \int_a^b\lambda_0\,dx }. $$

The constant density cancels, giving:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{a+b}{2} }. $$

Thus a uniform rod balances at its midpoint.

Nonuniform Rod

If density increases toward one end, the center of mass shifts toward the denser region.

$$ \boxed{ \text{greater density} \Rightarrow \text{greater influence on center of mass} }. $$

Planar Lamina

For a thin planar region $R$ with surface density $\rho(x,y)$:

$$ dm=\rho(x,y)\,dA. $$

The total mass is:

$$ \boxed{ M= \iint_R \rho(x,y)\,dA }. $$

x-Coordinate of the Center of Mass

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac1M \iint_R x\rho(x,y)\,dA }. $$

y-Coordinate of the Center of Mass

$$ \boxed{ \bar{y} = \frac1M \iint_R y\rho(x,y)\,dA }. $$

Moments of a Lamina

The first moment about the $y$-axis is:

$$ \boxed{ M_y = \iint_R x\rho(x,y)\,dA }. $$

The first moment about the $x$-axis is:

$$ \boxed{ M_x = \iint_R y\rho(x,y)\,dA }. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x}=\frac{M_y}{M}, \qquad \bar{y}=\frac{M_x}{M} }. $$

Why the Moment Subscripts Look Reversed

The moment about the $y$-axis uses horizontal distance $x$, while the moment about the $x$-axis uses vertical distance $y$.

$$ \boxed{ M_y \text{ uses }x, \qquad M_x \text{ uses }y }. $$

Uniform Lamina

If $\rho$ is constant:

$$ \rho(x,y)=\rho_0, $$

then density cancels from the center-of-mass formulas.

The center of mass then coincides with the geometric centroid of the region.

$$ \boxed{ \text{uniform density} \Rightarrow \text{center of mass}= \text{centroid} }. $$

Lamina between Two Curves

Suppose:

$$ R= \{(x,y): a\le x\le b,\, g(x)\le y\le f(x)\} $$

with constant density.

The area is:

$$ A= \int_a^b [f(x)-g(x)]\,dx. $$

The centroid coordinate $\bar{x}$ is:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{ \int_a^b x[f(x)-g(x)]\,dx }{ A } }. $$

The centroid coordinate $\bar{y}$ is:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{y} = \frac{ \frac12 \int_a^b [f(x)^2-g(x)^2]\,dx }{ A } }. $$

Three-Dimensional Solid

For a solid region $V$ with volume density $\rho(x,y,z)$:

$$ dm= \rho(x,y,z)\,dV. $$

Total mass:

$$ \boxed{ M= \iiint_V \rho(x,y,z)\,dV }. $$

Center of Mass of a Solid

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac1M \iiint_V x\rho\,dV } $$
$$ \boxed{ \bar{y} = \frac1M \iiint_V y\rho\,dV } $$
$$ \boxed{ \bar{z} = \frac1M \iiint_V z\rho\,dV }. $$

Vector Formula for a Continuous Body

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf r_{\text{cm}} = \frac{ \iiint_V \mathbf r\,\rho\,dV }{ \iiint_V \rho\,dV } }. $$

Symmetry

Symmetry can determine center-of-mass coordinates without direct integration.

If a body and its density distribution are symmetric about the $y$-axis, then:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x}=0 }. $$

Similar reasoning applies to other symmetry axes and planes.

Geometric Symmetry Is Not Enough if Density Is Asymmetric

A geometrically symmetric body can have an off-center center of mass if its density distribution is not symmetric.

$$ \boxed{ \text{shape symmetry alone} \not\Rightarrow \text{mass symmetry} }. $$

Balance Interpretation

For a rigid body in a uniform gravitational field, the center of mass is the point through which the resultant gravitational force may be treated as acting for many mechanical calculations.

For a one-dimensional balance problem:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_i m_i(x_i-\bar{x}) = 0 }. $$

The positive and negative first moments about the center of mass cancel.

Moment-Balance Identity

Since:

$$ M\bar{x} = \sum_i m_i x_i, $$

it follows that:

$$ \sum_i m_i(x_i-\bar{x}) = 0. $$

Center of Mass of Two Particles

For two masses:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{ m_1x_1+m_2x_2 }{ m_1+m_2 } }. $$

If $m_1=m_2$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{x_1+x_2}{2} }. $$

Equal masses place the center of mass at the midpoint.

Ratio Property for Two Masses

For two positive masses on a line, the center of mass divides the segment between them inversely according to mass.

If:

$$ x_1<\bar{x}

then:

$$ \boxed{ m_1(\bar{x}-x_1) = m_2(x_2-\bar{x}) }. $$

The heavier mass lies closer to the center of mass.

Center of Mass Lies in the Convex Hull

For positive masses, the center of mass is a weighted average with positive weights summing to one:

$$ \mathbf r_{\text{cm}} = \sum_i \left( \frac{m_i}{M} \right) \mathbf r_i. $$

Therefore it lies within the convex hull of the mass locations.

Center of Mass May Lie Outside the Material

The center of mass need not lie inside the physical material itself.

A ring, hollow sphere, or curved object can have its center of mass in empty space.

Center of Mass versus Centroid

A centroid is a geometric center based on shape or measure. A center of mass incorporates density.

$$ \boxed{ \text{uniform density} \Rightarrow \text{center of mass} = \text{centroid} }. $$

With nonuniform density, they may differ.

Center of Mass versus Center of Gravity

In a uniform gravitational field, center of gravity and center of mass coincide.

In a significantly nonuniform gravitational field, the two concepts can require distinction.

Units

If position is measured in meters, then the center-of-mass coordinates are also measured in meters.

For a one-dimensional moment:

$$ [mx] = \text{mass}\times\text{length}. $$

Dividing by total mass returns units of length:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{ \text{mass}\cdot\text{length} }{ \text{mass} } = \text{length} }. $$

Linear Density

Linear density has units:

$$ \boxed{ \lambda = \frac{\text{mass}}{\text{length}} }. $$

Surface Density

$$ \boxed{ \rho = \frac{\text{mass}}{\text{area}} }. $$

Volume Density

$$ \boxed{ \rho = \frac{\text{mass}}{\text{volume}} }. $$

Integral as Continuous Weighted Average

The continuous center-of-mass formula has the same structure as a weighted mean:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{ \int x\,dm }{ \int dm } }. $$

The only difference from the discrete formula is that infinitely many small mass contributions are accumulated by integration.

Common Misconception: Average the Coordinates Directly

Direct averaging is correct only when all masses are equal.

$$ \boxed{ \frac{x_1+\cdots+x_n}{n} \text{ is valid only for equal masses} }. $$

Common Misconception: The Center of Mass Must Be Inside the Object

It may lie in empty space, particularly for hollow or curved bodies.

Common Misconception: Centroid and Center of Mass Are Always the Same

They coincide under uniform density, but variable density can shift the center of mass away from the geometric centroid.

Common Misconception: The Denominator Is Number of Masses

The denominator is total mass:

$$ \boxed{ M=\sum m_i } $$

rather than the number of particles.

Common Misconception: Moment of Mass and Moment of Inertia Are the Same

First moments are used to locate the center of mass. Moments of inertia involve squared distances and measure resistance to rotational acceleration.

$$ \boxed{ \text{first moment} \ne \text{moment of inertia} }. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\bar{x}=\dfrac{\sum m_ix_i}{\sum m_i}$ "x bar equals the sum of mass times x-coordinate divided by total mass."
$M=\int dm$ "Total mass is the integral of the differential mass."
$\bar{x}=\dfrac1M\int x\,dm$ "The x-coordinate of the center of mass is one over total mass times the integral of x with respect to mass."
$M_x=\iint_R y\rho\,dA$ "M sub x is the first moment about the x-axis."
$\bar{y}=M_x/M$ "y bar equals the moment about the x-axis divided by total mass."
$\mathbf r_{\text{cm}}=\frac1M\int\mathbf r\,dm$ "The center-of-mass position vector is the mass-weighted average position."

The notation $\bar{x}$, $\bar{y}$, and $\bar{z}$ is common for center-of-mass coordinates, though $\mathbf r_{\text{cm}}$ is often preferable when working in vector form.

Defense Notation Advice: Identify the dimensional type of the object first—discrete particles, rod, lamina, or solid—and choose the correct mass element $dm$. Then compute total mass before dividing moments by that total. Keep first moments distinct from moments of inertia.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: Center of mass is the mass-weighted average position of a system.
  • Discrete One-Dimensional Formula: $$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{\sum m_ix_i}{\sum m_i} }. $$
  • Discrete Two-Dimensional Formula: $$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{\sum m_ix_i}{M}, \qquad \bar{y} = \frac{\sum m_iy_i}{M} }. $$
  • Vector Formula: $$ \boxed{ \mathbf r_{\text{cm}} = \frac1M \sum m_i\mathbf r_i }. $$
  • Continuous Vector Formula: $$ \boxed{ \mathbf r_{\text{cm}} = \frac1M \int \mathbf r\,dm }. $$
  • Rod Mass: $$ \boxed{ M= \int_a^b \lambda(x)\,dx }. $$
  • Rod Center of Mass: $$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{ \int_a^b x\lambda(x)\,dx }{ M } }. $$
  • Lamina Mass: $$ \boxed{ M= \iint_R \rho(x,y)\,dA }. $$
  • Lamina Coordinates: $$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac1M \iint_R x\rho\,dA, \qquad \bar{y} = \frac1M \iint_R y\rho\,dA }. $$
  • Solid Mass: $$ \boxed{ M= \iiint_V \rho(x,y,z)\,dV }. $$
  • Solid Coordinates: $$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac1M\iiint_V x\rho\,dV, \quad \bar{y} = \frac1M\iiint_V y\rho\,dV, \quad \bar{z} = \frac1M\iiint_V z\rho\,dV }. $$
  • Uniform Density: Center of mass coincides with centroid.
  • Symmetry: Symmetric density distributions can force one or more center-of-mass coordinates.
  • Balance Interpretation: First moments about the center of mass sum to zero.
  • Two-Mass Rule: The center of mass lies closer to the heavier mass.
  • Positive Masses: The center of mass lies in the convex hull of the mass distribution.
  • Centroid Contrast: Centroid depends on geometry; center of mass depends on geometry and density.
  • Center of Gravity Contrast: Center of mass and center of gravity coincide in a uniform gravitational field.
  • Common Error: Do not average coordinates without mass weights unless all masses are equal.
  • Common Error: Do not divide by the number of masses; divide by total mass.
  • Common Error: Do not assume the center of mass must lie inside the material body.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse first moments with moments of inertia.
  • Terminological Contrast: Center of mass is the mass-weighted average position. Centroid is the geometric center associated with uniform measure. First moment is a mass-times-distance quantity used to locate the center of mass. Moment of inertia uses squared distance and concerns rotational dynamics. Center of gravity is the effective point of gravitational action and coincides with center of mass under uniform gravity.
