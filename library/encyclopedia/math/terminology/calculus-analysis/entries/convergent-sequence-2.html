Convergent Sequence

Subfield: Calculus & Analysis

Etymology & Roots

A convergent sequence is a sequence whose terms approach a single finite value as the index increases without bound.

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=L } $$

The word convergent reflects the idea of values drawing toward a common destination. The number $L$ is called the limit of the sequence.

Operational Nuance

Formal Definition

The sequence $(a_n)$ converges to $L$ if, for every $\varepsilon>0$, there exists an integer $N$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ n\geq N \Longrightarrow |a_n-L|<\varepsilon }. $$

In practical terms, the terms eventually become arbitrarily close to $L$ and remain that close thereafter.

Example

For:

$$ a_n=\frac{1}{n}, $$

the terms approach zero:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}=0 }. $$

Uniqueness of the Limit

A convergent sequence has exactly one limit. It cannot converge to two distinct real numbers.

Boundedness

Every convergent real sequence is bounded:

$$ \boxed{ a_n\to L \Longrightarrow \exists M>0 \text{ such that } |a_n|\leq M } $$

The converse is false: a bounded sequence need not converge.

Oscillation

The sequence $a_n=(-1)^n$ is bounded but does not converge because its terms alternate between $1$ and $-1$ and do not approach a single value.

Monotone Convergence

A real sequence that is monotone and bounded converges. This is the Monotone Convergence Theorem for sequences.

$$ \boxed{ \text{monotone} + \text{bounded} \Longrightarrow \text{convergent} }. $$

Subsequences

Every subsequence of a convergent sequence converges to the same limit:

$$ \boxed{ a_n\to L \Longrightarrow a_{n_k}\to L }. $$

Algebra of Limits

If $a_n\to A$ and $b_n\to B$, then under the usual conditions:

$$ a_n+b_n\to A+B, \qquad a_nb_n\to AB, $$
$$ \frac{a_n}{b_n}\to\frac{A}{B} \qquad \text{if }B\neq0 \text{ and }b_n\neq0\text{ eventually}. $$

Sequence vs. Series

A sequence $(a_n)$ converges when its individual terms approach a limit. An infinite series $\sum a_n$ converges when its sequence of partial sums converges. These are different statements.

Common Misconception

A convergent sequence does not have to reach its limit. It only has to become arbitrarily close to that limit and remain close as $n$ grows.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$a_n\to L$ "A sub n converges to L."
$\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=L$ "The limit of a sub n as n approaches infinity is L."
$|a_n-L|<\varepsilon$ "A sub n lies within epsilon of L."
$n\geq N$ "For all sufficiently large n."
$(a_{n_k})$ "A subsequence of the original sequence."
$a_n\nrightarrow L$ "The sequence does not converge to L."

The phrases converges to $L$, has limit $L$, and tends to $L$ are commonly used for the same limiting behavior.

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish the sequence term $a_n$ from its limit $L$, and distinguish convergence of $(a_n)$ from convergence of the infinite series $\sum a_n$.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: A sequence converges when its terms approach one finite limit.
  • Epsilon Definition: Terms eventually lie within every prescribed positive distance of the limit.
  • Uniqueness: A convergent sequence has only one limit.
  • Boundedness: Every convergent real sequence is bounded.
  • Converse Failure: A bounded sequence may still oscillate and fail to converge.
  • Monotone Sequences: Bounded monotone real sequences converge.
  • Subsequences: Every subsequence of a convergent sequence has the same limit.
  • Recursive Sequences: Convergence is often studied by proving boundedness and monotonicity, then identifying the possible limit from the recurrence.
  • Numerical Analysis: Iterative algorithms are often judged by whether their generated sequence converges to a desired solution.
  • Series Theory: Infinite-series convergence is defined through convergence of the sequence of partial sums.
  • Common Error: Assuming boundedness alone guarantees convergence.
  • Common Error: Assuming a sequence must actually attain its limit.
  • Common Error: Confusing $a_n\to0$ with convergence of $\sum a_n$.
  • Common Error: Treating visual closeness in a finite list of terms as proof of convergence.
  • Core Interpretation: Convergence is an eventual closeness condition: $$ \boxed{ \forall\varepsilon>0, \ \exists N \text{ such that } n\geq N \Longrightarrow |a_n-L|<\varepsilon }. $$ It provides one of the foundational mathematical languages for describing limiting behavior, infinite processes, approximation, and analysis.
