Bounded Set of Numbers

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

A bounded set of numbers is a numerical set whose elements remain within finite limits.

For a set $S\subseteq\mathbb R$, the set is bounded if there exist real numbers $m$ and $M$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ m\le x\le M \qquad \text{for every }x\in S }. $$

The number $m$ is a lower bound, while $M$ is an upper bound.

$$ \boxed{ \text{bounded} = \text{bounded above and bounded below} }. $$

Boundedness concerns the numerical extent of the set. It does not mean that the set contains only finitely many elements.

Operational Nuance

Bounded Above

A set $S\subseteq\mathbb R$ is bounded above if there exists a real number $M$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ x\le M \qquad \forall x\in S }. $$

Any number satisfying this condition is an upper bound of $S$.

Bounded Below

A set is bounded below if there exists a real number $m$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ m\le x \qquad \forall x\in S }. $$

Any such number is a lower bound.

Bounded on Both Sides

A set is bounded when both types of bounds exist:

$$ \boxed{ \exists\,m,M\in\mathbb R \text{ such that } m\le x\le M \quad \forall x\in S }. $$

Absolute-Value Form

For subsets of the real numbers, boundedness is equivalent to the existence of some $K\ge0$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ |x|\le K \qquad \forall x\in S }. $$

This means:

$$ S\subseteq[-K,K]. $$

Interval Interpretation

A real set is bounded exactly when it fits inside some finite closed interval:

$$ \boxed{ S\text{ bounded} \iff S\subseteq[m,M] \text{ for some }m,M\in\mathbb R }. $$

Example: Finite Set

Consider:

$$ S=\{-3,1,4,8\}. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ -3\le x\le8 \qquad \forall x\in S }. $$

Therefore $S$ is bounded.

Every Finite Real Set Is Bounded

Any nonempty finite set of real numbers has both a smallest and a largest element. Consequently:

$$ \boxed{ \text{finite subset of }\mathbb R \Rightarrow \text{bounded} }. $$

Infinite Sets May Also Be Bounded

The open interval:

$$ (0,1) $$

contains infinitely many real numbers but satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ 0

Thus it is bounded.

Bounded Does Not Mean Finite

$$ \boxed{ \text{bounded} \not\Rightarrow \text{finite} }. $$

Example: Bounded Above but Not Below

Consider:

$$ S=(-\infty,5]. $$

The set is bounded above by $5$, but it has no finite lower bound.

Example: Bounded Below but Not Above

The interval:

$$ [2,\infty) $$

is bounded below by $2$ but unbounded above.

Example: Unbounded on Both Sides

The set:

$$ \mathbb R $$

has neither a finite upper bound nor a finite lower bound.

Upper Bounds Are Not Unique

If $M$ is an upper bound, then any larger number is also an upper bound.

For example, if:

$$ S=[0,3], $$

then $3$, $4$, $10$, and $100$ are all upper bounds.

Lower Bounds Are Not Unique

Similarly, if $m$ is a lower bound, then every smaller number is also a lower bound.

Least Upper Bound

The smallest possible upper bound is the supremum:

$$ \boxed{ \sup S }. $$

Greatest Lower Bound

The largest possible lower bound is the infimum:

$$ \boxed{ \inf S }. $$

Example: Open Interval

For:

$$ S=(0,1), $$

one has:

$$ \boxed{ \inf S=0, \qquad \sup S=1 }. $$

Neither $0$ nor $1$ belongs to the set.

Maximum versus Supremum

A maximum must be an actual element of the set.

A supremum need not be.

$$ \boxed{ \max S\in S, \qquad \sup S\text{ need not belong to }S }. $$

Minimum versus Infimum

Likewise:

$$ \boxed{ \min S\in S, \qquad \inf S\text{ need not belong to }S }. $$

Closed Interval

For:

$$ S=[a,b], $$

one has:

$$ \boxed{ \min S=\inf S=a, \qquad \max S=\sup S=b }. $$

Open Interval

For:

$$ S=(a,b), $$

one has:

$$ \boxed{ \inf S=a, \qquad \sup S=b } $$

but no minimum or maximum.

Half-Open Interval

For:

$$ [a,b), $$

the minimum is $a$, while the supremum is $b$ but no maximum exists.

Least-Upper-Bound Property of the Reals

A fundamental completeness property of $\mathbb R$ states:

$$ \boxed{ S\subseteq\mathbb R,\; S\ne\varnothing,\; S\text{ bounded above} \Rightarrow \sup S\in\mathbb R }. $$

This is called the least-upper-bound property or supremum property.

Greatest-Lower-Bound Property

Equivalently, every nonempty real set bounded below has a real infimum:

$$ \boxed{ S\ne\varnothing,\; S\text{ bounded below} \Rightarrow \inf S\in\mathbb R }. $$

Why Completeness Matters

The analogous statement fails if one insists that the supremum remain rational.

For example:

$$ S= \{q\in\mathbb Q:q^2<2\} $$

is bounded above in $\mathbb Q$, but its natural least upper bound is $\sqrt2$, which is not rational.

This illustrates an important distinction between the completeness of $\mathbb R$ and $\mathbb Q$.

Natural Numbers

The set:

$$ \mathbb N $$

is bounded below under the usual conventions but is not bounded above.

Integers

The set:

$$ \mathbb Z $$

is unbounded both above and below.

Rationals in a Finite Interval

The set:

$$ \mathbb Q\cap[0,1] $$

is infinite and bounded:

$$ \boxed{ 0\le q\le1 }. $$

Irrationals in a Finite Interval

Likewise:

$$ (\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q)\cap[0,1] $$

is infinite and bounded.

Bounded Set and Diameter

For a nonempty subset $S\subseteq\mathbb R$, define:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{diam}(S) = \sup\{|x-y|:x,y\in S\} }. $$

The set is bounded exactly when its diameter is finite.

$$ \boxed{ S\text{ bounded} \iff \operatorname{diam}(S)<\infty }. $$

Diameter of an Interval

For any of the intervals with endpoints $a

$$ (a,b), \quad [a,b], \quad [a,b), \quad (a,b], $$

the diameter is:

$$ \boxed{ b-a }. $$

Even when the endpoints are excluded, the supremum of pairwise distances remains $b-a$.

Subsets of Bounded Sets

Every subset of a bounded numerical set is bounded.

$$ \boxed{ A\subseteq B \text{ and }B\text{ bounded} \Rightarrow A\text{ bounded} }. $$

Finite Unions

A finite union of bounded subsets of $\mathbb R$ is bounded.

$$ \boxed{ A_1,\ldots,A_n\text{ bounded} \Rightarrow \bigcup_{k=1}^{n}A_k \text{ bounded} }. $$

Infinite Union Caution

An infinite union of bounded sets need not be bounded.

For example:

$$ A_n=[-n,n]. $$

Every $A_n$ is bounded, but:

$$ \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_n = \mathbb R, $$

which is unbounded.

Intersection

If at least one of several intersected sets is bounded, then their intersection is bounded because it is a subset of that bounded set.

Translation

Adding a fixed number $c$ to every element preserves boundedness.

If:

$$ S+c=\{x+c:x\in S\}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ S\text{ bounded} \iff S+c\text{ bounded} }. $$

Scaling

Multiplying every element by a fixed finite scalar $c$ preserves boundedness.

$$ \boxed{ S\text{ bounded} \Rightarrow cS\text{ bounded} }. $$

If $|x|\le M$, then:

$$ |cx|\le |c|M. $$

Sum of Bounded Sets

If $A$ and $B$ are bounded, then their Minkowski sum:

$$ A+B= \{a+b:a\in A,\ b\in B\} $$

is bounded.

If:

$$ |a|\le M, \qquad |b|\le N, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ |a+b|\le M+N }. $$

Product of Bounded Sets

If $A$ and $B$ are bounded subsets of $\mathbb R$, then:

$$ AB= \{ab:a\in A,\ b\in B\} $$

is also bounded because:

$$ \boxed{ |ab|\le MN }. $$

Reciprocal Set Caution

If a bounded set approaches $0$, taking reciprocals may produce an unbounded set.

For example:

$$ S= \left\{ \frac1n:n\in\mathbb N \right\} $$

is bounded, but:

$$ \left\{ \frac1x:x\in S \right\} = \mathbb N, $$

which is unbounded above.

Image of a Bounded Sequence

A sequence $(a_n)$ is bounded exactly when its set of terms:

$$ \{a_n:n\in\mathbb N\} $$

is a bounded set of numbers.

$$ \boxed{ (a_n)\text{ bounded} \iff \{a_n:n\in\mathbb N\}\text{ bounded} }. $$

Range of a Bounded Function

Likewise, a real-valued function $f:D\to\mathbb R$ is bounded precisely when its image:

$$ f(D) $$

is a bounded set of real numbers.

Closed and Bounded Sets

In $\mathbb R$, a set may be bounded without being closed:

$$ (0,1) $$

is bounded but not closed.

Likewise, a set may be closed but unbounded:

$$ [0,\infty) $$

is closed but unbounded.

Heine-Borel Theorem

In $\mathbb R$, as in $\mathbb R^n$:

$$ \boxed{ K\text{ compact} \iff K\text{ closed and bounded} }. $$

Bounded Does Not Mean Compact

The interval $(0,1)$ is bounded but not compact in the usual Euclidean topology because it is not closed.

Common Misconception: Bound Must Belong to the Set

An upper or lower bound need not be an element of the set.

For:

$$ S=(0,1), $$

both $0$ and $1$ are bounds, even though neither belongs to $S$.

Common Misconception: Supremum Means Maximum

A supremum becomes a maximum only when it belongs to the set.

$$ \boxed{ \sup S\in S \Rightarrow \max S=\sup S }. $$

Common Misconception: Bounded Above Means Bounded

A set must be bounded both above and below to be called bounded without further qualification.

Common Misconception: Infinite Means Unbounded

Infinite cardinality and unbounded numerical extent are different properties. Intervals such as $[0,1]$ contain infinitely many numbers while remaining bounded.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$x\le M$ for all $x\in S$ "S is bounded above by M."
$m\le x$ for all $x\in S$ "S is bounded below by m."
$m\le x\le M$ for all $x\in S$ "S is a bounded set of real numbers."
$|x|\le K$ for all $x\in S$ "Every element of S has absolute value at most K."
$\sup S$ "The supremum, or least upper bound, of S."
$\inf S$ "The infimum, or greatest lower bound, of S."

In standard mathematical usage, saying simply that a numerical set is bounded normally means bounded both above and below. If only one side is controlled, the more precise phrases are bounded above and bounded below.

Defense Notation Advice: Keep four ideas distinct: an upper bound need not be the least one, a supremum need not belong to the set, a maximum must belong to the set, and boundedness requires finite control on both sides.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: A set of real numbers is bounded if all of its elements lie between two finite real numbers.
  • Two-Sided Form: $$ \boxed{ m\le x\le M \qquad \forall x\in S }. $$
  • Absolute-Value Form: $$ \boxed{ |x|\le K \qquad \forall x\in S }. $$
  • Bounded Above: There exists a finite upper bound $M$.
  • Bounded Below: There exists a finite lower bound $m$.
  • Interval Form: A bounded real set fits inside some finite interval $[m,M]$.
  • Finite Sets: Every finite subset of $\mathbb R$ is bounded.
  • Infinite Sets: Infinite sets may still be bounded.
  • Supremum: The least upper bound of a nonempty set bounded above.
  • Infimum: The greatest lower bound of a nonempty set bounded below.
  • Maximum: An attained greatest element.
  • Minimum: An attained least element.
  • Open-Interval Example: $(0,1)$ is bounded, with infimum $0$ and supremum $1$, but has neither a minimum nor a maximum.
  • Closed-Interval Example: $[0,1]$ is bounded and has minimum $0$ and maximum $1$.
  • Least-Upper-Bound Property: Every nonempty real set bounded above has a real supremum.
  • Greatest-Lower-Bound Property: Every nonempty real set bounded below has a real infimum.
  • Completeness: These supremum and infimum properties are fundamental features of the real numbers.
  • Diameter: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{diam}(S) = \sup\{|x-y|:x,y\in S\} }. $$
  • Finite-Diameter Criterion: A nonempty real set is bounded exactly when its diameter is finite.
  • Subset Property: Every subset of a bounded set is bounded.
  • Finite Union: A finite union of bounded sets is bounded.
  • Infinite Union Caution: An infinite union of bounded sets may be unbounded.
  • Translation: Adding a fixed constant preserves boundedness.
  • Scaling: Multiplication by a fixed finite scalar preserves boundedness.
  • Sum of Sets: The sum of two bounded numerical sets is bounded.
  • Product of Sets: The product of two bounded numerical sets is bounded.
  • Reciprocal Caution: Reciprocals of a bounded set may be unbounded if the set approaches zero.
  • Sequence Connection: A sequence is bounded exactly when its set of values is bounded.
  • Function Connection: A real-valued function is bounded exactly when its range is a bounded set of numbers.
  • Closedness Contrast: A bounded set need not be closed, and a closed set need not be bounded.
  • Compactness: In $\mathbb R$, compact sets are exactly the closed and bounded sets.
  • Common Error: Do not assume a bound must belong to the set.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse supremum with maximum or infimum with minimum.
  • Common Error: Do not interpret bounded above as fully bounded.
  • Common Error: Do not interpret an infinite set as automatically unbounded.
  • Terminological Contrast: Upper bound is any number no smaller than every element of the set. Lower bound is any number no larger than every element. Bounded set has finite bounds on both sides. Supremum is the least upper bound. Infimum is the greatest lower bound. Maximum and minimum are bounds attained by elements of the set. Diameter measures the supremum of pairwise distances between elements.
