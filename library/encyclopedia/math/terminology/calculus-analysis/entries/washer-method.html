Washer Method

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

The Washer Method is a calculus technique for finding the volume of a solid of revolution when cross-sections perpendicular to the axis of rotation are shaped like washers: circular disks with concentric circular holes removed.

The method gets its name from the resemblance of such a cross-section to a mechanical washer. If the outer radius is $R$ and the inner radius is $r$, then the cross-sectional area is the area of the larger circle minus the area of the smaller circle:

$$ \boxed{ A=\pi R^2-\pi r^2 } $$

or equivalently:

$$ \boxed{ A=\pi(R^2-r^2) }. $$

The Washer Method is therefore a direct extension of the Disk Method. A disk can be viewed as a washer whose inner radius is zero.

The term belongs to the broader family of techniques for finding volumes of revolution, which arise when a planar region is rotated around a line such as the $x$-axis, $y$-axis, or another horizontal or vertical line.

Operational Nuance

Basic Cross-Sectional Idea

Suppose a planar region is rotated about an axis and a typical perpendicular slice produces a washer with outer radius $R(x)$ and inner radius $r(x)$.

The area of that washer is:

$$ A(x) = \pi[R(x)]^2 - \pi[r(x)]^2. $$

Factoring out $\pi$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ A(x) = \pi \left( [R(x)]^2-[r(x)]^2 \right) }. $$

Integrating these cross-sectional areas gives the volume:

$$ \boxed{ V = \pi \int_a^b \left( [R(x)]^2-[r(x)]^2 \right)dx }. $$

Why the Radii Are Squared

Each cross-section is circular, so its area depends on the square of the radius:

$$ A_{\text{circle}}=\pi r^2. $$

A washer has two radii, so its area is:

$$ \pi R^2-\pi r^2. $$

The order matters. One must subtract:

$$ \boxed{ \text{outer radius squared} - \text{inner radius squared} }. $$

Example About the x-Axis

Suppose the region between:

$$ y=x+2 $$

and:

$$ y=1 $$

for:

$$ 0\le x\le2 $$

is revolved around the $x$-axis.

The outer radius is the distance from the $x$-axis to the upper curve:

$$ R(x)=x+2. $$

The inner radius is:

$$ r(x)=1. $$

Therefore:

$$ V = \pi \int_0^2 \left[ (x+2)^2-1^2 \right]dx. $$

Expanding:

$$ (x+2)^2-1 = x^2+4x+3. $$

Thus:

$$ V = \pi \int_0^2 (x^2+4x+3)\,dx. $$

Evaluating:

$$ V = \pi \left[ \frac{x^3}{3}+2x^2+3x \right]_0^2 $$ $$ = \pi \left( \frac83+8+6 \right) $$ $$ = \boxed{ \frac{50\pi}{3} }. $$

Rotation About a Shifted Horizontal Line

If the axis of rotation is:

$$ y=c, $$

then radii must be measured as vertical distances from the curves to $y=c$, not automatically from the $x$-axis.

For example, if an upper curve is $y=f(x)$ and the rotation axis lies below it, the radius may be:

$$ R(x)=f(x)-c. $$

Integration with Respect to y

If washers are perpendicular to the $y$-axis or another vertical axis, it is often natural to integrate with respect to $y$:

$$ \boxed{ V = \pi \int_c^d \left( [R(y)]^2-[r(y)]^2 \right)dy }. $$

In this setting, the radii are horizontal distances.

Disk Method as a Special Case

If there is no central hole:

$$ r(x)=0. $$

Then:

$$ V = \pi \int_a^b [R(x)]^2\,dx, $$

which is the Disk Method.

Common Misconception: The outer and inner radii are distances from the axis of rotation, not simply the upper and lower function values. When the axis is shifted away from a coordinate axis, the appropriate distances must be calculated.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$R(x)$ "R of x," or "the outer radius."
$r(x)$ "Little r of x," or "the inner radius."
$\pi(R^2-r^2)$ "Pi times outer radius squared minus inner radius squared."
$V=\pi\int_a^b(R^2-r^2)\,dx$ "Volume equals pi times the integral from a to b of outer radius squared minus inner radius squared with respect to x."
$r=0$ "The inner radius is zero."
$y=c$ "The horizontal line y equals c."

The uppercase and lowercase radius notation is conventional:

$$ \boxed{ R=\text{outer radius}, \qquad r=\text{inner radius} }. $$

Other symbols may be used, but the conceptual distinction must remain clear.

Defense Notation Advice: Parentheses should make the subtraction of squared radii explicit:

$$ \pi\left(R^2-r^2\right). $$

This is not equivalent to:

$$ \pi(R-r)^2. $$

Indeed:

$$ R^2-r^2 = (R-r)(R+r), $$

whereas:

$$ (R-r)^2 = R^2-2Rr+r^2. $$

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Basic Washer Formula: $$ \boxed{ A = \pi(R^2-r^2) }. $$
  • Volume with Vertical Slices: $$ \boxed{ V = \pi \int_a^b \left( [R(x)]^2-[r(x)]^2 \right)dx }. $$
  • Volume with Horizontal Slices: $$ \boxed{ V = \pi \int_c^d \left( [R(y)]^2-[r(y)]^2 \right)dy }. $$
  • Outer Radius: The outer radius is the distance from the axis of rotation to the boundary of the region farther from that axis.
  • Inner Radius: The inner radius is the distance from the axis of rotation to the nearer boundary.
  • Distance Requirement: Radii are geometric distances and should therefore be nonnegative: $$ R\ge0,\qquad r\ge0. $$
  • Ordering: Throughout the interval of a standard washer setup: $$ \boxed{ R\ge r }. $$ If that relation changes, the integral may need to be split.
  • Disk Method: If: $$ r=0, $$ then: $$ \pi(R^2-r^2)=\pi R^2, $$ so the Washer Method reduces to the Disk Method.
  • Axis of Rotation: The slices used for washers are perpendicular to the axis of rotation.
  • Horizontal Axis: Rotation about: $$ y=c $$ usually pairs naturally with vertical slices and integration with respect to $x$.
  • Vertical Axis: Rotation about: $$ x=c $$ usually pairs naturally with horizontal slices and integration with respect to $y$.
  • Rotation About the x-Axis: If the region lies above the axis between: $$ y=f(x) $$ and: $$ y=g(x), $$ with: $$ f(x)\ge g(x)\ge0, $$ then: $$ R(x)=f(x), \qquad r(x)=g(x). $$
  • Rotation About y = c: If the axis lies below both curves: $$ y=c, $$ then: $$ R(x)=f(x)-c $$ and: $$ r(x)=g(x)-c. $$
  • Axis Above the Region: If: $$ y=c $$ lies above both curves, distances reverse algebraically: $$ R(x)=c-g(x), $$ $$ r(x)=c-f(x), $$ assuming $f(x)\ge g(x)$.
  • Crossing the Axis: If the region crosses the axis of rotation, a washer may become a disk or the identity of the outer boundary may change. The setup should be examined geometrically rather than applying a memorized formula blindly.
  • Piecewise Integrals: If $R$ or $r$ changes formula within the interval, write the volume as a sum of separate integrals over the appropriate subintervals.
  • Difference of Areas: The method may be understood as: $$ \boxed{ \text{washer area} = \text{outer disk area} - \text{inner disk area} }. $$
  • Riemann Sum Interpretation: For thin washers of thickness $\Delta x$: $$ \Delta V \approx \pi \left( R(x_i)^2-r(x_i)^2 \right) \Delta x. $$ Summing and taking a limit produces the volume integral.
  • General Cross-Section Principle: The Washer Method is a special case of: $$ \boxed{ V=\int_a^bA(x)\,dx }, $$ where $A(x)$ is the cross-sectional area.
  • Units: If radii and thickness are measured in centimeters, the integral produces: $$ \text{cm}^3. $$
  • Comparison with Shell Method: Washers use slices perpendicular to the axis of rotation. Cylindrical shells use slices parallel to the axis.
  • Shell Method Formula: A typical shell volume takes the form: $$ V = 2\pi \int (\text{radius}) (\text{height}) \,d(\text{variable}). $$ This should not be confused with the washer formula.
  • Choosing a Method: A problem may be solvable by washers, disks, or shells. The best method is generally the one that describes the relevant radii or heights most simply.
  • Outer Minus Inner: Washer problems require: $$ \boxed{ R^2-r^2 }, $$ not: $$ r^2-R^2. $$ Reversing the order produces negative cross-sectional area.
  • Not Radius Difference Squared: The washer area is: $$ \boxed{ \pi(R^2-r^2) }, $$ not: $$ \pi(R-r)^2. $$
  • Radius vs. Function Value: A function value equals a radius only when that value actually measures distance from the chosen axis of rotation.
  • Exact vs. Approximate Volume: A definite integral may produce an exact expression such as: $$ \frac{50\pi}{3}, $$ while decimal approximation may be used when an application requires numerical measurement.
  • Terminological Contrast: The Disk Method uses solid circular cross-sections with inner radius zero. The Washer Method uses annular cross-sections with an outer and inner radius. The Shell Method instead accumulates thin cylindrical shells parallel to the axis of rotation.
