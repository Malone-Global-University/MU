Extreme Values of a Polynomial

Subfield: Calculus & Analysis

Etymology & Roots

The extreme values of a polynomial are its greatest or least attained values on a specified domain. The term extreme refers to values at the upper or lower edge of the polynomial's range over that domain.

$$ \boxed{ \text{maximum value} = \max p(x), \qquad \text{minimum value} = \min p(x) } $$

The distinction between absolute and local extrema is essential: an absolute extremum compares the function with every value on the domain, while a local extremum compares only with nearby values.

Operational Nuance

Critical Points

Since every polynomial is differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$, its interior local extrema can occur only at critical numbers satisfying:

$$ \boxed{ p'(x)=0 }. $$

These critical numbers are candidates, not automatic extrema.

First-Derivative Test

If $p'$ changes from positive to negative at a critical number, the polynomial has a local maximum there. If $p'$ changes from negative to positive, it has a local minimum.

$$ \boxed{ +\to- \Rightarrow \text{local maximum}, \qquad -\to+ \Rightarrow \text{local minimum} } $$

Second-Derivative Test

When $p'(c)=0$ and $p''(c)\neq0$:

$$ \boxed{ p''(c)>0 \Rightarrow \text{local minimum}, \qquad p''(c)<0 \Rightarrow \text{local maximum} }. $$

If $p''(c)=0$, the test is inconclusive.

Even-Degree Polynomials on $\mathbb{R}$

For an even-degree polynomial, both ends move in the same direction. If the leading coefficient is positive, the polynomial has an absolute minimum on $\mathbb{R}$; if the leading coefficient is negative, it has an absolute maximum.

$$ \boxed{ \deg p\text{ even},\ a_n>0 \Rightarrow \text{absolute minimum exists} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \deg p\text{ even},\ a_n<0 \Rightarrow \text{absolute maximum exists} } $$

Odd-Degree Polynomials on $\mathbb{R}$

A nonconstant odd-degree polynomial is unbounded above and below on $\mathbb{R}$, so it has neither an absolute maximum nor an absolute minimum there.

Closed Intervals

On a closed interval $[a,b]$, every polynomial attains both an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum by the Extreme Value Theorem.

$$ \boxed{ \text{evaluate }p \text{ at critical numbers in }(a,b) \text{ and at }a,b } $$

Number of Possible Turning Points

A polynomial of degree $n$ has derivative of degree $n-1$, so it can have at most $n-1$ distinct real critical numbers and therefore at most $n-1$ turning points.

Common Misconception

Every solution of $p'(x)=0$ is not necessarily a maximum or minimum. For example, $p(x)=x^3$ has $p'(0)=0$, but $x=0$ is not an extremum.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$p'(c)=0$ "C is a critical number of the polynomial."
$p(c)=\max p(x)$ "P of c is the absolute maximum value."
$p(c)=\min p(x)$ "P of c is the absolute minimum value."
$p''(c)>0$ "The graph is concave up at c."
$p''(c)<0$ "The graph is concave down at c."
$p(a),p(b),p(c_1),\ldots$ "Compare the endpoint and critical-point values."

The extreme point refers to an input-output location such as $(c,p(c))$, while the extreme value refers specifically to the output $p(c)$.

Defense Notation Advice: Always state the domain. A polynomial can have no absolute extrema on $\mathbb{R}$ yet have both absolute extrema on a closed interval.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Local Maximum: A polynomial value greater than or equal to nearby values.
  • Local Minimum: A polynomial value less than or equal to nearby values.
  • Absolute Maximum: The greatest attained polynomial value on the entire stated domain.
  • Absolute Minimum: The least attained polynomial value on the entire stated domain.
  • Critical Numbers: For polynomials, interior extrema can occur only where $p'(x)=0$.
  • Positive Even Degree: The polynomial rises at both ends and possesses an absolute minimum on $\mathbb{R}$.
  • Negative Even Degree: The polynomial falls at both ends and possesses an absolute maximum on $\mathbb{R}$.
  • Odd Degree: A nonconstant odd-degree polynomial has neither absolute extreme value on $\mathbb{R}$.
  • Closed Interval: Both absolute extrema exist and are found among endpoints and interior critical numbers.
  • Turning Points: A degree-$n$ polynomial has at most $n-1$ turning points.
  • Optimization: Polynomial extrema arise naturally in constrained design, cost, area, revenue, and approximation problems.
  • Common Error: Assuming every critical number is an extremum.
  • Common Error: Forgetting endpoints when finding absolute extrema on a closed interval.
  • Common Error: Confusing the $x$-coordinate where an extremum occurs with the extreme function value.
  • Common Error: Ignoring degree parity and leading coefficient when reasoning about global polynomial behavior.
  • Core Interpretation: Extreme-value analysis of a polynomial combines derivative information with global end behavior and domain restrictions: $$ \boxed{ \text{critical numbers} + \text{end behavior or endpoints} \longrightarrow \text{local and absolute extrema} }. $$ The derivative locates candidates, while the domain and polynomial end behavior determine whether those candidates represent global extremes.
