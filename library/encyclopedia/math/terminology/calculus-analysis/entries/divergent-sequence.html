Divergent Sequence

Subfield: Calculus & Analysis

Etymology & Roots

A divergent sequence is a sequence that does not converge to a finite limit. The term divergent comes from the idea of values failing to draw together toward one limiting destination.

$$ \boxed{ (a_n)\text{ diverges} \iff \nexists L\in\mathbb{R} \text{ such that } a_n\to L } $$

Divergence may result from unbounded growth, persistent oscillation, or incompatible limiting behavior among subsequences.

Operational Nuance

Failure of a Finite Limit

A real sequence is convergent only if its terms eventually become arbitrarily close to one finite number. Failure of this condition means the sequence diverges.

Unbounded Divergence

The sequence:

$$ a_n=n $$

diverges because its terms increase without bound. In extended notation:

$$ \boxed{ a_n\to+\infty }. $$

This describes a specific form of divergence rather than convergence to a finite real number.

Oscillatory Divergence

The bounded sequence:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=(-1)^n } $$

diverges because it alternates between $1$ and $-1$ instead of approaching one value.

Bounded Does Not Imply Convergent

A divergent sequence may remain within fixed bounds. Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \text{bounded} \centernot\Longrightarrow \text{convergent} }. $$

Subsequence Test

If two subsequences approach different limits, then the original sequence cannot converge. For $a_n=(-1)^n$:

$$ a_{2n}=1, \qquad a_{2n+1}=-1. $$

These subsequences have different limits, proving divergence.

Unbounded Sequences

Every convergent real sequence is bounded. Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \text{unbounded} \Longrightarrow \text{divergent} }. $$

The converse is false because bounded oscillating sequences can also diverge.

Sequence vs. Series

A divergent sequence $(a_n)$ concerns the behavior of the individual terms. A divergent series $\sum a_n$ concerns the behavior of its partial sums. These are distinct concepts.

Common Misconception

Divergent does not mean that the terms must become arbitrarily large. A sequence can remain bounded forever and still diverge because it fails to approach one limiting value.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$(a_n)$ diverges "The sequence a sub n diverges."
$a_n\nrightarrow L$ "A sub n does not converge to L."
$a_n\to+\infty$ "The sequence grows without bound toward positive infinity."
$a_n=(-1)^n$ "The sequence alternates between positive one and negative one."
$a_{n_k}\to L_1$ "A subsequence converges to L one."
$L_1\neq L_2$ "Different subsequential limits prove the original sequence cannot converge."

The verb is diverge, the adjective is divergent, and the noun is divergence. Statements such as $a_n\to\infty$ describe a particular type of divergent behavior in the extended-real sense.

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish failure to converge from growth to infinity, and distinguish divergence of the sequence of terms from divergence of a series formed from those terms.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: A divergent sequence has no finite limit.
  • Unbounded Case: Sequences such as $a_n=n$ diverge by growing without bound.
  • Oscillatory Case: Sequences such as $(-1)^n$ diverge while remaining bounded.
  • Boundedness: Boundedness alone does not guarantee convergence.
  • Unboundedness: Every unbounded real sequence is divergent.
  • Subsequences: Two subsequences with distinct limits prove divergence.
  • Monotone Sequences: An unbounded monotone real sequence diverges in the ordinary finite-limit sense.
  • Numerical Methods: An iterative algorithm may be described as divergent when its approximations fail to approach a stable solution.
  • Dynamical Systems: Generated state sequences may diverge, oscillate, or exhibit more complicated nonconvergent behavior.
  • Series Distinction: Convergence of $a_n$ and convergence of $\sum a_n$ are separate questions.
  • Common Error: Assuming every divergent sequence tends to positive or negative infinity.
  • Common Error: Assuming every bounded sequence converges.
  • Common Error: Calling a sequence divergent merely because its first several terms fluctuate.
  • Common Error: Confusing divergent terms with divergent partial sums.
  • Core Interpretation: A divergent sequence fails to settle toward one finite limiting value: $$ \boxed{ \text{unbounded growth} \quad\text{or}\quad \text{persistent oscillation} \quad\text{or}\quad \text{other failure of convergence} }. $$ Divergence therefore describes failure of stabilization rather than any single geometric pattern.
