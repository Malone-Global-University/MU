Mean Value Theorem for Integrals

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

The Mean Value Theorem for Integrals is named for the mean, or average, value of a function over an interval. It establishes that when a function is continuous, this average value is not merely an abstract number: the function must actually attain that value somewhere on the interval.

The theorem is closely related in spirit to the ordinary Mean Value Theorem for derivatives, but the two results concern different kinds of averages. The derivative theorem compares an instantaneous rate of change with an average rate of change, whereas the integral theorem compares one function value with the average value of the function itself.

It is also called the Mean Value Theorem for Definite Integrals, the Integral Mean Value Theorem, or simply the Mean Value Theorem for Integrals. Because the ordinary Mean Value Theorem usually refers to derivatives, including the phrase for Integrals avoids ambiguity.

Operational Nuance

Suppose $f$ is continuous on the closed interval:

$$ [a,b]. $$

Then there exists at least one point:

$$ c\in[a,b] $$

such that:

$$ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = f(c)(b-a) }. $$

Dividing both sides by the length of the interval gives the equivalent form:

$$ \boxed{ f(c) = \frac{1}{b-a} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx }. $$

The expression:

$$ \frac{1}{b-a} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx $$

is called the average value of $f$ on $[a,b]$.

Therefore the theorem can be summarized conceptually as:

$$ \boxed{ \text{A continuous function attains its average value somewhere on the interval.} } $$

For example, consider:

$$ f(x)=x^2 $$

on:

$$ [0,2]. $$

The average value is:

$$ f_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{1}{2-0} \int_0^2 x^2\,dx. $$

Evaluating the integral:

$$ f_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac12 \left[ \frac{x^3}{3} \right]_0^2 $$ $$ = \frac12\left(\frac83\right) $$ $$ = \frac43. $$

The theorem guarantees some $c\in[0,2]$ for which:

$$ f(c)=\frac43. $$

Since:

$$ f(c)=c^2, $$

we solve:

$$ c^2=\frac43. $$

Thus:

$$ c=\frac{2}{\sqrt3}. $$

The negative solution is not in the interval $[0,2]$, so the relevant point is:

$$ \boxed{ c=\frac{2}{\sqrt3} }. $$

Geometrically, the theorem says that the signed area represented by:

$$ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx $$

can be represented as the area of a rectangle whose width is:

$$ b-a $$

and whose signed height is some actually attained function value:

$$ f(c). $$

Hence:

$$ \text{signed accumulation} = \text{average height}\times\text{width}. $$

Common Misconception: The theorem does not say that the required point is the midpoint:

$$ c=\frac{a+b}{2}. $$

It guarantees only that some point $c$ exists where the function takes its average value. That point may or may not be the midpoint.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$f$ continuous on $[a,b]$ "F is continuous on the closed interval from a to b."
$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ "The integral from a to b of f of x d x."
$f(c)(b-a)$ "F of c times b minus a."
$f_{\mathrm{avg}}$ "The average value of f."
$f_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ "The average value of f equals one over b minus a times the integral from a to b of f of x d x."
$f(c)=f_{\mathrm{avg}}$ "F of c equals the average value of f."

The theorem is often written in either of two equivalent forms:

$$ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = f(c)(b-a) $$

or:

$$ f(c) = \frac{1}{b-a} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx. $$

The first form emphasizes the integral as:

$$ \text{height}\times\text{width}, $$

while the second emphasizes the average value of the function.

Defense Notation Advice: Do not confuse:

$$ f_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx $$

with the ordinary arithmetic mean of the endpoint values:

$$ \frac{f(a)+f(b)}{2}. $$

These quantities are generally different.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Average Value of a Function: The average value of a continuous function on $[a,b]$ is: $$ \boxed{ f_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{1}{b-a} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx }. $$ The theorem guarantees at least one $c$ satisfying: $$ f(c)=f_{\mathrm{avg}}. $$
  • Geometric Interpretation: The integral: $$ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx $$ equals the signed area of a rectangle with width $b-a$ and height $f(c)$: $$ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = (b-a)f(c). $$
  • Signed Area: The basic theorem concerns the definite integral, which represents signed accumulation. If $f$ takes negative values, then $f(c)$ may also be negative. The theorem should therefore not automatically be interpreted as a statement about ordinary nonnegative geometric area.
  • Nonnegative Functions: If: $$ f(x)\ge0 $$ on $[a,b]$, then the integral represents ordinary area under the curve, and the theorem gives a literal rectangle of equal area: $$ \text{area} = f(c)(b-a). $$
  • Why Continuity Matters: Continuity ensures that the function actually attains the average value. Without continuity, a function can have an average value that it never assumes.
  • Connection to the Intermediate Value Theorem: If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, the Extreme Value Theorem gives values: $$ m\le f(x)\le M. $$ Integrating gives: $$ m(b-a) \le \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \le M(b-a). $$ Therefore: $$ m \le \frac{1}{b-a} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \le M. $$ Since $f$ is continuous, the Intermediate Value Theorem ensures that it attains this intermediate value at some point $c$.
  • Relationship to the Extreme Value Theorem: The Extreme Value Theorem guarantees that a continuous function on $[a,b]$ has an absolute minimum $m$ and maximum $M$. These bounds trap the average value between: $$ m\le f_{\mathrm{avg}}\le M. $$
  • Relationship to the Ordinary Mean Value Theorem: The ordinary Mean Value Theorem for derivatives states: $$ f'(c) = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}. $$ The Mean Value Theorem for Integrals instead states: $$ f(c) = \frac{1}{b-a} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx. $$ The first matches an instantaneous rate to an average rate; the second matches an instantaneous function value to an average function value.
  • Physical Interpretation: Suppose $T(t)$ gives temperature during a time interval. Then: $$ \frac{1}{b-a} \int_a^b T(t)\,dt $$ is the average temperature over that interval. If $T$ is continuous, the theorem guarantees that at some actual time $c$: $$ T(c)=T_{\mathrm{avg}}. $$
  • Velocity Example: If $v(t)$ is continuous, then: $$ v_{\mathrm{avg,value}} = \frac{1}{b-a} \int_a^b v(t)\,dt. $$ Since the integral of velocity equals displacement, this quantity also equals: $$ \frac{s(b)-s(a)}{b-a}, $$ the average velocity over the interval.
  • Probability Density Functions: Integral averages appear throughout probability and statistics. While the specific probability interpretation depends on weighting and normalization, the same general principle of integrating a quantity and dividing by an interval or total weight underlies many notions of mean.
  • Weighted Mean Value Theorem: A more general version incorporates a weighting function. Under suitable continuity and sign conditions on $g$, there exists $c\in[a,b]$ such that: $$ \int_a^b f(x)g(x)\,dx = f(c)\int_a^b g(x)\,dx. $$ The ordinary theorem results when: $$ g(x)=1. $$
  • No Uniqueness Guarantee: The theorem guarantees: $$ \exists c\in[a,b], $$ but not: $$ \exists!c\in[a,b]. $$ A function may attain its average value at several different points.
  • Constant Functions: If: $$ f(x)=K $$ throughout the interval, then: $$ \frac{1}{b-a} \int_a^b K\,dx = K. $$ Every point in $[a,b]$ therefore satisfies the theorem.
  • Symmetric Functions: In special symmetric situations, the average value may be easy to calculate, but symmetry alone does not generally determine the point $c$ at which that value is attained.
  • Terminological Contrast: The average value of a function is: $$ \frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx. $$ The Mean Value Theorem for Integrals guarantees that a continuous function actually attains this value. The ordinary Mean Value Theorem instead equates a derivative value with an average rate of change.
