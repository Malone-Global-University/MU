Disk Method

Subfield: Calculus & Analysis

Etymology & Roots

The disk method is an integration technique for finding the volume of a solid of revolution by decomposing the solid into thin circular cross sections called disks.

$$ \boxed{ dV=\pi r^2\,d(\text{thickness variable}) } $$

The name comes directly from the geometry of each slice: when a region touches the axis of rotation, rotating a perpendicular strip produces a solid circular disk rather than a washer with a central hole.

Operational Nuance

Cross-Sectional Principle

A disk of radius $r$ has area:

$$ \boxed{ A=\pi r^2 }. $$

A thin disk of thickness $dx$ therefore contributes approximately:

$$ dV=\pi[r(x)]^2\,dx. $$

Rotation about the $x$-Axis

If $y=f(x)\geq0$ on $[a,b]$ and the region between the graph and the $x$-axis is revolved about the $x$-axis, then:

$$ \boxed{ V= \pi\int_a^b[f(x)]^2\,dx }. $$

Here $f(x)$ is the radius of each disk.

Rotation about the $y$-Axis

If the region is naturally described by $x=g(y)$ and is revolved about the $y$-axis, horizontal slices give:

$$ \boxed{ V= \pi\int_c^d[g(y)]^2\,dy }. $$

Shifted Axis of Rotation

If the axis of rotation is $y=k$, the radius is the perpendicular distance from the curve to that axis:

$$ \boxed{ r(x)=|f(x)-k| }. $$

Because the radius is squared in the disk formula, the sign of the distance does not affect the cross-sectional area.

Example

Revolve the region under $y=x$ from $x=0$ to $x=2$ about the $x$-axis:

$$ V= \pi\int_0^2x^2\,dx = \pi\left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^2 = \boxed{\frac{8\pi}{3}}. $$

Disk vs. Washer

A disk has no central hole. If rotation produces both an outer radius $R$ and a positive inner radius $r$, the appropriate cross section is a washer:

$$ \boxed{ A_{\text{washer}} = \pi(R^2-r^2) }. $$

The disk method is therefore the special washer case with inner radius zero.

Disk vs. Shell Method

Disk and washer methods use slices perpendicular to the axis of rotation. The cylindrical shell method instead uses slices parallel to the axis.

$$ \boxed{ \text{disks: perpendicular slices} \qquad \text{shells: parallel slices} }. $$

Common Misconception

The radius is not automatically the function value. It is the distance from the representative strip to the axis of rotation. A shifted axis therefore changes the radius expression.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$A=\pi r^2$ "The cross-sectional area is pi times the radius squared."
$V=\pi\int_a^b[r(x)]^2\,dx$ "Integrate the disk areas from a to b."
$r(x)=|f(x)-k|$ "The radius is the distance from the curve to the axis y equals k."
$dx$ "The thickness of a vertical disk."
$dy$ "The thickness of a horizontal disk."
$r=0$ inner radius "The cross section is a disk rather than a washer."

The terms disk method and method of disks are interchangeable. Some textbooks present disks as a special case of the more general washer method.

Defense Notation Advice: Identify the axis of rotation before writing the radius. Then choose slices perpendicular to that axis and square the entire radius expression inside the integral.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Formula: $V=\pi\int r^2\,d(\text{thickness variable})$.
  • Solid of Revolution: The method applies when rotation produces solid circular cross sections.
  • Radius: Always represents perpendicular distance to the axis of rotation.
  • Vertical Slices: Produce disks when they are perpendicular to a horizontal axis.
  • Horizontal Slices: Produce disks when they are perpendicular to a vertical axis.
  • Shifted Axis: The radius must be expressed relative to the shifted line rather than to a coordinate axis automatically.
  • Washer Connection: A disk is a washer whose inner radius is zero.
  • Shell Contrast: Shells use parallel slices and circumference-based volume elements.
  • Cross-Sectional View: The method is an application of the general volume formula $V=\int A(s)\,ds$.
  • Common Error: Forgetting to square the radius.
  • Common Error: Forgetting the factor $\pi$.
  • Common Error: Using $f(x)$ as the radius when the axis of rotation is shifted.
  • Common Error: Applying the disk method when the cross section has a nonzero inner radius.
  • Common Error: Choosing slices parallel to the axis while attempting to use a disk formula.
  • Core Interpretation: The disk method constructs a volume from infinitesimally thin circular cross sections: $$ \boxed{ dV = (\text{disk area})(\text{thickness}) = \pi r^2\,ds }. $$ It translates the geometry of rotation into a one-variable definite integral.
