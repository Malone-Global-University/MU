Derivative of a Power Series

Subfield: Calculus & Analysis

Etymology & Roots

The derivative of a power series is obtained, within the series' interval of convergence, by differentiating its terms one at a time. If

$$ f(x)= \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n(x-c)^n, $$

then inside its radius of convergence:

$$ \boxed{ f'(x)= \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n a_n(x-c)^{n-1} }. $$

The terminology reflects the special stability of power series under differentiation: an infinite sum behaves locally much like a polynomial while remaining within its convergence region.

Operational Nuance

Term-by-Term Differentiation

Each power-series term is differentiated using the ordinary power rule:

$$ \frac{d}{dx} \left[ a_n(x-c)^n \right] = n a_n(x-c)^{n-1}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_n(x-c)^n = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n a_n(x-c)^{n-1} } $$

for every $x$ strictly inside the original radius of convergence.

Radius of Convergence

The differentiated power series has the same radius of convergence $R$ as the original power series:

$$ \boxed{ |x-c|<R }. $$

This does not mean the two series necessarily behave the same way at the endpoints $x=c-R$ and $x=c+R$.

Endpoints Must Be Retested

Differentiation can change endpoint convergence. After differentiating, any finite boundary points of the interval of convergence must be tested separately.

Example: Geometric Series

Starting with:

$$ \frac{1}{1-x} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^n, \qquad |x|<1, $$

differentiation gives:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{1}{(1-x)^2} = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n x^{n-1}, \qquad |x|<1 }. $$

Repeated Differentiation

A power series may be differentiated repeatedly inside its radius of convergence. For an integer $k\geq1$:

$$ \boxed{ f^{(k)}(x) = \sum_{n=k}^{\infty} \frac{n!}{(n-k)!} a_n(x-c)^{n-k} }. $$

Recovering Coefficients

Evaluating repeated derivatives at the center $x=c$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ f^{(n)}(c)=n!a_n } $$

and therefore:

$$ \boxed{ a_n= \frac{f^{(n)}(c)}{n!} }. $$

This relationship is the basis of Taylor-series coefficients.

Analytic Behavior

A function represented locally by a convergent power series is analytic there. Within the convergence interval, it possesses derivatives of every order, obtained by repeated term-by-term differentiation.

Common Misconception

One may not automatically differentiate an arbitrary infinite series term by term. Power series have special convergence properties that justify this operation inside their radius of convergence.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_n(x-c)^n$ "A power series centered at c."
$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}na_n(x-c)^{n-1}$ "The term-by-term derivative of the power series."
$|x-c|<R$ "Inside the radius of convergence."
$f^{(n)}(c)=n!a_n$ "The nth derivative at the center equals n factorial times the nth coefficient."
$a_n=f^{(n)}(c)/n!$ "The nth coefficient is the nth derivative at the center divided by n factorial."
$f^{(k)}(x)$ "The kth derivative of the function."

The phrases differentiate a power series term by term and termwise differentiation describe the same operation.

Defense Notation Advice: Preserve the center $(x-c)$ while differentiating, begin the derivative sum at $n=1$, and distinguish the unchanged radius of convergence from endpoint behavior, which must be reconsidered separately.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Rule: Differentiate each term using the ordinary power rule.
  • Interior Validity: Term-by-term differentiation is valid throughout the open interval determined by the radius of convergence.
  • Radius: The derivative series has the same radius of convergence as the original series.
  • Endpoints: Endpoint convergence can change and must be tested again.
  • Repeated Derivatives: Power series can be differentiated term by term any finite number of times inside the radius.
  • Taylor Series: The identity $a_n=f^{(n)}(c)/n!$ connects power-series coefficients with derivatives.
  • Geometric Series: Differentiating known geometric expansions generates many useful new series identities.
  • Differential Equations: Power-series solutions are differentiated term by term and substituted into differential equations.
  • Analysis: Power-series representations imply smoothness and analyticity within the radius of convergence.
  • Common Error: Starting the differentiated sum at $n=0$ without accounting for the vanished constant term.
  • Common Error: Assuming the derivative series has exactly the same endpoint convergence as the original.
  • Common Error: Differentiating an arbitrary infinite series term by term without sufficient justification.
  • Common Error: Forgetting the factor $n$ or failing to reduce the exponent by one.
  • Core Interpretation: Inside its radius of convergence, a power series differentiates like an infinite polynomial: $$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n(x-c)^n \quad\xrightarrow{\ d/dx\ }\quad \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n a_n(x-c)^{n-1} }. $$ This stability under differentiation is one of the central reasons power series are so useful in calculus, analysis, approximation, and differential equations.
