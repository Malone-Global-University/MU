Area between Curves

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

Area between curves is the geometric area of a region bounded by two or more curves. In elementary calculus, this usually means finding the area between two function graphs over a specified interval.

If:

$$ y=f(x) $$

lies above:

$$ y=g(x) $$

throughout $[a,b]$, then the area between them is:

$$ \boxed{ A= \int_a^b \bigl(f(x)-g(x)\bigr)\,dx }. $$

The formula expresses a basic geometric principle:

$$ \boxed{ \text{area} = \int \text{distance between boundaries} }. $$

The subtraction order matters. For vertical slices, use top minus bottom. For horizontal slices, use right minus left.

Operational Nuance

Vertical Slices

Suppose the upper boundary is:

$$ y=f(x) $$

and the lower boundary is:

$$ y=g(x). $$

A thin vertical rectangle has width:

$$ dx $$

and height:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)-g(x) }. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ dA= [f(x)-g(x)]\,dx }. $$

Top Minus Bottom

The standard vertical-slice rule is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{height} = \text{top} - \text{bottom} }. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ A= \int_a^b (\text{top}-\text{bottom})\,dx }. $$

Why the Difference Gives Area

At each value of $x$, the vertical distance between the two curves is:

$$ f(x)-g(x). $$

Multiplying by the infinitesimal width $dx$ gives a thin strip of area:

$$ [f(x)-g(x)]\,dx. $$

Integration accumulates all of these strips.

Horizontal Slices

Some regions are more naturally described in terms of $y$.

Suppose:

$$ x=R(y) $$

is the right boundary and:

$$ x=L(y) $$

is the left boundary.

A horizontal strip has width:

$$ \boxed{ R(y)-L(y) } $$

and thickness:

$$ dy. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ A= \int_c^d [R(y)-L(y)]\,dy }. $$

Right Minus Left

$$ \boxed{ A= \int (\text{right}-\text{left})\,dy }. $$

Finding the Bounds

The integration bounds are often found from intersection points.

If:

$$ f(x)=g(x), $$

solving this equation identifies the $x$-coordinates where the curves meet.

Example

Consider:

$$ y=x $$

and:

$$ y=x^2. $$

Their intersections satisfy:

$$ x=x^2. $$

Thus:

$$ x(x-1)=0, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ x=0,\qquad x=1 }. $$

On $[0,1]$:

$$ x\ge x^2. $$

Therefore:

$$ A= \int_0^1 (x-x^2)\,dx. $$

Evaluating:

$$ A= \left[ \frac{x^2}{2} - \frac{x^3}{3} \right]_0^1 = \boxed{ \frac16 }. $$

Curves That Cross

The expression:

$$ \int_a^b[f(x)-g(x)]\,dx $$

gives geometric area only if $f(x)\ge g(x)$ throughout the interval.

If the curves exchange positions, the interval should be split at the crossing points.

Absolute-Difference Form

In compact form, the total geometric area between two curves may be expressed as:

$$ \boxed{ A= \int_a^b |f(x)-g(x)|\,dx }. $$

In actual calculations, splitting at intersections often produces simpler integrals than integrating an absolute-value expression directly.

Piecewise Setup

Suppose $f$ lies above $g$ on $[a,c]$ but $g$ lies above $f$ on $[c,b]$. Then:

$$ \boxed{ A= \int_a^c[f(x)-g(x)]\,dx + \int_c^b[g(x)-f(x)]\,dx }. $$

Curves May Both Be Below the x-Axis

The method does not depend on either curve being above the $x$-axis.

For example, if:

$$ f(x)=-2 \qquad\text{and}\qquad g(x)=-5, $$

then:

$$ f(x)-g(x) = -2-(-5) = 3. $$

The vertical distance between them is still positive.

Area between a Curve and the x-Axis

The ordinary area-under-a-curve formula is a special case with:

$$ g(x)=0. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ A= \int_a^b [f(x)-0]\,dx } $$

whenever $f(x)\ge0$.

Area between a Curve and a Horizontal Line

If the lower boundary is:

$$ y=c, $$

and $f(x)\ge c$, then:

$$ \boxed{ A= \int_a^b [f(x)-c]\,dx }. $$

Area between a Curve and a Vertical Line

When the natural slices are horizontal, a vertical line can serve as the left or right boundary.

For example, if:

$$ x=g(y) $$

lies to the right of:

$$ x=c, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ A= \int [g(y)-c]\,dy }. $$

Choosing dx or dy

A region may often be evaluated using either vertical or horizontal slices. The better choice is usually the one requiring fewer pieces or simpler boundary equations.

$$ \boxed{ \text{choose the slicing direction that simplifies the region} }. $$

Multiple Curves

A region bounded by three or more curves may require different formulas on different subintervals.

The essential rule remains:

$$ \boxed{ \text{identify the two boundaries touched by each slice} }. $$

Riemann-Sum Interpretation

For vertical slices, the region can be approximated by rectangles:

$$ \sum_i [f(x_i^*)-g(x_i^*)] \Delta x_i. $$

Taking the limit gives:

$$ \boxed{ A= \lim_{\|P\|\to0} \sum_i [f(x_i^*)-g(x_i^*)] \Delta x_i }. $$

Improper Regions

The boundaries need not enclose a finite horizontal interval. An unbounded region may still have finite area if the corresponding improper integral converges.

For example, the area between:

$$ y=\frac1{x^2} $$

and:

$$ y=0 $$

for $x\ge1$ is:

$$ \int_1^\infty\frac1{x^2}\,dx = \boxed{1}. $$

Polar Curves

Area between curves also appears in polar coordinates. If:

$$ r=R(\theta) $$

is the outer curve and:

$$ r=r(\theta) $$

is the inner curve, then:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \int_\alpha^\beta \left( R(\theta)^2-r(\theta)^2 \right)\,d\theta }. $$

This is the polar analogue of subtracting the inner boundary from the outer one.

Why Polar Area Uses Squares

A sector of radius $r$ and infinitesimal angle $d\theta$ has approximate area:

$$ dA=\frac12r^2\,d\theta. $$

Therefore an area between polar curves involves:

$$ \boxed{ \frac12 (R^2-r^2)\,d\theta }. $$

Common Misconception: Area between curves is not generally found by integrating each function and subtracting without first determining which function is above the other. The integrand must represent a nonnegative geometric distance between the relevant boundaries on each portion of the region.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$A=\int_a^b[f(x)-g(x)]\,dx$ "Area equals the integral of the upper function minus the lower function."
$A=\int_a^b|f(x)-g(x)|\,dx$ "Area equals the integral of the absolute difference between the functions."
$dA=[f(x)-g(x)]\,dx$ "D A equals top minus bottom times d x."
$A=\int_c^d[R(y)-L(y)]\,dy$ "Area equals the integral of right minus left with respect to y."
$f(x)=g(x)$ "Set the curves equal to find their intersections."
$\frac12\int(R^2-r^2)\,d\theta$ "One-half the integral of outer radius squared minus inner radius squared."

The phrases upper function and lower function refer to geometric position on the relevant interval, not to which formula is written first in a problem.

Likewise, right function and left function refer to the horizontal boundaries encountered by a horizontal slice.

Defense Notation Advice: Before writing an integral, state the slice orientation and identify the two boundaries it touches. Then use top minus bottom for $dx$ or right minus left for $dy$.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: Area between curves is the geometric area enclosed between two or more graph boundaries.
  • Vertical-Slice Formula: $$ \boxed{ A= \int_a^b [f(x)-g(x)]\,dx } $$ when $f(x)\ge g(x)$.
  • Vertical Rule: $$ \boxed{ \text{top}-\text{bottom} }. $$
  • Horizontal-Slice Formula: $$ \boxed{ A= \int_c^d [R(y)-L(y)]\,dy }. $$
  • Horizontal Rule: $$ \boxed{ \text{right}-\text{left} }. $$
  • Differential Area: $$ \boxed{ dA= (\text{boundary distance}) (\text{slice thickness}) }. $$
  • Intersection Points: Solve: $$ \boxed{ f(x)=g(x) } $$ to locate possible integration bounds and boundary changes.
  • Absolute-Difference Formula: $$ \boxed{ A= \int_a^b |f(x)-g(x)|\,dx }. $$
  • Piecewise Requirement: Split the integral whenever the curves exchange upper and lower positions.
  • Both Curves Positive: The formula remains top minus bottom.
  • Both Curves Negative: The formula still remains top minus bottom.
  • Crossing the x-Axis: A curve crossing the $x$-axis does not itself force a split if the relative ordering of the two boundary curves remains unchanged.
  • Crossing Each Other: Curves crossing each other usually requires splitting the area computation.
  • Area below One Curve: Set: $$ g(x)=0 $$ to recover the ordinary area-under-a-curve case.
  • Horizontal-Line Boundary: If $f(x)\ge c$: $$ \boxed{ A= \int_a^b [f(x)-c]\,dx }. $$
  • Vertical-Line Boundary: With horizontal slices: $$ \boxed{ A= \int [x_{\text{right}}-x_{\text{left}}]\,dy }. $$
  • Choose dx: Use vertical slices when the upper and lower boundaries are naturally functions of $x$.
  • Choose dy: Use horizontal slices when the right and left boundaries are more naturally functions of $y$.
  • Efficiency: The best integration direction is usually the one requiring fewer pieces and simpler expressions.
  • Multiple Boundaries: A region bounded by several curves may require different formulas on different intervals.
  • Riemann-Sum Interpretation: $$ \boxed{ \sum_i [f(x_i^*)-g(x_i^*)]\Delta x_i } $$ approximates the area with vertical rectangles.
  • Limit Interpretation: $$ \boxed{ A= \lim_{\|P\|\to0} \sum_i [f(x_i^*)-g(x_i^*)]\Delta x_i }. $$
  • Geometric Nonnegativity: Area satisfies: $$ \boxed{ A\ge0 }. $$
  • Signed Integral Contrast: Integrating $f-g$ in the wrong order can produce a negative number, but geometric area itself is never negative.
  • Improper Regions: Areas extending indefinitely are evaluated using improper integrals.
  • Finite Infinite Region: An unbounded region can have finite total area if the improper integral converges.
  • Polar Area between Curves: $$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \int_\alpha^\beta [R(\theta)^2-r(\theta)^2]\,d\theta }. $$
  • Polar Rule: $$ \boxed{ \text{outer radius squared} - \text{inner radius squared} }. $$
  • Probability Connection: Differences between cumulative areas under density curves can represent probabilities over intervals, though probability densities themselves are nonnegative.
  • Economics: Areas between supply and demand curves can represent surplus measures under appropriate economic conventions.
  • Physics: Areas between two rate or state curves can measure accumulated differences when the axis units make such an interpretation meaningful.
  • Numerical Integration: If exact antiderivatives are unavailable, the area can be approximated using numerical integration methods.
  • Units: If both axes measure length, the result is measured in square units.
  • Dimensional Interpretation: When the axes represent different physical quantities, the integral has units equal to the product of the vertical and horizontal units rather than necessarily physical geometric area.
  • Terminological Contrast: Area under a curve commonly compares one function with the $x$-axis. Area between curves compares two or more boundaries. A definite integral may be signed, whereas geometric area is nonnegative and requires the boundary difference to be taken in the correct order.
