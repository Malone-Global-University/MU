Convergent Series

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

A convergent series is an infinite sum whose sequence of partial sums approaches a finite limit.

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n=S } $$

means that:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{N\to\infty} \sum_{n=1}^{N}a_n = S }. $$

The word series refers to an ordered sum of terms, while convergent indicates that the associated partial sums approach a definite finite value.

Thus, convergence of a series is not defined by asking whether the individual terms approach a number other than zero. It is defined by asking whether the accumulated sums approach a finite limit.

$$ \boxed{ \text{series convergence} = \text{convergence of the partial-sum sequence} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Infinite Series

An infinite series is written:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n = a_1+a_2+a_3+\cdots. $$

This notation represents a limiting process rather than a completed finite addition.

Partial Sums

The $N$th partial sum is:

$$ \boxed{ S_N = \sum_{n=1}^{N}a_n }. $$

Thus:

$$ S_1=a_1, $$
$$ S_2=a_1+a_2, $$
$$ S_3=a_1+a_2+a_3, $$

and so on.

Definition of Convergence

The series converges to $S$ if:

$$ \boxed{ S_N\to S }. $$

Equivalently:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{N\to\infty}S_N=S }. $$

Epsilon Definition

A series converges to $S$ if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists an integer $N$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ m\ge N \Rightarrow |S_m-S|<\varepsilon }. $$

This is simply the ordinary sequence definition applied to the sequence of partial sums.

Example: Geometric Series

Consider:

$$ 1+\frac12+\frac14+\frac18+\cdots. $$

This is:

$$ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac12\right)^n. $$

Its partial sums are:

$$ S_N = \frac{1-(1/2)^{N+1}}{1-1/2}. $$

Hence:

$$ S_N = 2\left( 1-\frac1{2^{N+1}} \right). $$

As $N\to\infty$:

$$ \boxed{ S_N\to2 }. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \left(\frac12\right)^n = 2 }. $$

Geometric-Series Criterion

A geometric series:

$$ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}ar^n $$

converges exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ |r|<1 }. $$

Its sum is then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{a}{1-r} }. $$

Divergent Geometric Series

If:

$$ |r|\ge1, $$

the ordinary infinite geometric series does not converge to a finite sum, except in trivial degenerate cases such as $a=0$.

Necessary Condition on the Terms

If:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n $$

converges, then necessarily:

$$ \boxed{ a_n\to0 }. $$

Why Terms Must Approach Zero

Since:

$$ a_n=S_n-S_{n-1}, $$

and both partial sums approach the same limit $S$, their difference must approach zero.

Term Limit Zero Is Not Sufficient

The converse fails:

$$ \boxed{ a_n\to0 \not\Rightarrow \sum a_n\text{ converges} }. $$

Harmonic Series

The classic counterexample is:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1n. $$

Although:

$$ \frac1n\to0, $$

the series diverges.

p-Series

A $p$-series has form:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{n^p}. $$

It converges exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ p>1 }. $$

It diverges when:

$$ \boxed{ p\le1 }. $$

Absolute Convergence

A series:

$$ \sum a_n $$

is absolutely convergent if:

$$ \boxed{ \sum|a_n| \text{ converges} }. $$

Absolute Convergence Implies Convergence

For real or complex series:

$$ \boxed{ \sum|a_n| \text{ convergent} \Rightarrow \sum a_n \text{ convergent} }. $$

Conditional Convergence

A convergent series is conditionally convergent if:

$$ \boxed{ \sum a_n \text{ converges} \quad\text{but}\quad \sum|a_n| \text{ diverges} }. $$

Alternating Harmonic Series

The standard example is:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}. $$

This converges, while:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1n $$

diverges.

Therefore the alternating harmonic series converges conditionally.

Classification of Convergent Scalar Series

A convergent real or complex series is either:

  • absolutely convergent, or
  • conditionally convergent.
$$ \boxed{ \text{convergent} = \text{absolute} \cup \text{conditional} }. $$

Positive-Term Series

If:

$$ a_n\ge0, $$

then:

$$ |a_n|=a_n. $$

Therefore any convergent positive-term series is automatically absolutely convergent.

Series versus Sequence

The sequence:

$$ (a_n) $$

asks what happens to the individual terms.

The series:

$$ \sum a_n $$

asks what happens to their accumulated partial sums.

Convergent Sequence Does Not Imply Convergent Series

The sequence:

$$ a_n=\frac1n $$

converges to $0$, but:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1n $$

diverges.

Partial-Sum Sequence

Every series determines a sequence:

$$ S_1,S_2,S_3,\ldots. $$

The original series is convergent exactly when this derived sequence is convergent.

Cauchy Criterion for Series

A real or complex series converges if and only if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists $N$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ m>n\ge N \Rightarrow \left| \sum_{k=n+1}^{m}a_k \right| <\varepsilon }. $$

This is the Cauchy criterion applied to the sequence of partial sums.

Tail of a Convergent Series

If:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n=S, $$

then the remainder after $N$ terms is:

$$ \boxed{ R_N = S-S_N = \sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}a_n }. $$

Remainder Tends to Zero

For every convergent series:

$$ \boxed{ R_N\to0 }. $$

Approximation by Partial Sums

A convergent series can be approximated by finite sums:

$$ S\approx S_N $$

when the remainder $R_N$ is sufficiently small.

Convergence Rate

Two convergent series may require very different numbers of terms to achieve the same numerical accuracy.

Convergence therefore describes the existence of a limit, not the speed at which partial sums approach it.

Slowly Convergent Series

A series may converge extremely slowly even though its limit exists.

Thus classification as convergent does not by itself guarantee efficient numerical approximation.

Convergence Tests

Common methods for proving series convergence include:

  • geometric-series recognition,
  • $p$-series comparison,
  • direct comparison,
  • limit comparison,
  • Integral Test,
  • Ratio Test,
  • Root Test,
  • Alternating Series Test,
  • Dirichlet's Test,
  • Abel's Test, and
  • Cauchy condensation.

Tests Usually Classify Rather than Sum

A test may prove:

$$ \sum a_n \text{ converges} $$

without identifying the exact numerical value of the sum.

Direct Comparison

If:

$$ 0\le a_n\le b_n $$

eventually and $\sum b_n$ converges, then:

$$ \boxed{ \sum a_n \text{ converges} }. $$

Limit Comparison

If $a_n,b_n>0$ eventually and:

$$ \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{a_n}{b_n} = L, \qquad 0

then $\sum a_n$ and $\sum b_n$ have the same convergence behavior.

Ratio Test

If:

$$ L= \lim_{n\to\infty} \left| \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} \right| <1, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \sum a_n \text{ converges absolutely} }. $$

Root Test

If:

$$ L= \limsup_{n\to\infty} \sqrt[n]{|a_n|} <1, $$

then the series converges absolutely.

Alternating Series

A series:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n+1}b_n $$

converges if $b_n$ decreases eventually and:

$$ \boxed{ b_n\to0 }. $$

Alternating-Series Remainder

Under the usual hypotheses:

$$ \boxed{ |S-S_N| \le b_{N+1} }. $$

Integral-Test Remainder

If $a_n=f(n)$ and the Integral Test hypotheses apply, then:

$$ \boxed{ \int_{N+1}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx \le R_N \le \int_N^\infty f(x)\,dx }. $$

Telescoping Series

Some series converge because their partial sums simplify through cancellation.

For example:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left( \frac1n-\frac1{n+1} \right). $$

The partial sums satisfy:

$$ S_N = 1-\frac1{N+1}, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ S_N\to1 }. $$

Finite Initial Changes

Adding, removing, or changing finitely many terms does not change whether a series converges.

$$ \boxed{ \text{series convergence is a tail property} }. $$

Finite Changes Can Change the Sum

Although finite alterations do not change convergence classification, they generally change the numerical value of the sum by the corresponding finite amount.

Scalar Multiplication

If:

$$ \sum a_n=A, $$

then for any finite scalar $c$:

$$ \boxed{ \sum ca_n=cA }. $$

Sum of Convergent Series

If:

$$ \sum a_n=A \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sum b_n=B, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \sum(a_n+b_n)=A+B }. $$

Difference of Convergent Series

$$ \boxed{ \sum(a_n-b_n)=A-B }. $$

Rearrangement of Absolutely Convergent Series

If a scalar series converges absolutely, any rearrangement of its terms converges to the same sum.

$$ \boxed{ \text{absolute convergence} \Rightarrow \text{rearrangement invariance} }. $$

Conditional Convergence and Rearrangement

A conditionally convergent real series behaves differently. Suitable rearrangements can alter the sum or produce divergence.

Grouping Consecutive Terms

Regrouping a convergent series into finite consecutive blocks without changing the order preserves its sum.

Infinite Reordering Is Different from Grouping

Regrouping consecutive terms preserves their relative order. Arbitrary rearrangement may change infinitely many relative positions and is a more delicate operation.

Power Series

A power series has form:

$$ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_n(x-c)^n. $$

There exists a radius of convergence $R$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ |x-c|

Outside the Radius

$$ \boxed{ |x-c|>R \Rightarrow \text{divergence} }. $$

Endpoints

At:

$$ |x-c|=R, $$

a power series may converge absolutely, converge conditionally, or diverge.

Convergent Fourier and Function Series

In more advanced analysis, a series may consist of functions:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f_n(x). $$

Convergence can then be interpreted pointwise, uniformly, in norm, or under another specified mode.

Pointwise Series Convergence

A function series converges pointwise to $f$ if:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f_n(x) = f(x) } $$

for each fixed $x$ where the corresponding numerical series converges.

Uniform Series Convergence

Uniform convergence requires the partial-sum functions:

$$ S_N(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_n(x) $$

to converge uniformly to a limiting function.

Weierstrass M-Test

If:

$$ |f_n(x)|\le M_n $$

for all $x$ in the domain and:

$$ \sum M_n $$

converges, then:

$$ \boxed{ \sum f_n(x) \text{ converges uniformly and absolutely} }. $$

Vector and Banach-Space Series

In a normed vector space, one may consider:

$$ \sum x_n. $$

The series converges if the sequence of partial sums converges in the norm or other specified topology.

Absolute Convergence in a Banach Space

If:

$$ \sum\|x_n\|<\infty $$

and the space is Banach, then:

$$ \boxed{ \sum x_n \text{ converges} }. $$

Infinite Sums Depend on Order

For absolutely convergent scalar series, order is harmless. For conditionally convergent series, order may matter profoundly.

Thus infinite addition is subtler than finite addition.

Convergent Does Not Mean Finite Number of Nonzero Terms

A convergent series may contain infinitely many nonzero terms.

For example:

$$ 1+\frac12+\frac14+\cdots=2. $$

Convergent Does Not Mean the Partial Sums Become Constant

Partial sums may continue changing forever while approaching their limit.

Convergent Does Not Mean Positive Terms Only

A convergent series may contain positive, negative, alternating, or complex terms.

Convergent Does Not Mean Absolutely Convergent

Conditional convergence shows that a series can converge without the absolute-value series converging.

Convergent Does Not Mean Rapidly Convergent

The existence of a finite sum says nothing by itself about how quickly partial sums approximate that sum.

Common Misconception: If Terms Go to Zero, the Series Converges

Termwise convergence to zero is necessary but not sufficient.

Common Misconception: A Series Is an Infinite Sequence

A sequence lists terms. A series sums them. The partial sums form a new sequence whose convergence determines the convergence of the series.

Common Misconception: Convergence Means We Literally Finish Infinite Addition

The infinite sum is defined through the limit of finite partial sums.

Common Misconception: Every Convergent Series Converges Absolutely

Conditionally convergent series are counterexamples.

Common Misconception: Rearranging Terms Never Changes an Infinite Sum

Arbitrary rearrangement preserves the sum of absolutely convergent scalar series, but may alter conditionally convergent ones.

Common Misconception: Changing the First Few Terms Changes Convergence

Finite changes alter the sum but not the convergence classification.

Common Misconception: A Convergence Test Must Produce the Sum

Many tests prove existence of a finite sum without evaluating it.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n=S$ "The series converges to S."
$S_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_n$ "S sub N is the Nth partial sum."
$S_N\to S$ "The partial sums converge to S."
$a_n\to0$ "The terms approach zero, a necessary condition for series convergence."
$\sum|a_n|<\infty$ "The series converges absolutely."
$R_N=S-S_N$ "R sub N is the remainder after N terms."

The phrase convergent series means that the partial-sum sequence has a finite limit. Saying that “the terms converge” is not equivalent to saying that “the series converges.”

The notation:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n=S $$

is shorthand for the limiting statement:

$$ \lim_{N\to\infty} \sum_{n=1}^{N}a_n=S. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish the term sequence $(a_n)$ from the partial-sum sequence $(S_N)$. Never use $a_n\to0$ alone as proof that $\sum a_n$ converges. Specify whether convergence is absolute or conditional when that distinction matters. Treat infinite rearrangements cautiously unless absolute convergence has been established. When approximating a convergent series by a partial sum, identify or bound the remainder whenever accuracy matters.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: A convergent series is an infinite series whose partial sums converge to a finite limit.
  • Standard Notation: $$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n=S }. $$
  • Partial Sum: $S_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_n$.
  • Convergence Criterion: $S_N\to S$.
  • Necessary Term Condition: Convergence implies $a_n\to0$.
  • Converse Warning: $a_n\to0$ does not imply series convergence.
  • Geometric Series: $\sum ar^n$ converges when $|r|<1$.
  • p-Series: $\sum1/n^p$ converges when $p>1$.
  • Absolute Convergence: $\sum|a_n|$ converges.
  • Conditional Convergence: $\sum a_n$ converges but $\sum|a_n|$ diverges.
  • Remainder: $R_N=S-S_N$ and $R_N\to0$.
  • Approximation: Finite partial sums approximate the infinite sum.
  • Cauchy Criterion: Tails of a convergent series become arbitrarily small in aggregate.
  • Comparison Tests: Benchmark series can establish convergence or divergence.
  • Ratio and Root Tests: Commonly establish absolute convergence.
  • Alternating Series: Controlled sign alternation may produce convergence.
  • Telescoping Series: Convergence may follow from simplification of partial sums.
  • Power Series: Converge absolutely inside their radius of convergence.
  • Function Series: May converge pointwise, uniformly, in norm, or under another stated mode.
  • Banach-Space Series: Convergence is defined through convergence of partial sums in the chosen norm.
  • Finite Changes: Altering finitely many terms does not change convergence classification.
  • Absolute Rearrangement: Absolutely convergent scalar series may be rearranged without changing their sum.
  • Conditional Rearrangement: Rearranging a conditionally convergent real series may change its sum.
  • Sequence Contrast: A sequence converges when its terms approach a limit; a series converges when its partial sums approach a limit.
  • Divergent Series Contrast: A divergent series has no finite ordinary limit of partial sums.
  • Common Error: Do not infer convergence merely because $a_n\to0$.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse the sequence of terms with the sequence of partial sums.
  • Common Error: Do not assume every convergent series is absolutely convergent.
  • Common Error: Do not treat arbitrary infinite rearrangement as harmless without sufficient convergence conditions.
  • Common Error: Do not assume convergence determines how quickly partial sums approach the sum.
  • Terminological Contrast: A series is an ordered sum of terms. A convergent series is one whose partial sums approach a finite limit. A divergent series fails to have such a limit. A convergent sequence concerns the terms themselves rather than their accumulated sums. An absolutely convergent series remains convergent after absolute values are applied termwise. A conditionally convergent series converges in its original form but not absolutely. A partial sum is a finite approximation to the infinite sum, while the remainder measures the difference between that approximation and the limiting value.
