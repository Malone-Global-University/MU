Continuous

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

In mathematics, continuous most commonly describes a function whose output changes without a break at the point or region under consideration. More formally, a function $f$ is continuous at a point $a$ when the value of the function agrees with the limit approached by nearby function values.

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a) }. $$

The word comes from roots associated with holding together or proceeding without interruption. This ordinary-language intuition helps motivate the mathematical term, but the formal definition is expressed through limits rather than merely through the visual appearance of a graph.

The word continuous also appears more broadly in mathematics to describe continuously varying quantities, continuous random variables, continuous domains, continuous transformations, and continuous maps in topology.

$$ \boxed{ \text{continuous} \approx \text{preserving sufficiently small changes without abrupt separation} } $$

though the exact technical meaning depends on the mathematical structure being studied.

Operational Nuance

Continuity at a Point

A real-valued function $f$ is continuous at $x=a$ when three conditions hold:

  • $f(a)$ is defined,
  • $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)$ exists, and
  • $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a)$.
$$ \boxed{ f\text{ continuous at }a \iff \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a) }. $$

Why All Three Conditions Matter

A limit can exist even when the function is not defined at the point. Likewise, the function can be defined at the point even when nearby values approach something different.

Continuity requires the local behavior and the actual function value to agree.

Example

Consider:

$$ f(x)=x^2. $$

At $x=3$:

$$ f(3)=9, $$

and:

$$ \lim_{x\to3}x^2=9. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ f\text{ is continuous at }x=3 }. $$

Continuity on a Set

A function is continuous on a set if it is continuous at every point of that set, with appropriate one-sided interpretations at interval endpoints when relevant.

$$ \boxed{ f\text{ continuous on }D \iff f\text{ is continuous at every }a\in D }. $$

Continuity on an Open Interval

A function is continuous on $(a,b)$ if it is continuous at every point:

$$ x\in(a,b). $$

Continuity on a Closed Interval

A function is continuous on $[a,b]$ when it is continuous at every interior point, right-continuous at $a$, and left-continuous at $b$.

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)=f(a), \qquad \lim_{x\to b^-}f(x)=f(b) }. $$

Left Continuity

A function is left-continuous at $a$ when:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a^-}f(x)=f(a) }. $$

Right Continuity

A function is right-continuous at $a$ when:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)=f(a) }. $$

Two-Sided Continuity

For an interior point of the domain, ordinary continuity requires agreement from both sides:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a^-}f(x) = \lim_{x\to a^+}f(x) = f(a) }. $$

Epsilon-Delta Definition

A function $f$ is continuous at $a$ if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists a $\delta>0$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ |x-a|<\delta \Rightarrow |f(x)-f(a)|<\varepsilon }. $$

In a domain-sensitive formulation, $x$ is taken from the domain of $f$.

Meaning of Epsilon and Delta

The quantity $\varepsilon$ specifies how close the outputs must be:

$$ |f(x)-f(a)|<\varepsilon. $$

The quantity $\delta$ specifies how close the inputs must be:

$$ |x-a|<\delta. $$

Continuity means that arbitrarily tight output control can be guaranteed by taking the input sufficiently close to $a$.

Sequence Characterization

In metric spaces such as $\mathbb R$, continuity at $a$ can be characterized using sequences:

$$ \boxed{ x_n\to a \Rightarrow f(x_n)\to f(a) }. $$

This must hold for every sequence $(x_n)$ in the domain converging to $a$.

Topological Definition

In topology, a function:

$$ f:X\to Y $$

is continuous when the preimage of every open set in $Y$ is open in $X$:

$$ \boxed{ U\subseteq Y\text{ open} \Rightarrow f^{-1}(U)\text{ open in }X }. $$

This formulation generalizes continuity beyond numerical distance.

The “No Breaks” Intuition

In elementary calculus, a continuous graph is often described informally as one that can be drawn without lifting the pencil.

This can help develop intuition, but it is not a definition and can become misleading for complicated domains, higher dimensions, or abstract spaces.

Continuity and Graphs

For ordinary real-valued functions of one real variable, continuity means the graph has no local gap, jump, or incompatible point value at the point under discussion.

Removable Discontinuity

Consider:

$$ f(x)=\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}. $$

For $x\ne1$:

$$ f(x)=x+1. $$

The limit exists:

$$ \lim_{x\to1}f(x)=2, $$

but the original expression is undefined at $x=1$.

This creates a removable discontinuity.

Repairing a Removable Discontinuity

If one defines:

$$ f(1)=2, $$

then the function becomes continuous at $1$.

Jump Discontinuity

A jump discontinuity occurs when the left- and right-hand limits exist but are unequal:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a^-}f(x) \ne \lim_{x\to a^+}f(x) }. $$

Infinite Discontinuity

A function may become unbounded near a point, as with:

$$ f(x)=\frac1x $$

near $x=0$.

Such behavior prevents continuity at that point.

Oscillatory Discontinuity

A function can fail to approach any single value because of increasingly rapid oscillation.

For example:

$$ f(x)=\sin\left(\frac1x\right) $$

has no limit as $x\to0$.

Polynomials

Every polynomial is continuous for all real inputs:

$$ \boxed{ p(x)\text{ is continuous on }\mathbb R }. $$

Rational Functions

A rational function:

$$ f(x)=\frac{p(x)}{q(x)} $$

is continuous wherever:

$$ \boxed{ q(x)\ne0 }. $$

Exponential Functions

Standard exponential functions:

$$ f(x)=a^x, \qquad a>0, $$

are continuous on $\mathbb R$.

Logarithmic Functions

Standard logarithmic functions are continuous on their domains:

$$ \boxed{ \log_a x\text{ is continuous for }x>0 }. $$

Trigonometric Functions

Sine and cosine are continuous everywhere:

$$ \boxed{ \sin x,\cos x \text{ are continuous on }\mathbb R }. $$

Tangent is continuous wherever $\cos x\ne0$.

Absolute Value

The function:

$$ f(x)=|x| $$

is continuous everywhere even though it is not differentiable at $x=0$.

This gives an important distinction:

$$ \boxed{ \text{continuous} \not\Rightarrow \text{differentiable} }. $$

Differentiability Implies Continuity

If a function is differentiable at $a$, then it is continuous at $a$:

$$ \boxed{ f'(a)\text{ exists} \Rightarrow f\text{ continuous at }a }. $$

The Converse Fails

Continuity alone does not guarantee differentiability.

The absolute value function provides the standard example:

$$ f(x)=|x|. $$

It is continuous at $0$, but the left and right derivatives there differ.

Sum of Continuous Functions

If $f$ and $g$ are continuous at $a$, then:

$$ \boxed{ f+g \text{ is continuous at }a }. $$

Difference of Continuous Functions

$$ \boxed{ f-g \text{ is continuous at }a }. $$

Product of Continuous Functions

$$ \boxed{ fg \text{ is continuous at }a }. $$

Quotient of Continuous Functions

If $g(a)\ne0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac fg \text{ is continuous at }a }. $$

Composition of Continuous Functions

If $g$ is continuous at $a$ and $f$ is continuous at $g(a)$, then:

$$ \boxed{ f\circ g \text{ is continuous at }a }. $$

Limit through a Continuous Function

When $f$ is continuous at $L$ and:

$$ \lim_{x\to a}g(x)=L, $$

one may pass the limit through $f$:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a}f(g(x)) = f(L) }. $$

Intermediate Value Theorem

If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, then every value between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ is attained somewhere between $a$ and $b$.

$$ \boxed{ N\text{ between }f(a)\text{ and }f(b) \Rightarrow \exists c\in[a,b]: f(c)=N }. $$

Root Existence

A common special case of the Intermediate Value Theorem states that if:

$$ f(a)f(b)<0, $$

and $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \exists c\in(a,b) \text{ such that }f(c)=0 }. $$

Extreme Value Theorem

If $f$ is continuous on a closed bounded interval $[a,b]$, then $f$ attains both an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum.

$$ \boxed{ f\text{ continuous on }[a,b] \Rightarrow f\text{ attains max and min} }. $$

Boundedness on Closed Intervals

A continuous real-valued function on a closed bounded interval is bounded.

Uniform Continuity

A function is uniformly continuous when one choice of $\delta$ works throughout the entire domain for each chosen $\varepsilon$.

$$ \boxed{ \forall\varepsilon>0\, \exists\delta>0\, \forall x,y: |x-y|<\delta \Rightarrow |f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon }. $$

Continuous versus Uniformly Continuous

Ordinary continuity allows the required $\delta$ to depend on the point under consideration. Uniform continuity does not.

$$ \boxed{ \text{uniformly continuous} \Rightarrow \text{continuous} } $$

but the converse does not hold on every domain.

Heine-Cantor Theorem

Every continuous function on a compact metric-space domain is uniformly continuous. In elementary real analysis:

$$ \boxed{ f\text{ continuous on }[a,b] \Rightarrow f\text{ uniformly continuous on }[a,b] }. $$

Continuous Extension

A function defined on a punctured domain may sometimes be extended continuously to a missing point by assigning the limiting value there.

If:

$$ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L, $$

then defining:

$$ \boxed{ f(a)=L } $$

can remove a removable discontinuity.

Piecewise Functions

A piecewise-defined function is continuous at a boundary point only if the formulas join with matching limits and function value.

If:

$$ f(x)= \begin{cases} g(x),&x

then continuity at $a$ requires:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a^-}g(x) = h(a) = \lim_{x\to a^+}h(x) }. $$

Example with a Parameter

Consider:

$$ f(x)= \begin{cases} 2x+1,&x<3,\\ k,&x=3,\\ x+4,&x>3. \end{cases} $$

The left-hand limit is:

$$ 2(3)+1=7, $$

and the right-hand limit is:

$$ 3+4=7. $$

Therefore continuity requires:

$$ \boxed{k=7}. $$

Continuous Random Variable

In probability, a continuous random variable is not defined merely by a continuous function. Rather, it is a random variable whose probability distribution is described over a continuum, commonly through a density function.

For an ordinary continuous distribution:

$$ \boxed{ P(X=x)=0 } $$

for each individual point $x$.

Probability over an Interval

If $X$ has density $f$, then:

$$ \boxed{ P(a\le X\le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx }. $$

Continuous Variable

A continuous variable is commonly understood as a variable modeled as capable of taking values throughout an interval or continuum rather than only isolated discrete values.

Continuous versus Discrete

Continuous Discrete
Values may vary through a continuum Values occur as separated or countable possibilities
Often modeled with intervals Often modeled with finite or countable sets
Integration frequently appears Summation frequently appears

Continuous Change

In modeling, continuous change usually means that the state variable is represented as changing through intermediate values rather than jumping only between separated states.

Differential equations are a primary mathematical language for continuous change.

Continuous-Time Models

A continuous-time model uses a time variable such as:

$$ t\in\mathbb R $$

or an interval of real numbers, rather than restricting time to integer steps.

Continuous versus Continuously Differentiable

A function may be continuous without having a derivative.

A function described as continuously differentiable has a derivative that exists and is itself continuous.

$$ \boxed{ f\in C^1 \Rightarrow f\text{ is differentiable and }f'\text{ is continuous} }. $$

Continuity Classes

The notation:

$$ \boxed{ C^0 } $$

commonly denotes continuous functions.

More generally:

$$ C^k $$

denotes functions with continuous derivatives through order $k$.

Smooth Functions

A smooth function is typically infinitely differentiable:

$$ \boxed{ f\in C^\infty }. $$

Smoothness is therefore much stronger than mere continuity.

Analytic Functions

An analytic function is locally represented by a convergent power series. Analytic functions are continuous, but continuity alone does not imply analyticity.

$$ \boxed{ \text{analytic} \Rightarrow \text{smooth} \Rightarrow \text{continuous} } $$

under the usual real or complex analytic settings.

Continuous Maps in Topology

In topology, continuity is fundamentally about preservation of open-set structure through inverse images rather than about slopes, formulas, or graphs.

This makes continuity applicable to spaces with no ordinary numerical coordinates.

Homeomorphisms

A homeomorphism is a bijective continuous map whose inverse is also continuous.

$$ \boxed{ f:X\to Y \text{ homeomorphism} \Rightarrow f,f^{-1}\text{ continuous} }. $$

Connectedness Preservation

The continuous image of a connected set is connected.

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ connected} \Rightarrow f(A)\text{ connected} } $$

for continuous $f$.

Compactness Preservation

The continuous image of a compact set is compact.

$$ \boxed{ K\text{ compact} \Rightarrow f(K)\text{ compact} }. $$

Continuous Does Not Mean Constant Rate

A continuous function may change at a varying rate.

For example:

$$ f(x)=x^2 $$

is continuous even though its slope:

$$ f'(x)=2x $$

changes with $x$.

Continuous Does Not Mean Monotonic

A continuous function can increase, decrease, oscillate, or change direction.

For example:

$$ f(x)=\sin x $$

is continuous everywhere but repeatedly increases and decreases.

Continuous Does Not Mean Bounded

Continuity on an unbounded or noncompact domain does not guarantee boundedness.

For example:

$$ f(x)=x $$

is continuous on $\mathbb R$ but unbounded.

Continuous Does Not Mean Invertible

A continuous function may fail to be one-to-one.

For example:

$$ f(x)=x^2 $$

is continuous on $\mathbb R$ but not injective there.

Continuous Does Not Mean Real-Valued Only

Continuity applies to vector-valued, complex-valued, matrix-valued, and abstract mappings whenever suitable topological or metric structures are defined.

Vector-Valued Continuity

A vector-valued function:

$$ \mathbf r(t) = \langle x(t),y(t),z(t)\rangle $$

is continuous when its component functions are continuous.

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf r\text{ continuous} \iff x,y,z\text{ continuous} }. $$

Multivariable Continuity

A function:

$$ f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m $$

is continuous at $\mathbf a$ when:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{\mathbf x\to\mathbf a}f(\mathbf x) = f(\mathbf a) }. $$

Path Tests Are Not Always Sufficient

In several variables, checking only a few paths toward a point cannot generally prove continuity. Different possible approaches must all be controlled.

Restriction of a Continuous Function

If $f$ is continuous on a domain $D$, then its restriction to a subset $A\subseteq D$ is continuous with the subspace topology.

Constant Functions Are Continuous

Every constant function:

$$ f(x)=c $$

is continuous on its domain.

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a}c=c=f(a) }. $$

Identity Function Is Continuous

The identity function:

$$ I(x)=x $$

is continuous on $\mathbb R$ and, more generally, the identity map on any topological space is continuous.

Common Misconception: Continuous Means Differentiable

Differentiability implies continuity, but continuity does not imply differentiability.

Common Misconception: Continuous Means the Graph Is Smooth

A continuous graph may have sharp corners or cusps. Smoothness requires stronger differentiability conditions.

Common Misconception: Continuous Means No Vertical Asymptotes Anywhere

A function may be continuous on each interval of its domain while having points outside the domain where vertical asymptotes occur.

For example:

$$ f(x)=\frac1x $$

is continuous on:

$$ (-\infty,0)\cup(0,\infty), $$

despite having a vertical asymptote at $x=0$.

Common Misconception: A Function with a Hole Cannot Be Continuous Anywhere

Continuity is local. A function can fail to be continuous at one point while remaining continuous everywhere else in its domain.

Common Misconception: Continuous Means the Domain Contains Every Real Number

A function can be continuous on a restricted domain. Continuity is evaluated relative to the function's domain.

Common Misconception: The Pencil Test Is the Definition

“Draw without lifting your pencil” is an elementary visual heuristic, not the formal mathematical definition of continuity.

Common Misconception: Continuous Random Variable Means Its Density Is Continuous

A continuous random variable is characterized by the nature of its distribution, not by a requirement that its density function itself be continuous everywhere.

Common Misconception: Continuous and Discrete Are Simple Opposites in Every Field

The contrast is useful, but the exact meanings vary among analysis, probability, topology, numerical modeling, and applied mathematics.

In ordinary calculus, the statement “$f$ is continuous at $a$” refers to agreement between the limit and the function value. Saying “$f$ is continuous” without further qualification ordinarily means continuous at every point of its stated domain or on the region currently under discussion.

The word continuous should not be used as a loose synonym for differentiable, smooth, or slowly changing. These are stronger or different properties.

Defense Notation Advice: State the point, interval, set, or domain on which continuity is claimed. Distinguish ordinary continuity from one-sided and uniform continuity. In piecewise functions, compare the left-hand limit, right-hand limit, and actual function value explicitly. Do not infer differentiability from continuity, and do not treat the visual “no breaks” description as a substitute for the limit or topological definition.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Calculus Meaning: A function is continuous at $a$ when its limit at $a$ equals its actual value.
  • Pointwise Definition: $$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a) }. $$
  • Epsilon-Delta Definition: For every $\varepsilon>0$, sufficiently small input changes produce output changes smaller than $\varepsilon$.
  • Sequential Definition: $x_n\to a$ implies $f(x_n)\to f(a)$ in the usual metric-space setting.
  • Topological Definition: Preimages of open sets are open.
  • Left Continuity: $\lim_{x\to a^-}f(x)=f(a)$.
  • Right Continuity: $\lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)=f(a)$.
  • Closed Interval: Uses ordinary continuity in the interior and one-sided continuity at endpoints.
  • Polynomials: Continuous everywhere on $\mathbb R$.
  • Rational Functions: Continuous wherever the denominator is nonzero.
  • Exponential and Logarithmic Functions: Continuous throughout their natural real domains.
  • Trigonometric Functions: Continuous wherever defined.
  • Constant Functions: Continuous throughout their domains.
  • Algebra of Continuous Functions: Sums, differences, products, and appropriate quotients of continuous functions remain continuous.
  • Composition: Compositions of continuous functions are continuous where defined.
  • Differentiability: Differentiability implies continuity.
  • Converse Warning: Continuity does not imply differentiability.
  • Intermediate Value Theorem: Continuous functions on intervals attain intermediate output values.
  • Extreme Value Theorem: Continuous real-valued functions on closed bounded intervals attain maxima and minima.
  • Uniform Continuity: Stronger than pointwise continuity because the same $\delta$ works throughout the domain.
  • Piecewise Functions: Continuity at boundaries requires matching one-sided limits and function value.
  • Removable Discontinuity: Can sometimes be repaired by assigning the limiting value.
  • Jump Discontinuity: Left and right limits differ.
  • Infinite Discontinuity: Function values become unbounded near the point.
  • Continuous Variable: A variable modeled as ranging through a continuum of values.
  • Continuous Random Variable: A random variable having a continuous-type distribution rather than isolated point masses in the ordinary elementary classification.
  • Continuous Time: Time is modeled over a real interval rather than discrete steps.
  • Topology: Continuity generalizes to arbitrary topological spaces through inverse images of open sets.
  • Vector-Valued Functions: Continuity can be checked componentwise in ordinary Euclidean spaces.
  • Multivariable Functions: Continuity requires control under all ways of approaching a point, not merely a few chosen paths.
  • Discrete Contrast: Continuous models permit intermediate values; discrete models use separated or countable states.
  • Differentiable Contrast: Differentiability is stronger than continuity.
  • Smooth Contrast: Smoothness generally means infinitely differentiable and is much stronger than continuity.
  • Uniformly Continuous Contrast: Uniform continuity imposes global control that ordinary pointwise continuity does not require.
  • Common Error: Do not equate continuity with differentiability.
  • Common Error: Do not assume continuous means constant slope or constant rate.
  • Common Error: Do not assume continuity implies boundedness on every domain.
  • Common Error: Do not assume a function must be defined on all real numbers to be continuous.
  • Common Error: Do not rely solely on the “draw without lifting the pencil” heuristic.
  • Common Error: Do not assume a continuous random variable requires a density that is itself continuous everywhere.
  • Terminological Contrast: Continuous most commonly means that a function preserves limiting behavior so that $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a)$. Discrete describes separated or countable values or states. Differentiable means a derivative exists and implies continuity at that point. Continuously differentiable means the derivative itself is continuous. Uniformly continuous strengthens continuity by requiring one input-control scale to work throughout the domain. Smooth usually means differentiable to all orders. A continuous random variable is a probability-theoretic concept and should not be confused with a continuous function.
