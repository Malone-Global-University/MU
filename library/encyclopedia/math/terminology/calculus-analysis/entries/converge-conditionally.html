Converge Conditionally

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

To converge conditionally means that an infinite series converges in its original signed or complex form but fails to converge after each term is replaced by its absolute value or magnitude.

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n \text{ converges conditionally} } $$

precisely when:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n \text{ converges} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|a_n| \text{ diverges} }. $$

The word converge describes approach to a finite sum. The qualifier conditionally indicates that the convergence depends on the original arrangement and interaction of the terms, often through cancellation between positive and negative contributions.

Conditional convergence is therefore weaker than absolute convergence:

$$ \boxed{ \text{absolute convergence} \Rightarrow \text{convergence}, \qquad \text{conditional convergence} = \text{convergence without absolute convergence} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Core Test

To determine whether a series converges conditionally, two separate questions must be answered:

  1. Does $\sum a_n$ converge?
  2. Does $\sum |a_n|$ diverge?

Only when both answers hold simultaneously is the series conditionally convergent.

Standard Example

Consider the alternating harmonic series:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}. $$

It converges by the Alternating Series Test, but:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1n $$

diverges.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n} \text{ converges conditionally} }. $$

Why Cancellation Matters

In a conditionally convergent real series, positive and negative terms can partially offset one another.

When absolute values are taken:

$$ a_n \mapsto |a_n|, $$

the sign cancellation disappears.

If the resulting positive-term series diverges, the original convergence depended essentially on that cancellation.

Classification of Convergent Scalar Series

Every convergent real or complex scalar series is either absolutely convergent or conditionally convergent.

$$ \boxed{ \text{convergent} = \text{absolute} \cup \text{conditional} }. $$

These categories are mutually exclusive.

Absolute Convergence Rules Out Conditional Convergence

If:

$$ \sum|a_n| $$

converges, then the original series converges absolutely, not conditionally.

Term Test

Conditional convergence still requires:

$$ \boxed{ a_n\to0 }. $$

If the terms do not approach zero, the original series diverges and therefore cannot converge conditionally.

Term Test Is Not Sufficient

The fact that $a_n\to0$ does not show that the series converges.

The harmonic series:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1n $$

diverges even though $1/n\to0$.

Alternating Series Test

A common source of conditional convergence is an alternating series:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n+1}b_n, \qquad b_n\ge0. $$

If:

$$ b_{n+1}\le b_n $$

eventually and:

$$ b_n\to0, $$

then the alternating series converges.

Absolute Test Must Still Be Performed

The Alternating Series Test proves convergence but does not determine whether the convergence is absolute or conditional.

One must separately test:

$$ \boxed{ \sum b_n }. $$

Conditional Convergence and Rearrangement

Conditional convergence is especially important because rearranging terms can alter the resulting sum.

This is unlike absolutely convergent series, for which arbitrary rearrangement preserves the sum.

Riemann Rearrangement Theorem

For a conditionally convergent real series, suitable rearrangements of the terms can produce strikingly different behavior.

A rearrangement may be constructed to converge to any prescribed real number, or to diverge in certain ways.

$$ \boxed{ \text{conditional convergence} \Rightarrow \text{order of terms can matter} }. $$

Why Rearrangement Changes the Sum

In a conditionally convergent real series, the positive terms and negative terms each possess divergent total magnitude.

Their original interleaving controls how the two divergent contributions offset one another.

Positive and Negative Parts

Define:

$$ a_n^+=\max(a_n,0), \qquad a_n^-=\max(-a_n,0). $$

Then:

$$ a_n=a_n^+-a_n^- $$

and:

$$ |a_n| = a_n^++a_n^-. $$

Structure of a Conditionally Convergent Real Series

For a conditionally convergent real series:

$$ \boxed{ \sum a_n^+=\infty, \qquad \sum a_n^-=\infty } $$

while:

$$ \sum(a_n^+-a_n^-) $$

converges because of cancellation.

Finite Rearrangements

Changing or reordering only finitely many terms does not affect convergence or the final sum.

The rearrangement phenomenon concerns potentially infinite reordering.

Grouping Terms

Grouping consecutive terms of a convergent series into finite blocks preserves the sum when the order of terms is not changed.

This should not be confused with an arbitrary permutation of infinitely many terms.

Conditional Convergence of Improper Integrals

The terminology also applies to improper integrals.

An improper integral:

$$ \int_a^\infty f(x)\,dx $$

converges conditionally if:

$$ \boxed{ \int_a^\infty f(x)\,dx \text{ converges} } $$

while:

$$ \boxed{ \int_a^\infty |f(x)|\,dx \text{ diverges} }. $$

Oscillatory Integral Example

A standard example is:

$$ \int_1^\infty \frac{\sin x}{x}\,dx. $$

This improper integral converges but not absolutely, so it is conditionally convergent.

Cancellation in Integrals

Just as signs can cancel in an infinite series, positive and negative regions of an integrand may cancel in an improper integral.

Taking $|f(x)|$ removes that cancellation.

Dirichlet-Type Convergence

Conditional convergence often arises in oscillatory series and integrals where bounded cumulative oscillation combines with a factor tending toward zero.

Dirichlet's test is one important method for establishing such convergence.

Dirichlet Test for Series

One common form states that:

$$ \sum a_nb_n $$

converges if the partial sums of $\sum a_n$ are bounded and $b_n$ decreases to $0$ under suitable hypotheses.

Such convergence may be conditional.

Abel Test

Abel-type tests also establish convergence in situations involving products of sequences with controlled monotonicity and convergent factors.

A power series:

$$ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_n(x-c)^n $$

converges absolutely for:

At endpoints satisfying $|x-c|=R$, however, the series may converge conditionally.

Consider:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{x^n}{n}. $$

Its radius of convergence is $1$. At:

$$ x=-1, $$

the series becomes:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{n}, $$

which converges conditionally.

At:

$$ x=1, $$

the same power series becomes the harmonic series:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1n, $$

which diverges.

Conditional Convergence and Complex Series

A complex series:

$$ \sum z_n $$

may likewise converge without:

$$ \sum|z_n| $$

converging.

Such a series is conditionally convergent in the usual scalar sense.

Magnitude Removes Phase Cancellation

For complex terms, absolute convergence removes not merely positive-negative cancellation but all cancellation produced by differing complex directions.

Conditional Convergence Is Not a Weak Limit Concept

The word conditionally does not mean that the limiting value is tentative or only approximately valid.

The original series genuinely converges. The qualification describes the failure of the stronger absolute-convergence property.

Conditional Convergence and Summation Order

A conditionally convergent scalar series should therefore be treated with caution whenever an algebraic manipulation changes the order of infinitely many terms.

Algebraic Operations

Finite algebraic manipulations of convergent series are generally much safer than infinite rearrangements. Conditional convergence means one cannot casually transfer every law of finite sums to arbitrary infinite reorderings.

Adding Two Conditionally Convergent Series

If:

$$ \sum a_n \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sum b_n $$

converge, then termwise addition:

$$ \sum(a_n+b_n) $$

converges to the sum of the two series.

However, the resulting series may be absolutely convergent, conditionally convergent, or even become eventually zero depending on the terms.

Conditional Is Not Preserved by Addition

Two conditionally convergent series need not have a conditionally convergent sum.

Thus conditionally convergent is not an algebraic class closed under every natural operation.

Scalar Multiplication

If $\sum a_n$ converges conditionally and $c\ne0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \sum ca_n \text{ converges conditionally} }. $$

Multiplying all terms by a nonzero finite scalar does not change the distinction between absolute and conditional convergence.

The Zero Scalar Case

If:

$$ c=0, $$

then:

$$ \sum ca_n = \sum0 $$

is absolutely convergent, so the nonzero-scalar condition matters.

Tail Property

Conditional convergence depends only on the tail of the series.

Altering finitely many terms changes the numerical sum by a finite amount but does not change the classification as absolute, conditional, or divergent.

Comparison with Positive Series

A series with all nonnegative terms cannot be conditionally convergent because:

$$ |a_n|=a_n. $$

Hence convergence automatically implies absolute convergence.

Sign or Phase Variation Is Necessary

Conditional convergence therefore requires some form of cancellation among terms. For real series this means, in particular, both positive and negative contributions must occur sufficiently far out in the sequence.

Conditional Convergence Is Tail-Sensitive but Not Initial-Term-Sensitive

Initial terms may be arbitrarily large or irregular without affecting the eventual convergence classification, provided only finitely many terms are involved.

Partial Sums

The series:

$$ \sum a_n $$

converges conditionally only if its partial sums:

$$ S_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_n $$

converge to a finite limit while the partial sums:

$$ A_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}|a_n| $$

fail to approach a finite limit.

Graphical View of Partial Sums

For alternating conditionally convergent series, successive partial sums often oscillate above and below the eventual sum with decreasing error.

The convergence comes from shrinking overshoots, not from each term being nonnegative.

Alternating-Series Error Bound

Under the usual Alternating Series Test hypotheses, if:

$$ S=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n+1}b_n, $$

then the error after $N$ terms satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ |S-S_N| \le b_{N+1} }. $$

This provides practical approximation control even when convergence is only conditional.

Conditional Does Not Mean Slow

Conditional convergence does not by itself specify a convergence rate. A conditionally convergent series can converge faster or slower than another series depending on its terms.

Conditional Does Not Mean Unstable in Every Sense

The original ordering has a perfectly well-defined sum. The sensitivity concerns operations such as arbitrary infinite rearrangement, not the existence of the original limit.

Common Misconception: Conditional Means the Series Might Not Really Converge

A conditionally convergent series genuinely converges. The word indicates only that it does not converge absolutely.

Common Misconception: Every Alternating Series Converges Conditionally

Alternating signs alone guarantee nothing. The series may diverge or converge absolutely.

Common Misconception: If Absolute Values Diverge, the Original Series Must Diverge

This is precisely where conditional convergence can occur.

Common Misconception: Conditional Convergence Is the Same as the Alternating Series Test

The Alternating Series Test is one method for proving convergence. Conditional convergence is a classification determined only after absolute convergence has also been ruled out.

Common Misconception: Rearrangement Never Changes a Sum

That familiar property of finite sums extends safely to absolutely convergent infinite scalar series, but arbitrary rearrangements of conditionally convergent real series can change the sum.

Common Misconception: Positive-Term Series Can Converge Conditionally

For nonnegative terms, absolute values do nothing, so convergence is automatically absolute.

Common Misconception: A Conditionallly Convergent Series Has a Divergent Partial-Sum Sequence

The opposite is true: its ordinary partial sums converge. It is the absolute-value series whose partial sums diverge.

Common Misconception: Conditional Convergence Depends on the First Few Terms

Altering finitely many terms does not change the classification.

Common Misconception: Conditional Convergence Applies Only to Alternating Signs

Alternating series provide familiar examples, but conditional convergence can arise in more general oscillatory real or complex series.

The standard verb phrase is converges conditionally, while the noun phrase is conditional convergence. In most contexts, the unqualified word converges refers to ordinary convergence; the qualifier is added when the distinction from absolute convergence matters.

To classify a series accurately, it is not enough to say that an alternating or oscillatory series converges. One must also determine whether the corresponding absolute-value series converges.

Defense Notation Advice: Reserve the phrase converges conditionally for the two-part situation in which the original series converges and the absolute-value series diverges. Do not infer conditional convergence merely from alternating signs or from use of the Alternating Series Test. When rearranging infinitely many terms, distinguish carefully between absolutely and conditionally convergent series. For improper integrals, compare $\int f$ with $\int|f|$ under the appropriate limiting interpretation.

  • Terminological Contrast: To converge means that the relevant partial sums or limiting expressions approach a finite value. To converge absolutely means that convergence remains after replacing terms by their absolute values or magnitudes. To converge conditionally means that the original object converges while its absolute-value counterpart diverges. Conditional convergence therefore depends on cancellation and may be sensitive to infinite rearrangement. Absolute convergence is the stronger and more rearrangement-stable condition. Divergence means that the original series or improper integral fails to converge under the ordinary criterion.
