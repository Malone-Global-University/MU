Squeeze Theorem

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

The name Squeeze Theorem comes from the visual and logical idea of one quantity being squeezed between two others. If a middle function is trapped between a lower bound and an upper bound, and both bounding functions approach the same limit, the middle function has no remaining room to approach anything else.

The same result is also commonly called the Sandwich Theorem, because the middle function lies between two bounding functions like a filling between two layers. Other names include Squeezing Theorem and, less commonly, Pinching Theorem. In contemporary calculus instruction, Squeeze Theorem is one of the most common names.

The terminology describes the proof mechanism rather than a particular algebraic operation. Nothing is literally compressed; instead, inequalities constrain the possible behavior of a function or sequence until its limiting value is forced.

Operational Nuance

Suppose three functions satisfy:

$$ f(x)\le g(x)\le h(x) $$

for all $x$ sufficiently close to $a$, possibly excluding $x=a$ itself. If:

$$ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L $$

and:

$$ \lim_{x\to a}h(x)=L, $$

then the Squeeze Theorem guarantees:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a}g(x)=L } $$

The central requirement is that the two bounding functions approach the same value. Merely trapping $g(x)$ between two functions is not sufficient if those outer functions approach different limits.

For example, consider:

$$ g(x)=x^2\cos\left(\frac{1}{x}\right). $$

The factor:

$$ \cos\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) $$

oscillates rapidly as $x\to0$, so it does not itself approach a limit. However:

$$ -1\le \cos\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) \le1. $$

Because $x^2\ge0$, multiplication gives:

$$ -x^2 \le x^2\cos\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) \le x^2. $$

Both bounding functions approach zero:

$$ \lim_{x\to0}(-x^2)=0 $$ $$ \lim_{x\to0}x^2=0. $$

Therefore:

$$ \lim_{x\to0} x^2\cos\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) = 0. $$

This illustrates one of the theorem's most important uses: a function may oscillate indefinitely while still having a limit if the size of that oscillation is forced into an interval that shrinks to a single value.

A particularly useful form involves absolute values. If:

$$ |g(x)-L|\le h(x) $$

and:

$$ \lim_{x\to a}h(x)=0, $$

then:

$$ -h(x) \le g(x)-L \le h(x). $$

Applying the Squeeze Theorem gives:

$$ \lim_{x\to a}[g(x)-L]=0, $$

and therefore:

$$ \lim_{x\to a}g(x)=L. $$

Common Misconception: The middle function does not need to approach its limit monotonically or smoothly. It may oscillate infinitely often. What matters is that its values remain trapped between two bounds whose separation collapses to zero around the same limiting value.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$f(x)\le g(x)\le h(x)$ "G of x is squeezed between f of x and h of x."
$\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L$ "The limit of f of x as x approaches a equals L."
$\lim_{x\to a}h(x)=L$ "The limit of h of x as x approaches a equals L."
$\lim_{x\to a}g(x)=L$ "Therefore, the limit of g of x as x approaches a equals L."
$|g(x)|\le h(x)$ "The absolute value of g of x is bounded above by h of x."
$a_n\le b_n\le c_n$ "B sub n is squeezed between a sub n and c sub n."

A compact symbolic statement of the theorem is:

$$ f(x)\le g(x)\le h(x), \qquad \lim_{x\to a}f(x) = \lim_{x\to a}h(x) = L $$ $$ \Longrightarrow \lim_{x\to a}g(x)=L. $$

In written solutions, it is important to show both parts of the argument: first establish the inequality, then establish the common limits of the bounding functions. Merely saying "by the Squeeze Theorem" without identifying the bounds leaves the essential reasoning unstated.

Defense Notation Advice: The inequalities need only hold in some punctured neighborhood of the limiting point. They do not have to hold for every real number, and the functions do not necessarily need to be defined at $x=a$. Limits concern behavior near the point.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Limits of Functions: The Squeeze Theorem determines a difficult limit by replacing direct evaluation with inequalities: $$ f(x)\le g(x)\le h(x). $$ If both outer functions approach $L$, the middle function must also approach $L$.
  • Oscillating Expressions: The theorem is especially useful for expressions containing bounded oscillatory factors such as: $$ \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) \qquad\text{or}\qquad \cos\left(\frac{1}{x}\right). $$ Although these factors may not possess limits themselves, multiplication by a sufficiently small quantity can force the entire expression toward a limit.
  • Trigonometric Limits: One classical use of the theorem is in proving: $$ \lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x}=1. $$ Appropriate geometric inequalities place the expression between two quantities that both approach $1$.
  • Absolute-Value Bounds: If: $$ |f(x)-L|\le g(x) $$ and: $$ g(x)\to0, $$ then: $$ f(x)\to L. $$ This form is particularly important because it interprets $g(x)$ as a shrinking upper bound on the error between $f(x)$ and its proposed limit.
  • Sequences: The theorem has a direct sequence version. If: $$ a_n\le b_n\le c_n $$ for all sufficiently large $n$, and: $$ \lim_{n\to\infty}a_n = \lim_{n\to\infty}c_n = L, $$ then: $$ \lim_{n\to\infty}b_n=L. $$
  • Error Analysis: Suppose an approximation has error $E_n$ satisfying: $$ |E_n|\le M_n $$ with: $$ M_n\to0. $$ Then the Squeeze Theorem gives: $$ E_n\to0. $$ This reasoning appears frequently in approximation theory and numerical analysis.
  • Comparison Arguments: The theorem is an example of a broader strategy in analysis: difficult objects can often be understood by comparing them with simpler upper and lower bounds. Exact calculation is not always necessary if sufficiently strong inequalities are available.
  • Infinite Limits: Related order arguments are used for infinite limits. For example, if: $$ f(x)\le g(x) $$ near $a$ and: $$ f(x)\to+\infty, $$ then $g(x)$ must also tend to $+\infty$. This is closely related to squeezing, although it is often stated as a separate comparison theorem.
  • Proofs in Real Analysis: The Squeeze Theorem is ultimately grounded in the order structure of the real numbers. If two bounds become arbitrarily close to the same number, no quantity permanently trapped between them can remain a positive distance away from that number.
  • Terminological Contrast: Squeeze Theorem, Sandwich Theorem, Squeezing Theorem, and Pinching Theorem are alternate names for the same standard limit principle. The Ham Sandwich Theorem, despite the similar name, is a completely different theorem concerning the simultaneous division of measurable sets in higher-dimensional geometry.
