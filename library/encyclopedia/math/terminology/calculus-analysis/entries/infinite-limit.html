Infinite Limit

Subfield: Calculus & Analysis

Etymology & Roots

An infinite limit describes function values that grow without bound in the positive or negative direction as the input approaches a specified value.

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=\infty } $$

The word infinite refers to unbounded behavior. It does not mean that infinity is an ordinary real-number value attained by the function.

Operational Nuance

Positive Infinite Limit

The notation:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=\infty } $$

means that $f(x)$ can be made larger than any prescribed positive bound by taking $x$ sufficiently close to $a$ within the relevant domain.

Negative Infinite Limit

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=-\infty } $$

means that the function decreases without bound as $x$ approaches $a$.

Formal Positive Definition

The statement $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=\infty$ means that for every $M>0$, there exists $\delta>0$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ 0<|x-a|<\delta \Longrightarrow f(x)>M }. $$

One-Sided Infinite Limits

Infinite behavior may differ on opposite sides of the same input:

$$ \lim_{x\to a^-}f(x)=\infty, \qquad \lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)=-\infty. $$

In such a case, the two-sided infinite limit is not represented by one common signed infinity.

Vertical Asymptotes

If at least one one-sided limit becomes positively or negatively infinite as $x\to a$, then $x=a$ is commonly identified as a vertical asymptote:

$$ \boxed{ x=a \text{ is a vertical asymptote} } $$

Example

$$ \lim_{x\to0}\frac{1}{x^2}=\infty. $$

Both sides approach positive infinity because $x^2$ remains positive and tends toward zero.

Infinite Limit vs. Limit at Infinity

These phrases describe different roles for infinity:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=\infty \quad\text{vs.}\quad \lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=L }. $$

In the first, the function values become unbounded. In the second, the input grows without bound.

Common Misconception

Writing a limit equal to $\infty$ does not mean the function reaches a real-number value called infinity. It is notation describing unbounded growth.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=\infty$ "F of x increases without bound as x approaches a."
$\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=-\infty$ "F of x decreases without bound as x approaches a."
$\lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)=\infty$ "The right-hand limit is positive infinity."
$\lim_{x\to a^-}f(x)=-\infty$ "The left-hand limit is negative infinity."
$x=a$ "X equals a is a vertical asymptote."
$f(x)>M$ "The function exceeds any prescribed positive bound."

The notation $=\infty$ is conventional limit notation for divergence in a specific direction. In ordinary real arithmetic, $\infty$ is not treated as a real number.

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish carefully between $x\to\infty$ and $f(x)\to\infty$, and use one-sided notation when the behavior differs on the two sides of the approach point.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: Function values become unbounded as the input approaches a specified point.
  • Positive Infinity: Values eventually exceed every prescribed real bound.
  • Negative Infinity: Values eventually fall below every prescribed negative bound.
  • One-Sided Limits: Left-hand and right-hand infinite behavior may differ.
  • Vertical Asymptotes: Infinite one-sided behavior commonly signals a vertical asymptote.
  • Rational Functions: Denominator zeros often produce infinite limits when uncanceled factors remain.
  • Sign Analysis: The sign of numerator and denominator near a singularity determines whether behavior tends toward $+\infty$ or $-\infty$.
  • Infinite Discontinuity: Infinite limits are associated with a nonremovable type of discontinuity.
  • Extended Real Numbers: Advanced analysis may formally adjoin $+\infty$ and $-\infty$, but they remain distinct from ordinary real numbers.
  • Common Error: Treating infinity as an ordinary number that can be substituted directly.
  • Common Error: Confusing an infinite limit with a limit at infinity.
  • Common Error: Ignoring left-hand and right-hand behavior near a vertical asymptote.
  • Common Error: Assuming all denominator zeros automatically produce infinite limits; canceled factors may instead produce holes.
  • Core Interpretation: An infinite limit describes unbounded output behavior near an input: $$ \boxed{ x\to a \quad\Longrightarrow\quad |f(x)|\text{ grows beyond every finite bound} }. $$ The notation records the direction of that unbounded behavior rather than a finite limit value.
