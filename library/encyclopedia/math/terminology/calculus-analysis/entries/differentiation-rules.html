Differentiation Rules

Subfield: Calculus & Analysis

Etymology & Roots

Differentiation rules are reusable formulas and structural laws for computing derivatives. The phrase is commonly interchangeable with derivative rules, although differentiation rules emphasizes the process being performed.

$$ \boxed{ f \quad\xrightarrow{\text{differentiation rules}}\quad f' } $$

These rules arise from the limit definition of the derivative but allow derivatives to be calculated efficiently without rebuilding the limit argument for every function.

Operational Nuance

Constant and Constant-Multiple Rules

$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}(c)=0, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}[c f(x)] = c f'(x) }. $$

Power Rule

$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}(x^n) = nx^{n-1} } $$

wherever the relevant real or complex expression is defined and differentiable.

Sum and Difference Rules

$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}[f(x)\pm g(x)] = f'(x)\pm g'(x) }. $$

Product Rule

$$ \boxed{ (fg)' = f'g+fg' }. $$

Both factors contribute to the local change of a product.

Quotient Rule

$$ \boxed{ \left(\frac{f}{g}\right)' = \frac{f'g-fg'}{g^2} } $$

wherever $g\neq0$ and the required derivatives exist.

Chain Rule

$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}f(g(x)) = f'(g(x))g'(x) }. $$

The chain rule is the principal differentiation law for composite functions.

Exponential and Logarithmic Rules

$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}e^x=e^x, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}a^x=a^x\ln a } $$
$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}\ln|u(x)| = \frac{u'(x)}{u(x)} } $$

wherever $u(x)\neq0$ and the logarithmic expression is defined.

Trigonometric Rules

$$ \boxed{ (\sin x)'=\cos x, \qquad (\cos x)'=-\sin x, \qquad (\tan x)'=\sec^2x }. $$

Implicit Differentiation

When $y$ is defined implicitly as a function of $x$, the same differentiation rules apply while each occurrence of $y$ is differentiated through the chain rule:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}(y^2) = 2y\frac{dy}{dx}. $$

Logarithmic Differentiation

Logarithmic differentiation can reorganize complicated products, quotients, and variable powers before ordinary differentiation rules are applied.

Rule Selection

$$ \boxed{ \text{recognize structure} \longrightarrow \text{select rule} \longrightarrow \text{differentiate} \longrightarrow \text{simplify} }. $$

Common Misconception

Differentiation rules are determined by mathematical structure, not by visual complexity. A long expression may require only the sum rule, while a short nested expression may require repeated applications of the chain rule.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$(x^n)'=nx^{n-1}$ "Use the power rule."
$(fg)'=f'g+fg'$ "Differentiate a product using the product rule."
$\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)'=\frac{f'g-fg'}{g^2}$ "Use the quotient rule."
$(f\circ g)'=(f'\circ g)g'$ "Use the chain rule for the composition."
$\frac{d}{dx}[f+g]=f'+g'$ "Differentiate the sum term by term."
$\frac{d}{dx}(y^n)=ny^{n-1}y'$ "Differentiate y to the nth power and include y prime by the chain rule."

Differentiation rules, derivative rules, and rules of differentiation are common variants. Some texts separate derivatives of standard functions from structural rules such as product and chain rules.

Defense Notation Advice: Parse parentheses and nested operations before differentiating. In particular, distinguish products from compositions and remember that implicit dependence introduces chain-rule factors such as $dy/dx$.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Constant Rule: A quantity independent of the differentiation variable has derivative zero.
  • Power Rule: Handles powers efficiently without returning to the difference quotient.
  • Linearity: Sums, differences, and constant multiples differentiate componentwise.
  • Product Rule: Accounts for changes in both multiplied factors.
  • Quotient Rule: Handles ratios where the denominator remains nonzero.
  • Chain Rule: Governs differentiation of nested functions.
  • Implicit Differentiation: Applies the same rules when dependent variables are not isolated explicitly.
  • Logarithmic Differentiation: Simplifies certain products, quotients, and variable-exponent expressions.
  • Trigonometric Functions: Use a standard family of derivative identities.
  • Inverse Functions: Their derivatives require appropriate invertibility and nonzero derivative conditions.
  • Higher Derivatives: Differentiation rules can be applied repeatedly when sufficient differentiability exists.
  • Common Error: Writing $(fg)'=f'g'$.
  • Common Error: Forgetting the inner derivative in a composite function.
  • Common Error: Reversing the terms $f'g-fg'$ in the quotient-rule numerator.
  • Common Error: Applying a standard derivative formula outside the domain where the original function is defined.
  • Core Interpretation: Differentiation rules form a reusable operational grammar for derivatives: $$ \boxed{ \text{function structure} \longrightarrow \text{appropriate rule} \longrightarrow \text{derivative} }. $$ Their purpose is to preserve the meaning of the limit-based derivative while making differentiation practical for increasingly complex expressions.
