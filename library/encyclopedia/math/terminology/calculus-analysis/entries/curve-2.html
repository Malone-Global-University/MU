Curve

Subfield: Geometry & Topology

Etymology & Roots

A curve is a one-dimensional geometric object that traces a path through a space. Informally, it may be thought of as a line that can bend rather than remaining straight.

The word comes through Latin curvus, meaning "bent" or "crooked." Modern mathematics broadens the idea well beyond visibly bent lines: straight lines, circles, function graphs, parameterized paths, and many abstract objects may all qualify as curves.

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{r}:I\to\mathbb{R}^n } $$

is a common formal representation, where an interval $I$ supplies a parameter and $\mathbf{r}(t)$ gives a point on the curve.

Operational Nuance

Graph of a Function

A familiar plane curve is the graph of a function:

$$ \boxed{ y=f(x) }. $$

Such curves pass the vertical-line test because each permitted $x$ corresponds to at most one value of $y$.

Parametric Curve

A curve can be described without solving for $y$ as a function of $x$:

$$ \boxed{ x=x(t), \qquad y=y(t) }. $$

Parametric descriptions allow loops, vertical tangents, repeated tracing, and motion along the curve.

Implicit Curve

A plane curve may also be defined by an equation relating the coordinates:

$$ \boxed{ F(x,y)=0 }. $$

For example, the circle $x^2+y^2=r^2$ is naturally expressed in implicit form.

Plane and Space Curves

A plane curve lies in $\mathbb{R}^2$, while a space curve may be represented by:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{r}(t) = \langle x(t),y(t),z(t)\rangle }. $$

Regularity

In differential geometry, a parameterized curve is often called regular where its velocity vector is nonzero:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{r}'(t)\neq\mathbf{0} }. $$

Regularity permits a well-defined tangent direction locally.

Simple and Closed Curves

A simple curve does not cross itself except possibly at matching endpoints in a closed representation. A closed curve returns to its starting point:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{r}(a)=\mathbf{r}(b) }. $$

Curve vs. Parameterization

The same geometric curve can have many different parameterizations. Changing speed or direction along the path does not necessarily change the set of geometric points.

Common Misconception

A mathematical curve does not have to be the graph of a function $y=f(x)$. Circles, loops, vertical lines, and space paths are all standard examples of curves.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$y=f(x)$ "The curve given by the graph of f."
$F(x,y)=0$ "An implicitly defined plane curve."
$\mathbf{r}(t)$ "A parameterized curve."
$\mathbf{r}'(t)$ "The tangent or velocity vector along the parameterized curve."
$\mathbf{r}(a)=\mathbf{r}(b)$ "The parameterized path is closed over the interval."
$C$ "A curve, often denoted by capital C."

In elementary algebra, curve often means the visible graph of an equation. In calculus and differential geometry, the term usually emphasizes a path, parameterization, tangent behavior, or geometric image.

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish the geometric curve from a particular parameterization of that curve. When necessary, state whether the term refers to the image of $\mathbf{r}$, the mapping $\mathbf{r}$ itself, or the graph of an equation.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Coordinate Geometry: Curves appear as graphs and loci satisfying equations.
  • Function Graphs: $y=f(x)$ gives one important class of plane curves.
  • Parametric Geometry: Curves are commonly represented as $\mathbf{r}(t)$.
  • Implicit Geometry: Equations such as $F(x,y)=0$ can define curves without isolating a coordinate.
  • Calculus: Tangent lines, derivatives, arc length, curvature, and line integrals are studied along curves.
  • Differential Geometry: Smoothness and regularity control tangent and curvature behavior.
  • Topology: Curves may be treated as continuous images of intervals, with attention to connectedness, loops, and embeddings.
  • Motion: A parameterized curve can represent the trajectory of a moving object.
  • Closed Curve: Ends at the same geometric point at which it begins.
  • Simple Curve: Avoids self-intersection under the relevant definition.
  • Common Error: Assuming every curve must be expressible globally as $y=f(x)$.
  • Common Error: Confusing the path's geometric image with the speed or direction of a particular parameterization.
  • Common Error: Assuming a curve must visibly bend; a straight line is also a curve in standard mathematical usage.
  • Core Interpretation: A curve is fundamentally a one-dimensional path through a space: $$ \boxed{ t \longmapsto \mathbf{r}(t) } $$ with its geometric form determined by the points traced rather than by any single coordinate representation.
