Intermediate Value Theorem

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

The name Intermediate Value Theorem describes exactly what the theorem guarantees: a continuous function takes every intermediate value lying between two of its attained function values.

The adjective intermediate means "lying between." Thus, if a continuous function has value $2$ at one endpoint and value $8$ at another, then every number between $2$ and $8$ must occur somewhere in between.

The theorem is one of the foundational results concerning continuity. It formalizes the intuitive idea that a continuous graph cannot jump from one height to another without passing through all heights in between.

This intuitive picture is useful, but the theorem is stronger than a visual slogan. It is a precise existence theorem: under stated hypotheses, it guarantees that a particular input exists even if the theorem gives no formula for finding that input.

Operational Nuance

Suppose:

$$ f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R} $$

is continuous on the closed interval $[a,b]$.

Let $N$ be any number lying between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$. Then the Intermediate Value Theorem guarantees that there exists at least one point:

$$ c\in[a,b] $$

such that:

$$ \boxed{ f(c)=N }. $$

If:

$$ f(a)

then the corresponding point must occur in the open interval:

$$ c\in(a,b). $$

The same conclusion applies when the endpoint values occur in the opposite order:

$$ f(b)

A compact way to state the theorem is:

$$ \boxed{ N\text{ between }f(a)\text{ and }f(b) \Longrightarrow \exists c\in[a,b]\text{ such that }f(c)=N } $$

provided that $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$.

For example, suppose:

$$ f(1)=3 $$

and:

$$ f(5)=11, $$

with $f$ continuous on $[1,5]$.

Since:

$$ 3<7<11, $$

the Intermediate Value Theorem guarantees some:

$$ c\in(1,5) $$

for which:

$$ f(c)=7. $$

The theorem does not tell us what $c$ is. It tells us that such a point must exist.

A particularly important special case occurs when the endpoint values have opposite signs:

$$ f(a)<0

or:

$$ f(b)<0

Since $0$ lies between the endpoint values, there must exist:

$$ c\in(a,b) $$

such that:

$$ \boxed{ f(c)=0 }. $$

This special application is often called the Bolzano zero theorem or simply the root form of the Intermediate Value Theorem.

Common Misconception: The theorem does not guarantee that there is exactly one such point $c$. A continuous function may take the same intermediate value many times.

For instance:

$$ f(x)=\sin x $$

may equal the same value repeatedly over a sufficiently large interval. The theorem guarantees existence, not uniqueness.

The condition:

$$ f(a)f(b)<0 $$

is a convenient shorthand indicating that $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ have opposite signs. When $f$ is continuous, this implies that the function must cross or at least meet the $x$-axis somewhere between $a$ and $b$.

However, the general theorem is broader than zero-finding. The intermediate value can be any real number $N$ between the two endpoint values:

$$ f(a)

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish the input $c$ from the intermediate function value $N$. The theorem guarantees a point $c$ in the domain such that the corresponding output is $N$:

$$ c\longmapsto f(c)=N. $$

The number $N$ is not generally the location on the $x$-axis.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Existence of Roots: If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and: $$ f(a)f(b)<0, $$ then: $$ \exists c\in(a,b) \text{ such that } f(c)=0. $$ This is one of the most common applications of the theorem.
  • Example of Root Existence: Consider: $$ f(x)=x^3-x-2. $$ Then: $$ f(1)=-2 $$ and: $$ f(2)=4. $$ Since $f$ is a polynomial and therefore continuous: $$ f(1)<0
  • Existence Without Exact Solution: The Intermediate Value Theorem can prove that a solution exists even when the solution cannot easily be written in elementary closed form. This makes it an existence theorem rather than a direct solving algorithm.
  • Bisection Method: The theorem provides the theoretical basis for the numerical bisection method. If opposite signs occur at two endpoints, repeatedly halving the interval and retaining a sign-changing half produces increasingly narrow intervals containing a root.
  • No Guarantee of Uniqueness: Knowing: $$ f(a)f(b)<0 $$ proves at least one root exists, but several roots may lie inside the interval. Additional information, such as strict monotonicity, is needed to guarantee uniqueness.
  • Continuity Is Essential: Consider: $$ f(x)= \begin{cases} -1, & x<0,\\ 1, & x\ge0. \end{cases} $$ The function takes negative and positive values but never equals $0$. The jump discontinuity prevents the Intermediate Value Theorem from applying.
  • Endpoint Order Does Not Matter: The theorem works whether: $$ f(a)
  • Intermediate Values of Continuous Functions: If a continuous function takes values $u$ and $v$, then it takes every real value between them. Continuous functions therefore cannot omit an interior value while taking values on both sides of it.
  • Images of Intervals: A deeper formulation says that the continuous image of an interval is itself an interval. This is the structural reason continuous real functions possess the intermediate value property.
  • Connectedness: In topology and real analysis, intervals are connected subsets of $\mathbb{R}$, and continuous functions preserve connectedness. Since connected subsets of $\mathbb{R}$ are intervals, a continuous image cannot contain two values while omitting a value between them.
  • Darboux Property: The property of taking every intermediate value is called the intermediate value property or Darboux property. Every continuous real-valued function has this property.
  • Derivatives: Remarkably, derivatives have the intermediate value property even when the derivative itself is not continuous. This result is known as Darboux's Theorem. Thus derivatives cannot have ordinary jump discontinuities.
  • Relationship to the Extreme Value Theorem: Both the Intermediate Value Theorem and Extreme Value Theorem rely on continuity, but they guarantee different things. The Intermediate Value Theorem guarantees that values between two attained outputs are also attained. The Extreme Value Theorem guarantees that a continuous function on a closed bounded interval actually attains a highest and lowest value.
  • Relationship to the Mean Value Theorem: The Intermediate Value Theorem concerns function values and requires continuity. The Mean Value Theorem concerns derivatives and additionally requires differentiability on the interior of the interval.
  • Root-Finding vs. Root-Existence: The theorem can prove: $$ \exists c\text{ such that }f(c)=0, $$ but it does not by itself calculate $c$. Algebraic methods, numerical methods, or other theorems may be needed to approximate or determine the root.
  • Terminological Contrast: An intermediate value is an output lying between two other function values. A zero is an input where the function equals $0$. The Intermediate Value Theorem guarantees that continuous functions attain all intervening values, while its zero-finding application uses $N=0$ to prove that a root exists between opposite-sign endpoint values.
