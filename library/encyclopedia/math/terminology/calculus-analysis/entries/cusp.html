Cusp

Subfield: Calculus & Analysis

Etymology & Roots

A cusp is a sharp singular point of a curve where two branches meet with a common tangent direction but the curve fails to be smoothly regular there. Visually, a cusp often resembles a pointed tip.

The word comes from Latin cuspis, meaning "point" or "tip." In mathematics, it describes a particular kind of nonsmooth behavior rather than every sharp-looking point.

$$ \boxed{ \text{cusp} = \text{pointed singular behavior of a curve} } $$

Operational Nuance

Standard Function Example

A common elementary example is:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=x^{2/3} } $$

whose graph has a cusp at $x=0$. For $x\neq0$:

$$ f'(x)=\frac{2}{3}x^{-1/3}. $$

As $x\to0^-$, the derivative tends to $-\infty$, while as $x\to0^+$ it tends to $+\infty$.

Parametric Form

The same semicubical cusp can be written parametrically as:

$$ \boxed{ x=t^3, \qquad y=t^2 } $$

which satisfies:

$$ y^3=x^2. $$

Loss of Regularity

In a parametric description $\mathbf{r}(t)$, a cusp often occurs where the velocity vector vanishes:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{r}'(t_0)=\mathbf{0} } $$

although vanishing velocity alone does not automatically guarantee that the geometric image has a cusp.

Cusp vs. Corner

At a corner, two finite one-sided slopes typically differ. At a cusp, the one-sided tangent behavior usually becomes unbounded or the curve reverses direction through a shared tangent line.

Cusp vs. Vertical Tangent

A vertical tangent may occur when both sides approach the same infinite slope direction while the curve passes smoothly through the point. A cusp typically involves a reversal or pointed singularity rather than smooth passage.

Critical-Number Connection

If a function is defined at a cusp but its derivative does not exist there, the input is commonly included among the function's critical numbers.

Common Misconception

Every nondifferentiable point is not a cusp. Corners, discontinuities, vertical tangents, and other singularities are distinct phenomena.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$f(x)=x^{2/3}$ "A standard function with a cusp at the origin."
$f'(x)\to-\infty$ from the left "The left-hand slope becomes negatively unbounded."
$f'(x)\to+\infty$ from the right "The right-hand slope becomes positively unbounded."
$x=t^3,\ y=t^2$ "A parametric representation of a semicubical cusp."
$\mathbf{r}'(t_0)=\mathbf{0}$ "The parameterization is not regular at t sub zero."
$y^3=x^2$ "The implicit equation of a standard semicubical cusp."

In elementary calculus, cusp often refers to a graph point where the derivative becomes oppositely infinite from the two sides. In differential and algebraic geometry, the word is used more broadly for specific singularities of curves.

Defense Notation Advice: Do not label a point a cusp solely because $f'(c)$ is undefined. Examine the one-sided tangent behavior or the local parametric geometry to distinguish cusps from corners and vertical tangents.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Calculus: A cusp is a characteristic type of nondifferentiable point.
  • Standard Example: $y=x^{2/3}$ has a cusp at the origin.
  • Parametric Curves: Cusps often correspond to parameter values where the velocity vector vanishes.
  • Semicubical Cusp: A classical model is $x=t^3,\ y=t^2$, equivalently $y^3=x^2$.
  • Critical Numbers: A cusp can create a critical number because the derivative fails to exist while the function remains defined.
  • Geometry: A cusp has a pointed local shape and generally fails smooth regularity.
  • Differential Geometry: The parameterized curve may fail to be regular at the cusp.
  • Algebraic Geometry: Cusps are studied as singular points of algebraic curves.
  • Corner Contrast: Corners typically have distinct finite one-sided tangent slopes.
  • Vertical-Tangent Contrast: Infinite slope alone does not establish a cusp.
  • Common Error: Calling every point where $f'$ is undefined a cusp.
  • Common Error: Confusing a cusp with a corner because both appear visually sharp.
  • Common Error: Assuming $\mathbf{r}'(t_0)=\mathbf{0}$ by itself proves the geometric curve has a cusp.
  • Core Interpretation: A cusp is a specific singular form of sharp curve behavior: $$ \boxed{ \text{smooth branch} \longrightarrow \text{pointed singularity} \longrightarrow \text{smooth branch} }. $$ Its identification depends on local tangent and parameter behavior, not merely on the failure of an ordinary derivative.
