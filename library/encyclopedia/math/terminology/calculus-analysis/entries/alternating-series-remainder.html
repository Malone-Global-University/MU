Alternating Series Remainder

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

The alternating series remainder is the portion of a convergent alternating series that remains after a finite number of terms has been used to approximate the full infinite sum.

If:

$$ S=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n $$

is the exact sum and:

$$ S_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_n $$

is the $N$th partial sum, then the remainder is:

$$ \boxed{ R_N=S-S_N }. $$

The word remainder therefore refers to what remains after truncating the infinite series at term $N$.

In the special case of an alternating series satisfying the standard convergence conditions, the remainder has a particularly useful bound:

$$ \boxed{ |R_N| \le b_{N+1} }. $$

Here $b_{N+1}$ is the magnitude of the first omitted term.

Operational Nuance

Alternating Series Setup

Consider a series of the form:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n-1}b_n $$

where:

$$ b_n\ge0. $$

Suppose the magnitudes are eventually nonincreasing and satisfy:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{n\to\infty}b_n=0 }. $$

Then the series converges by the Alternating Series Test.

Partial Sum

The $N$th partial sum is:

$$ \boxed{ S_N = \sum_{n=1}^{N}(-1)^{n-1}b_n }. $$

This is the finite approximation obtained by stopping after the first $N$ terms.

Remainder

The exact error in that approximation is:

$$ \boxed{ R_N=S-S_N }. $$

Because $S$ is usually unknown, $R_N$ cannot normally be computed directly. The usefulness of the alternating-series remainder theorem is that it bounds the error without requiring the exact sum.

Alternating Series Remainder Estimate

If the Alternating Series Test conditions are satisfied, then:

$$ \boxed{ |S-S_N| \le b_{N+1} }. $$

Equivalently:

$$ \boxed{ |R_N| \le b_{N+1} }. $$

The magnitude of the error is no larger than the magnitude of the first omitted term.

Why the First Omitted Term Controls the Error

In a decreasing alternating series, successive terms pull the partial sums back and forth across the limiting value by progressively smaller amounts.

The next unused term represents the maximum size of the next correction. Since later corrections are smaller and alternate direction, the true sum must lie within that first omitted amount of the current partial sum.

Odd and Even Partial Sums

For a positive-first alternating series with decreasing term magnitudes:

$$ S_1,S_3,S_5,\ldots $$

approach the sum from one side, while:

$$ S_2,S_4,S_6,\ldots $$

approach from the other.

Thus consecutive partial sums bracket the true sum.

Sign of the Remainder

Under the standard decreasing alternating setup, the remainder has the same sign as the first omitted term.

If:

$$ a_{N+1}>0, $$

then:

$$ R_N>0. $$

If:

$$ a_{N+1}<0, $$

then:

$$ R_N<0. $$

Upper and Lower Bounds

If consecutive partial sums are $S_N$ and $S_{N+1}$, then the exact sum lies between them:

$$ \boxed{ \min(S_N,S_{N+1}) \le S \le \max(S_N,S_{N+1}) }. $$

This gives more information than a simple error magnitude because it identifies an actual interval containing the sum.

Alternating Harmonic Example

Consider:

$$ S= 1-\frac12+\frac13-\frac14+\cdots. $$

If the approximation stops after $10$ terms, then:

$$ |R_{10}| \le \frac1{11}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ |S-S_{10}| \le \frac1{11} }. $$

Required Accuracy

Suppose an approximation must have error less than $\varepsilon$. It is sufficient to choose $N$ so that:

$$ \boxed{ b_{N+1}<\varepsilon }. $$

For example, to make the alternating harmonic error less than $0.001$, require:

$$ \frac1{N+1}<0.001. $$

Hence:

$$ N+1>1000, $$

so one may take:

$$ \boxed{N=1000}. $$

Strict vs. Nonstrict Accuracy

If the problem requires:

$$ |R_N|<\varepsilon, $$

then use:

$$ b_{N+1}<\varepsilon. $$

If the requirement is:

$$ |R_N|\le\varepsilon, $$

then:

$$ b_{N+1}\le\varepsilon $$

is sufficient.

Not the Exact Error

The inequality:

$$ |R_N|\le b_{N+1} $$

gives an upper bound, not generally the exact value of the error.

Thus one should not write:

$$ R_N=b_{N+1} $$

unless a separate argument establishes that equality.

Common Misconception: The remainder estimate does not apply merely because a series alternates signs. The hypotheses behind the Alternating Series Test must be satisfied.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$S$ "The exact sum of the series."
$S_N$ "The Nth partial sum."
$R_N=S-S_N$ "The remainder after N terms equals the exact sum minus the Nth partial sum."
$|R_N|\le b_{N+1}$ "The error is at most the magnitude of the first omitted term."
$b_{N+1}<\varepsilon$ "The first omitted term is smaller than epsilon."
$|S-S_N|<0.001$ "The approximation error is less than one thousandth."

The words remainder and error are closely related but emphasize different viewpoints.

The remainder is the signed quantity:

$$ R_N=S-S_N. $$

The approximation error is often reported as its magnitude:

$$ |R_N|. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Keep the index straight. If $S_N$ includes terms through $n=N$, the first omitted term is the $(N+1)$st term, so the error bound involves $b_{N+1}$, not $b_N$.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Exact Sum: $$ S=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n. $$
  • Partial Sum: $$ \boxed{ S_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_n }. $$
  • Remainder: $$ \boxed{ R_N=S-S_N }. $$
  • Error Magnitude: $$ \boxed{ |R_N|=|S-S_N| }. $$
  • Alternating Series Remainder Bound: $$ \boxed{ |R_N|\le b_{N+1} }. $$
  • First Omitted Term: If $S_N$ stops at term $N$, then term $N+1$ is the first omitted term.
  • Hypothesis: The standard estimate requires the term magnitudes to eventually decrease and satisfy: $$ \lim_{n\to\infty}b_n=0. $$
  • Alternating Series Test Connection: The same hypotheses that establish convergence also produce the standard remainder estimate.
  • Error Tolerance: To guarantee: $$ |R_N|<\varepsilon, $$ it is sufficient to require: $$ \boxed{ b_{N+1}<\varepsilon }. $$
  • Number of Terms: Solve the inequality involving $b_{N+1}$ to determine how many terms are needed.
  • Sign of Remainder: Under the standard hypotheses, $R_N$ has the same sign as the first omitted term.
  • Overestimate: If the first omitted term is negative, then the current partial sum is above the exact sum.
  • Underestimate: If the first omitted term is positive, then the current partial sum is below the exact sum.
  • Bracketing: Consecutive partial sums bracket the exact value: $$ \boxed{ \min(S_N,S_{N+1}) \le S\le \max(S_N,S_{N+1}) }. $$
  • Alternating Harmonic Series: For: $$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}n, $$ the error after $N$ terms satisfies: $$ \boxed{ |R_N|\le\frac1{N+1} }. $$
  • Alternating p-Series: For: $$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n^p}, \qquad p>0, $$ one has: $$ \boxed{ |R_N| \le \frac1{(N+1)^p} }. $$
  • Alternating Geometric Series: For a convergent alternating geometric series, the exact geometric remainder can sometimes be computed, while the alternating-series estimate provides a simpler general bound.
  • Taylor Series: Alternating Taylor series can often use the same first-omitted-term estimate when their term magnitudes decrease appropriately.
  • Sine Approximation: For suitable $x$, truncating: $$ \sin x = x-\frac{x^3}{3!}+\frac{x^5}{5!}-\cdots $$ may permit an alternating-series remainder estimate.
  • Cosine Approximation: Likewise: $$ \cos x = 1-\frac{x^2}{2!}+\frac{x^4}{4!}-\cdots. $$
  • Logarithm Approximation: For appropriate $x$: $$ \ln(1+x) = x-\frac{x^2}{2}+\frac{x^3}{3}-\cdots $$ can yield an alternating-series error bound.
  • Not the Lagrange Remainder: The alternating-series remainder estimate is distinct from the Lagrange remainder formula used for general Taylor polynomials.
  • Lagrange Remainder Contrast: A Taylor polynomial may instead use: $$ R_n(x) = \frac{f^{(n+1)}(c)}{(n+1)!} (x-a)^{n+1} $$ for some appropriate $c$.
  • Bound vs. Exact Value: $$ |R_N|\le b_{N+1} $$ does not generally imply: $$ |R_N|=b_{N+1}. $$
  • Absolute Convergence: An alternating series may converge absolutely, but the alternating-series remainder theorem can still provide a convenient approximation bound when its hypotheses hold.
  • Conditional Convergence: The estimate is especially useful for conditionally convergent alternating series, where cancellation is essential.
  • Numerical Approximation: The theorem gives an efficient stopping criterion without requiring the exact value of the infinite sum.
  • Precision: To guarantee $k$ decimal places, convert the desired decimal accuracy into an explicit error tolerance and solve: $$ b_{N+1}<\varepsilon. $$
  • Rounding Caution: An error bound should be sufficiently small relative to the desired rounding place if one wants the rounded decimal to be guaranteed correct.
  • Indexing Caution: If a series begins at $n=0$ instead of $n=1$, identify the first omitted term from the actual truncation rather than mechanically using the same index.
  • Monotonicity Caution: The magnitude sequence need only be eventually nonincreasing; a finite number of early irregularities does not affect convergence or sufficiently late remainder estimates.
  • Terminological Contrast: The partial sum $S_N$ is the finite approximation. The remainder $R_N=S-S_N$ is what remains. The alternating-series remainder estimate gives $|R_N|\le b_{N+1}$ when the standard decreasing-to-zero hypotheses are satisfied.
