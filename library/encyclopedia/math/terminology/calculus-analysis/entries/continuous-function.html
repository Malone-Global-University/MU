Continuous Function

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

A continuous function is a function that preserves limiting behavior: when inputs approach a point in the domain, the corresponding outputs approach the function's actual value at that point.

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a) }. $$

The word continuous comes from roots associated with holding together or proceeding without interruption. In elementary graphing language, this motivates the familiar idea of a curve without a local break, hole, or jump.

The mathematical concept is more precise than the visual intuition. Continuity is fundamentally a relationship among nearby inputs, nearby outputs, limits, and the value actually assigned by the function.

$$ \boxed{ \text{continuous function} = \text{function preserving sufficiently close behavior} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Continuity at a Point

A function $f$ is continuous at $x=a$ when:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a) }. $$

In elementary calculus this is often separated into three requirements:

  • $f(a)$ is defined,
  • $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)$ exists, and
  • $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a)$.

Why the Function Value Matters

A limit describes nearby behavior, while $f(a)$ describes the value actually assigned at the point.

These can differ. For example, suppose:

$$ f(x)= \begin{cases} x+2,&x\ne1,\\ 7,&x=1. \end{cases} $$

Then:

$$ \lim_{x\to1}f(x)=3 $$

but:

$$ f(1)=7. $$

Therefore $f$ is not continuous at $1$.

Continuity throughout a Domain

A function is called continuous on a set $D$ when it is continuous at every point of that set, with the topology or endpoint conventions appropriate to the setting.

$$ \boxed{ f\text{ continuous on }D \iff f\text{ continuous at every point of }D }. $$

Example: Polynomial Function

Let:

$$ f(x)=x^3-4x+1. $$

Polynomial functions are continuous on all real numbers:

$$ \boxed{ f\text{ is continuous on }\mathbb R }. $$

Direct Substitution

If a function is known to be continuous at $a$, its limit can be evaluated by direct substitution:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a) }. $$

For example:

$$ \lim_{x\to2}(x^2+3x) = 2^2+3(2) = 10. $$

Epsilon-Delta Definition

Formally, a real-valued function $f$ is continuous at $a$ if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists $\delta>0$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ |x-a|<\delta \Rightarrow |f(x)-f(a)|<\varepsilon }. $$

Here $x$ is restricted to the domain of $f$.

Interpretation of Epsilon and Delta

$\varepsilon$ specifies a desired tolerance in the output:

$$ |f(x)-f(a)|<\varepsilon. $$

$\delta$ specifies how closely the input must remain near $a$:

$$ |x-a|<\delta. $$

Continuity means that every required degree of output closeness can be guaranteed by sufficiently restricting the input.

Sequential Criterion

In metric spaces such as $\mathbb R$, $f$ is continuous at $a$ exactly when every sequence approaching $a$ has images approaching $f(a)$:

$$ \boxed{ x_n\to a \Rightarrow f(x_n)\to f(a) }. $$

One-Sided Continuity

A function may be continuous from only one side.

Right continuity at $a$ means:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)=f(a) }. $$

Left continuity at $a$ means:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a^-}f(x)=f(a) }. $$

Continuity on a Closed Interval

A real-valued function is continuous on $[a,b]$ when it is:

  • continuous at every point in $(a,b)$,
  • right-continuous at $a$, and
  • left-continuous at $b$.
$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)=f(a), \qquad \lim_{x\to b^-}f(x)=f(b) }. $$

Graphical Interpretation

For ordinary real functions, a continuous graph has no local gap, hole, or jump at the point where continuity is asserted.

The informal phrase “draw the graph without lifting the pencil” can be useful for intuition, but it is not the formal definition.

Removable Discontinuity

Consider:

$$ f(x)=\frac{x^2-4}{x-2}. $$

For $x\ne2$:

$$ f(x)=x+2. $$

Therefore:

$$ \lim_{x\to2}f(x)=4, $$

but $f(2)$ is not defined in the original expression.

The discontinuity is removable because defining:

$$ \boxed{ f(2)=4 } $$

creates a continuous extension.

Jump Discontinuity

A jump occurs when:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a^-}f(x) \ne \lim_{x\to a^+}f(x) }. $$

The two-sided limit therefore fails to exist.

Infinite Discontinuity

A function such as:

$$ f(x)=\frac1{x-3} $$

becomes unbounded near $x=3$, so it is not continuous there.

Oscillatory Discontinuity

A function can fail to have a limit because its values oscillate indefinitely near a point.

A standard example is:

$$ f(x)=\sin\left(\frac1x\right) $$

as $x\to0$.

Polynomials

Every real polynomial function is continuous on $\mathbb R$:

$$ \boxed{ p(x)\text{ continuous for every }x\in\mathbb R }. $$

Rational Functions

If:

$$ f(x)=\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}, $$

then $f$ is continuous wherever:

$$ \boxed{ q(x)\ne0 }. $$

Root Functions

Root functions are continuous on their real domains. For example:

$$ f(x)=\sqrt{x} $$

is continuous for:

$$ \boxed{x\ge0}. $$

Exponential Functions

For $a>0$:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=a^x \text{ is continuous on }\mathbb R }. $$

Logarithmic Functions

For $a>0$ and $a\ne1$:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=\log_a x \text{ is continuous for }x>0 }. $$

Trigonometric Functions

Sine and cosine are continuous for all real $x$:

$$ \boxed{ \sin x,\cos x \text{ continuous on }\mathbb R }. $$

Tangent and secant are continuous wherever $\cos x\ne0$; cotangent and cosecant are continuous wherever $\sin x\ne0$.

Absolute Value Function

The function:

$$ f(x)=|x| $$

is continuous everywhere.

It is not differentiable at $x=0$, providing a fundamental example that continuity does not imply differentiability.

Constant Functions

Every constant function:

$$ f(x)=c $$

is continuous throughout its domain:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a}c=c=f(a) }. $$

Identity Function

The identity function:

$$ I(x)=x $$

is continuous on $\mathbb R$.

Sum of Continuous Functions

If $f$ and $g$ are continuous at $a$, then:

$$ \boxed{ f+g \text{ is continuous at }a }. $$

Difference of Continuous Functions

$$ \boxed{ f-g \text{ is continuous at }a }. $$

Product of Continuous Functions

$$ \boxed{ fg \text{ is continuous at }a }. $$

Quotient of Continuous Functions

If $f$ and $g$ are continuous at $a$ and $g(a)\ne0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{f}{g} \text{ is continuous at }a }. $$

Constant Multiples

If $f$ is continuous and $c$ is constant:

$$ \boxed{ cf \text{ is continuous} }. $$

Composition

If $g$ is continuous at $a$ and $f$ is continuous at $g(a)$, then:

$$ \boxed{ (f\circ g)(x)=f(g(x)) \text{ is continuous at }a }. $$

Passing a Limit through a Continuous Function

If:

$$ \lim_{x\to a}g(x)=L $$

and $f$ is continuous at $L$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a}f(g(x)) = f(L) }. $$

Piecewise Continuous Matching

Suppose:

$$ f(x)= \begin{cases} g(x),&x

To be continuous at $a$, the pieces must meet:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a^-}g(x) = h(a) = \lim_{x\to a^+}h(x) }. $$

Parameter Example

Consider:

$$ f(x)= \begin{cases} x^2+1,&x<2,\\ kx-1,&x\ge2. \end{cases} $$

For continuity at $2$, the left-hand value approaches:

$$ 2^2+1=5. $$

The right-hand formula gives:

$$ 2k-1. $$

Therefore:

$$ 2k-1=5, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{k=3}. $$

Differentiability Implies Continuity

If $f$ is differentiable at $a$, then $f$ is continuous there:

$$ \boxed{ f'(a)\text{ exists} \Rightarrow f\text{ continuous at }a }. $$

Continuity Does Not Imply Differentiability

The converse fails:

$$ \boxed{ \text{continuous} \not\Rightarrow \text{differentiable} }. $$

The function $|x|$ at $x=0$ is the standard example.

Intermediate Value Theorem

If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, then every number $N$ lying between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ occurs as a function value somewhere on that interval:

$$ \boxed{ \exists c\in[a,b] \text{ such that } f(c)=N }. $$

Root Existence Corollary

If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and:

$$ f(a)f(b)<0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \exists c\in(a,b) \text{ such that }f(c)=0 }. $$

Extreme Value Theorem

A real-valued function continuous on a closed bounded interval attains both an absolute minimum and an absolute maximum.

$$ \boxed{ f\text{ continuous on }[a,b] \Rightarrow f\text{ attains min and max} }. $$

Boundedness on Compact Sets

A continuous real-valued function on a compact set is bounded.

Uniform Continuity

Uniform continuity strengthens ordinary continuity. For every $\varepsilon>0$, there must be a single $\delta>0$ that works throughout the domain:

$$ \boxed{ |x-y|<\delta \Rightarrow |f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon }. $$

Pointwise versus Uniform Continuity

In ordinary pointwise continuity, the required $\delta$ may depend on the point. In uniform continuity, it may not.

$$ \boxed{ \text{uniform continuity} \Rightarrow \text{continuity} }. $$

Heine-Cantor Theorem

Every continuous function on a compact metric-space domain is uniformly continuous. In particular:

$$ \boxed{ f\text{ continuous on }[a,b] \Rightarrow f\text{ uniformly continuous on }[a,b] }. $$

Continuous Functions and Connected Sets

The continuous image of a connected set is connected.

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ connected} \Rightarrow f(A)\text{ connected} }. $$

The Intermediate Value Theorem is closely related to this principle on intervals.

Continuous Functions and Compact Sets

The continuous image of a compact set is compact:

$$ \boxed{ K\text{ compact} \Rightarrow f(K)\text{ compact} }. $$

Topological Definition

Let:

$$ f:X\to Y. $$

In topology, $f$ is continuous if the preimage of every open subset of $Y$ is open in $X$:

$$ \boxed{ U\subseteq Y\text{ open} \Rightarrow f^{-1}(U)\text{ open in }X }. $$

Why Preimages Are Used

Images of open sets under a continuous function need not be open. Continuity is defined using inverse images of open sets.

Continuous Maps

In topology, the expressions continuous function and continuous map are often synonymous.

Homeomorphism

A bijection:

$$ f:X\to Y $$

is a homeomorphism if both $f$ and $f^{-1}$ are continuous.

$$ \boxed{ f\text{ homeomorphism} \iff f\text{ bijective},\ f\text{ continuous},\ f^{-1}\text{ continuous} }. $$

Vector-Valued Functions

A curve:

$$ \mathbf r(t) = \langle x(t),y(t),z(t)\rangle $$

is continuous when its component functions are continuous:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf r \text{ continuous} \iff x,y,z \text{ continuous} }. $$

Multivariable Continuous Functions

For:

$$ f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m, $$

continuity at $\mathbf a$ means:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{\mathbf x\to\mathbf a}f(\mathbf x) = f(\mathbf a) }. $$

Approaching from Many Directions

In several variables, a point can be approached along infinitely many paths. Matching limits along only a few selected paths does not generally prove continuity.

Componentwise Continuity

For:

$$ F(\mathbf x) = \langle f_1(\mathbf x),\ldots,f_m(\mathbf x)\rangle, $$

$F$ is continuous exactly when each component $f_i$ is continuous.

Restriction of a Continuous Function

If $f:D\to Y$ is continuous and $A\subseteq D$, then the restriction:

$$ f|_A:A\to Y $$

is continuous with the induced topology.

Inverse of a Continuous Function

A continuous bijection does not automatically have a continuous inverse on arbitrary spaces.

Additional hypotheses may be needed, or continuity of the inverse must be verified separately.

Monotone Continuous Functions

A continuous strictly monotone real function on an interval is one-to-one and has a continuous inverse on its range.

Continuous Function versus Continuous Variable

A continuous function is a property of a mapping. A continuous variable is a variable modeled as ranging through a continuum of values.

The two uses of continuous are related conceptually but not identical.

Continuous Function versus Continuous Random Variable

A continuous random variable is a probability-theoretic object. It does not mean that the random-variable mapping must be continuous as a function in the calculus sense.

Continuous Function versus Differentiable Function

Differentiability is stronger:

$$ \boxed{ \text{differentiable} \Rightarrow \text{continuous} }. $$

Continuous Function versus Smooth Function

A smooth function is normally infinitely differentiable:

$$ \boxed{ f\in C^\infty }. $$

Smooth functions are continuous, but continuous functions need not be smooth.

Continuity-Class Notation

The notation:

$$ \boxed{C^0} $$

commonly denotes the class of continuous functions.

$C^1$ denotes continuously differentiable functions, while $C^k$ denotes functions whose derivatives through order $k$ are continuous.

Analytic Functions

Analytic functions are locally represented by convergent power series. Under the usual real or complex settings:

$$ \boxed{ \text{analytic} \Rightarrow \text{smooth} \Rightarrow \text{continuous} }. $$

Continuous but Nowhere Differentiable

Some functions are continuous at every point yet differentiable nowhere. Such examples demonstrate that continuity is substantially weaker than smoothness.

Constant Function as a Continuous Function

If:

$$ f(x)=c, $$

then the output difference between any two inputs is zero:

$$ |f(x)-f(y)|=0. $$

Thus every constant function is continuous and uniformly continuous.

Continuity Is Local

A function may fail to be continuous at one point but remain continuous everywhere else.

$$ \boxed{ \text{one discontinuity} \not\Rightarrow \text{discontinuous everywhere} }. $$

Continuity Is Relative to the Domain

The function:

$$ f(x)=\frac1x $$

is continuous on its natural real domain:

$$ \boxed{ (-\infty,0)\cup(0,\infty) }. $$

The fact that $x=0$ is excluded does not prevent the function from being continuous everywhere it is defined.

Continuous Does Not Mean Bounded

The function:

$$ f(x)=x $$

is continuous on $\mathbb R$ but unbounded.

Continuous Does Not Mean Monotonic

The function:

$$ f(x)=\sin x $$

is continuous but repeatedly changes between increasing and decreasing.

Continuous Does Not Mean One-to-One

The function:

$$ f(x)=x^2 $$

is continuous on $\mathbb R$ but not injective.

Continuous Does Not Mean Constant Rate

A continuous function may have a changing derivative. For:

$$ f(x)=x^2, $$

the derivative is:

$$ f'(x)=2x. $$

Continuous Does Not Mean Visually Smooth

Corners, cusps, and other nonsmooth behavior may occur without breaking continuity.

Common Misconception: The Pencil Test Is a Formal Definition

“Drawing without lifting the pencil” is only an informal aid. Formal continuity uses limits, neighborhoods, sequences, or inverse images of open sets.

Common Misconception: A Hole Means the Function Is Discontinuous Everywhere

A removable hole creates a local failure of continuity at that point only.

Common Misconception: If a Limit Exists, the Function Is Continuous

The function value must also exist and equal the limit.

Common Misconception: If f(a) Exists, the Function Is Continuous

Merely assigning a value at $a$ does not guarantee that nearby function values approach that same value.

Common Misconception: Every Continuous Function Is Differentiable

Continuity is necessary for differentiability but not sufficient.

Common Misconception: Every Continuous Function Is Uniformly Continuous

Uniform continuity is stronger. For example:

$$ f(x)=x^2 $$

is continuous on $\mathbb R$ but not uniformly continuous there.

Common Misconception: Continuity Requires a Real Interval Domain

Continuity applies to functions between general metric and topological spaces, far beyond real-variable calculus.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a)$ "f is continuous at a."
$f\in C^0$ "f is continuous."
$f\in C^1$ "f is continuously differentiable."
$\lim_{x\to a^-}f(x)=f(a)$ "f is left-continuous at a."
$\lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)=f(a)$ "f is right-continuous at a."
$x_n\to a\Rightarrow f(x_n)\to f(a)$ "f preserves convergence at a."
$f^{-1}(U)$ is open for every open $U$ "f is continuous in the topological sense."

The phrase continuous function ordinarily means a function that is continuous at every point of the stated domain or every point of the region being discussed. When continuity is claimed only at one point, mathematical writing usually says explicitly that the function is continuous at $a$.

In elementary calculus, continuity is often spoken of graphically. In analysis and topology, however, the same term is understood through precise limit, neighborhood, sequence, or open-set formulations.

Defense Notation Advice: Specify the domain or interval on which continuity is claimed. At endpoints, use the appropriate one-sided notion. For piecewise functions, verify the function value and both applicable one-sided limits. Do not use continuous as a synonym for differentiable, smooth, bounded, or uniformly continuous. In topology, remember that continuity is defined using inverse images of open sets, not by requiring images of open sets to remain open.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: A continuous function preserves limiting behavior so that $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a)$.
  • Pointwise Continuity: Continuity may be asserted at an individual point.
  • Continuity on a Set: Means continuity at every relevant point of that set.
  • Epsilon-Delta Form: Arbitrarily small output tolerances can be guaranteed through sufficiently small input tolerances.
  • Sequential Form: $x_n\to a$ implies $f(x_n)\to f(a)$ in the usual metric-space setting.
  • Topological Form: Preimages of open sets are open.
  • Right Continuity: $\lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)=f(a)$.
  • Left Continuity: $\lim_{x\to a^-}f(x)=f(a)$.
  • Closed Intervals: Ordinary continuity in the interior with appropriate one-sided continuity at endpoints.
  • Polynomials: Continuous on all of $\mathbb R$.
  • Rational Functions: Continuous wherever the denominator is nonzero.
  • Root Functions: Continuous on their real domains.
  • Exponential Functions: Continuous on their natural domains.
  • Logarithmic Functions: Continuous for positive arguments.
  • Trigonometric Functions: Continuous wherever defined.
  • Constant Functions: Always continuous on their domains.
  • Algebra of Continuous Functions: Sums, differences, products, constant multiples, and appropriate quotients preserve continuity.
  • Composition: Composition of continuous functions is continuous wherever defined.
  • Piecewise Functions: Boundary formulas must join through matching one-sided limits and function value.
  • Removable Discontinuity: May be repaired by assigning the limiting value.
  • Jump Discontinuity: Occurs when one-sided limits disagree.
  • Infinite Discontinuity: Occurs when function values become unbounded near a point.
  • Differentiability: Differentiability implies continuity.
  • Converse Warning: Continuity does not imply differentiability.
  • Intermediate Value Theorem: Continuous functions on intervals attain every intermediate value.
  • Extreme Value Theorem: Continuous real functions on closed bounded intervals attain absolute maxima and minima.
  • Uniform Continuity: Stronger than ordinary continuity because one $\delta$ must work throughout the domain for each $\varepsilon$.
  • Compactness: Continuous images of compact sets are compact.
  • Connectedness: Continuous images of connected sets are connected.
  • Topology: A continuous function is commonly called a continuous map.
  • Vector-Valued Functions: Continuity can be checked componentwise in Euclidean spaces.
  • Multivariable Functions: Continuity requires agreement under all approaches to a point.
  • Continuous-Variable Contrast: A continuous variable ranges through a continuum; a continuous function is a property of a mapping.
  • Continuous-Random-Variable Contrast: A continuous random variable is a probability concept and does not mean the mapping itself must be continuous in the calculus sense.
  • Differentiable Contrast: Differentiability requires a derivative and is stronger than continuity.
  • Smooth Contrast: Smooth functions are typically infinitely differentiable and therefore satisfy much stronger conditions.
  • Uniformly Continuous Contrast: Uniform continuity requires global rather than merely pointwise control.
  • Common Error: Do not assume existence of a limit alone guarantees continuity.
  • Common Error: Do not assume existence of $f(a)$ alone guarantees continuity.
  • Common Error: Do not equate continuity with differentiability.
  • Common Error: Do not assume continuity implies boundedness on arbitrary domains.
  • Common Error: Do not assume continuity implies monotonicity or injectivity.
  • Common Error: Do not treat the pencil-test description as the formal definition.
  • Common Error: Do not assume the domain must be an interval or even a subset of $\mathbb R$.
  • Terminological Contrast: A continuous function is a mapping whose nearby behavior agrees with its assigned function values. A function is continuous at a point when $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a)$. A discontinuous function fails continuity at one or more points. A differentiable function has a derivative and is necessarily continuous at points of differentiability. A smooth function ordinarily has derivatives of all orders. A uniformly continuous function satisfies a stronger form of continuity with one input tolerance working globally for each output tolerance. A continuous map is the standard topological generalization of the same concept.
