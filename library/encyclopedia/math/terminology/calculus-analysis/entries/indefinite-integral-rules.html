Indefinite Integral Rules

Subfield: Calculus & Analysis

Etymology & Roots

Indefinite integral rules are standard formulas and structural laws used to find families of antiderivatives. They reverse familiar derivative patterns and preserve the constant of integration.

$$ \boxed{ \int f(x)\,dx=F(x)+C \quad\text{when}\quad F'(x)=f(x) } $$

The word indefinite indicates that no integration bounds are present, while rules refers to reusable operational patterns for recovering antiderivatives.

Operational Nuance

Constant-Multiple Rule

$$ \boxed{ \int c\,f(x)\,dx = c\int f(x)\,dx } $$

where $c$ is constant.

Sum and Difference Rules

$$ \boxed{ \int[f(x)\pm g(x)]\,dx = \int f(x)\,dx \pm \int g(x)\,dx } $$

Power Rule

For $n\neq-1$:

$$ \boxed{ \int x^n\,dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}+C } $$

The exponent increases by one, and the result is divided by the new exponent.

Logarithmic Exception

The case $n=-1$ is not handled by the power rule:

$$ \boxed{ \int\frac{1}{x}\,dx = \ln|x|+C } $$

on intervals that do not cross $x=0$.

Exponential Rules

$$ \boxed{ \int e^x\,dx=e^x+C } $$
$$ \boxed{ \int a^x\,dx = \frac{a^x}{\ln a}+C } $$

for $a>0$ and $a\neq1$.

Basic Trigonometric Rules

$$ \int\sin x\,dx=-\cos x+C, \qquad \int\cos x\,dx=\sin x+C. $$
$$ \int\sec^2x\,dx=\tan x+C, \qquad \int\csc^2x\,dx=-\cot x+C. $$

Substitution Pattern

The substitution rule reverses the chain rule. If $u=g(x)$ and $du=g'(x)\,dx$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \int f(g(x))g'(x)\,dx = \int f(u)\,du } $$

Constant of Integration

A final indefinite integral should normally include $+C$:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}[F(x)+C]=F'(x) }. $$

Verification Rule

Differentiate the proposed antiderivative. If the derivative reproduces the integrand on the intended domain, the integration is correct.

Common Misconception

There is no general rule saying that the integral of a product equals the product of the integrals. Products often require substitution, integration by parts, or another technique.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\int x^n\,dx$ "Integrate x to the nth power with respect to x."
$\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}+C$ "Increase the exponent by one, divide by the new exponent, and add C."
$\int\frac{1}{x}\,dx$ "The antiderivative is natural log of absolute x plus C."
$u=g(x)$ "Let u equal g of x."
$du=g'(x)\,dx$ "The differential du equals g prime of x dx."
$+C$ "Add the constant of integration."

Indefinite integral rules and antiderivative rules are closely related phrases. Some texts reserve integration techniques for broader methods such as substitution, integration by parts, and partial fractions.

Defense Notation Advice: Check whether a proposed rule genuinely reverses a derivative rule. Preserve $+C$, respect domain restrictions, and do not apply the power rule to the exceptional exponent $-1$.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Linearity: Constants factor out, and sums and differences integrate term by term.
  • Power Rule: Applies to $x^n$ when $n\neq-1$ on suitable domains.
  • Logarithmic Case: The antiderivative of $1/x$ is $\ln|x|+C$.
  • Exponential Functions: Standard exponential derivatives reverse directly into integration rules.
  • Trigonometric Functions: Common trig derivative identities generate corresponding antiderivative rules.
  • Substitution: Reverses composite-function differentiation patterns.
  • Integration by Parts: Provides the principal reverse form of the product rule.
  • Partial Fractions: Rational functions may first require algebraic decomposition before standard antiderivative rules apply.
  • Constants of Integration: All antiderivatives differing by a constant have the same derivative on a connected interval.
  • Differential Equations: Indefinite integral rules are central to recovering unknown functions from known derivatives.
  • Verification: Differentiation provides a direct correctness check.
  • Common Error: Omitting $+C$.
  • Common Error: Applying the power rule to $x^{-1}$.
  • Common Error: Assuming $\int fg\,dx=(\int f\,dx)(\int g\,dx)$.
  • Common Error: Forgetting a required inner-derivative factor when attempting substitution.
  • Core Interpretation: Indefinite integral rules are reverse-differentiation patterns: $$ \boxed{ \text{recognize derivative structure} \longrightarrow \text{recover an antiderivative} \longrightarrow \text{add }C }. $$ Their purpose is to make antiderivative recovery systematic while preserving the domain and structural conditions under which each rule is valid.
