Convergent Sequence

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

A convergent sequence is a sequence whose terms approach a single limiting value as the index increases without bound.

$$ \boxed{ a_n\to L } $$

means that the sequence $(a_n)$ converges to the limit $L$.

The word convergent comes from the idea of coming together or approaching a common destination. In mathematical analysis, the destination is the limit.

The sequence does not need to reach the limit exactly, nor must its terms move monotonically toward it. What matters is that the terms eventually remain arbitrarily close to the same value.

$$ \boxed{ \text{convergent sequence} = \text{sequence with a finite limit in the stated space} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Basic Definition

A real sequence:

$$ a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots $$

converges to $L$ if:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=L }. $$

Epsilon Definition

Formally, $(a_n)$ converges to $L$ if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists a positive integer $N$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ n\ge N \Rightarrow |a_n-L|<\varepsilon }. $$

This is the standard $\varepsilon$-$N$ definition of sequence convergence.

Interpretation of Epsilon

$\varepsilon$ represents any desired tolerance around the limit:

$$ L-\varepsilon < a_n < L+\varepsilon. $$

Convergence means that all sufficiently late terms lie inside every such interval.

Interpretation of N

The index $N$ marks a point beyond which every sequence term satisfies the required tolerance.

$$ \boxed{ n\ge N \Rightarrow a_n\text{ stays close to }L }. $$

Example: Reciprocal Sequence

Consider:

$$ a_n=\frac1n. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac1n\to0 }. $$

The terms never equal $0$, but they become arbitrarily close to $0$.

Example: Shifted Reciprocal

For:

$$ a_n=5+\frac1n, $$

we have:

$$ \boxed{ a_n\to5 }. $$

Constant Sequence

A constant sequence:

$$ a_n=c $$

converges to $c$:

$$ \boxed{ a_n\to c }. $$

Oscillating but Convergent Sequence

A sequence can alternate while still converging. For example:

$$ a_n=\frac{(-1)^n}{n}. $$

Although the sign changes:

$$ \boxed{ a_n\to0 }. $$

The oscillation shrinks toward the limit.

Oscillating and Divergent Sequence

By contrast:

$$ a_n=(-1)^n $$

alternates between $1$ and $-1$ without approaching one value, so it diverges.

Convergence Does Not Require Monotonicity

A sequence need not move steadily upward or downward toward its limit. It may oscillate, overshoot, or change direction repeatedly.

Monotone Sequences

A sequence is monotone increasing if:

$$ a_{n+1}\ge a_n $$

and monotone decreasing if:

$$ a_{n+1}\le a_n. $$

Monotone Convergence Theorem for Sequences

A monotone bounded real sequence converges.

$$ \boxed{ \text{monotone} + \text{bounded} \Rightarrow \text{convergent} }. $$

Increasing Bounded Sequence

If $(a_n)$ is increasing and bounded above, then:

$$ \boxed{ a_n\to\sup\{a_n:n\in\mathbb N\} }. $$

Decreasing Bounded Sequence

If $(a_n)$ is decreasing and bounded below, then:

$$ \boxed{ a_n\to\inf\{a_n:n\in\mathbb N\} }. $$

Every Convergent Real Sequence Is Bounded

$$ \boxed{ a_n\text{ convergent} \Rightarrow (a_n)\text{ bounded} }. $$

This gives a necessary condition for convergence.

Bounded Does Not Imply Convergent

The converse fails:

$$ \boxed{ \text{bounded} \not\Rightarrow \text{convergent} }. $$

Again, $(-1)^n$ is a standard counterexample.

Uniqueness of the Limit

A convergent real sequence has exactly one limit.

$$ \boxed{ a_n\to L \text{ and } a_n\to M \Rightarrow L=M }. $$

Why the Limit Is Unique

If two distinct numbers $L$ and $M$ were both limits, sufficiently late terms would have to lie arbitrarily close to both values. Choosing neighborhoods small enough to be disjoint makes that impossible.

Finite Initial Terms Do Not Affect Convergence

Changing finitely many terms of a sequence does not alter whether it converges or what its limit is.

$$ \boxed{ \text{convergence depends on eventual behavior} }. $$

Tail of a Sequence

The sequence:

$$ a_N,a_{N+1},a_{N+2},\ldots $$

is called a tail of the original sequence.

A sequence and any of its tails have the same convergence behavior and the same limit.

Subsequences

A subsequence is formed by selecting terms whose indices increase:

$$ a_{n_1},a_{n_2},a_{n_3},\ldots $$

where:

$$ n_1

Subsequences of Convergent Sequences

If:

$$ a_n\to L, $$

then every subsequence also converges to $L$:

$$ \boxed{ a_{n_k}\to L }. $$

Subsequence Test for Divergence

If a sequence has two subsequences converging to different limits, then the original sequence diverges.

For $(-1)^n$:

$$ a_{2n}=1, \qquad a_{2n-1}=-1. $$

Thus the sequence cannot converge.

Algebra of Limits: Sum

If:

$$ a_n\to A, \qquad b_n\to B, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a_n+b_n\to A+B }. $$

Difference Rule

$$ \boxed{ a_n-b_n\to A-B }. $$

Constant Multiple Rule

For a constant $c$:

$$ \boxed{ ca_n\to cA }. $$

Product Rule

$$ \boxed{ a_nb_n\to AB }. $$

Quotient Rule

If $B\ne0$ and $b_n\ne0$ eventually:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{a_n}{b_n} \to \frac AB }. $$

Power Rule

Under ordinary real-domain conditions:

$$ \boxed{ a_n\to A \Rightarrow a_n^k\to A^k } $$

for positive integers $k$.

Absolute-Value Rule

If:

$$ a_n\to A, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ |a_n|\to|A| }. $$

Continuous Functions Preserve Sequence Limits

If $a_n\to L$ and $f$ is continuous at $L$, then:

$$ \boxed{ f(a_n)\to f(L) }. $$

Squeeze Theorem for Sequences

Suppose:

$$ a_n\le b_n\le c_n $$

eventually, and:

$$ a_n\to L, \qquad c_n\to L. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ b_n\to L }. $$

Example Using the Squeeze Theorem

For:

$$ a_n=\frac{\sin n}{n}, $$

since:

$$ -\frac1n \le \frac{\sin n}{n} \le \frac1n $$

and both outer sequences converge to $0$:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\sin n}{n}\to0 }. $$

Cauchy Sequence

A sequence is Cauchy if its terms eventually become arbitrarily close to each other:

$$ \boxed{ \forall\varepsilon>0\, \exists N: m,n\ge N \Rightarrow |a_m-a_n|<\varepsilon }. $$

Convergent Implies Cauchy

Every convergent sequence in a metric space is Cauchy:

$$ \boxed{ \text{convergent} \Rightarrow \text{Cauchy} }. $$

Cauchy Implies Convergent in Complete Spaces

In a complete metric space:

$$ \boxed{ \text{Cauchy} \Rightarrow \text{convergent} }. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \text{complete space} \Rightarrow \text{Cauchy} \iff \text{convergent} }. $$

Real Numbers Are Complete

Every Cauchy sequence of real numbers converges to a real number.

Rational Numbers Are Not Complete

A sequence of rational numbers can be Cauchy while approaching an irrational number, so it does not converge within $\mathbb Q$.

Metric-Space Convergence

In a metric space $(X,d)$, a sequence $(x_n)$ converges to $x\in X$ when:

$$ \boxed{ d(x_n,x)\to0 }. $$

Epsilon Definition in a Metric Space

$$ \boxed{ \forall\varepsilon>0\, \exists N: n\ge N \Rightarrow d(x_n,x)<\varepsilon }. $$

Vector Sequences

A vector sequence:

$$ \mathbf x_n\in\mathbb R^m $$

converges to $\mathbf x$ if:

$$ \boxed{ \|\mathbf x_n-\mathbf x\|\to0 }. $$

Componentwise Convergence

In finite-dimensional Euclidean space:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf x_n\to\mathbf x \iff x_{n,j}\to x_j \text{ for every component }j }. $$

Complex Sequences

A complex sequence $z_n$ converges to $z$ if:

$$ \boxed{ |z_n-z|\to0 }. $$

Equivalently, the real and imaginary parts converge separately.

Recursive Sequences

A recursive sequence may be defined by:

$$ a_{n+1}=F(a_n). $$

If $a_n\to L$ and $F$ is continuous at $L$, then taking limits formally gives:

$$ \boxed{ L=F(L) }. $$

Thus a possible limit must be a fixed point of $F$.

Fixed-Point Equation Is Necessary, Not Sufficient

Solving:

$$ L=F(L) $$

identifies possible limits but does not prove that the sequence actually converges.

Convergence must be established separately, often through monotonicity, boundedness, contraction arguments, or another theorem.

Example of Recursive Convergence

Suppose:

$$ a_{n+1} = \frac{a_n+2}{2}. $$

If the sequence converges to $L$, then:

$$ L=\frac{L+2}{2}. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{L=2}. $$

Geometric Sequences

Consider:

$$ a_n=ar^n. $$

If:

$$ |r|<1, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ ar^n\to0 }. $$

Geometric Sequence with r = 1

If $r=1$:

$$ a_n=a, $$

so the sequence converges to $a$.

Geometric Sequence with r = -1

If $a\ne0$ and $r=-1$, the sequence alternates:

$$ a,-a,a,-a,\ldots $$

and diverges.

Geometric Sequence with |r| > 1

If $a\ne0$ and:

$$ |r|>1, $$

the term magnitudes grow without bound, so the sequence does not converge to a finite real limit.

Rate of Convergence

If:

$$ a_n\to L, $$

the error:

$$ e_n=|a_n-L| $$

measures how far the $n$th term is from the limit.

Faster and Slower Convergence

Two sequences may converge to the same value while doing so at very different rates.

For example:

$$ \frac1n\to0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac1{2^n}\to0, $$

but $1/2^n$ approaches $0$ much more rapidly.

Order of Convergence

In numerical analysis, convergence may be classified by error relations such as:

$$ e_{n+1} \approx C e_n^p. $$

The exponent $p$ describes the order of convergence under suitable definitions.

Convergence to Infinity

A sequence may satisfy:

$$ a_n\to\infty, $$

meaning that for every real $M$, eventually:

$$ a_n>M. $$

In ordinary real analysis, this is usually not called convergence to a finite real limit and is often classified as divergence to infinity.

Extended-Real Convergence

If the extended real line:

$$ [-\infty,\infty] $$

is being used, convergence to $\infty$ or $-\infty$ may be treated as convergence within that enlarged setting.

Convergent Sequence versus Convergent Series

A sequence:

$$ a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots $$

converges when the terms themselves approach a limit.

A series:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n $$

converges when its partial sums converge.

These are different concepts.

Sequence Terms Going to Zero Do Not Guarantee Series Convergence

The sequence:

$$ \frac1n $$

converges to zero, while the series:

$$ \sum\frac1n $$

diverges.

Bolzano-Weierstrass Theorem

Every bounded sequence in $\mathbb R^n$ has a convergent subsequence.

$$ \boxed{ \text{bounded sequence in }\mathbb R^n \Rightarrow \text{convergent subsequence} }. $$

This does not mean that the entire bounded sequence must converge.

Cluster Points

A number $L$ is a subsequential limit or cluster point of a sequence if some subsequence converges to $L$.

A convergent sequence has exactly one subsequential limit, namely its limit.

lim sup and lim inf

For bounded real sequences, the quantities:

$$ \limsup_{n\to\infty}a_n \qquad\text{and}\qquad \liminf_{n\to\infty}a_n $$

describe extreme limiting behavior.

The sequence converges exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ \limsup a_n = \liminf a_n }. $$

Topological Convergence

In a topological space, $x_n\to x$ means that every neighborhood of $x$ eventually contains all terms $x_n$.

$$ \boxed{ x_n\to x \iff \forall U\ni x,\, x_n\in U \text{ eventually} }. $$

Uniqueness Depends on the Space

In Hausdorff topological spaces, sequence limits are unique. In more general non-Hausdorff spaces, a sequence can have more than one limit.

Convergent Sequence in Function Spaces

A sequence of functions may itself be treated as a sequence in a function space, where convergence depends on the chosen norm or topology.

Thus:

$$ f_n\to f $$

may mean pointwise, uniform, norm, weak, or another form of convergence.

Common Misconception: A Convergent Sequence Must Reach Its Limit

Exact equality is not required. The sequence need only become arbitrarily close to the limit.

Common Misconception: A Convergent Sequence Must Be Monotone

Oscillation with shrinking amplitude can still produce convergence.

Common Misconception: Every Bounded Sequence Converges

Boundedness alone is insufficient. It guarantees a convergent subsequence in $\mathbb R^n$, not convergence of the whole sequence.

Common Misconception: Every Monotone Sequence Converges

Monotonicity alone is insufficient. A monotone sequence must also be bounded in the appropriate direction to converge to a finite real limit.

Common Misconception: A Sequence Can Have Two Real Limits

Ordinary real sequence limits are unique.

Common Misconception: Changing the First Term Changes Convergence

Any finite number of initial modifications leaves the limit unchanged.

Common Misconception: Convergent Terms Imply a Convergent Series

A sequence of terms can converge, even to zero, while the series formed from those terms diverges.

Common Misconception: Solving the Fixed-Point Equation Proves Recursive Convergence

The equation $L=F(L)$ identifies a candidate limit only. The sequence must still be shown to converge.

Common Misconception: Convergence Means the Same Thing in Every Space

The precise meaning depends on the metric, norm, topology, or other structure used to define closeness.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$a_n\to L$ "a sub n converges to L."
$\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=L$ "The limit of a sub n as n approaches infinity is L."
$|a_n-L|<\varepsilon$ "a sub n lies within epsilon of L."
$a_{n_k}\to L$ "The subsequence a sub n sub k converges to L."
$d(x_n,x)\to0$ "x sub n converges to x in the metric."
$\mathbf x_n\to\mathbf x$ "The vector sequence converges to x."

The noun phrase convergent sequence describes a sequence possessing a limit. The verb phrase the sequence converges to $L$ identifies that limit explicitly.

The arrow notation $a_n\to L$ should be read relative to the surrounding space. For real numbers it usually means ordinary numerical convergence; for vectors, functions, or other objects, the relevant metric, norm, topology, or mode of convergence may need to be stated.

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish the sequence $(a_n)$ from the series $\sum a_n$. State the limit explicitly when possible. In formal proofs, use the $\varepsilon$-$N$ definition correctly: for every $\varepsilon>0$, one $N$ must work for all $n\ge N$. Do not infer convergence from boundedness alone. For recursive sequences, do not mistake a fixed-point equation for a convergence proof. In general spaces, identify the metric, norm, or topology when the notion of convergence is not obvious.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: A convergent sequence is a sequence whose terms approach a single limit.
  • Standard Notation: $$ \boxed{ a_n\to L }. $$
  • Epsilon-N Definition: For every $\varepsilon>0$, all sufficiently late terms lie within $\varepsilon$ of $L$.
  • Limit: The value $L$ is the sequence's limit.
  • Uniqueness: A real convergent sequence has exactly one limit.
  • Boundedness: Every convergent real sequence is bounded.
  • Converse Warning: A bounded sequence need not converge.
  • Monotone Convergence: A bounded monotone real sequence converges.
  • Subsequences: Every subsequence of a convergent sequence converges to the same limit.
  • Bolzano-Weierstrass: Every bounded sequence in $\mathbb R^n$ has a convergent subsequence.
  • Cauchy Property: Every convergent metric-space sequence is Cauchy.
  • Completeness: In complete metric spaces, every Cauchy sequence converges.
  • Algebra of Limits: Sums, differences, products, constant multiples, and appropriate quotients preserve convergence.
  • Squeeze Theorem: A sequence trapped between two sequences with the same limit has that limit.
  • Continuous Functions: Continuous functions preserve convergent sequence limits.
  • Recursive Sequences: If a recursive sequence converges and its defining map is continuous, the limit satisfies the corresponding fixed-point equation.
  • Geometric Sequences: $ar^n\to0$ when $|r|<1$.
  • Vector Sequences: Convergence in finite-dimensional Euclidean space is equivalent to componentwise convergence.
  • Complex Sequences: Convergence is defined using complex modulus.
  • Metric Spaces: $x_n\to x$ means $d(x_n,x)\to0$.
  • Topology: Convergence means eventual membership in every neighborhood of the limit.
  • Function Spaces: Sequences of functions may converge under pointwise, uniform, norm, weak, or other notions.
  • Tail Property: Finite changes to initial terms do not affect convergence or the limit.
  • Oscillation: Oscillation does not prevent convergence if its magnitude shrinks appropriately.
  • Divergent Contrast: A divergent sequence fails to approach a single limit under the relevant convergence notion.
  • Series Contrast: A convergent sequence concerns limits of terms; a convergent series concerns limits of partial sums.
  • Infinity Nuance: $a_n\to\infty$ is usually called divergence to infinity in ordinary real analysis, though extended-real conventions may treat it as convergence.
  • Common Error: Do not assume the terms must ever equal the limit.
  • Common Error: Do not assume convergence requires monotonicity.
  • Common Error: Do not assume boundedness alone proves convergence.
  • Common Error: Do not assume monotonicity alone proves finite convergence without boundedness.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse a convergent sequence with a convergent series.
  • Common Error: Do not use the fixed-point equation of a recurrence as the entire convergence proof.
  • Terminological Contrast: A sequence is an ordered list of terms. A convergent sequence approaches a single limit. A divergent sequence fails to do so under the stated notion. A bounded sequence remains within fixed bounds but need not converge. A monotone sequence moves in one direction but requires appropriate boundedness for finite convergence. A Cauchy sequence has terms that become arbitrarily close to one another; in complete spaces this is equivalent to convergence. A convergent series is different: its sequence of partial sums, rather than its individual terms, converges.
