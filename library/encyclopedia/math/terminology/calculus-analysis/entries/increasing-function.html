Increasing Function

Subfield: Calculus & Analysis

Etymology & Roots

An increasing function is a function whose output values do not decrease as the input values increase. In many texts, increasing means nondecreasing, while other texts use it to mean strictly increasing, so the convention should be stated.

$$ \boxed{ x_1<x_2 \Longrightarrow f(x_1)\leq f(x_2) } $$

The term comes from ordinary language: moving to larger inputs produces outputs that remain the same or become larger.

Operational Nuance

Increasing vs. Strictly Increasing

Under the non-strict convention:

$$ \boxed{ x_1<x_2 \Longrightarrow f(x_1)\leq f(x_2) }. $$

A strictly increasing function instead satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ x_1<x_2 \Longrightarrow f(x_1)<f(x_2) }. $$

Derivative Criterion

If $f$ is differentiable on an interval and:

$$ \boxed{ f'(x)>0 } $$

throughout that interval, then $f$ is strictly increasing there.

More generally, if $f'(x)\geq0$ throughout an interval, then $f$ is nondecreasing there.

Graphical Interpretation

On an increasing interval, the graph moves upward or remains level as one reads from left to right. For strict increase, larger inputs always correspond to strictly larger outputs.

Increasing on an Interval

A function need not be increasing on its entire domain. It may increase on one interval and decrease on another. Calculus commonly identifies these intervals through the sign of the first derivative.

$$ \boxed{ f'(x)>0 \Longrightarrow \text{increasing behavior} } $$

Critical Numbers

Critical numbers often divide a domain into intervals where the derivative has a consistent sign. A derivative sign chart can therefore locate increasing and decreasing intervals.

Constant Portions

A constant segment satisfies the nondecreasing definition because outputs never fall, but it fails the strictly increasing definition because distinct inputs can have equal outputs.

Order Preservation

Increasing functions preserve the order of inputs:

$$ x_1<x_2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f(x_1)\leq f(x_2). $$

A strictly increasing function preserves strict order and is therefore one-to-one.

Common Misconception

Increasing does not mean that all function values are positive. A function can have negative values and still be increasing if its outputs rise as its inputs increase.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$x_1<x_2\Rightarrow f(x_1)\leq f(x_2)$ "F is increasing, or nondecreasing, under this convention."
$x_1<x_2\Rightarrow f(x_1)<f(x_2)$ "F is strictly increasing."
$f'(x)>0$ "F prime is positive, so f is strictly increasing on the interval."
$f'(x)\geq0$ "F prime is nonnegative, so f is nondecreasing."
$f$ increasing on $(a,b)$ "F increases on the open interval from a to b."
$f$ strictly increasing "Larger inputs always produce larger outputs."

Terminology varies: some authors reserve increasing for the strict inequality and use nondecreasing for the weak inequality. Formal writing should specify the intended inequality when ambiguity matters.

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish the sign of $f'$ from the sign of $f$. The function's value may be negative while its derivative is positive and the function is increasing.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: Larger inputs produce outputs that do not decrease.
  • Strict Increase: Larger inputs always produce strictly larger outputs.
  • Nondecreasing Convention: Equal outputs at different inputs may be permitted.
  • Derivative Test: A positive derivative throughout an interval guarantees strict increase there.
  • Nonnegative Derivative: A nonnegative derivative throughout an interval guarantees nondecreasing behavior.
  • Sign Charts: Critical numbers divide the domain into intervals for monotonicity analysis.
  • Graphical Reading: Increasing behavior appears as upward or level movement from left to right under the non-strict convention.
  • One-to-One Connection: Every strictly increasing function is injective on the interval where strict increase holds.
  • Inverse Functions: A strictly increasing function has an increasing inverse on its range when the inverse exists.
  • Optimization: Increasing intervals help locate local maxima and minima through derivative sign changes.
  • Sequences: The analogous idea appears when $a_{n+1}\geq a_n$ or, for strict increase, $a_{n+1}>a_n$.
  • Common Error: Assuming an increasing function must have positive outputs.
  • Common Error: Confusing positive $f(x)$ with positive $f'(x)$.
  • Common Error: Failing to state whether "increasing" is being used strictly or non-strictly.
  • Core Interpretation: An increasing function preserves the direction of order: $$ \boxed{ \text{input rises} \Longrightarrow \text{output does not fall} }. $$ In calculus, this order behavior is closely connected to the sign of the first derivative on an interval.
