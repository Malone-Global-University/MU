Acceleration

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

Acceleration is the rate at which velocity changes with respect to time. In one-dimensional calculus, if $v(t)$ represents velocity, then instantaneous acceleration is:

$$ \boxed{ a(t)=\frac{dv}{dt} }. $$

Since velocity is itself the derivative of position, acceleration is also the second derivative of position:

$$ \boxed{ a(t)=s''(t)=\frac{d^2s}{dt^2} }. $$

The word comes from the idea of increasing speed, but its mathematical meaning is broader. Acceleration describes any change in velocity. Because velocity includes direction as well as speed, an object may accelerate even while moving at constant speed if its direction changes.

This distinction is especially important in vector calculus and mechanics, where acceleration is treated as a vector quantity rather than merely a numerical increase in speed.

Operational Nuance

Average Acceleration

Over a time interval from $t_1$ to $t_2$, average acceleration is the change in velocity divided by the elapsed time:

$$ \boxed{ a_{\text{avg}} = \frac{v(t_2)-v(t_1)}{t_2-t_1} = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} }. $$

Average acceleration describes the overall rate of velocity change across an interval.

Instantaneous Acceleration

Instantaneous acceleration is obtained by taking the limit of average acceleration as the time interval shrinks:

$$ \boxed{ a(t) = \lim_{\Delta t\to0} \frac{v(t+\Delta t)-v(t)}{\Delta t} }. $$

This is exactly the derivative:

$$ a(t)=v'(t). $$

Acceleration from Position

If position is given by:

$$ s=s(t), $$

then velocity is:

$$ v(t)=s'(t), $$

and acceleration is:

$$ \boxed{ a(t)=s''(t) }. $$

Example

Suppose:

$$ s(t)=t^3-6t^2+9t. $$

Then:

$$ v(t) = s'(t) = 3t^2-12t+9. $$

Differentiating again:

$$ a(t) = v'(t) = 6t-12. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ a(t)=6t-12 }. $$

Positive and Negative Acceleration

In one-dimensional motion, the sign of acceleration indicates its direction relative to the chosen coordinate axis:

$$ a(t)>0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{acceleration in the positive direction}, $$
$$ a(t)<0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{acceleration in the negative direction}. $$

Negative acceleration does not automatically mean that an object is slowing down. Whether speed increases or decreases depends on the relationship between the signs of velocity and acceleration.

Speeding Up and Slowing Down

In one-dimensional motion:

$$ \boxed{ v(t)a(t)>0 \Longrightarrow \text{speeding up} } $$

because velocity and acceleration have the same sign.

Similarly:

$$ \boxed{ v(t)a(t)<0 \Longrightarrow \text{slowing down} }. $$

Constant Acceleration

If acceleration is constant:

$$ a(t)=a, $$

then velocity varies linearly with time:

$$ \boxed{ v(t)=v_0+at }. $$

Integrating once more gives position:

$$ \boxed{ s(t) = s_0+v_0t+\frac12at^2 }. $$

Vector Acceleration

In two or three dimensions, position is represented by a vector:

$$ \mathbf{r}(t). $$

Velocity and acceleration are then:

$$ \mathbf{v}(t) = \mathbf{r}'(t) $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a}(t) = \mathbf{r}''(t) }. $$

Componentwise:

$$ \mathbf{a}(t) = \left\langle x''(t), y''(t), z''(t) \right\rangle. $$

Acceleration Without Changing Speed

An object moving around a circle at constant speed still accelerates because the direction of its velocity changes continuously. The inward acceleration is called centripetal acceleration:

$$ \boxed{ a_c=\frac{v^2}{r} }. $$

Common Misconception: Acceleration is not the same as speed. Speed describes how rapidly position changes in magnitude; acceleration describes how rapidly velocity changes.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$a(t)$ "Acceleration as a function of time."
$a(t)=v'(t)$ "Acceleration equals the derivative of velocity."
$a(t)=s''(t)$ "Acceleration equals the second derivative of position."
$\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$ "Change in velocity divided by change in time."
$\frac{d^2s}{dt^2}$ "The second derivative of position with respect to time."
$\mathbf{a}(t)$ "The acceleration vector."

A common physical unit is:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} }. $$

This is read:

$$ \text{"meters per second squared."} $$

The unit can also be understood as:

$$ \frac{\text{m/s}}{\text{s}}, $$

meaning a change in velocity, measured in meters per second, for each second of time.

Defense Notation Advice: Do not read $\text{m/s}^2$ as though only the number of seconds were being squared in an ordinary arithmetic expression. It represents a rate of change of velocity with respect to time.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Average Acceleration: $$ \boxed{ a_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} }. $$
  • Instantaneous Acceleration: $$ \boxed{ a(t)=\frac{dv}{dt} }. $$
  • Position Relationship: $$ \boxed{ a(t) = \frac{d^2s}{dt^2} }. $$
  • Velocity Relationship: If acceleration is known: $$ \boxed{ v(t) = v(t_0) + \int_{t_0}^{t}a(u)\,du }. $$
  • Change in Velocity: $$ \boxed{ \Delta v = \int_{t_1}^{t_2}a(t)\,dt }. $$
  • Constant Acceleration: If $a$ is constant: $$ \boxed{ v_f=v_i+a\Delta t }. $$
  • Constant-Acceleration Position: $$ \boxed{ \Delta s = v_i t+\frac12at^2 }. $$
  • Time-Eliminated Kinematics: Under constant acceleration: $$ \boxed{ v_f^2 = v_i^2+2a\Delta s }. $$
  • Units: Acceleration has dimensions: $$ \boxed{ LT^{-2} }, $$ where $L$ represents length and $T$ represents time.
  • SI Unit: $$ \boxed{ \text{m/s}^2 }. $$
  • Positive Acceleration: Positive acceleration points in the positive coordinate direction. It does not necessarily mean increasing speed.
  • Negative Acceleration: Negative acceleration points in the negative coordinate direction. It is not automatically synonymous with slowing down.
  • Speeding Up: In one dimension: $$ \boxed{ va>0 } $$ indicates that speed is increasing.
  • Slowing Down: In one dimension: $$ \boxed{ va<0 } $$ indicates that speed is decreasing.
  • Velocity-Time Graph: Acceleration is the slope of the velocity graph: $$ \boxed{ a(t)=v'(t) }. $$
  • Position-Time Graph: Acceleration is the second derivative of the position graph: $$ \boxed{ a(t)=s''(t) }. $$
  • Acceleration-Time Graph: The signed area under an acceleration-versus-time graph gives change in velocity: $$ \boxed{ \int_{t_1}^{t_2}a(t)\,dt = v(t_2)-v(t_1) }. $$
  • Zero Acceleration: If: $$ a(t)=0 $$ throughout an interval, then velocity is constant on that interval.
  • Zero Velocity Does Not Imply Zero Acceleration: At the highest point of vertical motion, velocity may momentarily equal zero while acceleration remains nonzero.
  • Free Fall: Near Earth's surface and neglecting air resistance, gravitational acceleration is approximately constant and directed downward.
  • Sign Convention in Free Fall: If upward is chosen positive, one commonly writes: $$ a=-g. $$ If downward is chosen positive, the same physical acceleration is written: $$ a=g. $$
  • Vector Form: For: $$ \mathbf{r}(t) = \langle x(t),y(t),z(t)\rangle, $$ $$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a}(t) = \langle x''(t), y''(t), z''(t) \rangle }. $$
  • Magnitude of Acceleration: For vector acceleration: $$ \boxed{ \|\mathbf{a}\| = \sqrt{a_x^2+a_y^2+a_z^2} }. $$
  • Circular Motion: Constant speed does not imply zero acceleration. Uniform circular motion has inward centripetal acceleration: $$ \boxed{ a_c=\frac{v^2}{r} }. $$
  • Tangential Acceleration: A tangential component changes the magnitude of velocity and therefore changes speed.
  • Normal Acceleration: A normal or centripetal component changes the direction of velocity.
  • Curvilinear Motion: Acceleration can be decomposed into tangential and normal components: $$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a} = a_T\mathbf{T} + a_N\mathbf{N} }. $$
  • Tangential Component: $$ \boxed{ a_T=\frac{d}{dt}\|\mathbf{v}\| } $$ measures the rate of change of speed.
  • Normal Component: For speed $v$ and radius of curvature $\rho$: $$ \boxed{ a_N=\frac{v^2}{\rho} }. $$
  • Newton's Second Law: In mechanics, acceleration is related to net force by: $$ \boxed{ \mathbf{F}_{\text{net}} = m\mathbf{a} } $$ for constant mass in the standard Newtonian setting.
  • Calculus Interpretation: Acceleration is fundamentally a derivative concept: it measures an instantaneous rate of change.
  • Antiderivative Interpretation: Recovering velocity from acceleration requires integration and an initial velocity condition.
  • Higher Derivatives: The derivative of acceleration: $$ \frac{da}{dt} $$ is commonly called jerk.
  • Terminological Contrast: Position describes location, velocity describes the rate and direction of position change, speed is the magnitude of velocity, and acceleration describes the rate at which velocity changes.
