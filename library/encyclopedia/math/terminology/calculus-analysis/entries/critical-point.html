Critical Point

Subfield: Calculus & Analysis

Etymology & Roots

A critical point is a point at which a function's derivative behavior becomes especially significant for analyzing extrema, monotonicity, geometry, or local structure.

In single-variable calculus, if $c$ is a critical number of a function $f$, the corresponding point:

$$ \boxed{(c,f(c))} $$

is commonly called a critical point of the graph.

Under the standard single-variable definition, $c$ is critical when:

$$ \boxed{ c\in\operatorname{Dom}(f) \quad\text{and}\quad \left[ f'(c)=0 \text{ or } f'(c)\text{ does not exist} \right] }. $$

The adjective critical reflects the role these points play in identifying locations where a function may change from increasing to decreasing, attain an extremum, develop a corner or cusp, or exhibit another locally significant feature.

In multivariable calculus, the term is usually applied directly to an input point in the domain. For a differentiable scalar-valued function:

$$ f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}, $$

a critical point commonly satisfies:

$$ \boxed{\nabla f=\mathbf{0}}. $$

Broader definitions may also include domain points at which the derivative or gradient fails to exist.

Operational Nuance

Single-Variable Interpretation

For a function:

$$ y=f(x), $$

a critical point commonly has the form:

$$ \boxed{(c,f(c))} $$

where $c$ is a critical number.

Thus the distinction is often:

$$ \boxed{ \text{critical number}=c } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{critical point}=(c,f(c)) }. $$

Derivative Equal to Zero

A differentiable critical point often occurs where:

$$ \boxed{f'(c)=0}. $$

At such a point, the tangent line is horizontal.

Consider:

$$ f(x)=x^2-4x+5. $$

Then:

$$ f'(x)=2x-4. $$

Setting:

$$ 2x-4=0 $$

gives:

$$ x=2. $$

Since:

$$ f(2)=1, $$

the corresponding critical point is:

$$ \boxed{(2,1)}. $$

Derivative Does Not Exist

Critical points can also occur where the function is defined but the derivative does not exist.

For:

$$ f(x)=|x|, $$

the function is defined at $x=0$, but:

$$ f'(0) $$

does not exist.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{(0,0)} $$

is a critical point.

Domain Requirement

A point cannot be a critical point of the graph if the function itself is not defined there.

For example:

$$ f(x)=\frac1x $$

is undefined at:

$$ x=0. $$

Therefore there is no graph point:

$$ (0,f(0)), $$

and $x=0$ is not a critical point of the function.

Critical Point Does Not Mean Extremum

A critical point is a candidate for significant local behavior, not necessarily a maximum or minimum.

Consider:

$$ f(x)=x^3. $$

Since:

$$ f'(x)=3x^2, $$

one has:

$$ f'(0)=0. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{(0,0)} $$

is a critical point, but it is neither a local maximum nor a local minimum.

It is instead a stationary inflection point.

Local Maximum

If the derivative changes sign:

$$ +\longrightarrow-, $$

then the critical point is a local maximum.

Local Minimum

If the derivative changes sign:

$$ -\longrightarrow+, $$

then the critical point is a local minimum.

No Extremum

If the derivative keeps the same sign across the critical number:

$$ +\longrightarrow+ $$

or:

$$ -\longrightarrow-, $$

the critical point does not produce a local extremum.

Stationary Point

A stationary point is specifically a point at which:

$$ \boxed{f'(c)=0}. $$

Therefore every stationary point of a differentiable single-variable function is critical, but not every critical point is stationary.

A corner, cusp, or vertical tangent can be critical without being stationary.

First Derivative Test

The first derivative test analyzes the sign of $f'$ immediately before and after a critical number.

$$ \boxed{ \begin{array}{ccl} +\to- &\Rightarrow& \text{local maximum},\\[4pt] -\to+ &\Rightarrow& \text{local minimum},\\[4pt] +\to+ &\Rightarrow& \text{no extremum},\\[4pt] -\to- &\Rightarrow& \text{no extremum}. \end{array} } $$

Second Derivative Test

Suppose:

$$ f'(c)=0. $$

If:

$$ f''(c)>0, $$

then $(c,f(c))$ is a local minimum.

If:

$$ f''(c)<0, $$

then $(c,f(c))$ is a local maximum.

If:

$$ f''(c)=0, $$

the test is inconclusive.

Multivariable Critical Points

For a differentiable function:

$$ f(x,y), $$

an interior critical point satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ \nabla f(x,y) = \begin{bmatrix} f_x\\ f_y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0\\ 0 \end{bmatrix} }. $$

Equivalently:

$$ \boxed{ f_x(x,y)=0, \qquad f_y(x,y)=0 }. $$

Multivariable Example

Consider:

$$ f(x,y)=x^2+y^2. $$

The gradient is:

$$ \nabla f=(2x,2y). $$

Solving:

$$ 2x=0,\qquad2y=0 $$

gives:

$$ \boxed{(0,0)}. $$

This is a critical point and an absolute minimum of the function.

Saddle Point

A multivariable critical point need not be a maximum or minimum.

For:

$$ f(x,y)=x^2-y^2, $$

the gradient is:

$$ \nabla f=(2x,-2y). $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{(0,0)} $$

is a critical point.

However, the function increases in some directions and decreases in others. The origin is therefore a saddle point.

Hessian Classification

For a twice-differentiable function $f(x,y)$, define:

$$ \boxed{ D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-(f_{xy})^2 }. $$

At a critical point:

  • if $D>0$ and $f_{xx}>0$, there is a local minimum;
  • if $D>0$ and $f_{xx}<0$, there is a local maximum;
  • if $D<0$, there is a saddle point;
  • if $D=0$, the test is inconclusive.

Critical Points and Optimization

Critical points provide candidates for optimal values. In unconstrained optimization, one typically:

  1. find the critical points,
  2. classify them, and
  3. compare function values when absolute extrema are required.

Common Misconception: A critical point is not automatically a maximum or minimum. It may be a local maximum, local minimum, saddle point, stationary inflection point, cusp, corner, vertical-tangent point, or another location requiring further analysis.

In single-variable calculus, it is useful to distinguish explicitly between:

$$ \boxed{ \text{critical number}:c } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \text{critical point}:(c,f(c)) }. $$

In multivariable calculus, an input such as:

$$ (a,b) $$

is itself typically called the critical point because the domain is already multidimensional.

If the corresponding point on the surface:

$$ z=f(x,y) $$

is required, it may be written:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b,f(a,b)) }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: The term critical point is not completely uniform across all textbooks. Some single-variable texts use it for the input $c$, while others reserve it for $(c,f(c))$. State or infer the local convention from context.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Single-Variable Critical Point: If $c$ is a critical number of $f$, the corresponding graph point is: $$ \boxed{(c,f(c))}. $$
  • Derivative-Zero Case: $$ \boxed{ f'(c)=0 } $$ produces a stationary critical point.
  • Nondifferentiable Case: If $f(c)$ exists but $f'(c)$ does not, the location may still be a critical point.
  • Domain Requirement: A hole or vertical asymptote outside the domain is not a critical point of the function.
  • Local Maximum: A derivative sign change: $$ +\rightarrow- $$ indicates a local maximum.
  • Local Minimum: A derivative sign change: $$ -\rightarrow+ $$ indicates a local minimum.
  • Stationary Inflection Point: For: $$ f(x)=x^3, $$ the point: $$ \boxed{(0,0)} $$ is critical but is neither a maximum nor a minimum.
  • Corner: For: $$ f(x)=|x|, $$ the point: $$ \boxed{(0,0)} $$ is critical because the derivative does not exist there.
  • Cusp: A cusp may be a critical point when the function is defined but the derivative fails to exist.
  • Vertical Tangent: A point with a vertical tangent may be critical under the usual derivative-based definition if the function remains defined there.
  • Fermat's Theorem: If a differentiable function has an interior local extremum at $c$, then: $$ \boxed{f'(c)=0}. $$
  • Converse Failure: $$ f'(c)=0 $$ does not imply that $(c,f(c))$ is a local extremum.
  • First Derivative Test: Critical points are classified by examining the sign of $f'$ on adjacent intervals.
  • Second Derivative Test: At a stationary critical point: $$ f''(c)>0 $$ implies a local minimum, while: $$ f''(c)<0 $$ implies a local maximum.
  • Second Derivative Inconclusive: $$ f''(c)=0 $$ does not determine the type of critical point.
  • Polynomial Functions: Since polynomials are differentiable everywhere, their critical points arise from solutions of: $$ \boxed{f'(x)=0}. $$
  • Rational Functions: Inputs excluded from the original domain cannot be critical points merely because the derivative is undefined there.
  • Piecewise Functions: Boundary points between formulas should be checked for derivative failure, corners, cusps, and local extrema.
  • Graph Sketching: Critical points help identify turning behavior, stationary behavior, and intervals of increase and decrease.
  • Optimization: Critical points are candidate locations for maximum or minimum values in unconstrained optimization.
  • Closed-Interval Optimization: For a continuous function on: $$ [a,b], $$ absolute extrema are found by comparing values at interior critical points and the endpoints.
  • Multivariable Definition: For differentiable: $$ f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}, $$ an interior critical point satisfies: $$ \boxed{\nabla f=\mathbf{0}}. $$
  • Two Variables: For: $$ f(x,y), $$ solve: $$ \boxed{ f_x=0,\qquad f_y=0 }. $$
  • Three Variables: For: $$ f(x,y,z), $$ a differentiable critical point satisfies: $$ \boxed{ f_x=f_y=f_z=0 }. $$
  • Gradient Interpretation: The gradient points in the direction of steepest increase. At a smooth critical point: $$ \nabla f=\mathbf{0}, $$ so there is no nonzero first-order direction of steepest increase.
  • Local Minimum in Several Variables: For: $$ f(x,y)=x^2+y^2, $$ the origin is a critical point and an absolute minimum.
  • Local Maximum in Several Variables: For: $$ f(x,y)=-x^2-y^2, $$ the origin is a critical point and an absolute maximum.
  • Saddle Point: For: $$ f(x,y)=x^2-y^2, $$ the origin is a critical point but neither a maximum nor a minimum.
  • Hessian: Second-order derivatives are organized in the Hessian matrix: $$ \boxed{ H_f= \begin{bmatrix} f_{xx} & f_{xy}\\ f_{yx} & f_{yy} \end{bmatrix} }. $$
  • Two-Variable Discriminant: At a critical point: $$ \boxed{ D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-(f_{xy})^2 }. $$
  • Hessian Minimum Test: If: $$ D>0 \quad\text{and}\quad f_{xx}>0, $$ the critical point is a local minimum.
  • Hessian Maximum Test: If: $$ D>0 \quad\text{and}\quad f_{xx}<0, $$ the critical point is a local maximum.
  • Hessian Saddle Test: If: $$ D<0, $$ the critical point is a saddle point.
  • Hessian Inconclusive Case: If: $$ D=0, $$ the usual second derivative test is inconclusive.
  • Constrained Optimization: Under constraints, critical candidates may be found using methods such as Lagrange multipliers rather than simply solving: $$ \nabla f=\mathbf{0}. $$
  • Lagrange Multiplier Condition: For a constraint: $$ g(x,y)=c, $$ constrained critical candidates may satisfy: $$ \boxed{ \nabla f=\lambda\nabla g }. $$
  • Dynamical Systems: The phrase critical point may also be used in differential equations for an equilibrium point of a system. That usage is related historically but should not be confused automatically with the derivative definition for scalar functions.
  • Complex Analysis: For a holomorphic function, a point where: $$ f'(z)=0 $$ is commonly called a critical point, and its image is called a critical value.
  • Critical Value: If $c$ is a critical input, the output: $$ \boxed{f(c)} $$ may be called a critical value.
  • Critical Number Contrast: In elementary single-variable calculus: $$ \boxed{ c = \text{critical number}, \qquad (c,f(c)) = \text{critical point} }. $$
  • Stationary Point Contrast: A stationary point requires: $$ f'(c)=0, $$ while a critical point may also arise where the derivative fails to exist.
  • Extremum Contrast: A critical point is a candidate location. An extremum is a point whose function value actually satisfies a maximum or minimum condition.
  • Terminological Variation: Some authors use critical point and critical number interchangeably in one-variable calculus. The intended distinction should be determined from the surrounding definitions.
  • Core Interpretation: A critical point marks a location where first-order differential behavior becomes exceptional or stationary: $$ \boxed{ \text{critical point} \Longrightarrow \text{candidate for significant local behavior} }. $$ It must be classified rather than automatically assumed to be an extremum.
