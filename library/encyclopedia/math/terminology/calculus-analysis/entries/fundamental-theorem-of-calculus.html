Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

Subfield: Calculus & Analysis

Etymology & Roots

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, often abbreviated FTC, establishes the central connection between differentiation and integration. It shows that accumulation through a definite integral produces a function whose derivative recovers the original integrand, and that definite integrals can be evaluated using antiderivatives.

$$ \boxed{ \text{differentiation} \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad \text{integration} } $$

The word fundamental reflects the theorem's role in unifying the two major operations of elementary calculus.

Operational Nuance

Part I: Accumulation Produces an Antiderivative

If $f$ is continuous and:

$$ F(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ F'(x)=f(x) }. $$

Thus the instantaneous rate of change of accumulated quantity is the current value of the function being accumulated.

Part II: Evaluation by Antiderivatives

If $F$ is any antiderivative of $f$ on $[a,b]$, so that $F'=f$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b)-F(a) }. $$

This converts the limiting definition of a definite integral into an efficient endpoint calculation.

Why the Two Parts Fit Together

Part I says integration constructs an antiderivative. Part II says an antiderivative evaluates accumulated change.

$$ \boxed{ f \xrightarrow{\int} F \xrightarrow{d/dx} f } $$

Example

Since an antiderivative of $x^2$ is $x^3/3$:

$$ \int_0^3 x^2\,dx = \left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^3 = 9. $$

Variable Upper Limit

For:

$$ A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt, $$

the symbol $t$ is a dummy variable of integration, while $x$ controls the upper limit and therefore determines the accumulated amount.

Chain-Rule Extension

If the upper limit is itself a differentiable function $g(x)$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx} \int_a^{g(x)} f(t)\,dt = f(g(x))g'(x) }. $$

Net Change Interpretation

If $Q'(x)=r(x)$, then:

$$ \boxed{ Q(b)-Q(a) = \int_a^b r(x)\,dx }. $$

This is the net-change form of the theorem and is central in motion, growth, accumulation, and applied modeling.

Common Misconception

The theorem does not say that every integral and derivative simply cancel without conditions. Bounds, constants of integration, domain restrictions, and chain-rule factors must still be handled correctly.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$F(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt$ "F is the accumulation function from a to x."
$F'(x)=f(x)$ "Differentiating the accumulation function recovers the integrand."
$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a)$ "Evaluate the antiderivative at the upper bound minus the lower bound."
$[F(x)]_a^b$ "F evaluated from a to b."
$Q(b)-Q(a)=\int_a^bQ'(x)\,dx$ "Accumulated rate equals net change."
$\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^{g(x)}f(t)\,dt$ "Differentiate an integral with a variable upper limit."

Textbooks commonly divide the theorem into FTC Part I and FTC Part II, although numbering conventions can be reversed. It is safest to identify the actual statement rather than rely on part numbers alone.

Defense Notation Advice: Keep the integration variable distinct from the variable upper limit, preserve the order upper-minus-lower, and include the chain-rule factor when an integration bound depends on another function.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Accumulation Functions: The derivative of accumulated area or quantity recovers the underlying rate function under appropriate continuity conditions.
  • Definite Integrals: Antiderivatives convert definite integrals into endpoint differences.
  • Antiderivatives: The theorem explains why antiderivatives are the computational engine of definite integration.
  • Net Change: Integrating a rate over an interval gives the total change in the associated quantity.
  • Kinematics: Integrating velocity gives displacement, while integrating acceleration gives change in velocity.
  • Variable Bounds: Differentiating an integral with a variable endpoint combines FTC with the chain rule.
  • Continuity: Introductory forms commonly assume the integrand is continuous; more advanced analysis extends the theorem under weaker hypotheses.
  • Signed Accumulation: A definite integral records net signed accumulation rather than automatically measuring geometric area.
  • Common Error: Writing $F(a)-F(b)$ instead of $F(b)-F(a)$.
  • Common Error: Adding an arbitrary constant when evaluating a definite integral.
  • Common Error: Forgetting the chain-rule factor when differentiating an integral with upper limit $g(x)$.
  • Common Error: Confusing the dummy integration variable with the variable controlling the accumulation function.
  • Common Error: Treating geometric area and signed definite integral as identical when the integrand changes sign.
  • Core Interpretation: The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus connects local rates with total accumulation: $$ \boxed{ \text{rate} \xrightarrow{\text{integration}} \text{accumulated change} \xrightarrow{\text{differentiation}} \text{rate} }. $$ It is the central theorem linking derivative information, antiderivatives, and definite integrals into one coherent calculus framework.
