Higher Derivative

Subfield: Calculus & Analysis

Etymology & Roots

A higher derivative is a derivative obtained by differentiating a function more than once. The phrase distinguishes second, third, and subsequent derivatives from the first derivative.

$$ \boxed{ f \longrightarrow f' \longrightarrow f'' \longrightarrow f^{(3)} \longrightarrow \cdots \longrightarrow f^{(n)} } $$

The order of a derivative records how many times differentiation has been applied. Thus $f''$ is second order and $f^{(n)}$ is the $n$th derivative.

Operational Nuance

Second Derivative

The second derivative is the derivative of the first derivative:

$$ \boxed{ f''(x)=\frac{d}{dx}\left(f'(x)\right) }. $$

It measures how the first derivative itself changes.

nth Derivative

Repeated differentiation is written compactly as:

$$ \boxed{ f^{(n)}(x) = \frac{d^n f}{dx^n} }. $$

This notation avoids increasingly long strings of prime marks.

Example

For $f(x)=x^4$:

$$ f'(x)=4x^3, \qquad f''(x)=12x^2, \qquad f^{(3)}(x)=24x, \qquad f^{(4)}(x)=24. $$

Every derivative of order greater than four is zero.

Concavity

The second derivative is commonly used to analyze concavity:

$$ \boxed{ f''(x)>0 \Rightarrow \text{concave up}, \qquad f''(x)<0 \Rightarrow \text{concave down} } $$

on intervals where the corresponding inequalities hold.

Motion

If $s(t)$ is position, then:

$$ \boxed{ s'(t)=v(t), \qquad s''(t)=a(t) }. $$

The third derivative of position is sometimes called jerk.

Higher-Order Approximation

Higher derivatives supply coefficients in Taylor approximations:

$$ f(x) \approx f(a) +f'(a)(x-a) +\frac{f''(a)}{2!}(x-a)^2 +\cdots. $$

Existence Is Not Automatic

A function may possess a first derivative without possessing a second derivative everywhere. Each additional derivative requires further differentiability.

Smoothness

The notation $f\in C^n$ means that derivatives through order $n$ exist and are continuous. A function described as smooth commonly means $f\in C^\infty$.

Common Misconception

A higher derivative is not a power of the first derivative. In general, $f''(x)\neq[f'(x)]^2$; the second derivative means differentiate twice, not square the derivative.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$f''(x)$ "The second derivative of f."
$f^{(3)}(x)$ "The third derivative of f."
$f^{(n)}(x)$ "The nth derivative of f."
$\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}$ "The second derivative of y with respect to x."
$\dfrac{d^ny}{dx^n}$ "The nth derivative of y with respect to x."
$f\in C^n$ "F is n-times continuously differentiable."

Prime notation is convenient for the first few derivative orders, while parenthetical notation $f^{(n)}$ and Leibniz notation $d^nf/dx^n$ are clearer for higher orders.

Defense Notation Advice: Do not confuse $f^{(n)}$ with the power $f^n$. Parentheses around the superscript $n$ identify derivative order rather than exponentiation.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Second Derivative: Measures change in the first derivative and supports concavity analysis.
  • Third and Higher Derivatives: Describe progressively higher-order changes in local behavior.
  • Kinematics: Position derivatives yield velocity, acceleration, jerk, and higher motion rates.
  • Curve Analysis: Second and higher derivatives help describe curvature and local shape.
  • Optimization: Second and higher derivative tests can classify stationary points under suitable conditions.
  • Taylor Series: Higher derivatives determine coefficients of local polynomial expansions.
  • Differential Equations: Equation order is determined by the highest derivative appearing in the equation.
  • Polynomial Functions: Repeated differentiation eventually produces zero.
  • Exponential Functions: Repeated differentiation of $e^x$ reproduces the same function.
  • Trigonometric Functions: Repeated derivatives of sine and cosine cycle through a periodic pattern.
  • Smoothness: The number and continuity of higher derivatives classify levels of regularity.
  • Multivariable Calculus: Higher derivatives generalize to second partial derivatives, Hessian matrices, and higher-order derivative tensors.
  • Common Error: Interpreting $f''$ as $(f')^2$.
  • Common Error: Assuming existence of $f'$ automatically guarantees existence of $f''$.
  • Common Error: Confusing $f^{(n)}$ with $[f(x)]^n$.
  • Core Interpretation: Higher derivatives repeatedly measure change in previous rates of change: $$ \boxed{ f \longrightarrow f' \longrightarrow f'' \longrightarrow \cdots \longrightarrow f^{(n)} }. $$ They reveal progressively finer information about motion, curvature, approximation, smoothness, and local function behavior.
