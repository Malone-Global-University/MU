Chain Rule

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

The Chain Rule is the differentiation rule used for a composition of functions.

If:

$$ y=f(g(x)), $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dy}{dx} = f'(g(x))\,g'(x) }. $$

The word chain reflects the layered dependency:

$$ \boxed{ x \longrightarrow g(x) \longrightarrow f(g(x)) }. $$

A change in $x$ first changes the inner function, and that change then passes through the outer function.

Operational Nuance

Standard Form

If:

$$ y=f(g(x)), $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ y' = f'(g(x))g'(x) }. $$

Outer-Then-Inner Interpretation

A practical way to read the Chain Rule is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{differentiate the outer function} \times \text{differentiate the inner function} }. $$

The inner expression remains in place while the outer function is differentiated.

Example: Power of a Linear Expression

Let:

$$ y=(3x+2)^5. $$

The outer function is:

$$ u^5, $$

and the inner function is:

$$ u=3x+2. $$

Differentiating:

$$ \frac{dy}{du}=5u^4 $$

and:

$$ \frac{du}{dx}=3. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dy}{dx} = 5(3x+2)^4(3) = 15(3x+2)^4 }. $$

Leibniz-Style Notation

If:

$$ y=f(u), \qquad u=g(x), $$

then the Chain Rule is often written:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du} \frac{du}{dx} }. $$

This notation is extremely useful because it visually tracks the dependency through the intermediate variable $u$.

The Differential Fractions Are Not Ordinary Fractions

The expression:

$$ \frac{dy}{du}\frac{du}{dx} $$

resembles cancellation of $du$, and that mnemonic is often useful, but derivatives are not defined merely as algebraic fractions.

The validity comes from the Chain Rule itself.

Function-Composition Notation

If:

$$ h=f\circ g, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ h'(x) = f'(g(x))g'(x) }. $$

Operator Form

$$ \boxed{ (f\circ g)' = (f'\circ g)\,g' }. $$

Example: Trigonometric Composition

Let:

$$ y=\sin(x^2). $$

Differentiate the outer sine:

$$ \cos(x^2), $$

then multiply by the derivative of $x^2$:

$$ \boxed{ y' = 2x\cos(x^2) }. $$

Example: Exponential Composition

If:

$$ y=e^{4x^3}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ y' = e^{4x^3}(12x^2) }. $$

Example: Logarithmic Composition

If:

$$ y=\ln(5x^2+1), $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ y' = \frac{10x}{5x^2+1} }. $$

Example: Square Root

Write:

$$ y=\sqrt{1+x^4} = (1+x^4)^{1/2}. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ y' = \frac{2x^3}{\sqrt{1+x^4}} }. $$

Multiple Layers

The Chain Rule can be repeated through several nested functions.

For:

$$ y=f(g(h(x))), $$

one obtains:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dy}{dx} = f'(g(h(x))) g'(h(x)) h'(x) }. $$

Example with Three Layers

Consider:

$$ y=\sin\left((x^2+1)^3\right). $$

Differentiating layer by layer:

$$ \boxed{ y' = \cos\left((x^2+1)^3\right) \cdot 3(x^2+1)^2 \cdot 2x }. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ y' = 6x(x^2+1)^2 \cos\left((x^2+1)^3\right) }. $$

General Nested Chain

If:

$$ x \to u_1 \to u_2 \to\cdots \to u_n \to y, $$

then schematically:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du_n} \frac{du_n}{du_{n-1}} \cdots \frac{du_2}{du_1} \frac{du_1}{dx} }. $$

Power Rule with an Inner Function

A particularly common form is:

$$ y=[u(x)]^n. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ y' = n[u(x)]^{n-1}u'(x) }. $$

Trigonometric Chain Rules

$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}\sin(u) = \cos(u)\,u' } $$
$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}\cos(u) = -\sin(u)\,u' } $$
$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}\tan(u) = \sec^2(u)\,u' }. $$

Exponential Chain Rules

$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}e^{u} = e^u u' } $$
$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}a^u = a^u\ln(a)\,u' }. $$

Logarithmic Chain Rule

$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}\ln|u| = \frac{u'}{u} } $$

wherever the expression is defined.

Inverse Trigonometric Example

If:

$$ y=\arctan(3x), $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ y' = \frac{3}{1+9x^2} }. $$

Product Rule and Chain Rule Together

Differentiation rules often appear together.

If:

$$ y=x^2\sin(x^3), $$

then the Product Rule handles the product and the Chain Rule handles $\sin(x^3)$:

$$ \boxed{ y' = 2x\sin(x^3) + 3x^4\cos(x^3) }. $$

Quotient Rule and Chain Rule Together

For:

$$ y= \frac{1}{1+\sin(x^2)}, $$

it may be rewritten as:

$$ y= [1+\sin(x^2)]^{-1}. $$

Applying the Chain Rule repeatedly gives:

$$ \boxed{ y' = -\frac{2x\cos(x^2)} {[1+\sin(x^2)]^2} }. $$

Implicit Differentiation

The Chain Rule is essential in implicit differentiation because $y$ is treated as a function of $x$.

For example:

$$ x^2+y^2=25. $$

Differentiating with respect to $x$:

$$ 2x+2y\frac{dy}{dx}=0. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{x}{y} }. $$

Why dy/dx Appears

Differentiating:

$$ y^2 $$

with respect to $x$ means differentiating the composition:

$$ [y(x)]^2. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}(y^2) = 2y\frac{dy}{dx} }. $$

Related Rates

Related-rates problems are also applications of the Chain Rule.

If the radius of a circle changes with time:

$$ A=\pi r^2, \qquad r=r(t), $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dA}{dt} = 2\pi r \frac{dr}{dt} }. $$

Derivative of an Inverse Function

The inverse-function derivative formula can be derived from the Chain Rule.

If:

$$ f(f^{-1}(x))=x, $$

differentiating gives:

$$ f'(f^{-1}(x)) (f^{-1})'(x) = 1. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ (f^{-1})'(x) = \frac{1} {f'(f^{-1}(x))} } $$

where the denominator is nonzero.

Second Derivative of a Composition

If:

$$ y=f(g(x)), $$

then:

$$ y' = f'(g(x))g'(x). $$

Differentiating again requires both the Product Rule and Chain Rule:

$$ \boxed{ y'' = f''(g(x))[g'(x)]^2 + f'(g(x))g''(x) }. $$

Multivariable Chain Rule

Suppose:

$$ z=f(x,y), \qquad x=x(t), \qquad y=y(t). $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dz}{dt} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \frac{dy}{dt} }. $$

Several Intermediate Variables

If:

$$ z=f(u_1,\ldots,u_n), \qquad u_i=u_i(t), $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dz}{dt} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{\partial f}{\partial u_i} \frac{du_i}{dt} }. $$

Jacobian Matrix Form

For differentiable vector-valued functions:

$$ g:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m, \qquad f:\mathbb R^m\to\mathbb R^p, $$

the multivariable Chain Rule is:

$$ \boxed{ D(f\circ g)(x) = Df(g(x))\,Dg(x) }. $$

Here the derivative is represented by a linear map or Jacobian matrix.

Order of Jacobian Multiplication Matters

Matrix multiplication is generally not commutative, so:

$$ \boxed{ Df(g(x))Dg(x) } $$

must appear in the order dictated by the composition.

Geometric Interpretation

The derivative measures local scaling. For a composition, the local effect of the inner map is followed by the local effect of the outer map.

$$ \boxed{ \text{local change of composition} = \text{outer local change} \circ \text{inner local change} }. $$

Rate Interpretation

If $y$ changes with $u$ and $u$ changes with $x$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \text{rate of }y\text{ with respect to }x = \text{rate of }y\text{ with respect to }u \times \text{rate of }u\text{ with respect to }x }. $$

Common Misconception: Differentiate Only the Outer Function

For:

$$ (x^2+1)^5, $$

writing only:

$$ 5(x^2+1)^4 $$

is incomplete.

The derivative of the inner function must also appear:

$$ \boxed{ 10x(x^2+1)^4 }. $$

Common Misconception: Differentiate the Inner Expression First and Replace It

The inner function itself remains inside the derivative of the outer function. Only after that is its derivative multiplied.

Common Misconception: Chain Rule Is the Product Rule

The Product Rule differentiates products:

$$ f(x)g(x). $$

The Chain Rule differentiates compositions:

$$ f(g(x)). $$

Common Misconception: Parentheses Automatically Mean Chain Rule

Parentheses alone do not determine the rule. The important question is whether one function is being applied to the output of another.

Common Misconception: One Application Is Always Enough

Deeply nested functions may require the Chain Rule repeatedly, once for each compositional layer.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\frac{d}{dx}f(g(x))=f'(g(x))g'(x)$ "Differentiate the outer function evaluated at the inner function, then multiply by the derivative of the inner function."
$\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{du}\frac{du}{dx}$ "The rate of y with respect to x equals the rate of y with respect to u times the rate of u with respect to x."
$(f\circ g)'=(f'\circ g)g'$ "The derivative of f composed with g is f prime composed with g times g prime."
$\frac{d}{dx}[u(x)]^n=n[u(x)]^{n-1}u'(x)$ "Differentiate the outer power, then multiply by u prime."
$D(f\circ g)=Df\circ g\; Dg$ "The derivative of the composition is the derivative of the outer map evaluated at the inner map, composed with the derivative of the inner map."

In spoken classroom language, the Chain Rule is often summarized as outside derivative times inside derivative. This is useful as a mnemonic, but the formal statement is the derivative rule for compositions.

Defense Notation Advice: Identify the compositional layers before differentiating. For nested functions, work from the outside inward while multiplying by the derivative of each successive inner layer. In multivariable settings, preserve the order of derivative maps or Jacobian matrices.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Rule: $$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}f(g(x)) = f'(g(x))g'(x) }. $$
  • Composition Notation: $$ \boxed{ (f\circ g)' = (f'\circ g)g' }. $$
  • Leibniz Form: $$ \boxed{ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du} \frac{du}{dx} }. $$
  • Power Form: $$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}[u(x)]^n = n[u(x)]^{n-1}u'(x) }. $$
  • Sine Composition: $$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}\sin(u) = \cos(u)u' }. $$
  • Exponential Composition: $$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}e^u = e^u u' }. $$
  • Logarithmic Composition: $$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}\ln|u| = \frac{u'}{u} }. $$
  • Nested Functions: Apply the Chain Rule repeatedly through every compositional layer.
  • Implicit Differentiation: Terms involving $y$ require factors of $dy/dx$ because $y=y(x)$.
  • Related Rates: Rates with respect to time are connected through dependent variables by the Chain Rule.
  • Inverse Functions: The inverse derivative formula follows from differentiating $f(f^{-1}(x))=x$.
  • Second Derivative: $$ \boxed{ \frac{d^2}{dx^2}f(g(x)) = f''(g(x))[g'(x)]^2 + f'(g(x))g''(x) }. $$
  • Multivariable Form: $$ \boxed{ \frac{dz}{dt} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} \frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} \frac{dy}{dt} }. $$
  • Jacobian Form: $$ \boxed{ D(f\circ g)(x) = Df(g(x))Dg(x) }. $$
  • Geometric Interpretation: The derivative of a composition combines successive local transformations.
  • Rate Interpretation: Rates multiply along a chain of dependent variables.
  • Common Error: Do not omit the derivative of the inner function.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse composition with multiplication.
  • Common Error: Do not stop after one Chain Rule step when several nested layers remain.
  • Common Error: Do not reverse Jacobian order in multivariable composition.
  • Terminological Contrast: Chain Rule differentiates function compositions. Product Rule differentiates products of functions. Quotient Rule differentiates quotients. Composition means using one function's output as another function's input. Implicit differentiation uses the Chain Rule when dependent variables appear without first being solved explicitly. Related rates apply the Chain Rule to quantities that vary together over time.
