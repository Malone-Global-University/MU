Critical Number

Subfield: Calculus & Analysis

Etymology & Roots

In calculus, a critical number of a function is an input value at which the derivative is zero or fails to exist, subject to the requirement that the input itself belongs to the domain of the function.

For a function $f$, a standard definition is:

$$ \boxed{ c\text{ is a critical number of }f \iff c\in\operatorname{Dom}(f) \text{ and } \left[f'(c)=0 \text{ or } f'(c)\text{ does not exist}\right] }. $$

The adjective critical reflects the importance of these values in analyzing a function. Critical numbers identify locations where ordinary differentiable behavior changes or where the tangent becomes horizontal, undefined, or otherwise exceptional.

They are especially important because local maxima and minima occurring at interior points of a function's domain must occur at critical numbers when the hypotheses of Fermat's theorem apply.

The term critical number refers to the input value $c$. The corresponding point on the graph:

$$ \boxed{(c,f(c))} $$

is commonly called a critical point, although terminology varies somewhat among textbooks and mathematical fields.

Operational Nuance

Derivative Equal to Zero

The first major source of critical numbers is:

$$ \boxed{f'(c)=0}. $$

At such a point, a differentiable graph has a horizontal tangent line.

For example, let:

$$ f(x)=x^2-4x+7. $$

Then:

$$ f'(x)=2x-4. $$

Setting the derivative equal to zero gives:

$$ 2x-4=0, $$
$$ \boxed{x=2}. $$

Therefore $2$ is a critical number of $f$.

Derivative Does Not Exist

A critical number can also occur where the derivative does not exist, provided the function itself is defined there.

Consider:

$$ f(x)=|x|. $$

The function is defined at:

$$ x=0, $$

but:

$$ f'(0) $$

does not exist because the graph has a corner there.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{x=0} $$

is a critical number.

Domain Requirement

A point where the derivative is undefined is not automatically a critical number. The original function must also be defined there.

Consider:

$$ f(x)=\frac1x. $$

The derivative:

$$ f'(x)=-\frac1{x^2} $$

is undefined at $x=0$, but the original function is also undefined there:

$$ 0\notin\operatorname{Dom}(f). $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ x=0\text{ is not a critical number of }f(x)=\frac1x }. $$

Critical Numbers and Local Extrema

Fermat's theorem states that if $f$ has a local maximum or minimum at an interior point $c$ and $f$ is differentiable at $c$, then:

$$ \boxed{f'(c)=0}. $$

Consequently, critical numbers are natural candidates for local extrema.

However, being a critical number does not guarantee that a local maximum or minimum occurs.

Critical Number Without an Extremum

Consider:

$$ f(x)=x^3. $$

Then:

$$ f'(x)=3x^2. $$

Therefore:

$$ f'(0)=0. $$

Thus $0$ is a critical number, but $f(x)=x^3$ has neither a local maximum nor a local minimum at $x=0$.

Instead, the point:

$$ (0,0) $$

is a stationary inflection point.

Critical Number at a Corner

For:

$$ f(x)=|x|, $$

$x=0$ is both a critical number and the location of a local and absolute minimum, even though:

$$ f'(0) $$

does not exist.

Critical Number at a Vertical Tangent

Consider:

$$ f(x)=x^{1/3}. $$

Its derivative for $x\neq0$ is:

$$ f'(x)=\frac{1}{3x^{2/3}}. $$

The function is defined at $x=0$, while the derivative is not finite there. Under the standard definition:

$$ \boxed{x=0} $$

is a critical number.

Again, it is not a local maximum or minimum.

Finding Critical Numbers

A systematic procedure is:

  1. Determine the domain of $f$.
  2. Compute $f'(x)$ where possible.
  3. Solve $f'(x)=0$.
  4. Find values where $f'(x)$ does not exist.
  5. Keep only values that belong to the domain of $f$.
$$ \boxed{ \text{Critical numbers} = \left\{ c\in\operatorname{Dom}(f): f'(c)=0 \text{ or } f'(c)\text{ DNE} \right\} }. $$

Example with Several Candidates

Consider:

$$ f(x)=x^{2/3}(x-2). $$

Differentiating for $x\neq0$ gives:

$$ f'(x) = \frac{2}{3}x^{-1/3}(x-2)+x^{2/3}. $$

Factoring:

$$ f'(x) = \frac{5x-4}{3x^{1/3}}. $$

The derivative is zero when:

$$ 5x-4=0, $$
$$ \boxed{x=\frac45}. $$

The derivative is undefined at:

$$ x=0. $$

Since $f(0)$ exists, $0$ is also a critical number.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ x=0 \quad\text{and}\quad x=\frac45 } $$

are the critical numbers.

Critical Numbers and the First Derivative Test

Once critical numbers are found, the sign of $f'$ on neighboring intervals can classify local behavior.

$$ + \longrightarrow - \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{local maximum} $$
$$ - \longrightarrow + \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{local minimum} $$
$$ + \longrightarrow + \quad\text{or}\quad - \longrightarrow - \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{no local extremum} $$

Critical Numbers and the Second Derivative Test

If:

$$ f'(c)=0 $$

and $f''(c)$ exists, then:

  • if $f''(c)>0$, $f$ has a local minimum at $c$;
  • if $f''(c)<0$, $f$ has a local maximum at $c$;
  • if $f''(c)=0$, the test is inconclusive.

Endpoint Convention

Textbooks are not entirely uniform about endpoints. Under a broad definition, an endpoint belonging to the domain may be called a critical number if the ordinary two-sided derivative does not exist there. Other texts reserve the term for interior domain points.

For optimization on a closed interval, endpoints must be checked regardless of which terminology convention is adopted.

Common Misconception: A critical number is not automatically a maximum or minimum. Critical numbers are candidates requiring additional analysis.

A critical number is an input value, so it is ordinarily written:

$$ \boxed{x=c}. $$

The corresponding graph location is:

$$ \boxed{(c,f(c))}. $$

Thus one may say:

$$ \text{"The critical number is }c, \text{ and the corresponding critical point is }(c,f(c))." $$

In ordinary calculus instruction, one frequently hears:

$$ \text{"Find the critical numbers by setting }f'(x)=0 \text{ and checking where }f'(x)\text{ is undefined."} $$

That instruction is incomplete unless one also verifies that each candidate belongs to the domain of the original function.

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish the critical number $c$ from the critical point $(c,f(c))$. Also check the original function's domain before treating a value where $f'$ is undefined as a critical number.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Standard Definition: $$ \boxed{ c\in\operatorname{Dom}(f), \qquad f'(c)=0 \text{ or } f'(c)\text{ does not exist} }. $$
  • Horizontal Tangent: If: $$ f'(c)=0, $$ then $c$ is a critical number and the tangent is horizontal when the derivative exists in the ordinary sense.
  • Corner: A corner can create a critical number because the derivative may fail to exist while the function remains defined.
  • Cusp: A cusp may produce a critical number when the function is defined but differentiability fails.
  • Vertical Tangent: A vertical tangent may correspond to a critical number under the common definition when $f(c)$ exists but $f'(c)$ is not a finite real number.
  • Discontinuity: A point outside the domain of $f$ is not a critical number merely because $f'$ is undefined there.
  • Polynomial Functions: Since polynomials are differentiable everywhere, their critical numbers occur exactly where: $$ \boxed{f'(x)=0}. $$
  • Rational Functions: Values making the original rational function undefined must be excluded, even if they also make the derivative undefined.
  • Absolute-Value Functions: Functions involving absolute value often have corners that create critical numbers.
  • Piecewise Functions: Junction points should be checked for continuity and differentiability. A defined junction at which the derivative fails to exist may be critical.
  • Local Maximum: If an interior local maximum occurs at $c$ and $f$ is differentiable at $c$, then: $$ \boxed{f'(c)=0}. $$
  • Local Minimum: If an interior local minimum occurs at $c$ and $f$ is differentiable at $c$, then: $$ \boxed{f'(c)=0}. $$
  • Fermat's Theorem: For an interior local extremum where $f$ is differentiable: $$ \boxed{ \text{local extremum at }c \Longrightarrow f'(c)=0 }. $$
  • Converse Failure: The converse of Fermat's theorem is false: $$ f'(c)=0 $$ does not imply that $c$ is a local extremum.
  • Stationary Point: A point where: $$ f'(c)=0 $$ is commonly called a stationary point. Every stationary input is critical, but a critical number can also arise where the derivative does not exist.
  • Critical Number vs. Stationary Number: $$ \boxed{ \text{stationary} \Longrightarrow \text{critical} } $$ under the standard terminology, but not every critical number is stationary.
  • First Derivative Test: A sign change: $$ + \rightarrow - $$ across a critical number indicates a local maximum.
  • First Derivative Test for Minimum: A sign change: $$ - \rightarrow + $$ indicates a local minimum.
  • No Sign Change: If $f'$ keeps the same sign across a critical number, that critical number does not produce a local extremum.
  • Second Derivative Test: If: $$ f'(c)=0 $$ and: $$ f''(c)>0, $$ then $f$ has a local minimum at $c$.
  • Second Derivative Test for Maximum: If: $$ f'(c)=0 $$ and: $$ f''(c)<0, $$ then $f$ has a local maximum at $c$.
  • Inconclusive Second Derivative: If: $$ f''(c)=0, $$ the second derivative test alone does not classify the critical number.
  • Inflection Point: A critical number may correspond to an inflection point, as with: $$ f(x)=x^3 $$ at: $$ x=0. $$
  • Closed-Interval Optimization: To find absolute extrema of a continuous function on: $$ [a,b], $$ evaluate the function at interior critical numbers and at both endpoints.
  • Closed Interval Method: Candidate values are: $$ \boxed{ \text{critical numbers in }(a,b) \quad+\quad a \quad+\quad b }. $$
  • Endpoints: Endpoints may be absolute extrema even when they are not classified as critical numbers under a convention requiring critical numbers to be interior points.
  • Optimization: In applied optimization, critical numbers identify candidate input values for maximizing or minimizing quantities such as cost, revenue, area, volume, distance, or efficiency.
  • Monotonicity: Critical numbers divide the domain into intervals on which the sign of the derivative can often be analyzed to determine where a function increases or decreases.
  • Graph Sketching: Critical numbers help organize a derivative sign chart and identify potentially significant features of the graph.
  • Multivariable Analogue: For a differentiable scalar-valued function of several variables, a critical point commonly satisfies: $$ \boxed{\nabla f=\mathbf{0}}, $$ with broader definitions also accounting for points where the derivative fails to exist.
  • Terminological Variation: Some texts use critical point for what other texts call a critical number, while others distinguish the input $c$ from the graph point $(c,f(c))$. The local convention should therefore be checked.
  • Domain Restriction: The condition: $$ \boxed{c\in\operatorname{Dom}(f)} $$ is essential. A hole, vertical asymptote, or other excluded input is not automatically a critical number.
  • Necessary but Not Sufficient: For differentiable interior extrema, being critical is necessary, but criticality alone is not sufficient for an extremum.
  • Terminological Contrast: A critical number is an input at which $f'=0$ or $f'$ does not exist while $f$ is defined. A critical point commonly refers to the corresponding point $(c,f(c))$. A stationary point specifically requires a zero derivative.
  • Core Interpretation: Critical numbers identify the input values where the ordinary derivative test for smooth nonzero slope breaks down: $$ \boxed{ f'(c)=0 \quad\text{or}\quad f'(c)\text{ does not exist}, \qquad c\in\operatorname{Dom}(f) }. $$ They are therefore the principal candidate locations for extrema and other significant changes in local behavior.
