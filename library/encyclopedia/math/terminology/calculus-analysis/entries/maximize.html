Maximize

Subfield: Calculus & Analysis

Etymology & Roots

To maximize a mathematical quantity means to find, attain, or select the greatest allowable value of that quantity under a specified domain or set of constraints.

If a function $f$ is defined on a feasible set $D$, the instruction:

$$ \boxed{ \text{maximize }f(x) \text{ for }x\in D } $$

asks for a point or points at which $f$ is as large as possible within $D$.

If $x^*\in D$ satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ f(x^*)\geq f(x) \quad\text{for every }x\in D }, $$

then $x^*$ is a maximizer and $f(x^*)$ is a maximum value.

The word derives from Latin maximus, meaning greatest or largest. Mathematical usage preserves this comparative meaning but requires a reference set: something is maximal or maximum only relative to an allowed collection of possibilities.

The operational contrast is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{maximize} \longleftrightarrow \text{make as large as allowed} } $$

whereas minimize seeks the smallest allowable value.

Operational Nuance

Objective Function

The quantity being maximized is often called the objective function.

$$ \boxed{ \text{maximize }f(x) } $$

means $f$ is the objective.

Feasible Set

The values of $x$ that are allowed form the feasible set.

$$ \boxed{ x\in D }. $$

Unconstrained Maximization

An unconstrained problem seeks the largest value over the natural or stated domain without additional equality or inequality restrictions.

$$ \boxed{ \max_x f(x) }. $$

Constrained Maximization

A constrained problem restricts the allowable inputs.

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \text{maximize } & f(\mathbf{x})\\ \text{subject to } & g_i(\mathbf{x})\leq0,\\ & h_j(\mathbf{x})=0 \end{aligned} } $$

Maximum Value

If a maximum exists:

$$ \boxed{ \max_{x\in D}f(x) } $$

denotes the greatest attained value.

Maximizer

A point where the maximum occurs is called a maximizer.

$$ \boxed{ x^* \in \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{x\in D}f(x) }. $$

Maximum vs. Maximizer

These are different objects:

$$ \boxed{ \text{maximum value}=f(x^*) } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{maximizer}=x^* }. $$

Argmax

The notation:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{x\in D}f(x) } $$

denotes the set of input values at which the maximum is attained.

Multiple Maximizers

A function can have more than one maximizer.

For example:

$$ f(x)=-(x^2-1)^2 $$

has maximum value $0$ at:

$$ \boxed{ x=-1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad x=1 }. $$

Unique Maximizer

A maximizer is unique when exactly one feasible input attains the greatest value.

Global Maximum

A point $x^*$ is a global maximizer when:

$$ \boxed{ f(x^*)\geq f(x) \quad \text{for every }x\in D }. $$

Local Maximum

A point $x^*$ is a local maximizer when $f(x^*)$ is at least as large as nearby feasible values.

$$ \boxed{ f(x^*)\geq f(x) \quad \text{for }x \text{ sufficiently near }x^* }. $$

Local vs. Global

A local maximum need not be the largest value over the entire domain.

Strict Local Maximum

A strict local maximum satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ f(x^*)>f(x) } $$

for all sufficiently nearby feasible $x\neq x^*$.

Absolute Maximum

Absolute maximum is a common synonym for global maximum.

Relative Maximum

Relative maximum is a common traditional synonym for local maximum.

Maximum Need Not Exist

A function can be bounded above without attaining its least upper bound.

For:

$$ f(x)=x, \qquad 0<x<1, $$

values can approach $1$, but no allowed input gives:

$$ f(x)=1. $$

Thus there is no maximum on $(0,1)$.

Supremum

The least upper bound in the previous example is:

$$ \boxed{ \sup_{0<x<1}x=1 }, $$

even though the maximum does not exist.

Maximum vs. Supremum

A maximum must be attained. A supremum need not be.

$$ \boxed{ \max D \text{ exists} \Longrightarrow \max D=\sup D } $$

when interpreted for a nonempty ordered set of real values.

Extreme Value Theorem

If $f$ is continuous on a closed bounded interval:

$$ \boxed{ [a,b] }, $$

then $f$ attains both an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum on that interval.

Closed-Interval Method

To maximize a differentiable function on $[a,b]$:

  1. find critical numbers in $(a,b)$;
  2. evaluate the function at those critical numbers;
  3. evaluate the endpoints;
  4. compare all resulting values.

Critical Number

A critical number $c$ is typically a domain point where:

$$ \boxed{ f'(c)=0 } $$

or where $f'(c)$ does not exist.

Fermat's Theorem

If $f$ has a local extremum at an interior point $c$ and is differentiable there, then:

$$ \boxed{ f'(c)=0 }. $$

Derivative Zero Is Not Sufficient

A critical point satisfying $f'(c)=0$ need not be a maximum.

For example:

$$ f(x)=x^3 $$

has:

$$ f'(0)=0, $$

but $x=0$ is neither a local maximum nor a local minimum.

First-Derivative Test

If $f'$ changes sign:

$$ \boxed{ +\longrightarrow- } $$

at $x=c$, then $f$ has a local maximum at $c$.

Second-Derivative Test

If:

$$ f'(c)=0 $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ f''(c)<0 }, $$

then $f$ has a strict local maximum at $c$.

Second-Derivative Test Can Be Inconclusive

If:

$$ f''(c)=0, $$

the test does not decide the nature of the critical point.

Concavity and Maximization

A concave function has a useful optimization structure: local maxima are global maxima on convex domains under standard conditions.

Strict Concavity

A strictly concave objective on a convex feasible set can have at most one global maximizer.

Quadratic Example

Consider:

$$ f(x) = -x^2+6x+1. $$

Complete the square:

$$ f(x) = -(x-3)^2+10. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \max f=10 } $$

attained at:

$$ \boxed{ x=3 }. $$

Vertex Method

For:

$$ f(x)=ax^2+bx+c $$

with $a<0$, the parabola opens downward and its vertex gives the maximum.

$$ \boxed{ x = -\frac{b}{2a} }. $$

Endpoint Maximum

A maximum on a restricted interval may occur at an endpoint rather than at a critical point.

Example

On:

$$ [0,2], $$

the function:

$$ f(x)=x $$

is maximized at:

$$ \boxed{ x=2 }. $$

Domain Restrictions Matter

Changing the feasible domain can change the maximizing input and maximum value.

Discrete Maximization

A domain need not be continuous.

To maximize:

$$ f(n) $$

over integers:

$$ \boxed{ n\in\mathbb{Z} }, $$

calculus-based critical-point methods may identify useful candidates, but integer feasibility must still be enforced.

Finite-Set Maximization

For a finite feasible set, one can in principle evaluate every candidate and choose the greatest objective value.

Optimization

Maximization is one of the two central directions of optimization:

$$ \boxed{ \text{optimization} = \text{maximization or minimization} }. $$

Converting Maximization to Minimization

Maximizing $f$ is equivalent to minimizing $-f$:

$$ \boxed{ \max_x f(x) = -\min_x[-f(x)] } $$

when the extrema exist under the same feasible set.

Profit Maximization

In an applied model:

$$ \boxed{ P(q) = R(q)-C(q) } $$

may represent profit as revenue minus cost.

Maximizing profit asks for the feasible quantity $q$ giving the greatest value of $P(q)$.

Marginal Condition

At an interior differentiable profit maximizer:

$$ P'(q)=0. $$

Since:

$$ P'(q) = R'(q)-C'(q), $$

a candidate may satisfy:

$$ \boxed{ R'(q)=C'(q) }. $$

This condition must still be interpreted with feasibility and second-order or global checks.

Area Maximization

Geometric optimization often constructs an objective function from constraints.

Rectangle Example

For a rectangle with fixed perimeter $P$:

$$ 2x+2y=P. $$

Then:

$$ y = \frac{P}{2}-x. $$

Area becomes:

$$ A(x) = x\left( \frac{P}{2}-x \right). $$

This downward-opening quadratic is maximized when:

$$ \boxed{ x=y=\frac{P}{4} }. $$

Thus among rectangles with a fixed perimeter, the square has greatest area.

Volume Maximization

Similar techniques can optimize dimensions of boxes, cylinders, or other solids under material or geometric constraints.

Distance Maximization

One may maximize:

$$ \boxed{ d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a}) } $$

over a bounded feasible set to locate points farthest from a reference point.

Squared Distance Shortcut

Because the square-root function is increasing on nonnegative inputs, maximizing:

$$ \sqrt{g(x)} $$

is equivalent to maximizing:

$$ \boxed{ g(x) } $$

whenever $g(x)\geq0$.

Monotone Transformations

If $\phi$ is strictly increasing, then $f$ and $\phi\circ f$ have the same maximizers.

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname*{arg\,max}_x f(x) = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_x \phi(f(x)) }. $$

Logarithm Shortcut

For a positive objective:

$$ f(x)>0, $$

maximizing $f$ is equivalent to maximizing:

$$ \boxed{ \ln f(x) } $$

because $\ln$ is strictly increasing.

Likelihood Maximization

Maximum likelihood estimation chooses parameter values maximizing a likelihood:

$$ \boxed{ \widehat{\theta} \in \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\theta} L(\theta) }. $$

Log-Likelihood

Because logarithms are increasing:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\theta}L(\theta) = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\theta}\log L(\theta) } $$

when $L(\theta)>0$.

Linear Programming

A linear-programming maximization problem may have form:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \text{maximize } & \mathbf{c}^T\mathbf{x}\\ \text{subject to } & A\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{b},\\ & \mathbf{x}\geq\mathbf{0} \end{aligned} }. $$

Corner-Point Principle

For a linear objective over a nonempty bounded polyhedral feasible region, a maximum, when considered under the usual finite-dimensional linear-programming assumptions, is attained at an extreme point, though it may also be attained along an entire edge or face.

Unbounded Maximization

A feasible optimization problem may have no finite maximum because the objective can increase without bound.

For example:

$$ \boxed{ \max_{x\geq0}x } $$

has no finite maximum.

Infeasible Problem

If no point satisfies all constraints, there is no feasible point to maximize over.

Multivariable Maximization

For:

$$ f(x_1,\ldots,x_n), $$

an interior differentiable maximizer must satisfy:

$$ \boxed{ \nabla f(\mathbf{x}^*) = \mathbf{0} } $$

under the usual unconstrained differentiability assumptions.

Gradient Condition Is Not Sufficient

A zero gradient identifies a critical point, which may be a maximum, minimum, or saddle point.

Hessian Test

If:

$$ \nabla f(\mathbf{x}^*)=\mathbf{0} $$

and the Hessian is negative definite at $\mathbf{x}^*$, then $f$ has a strict local maximum there.

$$ \boxed{ H_f(\mathbf{x}^*) \text{ negative definite} \Longrightarrow \mathbf{x}^* \text{ strict local maximizer} }. $$

Lagrange Multipliers

To maximize $f(\mathbf{x})$ subject to:

$$ g(\mathbf{x})=c, $$

an interior constrained candidate may satisfy:

$$ \boxed{ \nabla f = \lambda\nabla g }. $$

Interpretation of the Lagrange Condition

At a smooth constrained extremum, the objective gradient is aligned with the constraint normal under suitable regularity conditions.

Several Constraints

With constraints $g_i(\mathbf{x})=0$:

$$ \boxed{ \nabla f = \sum_i \lambda_i\nabla g_i }. $$

Inequality Constraints

More advanced constrained optimization uses conditions such as the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions under appropriate hypotheses.

Combinatorial Maximization

Some maximization problems involve permutations, graphs, subsets, or integer choices rather than continuous variables.

Graph Theory Example

A maximum matching problem seeks a matching with the greatest possible number or weight of edges.

Maximum vs. Maximal

These terms are not synonymous in partial-order and combinatorial settings.

A maximum element is at least as large as every other element. A maximal element merely has no strictly larger comparable element within the set.

Maximum Implies Maximal

$$ \boxed{ \text{maximum} \Longrightarrow \text{maximal} } $$

when these notions are defined in the same partially ordered set.

Maximal Need Not Mean Maximum

A partially ordered set may have several maximal elements and no maximum element.

Maximum of Two Numbers

For real numbers $a$ and $b$:

$$ \boxed{ \max(a,b) = \begin{cases} a, & a\geq b,\\ b, & b>a. \end{cases} } $$

Maximum of a Finite Collection

$$ \boxed{ \max\{x_1,\ldots,x_n\} } $$

denotes the greatest member.

Maximum Function

The function:

$$ \boxed{ m(a,b)=\max(a,b) } $$

returns the larger input.

Absolute Value through Maximum

$$ \boxed{ |x| = \max(x,-x) }. $$

Positive Part

The positive part of a real number can be written:

$$ \boxed{ x_+ = \max(x,0) }. $$

Pointwise Maximum

Given functions $f$ and $g$, their pointwise maximum is:

$$ \boxed{ h(x) = \max\{f(x),g(x)\} }. $$

Nondifferentiability of Max Functions

Even if $f$ and $g$ are differentiable, $\max(f,g)$ may fail to be differentiable where the active branch changes.

Example

$$ |x| = \max(x,-x) $$

is not differentiable at $x=0$.

Maximum Principle Language

In analysis and differential equations, maximum principle refers to theorems controlling where or how maxima of certain functions can occur. This is a specialized use of maximum terminology.

Expected-Value Maximization

In stochastic optimization, one may maximize:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{E}[U(X)] } $$

rather than a deterministic quantity.

Utility Maximization

Economic models may seek:

$$ \boxed{ \max_{\mathbf{x}} U(\mathbf{x}) } $$

subject to budget or resource constraints.

Portfolio Maximization

Financial optimization may maximize expected return, utility, or another objective subject to risk and allocation constraints.

Machine Learning

Some learning procedures maximize likelihood, reward, evidence, or another criterion, while others minimize loss. The two formulations can often be converted by changing a sign.

Reinforcement Learning

A policy may be selected to maximize expected cumulative reward.

Game Theory

Players may choose strategies intended to maximize payoff subject to the available strategy set and responses of other players.

Maximum Is Relative to the Feasible Set

The same function can have different maxima on different domains.

Example

For:

$$ f(x)=x^2, $$

there is no global maximum on $\mathbb{R}$, but on:

$$ [-2,3] $$

the maximum is:

$$ \boxed{ 9 } $$

attained at $x=3$.

Strict vs. Non-Strict Inequality

A global maximizer requires:

$$ f(x^*)\geq f(x), $$

not necessarily:

$$ f(x^*)>f(x) $$

for every other point, because multiple maximizers may exist.

Common Error: Maximizing the Input Instead of the Objective

"Maximize $f(x)$" asks for the largest value of $f$, not necessarily the largest allowable $x$.

Common Error: Reporting Only the Critical Number

If the question asks for the maximum value, report $f(x^*)$, not merely $x^*$.

Common Error: Reporting Only the Maximum Value

If the problem asks where the maximum occurs, report the maximizing input or inputs.

Common Error: Confusing Maximum with Maximizer

The maximum is an output value; the maximizer is an input.

Common Error: Assuming Every Critical Point Is a Maximum

Critical points can also be minima, saddle points, or neither.

Common Error: Ignoring Endpoints

On closed intervals, absolute maxima often occur at endpoints.

Common Error: Ignoring Nondifferentiable Points

A maximum can occur where the derivative does not exist.

Common Error: Assuming a Maximum Exists because the Function Is Bounded Above

A supremum may exist without being attained.

Common Error: Confusing Maximum with Supremum

A maximum belongs to the attainable value set; a supremum need not.

Common Error: Ignoring the Feasible Domain

A candidate outside the domain or violating constraints cannot maximize the problem.

Common Error: Assuming a Local Maximum Is Global

Local comparison does not automatically establish global optimality.

Common Error: Using the Second-Derivative Test without $f'(c)=0$

The standard one-variable second-derivative test is applied to an interior stationary point.

Common Error: Treating $f''(c)=0$ as Proof of No Maximum

A zero second derivative makes the test inconclusive rather than ruling out an extremum.

Common Error: Ignoring Constraints during Differentiation

Constrained optimization may require substitution, boundary analysis, Lagrange multipliers, or other methods beyond setting the unconstrained gradient to zero.

Common Error: Confusing Maximum with Maximal

In partially ordered or combinatorial settings, maximal means no larger feasible object contains or dominates it in the relevant order; it need not be globally greatest.

Common Error: Assuming a Maximization Problem Must Use Calculus

Finite search, algebra, inequalities, graphing, linear programming, dynamic programming, combinatorial algorithms, and other methods may be more appropriate.

Common Error: Assuming Larger Variable Values Always Increase the Objective

The objective may rise, fall, oscillate, or depend on several competing variables.

Common Error: Forgetting Units

The maximum value retains the units of the objective quantity, while the maximizer has the units of the input variable.

Common Error: Failing to Distinguish Exact and Approximate Maxima

Numerical optimization may return an approximate maximizer rather than a symbolic exact one.

Common Misconception: To maximize a function does not simply mean "make $x$ as large as possible." It means choose among the allowed inputs so that the objective value is as large as possible. The maximizing input, the maximum output, the feasible domain, and the existence of an attained optimum are separate pieces of the problem.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\max_{x\in D}f(x)$ "The maximum of f of x over x in D."
$x^*\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{x\in D}f(x)$ "X star is a maximizer of f over D."
$f(x^*)\geq f(x)$ for all $x\in D$ "F at x star is at least as large as f at every feasible x."
$f'(c)=0$ "C is a stationary-point candidate for an interior optimum."
$f''(c)<0$ "The second derivative is negative at c," supporting a strict local maximum when the standard test applies.
$\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^*)=\mathbf{0}$ "The gradient vanishes at the interior critical point."
$\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g$ "The objective gradient is proportional to the constraint gradient."

The verb maximize commonly appears as an instruction:

$$ \boxed{ \text{maximize }f(x) }. $$

The noun maximum refers to the greatest attained value, while maximizer refers to an input that attains that value.

The notation:

$$ \operatorname*{arg\,max} $$

is read "arg max" and returns maximizing arguments rather than objective values.

In applied work, the phrase subject to introduces constraints:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \text{maximize } & f(x)\\ \text{subject to } & x\in D \end{aligned} }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: State the feasible domain whenever it is not obvious, distinguish $\max$ from $\operatorname*{arg\,max}$, and specify whether the problem asks for a maximum value, a maximizing input, or both. Do not write $\max f$ if the domain or relevant variable is materially ambiguous. When calculus is used, check endpoints, nondifferentiable points, and constraints rather than relying only on $f'=0$.
