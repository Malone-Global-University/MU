Differentiable

Subfield: Calculus & Analysis

Etymology & Roots

A function is differentiable at a point if its derivative exists there. The term comes from differentiate, meaning to measure or describe change through differences.

$$ \boxed{ f'(a) = \lim_{h\to0} \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} } $$

If this limit exists as a finite real number, then $f$ is differentiable at $a$. Differentiability captures the idea that a function has a well-defined local linear behavior near that point.

Operational Nuance

Derivative Existence

The defining condition is the existence of the difference-quotient limit:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{h\to0} \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} \text{ exists} }. $$

Local Linear Approximation

Differentiability means that near $x=a$, the function behaves approximately like:

$$ \boxed{ f(a+h) = f(a)+f'(a)h+o(h) }. $$

Thus the tangent line gives the first-order local approximation.

Differentiability Implies Continuity

$$ \boxed{ f\text{ differentiable at }a \Longrightarrow f\text{ continuous at }a }. $$

The converse is false: a continuous function may fail to be differentiable.

Corner Example

The function:

$$ f(x)=|x| $$

is continuous at $0$ but not differentiable there because its left- and right-hand derivatives are different.

Cusps and Vertical Tangents

Cusps and certain vertical tangents can also cause failure of ordinary finite differentiability because the derivative does not exist as a finite real number.

Differentiable on an Interval

To say that $f$ is differentiable on an open interval means that it is differentiable at every point in that interval. At endpoints of a closed interval, one-sided derivatives may be used when explicitly stated.

Derivative Need Not Be Continuous

A differentiable function need not have a continuous derivative. The stronger condition that $f'$ is continuous is called continuously differentiable.

$$ \boxed{ f\in C^1 \Longrightarrow f\text{ differentiable} } $$

but differentiability alone does not imply $f\in C^1$.

Multivariable Meaning

For functions of several variables, differentiability means existence of a linear map that approximates the function to first order. Existence of all partial derivatives alone does not automatically guarantee differentiability.

Common Misconception

Differentiable does not simply mean "has a formula" or "has a slope-looking graph." It is a precise local limit property.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$f'(a)$ exists "F is differentiable at a."
$f'(a)=L$ "The derivative of f at a is L."
$f(a+h)=f(a)+f'(a)h+o(h)$ "F has a first-order linear approximation at a."
$f\in C^1$ "F is continuously differentiable."
$f'_-(a)\neq f'_+(a)$ "The one-sided derivatives do not agree."
$Df(a)$ "The derivative, or total derivative, at a in higher-dimensional settings."

The phrase differentiable at a point refers to a local property, while differentiable on an interval requires the property throughout the interval.

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish differentiability from continuity and from continuous differentiability. Also distinguish ordinary one-variable derivative existence from the stronger total-derivative requirement in multivariable analysis.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: A function is differentiable at a point when its derivative exists there.
  • Local Linearity: Differentiability means the function has a first-order linear approximation.
  • Continuity: Differentiability implies continuity at the same point.
  • Converse Failure: Continuity alone does not guarantee differentiability.
  • Corners: Distinct one-sided slopes prevent differentiability.
  • Cusps: Unbounded or incompatible tangent behavior can prevent finite differentiability.
  • Vertical Tangents: Ordinary real-valued derivatives may fail to exist finitely even when the curve has a clear tangent direction.
  • Intervals: Differentiability on an interval is a point-by-point condition.
  • Continuous Differentiability: $C^1$ is stronger than merely differentiable.
  • Higher Dimensions: Total differentiability requires approximation by a linear transformation.
  • Partial Derivatives: Their existence alone is not sufficient for multivariable differentiability.
  • Optimization: Differentiability allows derivative-based tests for extrema and local behavior.
  • Common Error: Assuming every continuous function is differentiable.
  • Common Error: Assuming existence of $f'(a)$ implies that $f'$ is continuous near $a$.
  • Common Error: Treating an infinite slope as an ordinary finite derivative.
  • Core Interpretation: Differentiability means that, at sufficiently small scales, a function is approximated by a linear change: $$ \boxed{ \Delta f = f'(a)\Delta x + \text{smaller-order error} }. $$ It is the central local regularity condition underlying tangent lines, derivatives, linearization, and differential analysis.
