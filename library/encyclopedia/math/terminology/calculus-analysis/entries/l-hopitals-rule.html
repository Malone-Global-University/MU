L'Hôpital's Rule

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

L'Hôpital's Rule is a theorem for evaluating certain limits of quotients whose numerator and denominator simultaneously approach $0$, or whose magnitudes both become unbounded. Under suitable hypotheses, the original quotient limit can be determined from the limit of the quotient of the derivatives.

The rule is named after the French mathematician Guillaume François Antoine, Marquis de l'Hôpital (1661–1704). His 1696 book Analyse des infiniment petits pour l'intelligence des lignes courbes was the first published textbook devoted to differential calculus and contained the procedure now associated with his name.

The historical attribution is more complicated than the modern name suggests. Johann Bernoulli taught l'Hôpital the new differential calculus and developed the rule appearing in l'Hôpital's book. Surviving correspondence and later-discovered lecture material strongly connect the result with Bernoulli. L'Hôpital himself openly acknowledged substantial reliance on the work of Johann and Jacob Bernoulli and Leibniz.

The name therefore reflects the work through which the rule became widely published, rather than a simple claim that l'Hôpital alone discovered the underlying result.

Operational Nuance

The Basic 0/0 Form

Suppose:

$$ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=0 $$

and:

$$ \lim_{x\to a}g(x)=0. $$

Under the hypotheses of L'Hôpital's Rule, if:

$$ \lim_{x\to a} \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)} = L, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to a} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = L }. $$

Symbolically, one often summarizes the procedure as:

$$ \frac00 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lim\frac{f}{g} = \lim\frac{f'}{g'}, $$

but $\frac00$ is not an actual numerical value. It is an indeterminate form describing the behavior of a limit.

Example: Exponential Function

Evaluate:

$$ \lim_{x\to0} \frac{e^x-1}{x}. $$

Direct substitution gives:

$$ \frac{e^0-1}{0} = \frac00. $$

Differentiate the numerator and denominator separately:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}(e^x-1)=e^x $$

and:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}(x)=1. $$

Therefore:

$$ \lim_{x\to0} \frac{e^x-1}{x} = \lim_{x\to0}e^x = 1. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{1}. $$

The Infinity-over-Infinity Form

L'Hôpital's Rule also applies, under suitable hypotheses, when both numerator and denominator grow without bound in magnitude:

$$ \frac{\infty}{\infty}. $$

For example:

$$ \lim_{x\to\infty} \frac{\ln x}{x}. $$

Both numerator and denominator tend to infinity:

$$ \frac{\infty}{\infty}. $$

Applying the rule:

$$ \lim_{x\to\infty} \frac{1/x}{1} = \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac1x = 0. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to\infty} \frac{\ln x}{x}=0 }. $$

Repeated Application

Sometimes one application produces another indeterminate quotient. Consider:

$$ \lim_{x\to0} \frac{1-\cos x}{x^2}. $$

Direct substitution gives:

$$ \frac00. $$

One application gives:

$$ \lim_{x\to0} \frac{\sin x}{2x}, $$

which is still:

$$ \frac00. $$

Applying the rule again:

$$ \lim_{x\to0} \frac{\cos x}{2} = \frac12. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to0} \frac{1-\cos x}{x^2} = \frac12 }. $$

Each application must be justified independently. One should verify that the new quotient again satisfies an appropriate form before applying the rule repeatedly.

What the Rule Does Not Say

L'Hôpital's Rule does not claim the algebraic identity:

$$ \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}. $$

In general, that statement is false. The rule concerns certain limits of these quotients.

For example:

$$ \frac{x^2}{x}=x, $$

while:

$$ \frac{2x}{1}=2x. $$

These functions are clearly not equal.

Common Misconception: One may not apply L'Hôpital's Rule simply because a limit contains a fraction. The quotient must first produce an appropriate indeterminate form such as $0/0$ or $\infty/\infty$, and the other hypotheses of the theorem must be satisfied.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$0/0$ "Zero over zero," or "the zero-over-zero indeterminate form."
$\infty/\infty$ "Infinity over infinity," or "the infinity-over-infinity indeterminate form."
$f'(x)/g'(x)$ "F prime of x divided by g prime of x."
$\lim_{x\to a}f(x)/g(x)$ "The limit as x approaches a of f of x over g of x."
$x\to\infty$ "As x approaches infinity."
$x\to a^+$ "As x approaches a from the right."

The surname is commonly written:

$$ \text{L'Hôpital} $$

with a circumflex over the o. The historical family spelling and older English-language sources also produce variants such as L'Hospital. Mathematical references may therefore contain more than one spelling of the rule.

Defense Notation Advice: Writing:

$$ \frac00=1 $$

or:

$$ \frac{\infty}{\infty}=1 $$

is incorrect. These symbols identify indeterminate forms, not arithmetic values. The associated limit can equal many different numbers, diverge, or fail to exist.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Zero-over-Zero Form: One principal setting is: $$ f(x)\to0, \qquad g(x)\to0. $$ Under the theorem's hypotheses, the quotient of derivatives can determine the original quotient limit.
  • Infinity-over-Infinity Form: The rule also handles quotients in which: $$ |f(x)|\to\infty $$ and: $$ |g(x)|\to\infty. $$
  • Derivative Requirement: The relevant functions must be differentiable on an appropriate punctured neighborhood of the limiting point, and the denominator derivative must not vanish there under the standard theorem hypotheses: $$ g'(x)\neq0. $$
  • Limit of Derivative Quotient: A standard form of the theorem requires the limit: $$ \lim_{x\to a} \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)} $$ to exist in the appropriate extended sense. Merely differentiating does not guarantee that the resulting limit is easier or that the theorem settles it.
  • One-Sided Limits: L'Hôpital's Rule has corresponding one-sided versions for: $$ x\to a^+ $$ and: $$ x\to a^-. $$
  • Limits at Infinity: The theorem also has versions for: $$ x\to\infty $$ and: $$ x\to-\infty. $$
  • Repeated Application: If applying the rule once leaves another valid indeterminate quotient, it may sometimes be applied again. Every application must still satisfy the necessary conditions.
  • Not the Quotient Rule: L'Hôpital's Rule says to compare: $$ \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}, $$ not to differentiate the quotient using: $$ \left(\frac fg\right)' = \frac{f'g-fg'}{g^2}. $$ These are completely different procedures.
  • 0 Times Infinity: The form: $$ 0\cdot\infty $$ is indeterminate but is not directly a quotient. It must first be rewritten, for example: $$ f(x)g(x) = \frac{f(x)}{1/g(x)} $$ or: $$ \frac{g(x)}{1/f(x)}, $$ so that a $0/0$ or $\infty/\infty$ form may emerge.
  • Infinity Minus Infinity: The form: $$ \infty-\infty $$ is also indeterminate. Algebraic manipulation—such as combining fractions, rationalizing, or factoring—is normally required before L'Hôpital's Rule can become relevant.
  • Zero to the Zero Power: The form: $$ 0^0 $$ is indeterminate in limit problems. One typically takes logarithms and rewrites the resulting expression as a quotient before considering L'Hôpital's Rule.
  • One to the Infinity Power: The form: $$ 1^\infty $$ is indeterminate. If: $$ y=f(x)^{g(x)}, $$ then: $$ \ln y=g(x)\ln f(x). $$ The logarithmic expression can often be transformed into a quotient suitable for L'Hôpital's Rule.
  • Infinity to the Zero Power: The form: $$ \infty^0 $$ is handled similarly by taking logarithms and transforming the resulting product into an appropriate quotient.
  • Indeterminate Forms: The classical indeterminate forms commonly listed in calculus are: $$ \boxed{ \frac00,\quad \frac{\infty}{\infty},\quad 0\cdot\infty,\quad \infty-\infty,\quad 0^0,\quad 1^\infty,\quad \infty^0 }. $$ Only the first two are direct quotient forms for L'Hôpital's Rule.
  • Why "Indeterminate" Matters: The form: $$ \frac00 $$ does not predict a unique answer. For example: $$ \lim_{x\to0}\frac{x}{x}=1, $$ while: $$ \lim_{x\to0}\frac{x^2}{x}=0, $$ and: $$ \lim_{x\to0}\frac{x}{x^2} $$ does not have a finite two-sided limit.
  • Infinity-over-Infinity Is Also Indeterminate: For example: $$ \frac{x}{x}\to1, $$ $$ \frac{x}{x^2}\to0, $$ and: $$ \frac{x^2}{x}\to\infty. $$ Thus the symbols $\infty/\infty$ do not determine a single limit.
  • Polynomial Growth: L'Hôpital's Rule can demonstrate that higher-degree powers dominate lower-degree powers. For example: $$ \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x^2}{x^3} = \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2x}{3x^2} = \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2}{6x} = 0. $$
  • Logarithmic vs. Polynomial Growth: For positive powers: $$ \ln x $$ grows more slowly than: $$ x^p, \qquad p>0. $$ L'Hôpital's Rule provides one standard way to establish such limits.
  • Exponential vs. Polynomial Growth: Repeated applications show that: $$ e^x $$ eventually dominates every fixed power: $$ x^n. $$ For example: $$ \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x^n}{e^x}=0. $$
  • Alternative Methods: L'Hôpital's Rule is not always the simplest approach. Factoring, rationalizing, trigonometric identities, standard limits, Taylor series, squeeze arguments, or algebraic simplification may be faster or reveal more structure.
  • Taylor-Series Connection: Local expansions can often determine the same limits. For instance: $$ e^x-1=x+\frac{x^2}{2}+\cdots, $$ so: $$ \frac{e^x-1}{x} = 1+\frac{x}{2}+\cdots \to1. $$
  • Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem: Modern proofs of L'Hôpital's Rule are closely connected with Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem, which compares ratios of changes in two functions through ratios of their derivatives.
  • Terminological Contrast: An indeterminate form describes insufficient information about a limit. L'Hôpital's Rule is a theorem that can resolve certain indeterminate quotient forms. The quotient rule is instead a differentiation formula for the derivative of a quotient and should not be confused with L'Hôpital's Rule.
