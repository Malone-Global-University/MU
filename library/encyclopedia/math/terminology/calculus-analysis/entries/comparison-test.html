Comparison Test

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

The Comparison Test is a convergence test for infinite series that determines the behavior of one positive-term series by comparing its terms with those of another series whose convergence or divergence is already known.

In its standard form, suppose:

$$ 0\le a_n\le b_n $$

for all sufficiently large $n$. Then:

$$ \boxed{ \sum b_n\text{ converges} \Rightarrow \sum a_n\text{ converges} } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \sum a_n\text{ diverges} \Rightarrow \sum b_n\text{ diverges} }. $$

The word comparison reflects the method directly: rather than evaluating the series itself, one compares its terms to those of a more familiar benchmark series.

The word test refers to a mathematical criterion used to determine whether an infinite series converges or diverges.

$$ \boxed{ \text{Comparison Test} = \text{infer convergence behavior through inequalities} }. $$

The method is also commonly called the Direct Comparison Test, especially when it must be distinguished from the Limit Comparison Test.

Operational Nuance

The Basic Idea

For nonnegative terms, a series whose terms are never larger than those of a known convergent series cannot accumulate more total sum than that convergent comparison series.

$$ \boxed{ 0\le a_n\le b_n \quad\text{and}\quad \sum b_n<\infty \Rightarrow \sum a_n<\infty }. $$

Conversely, if a series already known to diverge has terms no larger than those of another positive-term series, then the larger series must also diverge.

$$ \boxed{ 0\le a_n\le b_n \quad\text{and}\quad \sum a_n=\infty \Rightarrow \sum b_n=\infty }. $$

Convergence Direction

To prove that a positive-term series converges, compare it from above with a known convergent series.

$$ \boxed{ \text{smaller than convergent} \Rightarrow \text{convergent} }. $$

Symbolically:

$$ 0\le a_n\le b_n, \qquad \sum b_n\text{ converges}. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ \sum a_n\text{ converges} }. $$

Divergence Direction

To prove that a positive-term series diverges, compare it from below with a known divergent series.

$$ \boxed{ \text{larger than divergent} \Rightarrow \text{divergent} }. $$

Symbolically:

$$ 0\le a_n\le b_n, \qquad \sum a_n\text{ diverges}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \sum b_n\text{ diverges} }. $$

The Inequality Direction Matters

The Comparison Test is directional. Being larger than a convergent series tells us nothing by itself, and being smaller than a divergent series tells us nothing by itself.

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \text{smaller than convergent} &\Rightarrow \text{convergent},\\ \text{larger than divergent} &\Rightarrow \text{divergent}. \end{aligned} } $$

Why the Reverse Implications Fail

A series may be larger than a convergent series and still converge.

For example:

$$ \frac1{n^2} \le \frac1{n^{3/2}} $$

for $n\ge1$, but both corresponding $p$-series converge.

Likewise, a series may be smaller than a divergent series and either converge or diverge.

$$ \frac1{n^2}\le\frac1n, $$

yet:

$$ \sum\frac1{n^2} \text{ converges} $$

while:

$$ \sum\frac1n \text{ diverges}. $$

Positive-Term Requirement

The ordinary Direct Comparison Test is formulated for nonnegative terms.

$$ \boxed{ a_n\ge0, \qquad b_n\ge0 } $$

because the test relies on ordering partial sums.

Series containing positive and negative terms often require other techniques, such as absolute convergence, the Alternating Series Test, or comparison of absolute values.

Eventually Nonnegative Is Enough

The comparison need not necessarily hold for every initial term. It is sufficient for the required inequality to hold for all sufficiently large $n$.

$$ \boxed{ \exists N \text{ such that } 0\le a_n\le b_n \quad \forall n\ge N }. $$

A finite number of initial terms does not determine whether an infinite series converges or diverges.

Finite Initial Terms Do Not Matter

If:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n $$

differs from:

$$ \sum_{n=N}^{\infty}a_n $$

only by finitely many terms, then both series have the same convergence behavior.

$$ \boxed{ \text{adding or removing finitely many terms} \text{ does not change convergence} }. $$

The Partial-Sum Foundation

Suppose:

$$ 0\le a_n\le b_n. $$

Their partial sums satisfy:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{N}a_n \le \sum_{n=1}^{N}b_n }. $$

If the partial sums of the larger series remain bounded above, then the increasing partial sums of the smaller positive-term series are also bounded above. The monotone convergence principle then gives convergence.

Benchmark Series

The Comparison Test is useful only when the comparison series has known behavior. Some of the most important benchmarks are geometric series and $p$-series.

Geometric Series Benchmark

A geometric series:

$$ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}ar^n $$

converges when:

$$ \boxed{|r|<1}. $$

Positive geometric series therefore provide useful upper comparisons when terms decay exponentially.

p-Series Benchmark

The $p$-series:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{n^p} $$

satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{cases} \text{converges},&p>1,\\ \text{diverges},&p\le1. \end{cases} } $$

These series are among the most frequently used direct-comparison benchmarks.

The Harmonic Series

The harmonic series:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1n } $$

diverges.

It therefore serves as a standard lower comparison for proving divergence.

Example: Proving Convergence

Consider:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{n^2+5}. $$

Since:

$$ n^2+5\ge n^2, $$

taking positive reciprocals reverses the inequality:

$$ 0< \frac1{n^2+5} \le \frac1{n^2}. $$

Because:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{n^2} $$

is a convergent $p$-series with $p=2$, the Comparison Test gives:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{n^2+5} \text{ converges} }. $$

Example: Proving Divergence

Consider:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{\sqrt n}. $$

For $n\ge1$:

$$ \sqrt n\le n, $$

so:

$$ \frac1{\sqrt n}\ge\frac1n. $$

Since the harmonic series diverges:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1n=\infty, $$

it follows that:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{\sqrt n} \text{ diverges} }. $$

Example with a Polynomial Denominator

Consider:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{n^3+n}. $$

Since:

$$ n^3+n\ge n^3, $$

we obtain:

$$ 0< \frac1{n^3+n} \le \frac1{n^3}. $$

The comparison series:

$$ \sum\frac1{n^3} $$

converges, so the original series converges.

Dominant-Term Thinking

Rational expressions involving powers of $n$ are often compared with a $p$-series determined by their dominant powers.

For example:

$$ \frac{3n+4}{n^3+2} $$

behaves roughly like:

$$ \frac{3n}{n^3} = \frac3{n^2}. $$

This observation may suggest an appropriate comparison, although a direct comparison still requires proving an actual inequality.

Heuristic versus Proof

Saying that two terms look like the same dominant power can guide the choice of comparison series, but the Direct Comparison Test itself requires a valid inequality.

$$ \boxed{ \text{asymptotic resemblance} \ne \text{direct-comparison proof} }. $$

Comparison Test versus Limit Comparison Test

The Direct Comparison Test uses explicit inequalities:

$$ 0\le a_n\le b_n. $$

The Limit Comparison Test instead examines:

$$ \boxed{ L= \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{a_n}{b_n} }. $$

When Direct Comparison Is Convenient

Direct comparison is especially efficient when a simple inequality follows naturally from the expression.

For example:

$$ n^2+7\ge n^2 $$

immediately yields:

$$ \frac1{n^2+7}\le\frac1{n^2}. $$

When Limit Comparison May Be Easier

If establishing a clean inequality is cumbersome but the dominant behavior is clear, the Limit Comparison Test may provide a more convenient route.

For example, for:

$$ a_n=\frac{4n^2+3}{7n^4-2n+1}, $$

comparison with:

$$ b_n=\frac1{n^2} $$

is naturally motivated by the dominant powers.

Comparison with a Constant Multiple

A comparison series may be multiplied by a positive constant without changing its convergence behavior.

$$ \boxed{ \sum b_n \text{ converges} \iff \sum Cb_n \text{ converges} \qquad (C>0) }. $$

Thus proving:

$$ a_n\le\frac{5}{n^2} $$

is just as useful for convergence as comparing directly with $1/n^2$.

Absolute Convergence through Comparison

Comparison may also be applied to absolute values.

If:

$$ \boxed{ |a_n|\le b_n } $$

and:

$$ \sum b_n\text{ converges}, $$

then:

$$ \sum |a_n| $$

converges, so:

$$ \boxed{ \sum a_n \text{ converges absolutely} }. $$

Comparison of Improper Integrals

The same comparison principle also appears in the theory of improper integrals. Suppose:

$$ 0\le f(x)\le g(x) $$

for sufficiently large $x$. Then:

$$ \boxed{ \int_a^\infty g(x)\,dx \text{ converges} \Rightarrow \int_a^\infty f(x)\,dx \text{ converges} }. $$

Similarly:

$$ \boxed{ \int_a^\infty f(x)\,dx \text{ diverges} \Rightarrow \int_a^\infty g(x)\,dx \text{ diverges} }. $$

Series and Integral Versions Share the Same Logic

Both forms rely on the ordering of nonnegative quantities:

$$ \boxed{ \text{smaller nonnegative object} \le \text{larger nonnegative object} }. $$

A finite upper total controls the smaller quantity, while an infinite lower total forces the larger one to diverge.

Comparison Does Not Usually Give the Sum

The Comparison Test ordinarily determines only whether a series converges or diverges.

$$ \boxed{ \text{Comparison Test} \rightarrow \text{convergence classification} } $$

It generally does not determine the exact value of:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n. $$

Comparison Can Give Bounds

Although the test does not normally produce an exact sum, inequalities may provide useful upper or lower estimates.

If:

$$ 0\le a_n\le b_n, $$

then, when both sums exist:

$$ \boxed{ \sum a_n\le\sum b_n }. $$

Common Misconception: Smaller Than Divergent Means Divergent

This conclusion is invalid.

For example:

$$ \frac1{n^2}\le\frac1n, $$

but:

$$ \sum\frac1{n^2} \text{ converges} $$

while:

$$ \sum\frac1n \text{ diverges}. $$

Common Misconception: Larger Than Convergent Means Divergent

This is also invalid. A larger positive-term series may still converge.

$$ \boxed{ \text{larger than convergent} \Rightarrow \text{no conclusion} }. $$

Common Misconception: The Terms Need to Be Equal Eventually

The Comparison Test does not require two sequences to become equal. It requires an appropriate inequality.

Common Misconception: Comparison Must Hold from n = 1

It is sufficient for the comparison to hold beyond some finite index $N$.

$$ \boxed{ n\ge N \text{ is sufficient} }. $$

Common Misconception: Similar-Looking Terms Automatically Prove Comparison

Similar growth rates may suggest a benchmark series, but the Direct Comparison Test requires a demonstrated inequality. If only asymptotic similarity is readily available, the Limit Comparison Test may be more appropriate.

Common Misconception: Comparison Works Only with p-Series

Any series whose convergence behavior is already established may potentially serve as a comparison series. Geometric series and $p$-series are merely especially useful benchmarks.

In calculus, Comparison Test often refers specifically to the Direct Comparison Test. The fuller name is useful whenever the Limit Comparison Test is also under discussion.

Mathematicians frequently describe the reasoning informally by saying that one series is dominated by, bounded above by, or bounded below by another. The exact inequality should nevertheless be made explicit in a formal solution.

Defense Notation Advice: Write the comparison inequality before stating the conclusion. For convergence, identify a known convergent series that is greater than or equal to the target terms. For divergence, identify a known divergent series that is less than or equal to the target terms. State that the terms are nonnegative, at least eventually, and do not reverse the logical direction of the test. If the comparison is based primarily on dominant-term behavior rather than a clean inequality, use or explicitly invoke the Limit Comparison Test instead.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: The Comparison Test determines convergence or divergence of a nonnegative series by comparing it through inequalities with a series of known behavior.
  • Alternative Name: Direct Comparison Test.
  • Convergence Form: $$ \boxed{ 0\le a_n\le b_n, \qquad \sum b_n\text{ converges} \Rightarrow \sum a_n\text{ converges} }. $$
  • Divergence Form: $$ \boxed{ 0\le a_n\le b_n, \qquad \sum a_n\text{ diverges} \Rightarrow \sum b_n\text{ diverges} }. $$
  • Memory Principle for Convergence: Smaller than a known convergent positive-term series implies convergence.
  • Memory Principle for Divergence: Larger than a known divergent positive-term series implies divergence.
  • Nonnegative Terms: The ordinary test relies on $a_n,b_n\ge0$.
  • Eventually Sufficient: The comparison only needs to hold for all sufficiently large $n$.
  • Finite-Term Principle: Adding, deleting, or changing finitely many terms does not alter convergence.
  • Standard Convergent Benchmark: $$ \sum\frac1{n^p}, \qquad p>1. $$
  • Standard Divergent Benchmark: $$ \sum\frac1n. $$
  • Geometric Benchmark: $$ \sum ar^n $$ converges when $|r|<1$.
  • Denominator Rule: For positive quantities, increasing a denominator decreases the fraction.
  • Dominant-Power Strategy: Rational expressions in $n$ are often compared with $p$-series suggested by their highest powers.
  • Constant Multiples: Multiplying a comparison series by a positive finite constant does not change its convergence classification.
  • Absolute Convergence: If $|a_n|\le b_n$ and $\sum b_n$ converges, then $\sum a_n$ converges absolutely.
  • Improper Integral Version: Similar comparison principles apply to nonnegative improper integrals.
  • Partial-Sum Basis: The theorem follows from ordering nonnegative partial sums and applying bounded-monotone convergence.
  • Limit Comparison Contrast: Direct comparison proves inequalities; limit comparison examines the limiting ratio $a_n/b_n$.

  • Exact Sum: The Comparison Test ordinarily classifies convergence rather than calculating the exact value of the series.
  • Bounding Use: A termwise comparison may also provide upper or lower bounds on sums when the corresponding series converge.
  • Common Error: Do not infer divergence merely because a series is smaller than a divergent series.
