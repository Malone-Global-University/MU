Mean Value Theorem

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

The Mean Value Theorem takes its name from the idea of a mean, or average. In this theorem, the relevant mean is the average rate of change of a function over an interval:

$$ \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}. $$

The theorem states that, under suitable continuity and differentiability conditions, this average rate must equal the instantaneous rate of change at at least one point inside the interval.

The word mean has long been used in mathematics for quantities that lie between or summarize other quantities. In the Mean Value Theorem, however, the "mean value" is not the arithmetic mean of the function values $f(a)$ and $f(b)$. It is the average slope between the endpoints.

The theorem belongs to the central family of results developed during the rigorous formulation of differential calculus. It is closely related to Rolle's Theorem, which can be viewed as the special case in which the two endpoint function values are equal.

Operational Nuance

Suppose $f$ satisfies two conditions:

  • $f$ is continuous on the closed interval $[a,b]$;
  • $f$ is differentiable on the open interval $(a,b)$.

Then the Mean Value Theorem guarantees that there exists at least one point:

$$ c\in(a,b) $$

such that:

$$ \boxed{ f'(c) = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} }. $$

The expression:

$$ \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} $$

is the slope of the secant line joining:

$$ (a,f(a)) \qquad\text{and}\qquad (b,f(b)). $$

Meanwhile:

$$ f'(c) $$

is the slope of the tangent line to the graph at:

$$ (c,f(c)). $$

Thus the theorem has a geometric interpretation:

$$ \boxed{ \text{At some interior point, the tangent line is parallel to the endpoint secant line.} } $$

For example, let:

$$ f(x)=x^2 $$

on:

$$ [1,3]. $$

Since polynomials are continuous and differentiable everywhere, the hypotheses are satisfied.

The average rate of change is:

$$ \frac{f(3)-f(1)}{3-1} = \frac{9-1}{2} = 4. $$

Since:

$$ f'(x)=2x, $$

the theorem guarantees a point $c$ satisfying:

$$ 2c=4. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{c=2}. $$

At $x=2$, the tangent slope is exactly the same as the secant slope across the entire interval from $1$ to $3$.

The theorem guarantees at least one such point. Depending on the function, there may be several.

Common Misconception: The Mean Value Theorem does not say that:

$$ c=\frac{a+b}{2}. $$

The point $c$ need not be the midpoint of the interval. It is defined by the slope condition:

$$ f'(c) = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}. $$

In some examples the required point happens to be the midpoint, but that is not part of the theorem.

$f$ differentiable on $(a,b)$ "F is differentiable on the open interval from a to b."
$c\in(a,b)$ "C lies in the open interval from a to b."
$f'(c)$ "F-prime of c."
$\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$ "F of b minus f of a, divided by b minus a."
$f'(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$ "The instantaneous rate of change at c equals the average rate of change from a to b."

A compact statement of the theorem is:

$$ \left. \begin{array}{l} f\text{ continuous on }[a,b],\\ f\text{ differentiable on }(a,b) \end{array} \right\} \Longrightarrow \exists c\in(a,b) : f'(c) = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}. $$

The secant slope:

$$ \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} $$

may also be called the average rate of change of $f$ over $[a,b]$.

The derivative:

$$ f'(c) $$

represents the instantaneous rate of change at $c$.

Defense Notation Advice: The hypotheses deliberately use two different interval types. Continuity is required on:

$$ [a,b], $$

including the endpoints, while differentiability is required only on:

$$ (a,b). $$

Differentiability at the endpoints is not required by the theorem.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Average vs. Instantaneous Rate: The central relationship is: $$ \text{instantaneous rate at }c = \text{average rate over }[a,b]. $$ Symbolically: $$ f'(c) = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}. $$
  • Geometric Interpretation: The secant line through: $$ (a,f(a)) \quad\text{and}\quad (b,f(b)) $$ has the same slope as at least one tangent line to the graph at an interior point $c$.
  • Parallel Lines: Since equal slopes correspond to parallel nonvertical lines, the theorem is often summarized geometrically by saying that some tangent line is parallel to the secant line joining the endpoints.
  • Physical Interpretation: If a car travels a distance $s(t)$ between times $a$ and $b$, its average velocity is: $$ \frac{s(b)-s(a)}{b-a}. $$ Provided the position function satisfies the hypotheses of the theorem, there is some instant $c$ for which: $$ s'(c) = \frac{s(b)-s(a)}{b-a}. $$ Thus the car's instantaneous velocity equals its average velocity at least once.
  • Existence Theorem: Like the Intermediate Value Theorem and Extreme Value Theorem, the Mean Value Theorem is fundamentally an existence theorem. It guarantees that a suitable point $c$ exists but does not necessarily provide a convenient method for finding it.
  • No Uniqueness Guarantee: The theorem states: $$ \exists c\in(a,b), $$ not: $$ \exists!c\in(a,b). $$ Several interior points may have the required derivative.
  • Rolle's Theorem: If: $$ f(a)=f(b), $$ then the secant slope is: $$ \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}=0. $$ The Mean Value Theorem then gives: $$ f'(c)=0. $$ This is precisely the conclusion of Rolle's Theorem.
  • Rolle's Theorem as a Special Case: Conceptually: $$ \boxed{ \text{Rolle's Theorem} \subset \text{Mean Value Theorem} }. $$ Rolle's Theorem handles the special case of equal endpoint values.
  • Proof Using Rolle's Theorem: A standard proof subtracts the secant line from $f$. Define: $$ g(x) = f(x) - \left[ f(a) + \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}(x-a) \right]. $$ Then: $$ g(a)=g(b)=0. $$ Rolle's Theorem gives some $c$ satisfying: $$ g'(c)=0, $$ which rearranges to the Mean Value Theorem formula.

  • Equal Derivatives: If: $$ f'(x)=g'(x) $$ throughout an interval, then: $$ (f-g)'=0. $$ Hence $f-g$ is constant, so: $$ f(x)=g(x)+C. $$ This fact underlies the constant of integration in antiderivatives.

  • Derivative Bounds: If: $$ m\le f'(x)\le M $$ throughout $(a,b)$, then: $$ m(b-a) \le f(b)-f(a) \le M(b-a). $$ This turns derivative bounds into bounds on total change.
  • Error Estimates: If: $$ |f'(x)|\le M, $$ then the Mean Value Theorem gives: $$ |f(b)-f(a)| \le M|b-a|. $$ This inequality is widely used in approximation and error analysis.
  • Lipschitz-Type Bounds: A globally bounded derivative: $$ |f'(x)|\le M $$ implies: $$ |f(x)-f(y)| \le M|x-y|. $$ Thus differentiability plus a derivative bound controls how rapidly a function can change.
  • Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem: A broader form involves two functions $f$ and $g$. Under appropriate hypotheses, there exists $c\in(a,b)$ such that: $$ [f(b)-f(a)]g'(c) = [g(b)-g(a)]f'(c). $$ When: $$ g(x)=x, $$ this reduces to the ordinary Mean Value Theorem.
  • L'Hôpital's Rule: Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem provides an important theoretical foundation for proofs of L'Hôpital's Rule, showing how comparisons of differences can be related to comparisons of derivatives.
  • Continuity Requirement: A discontinuity on $[a,b]$ can destroy the conclusion. The theorem therefore cannot be applied merely because a derivative exists at many interior points.
  • Differentiability Requirement: Continuity alone is also insufficient. For example: $$ f(x)=|x| $$ is continuous on $[-1,1]$ and satisfies: $$ f(-1)=f(1)=1. $$ The secant slope is $0$, but there is no interior point at which: $$ f'(c)=0; $$ the derivative is $-1$ for $c<0$, $1$ for $c>0$, and undefined at $0$.
  • Relationship to the Intermediate Value Theorem: The Intermediate Value Theorem concerns the values attained by a continuous function. The Mean Value Theorem concerns the derivative and requires the stronger hypothesis of differentiability on the interior.
  • Relationship to the Extreme Value Theorem: The Extreme Value Theorem guarantees absolute extrema for continuous functions on closed bounded intervals. The Mean Value Theorem instead guarantees an interior derivative value equal to an average rate of change.
  • Terminological Contrast: The average rate of change is the secant slope: $$ \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}. $$ The instantaneous rate of change is a derivative: $$ f'(c). $$ The Mean Value Theorem guarantees that these two rates agree at at least one interior point when the required continuity and differentiability conditions hold.
