Minimize

Subfield: Calculus & Analysis

Etymology & Roots

To minimize a mathematical quantity means to find, attain, or select the smallest allowable value of that quantity over a specified domain or feasible set.

If a function $f$ is defined on a feasible set $D$, the instruction:

$$ \boxed{ \text{minimize }f(x) \text{ for }x\in D } $$

asks for a point or points at which $f$ is as small as possible within $D$.

If $x^*\in D$ satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ f(x^*)\leq f(x) \quad\text{for every }x\in D }, $$

then $x^*$ is a minimizer and $f(x^*)$ is a minimum value.

The word derives from Latin minimus, meaning smallest or least. In mathematical language, however, "smallest" is always relative to a specified set of admissible possibilities.

The operational contrast is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{minimize} \longleftrightarrow \text{make as small as allowed} } $$

while maximize seeks the greatest allowable value.

Operational Nuance

Objective Function

The quantity being minimized is commonly called the objective function.

$$ \boxed{ \text{minimize }f(x) }. $$

Feasible Set

The set of allowable inputs is the feasible set:

$$ \boxed{ x\in D }. $$

Unconstrained Minimization

An unconstrained problem seeks the smallest objective value over the stated or natural domain without additional constraints.

$$ \boxed{ \min_x f(x) }. $$

Constrained Minimization

A constrained problem restricts the allowable inputs:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \text{minimize } & f(\mathbf{x})\\ \text{subject to } & g_i(\mathbf{x})\leq0,\\ & h_j(\mathbf{x})=0 \end{aligned} }. $$

Minimum Value

If a minimum is attained:

$$ \boxed{ \min_{x\in D}f(x) } $$

denotes the smallest attained objective value.

Minimizer

An input that attains the minimum is called a minimizer.

$$ \boxed{ x^* \in \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{x\in D}f(x) }. $$

Minimum vs. Minimizer

$$ \boxed{ \text{minimum value}=f(x^*) } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{minimizer}=x^* }. $$

Argmin

The notation:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{x\in D}f(x) } $$

denotes the set of all feasible inputs where the minimum is attained.

Multiple Minimizers

A function can have several different minimizers with the same minimum value.

For example:

$$ f(x) = (x^2-1)^2 $$

has minimum value:

$$ \boxed{ 0 } $$

at:

$$ \boxed{ x=-1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad x=1 }. $$

Unique Minimizer

A minimizer is unique when exactly one feasible input attains the smallest objective value.

Global Minimum

A point $x^*$ is a global minimizer when:

$$ \boxed{ f(x^*)\leq f(x) \quad \text{for every }x\in D }. $$

Local Minimum

A point $x^*$ is a local minimizer if its function value is no greater than all sufficiently nearby feasible values.

$$ \boxed{ f(x^*)\leq f(x) \quad \text{for }x \text{ sufficiently near }x^* }. $$

Strict Local Minimum

A strict local minimum satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ f(x^*)<f(x) } $$

for all sufficiently nearby feasible $x\neq x^*$.

Absolute Minimum

Absolute minimum is a common synonym for global minimum.

Relative Minimum

Relative minimum is a traditional synonym for local minimum.

Minimum Need Not Exist

A function may be bounded below without attaining its greatest lower bound.

For:

$$ f(x)=x, \qquad 0<x<1, $$

function values approach $0$, but no allowed input gives $f(x)=0$.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \text{no minimum exists on }(0,1) }. $$

Infimum

In the preceding example:

$$ \boxed{ \inf_{0<x<1}x=0 }. $$

Minimum vs. Infimum

A minimum must be attained. An infimum need not be.

$$ \boxed{ \min D \text{ exists} \Longrightarrow \min D=\inf D }. $$

Extreme Value Theorem

If $f$ is continuous on a closed bounded interval $[a,b]$, then it attains both an absolute minimum and an absolute maximum.

Closed-Interval Method

To find an absolute minimum of a differentiable function on $[a,b]$:

  1. find critical numbers in $(a,b)$;
  2. evaluate $f$ at those critical numbers;
  3. evaluate $f(a)$ and $f(b)$;
  4. select the smallest resulting value.

Critical Number

A critical number $c$ is typically a domain point where:

$$ \boxed{ f'(c)=0 } $$

or where $f'(c)$ does not exist.

Fermat's Theorem

If $f$ has a local extremum at an interior point $c$ and is differentiable there, then:

$$ \boxed{ f'(c)=0 }. $$

Derivative Zero Is Not Sufficient

A stationary point need not be a minimum.

For:

$$ f(x)=x^3, $$

one has:

$$ f'(0)=0, $$

but $x=0$ is neither a local maximum nor a local minimum.

First-Derivative Test

If $f'$ changes sign:

$$ \boxed{ -\longrightarrow+ } $$

at $x=c$, then $f$ has a local minimum at $c$.

Second-Derivative Test

If:

$$ f'(c)=0 $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ f''(c)>0 }, $$

then $f$ has a strict local minimum at $c$.

Inconclusive Second-Derivative Test

If:

$$ f''(c)=0, $$

the standard second-derivative test gives no conclusion.

Convexity

Convex functions are central to minimization because every local minimum of a convex function on a convex feasible set is global.

Strict Convexity

A strictly convex function on a convex feasible set has at most one global minimizer.

Second-Derivative Convexity Test

In one dimension, if:

$$ \boxed{ f''(x)\geq0 } $$

throughout an interval, then $f$ is convex there under the usual differentiability assumptions.

Quadratic Example

Consider:

$$ f(x) = x^2-6x+11. $$

Complete the square:

$$ f(x) = (x-3)^2+2. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \min f=2 } $$

attained at:

$$ \boxed{ x=3 }. $$

Vertex Method

For:

$$ f(x)=ax^2+bx+c $$

with $a>0$, the parabola opens upward and the vertex gives the minimum.

$$ \boxed{ x = -\frac{b}{2a} }. $$

Endpoint Minimum

On a restricted interval, the minimum may occur at an endpoint.

For example, on $[0,2]$:

$$ f(x)=x $$

is minimized at:

$$ \boxed{ x=0 }. $$

Domain Dependence

The same function can have different minimizers on different domains.

Discrete Minimization

The feasible set need not be continuous.

$$ \boxed{ n\in\mathbb{Z} } $$

may be part of the problem, requiring integer rather than arbitrary real choices.

Finite Search

On a finite feasible set, minimization can be performed by comparing the objective value at every candidate.

Optimization

Minimization is one of the two basic directions of optimization:

$$ \boxed{ \text{optimization} = \text{minimization or maximization} }. $$

Converting Minimization to Maximization

Minimizing $f$ is equivalent to maximizing $-f$:

$$ \boxed{ \min_x f(x) = -\max_x[-f(x)] } $$

when the corresponding extrema exist.

Distance Minimization

Many geometric problems ask for the point minimizing distance to a set, curve, line, surface, or other object.

Squared-Distance Shortcut

Because the square-root function is increasing on nonnegative values, minimizing:

$$ \sqrt{g(x)} $$

is equivalent to minimizing:

$$ \boxed{ g(x) } $$

whenever $g(x)\geq0$.

Example: Distance to the Origin

Minimizing:

$$ d = \sqrt{x^2+y^2} $$

is equivalent to minimizing:

$$ \boxed{ d^2 = x^2+y^2 }. $$

Cost Minimization

In applied optimization, an objective may represent total cost:

$$ \boxed{ C(q) }. $$

The goal is to choose feasible decisions giving the least cost.

Error Minimization

Approximation and data analysis often minimize some measure of error.

Least Squares

Given residuals:

$$ r_i = y_i-\widehat{y}_i, $$

least squares minimizes:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{i=1}^{n}r_i^2 }. $$

Matrix Least Squares

For:

$$ A\mathbf{x} \approx \mathbf{b}, $$

least squares seeks:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\mathbf{x}} \|A\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|_2^2 }. $$

Normal Equations

Under standard least-squares conditions, a minimizer satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ A^TA\mathbf{x} = A^T\mathbf{b} }. $$

Loss Function

In statistics and machine learning, the minimized objective is often called a loss function.

$$ \boxed{ \min_\theta L(\theta) }. $$

Mean Squared Error

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{MSE} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i-\widehat{y}_i)^2 }. $$

Absolute Error

Another objective may minimize:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{i=1}^{n} |y_i-\widehat{y}_i| }. $$

Regularized Minimization

Optimization may balance data fit and model complexity:

$$ \boxed{ \min_\theta \left[ L(\theta) + \lambda R(\theta) \right] }. $$

Monotone Transformations

If $\phi$ is strictly increasing, then:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname*{arg\,min}_x f(x) = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_x \phi(f(x)) }. $$

Logarithmic Transformation

For a positive objective:

$$ f(x)>0, $$

minimizing $f$ is equivalent to minimizing:

$$ \boxed{ \ln f(x) } $$

because $\ln$ is strictly increasing.

Negative Log-Likelihood

Maximizing likelihood is equivalent to minimizing negative log-likelihood:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname*{arg\,max}_\theta L(\theta) = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_\theta [-\log L(\theta)] } $$

for positive likelihood.

Linear Programming

A linear minimization problem may take the form:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \text{minimize } & \mathbf{c}^T\mathbf{x}\\ \text{subject to } & A\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{b} \end{aligned} }. $$

Extreme Points in Linear Programming

When a finite optimum exists for a linear objective over a nonempty polyhedral feasible set, an optimum can be attained at an extreme point, although it may also be attained along an entire edge or face.

Unbounded Below

A minimization problem may have no finite minimum because the objective decreases without bound.

For example:

$$ \boxed{ \min_{x\in\mathbb{R}}x } $$

has no finite minimum.

Infeasible Problem

If no point satisfies all constraints, there is no feasible point at which an objective can be minimized.

Multivariable Minimization

For a differentiable unconstrained objective:

$$ f(x_1,\ldots,x_n), $$

an interior minimizer must satisfy:

$$ \boxed{ \nabla f(\mathbf{x}^*) = \mathbf{0} } $$

under the usual differentiability assumptions.

Gradient Condition Is Not Sufficient

A point where $\nabla f=0$ may be a minimum, maximum, saddle point, or degenerate critical point.

Hessian Test

If:

$$ \nabla f(\mathbf{x}^*)=\mathbf{0} $$

and the Hessian is positive definite at $\mathbf{x}^*$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x}^* \text{ is a strict local minimizer} }. $$

Positive Semidefinite Hessian

Positive semidefiniteness alone may be insufficient for strict local minimality.

Lagrange Multipliers

To minimize $f(\mathbf{x})$ subject to:

$$ g(\mathbf{x})=c, $$

a smooth constrained candidate may satisfy:

$$ \boxed{ \nabla f = \lambda\nabla g }. $$

Several Equality Constraints

$$ \boxed{ \nabla f = \sum_i \lambda_i\nabla g_i }. $$

Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions

Inequality-constrained minimization can be analyzed using KKT conditions under appropriate regularity and convexity assumptions.

Convex Optimization

Convex optimization studies minimization of convex objectives over convex feasible sets.

Why Convex Problems Are Special

In a convex minimization problem, every local minimum is global.

Strong Convexity

Strong convexity provides additional guarantees such as uniqueness of the minimizer and quantitative curvature.

Gradient Descent

A common iterative minimization method is:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{x}_k - \alpha_k \nabla f(\mathbf{x}_k) }. $$

The negative gradient points in the direction of steepest local decrease under the Euclidean norm.

Step Size

The parameter $\alpha_k>0$ controls the size of the update.

Newton's Method for Optimization

Near a suitable minimizer, Newton-type methods use curvature information:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{x}_k - H_f(\mathbf{x}_k)^{-1} \nabla f(\mathbf{x}_k) } $$

when the Hessian is invertible and the method is appropriate.

Numerical Minimization

Many objectives have no convenient symbolic minimizer, so numerical algorithms seek approximate solutions.

Approximate Minimizer

A numerical result may satisfy:

$$ f(\widehat{x}) \approx \inf_{x\in D}f(x) $$

rather than attaining an exact symbolic minimum.

Tolerance

Optimization algorithms often stop when changes in objective, variables, or gradients fall below a chosen tolerance.

Combinatorial Minimization

Some minimization problems involve discrete objects such as graphs, routes, subsets, schedules, or integer vectors.

Shortest Path

A shortest-path problem minimizes total path cost or distance.

$$ \boxed{ \text{minimize total edge weight} }. $$

Minimum Spanning Tree

A minimum spanning tree connects all vertices with smallest possible total edge weight.

Assignment Problems

Assignment models may minimize total cost while enforcing one-to-one allocation constraints.

Scheduling

Scheduling problems may minimize completion time, lateness, cost, idle time, or another operational objective.

Minimum vs. Minimal

In partially ordered sets, minimum and minimal are not synonyms.

A minimum element is less than or equal to every element in the set. A minimal element merely has no strictly smaller comparable element.

Minimum Implies Minimal

$$ \boxed{ \text{minimum} \Longrightarrow \text{minimal} }. $$

Minimal Need Not Mean Minimum

A partially ordered set can have several minimal elements and no minimum element.

Minimum of Two Numbers

$$ \boxed{ \min(a,b) = \begin{cases} a, & a\leq b,\\ b, & b<a. \end{cases} } $$

Minimum of a Finite Collection

$$ \boxed{ \min\{x_1,\ldots,x_n\} } $$

denotes the smallest listed value.

Minimum Function

The function:

$$ \boxed{ m(a,b)=\min(a,b) } $$

returns the smaller input.

Negative Part

One convention defines the negative part of $x$ by:

$$ \boxed{ x_- = \max(-x,0) }. $$

Another useful identity is:

$$ \boxed{ \min(x,0) = -\max(-x,0) }. $$

Pointwise Minimum

Given functions $f$ and $g$:

$$ \boxed{ h(x) = \min\{f(x),g(x)\} }. $$

Nondifferentiability of Pointwise Minima

Even when $f$ and $g$ are smooth, their pointwise minimum may fail to be differentiable where the active branch changes.

Projection as Minimization

The projection of a point $\mathbf{x}$ onto a closed set $C$ can be formulated as:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\mathbf{y}\in C} \|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\| }. $$

Orthogonal Projection

For a closed subspace of a finite-dimensional inner-product space, the orthogonal projection gives the point minimizing Euclidean distance.

Approximation Theory

Approximation problems seek an element of a simpler family that minimizes an error norm.

$$ \boxed{ \min_{g\in V} \|f-g\| }. $$

Energy Minimization

In mechanics and variational problems, equilibrium configurations may be characterized through minimization of an energy functional under appropriate conditions.

Calculus of Variations

Instead of minimizing a function of finitely many variables, one may minimize a functional:

$$ \boxed{ J[y] = \int_a^b F(x,y,y')\,dx }. $$

Euler-Lagrange Equation

A sufficiently smooth extremizing function can satisfy:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\partial F}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx} \left( \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} \right) = 0 }. $$

This is a necessary condition under standard hypotheses, not by itself a guarantee of a minimum.

Maximum Likelihood as Minimization

Statistical maximum-likelihood estimation is often implemented by minimizing:

$$ \boxed{ -\ell(\theta) } $$

where $\ell$ is the log-likelihood.

Bayesian Estimation

A maximum a posteriori estimate can equivalently be found by minimizing the negative log posterior when the posterior is positive.

Machine Learning

Training commonly means minimizing a loss over model parameters:

$$ \boxed{ \widehat{\theta} \in \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\theta} L(\theta) }. $$

Economics

Firms may minimize cost for a required output level, while consumers or firms may face expenditure-minimization problems under specified utility or production constraints.

Engineering Design

Design optimization can minimize mass, energy use, error, material, drag, stress, or cost subject to safety and performance constraints.

Operations Research

Transportation, routing, scheduling, inventory, and allocation problems frequently take minimization form.

Control Theory

An optimal-control problem may minimize a cumulative cost functional:

$$ \boxed{ J = \int_0^T L(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{u}(t),t)\,dt + \Phi(\mathbf{x}(T)) }. $$

Minimum Is Relative to the Domain

The same objective can have different minima under different restrictions.

For:

$$ f(x)=x^2, $$

the global minimum over $\mathbb{R}$ is:

$$ \boxed{ 0 } $$

at $x=0$, but over $[2,5]$ the minimum is:

$$ \boxed{ 4 } $$

at $x=2$.

Strict vs. Non-Strict Comparison

A global minimizer only requires:

$$ f(x^*)\leq f(x), $$

not strict inequality, because several points may share the minimum value.

Common Error: Minimizing the Input Instead of the Objective

"Minimize $f(x)$" does not mean "choose the smallest $x$." It means choose a feasible $x$ that makes $f(x)$ smallest.

Common Error: Confusing Minimum and Minimizer

The minimum is the objective value; the minimizer is the input where it occurs.

Common Error: Reporting Only the Critical Point

If a problem asks for the minimum value, evaluate the function there.

Common Error: Reporting Only the Minimum Value

If a problem asks where the minimum occurs, report the minimizing input or inputs.

Common Error: Assuming Every Critical Point Is a Minimum

Critical points can be maxima, minima, saddle points, or neither.

Common Error: Ignoring Endpoints

Absolute minima on closed intervals may occur at endpoints.

Common Error: Ignoring Nondifferentiable Points

A function can attain a minimum at a corner or cusp where the derivative does not exist.

Common Error: Assuming Bounded Below Guarantees a Minimum

A function can have an infimum without attaining it.

Common Error: Confusing Minimum with Infimum

A minimum is attained; an infimum need not be.

Common Error: Ignoring the Feasible Set

An infeasible candidate cannot be a minimizer.

Common Error: Assuming a Local Minimum Is Global

Local optimality does not imply global optimality for arbitrary nonconvex objectives.

Common Error: Treating $f''(c)=0$ as Proof of No Minimum

A zero second derivative makes the standard test inconclusive.

Common Error: Assuming $\nabla f=0$ Is Sufficient

A stationary point in several variables may be a saddle point.

Common Error: Ignoring Constraints

Constrained minimization may require boundary checks, substitution, Lagrange multipliers, KKT conditions, or specialized algorithms.

Common Error: Confusing Minimum with Minimal

In partially ordered sets, minimal means no strictly smaller comparable element; it does not necessarily mean globally least.

Common Error: Assuming Every Minimization Problem Requires Calculus

Algebra, geometry, inequalities, graph algorithms, finite search, linear programming, dynamic programming, and other methods may solve the problem more naturally.

Common Error: Ignoring Units

The minimum value has the units of the objective, while the minimizer has the units of the decision or input variable.

Common Error: Treating Numerical Output as Exact

Numerical optimization usually returns an approximation whose accuracy depends on the method, conditioning, initialization, and stopping tolerance.

Common Misconception: To minimize a function does not mean to make its input as small as possible. It means to choose among the allowed inputs so that the objective value is as small as possible. The feasible set, the minimizing input, the minimum value, and whether the minimum is actually attained are separate mathematical issues.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\min_{x\in D}f(x)$ "The minimum of f of x over x in D."
$x^*\in\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{x\in D}f(x)$ "X star is a minimizer of f over D."
$f(x^*)\leq f(x)$ for all $x\in D$ "F at x star is no greater than f at any feasible x."
$f'(c)=0$ "C is a stationary-point candidate for an interior optimum."
$f''(c)>0$ "The second derivative is positive at c," supporting a strict local minimum when the standard test applies.
$\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^*)=\mathbf{0}$ "The gradient vanishes at the interior critical point."
$\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g$ "The objective gradient is proportional to the constraint gradient."

The verb minimize commonly appears as an instruction:

$$ \boxed{ \text{minimize }f(x) }. $$

The noun minimum refers to the smallest attained objective value, while minimizer refers to an input that attains it.

The notation:

$$ \operatorname*{arg\,min} $$

is read "arg min" and returns minimizing arguments rather than minimum values.

In constrained optimization, subject to introduces the admissible conditions:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \text{minimize } & f(x)\\ \text{subject to } & x\in D \end{aligned} }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish $\min$ from $\operatorname*{arg\,min}$, identify the feasible set, and state whether the task asks for the minimum value, a minimizing input, or both. When calculus is used, check endpoints, nondifferentiable points, and constraints rather than assuming every solution of $f'=0$ is a minimum. Use infimum, not minimum, when the least lower bound is not attained.
