Gaussian Elimination

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

Gaussian elimination is an algorithm for solving systems of linear equations by using elementary row operations to transform a system into row-echelon form. Once echelon form is reached, the unknowns are typically recovered through back substitution.

The method is named for Carl Friedrich Gauss, although systematic elimination procedures for linear systems appeared in mathematical traditions centuries before Gauss. The modern name reflects Gauss's association with elimination methods in algebra, astronomy, geodesy, and least-squares computation.

In matrix language, Gaussian elimination performs:

$$ \boxed{ A \xrightarrow{\text{elementary row operations}} U } $$

where $U$ is in row-echelon form, often upper triangular when $A$ is square and has suitable pivot structure.

For an augmented system:

$$ \boxed{ [A\mid b] \longrightarrow [U\mid c] } $$

the transformed system has the same solution set as the original system.

The defining stopping point distinguishes Gaussian elimination from Gauss-Jordan elimination:

$$ \boxed{ \text{Gaussian elimination} \rightarrow \text{row-echelon form} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{Gauss-Jordan elimination} \rightarrow \text{reduced row-echelon form} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Linear-System Form

A linear system can be written:

$$ \boxed{ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b} }. $$

For example:

$$ \begin{aligned} x+y+z&=6,\\ 2x-y+z&=3,\\ x+2y-z&=2. \end{aligned} $$

Augmented Matrix

The system is represented by:

$$ \boxed{ \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 1 & 1 & 6\\ 2 & -1 & 1 & 3\\ 1 & 2 & -1 & 2 \end{array} \right] }. $$

Elementary Row Operations

Gaussian elimination uses three reversible elementary row operations.

Row Interchange

$$ \boxed{ R_i\leftrightarrow R_j }. $$

This exchanges two equations.

Row Scaling

$$ \boxed{ R_i\to cR_i, \qquad c\neq0 }. $$

This multiplies an equation by a nonzero constant.

Row Replacement

$$ \boxed{ R_i\to R_i+cR_j }. $$

This replaces one equation by itself plus a multiple of another.

Why Row Operations Preserve the Solution Set

Each elementary row operation is reversible. Thus no valid solution is lost and no invalid solution is introduced.

$$ \boxed{ \text{elementary row operations} \Longrightarrow \text{equivalent linear systems} }. $$

Forward Elimination

The main phase of Gaussian elimination is often called forward elimination. It creates zeros beneath successive pivot positions.

Pivot

A pivot is the leading nonzero entry selected in a row for elimination.

Entries below the pivot are eliminated using row replacement.

Pivot Position

A pivot position is the matrix location occupied by a leading entry in an echelon form.

Pivot Column

A column containing a pivot position is a pivot column.

Pivot Variable

A variable corresponding to a pivot column is called a pivot variable or basic variable.

Free Variable

A variable corresponding to a nonpivot column is a free variable. Free variables arise when the system has infinitely many solutions.

Row-Echelon Form

A matrix is in row-echelon form when:

  • all nonzero rows occur above all zero rows,
  • the leading entry of each lower nonzero row lies to the right of the leading entry above it, and
  • every entry below each leading entry is zero.

A typical pattern is:

$$ \boxed{ \left[ \begin{array}{cccc} * & * & * & *\\ 0 & * & * & *\\ 0 & 0 & * & *\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{array} \right] }. $$

Upper-Triangular Form

For many square full-rank systems, Gaussian elimination produces an upper-triangular coefficient matrix:

$$ \boxed{ U= \begin{bmatrix} u_{11} & u_{12} & \cdots & u_{1n}\\ 0 & u_{22} & \cdots & u_{2n}\\ \vdots & \ddots & \ddots & \vdots\\ 0 & \cdots & 0 & u_{nn} \end{bmatrix} }. $$

Back Substitution

After forward elimination, solve the last equation first and substitute upward.

For example:

$$ \begin{aligned} x+2y-z&=4,\\ 3y+2z&=7,\\ 5z&=10. \end{aligned} $$

The last equation gives:

$$ z=\boxed{2}. $$

Substitute into the second:

$$ 3y+4=7, $$

so:

$$ y=\boxed{1}. $$

Then substitute $y=1$ and $z=2$ into the first equation to obtain $x$.

Simple Elimination Example

Consider:

$$ \begin{aligned} x+y&=5,\\ 2x-y&=1. \end{aligned} $$

Its augmented matrix is:

$$ \left[ \begin{array}{cc|c} 1 & 1 & 5\\ 2 & -1 & 1 \end{array} \right]. $$

Apply:

$$ R_2\to R_2-2R_1. $$

This produces:

$$ \boxed{ \left[ \begin{array}{cc|c} 1 & 1 & 5\\ 0 & -3 & -9 \end{array} \right] }. $$

The second equation gives:

$$ y=3. $$

Back substitution into $x+y=5$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ x=2, \qquad y=3 }. $$

Gaussian Elimination Stops Earlier than Gauss-Jordan

In the preceding example, Gaussian elimination need not eliminate the $1$ above the second pivot. Back substitution completes the solution.

Reduced Row-Echelon Form Is Not Required

Gaussian elimination does not normally require each pivot to equal $1$, nor does it require entries above pivots to be zero.

REF vs. RREF

Row-echelon form might contain:

$$ \left[ \begin{array}{ccc} 2 & 3 & 1\\ 0 & -5 & 4\\ 0 & 0 & 7 \end{array} \right]. $$

This is suitable for Gaussian elimination even though the pivots are not $1$ and entries above later pivots are nonzero.

Unique Solution

A consistent system with a pivot in every variable column has a unique solution.

Free Variables and Infinite Solutions

Suppose elimination produces:

$$ \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 2 & -1 & 4\\ 0 & 1 & 3 & 2\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{array} \right]. $$

The third variable is free because its column contains no pivot.

Let:

$$ z=t. $$

Then:

$$ y+3t=2 $$

gives:

$$ y=2-3t. $$

Substituting upward determines $x$ in terms of $t$.

Inconsistent System

Gaussian elimination detects inconsistency when it produces a row such as:

$$ \boxed{ [0\ 0\ 0\mid1] }. $$

This represents the contradiction:

$$ 0=1. $$

Zero Row

A row:

$$ [0\ 0\ 0\mid0] $$

represents the identity $0=0$ and usually indicates a redundant equation rather than inconsistency.

Consistency Criterion

A system is consistent precisely when elimination does not produce:

$$ \boxed{ [0\ \cdots\ 0\mid c], \qquad c\neq0 }. $$

Homogeneous Systems

A homogeneous linear system:

$$ \boxed{ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0} } $$

is always consistent because $\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$ is always a solution.

Nontrivial Homogeneous Solutions

A homogeneous system has nonzero solutions if elimination reveals at least one free variable.

Rank

The number of pivots obtained during elimination equals the rank:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{rank}(A) = \text{number of pivot positions} }. $$

Rank and Consistency

For:

$$ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}, $$

the system is consistent exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{rank}(A) = \operatorname{rank}([A\mid\mathbf{b}]) }. $$

Rank and Unique Solutions

For an $n\times n$ matrix:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{rank}(A)=n } $$

means there is a pivot in every row and every column, so the matrix is invertible.

Invertibility

For square $A$:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ invertible} \iff \operatorname{rank}(A)=n }. $$

Determinant Connection

For a square matrix:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ invertible} \iff \det(A)\neq0 }. $$

Gaussian elimination can also aid determinant computation.

Determinants via Elimination

Once a square matrix is transformed to upper-triangular form, its determinant is the product of its diagonal entries, adjusted for row swaps and row scalings.

$$ \boxed{ \det(U) = u_{11}u_{22}\cdots u_{nn} }. $$

Effect of Row Swap on Determinant

Interchanging two rows changes the determinant's sign:

$$ \boxed{ \det(B)=-\det(A) } $$

when $B$ is obtained from $A$ by one row interchange.

Effect of Row Scaling on Determinant

Multiplying one row by $c$ multiplies the determinant by $c$.

Effect of Row Replacement on Determinant

Adding a multiple of one row to another leaves the determinant unchanged.

LU Factorization Connection

Gaussian elimination can often be organized as:

$$ \boxed{ A=LU } $$

where $L$ is lower triangular and $U$ is upper triangular.

Elimination Multipliers

The multipliers used to eliminate entries below pivots can be stored in $L$.

Permutation Matrix with Pivoting

When row interchanges are needed:

$$ \boxed{ PA=LU } $$

is a common factorization, where $P$ records the row permutations.

Why LU Is Useful

If several systems share the same coefficient matrix:

$$ A\mathbf{x}_1=\mathbf{b}_1, \qquad A\mathbf{x}_2=\mathbf{b}_2, \quad\ldots, $$

factoring $A$ once can be more efficient than repeating elimination from the beginning.

Forward Substitution

With:

$$ LU\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}, $$

first solve:

$$ L\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{b} $$

by forward substitution.

Back Substitution after LU

Then solve:

$$ U\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y} $$

by back substitution.

Pivoting

If a prospective pivot is zero, rows must be interchanged when a suitable nonzero entry exists below it.

Partial Pivoting

In numerical computation, partial pivoting typically selects an entry of largest available magnitude in the current pivot column.

This helps reduce amplification of floating-point error.

Scaled Partial Pivoting

Some algorithms compare pivot candidates relative to the sizes of entries in their rows rather than only their absolute magnitudes.

Complete Pivoting

Complete pivoting may search both remaining rows and columns for a favorable pivot, though this requires tracking column permutations and is more expensive.

Exact Arithmetic

In symbolic algebra, Gaussian elimination can be carried out using exact integer or rational arithmetic.

Floating-Point Arithmetic

Numerical software ordinarily uses finite-precision arithmetic. Small rounding errors can accumulate during elimination.

Numerical Stability

Pivot strategies are important because algebraically equivalent elimination sequences can behave very differently numerically.

Near-Zero Pivots

Dividing by a very small pivot can produce large intermediate values and magnify roundoff errors.

Operation Count

Solving a dense $n\times n$ linear system by Gaussian elimination requires on the order of:

$$ \boxed{ O(n^3) } $$

arithmetic operations.

Leading Cost

The forward-elimination phase for a dense square system uses approximately:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{2}{3}n^3 } $$

floating-point arithmetic operations in the standard large-$n$ count, with lower order terms omitted.

Back-Substitution Cost

Back substitution requires only order:

$$ \boxed{ O(n^2) } $$

additional work.

Why Gaussian Elimination Is Often Preferred to Gauss-Jordan

If the goal is merely to solve a numerical system, Gaussian elimination stops earlier and performs less arithmetic than full Gauss-Jordan elimination.

Gauss-Jordan Advantage

Gauss-Jordan elimination is often preferable pedagogically when one wants RREF, direct solution reading, or explicit identification of free-variable structure.

Gaussian Elimination and Matrix Inverses

Gaussian elimination can be used as part of inverse computation, but full Gauss-Jordan reduction of:

$$ [A\mid I] $$

is the common direct classroom algorithm for producing $A^{-1}$.

Solving without Explicitly Computing an Inverse

For:

$$ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}, $$

direct elimination is usually preferable to first finding $A^{-1}$ and then calculating:

$$ \mathbf{x}=A^{-1}\mathbf{b}. $$

Row Equivalence

Matrices connected by elementary row operations are row equivalent.

$$ \boxed{ A\sim B } $$

may be used for row equivalence when the notation is explicitly defined.

Row Space

Elementary row operations preserve the row space.

Column Space Caution

Row operations do not preserve the column space as a literal set of vectors. Pivot positions can identify which columns of the original matrix form a basis for the original column space.

Basis for the Row Space

The nonzero rows of an echelon form form a basis for the row space.

Basis for the Column Space

The original columns corresponding to pivot positions form a basis for the column space.

Null Space

Solving:

$$ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0} $$

by elimination identifies the null space:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Null}(A) }. $$

Rank-Nullity Connection

If $A$ has $n$ columns:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{rank}(A) + \operatorname{nullity}(A) = n }. $$

Linear Independence

The columns of $A$ are linearly independent exactly when:

$$ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0} $$

has only the trivial solution.

In pivot language:

$$ \boxed{ \text{pivot in every column} \iff \text{columns linearly independent} }. $$

Spanning Criterion

The columns of an $m\times n$ matrix span $\mathbb{R}^m$ precisely when there is a pivot in every row.

Overdetermined Systems

A system with more equations than unknowns may have one solution, no solution, or redundant equations. Elimination reveals which situation occurs.

Underdetermined Systems

A consistent system with more variables than pivot positions contains free variables and therefore infinitely many solutions.

Geometric Interpretation in Two Variables

Two linear equations may represent intersecting, parallel, or coincident lines. Elimination distinguishes these algebraically.

Geometric Interpretation in Three Variables

Linear equations represent planes. Their common intersection can be a point, a line, a plane, or empty.

Higher-Dimensional Interpretation

In higher dimensions, a consistent linear system defines an affine subspace.

Particular Plus Homogeneous Solution

Every consistent system has the structural form:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_p+\mathbf{x}_h } $$

where $\mathbf{x}_p$ is one particular solution and $\mathbf{x}_h\in\operatorname{Null}(A)$.

Connection to Linear Transformations

For:

$$ T(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x}, $$

Gaussian elimination reveals structural information about the kernel, image, rank, injectivity, and surjectivity of $T$.

Normal Equations and Least Squares

Classical least-squares problems can lead to linear systems such as:

$$ \boxed{ A^{T}A\mathbf{x}=A^{T}\mathbf{b} }. $$

These can be solved by elimination, although modern numerical practice often prefers QR or other factorizations for stability.

Sparse Systems

Large matrices may contain mostly zeros. Specialized sparse elimination methods attempt to preserve sparsity and reduce unnecessary computation.

Fill-In

During sparse Gaussian elimination, entries that were originally zero may become nonzero. This phenomenon is called fill-in.

Ordering Strategies

Sparse algorithms may reorder equations and variables to reduce fill-in and computational cost.

Computer Algebra Systems

Symbolic software may perform exact Gaussian elimination on matrices containing integers, rational numbers, algebraic numbers, or symbolic parameters.

Parameter-Dependent Systems

If matrix entries contain parameters, pivot availability may depend on parameter values.

For example, if a pivot candidate is:

$$ a-2, $$

elimination may require separate cases:

$$ \boxed{ a\neq2 \quad\text{and}\quad a=2 }. $$

Zero-Division Caution

Row scaling by a symbolic expression is valid only when that expression is known to be nonzero.

Gaussian Elimination vs. Substitution

Elementary substitution operates directly on equations. Gaussian elimination is the systematic matrix form of the same underlying elimination logic.

Gaussian Elimination vs. Addition-Elimination Method

The familiar two-equation method of multiplying equations and adding them to eliminate a variable is the small-system precursor of Gaussian elimination.

Gaussian Elimination vs. Gauss-Jordan Elimination

Gaussian elimination makes zeros below pivots and then uses back substitution. Gauss-Jordan elimination additionally makes zeros above pivots and normalizes pivots to $1$.

Gaussian Elimination vs. Cramer's Rule

Cramer's Rule uses determinants and applies directly only to square systems with nonzero determinant. Gaussian elimination handles square and rectangular systems and naturally exposes free variables and inconsistency.

Gaussian Elimination vs. LU Factorization

LU factorization records the elimination process in reusable matrix factors. Gaussian elimination is the operational process underlying the factorization.

Gaussian Elimination vs. RREF

RREF is a matrix form. Gaussian elimination is an algorithm. The algorithm's usual target is REF, not necessarily RREF.

Common Error: Changing Only Part of a Row

Every row operation must be performed across the entire row, including the augmented entry.

Common Error: Multiplying a Row by Zero

The operation:

$$ R_i\to0R_i $$

is invalid because it is not reversible.

Common Error: Dividing by a Zero Pivot

A zero pivot requires row interchange or movement to another pivot column. One cannot divide by zero.

Common Error: Eliminating Above the Pivot Unnecessarily

Eliminating above pivots is valid, but doing so at every stage turns the method toward Gauss-Jordan elimination and adds arithmetic that Gaussian elimination does not require.

Common Error: Forgetting Back Substitution

Reaching row-echelon form does not always complete the solution. Pivot variables must ordinarily be recovered from the bottom row upward.

Common Error: Treating a Free Variable as Zero

A free variable represents a degree of freedom. Assigning it zero gives only one particular solution, not the entire solution set.

Common Error: Confusing a Zero Row with Inconsistency

A zero row represents a redundant equation. A contradiction requires a nonzero augmented entry in an otherwise zero row.

Common Error: Ignoring Row Swaps

A zero or numerically poor pivot may require a row interchange. Refusing to swap rows can make an otherwise simple solvable system appear to fail.

Common Error: Ignoring Numerical Stability

Exact arithmetic and floating-point arithmetic behave differently. A formally correct pivot choice may be numerically poor.

Common Error: Using RREF Requirements as Gaussian Requirements

Gaussian elimination does not require pivot entries to be $1$ or zeros above pivots.

Common Misconception: Gaussian elimination is not the same as Gauss-Jordan elimination. Gaussian elimination ordinarily performs forward elimination until row-echelon or upper-triangular form is obtained, then solves by back substitution. Gauss-Jordan elimination continues farther to reduced row-echelon form, where the solution can often be read directly.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$R_i\leftrightarrow R_j$ "Interchange row i and row j."
$R_i\to cR_i$ "Multiply row i by c."
$R_i\to R_i+cR_j$ "Replace row i by row i plus c times row j."
$[A\mid b]$ "The augmented matrix A bar b."
$A\to U$ "Reduce A to an upper-triangular or echelon matrix U."
$A=LU$ "A factors as L times U."
$PA=LU$ "After permutation, A factors into lower- and upper-triangular factors."

The phrase Gaussian elimination commonly refers to the complete solving procedure:

$$ \boxed{ \text{forward elimination} + \text{back substitution} }. $$

In computational contexts, elimination may refer more narrowly to the forward phase that constructs the upper-triangular or echelon system.

Defense Notation Advice: Write each row operation explicitly when working by hand, keep the augmented column attached to the row operation, and distinguish the forward-elimination phase from back substitution. Do not call an ordinary echelon matrix RREF unless pivot $1$s are the only nonzero entries in their columns.
