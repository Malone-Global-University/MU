Descartes' Rule of Signs

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

Descartes' Rule of Signs is a theorem that uses changes in the signs of the coefficients of a real polynomial to restrict the possible numbers of its positive and negative real zeros.

For a real polynomial written in descending powers:

$$ f(x)=a_nx^n+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_1x+a_0, $$

count the changes from positive to negative or negative to positive among the sequence of nonzero coefficients.

$$ \boxed{ \text{positive real zeros} = V(f),\; V(f)-2,\; V(f)-4,\ldots } $$

where $V(f)$ denotes the number of sign changes in the coefficient sequence of $f(x)$.

To investigate negative real zeros, replace $x$ by $-x$ and count the sign changes in:

$$ \boxed{f(-x)}. $$

The theorem is named for René Descartes, who presented the rule in his seventeenth-century work on algebra and geometry. The word sign refers to the positive or negative sign of each nonzero coefficient.

The rule does not usually determine the roots themselves. It narrows the possible counts of positive and negative real roots before other methods such as factoring, graphing, the Rational Root Theorem, or numerical approximation are used.

Operational Nuance

What Counts as a Sign Change?

A sign change occurs when consecutive nonzero coefficients have opposite signs.

For example, the sign sequence:

$$ +,\;-,\;-,\;+, $$

contains:

  • one change from $+$ to $-$,
  • no change from $-$ to $-$, and
  • one change from $-$ to $+$.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{V=2}. $$

Positive Real Roots

Let $V(f)$ be the number of sign changes in $f(x)$. Then the number of positive real zeros of $f$, counted with multiplicity, is:

$$ \boxed{ V(f),\; V(f)-2,\; V(f)-4,\ldots } $$

continuing until a nonnegative number is reached.

Why the Count Drops by Two

The actual number of positive real roots can differ from the number of sign changes only by an even integer.

Thus if:

$$ V(f)=4, $$

the possible numbers of positive real roots are:

$$ \boxed{4,\;2,\;0}. $$

One Sign Change

If:

$$ V(f)=1, $$

then the polynomial has exactly:

$$ \boxed{1} $$

positive real root, counted with multiplicity.

No Sign Changes

If:

$$ V(f)=0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \text{there are no positive real roots} }. $$

Negative Real Roots

Negative real roots of $f(x)$ correspond to positive roots of:

$$ f(-x). $$

Therefore the possible number of negative real roots is:

$$ \boxed{ V(f(-x)),\; V(f(-x))-2,\; V(f(-x))-4,\ldots }. $$

Why $f(-x)$ Works

Suppose $r>0$ is a positive root of $f(-x)$. Then:

$$ f(-r)=0. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{-r} $$

is a negative root of the original polynomial $f(x)$.

Example 1

Consider:

$$ f(x)=x^3-6x^2+11x-6. $$

Its coefficient signs are:

$$ +,\;-,\;+,\;-. $$

Every adjacent pair changes sign:

$$ \boxed{V(f)=3}. $$

Therefore the polynomial has either:

$$ \boxed{3\text{ or }1} $$

positive real roots.

In fact:

$$ f(x)=(x-1)(x-2)(x-3), $$

so all three roots are positive:

$$ \boxed{1,\;2,\;3}. $$

Negative-Root Check for Example 1

Compute:

$$ f(-x) = -x^3-6x^2-11x-6. $$

All coefficients are negative, so:

$$ \boxed{V(f(-x))=0}. $$

Hence there are no negative real roots.

Example 2

Let:

$$ f(x)=x^4+x^3-3x^2-x+2. $$

The coefficient signs are:

$$ +,\;+,\;-,\;-,\;+. $$

There are two sign changes:

$$ \boxed{V(f)=2}. $$

Therefore the possible number of positive real roots is:

$$ \boxed{2\text{ or }0}. $$

Checking Negative Roots

Substitute $-x$:

$$ f(-x) = x^4-x^3-3x^2+x+2. $$

Its signs are:

$$ +,\;-,\;-,\;+,\;+. $$

Again there are:

$$ \boxed{2} $$

sign changes, so there may be:

$$ \boxed{2\text{ or }0} $$

negative real roots.

Zero Coefficients Are Ignored

Consider:

$$ f(x)=x^5-4x^3+2x. $$

Written with all powers displayed:

$$ f(x) = x^5+0x^4-4x^3+0x^2+2x+0. $$

The zero coefficients are omitted when counting signs. The relevant sequence is:

$$ +,\;-,\;+. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{V(f)=2}. $$

Zero Roots Must Be Treated Separately

If the constant term is zero, then $x$ is a factor.

For example:

$$ f(x)=x^4-3x^2=x^2(x^2-3). $$

The polynomial has:

$$ \boxed{x=0} $$

as a root of multiplicity $2$.

Descartes' Rule of Signs concerns positive and negative roots, so zero roots should be factored out and recorded separately.

Multiplicity Counts

Roots in Descartes' Rule of Signs are counted with multiplicity.

For example:

$$ f(x)=(x-1)^2(x+3). $$

The positive root $x=1$ has multiplicity $2$, so it contributes two to the positive-root count.

Repeated Roots

Because multiplicity is counted, a polynomial can have fewer distinct positive roots than its Descartes count suggests while still matching the theorem.

Real Coefficients

The standard rule applies to polynomials with real coefficients:

$$ \boxed{a_k\in\mathbb{R}}. $$

The notions of positive and negative coefficient signs require an ordered real coefficient field.

Nonreal Roots

Descartes' Rule of Signs does not directly count nonreal complex roots. However, after possible positive, negative, and zero real roots are accounted for, the degree and Fundamental Theorem of Algebra can constrain the remaining roots.

For a real polynomial, nonreal roots occur in complex-conjugate pairs.

Combining with Degree

Suppose a degree-$5$ polynomial has:

$$ 3\text{ or }1 $$

possible positive roots and:

$$ 2\text{ or }0 $$

possible negative roots.

The remaining roots, after multiplicity is accounted for, must fit the total:

$$ \boxed{5}. $$

Rule Gives Possibilities, Not Usually Exact Counts

If a polynomial has four sign changes, the rule does not assert that it has four positive roots.

It says:

$$ \boxed{ 4,\;2,\text{ or }0 } $$

positive real roots are possible.

When the Rule Is Exact

If the number of sign changes is $0$ or $1$, the result for that sign is exact:

$$ V(f)=0 \Longrightarrow 0\text{ positive roots}, $$
$$ V(f)=1 \Longrightarrow 1\text{ positive root}. $$

The same statements apply to negative roots using $f(-x)$.

Ordering the Polynomial

The polynomial should be written in descending powers before counting sign changes:

$$ \boxed{ a_nx^n+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_0 }. $$

Reordering terms arbitrarily can produce a meaningless sign-change count.

Relationship to the Rational Root Theorem

Descartes' Rule of Signs predicts possible numbers of positive and negative roots. The Rational Root Theorem supplies possible rational values for roots.

The two tools are therefore complementary.

$$ \boxed{ \text{Descartes} \rightarrow \text{possible root counts} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{Rational Root Theorem} \rightarrow \text{possible rational root values} }. $$

Relationship to Graphing

A positive real root corresponds to an $x$-intercept to the right of the origin, while a negative real root corresponds to an $x$-intercept to the left, provided distinct roots are being considered geometrically.

Repeated roots must be interpreted with multiplicity even though a repeated root corresponds to only one intercept location.

Common Misconception: The number of sign changes is not always the exact number of positive roots. If there are $4$ sign changes, the polynomial may have $4$, $2$, or $0$ positive real roots. The count can decrease only by an even number.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$V(f)=3$ "F has three sign changes."
$f(-x)$ "F of negative x."
$3,\;1$ "Three or one possible positive real roots."
$4,\;2,\;0$ "Four, two, or zero possible positive real roots."
$+,-,+,-$ "Positive, negative, positive, negative."
$x=0$ "X equals zero," handled separately as a zero root.
$a_n\neq0$ "The leading coefficient is nonzero."

The standard phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"sign change"} } $$

means a transition between consecutive nonzero coefficients of opposite signs:

$$ +\to- \qquad\text{or}\qquad -\to+. $$

Zero coefficients are skipped rather than being assigned a positive or negative sign.

When stating the theorem, precise language matters. One should say:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"The number of positive real zeros is }V,V-2,V-4,\ldots" } $$

rather than:

$$ \text{"The number of sign changes equals the number of roots."} $$

The latter statement is generally false.

Defense Notation Advice: Write the polynomial in descending powers, omit zero coefficients when counting signs, factor out any power of $x$ to identify zero roots separately, and explicitly report every count obtained by subtracting successive even integers.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Rule: If $V(f)$ is the number of sign changes in a real polynomial $f(x)$, then the number of positive real zeros is: $$ \boxed{ V(f),\; V(f)-2,\; V(f)-4,\ldots }. $$
  • Negative Roots: Count sign changes in: $$ \boxed{f(-x)}. $$
  • Negative-Root Possibilities: $$ \boxed{ V(f(-x)),\; V(f(-x))-2,\; V(f(-x))-4,\ldots }. $$
  • Positive-to-Negative Change: $$ +\to- $$ counts as one sign change.
  • Negative-to-Positive Change: $$ -\to+ $$ counts as one sign change.
  • No Change: $$ +\to+ \qquad\text{or}\qquad -\to- $$ contributes no sign change.
  • Zero Coefficients: Zero coefficients are ignored when counting sign changes.
  • Descending Order: Terms should be arranged from highest to lowest exponent before signs are counted.
  • No Positive Sign Changes: $$ V(f)=0 \Longrightarrow \boxed{0\text{ positive real roots}}. $$
  • Exactly One Positive Sign Change: $$ V(f)=1 \Longrightarrow \boxed{1\text{ positive real root}}. $$
  • Two Positive Sign Changes: $$ V(f)=2 \Longrightarrow \boxed{2\text{ or }0}. $$
  • Three Positive Sign Changes: $$ V(f)=3 \Longrightarrow \boxed{3\text{ or }1}. $$
  • Four Positive Sign Changes: $$ V(f)=4 \Longrightarrow \boxed{4,\;2,\text{ or }0}. $$
  • Five Positive Sign Changes: $$ V(f)=5 \Longrightarrow \boxed{5,\;3,\text{ or }1}. $$
  • Parity: The possible positive-root counts have the same parity as $V(f)$.
  • Same Rule for Negative Roots: The parity principle also applies to $V(f(-x))$.
  • Multiplicity: Real roots are counted according to multiplicity.
  • Repeated Positive Root: A positive root of multiplicity $2$ contributes two to the positive-root count.
  • Distinct Roots: The theorem does not directly state the number of distinct roots.
  • Zero Root: A root at: $$ x=0 $$ is neither positive nor negative.
  • Factor of $x$: If: $$ f(x)=x^kg(x),\qquad g(0)\neq0, $$ then: $$ \boxed{x=0} $$ is a root of multiplicity $k$ and should be recorded separately.
  • Real Coefficients: The standard theorem concerns polynomials whose coefficients have meaningful positive and negative signs, particularly real coefficients.
  • Complex Roots: The theorem does not directly locate or count nonreal roots.
  • Complex-Conjugate Pairs: For real polynomials, nonreal roots occur in conjugate pairs.
  • Degree Constraint: A degree-$n$ polynomial has $n$ complex roots counted with multiplicity, providing a total against which the Descartes possibilities can be checked.
  • Fundamental Theorem of Algebra: Descartes' Rule of Signs complements, rather than replaces, the theorem that a degree-$n$ polynomial has $n$ complex roots counted with multiplicity.
  • Upper Bound: $V(f)$ is an upper bound on the number of positive real roots counted with multiplicity.
  • Negative Upper Bound: $V(f(-x))$ is an upper bound on the number of negative real roots counted with multiplicity.
  • Not an Existence Guarantee in General: If $V(f)=4$, four positive roots are permitted but not guaranteed.
  • Exact Zero Case: No sign changes guarantees no positive roots.
  • Exact One Case: One sign change guarantees exactly one positive root counted with multiplicity.
  • Polynomial Example: $$ x^3-6x^2+11x-6 $$ has three sign changes and therefore either three or one positive roots.
  • Factored Verification: $$ x^3-6x^2+11x-6 = (x-1)(x-2)(x-3), $$ confirming three positive roots.
  • Negative Check: For the same polynomial: $$ f(-x)=-x^3-6x^2-11x-6, $$ which has zero sign changes and therefore no negative roots.
  • Even-Degree Example: A quartic with two sign changes may have either two or zero positive real roots.
  • Missing Terms: $$ x^4-5x^2+4 $$ has relevant signs: $$ +,\;-,\;+, $$ so there are: $$ \boxed{2} $$ sign changes.
  • Zero Coefficient Warning: The absent $x^3$ and $x$ terms in the preceding polynomial do not interrupt the sign sequence.
  • Rational Root Theorem: Descartes' Rule determines possible sign counts; the Rational Root Theorem can then suggest specific rational candidates.
  • Synthetic Division: Once a candidate root is verified, synthetic division can reduce the polynomial degree.
  • Factoring: Factoring ultimately reveals exact real roots when a suitable factorization is available.
  • Graphing: A graph can help determine which of the counts allowed by Descartes' Rule actually occurs.
  • Numerical Methods: Approximation methods may locate roots when symbolic factoring is not practical.
  • Intermediate Value Theorem: Sign changes in function values over intervals can establish the existence of real roots and help refine the possibilities predicted by Descartes' Rule.
  • Positive Root Location: Positive roots lie on: $$ \boxed{x>0}. $$
  • Negative Root Location: Negative roots lie on: $$ \boxed{x<0}. $$
  • Zero Root Location: Zero roots occur at: $$ \boxed{x=0}. $$
  • Sign Pattern: Only signs of nonzero coefficients matter for the rule; coefficient magnitudes do not directly enter the sign-change count.
  • Magnitude Contrast: $$ x^3-1000x^2+x-1 $$ and: $$ x^3-2x^2+7x-9 $$ have the same alternating coefficient-sign pattern even though their coefficient magnitudes differ greatly.
  • Coefficient Order Matters: The sign sequence is read in polynomial degree order, not in arbitrary written order.
  • Root Count vs. Root Value: The theorem limits how many roots of each sign can occur; it does not give their numerical locations.
  • Root Count vs. Intercepts: Multiplicity means the root count may exceed the number of distinct $x$-intercept locations.
  • Double Root: A double positive root counts twice under the theorem but appears at one horizontal coordinate on the graph.
  • Odd Multiplicity: A real root of odd multiplicity typically corresponds to a graph crossing the $x$-axis.
  • Even Multiplicity: A real root of even multiplicity typically corresponds to a graph touching and turning at the $x$-axis.
  • Pre-Solution Tool: Descartes' Rule of Signs is especially useful before detailed root solving because it narrows the possible structure of the answer.
  • Terminological Contrast: A sign change concerns adjacent nonzero coefficients, while a root is an input value satisfying: $$ f(x)=0. $$ The theorem connects these ideas but does not identify them as the same thing.
  • Core Interpretation: Descartes' Rule of Signs converts the coefficient-sign pattern of a real polynomial into restrictions on the numbers of positive and negative real roots: $$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \text{positive roots} &:\; V(f),V(f)-2,V(f)-4,\ldots,\\ \text{negative roots} &:\; V(f(-x)),V(f(-x))-2,V(f(-x))-4,\ldots \end{aligned} } $$ with roots counted by multiplicity and zero roots handled separately.
