Arithmetic Sequence

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

An arithmetic sequence is a sequence of numbers in which the difference between consecutive terms remains constant.

The word arithmetic refers to numerical calculation and number relationships, while sequence refers to an ordered arrangement whose terms occur in a specified position. The term therefore describes an ordered numerical pattern formed by repeatedly adding the same quantity.

Arithmetic sequence and arithmetic progression ordinarily refer to the same mathematical structure. Modern textbooks, especially in algebra and precalculus, often prefer the word sequence because it fits the broader notation and theory of sequences:

$$ a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots $$

The term progression remains common in other educational traditions and in historical mathematical writing.

Arithmetic sequences are contrasted with geometric sequences. Arithmetic sequences change through repeated addition or subtraction; geometric sequences change through repeated multiplication or division by a fixed ratio.

Operational Nuance

A sequence:

$$ a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots $$

is arithmetic when there exists a constant $d$ satisfying:

$$ \boxed{ a_{n+1}-a_n=d } $$

for each pair of consecutive terms.

The constant $d$ is called the common difference.

For example:

$$ 7,\ 12,\ 17,\ 22,\ 27,\ldots $$

is arithmetic because:

$$ 12-7=5, $$ $$ 17-12=5, $$ $$ 22-17=5. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{d=5}. $$

An arithmetic sequence can be described recursively:

$$ \boxed{ a_{n+1}=a_n+d }. $$

This tells us how to obtain each new term from the preceding term.

It can also be described explicitly. Beginning with $a_1$:

$$ a_2=a_1+d, $$ $$ a_3=a_1+2d, $$ $$ a_4=a_1+3d. $$

Continuing this pattern gives the nth-term formula:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d }. $$

For the sequence:

$$ 7,\ 12,\ 17,\ 22,\ldots, $$

we have:

$$ a_1=7, \qquad d=5. $$

Hence:

$$ a_n = 7+5(n-1). $$

Simplifying:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=5n+2 }. $$

The twentieth term is therefore:

$$ a_{20} = 7+19(5) $$ $$ =102. $$

The common difference can be positive, negative, or zero.

$$ d>0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{increasing arithmetic sequence}, $$
$$ d<0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{decreasing arithmetic sequence}, $$
$$ d=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{constant sequence}. $$

Common Misconception: A sequence does not have to increase to be arithmetic. For example:

$$ 20,\ 16,\ 12,\ 8,\ldots $$

is arithmetic with:

$$ d=-4. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$a_1$ "A sub one," or "the first term."
$a_n$ "A sub n," or "the nth term."
$d$ "D," or "the common difference."
$a_{n+1}-a_n=d$ "The difference between consecutive terms is d."
$a_{n+1}=a_n+d$ "The next term equals the current term plus the common difference."
$a_n=a_1+(n-1)d$ "The nth term equals the first term plus n minus one times the common difference."

Arithmetic sequences are commonly represented in three different ways.

Listed form:

$$ 4,\ 9,\ 14,\ 19,\ldots $$

Recursive form:

$$ a_1=4, \qquad a_{n+1}=a_n+5. $$

Explicit form:

$$ a_n=4+5(n-1). $$

All three representations describe the same sequence.

The explicit rule can also be written:

$$ a_n=dn+(a_1-d). $$

This form makes the sequence's connection with linear functions especially clear.

Defense Notation Advice: Always determine whether indexing begins with $n=1$ or $n=0$. If the initial value is denoted $a_0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=a_0+nd }. $$

Using $a_1+(n-1)d$ when the sequence actually begins at $a_0$ creates an off-by-one error.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Arithmetic Progression: In ordinary usage: $$ \boxed{ \text{arithmetic sequence} = \text{arithmetic progression} }. $$ They are two names for the same constant-difference pattern.
  • Common Difference: The defining test is: $$ a_{n+1}-a_n=d. $$ If these consecutive differences are not constant, the sequence is not arithmetic.
  • Explicit Formula: Given the first term and common difference: $$ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d. $$ This formula allows any term to be found without calculating all preceding terms.
  • Recursive Formula: An arithmetic sequence can be generated from: $$ a_{n+1}=a_n+d $$ together with an initial term such as: $$ a_1=k. $$
  • Finding the Common Difference: For consecutive terms: $$ a_n,\ a_{n+1}, $$ calculate: $$ \boxed{ d=a_{n+1}-a_n }. $$
  • Finding the First Term: If $a_n$, $n$, and $d$ are known, rearrange: $$ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d $$ to obtain: $$ a_1=a_n-(n-1)d. $$
  • Finding the Term Number: If: $$ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d, $$ then: $$ n = 1+\frac{a_n-a_1}{d} $$ when $d\neq0$. This can determine where a particular value occurs in the sequence.
  • Arithmetic Mean: If $a$, $m$, and $b$ are consecutive terms, then: $$ m-a=b-m. $$ Hence: $$ \boxed{ m=\frac{a+b}{2} }. $$ The middle term is the arithmetic mean of the outer terms.
  • Equally Spaced Terms: If two terms lie the same number of positions on opposite sides of $a_n$, then: $$ \boxed{ a_n = \frac{a_{n-k}+a_{n+k}}{2} }. $$ This symmetry follows from the constant difference.
  • Inserting Arithmetic Means: To insert $k$ arithmetic means between numbers $A$ and $B$, create an arithmetic sequence containing: $$ k+2 $$ total terms. The common difference is: $$ d=\frac{B-A}{k+1}. $$
  • Arithmetic Series: A sequence lists terms: $$ a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n. $$ A series adds them: $$ a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n. $$ Thus an arithmetic sequence and an arithmetic series are related but not interchangeable terms.
  • Sum of the First n Terms: If: $$ S_n=a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ S_n=\frac{n}{2}(a_1+a_n) }. $$
  • Sum Formula Using the Common Difference: Substituting: $$ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d $$ gives: $$ \boxed{ S_n= \frac{n}{2} \left[ 2a_1+(n-1)d \right] }. $$
  • Discrete Linear Structure: Since: $$ a_n=dn+(a_1-d), $$ an arithmetic sequence is linear as a function of its index $n$. The index changes in discrete integer steps rather than continuously.
  • Graphical Interpretation: If the terms are plotted as: $$ (1,a_1),(2,a_2),(3,a_3),\ldots, $$ the points lie on a straight line. Its slope is the common difference: $$ d. $$
  • First Differences: Arithmetic sequences are characterized by constant first differences: $$ \Delta a_n=a_{n+1}-a_n=d. $$ This language becomes especially useful when comparing arithmetic sequences with quadratic sequences, whose second differences are constant.
  • Geometric Sequence Contrast: An arithmetic sequence satisfies: $$ a_{n+1}-a_n=d, $$ whereas a geometric sequence satisfies: $$ \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}=r $$ whenever the quotient is defined. Arithmetic change is additive; geometric change is multiplicative.
  • Constant Sequences: A constant sequence such as: $$ 6,\ 6,\ 6,\ 6,\ldots $$ is arithmetic because: $$ d=0. $$
  • Finite Arithmetic Sequences: A sequence may terminate: $$ 2,\ 6,\ 10,\ 14,\ 18. $$ It remains arithmetic because every consecutive difference is $4$.
  • Infinite Arithmetic Sequences: A pattern may continue indefinitely: $$ 2,\ 6,\ 10,\ 14,\ldots. $$ The ellipsis indicates continuation according to the established rule.
  • Real-World Modeling: Arithmetic sequences model quantities changing by a fixed additive amount per step: a staircase rising by equal heights, savings increased by a fixed deposit each period, seats added by a fixed number per row, or yearly payments increasing by a constant dollar amount.
  • Terminological Contrast: An arithmetic sequence is an ordered constant-difference pattern. The common difference is the amount added at each step. An arithmetic progression is an alternate name for the same sequence, while an arithmetic series is the sum of its terms.
