Concentric

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

Concentric describes two or more geometric figures that have the same center.

$$ \boxed{ \text{concentric} = \text{sharing a common center} }. $$

The term is formed from roots expressing the idea of being together and having a common center. Its geometric meaning is therefore unusually transparent: concentric figures are centered at the same point.

The most familiar examples are concentric circles:

$$ \boxed{ (x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=r_1^2 } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ (x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=r_2^2 }. $$

Both circles have center $(h,k)$, although their radii may differ.

The adjective may also be applied to spheres, ellipses, and other figures for which a meaningful center has been defined.

Operational Nuance

Concentric Circles

Two circles are concentric when they have the same center.

For example:

$$ (x-3)^2+(y+2)^2=16 $$

and:

$$ (x-3)^2+(y+2)^2=49 $$

are concentric because each has center:

$$ \boxed{ (3,-2) }. $$

Their radii are $4$ and $7$.

General Equation Test

Circles written in standard form:

$$ (x-h_1)^2+(y-k_1)^2=r_1^2 $$

and:

$$ (x-h_2)^2+(y-k_2)^2=r_2^2 $$

are concentric exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ h_1=h_2 \qquad\text{and}\qquad k_1=k_2 }. $$

Radius Does Not Determine Concentricity

Concentricity concerns the center, not the radius.

Two circles may have very different radii and still be concentric:

$$ \boxed{ r_1\ne r_2 \quad\text{is compatible with concentricity} }. $$

Distinct Concentric Circles Do Not Intersect

If two concentric circles have different radii, they have no common points.

Suppose:

$$ r_1

Every point on the first circle is exactly $r_1$ units from the common center, while every point on the second is exactly $r_2$ units away.

Since no point can simultaneously be two different distances from the same center:

$$ \boxed{ C_1\cap C_2=\varnothing } $$

for distinct concentric circles.

Equal Radii Produce the Same Circle

If two circles have the same center and the same radius, then they are not merely two distinct nested circles. They describe the same geometric locus.

$$ \boxed{ \text{same center} + \text{same radius} \Rightarrow \text{coincident circles} }. $$

Radial Separation

If concentric circles have radii $r_1$ and $r_2$, their constant radial separation is:

$$ \boxed{ |r_2-r_1| }. $$

Along every ray from the common center, the two circles are separated by this same radial distance.

Annulus

The region between two concentric circles is called an annulus.

If:

$$ 0

then an annulus centered at $(h,k)$ may be described by:

$$ \boxed{ r^2 < (x-h)^2+(y-k)^2 < R^2 }. $$

Closed or half-open variants may include one or both boundary circles.

Area between Concentric Circles

The area of an annulus with inner radius $r$ and outer radius $R$ is:

$$ \boxed{ A=\pi(R^2-r^2) }. $$

Factoring gives:

$$ A=\pi(R-r)(R+r). $$

Concentric Circles in Polar Coordinates

When the common center is the origin, circles take especially simple polar forms:

$$ \boxed{ r=a, \qquad r=b }. $$

Here the polar radial coordinate is constant on each circle.

Concentric Spheres

The three-dimensional analogue consists of spheres sharing a common center.

For center $(h,k,\ell)$:

$$ \boxed{ (x-h)^2+(y-k)^2+(z-\ell)^2=R^2 }. $$

Two such spheres with different values of $R$ are concentric.

Spherical Shell

The region between two concentric spheres with radii $r$ and $R$ is a spherical shell.

Its volume is:

$$ \boxed{ V= \frac43\pi(R^3-r^3) }. $$

Concentric Ellipses

Ellipses can also be concentric when they share the same center.

For example:

$$ \frac{(x-h)^2}{a_1^2} + \frac{(y-k)^2}{b_1^2} =1 $$

and:

$$ \frac{(x-h)^2}{a_2^2} + \frac{(y-k)^2}{b_2^2} =1 $$

share the center $(h,k)$.

Concentric Ellipses Need Not Have the Same Foci

Sharing a center does not require sharing focal points.

For an axis-aligned ellipse with $a>b$, the focal distance is:

$$ c=\sqrt{a^2-b^2}. $$

Changing $a$ or $b$ can therefore change the foci even though the center remains fixed.

$$ \boxed{ \text{same center} \not\Rightarrow \text{same foci} }. $$

Concentric versus Similar

Concentric figures need not be similar merely because they share a center.

All circles are similar, so concentric circles are automatically similar. But two concentric ellipses can have different axis ratios and therefore need not be similar.

Concentric versus Congruent

Concentricity says nothing by itself about congruence.

Distinct concentric circles with different radii are not congruent:

$$ \boxed{ r_1\ne r_2 \Rightarrow C_1\not\cong C_2 }. $$

Concentric versus Coincident

Concentric means sharing a center. Coincident means occupying the same geometric position or representing the same geometric object.

Thus two circles with common center but different radii are concentric but not coincident.

$$ \boxed{ \text{concentric} \not\Rightarrow \text{coincident} }. $$

Concentric versus Tangent

Distinct concentric circles cannot be tangent because their center distance is zero, while tangency between distinct circles requires a center-distance relationship involving their radii.

External tangency requires:

$$ d=r_1+r_2, $$

and internal tangency requires:

$$ d=|r_1-r_2|. $$

For concentric circles:

$$ d=0. $$

Hence distinct positive-radius concentric circles are not tangent.

Concentric versus Eccentric

In ordinary geometric language, figures whose centers do not coincide may be described as eccentric relative to one another.

$$ \boxed{ \text{concentric} \leftrightarrow \text{common center} } $$

whereas eccentric placement involves an offset between centers.

Center Distance

If two circles have centers:

$$ C_1=(h_1,k_1), \qquad C_2=(h_2,k_2), $$

then the center distance is:

$$ d= \sqrt{(h_2-h_1)^2+(k_2-k_1)^2}. $$

They are concentric exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ d=0 }. $$

Concentric versus Coaxial

Coaxial and concentric should not be treated as synonyms.

In three-dimensional geometry, coaxial objects share an axis. For example, two cylindrical surfaces can have the same central axis while having different radii. Their relationship is naturally described as coaxial.

Concentricity instead emphasizes a common center point.

$$ \boxed{ \text{common axis} \ne \text{common center point} }. $$

Concentric Cylinders

The phrase concentric cylinders is frequently used informally for nested cylinders sharing the same axis, especially in applied contexts. Geometrically, coaxial cylinders is often the more precise description because an extended cylinder has an axis rather than one unique center point.

Concentric Circular Cylinders in Coordinates

Two infinite circular cylinders:

$$ x^2+y^2=r_1^2 $$

and:

$$ x^2+y^2=r_2^2 $$

share the $z$-axis. They are commonly described in applications as concentric cylinders, though their structural commonality is specifically coaxiality.

Concentric Polygons

The phrase concentric polygons can be less precise because a general polygon does not necessarily have a unique naturally distinguished center.

For regular polygons, however, the circumcenter, incenter, and symmetry center coincide, making the idea of concentric regular polygons well defined when they share that center.

Concentric Regular Polygons

Two regular polygons centered at the same point may be described as concentric even if they have different sizes or different numbers of sides.

For example, a regular hexagon and a square can share the same geometric center without being similar.

Concentricity and Dilation

A dilation centered at the center of a circle maps that circle to a concentric circle.

If a circle has radius $r$ and a dilation has scale factor $k>0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ r'=kr }. $$

The center remains fixed.

Nested Level Curves

Concentric circles naturally arise as level curves of radial functions.

For example:

$$ f(x,y)=x^2+y^2. $$

Its positive level sets:

$$ x^2+y^2=c $$

are concentric circles centered at the origin.

Distance Functions

More generally, sets of points having constant Euclidean distance from a fixed point naturally form concentric circles in two dimensions and concentric spheres in three dimensions.

$$ \boxed{ \|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{c}\|=r }. $$

Applications

Concentric geometry occurs in many mathematical and applied settings, including:

  • annular regions,
  • circular wavefront models,
  • radially symmetric fields,
  • polar-coordinate systems,
  • nested circular boundaries,
  • cross sections of coaxial structures, and
  • level curves of radially symmetric functions.

Radial Symmetry

A family of concentric circles often indicates radial symmetry about their common center.

A function of the form:

$$ f(x,y) = g\left( \sqrt{(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2} \right) $$

depends only on distance from $(h,k)$. Its suitable level curves are therefore concentric circles.

Common Misconception: Concentric Means Same Size

Concentricity concerns center location, not radius or size.

$$ \boxed{ \text{same center} \not\Rightarrow \text{same radius} }. $$

Common Misconception: Nested Circles Are Automatically Concentric

One circle can lie entirely inside another without sharing its center.

Nesting alone does not establish concentricity.

$$ \boxed{ C_1\subset\operatorname{interior}(C_2) \not\Rightarrow \text{same center} }. $$

Common Misconception: Concentric Circles Intersect at Their Center

The center is not a point on a positive-radius circle. It lies inside the circle. Therefore concentric circles do not share the center as a point of their circumferences.

Common Misconception: All Concentric Figures Are Similar

Concentricity specifies only a common center. Similarity requires preservation of shape and proportional dimensions.

Common Misconception: Same Center Means Coincident

Figures may share a center while remaining geometrically distinct.

Two circles become coincident only when both center and radius agree.

Common Misconception: Concentric and Coaxial Are Synonyms

A center is a point; an axis is a line. The terms describe different geometric relationships, even though some applied language uses them loosely for similar nested configurations.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=r_1^2$ and $(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=r_2^2$ "The circles are concentric with center h comma k."
$C_1=C_2=(h,k)$ "The two circles have the same center."
$d(C_1,C_2)=0$ "The distance between the centers is zero."
$|r_2-r_1|$ "The radial separation is the absolute difference of the radii."
$\pi(R^2-r^2)$ "Pi times the difference of the squares of the outer and inner radii."
$\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{c}\|=r$ "The point x is distance r from the common center c."

The adjective normally appears before the type of figure: concentric circles, concentric spheres, or concentric ellipses.

Saying that two figures are concentric at $(h,k)$ is understandable, but the more natural mathematical formulation is that they are concentric with common center $(h,k)$.

Defense Notation Advice: Verify concentricity from the centers, not from visual nesting. For circles in coordinate form, place each equation into standard form and compare $(h,k)$. Distinguish a common center from equal radii, coincidence, tangency, similarity, and coaxiality. When the object does not have a canonical center, state explicitly which notion of center is being used before calling figures concentric.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: Concentric figures share the same center.
  • Circle Test: $$ \boxed{ (h_1,k_1)=(h_2,k_2) }. $$
  • Center-Distance Test: $$ \boxed{ d(C_1,C_2)=0 }. $$
  • Radius: Concentric circles may have different radii.
  • Distinct Concentric Circles: Different radii imply that the circumferences do not intersect.
  • Coincident Case: Same center and same radius describe the same circle.
  • Radial Separation: $$ \boxed{ |r_2-r_1| }. $$
  • Annulus: The region between two concentric circles.
  • Annulus Area: $$ \boxed{ A=\pi(R^2-r^2) }. $$
  • Concentric Spheres: Spheres having the same center point.
  • Spherical-Shell Volume: $$ \boxed{ V=\frac43\pi(R^3-r^3) }. $$
  • Concentric Ellipses: Ellipses may share a center while having different semiaxes and different foci.
  • Dilation: A dilation centered at a circle's center produces a concentric image circle.
  • Polar Coordinates: Circles $r=a$ and $r=b$ are concentric about the polar origin.
  • Radial Functions: Level curves of radially symmetric functions frequently form concentric circles.
  • Similarity: Concentricity alone does not imply similarity for general figures.
  • Congruence: Concentricity alone does not imply congruence.
  • Coincident Contrast: Coincident figures occupy the same geometric position; concentric figures merely share a center.
  • Tangent Contrast: Distinct positive-radius concentric circles are not tangent.
  • Eccentric Contrast: Eccentric placement involves an offset between centers.
  • Coaxial Contrast: Coaxial figures share an axis rather than necessarily a single center point.
  • Cylindrical Usage: Concentric cylinders is common applied terminology, although coaxial cylinders may be geometrically more precise.
  • Polygon Usage: The term requires care because arbitrary polygons need not possess a unique canonical center.
  • Regular Polygons: Concentric regular polygons can be defined naturally through their common symmetry center.
  • Common Error: Do not interpret concentric as meaning equal in size.
  • Common Error: Do not assume nested circles are concentric without checking their centers.
  • Common Error: Do not treat the common center as a point lying on positive-radius concentric circles.
  • Common Error: Do not equate concentricity with coincidence, similarity, tangency, or coaxiality.
  • Terminological Contrast: Concentric means sharing a common center. Coincident means occupying the same geometric locus or position. Coaxial means sharing a common axis. Tangent means meeting at a point with the appropriate local contact. Similar means having the same shape up to scale. Congruent means having the same size and shape. Eccentric may describe an offset-center configuration.
