System of Inequalities

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

A system of inequalities is a collection of two or more inequalities that must all be satisfied simultaneously.

$$ \boxed{ \begin{cases} I_1,\\ I_2,\\ \vdots\\ I_m \end{cases} \qquad \text{with every inequality required} }. $$

The word system indicates that the inequalities are treated as one combined set of constraints. The solution is therefore the overlap of their individual solution sets.

Operational Nuance

Solution-Set Intersection

If $S_1,S_2,\ldots,S_m$ are the solution sets of the individual inequalities, then:

$$ \boxed{ S=S_1\cap S_2\cap\cdots\cap S_m }. $$

A value belongs to the system's solution set only if it satisfies every inequality.

One-Variable Example

Consider:

$$ \begin{cases} x>1,\\ x\leq5. \end{cases} $$

Both conditions together give:

$$ \boxed{ 1<x\leq5 } $$

or, in interval notation:

$$ \boxed{ (1,5] }. $$

Two-Variable Systems

A system such as:

$$ \begin{cases} y>x,\\ y\leq4 \end{cases} $$

describes all points lying above the line $y=x$ and on or below the line $y=4$. The solution is the overlapping region.

Boundary Lines

When graphing a linear inequality, replace the inequality temporarily by equality to identify its boundary:

$$ y>2x+1 \quad\longrightarrow\quad y=2x+1. $$

Strict inequalities exclude the boundary; non-strict inequalities include it.

Strict and Non-Strict Boundaries

$$ \boxed{ y>f(x) \Rightarrow \text{boundary excluded} } $$
$$ \boxed{ y\geq f(x) \Rightarrow \text{boundary included} }. $$

Shading

Each inequality identifies one side of its boundary. The system's graphical solution consists only of the region satisfying every shading condition.

Test Points

If the correct side of a boundary is not obvious, a point not on the boundary can be substituted into the inequality. If the statement is true, that side belongs to the solution region.

Algebraic Manipulation

Individual inequalities may be simplified before their solution sets are intersected. As always, multiplying or dividing by a negative quantity reverses the inequality:

$$ -2x<6 \Longrightarrow \boxed{x>-3}. $$

No Common Solution

A system may contain mutually incompatible conditions. For example:

$$ \begin{cases} x>4,\\ x<2 \end{cases} $$

has:

$$ \boxed{ S=\varnothing }. $$

Redundant Inequalities

One inequality may add no new restriction. For example:

$$ \begin{cases} x>5,\\ x>2 \end{cases} $$

reduces simply to:

$$ \boxed{x>5}. $$

Linear Programming

In optimization, a system of linear inequalities often defines the feasible region within which an objective function is optimized.

Common Misconception

The solution to a system of inequalities is not the union of all individually allowed values. It is the intersection: every listed inequality must hold simultaneously.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\begin{cases}x>1\\x\leq5\end{cases}$ "A system requiring x to be greater than one and at most five."
$1<x\leq5$ "X is greater than one and less than or equal to five."
$(1,5]$ "The interval from one excluded to five included."
$S_1\cap S_2$ "The intersection of the two solution sets."
$S=\varnothing$ "The system has no common solution."
$y\geq2x-1$ "Y is greater than or equal to two x minus one."

In graphical work, phrases such as common region, overlapping region, and feasible region often describe the intersection of the inequalities.

Defense Notation Advice: Treat each inequality as a separate constraint, preserve strict versus non-strict boundaries, and combine final solution sets by intersection. When graphing, distinguish excluded boundaries from included boundaries before identifying the overlap.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: Several inequalities required to hold simultaneously.
  • Solution Set: Intersection of the individual inequality solution sets.
  • One Variable: Solutions are often expressed using compound inequalities or interval notation.
  • Two Variables: Solutions are commonly represented as regions of the coordinate plane.
  • Boundary: Equality determines the dividing line or curve.
  • Strict Inequality: Boundary is excluded.
  • Non-Strict Inequality: Boundary is included.
  • Shading: Each inequality selects a side of its boundary.
  • Test Point: Can determine which side of a boundary satisfies an inequality.
  • Empty Intersection: Incompatible constraints produce no solution.
  • Redundant Constraint: An inequality may add no additional restriction.
  • Linear Programming: Systems of linear inequalities define feasible regions.
  • Optimization: Objective functions may be optimized subject to inequality constraints.
  • Modeling: Systems encode simultaneous limits such as budgets, capacities, ranges, and resource restrictions.
  • Common Error: Taking the union instead of the intersection of solution sets.
  • Common Error: Including a strict boundary.
  • Common Error: Excluding a boundary defined by $\leq$ or $\geq$.
  • Common Error: Failing to reverse an inequality after multiplication or division by a negative number.
  • Core Interpretation: A system of inequalities imposes several simultaneous constraints: $$ \boxed{ S = \bigcap_{i=1}^{m}S_i }. $$ Algebraically, the solution contains only values satisfying every condition; geometrically, it is the common region where all corresponding constraint regions overlap.
