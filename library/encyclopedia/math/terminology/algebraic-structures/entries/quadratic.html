Quadratic

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

In mathematics, quadratic describes an expression, equation, function, relation, or form whose defining polynomial degree is $2$.

$$ \boxed{ \text{quadratic} \Longrightarrow \text{degree }2 } $$

In one variable, the standard quadratic polynomial is:

$$ \boxed{ ax^2+bx+c } $$

with:

$$ \boxed{ a\neq0 }. $$

The restriction $a\neq0$ is essential. If $a=0$, the $x^2$ term disappears and the expression is no longer genuinely quadratic.

The word quadratic is historically connected with the idea of a square. This is reflected directly in the defining squared term:

$$ \boxed{ x^2 }. $$

This connection also explains why older algebraic language often described quadratic problems as problems involving squares or areas.

The term appears in several closely related constructions:

  • quadratic expression;
  • quadratic polynomial;
  • quadratic equation;
  • quadratic function;
  • quadratic formula;
  • quadratic inequality;
  • quadratic form;
  • quadratic approximation;
  • quadratic growth.

The shared structural idea is second degree, although the exact object and operations depend on context.

Operational Nuance

Quadratic Polynomial

A one-variable quadratic polynomial has form:

$$ \boxed{ P(x) = ax^2+bx+c, \qquad a\neq0 }. $$

Coefficients

In:

$$ ax^2+bx+c, $$

$a$ is the quadratic coefficient, $b$ is the linear coefficient, and $c$ is the constant term.

Leading Coefficient

Because the highest-degree term is $ax^2$, the leading coefficient is:

$$ \boxed{ a }. $$

Degree

$$ \boxed{ \deg(ax^2+bx+c)=2 } $$

provided $a\neq0$.

Quadratic Equation

A quadratic equation is commonly written:

$$ \boxed{ ax^2+bx+c=0, \qquad a\neq0 }. $$

Solutions or Roots

Values of $x$ satisfying the quadratic equation are called solutions or roots.

Zeros of a Quadratic Function

If:

$$ f(x)=ax^2+bx+c, $$

then roots of:

$$ ax^2+bx+c=0 $$

are the zeros of $f$.

Quadratic Function

$$ \boxed{ f(x) = ax^2+bx+c, \qquad a\neq0 }. $$

Graph

The graph of a real quadratic function is a parabola.

Opening Direction

If:

$$ a>0, $$

the parabola opens upward.

If:

$$ a<0, $$

it opens downward.

Vertical Stretch and Compression

The magnitude $|a|$ controls vertical scaling relative to the parent function:

$$ \boxed{ y=x^2 }. $$

Parent Quadratic Function

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=x^2 }. $$

Axis of Symmetry

For:

$$ f(x)=ax^2+bx+c, $$

the vertical axis of symmetry is:

$$ \boxed{ x=-\frac{b}{2a} }. $$

Vertex

The vertex occurs at:

$$ \boxed{ x_v = -\frac{b}{2a} }. $$

Its $y$-coordinate is:

$$ \boxed{ y_v = f \left( -\frac{b}{2a} \right) }. $$

Vertex as Extreme Point

If $a>0$, the vertex is a minimum. If $a<0$, it is a maximum.

Standard Form

$$ \boxed{ f(x) = ax^2+bx+c }. $$

Vertex Form

$$ \boxed{ f(x) = a(x-h)^2+k }. $$

The vertex is:

$$ \boxed{ (h,k) }. $$

Factored Form

When roots $r_1$ and $r_2$ exist in the coefficient field:

$$ \boxed{ f(x) = a(x-r_1)(x-r_2) }. $$

Repeated-Root Form

If the two roots coincide at $r$:

$$ \boxed{ f(x) = a(x-r)^2 }. $$

Factoring Method

Some quadratic equations can be solved by factoring.

Example

$$ x^2-5x+6=0. $$

Factor:

$$ (x-2)(x-3)=0. $$

By the zero-product property:

$$ \boxed{ x=2 \quad\text{or}\quad x=3 }. $$

Square-Root Method

A quadratic of the form:

$$ (x-h)^2=d $$

can be solved by:

$$ \boxed{ x-h = \pm\sqrt{d} }. $$

Example

$$ (x-4)^2=9 $$

gives:

$$ x-4=\pm3, $$

hence:

$$ \boxed{ x=1 \quad\text{or}\quad x=7 }. $$

Completing the Square

Any quadratic:

$$ ax^2+bx+c $$

with $a\neq0$ can be rewritten in vertex form by completing the square.

Normalize the Leading Coefficient

$$ ax^2+bx+c = a \left( x^2+\frac{b}{a}x \right) +c. $$

Complete the Square

Add and subtract:

$$ \left( \frac{b}{2a} \right)^2 $$

inside the grouped expression.

Vertex-Form Identity

$$ \boxed{ ax^2+bx+c = a \left( x+\frac{b}{2a} \right)^2 + c-\frac{b^2}{4a} }. $$

Vertex Coordinates from Completing the Square

$$ \boxed{ h=-\frac{b}{2a} } $$
$$ \boxed{ k = c-\frac{b^2}{4a} }. $$

Equivalent Vertex Height

Since:

$$ \Delta=b^2-4ac, $$

the vertex height may also be written:

$$ \boxed{ k = -\frac{\Delta}{4a} }. $$

Quadratic Formula

Every quadratic equation:

$$ ax^2+bx+c=0, \qquad a\neq0 $$

has solutions:

$$ \boxed{ x = \frac{ -b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac} }{ 2a } }. $$

Derivation Source

The quadratic formula is obtained by completing the square on the general quadratic equation.

Discriminant

The expression:

$$ \boxed{ \Delta = b^2-4ac } $$

is called the discriminant.

Positive Discriminant

If:

$$ \Delta>0, $$

there are two distinct real roots.

Zero Discriminant

If:

$$ \Delta=0, $$

there is one repeated real root:

$$ \boxed{ x=-\frac{b}{2a} }. $$

Negative Discriminant

If:

$$ \Delta<0, $$

there are no real roots and two nonreal complex conjugate roots when coefficients are real.

Complex Roots

For real coefficients and $\Delta<0$:

$$ \boxed{ x = \frac{ -b }{ 2a } \pm i \frac{ \sqrt{-\Delta} }{ 2a } }. $$

Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

A nonconstant quadratic polynomial over $\mathbb{C}$ has exactly two roots counting multiplicity.

Multiplicity

When:

$$ \Delta=0, $$

the single root has multiplicity $2$.

Sum of Roots

If $r_1$ and $r_2$ are roots of:

$$ ax^2+bx+c=0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ r_1+r_2 = -\frac{b}{a} }. $$

Product of Roots

$$ \boxed{ r_1r_2 = \frac{c}{a} }. $$

Vieta's Relations

The preceding sum and product relationships are elementary cases of Vieta's formulas.

Deriving from Factored Form

Expand:

$$ a(x-r_1)(x-r_2) = ax^2 - a(r_1+r_2)x + ar_1r_2. $$

Comparing coefficients with:

$$ ax^2+bx+c $$

gives the root relations.

Axis of Symmetry from Roots

If two real roots exist:

$$ \boxed{ x_{\text{axis}} = \frac{r_1+r_2}{2} }. $$

Using Vieta's relation:

$$ \frac{r_1+r_2}{2} = -\frac{b}{2a}. $$

$y$-Intercept

For:

$$ y=ax^2+bx+c, $$

setting $x=0$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ y=c }. $$

$x$-Intercepts

Real $x$-intercepts correspond to real solutions of:

$$ ax^2+bx+c=0. $$

Graph and Discriminant

For a real quadratic function:

  • $\Delta>0$: the parabola crosses the $x$-axis twice;
  • $\Delta=0$: the parabola touches the $x$-axis once;
  • $\Delta<0$: the parabola does not intersect the $x$-axis.

End Behavior

The leading term $ax^2$ controls behavior for large $|x|$.

If $a>0$:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)\to+\infty \quad \text{as } x\to\pm\infty }. $$

If $a<0$:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)\to-\infty \quad \text{as } x\to\pm\infty }. $$

Symmetry

Every quadratic parabola is symmetric about its vertical axis:

$$ \boxed{ x=-\frac{b}{2a} }. $$

Transformation from Parent Function

Vertex form:

$$ y=a(x-h)^2+k $$

shows:

  • horizontal shift by $h$;
  • vertical shift by $k$;
  • reflection across the $x$-axis if $a<0$;
  • vertical scaling by $|a|$.

Quadratic Inequality

A quadratic inequality may take form:

$$ \boxed{ ax^2+bx+c>0 } $$

or use another inequality relation.

Solving Quadratic Inequalities

Common methods include:

  • factoring and sign analysis;
  • graph interpretation;
  • critical-point interval testing;
  • vertex and discriminant analysis.

Example

Solve:

$$ x^2-5x+6<0. $$

Factor:

$$ (x-2)(x-3)<0. $$

The product is negative between the roots:

$$ \boxed{ 2<x<3 }. $$

Quadratic Growth

A quantity exhibits quadratic growth when its dominant dependence is proportional to the square of another quantity.

$$ \boxed{ y\sim kx^2 } $$

or exactly:

$$ y=kx^2 $$

in a pure quadratic model.

Difference Pattern

For a quadratic sequence sampled at equally spaced integer inputs, first differences change linearly and second differences are constant.

Example

For:

$$ f(n)=n^2, $$

values:

$$ 1,\ 4,\ 9,\ 16,\ 25,\ldots $$

have first differences:

$$ 3,\ 5,\ 7,\ 9,\ldots $$

and second differences:

$$ \boxed{ 2,\ 2,\ 2,\ldots }. $$

General Second Difference

For:

$$ f(n)=an^2+bn+c, $$

the constant second difference at unit spacing is:

$$ \boxed{ 2a }. $$

Calculus Connection

For:

$$ f(x)=ax^2+bx+c, $$

the first derivative is:

$$ \boxed{ f'(x)=2ax+b }. $$

Second Derivative

$$ \boxed{ f''(x)=2a }. $$

Thus a quadratic function has constant second derivative.

Vertex from Calculus

Setting:

$$ f'(x)=0 $$

gives:

$$ 2ax+b=0, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ x=-\frac{b}{2a} }. $$

Quadratic Approximation

Near a point, a smooth function may be approximated by a second-degree Taylor polynomial:

$$ \boxed{ f(x) \approx f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) + \frac{ f''(a) }{ 2 } (x-a)^2 }. $$

Why Quadratics Matter in Local Analysis

The quadratic term is the first term that captures curvature beyond linear behavior.

Projectile Motion

Under constant vertical acceleration, height often takes quadratic form:

$$ \boxed{ h(t) = h_0+v_0t-\frac{1}{2}gt^2 }. $$

Physical Meaning of the Vertex

For upward projectile motion under constant downward gravity, the vertex represents maximum height when the model applies.

Optimization

Many elementary maximum and minimum problems reduce to locating the vertex of a quadratic function.

Rectangle Example

Suppose a rectangle has fixed perimeter $P$. If one side is $x$, the other is:

$$ \frac{P}{2}-x. $$

Its area is:

$$ A(x) = x \left( \frac{P}{2}-x \right) = -x^2+\frac{P}{2}x. $$

This is quadratic and has a maximum at its vertex.

Quadratic Form

In several variables, a quadratic form consists of homogeneous degree-$2$ terms.

For two variables:

$$ \boxed{ Q(x,y) = ax^2+bxy+cy^2 }. $$

Matrix Representation of a Quadratic Form

A quadratic form may be written:

$$ \boxed{ Q(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^{T}A\mathbf{x} } $$

with a suitable symmetric matrix $A$.

Example

$$ 3x^2+4xy+5y^2 $$

can be represented using:

$$ A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 2\\ 2 & 5 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Conic Sections

General second-degree equations in two variables:

$$ \boxed{ Ax^2+Bxy+Cy^2+Dx+Ey+F=0 } $$

describe conic sections under appropriate nondegeneracy and classification conditions.

Conic Discriminant

The expression:

$$ \boxed{ B^2-4AC } $$

helps classify real conics, although this is a different use of a discriminant from the one-variable quadratic equation.

Quadratic Regression

Data may be modeled using:

$$ \boxed{ y=ax^2+bx+c } $$

when observed curvature suggests a second-degree relationship.

Quadratic Interpolation

Three distinct input-output points with distinct input values determine a unique quadratic polynomial of degree at most $2$, provided the data are compatible with the interpolation setup.

Degree at Most Two Caveat

The interpolating polynomial through three points may actually have lower degree if the data happen to lie on a line or constant function.

Finite Differences and Interpolation

Constant second differences provide a practical signature of quadratic data at equally spaced inputs.

Quadratic vs. Linear

Linear:

$$ \boxed{ ax+b } $$

has degree $1$ when $a\neq0$.

Quadratic:

$$ \boxed{ ax^2+bx+c } $$

has degree $2$ when $a\neq0$.

Quadratic vs. Cubic

A cubic has degree $3$:

$$ \boxed{ ax^3+bx^2+cx+d }. $$

Quadratic vs. Square

A quadratic expression need not itself be a perfect square.

Example

$$ x^2+x+1 $$

is quadratic but is not the square of a real linear polynomial.

Perfect-Square Quadratic

An expression such as:

$$ \boxed{ x^2+6x+9 = (x+3)^2 } $$

is both quadratic and a perfect-square polynomial.

Recognizing a Perfect-Square Trinomial

$$ \boxed{ a^2+2ab+b^2 = (a+b)^2 } $$
$$ \boxed{ a^2-2ab+b^2 = (a-b)^2 }. $$

Difference of Squares Is Not Quadratic by Name Alone

The expression:

$$ x^2-9 $$

is quadratic in $x$ and also a difference of squares:

$$ \boxed{ (x-3)(x+3) }. $$

Quadratic in Another Expression

Some equations are not degree $2$ in $x$ but are quadratic in a substituted expression.

Example

$$ x^4-5x^2+4=0. $$

Let:

$$ u=x^2. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ u^2-5u+4=0 }. $$

The original equation is quartic in $x$ but quadratic in $x^2$.

Quadratic in Trigonometric Functions

An equation such as:

$$ 2\sin^2x-3\sin x+1=0 $$

is quadratic in:

$$ \boxed{ \sin x }. $$

Substitution Strategy

Set:

$$ u=\sin x, $$

solve the quadratic in $u$, and then solve the resulting trigonometric equations.

Quadratic in Exponentials

An equation such as:

$$ 4^x-5(2^x)+4=0 $$

becomes quadratic under:

$$ u=2^x. $$

Since:

$$ 4^x=(2^x)^2, $$

the equation becomes:

$$ \boxed{ u^2-5u+4=0 }. $$

Numerical Stability

In numerical computation, directly using the familiar quadratic formula can lose precision for some coefficient combinations because of subtractive cancellation.

Alternative Numerical Root Form

A stable implementation may compute one root using a sign-aware expression and obtain the other from:

$$ \boxed{ r_1r_2 = \frac{c}{a} }. $$

This is a computational refinement rather than a change in the mathematical quadratic formula.

Common Error: Forgetting $a\neq0$

If $a=0$, then:

$$ ax^2+bx+c $$

reduces to a linear or constant expression.

Common Error: Calling Any Expression with an $x^2$ Term Quadratic

The highest surviving degree must be exactly $2$.

For example:

$$ x^3+x^2+1 $$

contains $x^2$ but is cubic.

Common Error: Determining Degree before Combining Like Terms

Consider:

$$ x^3-x^3+2x^2+1. $$

It simplifies to:

$$ \boxed{ 2x^2+1 }, $$

which is quadratic.

Common Error: Assuming Every Quadratic Factors over the Integers

For example:

$$ x^2+x+1 $$

does not factor into linear factors with integer coefficients.

Common Error: Assuming Every Quadratic Has Two Distinct Real Roots

The discriminant may be zero or negative.

Common Error: Saying a Repeated Root Gives Two Distinct Intercepts

A double root produces one $x$-intercept where the parabola is tangent to the axis.

Common Error: Forgetting the $\pm$ in the Quadratic Formula

The two branches are:

$$ \boxed{ -b+\sqrt{\Delta} \quad\text{and}\quad -b-\sqrt{\Delta} }. $$

Common Error: Dividing Only the Radical by $2a$

The denominator applies to the entire numerator:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{ -b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac} }{ 2a } }. $$

Common Error: Writing $b^2+4ac$

The discriminant is:

$$ \boxed{ b^2-4ac }. $$

Common Error: Forgetting to Put the Equation in Zero Form

To identify $a$, $b$, and $c$ reliably for the quadratic formula, first write:

$$ \boxed{ ax^2+bx+c=0 }. $$

Common Error: Losing Negative Coefficients

If:

$$ 2x^2-7x-4=0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a=2, \qquad b=-7, \qquad c=-4 }. $$

Common Error: Confusing $-b$ with a Negative Answer

If $b=-7$, then:

$$ \boxed{ -b=7 }. $$

Common Error: Assuming the Vertex Always Gives a Maximum

Upward-opening parabolas have a minimum; downward-opening parabolas have a maximum.

Common Error: Confusing the Axis of Symmetry with the Vertex

The axis:

$$ x=-\frac{b}{2a} $$

is a vertical line. The vertex is a point on that line.

Common Error: Reading Vertex Form Signs Literally

In:

$$ y=a(x-h)^2+k, $$

the horizontal coordinate is $h$, not the sign visibly written inside the parentheses.

Example

$$ y=(x+3)^2-4 $$

has vertex:

$$ \boxed{ (-3,-4) }. $$

Common Error: Assuming Standard, Vertex, and Factored Forms Are Different Functions

They can be equivalent representations of the same quadratic function.

Common Error: Confusing a Quadratic Function with a Quadratic Equation

A function:

$$ y=ax^2+bx+c $$

defines outputs, while:

$$ ax^2+bx+c=0 $$

asks for roots.

Common Error: Assuming a Negative Discriminant Means No Solutions at All

It means no real roots. Complex roots still exist.

Common Error: Assuming the Graph of Every Quadratic Crosses the $x$-Axis

A negative discriminant gives no real $x$-intercepts.

Common Error: Assuming Quadratic Means Perfect Square

Degree $2$ does not imply that the polynomial is the square of a linear expression.

Common Error: Assuming Quadratic Growth Means Doubling Inputs Doubles Outputs

For pure quadratic scaling:

$$ y\propto x^2, $$

doubling $x$ multiplies $y$ by:

$$ \boxed{ 4 }. $$

Common Error: Treating a Quartic in $x$ as Quadratic without Naming the Substitution

An equation such as:

$$ x^4-5x^2+4=0 $$

is quadratic in $x^2$, not quadratic in $x$.

Common Misconception: Quadratic does not merely mean "contains a square." It means that the relevant polynomial structure has degree exactly $2$. The general one-variable form is:

$$ \boxed{ ax^2+bx+c, \qquad a\neq0 }. $$

This structure produces parabolic graphs, constant second differences, constant second derivatives, at most two roots over a field, and a rich family of equivalent forms and solution methods.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$ax^2+bx+c$ "A x squared plus b x plus c," a general quadratic expression when a is nonzero.
$ax^2+bx+c=0$ "A x squared plus b x plus c equals zero," a quadratic equation.
$x=-\frac{b}{2a}$ "X equals negative b over two a," the axis-of-symmetry coordinate.
$\Delta=b^2-4ac$ "The discriminant equals b squared minus four a c."
$x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}$ "X equals negative b plus or minus the square root of b squared minus four a c, all over two a."
$a(x-h)^2+k$ "A times the quantity x minus h squared plus k," vertex form.
$a(x-r_1)(x-r_2)$ "A times x minus r one times x minus r two," factored form.

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"quadratic in }x\text{"} } $$

means the highest nonzero power of $x$ in the relevant polynomial is $2$.

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"quadratic in }u\text{"} } $$

means an expression can be treated as second degree in the substituted quantity $u$, even when it is higher degree in the original variable.

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"solve the quadratic"} } $$

ordinarily means find all values satisfying a quadratic equation, using factoring, square roots, completing the square, the quadratic formula, or another valid method.

Defense Notation Advice: Before calling an expression quadratic, simplify it and confirm that its highest surviving degree is exactly $2$. When using the quadratic formula, first place the equation in the form $ax^2+bx+c=0$, record signed values of $a$, $b$, and $c$, and keep the entire numerator over $2a$. Distinguish the function, its graph, and the equation obtained by setting the function equal to zero.
