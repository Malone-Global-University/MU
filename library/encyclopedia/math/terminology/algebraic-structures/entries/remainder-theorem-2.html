Remainder Theorem

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

The Remainder Theorem states that when a polynomial $P(x)$ is divided by a linear polynomial of the form $x-c$, the remainder is exactly $P(c)$.

$$ \boxed{ \text{remainder of }P(x)\div(x-c)=P(c) }. $$

The theorem links two apparently different operations: polynomial division and direct evaluation. Instead of carrying out the entire division, one can substitute $x=c$ into the polynomial and obtain the remainder immediately.

The word remainder refers to the lower-degree expression left after polynomial division. Because the divisor $x-c$ has degree $1$, the remainder must have degree less than $1$, so it is a constant.

Operational Nuance

Polynomial Division Structure

The polynomial division algorithm gives:

$$ P(x) = (x-c)Q(x)+R, $$

where $R$ is constant because:

$$ \deg R<\deg(x-c)=1. $$

Why the Theorem Works

Substitute $x=c$ into:

$$ P(x) = (x-c)Q(x)+R. $$

Then:

$$ P(c) = (c-c)Q(c)+R = R. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ R=P(c) }. $$

Example

Find the remainder when:

$$ P(x)=x^3-4x+7 $$

is divided by $x-2$.

$$ P(2) = 2^3-4(2)+7 = 8-8+7 = \boxed{7}. $$

The remainder is $7$.

Divisor of the Form $x+a$

Since:

$$ x+a=x-(-a), $$

dividing by $x+a$ requires evaluating at $-a$:

$$ \boxed{ \text{remainder}=P(-a) }. $$

Factor Theorem Connection

If the remainder is zero, then:

$$ P(c)=0. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ P(c)=0 \Longleftrightarrow x-c\text{ is a factor of }P(x) }. $$

This special consequence is known as the Factor Theorem.

Root Connection

The statements:

$$ \boxed{ P(c)=0, \qquad x-c\text{ is a factor}, \qquad c\text{ is a root} } $$

are equivalent for polynomial $P$ over the usual number systems where these expressions are defined.

Synthetic Division

Synthetic division provides a compact computational method for division by $x-c$. The final synthetic entry is the same number predicted by the Remainder Theorem:

$$ \boxed{ \text{final synthetic entry}=P(c) }. $$

Evaluation without Division

The theorem is especially useful when only the remainder is requested. Full long division is unnecessary.

Unknown Coefficients

The theorem can also determine unknown coefficients. Suppose:

$$ P(x)=x^2+kx+3 $$

leaves remainder $5$ when divided by $x-1$. Then:

$$ P(1)=5. $$

Hence:

$$ 1+k+3=5, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ k=1 }. $$

Divisibility Tests for Polynomials

To determine whether $x-c$ divides $P(x)$ exactly, compute $P(c)$:

$$ \boxed{ P(c)=0 \Longrightarrow \text{zero remainder} }. $$

Restriction to Linear Divisors

The familiar one-number form of the Remainder Theorem applies directly to divisors $x-c$. For higher-degree divisors, the remainder is generally a polynomial rather than a single constant.

For example, division by a quadratic divisor gives a remainder of degree at most $1$:

$$ \boxed{ R(x)=ax+b }. $$

Common Misconception

The theorem does not say that $P(c)$ is always zero. It says that $P(c)$ is the remainder. It becomes zero only when $x-c$ is actually a factor.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$P(c)$ "P of c."
$P(x)=(x-c)Q(x)+P(c)$ "P of x equals x minus c times the quotient plus P of c."
$P(c)=0$ "P of c equals zero."
$x-c\mid P(x)$ "X minus c divides P of x."
$x+a=x-(-a)$ "For divisor x plus a, evaluate the polynomial at negative a."
$R=P(c)$ "The remainder equals P evaluated at c."

The instruction:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"use the Remainder Theorem"} } $$

generally means identify $c$ from the divisor $x-c$ and evaluate $P(c)$ rather than carrying out full polynomial division.

Defense Notation Advice: Match the divisor carefully to $x-c$. If the divisor is $x+4$, then $c=-4$, not $4$. Distinguish the Remainder Theorem from the Factor Theorem: the former gives $P(c)$ as the remainder, while the latter identifies the special zero-remainder case.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Statement: Dividing $P(x)$ by $x-c$ leaves remainder $P(c)$.
  • Division Algorithm Form: $P(x)=(x-c)Q(x)+P(c)$.
  • Linear Divisor: The direct theorem applies to divisors of degree $1$.
  • Constant Remainder: Degree must be less than the degree-$1$ divisor.
  • Evaluation Shortcut: Full polynomial division is unnecessary when only the remainder is required.
  • Divisor $x+a$: Evaluate at $-a$.
  • Factor Theorem: $P(c)=0$ exactly when $x-c$ is a factor.
  • Root Test: $c$ is a root exactly when the corresponding remainder is zero.
  • Synthetic Division: Final synthetic entry equals $P(c)$.
  • Unknown Coefficients: A specified remainder produces an equation involving polynomial coefficients.
  • Divisibility: Zero remainder confirms exact polynomial division.
  • Polynomial Algebra: The theorem links division structure with evaluation maps.
  • Higher-Degree Divisor: The remainder may itself be a polynomial.
  • Common Error: Substituting the wrong sign from the divisor.
  • Common Error: Assuming every evaluated value must be zero.
  • Common Error: Confusing the remainder with the quotient.
  • Common Error: Treating $x+c$ as though it were $x-c$ with $c$ positive.
  • Common Error: Using the one-number theorem directly for a higher-degree divisor.
  • Common Error: Confusing the Remainder Theorem with the Factor Theorem.
  • Core Interpretation: The Remainder Theorem compresses polynomial division by a linear factor into evaluation: $$ \boxed{ P(x) = (x-c)Q(x)+P(c) }. $$ The remainder is therefore: $$ \boxed{ P(c) }. $$ When that value is zero, the theorem immediately connects to factorization and polynomial roots through the Factor Theorem.
