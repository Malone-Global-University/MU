Argument of a Complex Number

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

The argument of a complex number is an angle describing the direction of a nonzero complex number when it is represented as a point or vector in the complex plane.

If:

$$ z=a+bi\ne0, $$

then an argument of $z$ is any angle $\theta$ from the positive real axis to the vector from the origin to $(a,b)$.

$$ \boxed{ \arg z=\theta } $$

The argument is fundamentally a measure of direction, while the modulus:

$$ |z| $$

measures distance from the origin.

$$ \boxed{ \text{modulus}=\text{magnitude}, \qquad \text{argument}=\text{direction} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Geometric Definition

For:

$$ z=a+bi\ne0, $$

draw the vector from the origin to:

$$ (a,b). $$

Any angle from the positive real axis to this vector is an argument of $z$.

Arguments Are Not Unique

A complete rotation does not change direction. Therefore, if $\theta$ is one argument, then:

$$ \boxed{ \theta+2\pi k, \qquad k\in\mathbb Z } $$

is also an argument.

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \arg z= \theta+2\pi k, \qquad k\in\mathbb Z }. $$

Principal Argument

Because infinitely many arguments represent the same direction, mathematics often selects one distinguished value called the principal argument.

It is commonly written:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Arg}(z) }. $$

A widely used convention is:

$$ \boxed{ -\pi< \operatorname{Arg}(z) \le\pi }. $$

Another convention sometimes uses:

$$ 0\le \operatorname{Arg}(z) <2\pi. $$

Therefore the intended principal range should be checked when different textbooks, calculators, or software systems are compared.

Argument of Zero

The complex number:

$$ z=0 $$

has no direction from the origin because it is the origin itself.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \arg(0)\text{ is undefined} }. $$

Relationship to Rectangular Coordinates

For:

$$ z=a+bi, $$

a first approximation to the argument comes from:

$$ \tan\theta=\frac{b}{a}. $$

Thus:

$$ \theta= \arctan\left(\frac{b}{a}\right) $$

can be useful, but this expression alone does not reliably determine the correct quadrant.

Quadrant Caution

The quotient:

$$ \frac{b}{a} $$

is the same for points in opposite quadrants.

For example:

$$ 1+i \qquad\text{and}\qquad -1-i $$

both give:

$$ \frac{b}{a}=1, $$

but their directions differ by $\pi$.

Quadrant-Aware Angle

In computational settings, a two-argument arctangent function is often used:

$$ \boxed{ \theta=\operatorname{atan2}(b,a) }. $$

This uses the signs of both coordinates to determine the correct quadrant.

Quadrant I

If:

$$ a>0, \qquad b>0, $$

then:

$$ 0<\operatorname{Arg}(z)<\frac{\pi}{2}. $$

Quadrant II

If:

$$ a<0, \qquad b>0, $$

then under the common principal convention:

$$ \frac{\pi}{2} < \operatorname{Arg}(z) < \pi. $$

Quadrant III

If:

$$ a<0, \qquad b<0, $$

then under the convention $(-\pi,\pi]$:

$$ -\pi< \operatorname{Arg}(z) < -\frac{\pi}{2}. $$

Quadrant IV

If:

$$ a>0, \qquad b<0, $$

then:

$$ -\frac{\pi}{2} < \operatorname{Arg}(z) < 0. $$

Positive Real Axis

For:

$$ z=a, \qquad a>0, $$

the principal argument is:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Arg}(z)=0 }. $$

Negative Real Axis

For:

$$ z=a, \qquad a<0, $$

under the principal range $(-\pi,\pi]$:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Arg}(z)=\pi }. $$

The angle $-\pi$ represents the same direction but is excluded under that particular convention.

Positive Imaginary Axis

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Arg}(bi) = \frac{\pi}{2}, \qquad b>0 }. $$

Negative Imaginary Axis

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Arg}(bi) = -\frac{\pi}{2}, \qquad b<0 }. $$

Example: 1 + i

For:

$$ z=1+i, $$

the point lies in Quadrant I and:

$$ \tan\theta=1. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Arg}(1+i) = \frac{\pi}{4} }. $$

All arguments are:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\pi}{4}+2\pi k, \qquad k\in\mathbb Z }. $$

Example: -1 + i

The reference angle is:

$$ \frac{\pi}{4}, $$

but the point lies in Quadrant II.

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Arg}(-1+i) = \frac{3\pi}{4} }. $$

Example: -1 - i

Under the principal range $(-\pi,\pi]$:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Arg}(-1-i) = -\frac{3\pi}{4} }. $$

An equivalent positive argument is:

$$ \frac{5\pi}{4}. $$

Polar Form

If:

$$ r=|z| $$

and $\theta$ is an argument, then:

$$ \boxed{ z= r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta) }. $$

The modulus and argument therefore act like polar coordinates for a complex number.

Exponential Form

Using Euler's formula:

$$ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta, $$

we obtain:

$$ \boxed{ z=re^{i\theta} }. $$

Argument and Multiplication

If:

$$ z_1=r_1e^{i\theta_1}, \qquad z_2=r_2e^{i\theta_2}, $$

then:

$$ z_1z_2 = r_1r_2 e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)}. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ \arg(z_1z_2) = \arg z_1+\arg z_2 \pmod{2\pi} }. $$

Complex multiplication therefore adds directions while multiplying magnitudes.

Principal-Argument Caution for Products

Principal arguments require adjustment back into the chosen principal interval. Thus:

$$ \operatorname{Arg}(z_1z_2) $$

is congruent modulo $2\pi$ to:

$$ \operatorname{Arg}(z_1) + \operatorname{Arg}(z_2), $$

but need not equal that raw sum numerically.

Argument and Division

For $z_2\ne0$:

$$ \boxed{ \arg\left( \frac{z_1}{z_2} \right) = \arg z_1-\arg z_2 \pmod{2\pi} }. $$

Argument of a Reciprocal

For $z\ne0$:

$$ \boxed{ \arg\left(\frac1z\right) = -\arg z \pmod{2\pi} }. $$

Argument of a Conjugate

Complex conjugation reflects a point across the real axis.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \arg(\overline z) = -\arg z \pmod{2\pi} }. $$

At the level of principal arguments, the negative-real-axis branch convention requires special care.

Powers

If:

$$ z=re^{i\theta}, $$

then:

$$ z^n= r^ne^{in\theta}. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ \arg(z^n) = n\arg z \pmod{2\pi} }. $$

De Moivre's Theorem

$$ \boxed{ (\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^n = \cos(n\theta)+i\sin(n\theta) }. $$

This theorem makes the multiplicative behavior of arguments explicit.

Roots

Suppose:

$$ z=re^{i\theta}. $$

The $n$th roots have arguments:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\theta+2\pi k}{n}, \qquad k=0,1,\ldots,n-1 }. $$

These roots are equally spaced in angle around the complex plane.

Roots of Unity

For:

$$ z^n=1, $$

the arguments are:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{2\pi k}{n}, \qquad k=0,1,\ldots,n-1 }. $$

The roots form the vertices of a regular $n$-gon on the unit circle.

Argument of a Quotient as an Angle between Complex Vectors

The expression:

$$ \arg\left( \frac{z_2-z_0}{z_1-z_0} \right) $$

measures the directed angle from the vector $z_1-z_0$ to the vector $z_2-z_0$.

This makes argument especially useful in complex-plane geometry.

Argument Locus

A condition:

$$ \boxed{ \arg(z-z_0)=\theta } $$

describes a ray beginning at $z_0$ and extending in direction $\theta$, with the starting point itself excluded because the argument of zero is undefined.

Constant Argument

The set:

$$ \arg z=\theta $$

is a ray from the origin.

The set:

$$ \arg z=\theta+2\pi k $$

describes the same geometric ray.

Argument Inequalities

A condition such as:

$$ \alpha< \arg z < \beta $$

describes an angular region or sector of the complex plane, provided the chosen branch and angular interval are understood.

Argument and Rotation

Multiplying by:

$$ e^{i\phi} $$

rotates a complex number through angle $\phi$.

$$ \boxed{ \arg(ze^{i\phi}) = \arg z+\phi \pmod{2\pi} }. $$

Multiplication by i

Since:

$$ \arg i=\frac{\pi}{2}, $$

multiplication by $i$ adds:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\pi}{2} } $$

to the argument, representing a $90^\circ$ counterclockwise rotation.

Multiplication by -1

Since:

$$ \arg(-1)=\pi \pmod{2\pi}, $$

multiplying by $-1$ rotates a complex number through:

$$ \boxed{ \pi = 180^\circ }. $$

Complex Logarithm

The multivalued complex logarithm involves both modulus and argument:

$$ \boxed{ \log z = \ln|z| + i(\arg z) }. $$

Since the argument is multivalued:

$$ \log z = \ln|z| + i(\theta+2\pi k). $$

Principal Logarithm

Choosing a principal argument gives a single-valued principal logarithm:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Log}z = \ln|z| + i\operatorname{Arg}(z) }. $$

Branch Cuts

A continuous single-valued argument cannot be defined globally on all of $\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$ without making a cut. A common choice removes the nonpositive real axis or negative real axis, depending on convention.

This leads to the idea of a branch of the argument function.

Principal Branch

On a domain excluding the chosen branch cut, one may define:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Arg}(z) } $$

continuously using a selected interval of length $2\pi$.

Discontinuity across the Standard Branch Cut

Under the common principal convention:

$$ -\pi< \operatorname{Arg}(z) \le\pi, $$

approaching the negative real axis from above gives values near $\pi$, while approaching from below gives values near $-\pi$.

Thus the principal argument jumps by approximately:

$$ \boxed{ 2\pi }. $$

Argument Is Dimensionless

The argument is an angle rather than a length. In advanced mathematical work it is normally expressed in radians.

$$ \boxed{ \arg z\text{ is usually measured in radians} }. $$

Common Misconception

The argument is not simply:

$$ \arctan\left(\frac{b}{a}\right) $$

in every quadrant.

That expression provides a reference angle but may require a quadrant correction. A quadrant-aware method such as $\operatorname{atan2}(b,a)$ or direct geometric reasoning is safer.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\arg z=\theta$ "An argument of z is theta."
$\operatorname{Arg}(z)=\theta$ "The principal argument of z is theta."
$\arg z=\theta+2\pi k$ "The arguments of z are theta plus two pi k, where k is an integer."
$z=re^{i\theta}$ "Z equals r e to the i theta."
$\arg(z_1z_2)=\arg z_1+\arg z_2\pmod{2\pi}$ "The argument of a product is the sum of the arguments modulo two pi."
$\arg(z_1/z_2)=\arg z_1-\arg z_2\pmod{2\pi}$ "The argument of a quotient is the difference of the arguments modulo two pi."

Lowercase $\arg$ often refers to the multivalued notion of argument, while uppercase $\operatorname{Arg}$ commonly denotes a chosen principal value. Not every author follows this distinction perfectly, so the surrounding definition should control interpretation.

Defense Notation Advice: When writing a complete family of arguments, include $2\pi k$. When writing a principal argument, state or infer the chosen principal interval so the numerical value is unambiguous.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: The argument of a nonzero complex number gives its direction from the positive real axis.
  • General Arguments: $$ \boxed{ \arg z= \theta+2\pi k, \qquad k\in\mathbb Z }. $$
  • Principal Argument: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Arg}(z) } $$ selects one argument from a specified interval.
  • Common Principal Range: $$ \boxed{ -\pi< \operatorname{Arg}(z) \le\pi }. $$
  • Alternative Principal Range: $$ \boxed{ 0\le \operatorname{Arg}(z) <2\pi }. $$
  • Zero: $$ \boxed{ \arg(0)\text{ is undefined} }. $$
  • Rectangular Form: $$ \boxed{ z=a+bi }. $$
  • Tangent Relation: $$ \boxed{ \tan\theta=\frac{b}{a} } $$ when $a\ne0$, with quadrant correction required.
  • Quadrant-Aware Computation: $$ \boxed{ \theta=\operatorname{atan2}(b,a) }. $$
  • Positive Real Axis: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Arg}(a)=0, \qquad a>0 }. $$
  • Negative Real Axis: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Arg}(a)=\pi, \qquad a<0 } $$ under the range $(-\pi,\pi]$.
  • Positive Imaginary Axis: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Arg}(bi)=\frac{\pi}{2}, \qquad b>0 }. $$
  • Negative Imaginary Axis: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Arg}(bi)=-\frac{\pi}{2}, \qquad b<0 }. $$
  • Polar Form: $$ \boxed{ z= r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta) }. $$
  • Exponential Form: $$ \boxed{ z=re^{i\theta} }. $$
  • Modulus-Argument Pair: A nonzero complex number is determined by its modulus together with an argument, modulo full rotations.
  • Product Rule: $$ \boxed{ \arg(z_1z_2) = \arg z_1+\arg z_2 \pmod{2\pi} }. $$
  • Quotient Rule: $$ \boxed{ \arg\left(\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right) = \arg z_1-\arg z_2 \pmod{2\pi} }. $$
  • Reciprocal: $$ \boxed{ \arg(1/z) = -\arg z \pmod{2\pi} }. $$
  • Conjugate: $$ \boxed{ \arg(\overline z) = -\arg z \pmod{2\pi} }. $$
  • Powers: $$ \boxed{ \arg(z^n) = n\arg z \pmod{2\pi} }. $$
  • nth Roots: $$ \boxed{ \arg w_k= \frac{\theta+2\pi k}{n}, \qquad k=0,\ldots,n-1 }. $$
  • Roots of Unity: $$ \boxed{ \arg z_k= \frac{2\pi k}{n} }. $$
  • Rotation: Multiplication by $e^{i\phi}$ adds $\phi$ to the argument.
  • Multiplication by i: $$ \boxed{ \arg(iz) = \arg z+\frac{\pi}{2} \pmod{2\pi} }. $$
  • Multiplication by -1: $$ \boxed{ \arg(-z) = \arg z+\pi \pmod{2\pi} }. $$
  • Angle between Complex Vectors: $$ \boxed{ \arg\left( \frac{z_2-z_0}{z_1-z_0} \right) } $$ gives a directed angle between the corresponding vectors.
  • Ray Locus: $$ \boxed{ \arg(z-z_0)=\theta } $$ describes a ray from $z_0$.
  • Angular Region: Inequalities involving argument describe sectors or wedges of the complex plane.
  • Complex Logarithm: $$ \boxed{ \log z= \ln|z|+i(\arg z) }. $$
  • Principal Logarithm: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Log}z= \ln|z|+ i\operatorname{Arg}(z) }. $$
  • Branch: A branch of argument selects a continuous determination on a domain from which an appropriate curve or ray has been removed.
  • Branch-Cut Caution: Principal argument typically has a discontinuity across its chosen branch cut.
  • Radians: Advanced complex-number work normally expresses arguments in radians.
  • Common Error: $$ \boxed{ \arg z \ne \arctan\left(\frac{b}{a}\right) } $$ without regard to quadrant.
  • Common Error: An argument is not unique; adding any integer multiple of $2\pi$ preserves the same direction.
  • Common Error: The argument of zero is undefined.
  • Terminological Contrast: Modulus measures how far a complex number lies from the origin. Argument measures its direction. Principal argument selects one representative direction angle. Together, modulus and argument provide the polar description of a nonzero complex number.
