nth Root

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

The nth root of a number or expression is a quantity that produces the original value when raised to the $n$th power. If:

$$ x^n=a, $$

then $x$ is an $n$th root of $a$.

$$ \boxed{ x^n=a \Longrightarrow x\text{ is an }n\text{th root of }a } $$

The word root comes from the idea of a foundational source from which something grows. In arithmetic and algebra, a root is a number from which a given power can be produced.

The notation:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[n]{a} } $$

uses the radical symbol together with the index $n$.

The quantity beneath the radical sign is called the radicand.

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[n]{\text{radicand}} } $$

When the index is $2$, it is conventionally omitted:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[2]{a} = \sqrt{a} }. $$

Thus the phrase nth root generalizes square roots, cube roots, fourth roots, fifth roots, and all higher-index roots.

Operational Nuance

Defining Relationship

A number $x$ is an $n$th root of $a$ when:

$$ \boxed{ x^n=a }. $$

Radical Notation

The principal $n$th-root expression is written:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[n]{a} }. $$

Index

In:

$$ \sqrt[n]{a}, $$

$n$ is the index of the radical.

Radicand

The quantity $a$ is the radicand.

Square Root

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt{a} = \sqrt[2]{a} }. $$

Cube Root

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[3]{a} }. $$

Fourth Root

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[4]{a} }. $$

Perfect nth Power

If:

$$ a=b^n, $$

then $a$ is an exact $n$th power of $b$.

Example

$$ 2^5=32. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[5]{32}=2 }. $$

Even Index over the Reals

If $n$ is even, a real principal $n$th root requires a nonnegative radicand:

$$ \boxed{ a\geq0 }. $$

Example

$$ \sqrt[4]{16}=2. $$

Negative Radicand with Even Index

Over the real numbers:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[4]{-16} \notin\mathbb{R} }. $$

Odd Index over the Reals

If $n$ is odd, every real number has exactly one real $n$th root.

$$ \boxed{ n\text{ odd} \Longrightarrow \sqrt[n]{a}\in\mathbb{R} \text{ for every }a\in\mathbb{R} }. $$

Negative Radicand with Odd Index

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[3]{-8} = -2 }. $$

Principal Root

Radical notation usually denotes a selected principal root, not the full set of all roots.

Principal Even Root

For real $a\geq0$ and even $n$:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[n]{a}\geq0 }. $$

Important Square-Root Example

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt{9}=3 } $$

even though both $3$ and $-3$ satisfy:

$$ x^2=9. $$

Radical Expression vs. Equation Solutions

The radical expression:

$$ \sqrt{9} $$

denotes the principal value $3$.

The equation:

$$ x^2=9 $$

has two real solutions:

$$ \boxed{ x=\pm3 }. $$

General Even-Power Equation

If $n$ is even and $a>0$, then:

$$ x^n=a $$

has two real solutions:

$$ \boxed{ x=\pm\sqrt[n]{a} }. $$

General Odd-Power Equation

If $n$ is odd, then:

$$ x^n=a $$

has exactly one real solution:

$$ \boxed{ x=\sqrt[n]{a} }. $$

Zero

For every positive integer $n$:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[n]{0}=0 }. $$

One

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[n]{1}=1 } $$

for every positive integer $n$ under the usual principal real-root convention.

Rational Exponent Form

nth roots can be expressed using rational exponents:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[n]{a} = a^{1/n} } $$

where the expression is defined under the chosen number system and convention.

General Rational Exponent

$$ \boxed{ a^{m/n} = \left( \sqrt[n]{a} \right)^m } $$

under appropriate domain restrictions.

Equivalent Form

$$ \boxed{ a^{m/n} = \sqrt[n]{a^m} } $$

when the expressions are consistently defined.

Inverse Relationship with Powers

nth-root operations and $n$th powers are inverse operations on appropriately restricted domains.

Odd Index Identity

For odd positive integer $n$ and real $x$:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[n]{x^n}=x }. $$

Even Index Identity

For even positive integer $n$ and real $x$:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[n]{x^n} = |x| }. $$

Why Absolute Value Appears

The principal even root is defined to be nonnegative. Therefore:

$$ \sqrt{x^2} = |x|, $$

not necessarily $x$.

Example

If $x=-5$, then:

$$ \sqrt{x^2} = \sqrt{25} = 5 = |-5|. $$

Power after Principal Root

For appropriate real $a$:

$$ \boxed{ \left( \sqrt[n]{a} \right)^n = a }. $$

Product Rule

Under appropriate real-domain conditions:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[n]{ab} = \sqrt[n]{a}\sqrt[n]{b} }. $$

Even-Index Product Restrictions

For real even roots, a safe elementary version requires:

$$ \boxed{ a\geq0, \qquad b\geq0 }. $$

Odd-Index Product Rule

For odd $n$, the real product rule extends naturally to all real $a$ and $b$.

Quotient Rule

Under appropriate conditions:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[n]{\frac{a}{b}} = \frac{\sqrt[n]{a}}{\sqrt[n]{b}} } $$

with $b\neq0$ and any additional real-root restrictions enforced.

Simplifying Perfect nth-Power Factors

If the radicand contains a perfect $n$th-power factor:

$$ \sqrt[n]{a^n b}, $$

then the factor $a^n$ can be extracted carefully.

Odd Index Extraction

For odd $n$ and real $a$:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[n]{a^n b} = a\sqrt[n]{b} }. $$

Even Index Extraction

For even $n$:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[n]{a^n b} = |a|\sqrt[n]{b} } $$

under suitable real-domain conditions.

Example

$$ \sqrt{72} = \sqrt{36\cdot2} = \boxed{ 6\sqrt{2} }. $$

Cube-Root Example

$$ \sqrt[3]{54} = \sqrt[3]{27\cdot2} = \boxed{ 3\sqrt[3]{2} }. $$

Nested Roots

Under appropriate domain conditions:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[m]{\sqrt[n]{a}} = a^{1/(mn)} }. $$

Example

$$ \sqrt{\sqrt[3]{64}} = \sqrt{4} = 2. $$

Root of a Reciprocal

For nonzero appropriate $a$:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[n]{\frac{1}{a}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt[n]{a}} }. $$

Negative nth-Root Exponents

For nonzero appropriate $a$:

$$ \boxed{ a^{-1/n} = \frac{1}{\sqrt[n]{a}} }. $$

nth Roots of Fractions

For example:

$$ \sqrt[3]{\frac{8}{125}} = \frac{\sqrt[3]{8}}{\sqrt[3]{125}} = \boxed{ \frac{2}{5} }. $$

Irrational nth Roots

Not every root simplifies to a rational number.

For example:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[3]{2} } $$

is irrational.

nth-Root Function

The function:

$$ f(x)=\sqrt[n]{x} $$

behaves differently according to whether $n$ is even or odd.

Even nth-Root Function

For even $n$:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f) = [0,\infty) } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Ran}(f) = [0,\infty) }. $$

Odd nth-Root Function

For odd $n$:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f) = \mathbb{R} } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Ran}(f) = \mathbb{R} }. $$

Monotonicity

The real principal $n$th-root function is increasing on its natural real domain.

Inverse-Function Connection

For odd $n$:

$$ f(x)=x^n $$

is one-to-one on all of $\mathbb{R}$, and:

$$ \boxed{ f^{-1}(x)=\sqrt[n]{x} }. $$

Even-Power Restriction

For even $n$, the function $x^n$ is not one-to-one on all real numbers.

Restricting to:

$$ \boxed{ [0,\infty) } $$

makes the principal $n$th-root function its inverse.

Solving Radical Equations

An equation such as:

$$ \sqrt[n]{f(x)}=g(x) $$

is often approached by raising both sides to the $n$th power.

Powering Both Sides

$$ \left( \sqrt[n]{f(x)} \right)^n = g(x)^n. $$

Extraneous-Solution Risk

When $n$ is even, raising both sides to an even power is not a reversible operation without additional sign information. Candidate solutions must be checked in the original equation.

Example

Suppose:

$$ \sqrt{x}=x-2. $$

Squaring gives:

$$ x=(x-2)^2, $$

but any resulting candidates must satisfy the original requirements:

$$ \boxed{ x\geq0 \quad\text{and}\quad x-2\geq0 }. $$

Complex nth Roots

Every nonzero complex number has exactly $n$ distinct $n$th roots.

Polar Form

Let:

$$ z=re^{i\theta}, \qquad r>0. $$

Then its $n$th roots are:

$$ \boxed{ w_k = r^{1/n} e^{i(\theta+2\pi k)/n}, \qquad k=0,1,\ldots,n-1 }. $$

Equal Angular Spacing

The $n$ complex roots are equally spaced around a circle of radius:

$$ \boxed{ r^{1/n} }. $$

Angular Separation

$$ \boxed{ \frac{2\pi}{n} }. $$

Example: Fourth Roots of $1$

Solve:

$$ z^4=1. $$

The roots are:

$$ \boxed{ 1,\ i,\ -1,\ -i }. $$

Roots of Unity

The solutions of:

$$ z^n=1 $$

are the $n$th roots of unity:

$$ \boxed{ e^{2\pi i k/n}, \qquad k=0,1,\ldots,n-1 }. $$

Complex Root Geometry

nth roots of a nonzero complex number form the vertices of a regular $n$-gon centered at the origin.

Multiplicity of Roots of $z^n-a$

If $a\neq0$, the polynomial:

$$ z^n-a $$

has $n$ distinct complex roots because its derivative:

$$ nz^{n-1} $$

is nonzero at each nonzero root.

Zero as a Repeated Root

For:

$$ z^n=0, $$

zero is the only root, with multiplicity $n$ as a root of the polynomial $z^n$.

nth Root vs. Radical

A root is a mathematical value satisfying a power equation. A radical is the notation or expression used to denote a selected root.

nth Root vs. Reciprocal

The notation:

$$ a^{1/n} $$

means an $n$th root, while:

$$ a^{-1} $$

means the multiplicative inverse.

nth Root vs. $1/n$ of a Number

The $n$th root of $a$ is not:

$$ \frac{a}{n}. $$

Rather:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[n]{a} = a^{1/n} }. $$

nth Root vs. Dividing an Exponent

For appropriate expressions:

$$ \sqrt[n]{a^m} = a^{m/n}, $$

but exponent manipulation must preserve sign and domain conditions, especially for even roots.

Common Error: Forgetting the Principal-Root Convention

Incorrect:

$$ \sqrt{25}=\pm5. $$

Correct:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt{25}=5 }. $$

Common Error: Forgetting Both Solutions to an Even-Power Equation

Although:

$$ \sqrt{25}=5, $$

the equation:

$$ x^2=25 $$

has:

$$ \boxed{ x=\pm5 }. $$

Common Error: Treating Even and Odd Roots Identically

Odd roots permit negative real radicands; even roots do not.

Common Error: Writing $\sqrt{x^2}=x$ for All Real $x$

Correct:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt{x^2}=|x| }. $$

Common Error: Ignoring Absolute Value in Even-Index Simplification

For even $n$:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[n]{x^n}=|x| }. $$

Common Error: Assuming $\sqrt[n]{a+b}$ Distributes

In general:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[n]{a+b} \neq \sqrt[n]{a} + \sqrt[n]{b} }. $$

Common Error: Assuming $\sqrt[n]{a-b}$ Distributes

In general:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[n]{a-b} \neq \sqrt[n]{a} - \sqrt[n]{b} }. $$

Common Error: Confusing Root Index with an Exponent on the Radicand

In:

$$ \sqrt[5]{x}, $$

$5$ is the radical index, not an exponent applied directly to $x$.

Common Error: Treating the Square-Root Index as Missing Information

In ordinary notation:

$$ \sqrt{x} $$

means:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[2]{x} }. $$

Common Error: Dividing by the Index

Incorrect:

$$ \sqrt[3]{27}=\frac{27}{3}. $$

Correct:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt[3]{27}=3 }. $$

Common Error: Ignoring Domain Restrictions

Over the real numbers, even-root radicands must be nonnegative.

Common Error: Ignoring Denominator Restrictions

In:

$$ \frac{1}{\sqrt[n]{f(x)}}, $$

the denominator must also be nonzero.

Common Error: Assuming Radical Product Rules Have No Restrictions

Real even-root identities require attention to the signs and domains of the radicands.

Common Error: Confusing a Root with a Solution Set

Radical notation commonly selects one principal value, while a power equation may have several roots.

Common Error: Assuming a Complex Number Has Only One nth Root

Every nonzero complex number has exactly $n$ distinct complex $n$th roots.

Common Error: Forgetting Angular Periodicity in Complex Roots

Complex roots require:

$$ \theta+2\pi k $$

before dividing the angle by $n$.

Common Error: Repeating Equivalent Complex Roots Indefinitely

Only:

$$ \boxed{ k=0,1,\ldots,n-1 } $$

is needed to obtain all distinct $n$th roots.

Common Misconception: An $n$th root is not simply "the opposite of an exponent" without qualification. It is defined through the equation $x^n=a$. Radical notation $\sqrt[n]{a}$ normally denotes a principal root, while the equation $x^n=a$ may have multiple solutions. Even roots require special sign and domain care over the reals, while nonzero complex numbers have exactly $n$ distinct $n$th roots.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\sqrt[n]{a}$ "The nth root of a."
$\sqrt{a}$ "The square root of a," meaning the principal square root.
$\sqrt[3]{a}$ "The cube root of a."
$a^{1/n}$ "A to the one-over-n power," or "the nth root of a."
$x^n=a$ "X to the nth power equals a."
$x=\pm\sqrt[n]{a}$ "X equals plus or minus the nth root of a," for appropriate even-power equations.
$w_k=r^{1/n}e^{i(\theta+2\pi k)/n}$ "The kth complex nth root of z in polar form."

The ordinal is usually spoken as "nth root", with $n$ replaced by the specific index when known: square root, cube root, fourth root, fifth root, and so on.

The notation:

$$ \sqrt[n]{a} $$

should not be read as "root n of a." The index specifies which power operation is being reversed.

In written mathematics, the square-root index $2$ is normally omitted:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt{a} = \sqrt[2]{a} }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish the principal radical $\sqrt[n]{a}$ from the full solution set of $x^n=a$. Use $\pm$ when solving an even-power equation over the reals, not inside the definition of the principal radical itself. For even roots, preserve absolute values in identities such as $\sqrt[n]{x^n}=|x|$. State the ambient number system when negative radicands or complex roots are relevant.

  • Radical Notation: $\sqrt[n]{a}$.
  • Index: $n$ identifies which root is taken.
  • Radicand: Quantity beneath the radical symbol.
  • Square Root: $n=2$.
  • Cube Root: $n=3$.
  • Fourth Root: $n=4$.
  • Perfect nth Power: Number or expression of form $b^n$.
  • Principal Root: Radical notation conventionally selects a distinguished root.
  • Principal Even Root: Nonnegative over the real numbers.
  • Even Index: Real radicand must be nonnegative.
  • Odd Index: Every real radicand is permitted.
  • Negative Even Radicand: No real principal root.
  • Negative Odd Radicand: Has a negative real root.
  • Zero: $\sqrt[n]{0}=0$.
  • One: Principal root satisfies $\sqrt[n]{1}=1$.
  • Rational Exponent: $\sqrt[n]{a}=a^{1/n}$ under appropriate conventions.
  • General Rational Power: $a^{m/n}=(\sqrt[n]{a})^m$ when defined.
  • Odd-Power Inverse: $\sqrt[n]{x^n}=x$ for odd $n$ over the reals.
  • Even-Power Inverse: $\sqrt[n]{x^n}=|x|$ for even $n$ over the reals.
  • Power after Root: $(\sqrt[n]{a})^n=a$ where defined.
  • Product Rule: $\sqrt[n]{ab}=\sqrt[n]{a}\sqrt[n]{b}$ under appropriate domain conditions.
  • Quotient Rule: $\sqrt[n]{a/b}=\sqrt[n]{a}/\sqrt[n]{b}$ under appropriate conditions.
  • Perfect-Power Extraction: nth powers can be removed from radicals.
  • Even-Index Extraction: Absolute value may be required.
  • Odd-Index Extraction: Sign is preserved directly.
  • Nested Roots: Correspond to multiplied root indices under suitable conditions.
  • Reciprocal Root: $\sqrt[n]{1/a}=1/\sqrt[n]{a}$ when defined and nonzero.
  • Negative Fractional Exponent: $a^{-1/n}=1/\sqrt[n]{a}$.
  • Irrational Root: Many nth roots are irrational.
  • Even nth-Root Function: Natural real domain and range are $[0,\infty)$.
  • Odd nth-Root Function: Natural real domain and range are $\mathbb{R}$.
  • Monotonicity: Principal real nth-root function is increasing on its natural domain.
  • Inverse Function: nth root reverses the nth-power function on an appropriate restricted domain.
  • Even-Power Restriction: $x^n$ must be restricted to one side of zero to obtain a real inverse function.
  • Odd-Power Function: One-to-one on all real numbers.
  • Radical Equations: Often solved by raising both sides to the index power.
  • Extraneous Solutions: Even-power transformations require solution checking.
  • Complex Roots: Every nonzero complex number has exactly $n$ distinct nth roots.
  • Complex Polar Formula: $w_k=r^{1/n}e^{i(\theta+2\pi k)/n}$.
  • Complex Root Radius: $r^{1/n}$.
  • Complex Angular Separation: $2\pi/n$.
  • Roots of Unity: Solutions of $z^n=1$.
  • Roots-of-Unity Formula: $e^{2\pi i k/n}$.
  • Geometric Complex Interpretation: Roots form a regular $n$-gon around the origin.
  • Zero in Complex Polynomial: $z^n$ has zero as a root of multiplicity $n$.
  • Root vs. Radical: Root is the value; radical is notation selecting a root.
  • Root vs. Reciprocal: $a^{1/n}$ and $a^{-1}$ represent different operations.
  • Root vs. Division: nth root is not division by $n$.

  • Common Error: Forgetting $\pm$ when solving even-power equations.
  • Common Error: Treating even and odd root domains identically.
  • Common Error: Writing $\sqrt{x^2}=x$ for all real $x$.
  • Common Error: Omitting absolute value when simplifying even roots of even powers.
  • Common Error: Distributing radicals over addition.
  • Common Error: Distributing radicals over subtraction.
  • Common Error: Confusing the radical index with an exponent.
  • Common Error: Forgetting that an omitted radical index means $2$.
  • Common Error: Dividing the radicand by the index instead of taking a root.
  • Common Error: Ignoring real-domain restrictions.
  • Common Error: Ignoring denominator restrictions involving roots.
  • Common Error: Applying radical product rules without checking domains.
  • Common Error: Confusing a principal radical with the full solution set of a power equation.
