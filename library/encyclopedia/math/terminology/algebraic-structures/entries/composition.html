Composition

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

In mathematics, composition is the process of combining operations, functions, mappings, relations, or transformations so that the output of one becomes the input of another.

For functions $f$ and $g$, the composition of $g$ with $f$ is written:

$$ \boxed{ g\circ f } $$

and is defined by:

$$ \boxed{ (g\circ f)(x)=g(f(x)) }. $$

The word composition comes from Latin roots associated with placing or putting things together. The mathematical meaning preserves that idea: several processes are assembled into a single process.

$$ \boxed{ x \xrightarrow{f} f(x) \xrightarrow{g} g(f(x)) }. $$

Composition is therefore not merely a notation for combining formulas. It describes a structural principle in which one operation acts on the result produced by another.

Operational Nuance

Function Composition

Suppose:

$$ f:A\to B $$

and:

$$ g:B\to C. $$

Their composition is the function:

$$ \boxed{ g\circ f:A\to C } $$

defined by:

$$ \boxed{ (g\circ f)(x)=g(f(x)) }. $$

The Rightmost Function Acts First

In:

$$ g\circ f, $$

the function $f$ is applied first and $g$ second.

$$ \boxed{ g\circ f = \text{first }f,\text{ then }g }. $$

This convention mirrors nested notation:

$$ g(f(x)). $$

Example

Let:

$$ f(x)=x+3, \qquad g(x)=x^2. $$

Then:

$$ (g\circ f)(x) = g(x+3) = (x+3)^2. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ (g\circ f)(x)=(x+3)^2 }. $$

Reverse Composition

Reversing the order gives:

$$ (f\circ g)(x) = f(x^2) = x^2+3. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ g\circ f \ne f\circ g \quad\text{in general} }. $$

Composition Is Generally Noncommutative

Function composition depends on order. Even when both $g\circ f$ and $f\circ g$ are defined, they need not produce the same result.

$$ \boxed{ (g\circ f)(x) \ne (f\circ g)(x) \quad\text{in general} }. $$

Domain Compatibility

Composition is defined only where the output of the inner function belongs to the domain of the outer function.

For:

$$ (g\circ f)(x), $$

one must have:

$$ \boxed{ x\in\operatorname{Dom}(f) \quad\text{and}\quad f(x)\in\operatorname{Dom}(g) }. $$

Domain of a Composite Function

More precisely:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(g\circ f) = \{x\in\operatorname{Dom}(f): f(x)\in\operatorname{Dom}(g)\} }. $$

Example with a Domain Restriction

Let:

$$ f(x)=x-4, \qquad g(x)=\sqrt{x}. $$

Then:

$$ (g\circ f)(x)=\sqrt{x-4}. $$

Over the real numbers, this requires:

$$ x-4\ge0. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(g\circ f) = [4,\infty) }. $$

Composition of Three Functions

For functions $f$, $g$, and $h$:

$$ \boxed{ (h\circ g\circ f)(x) = h(g(f(x))) }. $$

The operations proceed from right to left:

$$ \boxed{ x \xrightarrow{f} f(x) \xrightarrow{g} g(f(x)) \xrightarrow{h} h(g(f(x))) }. $$

Associativity

Function composition is associative whenever the relevant compositions are defined:

$$ \boxed{ h\circ(g\circ f) = (h\circ g)\circ f }. $$

Both sides send $x$ to:

$$ h(g(f(x))). $$

Associative Does Not Mean Commutative

Associativity concerns grouping:

$$ h\circ(g\circ f) = (h\circ g)\circ f, $$

while commutativity would concern order:

$$ g\circ f=f\circ g. $$

The first is generally true; the second generally is not.

Identity Function

The identity function on a set $A$ is:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{id}_A(x)=x }. $$

It acts as an identity element for composition:

$$ \boxed{ f\circ\operatorname{id}_A=f } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{id}_B\circ f=f } $$

for $f:A\to B$.

Inverse Functions and Composition

If $f:A\to B$ is bijective, its inverse:

$$ f^{-1}:B\to A $$

satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ f^{-1}\circ f = \operatorname{id}_A } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ f\circ f^{-1} = \operatorname{id}_B }. $$

Composition Helps Define Invertibility

The inverse of a function is therefore defined operationally through composition: composing a function with its inverse restores the original input.

$$ \boxed{ x \xrightarrow{f} f(x) \xrightarrow{f^{-1}} x }. $$

Inverse of a Composition

If $f$ and $g$ are invertible and their compositions are compatible, then:

$$ \boxed{ (g\circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1}\circ g^{-1} }. $$

The order reverses because the last operation performed must be undone first.

Example of Reversing the Order

Suppose $f$ adds $3$ and $g$ squares a suitable restricted input. The composition:

$$ g\circ f $$

first adds $3$ and then squares. To reverse this process, one first undoes the squaring and then subtracts $3$.

Repeated Composition

A function may be composed with itself repeatedly.

One common notation is:

$$ \boxed{ f^{\circ n} } $$

for the $n$-fold iterate:

$$ f^{\circ n} = \underbrace{ f\circ f\circ\cdots\circ f }_{n\text{ times}}. $$

Iteration

Repeated composition is called iteration.

$$ \boxed{ x_{n+1}=f(x_n) } $$

generates a sequence by repeatedly applying the same function.

Composition versus Multiplication

The notation:

$$ g\circ f $$

does not mean:

$$ gf. $$

For functions:

$$ \boxed{ (g\circ f)(x)=g(f(x)) } $$

while ordinary pointwise multiplication means:

$$ \boxed{ (gf)(x)=g(x)f(x) }. $$

Composition versus Addition of Functions

Likewise:

$$ (f+g)(x)=f(x)+g(x) $$

is fundamentally different from:

$$ (f\circ g)(x)=f(g(x)). $$

Composite Function versus Composition

Composition names the operation. A composite function is the function produced by that operation.

$$ \boxed{ \text{composition} \longrightarrow \text{composite function} }. $$

Composition of Mappings

The term is not limited to formulas involving real variables. Any compatible mappings may be composed.

$$ A\xrightarrow{f}B\xrightarrow{g}C $$

gives:

$$ \boxed{ A\xrightarrow{g\circ f}C }. $$

Composition of Transformations

Geometric transformations can be composed by applying them successively.

If $R$ is a rotation and $T$ is a translation, then:

$$ T\circ R $$

means rotate first and translate second.

Order Matters for Transformations

In general:

$$ \boxed{ T\circ R \ne R\circ T }. $$

A translation followed by a rotation can place a figure differently from a rotation followed by the same translation.

Composite Transformations

A sequence of geometric transformations may itself be regarded as one transformation.

$$ \boxed{ T_n\circ\cdots\circ T_2\circ T_1 }. $$

Composition of Linear Transformations

Suppose:

$$ T:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^p $$

and:

$$ S:\mathbb R^p\to\mathbb R^m. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ S\circ T: \mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m }. $$

Matrix Representation

If $T$ is represented by matrix $A$ and $S$ by matrix $B$, then:

$$ \boxed{ S\circ T \longleftrightarrow BA }. $$

The order agrees with function composition: $A$ acts first, followed by $B$.

Matrix Dimensions Reflect Composition

If:

$$ A\text{ is }p\times n $$

and:

$$ B\text{ is }m\times p, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ BA \text{ is }m\times n }. $$

The matching inner dimension $p$ expresses the compatibility of the intermediate space.

Composition of Relations

Binary relations may also be composed.

If $R\subseteq A\times B$ and $S\subseteq B\times C$, then one standard convention defines:

$$ \boxed{ S\circ R = \{(a,c): \exists b\in B \text{ with } (a,b)\in R \text{ and } (b,c)\in S\} }. $$

As with functions, the relation $R$ is traversed first and $S$ second.

Relation Composition and Functions

Functions can be viewed as special kinds of relations, so ordinary function composition fits naturally within the broader idea of relation composition.

Composition in Permutations

Permutations are bijections from a set to itself. They are combined by composition.

$$ \boxed{ \sigma\circ\tau } $$

means apply $\tau$ first and then $\sigma$ under the usual right-to-left convention.

Permutation Groups

Composition of permutations is associative, has an identity permutation, and every permutation has an inverse. These properties form the foundation of symmetric groups.

$$ \boxed{ S_n = \text{permutations of }n\text{ objects under composition} }. $$

Composition in Category Theory

Composition is one of the central primitive ideas of category theory.

Given morphisms:

$$ A\xrightarrow{f}B \qquad B\xrightarrow{g}C, $$

category theory requires a composite morphism:

$$ \boxed{ g\circ f:A\to C }. $$

Composition must satisfy associativity and interact with identity morphisms.

Composition as Chaining

A useful conceptual interpretation is to think of composition as chaining compatible processes.

$$ \boxed{ \text{input} \to \text{process 1} \to \text{intermediate result} \to \text{process 2} \to \text{output} }. $$

This interpretation applies to functions, transformations, matrices, algorithms, and many other mathematical structures.

Decomposing a Composite Function

A formula may sometimes be interpreted as the composition of simpler functions.

For example:

$$ h(x)=\sqrt{3x+7}. $$

Define:

$$ f(x)=3x+7, \qquad g(u)=\sqrt{u}. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ h=g\circ f }. $$

Composition and the Chain Rule

In calculus, the derivative of a composite function is governed by the chain rule.

$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}g(f(x)) = g'(f(x))f'(x) }. $$

Thus function composition is the structural reason the chain rule is needed.

Composition and Substitution

Evaluating:

$$ g(f(x)) $$

involves substituting the entire expression $f(x)$ into the input position of $g$.

If:

$$ g(u)=u^2+1, $$

then:

$$ g(f(x)) = [f(x)]^2+1. $$

Parentheses are therefore essential when the substituted expression contains more than one term.

Common Misconception: Read Composition Left to Right

Under the standard convention:

$$ g\circ f $$

means apply $f$ first and $g$ second.

$$ \boxed{ g\circ f \Rightarrow f\text{ first} }. $$

Common Misconception: Composition Means Multiplication

The composition symbol $\circ$ does not mean multiplication.

$$ \boxed{ g(f(x)) \ne g(x)f(x) \quad\text{in general} }. $$

Common Misconception: Composition Is Always Defined

Even when $f$ and $g$ are individually valid functions, $g\circ f$ may be undefined for some inputs or may fail to be globally composable if their domains and codomains do not match appropriately.

Common Misconception: Composition Is Commutative

In most settings:

$$ \boxed{ g\circ f \ne f\circ g }. $$

Common Misconception: f² Always Means f Composed with Itself

The notation $f^2$ is potentially ambiguous. Depending on context, it may mean:

  • the pointwise square $[f(x)]^2$, or
  • the second iterate $f\circ f$.

To remove ambiguity, iteration may be written:

$$ \boxed{ f^{\circ2} = f\circ f }. $$

Common Misconception: The Inverse of a Composition Keeps the Same Order

In fact:

$$ \boxed{ (g\circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1}\circ g^{-1} }. $$

Undoing a sequence reverses its order.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$g\circ f$ "g composed with f" or "g after f."
$(g\circ f)(x)=g(f(x))$ "g composed with f of x equals g of f of x."
$A\xrightarrow{f}B\xrightarrow{g}C$ "f maps A to B, and g maps B to C."
$g\circ f:A\to C$ "g composed with f maps A to C."
$h\circ g\circ f$ "h composed with g composed with f."
$f^{-1}\circ f=\operatorname{id}$ "f inverse composed with f equals the identity."
$f^{\circ n}$ "The n-fold iterate of f."

The phrase g composed with f corresponds to $g\circ f$, but the operational order is $f$ first and then $g$. Another useful spoken form is g after f, which makes the chronology more explicit.

The circle symbol $\circ$ is called the composition symbol in this setting. Its meaning depends on the mathematical objects involved; it may indicate composition of functions, mappings, relations, transformations, or morphisms.

Defense Notation Advice: Preserve the right-to-left operational order in $g\circ f$. Distinguish composition from pointwise multiplication, and use parentheses when substituting a multi-term expression into another function. Check domains before declaring a composition defined. For repeated composition, prefer $f^{\circ n}$ when $f^n$ might be confused with pointwise exponentiation.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: Composition combines compatible processes so that the output of one becomes the input of another.
  • Function Form: $$ \boxed{ (g\circ f)(x)=g(f(x)) }. $$
  • Operational Order: In $g\circ f$, $f$ acts first and $g$ second.
  • Mapping Form: $$ \boxed{ A\xrightarrow{f}B\xrightarrow{g}C \Rightarrow g\circ f:A\to C }. $$
  • Domain Requirement: $f(x)$ must lie in the domain of $g$ wherever $g\circ f$ is defined.
  • Associativity: $$ \boxed{ h\circ(g\circ f) = (h\circ g)\circ f }. $$
  • Noncommutativity: $$ \boxed{ g\circ f \ne f\circ g \quad\text{in general} }. $$
  • Identity: $$ \boxed{ f\circ\operatorname{id} = \operatorname{id}\circ f = f } $$ with the appropriate identity maps.
  • Inverse: $$ \boxed{ f^{-1}\circ f = \operatorname{id} } $$ for an invertible function on the appropriate domain.
  • Inverse of a Composition: $$ \boxed{ (g\circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1}\circ g^{-1} }. $$
  • Iteration: Repeated composition of a function with itself.
  • Iterate Notation: $$ \boxed{ f^{\circ n} }. $$
  • Transformations: Geometric transformations compose by being applied successively.
  • Linear Algebra: Composition of linear transformations corresponds to matrix multiplication.
  • Matrix Order: If $A$ represents $T$ and $B$ represents $S$, then $S\circ T$ is represented by $BA$.
  • Relations: Relations compose through intermediate related elements.
  • Permutations: Permutations form groups under composition.
  • Category Theory: Composition of morphisms is one of the fundamental structural operations.
  • Calculus: The chain rule differentiates composite functions.
  • Substitution: Evaluating a composition requires substituting the inner output into the outer function.
  • Composite Function: The result produced by function composition.
  • Common Error: Do not read $g\circ f$ as applying $g$ first.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse composition with multiplication.
  • Common Error: Do not assume composition is commutative.
  • Common Error: Do not ignore the domain of the outer function.
  • Common Error: Do not assume $f^2$ unambiguously means $f\circ f$.
  • Terminological Contrast: Composition is the operation of chaining compatible functions, mappings, relations, or transformations. A composite function is a function produced by composition. Iteration is repeated self-composition. Function multiplication multiplies outputs pointwise and is not the same as composition. An identity function leaves inputs unchanged under composition. An inverse function reverses a function through composition with the appropriate identity map.
