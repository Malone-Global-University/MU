Complex Numbers

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

A complex number is a number that can be written in the form:

$$ \boxed{ z=a+bi } $$

where $a$ and $b$ are real numbers and $i$ is the imaginary unit satisfying:

$$ \boxed{ i^2=-1 }. $$

The collection of all complex numbers is denoted:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb C }. $$

The term complex number emerged historically as mathematicians developed a number system capable of incorporating both ordinary real quantities and expressions involving square roots of negative numbers. The word complex reflects the combined structure of a real component and an imaginary component.

$$ \boxed{ \text{complex number} = \text{real part} + \text{imaginary part} }. $$

The adjective imaginary is historical terminology. Imaginary and complex numbers are fully defined mathematical objects and are no less legitimate than real numbers.

Complex numbers extend the real number system in a way that makes every nonconstant polynomial with complex coefficients factor completely into linear factors over $\mathbb C$.

Operational Nuance

Standard Form

The standard, rectangular, or Cartesian form of a complex number is:

$$ \boxed{ z=a+bi }. $$

Here $a$ and $b$ are real numbers.

Real Part

For:

$$ z=a+bi, $$

the real part is:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Re}(z)=a }. $$

Imaginary Part

The imaginary part is:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Im}(z)=b }. $$

Under the standard convention, the imaginary part is the real coefficient $b$, not the entire term $bi$.

The Imaginary Unit

The imaginary unit $i$ is defined by:

$$ \boxed{ i^2=-1 }. $$

Consequently:

$$ i=\sqrt{-1} $$

is useful as an elementary mnemonic, although in rigorous constructions $i$ is defined as a number satisfying $i^2=-1$ rather than obtained by extending every real square-root rule without qualification.

Real Numbers Are Complex Numbers

Every real number can be written with imaginary part zero:

$$ a=a+0i. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb R\subset\mathbb C }. $$

Pure Imaginary Numbers

A number whose real part is zero has the form:

$$ z=bi. $$

A nonzero number of this form is commonly called a pure imaginary number.

Examples

$$ 3+4i,\qquad -2+7i,\qquad 5,\qquad -6i $$

are all complex numbers.

Equality of Complex Numbers

Two complex numbers are equal exactly when their corresponding real and imaginary parts are equal:

$$ \boxed{ a+bi=c+di \iff a=c \text{ and } b=d }. $$

Addition

Complex numbers are added componentwise:

$$ \boxed{ (a+bi)+(c+di) = (a+c)+(b+d)i }. $$

Example: Addition

$$ (3+2i)+(5-7i) = 8-5i. $$

Subtraction

$$ \boxed{ (a+bi)-(c+di) = (a-c)+(b-d)i }. $$

Multiplication

Complex multiplication uses distributivity together with $i^2=-1$:

$$ \boxed{ (a+bi)(c+di) = (ac-bd)+(ad+bc)i }. $$

Example: Multiplication

Consider:

$$ (2+3i)(4-i). $$

Expanding:

$$ 8-2i+12i-3i^2 = 8+10i+3. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ (2+3i)(4-i)=11+10i }. $$

Powers of i

Integer powers of $i$ repeat with period four:

$$ i^0=1,\qquad i^1=i,\qquad i^2=-1,\qquad i^3=-i,\qquad i^4=1. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ i^{n+4}=i^n }. $$

Complex Conjugate

If:

$$ z=a+bi, $$

its complex conjugate is:

$$ \boxed{ \overline z=a-bi }. $$

Complex conjugation reflects a complex number across the real axis in the complex plane.

Conjugate Product

Multiplying a complex number by its conjugate gives:

$$ \boxed{ z\overline z = a^2+b^2 }. $$

This result is always a nonnegative real number.

Modulus

The modulus or absolute value of $z=a+bi$ is:

$$ \boxed{ |z| = \sqrt{a^2+b^2} }. $$

Geometrically, $|z|$ is the distance from $z$ to the origin in the complex plane.

Modulus and Conjugate

$$ \boxed{ |z|^2=z\overline z }. $$

Reciprocal

For $z\ne0$:

$$ \boxed{ z^{-1} = \frac{\overline z}{|z|^2} }. $$

Thus for:

$$ z=a+bi\ne0, $$

we have:

$$ \boxed{ \frac1{a+bi} = \frac{a-bi}{a^2+b^2} }. $$

Division

Division by a nonzero complex number can be performed by multiplying numerator and denominator by the denominator's conjugate.

$$ \boxed{ \frac{a+bi}{c+di} = \frac{(ac+bd)+(bc-ad)i}{c^2+d^2} } $$

provided $c+di\ne0$.

The Complex Plane

The complex number:

$$ z=a+bi $$

corresponds to the point:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b) }. $$

The horizontal axis is the real axis and the vertical axis is the imaginary axis.

Argand Plane

The coordinate plane used to represent complex numbers is often called the complex plane or Argand plane.

Complex Numbers as Vectors

Under addition, the correspondence:

$$ a+bi\longleftrightarrow(a,b) $$

makes complex-number addition resemble vector addition in $\mathbb R^2$.

$$ \boxed{ (a,b)+(c,d) = (a+c,b+d) }. $$

Complex multiplication, however, adds additional structure beyond ordinary two-dimensional vector addition.

Distance between Complex Numbers

The distance between $z_1$ and $z_2$ is:

$$ \boxed{ |z_1-z_2| }. $$

If:

$$ z_1=a+bi, \qquad z_2=c+di, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ |z_1-z_2| = \sqrt{(a-c)^2+(b-d)^2} }. $$

Argument

For a nonzero complex number, an argument is an angle $\theta$ describing its direction from the positive real axis.

$$ \boxed{ \arg z=\theta \pmod{2\pi} }. $$

A complex number has infinitely many equivalent arguments:

$$ \theta+2\pi k, \qquad k\in\mathbb Z. $$

Polar Form

A nonzero complex number may be written:

$$ \boxed{ z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta) }. $$

Here:

$$ r=|z|. $$

Rectangular-to-Polar Conversion

From:

$$ z=a+bi, $$

calculate:

$$ \boxed{ r=\sqrt{a^2+b^2} }. $$

The angle must satisfy:

$$ \cos\theta=\frac ar, \qquad \sin\theta=\frac br. $$

Polar-to-Rectangular Conversion

$$ \boxed{ a=r\cos\theta, \qquad b=r\sin\theta }. $$

Euler's Formula

Euler's formula establishes:

$$ \boxed{ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta }. $$

Exponential Form

Consequently:

$$ \boxed{ z=re^{i\theta} }. $$

Polar and exponential forms are particularly useful for multiplication, division, powers, and roots.

Multiplication in Polar Form

If:

$$ z_1=r_1e^{i\theta_1}, \qquad z_2=r_2e^{i\theta_2}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ z_1z_2 = r_1r_2e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)} }. $$

Thus complex multiplication multiplies magnitudes and adds angles.

Geometric Meaning of Multiplication

Multiplication by a nonzero complex number combines scaling and rotation:

$$ \boxed{ \text{complex multiplication} = \text{scaling} + \text{rotation} }. $$

Multiplication by i

Since:

$$ i=e^{i\pi/2}, $$

multiplying by $i$ rotates a complex number counterclockwise by $90^\circ$ without changing its modulus.

$$ \boxed{ z\mapsto iz }. $$

Division in Polar Form

$$ \boxed{ \frac{z_1}{z_2} = \frac{r_1}{r_2} e^{i(\theta_1-\theta_2)} } $$

for $z_2\ne0$.

Division divides magnitudes and subtracts angles.

De Moivre's Theorem

For integer $n$:

$$ \boxed{ \left[ r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta) \right]^n = r^n \left[ \cos(n\theta)+i\sin(n\theta) \right] }. $$

Equivalently:

$$ \boxed{ (re^{i\theta})^n = r^ne^{in\theta} }. $$

Complex Roots

Suppose:

$$ z=re^{i\theta}, \qquad r>0. $$

The $n$ distinct solutions to:

$$ w^n=z $$

are:

$$ \boxed{ w_k= r^{1/n} e^{i(\theta+2\pi k)/n}, \qquad k=0,1,\ldots,n-1 }. $$

Roots Are Geometrically Distributed

The $n$th roots of a nonzero complex number have equal modulus:

$$ r^{1/n} $$

and are separated in argument by:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{2\pi}{n} }. $$

They therefore form equally spaced points on a circle centered at the origin.

Roots of Unity

The solutions of:

$$ z^n=1 $$

are:

$$ \boxed{ z_k=e^{2\pi ik/n}, \qquad k=0,\ldots,n-1 }. $$

Quadratic Equations

Complex numbers allow quadratic equations with negative real discriminants to have solutions.

For example:

$$ x^2+1=0 $$

gives:

$$ x^2=-1 $$

and therefore:

$$ \boxed{ x=\pm i }. $$

Quadratic Formula

The ordinary quadratic formula remains valid over $\mathbb C$:

$$ \boxed{ x= \frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a} }. $$

Complex numbers provide the required square roots when the discriminant is negative.

Complex Conjugate Root Theorem

If a polynomial has real coefficients and $a+bi$ with $b\ne0$ is a root, then:

$$ \boxed{ a-bi } $$

is also a root.

Example: Conjugate Root Pair

If a real polynomial has root:

$$ 3+2i, $$

then it also has root:

$$ \boxed{ 3-2i }. $$

Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

Every nonconstant polynomial with complex coefficients has at least one complex root.

Equivalently, a polynomial of degree $n\ge1$ over $\mathbb C$ has exactly $n$ roots counted with multiplicity.

$$ \boxed{ p(z) = a_n \prod_{k=1}^{n}(z-r_k) } $$

for suitable complex roots $r_k$.

Algebraic Closure

The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra is often summarized by saying that:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb C \text{ is algebraically closed} }. $$

Polynomial equations over $\mathbb C$ do not require another larger ordinary number system merely to obtain polynomial roots.

Complex Numbers Are Not Ordered Like Real Numbers

There is no ordering of $\mathbb C$ compatible with its field operations that makes it an ordered field in the same way as $\mathbb R$.

Consequently, expressions such as:

$$ z>w $$

do not have a standard field-order meaning for arbitrary complex numbers.

Modulus Provides a Size, Not an Order

One can compare:

$$ |z| \qquad\text{and}\qquad |w|, $$

because moduli are nonnegative real numbers. But:

$$ |z|>|w| $$

means only that $z$ is farther from the origin than $w$.

Complex Numbers in Engineering and Physics

Complex numbers are widely used to encode amplitude and phase in oscillatory systems, electrical engineering, signal analysis, quantum mechanics, wave theory, control systems, and differential equations.

Sinusoids and Complex Exponentials

Euler's formula allows trigonometric oscillations to be represented using complex exponentials:

$$ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta. $$

This converts many trigonometric calculations into algebraic operations involving exponentials.

Complex Numbers and Matrices

Matrices may contain complex entries, producing spaces such as:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb C^{m\times n} }. $$

Complex conjugation then extends entrywise to matrices, and the conjugate transpose becomes fundamental in complex linear algebra.

Common Misconception: Imaginary Means Fictional

The word imaginary is historical terminology. Imaginary numbers obey precise definitions and participate in a highly developed number system.

$$ \boxed{ \text{imaginary} \ne \text{mathematically unreal} }. $$

Common Misconception: Complex Numbers Exclude Real Numbers

Real numbers are contained within the complex numbers:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb R\subset\mathbb C }. $$

A real number is simply a complex number whose imaginary component is zero.

Common Misconception: The Imaginary Part of 3 + 4i Is 4i

Under standard notation:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Im}(3+4i)=4 }. $$

The term $4i$ is the imaginary component of the expression, but the imaginary part is its real coefficient $4$.

Common Misconception: i Is a Variable

In ordinary complex-number notation, $i$ denotes the fixed imaginary unit satisfying:

$$ i^2=-1. $$

It is not an unknown variable unless a context explicitly redefines the symbol.

Common Misconception: Every Square Root Rule Extends Unchanged

Familiar identities involving radicals over positive real numbers require care when extended to complex numbers.

In particular, the unrestricted rule:

$$ \sqrt{ab}=\sqrt a\sqrt b $$

does not hold universally under principal complex square-root conventions.

Common Misconception: A Complex Number Has One Polar Angle

A nonzero complex number has infinitely many arguments differing by multiples of $2\pi$.

$$ \boxed{ \theta,\ \theta+2\pi,\ \theta+4\pi,\ldots } $$

all describe the same direction.

Common Misconception: Complex Numbers Cannot Be Graphed

Every complex number corresponds naturally to a point in a two-dimensional plane:

$$ \boxed{ a+bi \leftrightarrow (a,b) }. $$

Complex numbers therefore possess a strong geometric interpretation.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\mathbb C$ "The complex numbers."
$z=a+bi$ "z equals a plus b i."
$i^2=-1$ "i squared equals negative one."
$\operatorname{Re}(z)=a$ "The real part of z is a."
$\operatorname{Im}(z)=b$ "The imaginary part of z is b."
$\overline z$ "z bar" or "the complex conjugate of z."
$|z|$ "The modulus of z" or "the absolute value of z."
$z=re^{i\theta}$ "z equals r e to the i theta."
$\arg z$ "An argument of z."
$\operatorname{Arg}(z)$ "The principal argument of z," when that convention has been defined.

The expression $a+bi$ is commonly called standard form, rectangular form, or Cartesian form. The expressions $r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)$ and $re^{i\theta}$ are called polar/trigonometric and exponential forms respectively.

The symbol $\mathbb C$ denotes the entire complex number system. A single complex number is normally represented by variables such as $z$ or $w$, reflecting a common convention in complex analysis.

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish $\operatorname{Im}(z)$ from the term $bi$, distinguish $\overline z$ from $|z|$, and distinguish an arbitrary argument $\arg z$ from a defined principal argument $\operatorname{Arg}z$. When converting between rectangular and polar forms, preserve quadrant information. When using radicals and logarithms over $\mathbb C$, state branch or principal-value conventions whenever they affect the result.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: A complex number has the form $a+bi$ with $a,b\in\mathbb R$ and $i^2=-1$.
  • Number System: $$ \boxed{ \mathbb C }. $$
  • Standard Form: $$ \boxed{ z=a+bi }. $$
  • Imaginary Unit: $$ \boxed{ i^2=-1 }. $$
  • Real Part: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Re}(z)=a }. $$
  • Imaginary Part: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Im}(z)=b }. $$
  • Real-Number Inclusion: $$ \boxed{ \mathbb R\subset\mathbb C }. $$
  • Pure Imaginary Form: A nonzero complex number with real part zero has form $bi$.
  • Equality: $$ \boxed{ a+bi=c+di \iff a=c,\ b=d }. $$
  • Addition: Components are added separately.
  • Subtraction: Components are subtracted separately.
  • Multiplication: Uses distributivity and $i^2=-1$.
  • Conjugate: $$ \boxed{ \overline{a+bi}=a-bi }. $$
  • Modulus: $$ \boxed{ |a+bi|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2} }. $$
  • Reciprocal: $$ \boxed{ z^{-1} = \frac{\overline z}{|z|^2} } $$ for $z\ne0$.
  • Complex Plane: $$ \boxed{ a+bi\leftrightarrow(a,b) }. $$
  • Distance: $$ \boxed{ d(z,w)=|z-w| }. $$
  • Polar Form: $$ \boxed{ z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta) }. $$
  • Exponential Form: $$ \boxed{ z=re^{i\theta} }. $$
  • Euler's Formula: $$ \boxed{ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta }. $$
  • Argument: Describes angular direction in the complex plane and is defined modulo $2\pi$.
  • Multiplication Geometry: Complex multiplication multiplies moduli and adds arguments.
  • Division Geometry: Complex division divides moduli and subtracts arguments.
  • De Moivre's Theorem: Provides a direct method for integer powers in polar form.
  • nth Roots: A nonzero complex number has $n$ distinct $n$th roots.
  • Roots of Unity: The solutions to $z^n=1$ are equally spaced around the unit circle.
  • Quadratic Equations: Complex numbers provide solutions when real quadratic equations have negative discriminants.
  • Conjugate Root Theorem: Nonreal roots of real-coefficient polynomials occur in conjugate pairs.
  • Fundamental Theorem of Algebra: Every nonconstant complex polynomial has a complex root and factors completely over $\mathbb C$.
  • Algebraic Closure: $\mathbb C$ is algebraically closed.
  • Ordering: $\mathbb C$ is not an ordered field analogous to $\mathbb R$.
  • Applications: Complex numbers are fundamental in electrical engineering, signal processing, quantum mechanics, waves, control theory, differential equations, and many areas of pure mathematics.
  • Common Error: Do not interpret imaginary as meaning mathematically fictitious.
  • Common Error: Do not exclude real numbers from the complex number system.
  • Common Error: Do not identify the imaginary part of $a+bi$ with $bi$; the conventional imaginary part is $b$.
  • Common Error: Do not treat $i$ as an ordinary variable in standard complex notation.
  • Common Error: Do not assume all real radical identities remain valid without modification for principal complex roots.
  • Terminological Contrast: A complex number has the form $a+bi$. A real number is a complex number with imaginary part zero. A pure imaginary number has real part zero and, under the usual nonzero convention, nonzero imaginary part. The complex conjugate changes the sign of the imaginary part. The modulus measures distance from the origin. The argument gives angular direction. Rectangular form, polar form, and exponential form are different representations of the same complex number.
