Determinant

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

The determinant is a scalar associated with a square matrix. It encodes several fundamental properties of the corresponding linear transformation, including whether the transformation is invertible, how it scales oriented volume, and whether it reverses orientation.

For a square matrix $A$, its determinant is written:

$$ \boxed{\det(A)} $$

or, when no confusion is likely:

$$ \boxed{|A|}. $$

For example, if:

$$ A= \begin{pmatrix} a & b\\ c & d \end{pmatrix}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A)=ad-bc }. $$

The word determinant reflects the fact that this quantity determines important structural information about a matrix. In particular:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A)\neq0 \iff A\text{ is invertible} }. $$

Determinants arose historically from the study of systems of linear equations and later became central to matrix algebra, geometry, calculus, differential equations, and many other areas of mathematics.

Operational Nuance

Square Matrices

In elementary linear algebra, determinants are defined for square matrices:

$$ \boxed{ A\in M_{n\times n} }. $$

A general rectangular $m\times n$ matrix with $m\neq n$ does not have an ordinary determinant.

Determinant of a $1\times1$ Matrix

For:

$$ A=(a), $$

the determinant is simply:

$$ \boxed{\det(A)=a}. $$

Determinant of a $2\times2$ Matrix

If:

$$ A= \begin{pmatrix} a & b\\ c & d \end{pmatrix}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A)=ad-bc }. $$

Example

Let:

$$ A= \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 2\\ 5 & 4 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Then:

$$ \det(A) = (3)(4)-(2)(5) = 12-10 = \boxed{2}. $$

Determinant of a $3\times3$ Matrix

For:

$$ A= \begin{pmatrix} a & b & c\\ d & e & f\\ g & h & i \end{pmatrix}, $$

expansion along the first row gives:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A) = a(ei-fh) - b(di-fg) + c(dh-eg) }. $$

Minor

The minor $M_{ij}$ of an entry $a_{ij}$ is the determinant of the matrix obtained by deleting row $i$ and column $j$.

$$ \boxed{ M_{ij} = \det(\text{matrix formed by deleting row }i\text{ and column }j) }. $$

Cofactor

The corresponding cofactor is:

$$ \boxed{ C_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j}M_{ij} }. $$

The signs follow the checkerboard pattern:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{pmatrix} + & - & + & \cdots\\ - & + & - & \cdots\\ + & - & + & \cdots\\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots \end{pmatrix} }. $$

Cofactor Expansion

Expanding along row $i$:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A) = \sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}C_{ij} }. $$

Expanding along column $j$:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ij}C_{ij} }. $$

Any row or column may be chosen. A row or column containing several zeros can make the calculation much shorter.

Permutation Formula

For an $n\times n$ matrix:

$$ A=(a_{ij}), $$

the determinant can be defined by the Leibniz formula:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A) = \sum_{\sigma\in S_n} \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma) \prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,\sigma(i)} }. $$

Here $S_n$ is the set of permutations of $\{1,\ldots,n\}$ and $\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)$ records whether the permutation is even or odd.

Identity Matrix

The identity matrix has determinant:

$$ \boxed{ \det(I_n)=1 }. $$

Zero Matrix

The zero matrix has determinant:

$$ \boxed{ \det(0)=0 }. $$

Diagonal Matrix

For:

$$ D= \begin{pmatrix} d_1 & 0 & \cdots & 0\\ 0 & d_2 & \cdots & 0\\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & d_n \end{pmatrix}, $$

one has:

$$ \boxed{ \det(D)=d_1d_2\cdots d_n }. $$

Triangular Matrix

For either an upper- or lower-triangular matrix, the determinant equals the product of the diagonal entries:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A) = \prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{ii} }. $$

Effect of Swapping Rows

Interchanging two rows reverses the sign of the determinant:

$$ \boxed{ \det(B)=-\det(A) } $$

when $B$ is obtained from $A$ by one row swap.

Effect of Scaling a Row

Multiplying one row by a scalar $k$ multiplies the determinant by $k$:

$$ \boxed{ \det(B)=k\det(A) }. $$

Effect of Row Replacement

Adding a multiple of one row to another row leaves the determinant unchanged:

$$ \boxed{ R_i\to R_i+kR_j \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \det(B)=\det(A) }. $$

Identical Rows

If two rows are equal, then:

$$ \boxed{\det(A)=0}. $$

The same statement holds for identical columns.

Linearly Dependent Rows or Columns

If the rows or columns of a square matrix are linearly dependent, then:

$$ \boxed{\det(A)=0}. $$

Invertibility

A square matrix is invertible exactly when its determinant is nonzero:

$$ \boxed{ A^{-1}\text{ exists} \iff \det(A)\neq0 }. $$

Singular Matrix

A square matrix is singular exactly when:

$$ \boxed{\det(A)=0}. $$

Product Rule

Determinants are multiplicative:

$$ \boxed{ \det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B) }. $$

Determinant of an Inverse

If $A$ is invertible:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A^{-1}) = \frac{1}{\det(A)} }. $$

Transpose

A matrix and its transpose have the same determinant:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A^T)=\det(A) }. $$

Scalar Multiple of a Matrix

For an $n\times n$ matrix:

$$ \boxed{ \det(kA)=k^n\det(A) }. $$

This is different from multiplying only one row by $k$, which multiplies the determinant by only $k$.

Determinant and Linear Systems

For the square system:

$$ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}, $$

if:

$$ \det(A)\neq0, $$

then the system has exactly one solution:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x}=A^{-1}\mathbf{b} }. $$

Cramer's Rule

When:

$$ \det(A)\neq0, $$

Cramer's Rule gives:

$$ \boxed{ x_i = \frac{\det(A_i)}{\det(A)} }, $$

where $A_i$ is obtained by replacing column $i$ of $A$ with the right-hand-side vector.

Adjugate Formula

An invertible square matrix satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(A)} \operatorname{adj}(A) }. $$

Geometric Meaning in Two Dimensions

If the columns of:

$$ A= \begin{pmatrix} a & b\\ c & d \end{pmatrix} $$

are regarded as vectors, then:

$$ \boxed{ |\det(A)| } $$

is the area of the parallelogram spanned by those vectors.

Signed Area

The determinant itself gives oriented or signed area:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A)>0 \text{ or } \det(A)<0 } $$

according to orientation.

Geometric Meaning in Three Dimensions

If a $3\times3$ matrix has vectors $\mathbf{u}$, $\mathbf{v}$, and $\mathbf{w}$ as columns, then:

$$ \boxed{ |\det(A)| } $$

equals the volume of the parallelepiped spanned by the three vectors.

Scalar Triple Product

In three dimensions:

$$ \boxed{ \det \begin{pmatrix} \vert & \vert & \vert\\ \mathbf{u} & \mathbf{v} & \mathbf{w}\\ \vert & \vert & \vert \end{pmatrix} = \mathbf{u}\cdot(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{w}) }. $$

Volume Scaling

A linear transformation represented by $A$ scales $n$-dimensional volume by:

$$ \boxed{ |\det(A)| }. $$

For example:

  • $|\det(A)|=2$ doubles volume,
  • $|\det(A)|=\frac12$ halves volume,
  • $|\det(A)|=1$ preserves volume,
  • $\det(A)=0$ collapses dimension and volume.

Orientation

For a real invertible matrix:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A)>0 } $$

indicates orientation preservation, while:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A)<0 } $$

indicates orientation reversal.

Dimension Collapse

When:

$$ \det(A)=0, $$

the linear transformation maps the space into a lower-dimensional subset.

In two dimensions, a region may collapse to a line or point. In three dimensions, a volume may collapse to a plane, line, or point.

Eigenvalues

If the eigenvalues of an $n\times n$ matrix over $\mathbb{C}$ are:

$$ \lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n $$

counted with algebraic multiplicity, then:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A) = \lambda_1\lambda_2\cdots\lambda_n }. $$

Zero Eigenvalue

A square matrix has zero as an eigenvalue exactly when:

$$ \boxed{\det(A)=0}. $$

Characteristic Polynomial

Eigenvalues are found from:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A-\lambda I)=0 } $$

or equivalently, under the alternative convention:

$$ \det(\lambda I-A)=0. $$

Jacobian Determinant

In multivariable calculus, if:

$$ \mathbf{F}:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^n, $$

then the determinant of its Jacobian matrix:

$$ \boxed{ \det(D\mathbf{F}) } $$

describes local oriented volume scaling.

Change of Variables

In multiple integration, the absolute value of a Jacobian determinant appears as the local volume-scaling factor:

$$ \boxed{ dV = |\det J|\,du_1\cdots du_n }. $$

Common Misconception: The determinant is not obtained by multiplying all entries of a matrix, nor is it defined for an arbitrary rectangular matrix. It is a structured scalar function of a square matrix with precise algebraic and geometric properties.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\det(A)$ "The determinant of A."
$|A|$ "The determinant of A," when matrix bars are being used.
$\det(A)=0$ "The determinant of A is zero."
$\det(A)\neq0$ "The determinant of A is nonzero."
$\det(AB)=\det(A)\det(B)$ "The determinant of A B equals the determinant of A times the determinant of B."
$C_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j}M_{ij}$ "C sub i j equals negative one to the i plus j times M sub i j."
$\det(A-\lambda I)=0$ "The determinant of A minus lambda I equals zero."

Determinant notation often uses vertical bars:

$$ \left| \begin{matrix} a & b\\ c & d \end{matrix} \right|. $$

This should not be confused with the absolute value of an ordinary scalar. Context distinguishes:

$$ |x| \qquad\text{from}\qquad |A|. $$

Because of that possible ambiguity, the notation:

$$ \boxed{\det(A)} $$

is often preferable in prose and advanced work.

A matrix is written with parentheses or brackets:

$$ \begin{pmatrix} a & b\\ c & d \end{pmatrix}, $$

whereas determinant bars indicate the scalar determinant:

$$ \boxed{ \left| \begin{matrix} a & b\\ c & d \end{matrix} \right| = ad-bc }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Do not use determinant bars and matrix brackets interchangeably. A matrix and its determinant are different mathematical objects:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ is a matrix}, \qquad \det(A)\text{ is a scalar} }. $$

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Basic Object: A determinant assigns a scalar to a square matrix.
  • Notation: $$ \boxed{\det(A)}. $$
  • Alternative Notation: $$ \boxed{|A|}. $$
  • $1\times1$ Case: $$ \boxed{\det(a)=a}. $$
  • $2\times2$ Formula: $$ \boxed{ \det \begin{pmatrix} a & b\\ c & d \end{pmatrix} = ad-bc }. $$
  • $3\times3$ Expansion: $$ \boxed{ \det(A) = a(ei-fh)-b(di-fg)+c(dh-eg) }. $$
  • Square-Matrix Requirement: Ordinary determinants are defined for: $$ \boxed{n\times n} $$ matrices.
  • Identity Matrix: $$ \boxed{\det(I_n)=1}. $$
  • Diagonal Matrix: $$ \boxed{ \det(D)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}d_i }. $$
  • Triangular Matrix: The determinant equals the product of the diagonal entries.
  • Row Swap: Interchanging two rows multiplies the determinant by: $$ \boxed{-1}. $$
  • Column Swap: Interchanging two columns also reverses the determinant's sign.
  • Row Scaling: Multiplying one row by $k$ multiplies the determinant by: $$ \boxed{k}. $$
  • Column Scaling: Multiplying one column by $k$ likewise multiplies the determinant by $k$.
  • Row Replacement: Adding a multiple of one row to another does not change the determinant.
  • Column Replacement: Adding a multiple of one column to another likewise leaves the determinant unchanged.
  • Zero Row: A square matrix containing an all-zero row has: $$ \boxed{\det(A)=0}. $$
  • Zero Column: An all-zero column also forces: $$ \boxed{\det(A)=0}. $$
  • Equal Rows: Two equal rows imply: $$ \boxed{\det(A)=0}. $$
  • Proportional Rows: Two proportional rows imply linear dependence and therefore determinant zero.
  • Linear Dependence: Linearly dependent rows or columns imply: $$ \boxed{\det(A)=0}. $$
  • Full Rank: For an $n\times n$ matrix: $$ \boxed{ \det(A)\neq0 \iff \operatorname{rank}(A)=n }. $$
  • Invertibility: $$ \boxed{ \det(A)\neq0 \iff A^{-1}\text{ exists} }. $$
  • Singularity: $$ \boxed{ \det(A)=0 \iff A\text{ is singular} }. $$
  • Null Space: For a square matrix: $$ \det(A)=0 $$ exactly when the homogeneous system: $$ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0} $$ has a nonzero solution.
  • Unique Linear-System Solution: If: $$ \det(A)\neq0, $$ then: $$ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b} $$ has exactly one solution for every $\mathbf{b}$.
  • Cramer's Rule: $$ \boxed{ x_i=\frac{\det(A_i)}{\det(A)} } $$ when $\det(A)\neq0$.
  • Minor: $M_{ij}$ is obtained by deleting row $i$ and column $j$ and taking the remaining determinant.
  • Cofactor: $$ \boxed{ C_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j}M_{ij} }. $$
  • Laplace Expansion: Determinants may be expanded along any row or column using cofactors.
  • Product: $$ \boxed{ \det(AB)=\det(A)\det(B) }. $$
  • Noncommutativity Contrast: Although matrix multiplication generally satisfies: $$ AB\neq BA, $$ one has: $$ \det(AB)=\det(BA) $$ for square matrices of the same size because both equal $\det(A)\det(B)$.
  • Inverse: $$ \boxed{ \det(A^{-1}) = [\det(A)]^{-1} }. $$
  • Transpose: $$ \boxed{ \det(A^T)=\det(A) }. $$
  • Power: For positive integer $m$: $$ \boxed{ \det(A^m) = [\det(A)]^m }. $$
  • Scalar Matrix Multiplication: For an $n\times n$ matrix: $$ \boxed{ \det(kA)=k^n\det(A) }. $$
  • Similarity: Similar matrices have the same determinant: $$ \boxed{ B=P^{-1}AP \Longrightarrow \det(B)=\det(A) }. $$
  • Change of Basis: Because similar matrices represent the same linear transformation in different bases, determinant is basis-independent.
  • Eigenvalue Product: $$ \boxed{ \det(A) = \prod_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_i } $$ with eigenvalues counted algebraically.
  • Zero Eigenvalue: $$ \boxed{ 0\text{ is an eigenvalue} \iff \det(A)=0 }. $$
  • Characteristic Equation: $$ \boxed{ \det(A-\lambda I)=0 } $$ identifies eigenvalues.
  • Trace Contrast: The determinant is the product of eigenvalues, while the trace is their sum, counted with algebraic multiplicity over an appropriate splitting field.
  • Area: For two vectors forming the columns of a $2\times2$ matrix: $$ \boxed{ |\det(A)| } $$ is the parallelogram area.
  • Triangle Area: If vectors $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ share an initial point: $$ \boxed{ A_{\triangle} = \frac12 |\det(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})| }. $$
  • Three-Dimensional Volume: $$ \boxed{ V = |\det(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})| }. $$
  • Scalar Triple Product: $$ \boxed{ \mathbf{u}\cdot (\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{w}) = \det(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}) }. $$
  • Coplanarity: Three vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$ are linearly dependent, and therefore geometrically coplanar through the origin, when: $$ \boxed{ \det(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})=0 }. $$
  • Collinearity: In coordinate geometry, a suitable $3\times3$ determinant can test whether three planar points are collinear.
  • Orientation in $\mathbb{R}^2$: The sign of a determinant distinguishes clockwise from counterclockwise orientation once a coordinate orientation is fixed.
  • Orientation Preservation: $$ \boxed{ \det(A)>0 } $$ for a real invertible linear transformation indicates preserved orientation.
  • Orientation Reversal: $$ \boxed{ \det(A)<0 } $$ indicates reversed orientation.
  • Volume Preservation: If: $$ |\det(A)|=1, $$ the transformation preserves ordinary volume.
  • Special Linear Group: Matrices satisfying: $$ \boxed{\det(A)=1} $$ form the special linear group: $$ SL(n). $$
  • Orthogonal Matrices: If: $$ A^TA=I, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ \det(A)=\pm1 }. $$
  • Rotation Matrix: A proper rotation matrix has: $$ \boxed{\det(A)=1}. $$
  • Reflection Matrix: A reflection in Euclidean space has determinant: $$ \boxed{-1}. $$
  • Jacobian: In multivariable calculus: $$ \boxed{ J= \det \left[ \frac{\partial(x_1,\ldots,x_n)} {\partial(u_1,\ldots,u_n)} \right] } $$ represents local oriented volume scaling under a coordinate transformation.
  • Change of Variables: The absolute Jacobian determinant adjusts area or volume elements in multiple integrals.
  • Inverse Function Theorem: A differentiable map: $$ F:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^n $$ is locally invertible near a point when its derivative matrix has nonzero determinant there, subject to the theorem's smoothness hypotheses.
  • Degeneracy: A zero determinant often signals geometric or algebraic degeneracy.
  • Dimension Collapse: $$ \det(A)=0 $$ means the corresponding linear transformation collapses $n$-dimensional volume to zero.
  • Multilinearity: The determinant is linear in each row separately when all other rows are held fixed.
  • Alternating Property: Swapping two rows changes the sign; in particular, equal rows force the determinant to vanish.
  • Normalization: The determinant is normalized by: $$ \boxed{\det(I)=1}. $$
  • Abstract Characterization: Over a commutative field or ring, determinant is characterized as the unique alternating multilinear function of the rows, normalized to equal $1$ on the identity matrix.
  • Computational Practice: For large matrices, row reduction or numerical factorization is generally more practical than direct cofactor expansion.
  • Cofactor Expansion Use: Cofactor expansion is particularly convenient for small matrices or rows and columns containing many zeros.
  • Terminological Contrast: A matrix is an array of entries; its determinant is a scalar derived from that array.
  • Core Interpretation: The determinant compresses central algebraic and geometric information about a square matrix into one scalar: $$ \boxed{ \det(A)\neq0 \iff A\text{ is invertible} } $$ while: $$ \boxed{ |\det(A)| = \text{volume-scaling factor} }. $$ Its magnitude measures scaling, its sign records orientation for real transformations, and zero signals loss of dimension and invertibility.
