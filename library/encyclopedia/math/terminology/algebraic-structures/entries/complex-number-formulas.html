Complex Number Formulas

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

Complex number formulas are the standard algebraic and geometric relationships used to represent, combine, measure, transform, and solve expressions involving complex numbers.

A complex number is commonly written in standard form as:

$$ \boxed{ z=a+bi } $$

where $a,b\in\mathbb R$ and:

$$ \boxed{ i^2=-1 }. $$

The word complex reflects the historical development of numbers containing both real and imaginary components. The word formula refers to a symbolic relationship expressing how mathematical quantities are connected.

$$ \boxed{ \text{complex number formulas} = \text{standard relationships governing numbers in }\mathbb C }. $$

These formulas include ordinary arithmetic rules, conjugation and modulus identities, polar and exponential representations, powers and roots, and formulas connecting algebraic coordinates with geometric position in the complex plane.

Operational Nuance

Standard Form

The Cartesian or rectangular form of a complex number is:

$$ \boxed{ z=a+bi }. $$

Here:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Re}(z)=a, \qquad \operatorname{Im}(z)=b }. $$

The number $a$ is the real part and $b$ is the imaginary part coefficient.

Equality of Complex Numbers

Two complex numbers are equal exactly when their real parts and imaginary parts are equal:

$$ \boxed{ a+bi=c+di \iff a=c \text{ and } b=d }. $$

Addition

Complex numbers are added componentwise:

$$ \boxed{ (a+bi)+(c+di) = (a+c)+(b+d)i }. $$

Subtraction

$$ \boxed{ (a+bi)-(c+di) = (a-c)+(b-d)i }. $$

Multiplication

Multiply using ordinary distributive algebra and the relation $i^2=-1$:

$$ (a+bi)(c+di) = ac+adi+bci+bd i^2. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ (a+bi)(c+di) = (ac-bd)+(ad+bc)i }. $$

Complex Conjugate

The conjugate of:

$$ z=a+bi $$

is:

$$ \boxed{ \overline z=a-bi }. $$

Conjugation changes the sign of the imaginary part while preserving the real part.

Product with the Conjugate

$$ \boxed{ z\overline z = (a+bi)(a-bi) = a^2+b^2 }. $$

This identity is central to modulus, reciprocals, and division.

Modulus

The modulus, or absolute value, of $z=a+bi$ is:

$$ \boxed{ |z| = \sqrt{a^2+b^2} }. $$

Geometrically, this is the distance from the origin to the point $(a,b)$ in the complex plane.

Squared Modulus

$$ \boxed{ |z|^2 = z\overline z = a^2+b^2 }. $$

Real Part from z and Its Conjugate

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Re}(z) = \frac{z+\overline z}{2} }. $$

Imaginary Part from z and Its Conjugate

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Im}(z) = \frac{z-\overline z}{2i} }. $$

Reciprocal

For $z\ne0$:

$$ \boxed{ \frac1z = \frac{\overline z}{|z|^2} }. $$

In rectangular form:

$$ \boxed{ \frac1{a+bi} = \frac{a-bi}{a^2+b^2} } $$

provided $a$ and $b$ are not both zero.

Division

For:

$$ z_1=a+bi, \qquad z_2=c+di, \qquad z_2\ne0, $$

multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the denominator:

$$ \frac{a+bi}{c+di} \cdot \frac{c-di}{c-di}. $$

This gives:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{a+bi}{c+di} = \frac{(ac+bd)+(bc-ad)i}{c^2+d^2} }. $$

Conjugate of a Sum

$$ \boxed{ \overline{z+w} = \overline z+\overline w }. $$

Conjugate of a Product

$$ \boxed{ \overline{zw} = \overline z\,\overline w }. $$

Conjugate of a Quotient

$$ \boxed{ \overline{\left(\frac zw\right)} = \frac{\overline z}{\overline w} } $$

for $w\ne0$.

Double Conjugation

$$ \boxed{ \overline{\overline z}=z }. $$

Modulus of a Product

$$ \boxed{ |zw| = |z||w| }. $$

Modulus of a Quotient

$$ \boxed{ \left|\frac zw\right| = \frac{|z|}{|w|} } $$

for $w\ne0$.

Triangle Inequality

Complex numbers satisfy:

$$ \boxed{ |z+w| \le |z|+|w| }. $$

This is the complex-number form of the triangle inequality.

Reverse Triangle Inequality

$$ \boxed{ \bigl||z|-|w|\bigr| \le |z-w| }. $$

Distance between Complex Numbers

The distance between complex numbers $z_1$ and $z_2$ is:

$$ \boxed{ d(z_1,z_2) = |z_1-z_2| }. $$

If:

$$ z_1=a+bi, \qquad z_2=c+di, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ |z_1-z_2| = \sqrt{(a-c)^2+(b-d)^2} }. $$

Polar Form

A nonzero complex number may be written as:

$$ \boxed{ z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta) } $$

where:

$$ \boxed{ r=|z| }. $$

The angle $\theta$ is an argument of $z$.

Recovering Rectangular Coordinates

From:

$$ z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta), $$

we obtain:

$$ \boxed{ a=r\cos\theta, \qquad b=r\sin\theta }. $$

Finding the Modulus from Rectangular Form

$$ \boxed{ r=\sqrt{a^2+b^2} }. $$

Argument from Rectangular Coordinates

When quadrant information is handled correctly:

$$ \tan\theta=\frac ba. $$

Thus one may write informally:

$$ \theta=\arctan\left(\frac ba\right), $$

but this formula alone does not distinguish all quadrants. A quadrant-aware argument function such as $\operatorname{atan2}(b,a)$ is often more reliable computationally.

Arguments Are Not Unique

If $\theta$ is an argument of a nonzero complex number, then:

$$ \boxed{ \theta+2\pi k, \qquad k\in\mathbb Z } $$

represents the same direction.

Principal Argument

A single distinguished argument may be chosen by restricting the angle to a stated interval. A common convention is:

$$ \boxed{ -\pi<\operatorname{Arg}(z)\le\pi }. $$

Other conventions occur, so the chosen branch should be stated.

Euler's Formula

Euler's formula connects exponential and trigonometric forms:

$$ \boxed{ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta }. $$

Exponential Form

Using Euler's formula:

$$ \boxed{ z=re^{i\theta} }. $$

This is the exponential form of a nonzero complex number.

Multiplication in Polar Form

Let:

$$ z_1=r_1e^{i\theta_1}, \qquad z_2=r_2e^{i\theta_2}. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ z_1z_2 = r_1r_2e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)} }. $$

Thus multiplication multiplies moduli and adds arguments.

Division in Polar Form

For $z_2\ne0$:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{z_1}{z_2} = \frac{r_1}{r_2} e^{i(\theta_1-\theta_2)} }. $$

Division divides moduli and subtracts arguments.

Conjugate in Polar Form

$$ \boxed{ \overline z = re^{-i\theta} }. $$

The modulus remains unchanged while the argument changes sign.

Reciprocal in Polar Form

For $z\ne0$:

$$ \boxed{ z^{-1} = \frac1r e^{-i\theta} }. $$

De Moivre's Theorem

For integer $n$:

$$ \boxed{ \left( r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta) \right)^n = r^n \left( \cos(n\theta)+i\sin(n\theta) \right) }. $$

In exponential form:

$$ \boxed{ (re^{i\theta})^n = r^ne^{in\theta} }. $$

Powers of a Complex Number

De Moivre's theorem makes repeated multiplication especially simple:

$$ \boxed{ |z^n|=|z|^n } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \arg(z^n) \equiv n\arg(z) \pmod{2\pi} }. $$

nth Roots

Suppose:

$$ z=re^{i\theta}, \qquad r>0. $$

The $n$ distinct $n$th roots are:

$$ \boxed{ w_k = r^{1/n} e^{\,i(\theta+2\pi k)/n}, \qquad k=0,1,\ldots,n-1 }. $$

Trigonometric Form of the nth Roots

$$ \boxed{ w_k = r^{1/n} \left[ \cos\left(\frac{\theta+2\pi k}{n}\right) + i\sin\left(\frac{\theta+2\pi k}{n}\right) \right] }. $$

Roots Are Equally Spaced

Consecutive $n$th roots differ in argument by:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{2\pi}{n} }. $$

Geometrically, they lie at equally spaced points on the circle of radius $r^{1/n}$.

Roots of Unity

The solutions to:

$$ z^n=1 $$

are:

$$ \boxed{ z_k = e^{2\pi ik/n}, \qquad k=0,1,\ldots,n-1 }. $$

Complex Conjugate Root Theorem

If a polynomial has real coefficients and:

$$ a+bi $$

is a nonreal root, then:

$$ \boxed{ a-bi } $$

is also a root.

Quadratic Formula over the Complex Numbers

For:

$$ ax^2+bx+c=0, \qquad a\ne0, $$

the quadratic formula remains:

$$ \boxed{ x= \frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a} }. $$

If:

$$ b^2-4ac<0 $$

for real coefficients, the square root can be expressed using $i$.

Square Root of a Negative Real Number

For $a>0$:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt{-a} = i\sqrt a } $$

when $\sqrt{\phantom{x}}$ denotes the principal square root.

The equation:

$$ z^2=-a $$

has both roots:

$$ \boxed{ z=\pm i\sqrt a }. $$

Powers of i

Integer powers of $i$ repeat every four powers:

$$ i^0=1,\qquad i^1=i,\qquad i^2=-1,\qquad i^3=-i,\qquad i^4=1. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ i^{n+4}=i^n }. $$

Reducing Powers of i

The value of $i^n$ depends on $n$ modulo $4$:

$$ \boxed{ i^n= \begin{cases} 1,&n\equiv0\pmod4,\\ i,&n\equiv1\pmod4,\\ -1,&n\equiv2\pmod4,\\ -i,&n\equiv3\pmod4. \end{cases} } $$

Real Numbers inside the Complex System

Every real number is a complex number with zero imaginary part:

$$ \boxed{ a=a+0i }. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb R\subset\mathbb C }. $$

Pure Imaginary Numbers

A number of the form:

$$ bi $$

has real part zero.

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Re}(bi)=0 }. $$

Complex Plane Coordinates

The correspondence:

$$ \boxed{ a+bi \longleftrightarrow (a,b) } $$

identifies the real part with the horizontal coordinate and the imaginary part with the vertical coordinate.

Multiplication as Scaling and Rotation

In polar form:

$$ z_1z_2 = r_1r_2e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)}. $$

Thus multiplication by a nonzero complex number simultaneously:

  • scales modulus by its own modulus, and
  • rotates argument by its own argument.
$$ \boxed{ \text{complex multiplication} = \text{scaling + rotation} }. $$

Multiplication by i

Since:

$$ i=e^{i\pi/2}, $$

multiplication by $i$ rotates a complex number counterclockwise by $90^\circ$:

$$ \boxed{ z\mapsto iz } $$

while preserving its modulus.

Euler's Identity

Setting $\theta=\pi$ in Euler's formula gives:

$$ e^{i\pi}=-1. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ e^{i\pi}+1=0 }. $$

Common Misconception: The Imaginary Part Is bi

For:

$$ z=a+bi, $$

the imaginary part is conventionally:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Im}(z)=b }, $$

not $bi$.

Common Misconception: |a + bi| = |a| + |b|

The modulus is Euclidean distance:

$$ \boxed{ |a+bi| = \sqrt{a^2+b^2} }. $$

It is not generally equal to $|a|+|b|$.

Common Misconception: Multiplication Is Componentwise

Complex multiplication is not:

$$ (a+bi)(c+di) \ne ac+bdi $$

in general.

Cross terms and the relation $i^2=-1$ must be included.

Common Misconception: An Argument Is Unique

A nonzero complex number has infinitely many arguments differing by integer multiples of $2\pi$.

$$ \boxed{ \theta \sim \theta+2\pi k }. $$

Common Misconception: arctan(b/a) Always Gives the Correct Argument

The ordinary arctangent formula can lose quadrant information. The signs of both $a$ and $b$ must be considered.

Common Misconception: Every Complex Root Is Unique

Equations such as:

$$ z^n=w $$

generally have $n$ distinct complex roots when $w\ne0$.

Common Misconception: The Square Root Symbol Lists Both Roots

The notation $\sqrt z$ normally denotes a chosen principal square root when a branch convention has been established. Solving:

$$ w^2=z $$

asks for all square roots and therefore generally produces two values for $z\ne0$.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$z=a+bi$ "z equals a plus b i."
$i^2=-1$ "i squared equals negative one."
$\overline z=a-bi$ "z bar equals a minus b i."
$|z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ "The modulus of z equals the square root of a squared plus b squared."
$z\overline z=|z|^2$ "z times z bar equals the modulus of z squared."
$z=re^{i\theta}$ "z equals r e to the i theta."
$z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)$ "z equals r times cosine theta plus i sine theta."
$e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$ "e to the i theta equals cosine theta plus i sine theta."
$z^n=r^ne^{in\theta}$ "z to the n equals r to the n times e to the i n theta."
$d(z_1,z_2)=|z_1-z_2|$ "The distance between z one and z two is the modulus of z one minus z two."

The notation $a+bi$ is often called standard form, rectangular form, or Cartesian form. The expression $r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)$ is commonly called polar or trigonometric form, while $re^{i\theta}$ is called exponential form.

The notation $\overline z$ is normally read z bar, while $|z|$ is read the modulus of z or the absolute value of z. These symbols should not be confused even though they occur together in identities such as $z\overline z=|z|^2$.

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish carefully among $\operatorname{Im}(z)$, $bi$, $\overline z$, and $|z|$. When converting to polar form, determine the argument using the actual quadrant rather than relying blindly on $\arctan(b/a)$. State whether $\arg z$ denotes any argument or whether $\operatorname{Arg}z$ denotes a chosen principal argument. When taking complex roots, distinguish a principal root from the full set of solutions.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Imaginary Unit: $$ \boxed{ i^2=-1 }. $$
  • Real Part: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Re}(z)=a }. $$
  • Imaginary Part: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Im}(z)=b }. $$
  • Addition: $$ \boxed{ (a+bi)+(c+di) = (a+c)+(b+d)i }. $$
  • Subtraction: $$ \boxed{ (a+bi)-(c+di) = (a-c)+(b-d)i }. $$
  • Multiplication: $$ \boxed{ (a+bi)(c+di) = (ac-bd)+(ad+bc)i }. $$
  • Conjugate: $$ \boxed{ \overline z=a-bi }. $$
  • Modulus: $$ \boxed{ |z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2} }. $$
  • Conjugate Product: $$ \boxed{ z\overline z=|z|^2 }. $$
  • Reciprocal: $$ \boxed{ z^{-1} = \frac{\overline z}{|z|^2} } $$ for $z\ne0$.
  • Division: $$ \boxed{ \frac{a+bi}{c+di} = \frac{(ac+bd)+(bc-ad)i}{c^2+d^2} } $$ when $c+di\ne0$.
  • Distance: $$ \boxed{ d(z_1,z_2)=|z_1-z_2| }. $$
  • Polar Form: $$ \boxed{ z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta) }. $$
  • Exponential Form: $$ \boxed{ z=re^{i\theta} }. $$
  • Euler's Formula: $$ \boxed{ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta }. $$
  • Rectangular-to-Polar Modulus: $$ \boxed{ r=\sqrt{a^2+b^2} }. $$
  • Polar-to-Rectangular: $$ \boxed{ a=r\cos\theta, \qquad b=r\sin\theta }. $$
  • Polar Multiplication: $$ \boxed{ r_1e^{i\theta_1}r_2e^{i\theta_2} = r_1r_2e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)} }. $$
  • Polar Division: $$ \boxed{ \frac{r_1e^{i\theta_1}} {r_2e^{i\theta_2}} = \frac{r_1}{r_2} e^{i(\theta_1-\theta_2)} }. $$
  • De Moivre's Theorem: $$ \boxed{ (re^{i\theta})^n = r^ne^{in\theta} }. $$
  • nth Roots: $$ \boxed{ w_k = r^{1/n} e^{\,i(\theta+2\pi k)/n}, \qquad k=0,\ldots,n-1 }. $$
  • Roots of Unity: $$ \boxed{ e^{2\pi ik/n}, \qquad k=0,\ldots,n-1 }. $$
  • Powers of i: Integer powers repeat with period $4$.
  • Triangle Inequality: $$ \boxed{ |z+w|\le|z|+|w| }. $$
  • Product Modulus: $$ \boxed{ |zw|=|z||w| }. $$
  • Quotient Modulus: $$ \boxed{ |z/w|=|z|/|w| } $$ for $w\ne0$.
  • Conjugate-Root Principle: Nonreal roots of real-coefficient polynomials occur in conjugate pairs.
  • Geometric Interpretation: $a+bi$ corresponds to the point $(a,b)$ in the complex plane.
  • Multiplicative Geometry: Complex multiplication multiplies moduli and adds arguments.
  • Common Error: Do not identify $\operatorname{Im}(a+bi)$ with $bi$; the imaginary part is $b$.
  • Common Error: Do not use $|a|+|b|$ as the modulus of $a+bi$.
  • Common Error: Do not treat complex multiplication as componentwise multiplication.
  • Common Error: Do not assume a complex argument is unique.
  • Common Error: Do not use $\arctan(b/a)$ without checking the quadrant.
  • Terminological Contrast: Standard form writes a complex number as $a+bi$. Polar form writes it as $r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)$. Exponential form writes it as $re^{i\theta}$. The modulus is its distance from the origin. The argument describes its direction from the positive real axis. The complex conjugate reflects the number across the real axis. De Moivre's theorem provides a compact rule for integer powers and helps derive formulas for complex roots.
