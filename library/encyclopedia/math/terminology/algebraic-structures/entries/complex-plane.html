Complex Plane

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

The complex plane is a two-dimensional coordinate plane used to represent complex numbers geometrically. A complex number:

$$ \boxed{ z=a+bi } $$

is represented by the point:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b) }. $$

The horizontal coordinate records the real part and the vertical coordinate records the imaginary part.

$$ \boxed{ a+bi \longleftrightarrow (a,b) }. $$

The horizontal axis is called the real axis, while the vertical axis is called the imaginary axis.

The complex plane is also historically called the Argand plane, after Jean-Robert Argand, whose geometric treatment helped popularize the representation of complex numbers as directed magnitudes in a plane.

The word plane emphasizes that the complex numbers, although forming a one-dimensional number system over themselves, can be represented as a two-dimensional real coordinate space:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb C \cong \mathbb R^2 \quad \text{as real vector spaces} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Coordinate Representation

For:

$$ z=a+bi, $$

the coordinate representation is:

$$ \boxed{ z\leftrightarrow(a,b) }. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Re}(z)=a, \qquad \operatorname{Im}(z)=b }. $$

Example

The complex number:

$$ 3+4i $$

is represented by the point:

$$ \boxed{ (3,4) }. $$

Likewise:

$$ -2-5i $$

corresponds to:

$$ \boxed{ (-2,-5) }. $$

The Real Axis

Complex numbers with imaginary part zero lie on the horizontal axis:

$$ z=a+0i=a. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \text{real axis} = \{a+0i:a\in\mathbb R\} }. $$

The Imaginary Axis

Complex numbers with real part zero lie on the vertical axis:

$$ z=bi. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \text{imaginary axis} = \{bi:b\in\mathbb R\} }. $$

The Origin

The complex number zero corresponds to:

$$ \boxed{ 0+0i \longleftrightarrow (0,0) }. $$

This is the origin of the complex plane.

Quadrants

As in the Cartesian plane, the real and imaginary axes divide the complex plane into four quadrants.

Quadrant Real Part Imaginary Part
I $a>0$ $b>0$
II $a<0$ $b>0$
III $a<0$ $b<0$
IV $a>0$ $b<0$

Complex Numbers as Position Vectors

A complex number may be visualized either as a point $(a,b)$ or as a directed vector from the origin to that point.

$$ \boxed{ z=a+bi \longleftrightarrow \begin{pmatrix} a\\ b \end{pmatrix} }. $$

This interpretation makes complex addition geometrically similar to vector addition.

Addition in the Complex Plane

If:

$$ z_1=a+bi, \qquad z_2=c+di, $$

then:

$$ z_1+z_2=(a+c)+(b+d)i. $$

Geometrically:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b)+(c,d) = (a+c,b+d) }. $$

This is ordinary vector addition in the plane.

Subtraction in the Complex Plane

$$ \boxed{ z_1-z_2 \longleftrightarrow (a-c,b-d) }. $$

The difference $z_1-z_2$ represents the displacement from $z_2$ to $z_1$.

Modulus as Distance from the Origin

For:

$$ z=a+bi, $$

the modulus is:

$$ \boxed{ |z| = \sqrt{a^2+b^2} }. $$

By the Pythagorean theorem, this is precisely the Euclidean distance from $(0,0)$ to $(a,b)$.

Distance between Two Complex Numbers

The distance between $z_1$ and $z_2$ is:

$$ \boxed{ d(z_1,z_2) = |z_1-z_2| }. $$

If:

$$ z_1=a+bi, \qquad z_2=c+di, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ |z_1-z_2| = \sqrt{(a-c)^2+(b-d)^2} }. $$

Argument

For a nonzero complex number $z$, an argument is an angle between the positive real axis and the ray from the origin to $z$.

$$ \boxed{ \arg z=\theta \pmod{2\pi} }. $$

Because full rotations lead to the same ray, the argument is not unique.

Arguments Differ by Full Rotations

$$ \boxed{ \theta+2\pi k, \qquad k\in\mathbb Z } $$

all represent the same angular direction.

Principal Argument

A single distinguished argument may be selected by restricting the angle to a chosen interval. One common convention is:

$$ \boxed{ -\pi<\operatorname{Arg}(z)\le\pi }. $$

Other branch conventions may be used.

Polar Coordinates in the Complex Plane

A nonzero complex number can be described by its modulus $r$ and argument $\theta$:

$$ \boxed{ z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta) }. $$

where:

$$ \boxed{ r=|z| }. $$

Rectangular and Polar Coordinates

The two representations are related by:

$$ \boxed{ a=r\cos\theta, \qquad b=r\sin\theta }. $$

Conversely:

$$ \boxed{ r=\sqrt{a^2+b^2} }. $$

Exponential Form

Euler's formula:

$$ e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta $$

gives:

$$ \boxed{ z=re^{i\theta} }. $$

The complex plane therefore links Cartesian geometry, polar geometry, trigonometry, and exponential functions.

Complex Conjugation as Reflection

If:

$$ z=a+bi, $$

then:

$$ \overline z=a-bi. $$

Geometrically:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b) \longmapsto (a,-b) }. $$

Thus complex conjugation is reflection across the real axis.

Negation as a Half-Turn

The additive inverse of:

$$ z=a+bi $$

is:

$$ -z=-a-bi. $$

In coordinates:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b) \longmapsto (-a,-b) }. $$

This is a rotation of $180^\circ$ about the origin.

Multiplication by i

Multiplication by $i$ sends:

$$ a+bi \longmapsto -b+ai. $$

In coordinates:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b) \longmapsto (-b,a) }. $$

This is a counterclockwise rotation of $90^\circ$.

Multiplication by −i

$$ \boxed{ (a,b) \longmapsto (b,-a) }. $$

This corresponds to a clockwise rotation of $90^\circ$.

Multiplication by a Positive Real Number

Multiplying $z$ by a positive real number $r$ scales its distance from the origin:

$$ \boxed{ z\mapsto rz }. $$

The direction remains unchanged while the modulus becomes:

$$ |rz|=r|z|. $$

General Complex Multiplication as Scaling and Rotation

Let:

$$ w=\rho e^{i\phi}. $$

Then multiplication:

$$ z\mapsto wz $$

scales distances from the origin by $\rho$ and rotates angles by $\phi$:

$$ \boxed{ re^{i\theta} \longmapsto \rho r e^{i(\theta+\phi)} }. $$

Unit Circle

The unit circle in the complex plane is the set:

$$ \boxed{ |z|=1 }. $$

In Cartesian form:

$$ a^2+b^2=1. $$

In exponential form:

$$ \boxed{ z=e^{i\theta} }. $$

Circle Centered at a Complex Number

The equation:

$$ \boxed{ |z-z_0|=r } $$

represents the circle of radius $r$ centered at the point $z_0$.

Disk

The inequality:

$$ \boxed{ |z-z_0|

represents the open disk centered at $z_0$ with radius $r$.

The closed disk is:

$$ \boxed{ |z-z_0|\le r }. $$

Exterior of a Circle

$$ \boxed{ |z-z_0|>r } $$

represents the region exterior to the circle of radius $r$ centered at $z_0$.

Vertical Lines

The equation:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Re}(z)=c } $$

represents a vertical line in the complex plane.

Horizontal Lines

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Im}(z)=c } $$

represents a horizontal line.

Half-Planes

Inequalities in the real or imaginary parts describe half-planes:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Re}(z)>0 } $$

describes the open right half-plane, while:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Im}(z)>0 } $$

describes the open upper half-plane.

Perpendicular Bisectors as Loci

The set of points equally distant from two fixed complex numbers $z_1$ and $z_2$ is:

$$ \boxed{ |z-z_1| = |z-z_2| }. $$

Geometrically, this is the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining $z_1$ and $z_2$.

Arguments as Rays

An equation such as:

$$ \boxed{ \arg z=\theta } $$

describes a ray beginning at the origin, excluding the origin itself because $\arg 0$ is undefined.

Angular Regions

Inequalities involving arguments can describe sectors:

$$ \boxed{ \alpha<\arg z<\beta }. $$

Such notation is common in complex analysis, though branch conventions must be understood.

Roots of Unity in the Complex Plane

The solutions to:

$$ z^n=1 $$

are:

$$ \boxed{ z_k=e^{2\pi ik/n}, \qquad k=0,1,\ldots,n-1 }. $$

These points are equally spaced on the unit circle and form the vertices of a regular $n$-gon.

Complex Roots as Rotationally Symmetric Points

More generally, the $n$th roots of a nonzero complex number lie on a circle and are separated by the constant angular interval:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{2\pi}{n} }. $$

Thus complex-root formulas have an immediate geometric interpretation.

Complex Functions as Plane Transformations

A complex function:

$$ w=f(z) $$

can be interpreted geometrically as mapping points from one complex plane to another.

This viewpoint becomes central in complex analysis.

Example: Translation

The function:

$$ f(z)=z+c $$

translates every point by the complex displacement $c$.

$$ \boxed{ z\mapsto z+c = \text{translation} }. $$

Example: Rotation and Scaling

The function:

$$ f(z)=az, \qquad a\ne0, $$

rotates and scales the plane according to the argument and modulus of $a$.

Example: Conjugation

The mapping:

$$ f(z)=\overline z $$

reflects the plane across the real axis.

Example: Squaring

If:

$$ z=re^{i\theta}, $$

then:

$$ z^2=r^2e^{2i\theta}. $$

Thus squaring squares the modulus and doubles the argument.

$$ \boxed{ r\mapsto r^2, \qquad \theta\mapsto2\theta }. $$

The Complex Plane Is Not a Graph of Imaginary versus Real Functions

A point in the complex plane represents one complex number. The horizontal and vertical coordinates are the two components of that number.

This differs from an ordinary graph of a real function $y=f(x)$, in which one coordinate is interpreted as an input and the other as its output.

$$ \boxed{ (a,b) \text{ in the complex plane} \leftrightarrow a+bi }. $$

Complex Plane versus Cartesian Plane

Geometrically, both are ordinary two-dimensional planes with perpendicular axes. Their interpretations differ.

Plane Horizontal Coordinate Vertical Coordinate
Cartesian plane $x$ $y$
Complex plane $\operatorname{Re}(z)$ $\operatorname{Im}(z)$

Complex Plane versus Polar Plane

The complex plane can be described using either rectangular coordinates $(a,b)$ or polar coordinates $(r,\theta)$.

These are two coordinate descriptions of the same geometric point:

$$ \boxed{ a+bi = r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta) }. $$

Complex Plane versus Riemann Sphere

In advanced complex analysis, the complex plane may be extended by adjoining a point at infinity:

$$ \boxed{ \widehat{\mathbb C} = \mathbb C\cup\{\infty\} }. $$

This extended complex plane can be represented geometrically by the Riemann sphere.

Common Misconception: Imaginary Numbers Cannot Be Plotted

Pure imaginary numbers lie directly on the imaginary axis.

For example:

$$ 4i \longleftrightarrow (0,4). $$

Common Misconception: The Imaginary Axis Contains Only i

The imaginary axis contains every complex number of the form:

$$ \boxed{ bi, \qquad b\in\mathbb R }. $$

Common Misconception: The Vertical Coordinate Is bi

The coordinate corresponding to $a+bi$ is $(a,b)$, not $(a,bi)$.

$$ \boxed{ a+bi \leftrightarrow (a,b) }. $$

Common Misconception: The Argument Is Determined Only by arctan(b/a)

The expression:

$$ \arctan\left(\frac ba\right) $$

does not by itself distinguish all quadrants. The signs of both $a$ and $b$ must be considered.

Common Misconception: The Origin Has Argument Zero

The complex number zero has modulus zero but no defined direction from the origin. Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \arg 0 \text{ is undefined} }. $$

Common Misconception: Complex Multiplication Is Ordinary Coordinatewise Multiplication

Multiplying:

$$ (a,b)(c,d) $$

as complex numbers does not simply produce $(ac,bd)$.

Instead:

$$ \boxed{ (a+bi)(c+di) = (ac-bd)+(ad+bc)i }. $$

This special multiplication gives the complex plane its rotation-and-scaling structure.

The terms complex plane and Argand plane are often used interchangeably. Complex plane is the more direct modern description, while Argand plane emphasizes the historical geometric representation.

A complex number may be said to be plotted, represented, or located in the complex plane. One may also speak of its position vector, modulus, and argument.

Defense Notation Advice: Label the horizontal axis $\operatorname{Re}(z)$ or real and the vertical axis $\operatorname{Im}(z)$ or imaginary when there is any risk of confusing the complex plane with an ordinary $xy$-graph. Remember that $a+bi$ corresponds to $(a,b)$, not $(a,bi)$. When using $\arg z$, state any principal-argument convention if a unique angle is required, and never assign an argument to $z=0$.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: The complex plane represents the complex number $a+bi$ by the point $(a,b)$.
  • Coordinate Correspondence: $$ \boxed{ a+bi\leftrightarrow(a,b) }. $$
  • Real Axis: Contains all numbers $a+0i$.
  • Imaginary Axis: Contains all numbers $bi$.
  • Origin: Represents $0+0i$.
  • Alternative Name: Argand plane.
  • Real-Part Coordinate: $$ \boxed{ x=\operatorname{Re}(z) }. $$
  • Imaginary-Part Coordinate: $$ \boxed{ y=\operatorname{Im}(z) }. $$
  • Modulus: $$ \boxed{ |z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2} }. $$
  • Distance: $$ \boxed{ d(z_1,z_2)=|z_1-z_2| }. $$
  • Argument: Gives the angular direction of a nonzero complex number from the positive real axis.
  • Polar Form: $$ \boxed{ z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta) }. $$
  • Exponential Form: $$ \boxed{ z=re^{i\theta} }. $$
  • Conjugation: Reflection across the real axis.
  • Negation: Rotation by $180^\circ$ about the origin.
  • Multiplication by i: Rotation counterclockwise by $90^\circ$.
  • General Multiplication: Multiplication by a nonzero complex number combines scaling and rotation.
  • Unit Circle: $$ \boxed{ |z|=1 }. $$
  • Circle: $$ \boxed{ |z-z_0|=r }. $$
  • Open Disk: $$ \boxed{ |z-z_0|
  • Closed Disk: $$ \boxed{ |z-z_0|\le r }. $$
  • Vertical Line: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Re}(z)=c }. $$
  • Horizontal Line: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Im}(z)=c }. $$
  • Right Half-Plane: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Re}(z)>0 }. $$
  • Upper Half-Plane: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Im}(z)>0 }. $$
  • Equidistant Locus: $$ \boxed{ |z-z_1|=|z-z_2| } $$ describes the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining $z_1$ and $z_2$.
  • Roots of Unity: Lie equally spaced on the unit circle.
  • Complex Functions: Functions $w=f(z)$ may be viewed as transformations from one complex plane to another.
  • Vector-Space View: $\mathbb C$ is naturally identifiable with $\mathbb R^2$ as a real vector space.
  • Complex-Analysis Extension: Adding the point at infinity produces the extended complex plane $\widehat{\mathbb C}$, represented by the Riemann sphere.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse the imaginary coordinate $b$ with the imaginary term $bi$.
  • Common Error: Do not assume pure imaginary numbers cannot be plotted.
  • Common Error: Do not assign an argument to the origin.
  • Common Error: Do not treat complex multiplication as componentwise multiplication of ordered pairs.
  • Terminological Contrast: The complex plane represents complex numbers geometrically using real and imaginary coordinates. The Argand plane is a historical name for the same representation. The Cartesian plane uses ordinary ordered pairs without necessarily interpreting them as complex numbers. Rectangular form writes $z=a+bi$. Polar form writes $z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)$. The modulus gives distance from the origin, while the argument gives angular direction. The Riemann sphere represents the extended complex plane $\mathbb C\cup\{\infty\}$.
