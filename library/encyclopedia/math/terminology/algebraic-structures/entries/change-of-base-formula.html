Change of Base Formula

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

The Change of Base Formula is an identity that rewrites a logarithm in one base using logarithms in another base.

If $b$, $k$, and $x$ satisfy the usual logarithm conditions, then:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b x = \frac{\log_k x}{\log_k b} }. $$

The word base refers to the base of the logarithm. The formula changes the notation used to calculate the logarithm without changing the underlying numerical value.

$$ \boxed{ \text{same logarithmic value} \quad \text{written in a different base} }. $$

The most common choices for the new base are $10$ and $e$, producing formulas involving common logarithms or natural logarithms.

Operational Nuance

Standard Formula

$$ \boxed{ \log_b x = \frac{\log_k x}{\log_k b} }. $$

The new base $k$ may be any valid logarithmic base.

Domain Restrictions

For real logarithms, the formula requires:

$$ \boxed{ x>0,\qquad b>0,\qquad b\ne1,\qquad k>0,\qquad k\ne1 }. $$

Natural-Log Form

Choosing $k=e$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b x = \frac{\ln x}{\ln b} }. $$

Common-Log Form

Choosing $k=10$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b x = \frac{\log x}{\log b} } $$

when $\log$ denotes the common logarithm.

Why the Formula Works

Let:

$$ y=\log_b x. $$

By the definition of logarithm:

$$ b^y=x. $$

Take the logarithm of both sides in some new base $k$:

$$ \log_k(b^y)=\log_k x. $$

Using the logarithm power rule:

$$ y\log_k b=\log_k x. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ y= \frac{\log_k x}{\log_k b} }. $$

Since $y=\log_b x$, the Change of Base Formula follows.

Example: Base 2

To evaluate:

$$ \log_2 7, $$

one may write:

$$ \boxed{ \log_2 7 = \frac{\ln 7}{\ln 2} }. $$

or:

$$ \boxed{ \log_2 7 = \frac{\log 7}{\log 2} }. $$

Exact Change versus Decimal Approximation

The expression:

$$ \frac{\ln 7}{\ln 2} $$

is an exact alternative representation of $\log_2 7$. A decimal value is only an approximation unless the logarithm simplifies exactly.

Example with an Exact Value

Consider:

$$ \log_4 64. $$

Using change of base:

$$ \frac{\ln64}{\ln4}. $$

Since:

$$ 64=4^3, $$

the value is:

$$ \boxed{ \log_4 64=3 }. $$

The New Base Does Not Affect the Answer

For any two valid replacement bases $k$ and $m$:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\log_k x}{\log_k b} = \frac{\log_m x}{\log_m b} }. $$

Both expressions represent the same number $\log_b x$.

Changing between Two Logarithm Bases

The formula may also be written:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b x = \log_k x\cdot\log_b k }. $$

This follows because:

$$ \log_b k = \frac{1}{\log_k b}. $$

Reciprocal Identity

$$ \boxed{ \log_b k = \frac{1}{\log_k b} }. $$

For example:

$$ \boxed{ \log_2 8 = \frac{1}{\log_8 2} }. $$

Product Identity between Bases

$$ \boxed{ \log_a b\cdot\log_b c = \log_a c }. $$

This follows immediately from changing both logarithms to a common base.

Three-Base Cycle

$$ \boxed{ \log_a b \cdot \log_b c \cdot \log_c a = 1 }. $$

Calculator Use

Many calculators provide buttons only for:

  • $\log x$ — base $10$,
  • $\ln x$ — base $e$.

The Change of Base Formula allows those functions to evaluate logarithms in arbitrary valid bases.

Example: Calculator Conversion

To calculate:

$$ \log_5 17, $$

use either:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\ln17}{\ln5} } $$

or:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\log17}{\log5} }. $$

Solving Exponential Equations

Suppose:

$$ 3^x=20. $$

Taking logarithms:

$$ x=\log_3 20. $$

Change of base gives:

$$ \boxed{ x= \frac{\ln20}{\ln3} }. $$

Connection to Exponential Inverses

Since logarithms are inverse functions of exponentials:

$$ \boxed{ y=\log_b x \iff b^y=x }. $$

The Change of Base Formula shows that all logarithmic bases are related through a constant scaling factor.

Scaling Relationship between Logarithm Functions

For fixed valid bases $b$ and $k$:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b x = \frac{1}{\log_k b}\log_k x }. $$

Therefore changing the base vertically rescales the logarithm graph by a constant.

Derivative of a General Logarithm

Since:

$$ \log_b x = \frac{\ln x}{\ln b}, $$

differentiating gives:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}\log_b x = \frac{1}{x\ln b} }. $$

Thus the Change of Base Formula provides a direct bridge from the derivative of $\ln x$ to the derivative of logarithms in arbitrary bases.

Integral Connection

The same identity yields:

$$ \boxed{ \int \log_b x\,dx = \frac{1}{\ln b} \int\ln x\,dx }. $$

Bases between 0 and 1

The formula remains valid when:

$$ \boxed{ 0

In this case $\ln b<0$, so the logarithm function is decreasing rather than increasing.

Why Base 1 Is Forbidden

If $b=1$, then:

$$ 1^y=1 $$

for every real $y$, so there is no one-to-one exponential function whose inverse could define $\log_1 x$.

The Change of Base Formula also exposes the problem:

$$ \ln1=0, $$

which would create division by zero.

Why Negative Real Bases Are Excluded

In the standard real logarithm function, the exponential base is required to be positive. Negative bases do not define a real-valued exponential function on all real exponents and therefore do not produce the ordinary real logarithm inverse.

Common Misconception: Reverse the Fraction

The correct formula is:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b x = \frac{\log x}{\log b} }. $$

It is not:

$$ \frac{\log b}{\log x}. $$

Memory Pattern

A useful memory rule is:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\text{log of argument}} {\text{log of old base}} }. $$

Common Misconception: The Numerator and Denominator May Use Different Bases

Both logarithms in the quotient must be evaluated using the same replacement base:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\log_k x}{\log_k b} }. $$

Common Misconception: Change of Base Changes the Logarithm’s Value

It changes the representation, not the mathematical value.

Common Misconception: Only Base 10 Can Be Used

Any valid new base $k$ can be used. Base $10$ and base $e$ are merely convenient because calculators and analysis commonly use them.

Common Misconception: log Without a Subscript Always Means the Same Base

The notation $\log x$ is context-dependent. In many elementary settings it means base $10$, while in some higher mathematical contexts it may mean the natural logarithm. Explicit notation removes ambiguity.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\log_b x=\frac{\log_k x}{\log_k b}$ "Log base b of x equals log base k of x divided by log base k of b."
$\log_b x=\frac{\ln x}{\ln b}$ "Log base b of x equals natural log of x divided by natural log of b."
$\log_b x=\frac{\log x}{\log b}$ "Log base b of x equals common log of x divided by common log of b."
$\log_a b=\frac1{\log_b a}$ "Log base a of b is the reciprocal of log base b of a."
$\log_a b\log_b c=\log_a c$ "Log base a of b times log base b of c equals log base a of c."

In speech, the phrase change of base means rewriting a logarithm in a different base, usually so that the new form is easier to calculate or analyze.

Defense Notation Advice: Keep the same replacement base in the numerator and denominator, place the logarithm of the original argument on top, and place the logarithm of the original base on the bottom. State the base convention when writing an unsubscripted $\log$.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Formula: $$ \boxed{ \log_b x = \frac{\log_k x}{\log_k b} }. $$
  • Natural-Log Form: $$ \boxed{ \log_b x = \frac{\ln x}{\ln b} }. $$
  • Common-Log Form: $$ \boxed{ \log_b x = \frac{\log x}{\log b} }. $$
  • Real-Domain Conditions: $x>0$, $b>0$, $b\ne1$, $k>0$, and $k\ne1$.
  • Reciprocal Identity: $$ \boxed{ \log_a b = \frac1{\log_b a} }. $$
  • Composition Identity: $$ \boxed{ \log_a b\cdot\log_b c = \log_a c }. $$
  • Three-Base Cycle: $$ \boxed{ \log_a b\log_b c\log_c a=1 }. $$
  • Calculator Application: Arbitrary-base logarithms can be evaluated using $\ln$ or common $\log$.
  • Exponential Equations: $b^x=c$ implies $$ \boxed{ x= \frac{\ln c}{\ln b} }. $$
  • Derivative Connection: $$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}\log_b x = \frac1{x\ln b} }. $$
  • Graph Interpretation: Logarithms of different bases differ by a constant vertical scaling factor.
  • Base between 0 and 1: The formula still applies, but the logarithm function is decreasing.
  • Common Error: Do not reverse the quotient.
  • Common Error: Do not use different replacement bases in the numerator and denominator.
  • Common Error: Do not use base $1$.
  • Common Error: Do not allow a nonpositive argument in the standard real logarithm.
  • Common Error: Do not assume changing the base changes the value of the logarithm.
  • Terminological Contrast: Change of Base Formula rewrites one logarithmic base in terms of another. Common logarithm usually means base $10$. Natural logarithm means base $e$. Logarithm base is the exponential base whose inverse defines the logarithm. Argument is the positive quantity inside the logarithm. Exponential equation is an equation in which the unknown appears in an exponent and is often solved using logarithms.
