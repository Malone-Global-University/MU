Concave Down

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

A graph is concave down on an interval when its slope decreases as the independent variable increases. Visually, the graph bends downward like an upside-down bowl or arch.

$$ \boxed{ \text{concave down} \approx \text{decreasing slope} }. $$

For a twice-differentiable function, the most familiar elementary-calculus test is:

$$ \boxed{ f''(x)<0 \Rightarrow f\text{ is concave down} }. $$

The adjective concave comes from the idea of a hollow or inward-curving shape. The directional word down distinguishes this curvature from concave up, which bends in the opposite direction.

In much of mathematical analysis and optimization, a function called simply concave corresponds to what elementary calculus commonly calls concave down.

Operational Nuance

Second-Derivative Test

If $f$ is twice differentiable on an interval and:

$$ \boxed{ f''(x)<0 } $$

throughout that interval, then the graph is concave down there.

More generally, under standard regularity assumptions:

$$ \boxed{ f''(x)\le0 } $$

throughout an interval is associated with non-strict concavity.

What the Second Derivative Measures

The first derivative:

$$ f'(x) $$

measures instantaneous slope. The second derivative:

$$ f''(x) $$

measures how that slope is changing.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ f''(x)<0 \Rightarrow f'(x)\text{ is decreasing} }. $$

Decreasing Slope

Concave down does not mean the function itself must be decreasing. It means its slope is decreasing.

For example, the slopes might progress approximately as:

$$ 5,\quad4,\quad3,\quad2,\quad1. $$

The function is still increasing because the slopes are positive, but it is concave down because those slopes are becoming smaller.

Concave Down While Increasing

Consider:

$$ f(x)=\sqrt{x}, \qquad x>0. $$

Its derivatives are:

$$ f'(x)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}>0 $$

and:

$$ f''(x) = -\frac{1}{4x^{3/2}}<0. $$

Thus $\sqrt{x}$ is increasing and concave down on $(0,\infty)$.

Concave Down While Decreasing

Consider:

$$ f(x)=-e^x. $$

Then:

$$ f'(x)=-e^x<0 $$

and:

$$ f''(x)=-e^x<0. $$

Therefore the function is both decreasing and concave down.

Concave Down Quadratic

A basic example is:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=-x^2 }. $$

Since:

$$ f''(x)=-2<0, $$

the graph is concave down everywhere.

Parabolas

For:

$$ f(x)=ax^2+bx+c, $$

the second derivative is:

$$ f''(x)=2a. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ a<0 \Rightarrow \text{parabola is concave down} }. $$

Opening Downward

For quadratic functions, opens downward and concave down describe closely related geometric behavior.

For more general functions, however, concave down is the broader calculus term and should not be replaced by parabola-specific language.

Tangent-Line Interpretation

For a differentiable concave-down function, the tangent line at a point typically lies on or above the graph throughout the relevant interval.

$$ \boxed{ f(y) \le f(x)+f'(x)(y-x) }. $$

This is a standard tangent-line characterization of a concave function.

Tangent-Line Approximation

If $f$ is concave down near $x=a$, its tangent-line approximation:

$$ L(x)=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a) $$

tends locally to overestimate the function when the appropriate concavity conditions hold.

$$ \boxed{ f(x)\le L(x) } $$

on an interval where the supporting tangent inequality applies.

Secant-Line Interpretation

A concave-down graph lies on or above its secant chords.

For $x_1,x_2$ in an interval and $0\le\lambda\le1$:

$$ \boxed{ f\bigl(\lambda x_1+(1-\lambda)x_2\bigr) \ge \lambda f(x_1)+(1-\lambda)f(x_2) }. $$

Midpoint Interpretation

At the midpoint:

$$ \boxed{ f\left(\frac{x_1+x_2}{2}\right) \ge \frac{f(x_1)+f(x_2)}{2} }. $$

Thus the function value at the midpoint lies on or above the midpoint of the secant segment.

Concave Down versus Concave Up

Concave Down Concave Up
$f''(x)<0$ $f''(x)>0$
$f'(x)$ decreases $f'(x)$ increases
Graph lies above secant chords Graph lies below secant chords
Tangent lines tend to lie above graph Tangent lines tend to lie below graph

Concavity and Critical Points

If:

$$ f'(c)=0 $$

and:

$$ f''(c)<0, $$

then the second derivative test identifies $c$ as a local maximum:

$$ \boxed{ f'(c)=0,\quad f''(c)<0 \Rightarrow c\text{ is a local maximum} }. $$

Why Concavity Supports a Maximum

Near a stationary point on a concave-down graph, slopes typically change from positive to negative:

$$ +\quad\longrightarrow\quad0\quad\longrightarrow\quad-. $$

This creates a local peak.

Strict Concavity and Global Maxima

On a convex domain, a differentiable strictly concave function can have at most one stationary point, and if such a point exists under suitable conditions it is the unique global maximizer.

$$ \boxed{ \text{strict concavity} + \nabla f=0 \Rightarrow \text{unique global maximum} } $$

Inflection Points

An inflection point occurs where a graph changes concavity.

For example:

$$ f(x)=x^3 $$

has:

$$ f''(x)=6x. $$

Therefore:

$$ \begin{aligned} x<0&\Rightarrow f''(x)<0 &&\text{concave down},\\ x>0&\Rightarrow f''(x)>0 &&\text{concave up}. \end{aligned} $$

Concavity changes at $x=0$.

f'' = 0 Identifies Only a Candidate

Solving:

$$ f''(x)=0 $$

can identify possible points where concavity changes, but it does not by itself prove that an inflection point exists.

Example: No Concavity Change

Let:

$$ f(x)=x^4. $$

Then:

$$ f''(x)=12x^2. $$

Although:

$$ f''(0)=0, $$

$f''$ does not change from negative to positive or positive to negative at $0$. Therefore $0$ is not an inflection point.

Concavity Can Change Where f'' Does Not Exist

An inflection point need not require the second derivative to exist at the point itself. What matters fundamentally is a change in concavity across the point.

Thus second-derivative tests are powerful tools but should not be mistaken for the definition of an inflection point.

Intervals of Concavity

Concavity is usually stated on intervals:

$$ \boxed{ f\text{ is concave down on }(a,b) }. $$

This is more precise than saying merely that a graph is "concave down at $x=c$," because concavity describes curvature over a neighborhood or interval.

Using a Sign Chart

A common method for determining concavity is:

  1. compute $f''(x)$,
  2. identify points where $f''(x)=0$ or does not exist,
  3. divide the domain into intervals,
  4. determine the sign of $f''$ on each interval.

Where:

$$ f''<0, $$

the graph is concave down.

Example with Changing Concavity

Consider:

$$ f(x)=x^4-6x^2. $$

Then:

$$ f''(x)=12x^2-12=12(x^2-1). $$

The second derivative is negative when:

$$ x^2<1. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ f\text{ is concave down on }(-1,1) }. $$

Motion Interpretation

If position is modeled by $s(t)$, then:

$$ s'(t)=v(t) $$

and:

$$ s''(t)=a(t). $$

Thus a position graph is concave down when:

$$ \boxed{ a(t)<0 }. $$

This says the velocity is decreasing, not necessarily that the object is moving backward.

Projectile Motion

Ignoring air resistance near Earth's surface, vertical position may be modeled as:

$$ h(t)=h_0+v_0t-\frac12gt^2. $$

Then:

$$ h''(t)=-g<0. $$

Therefore the height-versus-time graph is concave down throughout the modeled motion.

Economics Interpretation

Concave-down functions frequently model diminishing marginal gains.

If production $Q(x)$ satisfies:

$$ Q'(x)>0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad Q''(x)<0, $$

then output is increasing, but each additional unit of input contributes a smaller marginal increase.

Logarithmic Function

The natural logarithm:

$$ f(x)=\ln x $$

satisfies:

$$ f''(x)=-\frac1{x^2}<0 \qquad(x>0). $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \ln x \text{ is concave down on }(0,\infty) }. $$

Sine Function

For:

$$ f(x)=\sin x, $$

the second derivative is:

$$ f''(x)=-\sin x. $$

Thus the sine graph is concave down wherever:

$$ \sin x>0. $$

For example:

$$ \boxed{ (0,\pi) } $$

is one interval of concavity down.

Concavity under Vertical Scaling

If $f$ is twice differentiable and:

$$ g(x)=af(x), $$

then:

$$ g''(x)=af''(x). $$

If $a>0$, the concavity direction is preserved. If $a<0$, the sign reverses and concavity switches.

$$ \boxed{ a<0 \Rightarrow \text{concave down and concave up interchange} }. $$

Horizontal Scaling and Concavity

If:

$$ g(x)=f(bx), $$

then:

$$ g''(x)=b^2f''(bx). $$

Since $b^2>0$ for $b\ne0$, horizontal scaling preserves the sign of the second derivative at corresponding points.

Reflection across the x-Axis

If:

$$ g(x)=-f(x), $$

then:

$$ g''(x)=-f''(x). $$

Therefore reflection across the $x$-axis changes concave down portions into concave up portions and vice versa.

Vertical Translation

If:

$$ g(x)=f(x)+k, $$

then:

$$ g''(x)=f''(x). $$

Vertical translation does not alter concavity.

Horizontal Translation

If:

$$ g(x)=f(x-h), $$

the concavity pattern is shifted horizontally but otherwise preserved.

Concave Down Does Not Mean Below the x-Axis

A graph may lie entirely above the $x$-axis and still be concave down.

For example:

$$ f(x)=100-x^2 $$

is concave down everywhere because:

$$ f''(x)=-2. $$

Concave Down Does Not Mean Decreasing

The sign of $f'$ determines increasing or decreasing behavior. The sign of $f''$ describes concavity.

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} f'(x)&>0 &&\Rightarrow\text{increasing},\\ f''(x)&<0 &&\Rightarrow\text{concave down}. \end{aligned} } $$

Both properties can occur simultaneously.

Concave Down Does Not Require a Maximum

A function may be concave down on an interval without attaining a maximum there.

For example:

$$ f(x)=\ln x $$

is concave down on $(0,\infty)$ but has no global maximum on that domain.

Non-Strict Concavity

Under the broader mathematical definition, affine functions are both concave and convex because their secant lines coincide with their graphs.

Thus:

$$ f(x)=mx+b $$

has:

$$ f''(x)=0. $$

Elementary calculus teachers may avoid describing a straight line as concave down, while analysis commonly includes affine functions among non-strictly concave functions. This is a convention worth distinguishing.

Common Misconception: Concave Down Means Negative Slope

Negative slope means:

$$ f'(x)<0. $$

Concave down means the slope is decreasing, commonly indicated by:

$$ f''(x)<0. $$

These are different conditions.

Common Misconception: f'' < 0 Means f < 0

The inequality:

$$ f''(x)<0 $$

describes curvature, not the sign of the original function.

Common Misconception: f'' = 0 Automatically Means Inflection Point

A point is an inflection point only when concavity changes across it.

$$ \boxed{ f''(c)=0 \not\Rightarrow c\text{ is an inflection point} }. $$

Common Misconception: Concavity Is Determined at One Isolated Point

Concavity describes behavior over an interval or neighborhood. A single second-derivative value may contribute evidence but does not by itself describe the curvature of an entire region.

Common Misconception: Concave Down Always Means the Graph Is Falling

A concave-down graph may rise while flattening, fall while steepening downward, or pass through a maximum and do both on different parts of the interval.

Common Misconception: Concave Down and Concave Are Always Different Terms

In elementary calculus, concave down is common. In analysis, optimization, and economics, the same curvature is often called simply concave.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$f''(x)<0$ "f double prime of x is negative, so the graph is concave down."
$f''(x)\le0$ "The second derivative is nonpositive."
$f'(x)$ decreasing "The slopes are decreasing as x increases."
$f(y)\le f(x)+f'(x)(y-x)$ "The graph lies below its tangent line."
$f(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)\ge\lambda f(x)+(1-\lambda)f(y)$ "The function lies above the chord joining the endpoint values."
$f'(c)=0,\ f''(c)<0$ "c is a stationary point with negative second derivative, so the second derivative test gives a local maximum."

In spoken mathematics, one commonly says that a graph is concave down on the interval, rather than simply saying it is concave down "at" a single point.

The phrase bends downward can be useful intuition, but the slope is decreasing is usually more mathematically informative.

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish the sign of $f'$ from the sign of $f''$. State concavity on intervals, not merely at isolated points. Use $f''<0$ as a standard sufficient test for strict downward concavity when the second derivative exists, and verify an actual change of concavity before naming an inflection point. When communicating across calculus, analysis, optimization, or economics, note that concave down is frequently shortened to concave.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: Concave down describes a graph whose slope decreases as the independent variable increases.
  • Standard Calculus Test: $$ \boxed{ f''(x)<0 \Rightarrow f\text{ is concave down} }. $$
  • Non-Strict Analytic Form: $$ \boxed{ f''(x)\le0 } $$ is associated with concavity under suitable differentiability assumptions.
  • First-Derivative Interpretation: $f'$ is decreasing or nonincreasing.
  • Secant Interpretation: A concave-down graph lies on or above its secant chords.
  • Tangent Interpretation: A differentiable concave-down graph lies on or below its supporting tangent lines.
  • Quadratic Case: $ax^2+bx+c$ is concave down when $a<0$.
  • Stationary-Point Test: $$ \boxed{ f'(c)=0,\quad f''(c)<0 \Rightarrow \text{local maximum at }c }. $$
  • Inflection Point: Requires a change between concave-down and concave-up behavior.
  • Candidate Point: $f''(c)=0$ alone does not prove an inflection point.
  • Motion: A position graph is concave down where acceleration is negative.
  • Economics: Increasing concave-down functions often represent diminishing marginal returns.
  • Logarithm: $\ln x$ is concave down on $(0,\infty)$.
  • Square Root: $\sqrt{x}$ is increasing and concave down on $(0,\infty)$.
  • Sine: $\sin x$ is concave down wherever $\sin x>0$.
  • Positive Vertical Scaling: Preserves concavity direction.
  • Negative Vertical Scaling: Reverses concavity direction.
  • Vertical Translation: Does not change concavity.
  • Horizontal Translation: Shifts intervals of concavity without changing their curvature type.
  • Horizontal Scaling: Preserves the sign of the second derivative at corresponding points because the scale factor enters as $b^2$.
  • Analysis Terminology: Concave often means what elementary calculus calls concave down.
  • Concave-Up Contrast: Concave up corresponds to increasing slope and typically $f''>0$.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse concave down with decreasing.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse a negative second derivative with negative function values.
  • Common Error: Do not infer an inflection point solely from $f''=0$.
  • Common Error: Do not assume a concave-down function must attain a maximum.
  • Terminological Contrast: Concave down means the graph's slope decreases as $x$ increases and is commonly associated with $f''<0$. Concave up means the slope increases and is commonly associated with $f''>0$. In analysis, a concave function normally corresponds to the concave-down orientation. A strictly concave function satisfies a strict chord inequality. An inflection point marks a change in concavity rather than merely a zero of the second derivative.
