Transpose of a Matrix

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

The transpose of a matrix is formed by interchanging the rows and columns of the original matrix.

$$ \boxed{ (A^T)_{ij}=A_{ji} }. $$

The word transpose means to exchange or move positions. In matrix language, the entry in row $i$, column $j$ becomes the entry in row $j$, column $i$.

Operational Nuance

Basic Example

$$ A= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3\\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix} $$

has transpose:

$$ \boxed{ A^T= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4\\ 2 & 5\\ 3 & 6 \end{bmatrix} }. $$

Dimension Reversal

If:

$$ A\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times n}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ A^T\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times m} }. $$

Transpose Twice

$$ \boxed{ (A^T)^T=A }. $$

Transposition is therefore an involution: applying it twice returns the original matrix.

Addition

$$ \boxed{ (A+B)^T=A^T+B^T }. $$

Scalar Multiplication

$$ \boxed{ (cA)^T=cA^T }. $$

Product Rule

Transposition reverses the order of multiplication:

$$ \boxed{ (AB)^T=B^TA^T }. $$

This reversal is essential because matrix multiplication is generally not commutative.

Inverse

If $A$ is invertible:

$$ \boxed{ (A^{-1})^T=(A^T)^{-1} }. $$

Determinant

For a square matrix:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A^T)=\det(A) }. $$

Trace

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{tr}(A^T)=\operatorname{tr}(A) }. $$

Symmetric Matrices

A square matrix is symmetric when it equals its transpose:

$$ \boxed{ A^T=A }. $$

Skew-Symmetric Matrices

A square matrix is skew-symmetric when:

$$ \boxed{ A^T=-A }. $$

Row and Column Vectors

Transposition converts a row vector into a column vector and vice versa:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} a & b & c \end{bmatrix}^T = \begin{bmatrix} a\\ b\\ c \end{bmatrix} }. $$

Dot Products

For real column vectors $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y} }. $$

Common Misconception

Transposing a matrix does not change the values of its entries or reverse their numerical signs. It changes only their row-column positions.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$A^T$ "A transpose," or "the transpose of A."
$(A^T)_{ij}=A_{ji}$ "Entry i j of A transpose equals entry j i of A."
$(A^T)^T=A$ "The transpose of the transpose is A."
$(AB)^T=B^TA^T$ "The transpose of a product reverses the factor order."
$A^T=A$ "A is symmetric."
$A^T=-A$ "A is skew-symmetric."

The notation $A^T$ is common, although some texts use $A^\mathsf{T}$ or other typographic variants. In complex linear algebra, the conjugate transpose is a different operation and is commonly written $A^*$ or $A^H$.

Defense Notation Advice: Track dimensions before and after transposition and remember that products reverse order. Do not confuse ordinary transpose with inverse or conjugate transpose.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: Interchange rows and columns.
  • Entry Rule: $(A^T)_{ij}=A_{ji}$.
  • Dimensions: An $m\times n$ matrix becomes an $n\times m$ matrix.
  • Double Transpose: $(A^T)^T=A$.
  • Addition: $(A+B)^T=A^T+B^T$.
  • Scalar Multiplication: $(cA)^T=cA^T$.
  • Products: $(AB)^T=B^TA^T$.
  • Inverse: $(A^{-1})^T=(A^T)^{-1}$ when $A$ is invertible.
  • Determinant: Transposition preserves determinant.
  • Trace: Transposition preserves trace.
  • Symmetric Matrices: Characterized by $A^T=A$.
  • Skew-Symmetric Matrices: Characterized by $A^T=-A$.
  • Vectors: Converts row vectors to column vectors and vice versa.
  • Inner Products: For real column vectors, $\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y}$ represents the standard dot product.
  • Complex Matrices: Ordinary transpose does not conjugate entries; conjugate transpose is a separate operation.
  • Common Error: Failing to reverse multiplication order in $(AB)^T$.
  • Common Error: Confusing $A^T$ with $A^{-1}$.
  • Common Error: Negating entries when transposing.
  • Common Error: Treating transpose and conjugate transpose as interchangeable for complex matrices.
  • Core Interpretation: The transpose reflects a matrix across its main diagonal in the sense of exchanging row and column indices: $$ \boxed{ (A^T)_{ij}=A_{ji} }. $$ This simple index exchange underlies symmetry, vector orientation, matrix products, inner products, and many structural identities in linear algebra.
