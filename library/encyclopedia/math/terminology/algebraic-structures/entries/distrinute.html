Distribute

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

In algebra, to distribute means to apply an operation to each term of a grouped expression according to a distributive law.

The most familiar example is multiplication distributed over addition:

$$ \boxed{ a(b+c)=ab+ac }. $$

Multiplication also distributes over subtraction:

$$ \boxed{ a(b-c)=ab-ac }. $$

The verb distribute comes from the idea of spreading or assigning something across several recipients. In:

$$ 3(x+4), $$

the factor $3$ is distributed to both terms inside the parentheses:

$$ \boxed{ 3(x+4)=3x+12 }. $$

The process is justified by the distributive property; it is not merely a procedural convention.

Operational Nuance

Basic Distribution over Addition

$$ \boxed{ a(b+c)=ab+ac }. $$

The outside factor $a$ multiplies every term inside the grouped sum.

Example

$$ 5(x+2) = 5x+10. $$

Distribution over Subtraction

$$ \boxed{ a(b-c)=ab-ac }. $$

For example:

$$ 4(x-3) = \boxed{4x-12}. $$

Negative Factors

A negative factor must also be distributed to every term:

$$ -2(x+5) = -2x-10. $$

Negative Sign as Multiplication by $-1$

A minus sign in front of parentheses can be interpreted as multiplication by $-1$:

$$ -(a+b) = (-1)(a+b). $$

Distributing gives:

$$ \boxed{ -(a+b)=-a-b }. $$

Subtracting a Difference

$$ -(a-b) = -a+b. $$

More generally:

$$ \boxed{ x-(a-b)=x-a+b }. $$

More Than Two Terms

Distribution extends across any finite sum:

$$ \boxed{ a(b+c+d) = ab+ac+ad }. $$

For example:

$$ 2(x+y+3) = 2x+2y+6. $$

Variable Factors

The outside factor may itself contain variables:

$$ x(x+4) = \boxed{x^2+4x}. $$

Monomial Distribution

A monomial can be distributed across a polynomial:

$$ 3x^2(2x^3-5x+4). $$

Multiplying each term gives:

$$ \boxed{ 6x^5-15x^3+12x^2 }. $$

Exponent Laws During Distribution

When multiplying monomials, coefficients multiply and exponents of like bases add:

$$ x^m x^n=x^{m+n}. $$

Thus:

$$ 2x^3(5x^4) = 10x^7. $$

Distribution with Fractions

Fractional factors distribute normally:

$$ \frac12(6x+8) = \boxed{3x+4}. $$

Distribution with Radicals

A radical expression can be used as a factor:

$$ \sqrt2(x+3) = \boxed{x\sqrt2+3\sqrt2}. $$

Distribution from the Right

In commutative arithmetic, one may also write:

$$ (a+b)c=ac+bc. $$

In more general algebraic structures, left and right distributive laws are distinguished:

$$ \boxed{ a(b+c)=ab+ac } $$
$$ \boxed{ (a+b)c=ac+bc }. $$

Distribution in Matrix Algebra

Matrix multiplication distributes over matrix addition whenever dimensions are compatible:

$$ \boxed{ A(B+C)=AB+AC } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ (A+B)C=AC+BC }. $$

Matrix multiplication is generally not commutative, so these left and right distributive laws should not be conflated.

Distribution with Functions

Scalar multiplication distributes over addition of functions:

$$ \boxed{ a(f+g)=af+ag }. $$

Pointwise:

$$ a(f(x)+g(x)) = af(x)+ag(x). $$

Distribution in Vector Spaces

Scalar multiplication satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ a(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}) = a\mathbf{u}+a\mathbf{v} } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ (a+b)\mathbf{v} = a\mathbf{v}+b\mathbf{v} }. $$

Distribution and Expansion

Distribution is one of the principal operations used to expand algebraic expressions.

For example:

$$ (x+2)(x+5) $$

can be expanded by repeated distribution:

$$ x(x+5)+2(x+5) $$

and then:

$$ x^2+5x+2x+10 = \boxed{x^2+7x+10}. $$

FOIL as a Special Case

The mnemonic FOIL for multiplying two binomials is not a separate algebraic law. It is a shortcut for repeated use of distribution:

$$ \boxed{ (a+b)(c+d) = ac+ad+bc+bd }. $$

Distribution and Factoring Are Reverse Processes

Distribution converts:

$$ a(b+c) $$

into:

$$ ab+ac. $$

Factoring reverses this:

$$ \boxed{ ab+ac=a(b+c) }. $$

Common Factor Recognition

In:

$$ 6x+12, $$

the common factor $6$ can be extracted:

$$ 6x+12 = \boxed{6(x+2)}. $$

Distribution Across an Equation

If:

$$ 3(x+4)=21, $$

distribution gives:

$$ 3x+12=21. $$

This is an equivalent equation because the expression on one side has merely been rewritten using a valid identity.

Distribution Can Sometimes Be Avoided

Although distribution is valid, it is not always the most efficient step. For:

$$ 5(x-2)=35, $$

dividing both sides by $5$ first gives:

$$ x-2=7, $$

which may be simpler than expanding first.

Do Not Distribute Exponents over Addition

Exponentiation does not generally distribute over addition:

$$ \boxed{ (a+b)^2\neq a^2+b^2 } $$

in general.

Instead:

$$ \boxed{ (a+b)^2 = a^2+2ab+b^2 }. $$

Do Not Distribute Radicals over Addition

In general:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt{a+b} \neq \sqrt a+\sqrt b }. $$

Do Not Distribute Division over a Sum in the Denominator

In general:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{a}{b+c} \neq \frac{a}{b} + \frac{a}{c} }. $$

However, division of an entire sum by a common nonzero denominator may be separated:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{a+b}{c} = \frac{a}{c} + \frac{b}{c}, \qquad c\neq0 }. $$

This works because division by $c$ is multiplication by $1/c$:

$$ \frac1c(a+b) = \frac ac+\frac bc. $$

Distribution and Absolute Value

Absolute value does not distribute over addition:

$$ \boxed{ |a+b| \neq |a|+|b| } $$

in general.

But it is multiplicative over products:

$$ \boxed{ |ab|=|a||b| }. $$

Distribution in Modular Arithmetic

Modular multiplication also respects distributivity:

$$ \boxed{ a(b+c) \equiv ab+ac \pmod n }. $$

Distribution in Rings

In abstract algebra, distributive laws connect multiplication and addition in rings:

$$ \boxed{ a(b+c)=ab+ac } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ (a+b)c=ac+bc }. $$

These laws remain fundamental even when multiplication is not commutative.

Common Misconception: To distribute does not mean to apply every visible operation separately to every term. Distribution is valid only when an actual distributive law applies. Multiplication distributes over addition, but exponentiation, radicals, and many other operations do not.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$a(b+c)=ab+ac$ "Distribute a across b plus c."
$3(x+4)=3x+12$ "Distribute three to both terms."
$-(x-5)=-x+5$ "Distribute negative one across the parentheses."
$(a+b)c=ac+bc$ "Distribute c across the sum from the right."
$A(B+C)=AB+AC$ "A distributes over B plus C."
$ab+ac=a(b+c)$ "Factor out the common factor a."
$(a+b)^2\neq a^2+b^2$ "Squaring does not distribute over addition."

In classroom language, instructions such as:

$$ \boxed{\text{"distribute the 4"}} $$

ordinarily mean that $4$ is to multiply every term inside the associated grouping:

$$ 4(x+y-2) = 4x+4y-8. $$

The more formal statement is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"apply the distributive property of multiplication over addition"} }. $$

The phrase distribute the negative sign is shorthand for multiplying each term in the group by $-1$.

Defense Notation Advice: When distributing a negative factor, write the multiplication explicitly when signs are complicated:

$$ -3(x-2y+5) = (-3)x+(-3)(-2y)+(-3)(5) $$

before simplifying to:

$$ \boxed{-3x+6y-15}. $$

This reduces sign errors and makes clear that every term receives the factor.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Basic Meaning: To distribute is to apply a factor to each term of a grouped sum or difference according to a distributive law.
  • Left Distribution: $$ \boxed{ a(b+c)=ab+ac }. $$
  • Right Distribution: $$ \boxed{ (a+b)c=ac+bc }. $$
  • Subtraction: $$ \boxed{ a(b-c)=ab-ac }. $$
  • Three Terms: $$ \boxed{ a(b+c+d)=ab+ac+ad }. $$
  • Negative Factor: $$ \boxed{ -a(b+c)=-ab-ac }. $$
  • Negative One: $$ \boxed{ -(a+b)=-a-b }. $$
  • Negative Difference: $$ \boxed{ -(a-b)=-a+b }. $$
  • Numerical Example: $$ \boxed{ 6(3+2)=18+12=30 }. $$
  • Variable Example: $$ \boxed{ 5(x+7)=5x+35 }. $$
  • Polynomial Example: $$ \boxed{ 2x(x^2-3x+4) = 2x^3-6x^2+8x }. $$
  • Fraction Example: $$ \boxed{ \frac13(6x+9)=2x+3 }. $$
  • Decimal Example: $$ 0.5(8x+6)=4x+3. $$
  • Radical Factor: $$ \sqrt3(x+2)=x\sqrt3+2\sqrt3. $$
  • Expansion: Distribution is a primary method for expanding products involving sums.
  • Factoring: Factoring reverses distribution: $$ \boxed{ ab+ac=a(b+c) }. $$
  • Greatest Common Factor: Factoring out a GCF uses the distributive property in reverse.
  • Two Binomials: $$ \boxed{ (a+b)(c+d)=ac+ad+bc+bd }. $$
  • FOIL: FOIL is a mnemonic for repeated distribution, not an independent algebraic law.
  • Square of a Binomial: $$ \boxed{ (a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2 }. $$
  • Difference of Squares Connection: $$ (a-b)(a+b)=a^2-b^2. $$
  • Equation Simplification: Distribution can remove parentheses before combining like terms.
  • Inequalities: Distribution inside one side of an inequality is simply an equivalent algebraic rewrite and does not reverse the inequality sign.
  • Negative Multiplication Contrast: The inequality sign reverses only when both sides of an inequality are multiplied or divided by a negative quantity, not merely because a negative factor is distributed within an expression.
  • Like Terms: Distribution and combining like terms are distinct operations.
  • Example of Distinction: $$ 2(x+3)+5x = 2x+6+5x = 7x+6. $$ The first step distributes; the second combines like terms.
  • Order of Operations: Distribution is an algebraic rewriting tool and can sometimes simplify a grouped multiplication before ordinary arithmetic is completed.
  • Expression Equivalence: $$ a(b+c) $$ and: $$ ab+ac $$ are equivalent expressions wherever the operations are defined.
  • Identity: The distributive property is an identity, meaning it is valid for all permitted values in the relevant algebraic structure.
  • Matrix Left Distribution: $$ \boxed{ A(B+C)=AB+AC }. $$
  • Matrix Right Distribution: $$ \boxed{ (A+B)C=AC+BC }. $$
  • Matrix Caution: Matrix multiplication distributes despite generally not being commutative.
  • Vectors: $$ \boxed{ a(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}) = a\mathbf{u}+a\mathbf{v} }. $$
  • Scalars over Vectors: $$ \boxed{ (a+b)\mathbf{v} = a\mathbf{v}+b\mathbf{v} }. $$
  • Functions: $$ \boxed{ a(f+g)=af+ag }. $$
  • Linear Transformations: A linear map satisfies: $$ \boxed{ T(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}) = T(\mathbf{u})+T(\mathbf{v}) }. $$
  • Linear Combination: More generally: $$ \boxed{ T(a\mathbf{u}+b\mathbf{v}) = aT(\mathbf{u})+bT(\mathbf{v}) }. $$
  • Modular Arithmetic: $$ \boxed{ a(b+c)\equiv ab+ac\pmod n }. $$
  • Ring Theory: Left and right distributivity are defining compatibility laws between multiplication and addition in a ring.
  • Boolean Algebra: Boolean operations satisfy distributive identities such as: $$ A\cap(B\cup C) = (A\cap B)\cup(A\cap C). $$
  • Set Intersection: $$ \boxed{ A\cap(B\cup C) = (A\cap B)\cup(A\cap C) }. $$
  • Set Union: $$ \boxed{ A\cup(B\cap C) = (A\cup B)\cap(A\cup C) }. $$
  • Logical Conjunction: $$ p\land(q\lor r) \equiv (p\land q)\lor(p\land r). $$
  • Logical Disjunction: $$ p\lor(q\land r) \equiv (p\lor q)\land(p\lor r). $$
  • Division of a Sum: For $c\neq0$: $$ \boxed{ \frac{a+b}{c} = \frac ac+\frac bc }. $$
  • Division Warning: In general: $$ \boxed{ \frac a{b+c} \neq \frac ab+\frac ac }. $$
  • Exponent Warning: $$ \boxed{ (a+b)^n \neq a^n+b^n } $$ in general.
  • Square Warning: $$ \boxed{ (a+b)^2 \neq a^2+b^2 } $$ unless special values make the equality happen accidentally.
  • Radical Warning: $$ \boxed{ \sqrt{a+b} \neq \sqrt a+\sqrt b } $$ in general.
  • Absolute Value Warning: $$ \boxed{ |a+b| \neq |a|+|b| } $$ in general.
  • Logarithm Warning: Logarithms do not distribute over addition: $$ \boxed{ \log(a+b) \neq \log a+\log b }. $$
  • Trig Warning: Trigonometric functions generally do not distribute over sums: $$ \sin(a+b)\neq\sin a+\sin b. $$
  • Efficient Solving: Distribution is valid but not mandatory whenever another reversible algebraic operation simplifies the equation more efficiently.
  • Terminological Contrast: Distribute is the action; the distributive property is the law that justifies the action.
  • Expansion Contrast: Expand describes the broader goal of rewriting a product as a sum; distribution is one principal method used to do so.
  • Factoring Contrast: Distribution moves from factored form to expanded form, while factoring commonly moves in the reverse direction.
  • Core Interpretation: To distribute is to apply a factor across every term in a grouped sum or difference according to a valid distributive law: $$ \boxed{ a(b+c)=ab+ac }. $$ The operation is foundational in arithmetic, algebra, matrices, vectors, functions, sets, logic, and abstract algebra, but it must not be extended to operations that are not actually distributive.
