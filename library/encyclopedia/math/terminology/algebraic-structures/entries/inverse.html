Inverse

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

In mathematics, an inverse is an object, operation, or process that reverses the effect of another object, operation, or process in a specified structure.

$$ \boxed{ \text{apply something} \longrightarrow \text{apply its inverse} \longrightarrow \text{return to the identity} }. $$

The word inverse comes from Latin roots associated with turning, reversing, or turning back. That linguistic idea is remarkably stable across mathematics: an inverse undoes an action relative to the identity appropriate to the operation.

For addition:

$$ \boxed{ a+(-a)=0 }. $$

For multiplication:

$$ \boxed{ a\cdot a^{-1}=1 } $$

when $a$ has a multiplicative inverse.

For functions:

$$ \boxed{ f^{-1}\circ f = \operatorname{id} }. $$

For matrices:

$$ \boxed{ A^{-1}A = AA^{-1} = I }. $$

The unifying idea is therefore not merely "opposite" but reversal relative to an identity element or identity transformation.

Operational Nuance

General Algebraic Definition

Suppose a set has a binary operation $\ast$ with identity element $e$. An inverse of an element $a$ is an element $a^{-1}$ satisfying:

$$ \boxed{ a\ast a^{-1} = a^{-1}\ast a = e }. $$

This definition depends on the operation. There is no single universal inverse independent of context.

Additive Inverse

Under addition, the identity element is $0$. The additive inverse of $a$ is the number that combines with $a$ to produce $0$.

$$ \boxed{ a+(-a)=0 }. $$

Thus the additive inverse of $7$ is $-7$, and the additive inverse of $-3$ is $3$.

Opposite

In elementary arithmetic, opposite is a common synonym for additive inverse.

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{opp}(a)=-a }. $$

Multiplicative Inverse

Under multiplication, the identity element is $1$. A multiplicative inverse of $a$ satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ aa^{-1}=1 }. $$

For a nonzero real number:

$$ \boxed{ a^{-1} = \frac{1}{a} }. $$

Reciprocal

For nonzero numbers, the reciprocal is the multiplicative inverse:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{reciprocal}(a) = \frac{1}{a} }. $$

Inverse vs. Reciprocal

Inverse is the broader structural term. Reciprocal specifically refers to multiplicative inversion of a nonzero number or analogous scalar quantity.

$$ \boxed{ \text{reciprocal} \subset \text{multiplicative inverse} \subset \text{inverse} }. $$

Saying that the inverse of $5$ is $1/5$ is understandable only when multiplication is the intended operation. Under addition, the inverse of $5$ is $-5$.

Zero Has No Multiplicative Inverse

There is no real or complex number $x$ satisfying:

$$ 0x=1. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ 0^{-1} \text{ is undefined} }. $$

Every Number Has an Additive Inverse

In familiar number systems such as $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, and $\mathbb{C}$:

$$ \boxed{ a+(-a)=0 } $$

for every $a$.

Inverse Operations

Two operations are often called inverse operations when one reverses the effect of the other under appropriate conditions.

$$ \boxed{ \text{addition} \longleftrightarrow \text{subtraction} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{multiplication} \longleftrightarrow \text{division} }. $$

Exponentiation and Radicalization

Exponentiation and radicalization can act as inverse processes under suitable domain restrictions.

$$ \boxed{ \left(\sqrt[n]{x}\right)^n=x } $$

where the expression is appropriately defined.

Even-Power Caveat

Squaring and square rooting are not unrestricted two-sided inverses over all real numbers because:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt{x^2} = |x| } $$

rather than always $x$.

Exponentials and Logarithms

For $b>0$ and $b\neq1$:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(b^x)=x } $$

and for $x>0$:

$$ \boxed{ b^{\log_b x}=x }. $$

Function Inverse

If $f:A\to B$ is bijective, its inverse function:

$$ \boxed{ f^{-1}:B\to A } $$

reverses the mapping performed by $f$.

Inverse Function Conditions

$$ \boxed{ f^{-1}(f(x))=x } $$

for $x\in A$, and:

$$ \boxed{ f(f^{-1}(y))=y } $$

for $y\in B$.

Composition Form

$$ \boxed{ f^{-1}\circ f = \operatorname{id}_A } $$
$$ \boxed{ f\circ f^{-1} = \operatorname{id}_B }. $$

Bijectivity

A function has a two-sided inverse function precisely when it is bijective between its stated domain and codomain.

$$ \boxed{ \text{invertible function} \iff \text{bijective function} }. $$

One-to-One Requirement

If two different inputs produce the same output, an inverse function cannot determine which original input to return.

Onto Requirement

Relative to a stated codomain, every codomain value must be reached for the inverse to be defined on that entire codomain.

Horizontal-Line Test

A function is one-to-one exactly when every horizontal line intersects its graph at most once.

Finding an Inverse Function Algebraically

Starting with:

$$ y=f(x), $$

a common procedure is:

  1. exchange $x$ and $y$;
  2. solve for $y$;
  3. write the resulting function as $f^{-1}(x)$.

Example

Let:

$$ f(x)=3x-5. $$

Write:

$$ y=3x-5. $$

Exchange variables:

$$ x=3y-5. $$

Solve:

$$ y=\frac{x+5}{3}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ f^{-1}(x) = \frac{x+5}{3} }. $$

Verification by Composition

$$ f^{-1}(f(x)) = \frac{(3x-5)+5}{3} = x. $$

Inverse Function vs. Reciprocal Function

This is one of the most important notation distinctions in elementary algebra.

$$ \boxed{ f^{-1}(x) \neq \frac{1}{f(x)} } $$

in general.

The notation $f^{-1}$ means inverse under function composition, not reciprocal under multiplication.

Reciprocal of a Function Value

The reciprocal of $f(x)$ is:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{1}{f(x)} }. $$

Example of the Distinction

If:

$$ f(x)=2x, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ f^{-1}(x)=\frac{x}{2} } $$

but:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{1}{f(x)} = \frac{1}{2x} }. $$

Inverse Relation

Every relation $R$ has an inverse relation obtained by reversing each ordered pair:

$$ \boxed{ R^{-1} = \{(y,x):(x,y)\in R\} }. $$

Inverse Relation Need Not Be a Function

A relation can always be reversed, but the reversed relation is a function only when the original relation satisfies the required uniqueness condition.

Graph of an Inverse Relation

Swapping coordinates:

$$ (x,y)\mapsto(y,x) $$

reflects a graph across:

$$ \boxed{ y=x }. $$

Domain-Range Exchange

For an invertible function:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f^{-1}) = \operatorname{Range}(f) } $$
$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Range}(f^{-1}) = \operatorname{Dom}(f) }. $$

Restricted Domains

A function that is not one-to-one globally may become invertible after restricting its domain.

Quadratic Example

The function:

$$ f(x)=x^2 $$

is not one-to-one over $\mathbb{R}$ because:

$$ f(2)=f(-2)=4. $$

Restricting to:

$$ \boxed{ x\geq0 } $$

produces an invertible function whose inverse is:

$$ \boxed{ f^{-1}(x)=\sqrt{x} }. $$

Inverse Trigonometric Functions

Sine, cosine, and tangent require appropriate domain restrictions before inverse functions can be defined.

Inverse Sine

$$ \boxed{ \arcsin x = \sin^{-1}x } $$

is the inverse of sine restricted to its standard principal domain.

Notation Danger in Trigonometry

The notation:

$$ \sin^{-1}x $$

conventionally means inverse sine, not:

$$ \frac{1}{\sin x}. $$

The reciprocal of sine is:

$$ \boxed{ \csc x = \frac{1}{\sin x} }. $$

Inverse Cosine vs. Secant

$$ \boxed{ \cos^{-1}x = \arccos x } $$

while:

$$ \boxed{ \sec x = \frac{1}{\cos x} }. $$

Inverse Tangent vs. Cotangent

$$ \boxed{ \tan^{-1}x = \arctan x } $$

while:

$$ \boxed{ \cot x = \frac{1}{\tan x} }. $$

Matrix Inverse

A square matrix $A$ is invertible if there exists a matrix $A^{-1}$ satisfying:

$$ \boxed{ AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I }. $$

Invertible Matrix Criterion

For a square matrix over a field:

$$ \boxed{ A \text{ invertible} \iff \det(A)\neq0 }. $$

Two-by-Two Matrix Inverse

For:

$$ A= \begin{pmatrix} a & b\\ c & d \end{pmatrix}, $$

if:

$$ ad-bc\neq0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad-bc} \begin{pmatrix} d & -b\\ -c & a \end{pmatrix} }. $$

Singular Matrix

A square matrix with determinant zero has no ordinary matrix inverse.

$$ \boxed{ \det(A)=0 \Longrightarrow A^{-1} \text{ does not exist} }. $$

Solving Linear Systems with an Inverse

If:

$$ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b} $$

and $A$ is invertible, then:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b} }. $$

Inverse of a Product

For invertible matrices or group elements:

$$ \boxed{ (AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1} }. $$

The order reverses.

Why Product Order Reverses

$$ (AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = A(BB^{-1})A^{-1} = AIA^{-1} = I. $$

Inverse of an Inverse

$$ \boxed{ (a^{-1})^{-1}=a }. $$

Inverse of Identity

$$ \boxed{ e^{-1}=e } $$

in a group.

Uniqueness of an Inverse

In a group, each element has exactly one inverse.

If both $b$ and $c$ are inverses of $a$, then:

$$ b = be = b(ac) = (ba)c = ec = c. $$

Group Theory

A group is an algebraic structure in which every element has an inverse relative to the group operation.

$$ \boxed{ aa^{-1} = a^{-1}a = e }. $$

Additive Groups

When the group operation is written additively, the inverse of $a$ is written:

$$ \boxed{ -a } $$

rather than $a^{-1}$.

Multiplicative Groups

When the operation is written multiplicatively, inverse notation is commonly:

$$ \boxed{ a^{-1} }. $$

Units in a Ring

In a ring with multiplicative identity, an element possessing a multiplicative inverse is called a unit.

$$ \boxed{ a \text{ is a unit} \iff \exists b:\ ab=ba=1 }. $$

Not Every Ring Element Is Invertible

In $\mathbb{Z}$, for example, only $1$ and $-1$ have multiplicative inverses that remain integers.

Fields

In a field, every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse.

$$ \boxed{ a\neq0 \Longrightarrow a^{-1} \text{ exists} }. $$

Modular Inverses

An integer $a$ has a multiplicative inverse modulo $n$ when there exists $b$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ ab\equiv1\pmod n }. $$

Modular Invertibility Criterion

$$ \boxed{ a \text{ invertible mod }n \iff \gcd(a,n)=1 }. $$

Example Modulo Seven

Since:

$$ 3\cdot5=15\equiv1\pmod7, $$

$5$ is the multiplicative inverse of $3$ modulo $7$.

Inverse Transformation

A transformation $T$ has an inverse $T^{-1}$ when:

$$ \boxed{ T^{-1}\circ T = T\circ T^{-1} = I }. $$

Translation Inverse

Translation by vector $\mathbf{v}$ is undone by translation by $-\mathbf{v}$.

$$ \boxed{ T_{\mathbf{v}}^{-1} = T_{-\mathbf{v}} }. $$

Rotation Inverse

Rotation by angle $\theta$ is undone by rotation through $-\theta$:

$$ \boxed{ R_{\theta}^{-1} = R_{-\theta} }. $$

Reflection

A reflection is its own inverse:

$$ \boxed{ R^{-1}=R } $$

because applying the same reflection twice returns every point to its original position.

Self-Inverse Object

An element or transformation satisfying:

$$ \boxed{ a^{-1}=a } $$

is called self-inverse or, in many group contexts, an involution when the corresponding operation squares to the identity.

Involution

$$ \boxed{ f(f(x))=x }. $$

Composition Order

Inverses undo a sequence of operations in reverse order.

If:

$$ h=f\circ g, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ h^{-1} = g^{-1}\circ f^{-1} }. $$

Everyday Operational Analogy

If one first puts on a coat and then a backpack, reversing the process requires removing the backpack first and the coat second. Algebraic composition follows the same reverse-order principle.

Inverse Derivative Rule

If $f$ is differentiable and locally invertible with $f'(x)\neq0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ (f^{-1})'(y) = \frac{1}{f'(x)} } $$

where $y=f(x)$.

Common Alternate Form

$$ \boxed{ (f^{-1})'(x) = \frac{1} {f'(f^{-1}(x))} }. $$

Inverse Function Theorem

In higher-dimensional analysis, an invertible derivative can provide local invertibility of a differentiable mapping.

Jacobian Criterion

For a differentiable map $F:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^n$, a nonzero Jacobian determinant at a point is the standard local criterion:

$$ \boxed{ \det DF(a)\neq0 }. $$

Inverse Operator

In linear and functional analysis, an operator $T$ is invertible if there exists $T^{-1}$ satisfying the appropriate identity relations.

Left Inverse

A map $g$ is a left inverse of $f$ if:

$$ \boxed{ g\circ f = \operatorname{id} }. $$

Right Inverse

A map $h$ is a right inverse of $f$ if:

$$ \boxed{ f\circ h = \operatorname{id} }. $$

One-Sided Inverses

In more general settings, a left inverse and right inverse need not both exist. When both exist for the same object, they coincide.

Injectivity and Left Inverses

A function with a left inverse must be injective.

Surjectivity and Right Inverses

A function with a right inverse must be surjective onto the relevant codomain.

Pseudoinverse

A matrix that is not ordinarily invertible may still possess a generalized inverse such as the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse:

$$ \boxed{ A^{+} }. $$

A pseudoinverse is not the same as an ordinary two-sided inverse.

Inverse Image

For a function $f:A\to B$ and subset $S\subseteq B$, the inverse image or preimage is:

$$ \boxed{ f^{-1}(S) = \{x\in A:f(x)\in S\} }. $$

Inverse Image Does Not Require an Inverse Function

The notation $f^{-1}(S)$ for a preimage is valid even when $f$ is not bijective and has no inverse function.

Notation Depends on Type

The superscript $-1$ may therefore denote several related but distinct ideas:

  • multiplicative inverse;
  • inverse function;
  • inverse relation;
  • inverse transformation;
  • inverse matrix;
  • preimage of a set.

Context Determines Meaning

The mathematical type of the object determines how $^{-1}$ should be interpreted.

Inverse vs. Negative Exponent

For a nonzero scalar:

$$ \boxed{ a^{-1} = \frac{1}{a} }. $$

Here the exponent $-1$ encodes multiplicative inversion.

Higher Negative Powers

$$ \boxed{ a^{-n} = \frac{1}{a^n} } $$

for $a\neq0$.

Function Superscript Is Different

In $f^{-1}$, the notation does not ordinarily mean raising the output of $f$ to the power $-1$.

Inverse vs. Opposite

Opposite usually means additive inverse:

$$ \boxed{ -a }. $$

It should not be used as a universal synonym for every kind of inverse.

Inverse vs. Complement

A complement in set theory or logic is not generally an inverse in the algebraic sense, although some structures make complementary operations resemble reversal.

Inverse vs. Converse

In logic, the inverse of a conditional:

$$ P\to Q $$

is traditionally:

$$ \boxed{ \neg P\to\neg Q }. $$

This logical use is different from algebraic inversion.

Logical Converse

$$ \boxed{ Q\to P } $$

is the converse, not the inverse.

Logical Contrapositive

$$ \boxed{ \neg Q\to\neg P }. $$

Common Error: Calling a Reciprocal the Only Kind of Inverse

Reciprocal is only multiplicative inverse. Additive, functional, matrix, modular, transformational, and group inverses are different manifestations of the broader concept.

Common Error: Saying the Inverse of $a$ Is Always $1/a$

Under addition, the inverse of $a$ is $-a$. The operation must be specified.

Common Error: Confusing $f^{-1}(x)$ with $1/f(x)$

These generally represent different functions.

Common Error: Assuming Every Function Has an Inverse Function

A global inverse function requires bijectivity between the stated domain and codomain.

Common Error: Swapping $x$ and $y$ without Checking One-to-One Behavior

Swapping coordinates always produces an inverse relation, but it may fail to produce a function.

Common Error: Forgetting Domain Restrictions

Restricting a function's domain may be necessary to obtain an inverse function.

Common Error: Forgetting Reverse Order in Product Inversion

$$ \boxed{ (AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1} } $$

rather than $A^{-1}B^{-1}$ in general.

Common Error: Assuming Every Matrix Has an Inverse

Only invertible square matrices possess ordinary two-sided matrix inverses.

Common Error: Trying to Invert Zero Multiplicatively

Zero has no multiplicative inverse in a field.

Common Error: Confusing Preimage with Inverse Function

The preimage notation $f^{-1}(S)$ does not require $f$ itself to be invertible.

Common Misconception: "Inverse" does not mean one fixed operation such as "take the reciprocal." It means an undoing object relative to a specified operation or composition law. The additive inverse of $a$ is $-a$, the multiplicative inverse of nonzero $a$ is $1/a$, the inverse function reverses a bijection, and an inverse matrix reverses an invertible linear transformation. The identity being restored tells you which kind of inverse is meant.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$-a$ "The additive inverse, or opposite, of a."
$a^{-1}=\frac{1}{a}$ "The multiplicative inverse, or reciprocal, of a, for nonzero a."
$f^{-1}$ "The inverse function of f."
$f^{-1}\circ f=\operatorname{id}$ "The inverse composed with the original function gives the identity."
$A^{-1}$ "A inverse; the inverse matrix of A."
$(AB)^{-1}=B^{-1}A^{-1}$ "The inverse of a product reverses the order of the factors."
$f^{-1}(S)$ "The inverse image, or preimage, of S under f."

The superscript $-1$ is heavily overloaded in mathematics. Its interpretation must follow the type of object being discussed.

For a scalar:

$$ a^{-1} $$

usually means multiplicative inverse.

For a function:

$$ f^{-1} $$

means inverse under composition.

For a set $S$:

$$ f^{-1}(S) $$

commonly means preimage, even if $f$ has no inverse function.

Defense Notation Advice: Always identify the operation being inverted. Reserve reciprocal for multiplicative inversion of nonzero scalar quantities, use opposite for additive inverse when helpful, and never interpret $f^{-1}$ automatically as $1/f$. For inverse functions, verify bijectivity or specify a restricted domain; for matrix inverses, verify invertibility; and for products or compositions, remember that inverses undo operations in reverse order.
