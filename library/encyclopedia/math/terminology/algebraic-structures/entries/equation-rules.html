Equation Rules

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

Equation rules are the algebraic rules used to transform, simplify, and solve equations while preserving or appropriately tracking their solution sets.

An equation asserts that two expressions are equal:

$$ \boxed{ L=R }. $$

where $L$ and $R$ denote the left-hand and right-hand sides.

The central principle behind most equation rules is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{apply a legitimate corresponding transformation to both sides} }. $$

For example, from:

$$ x+5=12, $$

subtracting $5$ from both sides produces:

$$ x+5-5=12-5, $$

hence:

$$ \boxed{x=7}. $$

The word rule here refers to a justified algebraic transformation, not merely a memorized instruction. Proper equation solving depends on the properties of equality, inverse operations, domain restrictions, and whether a transformation is reversible.

Operational Nuance

Rule 1: Add the Same Quantity to Both Sides

If:

$$ a=b, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a+c=b+c }. $$

Example

$$ x-4=9 $$

becomes:

$$ x-4+4=9+4, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{x=13}. $$

Rule 2: Subtract the Same Quantity from Both Sides

$$ \boxed{ a=b \Longrightarrow a-c=b-c }. $$

This is equivalent to adding $-c$ to both sides.

Rule 3: Multiply Both Sides by the Same Quantity

$$ \boxed{ a=b \Longrightarrow ac=bc }. $$

Multiplication by any defined quantity preserves a true equality.

However, multiplying by zero is not reversible because all values collapse to zero.

Rule 4: Divide Both Sides by the Same Nonzero Quantity

If:

$$ a=b, \qquad c\neq0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac ac=\frac bc }. $$

Division by zero is never permitted.

Rule 5: Substitute Equals for Equals

If:

$$ a=b, $$

one may replace $a$ by $b$ in any expression where the substitution is valid.

$$ \boxed{ a=b \Longrightarrow F(a)=F(b) } $$

whenever $F$ is defined.

Rule 6: Simplify Either Side Without Changing Its Value

Equivalent expressions may replace one another.

For example:

$$ 2(x+3)=14 $$

may be rewritten:

$$ \boxed{ 2x+6=14 } $$

using the distributive property.

Rule 7: Combine Like Terms

Like terms on the same side of an equation may be combined:

$$ 3x+5x-2=14 $$

becomes:

$$ \boxed{ 8x-2=14 }. $$

Rule 8: Use Inverse Operations to Isolate an Unknown

Addition is reversed by subtraction, multiplication by division, exponentiation by appropriate roots or logarithms, and so forth.

$$ \boxed{ \text{operation} \longleftrightarrow \text{inverse operation} } $$

Example with Multiplication

$$ 6x=42 $$

gives:

$$ \frac{6x}{6} = \frac{42}{6}, $$

hence:

$$ \boxed{x=7}. $$

Rule 9: Keep Domain Restrictions

An equation may contain operations that are not defined for every candidate input.

For:

$$ \frac1{x-3}=2, $$

the original restriction is:

$$ \boxed{x\neq3}. $$

This restriction remains part of the problem even if denominators are later cleared.

Rule 10: Clearing Denominators Requires Nonzero Denominators

Consider:

$$ \frac{x}{3}+\frac12=5. $$

Multiplying by the least common denominator $6$ gives:

$$ 2x+3=30. $$

This transformation is reversible because the multiplier $6$ is nonzero.

Variable Denominators

For:

$$ \frac1{x-2}=\frac3{x+1}, $$

first record:

$$ \boxed{ x\neq2, \qquad x\neq-1 }. $$

Only then should the denominators be cleared.

Rule 11: Cross Multiplication Has Conditions

If:

$$ \frac ab=\frac cd, \qquad b\neq0, \qquad d\neq0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ ad=bc }. $$

Cross multiplication is shorthand for multiplying both sides by $bd$.

Rule 12: Squaring Both Sides Preserves Solutions but May Add Candidates

If:

$$ a=b, $$

then:

$$ a^2=b^2. $$

But:

$$ a^2=b^2 $$

implies only:

$$ \boxed{ a=\pm b }. $$

Therefore squaring is not generally reversible.

Extraneous Solution Example

Suppose:

$$ \sqrt{x}=x-2. $$

Squaring both sides may generate candidates that must be checked in the original equation.

Rule 13: Taking a Principal Square Root Requires Care

From:

$$ x^2=25, $$

one must conclude:

$$ \boxed{x=\pm5}. $$

Writing only:

$$ x=\sqrt{25}=5 $$

loses the negative solution.

Rule 14: Even Powers Require Sign Cases

More generally:

$$ x^{2n}=a, \qquad a>0, $$

may produce:

$$ \boxed{ x=\pm a^{1/(2n)} } $$

over the real numbers.

Rule 15: Odd Powers Have a Unique Real Inverse

For:

$$ x^3=8, $$

applying the real cube root gives:

$$ \boxed{x=2}. $$

Odd-power functions are one-to-one on $\mathbb{R}$.

Rule 16: Exponential Equations May Be Solved with Logarithms

If:

$$ e^x=7, $$

apply $\ln$ to both sides:

$$ \ln(e^x)=\ln7, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{x=\ln7}. $$

Rule 17: Logarithmic Equations Must Respect Positive Arguments

For real logarithms:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(g(x)) \text{ requires } g(x)>0 }. $$

Candidate solutions violating this condition must be rejected.

Rule 18: Equal Bases Can Be Compared When the Exponential Function Is Injective

For:

$$ a^u=a^v, \qquad a>0, \qquad a\neq1, $$

one may conclude:

$$ \boxed{u=v}. $$

Rule 19: Equal Logarithms Can Be Compared

If:

$$ \log_b u=\log_b v, \qquad u>0, \qquad v>0, \qquad b>0, \qquad b\neq1, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{u=v}. $$

Rule 20: Factoring Uses the Zero-Product Property

If:

$$ ab=0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a=0 \quad\text{or}\quad b=0 }. $$

Polynomial Example

Solve:

$$ x^2-5x+6=0. $$

Factor:

$$ (x-2)(x-3)=0. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ x=2 \quad\text{or}\quad x=3 }. $$

Rule 21: Zero-Product Property Applies Only to a Product Equal to Zero

From:

$$ (x-2)(x-3)=5, $$

one cannot conclude:

$$ x-2=5 \quad\text{or}\quad x-3=5. $$

The zero-product property specifically requires the product to equal zero.

Rule 22: Move Terms by Performing Operations, Not by Literal Transport

The classroom phrase:

$$ \boxed{\text{"move the 5 to the other side"}} $$

is shorthand.

From:

$$ x+5=12, $$

the rigorous step is:

$$ x+5-5=12-5. $$

Nothing literally crosses the equality sign.

Rule 23: Sign Changes Come from Inverse Operations

In:

$$ x+7=20, $$

the $+7$ appears as $-7$ on the other side only because $7$ is subtracted from both sides.

Likewise:

$$ 4x=20 $$

becomes:

$$ x=\frac{20}{4} $$

because both sides are divided by $4$.

Rule 24: Collect Variable Terms Strategically

For:

$$ 5x+3=2x+18, $$

subtract $2x$ from both sides:

$$ 3x+3=18. $$

Then subtract $3$:

$$ 3x=15, $$

hence:

$$ \boxed{x=5}. $$

Rule 25: Reversible Steps Preserve the Solution Set Exactly

If each transformation is reversible on the relevant domain:

$$ E_1 \Longleftrightarrow E_2 \Longleftrightarrow E_3, $$

then all equations have the same solution set.

One-Way Steps

If a step is only implication-preserving:

$$ E_1 \Longrightarrow E_2, $$

then every solution of $E_1$ solves $E_2$, but $E_2$ may contain additional solutions.

Rule 26: Verify Candidates in the Original Equation

Verification is especially important after:

  • squaring,
  • raising to even powers,
  • clearing variable denominators,
  • introducing radicals,
  • taking reciprocals, or
  • other potentially nonreversible steps.

Rule 27: Distinguish Identity, Conditional Equation, and Contradiction

An identity is true for every admissible input:

$$ \boxed{ 2(x+3)=2x+6 }. $$

A conditional equation is true only for selected inputs:

$$ x+3=7. $$

A contradiction yields no solution:

$$ \boxed{0=5}. $$

Rule 28: Preserve Units and Dimensions

In applied equations, quantities being added or equated must be dimensionally compatible.

An equation such as:

$$ \text{3 meters}=\text{5 seconds} $$

is not a meaningful physical equality.

Rule 29: Preserve Matrix Order in Matrix Equations

In matrix algebra, multiplication is generally noncommutative.

If:

$$ AX=B $$

and $A$ is invertible, multiply on the left by $A^{-1}$:

$$ A^{-1}AX=A^{-1}B, $$

giving:

$$ \boxed{ X=A^{-1}B }. $$

Writing $BA^{-1}$ instead is generally incorrect.

Rule 30: Equation Rules Depend on the Underlying Structure

Arithmetic equations over $\mathbb{R}$, matrix equations, modular equations, vector equations, and equations in abstract algebra may obey different operational constraints even though equality itself remains central.

Common Misconception: Equation solving is not the process of arbitrarily "moving" terms across an equals sign and changing their signs. Each legitimate step comes from applying a defined algebraic operation or replacing an expression by an equal expression. Domain restrictions and reversibility determine whether the resulting equation is truly equivalent to the original.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$a=b\Rightarrow a+c=b+c$ "Add c to both sides."
$a=b\Rightarrow a-c=b-c$ "Subtract c from both sides."
$a=b\Rightarrow ac=bc$ "Multiply both sides by c."
$a=b,\ c\neq0\Rightarrow a/c=b/c$ "Divide both sides by the same nonzero quantity."
$E_1\Longleftrightarrow E_2$ "The two equations are equivalent."
$E_1\Longrightarrow E_2$ "The first equation implies the second."
$ab=0\Rightarrow a=0\text{ or }b=0$ "By the zero-product property, at least one factor is zero."

Common informal classroom phrases include:

$$ \boxed{\text{"move it to the other side"}} $$
$$ \boxed{\text{"change the sign"}} $$
$$ \boxed{\text{"cross multiply"}}. $$

These can be useful shortcuts in speech, but the underlying operations should remain clear.

For example:

$$ x+5=12 $$

should be understood as:

$$ \boxed{ x+5-5=12-5 } $$

rather than as a literal movement of the $5$.

Defense Notation Advice: Use:

$$ \boxed{\Longleftrightarrow} $$

only for reversible transformations. When squaring, multiplying by a possibly zero expression, or using another one-way transformation, prefer:

$$ \boxed{\Longrightarrow}. $$

Then verify final candidates in the original equation.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Basic Principle: Equation rules transform an equation while preserving or carefully tracking its solution set.
  • Addition Rule: $$ \boxed{ a=b\Rightarrow a+c=b+c }. $$
  • Subtraction Rule: $$ \boxed{ a=b\Rightarrow a-c=b-c }. $$
  • Multiplication Rule: $$ \boxed{ a=b\Rightarrow ac=bc }. $$
  • Division Rule: For $c\neq0$: $$ \boxed{ a=b\Rightarrow\frac ac=\frac bc }. $$
  • Substitution Rule: Equal expressions may replace one another.
  • Simplification Rule: An expression may be replaced by an algebraically equal expression.
  • Distribution: $$ a(b+c)=ab+ac $$ may be used within either side of an equation.
  • Factoring: $$ ab+ac=a(b+c) $$ may reverse distribution.
  • Combining Like Terms: Like terms on the same side may be combined.
  • Inverse Operations: Use inverse operations to undo operations surrounding the unknown.
  • Addition–Subtraction Pair: Addition and subtraction are inverse operations.
  • Multiplication–Division Pair: Multiplication by nonzero values and division by those values are inverse operations.
  • Exponent–Root Relationship: Powers and roots may serve as inverse operations subject to domain and sign conditions.
  • Exponential–Logarithm Relationship: Logarithms invert exponential functions on suitable domains.
  • Linear Equations: Reversible addition and multiplication steps usually solve them directly.
  • Variables on Both Sides: Variable terms may be collected on one side by adding or subtracting equal expressions from both sides.
  • Literal Equations: The same rules apply when solving formulas for a chosen variable.
  • Fractional Equations: Denominators may be cleared only with their nonzero restrictions retained.
  • Variable Denominators: Excluded values must be recorded before clearing denominators.
  • Cross Multiplication: Valid for proportions only when the original denominators are nonzero.
  • Cross-Multiplication Basis: It is shorthand for multiplying both sides by a common denominator.
  • Radical Equations: Isolate radicals before raising both sides to a power when practical.
  • Radical Verification: Check candidates in the original equation after squaring.
  • Even Powers: Taking an even root generally creates positive and negative cases.
  • Odd Powers: Odd real powers are one-to-one and have unique real inverse roots.
  • Exponential Equations: Take logarithms when the variable occurs in an exponent and equal-base rewriting is inconvenient.
  • Equal Exponential Bases: For $a>0$, $a\neq1$: $$ a^u=a^v\Rightarrow u=v. $$
  • Logarithmic Equations: Logarithm arguments must remain positive in real-variable work.
  • Equal Logarithms: Equal logarithms with the same valid base have equal positive arguments.
  • Polynomial Equations: Rearranging to zero often permits factoring or other root-finding methods.
  • Zero-Product Property: $$ \boxed{ ab=0 \Rightarrow a=0\text{ or }b=0 }. $$
  • Zero-Product Restriction: It does not apply when the product equals a nonzero number.
  • Quadratic Equations: Completing the square, factoring, square-root methods, or the quadratic formula may be used.
  • Absolute-Value Equations: $$ |x|=a $$ with $a>0$ gives: $$ x=a\text{ or }x=-a. $$
  • Absolute-Value Impossibility: $$ |x|=-3 $$ has no real solution.
  • Identity: An equation true for every admissible input.
  • Conditional Equation: An equation true for only certain inputs.
  • Contradiction: A false terminal equality such as: $$ 0=4 $$ signals no solution.
  • Equivalent Equations: Equations with exactly the same solution set.
  • Reversible Step: A transformation that can be undone without changing the solution set.
  • One-Way Step: A transformation that preserves original solutions but may introduce additional candidates.
  • Squaring: Usually a one-way step unless sign restrictions ensure injectivity.
  • Multiplying by Zero: Preserves truth but destroys reversibility.
  • Dividing by an Unknown: Requires knowing that the divisor is nonzero or separating cases.
  • Cancellation: Only common multiplicative factors may be canceled; terms across addition cannot be canceled.
  • Domain Restrictions: Candidate solutions must belong to the original domain.
  • Extraneous Solutions: Candidates produced by nonreversible steps may fail the original equation.
  • Verification: Substitute each candidate into the original equation.
  • Units: Applied equations should maintain dimensional compatibility.
  • Physical Equations: Algebraic operations may be valid symbolically while the model itself still imposes physical restrictions such as positive time or nonnegative length.
  • Parameter Equations: Some parameter values may change the validity of division or the number of solutions.
  • Case Splitting: If a divisor or coefficient may be zero, separate the zero and nonzero cases rather than dividing blindly.
  • Example of Case Splitting: In: $$ ax=b, $$ the usual solution: $$ x=b/a $$ requires: $$ a\neq0. $$
  • When $a=0$: The equation $0x=b$ may have no solution if $b\neq0$ or infinitely many solutions if $b=0$.
  • Matrix Equations: Preserve multiplication order because matrix multiplication is generally noncommutative.
  • Matrix Left Inverse Step: From $AX=B$, use: $$ X=A^{-1}B $$ when $A^{-1}$ exists.
  • Matrix Right Inverse Step: From $XA=B$, use: $$ X=BA^{-1}. $$
  • Vector Equations: Equality is componentwise in coordinate form.
  • Complex Equations: Real and imaginary parts may be equated when two complex numbers are equal.
  • Function Equations: Equality may be interpreted pointwise over the stated domain.
  • Equation vs. Expression: An expression has no equals sign; an equation asserts equality between expressions.
  • Equation vs. Identity: Not every equation is an identity.
  • Equation vs. Formula: A formula is often an equation expressing a general relationship among quantities.
  • "Move Across" Language: A verbal shortcut for performing inverse operations on both sides.
  • "Change the Sign" Language: Usually shorthand for adding or subtracting the same term on both sides, not an independent algebraic law.
  • "Flip the Fraction" Language: Taking reciprocals requires both sides to be nonzero.
  • Logical Precision: Use $\Longleftrightarrow$ for equivalence and $\Longrightarrow$ for one-way implication.
  • Core Interpretation: Equation rules are the justified transformations used to preserve or correctly track the solutions of an equation: $$ \boxed{ L=R \longrightarrow \text{apply valid corresponding operations to both sides} }. $$ The safest equation-solving process combines equality-preserving operations, inverse operations, domain restrictions, reversible transformations, case analysis when necessary, and final verification in the original equation.
