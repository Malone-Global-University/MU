Adjoint

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

The mathematical term adjoint refers broadly to an object paired with another object through a defining algebraic relationship. Its exact meaning is highly context-dependent.

In modern linear algebra and functional analysis, the adjoint of a linear operator $A$ is usually written:

$$ \boxed{A^*} $$

and is characterized by how it interacts with the inner product:

$$ \boxed{ \langle Ax,y\rangle = \langle x,A^*y\rangle }. $$

For finite-dimensional complex inner-product spaces with the standard inner product, the matrix of the adjoint is the conjugate transpose:

$$ \boxed{ A^*=\overline{A}^{\,T} }. $$

Over the real numbers, complex conjugation has no effect, so:

$$ \boxed{ A^*=A^T }. $$

However, older matrix terminology sometimes uses the word adjoint for what is now more precisely called the adjugate:

$$ \operatorname{adj}(A). $$

These meanings should not be merged. The operator adjoint $A^*$ and the adjugate $\operatorname{adj}(A)$ are different constructions.

Operational Nuance

Adjoint of a Linear Operator

Let:

$$ A:V\to W $$

be a linear map between inner-product spaces. Its adjoint is a map:

$$ A^*:W\to V $$

satisfying:

$$ \boxed{ \langle Ax,y\rangle_W = \langle x,A^*y\rangle_V } $$

for all appropriate vectors $x$ and $y$.

The adjoint therefore transfers the action of $A$ from one side of an inner product to the other.

Matrix Adjoint in the Standard Complex Inner Product

If:

$$ A= \begin{pmatrix} a_{11} & a_{12}\\ a_{21} & a_{22} \end{pmatrix}, $$

then:

$$ \overline A= \begin{pmatrix} \overline{a_{11}} & \overline{a_{12}}\\ \overline{a_{21}} & \overline{a_{22}} \end{pmatrix}, $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ A^* = \overline A^{\,T} = \begin{pmatrix} \overline{a_{11}} & \overline{a_{21}}\\ \overline{a_{12}} & \overline{a_{22}} \end{pmatrix} }. $$

Example

Let:

$$ A= \begin{pmatrix} 1+i & 2\\ 3i & 4-i \end{pmatrix}. $$

First conjugate every entry:

$$ \overline A= \begin{pmatrix} 1-i & 2\\ -3i & 4+i \end{pmatrix}. $$

Then transpose:

$$ \boxed{ A^* = \begin{pmatrix} 1-i & -3i\\ 2 & 4+i \end{pmatrix} }. $$

Real Matrices

If every entry of $A$ is real, then:

$$ \overline A=A, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ A^*=A^T }. $$

Self-Adjoint Operators

An operator is self-adjoint when:

$$ \boxed{ A=A^* }. $$

In finite-dimensional complex matrix language, such a matrix is called Hermitian.

Unitary Operators

A matrix $U$ is unitary when:

$$ \boxed{ U^*U=UU^*=I }. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ U^{-1}=U^* }. $$

Adjugate: Older Matrix Use of "Adjoint"

In some older textbooks, the phrase adjoint of a matrix refers to the transpose of its cofactor matrix:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{adj}(A) = C^T }, $$

where $C$ is the cofactor matrix.

For:

$$ A= \begin{pmatrix} a & b\\ c & d \end{pmatrix}, $$

the adjugate is:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{adj}(A) = \begin{pmatrix} d & -b\\ -c & a \end{pmatrix} }. $$

Adjugate and Matrix Inverse

If:

$$ \det(A)\ne0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(A)} \operatorname{adj}(A) }. $$

This identity concerns the adjugate, not the operator adjoint $A^*$.

Common Misconception: The symbols $A^*$, $\operatorname{adj}(A)$, and $A^{-1}$ generally represent three different objects. They coincide only in special circumstances.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$A^*$ "A star," or "the adjoint of A."
$A^\dagger$ "A dagger," often used for the adjoint in physics.
$\overline A^{\,T}$ "The conjugate transpose of A."
$\langle Ax,y\rangle=\langle x,A^*y\rangle$ "A star is defined by moving A across the inner product."
$A=A^*$ "A is self-adjoint."
$\operatorname{adj}(A)$ "The adjugate of A."

Several notations are encountered for the operator adjoint:

$$ A^*,\qquad A^\dagger. $$

The notation $A^\dagger$ is especially common in quantum mechanics and physics.

By contrast:

$$ \operatorname{adj}(A) $$

should preferably be read as the adjugate of A to avoid confusion.

Defense Notation Advice: When the word adjoint appears without notation, inspect the surrounding subject. Inner products, Hilbert spaces, Hermitian matrices, or $A^*$ indicate the operator adjoint. Cofactors, determinants, and inverse formulas usually indicate the adjugate.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Operator Definition: $$ \boxed{ \langle Ax,y\rangle = \langle x,A^*y\rangle }. $$
  • Domain and Codomain: If: $$ A:V\to W, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ A^*:W\to V }. $$
  • Finite-Dimensional Complex Case: Under the standard inner product: $$ \boxed{ A^*=\overline A^{\,T} }. $$
  • Finite-Dimensional Real Case: $$ \boxed{ A^*=A^T }. $$
  • Double Adjoint: $$ \boxed{ (A^*)^*=A }. $$
  • Adjoint of a Sum: $$ \boxed{ (A+B)^*=A^*+B^* }. $$
  • Adjoint of a Scalar Multiple: Over $\mathbb{C}$: $$ \boxed{ (\lambda A)^* = \overline{\lambda}A^* }. $$
  • Adjoint of a Product: $$ \boxed{ (AB)^* = B^*A^* }. $$ The order reverses.
  • Adjoint of an Inverse: If $A$ is invertible: $$ \boxed{ (A^{-1})^* = (A^*)^{-1} }. $$
  • Self-Adjoint: $$ \boxed{ A=A^* }. $$
  • Hermitian Matrix: In complex matrix theory, a self-adjoint matrix is Hermitian.
  • Symmetric Matrix: Over the real numbers: $$ A=A^T $$ means the matrix is symmetric, which is the real analogue of Hermitian.
  • Skew-Adjoint: An operator satisfying: $$ \boxed{ A^*=-A } $$ is called skew-adjoint.
  • Unitary Matrix: $$ \boxed{ U^*U=I } $$ implies: $$ U^{-1}=U^*. $$
  • Orthogonal Matrix: Over $\mathbb{R}$: $$ Q^TQ=I, $$ so: $$ \boxed{ Q^{-1}=Q^T=Q^* }. $$
  • Normal Matrix: A matrix is normal when: $$ \boxed{ AA^*=A^*A }. $$
  • Eigenvalues of Self-Adjoint Operators: Self-adjoint operators on finite-dimensional complex inner-product spaces have real eigenvalues.
  • Orthogonal Eigenvectors: Eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues of a self-adjoint operator are orthogonal.
  • Spectral Theorem: Finite-dimensional self-adjoint operators admit an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors and are unitarily diagonalizable.
  • Inner-Product Dependence: The adjoint of a linear operator depends on the chosen inner products. Changing the inner product can change the matrix representing the adjoint.
  • Nonstandard Inner Products: Under a weighted inner product, the adjoint need not be the ordinary conjugate transpose in the original coordinates.
  • Functional Analysis: In Hilbert spaces, adjoints extend the finite-dimensional idea to bounded linear operators and, with additional domain care, to important unbounded operators.
  • Differential Operators: Differential operators may have formal or Hilbert-space adjoints obtained through integration by parts together with boundary conditions.
  • Quantum Mechanics: Observables are represented by self-adjoint operators, while the dagger notation: $$ A^\dagger $$ is commonly used for adjoints.
  • Adjugate Definition: For a square matrix $A$, the adjugate is: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{adj}(A) = (\text{cofactor matrix})^T }. $$
  • Adjugate Identity: $$ \boxed{ A\operatorname{adj}(A) = \operatorname{adj}(A)A = \det(A)I }. $$
  • Inverse via Adjugate: If: $$ \det(A)\ne0, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ A^{-1} = \frac{\operatorname{adj}(A)}{\det(A)} }. $$
  • Terminological Caution: Some older sources use adjoint matrix where modern texts prefer adjugate matrix. This usage should be identified from context.
  • Conjugate Transpose Contrast: $$ \boxed{ A^*=\overline A^{\,T} } $$ is constructed by conjugating and transposing entries, not by computing cofactors.
  • Adjugate Contrast: $$ \operatorname{adj}(A) $$ is constructed from cofactors and is closely related to determinants and inverse formulas.
  • Inverse Contrast: $$ A^{-1} $$ is the multiplicative inverse satisfying: $$ AA^{-1}=A^{-1}A=I. $$
  • Not Generally Equal: In general: $$ \boxed{ A^* \ne \operatorname{adj}(A) \ne A^{-1} }. $$
  • Terminological Contrast: In modern operator theory, the adjoint $A^*$ is defined through the inner product and is represented by the conjugate transpose in standard complex coordinates. The adjugate $\operatorname{adj}(A)$ is the transpose of the cofactor matrix. An inverse $A^{-1}$ is a multiplicative inverse. Context is essential because older sources may call the adjugate an adjoint.
