Double Root

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

A double root is a root of a polynomial that occurs with multiplicity $2$.

If $r$ is a double root of a polynomial $p(x)$, then:

$$ \boxed{ (x-r)^2 \text{ divides } p(x) } $$

but, for multiplicity exactly $2$:

$$ \boxed{ (x-r)^3 \text{ does not divide } p(x) }. $$

Equivalently, the polynomial can be written:

$$ \boxed{ p(x)=(x-r)^2q(x) } $$

where:

$$ \boxed{q(r)\neq0}. $$

The word root refers to a value that makes a polynomial zero, while double indicates that the corresponding linear factor occurs twice.

The terms double root, root of multiplicity two, and repeated root of multiplicity two are mathematically equivalent.

Operational Nuance

Basic Definition

If:

$$ p(r)=0, $$

then $r$ is a root. It is a double root when the corresponding factor appears twice:

$$ \boxed{ p(x)=(x-r)^2q(x), \qquad q(r)\neq0 }. $$

Example

Consider:

$$ p(x)=(x-3)^2(x+1). $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{x=3} $$

is a double root, while:

$$ \boxed{x=-1} $$

is a simple root.

Expanded Example

Expanding:

$$ (x-3)^2 = x^2-6x+9. $$

Therefore:

$$ x^2-6x+9=0 $$

has:

$$ \boxed{x=3} $$

as a double root.

Quadratic Discriminant Criterion

For a quadratic:

$$ ax^2+bx+c=0, \qquad a\neq0, $$

a double root occurs exactly when the discriminant is zero:

$$ \boxed{ b^2-4ac=0 }. $$

Double Root Formula for a Quadratic

When:

$$ b^2-4ac=0, $$

the quadratic formula becomes:

$$ x = \frac{-b\pm0}{2a}, $$

so the repeated root is:

$$ \boxed{ x=-\frac{b}{2a} }. $$

Example Using the Discriminant

For:

$$ 4x^2-12x+9=0, $$

the discriminant is:

$$ (-12)^2-4(4)(9) = 144-144 = \boxed{0}. $$

Therefore the equation has a double root:

$$ \boxed{ x=\frac{12}{8}=\frac32 }. $$

Factored Form

The same quadratic factors as:

$$ 4x^2-12x+9 = (2x-3)^2. $$

The repeated factor makes the double root visible immediately.

Multiplicity

If:

$$ p(x)=(x-r)^m q(x), \qquad q(r)\neq0, $$

then $r$ has multiplicity $m$.

A double root is the case:

$$ \boxed{m=2}. $$

Simple vs. Double Root

A simple root has multiplicity $1$:

$$ (x-r) $$

divides the polynomial, but $(x-r)^2$ does not.

A double root has multiplicity $2$:

$$ (x-r)^2 $$

divides the polynomial, but $(x-r)^3$ does not.

Derivative Criterion

If $r$ is a double root of a polynomial $p$, then:

$$ \boxed{ p(r)=0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad p'(r)=0 }. $$

For multiplicity exactly $2$, one also has:

$$ \boxed{ p''(r)\neq0 } $$

over characteristic-zero settings such as $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$.

Why the Derivative Also Vanishes

Suppose:

$$ p(x)=(x-r)^2q(x). $$

Differentiating:

$$ p'(x) = 2(x-r)q(x) + (x-r)^2q'(x). $$

Substituting $x=r$ gives:

$$ \boxed{p'(r)=0}. $$

Graphical Behavior

For a real polynomial, a double root typically corresponds to a point where the graph touches the $x$-axis and turns around rather than crossing it.

Locally, the factor:

$$ (x-r)^2 $$

is nonnegative on both sides of $r$, so the sign of the polynomial ordinarily remains the same immediately before and after the root.

Example of Tangency

For:

$$ f(x)=(x-2)^2, $$

the graph touches the $x$-axis at:

$$ \boxed{(2,0)} $$

and turns upward.

Even Multiplicity Behavior

More generally, roots of even multiplicity do not cause a real polynomial to change sign locally.

Thus a double root shares the characteristic behavior of all even-multiplicity roots.

Odd Multiplicity Contrast

A root of odd multiplicity generally causes the graph to cross the $x$-axis.

For example:

$$ f(x)=(x-1)^3 $$

crosses the axis at $x=1$, whereas:

$$ f(x)=(x-1)^2 $$

touches and turns.

Counting Roots with Multiplicity

The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra counts roots with multiplicity. A degree-$n$ polynomial over $\mathbb{C}$ has exactly $n$ roots counted with multiplicity.

Therefore a double root contributes:

$$ \boxed{2} $$

to the total root count.

Example of Degree Counting

If:

$$ p(x)=(x-1)^2(x+4)^3, $$

then the polynomial has degree:

$$ 2+3=\boxed{5}. $$

It has two distinct roots, but five roots counted with multiplicity.

Double Root and Repeated Factor

The statements:

$$ \boxed{ r\text{ is a double root} } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ (x-r)^2\text{ is a factor} } $$

are equivalent when the multiplicity is exactly two and the remaining factor is nonzero at $r$.

Repeated Root Detection with GCD

A polynomial has a repeated root when it shares a nonconstant common factor with its derivative:

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(p,p')\neq1 } $$

over a suitable polynomial field.

Square-Free Polynomial Contrast

A polynomial is square-free when it has no repeated irreducible factors. Over a splitting field, this means it has no repeated roots.

Therefore a polynomial with a double root is not square-free.

Parameter Problems

Double roots often arise in equations containing an unknown parameter.

For example:

$$ x^2+kx+9=0. $$

A double root occurs when:

$$ k^2-36=0. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{k=\pm6}. $$

Tangency Problems

When a line and a parabola intersect, substitution often produces a quadratic. A double root of that quadratic indicates that the two curves meet at only one intersection point with multiplicity two, typically corresponding to tangency.

Example of Tangency

Suppose a line and parabola reduce to:

$$ x^2-4x+4=0. $$

Since:

$$ (x-2)^2=0, $$

the intersection has:

$$ \boxed{x=2} $$

as a double solution.

Double Eigenvalues

In linear algebra, an eigenvalue may be called a double root of the characteristic polynomial when its algebraic multiplicity is $2$.

If:

$$ \chi_A(\lambda) = (\lambda-3)^2(\lambda+1), $$

then:

$$ \boxed{\lambda=3} $$

is a double root of the characteristic polynomial.

Algebraic vs. Geometric Multiplicity

A double eigenvalue has algebraic multiplicity $2$, but its eigenspace may have dimension $1$ or $2$.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \text{double eigenvalue} \not\Rightarrow \text{two independent eigenvectors} }. $$

Complex Double Roots

Double roots need not be real. For example:

$$ p(x)=(x-i)^2(x+i)^2 $$

has:

$$ \boxed{i\text{ and }-i} $$

each as double roots.

Real Coefficient Conjugacy

If a polynomial has real coefficients and a nonreal root $a+bi$, then $a-bi$ is also a root with the same multiplicity.

Thus a nonreal double root of a real polynomial is accompanied by its conjugate as another double root.

Double Root vs. Two Distinct Roots

A quadratic with roots $2$ and $5$ has two distinct simple roots:

$$ (x-2)(x-5). $$

A quadratic:

$$ (x-2)^2 $$

has one distinct root, but that root counts twice by multiplicity.

Double Root vs. Double Solution

For polynomial equations, the phrases are often used informally as synonyms. More precisely, root refers to the zero of the polynomial and solution refers to a value satisfying the equation.

Numerical Sensitivity

Repeated roots can be numerically sensitive. A small perturbation of the coefficients may split one double root into two nearby simple roots or eliminate real coincidence.

For example:

$$ (x-1)^2 = x^2-2x+1 $$

has a double root at $1$, while:

$$ x^2-2x+1-\varepsilon $$

has two distinct real roots when $\varepsilon>0$.

Common Misconception: A double root does not mean that there are two different root values. It means one root value occurs twice in the factorization and has multiplicity $2$.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$(x-r)^2\mid p(x)$ "X minus r squared divides p of x."
$p(x)=(x-r)^2q(x)$ "P of x contains x minus r squared as a factor."
$q(r)\neq0$ "Q of r is not zero."
$p(r)=p'(r)=0$ "P of r and p prime of r are both zero."
$p''(r)\neq0$ "P double prime of r is nonzero."
$b^2-4ac=0$ "The discriminant is zero."
$x=-b/(2a)$ "X equals negative b over two a."

The phrases:

$$ \boxed{\text{"double root"}} $$
$$ \boxed{\text{"root of multiplicity two"}} $$

describe the same algebraic condition.

For example, if:

$$ p(x)=(x+4)^2(x-1), $$

one may say:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"negative four is a double root of }p }. $$

or:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"negative four has multiplicity two"} }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: When reporting roots, distinguish between the number of distinct roots and the number counted with multiplicity. Instead of merely writing "$x=3$ twice," prefer:

$$ \boxed{ x=3 \text{ with multiplicity }2 }. $$

This states the algebraic structure unambiguously.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Basic Definition: A double root is a root of multiplicity $2$.
  • Factor Criterion: $$ \boxed{ (x-r)^2\mid p(x) }. $$
  • Exact Multiplicity: For a root to be exactly double: $$ \boxed{ (x-r)^2\mid p(x) \quad\text{but}\quad (x-r)^3\nmid p(x) }. $$
  • Factorized Form: $$ \boxed{ p(x)=(x-r)^2q(x), \qquad q(r)\neq0 }. $$
  • Multiplicity: $$ \boxed{m=2}. $$
  • Repeated Root: Every double root is a repeated root.
  • Repeated Root Contrast: A repeated root can have multiplicity $2,3,4,\ldots$; a double root specifically has multiplicity $2$.
  • Simple Root Contrast: A simple root has multiplicity $1$.
  • Triple Root Contrast: A triple root has multiplicity $3$.
  • Quadratic Criterion: $$ \boxed{ b^2-4ac=0 } $$ exactly characterizes a double root for a quadratic.
  • Quadratic Root: When the discriminant is zero: $$ \boxed{ r=-\frac{b}{2a} }. $$
  • Quadratic Factorization: A quadratic with a double root can be written: $$ \boxed{ a(x-r)^2 }. $$
  • Perfect-Square Trinomial: Many quadratics with double roots appear as perfect-square trinomials.
  • Example: $$ x^2+10x+25 = \boxed{(x+5)^2}. $$
  • Root in Example: $$ \boxed{x=-5} $$ is a double root.
  • Derivative Criterion: A repeated polynomial root satisfies: $$ \boxed{ p(r)=p'(r)=0 }. $$
  • Exact Double Root in Characteristic Zero: $$ \boxed{ p(r)=0,\quad p'(r)=0,\quad p''(r)\neq0 }. $$
  • Graphical Tangency: A real double root generally appears where the graph touches the $x$-axis.
  • No Local Sign Change: A real polynomial generally keeps the same sign across a double root.
  • Even Multiplicity: A double root is an even-multiplicity root.
  • Turning Behavior: The graph typically turns around at a double root rather than crossing.
  • Horizontal Tangent: Since $p'(r)=0$, the graph has a horizontal tangent at a double real root when viewed as $y=p(x)$.
  • Axis Contact: At the root: $$ \boxed{(r,0)} $$ lies on the graph.
  • Fundamental Theorem of Algebra: A double root contributes two roots to the degree count.
  • Distinct-Root Count: One double root is only one distinct root value.
  • Multiplicity Count: The same root contributes $2$ when roots are counted with multiplicity.
  • Degree Example: $$ (x-2)^2(x+1) $$ is cubic even though it has only two distinct roots.
  • Repeated Factor: A squared linear factor signals a double root.
  • Square-Free Contrast: A polynomial containing a double root is not square-free.
  • GCD Test: Repeated roots are detected through: $$ \boxed{ \gcd(p,p') } $$ over appropriate coefficient fields.
  • Discriminant of a Quadratic: Zero discriminant means the two formal quadratic-formula branches coincide.
  • Discriminant More Generally: Polynomial discriminants vanish when repeated roots occur.
  • Parameter Problems: Setting a discriminant equal to zero is a standard method for finding parameter values that produce a double root.
  • Tangency Problems: A double solution in an intersection equation often signals tangency.
  • Line-Parabola Tangency: Substitution yields a quadratic with discriminant zero.
  • Eigenvalues: An eigenvalue can be a double root of the characteristic polynomial.
  • Algebraic Multiplicity: A double eigenvalue has algebraic multiplicity $2$.
  • Geometric Multiplicity: A double eigenvalue may have geometric multiplicity $1$ or $2$.
  • Diagonalizability: A double eigenvalue does not by itself determine whether a matrix is diagonalizable.
  • Complex Roots: Double roots may be real or complex.
  • Real-Coefficient Conjugacy: A nonreal double root of a real polynomial is accompanied by its complex conjugate with the same multiplicity.
  • Numerical Analysis: Multiple roots are often more sensitive to coefficient perturbations than simple roots.
  • Newton's Method: Standard Newton iteration generally loses its usual quadratic convergence near a repeated root unless modified for multiplicity.
  • Factor Theorem: If $r$ is a root, $(x-r)$ divides $p(x)$; if $r$ is double, $(x-r)^2$ divides $p(x)$.
  • Remainder Interpretation: $$ p(r)=0 $$ is necessary for any root, including a double root.
  • Derivative Interpretation: $$ p'(r)=0 $$ distinguishes repeated roots from simple roots in characteristic-zero polynomial settings.
  • Local Model: Near a double root: $$ p(x)\approx C(x-r)^2 $$ when the remaining factor is nonzero and smooth.
  • Local Shape: This quadratic local behavior explains the characteristic touch-and-turn geometry.
  • Root vs. Zero: Root and zero are ordinarily synonymous for polynomial functions.
  • Root vs. Solution: A root of $p$ is a solution of: $$ p(x)=0. $$
  • Double Root vs. Two Roots: A double root is one value of multiplicity two, not two distinct values.
  • Double Root vs. Repeated Evaluation: Substituting the same number twice does not make it a double root; multiplicity is determined by factorization or equivalent algebraic criteria.
  • Terminological Variant: Double zero is sometimes used as a synonym for double root.
  • Terminological Precision: Prefer "root of multiplicity two" when multiplicity must be stated explicitly.
  • Core Interpretation: A double root is one polynomial root occurring twice in the algebraic factorization: $$ \boxed{ p(x)=(x-r)^2q(x), \qquad q(r)\neq0 }. $$ For a quadratic it occurs exactly when the discriminant is zero, and for a real polynomial it typically appears graphically as a point where the curve touches the axis without crossing it.
