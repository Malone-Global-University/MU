Compatible Matrices

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

Compatible matrices are matrices whose dimensions satisfy the requirements of a particular matrix operation.

Compatibility is therefore not a single universal relationship between two matrices. It depends on what operation is being attempted.

$$ \boxed{ \text{matrix compatibility} = \text{dimensions satisfy the requirements of an operation} } $$

For example, two matrices are compatible for addition only when they have the same dimensions, while they are compatible for multiplication when the number of columns of the first matrix equals the number of rows of the second.

The adjective compatible comes from the general idea of things being able to function or exist together without conflict. In matrix mathematics, the conflict being avoided is dimensional mismatch.

The related term conformable is also used in linear algebra, particularly in phrases such as conformable for multiplication.

Operational Nuance

Matrix Dimensions

A matrix with $m$ rows and $n$ columns has dimensions:

$$ \boxed{ m\times n }. $$

The row count is written first and the column count second.

Thus:

$$ A= \begin{pmatrix} 1&2&3\\ 4&5&6 \end{pmatrix} $$

has two rows and three columns, so:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ is }2\times3 }. $$

Compatibility Depends on the Operation

The same pair of matrices may be compatible for one operation and incompatible for another.

$$ \boxed{ \text{compatible} \text{ is operation-dependent} }. $$

Compatibility for Addition

Two matrices can be added only if they have the same dimensions.

If:

$$ A\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}, \qquad B\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ A+B \text{ is defined} }. $$

Why Addition Requires Equal Dimensions

Matrix addition is performed entry by entry:

$$ \boxed{ (A+B)_{ij}=a_{ij}+b_{ij} }. $$

Every entry in one matrix must therefore have a corresponding entry in the other.

Example: Compatible for Addition

Let:

$$ A= \begin{pmatrix} 1&2\\ 3&4 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad B= \begin{pmatrix} 5&6\\ 7&8 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Both matrices are $2\times2$, so they are compatible for addition:

$$ A+B= \begin{pmatrix} 6&8\\ 10&12 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Example: Incompatible for Addition

Suppose:

$$ A\text{ is }2\times3, \qquad B\text{ is }3\times2. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ A+B \text{ is undefined} }. $$

The matrices contain the same total number of entries, but their positions do not correspond.

Compatibility for Subtraction

Matrix subtraction has the same dimensional requirement as matrix addition:

$$ \boxed{ A-B \text{ is defined only when } A\text{ and }B\text{ have the same dimensions} }. $$

Compatibility for Equality

Two matrices can be equal only if they have the same dimensions and all corresponding entries are equal.

$$ \boxed{ A=B \iff a_{ij}=b_{ij} \text{ for every corresponding position} }. $$

Matrices of different dimensions cannot be equal.

Compatibility for Scalar Multiplication

Scalar multiplication does not require a second matrix. Any scalar $c$ may multiply a matrix $A$ over the same scalar field:

$$ \boxed{ cA }. $$

The resulting matrix has the same dimensions as $A$.

Compatibility for Matrix Multiplication

Matrix multiplication has a different dimensional rule.

Suppose:

$$ A\text{ is }m\times n $$

and:

$$ B\text{ is }p\times q. $$

The product $AB$ exists exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ n=p }. $$

In words:

$$ \boxed{ \text{columns of the first} = \text{rows of the second} }. $$

The Inner-Dimension Rule

The multiplication condition is often remembered by placing the dimensions side by side:

$$ (m\times\boxed n)(\boxed n\times q). $$

The inner dimensions must agree.

The Outer Dimensions Determine the Product

When:

$$ A\text{ is }m\times n \qquad\text{and}\qquad B\text{ is }n\times p, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ AB\text{ is }m\times p }. $$

The inner dimensions determine whether multiplication is possible; the outer dimensions determine the dimensions of the result.

Example: Compatible for Multiplication

Suppose:

$$ A\text{ is }2\times3, \qquad B\text{ is }3\times4. $$

Since the inner dimensions agree:

$$ (2\times\boxed3)(\boxed3\times4), $$

the product is defined and has dimensions:

$$ \boxed{ AB\text{ is }2\times4 }. $$

Example: Incompatible for Multiplication

Suppose:

$$ A\text{ is }2\times3, \qquad B\text{ is }2\times4. $$

Then:

$$ (2\times\boxed3)(\boxed2\times4). $$

Since:

$$ 3\ne2, $$

the product:

$$ \boxed{ AB \text{ is undefined} }. $$

AB May Exist While BA Does Not

Compatibility for matrix multiplication depends on order.

Suppose:

$$ A\text{ is }2\times3, \qquad B\text{ is }3\times4. $$

Then:

$$ AB: \quad (2\times3)(3\times4) $$

is defined, but:

$$ BA: \quad (3\times4)(2\times3) $$

is not, because $4\ne2$.

$$ \boxed{ AB\text{ defined} \not\Rightarrow BA\text{ defined} }. $$

AB and BA Can Have Different Dimensions

Suppose:

$$ A\text{ is }2\times3, \qquad B\text{ is }3\times2. $$

Then both products exist:

$$ AB\text{ is }2\times2 $$

while:

$$ BA\text{ is }3\times3. $$

Thus the products cannot be equal because they do not even have the same dimensions.

Compatibility Does Not Imply Commutativity

Even if both $AB$ and $BA$ exist and have the same dimensions, matrix multiplication generally remains noncommutative:

$$ \boxed{ AB\ne BA \quad\text{in general} }. $$

Compatibility tells us whether an operation is defined, not whether two differently ordered operations give the same result.

Why the Multiplication Rule Exists

Each entry of a matrix product is formed by taking a row from the first matrix and a column from the second.

If $A$ is $m\times n$, each row of $A$ contains $n$ entries. If $B$ is $n\times p$, each column of $B$ also contains $n$ entries.

Their lengths therefore match, allowing a dot product:

$$ \boxed{ (AB)_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{ik}b_{kj} }. $$

Row-by-Column Compatibility

Matrix multiplication can therefore be viewed operationally as repeated row-by-column dot products.

$$ \boxed{ \text{row length of }A = \text{column length of }B }. $$

That equality is exactly the inner-dimension condition.

Square Matrices

Two square matrices of the same order are automatically dimensionally compatible for addition, subtraction, and multiplication in either order.

If:

$$ A,B\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}, $$

then:

$$ A+B,\quad A-B,\quad AB,\quad BA $$

are all dimensionally defined.

This does not imply:

$$ AB=BA. $$

Rectangular Matrices

Rectangular matrices may also be compatible for multiplication. Matrices do not have to be square in order to multiply.

$$ \boxed{ (m\times n)(n\times p) \text{ is valid for any positive }m,n,p }. $$

Matrix-Vector Compatibility

A column vector with $n$ entries may be treated as an $n\times1$ matrix.

Thus if:

$$ A\text{ is }m\times n $$

and:

$$ \mathbf x\text{ is }n\times1, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ A\mathbf x \text{ is }m\times1 }. $$

Linear Systems

A matrix equation:

$$ A\mathbf x=\mathbf b $$

requires compatible dimensions.

If:

$$ A\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}, $$

then:

$$ \mathbf x\in\mathbb R^n \qquad\text{and}\qquad \mathbf b\in\mathbb R^m. $$

In matrix dimensions:

$$ \boxed{ (m\times n)(n\times1) = (m\times1) }. $$

Compatibility for Augmented Matrices

To augment a coefficient matrix $A$ with a column vector $\mathbf b$, they must have the same number of rows.

If:

$$ A\text{ is }m\times n \qquad\text{and}\qquad \mathbf b\text{ is }m\times1, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ [A\mid\mathbf b] \text{ is }m\times(n+1) }. $$

Horizontal Concatenation

More generally, matrices may be placed side by side only when they have the same number of rows.

$$ \boxed{ [A\ B] \text{ requires equal row counts} }. $$

Vertical Concatenation

Matrices may be stacked vertically only when they have the same number of columns.

$$ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} A\\ B \end{bmatrix} \text{ requires equal column counts} }. $$

Compatibility and Linear Transformations

An $m\times n$ matrix represents a linear transformation:

$$ \boxed{ T:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m }. $$

The number of columns matches the dimension of the input space, while the number of rows matches the dimension of the output space.

From this viewpoint, matrix-multiplication compatibility expresses whether the output space of one transformation fits the input space of the next.

Composition of Linear Transformations

Suppose:

$$ T:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^p $$

and:

$$ S:\mathbb R^p\to\mathbb R^m. $$

Their composition:

$$ S\circ T:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m $$

is possible because the output dimension of $T$ matches the input dimension of $S$.

The corresponding matrix dimensions are:

$$ (m\times p)(p\times n) = (m\times n). $$

Transpose Changes Compatibility

If:

$$ A\text{ is }m\times n, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ A^T\text{ is }n\times m }. $$

Transposition can therefore change which matrix products are dimensionally valid.

ATA and AAT

For any real $m\times n$ matrix $A$:

$$ A^TA $$

has dimensions:

$$ (n\times m)(m\times n) = n\times n, $$

while:

$$ AA^T $$

has dimensions:

$$ (m\times n)(n\times m) = m\times m. $$

Both products are always dimensionally defined, though they are generally different matrices.

Compatibility and Inverses

The ordinary two-sided matrix inverse is defined for square invertible matrices.

If $A$ is $n\times n$ and invertible:

$$ \boxed{ AA^{-1}=A^{-1}A=I_n }. $$

The dimensions are automatically compatible because both matrices are $n\times n$.

Compatibility Is Necessary, Not Sufficient

Dimensional compatibility means that an operation can be formed. It does not guarantee any additional mathematical property.

For example, compatible square matrices may multiply without being invertible, commuting, diagonalizable, or equal.

$$ \boxed{ \text{compatible} \Rightarrow \text{operation is dimensionally defined} } $$

but not:

$$ \boxed{ \text{compatible} \Rightarrow \text{special algebraic relationship} }. $$

Compatibility Does Not Mean Equal Dimensions for Multiplication

One of the most important distinctions is that equal dimensions are required for addition and subtraction, but not for multiplication.

Operation Compatibility Requirement
$A+B$ Same rows and same columns
$A-B$ Same rows and same columns
$AB$ Columns of $A$ = rows of $B$
$A=B$ Same dimensions, then corresponding entries must agree

Common Misconception: Same Number of Entries Means Compatible

A $2\times6$ matrix and a $3\times4$ matrix both contain twelve entries, but they cannot be added because their dimensions differ.

$$ \boxed{ \text{same number of entries} \ne \text{same dimensions} }. $$

Common Misconception: Matrices Must Have the Same Size to Multiply

This is false. For multiplication, only the inner dimensions must match.

$$ \boxed{ (2\times3)(3\times7) \text{ is valid} }. $$

Common Misconception: If AB Exists, BA Exists

Matrix multiplication is order-sensitive even at the level of dimensions.

$$ \boxed{ AB\text{ defined} \not\Rightarrow BA\text{ defined} }. $$

Common Misconception: Compatible Means Commutative

Compatibility means the operation is defined. Commutativity means reversing the operands produces the same result.

$$ \boxed{ \text{compatibility} \ne \text{commutativity} }. $$

Common Misconception: Inner Dimensions Give the Product Size

The inner dimensions determine whether multiplication is possible. The outer dimensions determine the size of the product.

$$ \boxed{ (m\times\color{inherit}{n}) (\color{inherit}{n}\times p) \longrightarrow m\times p }. $$

Common Misconception: Compatibility Guarantees a Solution

In a system:

$$ A\mathbf x=\mathbf b, $$

compatible dimensions only make the equation meaningful. They do not guarantee that a solution exists or that it is unique.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$A\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}$ "A is an m by n real matrix."
$A,B\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}$ "A and B have the same dimensions and are compatible for addition."
$(m\times n)(n\times p)$ "An m by n matrix times an n by p matrix."
$AB\in\mathbb R^{m\times p}$ "A B is an m by p matrix."
$AB$ undefined "A and B are not conformable for multiplication in this order."
$A\mathbf x=\mathbf b$ "A times x equals b."
$[A\mid\mathbf b]$ "The augmented matrix A bar b."
$A^TA$ "A transpose A."

In linear algebra, compatible and conformable are often used in similar ways. A text may say that matrices are conformable for multiplication when their inner dimensions agree.

It is preferable to state the operation when saying that matrices are compatible. The phrase A and B are compatible can be ambiguous because compatibility requirements differ between addition, multiplication, augmentation, and other constructions.

Defense Notation Advice: Write matrix dimensions before performing an unfamiliar product. For $AB$, compare the columns of $A$ with the rows of $B$. If they agree, use the outer dimensions for the size of $AB$. Never assume that the existence of $AB$ guarantees the existence of $BA$, and never use equal dimensions as the general multiplication criterion.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: Compatible matrices have dimensions satisfying the requirements of the operation being performed.
  • Alternative Term: Conformable matrices.
  • Dimension Notation: An $m\times n$ matrix has $m$ rows and $n$ columns.
  • Addition: Matrices must have identical dimensions.
  • Subtraction: Matrices must have identical dimensions.
  • Equality: Matrices must have identical dimensions and equal corresponding entries.
  • Scalar Multiplication: A scalar may multiply a matrix without a second matrix-dimension condition.
  • Matrix Multiplication: $$ \boxed{ (m\times n)(n\times p) \text{ is defined} }. $$
  • Inner Dimensions: Must match for matrix multiplication.
  • Outer Dimensions: Determine the dimensions of the matrix product.
  • Product Size: $$ \boxed{ (m\times n)(n\times p) = m\times p }. $$
  • Order Dependence: $AB$ may be defined when $BA$ is not.
  • Noncommutativity: Even when both products exist, $AB$ and $BA$ need not be equal.
  • Square Matrices: Two $n\times n$ matrices are dimensionally compatible for multiplication in either order.
  • Rectangular Matrices: May be multiplied whenever their inner dimensions match.
  • Matrix-Vector Product: An $m\times n$ matrix can multiply an $n\times1$ vector to produce an $m\times1$ vector.
  • Linear Systems: $$ \boxed{ (m\times n)(n\times1) = m\times1 }. $$
  • Augmentation: Matrices placed side by side must have the same number of rows.
  • Vertical Stacking: Matrices stacked vertically must have the same number of columns.
  • Linear Transformation Interpretation: An $m\times n$ matrix maps vectors from an $n$-dimensional input space to an $m$-dimensional output space.
  • Composition Interpretation: Matrix multiplication is compatible when the output dimension of one linear map matches the input dimension of the next.
  • Transpose: If $A$ is $m\times n$, then $A^T$ is $n\times m$.
  • Transpose Products: $A^TA$ and $AA^T$ are both always dimensionally defined for a finite rectangular matrix $A$.
  • Necessary, Not Sufficient: Compatibility guarantees only that the operation is dimensionally meaningful.
  • Common Error: Do not use the total number of matrix entries to determine compatibility.
  • Common Error: Do not require matrices to have equal dimensions for multiplication.
  • Common Error: Do not assume $BA$ exists merely because $AB$ exists.
  • Common Error: Do not use the inner dimensions as the dimensions of the product.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse compatibility with commutativity.
  • Terminological Contrast: Compatible matrices satisfy the dimensional requirements of a specified operation. Conformable matrices is a closely related term, particularly for matrix multiplication. Same dimensions specifically means equal row and column counts and is required for addition and subtraction. Compatible for multiplication means the first matrix has as many columns as the second has rows. Commuting matrices are matrices satisfying $AB=BA$, which is a separate property from dimensional compatibility.
