Factor of a Polynomial

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

A factor of a polynomial is a polynomial that divides another polynomial exactly, leaving a polynomial quotient and zero remainder.

If:

$$ \boxed{ P(x)=A(x)B(x) }, $$

then both $A(x)$ and $B(x)$ are factors of $P(x)$.

Equivalently, $A(x)$ is a factor of $P(x)$ if there exists a polynomial $Q(x)$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ P(x)=A(x)Q(x) }. $$

The term factor comes from the broader arithmetic idea of quantities whose product forms another quantity. In polynomial algebra, the same multiplicative language is extended from numbers to polynomial expressions.

For example:

$$ x^2-9 = (x-3)(x+3). $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ x-3 \text{ and } x+3 \text{ are factors of } x^2-9 }. $$

Operational Nuance

Divisibility Definition

A polynomial $D(x)$ is a factor of $P(x)$ precisely when:

$$ \boxed{ D(x)\mid P(x) } $$

meaning that:

$$ \boxed{ P(x)=D(x)Q(x) } $$

for some polynomial $Q(x)$ in the same coefficient system under discussion.

Polynomial Division Criterion

By polynomial division:

$$ P(x)=D(x)Q(x)+R(x), $$

where:

$$ \deg R<\deg D $$

whenever $D(x)\neq0$.

Then:

$$ \boxed{ D(x)\text{ is a factor of }P(x) \iff R(x)=0 }. $$

Example

Since:

$$ x^3-4x = x(x^2-4) = x(x-2)(x+2), $$

each of:

$$ \boxed{ x,\quad x-2,\quad x+2 } $$

is a factor of $x^3-4x$.

Factor Theorem

The Factor Theorem connects linear factors with polynomial zeros:

$$ \boxed{ x-c \text{ is a factor of }P(x) \iff P(c)=0 }. $$

Example of the Factor Theorem

Let:

$$ P(x)=x^3-6x^2+11x-6. $$

Evaluate:

$$ P(1)=1-6+11-6=0. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ x-1 \text{ is a factor of }P(x) }. $$

Root-Factor Connection

If $c$ is a root or zero of $P$, then:

$$ \boxed{ P(c)=0 } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ x-c \text{ is a factor of }P(x) }. $$

Factor vs. Root

A root is a number; a factor is a polynomial expression.

$$ \boxed{ c=\text{root} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad x-c=\text{linear factor} }. $$

For example, if $3$ is a root, then $x-3$ is the corresponding factor.

Sign Reversal in Linear Factors

The factor corresponding to the root $c$ is:

$$ \boxed{x-c}. $$

Thus a root of $-4$ corresponds to:

$$ \boxed{x+4}. $$

Factored Form

A polynomial written as a product of factors is in factored form:

$$ \boxed{ P(x)=a(x-r_1)(x-r_2)\cdots(x-r_n) }. $$

When such a complete linear factorization exists over the coefficient field, the roots are visible directly.

Leading Constant

The constant factor $a$ is also a factor of the polynomial:

$$ P(x)=aQ(x). $$

In elementary factoring, nonzero constants may be called constant factors, although discussion often focuses on nonconstant polynomial factors.

Greatest Common Factor

Terms of a polynomial may share a common polynomial or monomial factor.

For example:

$$ 6x^3+9x^2 = 3x^2(2x+3). $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ 3x^2 } $$

is a greatest common monomial factor of the terms.

Factoring Out a Common Factor

The distributive law:

$$ ab+ac=a(b+c) $$

underlies common-factor extraction.

Difference of Squares

The identity:

$$ \boxed{ a^2-b^2=(a-b)(a+b) } $$

provides a standard polynomial factorization.

Example

$$ x^2-25 = \boxed{ (x-5)(x+5) }. $$

Perfect-Square Trinomial

$$ \boxed{ a^2+2ab+b^2=(a+b)^2 }. $$

Likewise:

$$ \boxed{ a^2-2ab+b^2=(a-b)^2 }. $$

Difference of Cubes

$$ \boxed{ a^3-b^3 = (a-b)(a^2+ab+b^2) }. $$

Sum of Cubes

$$ \boxed{ a^3+b^3 = (a+b)(a^2-ab+b^2) }. $$

Quadratic Factorization

A quadratic:

$$ ax^2+bx+c $$

may factor into:

$$ \boxed{ a(x-r_1)(x-r_2) } $$

when suitable roots $r_1$ and $r_2$ exist in the coefficient field.

Example

$$ x^2-5x+6 = \boxed{ (x-2)(x-3) }. $$

Multiplicity

If:

$$ (x-c)^m $$

divides $P(x)$ but:

$$ (x-c)^{m+1} $$

does not, then $c$ is a root of multiplicity $m$.

$$ \boxed{ P(x)=(x-c)^mQ(x), \qquad Q(c)\neq0 }. $$

Repeated Factor

A factor appearing more than once is called a repeated factor.

For example:

$$ P(x)=(x-2)^3(x+1) $$

contains the factor $x-2$ with multiplicity $3$.

Degree and Factors

If:

$$ P(x)=A(x)B(x), $$

with nonzero polynomials over a field or integral domain, then:

$$ \boxed{ \deg P = \deg A+\deg B }. $$

Proper Factor

Informally, a proper factor is a nonconstant factor of lower degree than the polynomial itself, although exact conventions may vary.

Trivial Factors

Depending on context, units and associates may be treated as trivial factors. For example, over $\mathbb{R}[x]$, every nonzero constant is a unit.

Factorization Depends on the Coefficient System

Whether a polynomial factors depends on the field or ring in which its coefficients and factors are required to lie.

Example over the Real Numbers

The polynomial:

$$ x^2+1 $$

has no linear factors over $\mathbb{R}$.

Example over the Complex Numbers

Since:

$$ i^2=-1, $$

one has:

$$ \boxed{ x^2+1=(x-i)(x+i) }. $$

Irreducible Polynomial

A nonconstant polynomial is irreducible over a specified field or ring if it cannot be factored there into nonconstant factors of smaller degree.

Irreducibility Is Relative

The polynomial:

$$ x^2-2 $$

is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ but factors over $\mathbb{R}$:

$$ \boxed{ x^2-2 = (x-\sqrt2)(x+\sqrt2) }. $$

Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

Every nonconstant polynomial of degree $n$ with complex coefficients factors completely over $\mathbb{C}$ into $n$ linear factors when multiplicity is counted.

$$ \boxed{ P(x) = a \prod_{k=1}^{n}(x-r_k) }. $$

Real Polynomial Factorization

A real polynomial can be factored over $\mathbb{R}$ into linear factors and irreducible quadratic factors.

Complex Conjugate Roots

If a polynomial has real coefficients and $a+bi$ is a nonreal root, then:

$$ a-bi $$

is also a root.

The corresponding pair of linear factors multiplies to a real quadratic factor.

Remainder Theorem Connection

Dividing $P(x)$ by $x-c$ produces remainder:

$$ \boxed{P(c)}. $$

Thus:

$$ P(c)=0 $$

precisely when $x-c$ divides $P(x)$ exactly.

Synthetic Division

Synthetic division provides an efficient way to test or remove a linear factor of the form:

$$ x-c. $$

Example

If synthetic division of $P(x)$ by $x-4$ produces remainder $0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ x-4\mid P(x) }. $$

Rational Root Theorem Connection

For a polynomial with integer coefficients, the Rational Root Theorem supplies possible rational roots and therefore possible rational linear factors.

If:

$$ \frac pq $$

is a rational root in lowest terms of:

$$ a_nx^n+\cdots+a_0, $$

then:

$$ p\mid a_0, \qquad q\mid a_n. $$

Factorization and Solving Equations

If:

$$ P(x)=0 $$

and:

$$ P(x)=A(x)B(x), $$

then the zero-product property gives:

$$ \boxed{ A(x)=0 \quad\text{or}\quad B(x)=0 }. $$

Factorization Preserves the Polynomial

Factoring rewrites a polynomial without changing its value:

$$ \boxed{ x^2-5x+6 = (x-2)(x-3) }. $$

These are two equivalent forms of the same polynomial.

Factoring vs. Expanding

Factoring converts a sum or expanded expression into a product:

$$ x^2-9 \longrightarrow (x-3)(x+3). $$

Expanding reverses this process:

$$ (x-3)(x+3) \longrightarrow x^2-9. $$

Factor vs. Term

Factors are multiplied; terms are added or subtracted.

In:

$$ 3x(x+2), $$

$3$, $x$, and $x+2$ are factors.

In:

$$ x^2+2x+1, $$

$x^2$, $2x$, and $1$ are terms.

Factor vs. Coefficient

A coefficient is a numerical or algebraic multiplier attached to a term. It may itself be a factor, but the terms are not synonymous.

Factor Pair

If:

$$ P(x)=A(x)B(x), $$

the pair $A(x)$ and $B(x)$ may be described as a factor pair of $P(x)$.

Factors Need Not Be Linear

A factor of a polynomial can have degree greater than one.

For example:

$$ x^4+5x^2+6 = (x^2+2)(x^2+3). $$

Both quadratic expressions are factors.

Factorization over Integers

In elementary algebra, "factor completely" often means to factor as far as possible using integer or rational coefficients unless another coefficient system is specified.

Primitive Polynomial

For integer-coefficient polynomials, one may first factor out the numerical greatest common divisor of all coefficients, called the content.

Unit Factors

In a polynomial ring over a field, every nonzero constant is invertible and is therefore a unit. Factorizations differing only by nonzero constant factors are often considered essentially the same.

Uniqueness of Factorization

Polynomial rings over fields are unique factorization domains. Thus a nonzero polynomial factors into irreducible polynomials uniquely up to order and multiplication by units.

Derivative and Repeated Factors

If $c$ is a repeated root of $P(x)$, then:

$$ \boxed{ P(c)=0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad P'(c)=0 }. $$

Therefore $P$ and $P'$ share the factor $x-c$.

Detecting Repeated Factors

The greatest common divisor:

$$ \gcd(P,P') $$

can reveal repeated polynomial factors.

Graphical Interpretation

A real linear factor $x-c$ corresponds to an $x$-intercept at:

$$ \boxed{(c,0)}. $$

Odd Multiplicity

At a real zero of odd multiplicity, the graph changes sign and ordinarily crosses the $x$-axis.

Even Multiplicity

At a real zero of even multiplicity, the graph does not change sign and ordinarily touches the $x$-axis and turns.

Factors and Sign Analysis

Factored form:

$$ P(x)=a\prod_k(x-r_k)^{m_k} $$

makes it easier to analyze where $P(x)$ is positive, negative, or zero.

Factors in Rational Functions

Common polynomial factors in numerators and denominators may cancel algebraically, but original domain exclusions remain.

For example:

$$ \frac{(x-2)(x+1)}{x-2} = x+1, \qquad x\neq2. $$

The canceled factor creates a removable hole in the original rational function.

Polynomial GCD

Two polynomials may share a greatest common divisor, defined up to multiplication by units.

For example:

$$ P(x)=(x-1)^2(x+3), $$
$$ Q(x)=(x-1)(x+5), $$

so a greatest common factor is:

$$ \boxed{x-1}. $$

Multivariable Polynomials

Factor language extends to several variables.

For example:

$$ x^2-y^2 = \boxed{ (x-y)(x+y) }. $$

Factorization Algorithms

Computer algebra systems use specialized algorithms to factor polynomials over integers, rationals, finite fields, algebraic number fields, and other coefficient domains.

Factoring Is Domain-Dependent

A symbolic system must know the intended coefficient domain before the phrase "factor completely" has a unique mathematical interpretation.

Common Misconception: A factor of a polynomial is not the same thing as a root. A root is a value $c$ for which $P(c)=0$; the corresponding linear factor is $x-c$. Also, whether a polynomial can be factored further depends on the coefficient system. A polynomial may be irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ yet reducible over $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$P(x)=A(x)B(x)$ "A of x and B of x are factors of P of x."
$D(x)\mid P(x)$ "D of x divides P of x."
$P(x)=D(x)Q(x)$ "P of x equals D of x times Q of x."
$P(c)=0$ "C is a zero, or root, of P."
$x-c\mid P(x)$ "X minus c is a factor of P of x."
$(x-c)^m\mid P(x)$ "X minus c occurs as a factor with at least multiplicity m."
$P(x)=a\prod_{k=1}^{n}(x-r_k)$ "P is written as its leading constant times its linear factors."

Typical instructions include:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"factor the polynomial"} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{"factor completely"} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{"determine whether }x-c\text{ is a factor"} }. $$

The phrase factor completely should be interpreted relative to the allowed coefficient system.

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish carefully between a root and its corresponding factor:

$$ \boxed{ c\text{ is a root} \iff x-c\text{ is a factor} }. $$

Also state the coefficient domain when irreducibility matters. Saying that $x^2+1$ "cannot be factored" is incomplete unless the intended setting, such as $\mathbb{R}[x]$, is understood.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Definition: A factor of a polynomial divides that polynomial with zero remainder.
  • Product Form: $$ \boxed{ P(x)=A(x)B(x) }. $$
  • Divisibility Notation: $$ \boxed{ A(x)\mid P(x) }. $$
  • Quotient Requirement: If $A$ is a factor, then: $$ P=AQ $$ for some polynomial $Q$.
  • Zero-Remainder Test: A divisor is a factor exactly when polynomial division gives remainder zero.
  • Linear Factor: A polynomial of the form $x-c$.
  • Factor Theorem: $$ \boxed{ x-c\mid P(x) \iff P(c)=0 }. $$
  • Root: $c$ is a root when $P(c)=0$.
  • Root-Factor Correspondence: Root $c$ corresponds to factor $x-c$.
  • Sign Caution: Root $-3$ corresponds to factor $x+3$.
  • Remainder Theorem: Dividing by $x-c$ leaves remainder $P(c)$.
  • Synthetic Division: Efficiently tests and removes linear factors.
  • Common Factor: A factor shared by all terms may be extracted first.
  • Greatest Common Factor: The largest shared monomial or polynomial factor under the chosen convention.
  • Difference of Squares: $$ a^2-b^2=(a-b)(a+b). $$
  • Perfect-Square Trinomial: $$ a^2+2ab+b^2=(a+b)^2. $$
  • Difference of Cubes: $$ a^3-b^3=(a-b)(a^2+ab+b^2). $$
  • Sum of Cubes: $$ a^3+b^3=(a+b)(a^2-ab+b^2). $$
  • Quadratic Factoring: Quadratics may factor into two linear factors when their roots lie in the coefficient field.
  • Example: $$ x^2-7x+12=(x-3)(x-4). $$
  • Repeated Factor: A factor may occur more than once.
  • Multiplicity: $(x-c)^m$ indicates root multiplicity $m$ when the next higher power does not divide the polynomial.
  • Simple Root: Multiplicity $1$.
  • Double Root: Multiplicity $2$.
  • Triple Root: Multiplicity $3$.
  • Degree Relation: For nonzero polynomials: $$ \deg(AB)=\deg A+\deg B. $$
  • Constant Factor: Nonzero constants may appear as factors.
  • Leading Coefficient: Complete factored form often retains a leading constant.
  • Factored Form: $$ P(x)=a\prod_k(x-r_k)^{m_k}. $$
  • Expanded Form: Factoring and expanding are inverse rewriting processes.
  • Zero-Product Property: Factored polynomial equations are solved by setting factors equal to zero.
  • Factor vs. Root: A factor is a polynomial; a root is a value.
  • Factor vs. Term: Factors multiply, while terms add or subtract.
  • Factor vs. Coefficient: A coefficient is a multiplier of a term and may itself be one factor.
  • Nonlinear Factor: Factors need not be linear.
  • Quadratic Factor: An irreducible quadratic may remain after factoring over the reals.
  • Coefficient Domain: Factorization depends on whether factors are allowed over $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, or another ring or field.
  • Irreducible Polynomial: Cannot be expressed as a product of lower-degree nonconstant factors over the specified coefficient system.
  • Relative Irreducibility: A polynomial may be irreducible over one field and reducible over another.
  • Example over $\mathbb{Q}$: $x^2-2$ is irreducible over the rationals.
  • Example over $\mathbb{R}$: $$ x^2-2=(x-\sqrt2)(x+\sqrt2). $$
  • Example over $\mathbb{R}$: $x^2+1$ is irreducible over the reals.
  • Example over $\mathbb{C}$: $$ x^2+1=(x-i)(x+i). $$
  • Fundamental Theorem of Algebra: Every nonconstant complex polynomial factors completely into linear factors.
  • Real Factorization: Real polynomials factor into real linear and irreducible quadratic factors.
  • Complex Conjugates: Nonreal roots of real polynomials occur in conjugate pairs.
  • Rational Root Theorem: Helps identify possible rational linear factors.
  • Integer Coefficients: Candidate rational roots are constrained by the leading and constant coefficients.
  • Primitive Factorization: Integer-coefficient polynomials may first have their numerical content factored out.
  • Unit Factor: Nonzero constants are units in polynomial rings over fields.
  • Associates: Factors differing by multiplication by a unit are often regarded as essentially equivalent.
  • Unique Factorization: Over a field, polynomial factorization into irreducibles is unique up to units and order.
  • Repeated-Root Test: A repeated root $c$ satisfies: $$ P(c)=P'(c)=0. $$
  • Polynomial GCD: $\gcd(P,P')$ can reveal repeated factors.
  • Graphical Zero: A real factor $x-c$ corresponds to an $x$-intercept at $(c,0)$.
  • Odd Multiplicity: The graph typically crosses the axis at the corresponding root.
  • Even Multiplicity: The graph typically touches the axis and turns.
  • Sign Analysis: Factored form helps determine the sign of a polynomial on intervals.
  • Polynomial Inequalities: Factors identify boundary points for sign charts.
  • Rational Functions: Polynomial factors may occur in both numerator and denominator.
  • Cancellation: Common factors may cancel algebraically where the canceled factor is nonzero.
  • Domain Preservation: Canceling a factor does not restore an excluded input from the original denominator.
  • Hole: A canceled numerator-denominator factor can correspond to a removable discontinuity.
  • Multivariable Polynomial: Factorization extends to polynomials in several variables.
  • Multivariable Example: $$ x^2-y^2=(x-y)(x+y). $$
  • Computer Algebra: Symbolic factoring depends on the specified or inferred coefficient domain.
  • Algorithmic Factoring: Different algorithms are used over integers, rationals, finite fields, and algebraic extensions.
  • Complete Factorization: The phrase has meaning only relative to the permitted coefficient system.
  • Verification: Multiplying proposed factors should reproduce the original polynomial.
  • Division Verification: A proposed factor can be tested by polynomial or synthetic division.
  • Evaluation Verification: A proposed linear factor $x-c$ can be tested by checking whether $P(c)=0$.
  • Core Interpretation: A factor of a polynomial is a polynomial divisor that produces zero remainder: $$ \boxed{ D(x)\mid P(x) \iff P(x)=D(x)Q(x) } $$ for some polynomial $Q(x)$. Linear factors correspond directly to roots through the Factor Theorem, repeated factors encode multiplicity, and the question of whether a polynomial factors further always depends on the coefficient system in which the factorization is being considered.
