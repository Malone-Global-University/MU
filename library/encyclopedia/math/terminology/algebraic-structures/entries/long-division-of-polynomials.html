Long Division of Polynomials

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

Long division of polynomials is an algorithm for dividing one polynomial by another nonzero polynomial. It mirrors ordinary long division of integers: determine the next quotient term, multiply, subtract, and repeat until the remainder has lower degree than the divisor.

If a polynomial $P(x)$ is divided by a nonzero polynomial $D(x)$, the process seeks polynomials $Q(x)$ and $R(x)$ satisfying:

$$ \boxed{ P(x) = D(x)Q(x)+R(x) } $$

with:

$$ \boxed{ R(x)=0 \quad\text{or}\quad \deg R<\deg D }. $$

The terminology parallels integer division:

  • $P(x)$ is the dividend;
  • $D(x)$ is the divisor;
  • $Q(x)$ is the quotient;
  • $R(x)$ is the remainder.

The word long distinguishes the full written algorithm from shorter specialized methods such as synthetic division. The essential idea is repeated cancellation of the current leading term of the dividend or remainder.

Operational Nuance

Polynomial Division Algorithm

For polynomials $P(x)$ and nonzero $D(x)$ over a field, there exist unique polynomials $Q(x)$ and $R(x)$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ P(x) = D(x)Q(x)+R(x) } $$

where:

$$ \boxed{ R(x)=0 \quad\text{or}\quad \deg R<\deg D }. $$

Quotient-Remainder Form

Dividing both sides by $D(x)$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{P(x)}{D(x)} = Q(x) + \frac{R(x)}{D(x)} }. $$

First Operational Step

Divide the leading term of the current dividend by the leading term of the divisor.

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\operatorname{LT}(P)} {\operatorname{LT}(D)} } $$

This produces the next term of the quotient.

Why Leading Terms Matter

The quotient term is chosen specifically so that multiplying it by the divisor cancels the current highest-degree term.

Basic Cycle

Polynomial long division repeatedly performs four actions:

  1. divide the leading terms;
  2. multiply the divisor by the resulting quotient term;
  3. subtract;
  4. repeat with the new remainder.

Mnemonic

A common classroom mnemonic is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{Divide} \rightarrow \text{Multiply} \rightarrow \text{Subtract} \rightarrow \text{Bring Down} } $$

though in polynomial work, "bring down" means continue with the remaining lower terms rather than literally copying digits as in integer division.

Example

Divide:

$$ x^2+5x+6 $$

by:

$$ x+2. $$

Step 1: Divide Leading Terms

$$ \frac{x^2}{x}=x. $$

Therefore the first quotient term is:

$$ \boxed{ x }. $$

Step 2: Multiply

$$ x(x+2) = x^2+2x. $$

Step 3: Subtract

$$ (x^2+5x+6) - (x^2+2x) = 3x+6. $$

Step 4: Divide Again

$$ \frac{3x}{x}=3. $$

The next quotient term is:

$$ \boxed{ 3 }. $$

Multiply Again

$$ 3(x+2)=3x+6. $$

Subtract Again

$$ (3x+6)-(3x+6)=0. $$

Final Result

$$ \boxed{ \frac{x^2+5x+6}{x+2} = x+3 }. $$

Exact Division

When the remainder is zero:

$$ \boxed{ R(x)=0 } $$

the divisor divides the dividend exactly.

Factor Interpretation

If:

$$ P(x)=D(x)Q(x), $$

then $D(x)$ is a factor of $P(x)$.

Example with a Remainder

Divide:

$$ x^2+1 $$

by:

$$ x+1. $$

First:

$$ \frac{x^2}{x}=x. $$

Multiply:

$$ x(x+1)=x^2+x. $$

Subtract:

$$ (x^2+0x+1)-(x^2+x) = -x+1. $$

Divide again:

$$ \frac{-x}{x}=-1. $$

Multiply:

$$ -1(x+1) = -x-1. $$

Subtract:

$$ (-x+1)-(-x-1) = 2. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ x^2+1 = (x+1)(x-1)+2 }. $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{x^2+1}{x+1} = x-1+\frac{2}{x+1} }. $$

Stopping Condition

Stop when:

$$ \boxed{ \deg R<\deg D }. $$

At that point, no further polynomial quotient term can cancel the leading term of the remainder.

Why the Degree Must Drop

Every successful subtraction cancels the current leading term, causing the degree of the working remainder to decrease.

Degree of the Quotient

If $P$ and $D$ are nonzero and:

$$ \deg P\geq\deg D, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \deg Q = \deg P-\deg D }. $$

Divisor Degree Larger than Dividend Degree

If:

$$ \deg P<\deg D, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ Q(x)=0, \qquad R(x)=P(x) }. $$

No long-division step is needed.

Standard Form before Division

Arrange dividend and divisor in descending powers before beginning.

For example:

$$ 2+3x^3-x $$

should be written:

$$ \boxed{ 3x^3-x+2 }. $$

Missing Terms

Missing powers should usually be represented by zero-coefficient placeholders.

For example:

$$ x^3+2x-5 $$

can be organized as:

$$ \boxed{ x^3+0x^2+2x-5 }. $$

Why Zero Placeholders Help

They preserve column alignment and reduce the risk of subtracting terms of different degrees.

Example with a Missing Term

Divide:

$$ x^3+0x^2-4x+4 $$

by:

$$ x-2. $$

The zero coefficient for $x^2$ keeps each degree in its proper column throughout the calculation.

Signs during Subtraction

Each multiplication result must be subtracted as an entire polynomial.

Thus:

$$ A(x)-\bigl(B(x)\bigr) $$

requires changing every sign in $B(x)$ when distributing the subtraction.

Example of Sign Handling

$$ (3x^2+2x-1) - (3x^2-6x+3) $$

becomes:

$$ 3x^2+2x-1-3x^2+6x-3. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ 8x-4 }. $$

Leading-Coefficient Division

If the leading terms are:

$$ 12x^5 \qquad\text{and}\qquad 3x^2, $$

then the next quotient term is:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{12x^5}{3x^2} = 4x^3 }. $$

Coefficient and Exponent Rules

Divide coefficients and subtract exponents:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{ax^m}{bx^n} = \frac{a}{b}x^{m-n} } $$

when the coefficient division is valid and $b\neq0$.

Long Division as Repeated Leading-Term Cancellation

Conceptually, polynomial long division is not primarily about the visual bracket. It is an algorithm that repeatedly eliminates the highest-degree term until no further elimination is possible.

Verification Identity

After division, verify:

$$ \boxed{ \text{Dividend} = (\text{Divisor})(\text{Quotient}) + \text{Remainder} }. $$

Verification Example

If:

$$ \frac{x^2+1}{x+1} = x-1+\frac{2}{x+1}, $$

then:

$$ (x+1)(x-1)+2 = x^2-1+2 = x^2+1. $$

Remainder Must Be Smaller in Degree

A result is incomplete if the remainder still has degree at least as large as the divisor.

Remainder Theorem

When dividing $P(x)$ by a linear divisor:

$$ x-c, $$

the remainder is:

$$ \boxed{ P(c) }. $$

Why the Remainder Theorem Follows

Polynomial division gives:

$$ P(x) = (x-c)Q(x)+R, $$

where $R$ is constant because the divisor has degree $1$.

Setting $x=c$ gives:

$$ P(c) = 0\cdot Q(c)+R. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ R=P(c) }. $$

Factor Theorem

A linear polynomial $x-c$ is a factor of $P(x)$ exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ P(c)=0 }. $$

Thus the factor theorem is the zero-remainder case of the remainder theorem.

Synthetic Division

Synthetic division is a compressed algorithm for division by a divisor of the form:

$$ \boxed{ x-c }. $$

It uses coefficients rather than writing every polynomial term explicitly.

Long Division vs. Synthetic Division

Long division works for general polynomial divisors, while elementary synthetic division is typically restricted to linear divisors of the form $x-c$.

Example of a Divisor Not Suited to Basic Synthetic Division

Division by:

$$ x^2+1 $$

is naturally handled by polynomial long division rather than the standard one-number synthetic-division format.

Rational Expressions

Long division converts an improper rational expression into a polynomial plus a proper rational expression.

Improper Rational Expression

If:

$$ \deg P\geq\deg D, $$

then:

$$ \frac{P(x)}{D(x)} $$

is commonly called an improper rational expression.

Proper Remainder Fraction

After long division:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{R(x)}{D(x)} } $$

is proper because:

$$ \deg R<\deg D. $$

Asymptotic Interpretation

The quotient polynomial can describe the large-$|x|$ behavior of an improper rational function.

If:

$$ \frac{P(x)}{D(x)} = Q(x)+\frac{R(x)}{D(x)}, $$

and:

$$ \frac{R(x)}{D(x)}\to0 $$

as $|x|\to\infty$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{P(x)}{D(x)} \sim Q(x) }. $$

Slant Asymptote

If:

$$ \deg P = \deg D+1, $$

the quotient is linear and often gives a slant asymptote.

Polynomial Asymptote

If the numerator degree exceeds the denominator degree by more than one, long division can reveal a higher-degree polynomial asymptote.

Partial Fractions

Before decomposing an improper rational expression into partial fractions, one usually performs polynomial long division first.

Integration of Rational Functions

Long division is often an initial step in integrating rational functions when the numerator degree is at least the denominator degree.

Example Structure

$$ \int \frac{P(x)}{D(x)} \,dx $$

may first be rewritten as:

$$ \boxed{ \int Q(x)\,dx + \int \frac{R(x)}{D(x)} \,dx }. $$

Polynomial GCD Computation

Repeated polynomial division underlies the Euclidean algorithm for finding a greatest common divisor of two polynomials.

Euclidean Algorithm Pattern

$$ P=DQ_1+R_1, $$
$$ D=R_1Q_2+R_2, $$
$$ R_1=R_2Q_3+R_3, $$

and so on until the remainder becomes zero.

Polynomial Rings over a Field

The familiar division algorithm works especially cleanly when polynomial coefficients lie in a field such as:

$$ \mathbb{Q}, \qquad \mathbb{R}, \qquad \mathbb{C}. $$

Coefficient-System Caveat

Over rings where leading coefficients are not always invertible, polynomial division may require extra qualifications.

Example over the Integers

Dividing:

$$ x $$

by:

$$ 2 $$

does not produce a quotient in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, although it does in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$.

Field Context

Over $\mathbb{Q}[x]$:

$$ \boxed{ x = 2\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)+0 }. $$

Uniqueness

Over a field, the quotient and remainder satisfying the degree condition are unique.

Why Uniqueness Matters

Polynomial long division does not merely produce one convenient decomposition; it produces the uniquely determined quotient and remainder for the chosen dividend and nonzero divisor.

Zero Divisor

Division by the zero polynomial is undefined.

$$ \boxed{ D(x)\neq0 } $$

is therefore essential.

Zero Dividend

If the dividend is zero and the divisor is nonzero:

$$ \boxed{ 0 = D(x)\cdot0+0 }. $$

Thus quotient and remainder are both zero.

Constant Divisor

Dividing by a nonzero constant divides every coefficient by that constant in a coefficient field.

For example:

$$ \frac{6x^2-9x+3}{3} = \boxed{ 2x^2-3x+1 }. $$

Degree-Zero Divisor

A nonzero constant polynomial has degree $0$, so a remainder after division by it must be zero over a coefficient field.

Multivariable Polynomials

Polynomial division in several variables is more complicated because one must specify a monomial order.

Why Univariate Division Is Simpler

Powers of one variable have a natural descending order:

$$ x^n>x^{n-1}>\cdots>1. $$

In several variables, no single natural monomial ordering exists.

Gröbner-Basis Connection

Multivariable polynomial division and systematic remainder reduction lead to the theory of Gröbner bases.

Algorithmic Interpretation

Long division can be described abstractly as:

  1. compare leading terms;
  2. construct a quotient term that cancels the dividend leader;
  3. subtract the corresponding multiple of the divisor;
  4. iterate until the leading term is no longer divisible in the required degree sense.

Common Error: Dividing Entire Polynomials Term by Term

In general:

$$ \frac{a+b}{c+d} $$

cannot be replaced by:

$$ \frac{a}{c}+\frac{b}{d}. $$

Polynomial division requires the division algorithm, factoring, or another valid algebraic method.

Common Error: Dividing Every Dividend Term by Only the Leading Divisor Term

The leading divisor term determines each new quotient term, but the entire divisor must then be multiplied and subtracted.

Common Error: Forgetting Missing Powers

Omitting placeholders such as $0x^2$ can cause terms of different degrees to be misaligned.

Common Error: Failing to Arrange Descending Powers

Long division depends on comparing current leading terms. Standard ordering makes that structure visible.

Common Error: Dividing Coefficients but Not Variable Powers

When dividing monomials, both coefficient and exponent operations matter.

Common Error: Adding Exponents during Division

For like bases:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{x^m}{x^n} = x^{m-n} } $$

not $x^{m+n}$.

Common Error: Forgetting to Multiply by the Entire Divisor

After choosing a quotient term, multiply that term by every term of the divisor.

Common Error: Subtracting Only the Leading Terms

The entire polynomial product must be subtracted.

Common Error: Losing Negative Signs during Subtraction

Subtracting a polynomial changes the sign of each of its terms before combination.

Common Error: Stopping Too Soon

Continue while the current remainder has degree greater than or equal to the degree of the divisor.

Common Error: Continuing Too Long

Stop once:

$$ \boxed{ \deg R<\deg D }. $$

Common Error: Calling a Nonzero Remainder a Quotient Term

The final remainder belongs in:

$$ \boxed{ Q(x)+\frac{R(x)}{D(x)} }. $$

Common Error: Forgetting the Remainder in the Final Answer

If $R\neq0$, writing only $Q$ changes the value of the original rational expression.

Common Error: Leaving a Remainder with Degree Too High

Such a remainder indicates that the division process is unfinished.

Common Error: Using Basic Synthetic Division for Every Divisor

Standard synthetic division is a shortcut for special divisors; polynomial long division is the general elementary method.

Common Error: Confusing Division with Factoring

Division can test or reveal factors, but the operations are not synonymous.

Common Error: Forgetting the Divisor Cannot Be Zero

Polynomial division by the zero polynomial is undefined.

Common Error: Assuming Integer Coefficients Guarantee an Integer-Coefficient Quotient

The coefficient domain matters. Division may naturally produce fractions.

Common Misconception: Polynomial long division is not just a formatting trick copied from integer arithmetic. Its mathematical core is repeated cancellation of leading terms. Each quotient term is chosen so that multiplication by the divisor eliminates the current highest-degree term, and the process ends precisely when the remainder has degree lower than the divisor.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$P(x)=D(x)Q(x)+R(x)$ "The dividend equals the divisor times the quotient plus the remainder."
$\deg R<\deg D$ "The remainder has degree less than the divisor."
$P(x)/D(x)$ "P of x divided by D of x."
$Q(x)+R(x)/D(x)$ "The quotient plus the remainder over the divisor."
$\operatorname{LT}(P)/\operatorname{LT}(D)$ "Divide the leading term of the dividend by the leading term of the divisor."
$P(c)$ "The remainder when P is divided by x minus c," in the remainder-theorem setting.
$R(x)=0$ "The division is exact," or "the divisor is a factor of the dividend."

The phrase divide leading terms does not mean that only the leading terms are involved in the full step. It means that the next quotient term is selected from the leading-term ratio; that quotient term is then multiplied by the entire divisor.

The phrase bring down survives from integer long division. In polynomial work, a more precise description is often include the next lower-degree term in the working remainder.

Defense Notation Advice: Write both polynomials in descending powers, insert zero coefficients for missing powers, align equal degrees vertically, and place parentheses around the polynomial being subtracted. Check the final result with $P=DQ+R$, and verify that the final remainder is either zero or has degree strictly less than the divisor.
