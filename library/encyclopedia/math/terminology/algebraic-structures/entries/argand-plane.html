Argand Plane

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

The Argand plane is a geometric representation of the complex numbers. It is also commonly called the complex plane or Argand diagram.

A complex number:

$$ \boxed{ z=a+bi } $$

is represented by the point:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b) }. $$

The horizontal coordinate records the real part of the number, while the vertical coordinate records the coefficient of the imaginary unit $i$.

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Re}(z)=a, \qquad \operatorname{Im}(z)=b }. $$

The terminology commemorates the geometric treatment of complex numbers associated with Argand. Historically, however, the same fundamental geometric interpretation had been published earlier by Caspar Wessel, so the name should not be interpreted as establishing sole priority for the idea.

Operational Nuance

The Two Axes

The horizontal axis is the real axis:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Im}(z)=0 }. $$

Points on it represent ordinary real numbers:

$$ a+0i=a. $$

The vertical axis is the imaginary axis:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Re}(z)=0 }. $$

Points on it represent pure imaginary numbers:

$$ bi. $$

Point Correspondence

The complex number:

$$ z=3+2i $$

corresponds to:

$$ \boxed{ (3,2) }. $$

Similarly:

$$ -4+3i \longleftrightarrow (-4,3). $$

A negative imaginary part places the point below the real axis:

$$ 2-5i \longleftrightarrow (2,-5). $$

Complex Number as a Vector

A complex number may also be visualized as the directed segment from the origin to its point:

$$ \boxed{ z=a+bi \longleftrightarrow \langle a,b\rangle }. $$

This interpretation connects complex arithmetic to vector geometry.

Complex Addition

Let:

$$ z_1=a+bi, \qquad z_2=c+di. $$

Then:

$$ z_1+z_2 = (a+c)+(b+d)i. $$

Geometrically:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b)+(c,d) = (a+c,b+d) }. $$

Thus complex addition behaves exactly like vector addition in the plane.

Complex Subtraction

$$ z_1-z_2 = (a-c)+(b-d)i. $$

Geometrically, this represents the displacement from $z_2$ to $z_1$.

Distance between Complex Numbers

The distance between points $z_1$ and $z_2$ is:

$$ \boxed{ |z_1-z_2| }. $$

If:

$$ z_1=a+bi, \qquad z_2=c+di, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ |z_1-z_2| = \sqrt{(a-c)^2+(b-d)^2} }. $$

Modulus

The distance from the origin to:

$$ z=a+bi $$

is its modulus or complex absolute value:

$$ \boxed{ |z|= \sqrt{a^2+b^2} }. $$

This is an immediate application of the Pythagorean theorem.

Example of Modulus

If:

$$ z=3+4i, $$

then:

$$ |z| = \sqrt{3^2+4^2} = \boxed{5}. $$

Argument

The argument of a nonzero complex number is an angle locating the number relative to the positive real axis.

$$ \boxed{ \arg z=\theta }. $$

Positive angles are conventionally measured counterclockwise.

Arguments Are Not Unique

If $\theta$ is an argument of $z$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \theta+2\pi k, \qquad k\in\mathbb Z } $$

represents the same direction.

Therefore a nonzero complex number has infinitely many arguments differing by full turns.

Principal Argument

A single representative is often selected and called the principal argument:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Arg}(z) }. $$

A common convention is:

$$ -\pi< \operatorname{Arg}(z) \le\pi, $$

although conventions may vary.

Polar Form

Let:

$$ r=|z| $$

and let $\theta$ be an argument. Then:

$$ a=r\cos\theta, \qquad b=r\sin\theta. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ z= r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta) }. $$

Exponential Form

Using Euler's formula:

$$ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta, $$

polar form becomes:

$$ \boxed{ z=re^{i\theta} }. $$

Rectangular and Polar Coordinates

The Argand plane connects the rectangular coordinates:

$$ (a,b) $$

with polar coordinates:

$$ (r,\theta). $$

The conversion formulas are:

$$ \boxed{ a=r\cos\theta, \qquad b=r\sin\theta } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ r=\sqrt{a^2+b^2} }. $$

Complex Conjugation

If:

$$ z=a+bi, $$

its conjugate is:

$$ \boxed{ \overline z=a-bi }. $$

On the Argand plane:

$$ (a,b) \longmapsto (a,-b). $$

Thus complex conjugation is reflection across the real axis.

Conjugate and Modulus

$$ z\overline z = (a+bi)(a-bi) = a^2+b^2. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ z\overline z=|z|^2 }. $$

Multiplication in Polar Form

Suppose:

$$ z_1=r_1e^{i\theta_1}, \qquad z_2=r_2e^{i\theta_2}. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ z_1z_2 = r_1r_2 e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)} }. $$

Complex multiplication therefore has a clear geometric interpretation:

  • multiply the moduli,
  • add the arguments.

Multiplication as Scaling and Rotation

Multiplication by:

$$ w=\rho e^{i\phi} $$

scales distances from the origin by $\rho$ and rotates directions through angle $\phi$.

$$ \boxed{ z\mapsto wz = \text{dilation by }\rho + \text{rotation by }\phi }. $$

Multiplication by i

Since:

$$ i=e^{i\pi/2}, $$

multiplying by $i$ rotates a point by:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\pi}{2} = 90^\circ } $$

counterclockwise about the origin.

Algebraically:

$$ i(a+bi) = -b+ai. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b)\mapsto(-b,a) }. $$

Multiplication by -1

$$ \boxed{ z\mapsto-z } $$

rotates every point through $180^\circ$ about the origin:

$$ (a,b)\mapsto(-a,-b). $$

Division

For nonzero complex numbers:

$$ \frac{z_1}{z_2} = \frac{r_1}{r_2} e^{i(\theta_1-\theta_2)}. $$

Therefore division:

  • divides the moduli,
  • subtracts the arguments.

Reciprocal

If:

$$ z=re^{i\theta}\ne0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac1z = \frac1r e^{-i\theta} }. $$

Its modulus is reciprocated and its argument is negated.

Powers

From De Moivre's theorem:

$$ \boxed{ \left( re^{i\theta} \right)^n = r^ne^{in\theta} }. $$

Raising to a power scales the modulus by a power and multiplies the argument by the exponent.

Roots of Unity

The solutions of:

$$ z^n=1 $$

are:

$$ \boxed{ z_k= e^{2\pi ik/n}, \qquad k=0,1,\ldots,n-1 }. $$

On the Argand plane, these points are equally spaced around the unit circle and form the vertices of a regular $n$-gon.

The Unit Circle

All complex numbers satisfying:

$$ \boxed{ |z|=1 } $$

lie on the unit circle centered at the origin.

They can be written:

$$ \boxed{ z=e^{i\theta} }. $$

Circle as a Complex Locus

The equation:

$$ \boxed{ |z-z_0|=r } $$

describes the circle centered at $z_0$ with radius $r$.

Disk

The inequality:

$$ \boxed{ |z-z_0|

describes the open disk centered at $z_0$.

Similarly:

$$ \boxed{ |z-z_0|\le r } $$

describes the closed disk.

Annulus

A ring-shaped region can be written:

$$ \boxed{ r_1<|z-z_0|

Vertical Lines

The condition:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Re}(z)=c } $$

describes the vertical line:

$$ x=c. $$

Horizontal Lines

The condition:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Im}(z)=c } $$

describes:

$$ y=c. $$

Half-Planes

For example:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Im}(z)>0 } $$

describes the open upper half-plane.

Likewise:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Re}(z)<0 } $$

describes the open left half-plane.

Perpendicular Bisector Locus

The equation:

$$ \boxed{ |z-z_1| = |z-z_2| } $$

describes all points equidistant from $z_1$ and $z_2$.

Geometrically, this is the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining the two complex points.

Argument Locus

A condition such as:

$$ \arg(z-z_0)=\theta $$

describes a ray beginning at $z_0$ and extending in direction $\theta$, subject to the chosen argument convention.

Translation

The transformation:

$$ \boxed{ z\mapsto z+c } $$

translates every point by the complex vector $c$.

General Affine Complex Transformation

For:

$$ \boxed{ w=az+b, \qquad a\ne0 }, $$

multiplication by $a$ produces rotation and scaling, followed by translation by $b$.

Such a map preserves angles and the general shape of figures up to similarity.

Conjugation as Reflection

$$ \boxed{ z\mapsto\overline z } $$

reflects the entire plane across the real axis.

Connection to the Cartesian Plane

As a geometric set, the Argand plane has the same underlying points as:

$$ \mathbb R^2. $$

The important additional interpretation is that:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b) \leftrightarrow a+bi }. $$

This correspondence equips the plane with complex-number multiplication in addition to ordinary coordinate operations.

Complex Plane versus Ordinary Coordinate Plane

In an ordinary Cartesian plane, $(x,y)$ is simply an ordered pair of real coordinates. In the Argand plane, the same pair is also interpreted as one number:

$$ \boxed{ z=x+iy }. $$

This is why geometric operations can encode complex arithmetic.

Not a Graph of y = f(x)

Plotting:

$$ z=a+bi $$

on the Argand plane does not mean that $b$ is a function value produced from input $a$.

Rather, $a$ and $b$ are the two real components of a single complex number.

Complex Functions

A complex function:

$$ w=f(z) $$

maps points of one complex plane to points of another.

Because both the input and output are two-dimensional over the real numbers, a complex function is not usually represented by an ordinary two-axis function graph in the same manner as $y=f(x)$.

Complex Analysis

In complex analysis, the Argand plane becomes the geometric setting for concepts such as:

  • complex limits and continuity,
  • analytic functions,
  • contours and contour integration,
  • zeros and poles,
  • disks and annuli,
  • conformal mappings,
  • residue theory.

Extended Complex Plane

In advanced complex analysis, one may adjoin a point at infinity:

$$ \boxed{ \widehat{\mathbb C} = \mathbb C\cup\{\infty\} }. $$

This is called the extended complex plane and is closely related to the Riemann sphere.

Common Misconception

The imaginary axis does not contain "non-real locations" outside mathematics. Both coordinates of a point on an Argand diagram are ordinary real numbers.

The complex interpretation comes from combining those coordinates as:

$$ \boxed{ a+bi }. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$z=a+bi$ "Z equals a plus b i."
$z\leftrightarrow(a,b)$ "The complex number z corresponds to the point a comma b."
$\operatorname{Re}(z)=a$ "The real part of z is a."
$\operatorname{Im}(z)=b$ "The imaginary part of z is b."
$|z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ "The modulus of z equals the square root of a squared plus b squared."
$\arg z=\theta$ "The argument of z is theta."
$z=re^{i\theta}$ "Z equals r e to the i theta."
$\overline z=a-bi$ "Z bar equals a minus b i," or "the complex conjugate of z equals a minus b i."

An important language distinction occurs with the phrase imaginary part. For:

$$ z=a+bi, $$

the imaginary part is:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Im}(z)=b }, $$

not $bi$ under the standard convention.

Defense Notation Advice: Keep the coordinate pair $(a,b)$ distinct from the complex expression $a+bi$. They represent the same point or number under the standard identification, but the first is ordered-pair notation and the second is complex-number notation.

  • Important Difference: The complex plane additionally carries multiplication of complex numbers, not merely ordinary coordinate-vector operations.
  • Complex-Function Caution: A point $a+bi$ is not an ordinary graph point where $b$ is necessarily a function of $a$.
  • Common Error: For $z=a+bi$: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Im}(z)=b } $$ rather than $bi$ under standard notation.
  • Common Error: The modulus $|z|$ is a distance and is therefore always nonnegative.
  • Common Error: The argument of a nonzero complex number is not unique unless a principal branch convention has been specified.
  • Terminological Contrast: Real part gives horizontal coordinate. Imaginary part gives vertical coordinate. Modulus gives distance from the origin. Argument gives direction. Argand plane combines all four ideas into a geometric model of complex arithmetic.
