Euler's Formula

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

Euler's Formula is the fundamental complex-number identity:

$$ \boxed{ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta }. $$

It connects the exponential function, the imaginary unit $i$, and the trigonometric functions sine and cosine in a single equation.

Here:

$$ \boxed{i^2=-1} $$

and $\theta$ is normally interpreted as an angle measured in radians.

The formula is named for the Swiss mathematician Leonhard Euler, whose work helped establish the modern relationship among exponential functions, trigonometry, and complex numbers.

Euler's Formula gives an exponential representation of points on the complex unit circle. Since:

$$ \cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1, $$

the complex number:

$$ \cos\theta+i\sin\theta $$

has modulus $1$. Thus:

$$ \boxed{ |e^{i\theta}|=1 }. $$

Euler's Formula therefore acts as a bridge between algebraic multiplication of complex numbers and geometric rotation in the complex plane.

Operational Nuance

Canonical Form

$$ \boxed{ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta }. $$

This is the standard form ordinarily meant by the term Euler's Formula.

Real and Imaginary Parts

Euler's Formula immediately identifies:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Re}(e^{i\theta})=\cos\theta } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Im}(e^{i\theta})=\sin\theta }. $$

Unit-Circle Interpretation

On the complex plane, the point:

$$ e^{i\theta} $$

has Cartesian coordinates:

$$ \boxed{ (\cos\theta,\sin\theta) }. $$

Thus increasing $\theta$ traces the unit circle counterclockwise.

Special Case: $\theta=0$

$$ e^{i0} = \cos0+i\sin0 = 1. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{e^0=1}. $$

Special Case: $\theta=\pi/2$

$$ e^{i\pi/2} = \cos\frac{\pi}{2} + i\sin\frac{\pi}{2} = \boxed{i}. $$

Special Case: $\theta=\pi$

$$ e^{i\pi} = \cos\pi+i\sin\pi = -1. $$

Rearranging gives the celebrated:

$$ \boxed{ e^{i\pi}+1=0 }. $$

This special case is known as Euler's Identity.

Euler's Formula vs. Euler's Identity

Euler's Formula is the general statement:

$$ \boxed{ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta }, $$

whereas Euler's Identity is the special case:

$$ \boxed{ e^{i\pi}+1=0 }. $$

Special Case: $\theta=2\pi$

$$ e^{i2\pi} = \cos2\pi+i\sin2\pi = \boxed{1}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ e^{2\pi i}=1 }. $$

Complex Exponential Periodicity

Since adding $2\pi$ to an angle does not change sine or cosine:

$$ \boxed{ e^{i(\theta+2\pi k)} = e^{i\theta}, \qquad k\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$

More generally:

$$ \boxed{ e^{z+2\pi i k}=e^z, \qquad k\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Negative Angles

Euler's Formula gives:

$$ e^{-i\theta} = \cos(-\theta) + i\sin(-\theta). $$

Using the evenness of cosine and oddness of sine:

$$ \boxed{ e^{-i\theta} = \cos\theta-i\sin\theta }. $$

Complex Conjugate

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \overline{e^{i\theta}} = e^{-i\theta} }. $$

Recovering Cosine

Add the positive- and negative-angle formulas:

$$ e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta} = 2\cos\theta. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ \cos\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta}}{2} }. $$

Recovering Sine

Subtract:

$$ e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta} = 2i\sin\theta. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \sin\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}}{2i} }. $$

Why Radians Matter

The clean form of Euler's Formula uses radian angle measure. The standard calculus identities:

$$ \frac{d}{d\theta}\sin\theta=\cos\theta, \qquad \frac{d}{d\theta}\cos\theta=-\sin\theta $$

hold without extra conversion factors only when $\theta$ is measured in radians.

Power-Series Derivation

The complex exponential is defined by:

$$ e^z = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{z^n}{n!}. $$

Setting $z=i\theta$:

$$ e^{i\theta} = 1+i\theta +\frac{(i\theta)^2}{2!} +\frac{(i\theta)^3}{3!} +\cdots. $$

Using:

$$ i^2=-1,\qquad i^3=-i,\qquad i^4=1, $$

separate real and imaginary terms:

$$ e^{i\theta} = \left( 1-\frac{\theta^2}{2!} +\frac{\theta^4}{4!} -\cdots \right) + i\left( \theta-\frac{\theta^3}{3!} +\frac{\theta^5}{5!} -\cdots \right). $$

Recognizing the Taylor series for cosine and sine gives:

$$ \boxed{ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta }. $$

Differential-Equation Interpretation

Define:

$$ f(\theta)=e^{i\theta}. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ f'(\theta)=if(\theta) }. $$

Multiplication by $i$ rotates a complex number by $90^\circ$, so the derivative is always perpendicular to the radial direction. This provides another route to the circular behavior of $e^{i\theta}$.

Polar Form of a Complex Number

A nonzero complex number:

$$ z=x+iy $$

may be written:

$$ \boxed{ z=re^{i\theta} } $$

where:

$$ r=|z| $$

and $\theta$ is an argument of $z$.

Connection to Trigonometric Form

Euler's Formula gives:

$$ \boxed{ re^{i\theta} = r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta) }. $$

Thus exponential form and trigonometric form are two notations for the same polar representation.

Cartesian Coordinates from Polar Form

If:

$$ z=re^{i\theta}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ x=r\cos\theta, \qquad y=r\sin\theta }. $$

Multiplication in Exponential Form

Let:

$$ z_1=r_1e^{i\theta_1}, \qquad z_2=r_2e^{i\theta_2}. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ z_1z_2 = r_1r_2e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)} }. $$

Thus complex multiplication multiplies magnitudes and adds angles.

Division in Exponential Form

For $z_2\neq0$:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{z_1}{z_2} = \frac{r_1}{r_2} e^{i(\theta_1-\theta_2)} }. $$

Division divides magnitudes and subtracts arguments.

Rotation

Multiplication by:

$$ e^{i\phi} $$

preserves magnitude and adds $\phi$ to the argument:

$$ \boxed{ re^{i\theta}e^{i\phi} = re^{i(\theta+\phi)} }. $$

Therefore multiplication by $e^{i\phi}$ represents rotation through angle $\phi$ about the origin.

De Moivre's Theorem

Raising Euler's Formula to an integer power gives:

$$ (e^{i\theta})^n=e^{in\theta}. $$

Rewriting each side trigonometrically produces:

$$ \boxed{ (\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^n = \cos(n\theta)+i\sin(n\theta) }. $$

Thus De Moivre's theorem follows naturally from exponential laws and Euler's Formula.

Roots of Unity

To solve:

$$ z^n=1, $$

write:

$$ 1=e^{2\pi i k}. $$

The distinct $n$th roots are:

$$ \boxed{ z_k = e^{2\pi i k/n}, \qquad k=0,1,\ldots,n-1 }. $$

Geometry of Roots of Unity

The $n$th roots of unity lie equally spaced around the unit circle, forming the vertices of a regular $n$-gon.

General Complex Roots

If:

$$ z=re^{i\theta}, $$

then its $n$th roots have the form:

$$ \boxed{ w_k = r^{1/n} e^{i(\theta+2\pi k)/n}, \qquad k=0,\ldots,n-1 }. $$

Complex Logarithm Connection

Because:

$$ e^{i(\theta+2\pi k)} = e^{i\theta}, $$

the complex logarithm is naturally multivalued:

$$ \boxed{ \log z = \ln|z| + i(\arg z+2\pi k), \qquad k\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Principal Argument and Principal Logarithm

To make a single-valued branch, one restricts the argument to a chosen interval, commonly:

$$ -\pi<\operatorname{Arg}z\leq\pi. $$

Then the principal logarithm is commonly written:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Log}z = \ln|z|+i\operatorname{Arg}z }. $$

Complex Powers

Expressions such as:

$$ z^w $$

may be defined using:

$$ z^w=e^{w\log z}. $$

Because $\log z$ may be multivalued, complex powers can also be multivalued unless a branch is specified.

Real Exponentials vs. Complex Exponentials

For real $x$:

$$ e^x>0. $$

But for purely imaginary exponents:

$$ \boxed{ |e^{i\theta}|=1 }. $$

Real exponential input controls magnitude, while imaginary exponential input controls angle.

General Complex Exponential

If:

$$ z=x+iy, $$

then:

$$ e^z = e^{x+iy} = e^xe^{iy}. $$

Euler's Formula gives:

$$ \boxed{ e^{x+iy} = e^x(\cos y+i\sin y) }. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ |e^{x+iy}|=e^x }. $$

Frequency and Oscillation

The function:

$$ e^{i\omega t} $$

represents complex oscillation:

$$ \boxed{ e^{i\omega t} = \cos(\omega t) + i\sin(\omega t) }. $$

This representation is central in differential equations, Fourier analysis, waves, signal processing, and electrical engineering.

Phase

A phase shift can be represented compactly:

$$ e^{i(\omega t+\phi)} = \cos(\omega t+\phi) + i\sin(\omega t+\phi). $$

Real Signals from Complex Exponentials

A cosine can be recovered as:

$$ \boxed{ A\cos(\omega t+\phi) = \operatorname{Re} \left( Ae^{i(\omega t+\phi)} \right) }. $$

The complex exponential can therefore simplify calculations even when the final physical quantity is real.

Not the Same as the Exponential Addition Law Alone

Euler's Formula is not merely the exponent rule:

$$ e^{a+b}=e^ae^b. $$

Rather, it identifies the value of the exponential at a purely imaginary argument in terms of trigonometric functions.

Not a Formula for Every Quantity Named Euler

Mathematics contains many results associated with Euler, including Euler's identity, Euler's theorem, Euler's polyhedron formula, Euler's method, and the Euler–Lagrange equation. The unqualified term Euler's Formula in complex-number and trigonometric contexts ordinarily means:

$$ \boxed{ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta }. $$

Common Misconception: Euler's Formula does not say that $e^{i\theta}$ is a real exponential growth quantity. A purely imaginary exponent produces motion around the complex unit circle. Its magnitude remains $1$ while its argument changes with $\theta$.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$ "E to the i theta equals cosine theta plus i sine theta."
$e^{-i\theta}=\cos\theta-i\sin\theta$ "E to the negative i theta equals cosine theta minus i sine theta."
$e^{i\pi}+1=0$ "E to the i pi plus one equals zero."
$z=re^{i\theta}$ "Z equals r times e to the i theta."
$|e^{i\theta}|=1$ "The modulus of e to the i theta is one."
$\cos\theta=\frac{e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta}}2$ "Cosine theta equals one half of e to the i theta plus e to the negative i theta."
$\sin\theta=\frac{e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}}{2i}$ "Sine theta equals e to the i theta minus e to the negative i theta, divided by two i."

The symbol:

$$ \boxed{i} $$

denotes the imaginary unit in standard mathematical notation:

$$ \boxed{i^2=-1}. $$

In some engineering disciplines, $j$ is used instead because $i$ is commonly reserved for electric current. The corresponding formula may therefore appear as:

$$ \boxed{ e^{j\theta} = \cos\theta+j\sin\theta }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish carefully among:

$$ \boxed{ e^{i\theta},\qquad e^{i\pi}+1=0,\qquad re^{i\theta} }. $$

The first is Euler's Formula, the second is Euler's Identity, and the third is the exponential or polar form of a general nonzero complex number. They are closely related but are not interchangeable names for the same statement.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Canonical Formula: $$ \boxed{ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta }. $$
  • Imaginary Unit: $$ \boxed{i^2=-1}. $$
  • Angle Convention: $\theta$ is normally measured in radians.
  • Real Part: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Re}(e^{i\theta})=\cos\theta }. $$
  • Imaginary Part: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Im}(e^{i\theta})=\sin\theta }. $$
  • Unit Modulus: $$ \boxed{|e^{i\theta}|=1}. $$
  • Unit Circle: $e^{i\theta}$ represents the point $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ on the complex unit circle.
  • Zero Angle: $$ \boxed{e^{i0}=1}. $$
  • Quarter Turn: $$ \boxed{e^{i\pi/2}=i}. $$
  • Half Turn: $$ \boxed{e^{i\pi}=-1}. $$
  • Full Turn: $$ \boxed{e^{i2\pi}=1}. $$
  • Euler's Identity: $$ \boxed{e^{i\pi}+1=0}. $$
  • Formula vs. Identity: Euler's Identity is the $\theta=\pi$ special case of Euler's Formula.
  • Negative Angle: $$ \boxed{ e^{-i\theta} = \cos\theta-i\sin\theta }. $$
  • Conjugate: $$ \boxed{ \overline{e^{i\theta}} = e^{-i\theta} }. $$
  • Reciprocal: $$ \boxed{ \frac1{e^{i\theta}} = e^{-i\theta} }. $$
  • Periodicity: $$ \boxed{ e^{i(\theta+2\pi k)} = e^{i\theta}, \quad k\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$
  • Complex Exponential Period: $2\pi i$ is a period of the complex exponential.
  • Cosine Exponential Form: $$ \boxed{ \cos\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta}}2 }. $$
  • Sine Exponential Form: $$ \boxed{ \sin\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}}{2i} }. $$
  • Power-Series Foundation: Euler's Formula follows naturally from the power series for $e^z$, $\cos z$, and $\sin z$.
  • Exponential Series: $$ \boxed{ e^z = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{z^n}{n!} }. $$
  • Complex Exponential: Euler's Formula extends ordinary exponential ideas to imaginary arguments.
  • General Complex Input: $$ \boxed{ e^{x+iy} = e^x(\cos y+i\sin y) }. $$
  • Magnitude of General Exponential: $$ \boxed{ |e^{x+iy}|=e^x }. $$
  • Argument: The imaginary part of the exponent determines angular position modulo $2\pi$.
  • Polar Form: $$ \boxed{z=re^{i\theta}}. $$
  • Trigonometric Form: $$ \boxed{ z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta) }. $$
  • Coordinate Conversion: $$ x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta. $$
  • Modulus: $$ r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}. $$
  • Argument: $\theta$ describes the direction of a nonzero complex number from the positive real axis.
  • Multiple Arguments: If $\theta$ is an argument of $z$, then so is: $$ \theta+2\pi k,\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}. $$
  • Complex Multiplication: $$ \boxed{ r_1e^{i\theta_1}r_2e^{i\theta_2} = r_1r_2e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)} }. $$
  • Complex Division: $$ \boxed{ \frac{r_1e^{i\theta_1}} {r_2e^{i\theta_2}} = \frac{r_1}{r_2} e^{i(\theta_1-\theta_2)} }. $$
  • Rotation: Multiplication by $e^{i\phi}$ rotates a complex number by angle $\phi$.
  • Rotation Magnitude: Multiplication by $e^{i\phi}$ preserves modulus.
  • Scaling and Rotation: Multiplication by $re^{i\phi}$ scales by $r$ and rotates by $\phi$.
  • De Moivre Connection: $$ \boxed{ (\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^n = \cos(n\theta)+i\sin(n\theta) }. $$
  • Powers: $$ \boxed{ (re^{i\theta})^n = r^ne^{in\theta} } $$ for integer $n$.
  • Roots of Unity: $$ \boxed{ z_k=e^{2\pi ik/n} } $$ for $k=0,\ldots,n-1$.
  • Root Geometry: The roots of unity are equally spaced on the unit circle.
  • Regular Polygons: The $n$th roots of unity form the vertices of a regular $n$-gon.
  • General $n$th Roots: $$ \boxed{ w_k = r^{1/n} e^{i(\theta+2\pi k)/n} }. $$
  • Multiple Roots: The $2\pi k$ term is essential for obtaining all distinct complex roots.
  • Complex Logarithm: Euler's Formula explains why the complex logarithm has multiple values.
  • Complex Log Values: $$ \boxed{ \log z = \ln|z| + i(\arg z+2\pi k) }. $$
  • Branch Choice: A principal logarithm requires restricting the argument to a chosen branch.
  • Complex Powers: $$ z^w=e^{w\log z} $$ can depend on logarithm branch choices.
  • Differential Equations: Complex exponentials often simplify solutions of linear differential equations with oscillatory behavior.
  • Oscillation: $$ \boxed{ e^{i\omega t} = \cos(\omega t)+i\sin(\omega t) }. $$
  • Angular Frequency: $\omega$ controls the rate of rotation or oscillation.
  • Phase: $\phi$ in $e^{i(\omega t+\phi)}$ represents an angular phase shift.
  • Fourier Analysis: Complex exponentials are fundamental building blocks of Fourier series and Fourier transforms.
  • Signal Processing: Sinusoidal signals are conveniently represented using complex exponentials.
  • Electrical Engineering: Phasor methods use complex exponentials to represent sinusoidal voltages and currents.
  • Engineering Notation: $j$ may replace $i$ as the imaginary unit: $$ e^{j\theta}=\cos\theta+j\sin\theta. $$
  • Wave Mechanics: Expressions of the form $e^{i(kx-\omega t)}$ compactly represent oscillatory wave behavior.
  • Quantum Mechanics: Complex phase factors of the form $e^{i\theta}$ occur extensively.
  • Linear Algebra: Complex eigenvalues of real matrices often correspond to combinations of exponential growth or decay with rotation.
  • Rotation Matrices: Complex multiplication by $e^{i\theta}$ parallels the real matrix: $$ \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}. $$
  • Trigonometric Identities: Many addition and multiple-angle identities can be derived efficiently using complex exponentials.
  • Angle Addition: The identity: $$ e^{i(\alpha+\beta)} = e^{i\alpha}e^{i\beta} $$ yields the sine and cosine addition formulas.
  • Cosine Addition Formula: Comparing real parts gives: $$ \cos(\alpha+\beta) = \cos\alpha\cos\beta - \sin\alpha\sin\beta. $$
  • Sine Addition Formula: Comparing imaginary parts gives: $$ \sin(\alpha+\beta) = \sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta. $$
  • Euler's Formula vs. Euler's Identity: The formula is general in $\theta$; the identity is its $\theta=\pi$ specialization.
  • Euler's Formula vs. De Moivre's Theorem: Euler's Formula represents a complex exponential; De Moivre's theorem describes integer powers of trigonometric-form complex numbers.
  • Euler's Formula vs. Polar Form: Euler's Formula supplies the identity that makes $re^{i\theta}$ equivalent to $r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)$.
  • Magnitude Caution: $e^{i\theta}$ does not grow in magnitude as $\theta$ increases.
  • Periodicity Caution: Unlike the real exponential, the complex exponential is not one-to-one.
  • Inverse Caution: The periodicity of $e^z$ is why the complex logarithm requires branch choices.
  • Degree Caution: If degree measure is used instead of radians, the angle must first be converted when applying the standard analytical form of Euler's Formula.
  • Core Interpretation: Euler's Formula: $$ \boxed{ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta } $$ identifies complex exponential rotation with trigonometric motion on the unit circle. It transforms complex multiplication into multiplication of magnitudes and addition of angles, underlies exponential forms of complex numbers, leads naturally to De Moivre's theorem and roots of unity, and provides a central algebraic connection among exponentials, trigonometry, geometry, and complex analysis.
