Common Logarithm

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

The common logarithm of a positive number is its logarithm to base 10. It answers the question: To what exponent must 10 be raised to produce this number?

$$ \boxed{ \log_{10}(x)=y \iff 10^y=x } $$

Because the base is fixed at $10$, the common logarithm is frequently written simply as:

$$ \boxed{ \log x=\log_{10}x } $$

when the mathematical context explicitly or conventionally establishes that $\log$ denotes base $10$.

The word logarithm was coined in the early seventeenth century by John Napier from Greek roots associated with ratio or reckoning and number. Logarithms were developed as computational tools that transformed difficult multiplication and division into easier addition and subtraction.

The familiar base-$10$ system became closely associated with Henry Briggs, whose work helped establish decimal logarithms as a practical computational standard. Common logarithms were therefore historically also called Briggsian logarithms.

$$ \boxed{ \text{common logarithm} = \text{base-10 logarithm} } $$

The adjective common reflects the historical widespread use of base-$10$ logarithms in numerical calculation, scientific tables, engineering, navigation, astronomy, and other quantitative work.

Operational Nuance

Logarithms Undo Exponentiation

A logarithm is an inverse operation to exponentiation. For the common logarithm, the relevant exponential base is $10$.

$$ \boxed{ 10^y=x \iff \log_{10}(x)=y } $$

For example:

$$ 10^3=1000 $$

therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \log_{10}(1000)=3 }. $$

Powers of Ten

Common logarithms are especially transparent when their arguments are exact powers of $10$.

$$ \boxed{ \log_{10}(10^n)=n } $$

Thus:

$$ \log_{10}(1)=0, \qquad \log_{10}(10)=1, \qquad \log_{10}(100)=2, \qquad \log_{10}(1000)=3. $$

Negative Exponents

A positive number smaller than $1$ may have a negative common logarithm.

$$ 10^{-2}=0.01 $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ \log_{10}(0.01)=-2 }. $$

A negative logarithm does not mean that the argument itself is negative.

Real Domain

For real-valued common logarithms, the argument must be positive:

$$ \boxed{ x>0 }. $$

Therefore:

$$ \log_{10}(0) \quad\text{is undefined over }\mathbb R, $$

and:

$$ \log_{10}(-5) \quad\text{is not a real number}. $$

Range

Although the real common logarithm accepts only positive inputs, its outputs may be any real number.

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{dom}(\log_{10})=(0,\infty), \qquad \operatorname{range}(\log_{10})=\mathbb R }. $$

Inverse Function Relationship

The functions:

$$ f(x)=10^x \qquad\text{and}\qquad f^{-1}(x)=\log_{10}x $$

are inverses.

$$ \boxed{ \log_{10}(10^x)=x } $$

and, for $x>0$:

$$ \boxed{ 10^{\log_{10}x}=x }. $$

Product Rule

For positive real numbers $x$ and $y$:

$$ \boxed{ \log_{10}(xy) = \log_{10}x+\log_{10}y }. $$

This property explains much of the historical computational importance of logarithms: multiplication could be converted into addition.

Quotient Rule

$$ \boxed{ \log_{10}\left(\frac{x}{y}\right) = \log_{10}x-\log_{10}y } $$

for $x>0$ and $y>0$.

Division is therefore transformed into subtraction.

Power Rule

$$ \boxed{ \log_{10}(x^r) = r\log_{10}x } $$

whenever the expression remains within the relevant logarithmic domain.

Exponents can therefore be moved outside a logarithm as multiplicative factors.

Root Interpretation

Since:

$$ \sqrt[n]{x}=x^{1/n}, $$

the power rule gives:

$$ \boxed{ \log_{10}\sqrt[n]{x} = \frac{1}{n}\log_{10}x }. $$

This historically allowed extraction of roots to be converted into division.

The Logarithm of 1

Since:

$$ 10^0=1, $$

it follows that:

$$ \boxed{ \log_{10}(1)=0 }. $$

The Logarithm of the Base

$$ \boxed{ \log_{10}(10)=1 } $$

because $10^1=10$.

Common Logarithm of a General Number

Most positive numbers are not exact integer powers of $10$.

For example:

$$ \boxed{ \log_{10}(2)\approx0.30103 }. $$

This means:

$$ 10^{0.30103}\approx2. $$

Interpreting the Integer Part

For a positive number $x$, the common logarithm indicates its position among powers of ten.

If:

$$ 10^n\le x<10^{n+1}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ n\le\log_{10}x

Thus the logarithm naturally encodes decimal order of magnitude.

Example: Locating a Number by Magnitude

Since:

$$ 10^3<5000<10^4, $$

we know immediately that:

$$ \boxed{ 3<\log_{10}(5000)<4 }. $$

In fact:

$$ \log_{10}(5000)\approx3.69897. $$

Connection with Scientific Notation

Suppose:

$$ x=a\times10^n, \qquad 1\le a<10. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ \log_{10}x = n+\log_{10}a }. $$

This makes the relationship between common logarithms and scientific notation especially direct.

Example with Scientific Notation

Consider:

$$ 3200=3.2\times10^3. $$

Therefore:

$$ \log_{10}(3200) = \log_{10}(3.2)+3 \approx3.50515. $$

Order of Magnitude

Common logarithms are useful for comparing quantities that span many powers of ten. A difference of $1$ between two base-$10$ logarithms corresponds to a multiplicative factor of $10$ between the original quantities.

$$ \boxed{ \log_{10}B-\log_{10}A=1 \iff \frac BA=10 }. $$

Similarly, a logarithmic difference of $2$ corresponds to a factor of $100$.

Logarithmic Scales

Base-$10$ logarithms are frequently used when numerical quantities vary across many orders of magnitude. A logarithmic scale compresses multiplicative changes into additive differences.

$$ \boxed{ \text{multiplication by }10 \longleftrightarrow \text{addition of }1\text{ on a base-10 logarithmic scale} }. $$

pH

The familiar pH scale uses a negative base-$10$ logarithm of hydrogen-ion activity, commonly approximated in elementary treatments using concentration.

$$ \boxed{ \mathrm{pH} \approx -\log_{10}[H^+] } $$

A one-unit change on the pH scale therefore corresponds, under the simplified concentration interpretation, to a factor of $10$ in hydrogen-ion concentration.

Decibels

Decibel measurements also employ common logarithms for ratios. For power ratios:

$$ \boxed{ L=10\log_{10}\left(\frac{P_2}{P_1}\right) \text{ dB} }. $$

The exact coefficient and ratio used depend on the physical quantity being measured.

Historical Logarithm Tables

Before electronic calculators and computers, tables of common logarithms allowed users to perform complicated numerical calculations by looking up logarithms, applying addition or subtraction, and then using an antilogarithm table to recover the result.

For multiplication:

$$ \log_{10}(xy) = \log_{10}x+\log_{10}y. $$

The multiplication itself was replaced by addition of tabulated logarithms.

Characteristic and Mantissa

In historical common-logarithm tables, a logarithm was often described as having a characteristic and a mantissa.

For:

$$ \log_{10}(3200)\approx3.50515, $$

the integer portion $3$ was called the characteristic, while the tabulated fractional portion was called the mantissa.

This terminology remains historically important but is substantially less central in modern calculator-based instruction.

Antilogarithm

The older term antilogarithm refers to reversing a logarithmic calculation.

If:

$$ y=\log_{10}x, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ x=10^y }. $$

Thus taking an antilogarithm means exponentiating with base $10$.

The LOG Key on a Calculator

On many scientific calculators, the key labeled LOG computes the common logarithm:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{LOG}(x)=\log_{10}x }. $$

The neighboring LN key normally computes the natural logarithm, whose base is $e$.

Common Logarithm versus Natural Logarithm

The common logarithm uses base $10$:

$$ \log_{10}x. $$

The natural logarithm uses base $e$:

$$ \ln x=\log_e x. $$

They are different functions, although one can be converted into the other.

Change of Base

The change-of-base formula allows a common logarithm to be computed using another logarithmic base:

$$ \boxed{ \log_{10}x = \frac{\ln x}{\ln10} }. $$

More generally:

$$ \boxed{ \log_bx = \frac{\log_kx}{\log_kb} }. $$

The Meaning of Bare “log” Is Context-Dependent

One important terminological complication is that the notation $\log x$ does not universally mean the same base in every mathematical field.

In many elementary algebra, calculator, scientific, and engineering contexts:

$$ \log x=\log_{10}x. $$

In substantial portions of higher mathematics, however, authors use:

$$ \log x=\ln x=\log_e x. $$

The intended base must therefore be determined from context or explicitly stated.

The Base Cannot Be 1

A logarithmic base $b$ must satisfy:

$$ \boxed{ b>0, \qquad b\ne1 }. $$

The common logarithm automatically satisfies these requirements because its base is $10$.

Graph of the Common Logarithm

The graph:

$$ y=\log_{10}x $$

passes through:

$$ (1,0),\qquad(10,1),\qquad(100,2),\qquad(0.1,-1). $$

It is increasing for every $x>0$ and has the vertical line $x=0$ as a vertical asymptote.

Monotonicity

Because the base $10$ is greater than $1$, the common logarithm is strictly increasing.

Solving Exponential Equations

Common logarithms can be applied to both sides of an exponential equation when an unknown appears in an exponent.

Suppose:

$$ 3^x=20. $$

Taking common logarithms gives:

$$ x\log_{10}3=\log_{10}20. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ x= \frac{\log_{10}20}{\log_{10}3} }. $$

The natural logarithm would produce the same final value.

Common Misconception: “log” Always Means Base 10

The notation $\log x$ frequently means base $10$ in elementary and calculator contexts, but this convention is not universal. Many mathematicians use $\log x$ for the natural logarithm.

Common Misconception: A Negative Logarithm Requires a Negative Input

A positive number between $0$ and $1$ has a negative common logarithm.

Common Misconception: The Logarithm of Zero Is Zero

This is false.

$$ \boxed{ \log_{10}(0) \text{ is undefined over }\mathbb R }. $$

There is no finite real exponent $y$ for which $10^y=0$.

Common Misconception: Logarithms Distribute over Addition

The product rule does not imply an addition rule.

$$ \boxed{ \log_{10}(x+y) \ne \log_{10}x+\log_{10}y } $$

in general.

Common Misconception: The Base Is Written as an Exponent

In:

$$ \log_{10}x, $$

the $10$ is a subscript identifying the base, not an exponent.

Common Misconception: Common Means Mathematically Fundamental

The word common refers principally to historical and practical convention. It does not mean that base $10$ is intrinsically more fundamental to mathematical analysis than every other valid logarithmic base.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\log_{10}x$ "The logarithm base ten of x" or "the common logarithm of x."
$\log x$ "Log x," with the base determined from context.
$\log_{10}(1000)=3$ "The logarithm base ten of one thousand is three."
$10^3=1000$ "Ten cubed equals one thousand" or "ten to the third power equals one thousand."
$\log_{10}(xy)=\log_{10}x+\log_{10}y$ "The log base ten of a product equals the sum of the logs."
$\log_{10}(x/y)$ "The log base ten of x divided by y."
$\log_{10}(x^r)=r\log_{10}x$ "The log base ten of x to the r equals r times the log base ten of x."
$10^{\log_{10}x}=x$ "Ten raised to the log base ten of x equals x."
$\log_{10}(10^x)=x$ "The log base ten of ten to the x equals x."
$\ln x$ "The natural log of x" or "ell-en of x."

In ordinary mathematical speech, common log is a standard shortened form of common logarithm. Saying merely log may be acceptable when the mathematical environment makes the base unambiguous.

The expression $\log_{10}x$ should not normally be read as "log ten x." The subscript identifies the logarithmic base, so the clearer professional reading is "log base ten of x."

Defense Notation Advice: When the logarithmic base could matter, write it explicitly. Use $\log_{10}x$ for the common logarithm and $\ln x$ for the natural logarithm unless the surrounding convention has been clearly established. Never assume that an unqualified $\log$ means base $10$ in every mathematical field. When applying logarithm laws, preserve the requirement that logarithmic arguments be positive in real-number calculations.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: The common logarithm is the logarithm to base $10$.
  • Defining Equivalence: $$ \boxed{ \log_{10}x=y \iff 10^y=x }. $$
  • Standard Explicit Notation: $\log_{10}x$.
  • Abbreviated Notation: $\log x$ is frequently used for the common logarithm when base $10$ has been established by context.
  • Historical Name: Common logarithms have also been called Briggsian logarithms because of Henry Briggs's major role in the development and dissemination of base-$10$ logarithmic tables.
  • Real Domain: $$ \boxed{x>0}. $$
  • Real Range: Every real number.
  • Inverse Function: The common logarithm is the inverse of $10^x$.
  • Basic Identity: $$ \boxed{ \log_{10}(10^x)=x }. $$
  • Reverse Identity: $$ \boxed{ 10^{\log_{10}x}=x } $$ for $x>0$.
  • Logarithm of One: $$ \boxed{ \log_{10}(1)=0 }. $$
  • Logarithm of the Base: $$ \boxed{ \log_{10}(10)=1 }. $$
  • Product Rule: $$ \boxed{ \log_{10}(xy) = \log_{10}x+\log_{10}y }. $$
  • Quotient Rule: $$ \boxed{ \log_{10}\left(\frac{x}{y}\right) = \log_{10}x-\log_{10}y }. $$
  • Power Rule: $$ \boxed{ \log_{10}(x^r) = r\log_{10}x }. $$
  • Scientific Notation Connection: If $x=a\times10^n$, then $$ \boxed{ \log_{10}x=n+\log_{10}a }. $$
  • Order-of-Magnitude Connection: Integer changes in a common logarithm correspond to powers-of-ten changes in the original quantity.
  • Logarithmic Scale: Multiplication by $10$ increases a base-$10$ logarithm by $1$.
  • Calculator Convention: The LOG key on many scientific calculators denotes $\log_{10}$.
  • Natural Logarithm Contrast: $$ \ln x=\log_e x $$ uses base $e$, not base $10$.
  • Change of Base: $$ \boxed{ \log_{10}x = \frac{\ln x}{\ln10} }. $$
  • Historical Computation: Common-logarithm tables transformed multiplication into addition, division into subtraction, powers into multiplication, and roots into division.
  • Characteristic: Historical terminology for the integer component associated with a tabulated common logarithm.
  • Mantissa: Historical logarithm-table terminology for the fractional tabulated portion of a common logarithm.
  • Antilogarithm: The inverse operation to taking a common logarithm is exponentiation with base $10$.
  • Science: Base-$10$ logarithms are useful for expressing and comparing quantities spanning many orders of magnitude.
  • Chemistry: pH uses a negative base-$10$ logarithmic relationship.
  • Acoustics and Engineering: Decibel formulas commonly use base-$10$ logarithms of physical ratios.
  • Algebra: Common logarithms can solve exponential equations by moving unknown exponents into multiplicative positions.
  • Higher Mathematics Convention: Some authors use unqualified $\log$ to mean the natural logarithm rather than the common logarithm.
  • Common Error: Do not assume that $\log$ universally means $\log_{10}$.
  • Common Error: Do not write $\log_{10}(0)=0$; the real logarithm of zero is undefined.
  • Common Error: Do not conclude that a negative logarithmic value requires a negative argument. Positive numbers between $0$ and $1$ have negative common logarithms.
  • Common Error: Do not apply logarithm laws to addition: $$ \log_{10}(x+y) \ne \log_{10}x+\log_{10}y $$ in general.
  • Terminological Contrast: A logarithm answers the exponent question for a specified base. The common logarithm specifically uses base $10$. The natural logarithm uses base $e$. A binary logarithm uses base $2$. An antilogarithm reverses logarithmic evaluation by exponentiation. The notation log alone is context-dependent and should not be assumed to identify a universal base.
