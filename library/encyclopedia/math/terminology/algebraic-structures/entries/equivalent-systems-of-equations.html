Equivalent Systems of Equations

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

Equivalent systems of equations are systems that have exactly the same solution set.

If two systems are denoted by $S_1$ and $S_2$, then they are equivalent when:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Sol}(S_1) = \operatorname{Sol}(S_2) }. $$

The equations themselves do not need to look alike. What matters is that every ordered tuple solving one system also solves the other, and vice versa.

For example:

$$ \begin{cases} x+y=5,\\ x-y=1 \end{cases} $$

is equivalent to:

$$ \begin{cases} x+y=5,\\ 2x=6 \end{cases} $$

because both systems have the unique solution:

$$ \boxed{(x,y)=(3,2)}. $$

The word equivalent emphasizes preservation of the mathematical solution set rather than preservation of the visual form of the equations. This idea is fundamental to substitution, elimination, Gaussian elimination, and row reduction.

Operational Nuance

Definition by Solution Set

Two systems are equivalent precisely when:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x}\text{ solves }S_1 \iff \mathbf{x}\text{ solves }S_2 }. $$

This biconditional is essential. One system must have neither more nor fewer solutions than the other.

Equivalent Systems May Look Different

Consider:

$$ S_1: \begin{cases} x+y=7,\\ 2x-y=2 \end{cases} $$

Adding the first equation to the second produces:

$$ 3x=9. $$

Therefore:

$$ S_2: \begin{cases} x+y=7,\\ 3x=9 \end{cases} $$

is equivalent to $S_1$.

Elementary Equation Operations

In linear systems, several transformations preserve equivalence.

These include:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} &1.\ \text{Interchange two equations.}\\ &2.\ \text{Multiply an equation by a nonzero constant.}\\ &3.\ \text{Replace one equation by itself plus a multiple of another.} \end{aligned} } $$

Interchanging Equations

The order in which equations are listed does not affect the simultaneous solution set.

$$ \begin{cases} x+y=4,\\ 2x-y=5 \end{cases} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \begin{cases} 2x-y=5,\\ x+y=4 \end{cases} $$

Multiplying One Equation by a Nonzero Constant

If:

$$ x+y=4, $$

then multiplying by $3$ gives the equivalent equation:

$$ \boxed{ 3x+3y=12 }. $$

The multiplier must be nonzero if the transformation is to be reversible.

Why Multiplication by Zero Is Not Equivalent

Multiplying:

$$ x+y=4 $$

by zero produces:

$$ 0=0. $$

The original restriction has disappeared. Every pair $(x,y)$ satisfies $0=0$, so the transformation changes the solution set.

Replacing an Equation by a Combination

Suppose a system contains equations $E_1$ and $E_2$. Replacing:

$$ E_2 $$

by:

$$ \boxed{ E_2+cE_1 } $$

preserves the solution set because the original equation can be recovered by subtracting $cE_1$.

Elimination

The elimination method deliberately constructs an equivalent system in which one variable has been eliminated from one equation.

For:

$$ \begin{cases} x+y=8,\\ x-y=2 \end{cases}, $$

adding the equations gives:

$$ 2x=10. $$

An equivalent system is:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{cases} x+y=8,\\ 2x=10 \end{cases} }. $$

Substitution

Substitution also creates equivalent systems when an equation is solved reversibly for one variable and the resulting equal expression is substituted into another equation.

For:

$$ \begin{cases} y=2x+1,\\ x+y=7 \end{cases}, $$

substitution gives:

$$ x+(2x+1)=7. $$

Thus the system can be reduced to:

$$ \begin{cases} y=2x+1,\\ 3x+1=7 \end{cases}. $$

Equivalent Systems and Augmented Matrices

A linear system can be represented by an augmented matrix.

For:

$$ \begin{cases} x+2y=5,\\ 3x-y=4 \end{cases}, $$

the augmented matrix is:

$$ \left( \begin{array}{cc|c} 1 & 2 & 5\\ 3 & -1 & 4 \end{array} \right). $$

Elementary row operations correspond directly to equivalence-preserving operations on the equations.

Elementary Row Operations

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} &R_i\leftrightarrow R_j,\\ &R_i\to cR_i,\qquad c\neq0,\\ &R_i\to R_i+cR_j. \end{aligned} } $$

These operations transform an augmented matrix into another augmented matrix representing an equivalent linear system.

Row Equivalence

Matrices connected by elementary row operations are called row equivalent.

For augmented matrices:

$$ \boxed{ [A\mid\mathbf{b}] \sim [C\mid\mathbf{d}] } $$

indicates that they represent equivalent systems when one is obtained from the other by elementary row operations.

Gaussian Elimination

Gaussian elimination repeatedly replaces a linear system with equivalent systems until the augmented matrix reaches row-echelon form.

$$ \boxed{ \text{original system} \Longleftrightarrow \text{echelon system} }. $$

Gauss–Jordan Elimination

Gauss–Jordan elimination continues until reduced row-echelon form is reached. Every row operation preserves the solution set.

Example of Row Reduction

Begin with:

$$ \left( \begin{array}{cc|c} 1 & 1 & 5\\ 2 & -1 & 4 \end{array} \right). $$

Apply:

$$ R_2\to R_2-2R_1. $$

This gives:

$$ \left( \begin{array}{cc|c} 1 & 1 & 5\\ 0 & -3 & -6 \end{array} \right). $$

Therefore the equivalent system is:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{cases} x+y=5,\\ -3y=-6 \end{cases} }. $$

Unique-Solution Systems

Equivalent systems with a unique solution must have the same unique ordered tuple.

If:

$$ \operatorname{Sol}(S_1)=\{(2,3)\}, $$

then any equivalent system $S_2$ must satisfy:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Sol}(S_2)=\{(2,3)\} }. $$

Inconsistent Systems

Two inconsistent systems are equivalent if both have the empty solution set:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Sol}(S_1) = \operatorname{Sol}(S_2) = \varnothing }. $$

Thus equivalent systems need not possess any solutions.

Systems with Infinitely Many Solutions

Equivalent systems can also share an infinite solution set.

For example:

$$ \begin{cases} x+y=3,\\ 2x+2y=6 \end{cases} $$

is equivalent to:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{cases} x+y=3,\\ 0=0 \end{cases} }. $$

Both describe every point on the line:

$$ x+y=3. $$

Redundant Equations

An equation is redundant when removing it does not change the solution set.

For example:

$$ \begin{cases} x+y=4,\\ 2x+2y=8 \end{cases} $$

has the same solution set as:

$$ \boxed{x+y=4}. $$

Adding a Consequence Is Not Always Enough for Equivalence

If every solution of $S_1$ satisfies an additional equation $E$, then adding $E$ to $S_1$ does not change the solution set.

But replacing the entire system by only $E$ may lose restrictions and enlarge the solution set.

Example of Lost Information

From:

$$ \begin{cases} x+y=5,\\ x-y=1 \end{cases}, $$

one can derive:

$$ 2x=6. $$

But the single equation:

$$ 2x=6 $$

alone is not equivalent to the original system because it permits every point with $x=3$, regardless of $y$.

Reversible Transformations

The safest route to an equivalent system is a sequence of transformations that can each be reversed.

$$ \boxed{ S_1 \Longleftrightarrow S_2 \Longleftrightarrow S_3 }. $$

One-Way Transformations

A transformation that gives only:

$$ S_1\Longrightarrow S_2 $$

does not establish equivalence. The second system may contain additional solutions.

Squaring Equations

Squaring an equation in a system may introduce extraneous solutions.

Since:

$$ a=b \Longrightarrow a^2=b^2 $$

but not conversely, replacing an equation by its square is not generally an equivalence-preserving operation.

Variable Multiplication

Multiplying an equation by an expression that may equal zero can alter the solution set.

For example:

$$ x=1 $$

implies:

$$ x(x-1)=0, $$

but the new equation has both:

$$ x=0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad x=1 $$

as solutions.

Division by an Expression

Dividing an equation by an expression that may equal zero can remove valid solutions.

For:

$$ x(x-2)=0, $$

dividing by $x$ would produce:

$$ x-2=0, $$

and incorrectly lose the solution:

$$ \boxed{x=0}. $$

Nonlinear Systems

The definition of equivalent systems applies equally to nonlinear systems: equivalence still means equality of solution sets.

However, the elementary row-operation machinery belongs specifically to linear systems.

Graphical Interpretation

A system's solutions are the points satisfying every equation simultaneously.

Equivalent systems therefore have exactly the same common solution set, even if the individual equations produce different graphs.

Important Graphical Distinction

Equivalent systems do not require corresponding equations to have identical graphs.

For example:

$$ \begin{cases} x+y=5,\\ x-y=1 \end{cases} $$

and:

$$ \begin{cases} x+y=5,\\ 2x=6 \end{cases} $$

contain different second equations, yet their intersections are identical.

Geometric Meaning in Linear Systems

In two variables, each linear equation represents a line. In three variables, each represents a plane. Equivalent systems may replace these lines or planes with different ones while preserving the common intersection.

Three-Variable Example

If three planes intersect at one point, elimination may replace one plane by a different plane passing through the same common intersection while preserving the system's unique solution.

Solution-Set Perspective

The most reliable way to understand equivalence is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{same system solutions, not necessarily same equations} }. $$

Common Misconception: Equivalent systems do not have to contain equations that are individually equivalent in matching positions. Nor do their equations need to have the same graphs. The systems are equivalent only when their complete simultaneous solution sets are exactly the same.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\operatorname{Sol}(S_1)=\operatorname{Sol}(S_2)$ "The two systems have the same solution set."
$S_1\Longleftrightarrow S_2$ "System one is equivalent to system two."
$R_i\leftrightarrow R_j$ "Interchange rows i and j."
$R_i\to cR_i$ "Multiply row i by the nonzero scalar c."
$R_i\to R_i+cR_j$ "Replace row i by row i plus c times row j."
$[A\mid\mathbf{b}]\sim[C\mid\mathbf{d}]$ "The augmented matrices are row equivalent."
$\operatorname{Sol}(S)=\varnothing$ "The system has no solutions."

Common classroom descriptions include:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"replace the system by an equivalent system"} } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"perform an elimination step"} }. $$

The central meaning in either case is that the set of simultaneous solutions is unchanged.

Defense Notation Advice: Use:

$$ \boxed{\Longleftrightarrow} $$

only when the transformation genuinely preserves the solution set in both directions. If a step only guarantees that every old solution remains a new solution, use:

$$ \boxed{\Longrightarrow} $$

and check whether extra solutions have been introduced.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Definition: Equivalent systems have exactly the same solution set.
  • Formal Criterion: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Sol}(S_1) = \operatorname{Sol}(S_2) }. $$
  • Biconditional Form: $$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x}\text{ solves }S_1 \iff \mathbf{x}\text{ solves }S_2 }. $$
  • Equation Appearance: Equivalent systems need not contain equations that look alike.
  • Equation Order: Reordering equations preserves the system.
  • Equation Scaling: Multiplying one equation by a nonzero constant preserves equivalence.
  • Zero Scaling Caution: Multiplication by zero can destroy information and does not preserve equivalence.
  • Equation Replacement: Replacing one linear equation by itself plus a multiple of another preserves equivalence.
  • Reversibility: Standard linear-system operations are equivalence-preserving because they can be undone.
  • Elimination: Elimination constructs simpler equivalent systems.
  • Substitution: Substitution preserves equivalence when based on a valid equality and reversible transformation.
  • Augmented Matrix: A linear system may be represented by an augmented matrix.
  • Row Interchange: $$ \boxed{ R_i\leftrightarrow R_j }. $$
  • Row Scaling: $$ \boxed{ R_i\to cR_i,\qquad c\neq0 }. $$
  • Row Replacement: $$ \boxed{ R_i\to R_i+cR_j }. $$
  • Row Equivalence: Augmented matrices related by elementary row operations represent equivalent linear systems.
  • Gaussian Elimination: Produces a row-echelon system equivalent to the original.
  • Gauss–Jordan Elimination: Produces a reduced row-echelon system equivalent to the original.
  • Back Substitution: Solving an echelon-form equivalent system recovers the solutions of the original system.
  • Unique Solution: Equivalent systems with one solution share the same unique solution.
  • No Solution: Two systems with empty solution set are equivalent to each other if their solution sets are both exactly empty.
  • Infinite Solutions: Equivalent systems may share infinitely many solutions.
  • Redundant Equation: An equation may be removed if it imposes no additional restriction on the system's solution set.
  • Dependent Linear Equation: A scalar multiple or linear consequence of existing equations may be redundant.
  • Zero Row: In an augmented matrix, a row: $$ [\,0\;\cdots\;0\mid0\,] $$ represents a redundant equation.
  • Contradictory Row: A row: $$ [\,0\;\cdots\;0\mid c\,], \qquad c\neq0, $$ shows that every equivalent system is inconsistent.
  • Consistency Preservation: Equivalent systems are either both consistent or both inconsistent.
  • Solution Count Preservation: Equivalent systems have the same number of solutions because they have the same solution set.
  • Free Variables: Equivalent linear systems represent the same parametrized solution set even if their equations display free variables differently.
  • Matrix Rank: Elementary row operations preserve rank-related information used to analyze linear systems.
  • Homogeneous Systems: Row-equivalent homogeneous systems have exactly the same solution vectors.
  • Nonhomogeneous Systems: Row operations on the entire augmented matrix preserve the full solution set.
  • Coefficient Matrix Caution: Row operations should be applied to the augmented matrix when the constants are part of the system being transformed.
  • Graphical Meaning: Equivalent systems have the same common intersection set.
  • Two Variables: Equivalent linear systems have the same line-intersection solution set.
  • Three Variables: Equivalent systems of planes have the same common intersection.
  • Individual Graphs: Corresponding equations in equivalent systems need not define the same graph.
  • System-Level Concept: Equivalence is determined by the entire system, not equation-by-equation matching.
  • Derived Equation: An equation obtained from a system may be true for every solution without being sufficient by itself to describe the entire solution set.
  • Lost Restriction: Replacing several equations by only one consequence can enlarge the solution set.
  • Added Consequence: Adding an equation already implied by the system does not alter the solution set.
  • One-Way Implication: $$ S_1\Longrightarrow S_2 $$ alone does not establish system equivalence.
  • Two-Way Equivalence: $$ \boxed{ S_1\Longleftrightarrow S_2 } $$ expresses equal solution sets.
  • Squaring Caution: Squaring equations can introduce extraneous solutions.
  • Variable Multiplication Caution: Multiplication by an expression that can equal zero may add solutions.
  • Variable Division Caution: Division by an expression that can equal zero may remove solutions.
  • Domain Restrictions: Transformations must preserve restrictions imposed by the original system.
  • Rational Systems: Denominator restrictions must be retained when clearing denominators.
  • Radical Systems: Squaring may require final verification because equivalence can fail.
  • Logarithmic Systems: Positive-argument restrictions remain part of the solution criteria.
  • Nonlinear Systems: Equivalent nonlinear systems are still defined by identical solution sets, even though row operations do not generally apply.
  • Linear vs. Nonlinear: Elementary row operations are special equivalence-preserving tools for linear systems.
  • Equation Equivalence Contrast: Two individual equations are equivalent if they have the same solution set; equivalent systems extend the same idea to simultaneous equations.
  • Identity of Systems: Two systems may be equivalent without being literally the same written system.
  • Notation Caution: The symbol $\sim$ may mean row equivalence, similarity, asymptotic equivalence, or another relation depending on context.
  • Computational Role: The purpose of producing equivalent systems is usually to replace a difficult system with one whose solution set is easier to read.
  • Structural Role: Row reduction reveals pivots, free variables, inconsistency, and dependency without changing the underlying solutions.
  • Verification: When transformations are not known to be reversible, candidate solutions should be tested in the original system.
  • Core Interpretation: Equivalent systems of equations are different representations of the same simultaneous constraints: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Sol}(S_1) = \operatorname{Sol}(S_2) }. $$ In linear algebra, substitution, elimination, and elementary row operations systematically construct equivalent systems so that the original solution set becomes easier to identify without being changed.
