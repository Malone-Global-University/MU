Compound Interest

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

Compound interest is interest calculated on an amount that already includes previously accumulated interest. In other words, once interest is added to the balance, later interest is calculated on the enlarged balance.

$$ \boxed{ \text{new balance} = \text{old balance} + \text{interest on the old balance} }. $$

The word compound carries the sense of combining or accumulating successive layers. In finance, interest is said to compound because each new interest calculation is incorporated into the amount on which future interest is calculated.

If an amount grows by rate $r$ during each compounding period, one period changes principal $P$ to:

$$ P+Pr=P(1+r). $$

A second period applies the same growth factor again:

$$ P(1+r)(1+r)=P(1+r)^2. $$

After $n$ periods:

$$ \boxed{ A=P(1+r)^n }. $$

Compound interest is therefore fundamentally an application of repeated multiplication and exponential growth.

Operational Nuance

Basic Compound-Interest Formula

If $P$ is the initial principal, $r$ is the interest rate per compounding period, and $n$ is the number of compounding periods, then:

$$ \boxed{ A=P(1+r)^n }. $$

Here $A$ is the accumulated amount after $n$ periods.

Meaning of the Variables

Symbol Meaning
$P$ Principal or initial amount
$A$ Accumulated amount or future value
$r$ Interest rate per compounding period, written as a decimal
$n$ Number of compounding periods

The Growth Factor

The quantity:

$$ \boxed{ 1+r } $$

is the growth factor for one compounding period.

For example, an interest rate of $5\%$ per period corresponds to:

$$ r=0.05 $$

and therefore:

$$ 1+r=1.05. $$

Each compounding period multiplies the current balance by $1.05$.

Example: Annual Compounding

Suppose $\$1000$ is invested at $6\%$ interest compounded annually for $4$ years.

$$ P=1000,\qquad r=0.06,\qquad n=4. $$

Then:

$$ A = 1000(1.06)^4. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ A\approx\$1262.48 }. $$

Interest Earned versus Accumulated Amount

The formula $A=P(1+r)^n$ gives the total accumulated balance, not merely the amount of interest earned.

The compound interest earned is:

$$ \boxed{ I=A-P }. $$

Combining this with the accumulation formula:

$$ \boxed{ I=P\left[(1+r)^n-1\right] }. $$

Recurrence Form

Compound growth can also be described recursively. If $A_k$ is the balance after $k$ periods, then:

$$ \boxed{ A_{k+1}=(1+r)A_k } $$

with:

$$ A_0=P. $$

Iterating the recurrence gives:

$$ A_n=P(1+r)^n. $$

Compound Interest as a Geometric Sequence

The balances:

$$ P,\quad P(1+r),\quad P(1+r)^2,\quad P(1+r)^3,\ldots $$

form a geometric sequence with common ratio:

$$ \boxed{ 1+r }. $$

This makes compound interest an important practical example of geometric growth.

Simple Interest versus Compound Interest

Simple interest is calculated repeatedly from the original principal. Compound interest is calculated from the updated balance.

Simple interest:

$$ \boxed{ A=P(1+rt) }. $$

Compound interest with one compounding period per time unit:

$$ \boxed{ A=P(1+r)^t }. $$
Interest Model Growth Type Interest Base
Simple interest Linear Original principal
Compound interest Exponential Current accumulated balance

Why Compound Growth Is Exponential

Under compound interest, the same multiplicative growth factor is repeatedly applied to a changing balance.

$$ P \to P(1+r) \to P(1+r)^2 \to P(1+r)^3 \to\cdots $$

The number of periods appears in the exponent because the multiplication is repeated.

Nominal Annual Rate with Multiple Compounding Periods

If a nominal annual interest rate $r$ is compounded $m$ times per year for $t$ years, the standard formula is:

$$ \boxed{ A = P\left(1+\frac{r}{m}\right)^{mt} }. $$

In this formula:

  • $r/m$ is the periodic interest rate, and
  • $mt$ is the total number of compounding periods.

Annual Compounding

For annual compounding:

$$ m=1. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ A=P(1+r)^t }. $$

Semiannual Compounding

For compounding twice per year:

$$ m=2. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ A = P\left(1+\frac r2\right)^{2t} }. $$

Quarterly Compounding

For four compounding periods per year:

$$ \boxed{ A = P\left(1+\frac r4\right)^{4t} }. $$

Monthly Compounding

$$ \boxed{ A = P\left(1+\frac r{12}\right)^{12t} }. $$

Daily Compounding

Under a convention of $365$ compounding periods per year:

$$ \boxed{ A = P\left(1+\frac r{365}\right)^{365t} }. $$

Financial contracts may use other day-count and compounding conventions, so the stated convention controls the calculation.

Example: Monthly Compounding

Suppose $\$5000$ is invested at a nominal annual rate of $4.8\%$, compounded monthly, for $3$ years.

$$ P=5000,\qquad r=0.048,\qquad m=12,\qquad t=3. $$

Then:

$$ A = 5000 \left( 1+\frac{0.048}{12} \right)^{36}. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ A\approx\$5772.11 }. $$

Effective Annual Rate

When a nominal annual rate $r$ is compounded $m$ times per year, the actual proportional growth over one year is:

$$ \boxed{ r_{\text{eff}} = \left(1+\frac rm\right)^m-1 }. $$

This is commonly called the effective annual rate.

Annual Percentage Yield

In many consumer-finance settings, an annual percentage yield (APY) expresses the effective annual growth after compounding.

$$ \boxed{ \mathrm{APY} = \left(1+\frac rm\right)^m-1 } $$

when $r$ is a nominal annual rate compounded evenly $m$ times per year under the stated model.

Nominal Rate versus Effective Rate

A nominal rate does not by itself state the actual one-year multiplicative growth unless the compounding convention is also known.

For example, a nominal $12\%$ rate compounded monthly has monthly rate:

$$ \frac{0.12}{12}=0.01. $$

Its effective annual rate is:

$$ \left(1.01\right)^{12}-1, $$

which exceeds $12\%$.

More Frequent Compounding

For a fixed positive nominal annual rate under the standard formula:

$$ P\left(1+\frac rm\right)^{mt}, $$

increasing the compounding frequency generally increases the accumulated amount, though the increases become progressively smaller.

Continuous Compounding

The limiting case as the number of compounding periods per year grows without bound leads to continuous compounding:

$$ \boxed{ A=Pe^{rt} }. $$

This follows from the fundamental limit:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{m\to\infty} \left(1+\frac rm\right)^{mt} = e^{rt} }. $$

Why e Appears

The number $e$ arises naturally from continuous proportional growth. In continuous compounding, the instantaneous growth rate is proportional to the current amount.

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dA}{dt}=rA }. $$

With initial condition $A(0)=P$, the solution is:

$$ \boxed{ A(t)=Pe^{rt} }. $$

Present Value

The compound-interest formula can be solved backward to determine how much must be invested now to produce a desired future amount.

From:

$$ A=P(1+r)^n, $$

solve for $P$:

$$ \boxed{ P = \frac{A}{(1+r)^n} = A(1+r)^{-n} }. $$

This quantity is the present value of future amount $A$ under the specified rate and time assumptions.

Discount Factor

The quantity:

$$ \boxed{ (1+r)^{-n} } $$

is a discount factor for $n$ periods at periodic rate $r$.

Future Value

The accumulated amount $A$ is frequently called the future value:

$$ \boxed{ FV=PV(1+r)^n }. $$

Here $PV$ denotes present value and $FV$ denotes future value.

Solving for the Interest Rate

If:

$$ A=P(1+r)^n, $$

divide by $P$:

$$ \frac AP=(1+r)^n. $$

Taking the $n$th root gives:

$$ \left(\frac AP\right)^{1/n}=1+r. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ r = \left(\frac AP\right)^{1/n}-1 }. $$

Solving for the Number of Periods

From:

$$ \frac AP=(1+r)^n, $$

take logarithms:

$$ \ln\left(\frac AP\right) = n\ln(1+r). $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ n = \frac{ \ln(A/P) }{ \ln(1+r) } }. $$

Solving for Time with Periodic Compounding

From:

$$ A = P\left(1+\frac rm\right)^{mt}, $$

we obtain:

$$ \boxed{ t = \frac{ \ln(A/P) }{ m\ln\left(1+\frac rm\right) } }. $$

Solving for Time with Continuous Compounding

From:

$$ A=Pe^{rt}, $$

take logarithms:

$$ \ln\left(\frac AP\right)=rt. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ t = \frac{\ln(A/P)}{r} }. $$

Doubling Time

Under discrete compounding with periodic rate $r$, doubling requires:

$$ 2P=P(1+r)^n. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ n = \frac{\ln2}{\ln(1+r)} }. $$

Under continuous compounding:

$$ 2P=Pe^{rt}, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ t=\frac{\ln2}{r} }. $$

Compound Growth and Percentage Increase

Repeated percentage increases multiply rather than add.

Two successive increases of $10\%$ produce:

$$ 1.10\cdot1.10=1.21. $$

Thus the total increase is:

$$ \boxed{ 21\% } $$

rather than $20\%$.

Different Rates over Different Periods

If the periodic rates vary, the accumulated amount is found by multiplying all period growth factors:

$$ \boxed{ A = P(1+r_1)(1+r_2)\cdots(1+r_n) }. $$

Constant-rate compound interest is the special case:

$$ r_1=r_2=\cdots=r_n=r. $$

Interest on Interest

The phrase interest on interest describes the defining effect of compounding.

After the first period:

$$ A_1=P+Pr. $$

During the next period, interest is calculated on the entire $A_1$, including the earlier interest $Pr$.

Compound Interest and Exponential Functions

If time is allowed to vary continuously in a mathematical model while the discrete compounding rule is expressed as:

$$ A(t)=P(1+r)^t, $$

then $A$ is an exponential function of $t$.

Its initial value is:

$$ A(0)=P, $$

and its multiplicative growth factor per unit interval is $1+r$.

Compound Interest and Geometric Progressions

When balances are sampled at equally spaced compounding periods, the account values form a geometric progression:

$$ \boxed{ A_n=Pq^n, \qquad q=1+r }. $$

This connects financial mathematics directly to sequences, exponentials, logarithms, and recurrence relations.

Compound Interest with Additional Deposits

The basic compound-interest formula assumes a single initial principal with no later deposits or withdrawals.

Repeated equal deposits lead instead to annuity formulas. For example, an ordinary annuity with payment $PMT$ at the end of each period has future value:

$$ \boxed{ FV = PMT \frac{(1+r)^n-1}{r} }. $$

This formula incorporates compound interest but models a different cash-flow structure from a single lump-sum investment.

Compound Interest with Withdrawals

Withdrawals change the balance before later compounding. The simple formula $P(1+r)^n$ therefore no longer describes the entire account history unless those cash flows are modeled separately.

Debt Also Compounds

Compounding is not limited to investments. Under suitable loan or account terms, unpaid interest may be incorporated into a balance so that later interest is calculated on a larger amount.

$$ \boxed{ \text{compounding can amplify assets or liabilities} }. $$

Negative Effective Growth

The same multiplicative mathematics can describe decay when:

$$ -1

Then:

$$ 0<1+r<1, $$

so:

$$ P(1+r)^n $$

decreases geometrically. In financial terminology this may represent loss rather than positive interest, so context matters.

Interest Rate Must Match the Compounding Period

In:

$$ A=P(1+r)^n, $$

$r$ must be the rate for one period and $n$ must count periods of that same length.

$$ \boxed{ \text{rate period} = \text{counted period} }. $$

Mixing an annual rate directly with a monthly period count is a common modeling error unless the formula explicitly converts the annual rate to the periodic rate.

Percentage versus Decimal Form

Interest rates must be converted from percentage notation to decimal form before substitution.

$$ \boxed{ 6\%=0.06 }. $$

Substituting $6$ in place of $0.06$ would represent a $600\%$ periodic rate.

Rounding

Financial calculations often involve currency, but premature rounding can alter the final answer. It is generally preferable to retain sufficient precision throughout intermediate calculations and round according to the relevant financial convention at the end or at contractually specified stages.

Compounding Convention Matters

Two accounts quoting the same nominal annual rate can produce different accumulated values if they compound at different frequencies.

$$ \boxed{ \text{rate alone} \ne \text{complete compounding specification} }. $$

Common Misconception: Compound Interest Adds the Same Amount Each Period

Under a constant positive percentage rate, the same percentage is applied each period, but the dollar amount of interest generally increases because the balance grows.

$$ \boxed{ I_k=rA_{k-1} }. $$

Common Misconception: 5% per Year for 10 Years Means Exactly 50% Growth

Under annual compounding:

$$ \boxed{ \frac AP=(1.05)^{10} } $$

rather than:

$$ 1+10(0.05)=1.50. $$

The latter is the corresponding simple-interest growth factor.

Common Misconception: More Frequent Compounding Multiplies the Annual Rate

If a nominal annual rate $r$ is compounded $m$ times annually under the standard convention, the periodic rate is:

$$ \boxed{ \frac rm } $$

rather than $mr$.

Common Misconception: The Exponent Is Always the Number of Years

The exponent counts compounding periods.

For monthly compounding over $t$ years:

$$ \boxed{ n=12t }. $$

Common Misconception: A Means Interest Earned

In the common formula:

$$ A=P(1+r)^n, $$

$A$ denotes the total accumulated amount. Interest alone is:

$$ \boxed{ I=A-P }. $$

Common Misconception: Compound Interest Requires Continuous Compounding

Continuous compounding is only one limiting form. Annual, quarterly, monthly, and other discrete compounding schedules are all compound-interest models.

Common Misconception: Compound Interest and Compound Fraction Use the Same Operation

The shared word compound means that something is built from or accumulates multiple parts, but the mathematical constructions are unrelated.

A compound fraction involves nested quotients. Compound interest involves repeated percentage growth applied to an updated balance.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$A=P(1+r)^n$ "A equals P times one plus r raised to the n."
$A=P\left(1+\frac rm\right)^{mt}$ "A equals P times one plus r over m, raised to the m t power."
$A=Pe^{rt}$ "A equals P e to the r t."
$I=A-P$ "Interest earned equals the accumulated amount minus the principal."
$FV=PV(1+r)^n$ "Future value equals present value times one plus r to the n."
$r_{\text{eff}}=\left(1+\frac rm\right)^m-1$ "The effective annual rate equals one plus r over m raised to the m, minus one."
$A_{k+1}=(1+r)A_k$ "The next balance equals one plus r times the current balance."
$P=A(1+r)^{-n}$ "Present value equals the future amount times one plus r to the negative n."

Financial mathematics uses several interchangeable symbol systems. The initial amount may be denoted $P$, $PV$, or sometimes $P_0$, while the accumulated amount may appear as $A$, $FV$, or $P_n$. The underlying relationship matters more than the particular letters chosen.

The phrase compounded monthly specifies that interest is credited or mathematically applied in monthly compounding periods under the stated model. Compounded continuously signals the exponential form $Pe^{rt}$ rather than a finite-period formula.

Defense Notation Advice: Define whether a stated rate is periodic, nominal annual, or effective annual before applying a formula. Match the interest rate to the length of one compounding period and make the exponent count periods of that same length. Convert percentages to decimals, distinguish total accumulated amount from interest earned, and state the compounding frequency whenever a nominal annual rate is used.
