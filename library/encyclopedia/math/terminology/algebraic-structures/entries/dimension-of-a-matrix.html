Dimensions of a Matrix

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

The dimensions of a matrix specify the number of rows and columns in the matrix.

If a matrix has $m$ rows and $n$ columns, its dimensions are written:

$$ \boxed{m\times n}. $$

The row count is always stated first and the column count second:

$$ \boxed{ \text{rows}\times\text{columns} }. $$

For example:

$$ A= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3\\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{pmatrix} $$

has $2$ rows and $3$ columns, so:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ is a }2\times3\text{ matrix} }. $$

The phrase dimensions of a matrix is closely related to the terms size and order of a matrix. In elementary linear algebra, all three may refer to the same $m\times n$ specification.

This use of dimension should not be confused with the dimension of a vector space, which counts the number of vectors in a basis rather than the number of rows and columns in one matrix.

Operational Nuance

Rows First, Columns Second

For:

$$ A\in M_{m\times n}, $$

$m$ denotes the number of rows and $n$ the number of columns.

$$ \boxed{ m\times n = \text{rows}\times\text{columns} }. $$

Example: $3\times2$ Matrix

$$ B= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 4\\ 2 & 5\\ 3 & 6 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Matrix $B$ has:

  • $3$ rows,
  • $2$ columns,

so:

$$ \boxed{ B\text{ has dimensions }3\times2 }. $$

Number of Entries

An $m\times n$ matrix contains:

$$ \boxed{mn} $$

entries.

Thus a $4\times5$ matrix contains:

$$ \boxed{4\cdot5=20} $$

entries.

Entry Notation

If:

$$ A=(a_{ij})_{m\times n}, $$

then $a_{ij}$ denotes the entry in row $i$ and column $j$.

$$ \boxed{ a_{ij} = \text{entry in row }i,\text{ column }j }. $$

Valid Indices

For an $m\times n$ matrix:

$$ \boxed{ 1\leq i\leq m, \qquad 1\leq j\leq n }. $$

Square Matrix

A matrix is square when its row and column counts are equal:

$$ \boxed{ n\times n }. $$

Examples include:

$$ 2\times2,\qquad 3\times3,\qquad 5\times5. $$

Rectangular Matrix

If:

$$ m\neq n, $$

the matrix is rectangular rather than square.

Row Matrix

A matrix with one row has dimensions:

$$ \boxed{1\times n}. $$

For example:

$$ \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 5 & 8 \end{pmatrix} $$

is a $1\times3$ row matrix.

Column Matrix

A matrix with one column has dimensions:

$$ \boxed{m\times1}. $$

For example:

$$ \begin{pmatrix} 2\\ 5\\ 8 \end{pmatrix} $$

is a $3\times1$ column matrix.

Matrix Addition

Two matrices can be added or subtracted only when they have the same dimensions.

$$ \boxed{ A+B\text{ is defined only if } A,B\in M_{m\times n} }. $$

For example, a $2\times3$ matrix can be added to another $2\times3$ matrix, but not to a $3\times2$ matrix.

Scalar Multiplication

Multiplying a matrix by a scalar does not change its dimensions:

$$ A\in M_{m\times n} \Longrightarrow cA\in M_{m\times n}. $$

Matrix Multiplication

Dimensions determine whether matrix multiplication is defined.

If:

$$ A\text{ is }m\times n $$

and:

$$ B\text{ is }n\times p, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ AB\text{ is }m\times p }. $$

Inner Dimensions

Matrix multiplication requires the inner dimensions to agree:

$$ \boxed{ (m\times\color{black}{n}) (\color{black}{n}\times p) }. $$

The shared value $n$ is the number of entries paired in each row-by-column dot product.

Outer Dimensions

The dimensions of the product are the outer dimensions:

$$ \boxed{ (m\times n)(n\times p) \longrightarrow m\times p }. $$

Example of Matrix Multiplication

If:

$$ A\text{ is }2\times3 $$

and:

$$ B\text{ is }3\times4, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ AB\text{ is }2\times4 }. $$

Order of Multiplication Matters

If $A$ is $2\times3$ and $B$ is $3\times4$, then $AB$ exists. But:

$$ B A $$

would require:

$$ (3\times4)(2\times3), $$

whose inner dimensions $4$ and $2$ do not agree. Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ BA\text{ is undefined} }. $$

Transpose

Transposition interchanges rows and columns.

If:

$$ A\text{ is }m\times n, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ A^T\text{ is }n\times m }. $$

Example of Transpose Dimensions

A $2\times5$ matrix becomes a:

$$ \boxed{5\times2} $$

matrix after transposition.

Determinant

An ordinary determinant is defined only for square matrices:

$$ \boxed{ n\times n }. $$

Inverse

Under the standard matrix inverse operation, only square matrices can have a two-sided inverse:

$$ \boxed{ A^{-1}\text{ requires }A\text{ to be square and invertible} }. $$

Identity Matrix

The identity matrix $I_n$ has dimensions:

$$ \boxed{n\times n}. $$

Augmented Matrices

A system with $m$ equations and $n$ unknowns has coefficient matrix:

$$ \boxed{m\times n}. $$

Appending one right-hand-side column produces an augmented matrix of size:

$$ \boxed{m\times(n+1)}. $$

Linear Transformation Interpretation

An $m\times n$ matrix naturally represents a linear transformation:

$$ \boxed{ T:\mathbb{F}^n\to\mathbb{F}^m }. $$

The number of columns corresponds to the dimension of the coordinate input space, and the number of rows corresponds to the dimension of the coordinate output space.

Matrix-Vector Multiplication

If:

$$ A\text{ is }m\times n, $$

then a compatible column vector $\mathbf{x}$ has dimensions:

$$ \boxed{n\times1}. $$

The product:

$$ A\mathbf{x} $$

then has dimensions:

$$ \boxed{m\times1}. $$

Columns as Vectors

An $m\times n$ matrix contains $n$ column vectors, each belonging to:

$$ \boxed{\mathbb{F}^m}. $$

Rows as Vectors

The same matrix contains $m$ row vectors, each having $n$ components.

Rank and Matrix Dimensions

For an $m\times n$ matrix:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{rank}(A) \leq \min(m,n) }. $$

Thus the matrix dimensions place an upper bound on its rank.

Dimension of the Matrix Space

The set of all $m\times n$ matrices over a field $\mathbb{F}$ forms a vector space:

$$ M_{m\times n}(\mathbb{F}). $$

Its vector-space dimension is:

$$ \boxed{mn}. $$

This is a different meaning of the word dimension from the dimensions $m\times n$ of an individual matrix.

Common Misconception: A $2\times3$ matrix has two rows and three columns, not three rows and two columns. Matrix dimensions are always stated as rows first, columns second.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$m\times n$ "M by n," meaning m rows by n columns.
$2\times3$ "Two by three."
$A\in M_{m\times n}$ "A is an m by n matrix."
$a_{ij}$ "A sub i j," the entry in row i and column j.
$(m\times n)(n\times p)$ "An m by n matrix times an n by p matrix."
$AB\in M_{m\times p}$ "A B is an m by p matrix."
$A^T\in M_{n\times m}$ "A transpose is an n by m matrix."

The notation:

$$ \boxed{m\times n} $$

should ordinarily be spoken:

$$ \boxed{\text{"m by n"}}. $$

It does not mean multiplication of the numbers $m$ and $n$ when used to describe matrix size, even though the same symbol $\times$ is also used for multiplication elsewhere.

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{\text{"a three by four matrix"}} $$

means:

$$ \boxed{ 3\text{ rows and }4\text{ columns} }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: When checking matrix multiplication, write the dimensions beside the factors:

$$ \boxed{ (m\times n)(n\times p) \to m\times p }. $$

This makes the compatibility condition visible before any entrywise computation is attempted.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Basic Definition: An $m\times n$ matrix has $m$ rows and $n$ columns.
  • Order Convention: $$ \boxed{ \text{rows first, columns second} }. $$
  • Example: A $2\times5$ matrix has two rows and five columns.
  • Entry Count: An $m\times n$ matrix contains: $$ \boxed{mn} $$ entries.
  • Entry Index: $$ a_{ij} $$ denotes row $i$, column $j$.
  • Row Index Range: $$ 1\leq i\leq m. $$
  • Column Index Range: $$ 1\leq j\leq n. $$
  • Square Matrix: $$ \boxed{n\times n}. $$
  • Rectangular Matrix: $$ \boxed{m\times n,\qquad m\neq n}. $$
  • Row Matrix: $$ \boxed{1\times n}. $$
  • Column Matrix: $$ \boxed{m\times1}. $$
  • Scalar Entry: A $1\times1$ matrix contains a single scalar entry.
  • Addition Compatibility: Matrices must have equal dimensions to be added.
  • Subtraction Compatibility: Matrices must have equal dimensions to be subtracted.
  • Equality: Equal matrices necessarily have the same dimensions.
  • Scalar Multiplication: Multiplying by a scalar does not change dimensions.
  • Matrix Multiplication Condition: $$ \boxed{ (m\times n)(n\times p) } $$ is compatible.
  • Matrix Multiplication Output: $$ \boxed{ (m\times n)(n\times p) \to m\times p }. $$
  • Inner Dimensions: The two inner dimensions must match.
  • Outer Dimensions: The two outer dimensions become the product dimensions.
  • Order Sensitivity: Compatibility of $AB$ does not imply compatibility of $BA$.
  • Noncommutativity: Even when both $AB$ and $BA$ exist, their dimensions may differ unless the surrounding dimensions permit both products to have comparable size.
  • Transpose: $$ \boxed{ m\times n \longrightarrow n\times m }. $$
  • Double Transpose: $$ \boxed{ (A^T)^T=A } $$ restores the original dimensions.
  • Determinant: Ordinary determinants require a square matrix.
  • Inverse: A standard two-sided inverse requires a square matrix.
  • Identity Matrix: $$ I_n $$ has dimensions: $$ \boxed{n\times n}. $$
  • Diagonal Matrix: A standard diagonal matrix is square.
  • Triangular Matrix: Standard upper- and lower-triangular matrices are square.
  • Symmetric Matrix: A matrix satisfying: $$ A^T=A $$ must be square.
  • Coefficient Matrix: A system of $m$ equations in $n$ unknowns has an $m\times n$ coefficient matrix.
  • Augmented Matrix: Adding one right-hand-side column produces an: $$ \boxed{m\times(n+1)} $$ augmented matrix.
  • Matrix-Vector Product: If $A$ is $m\times n$, then: $$ \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}^n $$ is represented as an $n\times1$ column vector.
  • Output Vector: $$ A\mathbf{x} $$ is an: $$ \boxed{m\times1} $$ column vector.
  • Linear Transformation: An $m\times n$ matrix represents a coordinate linear map: $$ \boxed{ \mathbb{F}^n\to\mathbb{F}^m }. $$
  • Domain Coordinate Dimension: The number of columns is $n$.
  • Codomain Coordinate Dimension: The number of rows is $m$.
  • Columns: An $m\times n$ matrix contains $n$ column vectors in $\mathbb{F}^m$.
  • Rows: It contains $m$ row vectors with $n$ components each.
  • Column Space: The column space is a subspace of: $$ \boxed{\mathbb{F}^m}. $$
  • Row Space: The row space may be viewed as a subspace of: $$ \boxed{\mathbb{F}^n}. $$
  • Rank Bound: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{rank}(A) \leq \min(m,n) }. $$
  • Full Column Rank: An $m\times n$ matrix has full column rank when: $$ \operatorname{rank}(A)=n, $$ which requires: $$ n\leq m. $$
  • Full Row Rank: It has full row rank when: $$ \operatorname{rank}(A)=m, $$ which requires: $$ m\leq n. $$
  • Nullity: For an $m\times n$ matrix representing a map from $\mathbb{F}^n$: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{rank}(A) + \operatorname{nullity}(A) = n }. $$
  • Matrix Space: $$ M_{m\times n}(\mathbb{F}) $$ denotes the set of all $m\times n$ matrices over $\mathbb{F}$.
  • Vector-Space Dimension of Matrix Space: $$ \boxed{ \dim M_{m\times n}(\mathbb{F})=mn }. $$
  • Terminological Caution: The dimensions $m\times n$ of a particular matrix are not the same concept as the vector-space dimension $mn$ of the space of all such matrices.
  • Order of a Matrix: Some textbooks use order as a synonym for matrix dimensions.
  • Size of a Matrix: Size is another common synonym for the row-by-column specification.
  • Computer Storage: A dense $m\times n$ matrix ordinarily requires storage for $mn$ entries.
  • Data Tables: A data matrix may use rows for observations and columns for variables, though the convention depends on the application.
  • Statistics: A dataset with $m$ observations and $n$ measured features is often stored as an $m\times n$ matrix.
  • Graphical Models & Networks: An adjacency matrix for a graph with $n$ vertices is typically: $$ \boxed{n\times n}. $$
  • Transformation Composition: Matrix dimensions encode whether two linear maps can be composed in a given order.
  • Example of Composition: If: $$ A:\mathbb{R}^3\to\mathbb{R}^2 $$ and: $$ B:\mathbb{R}^4\to\mathbb{R}^3, $$ then their matrices have dimensions: $$ 2\times3 \qquad\text{and}\qquad 3\times4, $$ so: $$ AB $$ has dimensions: $$ \boxed{2\times4}. $$
  • Dimension Check: Matrix dimensions provide a fast structural check before performing arithmetic.
  • Error Detection: If dimensions are incompatible, the proposed matrix operation is not defined under the standard rules.
  • Terminological Contrast: Matrix dimensions describe rows and columns; matrix rank measures the number of linearly independent row or column directions.
  • Core Interpretation: Matrix dimensions record the matrix's structural shape: $$ \boxed{ m\times n = m\text{ rows by }n\text{ columns} }. $$ They determine the number of entries and control which additions, products, transposes, determinants, inverses, and linear transformations are structurally possible.
