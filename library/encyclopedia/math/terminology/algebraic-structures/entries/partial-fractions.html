Partial Fractions

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

Partial fractions are simpler rational expressions whose sum reconstructs a more complicated rational expression. The process of rewriting a rational expression in this way is called partial-fraction decomposition.

$$ \boxed{ \text{complicated rational expression} = \text{sum of simpler rational expressions} } $$

For example:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{3x+5}{(x+1)(x+2)} = \frac{2}{x+1} + \frac{1}{x+2} }. $$

The adjective partial reflects the idea that each component fraction represents only part of the original rational expression. The plural fractions refers to the collection of simpler terms into which the original expression is decomposed.

The terminology is closely connected to factoring. A denominator is first broken into simpler polynomial factors, and then a corresponding collection of simpler fractions is assigned to those factors.

$$ \boxed{ \text{factor denominator} \longrightarrow \text{build partial fractions} \longrightarrow \text{solve coefficients} }. $$

The method is especially important in algebra and calculus because rational functions that are difficult to manipulate or integrate in one piece may become straightforward once decomposed.

Operational Nuance

Rational Expression Setting

Partial-fraction decomposition applies to rational expressions:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} } $$

where $P$ and $Q$ are polynomials and:

$$ Q(x)\neq0. $$

Proper Rational Expression

A rational expression is proper when:

$$ \boxed{ \deg P < \deg Q }. $$

Improper Rational Expression

If:

$$ \deg P \geq \deg Q, $$

polynomial division should ordinarily be performed first.

Division before Decomposition

$$ \boxed{ \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} = S(x) + \frac{R(x)}{Q(x)} } $$

with:

$$ \deg R < \deg Q. $$

The proper remainder fraction is then decomposed.

Core Principle

Over the real numbers, the denominator is factored into linear factors and irreducible quadratic factors.

Distinct Linear Factors

If:

$$ Q(x) = (x-a)(x-b), \qquad a\neq b, $$

then the decomposition has the form:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{P(x)} {(x-a)(x-b)} = \frac{A}{x-a} + \frac{B}{x-b} }. $$

Example

Decompose:

$$ \frac{3x+5}{(x+1)(x+2)}. $$

Write:

$$ \frac{3x+5}{(x+1)(x+2)} = \frac{A}{x+1} + \frac{B}{x+2}. $$

Clear Denominators

Multiply by $(x+1)(x+2)$:

$$ \boxed{ 3x+5 = A(x+2) + B(x+1) }. $$

Substitution Method

Set $x=-1$:

$$ 2=A. $$

Set $x=-2$:

$$ -1=-B, $$

so:

$$ B=1. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{3x+5}{(x+1)(x+2)} = \frac{2}{x+1} + \frac{1}{x+2} }. $$

Coefficient Comparison

The same identity:

$$ 3x+5 = A(x+2) + B(x+1) $$

can be expanded:

$$ 3x+5 = (A+B)x + (2A+B). $$

Matching coefficients gives:

$$ \boxed{ A+B=3, \qquad 2A+B=5 }. $$

Identity Principle

After clearing denominators, the resulting polynomial equality must hold for all $x$:

$$ \boxed{ P(x) \equiv \text{expanded numerator identity} }. $$

That is why coefficient comparison is valid.

Repeated Linear Factors

If the denominator contains:

$$ (x-a)^n, $$

include a term for every power from $1$ through $n$:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{A_1}{x-a} + \frac{A_2}{(x-a)^2} + \cdots + \frac{A_n}{(x-a)^n} }. $$

Example of Repeated Linear Factor Form

$$ \boxed{ \frac{P(x)} {(x-1)^3(x+2)} = \frac{A}{x-1} + \frac{B}{(x-1)^2} + \frac{C}{(x-1)^3} + \frac{D}{x+2} }. $$

Why Every Repeated Power Is Needed

Omitting intermediate denominator powers generally leaves too few degrees of freedom to reproduce an arbitrary numerator.

Irreducible Quadratic Factors

Over the real numbers, a quadratic factor:

$$ ax^2+bx+c $$

is irreducible when:

$$ \boxed{ b^2-4ac<0 }. $$

Numerator over an Irreducible Quadratic

The numerator must have degree less than the quadratic denominator:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{Ax+B}{ax^2+bx+c} }. $$

Why the Numerator Is Linear

A constant numerator would not provide enough flexibility in general, while any quadratic or higher numerator could first be reduced by polynomial division.

Example of Distinct Quadratic Factor

$$ \boxed{ \frac{P(x)} {(x-1)(x^2+4)} = \frac{A}{x-1} + \frac{Bx+C}{x^2+4} }. $$

Repeated Irreducible Quadratic Factors

If:

$$ (x^2+1)^n $$

occurs, include one linear numerator for each power:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{A_1x+B_1}{x^2+1} + \frac{A_2x+B_2}{(x^2+1)^2} + \cdots + \frac{A_nx+B_n}{(x^2+1)^n} }. $$

General Real Decomposition Pattern

A proper rational function over $\mathbb{R}$ can be decomposed according to the real factorization of its denominator into:

  • powers of linear factors;
  • powers of irreducible quadratic factors.

Linear-Factor Template

For:

$$ (x-a)^m, $$

use:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{k=1}^{m} \frac{A_k}{(x-a)^k} }. $$

Quadratic-Factor Template

For an irreducible quadratic $q(x)$ appearing to power $m$, use:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{k=1}^{m} \frac{A_kx+B_k}{q(x)^k} }. $$

Complete Example Setup

If:

$$ Q(x) = x(x-1)^2(x^2+4)^2, $$

then a complete decomposition form is:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{A}{x} + \frac{B}{x-1} + \frac{C}{(x-1)^2} + \frac{Dx+E}{x^2+4} + \frac{Fx+G}{(x^2+4)^2} }. $$

Unknown Coefficients

The symbols:

$$ A,B,C,\ldots $$

are constants to be determined.

Method 1: Strategic Substitution

Values that make factors zero can isolate coefficients associated with distinct linear factors.

Method 2: Coefficient Comparison

Expand both sides and equate coefficients of equal powers of $x$.

Method 3: Linear-System Solution

Coefficient comparison produces a linear system for the unknown partial-fraction constants.

Method 4: Mixed Strategy

In practice, strategic substitution can determine some coefficients quickly, with coefficient comparison used for the remainder.

Cover-Up Method

For simple distinct linear factors, the Heaviside cover-up method can provide a shortcut.

If:

$$ \frac{P(x)} {(x-a)R(x)} = \frac{A}{x-a} + \cdots, $$

then under the appropriate simple-factor conditions:

$$ \boxed{ A = \left. \frac{P(x)}{R(x)} \right|_{x=a} }. $$

Cover-Up Method Scope

The basic cover-up shortcut works directly for simple distinct linear factors. It should not be mechanically applied to repeated factors or general irreducible quadratics without appropriate extensions.

Residue-Like Formula for Simple Linear Poles

If:

$$ Q(a)=0, \qquad Q'(a)\neq0, $$

then the coefficient of $1/(x-a)$ in:

$$ \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} $$

is:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{P(a)}{Q'(a)} }. $$

Connection to Complex Analysis

This coefficient is the residue at a simple pole when the rational function is viewed as a complex function.

Domain Restrictions Remain

Partial-fraction decomposition rewrites a rational function but does not restore denominator zeros to the domain.

Example

$$ \frac{3x+5}{(x+1)(x+2)} = \frac{2}{x+1} + \frac{1}{x+2} $$

is interpreted on:

$$ \boxed{ x\neq-1, \qquad x\neq-2 }. $$

Identity vs. Function Equality

After clearing denominators, one obtains a polynomial identity valid for all real $x$. The original rational functions, however, remain undefined where their denominators vanish.

Cancellation before Decomposition

If numerator and denominator share a factor, algebraic simplification may reduce the expression before partial-fraction decomposition.

Hole Caveat

If a common factor is cancelled, the original rational function still excludes the cancelled denominator zero.

Example

$$ \frac{(x-1)(x+2)} {(x-1)(x+3)} = \frac{x+2}{x+3}, \qquad x\neq1,-3. $$

Simplification changes the displayed formula but not the original domain.

Uniqueness

Once a denominator factorization and standard numerator-degree conventions are fixed, the partial-fraction decomposition is unique.

$$ \boxed{ \text{standard partial-fraction decomposition is unique} }. $$

Why Uniqueness Matters

Solving for the coefficients is not merely finding one convenient decomposition; under the standard form, there is only one set of coefficients that works.

Polynomial Division Example

Consider:

$$ \frac{x^2+3x+5}{x^2-1}. $$

Since numerator and denominator have equal degree:

$$ \frac{x^2+3x+5}{x^2-1} = 1 + \frac{3x+6}{x^2-1}. $$

Factor:

$$ x^2-1 = (x-1)(x+1). $$

Then:

$$ \frac{3x+6}{(x-1)(x+1)} = \frac{A}{x-1} + \frac{B}{x+1}. $$

Integration Motivation

Partial fractions are especially useful because simpler rational terms often have standard antiderivatives.

Distinct Linear Factors in Integration

For:

$$ \frac{1}{(x-a)(x-b)}, $$

decomposition reduces the integral to logarithmic terms.

Basic Linear-Factor Integral

$$ \boxed{ \int \frac{A}{x-a}\,dx = A\ln|x-a| + C }. $$

Repeated Linear Factor Integral

For $k\geq2$:

$$ \boxed{ \int \frac{A}{(x-a)^k}\,dx = \frac{A}{1-k} (x-a)^{1-k} + C }. $$

Irreducible Quadratic Integration

Terms such as:

$$ \frac{Ax+B}{x^2+1} $$

may split into logarithmic and arctangent contributions.

Derivative-Matching Part

Since:

$$ \frac{d}{dx} \left( x^2+1 \right) = 2x, $$

the $Ax$ numerator component can be related to:

$$ \ln(x^2+1). $$

Arctangent Part

$$ \boxed{ \int \frac{1}{x^2+a^2} \,dx = \frac{1}{a} \arctan \left( \frac{x}{a} \right) + C } $$

for $a>0$.

Partial Fractions in Differential Equations

Separation of variables often produces rational integrals that are simplified by partial-fraction decomposition.

Logistic Equation Example

A separated logistic-growth expression often includes:

$$ \frac{1}{P(K-P)}, $$

which can be decomposed into:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{1}{P(K-P)} = \frac{1}{K} \left( \frac{1}{P} + \frac{1}{K-P} \right) } $$

with the sign convention handled through the derivative of $K-P$ during integration.

Laplace Transforms

In differential-equation methods, inverse Laplace transforms often require decomposing rational functions in the transform variable into partial fractions.

Example Pattern

$$ \boxed{ \frac{1}{s(s+a)} = \frac{1}{a} \left( \frac{1}{s} - \frac{1}{s+a} \right) }. $$

Control Theory

Transfer functions can be decomposed into simpler modes associated with poles.

Discrete Mathematics and Generating Functions

Rational generating functions can be decomposed into partial fractions to derive explicit sequence formulas.

Example Pattern

A generating function:

$$ \frac{1}{(1-\alpha x)(1-\beta x)} $$

can be decomposed into terms involving:

$$ \frac{1}{1-\alpha x} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{1}{1-\beta x}, $$

which correspond to geometric sequences.

Sequence Extraction

Since:

$$ \frac{1}{1-rx} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} r^n x^n $$

within its convergence region, partial fractions can expose closed-form sequence coefficients.

Difference Equations

Related decompositions appear when solving linear recurrences through generating functions or transform methods.

Real vs. Complex Factorization

Over $\mathbb{R}$, irreducible quadratics remain quadratic.

Over $\mathbb{C}$, every nonconstant polynomial factors into linear factors.

$$ \boxed{ \text{over }\mathbb{C}, \text{ partial fractions can be expressed using linear factors only} }. $$

Example

Over $\mathbb{R}$:

$$ x^2+1 $$

is irreducible.

Over $\mathbb{C}$:

$$ \boxed{ x^2+1 = (x-i)(x+i) }. $$

Field Dependence

The exact shape of a partial-fraction decomposition therefore depends on the field over which factorization is performed.

Repeated Poles and Higher Powers

Repeated denominator factors correspond to repeated poles in complex-function language and require multiple powers in the decomposition.

Algebraic Meaning

Partial fractions express a rational function relative to the factor structure of its denominator.

Local Denominator Contributions

Each partial fraction isolates the contribution associated with one denominator factor or repeated-factor level.

Structural Analogy

The process resembles decomposing a complicated algebraic object into simpler components adapted to its factorization.

Partial Fractions vs. Adding Fractions

Ordinary fraction addition moves from simpler fractions to one combined fraction.

$$ \boxed{ \frac{A}{x-a} + \frac{B}{x-b} \longrightarrow \frac{\text{combined numerator}} {(x-a)(x-b)} } $$

Partial-fraction decomposition reverses that process.

Reverse Fraction Addition

$$ \boxed{ \text{partial fractions} \approx \text{reverse common-denominator process} }. $$

Partial Fractions vs. Factoring

Factoring rewrites polynomials as products. Partial fractions uses such factors to rewrite rational expressions as sums.

Partial Fractions vs. Polynomial Division

Polynomial division handles improper rational expressions; partial fractions decomposes the proper rational remainder.

Partial Fractions vs. Rationalization

Rationalization removes radicals from selected denominator or numerator forms; partial fractions decomposes rational functions according to polynomial factors.

Partial Fractions vs. Cancellation

Cancellation removes common factors. Partial fractions generally splits a denominator into separate additive contributions.

Partial Fraction Is Not Arbitrary Splitting

In general:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{A+B}{C+D} \neq \frac{A}{C} + \frac{B}{D} }. $$

Partial-fraction decomposition works because unknown coefficients are chosen so that the resulting sum is algebraically identical to the original rational expression.

Common Error: Splitting the Denominator Directly

Incorrect:

$$ \frac{1}{(x+1)(x+2)} = \frac{1}{x+1} + \frac{1}{x+2}. $$

The right side actually equals:

$$ \frac{2x+3}{(x+1)(x+2)}. $$

Common Error: Decomposing an Improper Fraction before Division

If numerator degree is at least denominator degree, perform polynomial division first.

Common Error: Failing to Factor the Denominator Completely

The denominator should be factored into the appropriate irreducible factors over the chosen field before writing the decomposition template.

Common Error: Omitting Repeated Powers

For:

$$ (x-a)^3, $$

all three terms are needed:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{A}{x-a} + \frac{B}{(x-a)^2} + \frac{C}{(x-a)^3} }. $$

Common Error: Using a Constant Numerator over a Quadratic Factor

A general irreducible quadratic factor requires a linear numerator:

$$ \boxed{ Ax+B }. $$

Common Error: Using a Quadratic Numerator over a Quadratic Factor

The partial numerator should have lower degree than its denominator factor.

Common Error: Forgetting Repeated Quadratic Powers

For:

$$ (x^2+1)^2, $$

use:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{Ax+B}{x^2+1} + \frac{Cx+D}{(x^2+1)^2} }. $$

Common Error: Solving Coefficients before Clearing Denominators Correctly

Every term must be multiplied by the full common denominator with appropriate cancellation.

Common Error: Using Only Convenient Substitutions When They Do Not Determine All Coefficients

Strategic substitutions often isolate coefficients for linear factors, but coefficient comparison may still be required.

Common Error: Forgetting the Polynomial Identity Must Hold for All $x$

Matching only a few test values is sufficient only when enough independent conditions have been obtained to determine the coefficients and the resulting identity is thereby established.

Common Error: Ignoring Original Domain Restrictions

Decomposition does not make denominator zeros legal inputs.

Common Error: Restoring a Hole after Cancellation

The original rational function retains excluded points even if a simplified formula is defined there.

Common Error: Forgetting Absolute Values in Logarithmic Antiderivatives

For real integration:

$$ \boxed{ \int \frac{1}{x-a}\,dx = \ln|x-a| + C }. $$

Common Error: Treating Partial Fractions as an Integration-Only Technique

The decomposition is algebraic. Integration is one important application.

Common Error: Assuming Every Rational Expression Needs Partial Fractions

Simpler algebraic simplification, substitution, direct integration, or another technique may be preferable.

Common Error: Assuming the Real and Complex Decompositions Look Identical

Quadratic factors may remain irreducible over $\mathbb{R}$ but split over $\mathbb{C}$.

Common Error: Assuming Partial-Fraction Coefficients Are Functions of $x$

In the standard one-variable decomposition, the unknown symbols $A,B,C,\ldots$ are constants.

Common Error: Confusing Partial Fractions with Partial Derivatives

The word partial appears in both phrases, but the concepts are unrelated: partial fractions decompose rational expressions, while partial derivatives differentiate multivariable functions with respect to selected variables.

Common Misconception: Partial fractions are not obtained by simply splitting a denominator into pieces. The method begins with a completely factored denominator, assigns a carefully structured family of simpler rational terms, and then solves for constants so that their sum is identically equal to the original rational expression on its domain.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}$ "A rational expression P of x over Q of x."
$\frac{A}{x-a}+\frac{B}{x-b}$ "The partial-fraction decomposition over two distinct linear factors."
$\frac{A}{x-a}+\frac{B}{(x-a)^2}$ "The partial fractions required for a repeated linear factor of multiplicity two."
$\frac{Ax+B}{x^2+1}$ "A linear numerator over an irreducible quadratic factor."
$P(x)\equiv A(x-b)+B(x-a)$ "The polynomial identity obtained after clearing denominators."
$A=P(a)/Q'(a)$ "The coefficient associated with a simple linear factor, under the simple-root conditions."
$\int A/(x-a)\,dx$ "Integrate the simple partial fraction to obtain a logarithmic term."

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"decompose into partial fractions"} } $$

means to rewrite a rational expression as a sum of simpler rational terms adapted to the factors of its denominator.

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"set up the partial fractions"} } $$

often means to write the correct decomposition template before solving for the unknown coefficients.

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"clear the denominators"} } $$

means to multiply through by the least common denominator so the rational identity becomes a polynomial identity.

Defense Notation Advice: First ensure the rational expression is proper. Factor the denominator completely over the intended number system. Include every power of each repeated factor. Use constant numerators over linear factors and degree-one numerators over irreducible quadratic factors. After solving the coefficients, preserve all original domain restrictions and verify the decomposition by recombining the fractions or checking the resulting polynomial identity.

After a proper rational function's denominator is factored, simpler terms are assigned to each factor: $$ \boxed{ \frac{P(x)}{(x-a)(x-b)} = \frac{A}{x-a} + \frac{B}{x-b} }. $$ Repeated factors require every intermediate power: $$ \boxed{ \frac{A_1}{x-a} + \frac{A_2}{(x-a)^2} + \cdots + \frac{A_n}{(x-a)^n} } $$ while irreducible quadratics require linear numerators: $$ \boxed{ \frac{Ax+B}{x^2+px+q} }. $$ The coefficients are chosen so the sum is identically equal to the original rational expression on its domain. This decomposition exposes denominator structure and converts many difficult rational expressions into components that are easier to simplify, integrate, transform, or analyze.
