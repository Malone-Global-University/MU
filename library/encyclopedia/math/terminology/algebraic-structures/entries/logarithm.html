Logarithm

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

A logarithm is an exponent interpreted as the answer to the question: to what power must a given base be raised to produce a given number?

$$ \boxed{ b^x=y \iff \log_b(y)=x } $$

where, in the ordinary real-valued setting:

$$ \boxed{ b>0, \qquad b\neq1, \qquad y>0 }. $$

For example:

$$ 2^3=8 $$

is equivalent to:

$$ \boxed{ \log_2(8)=3 }. $$

The word logarithm was coined in the early seventeenth century by John Napier from Greek-derived elements associated with ratio and number. Historically, logarithms transformed difficult multiplication and division into easier addition and subtraction, making them indispensable in astronomy, navigation, engineering, and computation before electronic calculators.

The modern central meaning is structural rather than merely computational: logarithms are inverse operations to exponentiation.

$$ \boxed{ \text{exponentiation} \longleftrightarrow \text{logarithm} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Core Definition

For an admissible base $b$ and positive argument $x$:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(x)=y \iff b^y=x }. $$

Base

In:

$$ \log_b(x), $$

$b$ is the base.

Argument

The quantity $x$ inside the logarithm is the argument.

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(\text{argument}) }. $$

Value

The logarithm's output is the exponent needed on the base.

Example

$$ \log_3(81)=4 $$

because:

$$ \boxed{ 3^4=81 }. $$

Negative Logarithm Value

A logarithm may have a negative value even though its real argument must be positive.

$$ \boxed{ \log_{10}(0.01)=-2 } $$

because:

$$ 10^{-2}=0.01. $$

Fractional Logarithm Value

$$ \boxed{ \log_9(3)=\frac{1}{2} } $$

because:

$$ 9^{1/2}=3. $$

Why the Base Must Be Positive

In the ordinary real logarithm, a positive base ensures that $b^x$ is defined as a positive real quantity for every real exponent $x$.

Why the Base Cannot Equal One

If:

$$ b=1, $$

then:

$$ 1^x=1 $$

for every real $x$. This function is not one-to-one and therefore has no inverse logarithmic function.

Real Logarithm Domain

For:

$$ f(x)=\log_b(x), $$

the real domain is:

$$ \boxed{ x>0 }. $$

Range

The range is all real numbers:

$$ \boxed{ (-\infty,\infty) }. $$

Logarithm of One

Since:

$$ b^0=1, $$

every admissible base satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(1)=0 }. $$

Logarithm of the Base

Since:

$$ b^1=b, $$

we have:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(b)=1 }. $$

Logarithm of a Power of the Base

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(b^x)=x }. $$

Exponentiation of a Logarithm

For $x>0$:

$$ \boxed{ b^{\log_b(x)}=x }. $$

Inverse-Function Relationship

The exponential function:

$$ y=b^x $$

and logarithmic function:

$$ y=\log_b(x) $$

are inverse functions.

Coordinate Reversal

If:

$$ (a,b^a) $$

lies on $y=b^x$, then:

$$ \boxed{ (b^a,a) } $$

lies on $y=\log_b(x)$.

Reflection across $y=x$

The graphs of inverse exponential and logarithmic functions are reflections of one another across:

$$ \boxed{ y=x }. $$

Increasing Logarithm

If:

$$ b>1, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(x) \text{ is strictly increasing} }. $$

Decreasing Logarithm

If:

$$ 0<b<1, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(x) \text{ is strictly decreasing} }. $$

Vertical Asymptote

The logarithmic graph has vertical asymptote:

$$ \boxed{ x=0 }. $$

Behavior near Zero for $b>1$

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to0^+}\log_b(x) = -\infty }. $$

Behavior at Infinity for $b>1$

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to\infty}\log_b(x) = \infty }. $$

Slow Growth

Logarithms grow more slowly than every positive power of $x$.

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to\infty} \frac{\log_b(x)}{x^p} = 0, \qquad p>0 }. $$

Product Rule

For positive $M$ and $N$:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(MN) = \log_b(M)+\log_b(N) }. $$

Why the Product Rule Works

If:

$$ M=b^u, \qquad N=b^v, $$

then:

$$ MN = b^u b^v = b^{u+v}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \log_b(MN) = u+v. $$

Quotient Rule

For positive $M$ and $N$:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b\left(\frac{M}{N}\right) = \log_b(M)-\log_b(N) }. $$

Power Rule

For suitable real-valued arguments:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(M^r) = r\log_b(M) } $$

when $M>0$.

Root Rule

$$ \boxed{ \log_b\left(\sqrt[n]{M}\right) = \frac{1}{n}\log_b(M) } $$

where the real logarithm requires the resulting argument to be positive.

Reciprocal Rule

$$ \boxed{ \log_b\left(\frac{1}{M}\right) = -\log_b(M) }. $$

Expansion of a Logarithm

The laws can convert products, quotients, and powers into sums, differences, and scalar multiples.

For example:

$$ \log_b\left(\frac{x^3y^2}{z}\right) $$

becomes, for positive $x,y,z$:

$$ \boxed{ 3\log_b(x) + 2\log_b(y) - \log_b(z) }. $$

Condensing Logarithms

The rules can be reversed:

$$ 2\log_b(x) + \log_b(y) - \log_b(z) $$

becomes:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b\left(\frac{x^2y}{z}\right) } $$

under appropriate positivity conditions.

No Sum Rule

In general:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(M+N) \neq \log_b(M)+\log_b(N) }. $$

No Difference Rule

Likewise:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(M-N) \neq \log_b(M)-\log_b(N) }. $$

Change-of-Base Formula

For admissible bases $a$ and $b$:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(x) = \frac{\log_a(x)}{\log_a(b)} }. $$

Natural-Log Change of Base

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(x) = \frac{\ln(x)}{\ln(b)} }. $$

Common-Log Change of Base

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(x) = \frac{\log(x)}{\log(b)} } $$

when $\log$ denotes base $10$.

Reciprocal Base Identity

$$ \boxed{ \log_a(b) = \frac{1}{\log_b(a)} }. $$

Multiplicative Chain Identity

$$ \boxed{ \log_a(b)\log_b(c) = \log_a(c) }. $$

Common Logarithm

The common logarithm has base $10$:

$$ \boxed{ \log_{10}(x) }. $$

Many textbooks and calculators abbreviate this as:

$$ \boxed{ \log(x) }. $$

Natural Logarithm

The natural logarithm has base:

$$ e\approx2.718281828\ldots $$

and is written:

$$ \boxed{ \ln(x)=\log_e(x) }. $$

Binary Logarithm

Base $2$ logarithms are common in computer science:

$$ \boxed{ \log_2(x) }. $$

Context-Dependent Bare Log

The notation:

$$ \log(x) $$

is context-dependent. It may mean base $10$, base $e$, or another conventional base depending on the field, textbook, software language, or application.

Base Choice and Scaling

Different logarithmic bases differ only by a constant scale factor:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(x) = \frac{1}{\ln b}\ln x }. $$

Thus changing the base does not fundamentally change logarithmic structure.

Solving Exponential Equations by Inspection

Consider:

$$ 2^x=32. $$

Since $32=2^5$:

$$ \boxed{ x=5 }. $$

Solving Exponential Equations with Logarithms

If:

$$ 3^x=17, $$

then:

$$ x=\log_3(17). $$

Using change of base:

$$ \boxed{ x = \frac{\ln(17)}{\ln(3)} }. $$

General Exponential Equation

If:

$$ a b^x=c $$

with admissible positive quantities, first isolate the exponential:

$$ b^x=\frac{c}{a}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ x = \log_b\left(\frac{c}{a}\right) }. $$

Solving Logarithmic Equations

If:

$$ \log_b(x)=c, $$

rewrite exponentially:

$$ \boxed{ x=b^c }. $$

Example

$$ \log_5(x)=3 $$

gives:

$$ \boxed{ x=125 }. $$

Equation with a Shifted Argument

Solve:

$$ \log_2(x-1)=4. $$

Exponential form gives:

$$ x-1=16, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ x=17 }. $$

Domain Check

The original logarithmic argument must be positive:

$$ x-1>0. $$

The candidate $17$ satisfies the restriction.

Extraneous Solutions

Algebraic transformations of logarithmic equations can produce candidates that violate an original logarithm's domain. Every candidate should therefore be checked.

Example of Domain Restriction

In:

$$ \log(x-3)+\log(x+1), $$

both arguments must be positive:

$$ x-3>0, \qquad x+1>0. $$

Together:

$$ \boxed{ x>3 }. $$

Logarithmic Inequalities with Base Greater Than One

If $b>1$:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(M) < \log_b(N) \iff M<N } $$

for positive $M$ and $N$.

Logarithmic Inequalities with Fractional Base

If:

$$ 0<b<1, $$

the logarithmic function decreases, so order reverses:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(M) < \log_b(N) \iff M>N }. $$

Derivative of the Natural Logarithm

$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}\ln(x) = \frac{1}{x}, \qquad x>0 }. $$

Derivative of a General Logarithm

$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}\log_b(x) = \frac{1}{x\ln b} }. $$

Chain Rule

If $u(x)>0$:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}\ln(u(x)) = \frac{u'(x)}{u(x)} }. $$

Integral

$$ \boxed{ \int\frac{1}{x}\,dx = \ln|x|+C }. $$

The absolute value permits the antiderivative on intervals where $x$ is negative as well as positive, even though the real function $\ln(x)$ itself requires $x>0$.

Integral Definition of Natural Logarithm

For $x>0$, one can define:

$$ \boxed{ \ln(x) = \int_1^x \frac{1}{t}\,dt }. $$

Natural Logarithm and $e$

The number $e$ is the unique positive base satisfying:

$$ \boxed{ \ln(e)=1 }. $$

Exponential Growth and Decay

If:

$$ y=y_0e^{kt}, $$

then logarithms can isolate time:

$$ \boxed{ t = \frac{1}{k} \ln\left(\frac{y}{y_0}\right) } $$

when the involved quantities are positive and $k\neq0$.

Doubling Time

In:

$$ y=y_0e^{kt}, \qquad k>0, $$

doubling time satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ t_d = \frac{\ln2}{k} }. $$

Half-Life

For exponential decay:

$$ \boxed{ t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln2}{|k|} }. $$

Compound Interest

From:

$$ A=P(1+r)^t, $$

logarithms solve for time:

$$ \boxed{ t = \frac{\ln(A/P)} {\ln(1+r)} }. $$

Continuous Compounding

If:

$$ A=Pe^{rt}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ t = \frac{\ln(A/P)}{r} }. $$

Logarithmic Scales

Logarithmic scales encode multiplicative ratios as additive distances.

Because:

$$ \log_b(MN) = \log_b(M)+\log_b(N), $$

multiplying a quantity by a fixed factor produces a fixed additive shift on a logarithmic scale.

Orders of Magnitude

Base-$10$ logarithms naturally measure decimal orders of magnitude.

If:

$$ x=10^n, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \log_{10}(x)=n }. $$

Scientific Notation

If:

$$ x=a\cdot10^n, \qquad 1\leq a<10, $$

then:

$$ \log_{10}(x) = n+\log_{10}(a). $$

Characteristic and Mantissa

In historical common-logarithm tables, the integer part was called the characteristic, while the fractional tabulated component was traditionally called the mantissa. Modern terminology often avoids this use of mantissa because the word has different technical meanings in computing.

Historical Computation

Before calculators, logarithm tables transformed:

$$ MN $$

into:

$$ \log(M)+\log(N), $$

allowing multiplication to be replaced by addition followed by an inverse logarithm.

Slide Rules

Slide rules physically exploit logarithmic spacing so that distances corresponding to logarithms add when scales are shifted.

Information Theory

Logarithms measure information because independent multiplicative possibilities become additive information quantities.

Bits

Base $2$ is natural when counting binary alternatives:

$$ \boxed{ \log_2(N) } $$

measures the number of binary decisions associated with $N$ equally likely possibilities in idealized settings.

Entropy

Shannon entropy uses logarithms:

$$ \boxed{ H = -\sum_i p_i\log_b(p_i) }. $$

The base determines the information unit.

Base Two Entropy

Base $2$ yields bits.

Natural-Log Entropy

Base $e$ yields nats.

Computer Science

Logarithmic complexity appears when a problem size is repeatedly multiplied, divided, doubled, or halved.

Binary Search

Repeatedly halving a search space leads to approximately:

$$ \boxed{ O(\log_2 n) } $$

comparisons.

Why Logarithms Count Repeated Division

If:

$$ \frac{n}{2^k}\approx1, $$

then:

$$ 2^k\approx n $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ k\approx\log_2(n) }. $$

Digit Count

For positive integer $n$, the number of base-$10$ digits is:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor\log_{10}(n)\rfloor+1 }. $$

General Base Digit Count

For integer base $b\geq2$:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor\log_b(n)\rfloor+1 } $$

gives the number of base-$b$ digits of positive integer $n$.

Logarithmic Differentiation

Taking logarithms can simplify differentiation of products, quotients, and variable powers.

Example

If:

$$ y=x^x, \qquad x>0, $$

then:

$$ \ln y=x\ln x. $$

Differentiate:

$$ \frac{y'}{y} = \ln x+1. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ y' = x^x(\ln x+1) }. $$

Power-Law Linearization

If:

$$ y=ax^k, \qquad a>0,\ x>0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \ln y = \ln a+k\ln x }. $$

Thus a power law becomes linear in log-log coordinates.

Exponential Linearization

If:

$$ y=Ae^{kx}, \qquad A>0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \ln y = \ln A+kx }. $$

Thus an exponential model becomes linear on a semilog plot.

Logarithms and Significant Ratios

Logarithms are especially useful when data span many orders of magnitude because multiplicative differences become additive.

Scientific Applications

Logarithmic or approximately logarithmic measures appear in fields involving sound, acidity, astronomical brightness, information, growth, decay, and scale comparison. Each application has its own conventions and should not be reduced to one universal logarithmic formula.

Complex Logarithm

Over the complex numbers, logarithms become more subtle because the complex exponential function is periodic.

If:

$$ z=re^{i\theta}, \qquad r>0, $$

then complex logarithms have values:

$$ \boxed{ \log z = \ln r + i(\theta+2\pi k), \qquad k\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Multivalued Nature

Since:

$$ e^{i(\theta+2\pi k)} = e^{i\theta}, $$

a nonzero complex number has infinitely many logarithmic values unless a branch is selected.

Principal Complex Logarithm

A principal branch is commonly defined by choosing a principal argument $\operatorname{Arg}(z)$:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Log}(z) = \ln|z| + i\operatorname{Arg}(z) }. $$

Branch Cuts

A continuous single-valued complex logarithm cannot be defined globally on all nonzero complex numbers. Branch cuts are introduced to select a continuous branch on a restricted domain.

Complex Logarithm Laws Need Care

Identities that are straightforward for positive real arguments may require branch qualifications over complex numbers.

Logarithm of Zero

In ordinary real and complex logarithmic functions:

$$ \boxed{ \log(0) \text{ is undefined} }. $$

For real logarithms, the limiting behavior as $x\to0^+$ may be $-\infty$, but $-\infty$ is not a real function value at zero.

Logarithm of a Negative Real Number

In the real-number system:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(-x) \text{ is not real for }x>0 }. $$

Complex logarithms extend the concept beyond this restriction.

Dimensionless Arguments

In mathematically careful physical modeling, the argument of a logarithm should be dimensionless, often achieved by taking a ratio of quantities with the same units.

$$ \boxed{ \ln\left(\frac{x}{x_0}\right) } $$

is dimensionless when $x$ and $x_0$ have the same dimensions.

Why Units Matter

Writing $\ln(5\text{ meters})$ without normalization is conceptually problematic because changing the measurement unit would alter the numerical argument.

Common Error: Treating a Logarithm as Division

The notation:

$$ \log_b(x) $$

does not mean $x/b$ or $b/x$. It asks for an exponent.

Common Error: Swapping Base and Argument

In:

$$ \log_2(8)=3, $$

the base is $2$ and the argument is $8$.

Common Error: Forgetting the Inverse Relationship

The most reliable translation is:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(x)=y \iff b^y=x }. $$

Common Error: Taking the Logarithm of a Nonpositive Real Argument

Real logarithmic expressions require every logarithm argument to be strictly positive.

Common Error: Allowing Base One

Base $1$ does not produce an invertible exponential function.

Common Error: Allowing a Nonpositive Real Base

The ordinary real logarithmic function requires $b>0$ and $b\neq1$.

Common Error: Applying the Product Rule to a Sum

The identity:

$$ \log_b(MN) = \log_b(M)+\log_b(N) $$

does not imply:

$$ \log_b(M+N) = \log_b(M)+\log_b(N). $$

Common Error: Applying the Quotient Rule to a Difference

A quotient is multiplicative structure; subtraction is not.

Common Error: Forgetting Parentheses

Write:

$$ \log_b(x+1) $$

when the entire expression $x+1$ is the argument.

Common Error: Confusing $\log_b(x^2)$ with $(\log_b x)^2$

These expressions mean different things:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(x^2) = 2\log_b(x) } $$

for $x>0$, whereas:

$$ (\log_b x)^2 $$

means the logarithm value itself is squared.

Common Error: Ignoring Domain after Condensing

Algebraic condensation can obscure the original requirement that every separate logarithmic argument be positive.

Common Error: Treating $-\infty$ as $\log(0)$

A limit may diverge to $-\infty$ as the argument approaches zero from the right, but the logarithm at zero remains undefined.

Common Error: Assuming Bare $\log$ Always Means Base Ten

Notation varies by discipline. The base should be inferred from context or stated explicitly when ambiguity matters.

Common Error: Assuming Logarithms Are Only a Calculator Technique

Logarithms are inverse functions with deep algebraic, analytic, computational, and modeling roles.

Common Misconception: A logarithm is not a separate mysterious kind of number operation unrelated to exponents. It is precisely the inverse question of exponentiation. The statement $\log_b(x)=y$ means exactly that $b^y=x$. This viewpoint explains the logarithm laws, the graph, the domain, exponential-equation solving, and the widespread appearance of logarithms in multiplicative and scale-based phenomena.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\log_b(x)$ "The logarithm of x to base b," or "log base b of x."
$\log_b(x)=y$ "Log base b of x equals y."
$b^y=x$ "B to the y power equals x."
$\log_b(MN)=\log_b(M)+\log_b(N)$ "The logarithm of a product is the sum of the logarithms."
$\log_b(M/N)=\log_b(M)-\log_b(N)$ "The logarithm of a quotient is the difference of the logarithms."
$\log_b(M^r)=r\log_b(M)$ "An exponent on the argument becomes a coefficient of the logarithm."
$\ln(x)$ "The natural logarithm of x," or "natural log of x."

The subscript in:

$$ \log_b(x) $$

identifies the base. It is not an exponent and is not multiplied by the logarithm.

Parentheses around the argument are strongly recommended when the argument contains more than a single symbol:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(x+3) } $$

rather than notation that might visually obscure where the logarithm's argument ends.

The notation log without an explicit base is field-dependent. In many school contexts it means base $10$; in much of higher mathematics it may mean the natural logarithm; and in computer science base $2$ may be understood in some asymptotic discussions because constant base changes differ only by a multiplicative factor.

Defense Notation Advice: When the base matters, write it explicitly. Keep the base and argument visually distinct, use parentheses around compound arguments, and record real-domain restrictions before manipulating logarithmic equations. Remember that product, quotient, and power structures have logarithm laws, while ordinary sums and differences do not.
