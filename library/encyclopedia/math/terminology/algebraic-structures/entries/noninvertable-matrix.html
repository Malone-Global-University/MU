Noninvertible Matrix

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

A noninvertible matrix is a square matrix that does not possess a multiplicative inverse. Equivalently, there is no matrix $B$ of the same size such that:

$$ \boxed{ AB=BA=I }. $$

The standard synonym is singular matrix.

$$ \boxed{ \text{noninvertible matrix} = \text{singular matrix} } $$

The word invertible comes from the idea of reversing an operation. An invertible matrix represents a linear transformation whose effect can be completely undone. The prefix non- indicates that such a reverse transformation does not exist.

The term singular historically came to distinguish matrices at exceptional parameter values where inversion fails. Modern linear algebra uses it as the standard technical term for a square matrix with determinant zero.

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ is noninvertible} \iff \det(A)=0 } $$

for square matrices over a field.

Operational Nuance

Definition

A square matrix $A$ is noninvertible if no matrix $A^{-1}$ exists satisfying:

$$ \boxed{ AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I }. $$

Singular Matrix

The most common formal term for a noninvertible square matrix is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{singular matrix} }. $$

Determinant Criterion

For an $n\times n$ matrix over a field:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ is noninvertible} \iff \det(A)=0 }. $$

Two-by-Two Example

Consider:

$$ A= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2\\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Its determinant is:

$$ \det(A) = 1(4)-2(2) = 0. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ is noninvertible} }. $$

Row Dependence

In the example above, the second row is twice the first:

$$ \begin{bmatrix}2 & 4\end{bmatrix} = 2 \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2\end{bmatrix}. $$

Thus the rows are linearly dependent.

Column Dependence

The columns of a singular square matrix are also linearly dependent.

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ noninvertible} \iff \text{columns of }A\text{ are linearly dependent} }. $$

Rank Criterion

If $A$ is $n\times n$, then:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ noninvertible} \iff \operatorname{rank}(A)<n }. $$

Full Rank vs. Rank Deficiency

An invertible $n\times n$ matrix has full rank $n$. A noninvertible matrix is rank deficient.

Null-Space Criterion

A square matrix is noninvertible exactly when its null space contains a nonzero vector:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ noninvertible} \iff \operatorname{Null}(A) \neq \{\mathbf{0}\} }. $$

Equivalent Homogeneous-System Criterion

The homogeneous system:

$$ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0} $$

has a nontrivial solution precisely when $A$ is noninvertible.

$$ \boxed{ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0} \text{ has some }\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{0} \iff A\text{ is singular} }. $$

Pivot Criterion

A square matrix is noninvertible when row reduction fails to produce a pivot in every row and every column.

$$ \boxed{ \text{fewer than }n\text{ pivots} \Longrightarrow A\text{ noninvertible} }. $$

RREF Criterion

For a square matrix:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ noninvertible} \iff \operatorname{rref}(A)\neq I_n }. $$

Zero Row after Reduction

A zero row in the reduced form of a square matrix signals rank deficiency and hence noninvertibility.

Eigenvalue Criterion

A square matrix is noninvertible exactly when zero is an eigenvalue:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ noninvertible} \iff 0\in\sigma(A) }. $$

Why Zero Eigenvalue Causes Failure

If:

$$ A\mathbf{v} = 0\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0} $$

for some nonzero $\mathbf{v}$, then $A$ maps a nonzero vector to zero. That loss of information cannot be reversed.

Geometric Meaning

A noninvertible linear transformation collapses at least one direction.

For example, a map from $\mathbb{R}^2$ to $\mathbb{R}^2$ may collapse the entire plane onto a line.

Dimension Collapse

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{rank}(A)<n } $$

means the image of the transformation has dimension smaller than the domain.

Example of Projection

Consider:

$$ P= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Then:

$$ P \begin{bmatrix} x\\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x\\ 0 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Every point is projected onto the $x$-axis. The original $y$-coordinate is lost, so no inverse transformation can recover it.

One-to-One Criterion

A square matrix is noninvertible exactly when its associated linear transformation is not one-to-one.

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ noninvertible} \iff T_A\text{ is not injective} }. $$

Onto Criterion

For a square matrix:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ noninvertible} \iff T_A\text{ is not surjective} }. $$

Bijectivity Criterion

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ invertible} \iff T_A\text{ bijective} } $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ noninvertible} \iff T_A\text{ not bijective} }. $$

Linear Systems

If $A$ is noninvertible, the equation:

$$ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b} $$

cannot have exactly one solution for every possible $\mathbf{b}$.

Possible Outcomes

Depending on $\mathbf{b}$, a singular system may have:

  • no solution;
  • infinitely many solutions.

No Unique Solution for Every Right-Hand Side

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ singular} \Longrightarrow \text{not every }A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b} \text{ has a unique solution} }. $$

Consistent Singular System

If a singular system is consistent and has at least one free variable, it has infinitely many solutions.

Inconsistent Singular System

A right-hand side outside the column space of $A$ produces no solution.

Column-Space Interpretation

The equation:

$$ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b} $$

is solvable exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{b}\in\operatorname{Col}(A) }. $$

For a singular square matrix, the column space is a proper subspace of the codomain.

Determinant as Volume Scaling

The absolute determinant:

$$ |\det(A)| $$

measures volume scaling for a linear transformation.

Zero Determinant

If:

$$ \det(A)=0, $$

then an $n$-dimensional region is collapsed to lower-dimensional volume:

$$ \boxed{ |\det(A)|=0 }. $$

Area Collapse in Two Dimensions

A singular $2\times2$ matrix maps a unit square to a parallelogram of area zero, meaning the image lies on a line or at a point.

Volume Collapse in Three Dimensions

A singular $3\times3$ matrix maps three-dimensional volume into a plane, line, or point.

Dependent Rows Criterion

$$ \boxed{ \text{rows linearly dependent} \iff A\text{ singular} } $$

for square matrices.

Dependent Columns Criterion

$$ \boxed{ \text{columns linearly dependent} \iff A\text{ singular} }. $$

Zero Row

A square matrix with a zero row is automatically noninvertible.

Zero Column

A square matrix with a zero column is automatically noninvertible.

Repeated Proportional Rows

If one row is a scalar multiple of another, the rows are dependent and the square matrix is singular.

Repeated Proportional Columns

The same principle applies to columns.

Triangular Matrix Criterion

A triangular square matrix is noninvertible exactly when at least one diagonal entry is zero.

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ triangular} \Longrightarrow \det(A) = \prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{ii} }. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ a_{ii}=0 \text{ for some }i \Longrightarrow A\text{ singular} }. $$

Diagonal Matrix Criterion

A diagonal matrix:

$$ D = \operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_n) $$

is noninvertible exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ d_i=0 \text{ for at least one }i }. $$

Product Criterion

For square matrices:

$$ \boxed{ \det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B) }. $$

Therefore, if either factor is singular:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ singular or }B\text{ singular} \Longrightarrow AB\text{ singular} }. $$

Converse over Square Matrices

If $AB$ is singular, then at least one of $A$ or $B$ is singular.

$$ \boxed{ AB\text{ singular} \Longrightarrow A\text{ singular or }B\text{ singular} }. $$

Power Criterion

If $A$ is singular, then every positive integer power of $A$ is singular:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ singular} \Longrightarrow A^k\text{ singular} }. $$

Transpose Criterion

Since:

$$ \det(A^T)=\det(A), $$

one has:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ singular} \iff A^T\text{ singular} }. $$

Similarity Preserves Singularity

If:

$$ B=P^{-1}AP $$

with $P$ invertible, then:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ singular} \iff B\text{ singular} }. $$

Characteristic Polynomial

Zero is an eigenvalue exactly when the characteristic polynomial vanishes at zero.

$$ \boxed{ p_A(0)=0 \iff A\text{ singular} } $$

up to the sign convention used in defining $p_A$.

Adjugate Identity

Every square matrix satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ A\operatorname{adj}(A) = \operatorname{adj}(A)A = \det(A)I }. $$

If $\det(A)=0$, this becomes:

$$ A\operatorname{adj}(A)=0. $$

Why the Inverse Formula Fails

The formula:

$$ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(A)} \operatorname{adj}(A) $$

requires:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A)\neq0 }. $$

If $\det(A)=0$, division by the determinant is impossible.

Cancellation Failure

For scalars, a nonzero factor can usually be cancelled. For matrices, a common matrix factor can be cancelled only when that matrix is invertible.

Left Cancellation

If $A$ is invertible:

$$ AB=AC \Longrightarrow B=C. $$

Failure for Noninvertible $A$

If $A$ is singular, it is possible to have:

$$ \boxed{ AB=AC \quad\text{while}\quad B\neq C }. $$

Zero Divisors

Nonzero square matrices can multiply to the zero matrix.

For example:

$$ A= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad B= \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0\\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ AB=0 } $$

even though $A\neq0$ and $B\neq0$.

Nonzero Zero Divisors Are Singular

If a nonzero square matrix $A$ satisfies $AB=0$ for some nonzero $B$, then $A$ cannot be invertible.

Projection Matrices

A nontrivial projection matrix $P$ satisfying:

$$ P^2=P $$

is generally noninvertible unless:

$$ \boxed{ P=I }. $$

Proof for Invertible Projection

If $P$ were invertible and $P^2=P$, multiplying by $P^{-1}$ gives:

$$ P=I. $$

Nilpotent Matrices

A nilpotent matrix satisfies:

$$ A^k=0 $$

for some positive integer $k$.

Every nilpotent matrix is noninvertible.

$$ \boxed{ A^k=0 \Longrightarrow A\text{ singular} }. $$

Idempotent Matrices

A nonidentity idempotent square matrix is noninvertible:

$$ A^2=A, \qquad A\neq I \Longrightarrow A\text{ singular}. $$

Singular Values

A square matrix is noninvertible exactly when its smallest singular value is zero:

$$ \boxed{ \sigma_{\min}(A)=0 }. $$

Condition Number

The usual norm-based condition number:

$$ \kappa(A) = \|A\| \|A^{-1}\| $$

is not finite for a singular matrix because $A^{-1}$ does not exist.

In the standard extended convention:

$$ \boxed{ \kappa(A)=\infty } $$

for singular $A$.

Nearly Singular Matrix

A matrix can be invertible but close to singular. Such a matrix may have a very small smallest singular value and a very large condition number.

Noninvertible vs. Nearly Noninvertible

These are different concepts:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A)=0 \Longrightarrow \text{exactly singular} } $$

whereas numerical near-singularity concerns sensitivity rather than exact failure of invertibility.

Numerical Detection

In floating-point computation, deciding whether a matrix is effectively singular should not rely only on testing whether a computed determinant equals exactly zero. Rank-revealing factorizations and singular values are often more informative.

Pseudoinverse

A noninvertible matrix may still have a Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse:

$$ \boxed{ A^+ }. $$

The pseudoinverse is not an ordinary inverse.

Pseudoinverse Use

It is useful for least-squares solutions, minimum-norm solutions, and systems where ordinary inversion fails.

Least-Squares Interpretation

For appropriate systems:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x}=A^+\mathbf{b} } $$

provides a canonical generalized solution even when $A^{-1}$ does not exist.

Rectangular Matrices

The term noninvertible matrix is most precise when applied to a square matrix that fails to have an inverse.

A rectangular matrix does not possess an ordinary two-sided inverse because its dimensions prevent both products from being identity matrices of the required sizes.

One-Sided Inverses

Rectangular matrices can sometimes possess left or right inverses.

A tall full-column-rank matrix can have a left inverse, while a wide full-row-rank matrix can have a right inverse.

Ordinary Two-Sided Inverse

The conventional statement:

$$ \boxed{ A^{-1}\text{ exists} } $$

usually refers to a square matrix with a two-sided inverse.

Invertible Matrix Theorem

For an $n\times n$ matrix over a field, the following conditions are equivalent:

  • $A$ is invertible;
  • $\det(A)\neq0$;
  • $\operatorname{rank}(A)=n$;
  • $\operatorname{Null}(A)=\{\mathbf{0}\}$;
  • the columns are linearly independent;
  • the rows are linearly independent;
  • the columns span the codomain;
  • $A$ has a pivot in every row and column;
  • $\operatorname{rref}(A)=I_n$;
  • $0$ is not an eigenvalue;
  • $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ has a unique solution for every $\mathbf{b}$.

Negating the Invertible Matrix Theorem

A noninvertible matrix fails all of those equivalent invertibility conditions.

Determinant Zero

$$ \boxed{ \det(A)=0 }. $$

Rank Deficiency

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{rank}(A)<n }. $$

Nontrivial Kernel

$$ \boxed{ \ker(A)\neq\{\mathbf{0}\} }. $$

Linear Dependence

$$ \boxed{ \text{columns dependent} }. $$

Zero Eigenvalue

$$ \boxed{ 0\text{ is an eigenvalue} }. $$

Loss of Information

These equivalent descriptions all express the same structural problem: the matrix transformation merges distinct input vectors or collapses a direction.

Example of Distinct Inputs with Same Output

If $\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}$ lies in the null space, then for any $\mathbf{x}$:

$$ A(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{v}) = A\mathbf{x}+A\mathbf{v} = A\mathbf{x}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{x}+\mathbf{v} \quad\text{but}\quad A\mathbf{x} = A(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{v}) }. $$

The output cannot reveal which input was used.

Change-of-Basis Matrices

A valid change-of-basis matrix must be invertible. A noninvertible matrix cannot represent a genuine basis change because its columns cannot form a basis.

Coordinate Transformations

A singular matrix can represent a transformation, but not a reversible coordinate transformation.

Computer Graphics

A transformation that scales one coordinate by zero is singular because it flattens space along that direction.

Example: Flattening Transformation

$$ S= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}. $$

This transformation cannot be reversed because all original $y$-coordinates are discarded.

Statistics

Singular covariance or design-related matrices can arise from exact linear dependence among variables or predictors.

Regression

In ordinary least squares, if columns of a design matrix are linearly dependent, the normal-equation matrix:

$$ X^TX $$

is singular, so the usual inverse formula cannot be applied directly.

Multicollinearity

Exact multicollinearity can cause noninvertibility; near multicollinearity can cause ill conditioning.

Optimization

A singular Hessian cannot be inverted directly in a Newton step requiring $H^{-1}$.

Differential Equations

Singular coefficient matrices can indicate constraints, nonunique algebraic relations, or systems requiring techniques beyond ordinary inversion.

Markov and Stochastic Matrices

A stochastic matrix may be invertible or noninvertible. Stochasticity alone does not determine invertibility.

Adjacency Matrices

A graph adjacency matrix may be singular or nonsingular depending on the graph. Singularity is an algebraic property, not a defining property of adjacency matrices.

Common Error: Assuming Every Square Matrix Has an Inverse

Square shape is necessary for the standard two-sided inverse but does not guarantee invertibility.

Common Error: Assuming Nonzero Entries Guarantee Invertibility

A matrix can have no zero entries and still be singular.

Example:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2\\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Common Error: Assuming a Zero Entry Makes a Matrix Singular

Many invertible matrices contain zero entries.

For example:

$$ I= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

is invertible.

Common Error: Taking Entrywise Reciprocals

Matrix inversion is not performed by replacing each entry $a_{ij}$ with $1/a_{ij}$.

Common Error: Dividing by the Matrix

There is no general scalar-style operation:

$$ \frac{1}{A} $$

for a singular matrix. The notation $A^{-1}$ is reserved for an existing matrix inverse.

Common Error: Assuming Determinant Zero Means the Matrix Is the Zero Matrix

A singular matrix may contain many nonzero entries.

Common Error: Assuming Dependent Rows Must Be Identical

Linear dependence can involve any nontrivial linear combination, not merely exact repetition.

Common Error: Assuming Singular Systems Always Have No Solution

Singular systems may have no solutions or infinitely many solutions depending on the right-hand side.

Common Error: Assuming Singular Systems Have Exactly One Solution

A square singular coefficient matrix cannot produce a unique solution for every right-hand side, and a consistent system with a singular square coefficient matrix has nonuniqueness whenever free variables remain.

Common Error: Cancelling a Singular Matrix

From:

$$ AB=AC, $$

one cannot cancel $A$ unless $A$ is invertible.

Common Error: Assuming $AB=0$ Forces $A=0$ or $B=0$

Matrix rings contain nonzero zero divisors.

Common Error: Confusing Singular with Ill-Conditioned

A singular matrix is exactly noninvertible. An ill-conditioned matrix may still be invertible.

Common Error: Using a Tiny Determinant as the Sole Numerical Test

Determinant magnitude depends on scaling and dimension. Singular values and rank-revealing methods are generally better numerical diagnostics.

Common Error: Confusing Pseudoinverse with Inverse

A singular matrix may possess $A^+$ even though $A^{-1}$ does not exist.

Common Error: Calling Every Rectangular Matrix Singular

Singular is conventionally reserved for square matrices. Rectangular matrices are more precisely described by their rank or by whether they possess one-sided inverses.

Common Error: Assuming Zero Eigenvalue Is Irrelevant

Zero as an eigenvalue is exactly one of the defining signals of singularity.

Common Error: Forgetting Geometric Information Loss

Noninvertibility is not merely a failed formula. It means the transformation loses enough information that the original input cannot be uniquely recovered.

Common Misconception: A noninvertible matrix is not merely a matrix for which an inverse is inconvenient to calculate. It is a square matrix for which no ordinary inverse exists at all. The failure can be recognized through many equivalent conditions: determinant zero, deficient rank, dependent columns, nontrivial null space, zero eigenvalue, missing pivots, and loss of injectivity. These are different descriptions of the same structural collapse.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$A^{-1}$ does not exist "A is noninvertible," or "A is singular."
$\det(A)=0$ "The determinant of A is zero, so A is singular."
$\operatorname{rank}(A)<n$ "A is rank deficient."
$\ker(A)\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}$ "A has a nontrivial null space."
$A\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}$ with $\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}$ "A maps a nonzero vector to zero."
$0\in\sigma(A)$ "Zero is an eigenvalue of A."
$A^+$ "The Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of A," not the ordinary inverse.

The terms noninvertible and singular are usually interchangeable for square matrices.

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A)=0 } $$

is one of the fastest symbolic ways to communicate singularity when the determinant is available.

In computational contexts, one may instead say that the matrix is rank deficient or has a zero singular value. These descriptions emphasize different manifestations of the same structural failure.

Defense Notation Advice: Reserve $A^{-1}$ for a genuine ordinary inverse. Do not write an inverse formula after establishing $\det(A)=0$. For square matrices, "singular" is the standard concise term. For rectangular matrices, prefer rank language or specify whether a left inverse, right inverse, or pseudoinverse is intended. When solving systems, distinguish "no inverse" from "no solution": a singular coefficient matrix can produce either no solution or infinitely many, depending on the right-hand side.
