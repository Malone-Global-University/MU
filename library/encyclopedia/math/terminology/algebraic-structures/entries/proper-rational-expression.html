Proper Rational Expression

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

A proper rational expression is a rational expression whose numerator has lower polynomial degree than its denominator.

$$ \boxed{ \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} \text{ is proper} \iff \deg P<\deg Q } $$

where $P(x)$ and $Q(x)$ are polynomials and:

$$ \boxed{ Q(x)\neq0 } $$

as a polynomial.

The term combines three pieces of mathematical language:

  • rational, meaning formed as a quotient of polynomials;
  • expression, indicating a symbolic algebraic object;
  • proper, indicating that the numerator degree is strictly smaller than the denominator degree.

For example:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{x+3}{x^2-4} } $$

is proper because:

$$ \deg(x+3)=1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \deg(x^2-4)=2. $$

By contrast:

$$ \frac{x^3+1}{x^2-4} $$

is improper because:

$$ 3>2. $$

The terminology is deliberately analogous to proper fraction. In an ordinary positive numerical fraction, properness compares the magnitude of the numerator with the magnitude of the denominator. For rational expressions, numerical size is not fixed because the variable changes, so polynomial degree replaces numerical magnitude as the structural comparison.

$$ \boxed{ \text{proper fraction: numerator smaller} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{proper rational expression: numerator degree smaller} }. $$

Operational Nuance

General Form

A rational expression has form:

$$ \boxed{ R(x) = \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} } $$

where $P$ and $Q$ are polynomials and $Q$ is not the zero polynomial.

Properness Criterion

$$ \boxed{ \deg P<\deg Q }. $$

Improperness Criterion

A rational expression is improper when:

$$ \boxed{ \deg P\geq\deg Q }. $$

Example of a Proper Rational Expression

$$ \boxed{ \frac{2x^2+3x-1}{x^4+5} } $$

is proper because:

$$ 2<4. $$

Example with Equal Degrees

$$ \frac{3x^2+1}{x^2-7} $$

is improper because:

$$ \boxed{ \deg P=\deg Q=2 }. $$

Example with Greater Numerator Degree

$$ \frac{x^5+2}{x^3-x} $$

is improper because:

$$ \boxed{ 5>3 }. $$

Degree Is Determined after Combining Like Terms

Consider:

$$ \frac{x^3-x^3+x}{x^2+1}. $$

The numerator simplifies to:

$$ x. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{x}{x^2+1} } $$

is proper.

Zero Numerator

The zero rational expression:

$$ \frac{0}{Q(x)} $$

requires a convention because the zero polynomial has no ordinary finite degree. In practice, it behaves as a proper rational expression relative to any nonzero denominator polynomial, although texts may handle the classification separately.

Constant Numerator

A nonzero constant polynomial has degree $0$. Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{5}{x^2+1} } $$

is proper because:

$$ 0<2. $$

Constant Denominator

A rational expression with nonzero constant denominator cannot be proper unless the numerator is the zero polynomial, because a nonzero polynomial numerator has degree at least $0$.

Properness Depends on Polynomial Degree, Not Coefficient Size

The expression:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{1000000x}{x^2+1} } $$

is proper even though the numerator has a very large coefficient.

Properness Does Not Mean the Numerical Value Is Less than One

A proper rational expression can evaluate to a number greater than $1$ at some allowed input.

For example:

$$ R(x) = \frac{10x}{x^2+1}. $$

It is proper because:

$$ 1<2, $$

but:

$$ \boxed{ R(1)=5 }. $$

Structural, Not Pointwise, Classification

Properness describes the degrees of the numerator and denominator polynomials, not the pointwise inequality:

$$ |P(x)|<|Q(x)|. $$

Domain Restrictions

A rational expression:

$$ \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} $$

is undefined where:

$$ \boxed{ Q(x)=0 }. $$

Properness Does Not Remove Domain Restrictions

For:

$$ \frac{x}{x^2-1}, $$

the expression is proper, but its real domain excludes:

$$ \boxed{ x=\pm1 }. $$

Factorized Denominator

Factoring helps expose domain restrictions:

$$ x^2-1 = (x-1)(x+1). $$

Cancellation and Original Restrictions

Consider:

$$ \frac{x^2-1}{(x-1)(x^2+1)}. $$

Since:

$$ x^2-1 = (x-1)(x+1), $$

the formula simplifies for $x\neq1$ to:

$$ \frac{x+1}{x^2+1}. $$

The simplified expression is proper, but if the original rational function is being preserved, the original exclusion:

$$ \boxed{ x\neq1 } $$

remains part of its domain.

Properness before and after Cancellation

Degree classification should normally be applied to the rational expression in the form relevant to the task. When cancellation changes numerator and denominator degrees, distinguish the original expression from the reduced rational function.

Improper Rational Expression and Polynomial Division

If:

$$ \deg P\geq\deg Q, $$

polynomial division can rewrite:

$$ \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} $$

as:

$$ \boxed{ S(x) + \frac{R(x)}{Q(x)} } $$

where:

$$ \boxed{ \deg R<\deg Q }. $$

Polynomial Division Theorem

Over a field:

$$ \boxed{ P(x) = Q(x)S(x)+R(x) } $$

with either $R=0$ or:

$$ \deg R<\deg Q. $$

Rational-Expression Form

Dividing by $Q(x)$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} = S(x) + \frac{R(x)}{Q(x)} }. $$

The remainder fraction is proper.

Example

Divide:

$$ \frac{x^2+3x+5}{x+1}. $$

Polynomial division gives:

$$ x^2+3x+5 = (x+1)(x+2)+3. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{x^2+3x+5}{x+1} = x+2 + \frac{3}{x+1} }. $$

The final fractional part is proper because:

$$ 0<1. $$

Analogy with Mixed Numbers

Polynomial division plays a role analogous to converting an improper numerical fraction into a whole-number part plus a proper fraction.

$$ \boxed{ \frac{17}{5} = 3+\frac{2}{5} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \frac{P}{Q} = S+\frac{R}{Q} }. $$

Partial Fraction Decomposition

Standard partial-fraction decomposition begins most naturally with a proper rational expression.

Why Properness Matters for Partial Fractions

If a rational expression is improper, polynomial division is performed first so that only the proper remainder fraction is decomposed.

Example

$$ \frac{3x+5}{(x-1)(x+2)} $$

is proper because the numerator degree is $1$ and the denominator degree is $2$.

Distinct Linear Factors

One may write:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{3x+5}{(x-1)(x+2)} = \frac{A}{x-1} + \frac{B}{x+2} }. $$

Repeated Linear Factors

For:

$$ \frac{P(x)}{(x-a)^m}, $$

decomposition may require:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{A_1}{x-a} + \frac{A_2}{(x-a)^2} + \cdots + \frac{A_m}{(x-a)^m} }. $$

Irreducible Quadratic Factors over the Reals

A factor:

$$ ax^2+bx+c $$

that is irreducible over $\mathbb{R}$ requires a linear numerator:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{Ax+B}{ax^2+bx+c} }. $$

Field Dependence

Partial-fraction factorization depends on the coefficient field. A quadratic irreducible over $\mathbb{R}$ may split into linear factors over $\mathbb{C}$.

Asymptotic Behavior

For:

$$ R(x) = \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} $$

with:

$$ \deg P<\deg Q, $$

we have:

$$ \boxed{ R(x)\to0 \quad \text{as } x\to\pm\infty } $$

over the real numbers.

Horizontal Asymptote

A proper real rational function therefore has:

$$ \boxed{ y=0 } $$

as its horizontal asymptote at both ends, provided the function is considered over its natural real domain.

Why the Limit Is Zero

Let:

$$ \deg P=m, \qquad \deg Q=n, \qquad m<n. $$

For large $|x|$, the leading terms dominate:

$$ \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} \sim \frac{a_mx^m}{b_nx^n} = \frac{a_m}{b_n} x^{m-n}. $$

Since:

$$ m-n<0, $$

the factor $x^{m-n}$ tends to $0$ in magnitude.

Equal-Degree Contrast

If:

$$ \deg P=\deg Q, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} \to \frac{\text{leading coefficient of }P} {\text{leading coefficient of }Q} } $$

as $|x|\to\infty$, assuming nonzero leading coefficients.

Higher Numerator Degree

If the numerator degree exceeds the denominator degree, polynomial division identifies the polynomial asymptotic part.

Oblique Asymptote Case

If:

$$ \deg P = \deg Q+1, $$

the quotient from polynomial division is linear, often producing an oblique asymptote.

Properness and Infinity

Proper rational expressions are therefore characterized not only algebraically by degree but also analytically by decay toward zero at large magnitude.

Rate of Decay

If the degree difference is:

$$ n-m=k>0, $$

then asymptotically:

$$ \boxed{ R(x) = O \left( \frac{1}{x^k} \right) } $$

under ordinary real asymptotic assumptions.

Example of First-Order Decay

$$ \frac{x+1}{x^2+1} \sim \frac{1}{x}. $$

Example of Second-Order Decay

$$ \frac{3x+1}{x^3+2} \sim \frac{3}{x^2}. $$

Vertical Asymptotes

Properness alone does not determine vertical asymptotes. They depend on denominator zeros that remain after relevant cancellation.

Example

$$ \boxed{ \frac{1}{x-2} } $$

is proper and has a vertical asymptote at:

$$ \boxed{ x=2 }. $$

Hole vs. Vertical Asymptote

If a common factor cancels, the corresponding denominator zero may produce a hole rather than a vertical asymptote in the reduced function.

Example

$$ \frac{x-1}{(x-1)(x+2)} = \frac{1}{x+2} $$

for $x\neq1$.

The original rational function has a removable discontinuity at $x=1$ and a vertical asymptote at $x=-2$.

Properness and Integration

Proper rational expressions are especially suitable for integration by partial fractions once the denominator is factored over the chosen field.

Example

$$ \int \frac{1}{x^2-1}\,dx $$

begins with:

$$ \frac{1}{x^2-1} = \frac{1}{(x-1)(x+1)}. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{1}{x^2-1} = \frac{1}{2} \frac{1}{x-1} - \frac{1}{2} \frac{1}{x+1} }. $$

Integration Result

$$ \boxed{ \int \frac{1}{x^2-1}\,dx = \frac{1}{2}\ln|x-1| - \frac{1}{2}\ln|x+1| + C } $$

on intervals avoiding the denominator zeros.

Rational Functions

A proper rational expression determines a proper rational function once a domain is specified.

Expression vs. Function

The expression:

$$ \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} $$

is symbolic. The rational function additionally includes a domain and input-output interpretation.

Natural Domain over the Reals

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(R) = \{ x\in\mathbb{R}:Q(x)\neq0 \} }. $$

Natural Domain over the Complex Numbers

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(R) = \{ z\in\mathbb{C}:Q(z)\neq0 \} }. $$

Reduced Rational Expression

A rational expression is reduced when numerator and denominator share no nonconstant polynomial factor over the chosen coefficient field.

Proper and Reduced Are Different Properties

The expression:

$$ \frac{x^2-1}{x^3-x} $$

is proper because:

$$ 2<3, $$

but it is not reduced because:

$$ x^2-1 = (x-1)(x+1) $$

and:

$$ x^3-x = x(x-1)(x+1). $$

Reduced Form

Where cancellation is valid:

$$ \frac{x^2-1}{x^3-x} = \boxed{ \frac{1}{x} } $$

with the original exclusions retained if discussing the original rational function.

Properness and Multiplication

The product of two proper rational expressions is generally proper when their numerator and denominator polynomials are nonzero.

If:

$$ \deg P_1<\deg Q_1 $$

and:

$$ \deg P_2<\deg Q_2, $$

then over an integral domain:

$$ \deg(P_1P_2) = \deg P_1+\deg P_2 $$

and:

$$ \deg(Q_1Q_2) = \deg Q_1+\deg Q_2, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ \deg(P_1P_2) < \deg(Q_1Q_2) }. $$

Properness under Addition

The sum of two proper rational expressions need not be analyzed term-by-term using only their original degrees because common denominators and cancellation can alter the final form.

However, if both have the same denominator $Q$ and numerator degrees below $\deg Q$, then:

$$ \frac{P_1}{Q} + \frac{P_2}{Q} = \frac{P_1+P_2}{Q} $$

remains proper unless numerator cancellation produces the zero polynomial, which is still harmless under the usual convention.

Reciprocal

The reciprocal of a nonzero proper rational expression:

$$ \frac{P}{Q} $$

is:

$$ \frac{Q}{P}. $$

Since $\deg Q>\deg P$, the reciprocal is ordinarily improper.

Analogy with Numerical Proper Fractions

This parallels the fact that the reciprocal of a positive proper numerical fraction is greater than $1$.

Continued Fractions

Rational functions can be expanded using polynomial analogues of the Euclidean algorithm, producing continued-fraction structures in which proper rational remainders arise naturally.

Extended Euclidean Algorithm

Repeated polynomial division uses successively proper remainder ratios and is central to gcd computations in polynomial rings over fields.

Control Theory

In systems and control, a transfer function:

$$ G(s) = \frac{N(s)}{D(s)} $$

is commonly called proper when:

$$ \boxed{ \deg N\leq\deg D }. $$

Important Terminology Difference

Engineering usage often differs from elementary algebraic terminology.

In control theory:

$$ \boxed{ \deg N\leq\deg D } $$

is commonly called proper, while:

$$ \boxed{ \deg N<\deg D } $$

is called strictly proper.

Algebra Textbook Convention

In elementary algebra and partial-fractions contexts, proper rational expression usually means:

$$ \boxed{ \deg P<\deg Q }. $$

Field-Variation Warning

Because terminology changes by field, a definition should be checked whenever the phrase appears in engineering, systems theory, or applied analysis.

Strictly Proper Terminology

In contexts that distinguish proper from strictly proper:

$$ \boxed{ \deg P<\deg Q } $$

is called strictly proper.

Biproper or Equal-Degree Case

Some engineering literature uses specialized terminology for rational functions whose numerator and denominator have equal degree. Vocabulary varies, so the degree relation is more reliable than the label alone.

Common Error: Comparing Polynomial Values Instead of Degrees

Properness does not require:

$$ |P(x)|<|Q(x)| $$

for every input.

Common Error: Looking at the Number of Terms

Properness depends on degree, not on how many terms appear.

For example:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{x^3}{x^4+x^3+x^2+x+1} } $$

is proper despite the numerator having only one term and the denominator having five.

Common Error: Comparing Leading Coefficients

The sizes or signs of leading coefficients do not determine properness.

Common Error: Treating Equal Degrees as Proper

Under the standard algebraic definition:

$$ \boxed{ \deg P=\deg Q } $$

means the rational expression is improper.

Common Error: Applying the Control-Theory Convention to an Algebra Text

Engineering may call equal-degree transfer functions proper. Elementary algebra normally does not.

Common Error: Forgetting to Simplify the Polynomial before Determining Degree

Leading terms may cancel.

Common Error: Ignoring the Zero Polynomial

Degree of the zero polynomial requires separate treatment, so zero numerators are an edge case in formal definitions.

Common Error: Assuming Proper Means Reduced

A rational expression can be proper while numerator and denominator share factors.

Common Error: Assuming Reduced Means Proper

The expression:

$$ \frac{x^3+1}{x+2} $$

may be reduced but is still improper because the numerator degree exceeds the denominator degree.

Common Error: Canceling Terms Instead of Factors

From:

$$ \frac{x+1}{x}, $$

one cannot cancel the $x$ appearing as only part of the numerator sum.

Cancellation Requires Factors

$$ \boxed{ \frac{x(x+1)}{x} = x+1 } $$

for $x\neq0$ because the numerator contains $x$ as a factor.

Common Error: Forgetting Original Domain Restrictions after Cancellation

Simplification may remove a visible denominator factor without restoring an input excluded by the original expression.

Common Error: Performing Partial Fractions on an Improper Expression before Division

First separate the polynomial quotient, then decompose the proper remainder.

Common Error: Assuming Every Proper Rational Expression Has Only Linear Factors

Denominators may contain irreducible quadratic or higher-degree factors depending on the coefficient field.

Common Error: Assuming Properness Guarantees No Vertical Asymptotes

Properness controls degree at infinity, not denominator zeros at finite inputs.

Common Error: Assuming Properness Guarantees No Holes

Common factors can create removable discontinuities.

Common Error: Assuming Proper Rational Functions Are Always Less than One

Their values may be arbitrarily large near poles.

Common Error: Assuming Horizontal Asymptote $y=0$ Means the Graph Never Crosses the Axis

A horizontal asymptote describes end behavior and may be crossed.

Common Error: Assuming a Rational Expression Equal to Zero Must Be Improper

The zero numerator is a special degree case and does not violate the structural intuition of properness.

Common Error: Treating Proper Rational Expression and Proper Fraction as Identical Definitions

The analogy is useful, but numerical fractions compare integer magnitudes while rational expressions compare polynomial degrees.

Common Misconception: A proper rational expression is not one whose numerical value is always between $-1$ and $1$. Properness is an algebraic degree condition:

$$ \boxed{ \deg P<\deg Q }. $$

A proper rational function may exceed $1$, become negative, or grow arbitrarily large near a denominator zero. What properness guarantees in the standard polynomial setting is that the denominator has the higher degree and therefore dominates the numerator as $|x|$ becomes large.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}$ "P of x over Q of x."
$\deg P<\deg Q$ "The degree of the numerator is less than the degree of the denominator."
$\deg P\geq\deg Q$ "The rational expression is improper under the standard algebra convention."
$P=QS+R$ "P equals Q times the quotient polynomial S plus the remainder R."
$\frac{P}{Q}=S+\frac{R}{Q}$ "The improper rational expression is written as a polynomial plus a proper rational remainder."
$\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}=0$ "The proper rational function approaches zero as x approaches infinity."
$Q(x)=0$ "The denominator is zero, so the rational expression is undefined at that input."

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"proper rational expression"} } $$

in elementary algebra ordinarily means:

$$ \boxed{ \deg(\text{numerator}) < \deg(\text{denominator}) }. $$

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"make the rational expression proper"} } $$

usually means perform polynomial division until the remaining fractional part has numerator degree below denominator degree.

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"strictly proper"} } $$

is especially common in engineering and control theory for the same strict degree inequality.

Defense Notation Advice: Compare polynomial degrees only after combining like terms and identifying the true leading nonzero terms. Do not classify properness by numerical values, coefficient size, or number of terms. Distinguish proper from reduced, preserve domain restrictions after cancellation, and check the field-specific convention when working in control theory or engineering, where equal-degree rational functions may also be called proper.

  • Core Interpretation: A proper rational expression is a quotient of polynomials whose denominator has strictly greater degree: $$ \boxed{ \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} \text{ proper} \iff \deg P<\deg Q }. $$ This degree condition makes the denominator dominate at large magnitude: $$ \boxed{ \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} \to0 \quad \text{as } |x|\to\infty }. $$ Improper rational expressions can be converted by polynomial division into a polynomial plus a proper remainder fraction: $$ \boxed{ \frac{P}{Q} = S+\frac{R}{Q}, \qquad \deg R<\deg Q }. $$ Properness is therefore central to polynomial division, rational-function end behavior, partial fractions, integration, and the structural distinction between numerator and denominator degree.
