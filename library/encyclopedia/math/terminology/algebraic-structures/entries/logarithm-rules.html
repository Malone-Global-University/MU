Logarithm Rules

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

Logarithm rules are algebraic identities that govern how logarithms interact with products, quotients, powers, roots, reciprocals, and changes of base. They arise directly from the corresponding exponent rules.

The central relationship is:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(x)=y \iff b^y=x } $$

under the ordinary real-valued conditions:

$$ \boxed{ b>0, \qquad b\neq1, \qquad x>0 }. $$

Because multiplication of equal bases adds exponents:

$$ b^m b^n = b^{m+n}, $$

logarithms turn multiplication into addition:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(MN) = \log_b(M)+\log_b(N) }. $$

Likewise, because division subtracts exponents and exponentiation multiplies exponents, logarithms convert quotients into differences and powers into scalar multiples.

$$ \boxed{ \text{multiplication} \longrightarrow \text{addition} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{division} \longrightarrow \text{subtraction} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{power} \longrightarrow \text{coefficient} }. $$

Historically, this transformation of difficult multiplicative calculations into additive ones was one of the principal reasons logarithms became such powerful computational tools.

Operational Nuance

Real-Logarithm Conditions

Unless otherwise stated, the standard logarithm rules below assume:

$$ \boxed{ b>0, \qquad b\neq1 } $$

and every logarithmic argument is positive.

Product Rule

For $M>0$ and $N>0$:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(MN) = \log_b(M)+\log_b(N) }. $$

Derivation of the Product Rule

Let:

$$ \log_b(M)=m \qquad\text{and}\qquad \log_b(N)=n. $$

Then:

$$ M=b^m, \qquad N=b^n. $$

Therefore:

$$ MN = b^m b^n = b^{m+n}. $$

Taking the base-$b$ logarithm gives:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(MN) = m+n = \log_b(M)+\log_b(N) }. $$

Product Rule Example

$$ \log_2(8\cdot4) = \log_2(8)+\log_2(4) = 3+2 = \boxed{5}. $$

Quotient Rule

For $M>0$ and $N>0$:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b\left(\frac{M}{N}\right) = \log_b(M)-\log_b(N) }. $$

Derivation of the Quotient Rule

If:

$$ M=b^m, \qquad N=b^n, $$

then:

$$ \frac{M}{N} = \frac{b^m}{b^n} = b^{m-n}. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b\left(\frac{M}{N}\right) = m-n }. $$

Quotient Rule Example

$$ \log_3\left(\frac{81}{3}\right) = \log_3(81)-\log_3(3) = 4-1 = \boxed{3}. $$

Power Rule

For $M>0$ and real $r$:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(M^r) = r\log_b(M) }. $$

Derivation of the Power Rule

If:

$$ M=b^m, $$

then:

$$ M^r = (b^m)^r = b^{mr}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(M^r) = mr = r\log_b(M) }. $$

Power Rule Example

$$ \log_2(8^4) = 4\log_2(8) = 4(3) = \boxed{12}. $$

Root Rule

Because:

$$ \sqrt[n]{M} = M^{1/n}, $$

the power rule gives:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b\left(\sqrt[n]{M}\right) = \frac{1}{n}\log_b(M) }. $$

Square-Root Example

$$ \log_3(\sqrt{81}) = \frac{1}{2}\log_3(81) = \frac{1}{2}(4) = \boxed{2}. $$

Reciprocal Rule

Since:

$$ \frac{1}{M} = M^{-1}, $$

the power rule gives:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b\left(\frac{1}{M}\right) = -\log_b(M) }. $$

Reciprocal Example

$$ \log_{10}(0.001) = \log_{10}(10^{-3}) = \boxed{-3}. $$

Logarithm of One

Since $b^0=1$:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(1)=0 }. $$

Logarithm of the Base

Since $b^1=b$:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(b)=1 }. $$

Inverse Rule: Logarithm after Exponentiation

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(b^x)=x }. $$

Inverse Rule: Exponentiation after Logarithm

For $x>0$:

$$ \boxed{ b^{\log_b(x)}=x }. $$

Why the Inverse Rules Work

Logarithm and exponentiation with the same admissible base are inverse functions.

$$ \boxed{ b^x=y \iff \log_b(y)=x }. $$

Change-of-Base Rule

For admissible bases $a$ and $b$:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(x) = \frac{\log_a(x)} {\log_a(b)} }. $$

Natural-Log Version

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(x) = \frac{\ln(x)} {\ln(b)} }. $$

Common-Log Version

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(x) = \frac{\log(x)} {\log(b)} } $$

when $\log$ denotes the common logarithm.

Derivation of Change of Base

Let:

$$ y=\log_b(x). $$

Then:

$$ b^y=x. $$

Take logarithms to base $a$:

$$ \log_a(b^y) = \log_a(x). $$

Apply the power rule:

$$ y\log_a(b) = \log_a(x). $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ y = \frac{\log_a(x)} {\log_a(b)} }. $$

Reciprocal Base Rule

$$ \boxed{ \log_a(b) = \frac{1}{\log_b(a)} }. $$

Proof of Reciprocal Base Rule

From change of base:

$$ \log_a(b) = \frac{\ln b}{\ln a} $$

and:

$$ \log_b(a) = \frac{\ln a}{\ln b}. $$

Their product is:

$$ \boxed{ \log_a(b)\log_b(a)=1 }. $$

Chain Rule for Bases

$$ \boxed{ \log_a(b)\log_b(c) = \log_a(c) }. $$

Longer Logarithmic Chain

$$ \boxed{ \log_a(b) \log_b(c) \log_c(d) = \log_a(d) }. $$

Base-Power Rule

For suitable positive bases:

$$ \boxed{ \log_{b^k}(x) = \frac{1}{k}\log_b(x) } $$

when $k\neq0$ and $b^k\neq1$.

Argument and Base Powered Together

Under admissible conditions:

$$ \boxed{ \log_{b^k}(x^k) = \log_b(x) } $$

when the real arguments and bases remain valid.

Expansion

To expand a logarithm means to use logarithm rules to rewrite one logarithm of a multiplicative expression as several simpler logarithmic terms.

Basic Expansion Example

For positive $x$ and $y$:

$$ \log_b(xy) = \boxed{ \log_b(x)+\log_b(y) }. $$

Expansion with a Quotient

$$ \log_b\left(\frac{x}{y}\right) = \boxed{ \log_b(x)-\log_b(y) }. $$

Expansion with Powers

$$ \log_b(x^3y^2) = \boxed{ 3\log_b(x)+2\log_b(y) }. $$

Full Expansion Example

For positive $x,y,z$:

$$ \log_b\left( \frac{x^4\sqrt{y}}{z^3} \right) $$

expands to:

$$ \boxed{ 4\log_b(x) + \frac{1}{2}\log_b(y) - 3\log_b(z) }. $$

Condensation

To condense logarithms means to reverse the logarithm rules and combine several logarithmic terms into one.

Condensing a Sum

$$ \log_b(x)+\log_b(y) = \boxed{ \log_b(xy) }. $$

Condensing a Difference

$$ \log_b(x)-\log_b(y) = \boxed{ \log_b\left(\frac{x}{y}\right) }. $$

Condensing Coefficients

$$ 3\log_b(x) = \boxed{ \log_b(x^3) }. $$

Full Condensation Example

For positive arguments:

$$ 2\log_b(x) + \frac{1}{2}\log_b(y) - \log_b(z) $$

becomes:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b\left( \frac{x^2\sqrt{y}}{z} \right) }. $$

Order of Operations when Expanding

A practical sequence is:

  1. separate quotients;
  2. separate products;
  3. bring powers down as coefficients;
  4. simplify numerical logarithms when possible.

Order of Operations when Condensing

A practical sequence is:

  1. move coefficients up as exponents;
  2. combine sums as products;
  3. combine differences as quotients;
  4. check domain restrictions.

No Addition Rule

The product rule must not be misapplied to addition.

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(M+N) \neq \log_b(M)+\log_b(N) }. $$

Numerical Counterexample to the False Addition Rule

Let $b=10$, $M=1$, and $N=9$.

Then:

$$ \log_{10}(1+9) = \log_{10}(10) = 1, $$

but:

$$ \log_{10}(1)+\log_{10}(9) = 0+\log_{10}(9) \neq1. $$

No Subtraction Rule

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(M-N) \neq \log_b(M)-\log_b(N) }. $$

Why No Sum or Difference Rule Exists

Logarithm rules reflect exponent laws for multiplication, division, and powers. There is no corresponding exponent identity turning:

$$ b^m+b^n $$

into one power of $b$ by simply adding or subtracting $m$ and $n$.

Power Rule Is Not Distribution

The identity:

$$ \log_b(M^r) = r\log_b(M) $$

moves an exponent outside as a multiplier. It does not mean that arbitrary factors can be moved outside a logarithm.

Constant Multiplication inside an Argument

For $c>0$:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(cM) = \log_b(c)+\log_b(M) }. $$

It is generally not equal to $c\log_b(M)$.

Coefficient outside a Logarithm

A coefficient:

$$ c\log_b(M) $$

can be moved into the argument as an exponent:

$$ \boxed{ c\log_b(M) = \log_b(M^c) }. $$

Parentheses Matter

Compare:

$$ \log_b(x^2) $$

with:

$$ (\log_b x)^2. $$

The first simplifies by the power rule under suitable domain assumptions; the second squares the logarithm's output.

Real-Domain Subtlety of $\log_b(x^2)$

For real $x\neq0$:

$$ x^2>0, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(x^2) = 2\log_b|x| }. $$

Writing $2\log_b(x)$ would incorrectly exclude negative $x$ unless the original context had already imposed $x>0$.

Absolute-Value Defense

When extracting even powers from logarithms, absolute values can be required to preserve the original real domain.

Example

$$ \boxed{ \ln(x^2) = 2\ln|x|, \qquad x\neq0 }. $$

Odd Powers

Even if $x^3$ can be negative, the real logarithm:

$$ \ln(x^3) $$

requires:

$$ x>0. $$

Under that restriction:

$$ \boxed{ \ln(x^3)=3\ln(x) }. $$

Domain Is Part of the Identity

A symbolic logarithm rule should not be separated from the assumptions under which all expressions involved are defined.

Original-Domain Preservation

Consider:

$$ \ln(x)+\ln(x-1). $$

The original conditions are:

$$ x>0, \qquad x-1>0. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ x>1 }. $$

Condensing gives:

$$ \ln(x(x-1)), $$

but the original restriction $x>1$ must not be silently enlarged to every value satisfying $x(x-1)>0$.

Condensation Can Hide Restrictions

The condensed expression may be defined at some values where the original separated logarithms were not. When solving or transforming, preserve the original domain.

Using Log Rules to Solve Equations

Consider:

$$ \log_b(x)+\log_b(x-2)=1. $$

Condense:

$$ \log_b(x(x-2))=1. $$

Rewrite exponentially:

$$ x(x-2)=b. $$

Solve the resulting algebraic equation, then retain only candidates satisfying:

$$ \boxed{ x>2 }. $$

One-to-One Property

For an admissible base:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(M)=\log_b(N) \iff M=N } $$

when $M,N>0$.

Using the One-to-One Rule

If:

$$ \log_3(x+1) = \log_3(7), $$

then:

$$ x+1=7, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ x=6 }. $$

Logarithms Applied to Both Sides

If two positive expressions are equal:

$$ A=B, $$

then applying the same logarithm gives:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(A)=\log_b(B) }. $$

Solving Exponentials with Different Bases

Suppose:

$$ 2^x=7. $$

Take natural logarithms:

$$ \ln(2^x)=\ln7. $$

Apply the power rule:

$$ x\ln2=\ln7. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ x=\frac{\ln7}{\ln2} }. $$

Logarithmic Differentiation

Logarithm rules allow complicated products and powers to be transformed before differentiation.

Product Example

If:

$$ y=x^2(x+1)^3, \qquad x>0, $$

then:

$$ \ln y = 2\ln x + 3\ln(x+1). $$

Differentiating gives:

$$ \frac{y'}{y} = \frac{2}{x} + \frac{3}{x+1}. $$

Variable Exponents

For:

$$ y=x^x, \qquad x>0, $$

logarithmic differentiation uses:

$$ \boxed{ \ln y=x\ln x }. $$

Logarithmic Linearization

Product and power rules can turn multiplicative models into additive ones.

Power-Law Model

If:

$$ y=ax^k, \qquad a>0,\ x>0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \ln y = \ln a + k\ln x }. $$

Multiple-Factor Power Model

If:

$$ y = ax^p z^q, $$

with positive quantities, then:

$$ \boxed{ \ln y = \ln a + p\ln x + q\ln z }. $$

Why This Matters

Multiplicative scaling becomes additive after logarithmic transformation, which is useful in modeling, regression, dimensional scaling, and data analysis.

Computational Use

Historically:

$$ MN $$

was computed via:

$$ \log(MN) = \log(M)+\log(N). $$

Division similarly became subtraction.

Large Products in Computation

In numerical computing, sums of logarithms can replace products of many positive quantities:

$$ \boxed{ \log\left( \prod_{i=1}^{n}x_i \right) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(x_i) }. $$

Why Log-Space Computation Helps

Extremely large or small products may overflow or underflow machine arithmetic. Working in log space can improve numerical stability.

Log-Likelihood

In statistics, a likelihood written as a product:

$$ L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i\mid\theta) $$

becomes the log-likelihood:

$$ \boxed{ \ell(\theta) = \log L(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log f(x_i\mid\theta) }. $$

Monotonicity Advantage

Because logarithms with base greater than $1$ are strictly increasing, maximizing a positive function is equivalent to maximizing its logarithm.

Base Independence of Many Growth Classes

By change of base:

$$ \log_a(n) = \frac{\log_b(n)}{\log_b(a)}. $$

Therefore different constant logarithmic bases differ only by a constant factor. This is why computer-science complexity notation often writes:

$$ \boxed{ O(\log n) } $$

without specifying a base.

Logarithm Rules over Complex Numbers

Over the complex numbers, logarithms require additional care because the complex logarithm is multivalued unless a branch is chosen.

Complex Product Rule Caveat

The familiar real identity:

$$ \log(zw) = \log z+\log w $$

does not hold globally as an unrestricted single-valued identity for principal complex logarithms.

Branch Dependence

Arguments can differ by integer multiples of $2\pi$, so logarithm sums may differ by multiples of:

$$ \boxed{ 2\pi i }. $$

Principal Logarithm

A standard principal branch is:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Log}(z) = \ln|z| + i\operatorname{Arg}(z) }. $$

Real Rules Should Not Be Transferred Blindly

Product, quotient, and power identities that are straightforward for positive real arguments can require branch restrictions or correction terms in complex analysis.

Common Error: Splitting a Sum

Incorrect:

$$ \log_b(x+y) = \log_b(x)+\log_b(y). $$

There is no ordinary logarithm sum rule.

Common Error: Splitting a Difference

Incorrect:

$$ \log_b(x-y) = \log_b(x)-\log_b(y). $$

The subtraction rule belongs to a quotient, not to a difference.

Common Error: Multiplying Logarithms for a Product

Incorrect:

$$ \log_b(xy) = \log_b(x)\log_b(y). $$

Correct:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(xy) = \log_b(x)+\log_b(y) }. $$

Common Error: Dividing Logarithms for a Quotient

Incorrect:

$$ \log_b\left(\frac{x}{y}\right) = \frac{\log_b(x)}{\log_b(y)}. $$

Correct:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b\left(\frac{x}{y}\right) = \log_b(x)-\log_b(y) }. $$

Common Error: Confusing Quotient Rule with Change of Base

These formulas look superficially similar but serve different purposes:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b\left(\frac{M}{N}\right) = \log_b(M)-\log_b(N) } $$

versus:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(x) = \frac{\ln x}{\ln b} }. $$

Common Error: Forgetting to Move a Coefficient Up as an Exponent

To condense:

$$ 3\log_b(x), $$

write:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(x^3) } $$

not $\log_b(3x)$.

Common Error: Moving an Internal Coefficient Outside

In general:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(3x) = \log_b(3)+\log_b(x) } $$

rather than $3\log_b(x)$.

Common Error: Forgetting Argument Positivity

Every separate real logarithm requires a positive argument.

Common Error: Losing Original Restrictions after Condensing

Condensation can make an expression appear defined on a larger set than the original expression. Preserve original restrictions.

Common Error: Expanding $\ln(x^2)$ as $2\ln(x)$ without Conditions

The original expression is defined for every $x\neq0$, while $\ln(x)$ requires $x>0$. The domain-preserving real identity is:

$$ \boxed{ \ln(x^2)=2\ln|x| }. $$

Common Error: Treating the Base as an Argument

In:

$$ \log_b(x), $$

$b$ is the base and $x$ is the argument. They play different structural roles.

Common Error: Forgetting Base Conditions

Real logarithm bases must satisfy:

$$ \boxed{ b>0, \qquad b\neq1 }. $$

Common Error: Assuming Log Rules Are Arbitrary Memorized Formulas

The principal logarithm rules are direct translations of exponent rules through the inverse relationship between logarithms and exponentiation.

Common Misconception: Logarithm rules do not say that logarithms distribute across ordinary addition and subtraction. They convert multiplicative structure into additive structure: products become sums, quotients become differences, and exponents become coefficients. This pattern is the key to remembering the rules without treating them as disconnected formulas.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\log_b(MN)=\log_b(M)+\log_b(N)$ "The logarithm of a product is the sum of the logarithms."
$\log_b(M/N)=\log_b(M)-\log_b(N)$ "The logarithm of a quotient is the difference of the logarithms."
$\log_b(M^r)=r\log_b(M)$ "An exponent on the logarithm's argument comes down as a coefficient."
$\log_b(\sqrt[n]{M})=(1/n)\log_b(M)$ "The logarithm of an nth root is one nth times the logarithm."
$\log_b(1/M)=-\log_b(M)$ "The logarithm of a reciprocal is the negative logarithm."
$\log_b(x)=\ln(x)/\ln(b)$ "Change base b to natural logarithms by dividing log of the argument by log of the base."
$\log_b(b^x)=x$ "Log base b and exponentiation base b undo each other."

The words expand and condense are conventional opposites in logarithm manipulation.

To expand:

$$ \log_b\left(\frac{x^2y}{z}\right) \longrightarrow 2\log_b(x)+\log_b(y)-\log_b(z). $$

To condense:

$$ 2\log_b(x)+\log_b(y)-\log_b(z) \longrightarrow \log_b\left(\frac{x^2y}{z}\right). $$

Informal classroom language often says that an exponent "comes down in front" or that a coefficient "moves up as an exponent." These are useful operational shortcuts, but the precise statement is the power rule:

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(M^r) = r\log_b(M) }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Write compound logarithm arguments in parentheses, keep bases explicit when ambiguity matters, and preserve every original real-domain restriction during expansion or condensation. Remember the structural mnemonic: product $\to$ sum, quotient $\to$ difference, power $\to$ coefficient. There is no corresponding rule for an ordinary sum or difference inside a logarithm.
