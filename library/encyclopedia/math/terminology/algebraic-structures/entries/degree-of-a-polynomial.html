Degree of a Polynomial

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

The degree of a polynomial is the greatest exponent of the variable appearing with a nonzero coefficient after the polynomial has been simplified.

For a nonzero polynomial:

$$ p(x)=a_nx^n+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_1x+a_0, \qquad a_n\neq0, $$

the degree is:

$$ \boxed{\deg p=n}. $$

The term degree historically expresses rank, level, or order. In polynomial algebra, it identifies the highest power that materially occurs in the expression.

For example:

$$ p(x)=4x^5-3x^2+7 $$

has degree:

$$ \boxed{5}. $$

The degree is not the number of terms, the largest coefficient, or the sum of the coefficients. It is determined by the highest exponent attached to a nonzero term.

Operational Nuance

Standard Definition

Let:

$$ p(x)=a_0+a_1x+\cdots+a_nx^n $$

with:

$$ a_n\neq0. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ \deg p=n }. $$

Leading Term

The term containing the highest power is called the leading term.

In:

$$ p(x)=3x^4-5x+2, $$

the leading term is:

$$ \boxed{3x^4}, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{\deg p=4}. $$

Leading Coefficient

In the same polynomial:

$$ 3x^4-5x+2, $$

the leading coefficient is:

$$ \boxed{3}. $$

The leading coefficient affects scale and end behavior but does not itself determine the degree.

Zero Coefficients

A term whose coefficient is zero does not contribute to the degree.

For example:

$$ 0x^7+2x^3-x+4 $$

simplifies to:

$$ 2x^3-x+4, $$

so the degree is:

$$ \boxed{3}, $$

not $7$.

Simplify Before Determining Degree

Cancellation can lower the apparent degree.

Consider:

$$ (x^4+2x)-(x^4-x^2). $$

Simplifying gives:

$$ x^2+2x. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{\deg p=2}. $$

Degree Zero

Every nonzero constant polynomial has degree $0$.

$$ \boxed{ p(x)=c,\qquad c\neq0 \Longrightarrow \deg p=0 }. $$

For example:

$$ \deg(7)=0. $$

Degree One

A polynomial of the form:

$$ ax+b,\qquad a\neq0 $$

has degree:

$$ \boxed{1}. $$

Such a polynomial is called linear.

Degree Two

A polynomial:

$$ ax^2+bx+c,\qquad a\neq0 $$

has degree:

$$ \boxed{2}. $$

Such a polynomial is called quadratic.

Degree Three

A polynomial:

$$ ax^3+bx^2+cx+d,\qquad a\neq0 $$

has degree:

$$ \boxed{3}. $$

Such a polynomial is called cubic.

Degree Four and Five

Degree-$4$ polynomials are called quartic, and degree-$5$ polynomials are called quintic.

$$ \boxed{ \deg p=4\Rightarrow\text{quartic}, \qquad \deg p=5\Rightarrow\text{quintic} }. $$

The Zero Polynomial

The polynomial:

$$ p(x)=0 $$

has no nonzero term from which a largest exponent can be selected.

Consequently, in elementary algebra its degree is usually described as undefined.

$$ \boxed{ \deg 0\text{ is undefined} } $$

in the elementary convention.

In more advanced algebra, it is often convenient to define:

$$ \boxed{ \deg 0=-\infty } $$

so that degree formulas remain uniform.

Degree of a Sum

For nonzero polynomials $p$ and $q$:

$$ \boxed{ \deg(p+q) \leq \max(\deg p,\deg q) }. $$

Equality can fail if the leading terms cancel.

For example:

$$ p(x)=x^3+x,\qquad q(x)=-x^3+2. $$

Then:

$$ p(x)+q(x)=x+2, $$

so:

$$ \deg(p+q)=1, $$

even though:

$$ \deg p=\deg q=3. $$

Degree of a Product

Over an integral domain, such as the real or complex numbers:

$$ \boxed{ \deg(pq) = \deg p+\deg q } $$

for nonzero polynomials $p$ and $q$.

Example of Product Degree

If:

$$ \deg p=3, \qquad \deg q=5, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \deg(pq)=8 }. $$

Degree of a Power

If $p$ is nonzero and $m$ is a positive integer:

$$ \boxed{ \deg(p^m) = m\deg p }. $$

For example, if:

$$ \deg p=4, $$

then:

$$ \deg(p^3)=12. $$

Degree of a Composition

For nonconstant polynomials $p$ and $q$ over a field:

$$ \boxed{ \deg(p\circ q) = (\deg p)(\deg q) }. $$

Example of Composition

If:

$$ p(x)=x^3+1, \qquad q(x)=x^2-x, $$

then:

$$ \deg p=3, \qquad \deg q=2, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ \deg(p\circ q)=6 }. $$

Degree and Differentiation

For a nonconstant polynomial:

$$ \boxed{ \deg(p') = \deg p-1 } $$

over fields of characteristic zero, such as $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$.

For example:

$$ p(x)=4x^5-2x^2+1 $$

has:

$$ p'(x)=20x^4-4x, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ 5\longrightarrow4 }. $$

Characteristic-$p$ Caution

In fields of positive characteristic, differentiation can reduce degree by more than one or even produce the zero polynomial.

For example, in characteristic $p$:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}x^p = px^{p-1} = 0. $$

Thus the familiar derivative-degree rule requires the appropriate coefficient field assumptions.

Degree and Antiderivatives

Over characteristic zero, an antiderivative of a nonzero polynomial of degree $n$ has degree:

$$ \boxed{n+1}. $$

Degree and Number of Roots

A nonzero polynomial of degree $n$ over a field has at most $n$ distinct roots in that field.

$$ \boxed{ \#\{\text{distinct roots}\} \leq \deg p }. $$

Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

Over the complex numbers, every nonconstant polynomial of degree $n$ has exactly:

$$ \boxed{n} $$

complex roots counted with multiplicity.

Degree and Turning Points

A real polynomial function of degree $n$ can have at most:

$$ \boxed{n-1} $$

turning points.

This follows because its derivative has degree at most $n-1$.

Degree and End Behavior

The parity of the degree helps determine the relationship between the two ends of the graph.

For even degree, the two ends point in the same vertical direction.

For odd degree, the two ends point in opposite vertical directions.

Multivariable Monomial Degree

For a monomial:

$$ x^ay^bz^c, $$

its total degree is:

$$ \boxed{a+b+c}. $$

For example:

$$ x^2y^3z $$

has total degree:

$$ \boxed{2+3+1=6}. $$

Degree of a Multivariable Polynomial

The total degree of a multivariable polynomial is the greatest total degree among its nonzero monomials.

For:

$$ f(x,y)=x^3y^2+4x^2y^2+y^4+7, $$

the term:

$$ x^3y^2 $$

has total degree $5$, so:

$$ \boxed{\deg f=5}. $$

Degree in a Particular Variable

A multivariable polynomial may also have a degree with respect to one selected variable.

For:

$$ f(x,y)=x^4y^2+x^2y^7+1, $$

the degree in $x$ is:

$$ \boxed{\deg_x f=4}, $$

while the degree in $y$ is:

$$ \boxed{\deg_y f=7}. $$

The total degree is:

$$ \boxed{9}, $$

coming from $x^2y^7$.

Common Misconception: The degree of a polynomial is not found merely by looking for the largest exponent written anywhere before simplification. Terms with zero coefficients or terms that cancel do not contribute. Simplify first, then identify the highest remaining nonzero power.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\deg p=5$ "The degree of p is five."
$a_nx^n$ "A sub n times x to the nth power."
$a_n\neq0$ "A sub n is nonzero."
$\deg(pq)=\deg p+\deg q$ "The degree of the product equals the sum of the degrees."
$\deg(p+q)\leq\max(\deg p,\deg q)$ "The degree of p plus q is at most the maximum of the degrees of p and q."
$\deg_x f$ "The degree of f with respect to x."
$\deg 0$ "The degree of the zero polynomial."

A polynomial is often described directly by its degree:

$$ \boxed{ \text{degree }0:\text{ constant} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{degree }1:\text{ linear} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{degree }2:\text{ quadratic} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{degree }3:\text{ cubic} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{degree }4:\text{ quartic} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{degree }5:\text{ quintic} }. $$

In ordinary speech, one may say:

$$ \text{"This is a fifth-degree polynomial."} $$

or:

$$ \text{"This polynomial has degree five."} $$

Defense Notation Advice: State the convention for the zero polynomial when it matters. In elementary algebra, say its degree is undefined. In abstract algebra, authors often assign $\deg 0=-\infty$ for convenience.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • General Definition: For: $$ p(x)=a_nx^n+\cdots+a_0,\qquad a_n\neq0, $$ $$ \boxed{\deg p=n}. $$
  • Leading Term: The term: $$ a_nx^n $$ determines the degree.
  • Leading Coefficient: The coefficient: $$ a_n $$ must be nonzero.
  • Zero Coefficients: Terms with coefficient zero do not affect degree.
  • Simplification: Like terms and cancellations must be resolved before degree is identified.
  • Constant Polynomial: Every nonzero constant has: $$ \boxed{\deg p=0}. $$
  • Linear Polynomial: $$ ax+b,\qquad a\neq0 $$ has degree: $$ \boxed{1}. $$
  • Quadratic Polynomial: $$ ax^2+bx+c,\qquad a\neq0 $$ has degree: $$ \boxed{2}. $$
  • Cubic Polynomial: $$ ax^3+bx^2+cx+d,\qquad a\neq0 $$ has degree: $$ \boxed{3}. $$
  • Quartic Polynomial: Degree: $$ \boxed{4}. $$
  • Quintic Polynomial: Degree: $$ \boxed{5}. $$
  • Zero Polynomial: In elementary algebra: $$ \boxed{ \deg0\text{ is undefined} }. $$
  • Advanced Zero-Polynomial Convention: In some algebraic contexts: $$ \boxed{\deg0=-\infty}. $$
  • Sum Rule: $$ \boxed{ \deg(p+q) \leq \max(\deg p,\deg q) }. $$
  • Leading-Term Cancellation: Equality in the sum rule can fail when highest-degree terms cancel.
  • Unequal Degrees: If: $$ \deg p\neq\deg q, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ \deg(p+q)=\max(\deg p,\deg q) } $$ over the usual coefficient fields.
  • Product Rule: For nonzero polynomials over an integral domain: $$ \boxed{ \deg(pq)=\deg p+\deg q }. $$
  • Power Rule: $$ \boxed{ \deg(p^m)=m\deg p } $$ for nonzero $p$ and positive integer $m$.
  • Composition Rule: For nonconstant polynomials: $$ \boxed{ \deg(p\circ q) = (\deg p)(\deg q) }. $$
  • Derivative: Over characteristic zero: $$ \boxed{ \deg(p')=\deg p-1 } $$ for nonconstant $p$.
  • Repeated Differentiation: A polynomial of degree $n$ becomes zero after more than $n$ differentiations.
  • $n$th Derivative: If: $$ \deg p=n, $$ then: $$ p^{(n)} $$ is a nonzero constant over characteristic zero.
  • Next Derivative: $$ \boxed{ p^{(n+1)}=0 }. $$
  • Antiderivative: An antiderivative of a nonzero degree-$n$ polynomial has degree: $$ \boxed{n+1}. $$
  • Root Bound: A nonzero polynomial of degree $n$ has at most: $$ \boxed{n} $$ distinct roots over a field.
  • Complex Roots: By the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra, a degree-$n$ complex polynomial has: $$ \boxed{n} $$ roots counted with multiplicity.
  • Factorization: If: $$ p(x)=a\prod_{j=1}^{n}(x-r_j) $$ over $\mathbb{C}$, then: $$ \boxed{\deg p=n}. $$
  • Multiplicity: Root multiplicities contribute to the total degree when a polynomial is factored into linear factors.
  • Turning Points: A real polynomial of degree $n$ has at most: $$ \boxed{n-1} $$ turning points.
  • Even Degree: Even-degree polynomial graphs have matching end directions.
  • Odd Degree: Odd-degree polynomial graphs have opposite end directions.
  • Positive Leading Coefficient: The sign of the leading coefficient determines whether the right end rises or falls.
  • Dominant Term: For large $|x|$, the highest-degree term: $$ a_nx^n $$ dominates the lower-degree terms.
  • Asymptotic Ratio: For: $$ p(x)=a_nx^n+\cdots, $$ $$ \boxed{ \lim_{|x|\to\infty} \frac{p(x)}{a_nx^n} =1 }. $$
  • Polynomial Division: Dividing a polynomial of degree $m$ by one of degree $n$ with $m\geq n$ produces a quotient of degree: $$ \boxed{m-n} $$ when ordinary leading-term cancellation proceeds over a field.
  • Remainder Degree: In polynomial division: $$ p=qg+r, $$ the remainder satisfies: $$ \boxed{ \deg r<\deg g } $$ unless $r=0$.
  • Greatest Common Divisors: Polynomial Euclidean algorithms use degree as the decreasing measure that guarantees termination.
  • Polynomial Spaces: The set: $$ P_n $$ commonly denotes polynomials of degree at most $n$.
  • Important Distinction: Membership in: $$ P_n $$ does not require degree exactly $n$; lower-degree polynomials and usually the zero polynomial are included.
  • Dimension of Polynomial Space: Over a field: $$ P_n $$ has basis: $$ \boxed{ 1,x,x^2,\ldots,x^n } $$ and dimension: $$ \boxed{n+1}. $$
  • Interpolation: Through $n+1$ suitably specified points with distinct $x$-coordinates, there is a unique polynomial of degree at most $n$ interpolating the data.
  • Finite Differences: For a polynomial of degree $n$ sampled at equally spaced inputs, the $n$th finite differences are constant and the next finite differences are zero.
  • Multivariable Monomial: $$ x_1^{a_1}\cdots x_k^{a_k} $$ has total degree: $$ \boxed{ a_1+\cdots+a_k }. $$
  • Multivariable Polynomial: Its total degree is the maximum total degree of its nonzero monomials.
  • Degree in One Variable: $\deg_x f$ records the largest exponent of $x$ appearing with nonzero coefficient when the other variables are treated as coefficients.
  • Homogeneous Polynomial: A polynomial is homogeneous of degree $n$ when every nonzero monomial has total degree $n$.
  • Example of Homogeneity: $$ x^3+2x^2y+xy^2+y^3 $$ is homogeneous of degree: $$ \boxed{3}. $$
  • Nonhomogeneous Polynomial: A polynomial may have overall degree $n$ even though some of its terms have lower degree.
  • Coefficient Ring Caution: The familiar product-degree rule requires a coefficient ring without zero divisors.
  • Zero Divisors: Over rings containing zero divisors, leading coefficients can multiply to zero, so: $$ \deg(pq)=\deg p+\deg q $$ need not hold.
  • Terminological Contrast: The degree of a polynomial refers to its highest nonzero power. The degree of a term refers to the exponent total within that term.
  • Equation Degree: A polynomial equation is commonly classified by the degree of the polynomial obtained after moving all terms to one side and simplifying.
  • Example: $$ x^5+2=x^5+x^2 $$ simplifies to: $$ x^2-2=0, $$ so the resulting polynomial equation has degree: $$ \boxed{2}, $$ not $5$.
  • Core Interpretation: The degree identifies the highest surviving algebraic power in a nonzero polynomial: $$ \boxed{ p(x)=a_nx^n+\cdots+a_0,\quad a_n\neq0 \Longrightarrow \deg p=n }. $$ It controls fundamental features of polynomial arithmetic, roots, derivatives, graph behavior, and factorization.
