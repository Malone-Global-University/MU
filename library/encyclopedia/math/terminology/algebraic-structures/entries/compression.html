Compression

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

In mathematics, compression generally describes a transformation that reduces distances, dimensions, coordinates, or the apparent spread of an object in a specified direction.

In elementary algebra and precalculus, the term most commonly appears in function transformations. A graph may be compressed vertically or horizontally relative to an original graph.

$$ \boxed{ \text{compression} \approx \text{scaling toward an axis or reference set} }. $$

The word derives from the ordinary idea of pressing something into a smaller extent. The mathematical meaning preserves this geometric intuition: selected distances are multiplied by a scale factor whose magnitude is less than $1$.

The exact definition depends on context. Compression can describe a graph transformation, a geometric scaling, a contraction-like transformation, or, in advanced operator theory, a restriction-related construction. Therefore the object being compressed should always be identified.

Operational Nuance

Vertical Compression of a Function

Let:

$$ y=f(x). $$

A vertical compression is produced by:

$$ \boxed{ g(x)=af(x), \qquad 0

Every output is multiplied by $a$, so every vertical distance from the $x$-axis is reduced by the factor $a$.

Coordinate Rule for Vertical Compression

A point:

$$ (x,y) $$

on the original graph is transformed to:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y)\longmapsto(x,ay) }. $$

The $x$-coordinate remains unchanged while the $y$-coordinate is scaled.

Example: Vertical Compression

Let:

$$ f(x)=x^2. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ g(x)=\frac12x^2 } $$

is a vertical compression of the graph of $y=x^2$ by a scale factor of $1/2$. Every original output is halved.

Vertical Compression Changes Output Values

If:

$$ f(c)=y, $$

then for $g(x)=af(x)$:

$$ \boxed{ g(c)=ay }. $$

This is why multiplication outside the function acts vertically.

Horizontal Compression of a Function

A horizontal compression is produced by changing the function's input:

$$ \boxed{ g(x)=f(bx), \qquad b>1 }. $$

The graph is compressed toward the $y$-axis.

Horizontal Scale Factor

Although $b$ appears inside the function, the actual horizontal coordinate scale factor is:

$$ \boxed{ \frac1b }. $$

Thus $f(2x)$ compresses the graph horizontally so that horizontal distances are half their original size.

Coordinate Rule for Horizontal Compression

If $(x,y)$ lies on $y=f(x)$, then the corresponding point on:

$$ g(x)=f(bx) $$

is:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y) \longmapsto \left(\frac{x}{b},y\right) }. $$

Why the Horizontal Factor Appears Reversed

Suppose:

$$ f(c)=y. $$

For $g(x)=f(bx)$ to produce the same output $y$, its input must satisfy:

$$ bx=c. $$

Therefore:

$$ x=\frac cb. $$

The point that originally occurred at horizontal coordinate $c$ now occurs at:

$$ \boxed{ \frac cb }. $$

Example: Horizontal Compression

Let:

$$ f(x)=x^2. $$

Consider:

$$ \boxed{ g(x)=f(2x) }. $$

The graph is compressed horizontally by coordinate scale factor $1/2$.

Algebraically:

$$ g(x)=(2x)^2=4x^2. $$

This example also demonstrates why the visual description of a transformation should be distinguished from the simplified algebraic form.

General Function Transformation

A common transformation model is:

$$ \boxed{ g(x)=a\,f\!\left(b(x-h)\right)+k }. $$

In this form:

  • $a$ controls vertical scaling and possibly reflection,
  • $b$ controls horizontal scaling and possibly reflection,
  • $h$ controls horizontal translation, and
  • $k$ controls vertical translation.

then:

$$ g(x)=af(x) $$

$$ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(x,ay) }. $$

Horizontal Compression with Reflection

If $b<0$, the transformation:

$$ g(x)=f(bx) $$

includes reflection across the $y$-axis. When:

$$ |b|>1, $$

it also produces horizontal compression by coordinate factor:

$$ \boxed{ \frac1{|b|} }. $$

Compression Relative to an Axis

A vertical compression reduces distances measured perpendicular to the $x$-axis. A horizontal compression reduces distances measured perpendicular to the $y$-axis.

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \text{vertical compression} &\rightarrow \text{toward the }x\text{-axis},\\ \text{horizontal compression} &\rightarrow \text{toward the }y\text{-axis}. \end{aligned} } $$

Compression need not occur about the $x$-axis. For example:

with $0

Indeed:

$$ g(x)-k=a[f(x)-k]. $$

Compression about a Shifted Vertical Line

A horizontal compression toward $x=h$ can be described by transforming coordinates:

$$ \boxed{ x-h \longmapsto c(x-h), \qquad 0

The point's horizontal displacement from $x=h$ is multiplied by $c$.

Geometric Compression

More generally, a geometric compression can be represented by scaling one or more coordinates.

For example:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(ax,y), \qquad 0

compresses horizontal distances toward the $y$-axis.

Uniform Compression

If both coordinates are scaled by the same factor:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(cx,cy), \qquad 0

every distance from the origin is multiplied by $c$.

In geometry this is commonly described more specifically as a dilation with scale factor between $0$ and $1$.

Compression Is Usually Non-Rigid

A compression generally changes distances and therefore is not a rigid transformation.

$$ \boxed{ \text{compression} \not\Rightarrow \text{distance preservation} }. $$

Translations, rotations, and reflections preserve distance; ordinary compressions do not.

Anisotropic Scaling

If different coordinate directions receive different scale factors:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(ax,by) }, $$

the transformation is a nonuniform or anisotropic scaling. If either $|a|<1$ or $|b|<1$, the corresponding direction is compressed.

Matrix Representation

Coordinate compression can be represented using a diagonal matrix:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{pmatrix} x'\\ y' \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a&0\\ 0&b \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x\\ y \end{pmatrix} }. $$

A value such as:

$$ 0<|a|<1 $$

compresses the horizontal coordinate, while:

$$ 0<|b|<1 $$

compresses the vertical coordinate.

Area under Coordinate Compression

Under the scaling:

$$ (x,y)\mapsto(ax,by), $$

area is scaled by:

$$ \boxed{ |ab| }. $$

This is the absolute value of the determinant of the transformation matrix.

Uniform Compression in Higher Dimensions

In $\mathbb R^n$, uniform scaling by $c$:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x}\mapsto c\mathbf{x}, \qquad 0

multiplies all distances from the origin by $c$ and scales $n$-dimensional volume by:

$$ \boxed{ c^n }. $$

Compression and Periodic Functions

Horizontal compression changes the period of periodic functions.

If $f$ has period $T$ and:

$$ g(x)=f(bx), \qquad b\ne0, $$

then a corresponding period is:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{T}{|b|} }. $$

Example: Sine

The function:

$$ y=\sin x $$

has period $2\pi$. The function:

$$ y=\sin(3x) $$

is horizontally compressed and has period:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{2\pi}{3} }. $$

Vertical Compression and Amplitude

For a sinusoidal function:

$$ y=A\sin x, $$

multiplying by a factor $0

$$ y=cA\sin x. $$

The amplitude changes from $|A|$ to:

$$ \boxed{ c|A| }. $$

Compression and Inverse Transformations

A nonzero compression can generally be reversed by a corresponding stretch.

If:

$$ y\mapsto ay, \qquad 0

then the inverse scaling is:

$$ \boxed{ y\mapsto\frac1a y }. $$

Compression versus Contraction

The words compression and contraction can sound similar, but contraction has a more specialized meaning in metric-space mathematics.

A contraction mapping typically satisfies an inequality of the form:

$$ \boxed{ d(Tx,Ty) \le c\,d(x,y), \qquad 0\le c<1 }. $$

A graph compression may visually reduce one coordinate direction without satisfying the precise definition of a contraction mapping in every setting.

Compression in Operator Theory

In advanced mathematics, compression may have a different technical meaning involving operators and subspaces. One common construction begins with an operator $T$ on a Hilbert space and a closed subspace $M$ with orthogonal projection $P_M$.

An operator acting on $M$ may be formed through:

$$ \boxed{ P_M T|_M }. $$

Terminology varies by context, and some operator literature also uses compression in restriction-related senses. This use is conceptually different from graph compression.

Compression in Data and Information Contexts

The word compression also appears in information theory and computing to describe reducing the representation size of data. That meaning relies on coding, information, and algorithms rather than geometric graph scaling.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \text{graph compression} \ne \text{data compression} }. $$

Compression versus Translation

A translation moves points without changing distances between them:

$$ (x,y)\mapsto(x+h,y+k). $$

A compression changes selected distances:

$$ (x,y)\mapsto(ax,by). $$

The two transformations therefore perform fundamentally different geometric operations.

Compression versus Reflection

A reflection reverses orientation across an axis or line while preserving distances. A compression changes scale.

Negative scaling factors can combine both effects.

Compression versus Dilation

A dilation is a scaling transformation relative to a center. When its scale factor satisfies:

$$ 0

the dilation reduces distances from the center and may informally be described as a compression.

Dilation, however, is the more precise standard geometric term.

Common Misconception: Multiplying by 2 Always Stretches a Graph

Placement matters.

Outside the function:

$$ 2f(x) $$

gives a vertical stretch.

Inside the function:

$$ f(2x) $$

gives a horizontal compression.

$$ \boxed{ 2f(x) \ne f(2x) \quad\text{in general} }. $$

Common Misconception: Horizontal Compression Uses the Visible Factor Directly

In:

$$ f(bx), $$

the horizontal coordinate scale factor is $1/|b|$, not $|b|$.

$$ \boxed{ f(4x) \Rightarrow \text{horizontal coordinates scaled by }\frac14 }. $$

Common Misconception: Compression Always Makes a Graph Smaller in Every Direction

A vertical compression changes vertical distances but leaves horizontal coordinates unchanged. A horizontal compression changes horizontal distances but leaves vertical coordinates unchanged.

Common Misconception: A Compressed Graph Has a Smaller Domain

A horizontal compression changes the locations of points but does not necessarily change the qualitative form of the domain.

For example, if:

$$ \operatorname{Dom}(f)=\mathbb R, $$

then for nonzero $b$:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f(bx))=\mathbb R }. $$

Common Misconception: Compression Is the Same as Truncation

Compression rescales an object. Truncation removes or restricts part of an object. A compressed graph still represents transformed values rather than merely a cropped portion of the original graph.

Common Misconception: Compression Always Means a Contraction Mapping

Compression is broader and context-dependent. A contraction mapping has a specific metric definition requiring pairwise distances to decrease according to a uniform constant less than $1$.

The phrase compressed by a factor of can be ambiguous in informal instruction, especially for horizontal transformations. Some speakers refer to the multiplier appearing inside the function, while others refer to the actual coordinate scale factor.

For maximum precision, state the coordinate effect explicitly:

$$ \boxed{ f(3x) : x\text{-coordinates are multiplied by }\frac13 }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish factors placed outside the function from factors applied to its input. For $g(x)=af(x)$, $a$ directly scales vertical coordinates. For $g(x)=f(bx)$, horizontal coordinates are scaled by $1/b$ when $b>0$. When negative factors occur, identify the accompanying reflection separately. In advanced contexts, specify whether compression means graph scaling, geometric scaling, operator compression, contraction, or data compression.
