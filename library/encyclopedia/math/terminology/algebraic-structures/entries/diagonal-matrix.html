Diagonal Matrix

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

A diagonal matrix is a square matrix whose entries outside the main diagonal are all zero.

For an $n\times n$ matrix:

$$ D=(d_{ij}), $$

$D$ is diagonal when:

$$ \boxed{ d_{ij}=0 \quad\text{whenever}\quad i\neq j }. $$

Thus a diagonal matrix has the form:

$$ \boxed{ D= \begin{pmatrix} d_1 & 0 & \cdots & 0\\ 0 & d_2 & \cdots & 0\\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & d_n \end{pmatrix} }. $$

The adjective diagonal refers to the main diagonal, running from the upper-left entry to the lower-right entry:

$$ d_{11},d_{22},\ldots,d_{nn}. $$

The diagonal entries themselves may be zero or nonzero. The defining requirement is only that every off-diagonal entry be zero.

Operational Nuance

Basic Example

$$ D= \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & -2 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 5 \end{pmatrix} $$

is diagonal because every entry outside the main diagonal is zero.

Zeros on the Main Diagonal Are Allowed

The matrix:

$$ \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 7 \end{pmatrix} $$

is still diagonal.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \text{diagonal does not mean all diagonal entries are nonzero} }. $$

Compact Notation

A diagonal matrix is often written:

$$ \boxed{ D=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,d_2,\ldots,d_n) }. $$

For example:

$$ \operatorname{diag}(2,-1,5) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & -1 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 5 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Square-Matrix Requirement

Under the standard definition, a diagonal matrix is square:

$$ \boxed{n\times n}. $$

Rectangular matrices may have a distinguished diagonal, but they are not normally called diagonal matrices in elementary linear algebra.

Addition

Diagonal matrices of the same size add entrywise:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{diag}(a_1,\ldots,a_n) + \operatorname{diag}(b_1,\ldots,b_n) = \operatorname{diag}(a_1+b_1,\ldots,a_n+b_n) }. $$

Scalar Multiplication

$$ \boxed{ c\,\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_n) = \operatorname{diag}(cd_1,\ldots,cd_n) }. $$

Multiplication

The product of two diagonal matrices is diagonal:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{diag}(a_1,\ldots,a_n) \operatorname{diag}(b_1,\ldots,b_n) = \operatorname{diag}(a_1b_1,\ldots,a_nb_n) }. $$

Diagonal Matrices Commute with One Another

If $D_1$ and $D_2$ are diagonal matrices of the same size, then:

$$ \boxed{ D_1D_2=D_2D_1 }. $$

This is a special property. General matrices need not commute.

Powers

Raising a diagonal matrix to a positive integer power raises each diagonal entry to that power:

$$ \boxed{ D^k = \operatorname{diag} (d_1^k,d_2^k,\ldots,d_n^k) }. $$

Example of Powers

If:

$$ D= \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0\\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ D^4= \begin{pmatrix} 16 & 0\\ 0 & 81 \end{pmatrix} }. $$

Determinant

The determinant of a diagonal matrix is the product of its diagonal entries:

$$ \boxed{ \det(D) = d_1d_2\cdots d_n }. $$

Invertibility

A diagonal matrix is invertible exactly when every diagonal entry is nonzero:

$$ \boxed{ D\text{ invertible} \iff d_i\neq0 \text{ for every }i }. $$

This agrees with:

$$ \det(D)=d_1\cdots d_n\neq0. $$

Inverse

If all diagonal entries are nonzero:

$$ \boxed{ D^{-1} = \operatorname{diag} \left( \frac1{d_1}, \frac1{d_2}, \ldots, \frac1{d_n} \right) }. $$

Trace

The trace is the sum of the diagonal entries:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{tr}(D) = d_1+d_2+\cdots+d_n }. $$

Eigenvalues

The eigenvalues of a diagonal matrix are precisely its diagonal entries, counted with algebraic multiplicity:

$$ \boxed{ \lambda_1=d_1,\ldots,\lambda_n=d_n }. $$

Eigenvectors

The standard basis vector:

$$ \mathbf{e}_i $$

satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ D\mathbf{e}_i = d_i\mathbf{e}_i }. $$

Thus each standard basis direction is an eigenvector direction.

Characteristic Polynomial

For:

$$ D=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_n), $$

one convention gives:

$$ \boxed{ \det(\lambda I-D) = \prod_{i=1}^{n}(\lambda-d_i) }. $$

Linear Transformation Interpretation

A diagonal matrix acts independently along the coordinate axes.

If:

$$ D= \operatorname{diag}(d_1,d_2,\ldots,d_n), $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ D \begin{pmatrix} x_1\\ x_2\\ \vdots\\ x_n \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} d_1x_1\\ d_2x_2\\ \vdots\\ d_nx_n \end{pmatrix} }. $$

Each coordinate is scaled independently.

Geometric Scaling

In two dimensions:

$$ D= \begin{pmatrix} a & 0\\ 0 & b \end{pmatrix} $$

scales the $x$-direction by $a$ and the $y$-direction by $b$.

Reflections

Negative diagonal entries reverse the corresponding coordinate directions. For example:

$$ \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0\\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} $$

reflects the plane across the $y$-axis.

Projections

Diagonal entries equal to $0$ or $1$ can produce coordinate projections. For example:

$$ P= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} $$

projects:

$$ (x,y)\longmapsto(x,0). $$

Identity Matrix

The identity matrix is diagonal:

$$ \boxed{ I_n= \operatorname{diag}(1,1,\ldots,1) }. $$

Zero Matrix

The square zero matrix is also diagonal:

$$ \boxed{ 0_n= \operatorname{diag}(0,0,\ldots,0) }. $$

Scalar Matrix

A scalar matrix is a diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are all equal:

$$ \boxed{ cI_n= \operatorname{diag}(c,c,\ldots,c) }. $$

Therefore every scalar matrix is diagonal, but not every diagonal matrix is scalar.

Diagonal Matrix vs. Identity Matrix

Every identity matrix is diagonal, but a diagonal matrix need not have all diagonal entries equal to $1$.

$$ \boxed{ I_n\subset\text{diagonal matrices} } $$

in the sense of classification, not literal set inclusion of one matrix into another.

Diagonal Matrix vs. Triangular Matrix

Every diagonal matrix is both upper triangular and lower triangular:

$$ \boxed{ \text{diagonal} \Longrightarrow \text{upper triangular and lower triangular} }. $$

The converse is false in general.

Diagonal Matrix vs. Symmetric Matrix

Every real diagonal matrix is symmetric:

$$ \boxed{ D^T=D }. $$

But many symmetric matrices have nonzero off-diagonal entries and are not diagonal.

Diagonalization

A matrix $A$ is diagonalizable when there exists an invertible matrix $P$ and a diagonal matrix $D$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ A=PDP^{-1} } $$

or equivalently:

$$ \boxed{ D=P^{-1}AP }. $$

Why Diagonalization Is Useful

If:

$$ A=PDP^{-1}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ A^k = PD^kP^{-1} }. $$

Since powers of $D$ are easy to compute, powers of $A$ become much easier.

Functions of a Diagonal Matrix

Many matrix functions act entrywise on a diagonal matrix. For suitable scalar functions $f$:

$$ \boxed{ f(D) = \operatorname{diag} (f(d_1),\ldots,f(d_n)) }. $$

Matrix Exponential

In particular:

$$ \boxed{ e^D = \operatorname{diag} (e^{d_1},e^{d_2},\ldots,e^{d_n}) }. $$

Square Root of a Diagonal Matrix

For a real diagonal matrix with nonnegative diagonal entries:

$$ D= \operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_n), \qquad d_i\geq0, $$

a principal real square root is:

$$ \boxed{ D^{1/2} = \operatorname{diag} (\sqrt{d_1},\ldots,\sqrt{d_n}) }. $$

Rank

The rank of a diagonal matrix equals the number of nonzero diagonal entries:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{rank}(D) = \#\{i:d_i\neq0\} }. $$

Nullity

For an $n\times n$ diagonal matrix:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{nullity}(D) = \#\{i:d_i=0\} }. $$

Positive Definite Diagonal Matrices

A real diagonal matrix is positive definite exactly when every diagonal entry is positive:

$$ \boxed{ D\text{ positive definite} \iff d_i>0 \text{ for all }i }. $$

Common Misconception: A diagonal matrix does not require its diagonal entries to be nonzero or equal. Only the off-diagonal entries must be zero. Thus the zero matrix, identity matrix, and many singular matrices are all diagonal.

The word diagonal refers specifically to the main diagonal:

$$ \boxed{ a_{11},a_{22},\ldots,a_{nn} }. $$

The prefix off-diagonal refers to entries:

$$ \boxed{ a_{ij} \quad\text{with}\quad i\neq j }. $$

Thus a concise verbal definition is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"A square matrix whose off-diagonal entries are all zero."} }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Do not confuse a diagonal matrix with a matrix that merely has some zeros off the diagonal. Every off-diagonal entry must be zero. Also distinguish $\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_n)$, which constructs a diagonal matrix, from contexts where $\operatorname{diag}(A)$ may denote the diagonal entries extracted from an existing matrix.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Definition: $$ \boxed{ d_{ij}=0 \text{ whenever } i\neq j }. $$
  • Standard Form: $$ \boxed{ D= \operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_n) }. $$
  • Square Matrix: A diagonal matrix is ordinarily an: $$ \boxed{n\times n} $$ matrix.
  • Main Diagonal: The entries: $$ d_{11},d_{22},\ldots,d_{nn} $$ may be arbitrary scalars.
  • Off-Diagonal Entries: Every entry with: $$ i\neq j $$ must equal zero.
  • Zero Diagonal Entries: Individual diagonal entries are allowed to be zero.
  • Zero Matrix: Every square zero matrix is diagonal.
  • Identity Matrix: $$ \boxed{ I_n=\operatorname{diag}(1,\ldots,1) } $$ is diagonal.
  • Scalar Matrix: $$ \boxed{ cI_n= \operatorname{diag}(c,\ldots,c) } $$ is diagonal.
  • Classification: Every scalar matrix is diagonal, but not every diagonal matrix is scalar.
  • Symmetry: Every real diagonal matrix satisfies: $$ \boxed{D^T=D}. $$
  • Upper Triangular: Every diagonal matrix is upper triangular.
  • Lower Triangular: Every diagonal matrix is lower triangular.
  • Addition: The sum of two diagonal matrices of equal size is diagonal.
  • Subtraction: The difference of two diagonal matrices of equal size is diagonal.
  • Scalar Multiplication: A scalar multiple of a diagonal matrix remains diagonal.
  • Multiplication: The product of diagonal matrices of the same size is diagonal.
  • Commutativity: For diagonal matrices $D_1,D_2$ of the same size: $$ \boxed{ D_1D_2=D_2D_1 }. $$
  • Product Entries: $$ \boxed{ (D_1D_2)_{ii} = (D_1)_{ii}(D_2)_{ii} }. $$
  • Powers: $$ \boxed{ D^k= \operatorname{diag} (d_1^k,\ldots,d_n^k) }. $$
  • Determinant: $$ \boxed{ \det(D)=d_1d_2\cdots d_n }. $$
  • Trace: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{tr}(D) = d_1+\cdots+d_n }. $$
  • Invertibility: $$ \boxed{ D\text{ invertible} \iff d_i\neq0 \text{ for every }i }. $$
  • Singularity: A diagonal matrix is singular whenever at least one diagonal entry is zero.
  • Inverse: $$ \boxed{ D^{-1} = \operatorname{diag} (d_1^{-1},\ldots,d_n^{-1}) } $$ when every $d_i\neq0$.
  • Rank: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{rank}(D) = \text{number of nonzero diagonal entries} }. $$
  • Nullity: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{nullity}(D) = \text{number of zero diagonal entries} }. $$
  • Eigenvalues: The diagonal entries are the eigenvalues, counted with multiplicity.
  • Standard Basis Eigenvectors: $$ \boxed{ D\mathbf{e}_i = d_i\mathbf{e}_i }. $$
  • Repeated Diagonal Entries: Equal diagonal entries correspond to repeated eigenvalues.
  • Characteristic Polynomial: $$ \boxed{ \chi_D(\lambda) = \prod_{i=1}^{n}(\lambda-d_i) } $$ under the convention $\chi_D(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I-D)$.
  • Minimal Polynomial: For a diagonal matrix over a field containing its entries, the minimal polynomial is the product of the distinct factors: $$ \boxed{ \prod_{\mu\in S}(\lambda-\mu) } $$ where $S$ is the set of distinct diagonal entries.
  • Diagonalizability: Every diagonal matrix is already diagonalized.
  • Diagonalization: A matrix $A$ is diagonalizable when: $$ \boxed{ A=PDP^{-1} } $$ for some invertible $P$ and diagonal $D$.
  • Change of Basis: Diagonalization means that in a suitable basis, the transformation acts by independent scalar multiplication along basis directions.
  • Power Computation: Diagonalization simplifies powers because: $$ \boxed{ A^k=PD^kP^{-1} }. $$
  • Matrix Exponential: $$ \boxed{ e^D= \operatorname{diag} (e^{d_1},\ldots,e^{d_n}) }. $$
  • Polynomial in a Matrix: For a polynomial $p$: $$ \boxed{ p(D)= \operatorname{diag} (p(d_1),\ldots,p(d_n)) }. $$
  • Coordinate Scaling: $$ D(x_1,\ldots,x_n)^T = (d_1x_1,\ldots,d_nx_n)^T. $$
  • Stretching: If $|d_i|>1$, the corresponding coordinate direction is stretched in magnitude.
  • Compression: If: $$ 0<|d_i|<1, $$ the corresponding coordinate direction is compressed.
  • Reflection: A negative diagonal entry reverses the corresponding coordinate direction.
  • Coordinate Elimination: A zero diagonal entry collapses the corresponding coordinate direction.
  • Projection: A diagonal matrix with entries only $0$ and $1$ represents a coordinate projection and satisfies: $$ \boxed{D^2=D}. $$
  • Involution: A diagonal matrix with entries only $1$ and $-1$ satisfies: $$ \boxed{D^2=I}. $$
  • Orthogonal Diagonal Matrix: Over $\mathbb{R}$, a diagonal matrix is orthogonal exactly when every diagonal entry equals: $$ \boxed{\pm1}. $$
  • Positive Semidefinite: A real diagonal matrix is positive semidefinite exactly when: $$ \boxed{d_i\geq0\text{ for all }i}. $$
  • Positive Definite: A real diagonal matrix is positive definite exactly when: $$ \boxed{d_i>0\text{ for all }i}. $$
  • Determinant Geometry: The volume-scaling factor of a real diagonal transformation is: $$ \boxed{ |d_1d_2\cdots d_n| }. $$
  • Orientation: For an invertible real diagonal matrix, the sign of: $$ d_1d_2\cdots d_n $$ determines whether orientation is preserved or reversed.
  • Systems of Equations: A diagonal coefficient matrix decouples a linear system into independent scalar equations.
  • Example System: $$ \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0\\ 0 & 5 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x\\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 6\\ 20 \end{pmatrix} $$ is equivalent to: $$ 2x=6,\qquad5y=20. $$
  • Differential Equations: Diagonal systems: $$ \mathbf{x}'=D\mathbf{x} $$ separate into independent scalar differential equations: $$ x_i'=d_ix_i. $$
  • Solution of Diagonal System: $$ \boxed{ x_i(t)=C_ie^{d_it} }. $$
  • Numerical Computation: Diagonal matrices are computationally efficient because only $n$ entries, rather than $n^2$, may need to be stored explicitly.
  • Terminological Contrast: A diagonal matrix has zero entries everywhere off the main diagonal. A diagonalizable matrix need not initially be diagonal; it becomes diagonal after a suitable change of basis.
  • Core Interpretation: A diagonal matrix is the simplest square matrix structure for independent coordinatewise action: $$ \boxed{ D= \operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_n), \qquad D\mathbf{e}_i=d_i\mathbf{e}_i }. $$ Its determinant, inverse, powers, eigenvalues, rank, and matrix functions can all be read directly from its diagonal entries.
