Direct Variation

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

Direct variation describes a relationship in which one variable is a constant multiple of another.

If $y$ varies directly with $x$, then:

$$ \boxed{y=kx} $$

where $k$ is the constant of variation, also called the constant of proportionality.

For $x\neq0$, the defining equation can be rewritten:

$$ \boxed{\frac{y}{x}=k}. $$

Thus the ratio between the dependent quantity and the independent quantity remains constant.

The word variation emphasizes how one quantity changes as another changes. The adjective direct indicates that the quantities scale together by the same factor.

In elementary mathematics, direct variation and direct proportion usually describe the same algebraic structure:

$$ \boxed{ y\propto x \iff y=kx }. $$

Operational Nuance

Standard Equation

The standard direct-variation equation is:

$$ \boxed{y=kx}. $$

Here:

  • $x$ is the input or independent variable,
  • $y$ is the output or dependent variable, and
  • $k$ is the constant of variation.

Finding the Constant of Variation

If one nonzero corresponding pair $(x,y)$ is known, then:

$$ \boxed{k=\frac{y}{x}}. $$

Example

Suppose $y$ varies directly with $x$ and:

$$ y=24 \quad\text{when}\quad x=6. $$

Then:

$$ k=\frac{24}{6}=4. $$

Therefore the direct-variation equation is:

$$ \boxed{y=4x}. $$

Using the Equation

If:

$$ y=4x $$

and $x=9$, then:

$$ y=4(9)=\boxed{36}. $$

Constant Ratio

If $y$ varies directly with $x$, every nonzero pair satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{y}{x}=k }. $$

Consequently:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{y_1}{x_1} = \frac{y_2}{x_2} } $$

whenever $x_1$ and $x_2$ are nonzero.

Scaling Property

Suppose:

$$ y=kx. $$

Replacing $x$ by $cx$ gives:

$$ k(cx)=c(kx)=cy. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ x\to cx \Longrightarrow y\to cy }. $$

Doubling

$$ \boxed{ x\to2x \Longrightarrow y\to2y }. $$

Tripling

$$ \boxed{ x\to3x \Longrightarrow y\to3y }. $$

Halving

$$ \boxed{ x\to\frac{x}{2} \Longrightarrow y\to\frac{y}{2} }. $$

Graph of Direct Variation

The graph of:

$$ y=kx $$

is a straight line through:

$$ \boxed{(0,0)}. $$

Its slope is:

$$ \boxed{k}. $$

Why the Origin Is Required

Setting:

$$ x=0 $$

gives:

$$ y=k(0)=0. $$

Therefore every direct-variation graph contains the origin.

Table Test

Consider:

$x$ $y$ $y/x$
$2$ $6$ $3$
$4$ $12$ $3$
$7$ $21$ $3$

Since:

$$ \boxed{\frac{y}{x}=3} $$

for each pair, the data represent direct variation with:

$$ \boxed{k=3}. $$

Direct Variation vs. General Linear Relationship

A general affine equation:

$$ y=mx+b $$

is a direct variation only if:

$$ \boxed{b=0}. $$

Therefore:

$$ y=5x $$

represents direct variation, while:

$$ y=5x+2 $$

does not.

Constant Slope Is Not Enough

A line can have constant slope without representing direct variation.

For:

$$ y=3x+7, $$

the slope is constant, but:

$$ \frac{y}{x} = 3+\frac7x, $$

which is not constant.

Variation Statement

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"y varies directly as x"} } $$

translates to:

$$ \boxed{y=kx}. $$

Proportionality Symbol

The same relationship may be written:

$$ \boxed{y\propto x}. $$

The symbol $\propto$ is read "is proportional to."

Unit Rate

The variation constant:

$$ k=\frac{y}{x} $$

frequently represents a unit rate.

For example, if $8$ kilograms of material cost $\$40$, with cost varying directly with mass, then:

$$ k=\frac{40}{8} = \boxed{5\text{ dollars per kilogram}}. $$

Constant Speed

At constant speed $v$:

$$ \boxed{d=vt}. $$

Distance $d$ varies directly with elapsed time $t$.

Constant Unit Price

If each item costs $p$ dollars and there is no fixed charge:

$$ \boxed{C=pq}, $$

so total cost varies directly with quantity.

Circumference and Diameter

$$ \boxed{C=\pi d}. $$

Hence circumference varies directly with diameter.

Circumference and Radius

$$ \boxed{C=2\pi r}. $$

Hence circumference also varies directly with radius.

Mass and Volume

For a material with constant density $\rho$:

$$ \boxed{m=\rho V}. $$

Mass varies directly with volume.

Ohm's Law

For constant electrical resistance $R$:

$$ \boxed{V=IR}. $$

Voltage varies directly with current.

Simple Interest

Under simple interest:

$$ I=Prt. $$

If $P$ and $r$ are fixed, then:

$$ \boxed{I\propto t}. $$

Direct Variation with a Power

The phrase:

$$ \text{"y varies directly as the square of x"} $$

means:

$$ \boxed{y=kx^2}. $$

In this statement, $y$ varies directly with $x^2$, not directly with $x$.

Direct Variation with a Cube

Likewise:

$$ \text{"y varies directly as the cube of x"} $$

means:

$$ \boxed{y=kx^3}. $$

Joint Variation

If $z$ varies jointly with $x$ and $y$, then:

$$ \boxed{z=kxy}. $$

Holding one input fixed gives direct variation with respect to the other.

Direct vs. Inverse Variation

Direct variation:

$$ \boxed{y=kx} $$

has constant ratio:

$$ \boxed{\frac{y}{x}=k}. $$

Inverse variation:

$$ \boxed{y=\frac{k}{x}} $$

has constant product:

$$ \boxed{xy=k}. $$

Negative Constants

Algebraically:

$$ y=-3x $$

still has constant ratio:

$$ \frac{y}{x}=-3. $$

Accordingly, many algebra texts regard this as direct variation with:

$$ \boxed{k=-3}. $$

Informal explanations that say "both variables increase together" are therefore incomplete unless they specifically assume $k>0$.

Zero Constant

When:

$$ k=0, $$

the equation becomes:

$$ y=0. $$

Some formal treatments permit this as a degenerate proportional relation, while many elementary direct-variation conventions assume:

$$ \boxed{k\neq0}. $$

Not Every Increasing Relationship Is Direct Variation

The function:

$$ y=x^2 $$

increases for positive $x$, but:

$$ \frac{y}{x}=x $$

is not constant. Thus it is not direct variation between $y$ and $x$.

Common Misconception: Direct variation does not mean merely that two quantities change together. The defining requirement is a constant multiplicative relationship:

$$ \boxed{y=kx}. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$y\propto x$ "Y varies directly with x," or "y is proportional to x."
$y=kx$ "Y equals k times x."
$k=\frac{y}{x}$ "K equals y divided by x."
$y\propto x^2$ "Y varies directly as the square of x."
$z\propto xy$ "Z varies jointly with x and y."
$d=vt$ "Distance varies directly with time when velocity is constant."
$C=\pi d$ "Circumference varies directly with diameter."

Several common verbal forms are equivalent:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"y varies directly with x"} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{"y varies directly as x"} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{"y is directly proportional to x"} }. $$

Each normally translates to:

$$ \boxed{y=kx}. $$

However, wording such as:

$$ \text{"y varies directly as }x^2\text{"} $$

must be translated:

$$ \boxed{y=kx^2}, $$

not $y=kx$.

Defense Notation Advice: Always identify exactly what expression the variable varies directly with. The statement $y\propto x^2$ means $y=kx^2$, whereas $y\propto x$ means $y=kx$. The proportionality symbol cannot simply be replaced by an equals sign without introducing the constant of variation.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Basic Definition: $$ \boxed{y=kx}. $$
  • Proportionality Form: $$ \boxed{y\propto x}. $$
  • Constant of Variation: $$ \boxed{k=\frac{y}{x}},\qquad x\neq0. $$
  • Alternative Name: $k$ is also called the constant of proportionality.
  • Ratio Property: $$ \boxed{\frac{y}{x}=k}. $$
  • Equivalent Ratios: $$ \boxed{ \frac{y_1}{x_1} = \frac{y_2}{x_2} }. $$
  • Cross Multiplication: $$ \boxed{ y_1x_2=y_2x_1 }. $$
  • Graph: The graph of $y=kx$ is a straight line through the origin.
  • Slope: $$ \boxed{\text{slope}=k}. $$
  • Intercept: $$ \boxed{b=0}. $$
  • Origin: $$ \boxed{(0,0)} $$ lies on every standard direct-variation graph.
  • Scaling: Multiplying $x$ by $c$ multiplies $y$ by $c$.
  • Doubling: Doubling $x$ doubles $y$.
  • Tripling: Tripling $x$ triples $y$.
  • Halving: Halving $x$ halves $y$.
  • Table Recognition: A table represents direct variation when $y/x$ remains constant.
  • Equation Recognition: An equation is direct variation when it simplifies to: $$ \boxed{y=kx}. $$
  • Graph Recognition: A nonvertical straight line through the origin represents $y$ directly varying with $x$.
  • General Linear Contrast: $$ y=mx+b $$ is direct variation only when: $$ \boxed{b=0}. $$
  • Fixed Charge: A relationship with a nonzero fixed starting amount is not direct variation.
  • Unit Rate: The constant $k$ often represents the amount of $y$ per unit of $x$.
  • Cost: $$ \boxed{C=pq} $$ is direct variation when unit price $p$ is fixed and there is no fixed fee.
  • Distance: $$ \boxed{d=vt} $$ gives direct variation between distance and time when $v$ is fixed.
  • Circumference and Diameter: $$ \boxed{C=\pi d}. $$
  • Circumference and Radius: $$ \boxed{C=2\pi r}. $$
  • Mass and Volume: For fixed density: $$ \boxed{m=\rho V}. $$
  • Ohm's Law: For fixed resistance: $$ \boxed{V=RI}. $$
  • Hooke's Law: In the ideal linear elastic regime: $$ \boxed{F=kx}. $$
  • Simple Interest: If principal and rate are fixed: $$ \boxed{I=(Pr)t}. $$
  • Recipe Scaling: Ingredient amounts may vary directly with the number of servings.
  • Map Scale: Map distance varies directly with actual distance under a fixed scale.
  • Currency Conversion: Under a fixed conversion rate: $$ \boxed{y=kx}. $$
  • Percent Relationship: If $y$ is always $p\%$ of $x$: $$ \boxed{ y=\frac{p}{100}x }. $$
  • Direct Variation as a Square: $$ y\propto x^2 \Longrightarrow \boxed{y=kx^2}. $$
  • Direct Variation as a Cube: $$ y\propto x^3 \Longrightarrow \boxed{y=kx^3}. $$
  • Important Distinction: $y=kx^2$ is direct variation with $x^2$, not direct variation with $x$.
  • Joint Variation: $$ z\propto xy \Longrightarrow \boxed{z=kxy}. $$
  • Combined Variation: More complex variation statements may include direct and inverse factors, such as: $$ z=k\frac{xy}{w}. $$
  • Inverse Variation: $$ \boxed{ y=\frac{k}{x} }. $$
  • Direct Invariant: $$ \boxed{\frac{y}{x}=k}. $$
  • Inverse Invariant: $$ \boxed{xy=k}. $$
  • Direct Graph: A line through the origin.
  • Inverse Graph: A hyperbola for nonzero $k$.
  • Positive Constant: If $k>0$, the line has positive slope.
  • Negative Constant: If $k<0$, the line has negative slope while still satisfying a constant ratio.
  • Zero Constant: Some conventions allow $k=0$; many elementary variation definitions assume $k\neq0$.
  • Units of $k$: $$ \boxed{ [k]=\frac{[y]}{[x]} }. $$
  • Dimensional Interpretation: The constant of variation often carries the unit conversion needed to turn units of $x$ into units of $y$.
  • Not Merely Association: Two variables can be associated without satisfying direct variation.
  • Not Merely Correlation: Strong statistical correlation does not imply: $$ y=kx. $$
  • Not Every Line: A straight line with nonzero intercept is not a direct-variation graph.
  • Not Every Increasing Function: Functions such as $y=x^2$ may increase without representing direct variation between $y$ and $x$.
  • Direct Proportion: Direct proportion is ordinarily synonymous with direct variation in elementary algebra.
  • Terminological Emphasis: Direct variation emphasizes how a variable changes; direct proportion emphasizes the constant-ratio structure. The mathematical model is usually the same.
  • Core Interpretation: Direct variation means that one quantity remains a fixed multiple of another: $$ \boxed{ y=kx \iff \frac{y}{x}=k \quad(x\neq0) }. $$ The relationship preserves scaling, has a constant ratio, and graphs as a straight line through the origin.
