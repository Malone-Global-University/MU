Synthetic Division

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

Synthetic division is a streamlined method for dividing a polynomial by a linear divisor of the form:

$$ \boxed{x-c}. $$

Instead of repeatedly writing powers of $x$, the method works primarily with the polynomial's coefficients. The word synthetic refers to this compressed symbolic procedure rather than a different kind of division.

Operational Nuance

Core Setup

Suppose:

$$ P(x)=2x^3-3x^2+4x-5 $$

is divided by:

$$ x-2. $$

Use $c=2$ and the coefficient list:

$$ \boxed{2,\,-3,\,4,\,-5}. $$

Bring Down, Multiply, Add

The operational cycle is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{bring down} \longrightarrow \text{multiply by }c \longrightarrow \text{add} }. $$

Repeating this process produces the quotient coefficients and final remainder.

Example

Dividing $2x^3-3x^2+4x-5$ by $x-2$ gives quotient coefficients:

$$ 2,\ 1,\ 6 $$

with remainder:

$$ 7. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ 2x^3-3x^2+4x-5 = (x-2)(2x^2+x+6)+7 }. $$

Degree of the Quotient

Dividing a degree-$n$ polynomial by a nonzero linear polynomial produces a quotient of degree $n-1$ when the leading term does not vanish.

Missing Powers

Missing powers must be represented by zero coefficients. For example:

$$ x^3+5x-2 = x^3+0x^2+5x-2. $$

The synthetic coefficient list is therefore:

$$ \boxed{1,\ 0,\ 5,\ -2}. $$

Sign of the Synthetic Number

For divisor $x-c$, use $c$. Thus:

$$ x+3=x-(-3) $$

requires the synthetic number:

$$ \boxed{-3}. $$

Remainder Theorem

When $P(x)$ is divided by $x-c$, the remainder is:

$$ \boxed{P(c)}. $$

Synthetic division therefore evaluates $P(c)$ while simultaneously computing the quotient.

Factor Theorem

If the synthetic remainder is zero:

$$ P(c)=0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{x-c\text{ is a factor of }P(x)}. $$

Finding Polynomial Roots

Synthetic division is commonly used after identifying a known or suspected root. Once one factor is removed, the lower-degree quotient may be factored or solved by another method.

Repeated Roots

If dividing by $x-c$ gives remainder zero, the quotient can be tested again with the same $c$ to determine whether the root has multiplicity greater than one.

Restriction of the Basic Method

The elementary form of synthetic division is designed for divisors of the form $x-c$. Divisors such as:

$$ x^2+1 $$

generally require polynomial long division or a generalized synthetic procedure.

Common Misconception

Synthetic division does not divide the coefficients independently. Each new value depends on the previous result through the repeated multiply-and-add process.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$P(x)\div(x-c)$ "Divide P of x by x minus c using synthetic division."
$x+3=x-(-3)$ "Use negative three as the synthetic number."
$1,\ 0,\ 5,\ -2$ "Include a zero coefficient for the missing x-squared term."
$P(c)$ "The remainder is P evaluated at c."
$P(c)=0$ "C is a root, so x minus c is a factor."
$P(x)=(x-c)Q(x)+r$ "P equals x minus c times the quotient plus the remainder."

Synthetic division is often described verbally by the repeated instruction bring down, multiply, add. This shorthand summarizes the arithmetic cycle but does not replace careful attention to coefficient order and divisor sign.

Defense Notation Advice: Write every coefficient in descending power order, insert zeros for missing powers, and use the zero of the divisor rather than copying its visible constant sign. For $x-c$, use $c$; for $x+c$, use $-c$.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Purpose: Efficiently divide a polynomial by a linear divisor of the form $x-c$.
  • Data Used: Polynomial coefficients and the value $c$.
  • Operational Pattern: Bring down, multiply, and add repeatedly.
  • Quotient: All resulting entries except the last become quotient coefficients.
  • Remainder: The final synthetic entry is the remainder.
  • Remainder Theorem: The final remainder equals $P(c)$.
  • Factor Theorem: A zero remainder means $x-c$ is a factor.
  • Root Testing: Candidate zeros can be checked rapidly.
  • Polynomial Factoring: Known factors can be removed to reduce polynomial degree.
  • Repeated Roots: Repeated division can reveal multiplicity.
  • Missing Terms: Zero coefficients must be inserted.
  • Sign Convention: The synthetic number is the zero of the divisor.
  • Long-Division Contrast: Polynomial long division works for more general polynomial divisors.
  • Common Error: Using the wrong sign for $c$.
  • Common Error: Omitting zero coefficients for missing powers.
  • Common Error: Treating the last number as another quotient coefficient instead of the remainder.
  • Common Error: Applying the elementary method directly to a nonlinear divisor.
  • Core Interpretation: Synthetic division compresses linear polynomial division into coefficient arithmetic: $$ \boxed{ P(x)=(x-c)Q(x)+P(c) }. $$ It therefore connects polynomial division, evaluation, roots, factors, and the Remainder and Factor Theorems in a single efficient procedure.
