Axes

Subfield: Geometry, Spatial Analysis & Topology

Etymology & Roots

Axes is the plural of axis. In mathematics, an axis is generally a distinguished line or direction used as a reference for position, measurement, symmetry, rotation, or representation.

In elementary coordinate geometry, the term most often refers to the perpendicular coordinate lines of the Cartesian plane:

$$ \boxed{ \text{x-axis} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \text{y-axis} }. $$

These axes intersect at the origin:

$$ \boxed{ (0,0) }. $$

In three-dimensional Cartesian space, a third coordinate axis is added:

$$ \boxed{ \text{x-axis}, \quad \text{y-axis}, \quad \text{z-axis} }. $$

Outside coordinate geometry, axis can refer to other distinguished lines, such as an axis of symmetry or an axis of rotation. Context therefore determines the exact meaning.

Operational Nuance

Coordinate Axes in the Plane

The standard Cartesian plane uses two perpendicular axes:

$$ \boxed{ x\text{-axis}:\ y=0 } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ y\text{-axis}:\ x=0 }. $$

The Origin

The coordinate axes intersect at:

$$ \boxed{ O=(0,0) }. $$

The origin serves as the reference point from which coordinate values are measured.

Positive and Negative Directions

On the standard Cartesian plane:

  • positive $x$ extends to the right,
  • negative $x$ extends to the left,
  • positive $y$ extends upward,
  • negative $y$ extends downward.

These directional conventions establish the orientation of the coordinate system.

Coordinates Relative to the Axes

A point:

$$ P=(x,y) $$

is located by its signed displacement relative to the coordinate axes.

The first coordinate gives horizontal position and the second gives vertical position.

Points on the x-Axis

Every point on the $x$-axis has:

$$ \boxed{ y=0 }. $$

Thus such points have the form:

$$ \boxed{ (x,0) }. $$

Points on the y-Axis

Every point on the $y$-axis has:

$$ \boxed{ x=0 }. $$

Thus such points have the form:

$$ \boxed{ (0,y) }. $$

Quadrants

The two coordinate axes divide the plane into four quadrants.

$$ \boxed{ \begin{array}{c|c} \text{Quadrant} & (x,y)\text{ signs}\\ \hline I & (+,+)\\ II & (-,+)\\ III & (-,-)\\ IV & (+,-) \end{array} } $$

Points lying directly on an axis are not ordinarily said to lie in any quadrant.

Axes as Boundaries of Quadrants

The coordinate axes form the boundaries between adjacent quadrants.

For example, the positive $y$-axis lies between Quadrants I and II.

x-Intercept

An x-intercept is a point where a graph meets the $x$-axis. Since points on that axis satisfy $y=0$, x-intercepts are found by solving:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=0 }. $$

y-Intercept

A y-intercept occurs where a graph meets the $y$-axis. Since $x=0$ there:

$$ \boxed{ y=f(0) }. $$

Example

For:

$$ y=2x-6, $$

the x-intercept is found from:

$$ 0=2x-6, $$

giving:

$$ \boxed{ (3,0) }. $$

The y-intercept is:

$$ y=2(0)-6=-6, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ (0,-6) }. $$

Three-Dimensional Coordinate Axes

In Cartesian three-space, the axes are:

$$ \boxed{ x,\ y,\ z }. $$

A point is represented by:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y,z) }. $$

Coordinate Axes in Three Dimensions

The coordinate axes can be described as:

$$ x\text{-axis}: \quad y=0,\ z=0, $$
$$ y\text{-axis}: \quad x=0,\ z=0, $$
$$ z\text{-axis}: \quad x=0,\ y=0. $$

Coordinate Planes

Pairs of axes determine the three standard coordinate planes:

$$ \boxed{ xy,\quad xz,\quad yz }. $$

Their equations are:

$$ z=0, \qquad y=0, \qquad x=0, $$

respectively.

Octants

The three coordinate planes divide three-dimensional space into eight regions called octants.

The coordinate axes lie along intersections of those planes.

Orthogonal Axes

In the standard Cartesian system, different coordinate axes are perpendicular.

In two dimensions:

$$ \boxed{ x\text{-axis} \perp y\text{-axis} }. $$

Orthonormal Coordinate Axes

A Cartesian coordinate system is often taken to use orthonormal coordinate directions: the axes are mutually perpendicular and use equal unit scales.

Their standard basis vectors in two dimensions are:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf e_1= \begin{pmatrix} 1\\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf e_2= \begin{pmatrix} 0\\ 1 \end{pmatrix} }. $$

Axes and Basis Vectors

Coordinate axes are geometric lines, while basis vectors specify the positive directions and unit scales associated with those axes.

They are closely related but not identical objects.

Changing Coordinate Axes

A coordinate system need not use the standard horizontal and vertical directions. The axes may be translated, rotated, or otherwise replaced by a different basis.

The geometric point remains the same, while its coordinates may change.

$$ \boxed{ \text{point fixed} \not\Rightarrow \text{coordinates fixed} }. $$

Rotated Axes

If coordinate axes are rotated by an angle $\theta$, coordinates can be related by a rotation transformation.

One common convention is:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} x&=x'\cos\theta-y'\sin\theta,\\ y&=x'\sin\theta+y'\cos\theta. \end{aligned} } $$

The exact sign convention depends on whether the points or the coordinate axes are being rotated, so context must be checked carefully.

Translated Axes

If a new origin is placed at $(h,k)$ while keeping the axes parallel to the old ones, a common coordinate relation is:

$$ \boxed{ x=X+h, \qquad y=Y+k }. $$

Translation of axes can simplify equations whose natural center is not the original origin.

Axes in Conic Sections

Conic sections often possess distinguished geometric axes.

An ellipse has a:

  • major axis,
  • minor axis.

These pass through the center and align with the ellipse's principal directions.

Major Axis of an Ellipse

The major axis lies along the longest diameter through the center of an ellipse.

For:

$$ \frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1, \qquad a>b>0, $$

the major axis lies along the $x$-axis.

Minor Axis

In the same standard ellipse, the minor axis lies along the $y$-axis.

Axis of a Parabola

A parabola has an axis of symmetry passing through its vertex and focus.

For:

$$ y=a(x-h)^2+k, $$

the axis of symmetry is:

$$ \boxed{ x=h }. $$

Axis of Symmetry

An axis of symmetry is a line across which reflection leaves a geometric figure unchanged.

For a figure $F$ and reflection $R$ across a symmetry axis:

$$ \boxed{ R(F)=F }. $$

Examples of Symmetry Axes

A non-square rectangle has two axes of symmetry, while a square has four. A circle has infinitely many axes of symmetry: every diameter lies on one.

Axis of Rotation

In three-dimensional geometry, an axis of rotation is a line about which an object or coordinate system rotates.

Points lying on the axis remain fixed under a pure rotation about that axis.

Rotation about a Coordinate Axis

A rotation about the $z$-axis changes $x$ and $y$ coordinates while leaving $z$ unchanged.

One standard rotation matrix is:

$$ \boxed{ R_z(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta&-\sin\theta&0\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta&0\\ 0&0&1 \end{pmatrix} }. $$

Principal Axes

In geometry, linear algebra, mechanics, and quadratic-form analysis, principal axes are distinguished directions that simplify the structure being studied.

For example, rotating coordinate axes to principal directions can remove certain cross terms from quadratic equations.

Quadratic Forms

A quadratic expression such as:

$$ Ax^2+Bxy+Cy^2 $$

may contain a mixed $xy$ term. A suitable rotation of axes can sometimes transform it into:

$$ \boxed{ \lambda_1u^2+\lambda_2v^2 }, $$

where the new axes align with principal directions.

Axes on Graphs

Graphs commonly display horizontal and vertical axes even when the plotted quantities are not literally called $x$ and $y$.

For example:

  • time may appear on the horizontal axis,
  • temperature may appear on the vertical axis.

The coordinate roles are determined by the labels and scales, not by the physical direction alone.

Independent and Dependent Variables

In many function graphs, the independent variable is placed on the horizontal axis and the dependent variable on the vertical axis.

For:

$$ y=f(x), $$

the conventional assignment is:

$$ \boxed{ x\rightarrow\text{horizontal axis}, \qquad y\rightarrow\text{vertical axis} }. $$

This is a convention rather than an intrinsic law of functions.

Axis Scale

Equal physical distances on a graph need not represent equal numerical changes unless the scale is uniform.

Different axes may also use different numerical scales.

Truncated Axes

A graphing axis need not begin at zero. A truncated axis can be useful for showing fine variation, but it may visually exaggerate differences if interpreted carelessly.

Logarithmic Axes

An axis may use a logarithmic rather than linear scale.

On a logarithmic axis, equal physical distances represent equal multiplicative ratios rather than equal additive differences.

Polar Coordinate Axes

Polar coordinates use a reference direction rather than two perpendicular Cartesian axes in the same way.

The direction $\theta=0$ is called the polar axis.

$$ \boxed{ \theta=0 } $$

usually corresponds to the positive Cartesian $x$-direction.

Complex Plane Axes

In the complex plane:

$$ z=x+iy, $$

the horizontal axis is the real axis and the vertical axis is the imaginary axis.

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Re}(z)=x, \qquad \operatorname{Im}(z)=y }. $$

Number Line as a Single Axis

A one-dimensional coordinate system may be represented by a single axis:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{R} } $$

with an origin, orientation, and scale.

Axes versus Coordinates

Axes are reference lines or directions. Coordinates are numerical values describing location relative to those axes.

$$ \boxed{ \text{axes} \ne \text{coordinates} }. $$

Axes versus Basis

A basis is an algebraic collection of vectors from which vectors in a space can be constructed. Coordinate axes are geometric manifestations of the directions associated with a chosen basis.

The concepts are connected, but they should not be treated as exact synonyms.

Axes versus Axis Labels

The symbols $x$, $y$, and $z$ are labels assigned to axes. They are not the axes themselves.

A different coordinate system could use:

$$ u,\ v,\ w $$

for corresponding coordinate directions.

Common Misconception

The $x$-axis and $y$-axis are not merely the visible edges of a graphing window. Mathematically, they are complete lines extending indefinitely in both directions.

$$ \boxed{ \text{coordinate axis} = \text{infinite reference line} }. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$y=0$ "The x-axis."
$x=0$ "The y-axis."
$(0,0)$ "The origin."
$x=h$ "The vertical line x equals h," or "an axis of symmetry at x equals h" when context supports it.
$R_z(\theta)$ "A rotation through theta about the z-axis."
$\theta=0$ "The polar axis," in standard polar coordinates.

Axes is pronounced like AK-seez when used as the plural of axis. It should not be confused in writing with axes as the plural of the cutting tool axe; mathematical context ordinarily removes the ambiguity.

Defense Notation Advice: When discussing coordinate axes, specify which coordinate system is being used. The phrase the axis may refer to an $x$-, $y$-, or $z$-axis, a symmetry axis, a rotation axis, or another distinguished line depending on context.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Plural Form: Axes is the plural of axis.
  • General Meaning: Axes are distinguished reference lines or directions.
  • x-Axis: $$ \boxed{ y=0 }. $$
  • y-Axis: $$ \boxed{ x=0 }. $$
  • Origin: $$ \boxed{ (0,0) }. $$
  • Three Dimensions: Cartesian space uses $x$-, $y$-, and $z$-axes.
  • x-Axis in 3D: $$ \boxed{ y=z=0 }. $$
  • y-Axis in 3D: $$ \boxed{ x=z=0 }. $$
  • z-Axis in 3D: $$ \boxed{ x=y=0 }. $$
  • Quadrants: Two coordinate axes divide the Cartesian plane into four quadrants.
  • Octants: Three coordinate planes divide ordinary Cartesian space into eight octants.
  • x-Intercept: A point where a graph meets the x-axis and therefore has $y=0$.
  • y-Intercept: A point where a graph meets the y-axis and therefore has $x=0$.
  • Orthogonality: Standard Cartesian axes are mutually perpendicular.
  • Orientation: Axes have chosen positive and negative directions.
  • Scale: Coordinate values depend on the numerical scale assigned along each axis.
  • Change of Axes: Translating or rotating axes changes coordinate descriptions without changing the underlying geometric objects.
  • Axis of Symmetry: A line across which reflection leaves a figure unchanged.
  • Axis of Rotation: A line about which a geometric object is rotated.
  • Ellipse: Major and minor axes are distinguished perpendicular directions through its center.
  • Parabola: Its axis of symmetry passes through the vertex and focus.
  • Principal Axes: Distinguished directions chosen to simplify a geometric, algebraic, or physical structure.
  • Graphing Use: Horizontal and vertical axes may represent any appropriately labeled variables.
  • Logarithmic Axis: Equal physical spacing may represent equal ratios rather than equal differences.
  • Polar Axis: The reference direction $\theta=0$ in polar coordinates.
  • Complex Plane: The coordinate axes are called the real and imaginary axes.
  • One-Dimensional Case: A number line may serve as a single coordinate axis.
  • Basis Contrast: Basis vectors determine coordinate directions algebraically; axes represent those directions geometrically.
  • Coordinate Contrast: Axes are reference structures; coordinates are numbers locating points relative to them.
  • Common Error: Points on an axis are not ordinarily assigned to either neighboring quadrant.
  • Common Error: A graphing window displays only a finite portion of an axis; the mathematical line extends indefinitely.
  • Common Error: Do not assume all axes use the same numerical scale merely because they have the same physical length on a graph.
  • Terminological Contrast: Axis is one distinguished reference line or direction. Axes is its plural. Coordinate axes establish positional reference directions. Axis of symmetry describes reflectional invariance. Axis of rotation identifies the line around which rotation occurs. Principal axes identify mathematically distinguished directions that simplify a structure.
