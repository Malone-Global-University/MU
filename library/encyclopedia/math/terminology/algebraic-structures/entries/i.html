i

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

The symbol $i$ denotes the imaginary unit, the number defined by:

$$ \boxed{ i^2=-1 }. $$

Equivalently:

$$ \boxed{ i=\sqrt{-1} } $$

under the conventional choice of one square root of $-1$ in the complex number system.

The letter $i$ comes from the word imaginary. The terminology emerged historically when mathematicians began working systematically with square roots of negative numbers, objects that could not be represented within the real number system.

Although the historical adjective imaginary may suggest something unreal or fictitious in ordinary language, imaginary and complex numbers are fully established mathematical objects with precise algebraic and geometric structure.

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{C} = \{a+bi:a,b\in\mathbb{R}\} }. $$

The symbol $i$ extends the real numbers into the complex numbers and satisfies the polynomial equation:

$$ \boxed{ x^2+1=0 }. $$

Operational Nuance

Defining Relation

$$ \boxed{ i^2=-1 }. $$

Nearly every elementary calculation involving $i$ reduces ultimately to this identity.

Square Roots of Negative Numbers

For $a>0$:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt{-a} = i\sqrt{a} } $$

when using the principal square root convention.

Example

$$ \sqrt{-9} = \boxed{3i}. $$

Two Square Roots

The equation:

$$ x^2=-9 $$

has two solutions:

$$ \boxed{ x=\pm3i }. $$

Principal Square Root Distinction

The notation:

$$ \sqrt{-9}=3i $$

conventionally denotes one principal square root, while the equation $x^2=-9$ has both $3i$ and $-3i$ as solutions.

Powers of $i$

Successive powers repeat in a cycle of length four:

$$ \boxed{ i^1=i,\qquad i^2=-1,\qquad i^3=-i,\qquad i^4=1 }. $$

Power Cycle

$$ \boxed{ i^{n+4}=i^n }. $$

Reducing Large Powers

To simplify $i^n$, reduce the exponent modulo $4$.

For example:

$$ 37\equiv1\pmod4, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ i^{37}=i }. $$

Modulo-Four Rule

$$ \boxed{ i^n= \begin{cases} 1, & n\equiv0\pmod4,\\ i, & n\equiv1\pmod4,\\ -1, & n\equiv2\pmod4,\\ -i, & n\equiv3\pmod4. \end{cases} } $$

Negative Powers

Since:

$$ i(-i)=1, $$

one has:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{1}{i}=-i }. $$

Example with Negative Exponent

$$ i^{-1} = \boxed{-i}. $$

Square of $-i$

$$ (-i)^2 = i^2 = \boxed{-1}. $$

Two Roots of $x^2+1=0$

$$ \boxed{ x=i \qquad\text{or}\qquad x=-i }. $$

Complex Numbers

A complex number has the standard form:

$$ \boxed{ z=a+bi } $$

where $a,b\in\mathbb{R}$.

Real Part

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Re}(a+bi)=a }. $$

Imaginary Part

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Im}(a+bi)=b }. $$

The imaginary part is the real coefficient $b$, not the quantity $bi$.

Pure Imaginary Number

A number of the form:

$$ \boxed{ bi } $$

with real $b$ is called purely imaginary when $b\neq0$.

$i$ as a Complex Number

$$ \boxed{ i=0+1i }. $$

Therefore:

$$ \operatorname{Re}(i)=0, \qquad \operatorname{Im}(i)=1. $$

Addition of Complex Numbers

$$ \boxed{ (a+bi)+(c+di) = (a+c)+(b+d)i }. $$

Multiplication of Complex Numbers

$$ (a+bi)(c+di) = ac+adi+bci+bd i^2. $$

Using $i^2=-1$:

$$ \boxed{ (a+bi)(c+di) = (ac-bd)+(ad+bc)i }. $$

Example

$$ (2+3i)(4-i) = 8-2i+12i-3i^2. $$

Since $i^2=-1$:

$$ \boxed{ (2+3i)(4-i) = 11+10i }. $$

Complex Conjugate

The conjugate of:

$$ z=a+bi $$

is:

$$ \boxed{ \overline{z}=a-bi }. $$

Conjugate of $i$

$$ \boxed{ \overline{i}=-i }. $$

Product with Conjugate

$$ (a+bi)(a-bi) = a^2+b^2. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ z\overline{z} = |z|^2 }. $$

Modulus of $i$

$$ \boxed{ |i|=1 }. $$

Complex Plane

In the complex plane:

$$ a+bi \longleftrightarrow (a,b). $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ i\longleftrightarrow(0,1) }. $$

Imaginary Axis

Pure imaginary numbers lie on the vertical axis of the complex plane.

Multiplication by $i$ as Rotation

Let:

$$ z=a+bi. $$

Then:

$$ iz = ai+b i^2 = -b+ai. $$

In coordinate form:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b) \mapsto (-b,a) }. $$

This is a $90^\circ$ counterclockwise rotation about the origin.

Repeated Rotation

Multiplication by:

$$ i,\ i^2,\ i^3,\ i^4 $$

corresponds respectively to rotations through:

$$ \boxed{ 90^\circ,\ 180^\circ,\ 270^\circ,\ 360^\circ }. $$

Polar Form of $i$

Since $i$ has modulus $1$ and argument $\pi/2$:

$$ \boxed{ i = \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) + i\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) }. $$

Euler Form

Euler's formula:

$$ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta $$

gives:

$$ \boxed{ i=e^{i\pi/2} }. $$

Multiple Arguments

More generally:

$$ \boxed{ i = e^{i(\pi/2+2\pi k)} } $$

for every integer $k$.

Euler's Identity

Euler's formula with $\theta=\pi$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ e^{i\pi}+1=0 }. $$

Here $i$ connects exponentials, trigonometry, and complex arithmetic.

De Moivre's Theorem

For integer $n$:

$$ \boxed{ (\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^n = \cos(n\theta)+i\sin(n\theta) }. $$

The cyclic powers of $i$ are a simple special case.

Quadratic Formula

The imaginary unit allows the quadratic formula to represent solutions when the discriminant is negative.

For:

$$ ax^2+bx+c=0, $$

the solutions are:

$$ \boxed{ x = \frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a} }. $$

Negative Discriminant

If:

$$ b^2-4ac<0, $$

then:

$$ \sqrt{b^2-4ac} = i\sqrt{4ac-b^2}. $$

Example

Solve:

$$ x^2+4=0. $$

Then:

$$ x^2=-4, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ x=\pm2i }. $$

Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

The complex numbers, including $i$, make it possible for every nonconstant polynomial with complex coefficients to have at least one complex root.

Complex Conjugate Roots

If a polynomial has real coefficients and $a+bi$ is a nonreal root, then:

$$ \boxed{ a-bi } $$

is also a root.

Example

$$ x^2+1 = (x-i)(x+i). $$

Real Numbers inside the Complex Numbers

Every real number $a$ can be written:

$$ \boxed{ a=a+0i }. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{R}\subset\mathbb{C} }. $$

$i$ Is Not Real

No real number squares to $-1$, so:

$$ \boxed{ i\notin\mathbb{R} }. $$

$i$ Is Algebraic

The number $i$ is algebraic because it is a root of a nonzero polynomial with integer coefficients:

$$ \boxed{ x^2+1 }. $$

Minimal Polynomial over $\mathbb{Q}$

The minimal polynomial of $i$ over the rationals is:

$$ \boxed{ x^2+1 }. $$

Gaussian Integers

The Gaussian integers are:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{Z}[i] = \{a+bi:a,b\in\mathbb{Z}\} }. $$

Here $i$ extends the ordinary integers into a two-dimensional integer lattice in the complex plane.

Gaussian-Integer Units

$$ \boxed{ 1,\ -1,\ i,\ -i } $$

are exactly the units of $\mathbb{Z}[i]$.

Field Extension

Adjoining $i$ to the real numbers produces the complex field:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}(i) }. $$

Complex Conjugation as $i\mapsto-i$

Complex conjugation fixes all real numbers and sends:

$$ \boxed{ i\mapsto-i }. $$

Matrix Representation

Multiplication by $i$ on the complex plane can be represented by:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1\\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} }. $$

This matrix rotates vectors $90^\circ$ counterclockwise.

Matrix Square

Let:

$$ J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1\\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ J^2=-I }. $$

This mirrors the defining identity $i^2=-1$.

Eigenvalues of Rotation-Type Matrices

Real matrices can have complex eigenvalues involving $i$ even when all matrix entries are real.

Differential Equations

Complex exponentials simplify oscillatory differential equations because:

$$ e^{i\omega t} = \cos(\omega t) + i\sin(\omega t). $$

Second-Order Oscillation

The characteristic equation:

$$ r^2+\omega^2=0 $$

has roots:

$$ \boxed{ r=\pm i\omega }. $$

These lead naturally to sine and cosine solutions.

Electrical Engineering Notation

In electrical engineering, the symbol $j$ is often used instead of $i$ for the imaginary unit:

$$ \boxed{ j^2=-1 }. $$

This convention helps avoid confusion with $i$, which is frequently used for electric current.

Physics

The imaginary unit appears extensively in quantum mechanics, wave mechanics, and oscillatory systems.

Schrödinger Equation

A standard form begins:

$$ \boxed{ i\hbar \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = \hat{H}\psi }. $$

Here $i$ is essential to the complex phase structure of quantum evolution.

Fourier Analysis

Complex exponentials:

$$ \boxed{ e^{i\omega t} } $$

provide a compact language for representing oscillation and frequency.

Roots of Unity

The fourth roots of unity satisfy:

$$ z^4=1. $$

They are:

$$ \boxed{ 1,\ i,\ -1,\ -i }. $$

$i$ as a Root of Unity

Since:

$$ i^4=1, $$

$i$ is a fourth root of unity.

Primitive Fourth Root of Unity

The powers:

$$ i,\ i^2,\ i^3,\ i^4 $$

generate all fourth roots of unity, so $i$ is a primitive fourth root of unity.

Complex Logarithm

Since:

$$ i=e^{i(\pi/2+2\pi k)}, $$

the multivalued complex logarithm satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ \log i = i\left( \frac{\pi}{2}+2\pi k \right) } $$

for $k\in\mathbb{Z}$.

Principal Logarithm

Under the usual principal branch:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Log}(i) = \frac{\pi i}{2} }. $$

Complex Powers

Expressions such as:

$$ i^i $$

require complex logarithms and are therefore subtler than integer powers of $i$.

A Real Value of $i^i$

Using the principal logarithm:

$$ i^i = e^{i\operatorname{Log}(i)} = e^{-\pi/2}. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ i^i=e^{-\pi/2} } $$

on the principal branch.

Multivalued Complex-Power Caveat

Because the complex logarithm is multivalued, $i^i$ can have multiple values under the multivalued interpretation.

Square-Root Product Caution

Over the real nonnegative numbers:

$$ \sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}\sqrt{b} $$

holds under familiar conditions. It cannot be applied blindly to negative or complex values.

Classic Failure

A naive calculation might say:

$$ \sqrt{(-1)(-1)} = \sqrt{-1}\sqrt{-1} = i^2 = -1. $$

But:

$$ \sqrt{1}=1. $$

Therefore square-root product rules require careful domain conditions.

Common Error: Saying $i=-1$

The defining equation is:

$$ i^2=-1, $$

not $i=-1$.

Common Error: Saying $i^2=1$

$$ \boxed{ i^2=-1 }. $$

Common Error: Saying $\sqrt{-1}=\pm i$

The principal square root notation conventionally gives:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt{-1}=i }. $$

The equation:

$$ x^2=-1 $$

has the two solutions:

$$ \boxed{ x=\pm i }. $$

Common Error: Treating Imaginary Numbers as Undefined

Imaginary numbers are not undefined. They are well-defined elements of the complex number system.

Common Error: Saying the Imaginary Part of $a+bi$ Is $bi$

Standard notation defines:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Im}(a+bi)=b }. $$

Common Error: Forgetting the Four-Step Power Cycle

Powers of $i$ repeat modulo $4$, so high powers should be reduced using the exponent remainder modulo $4$.

Common Error: Assuming $i$ Has an Order Relation like Real Numbers

The usual real-number ordering does not extend to the complex numbers as an ordered field compatible with their field operations.

Thus statements such as:

$$ i>0 $$

have no standard meaning in the ordinary complex-number order structure.

Common Misconception: The imaginary unit $i$ is not a vague symbol for an impossible number. It is a precisely defined complex number satisfying $i^2=-1$. Its introduction creates a consistent algebraic extension of the real numbers in which negative numbers have square roots and every nonconstant polynomial can be studied within a single algebraically closed number system.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$i$ "The imaginary unit."
$i^2=-1$ "I squared equals negative one."
$\sqrt{-1}=i$ "The principal square root of negative one is i."
$a+bi$ "A plus b i," or "a plus b times the imaginary unit."
$\overline{i}=-i$ "The complex conjugate of i is negative i."
$i=e^{i\pi/2}$ "I equals e to the i pi over two."
$1,i,-1,-i$ "The four powers of i in one complete cycle."

The lowercase italic $i$ is standard mathematical notation for the imaginary unit. Context matters because the same letter may also denote an index, current, or another variable in other fields.

Electrical engineering often replaces $i$ with:

$$ \boxed{ j } $$

to avoid conflict with the conventional symbol $i$ for electric current.

Defense Notation Advice: Keep the defining identity $i^2=-1$ visible when simplifying complex expressions. Reduce integer powers of $i$ modulo $4$, distinguish $\sqrt{-1}=i$ from the equation $x^2=-1\Rightarrow x=\pm i$, and remember that $\operatorname{Im}(a+bi)=b$, not $bi$. In engineering contexts, verify whether the source uses $i$ or $j$ for the imaginary unit.
