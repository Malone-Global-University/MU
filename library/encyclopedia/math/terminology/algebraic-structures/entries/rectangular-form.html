Rectangular Form

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

Rectangular form most commonly refers to the representation of a complex number using its real and imaginary components:

$$ \boxed{ z=a+bi } $$

where $a,b\in\mathbb{R}$ and:

$$ \boxed{ i^2=-1 }. $$

The name rectangular comes from rectangular, or Cartesian, coordinates. The complex number $a+bi$ corresponds to the point:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b) } $$

in the complex plane. The horizontal coordinate records the real part and the vertical coordinate records the imaginary coefficient.

Rectangular form is also commonly called standard form or Cartesian form for a complex number.

Operational Nuance

General Form

$$ \boxed{ z=a+bi }. $$

Real Part

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Re}(z)=a }. $$

Imaginary Part

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Im}(z)=b }. $$

The imaginary part is the real coefficient $b$, not the entire term $bi$.

Complex-Plane Coordinates

$$ \boxed{ a+bi \longleftrightarrow (a,b) }. $$

Example

For:

$$ z=3-4i, $$

we have:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Re}(z)=3, \qquad \operatorname{Im}(z)=-4 }. $$

The corresponding point is $(3,-4)$.

Purely Real Numbers

A real number is already a complex number in rectangular form with $b=0$:

$$ \boxed{ 5=5+0i }. $$

Purely Imaginary Numbers

A purely imaginary number has $a=0$:

$$ \boxed{ -7i=0-7i }. $$

Equality in Rectangular Form

Two complex numbers in rectangular form are equal exactly when their corresponding real and imaginary parts are equal:

$$ \boxed{ a+bi=c+di \Longleftrightarrow a=c \text{ and } b=d }. $$

Addition

$$ \boxed{ (a+bi)+(c+di) = (a+c)+(b+d)i }. $$

Subtraction

$$ \boxed{ (a+bi)-(c+di) = (a-c)+(b-d)i }. $$

Multiplication

Multiply distributively and use $i^2=-1$:

$$ \boxed{ (a+bi)(c+di) = (ac-bd)+(ad+bc)i }. $$

Complex Conjugate

The conjugate of:

$$ z=a+bi $$

is:

$$ \boxed{ \overline{z}=a-bi }. $$

Conjugate Product

$$ \boxed{ z\overline{z} = a^2+b^2 }. $$

Modulus

The modulus of $z=a+bi$ is the distance from the origin to $(a,b)$:

$$ \boxed{ |z| = \sqrt{a^2+b^2} }. $$

Division

To divide rectangular complex numbers, multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the denominator:

$$ \frac{a+bi}{c+di} \cdot \frac{c-di}{c-di}. $$

For $c+di\neq0$:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{a+bi}{c+di} = \frac{ac+bd}{c^2+d^2} + \frac{bc-ad}{c^2+d^2}i }. $$

Rectangular vs. Polar Form

A nonzero complex number may also be represented in polar form:

$$ \boxed{ z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta) }. $$

where:

$$ \boxed{ r=|z| }. $$

Polar to Rectangular Conversion

Expand:

$$ r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta) = r\cos\theta+i(r\sin\theta). $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ a=r\cos\theta, \qquad b=r\sin\theta }. $$

Rectangular to Polar Conversion

From $z=a+bi$:

$$ \boxed{ r=\sqrt{a^2+b^2} }. $$

The angle $\theta$ must satisfy:

$$ \boxed{ \cos\theta=\frac{a}{r}, \qquad \sin\theta=\frac{b}{r} } $$

for $z\neq0$.

Argument

The direction angle of a nonzero complex number is called an argument:

$$ \boxed{ \arg z=\theta }. $$

Because angles differing by $2\pi$ describe the same direction:

$$ \boxed{ \theta+2\pi k, \qquad k\in\mathbb{Z} } $$

represent the same complex direction.

Quadrant Awareness

Using only:

$$ \tan\theta=\frac{b}{a} $$

may not identify the correct quadrant. The signs of both $a$ and $b$ must be used, or a quadrant-aware operation such as $\operatorname{atan2}(b,a)$ may be employed.

Exponential Form

Euler's formula gives:

$$ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ z=re^{i\theta} }. $$

Why Rectangular Form Is Useful

Rectangular form is especially convenient for addition, subtraction, equality comparison, coordinate interpretation, and separating real and imaginary parts.

Why Polar Form Is Useful

Polar or exponential form is often more convenient for multiplication, division, powers, roots, rotations, and magnitude-phase reasoning.

Example Conversion

Convert:

$$ 2\left( \cos\frac{\pi}{3} + i\sin\frac{\pi}{3} \right) $$

to rectangular form.

$$ a = 2\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = 1, $$
$$ b = 2\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \sqrt{3}. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ z=1+\sqrt{3}i }. $$

Common Misconception

Rectangular form does not mean that the complex number itself is a geometric rectangle. The term refers to representation by perpendicular Cartesian coordinate axes.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$a+bi$ "A plus b i," or "a complex number in rectangular form."
$\operatorname{Re}(z)=a$ "The real part of z is a."
$\operatorname{Im}(z)=b$ "The imaginary part of z is b."
$a+bi\leftrightarrow(a,b)$ "A plus b i corresponds to the point a comma b in the complex plane."
$r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)$ "R times cosine theta plus i sine theta," the polar trigonometric form.
$a=r\cos\theta,\ b=r\sin\theta$ "The rectangular coordinates are r cosine theta and r sine theta."

The instruction:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"write in rectangular form"} } $$

ordinarily means rewrite a complex number so that it appears as:

$$ \boxed{ a+bi }. $$

The terms rectangular form, Cartesian form, and standard form are often used interchangeably in elementary complex-number contexts, although terminology varies among texts.

Defense Notation Advice: In $a+bi$, identify $a$ as the real part and $b$ as the imaginary part; do not call $bi$ the imaginary part when using the standard function notation $\operatorname{Im}(z)$. When converting from polar form, use $a=r\cos\theta$ and $b=r\sin\theta$. When converting back, determine the argument with quadrant information rather than relying blindly on $\arctan(b/a)$.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Form: $z=a+bi$.
  • Real Part: $\operatorname{Re}(z)=a$.
  • Imaginary Part: $\operatorname{Im}(z)=b$.
  • Coordinate Interpretation: $a+bi$ corresponds to $(a,b)$.
  • Complex Plane: Real axis is horizontal and imaginary axis is vertical.
  • Purely Real Case: $b=0$.
  • Purely Imaginary Case: $a=0$.
  • Equality: Corresponding real and imaginary components must match.
  • Addition: Add real parts and imaginary parts separately.
  • Subtraction: Subtract corresponding components.
  • Multiplication: Distribute and use $i^2=-1$.
  • Division: Use the conjugate of the denominator.
  • Conjugate: $a+bi\mapsto a-bi$.
  • Modulus: $|z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$.
  • Polar Conversion: $a=r\cos\theta$ and $b=r\sin\theta$.
  • Polar Radius: $r=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$.
  • Argument: Direction angle determined by both rectangular coordinates.
  • Exponential Form: $z=re^{i\theta}$.
  • Arithmetic Advantage: Rectangular form is especially convenient for addition and subtraction.
  • Geometric Advantage: Directly displays Cartesian coordinates in the complex plane.
  • Polar Contrast: Polar form emphasizes magnitude and direction.
  • Terminology Variation: Often called Cartesian form or standard form.
  • Common Error: Calling $bi$ rather than $b$ the imaginary part in standard notation.
  • Common Error: Forgetting that $i^2=-1$ during multiplication.
  • Common Error: Reversing the real and imaginary coordinates.
  • Common Error: Using $\arctan(b/a)$ without correcting for quadrant.
  • Common Error: Treating rectangular and polar forms as different complex numbers rather than different representations of the same number.
  • Core Interpretation: Rectangular form represents a complex number by perpendicular real and imaginary components: $$ \boxed{ z=a+bi \longleftrightarrow (a,b) }. $$ It is the Cartesian representation of the complex plane and is particularly useful for componentwise arithmetic. It connects to polar form through: $$ \boxed{ a=r\cos\theta, \qquad b=r\sin\theta }, $$ with: $$ \boxed{ r=\sqrt{a^2+b^2} }. $$
