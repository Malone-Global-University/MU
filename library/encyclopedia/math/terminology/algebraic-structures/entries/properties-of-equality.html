Properties of Equality

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

The properties of equality are structural rules governing valid statements of the form:

$$ \boxed{ a=b }. $$

They describe what may legitimately be inferred from an equality and which transformations preserve equality.

The word equality comes from linguistic roots associated with equal, level, or of the same amount. In mathematics, equality is much stronger than approximate similarity or visual resemblance:

$$ \boxed{ a=b } $$

asserts that the two expressions denote the same mathematical value or object in the context under consideration.

The word property refers to a rule or structural characteristic that equality satisfies. Some equality properties describe equality itself as a relation:

  • reflexive property;
  • symmetric property;
  • transitive property.

Others describe operations that may be performed on equal quantities while preserving equality:

  • addition property of equality;
  • subtraction property of equality;
  • multiplication property of equality;
  • division property of equality;
  • substitution property of equality.
$$ \boxed{ \text{equal quantities remain equal under compatible identical operations} }. $$

This principle is one of the central operational foundations of algebra.

In equation solving, the familiar phrase do the same thing to both sides is an informal summary of several properties of equality. However, the precise rule depends on the operation, its domain, and whether that operation is reversible.

Operational Nuance

Equality as a Relation

Equality is an equivalence relation because it is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive.

Reflexive Property of Equality

Every mathematical object is equal to itself:

$$ \boxed{ a=a }. $$

Examples

$$ 7=7, \qquad x=x, \qquad A=A. $$

Why Reflexivity Matters

Reflexivity expresses the most basic self-identity requirement of equality.

Symmetric Property of Equality

If:

$$ a=b, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ b=a }. $$

Example

If:

$$ x+3=10, $$

then it is equally valid to write:

$$ \boxed{ 10=x+3 }. $$

Symmetry Does Not Change the Meaning

Reversing the two sides of an equation preserves the equality statement.

Transitive Property of Equality

If:

$$ a=b $$

and:

$$ b=c, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a=c }. $$

Example

If:

$$ x=5 $$

and:

$$ 5=y, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ x=y }. $$

Substitution Property of Equality

If two quantities are equal, one may replace the other in an expression whenever the replacement occurs in a mathematically valid context.

$$ \boxed{ a=b \Longrightarrow F(a)=F(b) } $$

whenever $F$ is defined at both inputs.

Example

If:

$$ x=4, $$

then:

$$ x^2+3 = 4^2+3 = 19. $$

Substitution in Formulas

If:

$$ A=\pi r^2 $$

and:

$$ r=5, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ A=25\pi }. $$

Addition Property of Equality

If:

$$ a=b, $$

then for any compatible quantity $c$:

$$ \boxed{ a+c=b+c }. $$

Example

From:

$$ x-4=7, $$

add $4$ to both sides:

$$ x-4+4 = 7+4. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ x=11 }. $$

Subtraction Property of Equality

If:

$$ a=b, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a-c=b-c }. $$

Example

From:

$$ x+9=14, $$

subtract $9$ from both sides:

$$ \boxed{ x=5 }. $$

Multiplication Property of Equality

If:

$$ a=b, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ ac=bc } $$

for every compatible factor $c$.

Example

From:

$$ \frac{x}{5}=3, $$

multiply both sides by $5$:

$$ \boxed{ x=15 }. $$

Multiplication by Zero

Multiplying both sides of a true equation by zero preserves truth:

$$ a=b \Longrightarrow 0a=0b. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ 0=0 }. $$

But Multiplication by Zero Is Not Reversible

From:

$$ 0a=0b, $$

one cannot conclude:

$$ a=b. $$

Multiplication by zero destroys information.

Division Property of Equality

If:

$$ a=b $$

and:

$$ c\neq0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{a}{c} = \frac{b}{c} }. $$

Nonzero Restriction

The condition:

$$ \boxed{ c\neq0 } $$

is essential because division by zero is undefined.

Example

From:

$$ 6x=42, $$

divide both sides by $6$:

$$ \boxed{ x=7 }. $$

Cancellation as Division

From:

$$ ac=bc $$

one may infer:

$$ \boxed{ a=b } $$

only when cancellation by $c$ is valid, such as when $c\neq0$ in a field or integral domain.

Cancellation Caveat

In rings with zero divisors, even a nonzero factor may fail to be cancellable.

Example Modulo $6$

$$ 2\cdot1 \equiv 2\cdot4 \pmod6, $$

because both sides are congruent to $2$, but:

$$ 1\not\equiv4\pmod6. $$

Thus cancellation depends on the algebraic structure.

Applying the Same Function to Both Sides

If:

$$ a=b, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ f(a)=f(b) } $$

whenever $f$ is defined at both $a$ and $b$.

Squaring Both Sides

From:

$$ a=b, $$

it follows that:

$$ \boxed{ a^2=b^2 }. $$

But Squaring Is Not Reversible over the Reals

From:

$$ a^2=b^2, $$

one can conclude only:

$$ \boxed{ a=b \quad\text{or}\quad a=-b }. $$

Example of Information Loss

$$ 3^2=(-3)^2 $$

even though:

$$ 3\neq-3. $$

Taking Square Roots

From:

$$ x^2=25, $$

the real solutions are:

$$ \boxed{ x=\pm5 }. $$

One must not write merely $x=\sqrt{25}=5$ and lose the negative solution.

Applying an Injective Function

If $f$ is one-to-one and:

$$ f(a)=f(b), $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a=b }. $$

Why Injectivity Matters

Reversing an equality after applying a function requires that distinct inputs not collapse to the same output.

Exponentiation

If:

$$ a=b, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ c^a=c^b } $$

wherever the expressions are defined.

Exponential Reversal

For a real exponential function with:

$$ c>0, \qquad c\neq1, $$

the function is injective, so:

$$ \boxed{ c^a=c^b \Longrightarrow a=b }. $$

Logarithms

If:

$$ a=b $$

with:

$$ a>0, \qquad b>0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \log_c a = \log_c b } $$

for a valid logarithm base $c>0$, $c\neq1$.

Logarithmic Reversal

Because logarithms are injective on their positive real domain:

$$ \boxed{ \log_c a = \log_c b \Longrightarrow a=b }. $$

Taking Reciprocals

If:

$$ a=b $$

and both are nonzero, then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{1}{a} = \frac{1}{b} }. $$

Reciprocal Reversal

Over nonzero real or complex numbers:

$$ \frac{1}{a} = \frac{1}{b} \Longrightarrow a=b. $$

Adding Different Quantities to the Two Sides

From:

$$ a=b, $$

it does not generally follow that:

$$ a+c=b+d $$

unless:

$$ c=d. $$

Same Transformation Principle

Equality is preserved by applying the same valid transformation to both sides:

$$ \boxed{ a=b \Longrightarrow T(a)=T(b) }. $$

Forward Preservation vs. Equivalence

A transformation may preserve truth in the forward direction without being reversible.

$$ \boxed{ a=b \Longrightarrow T(a)=T(b) } $$

does not automatically imply:

$$ T(a)=T(b) \Longrightarrow a=b. $$

Equivalent Transformations

When both directions hold:

$$ \boxed{ a=b \iff T(a)=T(b) }, $$

the transformation is equivalence-preserving under the stated conditions.

Equation Solving

Solving an equation means finding all values that make the equality true.

Solution-Preserving Step

A reversible equality transformation preserves exactly the same solution set.

Example

$$ 3x+5=20 $$

subtract $5$:

$$ 3x=15. $$

divide by $3$:

$$ \boxed{ x=5 }. $$

Equivalent Equation Chain

$$ \boxed{ 3x+5=20 \iff 3x=15 \iff x=5 }. $$

Extraneous Solutions

Nonreversible steps can enlarge the candidate solution set.

Example from Squaring

Start with:

$$ x=3. $$

Squaring gives:

$$ x^2=9. $$

But the squared equation also has:

$$ x=-3 $$

as a solution. Thus squaring created an additional candidate.

Verification

When solving equations using nonreversible operations, candidate solutions should be substituted back into the original equation.

Clearing Denominators

Multiplying an equation by a common denominator can simplify rational equations.

Domain Restriction

Denominator zeros must remain excluded.

For example:

$$ \frac{x+1}{x-2}=3 $$

requires:

$$ \boxed{ x\neq2 }. $$

Multiply by the Denominator

For allowed $x$:

$$ x+1 = 3(x-2). $$

Therefore:

$$ x+1=3x-6, $$
$$ 7=2x, $$
$$ \boxed{ x=\frac{7}{2} }. $$

Multiplying by an Expression That Could Be Zero

Care is required when multiplying or dividing by expressions containing variables. Their possible zero values must be tracked.

Example of Invalid Division

From:

$$ x(x-1)=0, $$

dividing both sides by $x$ would eliminate the valid solution:

$$ \boxed{ x=0 }. $$

Correct Zero-Product Reasoning

$$ \boxed{ x(x-1)=0 \Longrightarrow x=0 \text{ or } x=1 }. $$

Cross Multiplication

If:

$$ \frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d} $$

with:

$$ b\neq0, \qquad d\neq0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ ad=bc }. $$

Why Cross Multiplication Works

Multiply both sides by the nonzero product $bd$:

$$ bd \left( \frac{a}{b} \right) = bd \left( \frac{c}{d} \right). $$

Simplifying gives:

$$ ad=bc. $$

Cross Multiplication Is Not a Separate Law of Equality

It is a consequence of multiplication and cancellation under nonzero-denominator conditions.

Identity Equations

Some equalities are true for every allowed value in a domain.

$$ \boxed{ 2(x+3) = 2x+6 } $$

is an identity over ordinary real or complex arithmetic.

Conditional Equations

Other equalities are true only for particular values:

$$ \boxed{ x+3=10 } $$

is true only when:

$$ x=7. $$

Contradictory Equations

An equation may have no solutions.

For example:

$$ x+1=x+3 $$

subtracting $x$ from both sides yields:

$$ 1=3, $$

which is false.

Equality vs. Equivalence

Equality means the same mathematical value or object, while equivalence can mean sameness under a weaker relation.

Example: Congruence

$$ 8 \equiv 2 \pmod6 $$

does not mean:

$$ 8=2. $$

Example: Equivalent Fractions

In contrast:

$$ \frac{1}{2} = \frac{2}{4} $$

is literal equality as rational numbers.

Equality of Functions

Two functions are equal when they have the same domain, codomain under the adopted convention, and output for every input in the domain.

Pointwise Equality

$$ \boxed{ f=g \Longrightarrow f(x)=g(x) \text{ for every allowed }x }. $$

Equality of Matrices

Two matrices are equal when they have the same dimensions and corresponding entries are equal.

$$ \boxed{ A=B \iff a_{ij}=b_{ij} \text{ for all }i,j }. $$

Equality of Sets

Two sets are equal exactly when they contain the same elements.

$$ \boxed{ A=B \iff A\subseteq B \text{ and } B\subseteq A }. $$

Equality of Polynomials

Two formal polynomials are equal when corresponding coefficients are equal.

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{k=0}^{n}a_kx^k = \sum_{k=0}^{n}b_kx^k \iff a_k=b_k \text{ for all }k }. $$

Preserving Units

Equality transformations in applied problems must preserve dimensional consistency.

If:

$$ d=vt, $$

one may divide by $t$ only when:

$$ t\neq0. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ v=\frac{d}{t} }. $$

Balancing Interpretation

A scale-balance metaphor is often used to introduce equation transformations: whatever operation is performed on one side must also be performed compatibly on the other side.

Limits of the Balance Metaphor

The metaphor is useful for reversible arithmetic operations but does not by itself explain domain restrictions, noninjective transformations, zero divisors, or abstract algebraic settings.

Common Error: Changing Only One Side

From:

$$ x+5=12, $$

one cannot subtract $5$ only from the left:

$$ x=12. $$

The corresponding operation must be applied to both sides.

Common Error: Dividing by Zero

The division property requires a nonzero divisor.

Common Error: Canceling a Variable That Could Be Zero

Dividing by $x$ requires:

$$ \boxed{ x\neq0 }. $$

If $x=0$ is possible, cancellation can discard a valid case.

Common Error: Assuming Every Same-Side Operation Is Reversible

Squaring both sides preserves equality but may introduce additional solutions when the step is reversed.

Common Error: Forgetting Domain Restrictions

Logarithms require positive real arguments, denominators must be nonzero, and even real roots require nonnegative radicands.

Common Error: Taking Square Roots without $\pm$ in Equation Solving

From:

$$ x^2=a, $$

the real equation generally gives:

$$ \boxed{ x=\pm\sqrt{a} } $$

for $a>0$.

Common Error: Writing $\sqrt{x^2}=x$ for Every Real $x$

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt{x^2}=|x| }. $$

Common Error: Multiplying by Zero and Then Canceling Zero

Zero has no multiplicative inverse, so cancellation is impossible.

Common Error: Treating Cross Multiplication as Magic

Cross multiplication is multiplication by nonzero denominators followed by simplification.

Common Error: Treating Equality Like Approximation

The symbols:

$$ = \qquad\text{and}\qquad \approx $$

have different meanings.

Common Error: Reading the Equal Sign as "The Answer Is"

Equality is relational and symmetric. In:

$$ 4+5=9, $$

the symbol means both sides have the same value, not merely that the right side is an answer produced by the left.

Common Error: Chaining Unequal Expressions with Equal Signs

A chain:

$$ a=b=c $$

asserts:

$$ a=b, \qquad b=c, \qquad a=c. $$

Every member of the chain must therefore have the same value.

Common Error: Assuming a True Result Justifies Invalid Intermediate Steps

A correct final answer does not make an algebraically invalid transformation valid. Each equality in a derivation must be justified independently.

Common Error: Confusing Equality Properties with Inequality Properties

Multiplying or dividing an inequality by a negative quantity reverses its direction, while equality has no direction to reverse.

Common Error: Treating Substitution as Mere Numerical Plug-In

Substitution is a general structural principle: equal objects may replace one another in valid expressions, equations, formulas, functions, and proofs.

Common Misconception: "Do the same thing to both sides" is useful but incomplete. The operation must be defined, domain restrictions must be respected, and reversibility matters when solving equations. Adding the same quantity or multiplying by a nonzero scalar gives an equivalent equation, while squaring both sides may only give a one-way implication and can introduce extraneous solutions.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$a=a$ "A equals itself," the reflexive property of equality.
$a=b\Rightarrow b=a$ "If a equals b, then b equals a," the symmetric property.
$a=b,\ b=c\Rightarrow a=c$ "If a equals b and b equals c, then a equals c," the transitive property.
$a=b\Rightarrow a+c=b+c$ "Adding the same quantity to both sides preserves equality."
$a=b\Rightarrow ac=bc$ "Multiplying both sides by the same factor preserves equality."
$a=b,\ c\neq0\Rightarrow a/c=b/c$ "Dividing both sides by the same nonzero quantity preserves equality."
$a=b\Rightarrow f(a)=f(b)$ "Equal quantities may be substituted into the same defined function or expression."

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"add the same quantity to both sides"} } $$

refers to the addition property of equality.

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"divide both sides by }c\text{"} } $$

carries the hidden requirement:

$$ \boxed{ c\neq0 }. $$

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"substitute equals for equals"} } $$

expresses the substitution property of equality.

The symbol:

$$ \Longrightarrow $$

means one statement implies another, whereas:

$$ \Longleftrightarrow $$

indicates logical equivalence when both directions are valid.

Defense Notation Advice: Use an equal sign only when the two expressions genuinely have the same value or denote the same object. When solving equations, distinguish one-way implications from reversible equivalences. State nonzero restrictions before division or cancellation, preserve domain restrictions, and verify candidate solutions after noninjective transformations such as squaring.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: Structural rules governing equality and equality-preserving transformations.
  • Reflexive Property: $a=a$.
  • Symmetric Property: $a=b\Rightarrow b=a$.
  • Transitive Property: $a=b$ and $b=c$ imply $a=c$.
  • Substitution Property: Equal quantities may replace one another in valid contexts.
  • Addition Property: $a=b\Rightarrow a+c=b+c$.
  • Subtraction Property: $a=b\Rightarrow a-c=b-c$.
  • Multiplication Property: $a=b\Rightarrow ac=bc$.
  • Division Property: $a=b\Rightarrow a/c=b/c$ when $c\neq0$.
  • Same-Transformation Principle: Applying the same defined function to equal quantities preserves equality.
  • Forward Preservation: $a=b\Rightarrow T(a)=T(b)$.
  • Reversibility: Requires appropriate injectivity or inverse conditions.
  • Equivalent Equations: Reversible transformations preserve exactly the same solution set.
  • One-Way Transformations: May enlarge the candidate solution set.
  • Squaring: Preserves equality forward but is not injective over the reals.
  • Square-Root Equation Solving: $x^2=a$ may produce both positive and negative roots.
  • Absolute-Value Caveat: $\sqrt{x^2}=|x|$ for real $x$.
  • Exponentiation: Can be reversible when the exponential function is injective on the domain.
  • Logarithms: Require positive real arguments in standard real algebra.
  • Reciprocals: Require nonzero quantities.
  • Cancellation: Requires a cancellable factor.
  • Scalar Cancellation: Valid for nonzero factors over fields and integral domains.
  • Zero-Divisor Caveat: Cancellation can fail in more general rings.
  • Multiplication by Zero: Preserves a true equality but destroys reversibility.
  • Division by Zero: Undefined.
  • Equation Solving: Properties of equality justify isolation of unknown quantities.
  • Verification: Essential after potentially nonreversible steps.
  • Extraneous Solution: Candidate created by a transformation but not satisfying the original equation.
  • Rational Equations: Denominator restrictions must be preserved.
  • Clearing Denominators: Uses multiplication by a common denominator on the allowed domain.
  • Cross Multiplication: Consequence of multiplying by nonzero denominators.
  • Variable Division: Requires checking whether the variable expression can equal zero.
  • Zero-Product Property: Often safer than dividing by a possibly zero factor.
  • Identity Equation: True for every allowed input.
  • Conditional Equation: True only for selected inputs.
  • Contradictory Equation: Has no solutions.
  • Equality vs. Congruence: Equality is stronger than modular equivalence.
  • Equality vs. Approximation: $=$ and $\approx$ are distinct relations.
  • Function Equality: Requires matching outputs across the relevant domain and compatible function structure.
  • Matrix Equality: Corresponding entries must match.
  • Set Equality: Each set must contain exactly the same elements.
  • Polynomial Equality: Corresponding coefficients must be equal.
  • Formula Rearrangement: Equality properties justify solving formulas for different variables.
  • Units and Dimensions: Equality requires compatible dimensions in applied mathematics.
  • Proof: Equality properties support substitution and transitive chains of equal quantities.
  • Balance Metaphor: Useful elementary model for applying identical operations to both sides.
  • Balance-Metaphor Limitation: Does not encode every domain, invertibility, or abstract-algebra caveat.

  • Core Interpretation: The properties of equality formalize the idea that equal quantities may be manipulated consistently: $$ \boxed{ a=b \Longrightarrow a+c=b+c }, $$ $$ \boxed{ a=b \Longrightarrow ac=bc }, $$ and, when $c\neq0$, $$ \boxed{ a=b \Longrightarrow \frac{a}{c} = \frac{b}{c} }. $$ Equality is also reflexive, symmetric, and transitive, while substitution allows equal quantities to replace one another in valid mathematical contexts. These rules justify equation solving, formula rearrangement, algebraic proof, and symbolic transformation.
