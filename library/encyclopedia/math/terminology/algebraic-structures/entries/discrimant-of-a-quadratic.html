Discriminant of a Quadratic

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

The discriminant of a quadratic is the expression:

$$ \boxed{\Delta=b^2-4ac} $$

associated with a quadratic polynomial or equation:

$$ ax^2+bx+c, \qquad a\neq0. $$

The symbol $\Delta$, the uppercase Greek letter delta, is commonly used for the discriminant, although many elementary texts simply write:

$$ \boxed{b^2-4ac}. $$

The term discriminant comes from the idea of discriminating or distinguishing between cases. The sign and value of $b^2-4ac$ distinguish important properties of the roots of a quadratic equation.

In particular, for a quadratic equation with real coefficients:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \Delta>0 &\Longrightarrow \text{two distinct real roots},\\ \Delta=0 &\Longrightarrow \text{one repeated real root},\\ \Delta<0 &\Longrightarrow \text{two nonreal complex-conjugate roots}. \end{aligned} } $$

Thus the discriminant reveals the qualitative nature of the roots without requiring the roots to be fully calculated first.

Operational Nuance

Quadratic Standard Form

The discriminant applies to a quadratic written in standard form:

$$ \boxed{ ax^2+bx+c=0, \qquad a\neq0 }. $$

The coefficients are:

  • $a$ — quadratic coefficient,
  • $b$ — linear coefficient,
  • $c$ — constant term.

Definition

$$ \boxed{ \Delta=b^2-4ac }. $$

Connection with the Quadratic Formula

The quadratic formula is:

$$ \boxed{ x= \frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a} }. $$

The discriminant is exactly the expression beneath the square root:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt{\Delta} = \sqrt{b^2-4ac} }. $$

Consequently, the sign of $\Delta$ determines whether the square-root portion is positive real, zero, or nonreal.

Positive Discriminant

If:

$$ \boxed{\Delta>0}, $$

then $\sqrt{\Delta}$ is a positive real number. The expressions:

$$ \frac{-b+\sqrt{\Delta}}{2a} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{-b-\sqrt{\Delta}}{2a} $$

are different, giving:

$$ \boxed{\text{two distinct real roots}}. $$

Example: Positive Discriminant

Consider:

$$ x^2-5x+6=0. $$

Here:

$$ a=1,\qquad b=-5,\qquad c=6. $$

Therefore:

$$ \Delta = (-5)^2-4(1)(6) = 25-24 = \boxed{1}. $$

Since:

$$ \Delta>0, $$

there are two distinct real roots.

Indeed:

$$ x^2-5x+6 = (x-2)(x-3), $$

so:

$$ \boxed{x=2,\;3}. $$

Zero Discriminant

If:

$$ \boxed{\Delta=0}, $$

then:

$$ \sqrt{\Delta}=0. $$

Both branches of the quadratic formula give the same result:

$$ \boxed{ x=-\frac{b}{2a} }. $$

This is one real root of multiplicity $2$, often called a double root or repeated root.

Example: Zero Discriminant

Consider:

$$ x^2-6x+9=0. $$

Then:

$$ \Delta = (-6)^2-4(1)(9) = 36-36 = \boxed{0}. $$

Therefore the quadratic has one repeated real root:

$$ x^2-6x+9 = (x-3)^2, $$
$$ \boxed{x=3\text{ with multiplicity }2}. $$

Negative Discriminant

If:

$$ \boxed{\Delta<0}, $$

then $\sqrt{\Delta}$ is not real. Over the complex numbers:

$$ \sqrt{\Delta} = i\sqrt{-\Delta}. $$

Therefore the quadratic has:

$$ \boxed{\text{two nonreal complex-conjugate roots}}. $$

Example: Negative Discriminant

Consider:

$$ x^2+2x+5=0. $$

Here:

$$ \Delta = 2^2-4(1)(5) = 4-20 = \boxed{-16}. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{\Delta<0}, $$

and there are no real roots. Using the quadratic formula:

$$ x = \frac{-2\pm\sqrt{-16}}{2} = -1\pm2i. $$

Geometric Interpretation

For the quadratic function:

$$ y=ax^2+bx+c, $$

its real roots are the $x$-coordinates where the parabola intersects the $x$-axis.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \Delta>0 &\Longrightarrow \text{two }x\text{-intercepts},\\ \Delta=0 &\Longrightarrow \text{one tangent }x\text{-intercept},\\ \Delta<0 &\Longrightarrow \text{no }x\text{-intercepts}. \end{aligned} } $$

Vertex Connection

The vertex of:

$$ f(x)=ax^2+bx+c $$

occurs at:

$$ x_v=-\frac{b}{2a}. $$

Evaluating the function there gives:

$$ \boxed{ f\left(-\frac{b}{2a}\right) = -\frac{\Delta}{4a} }. $$

Thus the discriminant also encodes how the vertex lies relative to the $x$-axis.

Completed-Square Form

Completing the square gives:

$$ \boxed{ ax^2+bx+c = a\left( x+\frac{b}{2a} \right)^2 - \frac{\Delta}{4a} }. $$

This identity makes the connection among discriminant, vertex height, and root structure explicit.

Distance Between the Two Roots

When $\Delta>0$, the two real roots are:

$$ x_1=\frac{-b-\sqrt{\Delta}}{2a}, \qquad x_2=\frac{-b+\sqrt{\Delta}}{2a}. $$

Their difference is:

$$ x_2-x_1 = \frac{\sqrt{\Delta}}{a}. $$

Therefore their ordinary distance on the real line is:

$$ \boxed{ |x_2-x_1| = \frac{\sqrt{\Delta}}{|a|} }. $$

Rational vs. Irrational Roots

Suppose:

$$ a,b,c\in\mathbb{Q}, \qquad a\neq0. $$

If $\Delta$ is a square in $\mathbb{Q}$, then the roots are rational. If $\Delta>0$ but is not a square in $\mathbb{Q}$, the two real roots are irrational quadratic numbers.

Integer Coefficients

When:

$$ a,b,c\in\mathbb{Z}, $$

$\Delta$ is an integer. If $\Delta$ is a nonnegative perfect square, the roots are rational.

Factoring over the Reals

A real quadratic with:

$$ \Delta\geq0 $$

can be factored over $\mathbb{R}$ into linear factors:

$$ \boxed{ a(x-r_1)(x-r_2) }. $$

When $\Delta<0$, it is irreducible into real linear factors.

Factoring over the Complex Numbers

Every quadratic with complex coefficients and nonzero leading coefficient has two complex roots counted with multiplicity, so over $\mathbb{C}$ it factors into linear factors.

Repeated Factor Criterion

A quadratic has a repeated linear factor exactly when:

$$ \boxed{\Delta=0}. $$

In that case:

$$ \boxed{ ax^2+bx+c = a\left(x+\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 }. $$

Connection with the Derivative

Let:

$$ f(x)=ax^2+bx+c. $$

Then:

$$ f'(x)=2ax+b. $$

If $\Delta=0$, the repeated root:

$$ r=-\frac{b}{2a} $$

satisfies both:

$$ \boxed{ f(r)=0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad f'(r)=0 }. $$

Thus a repeated root is simultaneously a zero of the polynomial and its derivative.

Scaling the Equation

Multiplying the entire quadratic equation by a nonzero scalar $k$ changes:

$$ (a,b,c)\to(ka,kb,kc). $$

The new discriminant becomes:

$$ (kb)^2-4(ka)(kc) = k^2(b^2-4ac). $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \Delta_{\text{new}}=k^2\Delta }. $$

Since $k^2>0$ for nonzero real $k$, the sign of the discriminant is unchanged. Thus multiplying an equation by a nonzero constant does not alter its root classification.

Need to Simplify First

Before computing the discriminant, a quadratic equation should be rearranged into:

$$ ax^2+bx+c=0. $$

For example:

$$ x^2+4=5x $$

must first become:

$$ x^2-5x+4=0. $$

Then:

$$ \Delta = (-5)^2-4(1)(4) = \boxed{9}. $$

Sign of $b$ Must Be Preserved

If:

$$ b=-7, $$

then:

$$ b^2=(-7)^2=49. $$

Parentheses are especially useful when substituting negative coefficients.

Common Misconception: The discriminant is not the square root $\sqrt{b^2-4ac}$. The discriminant itself is:

$$ \boxed{b^2-4ac}. $$

The square root of the discriminant appears inside the quadratic formula.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\Delta=b^2-4ac$ "The discriminant equals b squared minus four a c."
$\Delta>0$ "The discriminant is positive."
$\Delta=0$ "The discriminant is zero."
$\Delta<0$ "The discriminant is negative."
$\sqrt{\Delta}$ "The square root of the discriminant."
$x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{\Delta}}{2a}$ "X equals negative b plus or minus the square root of the discriminant, divided by two a."
$f(-b/(2a))=-\Delta/(4a)$ "The function value at negative b over two a equals negative delta over four a."

The uppercase Greek letter delta:

$$ \boxed{\Delta} $$

is a common symbol for the discriminant, but it is not universally required. Many elementary texts simply refer to the expression:

$$ \boxed{b^2-4ac}. $$

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{\text{"the discriminant is positive"}} $$

means:

$$ b^2-4ac>0. $$

Similarly, "zero discriminant" means:

$$ b^2-4ac=0. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Identify $a$, $b$, and $c$ only after placing the quadratic in standard form:

$$ ax^2+bx+c=0. $$

Include the signs of the coefficients. For example, in:

$$ 2x^2-7x+3=0, $$

use:

$$ \boxed{ a=2,\qquad b=-7,\qquad c=3 }. $$

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Definition: $$ \boxed{\Delta=b^2-4ac}. $$
  • Quadratic Requirement: $$ \boxed{ ax^2+bx+c, \qquad a\neq0 }. $$
  • Quadratic Formula: $$ \boxed{ x= \frac{-b\pm\sqrt{\Delta}}{2a} }. $$
  • Positive Discriminant: $$ \boxed{ \Delta>0 \Longrightarrow 2\text{ distinct real roots} }. $$
  • Zero Discriminant: $$ \boxed{ \Delta=0 \Longrightarrow 1\text{ repeated real root} }. $$
  • Negative Discriminant: $$ \boxed{ \Delta<0 \Longrightarrow 2\text{ nonreal complex-conjugate roots} }. $$
  • Root Multiplicity: When $\Delta=0$, the single distinct root has multiplicity $2$.
  • Repeated Root Formula: $$ \boxed{ x=-\frac{b}{2a} } $$ when $\Delta=0$.
  • Graph with Positive Discriminant: The parabola crosses the $x$-axis at two distinct points.
  • Graph with Zero Discriminant: The parabola is tangent to the $x$-axis at its vertex.
  • Graph with Negative Discriminant: The parabola has no real $x$-intercepts.
  • Vertex $x$-Coordinate: $$ \boxed{ x_v=-\frac{b}{2a} }. $$
  • Vertex $y$-Coordinate: $$ \boxed{ y_v=-\frac{\Delta}{4a} }. $$
  • Completed-Square Identity: $$ \boxed{ ax^2+bx+c = a\left(x+\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 - \frac{\Delta}{4a} }. $$
  • Upward-Opening Parabola: If $a>0$, then $\Delta<0$ implies the vertex lies above the $x$-axis.
  • Downward-Opening Parabola: If $a<0$, then $\Delta<0$ implies the vertex lies below the $x$-axis.
  • Zero Discriminant Geometry: $$ \Delta=0 $$ means the vertex lies exactly on the $x$-axis.
  • Positive Discriminant Geometry: The vertex lies on the opposite side of the $x$-axis from the direction in which the parabola opens.
  • Real Factorization: If $\Delta>0$: $$ \boxed{ f(x)=a(x-r_1)(x-r_2) } $$ with distinct real $r_1,r_2$.
  • Repeated Factorization: If $\Delta=0$: $$ \boxed{ f(x)=a(x-r)^2 }. $$
  • Irreducibility over $\mathbb{R}$: If $\Delta<0$, a real quadratic cannot be factored into real linear factors.
  • Complex Factorization: When $\Delta<0$, the quadratic factors over $\mathbb{C}$ using a conjugate pair of nonreal roots.
  • Conjugate Root Pair: For real coefficients: $$ \boxed{ \alpha+\beta i,\qquad \alpha-\beta i }. $$
  • Rational Roots: For rational coefficients, if $\Delta$ is a square in $\mathbb{Q}$, both roots are rational.
  • Irrational Real Roots: For rational coefficients, if $\Delta>0$ but is not a rational square, the roots are irrational.
  • Integer Coefficients: If $a,b,c\in\mathbb{Z}$, then: $$ \boxed{\Delta\in\mathbb{Z}}. $$
  • Perfect-Square Test: For integer coefficients, a nonnegative perfect-square discriminant implies rational roots.
  • Root Sum: If the roots are $r_1,r_2$: $$ \boxed{ r_1+r_2=-\frac{b}{a} }. $$
  • Root Product: $$ \boxed{ r_1r_2=\frac{c}{a} }. $$
  • Root Difference: $$ \boxed{ (r_1-r_2)^2 = \frac{\Delta}{a^2} }. $$
  • Root Separation: When $\Delta>0$: $$ \boxed{ |r_1-r_2| = \frac{\sqrt{\Delta}}{|a|} }. $$
  • Repeated Root Criterion: $$ \boxed{ \Delta=0 \iff f\text{ and }f'\text{ share a root} }. $$
  • Derivative Connection: For: $$ f(x)=ax^2+bx+c, $$ $$ f'(x)=2ax+b. $$
  • Common Root with Derivative: When $\Delta=0$: $$ f\left(-\frac{b}{2a}\right)=0 $$ and: $$ f'\left(-\frac{b}{2a}\right)=0. $$
  • Scaling: Multiplying all coefficients by nonzero $k$ changes the discriminant by: $$ \boxed{ \Delta\to k^2\Delta }. $$
  • Sign Invariance under Scaling: Multiplying the entire equation by a nonzero real constant does not change whether the discriminant is positive, zero, or negative.
  • Coefficient Signs: Negative coefficient values must be substituted with their signs intact.
  • Example: If $b=-4$: $$ b^2=(-4)^2=16. $$
  • Standard-Form Requirement: Move all terms to one side before identifying $a$, $b$, and $c$.
  • Missing Linear Term: In: $$ 3x^2-12=0, $$ use: $$ \boxed{b=0}. $$
  • Missing Constant Term: In: $$ 2x^2+5x=0, $$ use: $$ \boxed{c=0}. $$
  • Zero Constant Term: If $c=0$: $$ \Delta=b^2, $$ so the discriminant is nonnegative.
  • Zero Linear Coefficient: If $b=0$: $$ \boxed{ \Delta=-4ac }. $$
  • Parameter Problems: The condition: $$ \Delta=0 $$ is often used to find parameter values that produce a repeated root or tangency.
  • Example Parameter Condition: For: $$ x^2+kx+9=0, $$ a repeated root occurs when: $$ k^2-36=0, $$ giving: $$ \boxed{k=\pm6}. $$
  • Intersection Problems: Substituting one curve into another can produce a quadratic whose discriminant determines whether there are two, one, or no real intersections.
  • Line-Parabola Intersection: A zero discriminant commonly corresponds to a tangent line.
  • Two Intersections: A positive discriminant corresponds to two distinct real intersections in an appropriate quadratic intersection problem.
  • No Real Intersections: A negative discriminant corresponds to no real intersections in such a problem.
  • Optimization: The completed-square identity involving $\Delta$ gives direct information about the minimum or maximum value of a quadratic.
  • Minimum Value: If $a>0$: $$ \boxed{ f_{\min} = -\frac{\Delta}{4a} }. $$
  • Maximum Value: If $a<0$: $$ \boxed{ f_{\max} = -\frac{\Delta}{4a} }. $$
  • General Algebraic Idea: The quadratic discriminant is the simplest member of a broader family of polynomial discriminants that detect repeated roots.
  • Terminological Contrast: The discriminant is $b^2-4ac$; the quadratic formula is the entire root formula: $$ x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}. $$
  • Square-Root Contrast: $$ \sqrt{b^2-4ac} $$ is the square root of the discriminant, not the discriminant itself.
  • Core Interpretation: The discriminant condenses the root structure of a real quadratic into one quantity: $$ \boxed{ \Delta=b^2-4ac } $$ with: $$ \boxed{ \Delta>0:\;2\text{ distinct real roots}, \qquad \Delta=0:\;1\text{ repeated real root}, \qquad \Delta<0:\;2\text{ nonreal conjugate roots} }. $$
