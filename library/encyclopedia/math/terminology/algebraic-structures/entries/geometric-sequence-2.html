Geometric Sequence

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

A geometric sequence is a sequence in which each term after the first is obtained by multiplying the preceding term by a fixed quantity called the common ratio.

$$ \boxed{ a,\ ar,\ ar^2,\ ar^3,\ldots } $$

Here $a$ is the initial term and $r$ is the common ratio.

The modern term geometric sequence is largely synonymous with the older phrase geometric progression. In contemporary American textbooks, sequence is often preferred because it aligns the topic with the general theory of functions defined on discrete index sets.

The word geometric comes from historical ideas of continued proportion and geometric mean. If three positive quantities $a,b,c$ satisfy:

$$ \frac{b}{a}=\frac{c}{b}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ b^2=ac }, $$

making $b$ the geometric mean of $a$ and $c$.

The central modern definition is the recurrence:

$$ \boxed{ a_{n+1}=ra_n }. $$

Geometric sequences therefore encode constant multiplicative change rather than constant additive change.

Operational Nuance

Sequence Structure

A geometric sequence has the form:

$$ \boxed{ a_1,\ a_1r,\ a_1r^2,\ a_1r^3,\ldots }. $$

Explicit Formula

If the first term is indexed as $a_1$, then:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=a_1r^{n-1} }. $$

Zero-Based Indexing

If the initial term is labeled $a_0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=a_0r^n }. $$

Recursive Formula

A geometric sequence can be defined recursively by:

$$ \boxed{ a_1=a, \qquad a_n=ra_{n-1} \quad(n\geq2) }. $$

Common Ratio

When $a_n\neq0$, the common ratio is:

$$ \boxed{ r=\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} }. $$

Example

The sequence:

$$ 5,\ 15,\ 45,\ 135,\ldots $$

has:

$$ \boxed{ r=3 }. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=5\cdot3^{n-1} }. $$

Fractional Common Ratio

The sequence:

$$ 64,\ 32,\ 16,\ 8,\ 4,\ldots $$

has:

$$ \boxed{ r=\frac{1}{2} }. $$

Negative Common Ratio

The sequence:

$$ 4,\ -8,\ 16,\ -32,\ldots $$

has:

$$ \boxed{ r=-2 }. $$

A negative common ratio causes alternating signs.

Ratio Greater than One

If $a_1>0$ and $r>1$, the sequence grows in magnitude.

Ratio between Zero and One

If:

$$ 0<r<1, $$

positive terms decay toward zero.

Ratio Equal to One

If $r=1$:

$$ \boxed{ a_1,\ a_1,\ a_1,\ldots }. $$

This is a constant sequence and still satisfies the geometric recurrence.

Ratio Equal to Negative One

If $r=-1$:

$$ \boxed{ a_1,\ -a_1,\ a_1,\ -a_1,\ldots }. $$

Ratio Equal to Zero

If $r=0$:

$$ \boxed{ a_1,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0,\ldots }. $$

The recursive definition remains valid even though later ratios such as $0/0$ are undefined.

Zero Sequence

If $a_1=0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=0 } $$

for every $n$. A ratio cannot be uniquely recovered from consecutive terms, so the recurrence formulation is more robust than defining geometric sequences only by quotients.

Finding the Common Ratio

For:

$$ 9,\ 6,\ 4,\ldots, $$

one obtains:

$$ r = \frac{6}{9} = \frac{4}{6} = \boxed{\frac{2}{3}}. $$

Finding a Later Term

If:

$$ a_1=7, \qquad r=2, $$

then:

$$ a_6 = 7\cdot2^5 = \boxed{224}. $$

Using a Known Intermediate Term

For any indices $m$ and $n$:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=a_mr^{n-m} }. $$

This avoids unnecessarily solving for the first term when another known term is more convenient.

Example from an Intermediate Term

If:

$$ a_4=12, \qquad r=2, $$

then:

$$ a_7 = 12\cdot2^3 = \boxed{96}. $$

Finding the Ratio from Nonconsecutive Terms

Since:

$$ a_n=a_mr^{n-m}, $$

one has:

$$ \boxed{ r^{n-m} = \frac{a_n}{a_m} } $$

when $a_m\neq0$.

Multiple Possible Ratios

If the index difference is even, solving for $r$ may produce multiple real possibilities.

For example:

$$ a_1=1, \qquad a_3=9 $$

gives:

$$ r^2=9, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ r=3 \text{ or } r=-3 }. $$

Geometric Mean

For positive numbers $x$ and $z$, the positive geometric mean is:

$$ \boxed{ y=\sqrt{xz} }. $$

Then:

$$ x,\ y,\ z $$

is a geometric sequence.

Three Consecutive Terms

If nonzero $x,y,z$ are consecutive geometric terms:

$$ \frac{y}{x} = \frac{z}{y}, $$

and therefore:

$$ \boxed{ y^2=xz }. $$

Geometric Sequence as a Function

A sequence may be viewed formally as a function:

$$ \boxed{ a:\mathbb{N}\to S }. $$

A geometric sequence is therefore a discrete function whose values satisfy:

$$ a(n+1)=r\,a(n). $$

Discrete Graph

When graphed as ordered pairs:

$$ (n,a_n), $$

a geometric sequence appears as discrete points rather than a continuous curve.

Connection to Exponential Functions

The formula:

$$ a_n=a_1r^{n-1} $$

is exponential in the index $n$.

Thus a geometric sequence is commonly viewed as a discrete exponential model.

$$ \boxed{ \text{geometric sequence} = \text{discrete constant-ratio model} }. $$

Arithmetic Sequence Contrast

An arithmetic sequence satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ a_{n+1}=a_n+d }. $$

A geometric sequence satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ a_{n+1}=ra_n }. $$

Constant Difference vs. Constant Ratio

$$ \boxed{ \text{arithmetic} \longleftrightarrow a_{n+1}-a_n=d } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{geometric} \longleftrightarrow \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}=r } $$

where the latter quotient is defined.

First Differences Are Usually Not Constant

In a nonconstant geometric sequence, successive differences generally change:

$$ a_{n+1}-a_n = a_n(r-1). $$

Percentage Change

For positive terms, a geometric sequence represents constant percentage change.

If:

$$ r=1.08, $$

each term is $8\%$ greater than the preceding term.

Growth Factor

A percentage increase of $p\%$ corresponds to:

$$ \boxed{ r=1+\frac{p}{100} }. $$

Decay Factor

A percentage decrease of $p\%$ corresponds to:

$$ \boxed{ r=1-\frac{p}{100} }. $$

Compound Growth

If an amount $P$ grows by rate $i$ each discrete period:

$$ \boxed{ A_n=P(1+i)^n }. $$

Successive balances form a geometric sequence with common ratio $1+i$.

Compound Decay

A quantity retaining fraction $r$ each period follows:

$$ \boxed{ Q_n=Q_0r^n }. $$

Doubling Sequence

$$ Q_n=Q_0\,2^n $$

has common ratio:

$$ \boxed{2}. $$

Halving Sequence

$$ Q_n=Q_0\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n $$

has common ratio:

$$ \boxed{\frac{1}{2}}. $$

Long-Term Behavior for $|r|<1$

If $a_1$ is finite and:

$$ |r|<1, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=0 }. $$

Long-Term Behavior for $r=1$

$$ \boxed{ a_n=a_1 } $$

for every $n$.

Long-Term Behavior for $r=-1$

If $a_1\neq0$, the sequence oscillates between $a_1$ and $-a_1$ and therefore does not converge.

Long-Term Behavior for $|r|>1$

If $a_1\neq0$, the magnitudes:

$$ |a_n| = |a_1||r|^{n-1} $$

grow without bound.

Special Case $a_1=0$

The zero sequence converges to zero regardless of the chosen multiplier.

Geometric Series

Adding the terms of a geometric sequence creates a geometric series:

$$ \boxed{ a_1+a_1r+a_1r^2+\cdots }. $$

Sequence vs. Series

A sequence is an ordered list:

$$ a_1,\ a_2,\ a_3,\ldots $$

while a series is a sum:

$$ a_1+a_2+a_3+\cdots. $$

Finite Geometric Sum

The first $n$ terms satisfy:

$$ \boxed{ S_n = a_1\frac{1-r^n}{1-r} } $$

for $r\neq1$.

Equivalent Sum Formula

$$ \boxed{ S_n = a_1\frac{r^n-1}{r-1} } $$

for $r\neq1$.

Infinite Geometric Sum

If:

$$ |r|<1, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_1r^{n-1} = \frac{a_1}{1-r} }. $$

Sequence Convergence vs. Series Convergence

These are different questions. The geometric sequence:

$$ a_n=r^{n-1} $$

may converge to zero, while the associated series has its own convergence condition and sum.

Logarithmic Linearization

For positive terms:

$$ a_n=a_1r^{n-1}, $$

taking logarithms yields:

$$ \boxed{ \ln a_n = \ln a_1+(n-1)\ln r }. $$

Thus the logarithms form an arithmetic sequence.

Geometric-to-Arithmetic Transformation

$$ \boxed{ \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}=r \Longrightarrow \ln a_{n+1}-\ln a_n=\ln r } $$

for positive terms.

Finding an Index with Logarithms

If:

$$ a_n=a_1r^{n-1} $$

with positive quantities and $r\neq1$, then:

$$ \boxed{ n = 1+ \frac{\ln(a_n/a_1)}{\ln r} }. $$

Recurrence Interpretation

Geometric sequences solve the first-order homogeneous recurrence:

$$ \boxed{ a_{n+1}-ra_n=0 }. $$

Initial-Value Dependence

The pair:

$$ \boxed{ (a_1,r) } $$

completely determines a geometric sequence.

Linear Recurrence Perspective

The map:

$$ a_n\mapsto ra_n $$

is linear, so geometric sequences are basic examples in the study of linear recurrence relations.

Eigenvalue Analogy

In more advanced settings, repeated multiplication by $r$ resembles repeated action of a linear transformation on an eigenvector:

$$ T^nv=\lambda^nv. $$

The scalar powers $\lambda^n$ form a geometric sequence.

Matrix Powers

The sequence:

$$ I,\ A,\ A^2,\ A^3,\ldots $$

is a matrix analogue of geometric progression.

Complex Geometric Sequence

If:

$$ r=\rho e^{i\theta}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a_n = a_1\rho^{n-1}e^{i(n-1)\theta} }. $$

Each step scales magnitude by $\rho$ and rotates argument by $\theta$.

Periodic Complex Sequence

If $r$ is a root of unity, repeated multiplication may become periodic.

For example:

$$ 1,\ i,\ -1,\ -i,\ 1,\ldots $$

is geometric with common ratio $i$.

Geometry and Similarity

Repeatedly applying the same scale factor to lengths produces a geometric sequence.

Area Scaling

If each linear dimension is multiplied by $k$, areas are multiplied by $k^2$. Repeating the scaling yields:

$$ \boxed{ A_n=A_0(k^2)^n }. $$

Volume Scaling

Repeated linear scale factor $k$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ V_n=V_0(k^3)^n }. $$

Financial Models

Fixed-period compound growth produces a geometric sequence of balances.

Depreciation Models

A fixed percentage loss per period produces geometric decay.

Population Models

Idealized generation-by-generation multiplication by a fixed reproduction factor produces a geometric sequence.

Radioactive or Biological Decay

Sampling a continuous exponential decay process at equally spaced time intervals produces a geometric sequence.

Computer Science

Repeated multiplication or division by a constant factor appears in algorithms. Repeated halving:

$$ n,\ \frac{n}{2},\ \frac{n}{4},\ \frac{n}{8},\ldots $$

is geometric with ratio $1/2$.

Fractal Construction

Successive scales in self-similar objects frequently form geometric sequences.

Signal Processing

Repeated attenuation or amplification by a constant gain factor generates geometric sequences of amplitudes.

Numerical Methods

Error sequences may exhibit geometric convergence when:

$$ e_{n+1}\approx re_n, \qquad |r|<1. $$

Geometric Convergence

In numerical analysis, the phrase geometric convergence can describe errors decreasing approximately like:

$$ \boxed{ Cq^n, \qquad 0<q<1 }. $$

Geometric Sequence vs. Geometric Progression

These terms ordinarily denote the same mathematical object. The difference is primarily terminological and regional rather than structural.

Geometric Sequence vs. Geometric Series

A sequence lists terms. A series adds terms.

Geometric Sequence vs. Geometric Mean

A geometric mean is one value; a geometric sequence is an ordered collection of values.

Geometric Sequence vs. Geometric Distribution

The geometric distribution is a probability distribution. It is not a synonym for geometric sequence.

Geometric Sequence vs. Exponential Function

A geometric sequence is indexed discretely. An exponential function may accept continuously varying real inputs.

Common Error: Looking for a Constant Difference

A geometric sequence is recognized by constant multiplication, not constant addition.

Common Error: Adding the Common Ratio

The correct recurrence is:

$$ \boxed{ a_{n+1}=ra_n }, $$

not:

$$ a_{n+1}=a_n+r. $$

Common Error: Exponent Off by One

With $a_1$ as the first term:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=a_1r^{n-1} }. $$

Using $r^n$ instead shifts every term by one multiplication.

Common Error: Confusing Common Ratio with a Percentage

An $8\%$ growth rate corresponds to common ratio:

$$ \boxed{ 1.08 }, $$

not $0.08$.

Common Error: Assuming the Ratio Must Be Positive

Negative ratios are valid and create alternating signs.

Common Error: Assuming the Ratio Must Be Rational

Depending on the setting, the common ratio may be irrational or complex.

Common Error: Confusing the Term Formula and Sum Formula

The term formula:

$$ a_n=a_1r^{n-1} $$

does not give the sum of the first $n$ terms.

Common Error: Treating the Graph as Continuous

A sequence graph consists of discrete indexed points. Connecting those points may help visualization, but the connecting curve is not part of the original sequence unless an interpolation is separately defined.

Common Error: Using Consecutive-Term Division at Zero

The quotient characterization fails when a denominator term is zero. The recurrence $a_{n+1}=ra_n$ remains the safer general definition.

Common Misconception: A geometric sequence is not simply a sequence whose values become large or small rapidly. Its defining property is constant multiplicative change. Once the initial term and common ratio are specified, every term is determined by $a_n=a_1r^{n-1}$.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$a_1,\ a_1r,\ a_1r^2,\ldots$ "A geometric sequence with first term a one and common ratio r."
$a_n=a_1r^{n-1}$ "The nth term equals a one times r to the n minus one."
$a_{n+1}=ra_n$ "The next term equals r times the current term."
$r=a_{n+1}/a_n$ "The common ratio is the next term divided by the preceding term."
$a_n=a_mr^{n-m}$ "The nth term equals the mth term times r to the n minus m."
$|r|<1$ "The magnitude of the common ratio is less than one."
$a_n\to0$ "The sequence approaches zero."

The braces in:

$$ \boxed{ \{a_n\} } $$

are often used informally to denote the sequence, although a sequence is more precisely an ordered function of an index and should not be confused with an unordered set.

One may also write:

$$ (a_n)_{n=1}^{\infty} $$

to emphasize the indexed sequence.

Defense Notation Advice: Specify whether indexing begins at $0$ or $1$, because this controls whether the exponent is $n$ or $n-1$. Distinguish the sequence $(a_n)$ from the series $\sum a_n$, and use the recurrence $a_{n+1}=ra_n$ when zero terms make consecutive-term quotients undefined.

  • Negative Ratio: Alternates sign.

  • Core Interpretation: A geometric sequence is the discrete model of repeated multiplicative change: $$ \boxed{ a_{n+1}=ra_n } $$ and, with one-based indexing: $$ \boxed{ a_n=a_1r^{n-1} }. $$ The initial term sets the starting value, while the common ratio determines growth, decay, sign alternation, periodicity, or constancy. This structure connects sequences directly to exponential models, recurrences, geometric series, compound growth, scaling laws, and repeated transformations.
