Singular Matrix

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

A singular matrix is a square matrix that does not have a multiplicative inverse.

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ singular} \Longleftrightarrow A^{-1}\text{ does not exist} }. $$

For a square matrix over a field, this is equivalent to having determinant zero:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A)=0 }. $$

The term singular historically marks a failure of the ordinary invertible case. Modern linear algebra treats singularity through several equivalent structural conditions involving determinant, rank, null space, linear dependence, and linear transformations.

Operational Nuance

Determinant Test

For an $n\times n$ matrix:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ singular} \Longleftrightarrow \det(A)=0 }. $$

Example

Consider:

$$ A= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2\\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Its determinant is:

$$ \det(A) = 1(4)-2(2) = \boxed{0}. $$

Therefore $A$ is singular.

Linear Dependence

In the example above, the second row is twice the first. The rows are linearly dependent, and so are the columns.

For a square matrix:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ singular} \Longleftrightarrow \text{its columns are linearly dependent} }. $$

Rank Deficiency

An $n\times n$ matrix is singular exactly when its rank is less than $n$:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{rank}(A)<n }. $$

Null Space

A singular matrix has a nonzero vector in its null space:

$$ \boxed{ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0} \text{ for some } \mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{0} }. $$

Thus the homogeneous system has a nontrivial solution.

Invertibility Failure

If $A$ were invertible and:

$$ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}, $$

multiplying by $A^{-1}$ would force $\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$. A nontrivial null vector therefore proves that $A$ cannot be invertible.

Row Reduction Test

A square matrix is nonsingular exactly when its reduced row-echelon form is the identity matrix. Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ singular} \Longleftrightarrow \operatorname{rref}(A)\neq I_n }. $$

For a singular $n\times n$ matrix, row reduction produces fewer than $n$ pivots.

Linear Systems

If $A$ is singular, the system:

$$ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b} $$

cannot have a unique solution for every possible $\mathbf{b}$. Depending on $\mathbf{b}$, a particular system may have no solution or multiple solutions.

Transformation Interpretation

A square matrix represents a linear transformation:

$$ T(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x}. $$

If $A$ is singular, then $T$ is not one-to-one and not onto its full $n$-dimensional codomain.

Geometric Interpretation

A singular linear transformation collapses at least one dimension. In $\mathbb{R}^2$, for example, a plane may be collapsed onto a line or a point.

This agrees with the determinant interpretation: determinant zero corresponds to zero signed area or volume scaling.

Eigenvalue Criterion

A square matrix is singular exactly when $0$ is one of its eigenvalues:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ singular} \Longleftrightarrow 0\text{ is an eigenvalue of }A }. $$

Characteristic Polynomial

If:

$$ p_A(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I-A), $$

then:

$$ p_A(0) = (-1)^n\det(A). $$

Therefore determinant zero means $\lambda=0$ is a root of the characteristic polynomial.

Product Rule

For square matrices of the same size:

$$ \det(AB)=\det(A)\det(B). $$

Hence if either factor is singular, the product is singular:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ singular} \Longrightarrow AB\text{ singular} } $$

and similarly if $B$ is singular.

Transpose

Since:

$$ \det(A^T)=\det(A), $$

singularity is preserved by transposition:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ singular} \Longleftrightarrow A^T\text{ singular} }. $$

Singular vs. Nearly Singular

In exact mathematics, singular means exactly noninvertible. In numerical computation, a matrix may be invertible but ill-conditioned, meaning that it behaves numerically as though it were close to singular. These concepts should not be conflated.

Common Misconception

A singular matrix is not merely a matrix with a zero entry or a zero on its main diagonal. Singularity concerns the entire matrix structure and is characterized by conditions such as determinant zero, deficient rank, or nontrivial null space.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\det(A)=0$ "A has determinant zero, so A is singular."
$A^{-1}$ does not exist "A is not invertible."
$\operatorname{rank}(A)<n$ "A has rank less than full rank."
$A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$ with $\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{0}$ "A has a nontrivial null-space vector."
$0\in\sigma(A)$ "Zero is an eigenvalue of A."
$\operatorname{rref}(A)\neq I_n$ "The reduced row-echelon form of A is not the identity."

The terms singular, noninvertible, and not nonsingular describe the same basic condition for square matrices over a field.

Defense Notation Advice: Use the term singular primarily for square matrices. For rectangular matrices, describe rank deficiency, lack of injectivity, or lack of a two-sided inverse more directly. In numerical work, distinguish exact singularity from poor conditioning.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: A square matrix with no multiplicative inverse.
  • Determinant Criterion: $\det(A)=0$.
  • Rank Criterion: An $n\times n$ singular matrix has rank less than $n$.
  • Pivot Criterion: A singular square matrix has fewer than $n$ pivot positions.
  • RREF Criterion: Its reduced row-echelon form is not $I_n$.
  • Column Criterion: Its columns are linearly dependent.
  • Row Criterion: Its rows are linearly dependent.
  • Null-Space Criterion: $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$ has a nonzero solution.
  • Eigenvalue Criterion: Zero is an eigenvalue.
  • Transformation View: The associated linear map loses information and is not bijective.
  • Geometry: At least one dimension is collapsed.
  • Determinant Geometry: Area or volume scaling is zero.
  • Linear Systems: $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ cannot have a unique solution for every $\mathbf{b}$.
  • Homogeneous Systems: A singular matrix produces nontrivial solutions to $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$.
  • Transpose: $A$ is singular exactly when $A^T$ is singular.
  • Products: A product containing a singular square factor is singular when the matrix sizes are compatible and square.
  • Numerical Linear Algebra: Exact singularity must be distinguished from near-singularity and ill-conditioning.
  • Common Error: Assuming any matrix containing zero is singular.
  • Common Error: Assuming a zero diagonal entry automatically implies singularity.
  • Common Error: Calling an arbitrary rectangular matrix singular without clarifying the intended generalized meaning.
  • Common Error: Assuming a singular coefficient matrix means every associated linear system has no solution.
  • Common Error: Treating an ill-conditioned but invertible matrix as mathematically singular.
  • Core Interpretation: For a square matrix over a field, the following conditions are equivalent: $$ \boxed{ A^{-1}\text{ does not exist}, \qquad \det(A)=0, \qquad \operatorname{rank}(A)<n } $$ and: $$ \boxed{ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0} \text{ has a nontrivial solution} }. $$ Singularity therefore unifies determinant, rank, null space, linear dependence, eigenvalues, geometric dimension loss, and failure of invertibility.
