Domain of Definition

Subfield: Foundations & Logic

Etymology & Roots

The domain of definition of a function, expression, relation, or formula is the set of inputs for which that mathematical object is actually defined.

In ordinary function language, the phrase is often synonymous with domain. If:

$$ f:A\to B, $$

then $A$ is the domain of $f$ and therefore its domain of definition:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f)=A }. $$

The longer phrase emphasizes that the set identifies exactly where the rule, formula, or function has mathematical meaning.

For a formula such as:

$$ f(x)=\frac{1}{x-3}, $$

the expression is defined for every real $x$ except $3$. Thus its natural real domain of definition is:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{R}\setminus\{3\} }. $$

The phrase is especially useful when distinguishing between a symbolic formula and the set on which that formula is intended or permitted to operate.

Operational Nuance

Domain vs. Domain of Definition

In most elementary and intermediate contexts:

$$ \boxed{ \text{domain of definition} = \text{domain} }. $$

The phrase domain of definition places extra emphasis on the question:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"For which inputs is this rule actually defined?"} } $$

Formula vs. Function

A symbolic formula does not always specify a complete function by itself.

For example:

$$ f(x)=x^2 $$

could define:

$$ f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R} $$

or:

$$ f:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty). $$

The formula is the same, but the declared domains of definition differ.

Natural Domain of Definition

If no domain is explicitly stated, the natural domain of definition is commonly taken to be the largest standard set of inputs for which the formula is meaningful in the number system under discussion.

For a real-valued polynomial:

$$ p(x)=x^3-2x+7, $$

the natural real domain of definition is:

$$ \boxed{\mathbb{R}}. $$

Denominator Restrictions

A denominator may not equal zero.

If:

$$ f(x)=\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ Q(x)\neq0 }. $$

Therefore the natural real domain of definition is:

$$ \boxed{ \{x\in\mathbb{R}:Q(x)\neq0\} }. $$

Example: Rational Expression

For:

$$ f(x)=\frac{x+4}{x^2-9}, $$

factor the denominator:

$$ x^2-9=(x-3)(x+3). $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ x\neq3,\qquad x\neq-3 }. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f) = \mathbb{R}\setminus\{-3,3\} }. $$

Restrictions Survive Simplification

Consider:

$$ f(x)=\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}. $$

Factoring gives:

$$ \frac{(x-1)(x+1)}{x-1}. $$

For $x\neq1$, this simplifies to:

$$ x+1. $$

But the original expression is not defined at $x=1$. Therefore its domain of definition remains:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{R}\setminus\{1\} }. $$

Even Radical Restrictions

For real-valued even roots:

$$ \sqrt{g(x)}, $$

require:

$$ \boxed{ g(x)\geq0 }. $$

Example: Square Root

For:

$$ f(x)=\sqrt{2x-6}, $$

require:

$$ 2x-6\geq0. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ x\geq3 } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f)=[3,\infty) }. $$

Odd Radical Domains

Odd roots accept every real radicand:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(\sqrt[3]{x}) = \mathbb{R} }. $$

Even Radical in a Denominator

If:

$$ f(x)=\frac1{\sqrt{g(x)}}, $$

then the radical must be real and the denominator nonzero. Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ g(x)>0 }. $$

Logarithmic Restrictions

For a real logarithm:

$$ \log_b(g(x)), $$

require:

$$ \boxed{ g(x)>0 }. $$

Example: Logarithm

For:

$$ f(x)=\ln(5-x), $$

require:

$$ 5-x>0. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ x<5 } $$

and the real domain of definition is:

$$ \boxed{ (-\infty,5) }. $$

Trigonometric Restrictions

Sine and cosine are defined for every real input:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(\sin) = \operatorname{Dom}(\cos) = \mathbb{R} }. $$

Tangent satisfies:

$$ \tan x=\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}, $$

so its real domain of definition excludes zeros of cosine:

$$ \boxed{ x\neq\frac{\pi}{2}+k\pi, \qquad k\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$

Composite Functions

For:

$$ (f\circ g)(x)=f(g(x)), $$

$x$ must belong to the domain of $g$, and the resulting output $g(x)$ must lie in the domain of $f$.

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f\circ g) = \{x\in\operatorname{Dom}(g): g(x)\in\operatorname{Dom}(f)\} }. $$

Example: Composite Domain

Let:

$$ f(u)=\sqrt{u}, \qquad g(x)=4-x. $$

Then:

$$ (f\circ g)(x)=\sqrt{4-x}. $$

Require:

$$ 4-x\geq0. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f\circ g) = (-\infty,4] }. $$

Sum and Difference of Functions

The natural domain of:

$$ f\pm g $$

is ordinarily:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f) \cap \operatorname{Dom}(g) }. $$

Product of Functions

Similarly:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(fg) = \operatorname{Dom}(f) \cap \operatorname{Dom}(g) }. $$

Quotient of Functions

For:

$$ \frac{f}{g}, $$

both functions must be defined and:

$$ g(x)\neq0. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}\left(\frac fg\right) = \{x\in \operatorname{Dom}(f)\cap\operatorname{Dom}(g): g(x)\neq0\} }. $$

Piecewise Functions

A piecewise function may have its domain of definition supplied directly by the branch conditions.

For example:

$$ f(x)= \begin{cases} x+1, & x<0,\\ x^2, & 0\leq x\leq3. \end{cases} $$

has domain:

$$ \boxed{ (-\infty,3] }. $$

Sequences

A sequence is a function whose domain of definition is typically discrete:

$$ \boxed{ a:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R} }. $$

Thus the domain of definition need not be an interval or even a subset with continuous structure.

Multivariable Functions

A function of two variables may have a domain of definition that is a region in the plane.

For example:

$$ f(x,y)=\sqrt{16-x^2-y^2}. $$

Require:

$$ 16-x^2-y^2\geq0. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ x^2+y^2\leq16 }. $$

The domain of definition is the closed disk of radius $4$ centered at the origin.

Dependence on the Number System

A domain of definition depends on the setting in which a formula is interpreted.

For instance:

$$ \sqrt{x} $$

has real domain:

$$ [0,\infty), $$

while complex square roots can be considered on broader sets subject to branch conventions.

Explicit Restrictions Override Natural Domain

Suppose:

$$ f(x)=x^2, \qquad -2\leq x\leq2. $$

Although $x^2$ is meaningful for every real number, the declared function has domain of definition:

$$ \boxed{[-2,2]}. $$

Domain Restrictions and Inverses

Restricting a domain can make a function one-to-one.

For:

$$ f(x)=x^2, $$

restricting the domain to:

$$ \boxed{[0,\infty)} $$

permits the inverse:

$$ \boxed{ f^{-1}(x)=\sqrt{x} }. $$

Candidate Solutions Must Belong to the Domain

When solving equations, a candidate value that lies outside the original domain of definition cannot be a valid solution.

Thus domain restrictions should be recorded before transformations that might obscure them.

Extraneous Solutions

Squaring, clearing denominators, or performing other non-equivalent transformations may produce candidate solutions. Each candidate must be checked against the original domain of definition and original equation.

Graphical Interpretation

For a graph of a relation in the Cartesian plane, the domain of definition is the set of horizontal coordinates for which at least one corresponding point exists:

$$ \boxed{ \{x:\exists y,\;(x,y)\text{ lies on the graph}\} }. $$

Advanced Function-Theoretic Usage

In more advanced mathematics, domain of definition may be used when a formula or mapping is meaningful only on part of a larger ambient space. The phrase emphasizes the subset on which the map is genuinely defined rather than merely the larger space in which its variables are written.

Rational Maps

In algebraic geometry, a rational expression may determine a map only where its coordinate expressions are simultaneously meaningful. The subset on which a representative map is defined may be called a domain of definition.

This is a more specialized use, but it preserves the same underlying idea:

$$ \boxed{ \text{domain of definition} = \text{where the rule actually makes sense} }. $$

Common Misconception: The domain of definition is not always automatically "all real numbers." It is determined by the declared function, the operations appearing in its formula, explicit restrictions, and the number system or mathematical setting being used.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\operatorname{Dom}(f)$ "The domain of f."
$f:A\to B$ "F is a function from A to B."
$x\in\operatorname{Dom}(f)$ "X belongs to the domain of definition of f."
$\mathbb{R}\setminus\{3\}$ "All real numbers except three."
$[2,\infty)$ "All real numbers greater than or equal to two."
$(-\infty,5)$ "All real numbers less than five."
$\{x\in\mathbb{R}:g(x)>0\}$ "The set of real x such that g of x is greater than zero."

The phrases:

$$ \boxed{\text{"domain"}} $$

and:

$$ \boxed{\text{"domain of definition"}} $$

are commonly interchangeable. The longer form is especially useful when emphasizing restrictions imposed by a formula.

For:

$$ f(x)=\frac1{x-2}, $$

its domain of definition may be expressed as:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{R}\setminus\{2\} } $$

or:

$$ \boxed{ \{x\in\mathbb{R}:x\neq2\} } $$

or:

$$ \boxed{ (-\infty,2)\cup(2,\infty) }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: When a formula has algebraic restrictions, state the domain before canceling factors, squaring equations, or otherwise rewriting the expression. This preserves information that may disappear syntactically even though it remains mathematically binding.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Basic Meaning: The domain of definition is the set of inputs for which a mathematical rule is defined.
  • Ordinary Synonym: In most function contexts, domain of definition means the same as domain.
  • Function Notation: $$ \boxed{ f:A\to B } $$ identifies $A$ as the domain of definition.
  • Domain Symbol: $$ \boxed{\operatorname{Dom}(f)}. $$
  • Input Interpretation: In $y=f(x)$, the domain of definition consists of allowable values of $x$.
  • Natural Domain: The largest standard set on which a formula is meaningful when no smaller domain is explicitly declared.
  • Declared Domain: An explicitly stated domain overrides the larger natural domain of a formula.
  • Polynomial: A real polynomial has natural domain of definition: $$ \boxed{\mathbb{R}}. $$
  • Rational Expression: Exclude all values that make a denominator zero.
  • Example: $$ \frac1{x+4} $$ has real domain: $$ \boxed{ \mathbb{R}\setminus\{-4\} }. $$
  • Factored Denominator: Factoring helps identify excluded domain values.
  • Cancellation: Cancellation does not restore an input that was excluded from the original expression.
  • Hole: A canceled denominator factor may leave a removable discontinuity at an excluded domain value.
  • Vertical Asymptote: A noncanceled denominator zero may correspond to a vertical asymptote.
  • Even Radical: $$ \sqrt{g(x)} $$ requires: $$ \boxed{g(x)\geq0}. $$
  • Odd Radical: Odd real roots accept negative, zero, and positive radicands.
  • Radical Denominator: $$ \frac1{\sqrt{g(x)}} $$ requires: $$ \boxed{g(x)>0}. $$
  • Logarithm: $$ \log_b(g(x)) $$ requires: $$ \boxed{g(x)>0}. $$
  • Exponential Function: Standard real exponentials $a^x$ with $a>0$ have domain $\mathbb{R}$.
  • Sine: $$ \operatorname{Dom}(\sin)=\mathbb{R}. $$
  • Cosine: $$ \operatorname{Dom}(\cos)=\mathbb{R}. $$
  • Tangent: Exclude: $$ \boxed{ \frac{\pi}{2}+k\pi } $$ for $k\in\mathbb{Z}$.
  • Cosecant: Exclude zeros of sine.
  • Secant: Exclude zeros of cosine.
  • Cotangent: Exclude zeros of sine.
  • Inverse Sine: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(\arcsin)=[-1,1] }. $$
  • Inverse Cosine: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(\arccos)=[-1,1] }. $$
  • Inverse Tangent: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(\arctan)=\mathbb{R} }. $$
  • Composite Function: $$ \boxed{ x\in\operatorname{Dom}(g) \text{ and } g(x)\in\operatorname{Dom}(f) } $$ are both required for $f\circ g$.
  • Function Sum: The natural domain is the intersection of the two domains.
  • Function Difference: The natural domain is likewise the intersection.
  • Function Product: The natural domain is ordinarily the intersection.
  • Function Quotient: The denominator function must additionally be nonzero.
  • Piecewise Function: The domain is assembled from the branch conditions.
  • Discrete Domain: A sequence may have domain: $$ \boxed{\mathbb{N}}. $$
  • Finite Domain: A domain of definition may contain finitely many elements.
  • Interval Domain: A real function may be defined only on an interval.
  • Disconnected Domain: A domain may be a union of separated intervals.
  • Multivariable Domain: A domain of definition may be a subset of: $$ \boxed{\mathbb{R}^n}. $$
  • Disk Example: $$ \sqrt{9-x^2-y^2} $$ has real domain: $$ \boxed{x^2+y^2\leq9}. $$
  • Graphical Domain: Read all horizontal coordinates for which the graph contains at least one point.
  • Relation: For: $$ R\subseteq A\times B, $$ $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(R) = \{a\in A:\exists b\in B,\;(a,b)\in R\} }. $$
  • Range Contrast: Domain concerns permitted inputs; range concerns outputs actually attained.
  • Codomain Contrast: The codomain is the declared target set, not necessarily the set of attained outputs.
  • Inverse Function: The domain of $f^{-1}$ is the range of $f$.
  • Restricted Domain: A function may be intentionally restricted to make an inverse possible.
  • Equation Solving: Valid solutions must belong to the domain of definition of the original equation.
  • Extraneous Candidate: A candidate outside the original domain is automatically invalid.
  • Clearing Denominators: Multiplication by a common denominator can hide excluded values, so those restrictions must be retained.
  • Squaring: Squaring an equation can introduce candidate solutions that require checking against the original domain and equation.
  • Number-System Dependence: A formula's domain of definition may differ over $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}$.
  • Complex Analysis: A function may be considered on a specified subset of $\mathbb{C}$, with branch choices affecting its domain of definition.
  • Differential Equations: A solution has a domain of definition on which the function and required derivatives satisfy the equation.
  • Integration: The phrase domain may also denote a region over which integration takes place.
  • Rational Maps: In more advanced geometry, a rational map may possess a domain of definition on which its representing expressions are simultaneously meaningful.
  • Ambient-Space Contrast: Variables may be written as belonging to a large ambient space even though the actual rule is defined only on a proper subset.
  • Formula Contrast: A formula is a symbolic rule; its domain of definition states where that rule is permitted to operate.
  • Terminological Contrast: Domain and domain of definition are usually synonymous, but the longer phrase more strongly emphasizes admissibility and restrictions.
  • Core Interpretation: The domain of definition answers the question: $$ \boxed{ \text{"For which inputs is this mathematical object defined?"} } $$ It is determined by explicit declarations, algebraic restrictions, composition, branch conditions, and the mathematical setting in which the object is interpreted.
