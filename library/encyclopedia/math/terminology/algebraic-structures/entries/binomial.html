Binomial

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

A binomial is an algebraic expression consisting of exactly two terms after like terms have been combined.

$$ \boxed{ \text{binomial} = \text{two-term expression} }. $$

Typical examples include:

$$ x+5, \qquad 3a-2b, \qquad x^2+4x. $$

The prefix bi- means two, while -nomial is associated with terms or names in polynomial terminology.

Thus the familiar sequence:

$$ \boxed{ \text{monomial} \rightarrow \text{binomial} \rightarrow \text{trinomial} } $$

corresponds respectively to one, two, and three terms.

Operational Nuance

What Counts as a Term?

Terms are separated by addition or subtraction at the outermost level of an expression.

In:

$$ 4x^2-7x, $$

the two terms are:

$$ \boxed{ 4x^2 \qquad\text{and}\qquad -7x }. $$

Subtraction Still Produces Two Terms

A binomial need not use an addition sign. Since subtraction can be interpreted as addition of an opposite:

$$ a-b = a+(-b), $$

the expression still has two terms.

Like Terms Must Be Combined First

An expression may appear to contain several written pieces but simplify to fewer terms.

For example:

$$ 2x+3x+4 = 5x+4. $$

After combining like terms, the expression is a binomial.

Example That Is Not a Binomial

The expression:

$$ x^2+x+1 $$

has three terms and is therefore a trinomial.

Binomial as a Polynomial

In elementary algebra, a binomial is usually a polynomial containing exactly two nonzero terms.

$$ \boxed{ ax^m+bx^n } $$

is a binomial when the two terms are distinct and nonzero under the relevant polynomial conventions.

Degree of a Binomial

For a one-variable polynomial binomial, the degree is the greatest exponent of the variable appearing with a nonzero coefficient.

For example:

$$ 3x^5-2x^2 $$

is a binomial of degree:

$$ \boxed{5}. $$

Constant Term

A constant may be one of the two terms:

$$ x^2-9. $$

Here the terms are $x^2$ and $-9$.

Multivariable Binomials

Binomials may involve several variables:

$$ 3x^2y-5yz^3. $$

The classification depends on the number of terms, not the number of variables.

Binomial Addition

Binomials can be added by removing grouping and combining like terms:

$$ (2x+3)+(5x-1) = 7x+2. $$

Binomial Subtraction

When subtracting a binomial, distribute the negative sign:

$$ (4x+7)-(x-2) = 4x+7-x+2 = \boxed{3x+9}. $$

Multiplying Two Binomials

Multiplication uses the distributive property:

$$ (a+b)(c+d) = ac+ad+bc+bd. $$

This is the underlying principle behind the familiar FOIL mnemonic.

FOIL

For two binomials:

$$ (a+b)(c+d), $$

FOIL refers to:

  • First: $ac$
  • Outer: $ad$
  • Inner: $bc$
  • Last: $bd$

The method is a mnemonic for repeated distribution rather than a separate algebraic law.

Example of Binomial Multiplication

$$ (x+3)(x+5) = x^2+5x+3x+15 = \boxed{x^2+8x+15}. $$

Square of a Binomial

A particularly important identity is:

$$ \boxed{ (a+b)^2 = a^2+2ab+b^2 }. $$

Square of a Difference

$$ \boxed{ (a-b)^2 = a^2-2ab+b^2 }. $$

Common Squaring Error

In general:

$$ \boxed{ (a+b)^2 \ne a^2+b^2 }. $$

The middle term $2ab$ comes from the two cross-products.

Conjugate Binomials

Binomials of the forms:

$$ a+b \qquad\text{and}\qquad a-b $$

are often called conjugates in elementary algebraic contexts.

Product of Conjugates

Their product is a difference of squares:

$$ \boxed{ (a+b)(a-b) = a^2-b^2 }. $$

Difference of Squares

Reversing that identity gives the factorization:

$$ \boxed{ a^2-b^2 = (a-b)(a+b) }. $$

Thus a difference of two square terms is itself a binomial that factors into two conjugate binomials.

Difference of Cubes

Another important binomial factorization is:

$$ \boxed{ a^3-b^3 = (a-b)(a^2+ab+b^2) }. $$

Sum of Cubes

$$ \boxed{ a^3+b^3 = (a+b)(a^2-ab+b^2) }. $$

Powers of a Binomial

Expressions such as:

$$ (a+b)^n $$

are called powers of a binomial.

Their expansions are governed by the Binomial Theorem.

Binomial Theorem

$$ \boxed{ (a+b)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} a^{n-k}b^k } $$

for nonnegative integer $n$.

Expanded Form

The theorem begins:

$$ (a+b)^n = \binom n0a^n + \binom n1a^{n-1}b + \binom n2a^{n-2}b^2 +\cdots+ \binom nn b^n. $$

Binomial Coefficients

The numbers:

$$ \boxed{ \binom nk } $$

are called binomial coefficients.

They are given by:

$$ \boxed{ \binom nk = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!} }. $$

Why They Are Called Binomial Coefficients

They appear as the coefficients in the expansion of powers of a binomial.

For example:

$$ (a+b)^4 = a^4+4a^3b+6a^2b^2+4ab^3+b^4. $$

The coefficients:

$$ \boxed{ 1,\ 4,\ 6,\ 4,\ 1 } $$

are the binomial coefficients for $n=4$.

Pascal's Triangle

Binomial coefficients can be arranged in Pascal's Triangle:

$$ \begin{array}{ccccccccc} &&&&1\\ &&&1&&1\\ &&1&&2&&1\\ &1&&3&&3&&1\\ 1&&4&&6&&4&&1 \end{array} $$

Each interior entry is the sum of the two entries above it.

Combinatorial Meaning

The coefficient:

$$ \binom nk $$

counts the number of ways to choose $k$ objects from $n$ objects without regard to order.

This creates a direct connection between binomial algebra and combinatorics.

Binomial Distribution Connection

The binomial distribution receives its name from the same coefficients and expansion structure.

For $n$ independent Bernoulli trials with success probability $p$:

$$ \boxed{ P(X=k) = \binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k} }. $$

Summing over all possible $k$ reflects:

$$ (p+(1-p))^n=1. $$

Binomial versus Binomial Distribution

A binomial is an algebraic expression with two terms. A binomial distribution is a probability distribution describing a count of successes in repeated Bernoulli trials.

The two ideas are historically and algebraically connected through the Binomial Theorem, but they are not the same mathematical object.

Binomial versus Monomial

A monomial has one term:

$$ 5x^3. $$

A binomial has two:

$$ 5x^3-2. $$

Binomial versus Trinomial

A trinomial has three terms:

$$ x^2+5x+6. $$

Binomial versus Polynomial

A binomial is a particular kind of polynomial classified by its number of terms.

$$ \boxed{ \text{binomial} \subset \text{polynomial expressions} } $$

under the usual elementary polynomial conventions.

Not Every Two-Part Written Expression Is a Binomial

Merely seeing two visible pieces does not guarantee that an expression is a polynomial binomial.

For example:

$$ \frac1x+2 $$

has two additive terms, but under the ordinary elementary definition it is not a polynomial because $x$ occurs with exponent $-1$.

It may informally be described as a two-term algebraic expression, but calling it a polynomial binomial requires attention to the convention being used.

Radicals and Binomial Expressions

Likewise:

$$ \sqrt{x}+1 $$

is a two-term expression but is not a polynomial in $x$ under standard elementary definitions because:

$$ \sqrt{x}=x^{1/2}. $$

Zero Coefficients

A term with coefficient zero disappears.

For example:

$$ x^2+0x = x^2. $$

After simplification, this is a monomial rather than a binomial.

Factoring Out a Common Factor

A binomial may have a greatest common factor:

$$ 6x^3+9x^2 = \boxed{ 3x^2(2x+3) }. $$

The expression inside the parentheses remains a binomial.

Binomial Equations

A binomial may appear as one side of an equation:

$$ x^2-9=0. $$

Factoring gives:

$$ (x-3)(x+3)=0, $$

and therefore:

$$ \boxed{ x=\pm3 }. $$

Binomial Functions

A polynomial function whose defining polynomial contains two nonzero terms may informally be described as a binomial polynomial function.

For example:

$$ f(x)=x^3-4x. $$

Common Misconception: Two Variables Means Binomial

The prefix bi- counts terms, not variables.

Thus:

$$ xy $$

contains two variables but only one term, so it is a monomial.

Common Misconception: Two Factors Means Binomial

In:

$$ (x+1)(x-2), $$

there are two binomial factors, but the product itself expands to:

$$ x^2-x-2, $$

which is a trinomial.

Common Misconception: Parentheses Make One Term

The expression:

$$ (x+5) $$

is still a binomial. Parentheses group the expression but do not merge its two additive terms into one polynomial term.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$x+3$ "The binomial x plus three."
$2a-5b$ "A two-term expression, two a minus five b."
$(a+b)^2$ "The square of the binomial a plus b."
$(a+b)(a-b)$ "The product of conjugate binomials."
$\binom nk$ "n choose k," or "the binomial coefficient n choose k."
$(a+b)^n$ "The nth power of the binomial a plus b."

In polynomial classification, binomial describes the number of nonzero terms after simplification. The word may also appear adjectivally in expressions such as binomial coefficient, binomial theorem, and binomial distribution.

Defense Notation Advice: Simplify and combine like terms before classifying an expression by number of terms. Also distinguish a polynomial binomial from a merely two-term expression containing negative or fractional exponents.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: A binomial is a polynomial expression with exactly two nonzero terms.
  • Typical Form: $$ \boxed{ A+B } $$ or $A-B$, where $A$ and $B$ are terms.
  • Term Count: The prefix bi- refers to two terms, not two variables or factors.
  • Like Terms: Combine like terms before deciding whether an expression is a binomial.
  • Polynomial Classification: A binomial is a special type of polynomial.
  • Degree: The degree is determined by the highest-degree nonzero term under the relevant polynomial convention.
  • Subtraction: A difference such as $a-b$ is still a binomial.
  • Multiplication: Products of binomials are expanded by the distributive property.
  • FOIL: A mnemonic for distributing two binomials.
  • Square of a Sum: $$ \boxed{ (a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2 }. $$
  • Square of a Difference: $$ \boxed{ (a-b)^2=a^2-2ab+b^2 }. $$
  • Conjugate Product: $$ \boxed{ (a+b)(a-b)=a^2-b^2 }. $$
  • Difference of Squares: $$ \boxed{ a^2-b^2=(a-b)(a+b) }. $$
  • Sum of Cubes: $$ \boxed{ a^3+b^3=(a+b)(a^2-ab+b^2) }. $$
  • Difference of Cubes: $$ \boxed{ a^3-b^3=(a-b)(a^2+ab+b^2) }. $$
  • Binomial Theorem: $$ \boxed{ (a+b)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom nk a^{n-k}b^k }. $$
  • Binomial Coefficient: $$ \boxed{ \binom nk = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!} }. $$
  • Pascal's Triangle: Provides the coefficients in successive binomial expansions.
  • Combinatorics: $\binom nk$ counts $k$-element selections from an $n$-element set.
  • Probability Connection: The binomial distribution uses binomial coefficients in probabilities for repeated Bernoulli trials.
  • Monomial Contrast: A monomial has one term.
  • Trinomial Contrast: A trinomial has three terms.
  • Polynomial Contrast: Polynomial is the broader category; binomial specifies a two-term polynomial.
  • Rational-Expression Caution: A two-term expression such as $1/x+2$ is not ordinarily a polynomial binomial.
  • Radical Caution: $\sqrt{x}+1$ has two terms but is not a polynomial in $x$ under the standard nonnegative-integer-exponent definition.
  • Zero-Coefficient Caution: A zero term disappears, so $x^2+0x$ simplifies to a monomial.
  • Common Error: Do not classify an expression before combining like terms.
  • Common Error: Do not assume an expression with two variables is a binomial.
  • Common Error: Do not assume a product of two binomials is itself necessarily a binomial.
  • Common Error: Do not square a binomial by squaring only its two terms; include the cross-term.
  • Terminological Contrast: Term is one additive component of an expression. Monomial has one term. Binomial has two terms. Trinomial has three terms. Polynomial is the broader finite sum of polynomial terms. Binomial coefficient is a combinatorial number appearing in powers of binomials. Binomial Theorem expands powers of a binomial. Binomial distribution is a probability distribution associated with repeated Bernoulli trials.
