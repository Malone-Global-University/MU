Algebraic Numbers

Subfield: Algebraic Structures & Number Theory

Etymology & Roots

From the Arabic al-jabr (الجبر), literally translating to "the restoration of broken parts" or "reunion," popularized by the 9th-century polymath al-Khwarizmi. The term algebraic references structural systems governed by operational equilibrium, equation solving, and balance. It is paired with the Latin numerus ("number"). Historically, early modern mathematicians introduced "algebraic number" to distinguish values that naturally emerge as solutions to polynomial balancing acts from numbers that exist completely outside the reach of basic rational polynomials.

Operational Nuance

An algebraic number is any real or complex number that is a solution to a non-zero polynomial equation with rational (or integer) coefficients. This means that substituting the number makes the polynomial equal zero.

In formal mathematics, an algebraic number is defined as:

$$ \alpha \in \mathbb{C} \quad \text{such that} \quad a_n\alpha^n + a_{n-1}\alpha^{n-1} + \dots + a_1\alpha + a_0 = 0 $$ $$ \text{where } n \ge 1, \, a_i \in \mathbb{Q}, \, \text{and } a_n \neq 0 $$

The set of all algebraic numbers is denoted by $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$.

Common Misconception: A common mistake is to assume that numbers written with radicals are automatically algebraic, while familiar constants such as $\pi$ and $e$ are transcendental simply because they are not expressed as roots. The notation itself is not what determines the classification. For example, $\sqrt{2}$ is algebraic because it satisfies the polynomial equation $x^2 - 2 = 0$ with rational coefficients. More generally, numbers obtained from rational or algebraic numbers through a finite combination of arithmetic operations and extraction of roots remain algebraic. However, not every algebraic number can be expressed using radicals. Beginning with polynomials of degree five, there exist polynomial equations with rational coefficients whose roots are algebraic but cannot be represented by any finite expression involving rational numbers, arithmetic operations, and radicals. This distinction, formalized through Galois theory, shows that being algebraic is a broader property than being expressible by radicals. By contrast, $\pi$ and $e$ are transcendental because neither satisfies any non-zero polynomial equation with rational coefficients.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\alpha \in \overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ "Alpha is in Q-bar," or equivalently, "Alpha is algebraic over the rationals."
$[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha) : \mathbb{Q}] = d$ "Q adjoin alpha has degree d over Q."
$f \in \mathbb{Q}[x], \, f(\alpha) = 0$ "f is a polynomial over Q, and f of alpha equals zero," or more naturally, "Alpha is a root of f."

Defense Notation Advice: During an oral thesis defense or mathematical presentation, avoid saying that "alpha is algebraic because it can be plugged into an equation." This is too vague, since virtually any number can appear in an equation. Instead, state precisely that "alpha is algebraic over $\mathbb{Q}$ because it is a root of a non-zero polynomial with rational coefficients," or, in more advanced terminology, that "alpha is annihilated by a non-zero polynomial in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$." Also distinguish carefully between an algebraic number and an algebraic integer. An algebraic integer is a complex number that is a root of a monic polynomial with integer coefficients.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Field Theory & Galois Theory: Within pure algebra, algebraic numbers are analyzed via their minimal polynomials, the unique monic polynomial of lowest degree that evaluates to zero at that number. This framework allows mathematicians to construct finite field extensions and study the symmetries of root permutations using Galois groups.
  • Algebraic Number Theory: When restricted to ring domains, focus shifts to algebraic integers. Here, standard factorization laws break down, requiring the introduction of Dedekind domains and ideals to restore unique prime ideal factorization when unique element factorization fails.
  • Diophantine Approximation (Analysis): Algebraic irrational numbers cannot be approximated arbitrarily well by fractions. Liouville's theorem places a mathematical limit on how closely rational numbers can approximate an algebraic number. Liouville used this fact in reverse: he constructed numbers that could be approximated by fractions far more accurately than any algebraic number could be. Those numbers therefore had to be transcendental. This provided one of the first methods for explicitly proving that particular transcendental numbers exist.
