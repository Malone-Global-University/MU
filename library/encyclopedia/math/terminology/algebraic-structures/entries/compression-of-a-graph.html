Compression of a Graph

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

A compression of a graph is a transformation that reduces the vertical or horizontal distances of points on a graph relative to a reference axis. The resulting graph appears squeezed toward that axis.

For a function:

$$ y=f(x), $$

a vertical compression may be written:

$$ \boxed{ y=af(x), \qquad 0

while a horizontal compression may be written:

$$ \boxed{ y=f(bx), \qquad b>1 }. $$

The word compression preserves its ordinary meaning of reducing extent. In graph transformations, however, it refers specifically to coordinate scaling rather than cropping, truncating, or deleting part of a graph.

$$ \boxed{ \text{compression} = \text{scaling graph coordinates toward an axis} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Vertical Compression

A vertical compression multiplies every output of a function by a factor between $0$ and $1$:

$$ \boxed{ g(x)=af(x), \qquad 0

The corresponding coordinate rule is:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(x,ay) }. $$

Horizontal coordinates remain unchanged while vertical distances from the $x$-axis are multiplied by $a$.

Example: Vertical Compression

Begin with:

$$ f(x)=x^2. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ g(x)=\frac12x^2 } $$

is a vertical compression by factor $1/2$.

For example, the point:

$$ (2,4) $$

becomes:

$$ \boxed{ (2,2) }. $$

Why Multiplication Outside Acts Vertically

If:

$$ f(c)=d, $$

then:

$$ g(c)=af(c)=ad. $$

The input $c$ is unchanged, but the output $d$ is multiplied by $a$.

$$ \boxed{ af(x) \Rightarrow \text{output scaling} }. $$

Horizontal Compression

A horizontal compression changes the input of the function:

$$ \boxed{ g(x)=f(bx), \qquad b>1 }. $$

The corresponding coordinate rule is:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y) \mapsto \left(\frac{x}{b},y\right) }. $$

Thus horizontal coordinates are multiplied by $1/b$.

The Reciprocal Effect

The most important notational subtlety of horizontal transformations is that the visible multiplier inside the function is the reciprocal of the actual horizontal coordinate scale factor.

$$ \boxed{ f(bx) \Rightarrow \text{horizontal scale factor } \frac1{|b|} }. $$

Therefore:

$$ f(2x) $$

compresses horizontal distances by:

$$ \boxed{ \frac12 }. $$

Why the Horizontal Factor Is Reciprocal

Suppose the original graph contains:

$$ (c,f(c)). $$

In the transformed graph $g(x)=f(bx)$, the same function input $c$ occurs when:

$$ bx=c. $$

Hence:

$$ x=\frac cb. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \left(c,f(c)\right) \mapsto \left(\frac cb,f(c)\right) }. $$

Example: Horizontal Compression

Consider:

$$ f(x)=x^2. $$

Then:

$$ g(x)=f(2x). $$

The graph is horizontally compressed by coordinate factor $1/2$.

Algebraically:

$$ g(x)=(2x)^2=4x^2. $$

Although the simplified expression $4x^2$ can also be described as a vertical stretch of $x^2$, the transformation $f(2x)$ specifically encodes a horizontal compression. A particular graph may therefore admit more than one equivalent transformation description.

Compression versus Stretch

A compression reduces distances from an axis; a stretch increases them.

Vertically:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} 0<|a|<1 &\Rightarrow \text{vertical compression},\\ |a|>1 &\Rightarrow \text{vertical stretch}. \end{aligned} } $$

Horizontally, in $f(bx)$:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} |b|>1 &\Rightarrow \text{horizontal compression},\\ 0<|b|<1 &\Rightarrow \text{horizontal stretch}. \end{aligned} } $$

Negative Factors Add Reflection

If:

$$ -1

then:

$$ g(x)=af(x) $$

combines vertical compression by $|a|$ with reflection across the $x$-axis.

Likewise, if:

$$ b<-1, $$

then $f(bx)$ combines horizontal compression by $1/|b|$ with reflection across the $y$-axis.

General Transformation Form

Compression commonly appears within the general graph-transformation form:

$$ \boxed{ g(x) = a\,f\!\left(b(x-h)\right)+k }. $$

Here:

  • $a$ controls vertical scale and possible reflection,
  • $b$ controls horizontal scale and possible reflection,
  • $h$ controls horizontal translation, and
  • $k$ controls vertical translation.

Compression around a Shifted Horizontal Axis

The graph need not be compressed toward $y=0$.

To compress vertical distances from $y=k$ by factor $a$:

$$ \boxed{ g(x)=k+a[f(x)-k] }. $$

Then:

$$ g(x)-k=a[f(x)-k]. $$

Compression around a Shifted Vertical Axis

A horizontal coordinate $x$ can be compressed toward $x=h$ according to:

$$ \boxed{ x' = h+c(x-h), \qquad 0

This scales horizontal displacement from the line $x=h$ rather than from the ordinary $y$-axis.

Effect on the Domain

If:

$$ g(x)=f(bx), $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(g) = \{x:bx\in\operatorname{Dom}(f)\} }. $$

Thus a horizontal compression transforms domain coordinates by the reciprocal scale factor.

Example: Bounded Domain

Suppose:

$$ \operatorname{Dom}(f)=[-4,4]. $$

For:

$$ g(x)=f(2x), $$

the condition:

$$ -4\le2x\le4 $$

gives:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(g)=[-2,2] }. $$

Effect on the Range

For:

$$ g(x)=af(x), $$

the output values are multiplied by $a$. Therefore, when $a>0$:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Range}(g) = a\operatorname{Range}(f) }, $$

interpreted as scalar multiplication of the range set.

Example: Range Compression

If:

$$ \operatorname{Range}(f)=[-6,10] $$

and:

$$ g(x)=\frac12f(x), $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Range}(g)=[-3,5] }. $$

Intercepts under Vertical Compression

For nonzero $a$, the zeros of:

$$ g(x)=af(x) $$

are the same as the zeros of $f$ because:

$$ af(x)=0 \iff f(x)=0. $$

Therefore vertical compression does not change the $x$-intercepts.

Vertical Compression of the y-Intercept

If the original $y$-intercept is:

$$ f(0)=c, $$

then:

$$ g(0)=ac. $$

Thus the $y$-intercept is vertically scaled with the rest of the graph.

Horizontal Compression of Zeros

Suppose:

$$ f(c)=0. $$

For:

$$ g(x)=f(bx), $$

the corresponding zero satisfies:

$$ bx=c. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ x=\frac cb }. $$

Horizontal Compression of Periodic Graphs

If $f$ has period $T$ and:

$$ g(x)=f(bx), $$

then a corresponding period is:

$$ \boxed{ T_g=\frac{T}{|b|} }. $$

Example: Sine Function

The graph:

$$ y=\sin x $$

has period:

$$ 2\pi. $$

The graph:

$$ y=\sin(4x) $$

is horizontally compressed and has period:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\pi}{2} }. $$

Vertical Compression of Sinusoids

For:

$$ y=A\sin x, $$

vertical compression by factor $c$, where $0

$$ y=cA\sin x. $$

The amplitude changes from $|A|$ to:

$$ \boxed{ c|A| }. $$

Compression and Frequency

Horizontal compression of a periodic graph increases the number of cycles visible over a fixed horizontal interval.

In a sinusoid:

$$ y=\sin(bx), $$

increasing $|b|$ shortens the period. In applications where $x$ represents time, this corresponds to increased frequency.

Compression of Exponential Graphs

For an exponential function:

$$ f(x)=a^x, $$

horizontal compression produces:

$$ f(bx)=a^{bx}. $$

This can also be written:

$$ \boxed{ a^{bx}=(a^b)^x }. $$

Thus horizontal compression changes the effective exponential base when the formula is algebraically rewritten.

Compression of Logarithmic Graphs

If:

$$ f(x)=\log x, $$

then:

$$ g(x)=\log(bx) $$

represents a horizontal transformation. Using logarithm laws:

$$ \log(bx)=\log b+\log x $$

where defined.

Thus the same graph may also be described through a vertical translation, again illustrating that algebraically equivalent formulas can produce multiple valid transformation descriptions.

Asymptotes under Vertical Compression

If $f(x)$ has horizontal asymptote:

$$ y=L, $$

then $g(x)=af(x)$ has corresponding horizontal asymptote:

$$ \boxed{ y=aL }. $$

Horizontal Compression of Vertical Asymptotes

If $f$ has a vertical asymptote at:

$$ x=c, $$

then $f(bx)$ has the corresponding vertical asymptote at:

$$ \boxed{ x=\frac cb }. $$

Compression of Piecewise Functions

Graph compression applies to piecewise functions as well. For horizontal compression, every domain boundary is transformed by the reciprocal coordinate factor.

If:

$$ f(x)= \begin{cases} f_1(x),&x

then $f(bx)$ changes the boundary to:

$$ \boxed{ x=\frac cb } $$

when $b>0$.

Compression of Scatter Plots versus Function Graphs

If a set of plotted points is compressed geometrically, the coordinate rule can be applied directly to every point. However, in mathematical terminology, compression of a graph often refers specifically to transformation of the graph of a function rather than statistical data compression.

Compression Can Be Reversed

A nonzero vertical compression:

$$ y\mapsto ay $$

is reversed by:

$$ \boxed{ y\mapsto\frac1a y }. $$

Likewise, horizontal coordinate compression by factor $c$ is reversed by horizontal scaling by $1/c$.

Compression Is Not Cropping

Cropping changes which portion of a graph is displayed. Compression changes the coordinates of the graph itself.

$$ \boxed{ \text{compression} \ne \text{restriction of viewing window} }. $$

Compression Is Not Domain Restriction

Restricting:

$$ f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R $$

to:

$$ f|_{[a,b]} $$

removes inputs outside the interval. It does not rescale existing horizontal coordinates and therefore is not graph compression.

Compression Is Not Translation

Translation moves a graph:

$$ f(x-h)+k, $$

while compression changes scale:

$$ af(bx). $$

Compression Is Not Reflection

Reflection reverses graph orientation relative to an axis:

$$ -f(x) \qquad\text{or}\qquad f(-x). $$

Compression reduces distances. Negative factors may combine both effects, but they remain conceptually distinct transformations.

Compression and Transformation Order

When several transformations occur together, algebraic grouping determines their interaction.

For example:

$$ g(x)=f(2(x-3)) $$

should be interpreted from its fully factored input structure rather than by mechanically reading visible terms from left to right.

Equivalent Forms Can Hide Transformations

Consider:

$$ g(x)=f(2x-6). $$

Factoring gives:

$$ \boxed{ g(x)=f(2(x-3)) }. $$

This form makes the horizontal scale and translation easier to interpret correctly.

Common Misconception: f(2x) Stretches Horizontally by 2

The visible multiplier and coordinate scale are reciprocal:

$$ \boxed{ f(2x) \Rightarrow x\text{-coordinates multiplied by }\frac12 }. $$

Common Misconception: One-Half Inside Means Horizontal Compression

Consider:

$$ f\left(\frac12x\right). $$

The horizontal coordinate factor is:

$$ \frac{1}{1/2}=2. $$

Therefore the graph is horizontally stretched by factor $2$, not compressed.

Common Misconception: Multipliers Inside and Outside Behave the Same Way

Outside scaling acts directly on outputs:

$$ af(x). $$

Inside scaling acts inversely on horizontal coordinates:

$$ f(bx). $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ af(x) \text{ and } f(ax) \text{ are different transformations} }. $$

Common Misconception: Compression Always Changes Both Axes

Vertical compression leaves $x$-coordinates unchanged. Horizontal compression leaves $y$-coordinates unchanged.

Common Misconception: Compression Necessarily Changes the Domain

If the original function has domain $\mathbb R$, then for nonzero $b$:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f(bx))=\mathbb R }. $$

The graph is horizontally compressed even though the domain remains all real numbers.

Common Misconception: Compression Necessarily Changes Every Intercept

Vertical compression by a nonzero factor preserves all $x$-intercepts because zeros remain zeros. Horizontal compression generally changes the locations of nonzero $x$-intercepts.

Common Misconception: A Compressed Parabola Must Be Narrower

The words wider and narrower can cause confusion because a vertical compression of:

$$ y=x^2 $$

produces:

$$ y=\frac12x^2, $$

which visually appears wider. The transformation is nevertheless a vertical compression because vertical coordinates are reduced.

$$ \boxed{ \text{transformation name follows coordinate action, not merely visual appearance} }. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$g(x)=\frac12f(x)$ "The graph of f is vertically compressed by a factor of one-half."
$g(x)=f(2x)$ "The graph of f is horizontally compressed so that x-coordinates are halved."
$(x,y)\mapsto(x,\frac12y)$ "Keep x fixed and multiply every y-coordinate by one-half."
$(x,y)\mapsto(\frac13x,y)$ "Multiply every x-coordinate by one-third."
$g(x)=a f(bx)$ "Scale vertically by a and horizontally by one over the magnitude of b."
$y=\sin(4x)$ "The sine graph is horizontally compressed so its period is pi over two."

The expression compressed by a factor of $b$ can be ambiguous for horizontal transformations. Some instructional sources call $f(2x)$ a "horizontal compression by a factor of 2," referring to the multiplier inside the function. Others describe it as a compression by factor $1/2$, referring to the actual coordinate scale.

The most unambiguous statement is:

$$ \boxed{ f(2x) : \text{horizontal coordinates are multiplied by }\frac12 }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Describe graph transformations by their actual coordinate effects whenever factor language could be ambiguous. For $g(x)=af(x)$, vertical coordinates are multiplied directly by $a$. For $g(x)=f(bx)$, horizontal coordinates are multiplied by $1/b$ when $b>0$. Factor expressions such as $f(2x-6)$ into $f(2(x-3))$ before interpreting combined transformations, and distinguish compression from cropping, domain restriction, translation, and reflection.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: Compression of a graph reduces vertical or horizontal coordinate distances relative to a reference axis.
  • Vertical Compression: $$ \boxed{ g(x)=af(x), \qquad 0<|a|<1 }. $$
  • Vertical Coordinate Rule: $$ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(x,ay) }. $$
  • Horizontal Compression: $$ \boxed{ g(x)=f(bx), \qquad |b|>1 }. $$
  • Horizontal Coordinate Rule: $$ \boxed{ (x,y) \mapsto \left(\frac{x}{b},y\right) }. $$
  • Reciprocal Rule: The horizontal coordinate scale associated with $f(bx)$ is $1/|b|$.
  • Outside Multiplier: Acts directly on outputs and therefore changes vertical scale.
  • Inside Multiplier: Acts on inputs and therefore changes horizontal scale inversely.
  • Negative Vertical Factor: Combines vertical scaling with reflection across the $x$-axis.
  • Negative Horizontal Factor: Combines horizontal scaling with reflection across the $y$-axis.
  • General Transformation: $$ \boxed{ g(x)=a\,f\!\left(b(x-h)\right)+k }. $$
  • Domain Effect: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f(bx)) = \{x:bx\in\operatorname{Dom}(f)\} }. $$
  • Range Effect: Vertical compression multiplies output values by its vertical scale factor.
  • Zeros: A nonzero vertical compression preserves $x$-intercepts.
  • Horizontal Zeros: A zero originally at $x=c$ moves to $x=c/b$ under $f(bx)$.
  • Periodic Functions: If $f$ has period $T$, then $f(bx)$ has corresponding period $T/|b|$.
  • Amplitude: Vertical compression reduces the amplitude of sinusoidal graphs.
  • Frequency: Horizontal compression increases cycle frequency over a fixed horizontal interval for periodic graphs.
  • Horizontal Asymptote: If $f$ has horizontal asymptote $y=L$, then $af(x)$ has corresponding asymptote $y=aL$.
  • Vertical Asymptote: If $f$ has a vertical asymptote at $x=c$, then $f(bx)$ has the corresponding asymptote at $x=c/b$.
  • Equivalent Descriptions: Algebraically equivalent formulas can sometimes allow both horizontal and vertical transformation descriptions.
  • Compression versus Stretch: Compression reduces coordinate distances; stretch enlarges them.
  • Compression versus Translation: Translation changes location; compression changes scale.
  • Compression versus Reflection: Reflection reverses orientation relative to an axis; compression reduces coordinate distances.
  • Compression versus Restriction: Domain restriction removes allowed inputs; compression rescales their graphical positions.
  • Compression versus Cropping: Cropping changes the viewing region rather than transforming the graph itself.
  • Common Error: Do not interpret $f(2x)$ as a horizontal stretch by $2$.
  • Common Error: Do not interpret $f(x/2)$ as horizontal compression; it produces a horizontal stretch by factor $2$.
  • Common Error: Do not assume factors inside and outside a function transform the graph in the same direction.
  • Common Error: Do not infer the transformation solely from whether the graph looks wider or narrower.
  • Terminological Contrast: A vertical compression multiplies output coordinates by a factor whose magnitude is less than $1$. A horizontal compression reduces input-coordinate spacing and is represented by $f(bx)$ with $|b|>1$. A stretch enlarges coordinate distances. A translation changes position without changing scale. A reflection reverses a graph relative to an axis. A domain restriction removes allowed inputs rather than rescaling them. Cropping changes the displayed viewing region rather than the mathematical graph.
