Function Operations

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

Function operations are operations that combine, transform, or otherwise act on functions to produce new functions. In elementary algebra, the principal operations include addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and composition.

If $f$ and $g$ are numerical functions, the pointwise arithmetic operations are:

$$ \boxed{ (f+g)(x)=f(x)+g(x) } $$
$$ \boxed{ (f-g)(x)=f(x)-g(x) } $$
$$ \boxed{ (fg)(x)=f(x)g(x) } $$
$$ \boxed{ \left(\frac{f}{g}\right)(x) = \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} } $$

wherever the expressions are defined.

Composition is different in structure:

$$ \boxed{ (f\circ g)(x)=f(g(x)) }. $$

The phrase function operations therefore covers two distinct ideas: operations performed pointwise on function values and operations such as composition that use the output of one function as the input of another.

Operational Nuance

Pointwise Operations

Addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division of numerical functions are usually defined pointwise.

This means that at each admissible input $x$, the corresponding function values are combined using ordinary arithmetic.

Function Addition

For functions $f$ and $g$:

$$ \boxed{ (f+g)(x)=f(x)+g(x) }. $$

Addition Example

Let:

$$ f(x)=x^2, \qquad g(x)=3x+1. $$

Then:

$$ (f+g)(x) = x^2+3x+1. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ f+g:x\mapsto x^2+3x+1 }. $$

Function Subtraction

$$ \boxed{ (f-g)(x)=f(x)-g(x) }. $$

Subtraction Example

With:

$$ f(x)=x^2, \qquad g(x)=3x+1, $$

one obtains:

$$ \boxed{ (f-g)(x)=x^2-3x-1 }. $$

Function Multiplication

The product of two numerical functions is:

$$ \boxed{ (fg)(x)=f(x)g(x) }. $$

Multiplication Example

If:

$$ f(x)=x+2, \qquad g(x)=x-5, $$

then:

$$ (fg)(x) = (x+2)(x-5) $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ (fg)(x)=x^2-3x-10 }. $$

Product Notation

The notation:

$$ fg $$

commonly means the pointwise product function, not composition.

Composition is written:

$$ f\circ g. $$

Function Division

The quotient function is:

$$ \boxed{ \left(\frac fg\right)(x) = \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} }. $$

It is defined only where both functions are defined and:

$$ \boxed{ g(x)\neq0 }. $$

Division Example

Let:

$$ f(x)=x^2-1, \qquad g(x)=x-1. $$

Then:

$$ \left(\frac fg\right)(x) = \frac{x^2-1}{x-1}. $$

Factoring:

$$ x^2-1=(x-1)(x+1), $$

gives:

$$ \left(\frac fg\right)(x)=x+1 $$

but the original quotient function still has the restriction:

$$ \boxed{x\neq1}. $$

Domain of a Sum

The natural domain of $f+g$ is typically the intersection of the domains:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f+g) = \operatorname{Dom}(f)\cap\operatorname{Dom}(g) }. $$

Domain of a Difference

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f-g) = \operatorname{Dom}(f)\cap\operatorname{Dom}(g) }. $$

Domain of a Product

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(fg) = \operatorname{Dom}(f)\cap\operatorname{Dom}(g) }. $$

Domain of a Quotient

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}\left(\frac fg\right) = \{x\in\operatorname{Dom}(f)\cap\operatorname{Dom}(g):g(x)\neq0\} }. $$

Example of Domain Intersection

Let:

$$ f(x)=\sqrt{x}, \qquad g(x)=\frac1{x-2}. $$

Then:

$$ \operatorname{Dom}(f)=[0,\infty), $$
$$ \operatorname{Dom}(g)=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{2\}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f+g) = [0,2)\cup(2,\infty) }. $$

Scalar Multiplication

Multiplying a function by a scalar $c$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ (cf)(x)=c\,f(x) }. $$

Scalar Example

If:

$$ f(x)=x^2-1, $$

then:

$$ (3f)(x) = \boxed{ 3x^2-3 }. $$

Negation of a Function

The additive inverse of $f$ is:

$$ \boxed{ (-f)(x)=-f(x) }. $$

Therefore:

$$ f+(-f)=0, $$

where $0$ denotes the zero function.

Zero Function

$$ \boxed{ 0(x)=0 } $$

for every input in its domain.

Function Addition Is Commutative

For real- or complex-valued functions:

$$ \boxed{ f+g=g+f } $$

wherever both sides have the same domain.

Function Multiplication Is Commutative for Scalar-Valued Functions

If outputs lie in a commutative number system:

$$ \boxed{ fg=gf }. $$

This may fail for matrix-valued or operator-valued functions because their outputs may multiply noncommutatively.

Associativity

Pointwise addition and multiplication inherit associativity from the output number system:

$$ \boxed{ (f+g)+h=f+(g+h) } $$
$$ \boxed{ (fg)h=f(gh) }. $$

Distributivity

$$ \boxed{ f(g+h)=fg+fh }. $$

Here multiplication is pointwise.

Composition

Composition uses the output of one function as the input of another:

$$ \boxed{ (f\circ g)(x) = f(g(x)) }. $$

Composition Order

In:

$$ f\circ g, $$

$g$ acts first and $f$ acts second.

$$ \boxed{ x \xrightarrow{g} g(x) \xrightarrow{f} f(g(x)) }. $$

Composition Example

Let:

$$ f(x)=2x+1, \qquad g(x)=x^2. $$

Then:

$$ (f\circ g)(x) = f(x^2) = \boxed{ 2x^2+1 }. $$

But:

$$ (g\circ f)(x) = g(2x+1) = \boxed{ (2x+1)^2 }. $$

Composition Is Generally Not Commutative

$$ \boxed{ f\circ g \neq g\circ f } $$

in general.

Composition Is Associative

$$ \boxed{ (f\circ g)\circ h = f\circ(g\circ h) }. $$

Thus compositions of several functions may often be written without excessive parentheses.

Identity Function for Composition

The identity function satisfies:

$$ \operatorname{id}(x)=x. $$

Consequently:

$$ \boxed{ f\circ\operatorname{id} = \operatorname{id}\circ f = f }. $$

Domain of a Composition

For:

$$ (f\circ g)(x)=f(g(x)), $$

one must require both:

  • $x$ belongs to the domain of $g$, and
  • $g(x)$ belongs to the domain of $f$.
$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f\circ g) = \{x\in\operatorname{Dom}(g): g(x)\in\operatorname{Dom}(f)\} }. $$

Composition Domain Example

Let:

$$ f(x)=\sqrt{x}, \qquad g(x)=x-3. $$

Then:

$$ (f\circ g)(x)=\sqrt{x-3}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f\circ g) = [3,\infty) }. $$

Reverse Composition May Have a Different Domain

With the same functions:

$$ (g\circ f)(x)=\sqrt{x}-3, $$

whose domain is:

$$ \boxed{ [0,\infty) }. $$

Inverse Function

If $f:A\to B$ is bijective, its inverse:

$$ \boxed{ f^{-1}:B\to A } $$

reverses the assignment of $f$.

Inverse as a Composition Operation

$$ \boxed{ f^{-1}\circ f = \operatorname{id}_A } $$
$$ \boxed{ f\circ f^{-1} = \operatorname{id}_B }. $$

Inverse Is Not Reciprocal

The inverse function:

$$ f^{-1} $$

is not the same as the reciprocal function:

$$ \frac1f. $$

For example, if:

$$ f(x)=2x+1, $$

then:

$$ f^{-1}(x)=\frac{x-1}{2}, $$

whereas:

$$ \frac1{f(x)} = \frac1{2x+1}. $$

Function Powers by Pointwise Multiplication

For a numerical function $f$:

$$ \boxed{ f^2(x) = (f(x))^2 } $$

may denote the pointwise square.

Iteration Powers

Repeated composition is better written:

$$ \boxed{ f^{\circ2}(x) = f(f(x)) }. $$

This avoids confusion between function multiplication and function iteration.

Example of the Distinction

If:

$$ f(x)=x+1, $$

then the pointwise square is:

$$ f(x)^2=(x+1)^2, $$

while the second iterate is:

$$ f^{\circ2}(x) = f(x+1) = \boxed{x+2}. $$

Absolute Value of a Function

For a real-valued function:

$$ \boxed{ |f|(x)=|f(x)| }. $$

Maximum and Minimum of Functions

Pointwise maximum and minimum may be defined:

$$ \boxed{ \max(f,g)(x) = \max\{f(x),g(x)\} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \min(f,g)(x) = \min\{f(x),g(x)\} }. $$

Reciprocal Function

Where $f(x)\neq0$:

$$ \boxed{ \left(\frac1f\right)(x) = \frac1{f(x)} }. $$

Polynomial Functions Are Closed under Basic Arithmetic

If $f$ and $g$ are polynomial functions, then:

  • $f+g$ is polynomial,
  • $f-g$ is polynomial,
  • $fg$ is polynomial.

The quotient $f/g$ is generally rational rather than polynomial.

Rational Functions

If $f$ and $g$ are rational functions, their sum, difference, product, and quotient are rational functions wherever denominators remain nonzero.

Continuous Functions

If $f$ and $g$ are continuous at $a$, then:

$$ f+g,\quad f-g,\quad fg $$

are continuous at $a$.

Also:

$$ \frac fg $$

is continuous at $a$ when:

$$ \boxed{ g(a)\neq0 }. $$

Composition of Continuous Functions

If $g$ is continuous at $a$ and $f$ is continuous at $g(a)$, then:

$$ \boxed{ f\circ g \text{ is continuous at }a }. $$

Derivative of a Sum

$$ \boxed{ (f+g)'=f'+g' }. $$

Derivative of a Difference

$$ \boxed{ (f-g)'=f'-g' }. $$

Derivative of a Scalar Multiple

$$ \boxed{ (cf)'=cf' }. $$

Product Rule

$$ \boxed{ (fg)' = f'g+fg' }. $$

Quotient Rule

$$ \boxed{ \left(\frac fg\right)' = \frac{f'g-fg'}{g^2} } $$

wherever $g\neq0$.

Chain Rule

Composition gives rise to the chain rule:

$$ \boxed{ (f\circ g)'(x) = f'(g(x))g'(x) }. $$

Integration and Linearity

Integration respects sums and scalar multiplication:

$$ \boxed{ \int(f+g) = \int f+\int g } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \int cf = c\int f }. $$

Vector Space of Functions

Many collections of functions form vector spaces under pointwise addition and scalar multiplication.

For example, real-valued functions on a set $X$ can be combined by:

$$ (f+g)(x)=f(x)+g(x), $$
$$ (cf)(x)=cf(x). $$

Function Algebra

When pointwise multiplication is also available, suitable collections of functions may form algebras or rings.

Closure

A collection of functions is closed under an operation when applying that operation to members of the collection produces another member of the same collection.

Example of Closure

Polynomial functions are closed under:

$$ \boxed{ +,\quad -,\quad \times }. $$

They are not generally closed under division.

Matrix-Valued Functions

If functions output matrices, pointwise addition is defined normally, but multiplication inherits matrix noncommutativity.

Thus:

$$ F(x)G(x) $$

need not equal:

$$ G(x)F(x). $$

Vector-Valued Functions

Vector-valued functions can be added and scaled pointwise:

$$ (\mathbf{f}+\mathbf{g})(t) = \mathbf{f}(t)+\mathbf{g}(t). $$

Dot Product of Vector Functions

For compatible vector-valued functions:

$$ \boxed{ (\mathbf{f}\cdot\mathbf{g})(t) = \mathbf{f}(t)\cdot\mathbf{g}(t) }. $$

This produces a scalar-valued function.

Cross Product of Vector Functions

In three-dimensional contexts:

$$ \boxed{ (\mathbf{f}\times\mathbf{g})(t) = \mathbf{f}(t)\times\mathbf{g}(t) }. $$

This produces another vector-valued function.

Transformation Operations

Function notation also supports transformations such as:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)+k } $$

for vertical translation, and:

$$ \boxed{ f(x-h) } $$

for horizontal translation.

Vertical Scaling

$$ \boxed{ af(x) } $$

scales output values by $a$.

Horizontal Scaling

$$ \boxed{ f(bx) } $$

changes the input scale.

Reflection across the $x$-Axis

$$ \boxed{ -f(x) }. $$

Reflection across the $y$-Axis

$$ \boxed{ f(-x) }. $$

Combined Transformation

A transformed function may have the form:

$$ \boxed{ g(x)=a\,f(b(x-h))+k }. $$

This combines vertical scaling or reflection, horizontal scaling or reflection, horizontal translation, and vertical translation.

Operation vs. Transformation

Function operations such as $f+g$ combine two functions, while transformations such as $f(x-h)+k$ modify the inputs or outputs of one function. Both produce new functions, but they describe different structural processes.

Common Error: Adding Inputs Instead of Outputs

Function addition means:

$$ \boxed{ (f+g)(x)=f(x)+g(x) }, $$

not:

$$ f(x+g(x)). $$

Common Error: Confusing Product and Composition

The product:

$$ (fg)(x)=f(x)g(x) $$

is different from:

$$ (f\circ g)(x)=f(g(x)). $$

Common Error: Reversing Composition

In:

$$ f\circ g, $$

the function $g$ acts first.

Common Error: Ignoring Composition Domains

It is not enough for $x$ to lie in $\operatorname{Dom}(g)$. One must also have:

$$ \boxed{ g(x)\in\operatorname{Dom}(f) }. $$

Common Error: Ignoring Quotient Restrictions

In:

$$ \frac fg, $$

inputs where $g(x)=0$ must be removed.

Common Error: Confusing Inverse and Reciprocal

$$ \boxed{ f^{-1} \neq \frac1f } $$

in general.

Common Error: Treating $f^2$ as Unambiguous

Depending on context:

$$ f^2 $$

may suggest pointwise squaring or repeated composition. Use:

$$ (f(x))^2 $$

for pointwise squaring and:

$$ f^{\circ2} $$

for iteration when ambiguity matters.

Common Misconception: Function operations do not all work in the same way. Addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division are normally performed pointwise on outputs, whereas composition sends the output of one function into another. Domains must be recalculated for every new function, especially for quotients and compositions.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$(f+g)(x)=f(x)+g(x)$ "F plus g, evaluated at x, equals f of x plus g of x."
$(f-g)(x)=f(x)-g(x)$ "F minus g, evaluated at x."
$(fg)(x)=f(x)g(x)$ "The product function f g evaluated at x."
$\left(\frac fg\right)(x)=\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}$ "The quotient function f over g evaluated at x."
$(f\circ g)(x)=f(g(x))$ "F composed with g, evaluated at x, equals f of g of x."
$(cf)(x)=cf(x)$ "The scalar multiple c f evaluated at x."
$f^{-1}\circ f=\operatorname{id}$ "F inverse composed with f is the identity."

The symbol:

$$ \boxed{\circ} $$

is called the composition symbol and is commonly read "composed with."

A useful operational reading is:

$$ \boxed{ (f\circ g)(x) = \text{"apply }g\text{ first, then }f\text{"} }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Keep product notation $fg$ distinct from composition notation $f\circ g$. When working with quotients, explicitly exclude zeros of the denominator function. When composing functions, check that every output of the inner function used in the calculation is an admissible input of the outer function. Use $f^{\circ n}$ when repeated composition might otherwise be confused with ordinary powers of function values.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Function Addition: $$ (f+g)(x)=f(x)+g(x). $$
  • Function Subtraction: $$ (f-g)(x)=f(x)-g(x). $$
  • Function Multiplication: $$ (fg)(x)=f(x)g(x). $$
  • Function Division: $$ \left(\frac fg\right)(x)=\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}. $$
  • Quotient Restriction: Require $g(x)\neq0$.
  • Scalar Multiplication: $$ (cf)(x)=cf(x). $$
  • Function Negation: $$ (-f)(x)=-f(x). $$
  • Zero Function: $0(x)=0$.
  • Pointwise Operations: Arithmetic is applied separately at each admissible input.
  • Domain of Sum: Usually the intersection of the domains of $f$ and $g$.
  • Domain of Difference: Usually the intersection of the domains.
  • Domain of Product: Usually the intersection of the domains.
  • Domain of Quotient: Intersection of domains with zeros of the divisor function removed.
  • Composition: $$ (f\circ g)(x)=f(g(x)). $$
  • Composition Order: The inner function $g$ acts first.
  • Composition Domain: Require $x\in\operatorname{Dom}(g)$ and $g(x)\in\operatorname{Dom}(f)$.
  • Composition Noncommutativity: $f\circ g$ need not equal $g\circ f$.
  • Composition Associativity: $(f\circ g)\circ h=f\circ(g\circ h)$.
  • Identity Function: Serves as the identity for composition.
  • Inverse Function: Reverses a bijection.
  • Inverse Composition: $f^{-1}\circ f=\operatorname{id}$ on the original domain.
  • Inverse vs. Reciprocal: $f^{-1}$ is generally not $1/f$.
  • Reciprocal Function: $(1/f)(x)=1/f(x)$ where $f(x)\neq0$.
  • Pointwise Square: $(f(x))^2$ squares each output.
  • Function Iteration: $f^{\circ n}$ means repeated composition.
  • Iteration Distinction: Pointwise powers and repeated composition are different operations.
  • Absolute Value: $|f|(x)=|f(x)|$.
  • Pointwise Maximum: $\max(f,g)(x)=\max\{f(x),g(x)\}$.
  • Pointwise Minimum: $\min(f,g)(x)=\min\{f(x),g(x)\}$.
  • Additive Commutativity: $f+g=g+f$ for ordinary scalar-valued functions.
  • Multiplicative Commutativity: $fg=gf$ when the output multiplication is commutative.
  • Additive Associativity: $(f+g)+h=f+(g+h)$.
  • Multiplicative Associativity: $(fg)h=f(gh)$.
  • Distributivity: $f(g+h)=fg+fh$ under pointwise multiplication.
  • Polynomial Functions: Closed under addition, subtraction, and multiplication.
  • Polynomial Quotient: Usually produces a rational function rather than a polynomial.
  • Rational Functions: Closed under basic arithmetic where denominators remain nonzero.
  • Continuous Functions: Sums, differences, and products preserve continuity.
  • Continuous Quotient: Preserves continuity where the denominator is nonzero.
  • Continuous Composition: Composition of appropriately continuous functions is continuous.
  • Differentiation: Interacts systematically with function operations.
  • Sum Rule: $(f+g)'=f'+g'$.
  • Difference Rule: $(f-g)'=f'-g'$.
  • Constant-Multiple Rule: $(cf)'=cf'$.
  • Product Rule: $(fg)'=f'g+fg'$.
  • Quotient Rule: $\left(f/g\right)'=(f'g-fg')/g^2$.
  • Chain Rule: $(f\circ g)'(x)=f'(g(x))g'(x)$.
  • Integration: Addition and scalar multiplication obey linearity.
  • Vector Space: Function collections often form vector spaces under pointwise addition and scalar multiplication.
  • Function Ring: Suitable scalar-valued function sets may form rings under pointwise addition and multiplication.
  • Function Algebra: A vector space of functions may also carry compatible multiplication.
  • Closure: Describes whether an operation keeps functions inside a specified class.
  • Vector-Valued Functions: Added and scaled pointwise.
  • Dot Product: May combine vector-valued functions into a scalar-valued function.
  • Cross Product: May combine three-dimensional vector-valued functions pointwise.
  • Matrix-Valued Functions: Pointwise multiplication may be noncommutative.
  • Operator-Valued Functions: Product order may matter.
  • Vertical Translation: $f(x)+k$.
  • Horizontal Translation: $f(x-h)$.
  • Vertical Scaling: $af(x)$.
  • Horizontal Scaling: $f(bx)$.
  • Reflection across the $x$-Axis: $-f(x)$.
  • Reflection across the $y$-Axis: $f(-x)$.
  • Combined Transformation: $a\,f(b(x-h))+k$.
  • Transformation Distinction: A transformation of one function is not the same operation as adding or multiplying two independent functions.
  • Precalculus: Function operations organize arithmetic, composition, inverses, and transformations.
  • Calculus: Derivative and integral rules are built around operations on functions.
  • Linear Algebra: Spaces of functions provide important examples of vector spaces and linear transformations.
  • Abstract Algebra: Pointwise operations can give collections of functions group, ring, field, module, or algebra structures under suitable conditions.
  • Analysis: Function operations interact with continuity, convergence, norms, and limits.
  • Probability: Random variables are functions and can be added, multiplied, transformed, and composed with ordinary functions.
  • Computer Science: Function composition models pipelines in which one output becomes the next input.

  • Common Error: Reversing the order of composition.
  • Common Error: Ignoring intersection of domains in arithmetic operations.
  • Common Error: Forgetting to exclude zeros of a denominator function.
  • Common Error: Failing to verify that $g(x)$ belongs to the domain of $f$ in $f\circ g$.
  • Common Error: Confusing an inverse function with a reciprocal function.
  • Common Error: Treating $f^2$ as automatically meaning only one type of operation.
  • Terminological Core: Function operations create new functions from existing ones.
  • Core Interpretation: Arithmetic operations on scalar-valued functions are usually defined pointwise: $$ \boxed{ (f+g)(x)=f(x)+g(x),\quad (fg)(x)=f(x)g(x),\quad \left(\frac fg\right)(x)=\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} }. $$ Composition has a different structure: $$ \boxed{ (f\circ g)(x)=f(g(x)) }. $$ Each operation produces a new function whose domain must be determined from the original domains and any new restrictions created by division, composition, radicals, logarithms, or other component operations.
