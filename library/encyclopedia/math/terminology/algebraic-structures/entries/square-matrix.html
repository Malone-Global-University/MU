Square Matrix

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

A square matrix is a matrix having the same number of rows and columns.

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ is square} \Longleftrightarrow A\text{ is }n\times n } $$

For example:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} a & b\\ c & d \end{bmatrix} } $$

is a $2\times2$ square matrix.

The term square refers to the equal row and column dimensions of the rectangular array, analogous to a geometric square having equal horizontal and vertical side counts.

Operational Nuance

Order of a Square Matrix

A square matrix with $n$ rows and $n$ columns is said to have order $n$:

$$ \boxed{ A\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n} }. $$

Main Diagonal

Square matrices have a main diagonal consisting of entries:

$$ \boxed{ a_{11},a_{22},\ldots,a_{nn} }. $$

Determinant

The determinant is defined for square matrices:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A) } $$

and plays a central role in determining invertibility, volume scaling, and linear dependence.

Inverse

A square matrix may have a two-sided multiplicative inverse $A^{-1}$ satisfying:

$$ \boxed{ AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I_n }. $$

Such an inverse exists exactly when $A$ is nonsingular.

Invertibility Criterion

Over a field:

$$ \boxed{ A^{-1}\text{ exists} \Longleftrightarrow \det(A)\neq0 }. $$

Identity Matrix

The identity matrix is square:

$$ \boxed{ I_n= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0\\ 0 & 1 & \cdots & 0\\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 \end{bmatrix} }. $$

Matrix Powers

Square matrices can be multiplied by themselves repeatedly:

$$ \boxed{ A^2=AA, \qquad A^3=AAA }. $$

Matrix powers are naturally defined because the inner dimensions continue to match.

Trace

The trace of a square matrix is the sum of its main-diagonal entries:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{tr}(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ii} }. $$

Eigenvalues

Eigenvalues are defined through:

$$ A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}, \qquad \mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}. $$

For a square matrix, they are roots of the characteristic equation:

$$ \boxed{ \det(\lambda I-A)=0 }. $$

Transpose

The transpose of an $n\times n$ square matrix is also $n\times n$:

$$ \boxed{ A^T\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n} }. $$

Symmetric Matrix

A square matrix is symmetric when:

$$ \boxed{ A^T=A }. $$

Diagonal and Triangular Matrices

Diagonal, upper-triangular, lower-triangular, identity, scalar, symmetric, and skew-symmetric matrices are all important subclasses of square matrices.

Linear Transformation Interpretation

An $n\times n$ matrix naturally represents a linear transformation from an $n$-dimensional vector space to itself:

$$ \boxed{ T:\mathbb{F}^n\to\mathbb{F}^n, \qquad T(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x} }. $$

Square Does Not Mean Invertible

Being square is necessary for an ordinary two-sided inverse in elementary matrix algebra, but it does not guarantee one. For example:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2\\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} $$

is square but singular because its determinant is zero.

Common Misconception

A square matrix is defined solely by having equal numbers of rows and columns. Its entries do not need to form a geometric pattern, be symmetric, or possess an inverse.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$A\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times3}$ "A is a three-by-three real square matrix."
$A=(a_{ij})_{n\times n}$ "A is an n-by-n square matrix."
$\det(A)$ "The determinant of A."
$\operatorname{tr}(A)$ "The trace of A."
$A^2$ "A squared," meaning A multiplied by itself.
$A^{-1}$ "The inverse of A," when it exists.

The phrase square matrix of order $n$ means an $n\times n$ matrix. The word order in this context should not be confused with ordering relations or the number of entries in the matrix.

Defense Notation Advice: Read matrix dimensions as rows by columns. Confirm that the two dimensions are equal before applying square-matrix constructions such as determinants, characteristic polynomials, ordinary matrix powers, or a two-sided inverse.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: Matrix with the same number of rows and columns.
  • Dimension: An $n\times n$ matrix is square of order $n$.
  • Main Diagonal: Contains $a_{11},a_{22},\ldots,a_{nn}$.
  • Determinant: Standard determinant theory applies to square matrices.
  • Inverse: A square matrix may be invertible or singular.
  • Identity Matrix: $I_n$ is square.
  • Matrix Powers: $A^k$ is naturally defined for nonnegative integer $k$.
  • Trace: Sum of main-diagonal entries.
  • Eigenvalues: Defined through the characteristic polynomial of a square matrix.
  • Symmetric Matrix: Square matrix satisfying $A^T=A$.
  • Triangular Matrix: Upper- and lower-triangular matrices are square.
  • Diagonal Matrix: A square matrix with zero off-diagonal entries.
  • Scalar Matrix: Matrix of the form $cI_n$.
  • Linear Transformations: Represents endomorphisms from an $n$-dimensional space to itself.
  • Linear Systems: An $n\times n$ coefficient matrix corresponds to a system with $n$ equations and $n$ unknowns, though uniqueness is not guaranteed.
  • Common Error: Assuming every square matrix is invertible.
  • Common Error: Assuming square means symmetric.
  • Common Error: Calling a rectangular $m\times n$ matrix square when $m\neq n$.
  • Common Error: Assuming an $n\times n$ linear system automatically has a unique solution.
  • Core Interpretation: A square matrix has matching row and column dimensions: $$ \boxed{ A\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n} }. $$ This equal-dimensional structure makes possible many of the central constructions of linear algebra, including determinants, traces, eigenvalues, characteristic polynomials, matrix powers, and the distinction between singular and invertible matrices.
