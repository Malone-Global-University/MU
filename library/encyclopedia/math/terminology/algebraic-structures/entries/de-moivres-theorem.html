De Moivre's Theorem

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

De Moivre's Theorem, also commonly called De Moivre's Formula, is named after Abraham de Moivre (1667–1754), a French-born mathematician who spent most of his professional life in England. De Moivre made major contributions to probability, analytic geometry, trigonometry, and the developing theory of complex numbers.

The result associated with his name connects multiplication and exponentiation of complex numbers with trigonometric angle addition. A closely related formula appeared in de Moivre's work in 1707, while the familiar form involving:

$$ (\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^n $$

appeared explicitly in a paper published in 1722.

The theorem became an important bridge between algebra, trigonometry, and complex analysis. It showed that raising a complex number to a power can be interpreted geometrically as raising its magnitude to that power while multiplying its angle.

Operational Nuance

In its standard unit-circle form, De Moivre's Theorem states that for an integer $n$:

$$ \boxed{ (\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^n = \cos(n\theta)+i\sin(n\theta) } $$

The theorem says something surprisingly simple: when a complex number on the unit circle is raised to an integer power, its angle is multiplied by that power.

For example:

$$ (\cos30^\circ+i\sin30^\circ)^3 $$

becomes:

$$ \cos90^\circ+i\sin90^\circ. $$

Therefore:

$$ (\cos30^\circ+i\sin30^\circ)^3=i. $$

The theorem becomes even more useful when the complex number has a magnitude other than $1$. Write a nonzero complex number in polar or trigonometric form:

$$ z = r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta), $$

where:

$$ r=|z|. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ z^n = r^n [\cos(n\theta)+i\sin(n\theta)] } $$

Thus exponentiation performs two separate geometric actions:

  • the magnitude $r$ becomes $r^n$;
  • the argument $\theta$ becomes $n\theta$.

Suppose:

$$ z = 2\left( \cos\frac{\pi}{3} + i\sin\frac{\pi}{3} \right). $$

Then:

$$ z^3 = 2^3 \left( \cos\pi+i\sin\pi \right). $$

Since:

$$ \cos\pi=-1, \qquad \sin\pi=0, $$

we obtain:

$$ z^3=-8. $$

This is generally much simpler than converting the original complex number into rectangular form and multiplying it by itself repeatedly.

Common Misconception: De Moivre's Theorem does not mean that both sine and cosine are individually raised to the power $n$:

$$ (\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^n \neq \cos^n\theta+i\sin^n\theta $$

in general. Instead, the entire complex expression is raised to the power, and the theorem converts that exponent into multiplication of the angle.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$ "Cosine theta plus i sine theta."
$(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^n$ "The quantity cosine theta plus i sine theta, raised to the nth power."
$\cos(n\theta)+i\sin(n\theta)$ "Cosine n theta plus i sine n theta."
$z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)$ "Z equals r times cosine theta plus i sine theta."
$|z|=r$ "The modulus of z equals r," or "the magnitude of z equals r."
$\arg z=\theta$ "The argument of z is theta."

The compact expression:

$$ \operatorname{cis}\theta = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta $$

is sometimes used to shorten De Moivre's formula:

$$ (\operatorname{cis}\theta)^n = \operatorname{cis}(n\theta). $$

The notation cis is read "cis theta" and is an abbreviation for "cosine plus i sine." It is common in some textbooks but is not universal.

An alternative modern notation uses Euler's formula:

$$ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta. $$

Then De Moivre's relationship appears naturally as:

$$ (e^{i\theta})^n=e^{in\theta}. $$

Defense Notation Advice: The angle in a polar representation is not unique. Since:

$$ \theta \quad\text{and}\quad \theta+2\pi k, \qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}, $$

describe the same direction, this periodicity must be included when using De Moivre's Theorem to find roots.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Powers of Complex Numbers: The principal elementary use of De Moivre's Theorem is efficient computation of: $$ z^n. $$ If: $$ z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta), $$ then: $$ z^n = r^n [\cos(n\theta)+i\sin(n\theta)]. $$
  • Roots of Complex Numbers: The theorem can be used in reverse to find the $n$th roots of a complex number. If: $$ z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta), $$ then its $n$ distinct $n$th roots are: $$ w_k = r^{1/n} \left[ \cos\left( \frac{\theta+2\pi k}{n} \right) + i\sin\left( \frac{\theta+2\pi k}{n} \right) \right], $$ where: $$ k=0,1,\ldots,n-1. $$
  • Roots of Unity: Solving: $$ z^n=1 $$ gives: $$ z_k = \cos\left(\frac{2\pi k}{n}\right) + i\sin\left(\frac{2\pi k}{n}\right), $$ for: $$ k=0,1,\ldots,n-1. $$ These are the $n$th roots of unity.
  • Geometry of Roots: The $n$th roots of a nonzero complex number are equally spaced around a circle. Successive roots differ in argument by: $$ \frac{2\pi}{n}. $$ Thus they form the vertices of a regular $n$-gon in the complex plane.
  • Multiple-Angle Identities: De Moivre's Theorem can generate trigonometric identities. For example: $$ (\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^2 = \cos2\theta+i\sin2\theta. $$ Expanding the left side gives: $$ \cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta + 2i\sin\theta\cos\theta. $$ Comparing real and imaginary parts produces: $$ \cos2\theta = \cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta $$ and: $$ \sin2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta. $$
  • Higher-Angle Identities: Similarly: $$ (\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^3 = \cos3\theta+i\sin3\theta $$ can be expanded to derive: $$ \cos3\theta = \cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta\sin^2\theta $$ and: $$ \sin3\theta = 3\sin\theta\cos^2\theta - \sin^3\theta. $$
  • Polar Multiplication: De Moivre's Theorem reflects the broader multiplication law for complex numbers. If: $$ z_1=r_1\operatorname{cis}\theta_1 $$ and: $$ z_2=r_2\operatorname{cis}\theta_2, $$ then: $$ z_1z_2 = r_1r_2 \operatorname{cis}(\theta_1+\theta_2). $$ Magnitudes multiply while arguments add.
  • Geometric Interpretation: Multiplication by a complex number: $$ r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta) $$ can be interpreted geometrically as a scaling by factor $r$ followed by a rotation through angle $\theta$. Raising the number to the nth power repeats this transformation $n$ times, producing scaling by $r^n$ and rotation by $n\theta$.
  • Euler's Formula: Euler's identity: $$ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta $$ provides a modern exponential interpretation of De Moivre's formula: $$ (e^{i\theta})^n=e^{in\theta}. $$ The two results are deeply connected, although historically De Moivre's work preceded Euler's mature exponential formulation.
  • Integer Exponents: The classical theorem is safest when stated for: $$ n\in\mathbb{Z}. $$ Positive powers, zero powers, and negative integer powers fit consistently for nonzero complex numbers.
  • Noninteger Powers: Extending: $$ z^\alpha $$ to fractional, real, or complex exponents introduces additional issues because complex arguments are multivalued modulo $2\pi$. These cases require more careful definitions involving branches of the complex logarithm and should not be treated as a simple unrestricted substitution into the elementary theorem.
  • Algebra & Trigonometry: De Moivre's Theorem is important because it translates a difficult algebraic operation—repeated multiplication of complex numbers—into comparatively simple operations on magnitudes and angles.
  • Terminological Contrast: De Moivre's Theorem describes powers of complex numbers in trigonometric form. Euler's Formula connects the same trigonometric expression with the complex exponential. The polar form of a complex number records its magnitude and argument, while rectangular form writes it as $a+bi$. These are different representations of the same complex-number structure.
