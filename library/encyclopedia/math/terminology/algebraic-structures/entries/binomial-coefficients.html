Binomial Coefficients

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

Binomial coefficients are the numerical coefficients that appear in the expansion of a nonnegative integer power of a binomial.

They are written:

$$ \boxed{ \binom{n}{k} } $$

and commonly read:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"n choose k"} }. $$

The same quantity has two central interpretations:

$$ \boxed{ \binom nk = \text{coefficient in a binomial expansion} = \text{number of }k\text{-element selections from }n\text{ objects} }. $$

This dual role creates an important bridge between algebra and combinatorics.

Operational Nuance

Factorial Formula

For integers $n\ge0$ and $0\le k\le n$:

$$ \boxed{ \binom nk = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!} }. $$

Example

Compute:

$$ \binom52. $$

Then:

$$ \binom52 = \frac{5!}{2!3!} = \frac{5\cdot4}{2\cdot1} = \boxed{10}. $$

Combination Interpretation

The expression:

$$ \binom nk $$

counts the number of ways to choose $k$ objects from $n$ distinct objects when order does not matter.

$$ \boxed{ \binom nk = \#\{\text{$k$-element subsets of an $n$-element set}\} }. $$

Why Order Does Not Matter

If $k$ objects are first arranged in order, each selected group is counted $k!$ times.

Starting from:

$$ \frac{n!}{(n-k)!}, $$

dividing by $k!$ removes the ordering:

$$ \boxed{ \binom nk = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!} }. $$

Boundary Values

There is exactly one way to choose nothing:

$$ \boxed{ \binom n0=1 }. $$

There is also exactly one way to choose all $n$ objects:

$$ \boxed{ \binom nn=1 }. $$

Choosing One Object

$$ \boxed{ \binom n1=n }. $$

Likewise:

$$ \boxed{ \binom n{n-1}=n }. $$

Symmetry Identity

Choosing $k$ objects to include is equivalent to choosing $n-k$ objects to leave out.

$$ \boxed{ \binom nk = \binom n{n-k} }. $$

Example of Symmetry

$$ \binom83 = \binom85 = 56. $$

Binomial Theorem

Binomial coefficients appear in:

$$ \boxed{ (a+b)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom nk a^{n-k}b^k }. $$

Coefficient Interpretation

In the expansion of $(a+b)^n$, the coefficient of:

$$ a^{n-k}b^k $$

is:

$$ \boxed{ \binom nk }. $$

Example: Fourth Power

$$ (a+b)^4 = a^4 + 4a^3b + 6a^2b^2 + 4ab^3 + b^4. $$

The binomial coefficients are:

$$ \boxed{ 1,\ 4,\ 6,\ 4,\ 1 }. $$

Why the Coefficients Count Choices

Expand:

$$ (a+b)^n = (a+b)(a+b)\cdots(a+b). $$

To produce a term containing $b^k$, one must choose the $b$ term from exactly $k$ of the $n$ factors and choose $a$ from the remaining $n-k$ factors.

The number of ways to choose those $k$ factors is:

$$ \boxed{ \binom nk }. $$

Pascal's Triangle

Binomial coefficients can be arranged in a triangular array:

$$ \begin{array}{ccccccccc} &&&&1\\ &&&1&&1\\ &&1&&2&&1\\ &1&&3&&3&&1\\ 1&&4&&6&&4&&1 \end{array} $$

Row $n$ contains:

$$ \boxed{ \binom n0, \binom n1, \ldots, \binom nn }. $$

Pascal's Identity

Every interior entry in Pascal's Triangle satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ \binom nk = \binom{n-1}{k-1} + \binom{n-1}{k} }. $$

Combinatorial Explanation of Pascal's Identity

Choose $k$ objects from an $n$-element set and focus on one distinguished object. Every selection either:

  • contains that object, or
  • does not contain it.

If it is included, choose the remaining $k-1$ objects from the other $n-1$:

$$ \binom{n-1}{k-1}. $$

If it is excluded, choose all $k$ from the other $n-1$:

$$ \binom{n-1}{k}. $$

Adding the two disjoint cases gives Pascal's identity.

Sum of a Row

Set $a=b=1$ in the Binomial Theorem:

$$ (1+1)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom nk. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom nk = 2^n }. $$

Combinatorial Meaning of the Row Sum

An $n$-element set has:

$$ \boxed{ 2^n } $$

subsets in total.

Grouping those subsets by size gives:

$$ \binom n0+\binom n1+\cdots+\binom nn=2^n. $$

Alternating Sum

Set $a=1$ and $b=-1$:

$$ (1-1)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} (-1)^k\binom nk. $$

For $n\ge1$:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{k=0}^{n} (-1)^k\binom nk = 0 }. $$

Weighted Sum Identity

A useful identity is:

$$ \boxed{ k\binom nk = n\binom{n-1}{k-1} }. $$

This can be interpreted by counting selections with one distinguished chosen element.

Sum of k Times the Coefficient

Summing the previous identity yields:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{k=0}^{n} k\binom nk = n2^{n-1} }. $$

Successive-Coefficient Ratio

Consecutive binomial coefficients satisfy:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\binom n{k+1}}{\binom nk} = \frac{n-k}{k+1} }. $$

This is useful for generating coefficients without repeatedly evaluating full factorial expressions.

Largest Binomial Coefficients

The largest entries in row $n$ occur near the middle.

When $n$ is even, the unique central coefficient is:

$$ \boxed{ \binom n{n/2} }. $$

When $n$ is odd, the two middle coefficients are equal.

Binomial Coefficients and Probability

For $n$ independent Bernoulli trials with success probability $p$, the probability of exactly $k$ successes is:

$$ \boxed{ P(X=k) = \binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k} }. $$

The coefficient $\binom nk$ counts the possible placements of the $k$ successes.

Example: Exactly Three Successes in Five Trials

There are:

$$ \boxed{ \binom53=10 } $$

distinct success-failure sequences containing exactly three successes.

Relation to Permutations

The number of ordered selections of $k$ objects from $n$ is:

$$ P(n,k) = \frac{n!}{(n-k)!}. $$

Since each unordered selection has $k!$ orderings:

$$ \boxed{ \binom nk = \frac{P(n,k)}{k!} }. $$

Combination versus Permutation

$$ \boxed{ \begin{array}{c|c} \text{Combination} & \text{Permutation}\\ \hline \text{order ignored} & \text{order matters}\\ \binom nk & \dfrac{n!}{(n-k)!} \end{array} } $$

Subset Interpretation

If a set $S$ has $n$ elements, then:

$$ \boxed{ \binom nk = \text{number of subsets of }S\text{ having size }k }. $$

Power-Set Connection

Since every subset has some size $k$ between $0$ and $n$:

$$ |\mathcal P(S)| = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom nk = \boxed{2^n}. $$

Diagonal Patterns in Pascal's Triangle

The first diagonals produce familiar sequences:

$$ \binom n0=1, $$
$$ \binom n1=n, $$
$$ \binom n2 = \frac{n(n-1)}2. $$

The last expression gives the triangular numbers when indexed appropriately.

Hockey-Stick Identity

Another useful identity is:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{j=r}^{n} \binom jr = \binom{n+1}{r+1} }. $$

Its entries form the familiar hockey-stick pattern in Pascal's Triangle.

Vandermonde's Identity

A deeper combinatorial identity is:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_k \binom rk \binom s{n-k} = \binom{r+s}{n} }. $$

It can be interpreted as choosing $n$ objects from two disjoint groups of sizes $r$ and $s$.

Binomial Coefficients as Integers

The factorial formula involves division, but for:

$$ 0\le k\le n, $$

the result is always a nonnegative integer because it counts a finite collection of subsets.

Efficient Evaluation

It is often unnecessary to compute complete factorials.

For example:

$$ \binom{20}{2} = \frac{20\cdot19}{2} = \boxed{190}. $$

Cancelling factors early reduces arithmetic and avoids unnecessarily large intermediate values.

Using Symmetry for Easier Computation

Instead of evaluating:

$$ \binom{100}{98}, $$

use:

$$ \binom{100}{98} = \binom{100}{2} = \boxed{4950}. $$

Zero Outside the Standard Range

In many combinatorial identities, it is convenient to adopt the convention:

$$ \boxed{ \binom nk=0 \quad\text{if }k<0\text{ or }k>n } $$

for nonnegative integer $n$.

This convention makes recurrence relations and summations cleaner.

Generalized Binomial Coefficients

In more advanced mathematics, the notation can be extended beyond nonnegative integer upper entries.

For suitable $r$ and nonnegative integer $k$:

$$ \boxed{ \binom rk = \frac{ r(r-1)(r-2)\cdots(r-k+1) }{ k! } }. $$

When $r=n$ is a nonnegative integer, this agrees with the ordinary factorial formula.

Generalized Binomial Series

The generalized coefficients appear in expansions such as:

$$ (1+x)^r = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \binom rkx^k $$

within the appropriate convergence conditions.

This is an extension of the finite Binomial Theorem rather than the elementary finite-polynomial case.

Binomial Coefficient versus Binomial

A binomial is a two-term algebraic expression such as:

$$ a+b. $$

A binomial coefficient is a number such as:

$$ \binom nk $$

that appears in powers of binomials and counts combinations.

Binomial Coefficient versus Exponent

In:

$$ \binom nk a^{n-k}b^k, $$

$\binom nk$ is a multiplicative coefficient, while $n-k$ and $k$ are exponents.

Binomial Coefficient versus Probability

A binomial coefficient is not itself generally a probability. It is a counting number.

In a binomial probability:

$$ \binom nkp^k(1-p)^{n-k}, $$

the coefficient counts arrangements while the powers supply the probability of each arrangement.

Common Misconception: n Choose k Means n Divided by k

The stacked notation:

$$ \binom nk $$

is not a fraction.

$$ \boxed{ \binom nk \ne \frac nk } $$

in general.

Common Misconception: Order Matters

In combinations, selecting $A,B,C$ is the same selection as $C,A,B$.

If order matters, a permutation formula is required instead.

Common Misconception: Factorial Formula Works Unchanged for All Inputs

The elementary formula:

$$ \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!} $$

is intended for nonnegative integer $n$ with $0\le k\le n$. Extensions beyond this setting require broader definitions.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\binom nk$ "n choose k."
$\binom nk=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}$ "n choose k equals n factorial over k factorial times n minus k factorial."
$\binom nk=\binom n{n-k}$ "Choosing k objects is equivalent to choosing which n minus k objects to leave out."
$\binom nk=\binom{n-1}{k-1}+\binom{n-1}{k}$ "Pascal's identity."
$(a+b)^n=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom nk a^{n-k}b^k$ "The Binomial Theorem."
$P(X=k)=\binom nkp^k(1-p)^{n-k}$ "The binomial probability of exactly k successes."

The notation $\binom nk$ is also sometimes written $C(n,k)$, ${}_nC_k$, or $C_n^k$, depending on textbook and regional convention. The stacked notation is especially useful because it aligns naturally with the Binomial Theorem and Pascal's Triangle.

Defense Notation Advice: Read $\binom nk$ as a single combinatorial object, not as a fraction. Before computing it, ask whether the problem is counting unordered selections, identifying a coefficient in a binomial expansion, or using the same quantity inside a probability formula.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: Binomial coefficients are the numbers appearing as coefficients in powers of a binomial.
  • Primary Notation: $$ \boxed{ \binom nk }. $$
  • Spoken Form: "n choose k."
  • Factorial Formula: $$ \boxed{ \binom nk = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!} }. $$
  • Combination Meaning: Counts unordered selections of $k$ objects from $n$ distinct objects.
  • Subset Meaning: Counts $k$-element subsets of an $n$-element set.
  • Boundary Values: $$ \boxed{ \binom n0=\binom nn=1 }. $$
  • Single Selection: $$ \boxed{ \binom n1=n }. $$
  • Symmetry: $$ \boxed{ \binom nk=\binom n{n-k} }. $$
  • Binomial Theorem: $$ \boxed{ (a+b)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom nk a^{n-k}b^k }. $$
  • Coefficient Role: $\binom nk$ is the coefficient of $a^{n-k}b^k$.
  • Pascal's Triangle: Row $n$ contains the coefficients $\binom n0,\ldots,\binom nn$.
  • Pascal's Identity: $$ \boxed{ \binom nk = \binom{n-1}{k-1} + \binom{n-1}{k} }. $$
  • Row Sum: $$ \boxed{ \sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom nk=2^n }. $$
  • Alternating Sum: For $n\ge1$: $$ \boxed{ \sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^k\binom nk=0 }. $$
  • Permutation Connection: $$ \boxed{ \binom nk = \frac{P(n,k)}{k!} }. $$
  • Probability Connection: Appears in the binomial distribution as the number of placements of $k$ successes among $n$ trials.
  • Binomial Probability: $$ \boxed{ P(X=k) = \binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k} }. $$
  • Power-Set Connection: $$ \boxed{ \sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom nk=2^n } $$ counts all subsets of an $n$-element set.
  • Successive Ratio: $$ \boxed{ \frac{\binom n{k+1}}{\binom nk} = \frac{n-k}{k+1} }. $$
  • Hockey-Stick Identity: $$ \boxed{ \sum_{j=r}^{n} \binom jr = \binom{n+1}{r+1} }. $$
  • Vandermonde's Identity: $$ \boxed{ \sum_k \binom rk \binom s{n-k} = \binom{r+s}{n} }. $$
  • Outside-Range Convention: For nonnegative integer $n$, many identities use $\binom nk=0$ when $k<0$ or $k>n$.
  • Generalized Form: $$ \boxed{ \binom rk = \frac{r(r-1)\cdots(r-k+1)}{k!} } $$ extends the idea to broader upper arguments.
  • Binomial Contrast: A binomial is a two-term expression; a binomial coefficient is a number associated with powers of binomials and combinations.
  • Permutation Contrast: Combinations ignore order; permutations count order.
  • Probability Contrast: A binomial coefficient is a count, not by itself a probability.
  • Common Error: Do not interpret $\binom nk$ as $n/k$.
  • Common Error: Do not use combinations when order matters.
  • Common Error: Do not forget the coefficient when counting all arrangements of $k$ successes in a binomial probability problem.
  • Common Error: Do not compute enormous factorials when cancellation, symmetry, or recurrence gives a simpler route.
  • Terminological Contrast: Binomial is a two-term algebraic expression. Binomial coefficient is the number $\binom nk$. Combination is an unordered selection counted by $\binom nk$. Permutation is an ordered arrangement. Pascal's Triangle organizes binomial coefficients recursively. Binomial Theorem uses them to expand $(a+b)^n$. Binomial distribution uses them to count success arrangements in repeated Bernoulli trials.
