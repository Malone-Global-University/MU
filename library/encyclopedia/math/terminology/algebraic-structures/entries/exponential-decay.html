Exponential Decay

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

Exponential decay describes a process in which a quantity decreases by a constant proportion or percentage over equal intervals, or decreases continuously at a rate proportional to its current amount.

A standard discrete exponential-decay model is:

$$ \boxed{ Q_n=Q_0b^n, \qquad 0<b<1 }. $$

Here $Q_0$ is the initial quantity, $b$ is the decay factor, and $n$ counts equal time intervals or repeated decay steps.

A standard continuous model is:

$$ \boxed{ Q(t)=Q_0e^{-kt}, \qquad k>0 }. $$

The adjective exponential refers to the variable appearing in the exponent. The noun decay indicates systematic decrease.

The central operational idea is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{equal intervals} \Longrightarrow \text{equal multiplicative factors} }. $$

This distinguishes exponential decay from linear decrease, where equal intervals produce equal additive decreases.

Operational Nuance

Discrete Exponential Decay

In discrete time:

$$ \boxed{ Q_n=Q_0b^n, \qquad 0<b<1 }. $$

Each step multiplies the preceding quantity by the same factor:

$$ \boxed{ Q_{n+1}=bQ_n }. $$

Decay Factor

The number $b$ is the fraction retained after each interval.

For example, if:

$$ b=0.8, $$

then $80\%$ of the quantity remains after each interval.

Decay Rate

If a fraction $r$ of the quantity is lost each interval, then:

$$ \boxed{ b=1-r }. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ Q_n=Q_0(1-r)^n, \qquad 0<r<1 }. $$

Percentage Decay

If a quantity decreases by $p\%$ each interval:

$$ r=\frac{p}{100}, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ Q_n = Q_0 \left(1-\frac{p}{100}\right)^n }. $$

Example: 20 Percent Decay

Suppose a quantity begins at $500$ and loses $20\%$ each year.

The retention factor is:

$$ 1-0.20=0.80. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ Q(t)=500(0.8)^t }. $$

Repeated Percentage Loss Is Multiplicative

After two intervals:

$$ Q_2=Q_0(0.8)^2=0.64Q_0. $$

Thus two successive $20\%$ decreases produce a total decrease of $36\%$, not $40\%$.

Why the Percentages Do Not Simply Add

The second percentage loss applies to the already-reduced quantity, not to the original quantity.

$$ \boxed{ (1-r)^2 \neq 1-2r } $$

in general.

Continuous Exponential Decay

A common continuous model is:

$$ \boxed{ Q(t)=Q_0e^{-kt}, \qquad k>0 }. $$

Here $k$ is the positive continuous decay constant.

Equivalent Signed-Rate Form

The same model may be written:

$$ Q(t)=Q_0e^{rt}, \qquad r<0. $$

In this convention, the exponential rate itself is negative.

Differential-Equation Characterization

Continuous exponential decay arises when the rate of change is proportional to the current quantity and opposite in sign:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dQ}{dt}=-kQ, \qquad k>0 }. $$

Solving this differential equation gives:

$$ \boxed{ Q(t)=Q_0e^{-kt} }. $$

Proportional Rate

Dividing by $Q$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{1}{Q}\frac{dQ}{dt}=-k }. $$

Thus the relative rate of change is constant.

Initial Value

At $t=0$:

$$ Q(0) = Q_0e^0 = \boxed{Q_0}. $$

Monotonic Decrease

If $Q_0>0$ and $k>0$, then:

$$ Q'(t) = -kQ_0e^{-kt} < 0. $$

Therefore the quantity decreases as $t$ increases.

Positive Values

If $Q_0>0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ Q(t)>0 } $$

for every finite real $t$.

Long-Term Behavior

For positive $k$:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{t\to\infty} Q_0e^{-kt} = 0 }. $$

Thus the horizontal line:

$$ \boxed{Q=0} $$

is a horizontal asymptote in the basic positive decay model.

Approaches Zero vs. Reaches Zero

In the ideal continuous model:

$$ Q(t)=Q_0e^{-kt}, $$

a positive quantity approaches zero but does not become exactly zero at any finite time.

Half-Life

The half-life $T_{1/2}$ is the time required for the quantity to fall to half its initial value:

$$ \boxed{ Q(T_{1/2})=\frac12Q_0 }. $$

Continuous Half-Life Formula

Starting with:

$$ \frac12Q_0 = Q_0e^{-kT_{1/2}}, $$

divide by $Q_0$:

$$ \frac12=e^{-kT_{1/2}}. $$

Taking logarithms:

$$ -\ln2=-kT_{1/2}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln2}{k} }. $$

Half-Life Form of Exponential Decay

A decay model can be written directly using half-life:

$$ \boxed{ Q(t) = Q_0 \left(\frac12\right)^{t/T_{1/2}} }. $$

This form makes repeated halvings immediately visible.

Repeated Half-Lives

After one half-life:

$$ Q=\frac12Q_0. $$

After two:

$$ Q=\frac14Q_0. $$

After three:

$$ Q=\frac18Q_0. $$

In general:

$$ \boxed{ Q(nT_{1/2}) = Q_0\left(\frac12\right)^n }. $$

Half-Life Is Independent of Initial Amount

In a pure exponential-decay model with fixed $k$, the half-life depends only on the decay constant:

$$ T_{1/2}=\frac{\ln2}{k}. $$

It does not depend on $Q_0$.

Discrete Half-Life

If:

$$ Q(t)=Q_0b^t, \qquad 0<b<1, $$

then solving:

$$ b^{T_{1/2}}=\frac12 $$

gives:

$$ \boxed{ T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln(1/2)}{\ln b} }. $$

Since both logarithms are negative, the half-life is positive.

Converting a Decay Factor to a Continuous Rate

If:

$$ b=e^{-k}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ k=-\ln b }. $$

Converting a Continuous Rate to a Decay Factor

$$ \boxed{ b=e^{-k} }. $$

Finding Time from a Known Remaining Fraction

Suppose:

$$ Q(t)=Q_0e^{-kt} $$

and the remaining fraction is:

$$ \frac{Q(t)}{Q_0}=f, \qquad 0<f<1. $$

Then:

$$ f=e^{-kt}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ t = -\frac{\ln f}{k} }. $$

Finding Time from Initial and Final Values

If:

$$ Q=Q_0e^{-kt}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ t = \frac1k \ln\left(\frac{Q_0}{Q}\right) } $$

for positive $Q_0$ and $Q$.

Finding the Continuous Decay Constant

If $Q_0$, $Q(t)$, and $t$ are known:

$$ \boxed{ k = \frac1t \ln\left(\frac{Q_0}{Q(t)}\right) }. $$

Estimating Decay from Two Measurements

Suppose:

$$ Q(t)=Ce^{-kt}. $$

Given measurements $(t_1,Q_1)$ and $(t_2,Q_2)$:

$$ \frac{Q_2}{Q_1} = e^{-k(t_2-t_1)}. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ k = -\frac{ \ln(Q_2/Q_1) }{ t_2-t_1 } }. $$

Log-Linear Transformation

Taking the natural logarithm of:

$$ Q(t)=Q_0e^{-kt} $$

gives:

$$ \boxed{ \ln Q(t) = \ln Q_0-kt }. $$

Thus a pure exponential-decay process becomes linear when $\ln Q$ is plotted against $t$.

Slope of a Log Plot

In:

$$ \ln Q=\ln Q_0-kt, $$

the slope is:

$$ \boxed{-k}. $$

Exponential Decay vs. Linear Decay

Linear decrease has the form:

$$ \boxed{ Q(t)=Q_0-ct }. $$

Equal time intervals subtract the same amount.

Exponential decay has the form:

$$ \boxed{ Q(t)=Q_0b^t } $$

or:

$$ \boxed{ Q(t)=Q_0e^{-kt} }. $$

Equal time intervals multiply by the same factor.

Constant Difference vs. Constant Ratio

Linear sequences exhibit constant differences:

$$ Q_{n+1}-Q_n=\text{constant}. $$

Exponential sequences exhibit constant ratios:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{Q_{n+1}}{Q_n}=b }. $$

Decay with a Nonzero Horizontal Asymptote

Some exponential models approach a baseline $L$ rather than zero:

$$ \boxed{ Q(t) = L+Ae^{-kt} }. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{t\to\infty}Q(t)=L }. $$

Decay Toward Equilibrium

In:

$$ Q(t)=L+(Q_0-L)e^{-kt}, $$

the difference from equilibrium satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ Q(t)-L = (Q_0-L)e^{-kt} }. $$

It is the deviation from $L$, rather than necessarily the total quantity, that decays exponentially.

Radioactive Decay

Radioactive-decay models commonly take the form:

$$ \boxed{ N(t)=N_0e^{-\lambda t} }. $$

Here $\lambda>0$ is the radioactive decay constant.

Radioactive Half-Life

$$ \boxed{ T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln2}{\lambda} }. $$

Population Decline

Under an idealized constant proportional decline:

$$ \boxed{ P(t)=P_0e^{-kt} }. $$

Drug Elimination

In a simple first-order elimination model, drug concentration may be approximated by:

$$ \boxed{ C(t)=C_0e^{-kt} }. $$

Cooling and Equilibrium Models

Newton's law of cooling gives exponential decay of the temperature difference from the surroundings:

$$ \boxed{ T(t)-T_s = (T_0-T_s)e^{-kt} }. $$

The temperature itself approaches $T_s$ rather than necessarily approaching zero.

Capacitor Discharge

In a simple resistor-capacitor circuit:

$$ \boxed{ V(t)=V_0e^{-t/(RC)} }. $$

The product $RC$ is the time constant.

Time Constant

In:

$$ Q(t)=Q_0e^{-t/\tau}, $$

$\tau$ is called the time constant.

$$ \boxed{ \tau=\frac1k }. $$

Amount Remaining After One Time Constant

At $t=\tau$:

$$ Q(\tau) = Q_0e^{-1} \approx 0.3679Q_0. $$

Thus about $36.8\%$ remains.

Relationship Between Time Constant and Half-Life

$$ \boxed{ T_{1/2} = \tau\ln2 }. $$

Probability

The exponential distribution uses a decay factor in its survival function:

$$ \boxed{ P(T>t) = e^{-\lambda t} }. $$

Exponential Density

For $t\geq0$:

$$ \boxed{ f(t)=\lambda e^{-\lambda t} }. $$

Financial Depreciation

A constant percentage depreciation model has the form:

$$ \boxed{ V_n=V_0(1-r)^n }. $$

This is an exponential-decay model when the same percentage is lost each period.

Repeated Percentage vs. Percentage Points

A quantity decreasing by $10\%$ each year is multiplied by $0.9$ each year. This should not be confused with subtracting ten percentage points from the original quantity every year.

Negative Growth Rate Language

Exponential decay is sometimes described as exponential growth with a negative rate:

$$ Q(t)=Q_0e^{rt}, \qquad r<0. $$

The mathematics is the same, although the word decay makes the direction of change explicit.

Decay Factor Must Be Between Zero and One

For the standard positive discrete model:

$$ \boxed{ 0<b<1 }. $$

If $b>1$, the process exhibits exponential growth instead.

What if the Base Is Negative?

A sequence such as:

$$ a_n=(-0.5)^n $$

decreases in magnitude but alternates sign. It is not ordinarily the standard positive-quantity exponential-decay model.

What if the Base Is Zero?

A factor $b=0$ collapses the quantity to zero after one positive integer step rather than producing the usual gradual exponential-decay pattern.

Exponential Decay Is Relative, Not Constant Absolute Loss

If $10\%$ is lost each year, the absolute amount lost becomes smaller as the quantity becomes smaller.

The percentage loss stays constant; the numerical loss generally does not.

Discrete and Continuous Models Are Not Identical

A model:

$$ Q_n=Q_0(1-r)^n $$

applies a discrete percentage change at intervals, whereas:

$$ Q(t)=Q_0e^{-kt} $$

represents continuous proportional decay.

Their parameters must be converted rather than simply identified:

$$ \boxed{ 1-r=e^{-k} } $$

when both refer to the same unit interval.

Units of the Decay Constant

Because the exponent $-kt$ must be dimensionless, $k$ has reciprocal-time units when $t$ is time.

For example, if $t$ is measured in years:

$$ [k]=\mathrm{year}^{-1}. $$

Model Limitations

Real processes may deviate from exponential decay when the proportional rate changes, external inputs occur, thresholds matter, multiple mechanisms operate, or the process approaches a nonzero equilibrium.

Common Misconception: Exponential decay does not mean subtracting the same amount each interval. It means retaining the same proportion each interval, or, continuously, changing at a rate proportional to the current quantity. Consequently, a positive ideal exponential-decay model typically approaches zero asymptotically rather than reaching zero after a fixed finite time.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$Q_n=Q_0b^n,\ 0<b<1$ "Q sub n equals the initial quantity times the decay factor to the nth power."
$Q_n=Q_0(1-r)^n$ "The quantity retains one minus r of its value each period."
$Q(t)=Q_0e^{-kt}$ "Q of t equals Q zero times e to the negative k t."
$Q'=-kQ$ "The rate of change is negative k times the current quantity."
$T_{1/2}=\ln2/k$ "The half-life equals the natural logarithm of two divided by the decay constant."
$Q(t)=Q_0(1/2)^{t/T_{1/2}}$ "The quantity is multiplied by one half for each elapsed half-life."
$\ln Q=\ln Q_0-kt$ "The logarithm of Q is linear in time with slope negative k."

The phrases decay rate, decay factor, and decay constant should not be used interchangeably without defining them.

$$ \boxed{ b=\text{retention or decay factor} } $$
$$ \boxed{ r=1-b=\text{fraction lost per discrete interval} } $$
$$ \boxed{ k=-\ln b=\text{continuous decay constant} } $$

when the discrete and continuous parameters refer to the same unit interval.

Defense Notation Advice: State whether a percentage is the fraction lost or the fraction remaining. A $15\%$ decay rate per period corresponds to a multiplicative factor of:

$$ \boxed{0.85}, $$

not $0.15$. Also attach units to $t$, half-life, and the decay constant so that the exponent remains dimensionless.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Discrete Model: $$ \boxed{ Q_n=Q_0b^n, \qquad 0<b<1 }. $$
  • Continuous Model: $$ \boxed{ Q(t)=Q_0e^{-kt}, \qquad k>0 }. $$

  • Decay Factor: $b$ is the proportion retained after each discrete interval.
  • Decay Fraction: If $r$ is the fraction lost: $$ \boxed{b=1-r}. $$
  • Percentage Decay: A decay of $p\%$ gives: $$ b=1-\frac{p}{100}. $$
  • Example: A $12\%$ loss per period gives: $$ b=0.88. $$
  • Repeated Decay: Equal intervals repeatedly multiply by the same decay factor.
  • Recursive Form: $$ \boxed{ Q_{n+1}=bQ_n }. $$
  • Constant Ratio: $$ \boxed{ \frac{Q_{n+1}}{Q_n}=b }. $$
  • Geometric Sequence: Discrete exponential decay produces a geometric sequence.
  • Continuous Rate: $$ \boxed{ Q'=-kQ }. $$
  • Relative Rate: $$ \boxed{ \frac{Q'}{Q}=-k }. $$
  • Signed Rate Convention: $Q=Q_0e^{rt}$ represents decay when $r<0$.
  • Positive Decay Constant Convention: $Q=Q_0e^{-kt}$ uses $k>0$.
  • Parameter Conversion: $$ \boxed{ b=e^{-k} } $$ for equal unit intervals.
  • Inverse Conversion: $$ \boxed{ k=-\ln b }. $$
  • Initial Value: $$ Q(0)=Q_0. $$
  • Long-Term Limit: $$ \boxed{ \lim_{t\to\infty}Q_0e^{-kt}=0 }. $$
  • Horizontal Asymptote: The basic positive decay model has asymptote $Q=0$.
  • Finite-Time Behavior: A positive pure exponential-decay model does not reach zero at finite time.
  • Half-Life Definition: $$ Q(T_{1/2})=\frac12Q_0. $$
  • Half-Life Formula: $$ \boxed{ T_{1/2}=\frac{\ln2}{k} }. $$
  • Half-Life Form: $$ \boxed{ Q(t) = Q_0 \left(\frac12\right)^{t/T_{1/2}} }. $$
  • One Half-Life: $50\%$ remains.
  • Two Half-Lives: $25\%$ remains.
  • Three Half-Lives: $12.5\%$ remains.
  • Repeated Halving: $$ Q(nT_{1/2})=Q_0/2^n. $$
  • Initial-Amount Independence: Half-life is independent of $Q_0$ in a fixed exponential model.
  • Discrete Half-Life: $$ \boxed{ T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln(1/2)}{\ln b} }. $$
  • Time to a Remaining Fraction: $$ \boxed{ t=-\frac{\ln f}{k} } $$ when $f=Q/Q_0$.
  • Time from Two Amounts: $$ \boxed{ t = \frac1k \ln\left(\frac{Q_0}{Q}\right) }. $$
  • Decay Constant from Data: $$ \boxed{ k = \frac1t \ln\left(\frac{Q_0}{Q(t)}\right) }. $$
  • Two-Point Estimate: $$ \boxed{ k = -\frac{\ln(Q_2/Q_1)} {t_2-t_1} }. $$
  • Log Transformation: $$ \boxed{ \ln Q = \ln Q_0-kt }. $$
  • Semilog Plot: Pure exponential decay appears linear when $\ln Q$ is plotted against time.
  • Semilog Slope: The slope is $-k$.
  • Linear Decay Contrast: Linear decay subtracts a constant amount.
  • Exponential Decay Contrast: Exponential decay multiplies by a constant factor.
  • Linear Model: $$ Q=Q_0-ct. $$
  • Exponential Model: $$ Q=Q_0b^t. $$
  • Percentage Caution: Repeated percentage decreases compound multiplicatively.
  • Example: Two $20\%$ decreases leave $64\%$, not $60\%$, of the original amount.
  • Absolute Loss: The amount lost per interval generally decreases as the quantity decreases.
  • Relative Loss: The fraction or percentage lost remains constant in the ideal model.
  • Nonzero Equilibrium: $$ \boxed{ Q(t)=L+Ae^{-kt} } $$ approaches $L$.
  • Equilibrium Form: $$ Q(t)-L=(Q_0-L)e^{-kt}. $$
  • Radioactive Decay: $$ N(t)=N_0e^{-\lambda t}. $$
  • Radioactive Half-Life: $$ T_{1/2}=\ln2/\lambda. $$
  • Drug Elimination: First-order pharmacokinetic models commonly use exponential decay.
  • Population Decline: A constant proportional population loss can be modeled exponentially.
  • Depreciation: Constant percentage loss of value produces a discrete exponential model.
  • Newton Cooling: The temperature difference from ambient conditions decays exponentially.
  • Capacitor Discharge: Voltage in an ideal RC discharge follows an exponential-decay law.
  • Time Constant: $$ \boxed{ \tau=\frac1k }. $$
  • Time-Constant Form: $$ Q(t)=Q_0e^{-t/\tau}. $$
  • One Time Constant: Approximately $36.8\%$ of the initial value remains.
  • Half-Life and Time Constant: $$ T_{1/2}=\tau\ln2. $$
  • Probability: Exponential survival probabilities have the form $e^{-\lambda t}$.
  • Exponential Distribution: $$ f(t)=\lambda e^{-\lambda t}, \qquad t\geq0. $$
  • Reliability: Constant-hazard lifetime models lead to exponential survival decay.
  • Signal Processing: Damped signals may contain factors such as $e^{-kt}$.
  • Damped Oscillation: An envelope such as $Ae^{-kt}$ can shrink the amplitude of an oscillation.
  • Example: $$ y(t)=Ae^{-kt}\cos(\omega t) $$ has exponentially decaying amplitude.
  • Base Condition: Standard discrete positive decay uses $0<b<1$.
  • Growth Contrast: $b>1$ produces exponential growth.
  • Constant Case: $b=1$ produces neither growth nor decay.
  • Negative Base Caution: $-1<b<0$ causes sign alternation and is not the standard positive decay model.
  • Zero Base Caution: $b=0$ gives immediate collapse after a positive integer step rather than ordinary gradual decay.
  • Unit Consistency: If $t$ is in years, the continuous decay constant has units of $\mathrm{year}^{-1}$.
  • Dimensionless Exponent: The product $kt$ must be dimensionless.
  • Model Fitting: Logarithms can be used to estimate decay parameters from observed data.
  • Model Verification: Approximately constant ratios over equal intervals support an exponential model.
  • Data Caution: Real data may only approximately follow exponential decay.
  • Changing Rates: If the proportional decay rate changes over time, a single constant-$k$ model may be inadequate.
  • External Input: A process receiving new material while losing old material may require a differential equation with an additional source term.
  • Multiple Decay Modes: Some systems are modeled by sums of exponentials rather than one exponential.
  • Terminological Distinction: A decay factor is the multiplicative amount retained; a decay rate is often the fraction lost; a decay constant commonly refers to $k$ in $e^{-kt}$.
  • Exponential Growth Relation: Growth and decay share the same exponential structure; the parameter or base determines the direction.
  • Core Interpretation: Exponential decay means that decrease is proportional rather than additive: $$ \boxed{ Q_n=Q_0b^n, \quad 0<b<1 } $$ in discrete form, or: $$ \boxed{ Q(t)=Q_0e^{-kt}, \quad k>0 } $$ in continuous form. Equal intervals preserve a constant ratio, half-life remains constant in the ideal model, and logarithms convert the exponential relationship into a linear one useful for analysis and parameter estimation.
