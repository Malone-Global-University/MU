Exponential Function

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

An exponential function is a function in which the independent variable appears in an exponent while the base remains constant.

The standard real exponential function has the form:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=a^x, \qquad a>0, \qquad a\neq1 }. $$

Here $a$ is the constant base and $x$ is the variable exponent.

For example:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=2^x } $$

is exponential because the variable $x$ occurs in the exponent.

By contrast:

$$ f(x)=x^2 $$

is a power function, not an exponential function, because the variable is the base and the exponent is constant.

The word exponential therefore refers to the structural position of the variable in the exponent, not merely to the presence of exponent notation.

Exponential functions naturally describe repeated multiplicative change:

$$ \boxed{ \text{equal input increments} \Longrightarrow \text{equal output ratios} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Canonical Form

The basic real exponential function is:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=a^x, \qquad a>0, \qquad a\neq1 }. $$

Why the Base Is Positive

Restricting $a$ to positive values allows $a^x$ to be defined as a real-valued function for every real $x$.

Negative bases create difficulties for arbitrary real exponents. For example:

$$ (-2)^{1/2} $$

is not real.

Why the Base Is Not One

If:

$$ a=1, $$

then:

$$ 1^x=1 $$

for every real $x$. This produces the constant function:

$$ \boxed{f(x)=1}, $$

which is normally excluded from the standard exponential-function family.

Value at Zero

Every basic exponential function passes through:

$$ \boxed{(0,1)}. $$

This follows because:

$$ a^0=1. $$

Value at One

$$ \boxed{ f(1)=a }. $$

Thus the point $(1,a)$ lies on the graph of $y=a^x$.

Domain

For $a>0$:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f)=\mathbb{R} }. $$

Range

Because positive bases raised to real powers remain positive:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Range}(f)=(0,\infty) }. $$

No Real Zeros

The basic exponential function:

$$ f(x)=a^x $$

never equals zero:

$$ \boxed{ a^x>0 }. $$

Horizontal Asymptote

For the basic exponential function:

$$ \boxed{y=0} $$

is a horizontal asymptote.

Exponential Growth

If:

$$ \boxed{a>1}, $$

then $a^x$ increases as $x$ increases.

This is called exponential growth.

Growth Example

$$ f(x)=2^x. $$

For integer steps:

$$ \ldots,\frac14,\frac12,1,2,4,8,16,\ldots $$

each increase of $1$ in $x$ multiplies the output by $2$.

Exponential Decay

If:

$$ \boxed{ 0<a<1 }, $$

then $a^x$ decreases as $x$ increases.

This is called exponential decay.

Decay Example

$$ f(x)=\left(\frac12\right)^x. $$

Each increase of $1$ in $x$ multiplies the output by $1/2$.

Constant Ratio Property

For:

$$ f(x)=a^x, $$

increasing the input by $h$ gives:

$$ f(x+h) = a^{x+h} = a^xa^h. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{f(x+h)}{f(x)} = a^h }. $$

The ratio depends only on the input increment $h$, not on the starting input $x$.

Unit-Step Ratio

Setting $h=1$:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{f(x+1)}{f(x)}=a }. $$

Thus the base itself is the multiplicative factor associated with a one-unit increase in the input.

Exponential vs. Linear Function

A linear function:

$$ f(x)=mx+b $$

has a constant additive change:

$$ f(x+1)-f(x)=m. $$

An exponential function has a constant multiplicative change:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{f(x+1)}{f(x)}=a }. $$

Difference vs. Ratio

A constant difference suggests linear behavior, while a constant ratio suggests exponential behavior.

Scaled Exponential Function

A common generalization is:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=Ca^x }. $$

Here $C$ determines the output at $x=0$:

$$ \boxed{ f(0)=C }. $$

Growth-Factor Form

If $r$ is a fractional growth rate per interval:

$$ \boxed{ f(t)=P_0(1+r)^t }. $$

Decay-Factor Form

If $r$ is the fraction lost per interval:

$$ \boxed{ f(t)=P_0(1-r)^t }. $$

Percentage Growth

Growth of $p\%$ per interval gives:

$$ \boxed{ a=1+\frac{p}{100} }. $$

Percentage Decay

Decay of $p\%$ per interval gives:

$$ \boxed{ a=1-\frac{p}{100} }. $$

Continuous Exponential Function

The natural exponential function uses the base:

$$ \boxed{ e\approx2.718281828459045\ldots }. $$

Its basic form is:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=e^x }. $$

General Continuous-Rate Form

$$ \boxed{ f(t)=Ce^{kt} }. $$

If $k>0$, the function grows exponentially; if $k<0$, it decays exponentially.

Natural Exponential Derivative

The function $e^x$ is distinguished by:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}e^x=e^x }. $$

Derivative of a General Base

For $a>0$:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}a^x = a^x\ln a }. $$

Derivative of $Ce^{kx}$

$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx} \left(Ce^{kx}\right) = kCe^{kx} }. $$

Thus its rate of change is proportional to the function itself.

Differential Equation Characterization

The differential equation:

$$ \boxed{ y'=ky } $$

has solutions:

$$ \boxed{ y=Ce^{kx} }. $$

This is one of the defining structural appearances of exponential functions in calculus and modeling.

Integration

$$ \boxed{ \int e^x\,dx=e^x+C }. $$

More generally, for $a>0$ and $a\neq1$:

$$ \boxed{ \int a^x\,dx = \frac{a^x}{\ln a}+C }. $$

Logarithmic Inverse

The inverse of:

$$ f(x)=a^x $$

is:

$$ \boxed{ f^{-1}(x)=\log_a x }. $$

Inverse Relationship

$$ \boxed{ a^{\log_a x}=x, \qquad x>0 }. $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \log_a(a^x)=x }. $$

Solving Equal-Base Equations

Since $a^x$ is one-to-one for $a>0$, $a\neq1$:

$$ \boxed{ a^u=a^v \iff u=v }. $$

Example

$$ 3^{2x-1}=3^7 $$

implies:

$$ 2x-1=7, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{x=4}. $$

Solving with Logarithms

For:

$$ a^x=b, $$

taking logarithms gives:

$$ \boxed{ x=\frac{\ln b}{\ln a} = \log_a b }. $$

General Transformed Form

A transformed exponential function is often written:

$$ \boxed{ f(x) = A\,a^{B(x-h)}+k }. $$

Vertical Scaling

The factor $A$ scales the graph vertically.

If $A<0$, it also reflects the graph across the $x$-axis.

Horizontal Scaling and Reflection

The factor $B$ affects horizontal scale. If $B<0$, it reflects the graph horizontally and reverses growth and decay orientation.

Horizontal Translation

The parameter $h$ shifts the graph horizontally:

$$ a^{x-h}. $$

Vertical Translation

The parameter $k$ shifts the graph vertically.

Shifted Horizontal Asymptote

For:

$$ f(x)=Aa^{B(x-h)}+k, $$

the horizontal asymptote is:

$$ \boxed{y=k}. $$

Range of a Shifted Positive Exponential

If $A>0$:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)>k }. $$

If $A<0$:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)<k }. $$

Exponential Function Is One-to-One

Every basic real exponential function with:

$$ a>0, \qquad a\neq1 $$

is strictly monotonic and therefore one-to-one.

Growth Monotonicity

If $a>1$, then $a^x$ is strictly increasing.

Decay Monotonicity

If $0<a<1$, then $a^x$ is strictly decreasing.

Concavity

Since:

$$ \frac{d^2}{dx^2}a^x = a^x(\ln a)^2, $$

and:

$$ a^x>0, \qquad (\ln a)^2>0, $$

every nonconstant basic real exponential function is concave up:

$$ \boxed{ f''(x)>0 }. $$

End Behavior for Growth

If $a>1$:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to\infty}a^x=\infty } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to-\infty}a^x=0 }. $$

End Behavior for Decay

If $0<a<1$:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to\infty}a^x=0 } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to-\infty}a^x=\infty }. $$

Reciprocal Relationship Between Growth and Decay Bases

$$ \boxed{ a^{-x} = \left(\frac1a\right)^x }. $$

Thus changing the sign of the exponent replaces a base by its reciprocal.

Reflection Relationship

The graph of:

$$ y=a^{-x} $$

is the reflection across the $y$-axis of:

$$ y=a^x. $$

Compound Interest

Periodic compounding can be modeled by:

$$ \boxed{ A = P\left(1+\frac rn\right)^{nt} }. $$

Here $P$ is principal, $r$ is the nominal annual rate, $n$ is the number of compounding periods per year, and $t$ is time in years.

Continuous Compounding

$$ \boxed{ A=Pe^{rt} }. $$

Doubling Time

If:

$$ Q(t)=Q_0e^{kt}, \qquad k>0, $$

the doubling time satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ T_d=\frac{\ln2}{k} }. $$

Half-Life

If:

$$ Q(t)=Q_0e^{-kt}, \qquad k>0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ T_{1/2}=\frac{\ln2}{k} }. $$

Population Models

Under ideal constant proportional growth:

$$ \boxed{ P(t)=P_0e^{kt} }. $$

Radioactive Decay

$$ \boxed{ N(t)=N_0e^{-\lambda t} }. $$

Newton Cooling

The difference between an object's temperature and the surrounding temperature may decay exponentially:

$$ \boxed{ T(t)-T_s = (T_0-T_s)e^{-kt} }. $$

Probability

The exponential distribution uses:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}, \qquad x\geq0 }. $$

Complex Exponential

Exponential functions extend naturally to complex inputs:

$$ \boxed{ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta }. $$

This is Euler's Formula.

General Complex Exponential

If:

$$ z=x+iy, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ e^z = e^x(\cos y+i\sin y) }. $$

Exponential vs. Polynomial Growth

For any fixed positive integer $n$ and base $a>1$:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{x\to\infty} \frac{x^n}{a^x} = 0 }. $$

Thus exponential growth eventually dominates polynomial growth.

Exponential vs. Factorial Growth

For fixed $a>0$, factorial growth eventually dominates $a^n$:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{a^n}{n!}\to0 } $$

as $n\to\infty$.

Exponential Function vs. Exponent

An exponent is the superscript quantity in:

$$ a^x. $$

An exponential function is the entire function in which the variable occurs in that exponent.

Exponential Function vs. Power Function

$$ \boxed{ \text{exponential: }a^x \qquad \text{power: }x^a }. $$

The distinction depends on which quantity is variable.

Exponential Function vs. Geometric Sequence

A geometric sequence:

$$ a_n=a_0r^n $$

is the discrete analogue of an exponential function. Its input is restricted to integer indices rather than all real values.

Exponential Function vs. Logistic Function

Pure exponential growth has no upper bound:

$$ Q(t)=Q_0e^{kt}. $$

Logistic growth introduces a limiting carrying capacity and is not purely exponential over its entire development.

Exponential Models Are Multiplicative

The defining modeling clue is repeated proportional change, not merely rapid growth or decline.

Common Misconception: A function is not exponential merely because an exponent appears somewhere in its formula. In the standard form, the variable itself must occur in an exponent while the base is constant. Thus $2^x$ is exponential, whereas $x^2$ is a power function. Exponential change is also multiplicative: equal input increments correspond to equal output ratios, not equal output differences.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$f(x)=a^x$ "F of x equals a to the x."
$a>1$ "The base is greater than one, giving exponential growth."
$0<a<1$ "The base is between zero and one, giving exponential decay."
$f(x)=Ce^{kx}$ "F of x equals C times e to the k x."
$f(x+1)/f(x)=a$ "Each one-unit increase in x multiplies the output by a."
$a^x=b\iff x=\log_a b$ "Exponentiation and logarithms are inverse operations."
$A=P(1+r)^t$ "The amount equals the initial principal times one plus the growth rate raised to time."
$Q(t)=Q_0e^{-kt}$ "Q of t equals Q zero times e to the negative k t."

The phrase exponential function should be distinguished from phrases such as exponential expression, exponential equation, and exponential model.

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=a^x } $$

denotes a function, while:

$$ 2^{x+1}=16 $$

is an exponential equation.

Defense Notation Advice: Identify clearly whether a parameter represents a multiplicative factor, a fractional rate, or a continuous rate. For example:

$$ \boxed{ Q(t)=Q_0(1+r)^t } $$

uses $1+r$ as the discrete growth factor, while:

$$ \boxed{ Q(t)=Q_0e^{kt} } $$

uses $k$ as a continuous proportional rate. These parameters are related but are not numerically identical in general.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Definition: An exponential function has a constant base and a variable exponent.
  • Basic Form: $$ \boxed{ f(x)=a^x }. $$
  • Base Conditions: $$ \boxed{ a>0, \qquad a\neq1 }. $$
  • Variable Position: The variable occurs in the exponent.
  • Basic Domain: $$ \boxed{\mathbb{R}}. $$
  • Basic Range: $$ \boxed{(0,\infty)}. $$
  • Y-Intercept: Every basic exponential graph contains $(0,1)$.
  • Reason: $$ a^0=1. $$
  • Point at One: $$ f(1)=a. $$
  • No Basic X-Intercept: $a^x$ is never zero for real $x$.
  • Horizontal Asymptote: $$ \boxed{y=0}. $$
  • Growth Condition: $$ \boxed{a>1}. $$
  • Decay Condition: $$ \boxed{0<a<1}. $$
  • Growth Monotonicity: If $a>1$, $a^x$ is strictly increasing.
  • Decay Monotonicity: If $0<a<1$, $a^x$ is strictly decreasing.
  • One-to-One Property: Every nonconstant basic real exponential function is one-to-one.
  • Inverse Function: $$ \boxed{ f^{-1}(x)=\log_a x }. $$
  • Constant Ratio: $$ \boxed{ \frac{f(x+1)}{f(x)}=a }. $$
  • General Increment: $$ \boxed{ \frac{f(x+h)}{f(x)}=a^h }. $$
  • Linear Contrast: Linear functions have constant differences rather than constant ratios.
  • Scaled Form: $$ \boxed{ f(x)=Ca^x }. $$
  • Initial Value: For $f(x)=Ca^x$: $$ f(0)=C. $$
  • Discrete Growth Model: $$ \boxed{ Q(t)=Q_0(1+r)^t }. $$
  • Discrete Decay Model: $$ \boxed{ Q(t)=Q_0(1-r)^t }. $$
  • Growth Factor: $1+r$ is the multiplicative amount retained plus gained per period.
  • Decay Factor: $1-r$ is the multiplicative fraction retained per period.
  • Natural Exponential: $$ \boxed{f(x)=e^x}. $$
  • Natural Base: $$ e\approx2.718281828459045\ldots. $$
  • Continuous Model: $$ \boxed{ Q(t)=Q_0e^{kt} }. $$
  • Continuous Growth: $k>0$.
  • Continuous Decay: $k<0$ in the signed-rate convention.
  • Derivative of $e^x$: $$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}e^x=e^x }. $$
  • Derivative of $a^x$: $$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}a^x=a^x\ln a }. $$
  • Differential Equation: $$ y'=ky $$ generates exponential solutions.
  • Relative Growth Rate: For $y=Ce^{kx}$: $$ \frac{y'}{y}=k. $$
  • Integral of $e^x$: $$ \int e^x\,dx=e^x+C. $$
  • Integral of $a^x$: $$ \int a^x\,dx=\frac{a^x}{\ln a}+C. $$
  • Logarithmic Inverse: $$ a^x=b\iff x=\log_a b. $$
  • Equal-Base Rule: $$ a^u=a^v\iff u=v $$ for a valid exponential base.
  • Change of Base: $$ \log_a b=\frac{\ln b}{\ln a}. $$
  • General Transformation: $$ f(x)=Aa^{B(x-h)}+k. $$
  • Vertical Scale: $A$ stretches or compresses output values.
  • Vertical Reflection: $A<0$ reflects the graph across the $x$-axis.
  • Horizontal Scale: $B$ changes horizontal scale.
  • Horizontal Reflection: $B<0$ reverses horizontal orientation.
  • Horizontal Shift: $h$ translates the graph horizontally.
  • Vertical Shift: $k$ translates the graph vertically.
  • Shifted Asymptote: $$ \boxed{y=k}. $$
  • Concavity: Basic exponential functions are concave up.
  • Second Derivative: $$ \frac{d^2}{dx^2}a^x=a^x(\ln a)^2>0. $$
  • Growth End Behavior: For $a>1$, $a^x\to\infty$ as $x\to\infty$.
  • Growth Left End: For $a>1$, $a^x\to0$ as $x\to-\infty$.
  • Decay Right End: For $0<a<1$, $a^x\to0$ as $x\to\infty$.
  • Decay Left End: For $0<a<1$, $a^x\to\infty$ as $x\to-\infty$.
  • Reciprocal Base: $$ a^{-x}=(1/a)^x. $$
  • Reflection: $a^{-x}$ is the $y$-axis reflection of $a^x$.
  • Compound Interest: $$ A=P\left(1+\frac rn\right)^{nt}. $$
  • Continuous Compounding: $$ A=Pe^{rt}. $$
  • Doubling Time: $$ T_d=\frac{\ln2}{k} $$ for continuous growth $e^{kt}$ with $k>0$.
  • Half-Life: $$ T_{1/2}=\frac{\ln2}{k} $$ for decay written $e^{-kt}$ with $k>0$.
  • Population Modeling: Constant proportional growth produces exponential models.
  • Radioactive Decay: Constant proportional loss produces exponential decay.
  • Drug Elimination: First-order elimination models often use exponential functions.
  • Cooling: Differences from equilibrium may decay exponentially.
  • Capacitor Discharge: Voltage may follow $V(t)=V_0e^{-t/(RC)}$.
  • Probability: Exponential distributions contain terms of the form $e^{-\lambda x}$.
  • Geometric Sequence: $$ a_n=a_0r^n $$ is a discrete exponential pattern.
  • Discrete vs. Continuous: Geometric sequences use discrete indices; exponential functions may use all real inputs.
  • Power Function Contrast: $$ a^x $$ is exponential; $x^a$ is a power function.
  • Polynomial Contrast: Exponential growth eventually exceeds every fixed-degree polynomial when the base is greater than one.
  • Logarithmic Contrast: Logarithmic functions are inverses of exponential functions.
  • Logarithmic Growth: Logarithmic growth is much slower than exponential growth.
  • Log-Linearization: If $Q=Ce^{kx}$, then: $$ \ln Q=\ln C+kx. $$
  • Semilog Plot: Exponential data appear linear when logarithms of positive outputs are plotted against the input.
  • Model Identification: Approximately constant ratios over equal intervals suggest exponential behavior.
  • Percentage Interpretation: Constant percentage change produces exponential rather than linear change.
  • Repeated Percentage Caution: Percent changes compound multiplicatively rather than adding directly.
  • Example: Two successive $10\%$ increases multiply by $1.1^2=1.21$, giving a total increase of $21\%$.
  • Base-One Caution: $1^x=1$ is constant and normally excluded from the exponential family.
  • Base-Zero Caution: $0^x$ does not define the standard real exponential function on all real inputs.
  • Negative-Base Caution: A negative constant base does not produce a real-valued function for all real exponents.
  • Complex Extension: Complex exponentials allow arbitrary complex inputs.
  • Euler's Formula: $$ e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta. $$
  • Complex Periodicity: $$ e^{z+2\pi i}=e^z. $$
  • Exponential vs. Exponent: The exponent is one component of the power; the exponential function is the entire variable-exponent mapping.
  • Exponential Equation: An equation such as $2^x=7$ contains the variable in an exponent and is solved using equal bases or logarithms.
  • Exponential Expression: An expression containing variable exponentiation need not by itself specify an entire function.
  • Exponential Model: A model uses exponential structure to represent multiplicative change in a real process.
  • Core Interpretation: An exponential function places the independent variable in the exponent: $$ \boxed{ f(x)=a^x, \qquad a>0, \qquad a\neq1 }. $$ Its defining operational feature is constant multiplicative change over equal input intervals. Bases greater than one produce growth, bases between zero and one produce decay, logarithms provide inverse functions, and the natural exponential $e^x$ provides the central continuous model for quantities whose rates of change are proportional to their current values.
