Half-Life

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

A half-life is the amount of time required for a quantity undergoing exponential decay to decrease to one-half of its current or initial value.

$$ \boxed{ N(T_{1/2})=\frac{N_0}{2} }. $$

Here $N_0$ is the initial quantity and $T_{1/2}$ denotes the half-life.

The term combines half, meaning one of two equal parts, with life, reflecting its historical use in describing the persistence and decay of radioactive substances. The mathematical concept now applies much more broadly to any exponential decay process.

If the half-life is $T_{1/2}$, then after each additional half-life the quantity is halved again:

$$ \boxed{ N(t) = N_0 \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^{t/T_{1/2}} }. $$

Thus half-life is fundamentally a statement about constant proportional decay, not constant subtraction.

$$ \boxed{ \text{equal time intervals} \longrightarrow \text{equal multiplicative decay factors} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Basic Half-Life Model

If a quantity begins at $N_0$ and has half-life $T_{1/2}$, then:

$$ \boxed{ N(t) = N_0 \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^{t/T_{1/2}} }. $$

After One Half-Life

Setting $t=T_{1/2}$ gives:

$$ N(T_{1/2}) = N_0 \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^1 = \boxed{ \frac{N_0}{2} }. $$

After Two Half-Lives

$$ N(2T_{1/2}) = N_0 \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^2 = \boxed{ \frac{N_0}{4} }. $$

After Three Half-Lives

$$ N(3T_{1/2}) = N_0 \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^3 = \boxed{ \frac{N_0}{8} }. $$

After $n$ Half-Lives

$$ \boxed{ N_n = N_0 \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^n }. $$

Remaining Fraction

After $n$ half-lives, the fraction remaining is:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{N_n}{N_0} = \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^n }. $$

Remaining Percentage

After $n$ half-lives:

$$ \boxed{ 100 \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^n \% } $$

remains.

Example: Three Half-Lives

After three half-lives:

$$ 100 \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^3 = \boxed{ 12.5\% }. $$

Example with an Initial Quantity

Suppose a substance begins with $80$ grams and has a half-life of $6$ hours. After $18$ hours:

$$ \frac{18}{6}=3 $$

half-lives have elapsed. Therefore:

$$ N(18) = 80 \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^3 = \boxed{ 10 }. $$

Half-Life as a Geometric Sequence

Sampling the quantity once per half-life produces:

$$ N_0,\quad \frac{N_0}{2},\quad \frac{N_0}{4},\quad \frac{N_0}{8},\ldots $$

This is a geometric sequence with common ratio:

$$ \boxed{ r=\frac{1}{2} }. $$

Discrete Half-Life Model

If time is measured in whole half-life intervals:

$$ \boxed{ N_{n+1} = \frac{1}{2}N_n }. $$

Continuous Exponential Model

Exponential decay is also commonly written:

$$ \boxed{ N(t)=N_0e^{-\lambda t} } $$

where $\lambda>0$ is the decay constant.

Half-Life and Decay Constant

At the half-life:

$$ \frac{N_0}{2} = N_0e^{-\lambda T_{1/2}}. $$

Dividing by $N_0$:

$$ \frac{1}{2} = e^{-\lambda T_{1/2}}. $$

Taking natural logarithms:

$$ -\ln2 = -\lambda T_{1/2}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln2}{\lambda} }. $$

Decay Constant from Half-Life

Rearranging:

$$ \boxed{ \lambda = \frac{\ln2}{T_{1/2}} }. $$

Equivalence of the Two Models

Starting from:

$$ N(t) = N_0e^{-\lambda t} $$

and substituting:

$$ \lambda = \frac{\ln2}{T_{1/2}}, $$

gives:

$$ N(t) = N_0 e^{-(\ln2)t/T_{1/2}} = \boxed{ N_0 \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^{t/T_{1/2}} }. $$

Fractional Numbers of Half-Lives

The model is not restricted to whole numbers of half-lives.

After half of one half-life:

$$ N = N_0 \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^{1/2} = \boxed{ \frac{N_0}{\sqrt{2}} }. $$

Quarter of a Half-Life

$$ \boxed{ N = N_0 \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^{1/4} }. $$

Finding Elapsed Time

Starting with:

$$ N = N_0 \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^{t/T_{1/2}}, $$

divide by $N_0$:

$$ \frac{N}{N_0} = \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^{t/T_{1/2}}. $$

Taking logarithms gives:

$$ \boxed{ t = T_{1/2} \frac{ \ln(N/N_0) }{ \ln(1/2) } }. $$

Equivalent Elapsed-Time Formula

Since $\ln(1/2)=-\ln2$:

$$ \boxed{ t = T_{1/2} \frac{ \ln(N_0/N) }{ \ln2 } }. $$

Finding the Number of Half-Lives

If the remaining fraction is known:

$$ f = \frac{N}{N_0}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ n = \frac{\ln f}{\ln(1/2)} = \frac{\ln(1/f)}{\ln2} }. $$

Example: One-Eighth Remaining

If:

$$ \frac{N}{N_0} = \frac{1}{8}, $$

then:

$$ \frac{1}{8} = \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^3. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ n=3 }. $$

Example: One-Tenth Remaining

If:

$$ \frac{N}{N_0} = \frac{1}{10}, $$

then:

$$ n = \frac{ \ln(1/10) }{ \ln(1/2) }. $$

This is not an integer, illustrating why half-life models naturally permit fractional numbers of half-lives.

Finding Half-Life from Two Measurements

If a quantity changes from $N_0$ to $N$ during time $t$, then:

$$ \frac{N}{N_0} = \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^{t/T_{1/2}}. $$

Solving for the half-life:

$$ \boxed{ T_{1/2} = t \frac{ \ln(1/2) }{ \ln(N/N_0) } }. $$

Equivalent Positive-Log Form

$$ \boxed{ T_{1/2} = t \frac{ \ln2 }{ \ln(N_0/N) } } $$

when $0<N<N_0$.

Constant Percentage Decay

Half-life behavior is one form of constant percentage decay. The quantity loses the same fraction during equal elapsed intervals, not the same absolute amount.

Not Linear Decay

Linear decay would have a form such as:

$$ N(t)=N_0-kt, $$

which subtracts the same amount per unit time.

Half-life decay instead has exponential form:

$$ \boxed{ N(t) = N_0 \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^{t/T_{1/2}} }. $$

Half-Life Does Not Mean Complete Disappearance after Two Half-Lives

After two half-lives:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{1}{4} } $$

remains, not zero.

Exponential Decay Approaches Zero

In the ideal continuous model:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{t\to\infty} N_0 \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^{t/T_{1/2}} = 0 }. $$

The model approaches zero asymptotically rather than reaching zero at a finite time.

Population Interpretation

When $N(t)$ represents a large population of independently decaying objects, the exponential formula describes an expected or statistical population behavior.

Individual Events Are Not Timed by the Half-Life

In stochastic decay processes, half-life characterizes a population or probability distribution. It does not mean that each individual object lasts exactly one half-life.

Survival Probability

If an individual entity has constant hazard rate $\lambda$, its survival probability can be modeled as:

$$ \boxed{ P(T>t) = e^{-\lambda t} }. $$

At the half-life:

$$ P(T>T_{1/2}) = \boxed{ \frac{1}{2} }. $$

Median Lifetime Connection

For an exponential lifetime distribution, the half-life is also the median lifetime because half the population is expected to survive beyond it and half to have decayed before it.

Mean Lifetime

For exponential decay with decay constant $\lambda$, the mean lifetime is:

$$ \boxed{ \tau = \frac{1}{\lambda} }. $$

Half-Life vs. Mean Lifetime

Using:

$$ T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln2}{\lambda}, $$

and:

$$ \tau = \frac{1}{\lambda}, $$

gives:

$$ \boxed{ T_{1/2} = \tau\ln2 }. $$

Thus half-life and mean lifetime are not the same quantity.

Radioactive Decay

Half-life is traditionally associated with radioactive nuclei. If the decay rate is proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei:

$$ \frac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N, $$

the solution is:

$$ \boxed{ N(t)=N_0e^{-\lambda t} }. $$

Activity

Radioactive activity is proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei:

$$ A(t)=\lambda N(t). $$

Therefore activity has the same half-life:

$$ \boxed{ A(t) = A_0 \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^{t/T_{1/2}} }. $$

Radiometric Dating

When an appropriate decay system and initial-condition model are available, measured parent or daughter quantities can be used with exponential decay equations to estimate elapsed time.

Biological and Chemical Elimination

In ideal first-order elimination, the concentration of a substance may follow:

$$ C(t)=C_0e^{-kt}. $$

The associated half-life is:

$$ \boxed{ T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln2}{k} }. $$

First-Order Chemical Kinetics

For a first-order reaction:

$$ [A](t) = [A]_0e^{-kt}, $$

the half-life is independent of the initial concentration:

$$ \boxed{ T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln2}{k} }. $$

Half-Life Independence from Initial Amount

In a true exponential decay model, changing $N_0$ does not change the half-life. The same proportional halving time applies from any starting level.

Successive Halving

If a quantity has fallen from $80$ to $40$, the next half-life takes it from $40$ to $20$, not from $40$ to $0$.

Half-Life as Scale-Invariant Time

Because exponential decay is proportional, the time required to move from $N$ to $N/2$ is the same as the time required to move from $N/2$ to $N/4$.

Doubling-Time Analogy

Exponential growth:

$$ P(t)=P_0e^{kt}, \qquad k>0, $$

has doubling time:

$$ \boxed{ T_2 = \frac{\ln2}{k} }. $$

Half-life is the corresponding decay concept.

Half-Life vs. Doubling Time

$$ \boxed{ \text{half-life} \rightarrow \text{multiply by }\frac{1}{2} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{doubling time} \rightarrow \text{multiply by }2 }. $$

Decay Factor per Unit Time

If the half-life is $T$, then the one-unit-time multiplier is:

$$ \boxed{ b = \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^{1/T} }. $$

Thus:

$$ N(t)=N_0b^t. $$

Half-Life from a Decay Factor

If:

$$ N(t)=N_0b^t, \qquad 0<b<1, $$

then the half-life satisfies:

$$ b^{T_{1/2}} = \frac{1}{2}. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ T_{1/2} = \frac{ \ln(1/2) }{ \ln b } }. $$

Percentage Decay per Period

If a quantity loses $p\%$ each period, then:

$$ b = 1-\frac{p}{100}. $$

The half-life in periods is:

$$ \boxed{ T_{1/2} = \frac{ \ln(1/2) }{ \ln\left(1-\frac{p}{100}\right) } }. $$

Units

Half-life carries units of time:

$$ \boxed{ \text{seconds, minutes, hours, days, years, }\ldots }. $$

The exponent $t/T_{1/2}$ is dimensionless because it is a ratio of two times.

Dimensional Consistency

In:

$$ N(t) = N_0 \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^{t/T_{1/2}}, $$

the exponent must be dimensionless. This requires $t$ and $T_{1/2}$ to use compatible time units.

Unit Conversion

If half-life is stated in hours while elapsed time is stated in days, convert one quantity before forming:

$$ \frac{t}{T_{1/2}}. $$

Graphical Interpretation

On an exponential decay graph, moving horizontally by one half-life multiplies the vertical value by $1/2$.

Horizontal Asymptote

In the ideal decay model:

$$ \boxed{ y=0 } $$

is a horizontal asymptote.

Monotonicity

For $N_0>0$ and positive half-life, the function:

$$ N(t) = N_0 \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^{t/T_{1/2}} $$

is strictly decreasing with time.

Concavity

The standard positive exponential decay curve is concave upward:

$$ \boxed{ N''(t)>0 }. $$

Rate of Decay

For:

$$ N(t)=N_0e^{-\lambda t}, $$

differentiation gives:

$$ \boxed{ N'(t) = -\lambda N(t) }. $$

The instantaneous rate of decrease is proportional to the amount remaining.

Percentage vs. Absolute Loss

Equal half-life intervals lose equal percentages but unequal absolute amounts.

From $100$ to $50$, the loss is $50$. From $50$ to $25$, the loss is $25$. Both are $50\%$ losses.

Reverse-Time Extrapolation

Mathematically, if an ideal exponential model is extended backward:

$$ N(-T_{1/2}) = 2N_0. $$

Whether such extrapolation is physically meaningful depends on the application.

Model Validity

A constant half-life requires exponential decay with a constant proportional decay rate. Processes whose decay rates change with time need not possess one fixed half-life over their entire evolution.

Multiple-Component Decay

A sum of exponentials:

$$ N(t) = A e^{-\lambda_1t} + B e^{-\lambda_2t} $$

generally does not have one universal constant half-life for the total quantity.

Effective Half-Life

In some applied fields, an effective half-life may summarize the combined effect of multiple removal mechanisms. Its precise definition depends on the model being used.

Common Error: Subtracting One-Half of the Original Amount Repeatedly

Half-life repeatedly halves the amount currently present:

$$ N_0 \rightarrow \frac{N_0}{2} \rightarrow \frac{N_0}{4} \rightarrow \frac{N_0}{8}. $$

It does not repeatedly subtract $N_0/2$.

Common Error: Assuming Two Half-Lives Remove Everything

After two half-lives:

$$ \boxed{ 25\% } $$

remains.

Common Error: Assuming Four Half-Lives Leave One-Fourth

Four repeated halvings give:

$$ \left( \frac{1}{2} \right)^4 = \boxed{ \frac{1}{16} }. $$

Common Error: Confusing Half-Life with Decay Constant

The half-life is a time. The decay constant $\lambda$ has reciprocal-time units. They satisfy:

$$ \boxed{ T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln2}{\lambda} }. $$

Common Error: Confusing Half-Life with Mean Lifetime

For exponential decay:

$$ T_{1/2} = \tau\ln2, $$

so the quantities are related but not identical.

Common Error: Treating Half-Life as the Lifetime of Every Individual

In stochastic decay, half-life summarizes population behavior. Individual decay times vary.

Common Error: Using Incompatible Time Units

The ratio $t/T_{1/2}$ must use compatible units.

Common Error: Using a Linear Model

A fixed half-life implies exponential rather than constant-amount decay.

Common Misconception: Half-life does not mean that half of the original amount disappears during every subsequent interval. It means the amount currently remaining is halved during each half-life. The sequence is therefore $N_0$, $N_0/2$, $N_0/4$, $N_0/8$, and so on, which is geometric and arises from exponential decay.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$T_{1/2}$ "The half-life," or "T sub one-half."
$N(T_{1/2})=N_0/2$ "After one half-life, one-half of the initial quantity remains."
$N(t)=N_0(1/2)^{t/T_{1/2}}$ "The quantity equals the initial amount times one-half raised to the number of elapsed half-lives."
$N(t)=N_0e^{-\lambda t}$ "The quantity decays exponentially with decay constant lambda."
$T_{1/2}=\ln2/\lambda$ "The half-life equals the natural logarithm of two divided by the decay constant."
$N_n/N_0=(1/2)^n$ "After n half-lives, the remaining fraction is one-half to the nth power."
$t=T_{1/2}\ln(N_0/N)/\ln2$ "Elapsed time equals the half-life times the logarithm of the initial-to-remaining ratio divided by the natural logarithm of two."

The notation $T_{1/2}$ is conventional but not universal. Depending on the field, one may also see symbols such as $t_{1/2}$.

The phrase one half-life has elapsed means:

$$ \boxed{ t=T_{1/2} }. $$

The phrase three half-lives have elapsed means:

$$ \boxed{ t=3T_{1/2} }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Define whether your model uses $N(t)=N_0e^{-\lambda t}$ or $N(t)=N_0(1/2)^{t/T_{1/2}}$, and keep time units consistent. Distinguish the half-life $T_{1/2}$ from the decay constant $\lambda$, and distinguish both from the mean lifetime $\tau$. When counting elapsed half-lives, use $n=t/T_{1/2}$ rather than assuming $n$ must be an integer.
