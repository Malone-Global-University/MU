Strict Inequality

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

A strict inequality compares two quantities while excluding equality. The strict comparison symbols are:

$$ \boxed{ a<b \qquad\text{or}\qquad a>b }. $$

The word strict indicates that the boundary case $a=b$ is not included. This distinguishes strict inequalities from non-strict inequalities using $\leq$ or $\geq$.

Operational Nuance

Basic Meaning

$$ 3<5 $$

means $3$ is less than $5$, while:

$$ 5>3 $$

expresses the same ordering from the opposite direction.

Solving a Strict Inequality

For:

$$ 2x+1<7, $$

subtract $1$ and divide by $2$:

$$ 2x<6 \Longrightarrow \boxed{x<3}. $$

Boundary Exclusion

Since the inequality is strict, the endpoint $3$ is not part of the solution set:

$$ \boxed{ x<3 \Longleftrightarrow x\in(-\infty,3) }. $$

Multiplying or Dividing by a Negative

Multiplying or dividing both sides by a negative quantity reverses the inequality:

$$ -2x<6 \Longrightarrow \boxed{x>-3}. $$

Addition and Subtraction

Adding the same quantity to both sides preserves strict order:

$$ a<b \Longrightarrow a+c<b+c. $$

Multiplication by a Positive Quantity

$$ a<b,\ c>0 \Longrightarrow ac<bc. $$

Multiplication by a Negative Quantity

$$ a<b,\ c<0 \Longrightarrow ac>bc. $$

Transitivity

$$ \boxed{ a<b \text{ and } b<c \Longrightarrow a<c }. $$

Chained Inequalities

A compound statement such as:

$$ \boxed{ 1<x<4 } $$

means both $1<x$ and $x<4$.

Interval Notation

$$ \boxed{ 1<x<4 \Longleftrightarrow x\in(1,4) }. $$

Parentheses indicate that neither endpoint is included.

Number-Line Graphing

A strict endpoint is conventionally shown with an open circle. The ray or interval extends through all values satisfying the comparison.

Functions

A strict functional inequality such as:

$$ f(x)>g(x) $$

asks where the value of $f$ is strictly greater than the value of $g$. Equality points are excluded.

Absolute-Value Example

$$ |x|<3 \Longleftrightarrow \boxed{-3<x<3}. $$

Strict vs. Non-Strict

$$ x<3 \qquad\text{versus}\qquad x\leq3. $$

The second statement includes the boundary value $3$; the first does not.

Common Misconception

The symbol $<$ does not mean "approximately less than." It is an exact order statement asserting that equality does not hold and the left quantity lies strictly below the right quantity in the relevant ordering.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$a<b$ "A is strictly less than b."
$a>b$ "A is strictly greater than b."
$x<3$ "X is less than three."
$1<x<4$ "X is strictly between one and four."
$x\in(1,4)$ "X belongs to the open interval from one to four."
$f(x)>g(x)$ "F of x is strictly greater than g of x."

The word strictly may be spoken for emphasis even though the symbols $<$ and $>$ already encode strictness.

Defense Notation Advice: Use $<$ and $>$ only when equality must be excluded. Use open endpoints in interval or number-line notation, and remember to reverse the inequality whenever multiplication or division by a negative quantity occurs.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: An order comparison that excludes equality.
  • Symbols: $<$ means strictly less than; $>$ means strictly greater than.
  • Boundary: The equality value is excluded.
  • Interval Notation: Strict finite endpoints are represented with parentheses.
  • Number Line: Strict endpoints are commonly represented by open circles.
  • Addition: Adding the same quantity preserves strict inequality.
  • Positive Scaling: Multiplication by a positive number preserves direction.
  • Negative Scaling: Multiplication by a negative number reverses direction.
  • Transitivity: $a<b$ and $b<c$ imply $a<c$.
  • Compound Inequalities: Chained strict comparisons can define open intervals.
  • Function Comparison: $f(x)>g(x)$ identifies where one function lies strictly above another.
  • Optimization: Strict constraints such as $g(x)<0$ exclude their boundary.
  • Analysis: Strict inequalities frequently express positivity, monotonicity, bounds, and neighborhood conditions.
  • Non-Strict Contrast: $\leq$ and $\geq$ permit equality.
  • Common Error: Including an endpoint that should be excluded.
  • Common Error: Failing to reverse the inequality after multiplying or dividing by a negative number.
  • Common Error: Using brackets instead of parentheses for a finite strict endpoint in interval notation.
  • Common Error: Treating $x<a$ as though $x=a$ were also permitted.
  • Core Interpretation: A strict inequality asserts order without equality: $$ \boxed{ a<b \quad\text{or}\quad a>b }. $$ Its defining operational feature is boundary exclusion, while its algebraic manipulation follows the ordinary order laws, including reversal under multiplication or division by a negative quantity.
