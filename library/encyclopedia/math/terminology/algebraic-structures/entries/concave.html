Concave

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

In mathematics, concave describes a shape, set, or function whose geometry bends inward or lies on a specified side of its chords or tangent lines. The exact definition depends on the mathematical object being discussed.

The word comes from Latin roots associated with a hollow or inward-curving surface. This physical image survives in geometric language, but mathematical definitions are more precise than visual impressions.

$$ \boxed{ \text{concave} = \text{an inward-bending or chord-relative geometric property} }. $$

The term is especially important in two distinct settings:

  • analysis: concave functions and concavity of graphs, and
  • geometry: concave polygons and other nonconvex figures.

Because these uses are related but not identical, the noun following concave matters. A concave function is defined differently from a concave polygon.

Operational Nuance

Concave Functions

A real-valued function $f$ defined on an interval is concave if the graph lies on or above every secant segment joining two points of the graph.

Formally, for $x,y$ in the interval and $0\le\lambda\le1$:

$$ \boxed{ f\bigl(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y\bigr) \ge \lambda f(x)+(1-\lambda)f(y) }. $$

This inequality is one of the standard definitions of a concave function.

Geometric Interpretation

The point:

$$ \lambda x+(1-\lambda)y $$

lies between $x$ and $y$. For a concave function, the function value there is at least as large as the corresponding weighted average of the endpoint values.

$$ \boxed{ \text{graph of a concave function} \text{ lies above its chords} }. $$

Midpoint Form

Taking $\lambda=1/2$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ f\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right) \ge \frac{f(x)+f(y)}{2} }. $$

This is the midpoint concavity condition. Under suitable regularity assumptions, midpoint concavity can imply full concavity.

Strict Concavity

A function is strictly concave if:

$$ \boxed{ f\bigl(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y\bigr) > \lambda f(x)+(1-\lambda)f(y) } $$

whenever $x\ne y$ and $0<\lambda<1$.

Geometrically, the graph lies strictly above the interior of each nontrivial secant segment.

Linear Functions

A linear or affine function satisfies equality:

$$ f\bigl(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y\bigr) = \lambda f(x)+(1-\lambda)f(y). $$

Therefore an affine function is both concave and convex, but it is not strictly concave or strictly convex.

Second-Derivative Test

If $f$ is twice differentiable on an interval and:

$$ \boxed{ f''(x)\le0 } $$

throughout the interval, then $f$ is concave there.

If:

$$ \boxed{ f''(x)<0 } $$

throughout an interval, then $f$ is strictly concave there.

Example: Concave Quadratic

Consider:

$$ f(x)=-x^2. $$

Its second derivative is:

$$ f''(x)=-2. $$

Since:

$$ -2<0, $$

the function is strictly concave on $\mathbb R$.

First-Derivative Interpretation

For a differentiable function, concavity can also be characterized by a nonincreasing derivative.

$$ \boxed{ f\text{ concave} \Rightarrow f'\text{ is nonincreasing} } $$

under the usual differentiability assumptions.

The slopes of tangent lines become smaller, or at least do not increase, as $x$ increases.

Tangent-Line Property

For a differentiable concave function, the tangent line at a point lies on or above the graph.

$$ \boxed{ f(y) \le f(x)+f'(x)(y-x) }. $$

This inequality is an important analytic characterization of concavity.

Concave Down

In elementary calculus, a graph is commonly said to be concave down on an interval when:

$$ \boxed{ f''(x)<0 } $$

throughout that interval.

In many advanced texts, such a function is simply called concave.

Terminology Convention

This creates an important language difference:

Elementary Calculus Language Common Analysis / Optimization Language
Concave down Concave
Concave up Convex

Authors should therefore define their convention when ambiguity is possible.

Concavity and Inflection Points

An inflection point is typically a point at which the graph changes concavity.

For example:

$$ f(x)=x^3 $$

has:

$$ f''(x)=6x. $$

Thus:

$$ \begin{aligned} x<0&\Rightarrow f''(x)<0,\\ x>0&\Rightarrow f''(x)>0. \end{aligned} $$

The concavity changes at $x=0$.

Second Derivative Equal to Zero

The equation:

$$ f''(c)=0 $$

does not by itself prove that $c$ is an inflection point.

Concavity must actually change across the point.

Example without an Inflection Point

Consider:

$$ f(x)=x^4. $$

Then:

$$ f''(x)=12x^2. $$

Although:

$$ f''(0)=0, $$

the second derivative is nonnegative on both sides of $0$, so the graph does not change from convex to concave there.

Concavity and Maximum Values

Concavity has powerful consequences in optimization. For a differentiable concave function, any interior point satisfying:

$$ f'(x)=0 $$

is a global maximizer under appropriate domain conditions.

If the function is strictly concave, such a maximizer is unique when it exists.

$$ \boxed{ \text{strict concavity} + \text{stationary point} \Rightarrow \text{unique global maximum} } $$

Example: Maximizing a Concave Quadratic

Let:

$$ f(x)=-x^2+6x+1. $$

Since:

$$ f''(x)=-2<0, $$

$f$ is strictly concave.

Solving:

$$ f'(x)=-2x+6=0 $$

gives:

$$ \boxed{x=3}. $$

Therefore $x=3$ is the unique global maximizer on $\mathbb R$.

Concavity in Several Variables

A function:

$$ f:C\subseteq\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R $$

defined on a convex set $C$ is concave if:

$$ \boxed{ f(\lambda\mathbf{x}+(1-\lambda)\mathbf{y}) \ge \lambda f(\mathbf{x})+(1-\lambda)f(\mathbf{y}) } $$

for all $\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in C$ and $0\le\lambda\le1$.

Hessian Test

If $f$ is twice differentiable on a convex domain, concavity can be tested using the Hessian matrix.

If:

$$ \boxed{ \nabla^2 f(\mathbf{x}) \text{ is negative semidefinite} } $$

throughout the domain, then $f$ is concave.

Negative definiteness provides a common sufficient condition for strict concavity.

Jensen's Inequality

Concavity reverses the familiar convex form of Jensen's inequality.

For weights $\lambda_i\ge0$ with:

$$ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_i=1, $$

a concave function satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ f\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_i x_i\right) \ge \sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_i f(x_i) }. $$

Example: Logarithm

The natural logarithm:

$$ f(x)=\ln x, \qquad x>0, $$

satisfies:

$$ f''(x)=-\frac1{x^2}<0. $$

Therefore $\ln x$ is strictly concave on $(0,\infty)$.

Example: Square Root

For:

$$ f(x)=\sqrt{x}, \qquad x>0, $$

we have:

$$ f''(x) = -\frac{1}{4x^{3/2}}<0. $$

Hence $\sqrt{x}$ is strictly concave on $(0,\infty)$.

Concavity in Economics

Concave functions often model diminishing marginal returns or diminishing marginal utility.

If utility $U(x)$ is increasing but concave:

$$ U'(x)>0, \qquad U''(x)<0, $$

then additional units increase utility, but each additional unit contributes less marginal utility than the previous one.

Concavity in Probability and Statistics

Concavity appears in likelihood theory, information theory, inequalities, and optimization. Logarithms of probability-related quantities are particularly important because logarithmic concavity often converts products into sums while preserving useful curvature properties.

Concave Polygon

In geometry, a polygon is concave if it is not convex.

Equivalently, a simple polygon is concave if at least one interior angle is greater than:

$$ \boxed{ 180^\circ } $$

or $\pi$ radians.

Geometric Chord Test for a Polygon

A convex polygon contains the entire line segment joining any two of its points. A concave polygon fails this property.

$$ \boxed{ \exists P,Q\in S \text{ such that } \overline{PQ}\not\subseteq S }. $$

Here $S$ denotes the polygonal region.

Reflex Vertex

A vertex of a simple polygon whose interior angle exceeds $180^\circ$ is called a reflex vertex.

$$ \boxed{ \theta>180^\circ \Rightarrow \text{reflex interior angle} }. $$

Every simple concave polygon has at least one reflex vertex.

Concave versus Convex Polygon

Convex Polygon Concave Polygon
Every interior angle is less than $180^\circ$ At least one interior angle exceeds $180^\circ$
Every chord between points of the region stays inside Some chord between points of the region passes outside
No inward indentation Has at least one inward indentation

Triangles Cannot Be Concave

Every ordinary Euclidean triangle is convex.

$$ \boxed{ \text{no simple Euclidean triangle is concave} }. $$

A concave simple polygon must have at least four vertices.

Concave Quadrilateral

A quadrilateral can be concave if one interior angle exceeds $180^\circ$. Such a quadrilateral has an inward-pointing vertex.

Concave Sets

In standard mathematical terminology, sets are ordinarily classified as convex or nonconvex. The phrase concave set is much less standard and can be ambiguous.

For clarity, a set that fails the convex-set condition is usually called nonconvex.

Hypograph of a Concave Function

A useful advanced characterization is that $f$ is concave precisely when its hypograph is convex:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{hypo}(f) = \{(x,t):t\le f(x)\} }. $$

This connects function concavity directly to convex geometry.

Negative of a Convex Function

A function $f$ is concave exactly when $-f$ is convex:

$$ \boxed{ f\text{ concave} \iff -f\text{ convex} }. $$

Sums of Concave Functions

If $f$ and $g$ are concave on the same convex domain, then:

$$ \boxed{ f+g \text{ is concave} }. $$

More generally, nonnegative weighted sums of concave functions are concave.

Positive Scaling

If $f$ is concave and $c\ge0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ cf \text{ is concave} }. $$

Multiplying by a negative constant reverses the curvature classification, converting concavity to convexity.

Concavity Is Not the Same as Decreasing

A concave function may be increasing, decreasing, or increase on one interval and decrease on another.

For example:

$$ f(x)=-x^2 $$

is concave everywhere but increases for $x<0$ and decreases for $x>0$.

$$ \boxed{ \text{concavity concerns changing slope, not simply slope sign} }. $$

Concavity Is Not the Same as Being Negative

Whether a function is above or below the $x$-axis has no direct bearing on whether it is concave.

For example:

$$ f(x)=10-x^2 $$

is concave even where its values are positive.

Concavity Is Not Determined by f'

The sign of $f'$ determines whether a differentiable function is increasing or decreasing. The sign of $f''$ is associated with concavity.

$$ \boxed{ f'>0 \Rightarrow \text{increasing}, \qquad f''<0 \Rightarrow \text{concave} }. $$

Common Misconception: Concave Means Concave Up

In optimization and analysis, concave ordinarily corresponds to what elementary calculus often calls concave down.

Therefore terminology must be read according to field convention.

Common Misconception: f''(x)=0 Means the Function Is Neither Concave nor Convex

A zero second derivative at isolated points does not determine the overall classification. Even identically zero second derivative corresponds to an affine function, which is both concave and convex under the non-strict definitions.

Common Misconception: f''(c)=0 Guarantees an Inflection Point

Concavity must change across $c$. The equation $f''(c)=0$ merely identifies a possible candidate under common calculus methods.

Common Misconception: A Concave Function Must Have a Maximum

Concavity alone does not guarantee that a maximum exists.

For example:

$$ f(x)=x $$

is concave on $\mathbb R$ under the non-strict definition but has no global maximum there.

Common Misconception: Concave Polygon Means All Angles Exceed 180°

A simple polygon is concave when at least one interior angle exceeds $180^\circ$. The remaining interior angles need not.

Common Misconception: A Figure That Looks Inward Is Automatically Concave in Every Sense

Concavity is object-specific. A curve, function, polygon, optimization problem, and set each require the appropriate mathematical definition rather than visual appearance alone.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$f''(x)<0$ "The second derivative is negative, so the function is concave down on the interval."
$f''(x)\le0$ "The second derivative is nonpositive, which supports concavity."
$f(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)\ge\lambda f(x)+(1-\lambda)f(y)$ "f of the weighted average is at least the weighted average of the function values."
$f(y)\le f(x)+f'(x)(y-x)$ "A concave differentiable function lies below its tangent line."
$\nabla^2f\preceq0$ "The Hessian is negative semidefinite."
$\theta>180^\circ$ "The polygon has a reflex interior angle."

In elementary calculus, the phrase concave down is often preferred because students also encounter concave up. In optimization, economics, and much of mathematical analysis, concave by itself normally means the chord-above-or-below relationship expressed by:

$$ f(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y) \ge \lambda f(x)+(1-\lambda)f(y). $$

The phrase the function is concave at a point should be used carefully. Concavity is naturally a property on an interval or convex domain, although local concavity near a point may be discussed.

Defense Notation Advice: Specify whether concave refers to a function, graph, polygon, or another object. In analysis, use the chord inequality when a definition independent of differentiability is needed. Use $f''\le0$ as a derivative criterion only when the required differentiability assumptions hold. Distinguish concave from concave down according to field convention, and do not declare an inflection point solely because $f''=0$.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: Concave describes an inward-bending or chord-relative property whose precise definition depends on the mathematical object.
  • Function Definition: $$ \boxed{ f(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y) \ge \lambda f(x)+(1-\lambda)f(y) }. $$
  • Strict Concavity: The inequality is strict for distinct points and $0<\lambda<1$.
  • Graph Interpretation: The graph of a concave function lies on or above its secant chords.
  • Tangent Interpretation: A differentiable concave function lies on or below its tangent lines.
  • Second-Derivative Criterion: $$ \boxed{ f''(x)\le0 } $$ is a standard sufficient criterion for concavity on an interval.
  • Strict Second-Derivative Criterion: $$ \boxed{ f''(x)<0 } $$ is a standard sufficient criterion for strict concavity.
  • First-Derivative View: The derivative of a differentiable concave function is nonincreasing.
  • Elementary Calculus Terminology: Concave down commonly corresponds to concave in analysis.
  • Convex Contrast: $$ \boxed{ f\text{ concave} \iff -f\text{ convex} }. $$
  • Inflection Point: An inflection point involves a change in concavity, not merely the equation $f''(c)=0$.
  • Optimization: Concavity makes local maxima particularly important; strict concavity can imply a unique global maximum.
  • Several Variables: Concavity is defined on convex domains using weighted combinations of vectors.
  • Hessian Test: A negative-semidefinite Hessian supports concavity for twice-differentiable functions on suitable convex domains.
  • Jensen's Inequality: For concave $f$: $$ \boxed{ f\left(\sum\lambda_i x_i\right) \ge \sum\lambda_i f(x_i) }. $$
  • Common Examples: $\ln x$ and $\sqrt{x}$ are strictly concave on their appropriate positive domains.
  • Economics: Concavity commonly models diminishing marginal utility or returns.
  • Geometry: A simple polygon is concave if it is not convex.
  • Polygon Angle Test: A simple polygon is concave if at least one interior angle exceeds $180^\circ$.
  • Reflex Vertex: A vertex whose interior angle exceeds $180^\circ$.
  • Polygon Segment Test: A concave polygon has some pair of points whose joining segment is not entirely contained in the polygonal region.
  • Triangle Case: Every ordinary Euclidean triangle is convex, so a simple triangle cannot be concave.
  • Set Terminology: Nonconvex set is generally preferred over concave set.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse concavity with whether a function is increasing or decreasing.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse concavity with whether function values are positive or negative.
  • Common Error: Do not use the sign of $f'$ alone to determine concavity.
  • Common Error: Do not assume $f''(c)=0$ guarantees an inflection point.
  • Common Error: Do not assume a concave function necessarily attains a maximum.
  • Terminological Contrast: A concave function lies above its secant chords. A strictly concave function lies strictly above nontrivial secant interiors. Concave down is elementary-calculus terminology commonly corresponding to concavity in analysis. A convex function satisfies the opposite chord inequality. A concave polygon is a nonconvex polygon with at least one reflex interior angle. An inflection point is a point associated with a change in concavity.
