Complex Conjugate

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

The complex conjugate of a complex number is obtained by reversing the sign of its imaginary part while leaving its real part unchanged.

If:

$$ z=a+bi, $$

where $a,b\in\mathbb R$, then the complex conjugate of $z$ is:

$$ \boxed{ \overline z=a-bi }. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ a+bi \longleftrightarrow a-bi }. $$

The word conjugate comes from Latin roots associated with joining or pairing together. In mathematics, conjugate objects commonly occur in naturally related pairs. A complex number and its conjugate are paired by having the same real part and imaginary parts of opposite sign.

Geometrically, conjugation reflects a point in the complex plane across the real axis.

$$ \boxed{ (a,b) \longmapsto (a,-b) }. $$

Complex conjugation is therefore simultaneously an algebraic operation and a geometric reflection.

Operational Nuance

Basic Definition

For:

$$ z=a+bi, $$

define:

$$ \boxed{ \overline z=a-bi }. $$

Example

If:

$$ z=4+7i, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \overline z=4-7i }. $$

Negative Imaginary Part

If:

$$ z=3-5i, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \overline z=3+5i }. $$

Conjugate of a Real Number

A real number has zero imaginary part:

$$ x=x+0i. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \overline x=x \qquad \text{for every }x\in\mathbb R }. $$

Real numbers are fixed points of complex conjugation.

Conjugate of a Pure Imaginary Number

If:

$$ z=bi, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \overline z=-bi }. $$

For example:

$$ \overline{6i}=-6i. $$

Conjugating Twice

Applying complex conjugation twice returns the original number:

$$ \boxed{ \overline{\overline z}=z }. $$

Complex conjugation is therefore an involution.

Real and Imaginary Parts

If:

$$ z=a+bi, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ z+\overline z=2a }. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Re}(z) = \frac{z+\overline z}{2} }. $$

Similarly:

$$ z-\overline z=2bi, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Im}(z) = \frac{z-\overline z}{2i} }. $$

Product with the Conjugate

One of the most important conjugate identities is:

$$ z\overline z = (a+bi)(a-bi). $$

Using the difference of squares:

$$ (a+bi)(a-bi) = a^2-b^2i^2 = a^2+b^2. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ z\overline z=a^2+b^2 }. $$

Connection with Modulus

The modulus of:

$$ z=a+bi $$

is:

$$ |z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ z\overline z=|z|^2 }. $$

Equivalently:

$$ \boxed{ |z|=\sqrt{z\overline z} }. $$

The Conjugate Has the Same Modulus

Since reflection across the real axis does not change distance from the origin:

$$ \boxed{ |\overline z|=|z| }. $$

Conjugate of a Sum

Conjugation distributes over addition:

$$ \boxed{ \overline{z+w} = \overline z+\overline w }. $$

Conjugate of a Difference

$$ \boxed{ \overline{z-w} = \overline z-\overline w }. $$

Conjugate of a Product

Conjugation also distributes over multiplication:

$$ \boxed{ \overline{zw} = \overline z\,\overline w }. $$

Conjugate of a Quotient

For $w\ne0$:

$$ \boxed{ \overline{\left(\frac zw\right)} = \frac{\overline z}{\overline w} }. $$

Conjugate of an Integer Power

For every nonnegative integer $n$:

$$ \boxed{ \overline{z^n} = (\overline z)^n }. $$

Reciprocal of a Complex Number

If $z\ne0$, then:

$$ \frac1z = \frac{\overline z}{z\overline z}. $$

Since:

$$ z\overline z=|z|^2, $$

we obtain:

$$ \boxed{ \frac1z = \frac{\overline z}{|z|^2} }. $$

Example: Reciprocal

Consider:

$$ z=3+4i. $$

Then:

$$ \overline z=3-4i $$

and:

$$ |z|^2=3^2+4^2=25. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \frac1{3+4i} = \frac{3-4i}{25} }. $$

Rationalizing a Complex Denominator

To simplify a quotient with a complex denominator, multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the denominator.

For example:

$$ \frac{1}{2+3i} \cdot \frac{2-3i}{2-3i} = \frac{2-3i}{(2+3i)(2-3i)}. $$

Since:

$$ (2+3i)(2-3i)=4+9=13, $$

we obtain:

$$ \boxed{ \frac1{2+3i} = \frac{2-3i}{13} }. $$

Why the Denominator Becomes Real

Multiplying a complex number by its conjugate removes the imaginary cross terms:

$$ (a+bi)(a-bi) = a^2+b^2. $$

The result is always a nonnegative real number.

Real-Number Test Using Conjugation

A complex number is real exactly when it equals its own conjugate:

$$ \boxed{ z\in\mathbb R \iff z=\overline z }. $$

If:

$$ a+bi=a-bi, $$

then:

$$ 2bi=0, $$

which forces $b=0$.

Pure Imaginary Test

A complex number is purely imaginary, including zero under a broad convention, when:

$$ \boxed{ \overline z=-z }. $$

For a nonzero pure imaginary number $z=bi$, conjugation simply changes its sign.

Geometric Interpretation

In the complex plane, $z=a+bi$ corresponds to the point:

$$ (a,b). $$

Its conjugate corresponds to:

$$ (a,-b). $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ z\mapsto\overline z \text{ is reflection across the real axis} }. $$

Conjugation in Polar Form

If:

$$ z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta), $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \overline z = r(\cos\theta-i\sin\theta) }. $$

Using trigonometric symmetry:

$$ \cos(-\theta)=\cos\theta, \qquad \sin(-\theta)=-\sin\theta, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ \overline z = r\bigl(\cos(-\theta)+i\sin(-\theta)\bigr) }. $$

Complex conjugation preserves the modulus and reverses the argument.

Exponential Form

If:

$$ z=re^{i\theta}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \overline z=re^{-i\theta} }. $$

Argument Relationship

Subject to the chosen branch convention:

$$ \boxed{ \arg(\overline z) \equiv -\arg(z) \pmod{2\pi} }. $$

Conjugate Roots of Real Polynomials

If a polynomial has real coefficients and a nonreal complex number $z$ is a root, then its complex conjugate $\overline z$ is also a root.

$$ \boxed{ p(z)=0, \quad p\in\mathbb R[x] \Rightarrow p(\overline z)=0 }. $$

This is often called the Complex Conjugate Root Theorem.

Example: Conjugate Roots

Suppose a real-coefficient polynomial has the root:

$$ 2+5i. $$

Then it must also have the root:

$$ \boxed{ 2-5i }. $$

Why Real Coefficients Matter

The conjugate-root pairing is guaranteed because conjugation leaves real coefficients unchanged.

If:

$$ p(x)=a_0+a_1x+\cdots+a_nx^n, \qquad a_k\in\mathbb R, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \overline{p(z)} = p(\overline z) }. $$

Complex Coefficients Do Not Guarantee Conjugate Roots

A polynomial with nonreal coefficients need not contain the conjugate of each nonreal root.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \text{nonreal root} \Rightarrow \text{conjugate root} } $$

requires the real-coefficient condition.

Quadratic Factors from a Conjugate Pair

If:

$$ a+bi \qquad\text{and}\qquad a-bi $$

are roots, then the corresponding factor pair is:

$$ (x-(a+bi))(x-(a-bi)). $$

Multiplying gives:

$$ \boxed{ x^2-2ax+(a^2+b^2) }. $$

The resulting quadratic has real coefficients.

Conjugation and Equations

If two complex numbers are equal:

$$ z=w, $$

then their conjugates are equal:

$$ \boxed{ \overline z=\overline w }. $$

Conjugation Preserves Addition and Multiplication

Algebraically, complex conjugation respects both field operations:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \overline{z+w} &= \overline z+\overline w,\\ \overline{zw} &= \overline z\,\overline w. \end{aligned} } $$

Together with $\overline{\overline z}=z$, this makes conjugation a fundamental symmetry of the complex number field.

Conjugation Is Not Complex-Linear

Although conjugation respects addition, it does not satisfy ordinary complex linearity.

For a complex scalar $\lambda$:

$$ \boxed{ \overline{\lambda z} = \overline\lambda\,\overline z } $$

rather than generally:

$$ \lambda\overline z. $$

Thus conjugation is conjugate-linear with respect to scalar multiplication.

Complex Conjugate of a Matrix

For a complex matrix:

$$ A=(a_{ij}), $$

its entrywise complex conjugate is:

$$ \boxed{ \overline A = (\overline{a_{ij}}) }. $$

Conjugate Transpose

The conjugate transpose of a complex matrix combines entrywise conjugation with transposition:

$$ \boxed{ A^* = \overline A^{\,T} }. $$

Common alternative notation includes:

$$ A^\dagger \qquad\text{or}\qquad A^H. $$

The conjugate transpose should not be confused with entrywise conjugation alone.

Hermitian Matrices

A complex square matrix is Hermitian when:

$$ \boxed{ A=A^* }. $$

This generalizes the idea of a real symmetric matrix.

Inner Products

Complex inner products use conjugation so that the squared length of a vector is real and nonnegative.

One common convention is:

$$ \boxed{ \langle x,y\rangle = \sum_k \overline{x_k}y_k }. $$

Other fields may choose the opposite argument as the conjugate-linear one, so local convention matters.

Conjugate Symmetry

A complex inner product satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ \langle x,y\rangle = \overline{\langle y,x\rangle} }. $$

This replaces ordinary real symmetry.

Norm from an Inner Product

For a complex vector $x$:

$$ \boxed{ \|x\|^2 = \langle x,x\rangle = \sum_k|x_k|^2 }. $$

Complex conjugation is what converts each product into a nonnegative real magnitude squared.

Conjugate versus Additive Inverse

The complex conjugate of:

$$ z=a+bi $$

is:

$$ \overline z=a-bi, $$

while the additive inverse is:

$$ -z=-a-bi. $$

These are generally different.

$$ \boxed{ \overline z\ne-z \quad\text{in general} }. $$

Conjugate versus Reciprocal

The reciprocal of a nonzero complex number is:

$$ \frac1z = \frac{\overline z}{|z|^2}. $$

Thus the conjugate and reciprocal coincide only when:

$$ |z|=1. $$

In that special case:

$$ \boxed{ z^{-1}=\overline z \qquad \text{when }|z|=1 }. $$

Unit Circle Connection

If:

$$ z=e^{i\theta}, $$

then $|z|=1$, so:

$$ \boxed{ \overline z = e^{-i\theta} = z^{-1} }. $$

Conjugate versus Negative Imaginary Part

Conjugation changes only the imaginary component:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Re}(\overline z) = \operatorname{Re}(z) } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Im}(\overline z) = -\operatorname{Im}(z) }. $$

Common Misconception: Conjugation Changes Both Signs

Changing both signs produces the additive inverse, not the conjugate.

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} z&=a+bi,\\ \overline z&=a-bi,\\ -z&=-a-bi. \end{aligned} } $$

Common Misconception: The Conjugate Is Always Different

Every real number equals its own conjugate.

$$ \boxed{ z=\overline z \iff z\in\mathbb R }. $$

Common Misconception: Conjugates Always Occur as Polynomial Roots

Conjugate pairing of nonreal roots is guaranteed for polynomials with real coefficients. It is not a universal property of all complex-coefficient polynomials.

Common Misconception: Conjugation Is the Same as Absolute Value

The conjugate is another complex number:

$$ \overline z=a-bi, $$

while the modulus is a nonnegative real number:

$$ |z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}. $$

Common Misconception: A Bar Always Means Complex Conjugation

A bar has several mathematical uses. Depending on context, it may denote complex conjugation, set closure, an average, repeating decimals, a line segment, or another defined operation.

Therefore notation must be interpreted from context.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\overline z$ "z bar" or "the complex conjugate of z."
$z=a+bi$ "z equals a plus b i."
$\overline z=a-bi$ "z bar equals a minus b i."
$z\overline z=|z|^2$ "z times z bar equals the modulus of z squared."
$\overline{z+w}=\overline z+\overline w$ "The conjugate of z plus w equals z bar plus w bar."
$\overline{zw}=\overline z\,\overline w$ "The conjugate of z w equals z bar times w bar."
$\overline{\overline z}=z$ "The conjugate of the conjugate of z is z."
$A^*=\overline A^{\,T}$ "A star is the conjugate transpose of A."

In spoken mathematics, $\overline z$ is very commonly read as z bar. When the meaning may be unclear, the complex conjugate of z is more explicit.

The phrase conjugate pair commonly refers to numbers of the form $a+bi$ and $a-bi$. In polynomial contexts, complex conjugate roots refers specifically to a nonreal root and its conjugate when the coefficient conditions guarantee the pair.

Defense Notation Advice: Define bar notation when several uses of an overline are possible. Distinguish $\overline z$ from $|z|$, $-z$, and $1/z$. When working with matrices, distinguish entrywise conjugation $\overline A$ from conjugate transpose $A^*$. When invoking the conjugate-root theorem, explicitly state that the polynomial has real coefficients.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: The complex conjugate reverses the sign of the imaginary part while preserving the real part.
  • Standard Form: $$ \boxed{ z=a+bi \Rightarrow \overline z=a-bi }. $$
  • Geometric Meaning: Reflection across the real axis in the complex plane.
  • Coordinate Form: $$ \boxed{ (a,b)\mapsto(a,-b) }. $$
  • Double Conjugation: $$ \boxed{ \overline{\overline z}=z }. $$
  • Real Number Criterion: $$ \boxed{ z=\overline z \iff z\in\mathbb R }. $$
  • Real Part: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Re}(z) = \frac{z+\overline z}{2} }. $$
  • Imaginary Part: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Im}(z) = \frac{z-\overline z}{2i} }. $$
  • Product with Conjugate: $$ \boxed{ z\overline z=|z|^2 }. $$
  • Equal Modulus: $$ \boxed{ |\overline z|=|z| }. $$
  • Addition: $$ \boxed{ \overline{z+w} = \overline z+\overline w }. $$
  • Subtraction: $$ \boxed{ \overline{z-w} = \overline z-\overline w }. $$
  • Multiplication: $$ \boxed{ \overline{zw} = \overline z\,\overline w }. $$
  • Division: $$ \boxed{ \overline{z/w} = \frac{\overline z}{\overline w} } $$ for $w\ne0$.
  • Reciprocal: $$ \boxed{ \frac1z = \frac{\overline z}{|z|^2} } $$ for $z\ne0$.
  • Denominator Rationalization: Multiplication by a denominator's conjugate converts the denominator to a real magnitude squared.
  • Polar Form: $$ \boxed{ z=re^{i\theta} \Rightarrow \overline z=re^{-i\theta} }. $$
  • Argument: Conjugation reverses the argument modulo $2\pi$.
  • Unit Circle: If $|z|=1$, then: $$ \boxed{ \overline z=z^{-1} }. $$
  • Real Polynomial Roots: Nonreal roots of real-coefficient polynomials occur in conjugate pairs.
  • Conjugate Root Form: $$ \boxed{ a+bi \leftrightarrow a-bi }. $$
  • Real Quadratic Factor: $$ \boxed{ (x-(a+bi))(x-(a-bi)) = x^2-2ax+a^2+b^2 }. $$
  • Matrix Conjugation: $\overline A$ means entrywise complex conjugation.
  • Conjugate Transpose: $$ \boxed{ A^*=\overline A^{\,T} }. $$
  • Hermitian Matrix: A complex square matrix satisfying $A=A^*$.
  • Complex Inner Products: Conjugation produces conjugate symmetry and real nonnegative squared norms.
  • Additive-Inverse Contrast: $\overline z$ changes only the imaginary sign; $-z$ changes both signs.
  • Reciprocal Contrast: $\overline z$ is generally not $1/z$ unless $|z|=1$.
  • Modulus Contrast: $\overline z$ is a complex number; $|z|$ is a nonnegative real number.
  • Common Error: Do not negate the real part when forming the conjugate.
  • Common Error: Do not assume a complex conjugate must differ from the original number; real numbers equal their own conjugates.
  • Common Error: Do not invoke conjugate-root pairing without checking that the polynomial coefficients are real.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse entrywise matrix conjugation with the conjugate transpose.
  • Terminological Contrast: The complex conjugate of $a+bi$ is $a-bi$. The additive inverse is $-a-bi$. The modulus is $\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$. The reciprocal is $\overline z/|z|^2$ for nonzero $z$. A conjugate pair consists of $a+bi$ and $a-bi$. The conjugate transpose of a matrix combines entrywise conjugation with transposition.
