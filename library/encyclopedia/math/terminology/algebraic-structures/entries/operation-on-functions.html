Operations on Functions

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

Operations on functions are procedures that combine, transform, or otherwise act on functions to produce new functions. Familiar arithmetic operations can be extended from numbers to function values, and functions can also be combined through the specifically functional operation of composition.

If $f$ and $g$ are functions, common operations include:

$$ \boxed{ f+g,\qquad f-g,\qquad fg,\qquad \frac{f}{g},\qquad f\circ g }. $$

The word operation comes from Latin roots associated with working, performing, or carrying out an action. In mathematics, an operation is a defined rule that takes mathematical objects as inputs and returns another mathematical object.

The word function developed from the idea of one quantity depending on another. Consequently, an operation on functions treats whole dependency rules as mathematical objects that can themselves be combined.

This is an important conceptual shift. In:

$$ (f+g)(x), $$

the symbols $f$ and $g$ are not merely numbers. They represent complete functions. The operation defines a new function whose output at each permitted input $x$ is determined from the corresponding outputs of $f$ and $g$.

Composition differs from ordinary arithmetic operations because it connects the output of one function to the input of another:

$$ \boxed{ (f\circ g)(x)=f(g(x)) }. $$

Thus operations on functions include both pointwise arithmetic and structural nesting.

Operational Nuance

Pointwise Operations

Addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division of functions are ordinarily defined pointwise. This means the operation is performed on the outputs corresponding to the same input.

Function Addition

$$ \boxed{ (f+g)(x) = f(x)+g(x) }. $$

Example

Let:

$$ f(x)=x^2, \qquad g(x)=3x+1. $$

Then:

$$ (f+g)(x) = x^2+3x+1. $$

Function Subtraction

$$ \boxed{ (f-g)(x) = f(x)-g(x) }. $$

Using the same functions:

$$ (f-g)(x) = x^2-3x-1. $$

Function Multiplication

$$ \boxed{ (fg)(x) = f(x)g(x) }. $$

Therefore:

$$ (fg)(x) = x^2(3x+1) = 3x^3+x^2. $$

Function Division

$$ \boxed{ \left( \frac{f}{g} \right)(x) = \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} } $$

provided:

$$ \boxed{ g(x)\neq0 }. $$

Example

$$ \left( \frac{f}{g} \right)(x) = \frac{x^2}{3x+1}, \qquad x\neq-\frac{1}{3}. $$

Domain of a Sum

The natural domain of $f+g$ consists of inputs for which both functions are defined:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f+g) = \operatorname{Dom}(f) \cap \operatorname{Dom}(g) }. $$

Domain of a Difference

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f-g) = \operatorname{Dom}(f) \cap \operatorname{Dom}(g) }. $$

Domain of a Product

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(fg) = \operatorname{Dom}(f) \cap \operatorname{Dom}(g) }. $$

Domain of a Quotient

Division introduces an additional restriction:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom} \left( \frac{f}{g} \right) = \{x\in \operatorname{Dom}(f) \cap \operatorname{Dom}(g): g(x)\neq0\} }. $$

Scalar Multiplication

A function may be multiplied by a scalar $c$:

$$ \boxed{ (cf)(x) = c\,f(x) }. $$

Example

If:

$$ f(x)=x^2+1, $$

then:

$$ (3f)(x) = 3x^2+3. $$

Negation of a Function

Multiplication by $-1$ produces the additive inverse:

$$ \boxed{ (-f)(x) = -f(x) }. $$

Zero Function

The additive identity is the zero function:

$$ \boxed{ 0(x)=0 }. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ f+0=f }. $$

Additive Inverse Identity

$$ \boxed{ f+(-f)=0 }. $$

Constant-One Function

Under pointwise multiplication, the multiplicative identity is the constant function:

$$ \boxed{ 1(x)=1 }. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ f\cdot1=f }. $$

Reciprocal Function

If $f(x)\neq0$ throughout the relevant domain, its pointwise reciprocal is:

$$ \boxed{ \left( \frac{1}{f} \right)(x) = \frac{1}{f(x)} }. $$

Reciprocal vs. Inverse Function

The reciprocal:

$$ \frac{1}{f(x)} $$

is not generally the same as the inverse function:

$$ f^{-1}(x). $$
$$ \boxed{ f^{-1} \neq \frac{1}{f} \text{ in general} }. $$

Composition

Function composition inserts the output of one function into another:

$$ \boxed{ (f\circ g)(x) = f(g(x)) }. $$

Read from Right to Left

In:

$$ f\circ g, $$

$g$ acts first, followed by $f$.

$$ \boxed{ x \xrightarrow{g} g(x) \xrightarrow{f} f(g(x)) }. $$

Composition Example

Let:

$$ f(x)=x^2+1, \qquad g(x)=3x. $$

Then:

$$ (f\circ g)(x) = f(3x) = (3x)^2+1 = \boxed{ 9x^2+1 }. $$

Reverse Composition

In the opposite order:

$$ (g\circ f)(x) = g(x^2+1) = \boxed{ 3x^2+3 }. $$

Composition Is Generally Not Commutative

$$ \boxed{ f\circ g \neq g\circ f \text{ in general} }. $$

Domain of a Composition

An input $x$ belongs to the domain of $f\circ g$ only if $g(x)$ is defined and its output lies in the domain of $f$.

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f\circ g) = \{x\in\operatorname{Dom}(g): g(x)\in\operatorname{Dom}(f)\} }. $$

Composition Domain Example

Let:

$$ f(x)=\sqrt{x}, \qquad g(x)=x-4. $$

Then:

$$ (f\circ g)(x) = \sqrt{x-4}. $$

The real-domain condition is:

$$ x-4\geq0, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f\circ g) = [4,\infty) }. $$

Identity Function under Composition

The identity function:

$$ \operatorname{id}(x)=x $$

satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ f\circ\operatorname{id} = \operatorname{id}\circ f = f }. $$

Associativity of Composition

Whenever the compositions are defined:

$$ \boxed{ f\circ(g\circ h) = (f\circ g)\circ h }. $$

Why Composition Is Associative

Both sides evaluate an input as:

$$ f(g(h(x))). $$

Inverse Function under Composition

If $f$ is invertible:

$$ \boxed{ f^{-1}\circ f = \operatorname{id} } $$

on the domain of $f$, and:

$$ \boxed{ f\circ f^{-1} = \operatorname{id} } $$

on the range of $f$.

Inverse of a Composition

If $f$ and $g$ are appropriately invertible:

$$ \boxed{ (f\circ g)^{-1} = g^{-1}\circ f^{-1} }. $$

The order reverses.

Function Powers: Ambiguous Notation

The notation:

$$ f^2 $$

can be ambiguous across fields.

It may mean the pointwise square:

$$ \boxed{ [f(x)]^2 } $$

or, in iterative contexts, it may be informally used for:

$$ f\circ f. $$

Safer Iteration Notation

To distinguish repeated composition from pointwise powers, write:

$$ \boxed{ f^{\circ n} } $$

or explicitly state that $f^n$ denotes the $n$th iterate.

Pointwise Square

$$ \boxed{ (f^2)(x) = [f(x)]^2 } $$

when the notation is defined pointwise.

Iterated Composition

$$ \boxed{ f^{\circ2}(x) = f(f(x)) }. $$

These Need Not Agree

If:

$$ f(x)=x+1, $$

then:

$$ [f(x)]^2 = (x+1)^2, $$

while:

$$ f(f(x)) = x+2. $$

Function Addition Is Commutative

For scalar-valued functions:

$$ \boxed{ f+g=g+f }. $$

Function Multiplication Is Commutative for Scalar Outputs

If the outputs lie in a commutative number system:

$$ \boxed{ fg=gf }. $$

Noncommutative Output Caveat

If functions take values in a noncommutative algebra, such as matrices, pointwise multiplication need not commute:

$$ f(x)g(x) \neq g(x)f(x) $$

in general.

Associativity of Function Addition

$$ \boxed{ (f+g)+h = f+(g+h) }. $$

Associativity of Pointwise Multiplication

$$ \boxed{ (fg)h = f(gh) } $$

when output multiplication is associative.

Distributive Law

$$ \boxed{ f(g+h) = fg+fh } $$

when juxtaposition means pointwise multiplication.

Potential Notational Ambiguity

The expression:

$$ f(g+h) $$

can be ambiguous if parentheses are interpreted as function evaluation instead of multiplication. In formal work, pointwise multiplication may be written more explicitly as:

$$ \boxed{ f\cdot(g+h) = f\cdot g+f\cdot h }. $$

Scalar Distributivity

$$ \boxed{ c(f+g) = cf+cg }. $$

Function Space Viewpoint

Collections of functions can themselves form algebraic structures.

For example, real-valued functions on a fixed domain $D$ can be added and scaled pointwise:

$$ (f+g)(x)=f(x)+g(x), \qquad (cf)(x)=cf(x). $$

Under these operations they form a vector space.

Function Algebra

If pointwise multiplication is included, many function spaces form an algebra over the underlying scalar field.

Closure Depends on the Function Class

Whether an operation stays within a particular family depends on the family.

Polynomial Closure under Addition

$$ \boxed{ \text{polynomial} + \text{polynomial} = \text{polynomial} }. $$

Polynomial Closure under Multiplication

$$ \boxed{ \text{polynomial} \cdot \text{polynomial} = \text{polynomial} }. $$

Polynomial Division Is Not Closed

The quotient of two polynomials need not be a polynomial:

$$ \frac{x}{x+1}. $$

Continuous Functions

Sums, differences, products, and scalar multiples of continuous functions are continuous wherever defined.

Continuous Quotients

If $f$ and $g$ are continuous and $g(x)\neq0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{f}{g} \text{ is continuous} }. $$

Composition of Continuous Functions

$$ \boxed{ f,g\text{ continuous} \Longrightarrow f\circ g\text{ continuous} } $$

where the composition is defined.

Differentiable Functions

Differentiability also interacts systematically with function operations.

Derivative of a Sum

$$ \boxed{ (f+g)' = f'+g' }. $$

Derivative of a Difference

$$ \boxed{ (f-g)' = f'-g' }. $$

Derivative of a Product

$$ \boxed{ (fg)' = f'g+fg' }. $$

Derivative of a Quotient

$$ \boxed{ \left( \frac{f}{g} \right)' = \frac{f'g-fg'}{g^2} } $$

where $g\neq0$.

Derivative of a Composition

$$ \boxed{ (f\circ g)'(x) = f'(g(x))g'(x) }. $$

This is the Chain Rule.

Integration and Addition

Integration is linear:

$$ \boxed{ \int (af+bg) = a\int f + b\int g } $$

with the usual interpretation of indefinite or definite integrals.

Function Operations and Graphs

Function operations alter graphs in different ways. Pointwise addition adds vertical coordinates, multiplication multiplies vertical coordinates, and composition changes the input-output pipeline.

Sum Graph

At each input $x$:

$$ y_{f+g} = y_f+y_g. $$

Product Graph

Zeros of either factor become zeros of the product:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=0 \text{ or } g(x)=0 \Longrightarrow (fg)(x)=0 }. $$

Quotient Graph

Zeros of the denominator are excluded from the quotient domain, even if algebraic simplification later removes a common factor.

Example of a Lost Domain Point

Consider:

$$ f(x)=x^2-1, \qquad g(x)=x-1. $$

Then:

$$ \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{(x-1)(x+1)}{x-1} = x+1 $$

only for:

$$ \boxed{ x\neq1 }. $$

Simplification Does Not Restore Excluded Inputs

The simplified formula may be defined at an input that the original quotient was not. The original function operation retains its original domain restriction.

Composition vs. Multiplication

These are completely different operations:

$$ \boxed{ (fg)(x) = f(x)g(x) } $$

while:

$$ \boxed{ (f\circ g)(x) = f(g(x)) }. $$

Example

If:

$$ f(x)=x+1, \qquad g(x)=x^2, $$

then:

$$ (fg)(x) = x^3+x^2, $$

but:

$$ (f\circ g)(x) = x^2+1. $$

Composition and Transformations

Many graph transformations can be understood through operations involving composition.

Horizontal Translation

$$ \boxed{ y=f(x-h) } $$

shifts the graph horizontally by $h$ units to the right.

Vertical Translation

$$ \boxed{ y=f(x)+k } $$

adds the constant function $k$ to $f$.

Vertical Scaling

$$ \boxed{ y=af(x) } $$

is scalar multiplication of the function.

Horizontal Scaling

$$ \boxed{ y=f(bx) } $$

arises from composing $f$ with the scaling function $g(x)=bx$.

Reflection across the x-Axis

$$ \boxed{ y=-f(x) }. $$

Reflection across the y-Axis

$$ \boxed{ y=f(-x) }. $$

Absolute Value of a Function

$$ \boxed{ |f|(x) = |f(x)| }. $$

This converts negative outputs to nonnegative outputs.

Maximum and Minimum Operations

Functions may also be combined pointwise through operations such as:

$$ \boxed{ h(x) = \max\{f(x),g(x)\} } $$

or:

$$ \boxed{ h(x) = \min\{f(x),g(x)\} }. $$

These are valid function operations even though they are not among the four basic arithmetic operations.

Pointwise Operations in General

If $\star$ is an operation defined on output values, it can often induce an operation on functions:

$$ \boxed{ (f\star g)(x) = f(x)\star g(x) }. $$

Composition Is Not Pointwise

Composition is structurally different because:

$$ f(g(x)) $$

uses one function's output as another function's input.

Common Error: Adding Inputs Instead of Outputs

Incorrect:

$$ (f+g)(x) = f(x+g). $$

Correct:

$$ \boxed{ (f+g)(x) = f(x)+g(x) }. $$

Common Error: Confusing Product with Composition

The notation $fg$ ordinarily means pointwise multiplication, while $f\circ g$ means composition.

Common Error: Assuming Composition Is Commutative

In general:

$$ \boxed{ f\circ g \neq g\circ f }. $$

Common Error: Performing Composition Left to Right

In:

$$ f\circ g, $$

$g$ acts first.

Common Error: Ignoring Domain Intersections

Even if formulas can be algebraically combined, the resulting function must only use inputs where the required original functions are defined.

Common Error: Forgetting Quotient Restrictions

A quotient function excludes all points where the denominator function equals zero.

Common Error: Restoring a Cancelled Domain Point

Cancelling a common factor simplifies a formula but does not restore a point excluded from the original quotient.

Common Error: Confusing Reciprocal and Inverse

The reciprocal operation:

$$ \frac{1}{f(x)} $$

differs from the inverse-function operation:

$$ f^{-1}(x). $$

Common Error: Treating $f^2$ as Unambiguous

Context may distinguish pointwise squaring from repeated composition. Explicit notation prevents confusion.

Common Error: Assuming Every Function Has a Reciprocal Function

The pointwise reciprocal requires:

$$ f(x)\neq0 $$

on the intended domain.

Common Error: Assuming Every Function Has an Inverse Function

An inverse function requires the original function to be one-to-one on the relevant domain.

Common Error: Forgetting Composition Domain Conditions

It is not enough for $x$ to belong to the domain of $g$. The output $g(x)$ must also belong to the domain of $f$.

Common Error: Treating $(f+g)(x)$ as $f(x+g(x))$

Function addition and function composition are different operations.

Common Error: Treating $(fg)(x)$ as $f(g(x))$

Juxtaposition $fg$ usually means multiplication, not composition.

Common Error: Assuming Function Families Are Closed under Every Operation

Polynomials are closed under addition and multiplication but not under unrestricted division.

Common Error: Assuming Arithmetic Laws Automatically Apply to Composition

Composition is associative but generally not commutative, so its algebra differs from ordinary scalar addition and multiplication.

Common Misconception: Operations on functions are not performed on function names as though $f$ and $g$ were ordinary numbers without context. Pointwise operations combine values at the same input, while composition sends an input through one function and then another. Every operation also carries domain requirements, and those restrictions are part of the resulting function.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$(f+g)(x)=f(x)+g(x)$ "F plus g evaluated at x equals f of x plus g of x."
$(f-g)(x)=f(x)-g(x)$ "F minus g at x equals f of x minus g of x."
$(fg)(x)=f(x)g(x)$ "The product function f g at x equals f of x times g of x."
$\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)(x)=\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}$ "The quotient function f over g at x equals f of x divided by g of x."
$(f\circ g)(x)=f(g(x))$ "F composed with g at x equals f of g of x."
$(cf)(x)=cf(x)$ "The scalar multiple c f at x equals c times f of x."
$f^{-1}\circ f=\operatorname{id}$ "The inverse composed with the original function gives the identity on the appropriate domain."

Parentheses around a combined function are operationally important. The notation:

$$ (f+g)(x) $$

means that the new function $f+g$ is being evaluated at $x$.

Similarly:

$$ (f\circ g)(x) $$

should be read as "f composed with g, evaluated at x," not "f times g."

The expression:

$$ fg $$

usually means pointwise multiplication, whereas composition should use:

$$ \boxed{ f\circ g }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Write composition explicitly with $\circ$, especially when multiplication is also present. Preserve domain restrictions when simplifying quotient functions. Distinguish $1/f$ from $f^{-1}$, and distinguish pointwise powers such as $[f(x)]^2$ from iteration such as $f^{\circ2}(x)=f(f(x))$. When reporting a combined function, give both its formula and any restrictions needed for its domain.

  • Negation: $(-f)(x)=-f(x)$.
  • Reciprocal: $(1/f)(x)=1/f(x)$ where $f(x)\neq0$.
  • Composition: $(f\circ g)(x)=f(g(x))$.
  • Pointwise Operation: Arithmetic performed on function outputs at the same input.
  • Composition Distinction: Output of one function becomes input of another.
  • Sum Domain: Intersection of the original domains.
  • Difference Domain: Intersection of the original domains.
  • Product Domain: Intersection of the original domains.
  • Quotient Domain: Domain intersection with denominator zeros removed.
  • Composition Domain: Inputs where $g(x)$ is defined and belongs to the domain of $f$.
  • Additive Identity: Zero function.
  • Additive Inverse: $-f$.
  • Multiplicative Identity: Constant-one function under pointwise multiplication.
  • Identity under Composition: $\operatorname{id}(x)=x$.
  • Composition Associativity: $f\circ(g\circ h)=(f\circ g)\circ h$.
  • Composition Commutativity: Generally fails.
  • Pointwise Addition: Commutative for ordinary scalar-valued functions.
  • Pointwise Multiplication: Commutative for real- or complex-valued functions.
  • Matrix-Valued Caveat: Pointwise multiplication need not commute.
  • Distributivity: Pointwise multiplication distributes over pointwise addition.
  • Scalar Distributivity: $c(f+g)=cf+cg$.
  • Vector-Space Structure: Fixed-domain scalar-valued functions support pointwise addition and scalar multiplication.
  • Function Algebra: Many function spaces also support pointwise multiplication.
  • Polynomial Addition: Closed within polynomials.
  • Polynomial Multiplication: Closed within polynomials.
  • Polynomial Division: Not generally closed within polynomials.
  • Continuity: Preserved under sums, differences, products, and scalar multiples.
  • Continuous Quotient: Preserved where denominator is nonzero.
  • Continuous Composition: Composition of continuous functions is continuous where defined.
  • Differentiation: Has corresponding sum, difference, product, quotient, and chain rules.
  • Derivative Sum Rule: $(f+g)'=f'+g'$.
  • Derivative Product Rule: $(fg)'=f'g+fg'$.

  • Graph Multiplication: Multiplies vertical coordinates pointwise.

  • Quotient Restrictions: Denominator zeros are excluded.
  • Cancellation Caveat: Algebraic cancellation does not restore originally excluded inputs.
  • Vertical Translation: Addition of a constant function.
  • Vertical Scaling: Scalar multiplication.
  • Horizontal Translation: Obtained through input composition $f(x-h)$.
  • Horizontal Scaling: Obtained through composition $f(bx)$.
  • x-Axis Reflection: $-f(x)$.
  • y-Axis Reflection: $f(-x)$.
  • Absolute Value Operation: $|f|(x)=|f(x)|$.
  • Maximum Operation: Pointwise maximum of two functions.
  • Minimum Operation: Pointwise minimum of two functions.
  • Inverse Function: Reverses a suitably one-to-one function under composition.
  • Inverse Composition: $f^{-1}\circ f$ and $f\circ f^{-1}$ give identity functions on appropriate sets.
  • Inverse of Composition: $(f\circ g)^{-1}=g^{-1}\circ f^{-1}$ when defined.
  • Reciprocal vs. Inverse: $1/f$ and $f^{-1}$ are different operations.
  • Pointwise Power: $[f(x)]^n$.
  • Iteration: Repeated composition such as $f^{\circ n}$.
  • Notation Caveat: $f^n$ can be ambiguous between powers and iterates.

  • Linear Algebra: Function spaces support vector-space operations.

  • Computer Science: Composition models pipelines in which one procedure's output becomes another's input.

  • Core Interpretation: Operations on functions treat functions themselves as mathematical objects. Pointwise arithmetic combines outputs at the same input: $$ \boxed{ (f+g)(x)=f(x)+g(x) } $$ $$ \boxed{ (fg)(x)=f(x)g(x) } $$ $$ \boxed{ \left( \frac{f}{g} \right)(x) = \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} } $$ while composition creates an input-output chain: $$ \boxed{ (f\circ g)(x) = f(g(x)) }. $$ The resulting formula alone is not the whole function: each operation also carries domain conditions. Function operations therefore combine algebraic rules with dependency structure, making them central to algebra, analysis, transformations, modeling, and higher mathematics.
