Inequality

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

An inequality is a mathematical statement comparing two quantities by asserting that one is less than, greater than, less than or equal to, or greater than or equal to the other.

$$ \boxed{ a<b,\qquad a>b,\qquad a\leq b,\qquad a\geq b }. $$

The word inequality combines the idea of equality with a prefix meaning not or unequal. In ordinary mathematical language, however, inequality is broader than simply saying two quantities are unequal. It usually expresses an order relation.

Thus:

$$ a\neq b $$

says only that two values are not equal, while:

$$ a<b $$

additionally identifies which one is smaller.

In algebra, an inequality involving variables describes all values for which the comparison is true.

$$ \boxed{ x+3>7 } $$

has the solution set:

$$ \boxed{ x>4 }. $$

Operational Nuance

Comparison Symbols

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} a<b &\quad \text{means }a\text{ is less than }b,\\ a>b &\quad \text{means }a\text{ is greater than }b,\\ a\leq b &\quad \text{means }a\text{ is less than or equal to }b,\\ a\geq b &\quad \text{means }a\text{ is greater than or equal to }b. \end{aligned} } $$

Strict Inequality

The symbols:

$$ \boxed{ <,\qquad > } $$

express strict inequalities. Equality is excluded.

Non-Strict Inequality

The symbols:

$$ \boxed{ \leq,\qquad \geq } $$

express non-strict inequalities. Equality is allowed.

Example of a Strict Inequality

$$ \boxed{ x<5 }. $$

The value $5$ itself is excluded.

Example of a Non-Strict Inequality

$$ \boxed{ x\leq5 }. $$

The value $5$ is included.

Inequality as a Proposition

An inequality with specific numerical values has a truth value.

$$ \boxed{ 3<7 } $$

is true, while:

$$ \boxed{ 9\leq4 } $$

is false.

Inequality with Variables

An inequality such as:

$$ 2x-1<7 $$

is true for some values of $x$ and false for others.

Solution Set

Solving an inequality means finding all values for which it is true.

$$ 2x-1<7 $$

gives:

$$ 2x<8, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ x<4 }. $$

Set-Builder Notation

$$ \boxed{ \{x\in\mathbb{R}:x<4\} }. $$

Interval Notation

$$ \boxed{ (-\infty,4) }. $$

Graphical Notation

On a number line, a strict boundary is usually drawn with an open point, while a non-strict boundary is usually drawn with a filled point.

Equivalent Inequalities

Two inequalities are equivalent when they have the same solution set.

$$ \boxed{ x+3>7 \iff x>4 }. $$

Adding the Same Quantity

Adding the same real quantity to both sides preserves order:

$$ \boxed{ a<b \Longrightarrow a+c<b+c }. $$

Subtracting the Same Quantity

$$ \boxed{ a<b \Longrightarrow a-c<b-c }. $$

Multiplying by a Positive Quantity

If $c>0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ a<b \Longrightarrow ac<bc }. $$

Dividing by a Positive Quantity

If $c>0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ a<b \Longrightarrow \frac{a}{c} < \frac{b}{c} }. $$

Multiplying by a Negative Quantity

Multiplying by a negative number reverses the order:

$$ \boxed{ a<b,\ c<0 \Longrightarrow ac>bc }. $$

Dividing by a Negative Quantity

Likewise:

$$ \boxed{ a<b,\ c<0 \Longrightarrow \frac{a}{c} > \frac{b}{c} }. $$

Why the Inequality Reverses

Multiplication by $-1$ reflects the number line across zero. Reflection reverses left and right, so it reverses order.

$$ 2<5 $$

becomes:

$$ \boxed{ -2>-5 }. $$

Example with a Negative Coefficient

Solve:

$$ -3x>12. $$

Dividing by $-3$ reverses the comparison:

$$ \boxed{ x<-4 }. $$

Multiplying by an Unknown-Sign Expression

One cannot multiply or divide an inequality by a variable expression without considering its sign.

If the factor might be positive, negative, or zero, separate cases may be required.

Transitivity

The usual order relation is transitive:

$$ \boxed{ a<b \text{ and } b<c \Longrightarrow a<c }. $$

Trichotomy

For real numbers $a$ and $b$, exactly one of the following is true:

$$ \boxed{ a<b, \qquad a=b, \qquad a>b }. $$

Order Reversal under Negation

$$ \boxed{ a<b \iff -a>-b }. $$

Reciprocal Inequalities

For positive $a$ and $b$:

$$ \boxed{ 0<a<b \Longrightarrow \frac{1}{a}>\frac{1}{b} }. $$

Reciprocal behavior must be handled carefully when signs or zero are involved.

Squaring Inequalities

Squaring is not globally order-preserving over all real numbers.

For example:

$$ -3<2 $$

but:

$$ \boxed{ 9>4 }. $$

Sign conditions must therefore be checked before squaring both sides.

Taking Square Roots

On nonnegative real numbers, the square-root function is increasing:

$$ \boxed{ 0\leq a\leq b \Longrightarrow \sqrt{a}\leq\sqrt{b} }. $$

Increasing Functions Preserve Inequality

If $f$ is strictly increasing:

$$ \boxed{ a<b \Longrightarrow f(a)<f(b) }. $$

Decreasing Functions Reverse Inequality

If $f$ is strictly decreasing:

$$ \boxed{ a<b \Longrightarrow f(a)>f(b) }. $$

Compound Inequality

A compound inequality combines two comparisons.

$$ \boxed{ 2<x\leq7 }. $$

This means:

$$ x>2 \quad\text{and}\quad x\leq7. $$

Interval Form of a Compound Inequality

$$ \boxed{ (2,7] }. $$

Conjunction

A bounded compound inequality ordinarily represents an AND condition:

$$ \boxed{ a<x<b \iff (x>a)\land(x<b) }. $$

Disjunction

Some inequalities produce OR conditions:

$$ \boxed{ x<a \quad\text{or}\quad x>b }. $$

Absolute-Value Inequality: Inside a Radius

For $r>0$:

$$ |x-a|<r $$

means $x$ is within distance $r$ of $a$:

$$ \boxed{ a-r<x<a+r }. $$

Absolute-Value Inequality: Outside a Radius

For $r>0$:

$$ |x-a|>r $$

gives:

$$ \boxed{ x<a-r \quad\text{or}\quad x>a+r }. $$

Non-Strict Absolute-Value Forms

$$ \boxed{ |x-a|\leq r \iff a-r\leq x\leq a+r } $$
$$ \boxed{ |x-a|\geq r \iff x\leq a-r \text{ or } x\geq a+r }. $$

Linear Inequality

A one-variable linear inequality has a form such as:

$$ \boxed{ ax+b<c }. $$

Polynomial Inequality

A polynomial inequality may be written:

$$ \boxed{ P(x)>0 }. $$

Example

Solve:

$$ x^2-5x+6>0. $$

Factor:

$$ (x-2)(x-3)>0. $$

The critical points are $2$ and $3$. Sign analysis gives:

$$ \boxed{ x<2 \quad\text{or}\quad x>3 }. $$

Boundary Points

Values making a factored expression zero are candidate boundaries of the solution intervals.

Sign Chart

A sign chart divides the number line at critical points and determines where an expression is positive or negative.

Repeated Factors

The sign of a polynomial changes across a root of odd multiplicity and ordinarily does not change across a root of even multiplicity.

Rational Inequality

A rational inequality has a form such as:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\geq0 }. $$

Domain Restrictions in Rational Inequalities

Values satisfying:

$$ Q(x)=0 $$

are always excluded.

Critical Values for Rational Inequalities

Sign analysis uses:

  • zeros of the numerator;
  • zeros of the denominator.

Boundary Inclusion

A numerator zero may be included under $\leq$ or $\geq$, but a denominator zero can never be included.

Exponential Inequality

If $b>1$, then $b^x$ is increasing:

$$ \boxed{ b^u<b^v \iff u<v }. $$

Exponential Inequality with Base between Zero and One

If $0<b<1$, then $b^x$ is decreasing:

$$ \boxed{ b^u<b^v \iff u>v }. $$

Logarithmic Inequality

For $b>1$:

$$ \boxed{ \log_bu<\log_bv \iff u<v } $$

provided $u>0$ and $v>0$.

Logarithmic Base between Zero and One

If $0<b<1$, the logarithm is decreasing and reverses order.

Inequality Systems

A system of inequalities requires all stated conditions to hold simultaneously unless an OR relation is specified.

One-Variable System

$$ \begin{aligned} x &\geq2,\\ x &<5 \end{aligned} $$

has solution:

$$ \boxed{ [2,5) }. $$

Two-Variable Linear Inequality

An inequality such as:

$$ \boxed{ 2x+y\leq6 } $$

describes a half-plane including its boundary line.

Boundary Line

Replace the inequality by equality:

$$ 2x+y=6. $$

This gives the geometric boundary.

Solid Boundary

For $\leq$ or $\geq$, the boundary is included and is usually drawn solid.

Dashed Boundary

For $<$ or $>$, the boundary is excluded and is usually drawn dashed.

Feasible Region

The common solution of several inequalities is their feasible region.

$$ \boxed{ \mathcal{F} = S_1\cap S_2\cap\cdots\cap S_n }. $$

Optimization

Linear and nonlinear optimization frequently use inequalities to impose constraints.

$$ \boxed{ g_i(\mathbf{x})\leq0 }. $$

Bounds

Inequalities express upper and lower bounds.

$$ \boxed{ L\leq x\leq U }. $$

Here $L$ is a lower bound and $U$ is an upper bound for the specified variable.

Bounded Set

A set $S\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ is bounded above if there exists $M$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ x\leq M \quad \text{for every }x\in S }. $$

Maximum vs. Upper Bound

An upper bound need not belong to the set. A maximum must belong to the set.

Minimum vs. Lower Bound

A lower bound need not belong to the set. A minimum must belong to the set.

Supremum

The least upper bound of a set is its supremum:

$$ \boxed{ \sup S }. $$

Infimum

The greatest lower bound is:

$$ \boxed{ \inf S }. $$

Triangle Inequality

One of the most fundamental inequalities is:

$$ \boxed{ |a+b| \leq |a|+|b| }. $$

Geometric Triangle Inequality

In a triangle with side lengths $a,b,c$:

$$ \boxed{ a+b>c,\qquad a+c>b,\qquad b+c>a }. $$

Vector Triangle Inequality

$$ \boxed{ \|\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\| \leq \|\mathbf{u}\|+\|\mathbf{v}\| }. $$

Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality

$$ \boxed{ |\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}| \leq \|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\| }. $$

Arithmetic-Geometric Mean Inequality

For nonnegative $a$ and $b$:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt{ab} \leq \frac{a+b}{2} }. $$

Equality holds exactly when $a=b$.

Mean Inequality

For positive values:

$$ \boxed{ H\leq G\leq A }. $$

Here $H$, $G$, and $A$ denote harmonic, geometric, and arithmetic means.

Bernoulli's Inequality

Under appropriate conditions:

$$ \boxed{ (1+x)^n \geq 1+nx }. $$

Classical Inequalities

In higher mathematics, named inequalities often provide estimates rather than solution sets. Examples include:

  • triangle inequality;
  • Cauchy-Schwarz inequality;
  • AM-GM inequality;
  • Hölder's inequality;
  • Minkowski's inequality;
  • Jensen's inequality.

Estimate Rather than Equation

Inequalities are central to bounding quantities that may be difficult or impossible to compute exactly.

Calculus

Inequalities are used to prove limits, estimate errors, establish monotonicity, bound integrals, and compare rates of growth.

Squeeze Principle

If:

$$ g(x)\leq f(x)\leq h(x) $$

and both outer functions approach the same limit $L$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \lim f(x)=L }. $$

Integral Bounds

If:

$$ m\leq f(x)\leq M $$

on $[a,b]$, then:

$$ \boxed{ m(b-a) \leq \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \leq M(b-a) }. $$

Error Bounds

Approximation theory frequently uses:

$$ \boxed{ |E| \leq B } $$

to guarantee that an error does not exceed a stated bound.

Probability Inequalities

Probability theory uses inequalities to bound event probabilities and deviations.

Markov's Inequality

For a nonnegative random variable $X$ and $a>0$:

$$ \boxed{ P(X\geq a) \leq \frac{E[X]}{a} }. $$

Chebyshev's Inequality

For a random variable with finite variance:

$$ \boxed{ P(|X-\mu|\geq k\sigma) \leq \frac{1}{k^2} } $$

for $k>0$.

Discrete Mathematics

Inequalities compare integer quantities, cardinalities, algorithmic costs, and combinatorial bounds.

Asymptotic Bounds

Algorithm analysis often expresses upper or lower estimates through inequalities, even when summarized with notation such as $O$, $\Omega$, or $\Theta$.

Matrix Inequalities

In advanced linear algebra, inequality notation may extend beyond ordinary scalar order, for example to positive-semidefinite order.

Thus:

$$ A\succeq0 $$

does not mean that every matrix entry is nonnegative. It means $A$ is positive semidefinite under the relevant convention.

Partial Orders

Not every mathematical set has a total order like the real numbers. Inequality-like notation may represent partial order relations in more abstract settings.

Complex Numbers

The complex numbers do not admit the ordinary real-number order as an ordered field.

Consequently:

$$ i>0 $$

has no standard meaning in ordinary complex arithmetic.

Not-Equal Symbol

$$ \boxed{ a\neq b } $$

expresses non-equality but does not specify which value is larger.

Inequality vs. Non-Equality

In elementary terminology, inequality usually refers to an ordered comparison such as $<$, $>$, $\leq$, or $\geq$, although $\neq$ may be grouped with inequality symbols in some introductory treatments.

Equality Case

In a non-strict inequality, identifying when equality occurs is often important.

For example:

$$ \sqrt{ab}\leq\frac{a+b}{2} $$

becomes equality exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ a=b }. $$

Sharp Inequality

An inequality or bound is sometimes called sharp when the stated constant or boundary cannot be improved.

Strictness and Equality Conditions

A theorem may state:

$$ A\leq B $$

together with a separate description of exactly when $A=B$.

Word-Problem Language

Common translations include:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \text{"more than"} &\longrightarrow >,\\ \text{"less than"} &\longrightarrow <,\\ \text{"at least"} &\longrightarrow \geq,\\ \text{"at most"} &\longrightarrow \leq,\\ \text{"no more than"} &\longrightarrow \leq,\\ \text{"no less than"} &\longrightarrow \geq. \end{aligned} } $$

Order of Spoken Quantities

"Five is less than eight" means:

$$ \boxed{ 5<8 }. $$

The comparison symbol points toward the smaller quantity.

Symbol Shape Mnemonic

A common mnemonic is that the narrow point faces the smaller number and the wide opening faces the larger number.

Common Error: Forgetting to Reverse the Sign

Multiplying or dividing by a negative number requires reversal of the inequality direction.

Common Error: Reversing the Sign after Addition or Subtraction

Addition and subtraction do not reverse the inequality.

Common Error: Multiplying by an Expression of Unknown Sign

If the sign is unknown, case analysis may be necessary.

Common Error: Treating an Inequality like an Equation at Boundary Points

Strict inequalities exclude equality, while non-strict inequalities include it.

Common Error: Including a Rational Denominator Zero

A denominator zero is excluded even if the inequality symbol itself is non-strict.

Common Error: Assuming Squaring Preserves Order

Squaring does not preserve order across all real numbers without sign conditions.

Common Error: Ignoring Domain Restrictions

Radical, rational, and logarithmic inequalities must respect the domains of all expressions.

Common Error: Treating AND as OR

A bounded compound inequality such as:

$$ 2<x<5 $$

requires both conditions simultaneously.

Common Error: Treating OR as AND

An outside absolute-value condition such as:

$$ |x|>3 $$

gives:

$$ \boxed{ x<-3 \text{ or } x>3 }. $$

Common Error: Assuming Every Inequality Has an Interval Solution

Some inequalities have empty solution sets, discrete solution sets, unions of intervals, or multidimensional regions.

Common Error: Assuming All Mathematical Objects Are Orderable Like Real Numbers

Order notation depends on the structure being used. Complex numbers, matrices, sets, and vectors require context-specific notions of comparison.

Common Misconception: Solving an inequality is not merely solving an equation and keeping the same comparison sign throughout. Inequalities encode order, so every algebraic transformation must preserve or correctly reverse that order. In particular, multiplying or dividing by a negative quantity reverses the inequality, and domain restrictions and endpoint inclusion remain essential parts of the final solution set.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$a<b$ "A is less than b."
$a>b$ "A is greater than b."
$a\leq b$ "A is less than or equal to b."
$a\geq b$ "A is greater than or equal to b."
$a\neq b$ "A is not equal to b."
$2<x\leq7$ "X is greater than two and less than or equal to seven."
$x\in(2,7]$ "X belongs to the interval from two exclusive to seven inclusive."

Common verbal phrases translate into inequality notation as follows:

  • "greater than" $\rightarrow >$;
  • "less than" $\rightarrow <$;
  • "at least" $\rightarrow \geq$;
  • "at most" $\rightarrow \leq$;
  • "no more than" $\rightarrow \leq$;
  • "no less than" $\rightarrow \geq$;
  • "between" $\rightarrow$ potentially ambiguous unless inclusivity is specified.

The expressions inequality sign, comparison sign, and order symbol are all encountered, though comparison symbol is often the broadest description.

Defense Notation Advice: Track whether each boundary is strict or inclusive, reverse the comparison only when multiplying or dividing by a negative quantity or applying a decreasing function, and never multiply through by an unknown-sign expression without case analysis. For rational inequalities, preserve denominator exclusions; for compound inequalities, distinguish AND from OR; and state the final solution in inequality, interval, set-builder, or graphical form as appropriate.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Definition: A mathematical comparison asserting an order relationship between quantities.
  • Less Than: $a<b$.
  • Greater Than: $a>b$.
  • Less Than or Equal To: $a\leq b$.
  • Greater Than or Equal To: $a\geq b$.
  • Not Equal: $a\neq b$, a non-equality relation that does not specify order.
  • Strict Inequality: Uses $<$ or $>$.
  • Non-Strict Inequality: Uses $\leq$ or $\geq$.
  • Equality Inclusion: Non-strict inequalities include their boundary values.
  • Solution: A value making the inequality true.
  • Solution Set: Collection of all values satisfying the inequality.
  • Set-Builder Form: $\{x:x>a\}$.
  • Interval Form: $(a,\infty)$ for $x>a$.
  • Number Line: Graphically displays one-variable inequality solutions.
  • Open Endpoint: Usually denotes a strict boundary.
  • Closed Endpoint: Usually denotes an included finite boundary.
  • Addition Property: Adding the same quantity preserves inequality direction.
  • Subtraction Property: Subtracting the same quantity preserves inequality direction.
  • Positive Multiplication: Preserves inequality direction.
  • Positive Division: Preserves inequality direction.
  • Negative Multiplication: Reverses inequality direction.
  • Negative Division: Reverses inequality direction.
  • Negation: Reverses order.
  • Transitivity: $a<b$ and $b<c$ imply $a<c$.
  • Trichotomy: For real numbers, exactly one of $a<b$, $a=b$, or $a>b$ holds.
  • Reciprocal Order: Positive reciprocals reverse magnitude order.
  • Squaring: Requires sign analysis because it is not increasing on all of $\mathbb{R}$.
  • Square Root: Preserves order on nonnegative real inputs.
  • Increasing Function: Preserves strict order.
  • Decreasing Function: Reverses strict order.
  • Compound Inequality: Combines multiple comparison conditions.
  • AND Condition: $a<x<b$ requires both inequalities.
  • OR Condition: May describe disjoint solution regions.
  • Absolute Value: Converts naturally to distance inequalities.
  • Inside Distance: $|x-a|<r$ gives $a-r<x<a+r$.
  • Outside Distance: $|x-a|>r$ gives two exterior intervals.
  • Linear Inequality: Has first-degree expressions in the variables.
  • Polynomial Inequality: Often solved by factoring and sign analysis.
  • Critical Point: Root or other boundary where sign behavior may change.
  • Sign Chart: Organizes positive and negative intervals.
  • Odd Multiplicity: Polynomial sign ordinarily changes across the root.
  • Even Multiplicity: Polynomial sign ordinarily remains the same across the root.
  • Rational Inequality: Requires numerator and denominator sign analysis.
  • Denominator Zero: Always excluded.
  • Numerator Zero: May be included under a non-strict inequality.
  • Exponential Inequality: Order behavior depends on whether the base is above or below $1$.
  • Logarithmic Inequality: Requires positive arguments and base-sensitive monotonicity.
  • System of Inequalities: Common solution set of several constraints.
  • Two-Variable Inequality: Often represents a planar region.
  • Boundary Line: Obtained by replacing the inequality symbol with equality.
  • Solid Boundary: Used when equality is included.
  • Dashed Boundary: Used when equality is excluded.
  • Feasible Region: Intersection of all constraint regions.
  • Optimization: Inequalities define admissible constraints.
  • Lower Bound: Quantity no larger than every relevant value.
  • Upper Bound: Quantity no smaller than every relevant value.
  • Supremum: Least upper bound.
  • Infimum: Greatest lower bound.
  • Maximum: Largest element actually belonging to a set.
  • Minimum: Smallest element actually belonging to a set.
  • Triangle Inequality: $|a+b|\leq|a|+|b|$.
  • Vector Triangle Inequality: $\|\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\|\leq\|\mathbf{u}\|+\|\mathbf{v}\|$.
  • Cauchy-Schwarz: $|\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}|\leq\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|$.
  • AM-GM: $\sqrt{ab}\leq(a+b)/2$ for nonnegative $a,b$.
  • Mean Inequality: $H\leq G\leq A$ for positive inputs.
  • Bernoulli's Inequality: $(1+x)^n\geq1+nx$ under appropriate conditions.
  • Calculus: Inequalities support limit proofs, estimates, and error bounds.
  • Squeeze Principle: Traps one quantity between two others.
  • Integral Bound: Bounds accumulated values using pointwise inequalities.
  • Error Analysis: Often written $|E|\leq B$.
  • Probability: Inequalities bound probabilities and deviations.
  • Markov's Inequality: Bounds tails of nonnegative random variables.
  • Chebyshev's Inequality: Bounds deviation probabilities using variance.
  • Discrete Mathematics: Used for integer, combinatorial, and complexity bounds.
  • Algorithm Analysis: Inequality estimates underlie asymptotic bounds.
  • Matrix Order: May use context-specific symbols such as $\succeq$.
  • Partial Order: Some mathematical structures permit comparison of only certain pairs.
  • Complex Numbers: Do not have the ordinary compatible total order of the real numbers.
  • Equality Case: Often identifies when a non-strict bound becomes exact.
  • Sharp Bound: A bound that cannot be improved under the stated conditions.
  • Word Problem: "At least" means $\geq$.
  • Word Problem: "At most" means $\leq$.
  • Word Problem: "More than" means $>$.
  • Word Problem: "Less than" means $<$.
  • Common Error: Forgetting to reverse inequality when multiplying or dividing by a negative quantity.
  • Common Error: Reversing the inequality after ordinary addition or subtraction.
  • Common Error: Multiplying by an unknown-sign quantity without considering cases.
  • Common Error: Including strict boundary points.
  • Common Error: Excluding a permitted equality boundary under $\leq$ or $\geq$.
  • Common Error: Including a denominator zero in a rational inequality.
  • Common Error: Assuming squaring preserves order without sign restrictions.
  • Common Error: Ignoring logarithmic, radical, or rational domain restrictions.
  • Common Error: Confusing AND conditions with OR conditions.
  • Common Error: Assuming every inequality solution is one continuous interval.
  • Common Error: Assuming every mathematical structure supports the same order as the real numbers.
  • Core Interpretation: An inequality compares quantities through order: $$ \boxed{ a<b,\quad a>b,\quad a\leq b,\quad a\geq b }. $$ Solving an inequality means finding every input for which the comparison is true. Algebraic operations such as addition preserve order, while multiplication or division by a negative quantity reverses it: $$ \boxed{ a<b,\ c<0 \Longrightarrow ac>bc }. $$ Beyond elementary algebra, inequalities express bounds, feasible regions, approximation errors, geometric constraints, probability estimates, and major structural results such as the triangle and Cauchy-Schwarz inequalities.
