Amplitude

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

Amplitude is a measure of the magnitude of oscillation of a periodic quantity. In elementary mathematics, it most often refers to the maximum vertical displacement of a sinusoidal function from its midline.

For a function of the form:

$$ y=A\sin x $$

or:

$$ y=A\cos x, $$

the amplitude is:

$$ \boxed{ |A| }. $$

The word is associated with magnitude or extent. In periodic mathematics, it describes how far the oscillation reaches from its equilibrium or midline position.

$$ \boxed{ \text{amplitude} = \text{maximum displacement from the midline} }. $$

Amplitude is therefore a nonnegative magnitude. A negative coefficient in front of a sine or cosine function reflects the graph but does not create a negative amplitude.

Operational Nuance

Basic Sine and Cosine

The basic functions:

$$ y=\sin x $$

and:

$$ y=\cos x $$

both have amplitude:

$$ \boxed{1}. $$

Their values range from $-1$ to $1$, so each reaches one unit above and one unit below its midline $y=0$.

General Sinusoidal Form

A common transformed sine or cosine function is:

$$ \boxed{ y=A\sin(B(x-C))+D } $$

or:

$$ \boxed{ y=A\cos(B(x-C))+D }. $$

In this form:

  • $|A|$ is the amplitude,
  • $B$ controls the period,
  • $C$ controls horizontal or phase shift, and
  • $D$ determines the midline.

Amplitude Formula

For either standard sinusoidal form:

$$ \boxed{ \text{Amplitude}=|A| }. $$

Why Absolute Value Is Used

Consider:

$$ y=-4\sin x. $$

The coefficient $-4$ reflects the sine graph across its midline and vertically stretches it by a factor of $4$.

Its amplitude is therefore:

$$ \boxed{ |-4|=4 }. $$

Amplitude measures size, not orientation.

Midline

For:

$$ y=A\sin(B(x-C))+D, $$

the midline is:

$$ \boxed{ y=D }. $$

The sinusoid oscillates above and below this line by a maximum distance of $|A|$.

Maximum and Minimum

For a sine or cosine function with amplitude $|A|$ and midline $D$:

$$ \boxed{ y_{\max}=D+|A| } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ y_{\min}=D-|A| }. $$

Range

Therefore the range is:

$$ \boxed{ [D-|A|,\;D+|A|] }. $$

Finding Amplitude from Maximum and Minimum

If the maximum and minimum values of a sinusoid are known, then:

$$ \boxed{ \text{Amplitude} = \frac{y_{\max}-y_{\min}}{2} }. $$

The midline is:

$$ \boxed{ D= \frac{y_{\max}+y_{\min}}{2} }. $$

Example

Suppose a periodic function has maximum value:

$$ 11 $$

and minimum value:

$$ 3. $$

The amplitude is:

$$ \frac{11-3}{2} = \boxed{4}. $$

Its midline is:

$$ \frac{11+3}{2} = \boxed{7}. $$

Total Vertical Range vs. Amplitude

The total distance from minimum to maximum is:

$$ y_{\max}-y_{\min}. $$

This is twice the amplitude:

$$ \boxed{ y_{\max}-y_{\min} = 2(\text{Amplitude}) }. $$

Thus amplitude is half the peak-to-peak vertical range.

Amplitude Does Not Control Period

In:

$$ y=A\sin(Bx), $$

changing $A$ changes the vertical size of the graph, while changing $B$ changes its horizontal repetition rate.

$$ \boxed{ |A| \longrightarrow \text{amplitude} } $$

while:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{2\pi}{|B|} \longrightarrow \text{period} }. $$

Vertical Stretch and Compression

If:

$$ |A|>1, $$

the basic sine or cosine graph is vertically stretched.

If:

$$ 0<|A|<1, $$

it is vertically compressed.

Zero Amplitude

If:

$$ A=0, $$

then:

$$ y=D. $$

The oscillation disappears and the amplitude is:

$$ \boxed{0}. $$

Amplitude and Waves

In wave models, amplitude represents maximum displacement from equilibrium. For:

$$ y(x,t) = A\sin(kx-\omega t+\phi), $$

the amplitude is:

$$ \boxed{|A|}. $$

The other parameters describe spatial and temporal behavior rather than oscillation size.

Amplitude and Simple Harmonic Motion

A displacement model such as:

$$ x(t)=A\cos(\omega t+\phi) $$

describes motion between the turning points:

$$ \boxed{ -|A| \le x \le |A| } $$

when the equilibrium position is zero.

Common Misconception: Amplitude is not the distance from the lowest point to the highest point of a sinusoid. That peak-to-peak distance is twice the amplitude.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$A$ "A," commonly the amplitude coefficient in a sinusoidal model.
$|A|$ "The absolute value of A," or "the amplitude."
$y=A\sin x$ "Y equals A sine x."
$y=A\cos(B(x-C))+D$ "Y equals A cosine of B times x minus C, plus D."
$y=D$ "The midline is y equals D."
$\frac{y_{\max}-y_{\min}}{2}$ "Half the difference between the maximum and minimum values."

Care is needed because the letter $A$ is often called the amplitude coefficient, while the amplitude itself is:

$$ \boxed{|A|}. $$

Thus if:

$$ A=-6, $$

one should say:

$$ \boxed{ \text{the amplitude is }6 }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish the signed coefficient $A$ from the nonnegative amplitude $|A|$. The sign of $A$ determines reflection; its magnitude determines amplitude.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: Amplitude is the maximum displacement of a periodic oscillation from its midline or equilibrium value.
  • Sine Function: For: $$ y=A\sin x, $$ the amplitude is: $$ \boxed{|A|}. $$
  • Cosine Function: For: $$ y=A\cos x, $$ the amplitude is: $$ \boxed{|A|}. $$
  • General Sinusoid: For: $$ y=A\sin(B(x-C))+D, $$ the amplitude is: $$ \boxed{|A|}. $$
  • Nonnegative Quantity: Amplitude is conventionally nonnegative.
  • Negative Coefficient: A negative $A$ reflects the graph about its midline but does not make the amplitude negative.
  • Example: For: $$ y=-7\cos x, $$ the amplitude is: $$ \boxed{7}. $$
  • Midline: For: $$ y=A\sin(B(x-C))+D, $$ the midline is: $$ \boxed{y=D}. $$
  • Maximum: $$ \boxed{ y_{\max}=D+|A| }. $$
  • Minimum: $$ \boxed{ y_{\min}=D-|A| }. $$
  • Range: $$ \boxed{ [D-|A|,\;D+|A|] }. $$
  • Amplitude from Extrema: $$ \boxed{ \text{Amplitude} = \frac{y_{\max}-y_{\min}}{2} }. $$
  • Midline from Extrema: $$ \boxed{ D= \frac{y_{\max}+y_{\min}}{2} }. $$
  • Peak-to-Peak Distance: $$ \boxed{ y_{\max}-y_{\min} = 2|A| }. $$
  • Vertical Stretch: If $|A|>1$, the graph is stretched vertically relative to the basic sine or cosine graph.
  • Vertical Compression: If: $$ 0<|A|<1, $$ the graph is vertically compressed.
  • Reflection: If $A<0$, the sinusoidal graph is reflected across its midline relative to the corresponding positive-$A$ form.
  • Zero Amplitude: If $A=0$, the sinusoidal term disappears and the function becomes constant.
  • Period Contrast: For: $$ y=A\sin(Bx), $$ amplitude depends on $A$, while period depends on $B$.
  • Period: $$ \boxed{ T=\frac{2\pi}{|B|} } $$ for sine and cosine written in radian measure.
  • Frequency Contrast: Amplitude describes vertical magnitude; frequency describes how rapidly cycles repeat.
  • Phase Shift Contrast: The parameter $C$ in: $$ A\sin(B(x-C))+D $$ shifts the oscillation horizontally but does not change its amplitude.
  • Vertical Shift Contrast: The parameter $D$ moves the midline but does not change the amplitude.
  • Sine and Cosine Equivalence: The same sinusoidal oscillation can often be represented using either sine or cosine with an appropriate phase shift, without changing amplitude.
  • Wave Function: For: $$ y(x,t)=A\sin(kx-\omega t+\phi), $$ the amplitude is: $$ \boxed{|A|}. $$
  • Simple Harmonic Motion: In: $$ x(t)=A\cos(\omega t+\phi), $$ amplitude gives the maximum displacement from equilibrium.
  • Turning Points: For equilibrium at zero, the motion reaches: $$ \boxed{ x=\pm|A| }. $$
  • Physical Units: In applied models, amplitude has the same units as the quantity represented on the vertical axis.
  • Displacement Example: If $x(t)$ is measured in centimeters, its amplitude is also measured in centimeters.
  • Voltage Example: A sinusoidal voltage model may have amplitude measured in volts.
  • Sound Models: Amplitude may describe the magnitude of a pressure or displacement oscillation, although perceived loudness is not numerically identical to amplitude.
  • Wave-Energy Caution: In many physical systems, energy or intensity depends on the square of an amplitude, but this is an applied relationship rather than the definition of amplitude itself.
  • Damped Oscillation: In a model such as: $$ y=Ae^{-kt}\cos(\omega t), $$ the oscillation has a changing envelope: $$ \boxed{ |A|e^{-kt} }. $$
  • Variable Amplitude: Not every oscillation has a constant amplitude. Modulated and damped signals may have amplitudes that vary with time or position.
  • Complex Exponential Representation: A sinusoid can be represented using: $$ Ae^{i(\omega t+\phi)}, $$ where the magnitude of the complex coefficient contributes to the oscillation's amplitude.
  • Fourier Analysis: A periodic function can be decomposed into sinusoidal components with different frequencies and amplitudes.
  • Fourier Coefficients: The size of each harmonic component is determined from its coefficients rather than from one universal amplitude for the entire function.
  • General Periodic Functions: The word amplitude is most straightforward for symmetric oscillations about a midline. For more irregular periodic functions, authors may instead specify maxima, minima, range, or particular Fourier-component amplitudes.
  • Not the Range: Amplitude is a number measuring displacement; the range is the set of output values attained by the function.
  • Not Peak-to-Peak: Amplitude is half the vertical distance from minimum to maximum for an ordinary sinusoid.
  • Not Frequency: Increasing amplitude makes an oscillation larger, not faster.
  • Not Period: Increasing amplitude does not by itself change the horizontal length of a cycle.
  • Graphing Interpretation: The amplitude can be visualized as the vertical distance from the midline to either a peak or a trough.
  • Terminological Contrast: Amplitude measures maximum displacement from the midline. Period measures the horizontal length of one complete cycle. Frequency measures cycles per unit input, and the midline is the central value about which the oscillation occurs.
