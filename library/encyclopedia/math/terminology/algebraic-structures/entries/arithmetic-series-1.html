Arithmetic Series

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

An arithmetic series is the sum of the terms of an arithmetic sequence. If:

$$ a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots $$

is an arithmetic sequence with constant common difference $d$, then:

$$ a_1+a_2+a_3+\cdots $$

is its associated arithmetic series.

The defining sequence satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ a_{n+1}-a_n=d }. $$

The word series signals addition. This distinguishes the series from the underlying sequence, which merely lists the terms.

$$ \boxed{ \text{sequence} = a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{series} = a_1+a_2+a_3+\cdots }. $$

Operational Nuance

Finite Arithmetic Series

The sum of the first $n$ terms of an arithmetic sequence is denoted:

$$ \boxed{ S_n= a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n }. $$

Its standard formula is:

$$ \boxed{ S_n= \frac{n}{2}(a_1+a_n) }. $$

Endpoint-Average Interpretation

The quantity:

$$ \frac{a_1+a_n}{2} $$

is the arithmetic mean of the first and last terms.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ S_n= n \left( \frac{a_1+a_n}{2} \right) }. $$

In words:

$$ \boxed{ \text{sum} = \text{number of terms} \times \text{average term} }. $$

Why the Endpoint Average Works

In a finite arithmetic sequence, terms equally far from the ends have the same sum:

$$ a_1+a_n = a_2+a_{n-1} = a_3+a_{n-2} = \cdots. $$

This symmetry makes the average of the first and last terms equal to the average of all the terms.

Pairing Derivation

Write the sum forward:

$$ S_n= a_1+a_2+a_3+\cdots+a_n. $$

Write the same sum backward:

$$ S_n= a_n+a_{n-1}+a_{n-2}+\cdots+a_1. $$

Add vertically:

$$ 2S_n= (a_1+a_n) +(a_2+a_{n-1}) +\cdots+ (a_n+a_1). $$

Every pair equals $a_1+a_n$, and there are $n$ pairs:

$$ 2S_n= n(a_1+a_n). $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ S_n= \frac n2(a_1+a_n) }. $$

Formula Using the Common Difference

Since:

$$ a_n= a_1+(n-1)d, $$

substitute into the sum formula:

$$ S_n= \frac n2 \left[ a_1+a_1+(n-1)d \right]. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ S_n= \frac n2 \left[ 2a_1+(n-1)d \right] }. $$

When to Use Each Formula

Use:

$$ \boxed{ S_n= \frac n2(a_1+a_n) } $$

when the first and last terms are known.

Use:

$$ \boxed{ S_n= \frac n2 [2a_1+(n-1)d] } $$

when the first term, common difference, and number of terms are known.

Basic Example

Find:

$$ 4+7+10+13+16. $$

Here:

$$ a_1=4, \qquad a_5=16, \qquad n=5. $$

Therefore:

$$ S_5= \frac52(4+16). $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ S_5=50 }. $$

Same Example Using the Common Difference

Since:

$$ d=3, $$

use:

$$ S_5= \frac52 [2(4)+(5-1)(3)]. $$

Thus:

$$ S_5= \frac52(20) = \boxed{50}. $$

Summation Notation

An arithmetic series may be written compactly with sigma notation:

$$ \boxed{ S_n= \sum_{k=1}^{n} \left[ a_1+(k-1)d \right] }. $$

Example in Sigma Notation

The series:

$$ 3+7+11+15+\cdots $$

has general term:

$$ a_k=3+4(k-1). $$

Therefore its first $n$ terms sum to:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{k=1}^{n} [3+4(k-1)] }. $$

Using Linearity of Summation

Since:

$$ a_k= a_1+(k-1)d, $$

we may write:

$$ S_n= \sum_{k=1}^{n}a_1 + d\sum_{k=1}^{n}(k-1). $$

Since:

$$ \sum_{k=1}^{n}a_1=na_1 $$

and:

$$ \sum_{k=1}^{n}(k-1) = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}, $$

we obtain:

$$ \boxed{ S_n= na_1+ \frac{dn(n-1)}{2} }. $$

This is algebraically equivalent to the standard formula.

Sum of the First n Positive Integers

The sequence:

$$ 1,2,3,\ldots,n $$

is arithmetic with:

$$ a_1=1, \qquad a_n=n. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ 1+2+\cdots+n = \frac{n(n+1)}{2} }. $$

Sum of the First n Odd Numbers

The odd numbers:

$$ 1,3,5,\ldots,2n-1 $$

form an arithmetic sequence.

Their sum is:

$$ S_n= \frac n2 [1+(2n-1)]. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ 1+3+5+\cdots+(2n-1) = n^2 }. $$

Sum of the First n Even Numbers

The sequence:

$$ 2,4,6,\ldots,2n $$

gives:

$$ S_n= \frac n2(2+2n). $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ 2+4+6+\cdots+2n = n(n+1) }. $$

Average-Term Interpretation

For a finite arithmetic sequence:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{S_n}{n} = \frac{a_1+a_n}{2} }. $$

Thus the arithmetic mean of all its terms equals the mean of the two endpoint terms.

Odd Number of Terms

If $n$ is odd, the middle term is:

$$ a_{(n+1)/2}. $$

For an arithmetic sequence:

$$ \boxed{ a_{(n+1)/2} = \frac{a_1+a_n}{2} }. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ S_n= n a_{(n+1)/2} }. $$

Finding the Last Term from the Sum

Starting with:

$$ S_n= \frac n2(a_1+a_n), $$

solve for $a_n$:

$$ \frac{2S_n}{n}=a_1+a_n. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ a_n= \frac{2S_n}{n}-a_1 }. $$

Finding the First Term from the Sum

Similarly:

$$ \boxed{ a_1= \frac{2S_n}{n}-a_n }. $$

Finding the Number of Terms

If the first term and last term are known:

$$ S_n= \frac n2(a_1+a_n). $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ n= \frac{2S_n}{a_1+a_n} } $$

when the denominator is nonzero and the resulting value is consistent with a valid term count.

Finding n from a₁, d, and Sₙ

Using:

$$ S_n= \frac n2 [2a_1+(n-1)d], $$

the unknown $n$ may appear quadratically.

Expanding:

$$ 2S_n= 2a_1n+d(n^2-n). $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ dn^2+ (2a_1-d)n - 2S_n = 0 }. $$

A quadratic equation may then be solved, with only meaningful positive-integer solutions retained.

Partial Sums

The value $S_n$ is called the nth partial sum:

$$ \boxed{ S_n= \sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k }. $$

The partial-sum sequence records how the accumulated total changes as more terms are included.

Recovering Terms from Partial Sums

Since:

$$ S_n= a_1+\cdots+a_n $$

and:

$$ S_{n-1}= a_1+\cdots+a_{n-1}, $$

subtraction gives:

$$ \boxed{ a_n= S_n-S_{n-1} }. $$

Quadratic Partial-Sum Formula

Expanding:

$$ S_n= \frac n2 [2a_1+(n-1)d] $$

gives:

$$ \boxed{ S_n= \frac d2n^2+ \left( a_1-\frac d2 \right)n }. $$

Therefore the partial sums of a nonconstant arithmetic sequence form a quadratic pattern.

Discrete Calculus Connection

If:

$$ S_n= \sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ S_n-S_{n-1}=a_n }. $$

Thus summation behaves as a discrete analogue of integration, while taking differences behaves like discrete differentiation.

Second Difference of Partial Sums

Since:

$$ \Delta S_n=a_{n+1} $$

and an arithmetic sequence has constant first difference $d$, the partial-sum sequence has constant second difference:

$$ \boxed{ \Delta^2S_n=d }. $$

Geometric Interpretation

The formula:

$$ S_n= n \left( \frac{a_1+a_n}{2} \right) $$

resembles the area formula for a trapezoid:

$$ A= h \left( \frac{b_1+b_2}{2} \right). $$

Both involve multiplying a width or count by the average of two endpoint values. This reflects the linear change underlying both structures.

Finite versus Infinite Arithmetic Series

A finite arithmetic series:

$$ a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n $$

always has a finite sum.

An infinite arithmetic series:

$$ a_1+a_2+a_3+\cdots $$

requires examining the sequence of partial sums.

Convergence of an Infinite Arithmetic Series

For convergence, a necessary condition for any infinite series is:

$$ \boxed{ a_n\to0 }. $$

An arithmetic sequence has:

$$ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d. $$

If $d\ne0$, the terms do not approach zero.

If $d=0$, the sequence is constant. Its terms approach zero only when:

$$ a_1=0. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \text{an ordinary infinite arithmetic series converges only in the trivial all-zero case} }. $$

Arithmetic Series versus Geometric Series

Arithmetic series arise from constant differences:

$$ a_{n+1}-a_n=d. $$

Geometric series arise from constant ratios:

$$ \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}=r. $$

Finite versions of both can be summed, but their formulas and infinite convergence behavior are fundamentally different.

Arithmetic-Series Growth

If $d>0$, then $S_n$ typically grows quadratically because later terms become progressively larger.

This contrasts with a constant sequence, whose partial sums grow linearly, and a geometric sequence with ratio greater than $1$, whose partial sums grow exponentially.

Translation of Every Term

Suppose $c$ is added to every term:

$$ b_k=a_k+c. $$

Then:

$$ \sum_{k=1}^{n}b_k = \sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k + \sum_{k=1}^{n}c. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ S_n^{(b)} = S_n^{(a)}+nc }. $$

Scaling Every Term

If:

$$ b_k=ca_k, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{k=1}^{n}b_k = cS_n }. $$

Termwise Sum of Two Arithmetic Sequences

If $a_k$ and $b_k$ are arithmetic sequences, then $a_k+b_k$ is also arithmetic. Consequently:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{k=1}^{n}(a_k+b_k) = \sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k+ \sum_{k=1}^{n}b_k }. $$

Real-World Use

Arithmetic series arise when a quantity changes by a fixed amount each period and the accumulated total is wanted.

Examples include:

  • total seating across rows that increase by a fixed number of seats,
  • total savings when deposits rise by a fixed amount each month,
  • total production when output increases linearly by period,
  • total distance from equal-time intervals with linearly changing discrete rates,
  • summation of consecutive integers or regularly spaced values.

Example: Seating Rows

Suppose a theater has $20$ seats in the first row and each following row contains $3$ more seats than the preceding row. If there are $15$ rows:

$$ a_1=20, \qquad d=3, \qquad n=15. $$

The final row has:

$$ a_{15} = 20+(14)(3) = 62. $$

The total number of seats is:

$$ S_{15} = \frac{15}{2}(20+62). $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ S_{15}=615 }. $$

Common Misconception

The formula:

$$ a_n= a_1+(n-1)d $$

finds one term of the sequence. It does not find the sum of the terms.

The sum requires:

$$ \boxed{ S_n= \frac n2(a_1+a_n) }. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$S_n=a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n$ "S sub n is the sum of the first n terms."
$S_n=\frac n2(a_1+a_n)$ "The sum of the first n terms equals n over two times the first term plus the nth term."
$S_n=\frac n2[2a_1+(n-1)d]$ "S sub n equals n over two times two a sub one plus n minus one times d."
$\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k$ "The sum from k equals one to n of a sub k."
$a_n=S_n-S_{n-1}$ "The nth term equals the nth partial sum minus the previous partial sum."
$1+2+\cdots+n=\frac{n(n+1)}2$ "The sum of the first n positive integers is n times n plus one over two."

The notation $S_n$ refers to a partial sum, while $a_n$ refers to a single term. The distinction is important because the two quantities generally follow different formulas and different patterns.

Defense Notation Advice: Before applying a formula, identify whether the question asks for a particular term or for an accumulated sum. If it asks "how much altogether," "total," or "sum of the first n terms," $S_n$ is typically the relevant quantity.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: An arithmetic series is the sum of the terms of an arithmetic sequence.
  • Underlying Sequence: $$ \boxed{ a_{n+1}-a_n=d }. $$
  • Partial Sum: $$ \boxed{ S_n= a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n }. $$
  • Endpoint Formula: $$ \boxed{ S_n= \frac n2(a_1+a_n) }. $$
  • Common-Difference Formula: $$ \boxed{ S_n= \frac n2 [2a_1+(n-1)d] }. $$
  • Average-Term Form: $$ \boxed{ S_n= n \left( \frac{a_1+a_n}{2} \right) }. $$
  • Average of Terms: $$ \boxed{ \frac{S_n}{n} = \frac{a_1+a_n}{2} }. $$
  • Sigma Form: $$ \boxed{ S_n= \sum_{k=1}^{n} [a_1+(k-1)d] }. $$
  • Expanded Polynomial Form: $$ \boxed{ S_n= \frac d2n^2+ \left( a_1-\frac d2 \right)n }. $$
  • Quadratic Growth: When $d\ne0$, the partial-sum formula is quadratic in $n$.
  • Symmetric-Term Property: $$ \boxed{ a_1+a_n = a_2+a_{n-1} = \cdots }. $$
  • Odd Number of Terms: $$ \boxed{ S_n= n(\text{middle term}) } $$ when $n$ is odd.
  • First n Integers: $$ \boxed{ 1+2+\cdots+n = \frac{n(n+1)}2 }. $$
  • First n Odd Integers: $$ \boxed{ 1+3+\cdots+(2n-1) = n^2 }. $$
  • First n Even Integers: $$ \boxed{ 2+4+\cdots+2n = n(n+1) }. $$
  • Recovering a Term from Partial Sums: $$ \boxed{ a_n= S_n-S_{n-1} }. $$
  • Finding the Last Term: $$ \boxed{ a_n= \frac{2S_n}{n}-a_1 }. $$
  • Finding the First Term: $$ \boxed{ a_1= \frac{2S_n}{n}-a_n }. $$
  • Finding the Number of Terms: $$ \boxed{ n= \frac{2S_n}{a_1+a_n} } $$ when applicable.
  • Unknown n with Common Difference: $$ \boxed{ dn^2+ (2a_1-d)n - 2S_n = 0 } $$ may arise.
  • Translation of Terms: Adding $c$ to every term increases the $n$th partial sum by $nc$.
  • Scaling of Terms: Multiplying every term by $c$ multiplies every partial sum by $c$.
  • Linearity of Summation: $$ \boxed{ \sum(a_k+b_k) = \sum a_k+\sum b_k }. $$
  • Discrete-Calculus Connection: Partial sums accumulate terms, while differences recover them.
  • Trapezoid Connection: Both the arithmetic-series formula and trapezoid-area formula use an endpoint average multiplied by an interval count or width.
  • Finite Series: Every finite arithmetic series has a well-defined finite sum.
  • Infinite-Series Convergence: An ordinary infinite arithmetic series converges only in the trivial case where every term is zero.
  • Arithmetic-Sequence Contrast: A sequence lists terms; a series sums them.
  • Geometric-Series Contrast: Arithmetic series arise from constant differences; geometric series arise from constant ratios.
  • Common Error: $$ \boxed{ a_n= a_1+(n-1)d } $$ finds the nth term, not the accumulated sum.
  • Common Error: The denominator $2$ in the arithmetic-series formula comes from averaging the first and last terms, not from there being two endpoints to count as terms.
  • Common Error: Do not use the infinite geometric-series formula for an arithmetic series.
  • Terminological Contrast: Arithmetic sequence is the ordered list of terms. Arithmetic series is their sum. Partial sum is the sum through a specified term. Common difference determines the additive spacing of the underlying sequence.
