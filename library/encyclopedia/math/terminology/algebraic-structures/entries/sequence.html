Sequence

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

A sequence is an ordered collection of mathematical objects, usually indexed by integers. Its members are called terms.

$$ \boxed{ a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots } $$

Formally, a sequence may be viewed as a function whose domain is an ordered discrete index set such as $\mathbb{N}$:

$$ \boxed{ a:\mathbb{N}\to S, \qquad n\mapsto a_n }. $$

The word sequence comes from the idea of things following one another in a specified order. Unlike an ordinary set, a sequence retains order and may contain repeated values.

Operational Nuance

Terms and Indices

In $a_n$, the symbol $n$ is the index and $a_n$ is the term at that index.

$$ \boxed{ a_n=\text{nth term} }. $$

Finite and Infinite Sequences

A finite sequence contains a specified number of terms:

$$ a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_N. $$

An infinite sequence continues without a final term:

$$ a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots $$

Order Matters

The sequences:

$$ 1,2,3 \qquad\text{and}\qquad 3,2,1 $$

are different even though they contain the same values.

Repeated Terms Are Allowed

A sequence may repeat values:

$$ \boxed{ 1,0,1,0,1,0,\ldots }. $$

Explicit Formula

An explicit formula gives a term directly from its index:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=2n+1 }. $$

This produces:

$$ 3,5,7,9,\ldots $$

Recursive Formula

A recursive definition specifies initial term information and a rule connecting later terms to earlier ones:

$$ \boxed{ a_1=3, \qquad a_n=a_{n-1}+2 \quad(n\geq2) }. $$

Arithmetic Sequence

An arithmetic sequence has constant difference $d$:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d }. $$

Geometric Sequence

A geometric sequence has constant ratio $r$:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=a_1r^{n-1} }. $$

Sequence as a Function

Viewing a sequence as a function clarifies why its inputs are indices rather than arbitrary real numbers. For example:

$$ a(n)=n^2, \qquad n\in\mathbb{N} $$

represents the sequence:

$$ 1,4,9,16,\ldots $$

Indexing Conventions

Sequences may begin at different indices:

$$ a_0,a_1,a_2,\ldots \qquad\text{or}\qquad a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots $$

Therefore formulas must be interpreted with the stated starting index.

Monotonicity

A sequence is increasing when:

$$ a_{n+1}\geq a_n $$

for the relevant indices, and decreasing when:

$$ a_{n+1}\leq a_n. $$

Bounded Sequences

A real sequence is bounded if there exists $M>0$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ |a_n|\leq M } $$

for every index $n$.

Convergence

A sequence converges to $L$ if its terms approach $L$ as the index grows:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=L }. $$

For example:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}=0 }. $$

Divergence

A sequence that does not converge to a finite limit is called divergent. Divergence may arise from unbounded growth, oscillation, or other failure to settle toward one value.

Sequence vs. Series

A sequence lists terms:

$$ a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots $$

while a series adds terms:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n }. $$

Common Misconception

A sequence is not merely a set of numbers. Order, indexing, and repetition are part of its structure.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$a_n$ "A sub n," or "the nth term of the sequence."
$(a_n)_{n=1}^{\infty}$ "The sequence a sub n for n from one to infinity."
$a_n=2n+1$ "The nth term is two n plus one."
$a_n=a_{n-1}+d$ "Each term equals the preceding term plus d."
$\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=L$ "The sequence a sub n converges to L."
$a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_N$ "A finite sequence from a sub one through a sub N."

Sequence notation may use:

$$ \boxed{ \{a_n\}, \qquad (a_n), \qquad (a_n)_{n=1}^{\infty} } $$

depending on context. Parentheses are often preferable when one wants to avoid confusion with set notation.

Defense Notation Advice: State the starting index whenever it affects the formula. Distinguish the sequence $(a_n)$ from an individual term $a_n$, and distinguish a sequence from both its set of attained values and the series formed by summing its terms.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: Ordered indexed collection of mathematical objects.
  • Functional View: A sequence is commonly a function from $\mathbb{N}$ or another discrete index set.
  • Term: $a_n$ denotes the value at index $n$.
  • Finite Sequence: Has a final indexed term.
  • Infinite Sequence: Continues indefinitely through its index set.
  • Order: Changing the order generally changes the sequence.
  • Repetition: Equal values may occur at multiple indices.
  • Explicit Sequence: Each term is computed directly from its index.
  • Recursive Sequence: Later terms are generated from earlier terms and initial conditions.
  • Arithmetic Sequence: Has a constant difference.
  • Geometric Sequence: Has a constant ratio where the relevant ratios are defined.
  • Monotone Sequence: Terms consistently move in one order direction.
  • Bounded Sequence: Terms remain within fixed numerical bounds.
  • Convergent Sequence: Terms approach a finite limit.
  • Divergent Sequence: Fails to converge to a finite limit.
  • Analysis: Sequences are fundamental to limits, convergence, continuity, series, and approximation.
  • Discrete Mathematics: Sequences encode ordered states, recurrences, algorithms, and combinatorial structures.
  • Computer Science: Strings, lists, state histories, and indexed outputs may be modeled as sequences.
  • Probability: Random variables or observations may be arranged as stochastic sequences.
  • Common Error: Treating a sequence as an unordered set.
  • Common Error: Confusing $a_n$ with the entire sequence $(a_n)$.
  • Common Error: Ignoring the starting index.
  • Common Error: Confusing a sequence with a series.
  • Core Interpretation: A sequence assigns an ordered value to each discrete index: $$ \boxed{ n\mapsto a_n }. $$ Its structure depends not only on which values occur but also on where and in what order they occur. This makes sequences fundamental to recursion, discrete change, algebraic patterns, convergence, and the transition from discrete mathematics to mathematical analysis.
