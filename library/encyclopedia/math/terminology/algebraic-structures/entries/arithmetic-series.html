Arithmetic Series

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

An arithmetic series is the sum of the terms of an arithmetic sequence. The adjective arithmetic indicates that the underlying sequence changes by a constant additive amount, while series indicates that its terms are being added.

This distinction between sequence and series is fundamental. A sequence is an ordered list:

$$ 3,\ 7,\ 11,\ 15,\ldots $$

whereas the corresponding series is the sum:

$$ 3+7+11+15+\cdots. $$

Thus an arithmetic series is not defined by a new pattern of terms. It inherits its structure from an arithmetic sequence and asks for the accumulated total of some or all of those terms.

The terminology also connects with the older expression arithmetic progression. Since an arithmetic progression and arithmetic sequence are ordinarily synonymous, the sum of a finite arithmetic progression is likewise an arithmetic series.

Operational Nuance

Suppose:

$$ a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots,a_n $$

is a finite arithmetic sequence with common difference $d$. Its corresponding arithmetic series is:

$$ S_n = a_1+a_2+a_3+\cdots+a_n. $$

The standard sum formula is:

$$ \boxed{ S_n = \frac{n}{2}(a_1+a_n) }. $$

In words:

$$ \boxed{ \text{sum} = \text{number of terms} \times \text{average of first and last terms} }. $$

Since the nth term of an arithmetic sequence is:

$$ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d, $$

substitution gives the alternate form:

$$ \boxed{ S_n = \frac{n}{2} \left[ 2a_1+(n-1)d \right] }. $$

For example, consider:

$$ 4+7+10+13+\cdots+31. $$

The first term is:

$$ a_1=4, $$

the common difference is:

$$ d=3, $$

and the final term is:

$$ a_n=31. $$

First determine the number of terms:

$$ 31=4+(n-1)(3). $$

Therefore:

$$ 27=3(n-1), $$ $$ 9=n-1, $$ $$ n=10. $$

Now apply the sum formula:

$$ S_{10} = \frac{10}{2}(4+31) $$ $$ =5(35) $$ $$ =175. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ 4+7+10+\cdots+31=175 }. $$

The formula can be understood by pairing terms from opposite ends:

$$ a_1+a_n, $$ $$ a_2+a_{n-1}, $$ $$ a_3+a_{n-2},\ldots $$

Because the sequence is arithmetic, every such pair has the same sum:

$$ a_1+a_n. $$

Equivalently, the average value of the terms is:

$$ \frac{a_1+a_n}{2}. $$

Multiplying this average by the number of terms gives:

$$ S_n = n\left(\frac{a_1+a_n}{2}\right). $$

Common Misconception: The formula:

$$ \frac{n}{2}(a_1+a_n) $$

does not give the nth term. It gives the sum of the first n terms. The nth-term formula is instead:

$$ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$S_n$ "S sub n," or "the sum of the first n terms."
$a_1$ "A sub one," or "the first term."
$a_n$ "A sub n," or "the nth term."
$d$ "The common difference."
$S_n=\frac{n}{2}(a_1+a_n)$ "The sum of the first n terms equals n over two times the first term plus the nth term."
$\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k$ "The sum from k equals one to n of a sub k."

Sigma notation provides a compact representation:

$$ S_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k. $$

If the arithmetic sequence has explicit formula:

$$ a_k=a_1+(k-1)d, $$

then:

$$ S_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \left[a_1+(k-1)d\right]. $$

This can be expanded using standard summation identities to recover:

$$ S_n = \frac{n}{2} \left[ 2a_1+(n-1)d \right]. $$

Defense Notation Advice: The ellipsis in:

$$ 2+5+8+\cdots+29 $$

should be used only when the intended pattern is sufficiently clear. If there is any ambiguity, give the common difference or use sigma notation.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Sequence vs. Series: An arithmetic sequence is: $$ a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots, $$ while an arithmetic series is: $$ a_1+a_2+a_3+\cdots. $$ A sequence lists; a series adds.
  • Arithmetic Progression: Since arithmetic progression is an alternate term for arithmetic sequence, the sum of a finite arithmetic progression is an arithmetic series.
  • First-n-Term Sum: The most common formula is: $$ \boxed{ S_n=\frac{n}{2}(a_1+a_n) }. $$ It is most convenient when the first and last terms are known.
  • Common-Difference Form: If $a_n$ is not known directly: $$ \boxed{ S_n= \frac{n}{2} \left[ 2a_1+(n-1)d \right] }. $$
  • Finding the Number of Terms: When the first term, final term, and common difference are known: $$ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d $$ can be solved for $n$ before applying the sum formula.
  • Average-Term Interpretation: In a finite arithmetic sequence: $$ \text{average of all terms} = \frac{a_1+a_n}{2}. $$ Therefore: $$ S_n = n\times\text{average term}. $$
  • Pairing Method: Writing the series forward: $$ S_n=a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n $$ and backward: $$ S_n=a_n+a_{n-1}+\cdots+a_1 $$ gives: $$ 2S_n = (a_1+a_n) +(a_2+a_{n-1}) +\cdots. $$ Each of the $n$ paired positions has sum $a_1+a_n$, so: $$ 2S_n=n(a_1+a_n). $$
  • Sum of the First n Positive Integers: The series: $$ 1+2+3+\cdots+n $$ is arithmetic with: $$ a_1=1, \qquad a_n=n. $$ Hence: $$ \boxed{ 1+2+\cdots+n = \frac{n(n+1)}{2} }. $$
  • Sum of the First n Odd Integers: The sequence: $$ 1,3,5,\ldots,2n-1 $$ is arithmetic. Its sum is: $$ \frac{n}{2}[1+(2n-1)] = n^2. $$ Therefore: $$ \boxed{ 1+3+5+\cdots+(2n-1)=n^2 }. $$
  • Sum of the First n Even Integers: For: $$ 2+4+6+\cdots+2n, $$ the arithmetic-series formula gives: $$ \boxed{ n(n+1) }. $$
  • Finite Series: Arithmetic-series formulas are primarily finite-sum formulas. A finite arithmetic series always has a well-defined sum.
  • Infinite Arithmetic Series: A nonconstant infinite arithmetic series does not converge. If: $$ d\neq0, $$ its terms do not even approach zero, violating a necessary condition for convergence.
  • Constant Infinite Series: If $d=0$, then the series has the form: $$ a+a+a+\cdots. $$ This diverges unless: $$ a=0. $$ Thus the only convergent infinite arithmetic series in the ordinary sense is: $$ 0+0+0+\cdots. $$
  • Partial Sums: Even when an infinite arithmetic series diverges, its finite partial sums: $$ S_n=a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n $$ are still given by the arithmetic-series formula.
  • Quadratic Growth of Partial Sums: Since: $$ S_n= \frac{n}{2} \left[ 2a_1+(n-1)d \right], $$ the partial sums are generally quadratic functions of $n$ when: $$ d\neq0. $$ This contrasts with the original arithmetic sequence, whose nth term is linear in $n$.
  • Discrete Calculus Connection: An arithmetic sequence has constant first differences. Its partial-sum sequence therefore has constant second differences. In this sense, summing an arithmetic sequence produces a discrete analogue of a quadratic function.
  • Geometric Series Contrast: Arithmetic series arise from constant differences, whereas geometric series arise from constant ratios. Their sum formulas and convergence behavior are fundamentally different.
  • Real-World Applications: Arithmetic series model accumulated quantities whose individual increments change by a fixed amount: total seating in rows that increase uniformly, cumulative payments that rise by a constant amount each period, or total distance traveled over stages whose lengths form an arithmetic sequence.
  • Terminological Contrast: An arithmetic sequence or arithmetic progression is an ordered list with constant difference. An arithmetic series is the sum of those terms. The symbol $a_n$ denotes an individual term, while $S_n$ denotes an accumulated sum.
