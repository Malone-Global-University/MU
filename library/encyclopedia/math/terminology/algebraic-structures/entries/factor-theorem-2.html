Factor Theorem

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

The Factor Theorem is a fundamental result connecting the zeros of a polynomial with its linear factors. It states that for a polynomial $P(x)$:

$$ \boxed{ x-c \text{ is a factor of }P(x) \iff P(c)=0 }. $$

In other words, the number $c$ is a root or zero of $P$ exactly when the polynomial $x-c$ divides $P(x)$ with zero remainder.

The theorem derives its name from this direct link between a numerical input and the multiplicative factorization of a polynomial.

For example, if:

$$ P(x)=x^2-5x+6, $$

then:

$$ P(2)=4-10+6=0. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ x-2 \text{ is a factor of }P(x) }. $$

Operational Nuance

Formal Statement

For a polynomial $P(x)$ and scalar $c$ in the relevant coefficient field:

$$ \boxed{ P(c)=0 \iff x-c\mid P(x) }. $$

Meaning of Divisibility

The notation:

$$ x-c\mid P(x) $$

means there exists a polynomial $Q(x)$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ P(x)=(x-c)Q(x) }. $$

Root-to-Factor Direction

If:

$$ P(c)=0, $$

then $x-c$ is a factor of $P(x)$.

Factor-to-Root Direction

If:

$$ P(x)=(x-c)Q(x), $$

then substituting $x=c$ gives:

$$ P(c) = (c-c)Q(c) = 0. $$

Therefore $c$ is a root.

Remainder Theorem Foundation

The Factor Theorem is a direct consequence of the Remainder Theorem.

When $P(x)$ is divided by $x-c$:

$$ P(x)=(x-c)Q(x)+R, $$

where $R$ is a constant because the divisor has degree $1$.

Substituting $x=c$ gives:

$$ P(c)=R. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ P(c)=0 \iff R=0 \iff x-c\mid P(x) }. $$

Remainder Theorem vs. Factor Theorem

The Remainder Theorem says:

$$ \boxed{ \text{remainder on division by }x-c=P(c) }. $$

The Factor Theorem is the special zero-remainder case:

$$ \boxed{ P(c)=0 \iff x-c\text{ is a factor} }. $$

Example

Let:

$$ P(x)=x^3-4x^2+x+6. $$

Test $c=2$:

$$ P(2)=8-16+2+6=0. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ x-2 \text{ is a factor} }. $$

Finding the Remaining Factor

Dividing by $x-2$ gives:

$$ P(x)=(x-2)(x^2-2x-3). $$

Factoring further:

$$ x^2-2x-3 = (x-3)(x+1). $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ P(x) = (x-2)(x-3)(x+1) }. $$

Roots Revealed by the Factorization

$$ \boxed{ x=2,\quad x=3,\quad x=-1 }. $$

Sign Reversal in Linear Factors

If $c$ is a root, the corresponding factor is:

$$ \boxed{x-c}. $$

Thus:

$$ c=-4 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x+4 \text{ is the factor}. $$

Testing a Proposed Factor

To test whether $x-c$ is a factor of $P(x)$, evaluate:

$$ \boxed{P(c)}. $$

If the value is zero, the factor test succeeds.

Example of a Nonfactor

Let:

$$ P(x)=x^2+x+1. $$

Test $x-1$:

$$ P(1)=1+1+1=3. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ x-1 \text{ is not a factor} }. $$

Synthetic Division Connection

Synthetic division provides a compact algorithm for dividing by a linear factor of the form:

$$ x-c. $$

The final synthetic value is the remainder:

$$ \boxed{P(c)}. $$

A final value of zero verifies the factor.

Polynomial Long Division

Polynomial long division gives the same result:

$$ P(x) = (x-c)Q(x)+P(c). $$

Thus the Factor Theorem does not depend on synthetic division; synthetic division is merely an efficient computational implementation for linear divisors.

Factor Theorem and Solving Polynomial Equations

Suppose:

$$ P(x)=0. $$

If one root $c$ is found, the Factor Theorem gives:

$$ P(x)=(x-c)Q(x). $$

The original degree is thereby reduced by one, allowing the remaining equation $Q(x)=0$ to be analyzed.

Degree Reduction

If:

$$ \deg P=n $$

and $x-c$ is a factor, then ordinarily:

$$ \boxed{ \deg Q=n-1 }. $$

Repeated Factors

A root may correspond to a factor appearing more than once.

If:

$$ P(x)=(x-c)^mQ(x), \qquad Q(c)\neq0, $$

then $c$ is a root of multiplicity $m$.

Multiplicity One

If $x-c$ divides $P$ but $(x-c)^2$ does not, then $c$ is a simple root.

Multiplicity Two

If:

$$ (x-c)^2\mid P(x) $$

but:

$$ (x-c)^3\nmid P(x), $$

then $c$ is a double root.

Derivative Test for Repeated Roots

If $c$ is a repeated root, then:

$$ \boxed{ P(c)=0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad P'(c)=0 }. $$

Thus repeated factors are closely connected with common factors of $P$ and $P'$.

Graphical Interpretation

A real root $c$ corresponds to a point:

$$ \boxed{(c,0)} $$

on the graph of $y=P(x)$.

Therefore the Factor Theorem connects:

$$ \boxed{ \text{root} \Longleftrightarrow \text{linear factor} \Longleftrightarrow \text{x-intercept} }. $$

Odd Multiplicity

At a real root of odd multiplicity, the polynomial changes sign locally and ordinarily crosses the $x$-axis.

Even Multiplicity

At a real root of even multiplicity, the polynomial does not change sign locally and ordinarily touches the $x$-axis and turns.

Rational Root Theorem Connection

The Rational Root Theorem generates possible rational roots of an integer-coefficient polynomial.

Each candidate $c$ can then be tested using the Factor Theorem:

$$ \boxed{ P(c)=0 \iff x-c\text{ is a factor} }. $$

Example Workflow

Suppose:

$$ P(x)=2x^3-3x^2-8x+12. $$

A rational-root search suggests testing $x=2$.

$$ P(2) = 16-12-16+12 = 0. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{x-2\mid P(x)}. $$

Division then reduces the cubic to a quadratic.

Factorization over Different Fields

The Factor Theorem applies relative to the coefficient system being used.

For example:

$$ P(x)=x^2-2. $$

Since:

$$ P(\sqrt2)=0, $$

one has:

$$ x-\sqrt2 $$

as a factor over $\mathbb{R}$, though not as a polynomial factor in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$.

Complex Roots

The theorem also applies to complex roots.

For:

$$ P(x)=x^2+1, $$

since:

$$ P(i)=i^2+1=0, $$

the Factor Theorem gives:

$$ \boxed{ x-i \text{ is a factor over }\mathbb{C} }. $$

Likewise:

$$ x+i $$

is also a factor.

Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

Every nonconstant polynomial of degree $n$ over $\mathbb{C}$ has $n$ roots counted with multiplicity.

Together with the Factor Theorem, this yields:

$$ \boxed{ P(x) = a \prod_{k=1}^{n}(x-r_k) }. $$

Constructing a Polynomial from Roots

The Factor Theorem can be used in reverse. If a polynomial has roots:

$$ r_1,r_2,\ldots,r_n, $$

then corresponding linear factors are:

$$ x-r_1,\ x-r_2,\ldots,x-r_n. $$

A polynomial with those roots may therefore be constructed as:

$$ \boxed{ P(x) = a (x-r_1)(x-r_2)\cdots(x-r_n) }. $$

Leading Coefficient

The nonzero constant $a$ determines the leading coefficient and overall vertical scaling of the polynomial.

Factor Theorem Does Not Find Roots by Itself

The theorem tells how to verify a proposed root or factor. It does not by itself generate all possible roots.

Additional methods such as factoring identities, the Rational Root Theorem, graphing, numerical approximation, or polynomial formulas may be required to produce candidates.

Factor Theorem vs. Zero-Product Property

The Factor Theorem identifies factors from zeros:

$$ P(c)=0 \iff x-c\mid P(x). $$

The zero-product property then extracts roots from a known factorization:

$$ A(x)B(x)=0 \Longrightarrow A(x)=0 \text{ or } B(x)=0. $$

Factor Theorem vs. Factoring

Factoring is the process of rewriting a polynomial as a product. The Factor Theorem is a criterion that tells whether a particular linear polynomial is one of those factors.

Factor Theorem vs. Remainder Theorem

The Remainder Theorem gives the complete remainder:

$$ R=P(c). $$

The Factor Theorem asks whether that remainder equals zero.

Factor Theorem vs. Rational Root Theorem

The Rational Root Theorem generates possible rational roots. The Factor Theorem confirms whether any candidate is an actual root.

Factor Theorem vs. Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra guarantees the existence of complex roots. The Factor Theorem translates each such root into a linear factor.

Common Misconception: The root $c$ and the factor $x-c$ are not the same object. One is a number and the other is a polynomial. Also, the sign reverses: a root of $-5$ corresponds to the factor $x+5$, not $x-5$.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$P(c)=0$ "C is a zero, or root, of P."
$x-c\mid P(x)$ "X minus c divides P of x."
$P(c)=0\iff x-c\mid P(x)$ "C is a root of P if and only if x minus c is a factor of P."
$P(x)=(x-c)Q(x)$ "P of x equals x minus c times Q of x."
$P(x)=(x-c)Q(x)+P(c)$ "The remainder on division by x minus c is P of c."
$(x-c)^m\mid P(x)$ "X minus c occurs as a factor with multiplicity at least m."
$P(x)=a\prod_{k=1}^{n}(x-r_k)$ "P factors as its leading constant times its linear factors."

A standard verbal form of the theorem is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"}x-c\text{ is a factor of }P(x) \text{ if and only if }P(c)=0\text{."} } $$

The phrase if and only if is important. The theorem works in both directions.

Defense Notation Advice: Keep the root-factor sign relation explicit:

$$ \boxed{ c \longleftrightarrow x-c }. $$

When testing the factor $x+4$, substitute $c=-4$, because:

$$ x+4=x-(-4). $$

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Statement: $$ \boxed{ P(c)=0 \iff x-c\mid P(x) }. $$
  • Root: $c$ is a root when $P(c)=0$.
  • Zero: Zero is another standard name for a root of a polynomial function.
  • Linear Factor: A root $c$ corresponds to the factor $x-c$.
  • Factor Meaning: $$ P(x)=(x-c)Q(x) $$ for some polynomial $Q$.
  • Division Test: $x-c$ is a factor exactly when division produces zero remainder.
  • Remainder Theorem: The remainder on division by $x-c$ equals $P(c)$.
  • Special Case: The Factor Theorem is the zero-remainder case of the Remainder Theorem.
  • Testing a Factor: Evaluate the polynomial at the corresponding root candidate.
  • Example: If $P(3)=0$, then $x-3$ is a factor.
  • Negative Root Example: If $P(-2)=0$, then $x+2$ is a factor.
  • Sign Rule: Root $c$ corresponds to factor $x-c$.
  • Synthetic Division: Efficiently tests and removes factors of the form $x-c$.
  • Synthetic Remainder: The final synthetic value equals $P(c)$.
  • Long Division: Polynomial long division gives the same quotient and remainder.
  • Degree Reduction: Removing one linear factor lowers the degree by one.
  • Polynomial Solving: Once a root is found, the theorem helps reduce the remaining equation.
  • Repeated Root: A repeated root corresponds to repeated occurrence of the same linear factor.
  • Multiplicity: If $(x-c)^m$ divides $P$ but $(x-c)^{m+1}$ does not, then $c$ has multiplicity $m$.
  • Simple Root: Corresponds to multiplicity $1$.
  • Double Root: Corresponds to factor $(x-c)^2$.
  • Triple Root: Corresponds to factor $(x-c)^3$.
  • Derivative Connection: Repeated root $c$ satisfies $P(c)=P'(c)=0$.
  • Graphical Connection: A real root $c$ corresponds to an $x$-intercept $(c,0)$.
  • Odd Multiplicity: The graph typically crosses the axis.
  • Even Multiplicity: The graph typically touches and turns.
  • Factored Form: $$ P(x)=a\prod(x-r_k)^{m_k}. $$
  • Zero-Product Property: Once factored, polynomial equations can be solved by setting individual factors equal to zero.
  • Rational Root Theorem: Supplies candidate rational roots.
  • Factor-Theorem Role: Tests whether those candidates are actual roots.
  • Fundamental Theorem of Algebra: Guarantees complex roots for nonconstant polynomials.
  • Factor-Theorem Role with Complex Roots: Converts each complex root into a linear factor.
  • Complex Example: $P(i)=0$ implies $x-i$ is a factor over $\mathbb{C}$.
  • Real Coefficients: Nonreal roots occur in conjugate pairs.
  • Conjugate Factors: If $a+bi$ is a root of a real polynomial, then both $x-(a+bi)$ and $x-(a-bi)$ are complex factors.
  • Field Dependence: The available factors depend on the coefficient field.
  • Rational Setting: A root outside $\mathbb{Q}$ does not yield a linear factor in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$.
  • Real Setting: Real roots correspond to real linear factors.
  • Complex Setting: Every nonconstant complex polynomial ultimately factors into linear factors.
  • Constructing Polynomials: Known roots can be converted directly into factors.
  • Example: Roots $2$ and $-5$ give factors $(x-2)$ and $(x+5)$.
  • Polynomial Construction: $$ P(x)=a(x-2)(x+5) $$ has roots $2$ and $-5$.
  • Leading Coefficient: Root information alone does not determine the nonzero scalar multiplier $a$.
  • Evaluation Method: Direct substitution can test a proposed root without performing division.
  • Division Method: Zero remainder confirms the same factor relationship.
  • Equivalent Tests: $$ P(c)=0,\quad R=0,\quad x-c\mid P(x) $$ are equivalent statements.
  • Factor vs. Root: A factor is a polynomial expression; a root is a scalar value.
  • Factor vs. Zero: Zero refers to a root, not to the factor itself.
  • Factor Theorem vs. Remainder Theorem: The former detects zero remainder; the latter gives any remainder.
  • Factor Theorem vs. Rational Root Theorem: The Rational Root Theorem proposes candidates; the Factor Theorem verifies them.
  • Factor Theorem vs. Factoring: Factoring is a rewriting process; the theorem is a criterion for identifying linear factors.
  • Factor Theorem vs. Zero-Product Property: The theorem identifies factors from roots; the zero-product property extracts roots from a factorization.
  • Factor Theorem vs. Fundamental Theorem of Algebra: One translates roots into factors; the other guarantees complex roots exist.
  • Polynomial Intercepts: Real linear factors identify horizontal-axis intersections.
  • Sign Analysis: Once factors are known, they help determine intervals where the polynomial is positive or negative.
  • Polynomial Inequalities: Roots supplied by factors serve as boundary points in sign charts.
  • Algorithmic Use: Computer algebra systems use equivalent evaluation and division principles in polynomial factorization algorithms.
  • Verification: Expanding identified factors should reproduce the original polynomial.
  • Terminological Precision: "C is a factor" is usually incorrect when $c$ is a root; the factor is $x-c$.
  • Core Interpretation: The Factor Theorem provides the exact bridge between polynomial evaluation and polynomial divisibility: $$ \boxed{ P(c)=0 \iff x-c\mid P(x) }. $$ A numerical root therefore corresponds to a linear polynomial factor, and a linear factor reveals a root. This relationship underlies synthetic division, polynomial solving, multiplicity, graph interpretation, rational root testing, and complete polynomial factorization.
