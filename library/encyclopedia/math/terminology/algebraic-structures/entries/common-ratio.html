Common Ratio

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

A common ratio is the constant multiplicative factor that relates each term of a geometric sequence to the preceding term.

If a sequence:

$$ a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots $$

is geometric, then there is a constant number $r$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ a_{n+1}=ra_n } $$

wherever the successive terms involved are defined.

Equivalently, when $a_n\ne0$:

$$ \boxed{ r=\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} }. $$

The word ratio refers to a quotient comparing two quantities. The adjective common means that the same ratio recurs throughout the sequence.

$$ \boxed{ \text{common ratio} = \text{constant ratio between consecutive geometric terms} }. $$

The term is closely associated with geometric sequences, geometric progressions, geometric series, exponential functions, compound growth, decay, and repeated scaling.

Operational Nuance

Finding the Common Ratio

In a geometric sequence with nonzero consecutive terms, divide a term by the term immediately before it:

$$ \boxed{ r=\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} }. $$

For example:

$$ 3,\ 12,\ 48,\ 192,\ldots $$

gives:

$$ \frac{12}{3} = \frac{48}{12} = \frac{192}{48} = 4. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{r=4}. $$

A Geometric Sequence Requires a Constant Ratio

It is not enough for successive ratios merely to exist. They must be equal.

Consider:

$$ 2,\ 6,\ 18,\ 54,\ldots $$

Since:

$$ \frac62=\frac{18}{6}=\frac{54}{18}=3, $$

the sequence is geometric with:

$$ \boxed{r=3}. $$

Nonexample

Consider:

$$ 2,\ 6,\ 12,\ 24,\ldots $$

The successive ratios are:

$$ 3,\ 2,\ 2,\ldots $$

Because the ratio is not constant throughout, this is not a geometric sequence as written.

Recursive Form

A geometric sequence may be defined recursively by specifying an initial term and repeatedly multiplying by the common ratio:

$$ \boxed{ a_{n+1}=ra_n }. $$

For example:

$$ a_1=5, \qquad a_{n+1}=2a_n $$

generates:

$$ 5,\ 10,\ 20,\ 40,\ldots $$

with common ratio $r=2$.

Explicit Form

If the first term is $a_1$ and the common ratio is $r$, then the $n$th term is:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=a_1r^{\,n-1} }. $$

The exponent $n-1$ counts how many times the common ratio has been applied after the first term.

Example: Explicit Formula

For:

$$ 7,\ 21,\ 63,\ 189,\ldots $$

we have:

$$ a_1=7, \qquad r=3. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=7\cdot3^{n-1} }. $$

Common Ratio versus Common Difference

A geometric sequence is defined through repeated multiplication. An arithmetic sequence is defined through repeated addition.

Sequence Type Repeated Relationship Constant
Arithmetic $a_{n+1}=a_n+d$ Common difference $d$
Geometric $a_{n+1}=ra_n$ Common ratio $r$
$$ \boxed{ \text{arithmetic} \rightarrow \text{constant difference} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{geometric} \rightarrow \text{constant ratio} }. $$

Ratio Greater Than 1

If:

$$ r>1, $$

and the starting term is positive, successive terms grow in magnitude.

For example:

$$ 2,\ 6,\ 18,\ 54,\ldots $$

has:

$$ \boxed{r=3}. $$

Ratio Between 0 and 1

If:

$$ 0

positive terms decrease geometrically toward zero.

For example:

$$ 80,\ 40,\ 20,\ 10,\ldots $$

has:

$$ \boxed{ r=\frac12 }. $$

Negative Common Ratio

A common ratio may be negative.

Consider:

$$ 2,\ -6,\ 18,\ -54,\ldots $$

The common ratio is:

$$ \boxed{ r=-3 }. $$

Multiplication by a negative ratio reverses the sign of each successive nonzero term.

Alternating Signs

A negative common ratio causes a geometric sequence to alternate in sign unless a term is zero.

$$ \boxed{ r<0 \Rightarrow \text{successive nonzero terms alternate sign} }. $$

Common Ratio Equal to 1

If:

$$ r=1, $$

every term equals the preceding term.

$$ 5,\ 5,\ 5,\ 5,\ldots $$

is therefore geometric with:

$$ \boxed{r=1}. $$

A constant sequence can thus be geometric.

Common Ratio Equal to −1

If:

$$ r=-1, $$

the sequence alternates between two opposite values:

$$ 4,\ -4,\ 4,\ -4,\ldots $$

Common Ratio Equal to 0

A sequence defined recursively by:

$$ a_{n+1}=0\cdot a_n $$

becomes zero after the first term:

$$ 8,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0,\ldots $$

It is natural from the recursive definition to regard $r=0$ as the multiplier. However, the quotient formula:

$$ r=\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} $$

cannot be used once the preceding term is zero because division by zero is undefined.

Zero Terms Require Care

The ratio-of-successive-terms test assumes that the denominator term is nonzero.

$$ \boxed{ r=\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} \quad\text{requires}\quad a_n\ne0 }. $$

The recursive relation $a_{n+1}=ra_n$ is therefore more fundamental than the quotient formula in cases involving zero.

Determining a Ratio from Nonconsecutive Terms

Suppose two terms of a geometric sequence satisfy:

$$ a_m \qquad\text{and}\qquad a_n. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=a_mr^{\,n-m} }. $$

Consequently:

$$ \boxed{ r^{\,n-m} = \frac{a_n}{a_m} } $$

when $a_m\ne0$.

Example: Nonconsecutive Terms

Suppose:

$$ a_2=6, \qquad a_5=162. $$

Since the terms are three steps apart:

$$ 162=6r^3. $$

Therefore:

$$ r^3=27 $$

and:

$$ \boxed{r=3}. $$

Even Gaps Can Produce More Than One Real Candidate

Suppose:

$$ a_1=2, \qquad a_3=18. $$

Then:

$$ 18=2r^2, $$

so:

$$ r^2=9. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ r=3 \quad\text{or}\quad r=-3 }. $$

Knowing only nonconsecutive terms may therefore be insufficient to determine the sign of the common ratio.

Geometric Mean Connection

If three positive numbers:

$$ a,\ b,\ c $$

are consecutive terms of a geometric sequence, then:

$$ \frac ba=\frac cb. $$

Cross-multiplication gives:

$$ b^2=ac. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ b=\sqrt{ac} } $$

for positive terms. The middle term is the geometric mean of its two neighbors.

Geometric Series

A geometric series is formed by adding the terms of a geometric sequence.

$$ a+ar+ar^2+ar^3+\cdots $$

The same common ratio $r$ governs the relationship between consecutive terms of the underlying sequence.

Finite Geometric Series

For $r\ne1$:

$$ \boxed{ S_n = a_1\frac{1-r^n}{1-r} }. $$

An equivalent form is:

$$ \boxed{ S_n = a_1\frac{r^n-1}{r-1} }. $$

Infinite Geometric Series

An infinite geometric series converges when:

$$ \boxed{ |r|<1 }. $$

In that case:

$$ \boxed{ S = \frac{a_1}{1-r} }. $$

Thus the magnitude of the common ratio determines whether the terms shrink rapidly enough for the infinite sum to approach a finite limit.

Why |r| < 1 Matters

If:

$$ |r|<1, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ r^n\to0 \quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty }. $$

If $|r|\ge1$, the terms generally do not approach zero unless the sequence is degenerate, so a nonzero infinite geometric series does not converge.

Exponential Function Connection

The explicit geometric-sequence formula:

$$ a_n=a_1r^{n-1} $$

is the discrete analogue of an exponential function.

For:

$$ f(x)=Ab^x, $$

increasing $x$ by $1$ multiplies the function value by $b$:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{f(x+1)}{f(x)}=b }. $$

Thus the exponential base plays the same structural role as the common ratio of a geometric sequence.

Growth Factor

In applied mathematics, a positive common ratio greater than $1$ may be described as a growth factor.

If a quantity grows by $p$ percent during each period, then:

$$ \boxed{ r=1+\frac{p}{100} }. $$

For example, $5\%$ growth corresponds to:

$$ \boxed{ r=1.05 }. $$

Decay Factor

If a quantity decreases by $p$ percent during each period, the multiplicative factor is:

$$ \boxed{ r=1-\frac{p}{100} }. $$

A $20\%$ decrease therefore corresponds to:

$$ \boxed{ r=0.80 }. $$

Percent Change Is Not the Same as the Common Ratio

If:

$$ r=1.12, $$

the quantity increases by $12\%$ per step, not by $112\%$.

$$ \boxed{ \text{percent change} = (r-1)\times100\% } $$

for positive growth factors.

Compound Interest

Repeated compound growth creates geometric relationships.

If a balance grows by a periodic interest rate $i$, successive balances satisfy:

$$ \boxed{ A_{n+1}=(1+i)A_n }. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ r=1+i }. $$

Population Growth

A discrete population model with a constant proportional growth factor may be written:

$$ P_{n+1}=rP_n. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ P_n=P_0r^n }. $$

Repeated Halving

Processes such as idealized half-life models produce:

$$ \boxed{ r=\frac12 }. $$

Each successive amount is half of the preceding amount.

Scaling

A geometric progression can be understood as repeated scaling. Each application of the common ratio rescales the current quantity.

$$ \boxed{ a \xrightarrow{\times r} ar \xrightarrow{\times r} ar^2 \xrightarrow{\times r} ar^3 }. $$

Common Ratio and Logarithms

If:

$$ a_n=a_1r^{n-1}, $$

logarithms can be used to solve for $n$ when the term value is known.

$$ \frac{a_n}{a_1}=r^{n-1}. $$

For appropriate positive quantities:

$$ \boxed{ n = 1+ \frac{\log(a_n/a_1)}{\log r} }. $$

Constant Ratio as a Multiplicative Rate

A common ratio describes multiplicative change from one discrete step to the next. This differs fundamentally from an additive rate.

$$ \boxed{ a_{n+1}=ra_n } $$

means that the next term is a fixed multiple of the current term, not that a fixed amount is added.

Common Misconception: The Common Ratio Is the Difference between Terms

The common ratio is found by division, not subtraction.

$$ \boxed{ r=\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} } $$

whereas:

$$ d=a_{n+1}-a_n $$

represents a common difference in an arithmetic sequence.

Common Misconception: A Ratio Must Be Greater Than 1

A common ratio may be greater than $1$, between $0$ and $1$, negative, equal to $1$, or even $0$ under an appropriate recursive definition.

Common Misconception: A Negative Ratio Means the Terms Decrease

A negative ratio primarily indicates sign reversal. The magnitudes may increase or decrease according to $|r|$.

$$ \boxed{ |r|>1 \Rightarrow \text{magnitudes tend to grow} } $$
$$ \boxed{ 0<|r|<1 \Rightarrow \text{magnitudes tend to shrink} }. $$

Common Misconception: Every Sequence Has a Common Ratio

Consecutive terms of many sequences can be divided, but a common ratio exists only when the same multiplicative factor governs every required step.

Common Misconception: A Common Ratio Belongs Only to Sequences

The term is most directly defined for geometric sequences and progressions, but the same multiplicative structure appears in geometric series, exponential models, compound interest, decay processes, and repeated scaling.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$r$ "The common ratio" or "r."
$r=\dfrac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}$ "The common ratio equals a sub n plus one divided by a sub n."
$a_{n+1}=ra_n$ "A sub n plus one equals r times a sub n."
$a_n=a_1r^{n-1}$ "A sub n equals a sub one times r to the n minus one."
$r=3$ "The common ratio is three."
$r=-2$ "The common ratio is negative two."
$r=\frac12$ "The common ratio is one-half."
$|r|<1$ "The absolute value of the common ratio is less than one."
$S_n=a_1\dfrac{1-r^n}{1-r}$ "The sum of the first n geometric terms equals a sub one times one minus r to the n over one minus r."

In ordinary mathematical speech, one commonly says that a geometric sequence has common ratio $r$, or that each term is obtained by multiplying the preceding term by $r$.

The phrase common ratio should not be shortened merely to ratio when several different ratios could occur in the discussion. Common communicates the crucial fact that the same multiplicative relationship repeats.

Defense Notation Advice: Define whether the sequence begins at $a_0$ or $a_1$ before writing an explicit geometric formula, because this changes the exponent. Use $a_n=a_1r^{n-1}$ for a sequence indexed from $1$ and $a_n=a_0r^n$ for one indexed from $0$. When finding $r$ by division, verify that the denominator term is nonzero and confirm the ratio across more than one pair of consecutive terms whenever sufficient data are available.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: The common ratio is the constant multiplicative factor between consecutive terms of a geometric sequence.
  • Quotient Form: $$ \boxed{ r=\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} } $$ when $a_n\ne0$.
  • Recursive Form: $$ \boxed{ a_{n+1}=ra_n }. $$
  • Explicit Form: $$ \boxed{ a_n=a_1r^{n-1} }. $$
  • Index-from-Zero Form: $$ \boxed{ a_n=a_0r^n }. $$
  • Geometric Sequence: A sequence whose successive terms are related by one constant multiplicative factor.
  • Geometric Progression: Another standard term for a geometric sequence.
  • Geometric Series: A sum of geometric terms governed by the same common ratio.
  • Arithmetic Contrast: Arithmetic sequences use a common difference; geometric sequences use a common ratio.
  • Positive Growth: For positive starting values, $r>1$ produces multiplicative growth.
  • Positive Decay: For positive starting values, $0
  • Negative Ratio: A negative common ratio causes successive nonzero terms to alternate in sign.
  • Magnitude: $|r|$ governs the multiplicative change in magnitude.
  • Constant Sequence: $r=1$ produces an unchanged sequence.
  • Alternating Constant Magnitude: $r=-1$ produces alternating signs with unchanged magnitude.
  • Zero Multiplier: $r=0$ sends every subsequent term to zero in the recursive relation $a_{n+1}=ra_n$.
  • Zero-Term Caution: The quotient $a_{n+1}/a_n$ is undefined when $a_n=0$.
  • Nonconsecutive Terms: $$ \boxed{ a_n=a_mr^{n-m} }. $$
  • Geometric Mean Connection: Consecutive positive geometric terms $a,b,c$ satisfy: $$ \boxed{ b^2=ac }. $$
  • Finite Geometric Series: For $r\ne1$: $$ \boxed{ S_n=a_1\frac{1-r^n}{1-r} }. $$
  • Infinite Geometric Series: A nonzero geometric series converges when: $$ \boxed{ |r|<1 }. $$
  • Infinite-Series Sum: For $|r|<1$: $$ \boxed{ S=\frac{a_1}{1-r} }. $$
  • Exponential Connection: The base of a discrete exponential model performs the same role as the common ratio of a geometric sequence.
  • Growth Factor: An increase of $p\%$ per period corresponds to: $$ \boxed{ r=1+\frac{p}{100} }. $$
  • Decay Factor: A decrease of $p\%$ per period corresponds to: $$ \boxed{ r=1-\frac{p}{100} }. $$
  • Compound Interest: A periodic interest rate $i$ gives the growth factor: $$ \boxed{ r=1+i }. $$
  • Half-Life Models: Repeated halving corresponds to $r=1/2$ for each half-life interval.
  • Repeated Scaling: The common ratio may be understood as the scale factor repeatedly applied from one stage to the next.
  • Logarithmic Connection: Logarithms can determine how many applications of the common ratio are required to move from one term value to another.
  • Common Error: Do not subtract consecutive terms when searching for a common ratio.
  • Common Error: Do not assume that the common ratio must be positive.
  • Common Error: Do not assume that a ratio smaller than $1$ is invalid; such ratios commonly model decay.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse a growth factor such as $1.08$ with an $108\%$ increase; it represents an $8\%$ increase.
  • Common Error: Do not infer a unique ratio from widely separated terms when an even power of $r$ leaves both positive and negative possibilities.
  • Terminological Contrast: A ratio is any quotient comparing two quantities. A common ratio is a ratio that remains constant between successive terms of a geometric sequence. A common difference is the constant additive change in an arithmetic sequence. A growth factor is a positive multiplicative factor used in applied exponential growth. A decay factor is a positive multiplicative factor between $0$ and $1$ used in applied decay models. A geometric sequence is the sequence generated by repeatedly applying the common ratio.
