Inconsistent System of Equations

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

An inconsistent system of equations is a system for which no single assignment of values satisfies every equation simultaneously.

$$ \boxed{ \text{inconsistent system} \iff \text{no solution} }. $$

The adjective inconsistent comes from the idea that the stated conditions cannot all stand together. Each equation may be individually valid as a mathematical statement or constraint, yet their combined requirements may be mutually incompatible.

For example:

$$ \begin{aligned} x+y &= 4,\\ x+y &= 7 \end{aligned} $$

is inconsistent because the same quantity $x+y$ cannot equal both $4$ and $7$ simultaneously.

The contrasting term is consistent system, meaning that at least one common solution exists.

$$ \boxed{ \text{consistent} \iff \text{one or more solutions} }. $$

Operational Nuance

System of Equations

A system asks for values that satisfy all listed equations at the same time.

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} E_1(x_1,\ldots,x_n) &= 0,\\ E_2(x_1,\ldots,x_n) &= 0,\\ &\vdots\\ E_m(x_1,\ldots,x_n) &= 0 \end{aligned} } $$

A solution must belong to every individual equation's solution set.

Solution-Set Intersection

If the equations have solution sets $S_1,S_2,\ldots,S_m$, then the system's solution set is:

$$ \boxed{ S = S_1\cap S_2\cap\cdots\cap S_m }. $$

The system is inconsistent when:

$$ \boxed{ S=\varnothing }. $$

Linear-System Form

A linear system may be written:

$$ \boxed{ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b} }. $$

It is inconsistent when no vector $\mathbf{x}$ satisfies the equation.

Simple Two-Equation Example

$$ \begin{aligned} 2x+y &= 5,\\ 2x+y &= 9. \end{aligned} $$

Subtracting the first equation from the second gives:

$$ \boxed{ 0=4 }. $$

Since this statement is impossible, the system is inconsistent.

Contradiction as the Diagnostic Signal

Algebraic elimination often reveals inconsistency by producing a false statement such as:

$$ \boxed{ 0=c, \qquad c\neq0 }. $$

Augmented Matrix

A linear system:

$$ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b} $$

may be represented by the augmented matrix:

$$ \boxed{ [A\mid\mathbf{b}] }. $$

Contradiction Row

After row reduction, inconsistency is revealed by a row of the form:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & \mid & c \end{bmatrix}, \qquad c\neq0 }. $$

This row represents:

$$ 0x_1+0x_2+\cdots+0x_n=c, $$

which simplifies to the contradiction $0=c$.

Example through Row Reduction

Consider:

$$ \begin{aligned} x+y &= 2,\\ 2x+2y &= 5. \end{aligned} $$

The augmented matrix is:

$$ \left[ \begin{array}{cc|c} 1 & 1 & 2\\ 2 & 2 & 5 \end{array} \right]. $$

Apply:

$$ R_2\leftarrow R_2-2R_1. $$

This gives:

$$ \left[ \begin{array}{cc|c} 1 & 1 & 2\\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array} \right]. $$

The final row means:

$$ \boxed{ 0=1 }. $$

Therefore the system has no solution.

Consistent Contrast

If row reduction instead produces:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & \mid & 0 \end{bmatrix}, $$

that row represents $0=0$ and does not create inconsistency.

Redundant Equation

A zero row:

$$ \boxed{ 0=0 } $$

usually indicates that one equation was redundant rather than contradictory.

Rank Criterion

For the linear system:

$$ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}, $$

inconsistency occurs precisely when:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{rank}(A) < \operatorname{rank}([A\mid\mathbf{b}]) }. $$

Rouché-Capelli Criterion

A linear system is consistent exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{rank}(A) = \operatorname{rank}([A\mid\mathbf{b}]) }. $$

Therefore unequal ranks diagnose inconsistency.

Column-Space Criterion

The system:

$$ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b} $$

is consistent exactly when $\mathbf{b}$ belongs to the column space of $A$:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{b}\in\operatorname{Col}(A) }. $$

Thus inconsistency means:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{b}\notin\operatorname{Col}(A) }. $$

Linear-Combination Interpretation

If the columns of $A$ are $\mathbf{a}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{a}_n$, then:

$$ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b} $$

asks whether:

$$ \boxed{ x_1\mathbf{a}_1+\cdots+x_n\mathbf{a}_n = \mathbf{b} }. $$

An inconsistent system is one in which no such linear combination produces $\mathbf{b}$.

Homogeneous Systems Cannot Be Inconsistent

A homogeneous system has the form:

$$ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}. $$

Since:

$$ A\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{0}, $$

every homogeneous linear system has at least the trivial solution.

$$ \boxed{ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0} \text{ is always consistent} }. $$

Geometry in Two Variables

Two linear equations in two variables represent two lines.

An inconsistent system occurs when the lines are parallel and distinct.

Parallel-Line Example

$$ \begin{aligned} y &= 2x+1,\\ y &= 2x-4. \end{aligned} $$

Both lines have slope $2$ but different intercepts, so they never intersect.

$$ \boxed{ \text{no intersection} \Longrightarrow \text{no solution} }. $$

Same-Line Contrast

If two equations represent the same line, the system has infinitely many solutions and is therefore consistent.

Intersecting-Line Contrast

If two distinct lines intersect once, the system has exactly one solution and is consistent.

Three-Dimensional Geometry

Linear equations in three variables represent planes.

A system may be inconsistent when the planes have no common point.

Parallel Planes

Two distinct parallel planes form an inconsistent system.

Three Planes with Pairwise Intersections

Three planes can intersect pairwise yet still fail to share one common point. Such a system is also inconsistent.

Geometric Meaning in General

Each equation defines a geometric set. The system is inconsistent exactly when the intersection of all those sets is empty.

$$ \boxed{ S_1\cap\cdots\cap S_m=\varnothing }. $$

Nonlinear Systems

Inconsistency is not limited to linear systems.

For example:

$$ \begin{aligned} x^2+y^2 &= 1,\\ x^2+y^2 &= 4 \end{aligned} $$

is inconsistent because no point can lie simultaneously on concentric circles of radii $1$ and $2$.

Equation and Inequality Systems

A mixed system may also be inconsistent.

For example:

$$ \begin{aligned} x &\geq 5,\\ x &< 2 \end{aligned} $$

has no common solution.

Feasible Region

For a system of inequalities, inconsistency means the feasible region is empty:

$$ \boxed{ \mathcal{F}=\varnothing }. $$

Contradictory Bounds

Bounds such as:

$$ x\geq7 \qquad\text{and}\qquad x\leq3 $$

are mutually incompatible.

Parameter-Dependent Systems

A system may be consistent for some parameter values and inconsistent for others.

For example:

$$ \begin{aligned} x+y &= 2,\\ 2x+2y &= k. \end{aligned} $$

Doubling the first equation gives:

$$ 2x+2y=4. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ k=4 } $$

gives infinitely many solutions, while:

$$ \boxed{ k\neq4 } $$

makes the system inconsistent.

Parameter Detection through Elimination

Row reduction may produce a final equation such as:

$$ 0=k-4. $$

The system is consistent only when $k-4=0$.

Overdetermined Systems

A system with more equations than unknowns is called overdetermined.

Such a system may be consistent or inconsistent.

More Equations Do Not Guarantee Inconsistency

Additional equations may merely repeat or follow from existing equations.

Example of Consistent Overdetermined System

$$ \begin{aligned} x+y &= 2,\\ 2x+2y &= 4,\\ 3x+3y &= 6 \end{aligned} $$

has infinitely many solutions because all three equations represent the same line.

Example of Inconsistent Overdetermined System

$$ \begin{aligned} x+y &= 2,\\ 2x+2y &= 4,\\ 3x+3y &= 8. \end{aligned} $$

The third equation contradicts the first two.

Data-Fitting Systems

Real measurements often contain noise, so equations inferred from data may not admit an exact common solution.

Least-Squares Approximation

For an inconsistent linear system:

$$ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}, $$

one may seek an approximate solution minimizing:

$$ \boxed{ \|A\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|^2 }. $$

Residual Vector

The residual is:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{b}-A\mathbf{x} }. $$

An exact solution would have $\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{0}$. In an inconsistent system, no choice of $\mathbf{x}$ makes the residual zero.

Normal Equations

A least-squares solution satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ A^{T}A\mathbf{x} = A^{T}\mathbf{b} }. $$

Projection Interpretation

Least squares replaces $\mathbf{b}$ by its projection onto the column space of $A$.

$$ \boxed{ A\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \operatorname{proj}_{\operatorname{Col}(A)}\mathbf{b} }. $$

Exact vs. Approximate Solution

A least-squares solution does not make an inconsistent system consistent in the exact sense. It solves a different approximation problem.

Linear Regression

In regression, many observed data equations are typically inconsistent with a single exact model line. Least squares chooses the line minimizing aggregate squared error.

Constraint Modeling

In optimization or engineering, inconsistency may signal that constraints cannot all be satisfied simultaneously.

Feasibility Analysis

Determining whether a system is consistent is equivalent to checking whether its feasible set is nonempty.

Linear Programming

An inconsistent collection of linear constraints produces an infeasible linear program.

Logical Interpretation

A system of equations can be viewed as a conjunction of conditions:

$$ \boxed{ E_1\land E_2\land\cdots\land E_m }. $$

Inconsistency means there is no assignment making the entire conjunction true.

Contradictory Information

A model may become inconsistent because two assumptions impose incompatible numerical requirements.

Error Detection

Inconsistency may indicate:

  • incorrect data;
  • measurement noise;
  • a transcription error;
  • an inappropriate model;
  • genuinely incompatible constraints.

Inconsistency Does Not Identify the Cause

Algebra can establish that the equations cannot all hold simultaneously, but additional context is needed to determine which assumption or measurement is responsible.

Consistent Independent System

A system with exactly one solution is often called consistent and independent in elementary terminology.

Consistent Dependent System

A system with infinitely many solutions is often called consistent and dependent.

Three Standard Linear-System Outcomes

$$ \boxed{ \begin{array}{c|c} \text{Outcome} & \text{Classification}\\ \hline \text{one solution} & \text{consistent}\\ \text{infinitely many solutions} & \text{consistent}\\ \text{no solution} & \text{inconsistent} \end{array} }. $$

Independent vs. Inconsistent

Independent does not mean inconsistent. In elementary linear-system language, an independent system typically has exactly one solution.

Dependent vs. Inconsistent

Dependent likewise does not mean inconsistent. A dependent system often contains redundant equations and has infinitely many solutions.

Substitution Method

Substitution can reveal inconsistency if replacing one variable leads to a false statement.

Example by Substitution

Consider:

$$ \begin{aligned} y &= x+2,\\ y &= x-3. \end{aligned} $$

Equating the right sides gives:

$$ x+2=x-3. $$

Subtracting $x$ yields:

$$ \boxed{ 2=-3 }. $$

Therefore the system is inconsistent.

Elimination Method

Elimination is especially useful because incompatible equations naturally reduce to contradictions of the form $0=c$.

Graphical Method

Graphing reveals inconsistency when the solution sets fail to intersect.

Numerical Methods

Numerical solvers may report failure, nonzero residuals, or infeasibility when no exact common solution exists.

Tolerance Caution

In floating-point computation, equations that are nearly inconsistent may require numerical tolerances rather than exact equality tests.

Near Inconsistency

Two constraints can be extremely close to compatible without being exactly compatible. Numerical conditioning becomes important in such cases.

Symbolic vs. Numerical Inconsistency

Symbolic algebra can establish exact contradiction. Numerical computation often determines practical inconsistency relative to a chosen tolerance.

Common Error: Calling Every System with Several Equations Inconsistent

The number of equations does not determine consistency. Only compatibility of the conditions does.

Common Error: Confusing $0=0$ with a Contradiction

The statement:

$$ 0=0 $$

is always true and signals redundancy, not inconsistency.

Common Error: Missing the Contradiction Row

A row:

$$ [0\ 0\ \cdots\ 0\mid c], \qquad c\neq0 $$

immediately establishes that no solution exists.

Common Error: Assuming Parallel Lines Always Represent the Same Line

Parallel lines with different intercepts are distinct and have no common point.

Common Error: Calling an Inconsistent System Dependent

Elementary terminology usually reserves dependent for consistent systems with infinitely many solutions.

Common Error: Assuming an Overdetermined System Must Be Inconsistent

Extra equations may be redundant and fully compatible.

Common Error: Treating Least Squares as an Exact Solution

Least squares minimizes error when an exact solution does not exist; it does not turn the original inconsistent system into an exact consistent one.

Common Error: Claiming a Homogeneous Linear System Is Inconsistent

A homogeneous system always has the zero solution.

Common Error: Using Determinant Zero as a Test for Inconsistency

For a square coefficient matrix, $\det(A)=0$ indicates singularity, not inconsistency by itself. The system may have infinitely many solutions or no solutions depending on $\mathbf{b}$.

Singular Matrix Example

The coefficient matrix of:

$$ \begin{aligned} x+y &=2,\\ 2x+2y &=4 \end{aligned} $$

is singular, yet the system is consistent with infinitely many solutions.

Common Misconception: An inconsistent system is not merely a difficult system or a system with many equations. It is one whose conditions cannot all be satisfied simultaneously. In a linear system, row reduction exposes this through a contradiction such as $0=1$, while geometrically the individual solution sets have no common intersection.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$S=\varnothing$ "The system's solution set is empty; the system is inconsistent."
$0=c,\ c\neq0$ "The elimination produces a contradiction."
$[0\ 0\ \cdots\ 0\mid c]$ "The augmented matrix contains a contradiction row."
$\operatorname{rank}(A)<\operatorname{rank}([A\mid\mathbf{b}])$ "The augmented matrix has greater rank than the coefficient matrix, so the system is inconsistent."
$\mathbf{b}\notin\operatorname{Col}(A)$ "B is not in the column space of A, so A x equals b has no solution."
$S_1\cap S_2=\varnothing$ "The two solution sets have no common point."
$\min_{\mathbf{x}}\|A\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|^2$ "Find the least-squares approximation when the system has no exact solution."

Common verbal expressions include:

  • "the system is inconsistent";
  • "the system has no solution";
  • "the equations are incompatible";
  • "the constraints are infeasible";
  • "the equations produce a contradiction."

In elementary linear algebra, no solution and inconsistent are normally equivalent descriptions.

Defense Notation Advice: During elimination or row reduction, distinguish carefully between $0=0$ and $0=c$ with $c\neq0$. The first indicates redundancy; the second proves inconsistency. For matrix systems, use the rank test or column-space criterion when appropriate, and do not infer inconsistency from a singular determinant alone.
