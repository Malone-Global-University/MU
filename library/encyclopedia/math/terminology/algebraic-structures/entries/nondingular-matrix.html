Nonsingular Matrix

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

A nonsingular matrix is a square matrix that possesses a multiplicative inverse. In modern linear algebra, nonsingular and invertible are standard synonyms.

$$ \boxed{ \text{nonsingular matrix} = \text{invertible matrix} } $$

If $A$ is nonsingular, then there exists a unique matrix $A^{-1}$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I }. $$

The prefix non- means "not," while singular historically came to describe the exceptional case in which matrix inversion fails. Thus nonsingular literally means "not singular."

In computational and applied mathematics, the word invertible is often preferred because it states the central operational fact directly: the matrix represents a transformation that can be reversed.

For square matrices over a field:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ nonsingular} \iff \det(A)\neq0 }. $$

Operational Nuance

Core Definition

A square matrix $A$ is nonsingular when its inverse exists:

$$ \boxed{ A^{-1}\text{ exists} }. $$

Inverse Identity

$$ \boxed{ AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I_n }. $$

Uniqueness of the Inverse

If an inverse exists, it is unique.

Suppose $B$ and $C$ are both inverses of $A$. Then:

$$ B = BI = B(AC) = (BA)C = IC = C. $$

Determinant Criterion

For an $n\times n$ matrix over a field:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ nonsingular} \iff \det(A)\neq0 }. $$

Two-by-Two Example

Consider:

$$ A= \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1\\ 3 & 2 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Its determinant is:

$$ \det(A) = 2(2)-1(3) = 1. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ is nonsingular} }. $$

Two-by-Two Inverse Formula

For:

$$ A= \begin{bmatrix} a & b\\ c & d \end{bmatrix}, $$

if $ad-bc\neq0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad-bc} \begin{bmatrix} d & -b\\ -c & a \end{bmatrix} }. $$

Full-Rank Criterion

An $n\times n$ matrix is nonsingular exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{rank}(A)=n }. $$

Rank Language

A nonsingular square matrix has full rank.

Null-Space Criterion

A square matrix is nonsingular exactly when its null space contains only the zero vector:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Null}(A) = \{\mathbf{0}\} }. $$

Homogeneous-System Criterion

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0} \Longrightarrow \mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0} }. $$

Column Independence

The columns of a nonsingular square matrix are linearly independent:

$$ \boxed{ c_1\mathbf{a}_1+\cdots+c_n\mathbf{a}_n=\mathbf{0} \Longrightarrow c_1=\cdots=c_n=0 }. $$

Row Independence

The rows are also linearly independent.

Spanning Criterion

The columns of an $n\times n$ nonsingular matrix span $\mathbb{F}^n$.

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Col}(A)=\mathbb{F}^n }. $$

Basis Criterion

Because they are both independent and spanning, the columns of a nonsingular matrix form a basis for $\mathbb{F}^n$.

Pivot Criterion

A square matrix is nonsingular exactly when it has a pivot in every row and every column.

$$ \boxed{ \text{number of pivots}=n }. $$

RREF Criterion

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ nonsingular} \iff \operatorname{rref}(A)=I_n }. $$

Row-Reduction Inverse Test

To compute an inverse, augment with the identity:

$$ [A\mid I]. $$

If row reduction produces:

$$ \boxed{ [I\mid A^{-1}] }, $$

then $A$ is nonsingular.

Eigenvalue Criterion

A square matrix is nonsingular exactly when zero is not an eigenvalue:

$$ \boxed{ 0\notin\sigma(A) }. $$

Why Zero Cannot Be an Eigenvalue

If:

$$ A\mathbf{v}=0\mathbf{v} $$

for a nonzero vector $\mathbf{v}$, then $A$ would map a nonzero input to zero and therefore could not be one-to-one. A nonsingular matrix does not do this.

Nonzero Eigenvalues

Every eigenvalue of a nonsingular matrix is nonzero.

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ nonsingular} \Longrightarrow \lambda\neq0 \text{ for every eigenvalue }\lambda }. $$

Eigenvalues of the Inverse

If:

$$ A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}, \qquad \lambda\neq0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ A^{-1}\mathbf{v} = \frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbf{v} }. $$

Linear Transformation Interpretation

A square matrix $A$ defines:

$$ T_A(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x}. $$

The matrix is nonsingular exactly when this transformation is reversible.

One-to-One Criterion

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ nonsingular} \iff T_A\text{ is injective} }. $$

Onto Criterion

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ nonsingular} \iff T_A\text{ is surjective} }. $$

Bijective Criterion

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ nonsingular} \iff T_A\text{ is bijective} }. $$

Inverse Transformation

If:

$$ \mathbf{y}=A\mathbf{x}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x}=A^{-1}\mathbf{y} }. $$

Information Preservation

A nonsingular matrix does not collapse distinct vectors into the same output.

$$ \boxed{ A\mathbf{x}_1=A\mathbf{x}_2 \Longrightarrow \mathbf{x}_1=\mathbf{x}_2 }. $$

Proof by Inverse

From:

$$ A\mathbf{x}_1=A\mathbf{x}_2, $$

multiply by $A^{-1}$:

$$ A^{-1}A\mathbf{x}_1 = A^{-1}A\mathbf{x}_2, $$

yielding:

$$ \mathbf{x}_1=\mathbf{x}_2. $$

Linear Systems

If $A$ is nonsingular, then:

$$ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b} $$

has exactly one solution for every $\mathbf{b}$.

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x}=A^{-1}\mathbf{b} }. $$

Unique-Solution Criterion

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ nonsingular} \iff A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b} \text{ has a unique solution for every }\mathbf{b} }. $$

Geometric Interpretation

A nonsingular linear transformation may stretch, shrink, rotate, reflect, shear, or otherwise deform space, but it does not collapse dimension.

Dimension Preservation

Since:

$$ \operatorname{rank}(A)=n, $$

the image remains $n$-dimensional.

Determinant as Volume Scaling

The absolute determinant:

$$ |\det(A)| $$

gives the factor by which $n$-dimensional volume is scaled.

Nonzero Volume Scaling

For a nonsingular matrix:

$$ \boxed{ |\det(A)|>0 }. $$

Thus an $n$-dimensional region is not flattened into a lower-dimensional object.

Orientation

For real matrices:

  • $\det(A)>0$ preserves orientation;
  • $\det(A)<0$ reverses orientation.

Both cases are still nonsingular because the determinant is nonzero.

Cancellation Law

Nonsingular matrices can be cancelled from matrix equations.

Left Cancellation

If:

$$ AB=AC $$

and $A$ is nonsingular, then:

$$ \boxed{ B=C }. $$

Right Cancellation

If:

$$ BA=CA $$

and $A$ is nonsingular, then:

$$ \boxed{ B=C }. $$

Product of Nonsingular Matrices

If $A$ and $B$ are nonsingular square matrices of compatible size, then $AB$ is nonsingular.

$$ \boxed{ (AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1} }. $$

Determinant Proof

$$ \det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B). $$

If each determinant is nonzero, their product is nonzero.

Product Converse

If $AB$ is nonsingular, then both $A$ and $B$ are nonsingular.

$$ \boxed{ AB\text{ nonsingular} \Longrightarrow A\text{ and }B\text{ nonsingular} }. $$

Powers

Every integer power of a nonsingular matrix is defined.

$$ \boxed{ A^{-k} = (A^{-1})^k } $$

for positive integers $k$.

Positive Powers Remain Nonsingular

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ nonsingular} \Longrightarrow A^k\text{ nonsingular} }. $$

Transpose

If $A$ is nonsingular, then $A^T$ is nonsingular.

$$ \boxed{ (A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T }. $$

Conjugate Transpose

For complex matrices:

$$ \boxed{ (A^*)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^* }. $$

Determinant of the Inverse

$$ \boxed{ \det(A^{-1}) = \frac{1}{\det(A)} }. $$

Adjugate Formula

A nonsingular matrix satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(A)} \operatorname{adj}(A) }. $$

Diagonal Matrix

A diagonal matrix:

$$ D= \operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_n) $$

is nonsingular exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ d_i\neq0 \text{ for every }i }. $$

Diagonal Inverse

$$ \boxed{ D^{-1} = \operatorname{diag} \left( \frac{1}{d_1}, \ldots, \frac{1}{d_n} \right) }. $$

Triangular Matrix

An upper- or lower-triangular square matrix is nonsingular exactly when every diagonal entry is nonzero.

Triangular Determinant

$$ \boxed{ \det(A) = \prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{ii} }. $$

Orthogonal Matrices

A real orthogonal matrix $Q$ satisfies:

$$ Q^TQ=I. $$

Therefore every orthogonal matrix is nonsingular, with:

$$ \boxed{ Q^{-1}=Q^T }. $$

Unitary Matrices

A complex unitary matrix $U$ satisfies:

$$ U^*U=I, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ U^{-1}=U^* }. $$

Permutation Matrices

Every permutation matrix is nonsingular.

$$ \boxed{ P^{-1}=P^T }. $$

Elementary Matrices

Every elementary matrix is nonsingular because each elementary row operation is reversible.

Rotation Matrices

A rotation matrix is nonsingular because rotation preserves dimension and can be undone by an opposite rotation.

$$ \boxed{ R(\theta)^{-1} = R(-\theta) }. $$

Reflection Matrices

Reflection matrices are also nonsingular. Applying the same reflection twice returns the original vector.

$$ \boxed{ R^2=I \Longrightarrow R^{-1}=R }. $$

Scaling Matrices

A diagonal scaling matrix is nonsingular exactly when no scaling factor is zero.

Nonzero Scaling

$$ S= \begin{bmatrix} a & 0\\ 0 & b \end{bmatrix}, \qquad a,b\neq0 $$

has inverse:

$$ \boxed{ S^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/a & 0\\ 0 & 1/b \end{bmatrix} }. $$

Shear Matrices

Standard shear matrices are nonsingular because their determinant is $1$.

Example

$$ H= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & k\\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det(H)=1. $$

Similarity

If:

$$ B=P^{-1}AP $$

with $P$ nonsingular, then:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ nonsingular} \iff B\text{ nonsingular} }. $$

Similarity Preserves Eigenvalues

Since similar matrices share eigenvalues, they also agree on whether zero is an eigenvalue.

Diagonalization

If:

$$ A=PDP^{-1}, $$

then $A$ is nonsingular exactly when every diagonal entry of $D$ is nonzero.

Inverse after Diagonalization

$$ \boxed{ A^{-1} = PD^{-1}P^{-1} }. $$

General Linear Group

The collection of all nonsingular $n\times n$ matrices over a field $\mathbb{F}$ forms the general linear group:

$$ \boxed{ GL_n(\mathbb{F}) }. $$

Group Structure

Under matrix multiplication, $GL_n(\mathbb{F})$ contains:

  • an identity matrix;
  • an inverse for every element;
  • closure under multiplication;
  • associativity inherited from matrix multiplication.

Change of Basis

A matrix whose columns form a basis is necessarily nonsingular.

Consequently, every legitimate change-of-basis matrix is nonsingular.

Coordinate Transformation

If $P$ converts coordinates from one basis to another, then $P^{-1}$ converts them back.

Computer Graphics

Reversible geometric transformations such as nonzero scaling, rotations, reflections, and shears are represented by nonsingular matrices.

Robotics

Nonsingular transformation matrices permit reversible conversion between coordinate frames.

Statistics

Nonsingular covariance matrices have no exact linear dependence among the corresponding variables and can be inverted in procedures requiring precision matrices.

Precision Matrix

For a nonsingular covariance matrix $\Sigma$, the inverse:

$$ \boxed{ \Sigma^{-1} } $$

is called the precision matrix.

Regression

If a design matrix $X$ has full column rank, then:

$$ \boxed{ X^TX } $$

is nonsingular in the ordinary real least-squares setting.

Normal Equations

Under full-column-rank conditions:

$$ \boxed{ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (X^TX)^{-1}X^T\mathbf{y} }. $$

Optimization

A nonsingular Hessian can be inverted in Newton-type methods when the other assumptions of the method are satisfied.

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{x}_k - H(\mathbf{x}_k)^{-1} \nabla f(\mathbf{x}_k) }. $$

Differential Equations

Nonsingular coefficient matrices permit ordinary algebraic isolation of vector derivatives or state variables in many systems.

Numerical Linear Algebra

Exact nonsingularity does not guarantee that inversion or system solving will be numerically stable.

Ill-Conditioned but Nonsingular

A matrix can be nonsingular and nevertheless have a very large condition number.

$$ \boxed{ \kappa(A) = \|A\| \|A^{-1}\| }. $$

Condition Number Interpretation

A large condition number indicates that small perturbations may produce large relative changes in a computed solution.

Nonsingular Does Not Mean Well-Conditioned

$$ \boxed{ \text{nonsingular} \not\Longrightarrow \text{well-conditioned} }. $$

Smallest Singular Value

A square matrix is nonsingular exactly when every singular value is positive:

$$ \boxed{ \sigma_{\min}(A)>0 }. $$

Condition Number in the Two-Norm

For nonsingular $A$:

$$ \boxed{ \kappa_2(A) = \frac{\sigma_{\max}(A)} {\sigma_{\min}(A)} }. $$

Near Singularity

If $\sigma_{\min}(A)$ is very small but positive, $A$ is still nonsingular but may be close to singular in a numerical sense.

Determinant Magnitude Caveat

Although $\det(A)\neq0$ proves nonsingularity exactly, the magnitude of the determinant alone is not a reliable scale-independent measure of numerical conditioning.

Explicit Inverse Is Often Unnecessary

Even when $A$ is nonsingular, solving:

$$ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b} $$

by explicitly computing $A^{-1}$ is often less efficient than using a matrix factorization.

LU Factorization

Nonsingular matrices are commonly handled using factorizations such as LU, with pivoting when appropriate.

QR and Other Methods

Depending on structure and purpose, QR, Cholesky, SVD, or other decompositions may be preferable to direct inversion.

Positive-Definite Matrices

Every real symmetric positive-definite matrix is nonsingular.

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x}^TA\mathbf{x}>0 \text{ for }\mathbf{x}\neq0 \Longrightarrow A\text{ nonsingular} }. $$

Proof Idea

If $A\mathbf{x}=0$ for a nonzero $\mathbf{x}$, then:

$$ \mathbf{x}^TA\mathbf{x}=0, $$

contradicting positive definiteness.

Strict Diagonal Dominance

A strictly diagonally dominant square matrix is nonsingular.

$$ \boxed{ |a_{ii}| > \sum_{j\neq i}|a_{ij}| \text{ for every }i \Longrightarrow A\text{ nonsingular} }. $$

Invertible Matrix Theorem

The term nonsingular matrix participates in the broad family of equivalent conditions usually collected under the Invertible Matrix Theorem.

For an $n\times n$ matrix $A$, the following are equivalent:

  • $A$ is nonsingular;
  • $A$ is invertible;
  • $A^{-1}$ exists;
  • $\det(A)\neq0$;
  • $\operatorname{rank}(A)=n$;
  • $\operatorname{Null}(A)=\{\mathbf{0}\}$;
  • the columns are linearly independent;
  • the rows are linearly independent;
  • the columns span $\mathbb{F}^n$;
  • the columns form a basis;
  • there is a pivot in every row and column;
  • $\operatorname{rref}(A)=I_n$;
  • $0$ is not an eigenvalue;
  • $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ has exactly one solution for every $\mathbf{b}$;
  • $T_A$ is one-to-one;
  • $T_A$ is onto;
  • $T_A$ is bijective.

Nonsingular vs. Singular

These terms are logical opposites for square matrices:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A)\neq0 \Longleftrightarrow A\text{ nonsingular} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \det(A)=0 \Longleftrightarrow A\text{ singular} }. $$

Nonsingular vs. Nonzero Matrix

A matrix being nonzero does not imply that it is nonsingular.

For example:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2\\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} $$

is nonzero but singular.

Nonsingular vs. No Zero Entries

A matrix may contain zero entries and still be nonsingular.

The identity matrix is the simplest example.

Nonsingular vs. Positive Determinant

The determinant need not be positive. A negative determinant still proves nonsingularity.

$$ \boxed{ \det(A)<0 \Longrightarrow A\text{ may still be nonsingular} }. $$

Nonsingular vs. Diagonalizable

A nonsingular matrix need not be diagonalizable.

For example:

$$ A= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1\\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

has determinant $1$ and is nonsingular, but it is not diagonalizable over the usual field because it has only one linearly independent eigenvector.

Nonsingular vs. Symmetric

A nonsingular matrix need not be symmetric, and a symmetric matrix need not be nonsingular.

Nonsingular vs. Orthogonal

Every orthogonal matrix is nonsingular, but most nonsingular matrices are not orthogonal.

Nonsingular vs. Identity

The identity matrix is nonsingular, but nonsingularity does not mean the matrix equals the identity.

Rectangular-Matrix Caveat

The words singular and nonsingular are conventionally applied to square matrices.

Rectangular matrices are more precisely described by rank or by the existence of one-sided inverses.

Left Inverse

A tall matrix with full column rank may have a left inverse:

$$ LA=I. $$

Right Inverse

A wide matrix with full row rank may have a right inverse:

$$ AR=I. $$

Square Case

For square matrices over a field, the existence of either a left inverse or a right inverse implies full invertibility.

Common Error: Thinking Nonsingular Means Nonzero

Nonsingular means invertible, not merely different from the zero matrix.

Common Error: Assuming Every Square Matrix Is Nonsingular

Square shape is required for the ordinary two-sided inverse but does not guarantee that one exists.

Common Error: Assuming a Zero Entry Causes Singularity

Many nonsingular matrices contain zero entries.

Common Error: Assuming All Entries Must Be Nonzero

Entrywise nonzero status is not the criterion. Linear independence and determinant structure matter.

Common Error: Requiring a Positive Determinant

The determinant need only be nonzero.

$$ \boxed{ \det(A)\neq0 } $$

Common Error: Confusing Nonsingular with Orthogonal

Orthogonality is a stronger special condition.

Common Error: Confusing Nonsingular with Diagonalizable

Invertibility and diagonalizability are independent properties except for specific relationships involving eigenvalues.

Common Error: Assuming Nonsingularity Guarantees Numerical Stability

A nonsingular matrix may be extremely ill-conditioned.

Common Error: Using Determinant Magnitude as a Complete Conditioning Measure

A nonzero determinant establishes exact invertibility, but its magnitude alone does not fully describe numerical sensitivity.

Common Error: Explicitly Inverting Every Nonsingular Matrix to Solve a System

An inverse exists mathematically, but practical numerical methods often solve systems more efficiently through factorization.

Common Error: Calling a Rectangular Full-Rank Matrix Nonsingular without Qualification

Standard terminology reserves nonsingular for square matrices. Rectangular matrices should usually be described as full column rank or full row rank.

Common Error: Forgetting the Eigenvalue Test

A square matrix is nonsingular precisely when none of its eigenvalues is zero.

Common Error: Forgetting the Null-Space Test

A nonsingular matrix cannot send a nonzero vector to the zero vector.

Common Misconception: A nonsingular matrix is not merely a matrix with mostly nonzero entries or one that happens to have a determinant that is convenient to compute. It is precisely an invertible square matrix. Nonzero determinant, full rank, trivial null space, independent columns, complete pivots, absence of the eigenvalue zero, and bijective transformation behavior are all equivalent descriptions of the same structure.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$A^{-1}$ exists "A is nonsingular," or "A is invertible."
$\det(A)\neq0$ "The determinant of A is nonzero."
$\operatorname{rank}(A)=n$ "A has full rank."
$\operatorname{Null}(A)=\{\mathbf{0}\}$ "A has a trivial null space."
$0\notin\sigma(A)$ "Zero is not an eigenvalue of A."
$\operatorname{rref}(A)=I_n$ "A row-reduces to the identity matrix."
$A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ "The system has a unique solution for every b because A is nonsingular."

Nonsingular is usually pronounced as two semantic parts: "non-singular." In most modern linear algebra contexts, saying that a matrix is invertible communicates the same mathematical property more directly.

Thus:

$$ \det(A)\neq0 $$

may be summarized as:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"A is nonsingular."} } $$

or:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"A is invertible."} }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Use nonsingular primarily for square matrices. State $\det(A)\neq0$ when determinant information is central, and use full-rank or null-space language when those structures are more relevant. Remember that nonsingularity establishes existence of $A^{-1}$ but does not imply orthogonality, symmetry, diagonalizability, or good numerical conditioning.
