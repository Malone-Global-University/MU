Cis

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

In complex-number notation, cis is shorthand for:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{cis}\theta = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta }. $$

The letters cis come directly from:

$$ \boxed{ c=\cos,\qquad i=i,\qquad s=\sin }. $$

Thus $\operatorname{cis}\theta$ compresses the standard trigonometric form of a unit complex number into a single convenient expression.

The notation is especially useful in polar form, multiplication of complex numbers, powers, roots, and geometric rotations.

Operational Nuance

Core Definition

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{cis}\theta = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta }. $$

Here $i$ is the imaginary unit satisfying:

$$ \boxed{ i^2=-1 }. $$

Unit-Modulus Complex Number

Since:

$$ \cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1, $$

the modulus of $\operatorname{cis}\theta$ is:

$$ \boxed{ |\operatorname{cis}\theta|=1 }. $$

Complex-Plane Interpretation

The complex number:

$$ \operatorname{cis}\theta = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta $$

corresponds to the point:

$$ \boxed{ (\cos\theta,\sin\theta) } $$

on the unit circle in the complex plane.

Argument

The angle $\theta$ is an argument of $\operatorname{cis}\theta$.

$$ \boxed{ \arg(\operatorname{cis}\theta) \equiv \theta \pmod{2\pi} }. $$

Periodicity

Since sine and cosine repeat every $2\pi$:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{cis}(\theta+2\pi k) = \operatorname{cis}\theta, \qquad k\in\mathbb Z }. $$

Polar Form of a Complex Number

A nonzero complex number $z$ can be written as:

$$ \boxed{ z= r\operatorname{cis}\theta } $$

where:

$$ r=|z|>0 $$

and $\theta$ is an argument of $z$.

Expanded Polar Form

$$ \boxed{ z= r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta) }. $$

Cartesian Coordinates from Cis Form

If:

$$ z=r\operatorname{cis}\theta, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ x=r\cos\theta, \qquad y=r\sin\theta }. $$

Converting Cartesian Form to Cis Form

If:

$$ z=x+iy, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2} }. $$

An appropriate argument $\theta$ satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ \cos\theta=\frac{x}{r}, \qquad \sin\theta=\frac{y}{r} }. $$

Example

Consider:

$$ z=1+i. $$

Its modulus is:

$$ r=\sqrt{1^2+1^2}=\sqrt2. $$

Its principal argument is:

$$ \theta=\frac{\pi}{4}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ 1+i = \sqrt2\operatorname{cis}\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) }. $$

Euler's Formula

Euler's formula states:

$$ \boxed{ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta }. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{cis}\theta = e^{i\theta} }. $$

Cis Form versus Exponential Form

The expressions:

$$ r\operatorname{cis}\theta $$

and:

$$ re^{i\theta} $$

represent the same complex number.

Multiplication Rule

Using trigonometric addition formulas:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{cis}\alpha \operatorname{cis}\beta = \operatorname{cis}(\alpha+\beta) }. $$

Multiplication in Polar Form

If:

$$ z_1=r_1\operatorname{cis}\alpha $$

and:

$$ z_2=r_2\operatorname{cis}\beta, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ z_1z_2 = r_1r_2 \operatorname{cis}(\alpha+\beta) }. $$

Thus multiplication multiplies moduli and adds arguments.

Division Rule

For nonzero denominator:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\operatorname{cis}\alpha} {\operatorname{cis}\beta} = \operatorname{cis}(\alpha-\beta) }. $$

Division in Polar Form

$$ \boxed{ \frac{ r_1\operatorname{cis}\alpha }{ r_2\operatorname{cis}\beta } = \frac{r_1}{r_2} \operatorname{cis}(\alpha-\beta) }. $$

Complex Conjugate

Since:

$$ \overline{ \cos\theta+i\sin\theta } = \cos\theta-i\sin\theta, $$

we have:

$$ \boxed{ \overline{\operatorname{cis}\theta} = \operatorname{cis}(-\theta) }. $$

Reciprocal

Because the modulus is $1$:

$$ \boxed{ \frac1{\operatorname{cis}\theta} = \operatorname{cis}(-\theta) }. $$

De Moivre's Theorem

For integer $n$:

$$ \boxed{ [\operatorname{cis}\theta]^n = \operatorname{cis}(n\theta) }. $$

Equivalently:

$$ \boxed{ (\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^n = \cos(n\theta)+i\sin(n\theta) }. $$

Powers in Polar Form

If:

$$ z=r\operatorname{cis}\theta, $$

then for integer $n$:

$$ \boxed{ z^n = r^n\operatorname{cis}(n\theta) }. $$

Example: Squaring

If:

$$ z=2\operatorname{cis}\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right), $$

then:

$$ z^2 = 4\operatorname{cis}\left(\frac{2\pi}{3}\right). $$

Roots of Complex Numbers

To solve:

$$ w^n= r\operatorname{cis}\theta, $$

the $n$ distinct roots are:

$$ \boxed{ w_k = r^{1/n} \operatorname{cis} \left( \frac{\theta+2\pi k}{n} \right), \qquad k=0,1,\ldots,n-1 }. $$

Geometric Arrangement of Roots

These roots have equal modulus and arguments separated by:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{2\pi}{n} }. $$

Therefore they form the vertices of a regular $n$-gon centered at the origin.

Roots of Unity

The solutions to:

$$ z^n=1 $$

are:

$$ \boxed{ z_k= \operatorname{cis} \left( \frac{2\pi k}{n} \right), \qquad k=0,\ldots,n-1 }. $$

Rotation Interpretation

Multiplying a complex number by $\operatorname{cis}\theta$ rotates it through angle $\theta$ about the origin without changing its modulus.

$$ \boxed{ z \mapsto z\operatorname{cis}\theta }. $$

Rotation and Scaling

Multiplication by:

$$ r\operatorname{cis}\theta $$

performs two geometric operations:

  • scale distances from the origin by $r$,
  • rotate angles by $\theta$.

Matrix Connection

Multiplication by $\operatorname{cis}\theta$ corresponds to the rotation matrix:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta \end{bmatrix} }. $$

Special Values

$\theta$ $\operatorname{cis}\theta$
$0$ $1$
$\frac{\pi}{2}$ $i$
$\pi$ $-1$
$\frac{3\pi}{2}$ $-i$
$2\pi$ $1$

Euler Identity

Since:

$$ \operatorname{cis}\pi=-1, $$

Euler's identity can be written:

$$ \boxed{ e^{i\pi}+1=0 }. $$

Angle Units

In advanced mathematics, $\theta$ in $\operatorname{cis}\theta$ is normally understood in radians unless another unit is explicitly indicated.

Cis Is Not a Separate Elementary Function in the Same Sense as Sine

The notation $\operatorname{cis}$ is defined from cosine and sine:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{cis}\theta := \cos\theta+i\sin\theta }. $$

It is primarily a compact notational convenience.

Common Misconception: cis θ Means cos(i sin θ)

It does not represent nested function notation.

It is explicitly defined as:

$$ \boxed{ \cos\theta+i\sin\theta }. $$

Common Misconception: i Is Part of the Angle

In $\operatorname{cis}\theta$, $i$ multiplies $\sin\theta$. It is the imaginary unit, not part of the angle $\theta$.

Common Misconception: cis Is Different from e to the iθ

Euler's formula gives:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{cis}\theta=e^{i\theta} }. $$

Common Misconception: The Argument Is Unique

Because:

$$ \operatorname{cis}(\theta+2\pi k) = \operatorname{cis}\theta, $$

a nonzero complex number generally has infinitely many arguments differing by integer multiples of $2\pi$.

Common Misconception: Multiplication Adds Moduli

In polar form, multiplication multiplies moduli and adds arguments:

$$ \boxed{ (r_1,\alpha)(r_2,\beta) \longrightarrow (r_1r_2,\alpha+\beta) }. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\operatorname{cis}\theta$ "Cis theta."
$\operatorname{cis}\theta=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$ "Cis theta equals cosine theta plus i sine theta."
$z=r\operatorname{cis}\theta$ "z equals r cis theta."
$\operatorname{cis}\alpha\operatorname{cis}\beta=\operatorname{cis}(\alpha+\beta)$ "Cis alpha times cis beta equals cis of alpha plus beta."
$[\operatorname{cis}\theta]^n=\operatorname{cis}(n\theta)$ "Cis theta to the nth power equals cis n theta."
$\operatorname{cis}\theta=e^{i\theta}$ "Cis theta equals e to the i theta."

The notation is usually typeset as an operator, $\operatorname{cis}\theta$, rather than as the product of separate variables $c$, $i$, and $s$.

Defense Notation Advice: Define $\operatorname{cis}\theta$ before using it in a context where readers may not know the convention. Remember that arguments are periodic modulo $2\pi$, and use radians unless another angle unit is explicitly stated.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{cis}\theta = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta }. $$
  • Unit Modulus: $$ \boxed{ |\operatorname{cis}\theta|=1 }. $$
  • Unit-Circle Point: $\operatorname{cis}\theta$ corresponds to $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$.
  • Polar Form: $$ \boxed{ z=r\operatorname{cis}\theta }. $$
  • Euler Form: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{cis}\theta=e^{i\theta} }. $$
  • Multiplication: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{cis}\alpha \operatorname{cis}\beta = \operatorname{cis}(\alpha+\beta) }. $$
  • Division: $$ \boxed{ \frac{\operatorname{cis}\alpha} {\operatorname{cis}\beta} = \operatorname{cis}(\alpha-\beta) }. $$
  • Conjugate: $$ \boxed{ \overline{\operatorname{cis}\theta} = \operatorname{cis}(-\theta) }. $$
  • De Moivre: $$ \boxed{ [\operatorname{cis}\theta]^n = \operatorname{cis}(n\theta) }. $$
  • nth Roots: $$ \boxed{ w_k= r^{1/n} \operatorname{cis} \left( \frac{\theta+2\pi k}{n} \right) }. $$
  • Roots of Unity: $$ \boxed{ z_k= \operatorname{cis} \left( \frac{2\pi k}{n} \right) }. $$
  • Geometric Meaning: Multiplication by $\operatorname{cis}\theta$ rotates a complex number through angle $\theta$ without changing its modulus.
  • Periodicity: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{cis}(\theta+2\pi k) = \operatorname{cis}\theta }. $$
  • Common Error: Do not interpret cis as three multiplied variables.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse $\operatorname{cis}\theta$ with $\cos(i\sin\theta)$.
  • Common Error: Do not treat the argument of a nonzero complex number as unique without selecting a principal branch.
  • Common Error: Do not add moduli when multiplying complex numbers in polar form.
  • Terminological Contrast: Cis abbreviates $\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$. Polar form writes a complex number as $r\operatorname{cis}\theta$. Exponential form writes the same number as $re^{i\theta}$. Modulus gives the distance from the origin. Argument gives the angular direction. De Moivre's Theorem describes powers of cis expressions. Euler's formula identifies $\operatorname{cis}\theta$ with $e^{i\theta}$.
