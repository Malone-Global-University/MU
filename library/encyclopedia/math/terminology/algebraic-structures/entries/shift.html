Shift

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

A shift is a transformation that moves the position, indexing, or argument of a mathematical object without fundamentally changing its underlying shape or structural pattern.

For functions, the most common shifts are horizontal and vertical translations:

$$ \boxed{ g(x)=f(x-h)+k }. $$

Here $h$ controls the horizontal shift and $k$ controls the vertical shift.

The ordinary word shift means to move from one position to another. Mathematics preserves this idea in graph transformations, sequences, operators, coordinates, signals, and periodic phenomena.

Operational Nuance

Vertical Shift

Adding a constant outside a function moves every output by that amount:

$$ \boxed{ g(x)=f(x)+k }. $$
  • If $k>0$, the graph shifts upward.
  • If $k<0$, the graph shifts downward.

Horizontal Shift

Replacing $x$ by $x-h$ produces:

$$ \boxed{ g(x)=f(x-h) }. $$

This shifts the graph horizontally by $h$ units:

  • If $h>0$, the graph shifts right.
  • If $h<0$, the graph shifts left.

Why the Horizontal Sign Looks Reversed

Suppose a point on the original graph satisfies $y=f(a)$. For:

$$ g(x)=f(x-h), $$

the same output occurs when:

$$ x-h=a, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ x=a+h }. $$

Thus subtracting $h$ inside the function moves the graph right by $h$.

Combined Shift

$$ \boxed{ g(x)=f(x-h)+k } $$

moves the graph right by $h$ and up by $k$ when $h,k>0$.

Coordinate Interpretation

Under the transformation:

$$ g(x)=f(x-h)+k, $$

a point:

$$ (x,y) $$

on the original graph corresponds to:

$$ \boxed{ (x+h,y+k) } $$

on the shifted graph.

Example

Starting from:

$$ f(x)=x^2, $$

the function:

$$ \boxed{ g(x)=(x-3)^2+2 } $$

shifts the parabola $3$ units right and $2$ units upward.

Shift of a Parent Function

Many transformed functions are interpreted relative to a parent function:

$$ \boxed{ y=a\,f(b(x-h))+k }. $$

The parameters $h$ and $k$ represent shifts, while $a$ and $b$ control scaling and reflection.

Phase Shift

For periodic functions, a horizontal shift is often called a phase shift. For example:

$$ y=\sin(x-h) $$

is the sine graph shifted horizontally by $h$.

Shift in Sequences

A sequence may be shifted by changing its index. If $(a_n)$ is a sequence, then:

$$ \boxed{ b_n=a_{n+1} } $$

is an index-shifted sequence that uses the next term of the original sequence at each index.

Shift Operator

In discrete mathematics and difference equations, a shift operator may be defined by:

$$ \boxed{ (Ea)_n=a_{n+1} }. $$

Different fields may use different notation and may define forward or backward shifts instead.

Translation

In geometry, a shift of position with no rotation or scaling is commonly called a translation.

$$ \boxed{ (x,y)\mapsto(x+h,y+k) }. $$

Domain and Range Effects

A horizontal shift translates the domain:

$$ \operatorname{Dom}(f(x-h)) = \{ x:x-h\in\operatorname{Dom}(f) \}. $$

A vertical shift translates the range by $k$.

Zeros under a Horizontal Shift

If $f(r)=0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ f(x-h)=0 \quad\text{at}\quad x=r+h }. $$

Thus roots move horizontally with the graph.

Asymptotes under Shifts

Shifts also move asymptotes. If $y=L$ is a horizontal asymptote of $f$, then $y=L+k$ is the corresponding horizontal asymptote of $f(x-h)+k$.

Likewise, a vertical asymptote $x=c$ moves to:

$$ \boxed{ x=c+h }. $$

Common Misconception

The sign inside a function transformation is interpreted opposite the apparent arithmetic direction: $f(x-4)$ shifts right $4$, while $f(x+4)$ shifts left $4$.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$f(x)+k$ "F shifted vertically by k units."
$f(x-h)$ "F shifted horizontally h units to the right," when h is positive.
$f(x+h)$ "F shifted h units to the left," when h is positive.
$f(x-h)+k$ "F shifted right by h and vertically by k."
$(x,y)\mapsto(x+h,y+k)$ "Translate each point by h horizontally and k vertically."
$b_n=a_{n+1}$ "B sub n is the sequence obtained by shifting the index forward by one."

The phrase shift right by $h$ corresponds to:

$$ \boxed{ f(x-h) } $$

rather than $f(x+h)$.

Defense Notation Advice: Separate inside changes from outside changes. Outside addition changes outputs directly, so its sign is visually intuitive. Inside changes act on the input required to reproduce an old output, so their apparent sign reverses. When discussing sequences or operators, state whether the shift moves indices forward or backward because conventions vary.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: Movement of a mathematical object from one position, input location, or index to another.
  • Vertical Function Shift: $f(x)+k$ translates outputs by $k$.
  • Horizontal Function Shift: $f(x-h)$ translates inputs geometrically by $h$.
  • Right Shift: $f(x-h)$ for $h>0$.
  • Left Shift: $f(x+h)$ for $h>0$.
  • Upward Shift: $f(x)+k$ for $k>0$.
  • Downward Shift: $f(x)-k$ for $k>0$.
  • Combined Translation: $f(x-h)+k$ shifts horizontally and vertically.
  • Parent Functions: Shifts are basic transformations used to generate function families.
  • Trigonometry: Horizontal shifts of periodic functions are commonly described as phase shifts.
  • Geometry: Coordinate shifts correspond to translations.
  • Sequences: Index replacement such as $a_n\mapsto a_{n+1}$ creates a shifted sequence.
  • Difference Equations: Shift operators encode movement between neighboring indices or time steps.
  • Signals: $f(t-\tau)$ represents a time shift or delay by $\tau$ under the usual convention.
  • Domain: Horizontal shifts translate allowable input locations.
  • Range: Vertical shifts translate output values.
  • Roots: Horizontal shifts move zeros by the same geometric displacement.
  • Asymptotes: Horizontal and vertical asymptotes translate with the corresponding graph shifts.
  • Shape Preservation: Pure shifts preserve shape, size, and orientation.
  • Common Error: Reading $f(x-h)$ as a shift left by $h$.
  • Common Error: Treating inside and outside transformations as though they follow the same sign convention.
  • Common Error: Confusing a shift with a stretch, compression, or reflection.
  • Common Error: Assuming all fields use the same convention for forward and backward shift operators.
  • Core Interpretation: A shift preserves an object's basic pattern while changing its location or indexing. For functions: $$ \boxed{ g(x)=f(x-h)+k } $$ represents a horizontal displacement by $h$ and a vertical displacement by $k$. The same structural idea extends to coordinate translations, periodic phase changes, sequence indices, time delays, and discrete shift operators.
