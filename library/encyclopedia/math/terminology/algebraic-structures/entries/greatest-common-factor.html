Greatest Common Factor

Subfield: Foundations & Logic

Etymology & Roots

The greatest common factor of two or more integers is the largest positive integer that divides every one of them without remainder.

For positive integers $a$ and $b$, the greatest common factor is commonly written:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{GCF}(a,b) }. $$

In more advanced mathematics, the more common notation is:

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(a,b) }, $$

read greatest common divisor.

The words in the phrase describe its logic directly:

  • factor means a number that divides another exactly;
  • common means the factor belongs to every number under consideration;
  • greatest means the largest positive such factor.

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ d=\gcd(a,b) } $$

means:

$$ \boxed{ d\mid a,\qquad d\mid b, \qquad \text{and every common divisor of }a,b\text{ divides }d }. $$

The phrases greatest common factor, greatest common divisor, and highest common factor usually denote the same underlying integer concept, though usage varies by educational tradition and region.

Operational Nuance

Basic Example

Consider $12$ and $18$.

The positive factors of $12$ are:

$$ 1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 4,\ 6,\ 12. $$

The positive factors of $18$ are:

$$ 1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 6,\ 9,\ 18. $$

Their common factors are:

$$ 1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 6. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{GCF}(12,18)=6 }. $$

Divisibility Definition

A positive integer $d$ is the GCF of $a$ and $b$ if:

$$ d\mid a \qquad\text{and}\qquad d\mid b, $$

and no larger positive integer has both properties.

Common Factor

A number $c$ is a common factor of $a$ and $b$ when:

$$ \boxed{ c\mid a \qquad\text{and}\qquad c\mid b }. $$

Greatest Common Factor

Among all positive common factors, the GCF is the largest.

Prime Factorization Method

Prime factorization gives a systematic method for finding a GCF.

Suppose:

$$ 84=2^2\cdot3\cdot7, $$
$$ 126=2\cdot3^2\cdot7. $$

Take each prime appearing in both factorizations with the smaller exponent:

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(84,126) = 2^1\cdot3^1\cdot7^1 = 42 }. $$

Prime-Exponent Formula

If:

$$ a=\prod_p p^{\alpha_p}, \qquad b=\prod_p p^{\beta_p}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(a,b) = \prod_p p^{\min(\alpha_p,\beta_p)} }. $$

Minimum Exponents

The GCF takes the smallest exponent of each prime appearing in the numbers being compared.

Example with Unequal Powers

Let:

$$ 360=2^3\cdot3^2\cdot5, $$
$$ 600=2^3\cdot3\cdot5^2. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(360,600) = 2^3\cdot3\cdot5 = 120 }. $$

Factor-Listing Method

For relatively small integers, list all positive factors of each number and select the largest one common to every list.

Factor Trees

Factor trees can be used to obtain prime factorizations before extracting the common prime factors.

Repeated Common Division

Another elementary technique repeatedly divides all numbers by shared prime factors until no further common prime factor remains. The product of the shared divisors is the GCF.

Euclidean Algorithm

For larger integers, the Euclidean algorithm is often more efficient.

If:

$$ a=bq+r, \qquad 0\leq r<b, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(a,b)=\gcd(b,r) }. $$

Euclidean Algorithm Example

Find:

$$ \gcd(252,105). $$

Divide:

$$ 252=2(105)+42. $$

Then:

$$ 105=2(42)+21. $$

Finally:

$$ 42=2(21)+0. $$

The last nonzero remainder is:

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(252,105)=21 }. $$

Why the Euclidean Algorithm Works

If:

$$ a=bq+r, $$

then any common divisor of $a$ and $b$ also divides:

$$ r=a-bq. $$

Conversely, any common divisor of $b$ and $r$ divides:

$$ a=bq+r. $$

Therefore the sets of common divisors coincide.

Extended Euclidean Algorithm

The extended Euclidean algorithm finds integers $x$ and $y$ satisfying:

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(a,b) = ax+by }. $$

This is Bézout's identity.

Bézout Identity

For integers $a$ and $b$, not both zero, there exist integers $x$ and $y$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ ax+by=\gcd(a,b) }. $$

Example of Bézout Representation

Since:

$$ \gcd(18,30)=6, $$

one representation is:

$$ \boxed{ 6=2(18)-1(30) }. $$

Coprime Integers

Two integers are coprime or relatively prime when:

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(a,b)=1 }. $$

Coprime Does Not Mean Individually Prime

For example:

$$ \gcd(8,15)=1, $$

even though both $8$ and $15$ are composite.

GCF of Identical Positive Integers

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(a,a)=a } $$

for $a>0$.

GCF with One

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(a,1)=1 }. $$

GCF When One Number Divides the Other

If:

$$ a\mid b, \qquad a>0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(a,b)=a }. $$

Example

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(8,40)=8 }. $$

GCF and Zero

For nonzero integer $a$:

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(a,0)=|a| }. $$

Every divisor of $a$ also divides $0$, so the greatest positive common divisor is $|a|$.

GCF of Zero and Zero

The expression:

$$ \gcd(0,0) $$

is commonly left undefined because every positive integer divides both zeros, so there is no greatest positive common divisor.

Negative Integers

Standard GCD notation uses a nonnegative result:

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(a,b) = \gcd(|a|,|b|) }. $$

More than Two Numbers

The GCF extends naturally to several integers:

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(a,b,c) = \gcd(\gcd(a,b),c) }. $$

Example with Three Numbers

$$ 24=2^3\cdot3, $$
$$ 36=2^2\cdot3^2, $$
$$ 60=2^2\cdot3\cdot5. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(24,36,60) = 2^2\cdot3 = 12 }. $$

Associativity of GCD

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(a,\gcd(b,c)) = \gcd(\gcd(a,b),c) }. $$

Commutativity of GCD

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(a,b) = \gcd(b,a) }. $$

Idempotent Property

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(a,a)=|a| }. $$

GCF and Least Common Multiple

For positive integers $a$ and $b$:

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(a,b) \operatorname{lcm}(a,b) = ab }. $$

LCM from the GCF

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{lcm}(a,b) = \frac{ab}{\gcd(a,b)} }. $$

Prime-Exponent Contrast with LCM

For prime factorizations:

$$ \boxed{ \gcd \rightarrow \text{minimum exponents} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{lcm} \rightarrow \text{maximum exponents} }. $$

Fraction Reduction

The GCF gives the largest factor that can be canceled from both numerator and denominator.

For:

$$ \frac{42}{56}, $$

one has:

$$ \gcd(42,56)=14. $$

Therefore:

$$ \frac{42}{56} = \frac{42\div14}{56\div14} = \boxed{ \frac{3}{4} }. $$

Lowest Terms

A fraction:

$$ \frac{a}{b} $$

is in lowest terms precisely when:

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(a,b)=1 }. $$

Ratio Simplification

Ratios can be simplified by dividing all terms by their GCF.

For example:

$$ 18:30 = \boxed{ 3:5 }, $$

because $\gcd(18,30)=6$.

Grouping Applications

GCF problems often ask for the largest equal-size grouping that divides several quantities exactly.

If there are $24$ red objects and $36$ blue objects and every group must contain the same number of each color without leftovers, the maximum number of equal groups is:

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(24,36)=12 }. $$

Measurement Applications

The GCF can determine the largest common unit length that measures multiple integer lengths exactly.

Example

For lengths $48$ and $60$ units:

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(48,60)=12 }. $$

Thus $12$ is the largest integer unit that divides both lengths exactly.

Rectangular Tiling

For an integer-sided rectangle of dimensions $a$ by $b$, the largest square tile with integer side length that fits exactly has side length:

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(a,b) }. $$

Example

A $24$ by $36$ rectangle can be tiled by largest possible integer-sided squares of side:

$$ \boxed{12}. $$

Euclidean Geometry Connection

The Euclidean algorithm historically has a geometric interpretation involving repeated measurement of lengths by smaller common measures.

Modular Arithmetic

The GCD determines whether an integer has a multiplicative inverse modulo $n$.

$$ \boxed{ a \text{ is invertible modulo }n \iff \gcd(a,n)=1 }. $$

Linear Congruences

The congruence:

$$ ax\equiv b\pmod n $$

is solvable precisely when:

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(a,n)\mid b }. $$

Diophantine Equations

The linear equation:

$$ ax+by=c $$

has integer solutions exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(a,b)\mid c }. $$

Relatively Prime Factors

If:

$$ \gcd(a,b)=1, $$

then $a$ and $b$ share no prime factors.

Euclid's Lemma Connection

If:

$$ \gcd(a,b)=1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad a\mid bc, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a\mid c }. $$

Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic Connection

Unique prime factorization guarantees that the minimum-exponent rule for the GCF is well defined.

GCF of Polynomial Terms

In algebra, the phrase greatest common factor is also used for monomials and polynomial expressions.

For example:

$$ 12x^3y^2 \qquad\text{and}\qquad 18x^2y^5. $$

The numerical GCF is:

$$ \gcd(12,18)=6. $$

For each variable, take the smaller exponent:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{GCF} \left( 12x^3y^2,\, 18x^2y^5 \right) = 6x^2y^2 }. $$

Factoring Expressions

The GCF is commonly extracted first when factoring a polynomial.

Example:

$$ 12x^3+18x^2 = \boxed{ 6x^2(2x+3) }. $$

Why Factor Out the GCF First

Removing the GCF simplifies the remaining expression and exposes additional factorization patterns more clearly.

Polynomial GCD

In more advanced algebra, polynomials themselves have greatest common divisors. For example, over a field:

$$ \gcd(f(x),g(x)) $$

is defined up to multiplication by a nonzero constant unless a normalization, such as choosing a monic GCD, is imposed.

Integer GCF vs. Polynomial GCD

The elementary integer GCF is a positive integer. In polynomial rings, units and normalization make the uniqueness convention slightly different.

GCF as a Lattice Operation

Under divisibility, $\gcd(a,b)$ can be interpreted as the greatest lower bound of $a$ and $b$ in the divisibility order.

Divisibility Order

If one defines:

$$ a\preceq b \iff a\mid b, $$

then the GCD acts as a meet operation on positive integers.

Common Error: Choosing the Largest Factor of Either Number

The GCF must divide every number being compared. The largest factor of one number alone is irrelevant unless it also divides the others.

Common Error: Confusing GCF and LCM

The GCF is a common divisor. The LCM is a common multiple.

$$ \boxed{ \text{GCF} \rightarrow \text{largest common divisor} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{LCM} \rightarrow \text{smallest positive common multiple} }. $$

Common Error: Using Maximum Prime Exponents

Maximum exponents belong to LCM calculations. GCF calculations use minimum exponents.

Common Error: Multiplying All Common Factors Together

The GCF is not the product of every distinct common factor listed. It is the single largest positive factor common to all numbers.

Common Error: Assuming Relatively Prime Means Both Numbers Are Prime

Composite numbers can be relatively prime if they share no prime factor.

Common Error: Forgetting the Positive Convention

Although both $d$ and $-d$ divide the same integers, the ordinary greatest common divisor is defined to be nonnegative.

Common Error: Saying $\gcd(0,0)=0$ without a Convention

Many elementary treatments leave $\gcd(0,0)$ undefined because there is no greatest positive common divisor. Some algebraic or computational conventions may define it as $0$, so the convention should be stated when relevant.

Common Error: Confusing a Common Factor with the Greatest Common Factor

For $12$ and $18$, both $2$ and $3$ are common factors, but:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{GCF}(12,18)=6 }. $$

Common Misconception: The greatest common factor is not found by selecting whichever factor looks largest in one number. It is the largest positive integer that divides every number in the collection. Prime factorization reveals it by taking shared primes with their smallest exponents, while the Euclidean algorithm finds the same quantity efficiently without requiring full factorization.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\operatorname{GCF}(12,18)=6$ "The greatest common factor of twelve and eighteen is six."
$\gcd(12,18)=6$ "The greatest common divisor of twelve and eighteen is six."
$d\mid a$ "D divides a."
$\gcd(a,b)=1$ "A and b are relatively prime," or "a and b are coprime."
$\gcd(a,b)=ax+by$ "The greatest common divisor is an integer linear combination of a and b."
$\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)=ab/\gcd(a,b)$ "The least common multiple equals a b divided by their greatest common divisor."
$\gcd(a,0)=|a|$ "The greatest common divisor of a and zero is the absolute value of a."

The abbreviation GCF is especially common in elementary and middle-school mathematics in the United States. The abbreviation GCD is more common in number theory, abstract algebra, and university-level mathematics.

Another common regional term is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{HCF} = \text{highest common factor} }. $$

These terms usually refer to the same positive integer.

Defense Notation Advice: Use $\gcd(a,b)$ in advanced mathematical writing because it is the most broadly standardized notation. When using GCF in elementary contexts, define whether the inputs are integers, monomials, or polynomials. For prime-factor methods, take the minimum exponent of every shared prime; for LCM, take the maximum.

  • Abstract Algebra: GCD concepts extend to integral domains where divisibility is defined.

  • Algorithmic Mathematics: Euclidean GCD computation is among the oldest important algorithms.

  • Core Interpretation: The greatest common factor is the largest positive quantity that divides every integer under consideration: $$ \boxed{ d=\gcd(a,b) } $$ when $d\mid a$, $d\mid b$, and every other common divisor divides $d$.
