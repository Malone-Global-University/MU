Geometric Series

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

A geometric series is the sum of the terms of a geometric sequence. The adjective geometric refers to the constant-ratio structure of the underlying sequence, while series indicates that the terms are being added.

For example, the geometric sequence:

$$ 3,\ 6,\ 12,\ 24,\ldots $$

gives the geometric series:

$$ 3+6+12+24+\cdots. $$

The word series is therefore not interchangeable with sequence. A sequence is an ordered list of terms, while a series represents the addition of those terms.

Geometric series have been studied for centuries because they arise naturally in repeated proportional change, subdivision, compound growth, repeating decimals, and infinite processes. They provide one of the simplest and most important examples of an infinite series whose convergence can be determined exactly.

Operational Nuance

A geometric sequence with first term $a_1$ and common ratio $r$ has terms:

$$ a_1,\quad a_1r,\quad a_1r^2,\quad a_1r^3,\ldots $$

Adding these terms produces a geometric series:

$$ a_1+a_1r+a_1r^2+\cdots. $$

Finite Geometric Series

The sum of the first $n$ terms is:

$$ S_n = a_1+a_1r+a_1r^2+\cdots+a_1r^{n-1}. $$

When:

$$ r\neq1, $$

the finite geometric-series formula is:

$$ \boxed{ S_n = a_1\frac{1-r^n}{1-r} }. $$

An equivalent version is:

$$ \boxed{ S_n = a_1\frac{r^n-1}{r-1} }. $$

For example:

$$ 2+6+18+54+162 $$

is a finite geometric series with:

$$ a_1=2, \qquad r=3, \qquad n=5. $$

Therefore:

$$ S_5 = 2\frac{1-3^5}{1-3} $$ $$ = 2\frac{1-243}{-2} $$ $$ =242. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ 2+6+18+54+162=242 }. $$

Why the Finite Formula Works

Begin with:

$$ S_n = a_1+a_1r+a_1r^2+\cdots+a_1r^{n-1}. $$

Multiply by $r$:

$$ rS_n = a_1r+a_1r^2+\cdots+a_1r^{n}. $$

Subtract:

$$ S_n-rS_n = a_1-a_1r^n. $$

Factor:

$$ S_n(1-r) = a_1(1-r^n). $$

Therefore:

$$ S_n = a_1\frac{1-r^n}{1-r}. $$

Infinite Geometric Series

An infinite geometric series has the form:

$$ a_1+a_1r+a_1r^2+a_1r^3+\cdots. $$

Its partial sums are:

$$ S_n = a_1\frac{1-r^n}{1-r}. $$

If:

$$ |r|<1, $$

then:

$$ r^n\to0. $$

Consequently:

$$ \boxed{ S_\infty = \frac{a_1}{1-r} } $$

for:

$$ \boxed{|r|<1}. $$

For example:

$$ 1+\frac12+\frac14+\frac18+\cdots $$

has:

$$ a_1=1, \qquad r=\frac12. $$

Since:

$$ \left|\frac12\right|<1, $$

the series converges and:

$$ S_\infty = \frac{1}{1-\frac12} = 2. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ 1+\frac12+\frac14+\frac18+\cdots=2 }. $$

Common Misconception: The infinite-sum formula:

$$ \frac{a_1}{1-r} $$

cannot be used for every geometric series. It applies only when:

$$ |r|<1. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$S_n$ "S sub n," or "the nth partial sum."
$a_1$ "A sub one," or "the first term."
$r$ "R," or "the common ratio."
$S_n=a_1\frac{1-r^n}{1-r}$ "S sub n equals the first term times one minus r to the nth power over one minus r."
$|r|<1$ "The absolute value of r is less than one."
$S_\infty=\frac{a_1}{1-r}$ "The infinite sum equals the first term divided by one minus the common ratio."

Sigma notation provides a compact form for a finite geometric series:

$$ \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_1r^k. $$

An infinite geometric series may be written:

$$ \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_1r^k. $$

When:

$$ |r|<1, $$

this becomes:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_1r^k = \frac{a_1}{1-r} }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Some texts use $a$ instead of $a_1$ for the first term:

$$ a+ar+ar^2+\cdots. $$

In that notation:

$$ S_n=a\frac{1-r^n}{1-r} $$

and:

$$ S_\infty=\frac{a}{1-r}, \qquad |r|<1. $$

The underlying formulas are identical; only the symbol used for the first term has changed.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Geometric Sequence vs. Geometric Series: A geometric sequence lists: $$ a_1,\ a_1r,\ a_1r^2,\ldots $$ whereas a geometric series adds: $$ a_1+a_1r+a_1r^2+\cdots. $$ A sequence lists; a series sums.
  • Geometric Progression: A geometric progression is an alternate name for a geometric sequence. Its sum is therefore a geometric series.
  • Finite Sum: For: $$ r\neq1, $$ $$ \boxed{ S_n = a_1\frac{1-r^n}{1-r} }. $$ This formula is valid regardless of whether $|r|$ is less than, equal to, or greater than $1$, provided the series contains only finitely many terms.
  • Special Case r = 1: If: $$ r=1, $$ every term equals $a_1$, so: $$ \boxed{ S_n=na_1 }. $$ The usual finite geometric formula cannot be used directly because its denominator would be zero.
  • Infinite Convergence Condition: An infinite geometric series converges exactly when: $$ \boxed{|r|<1} $$ apart from the trivial case $a_1=0$.
  • Infinite Sum: When: $$ |r|<1, $$ $$ \boxed{ S_\infty=\frac{a_1}{1-r} }. $$
  • Why |r| < 1 Matters: The finite sum contains the term: $$ r^n. $$ The infinite limit works because: $$ |r|<1 \Longrightarrow r^n\to0. $$
  • Ratio Greater Than or Equal to 1: If: $$ r\ge1 $$ and $a_1\neq0$, the terms fail to approach zero, so the infinite series diverges.
  • Ratio Less Than or Equal to -1: If: $$ r\le-1 $$ and $a_1\neq0$, the terms either oscillate without shrinking or grow in magnitude. The infinite series therefore diverges.
  • Negative Convergent Ratio: A geometric series may converge even when signs alternate. For example: $$ 1-\frac12+\frac14-\frac18+\cdots $$ has: $$ r=-\frac12. $$ Since: $$ |r|=\frac12<1, $$ its sum is: $$ \frac{1}{1+\frac12} = \frac23. $$
  • Partial Sums: An infinite series is understood through its partial sums: $$ S_1,\ S_2,\ S_3,\ldots. $$ The series converges to $S$ when: $$ \lim_{n\to\infty}S_n=S. $$
  • Necessary Condition for Convergence: Any convergent infinite series must have terms approaching zero: $$ a_n\to0. $$ For a geometric sequence: $$ a_n=a_1r^{n-1}, $$ this occurs for nonzero $a_1$ precisely when: $$ |r|<1. $$
  • Repeating Decimals: Repeating decimals can be expressed as infinite geometric series. For example: $$ 0.333\ldots = \frac3{10} + \frac3{100} + \frac3{1000} +\cdots. $$ Here: $$ a_1=\frac3{10}, \qquad r=\frac1{10}, $$ so: $$ 0.333\ldots = \frac{\frac3{10}}{1-\frac1{10}} = \frac13. $$
  • Another Repeating Decimal: The identity: $$ 0.999\ldots=1 $$ follows from: $$ 0.9+0.09+0.009+\cdots $$ with: $$ a_1=0.9, \qquad r=0.1. $$ Therefore: $$ \frac{0.9}{1-0.1}=1. $$
  • Compound Interest: Geometric sequences describe balances under proportional growth, while geometric series appear when repeated payments, deposits, or cash flows are accumulated over multiple periods.
  • Annuities: Many annuity formulas are derived from finite geometric series because equal payments are multiplied by successive powers of an interest-growth factor.
  • Present Value: Discounting future payments by a constant rate produces terms involving powers of a discount factor. Their sum is therefore often geometric.
  • Repeated Subdivision: If a length, area, or quantity is repeatedly reduced by the same proportion, the accumulated pieces may form an infinite geometric series.
  • Fractal Constructions: Geometric series frequently arise when calculating total lengths, areas, or volumes in recursively constructed geometric objects.
  • Probability: Repeated independent processes may produce probabilities of the form: $$ p,\ pq,\ pq^2,\ldots, $$ whose total is evaluated using a geometric series when $|q|<1$.
  • Calculus & Analysis: The geometric series: $$ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^n = \frac{1}{1-x}, \qquad |x|<1, $$ is foundational in the study of power series and provides a model for more advanced convergence arguments.
  • Power Series: The geometric series is the simplest major example of a power series: $$ 1+x+x^2+x^3+\cdots. $$ Its interval of convergence is: $$ |x|<1. $$
  • Arithmetic Series Contrast: Arithmetic series add terms whose consecutive differences are constant. Geometric series add terms whose consecutive ratios are constant. Their formulas and infinite convergence behavior are fundamentally different.
  • Terminological Contrast: A geometric sequence or geometric progression is an ordered constant-ratio pattern. A geometric series is the sum of those terms. A finite geometric series always has a finite sum, while an infinite geometric series has a finite ordinary sum only when its common ratio satisfies $|r|<1$.
