Quartic Polynomial

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

A quartic polynomial is a polynomial of degree exactly $4$. In one variable, its general form is:

$$ \boxed{ P(x) = ax^4+bx^3+cx^2+dx+e, \qquad a\neq0 }. $$

The adjective quartic refers to the fourth-degree structure of the polynomial. The defining feature is not merely the presence of an $x^4$ term, but the fact that the highest surviving power after simplification is exactly $4$.

$$ \boxed{ \deg P=4 }. $$

The noun polynomial identifies an expression formed from finitely many terms whose variable exponents are nonnegative integers. Thus a quartic polynomial combines the general polynomial structure with the specific degree condition $4$.

Examples include:

$$ \boxed{ x^4-5x^2+4 } $$
$$ \boxed{ 2x^4+3x^3-x+7 } $$
$$ \boxed{ (x-1)(x+2)(x^2+1) }. $$

Each has degree $4$ after expansion and simplification.

Quartic polynomials occupy an important historical position in algebra. General quartic equations are among the highest-degree polynomial equations that admit solution formulas by radicals for arbitrary coefficients. The general quintic, by contrast, does not admit such a formula using radicals alone.

Operational Nuance

General Form

$$ \boxed{ P(x) = ax^4+bx^3+cx^2+dx+e, \qquad a\neq0 }. $$

Degree

$$ \boxed{ \deg P=4 }. $$

Leading Term

In standard descending-power form, the leading term is:

$$ \boxed{ ax^4 }. $$

Leading Coefficient

$$ \boxed{ a }. $$

Constant Term

$$ \boxed{ e }. $$

Missing Terms Are Allowed

A quartic polynomial need not contain every lower power. For example:

$$ \boxed{ x^4-16 } $$

is quartic even though the cubic, quadratic, and linear coefficients are zero.

Degree after Simplification

One must simplify before determining the degree.

For example:

$$ x^5-x^5+3x^4-2x $$

simplifies to:

$$ \boxed{ 3x^4-2x }, $$

which is quartic.

Quartic Equation

A quartic equation has form:

$$ \boxed{ ax^4+bx^3+cx^2+dx+e=0, \qquad a\neq0 }. $$

Roots

The roots are values of $x$ satisfying:

$$ P(x)=0. $$

Maximum Number of Roots

A quartic polynomial has at most four distinct roots over any field.

Complex Root Count

By the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra, a quartic polynomial over $\mathbb{C}$ has exactly four roots counting multiplicity.

$$ \boxed{ r_1,r_2,r_3,r_4 } $$

Factored Form

Over $\mathbb{C}$:

$$ \boxed{ P(x) = a(x-r_1)(x-r_2)(x-r_3)(x-r_4) } $$

counting repeated roots according to multiplicity.

Real-Coefficient Conjugate Roots

If a quartic has real coefficients and:

$$ a+bi $$

is a nonreal root, then:

$$ \boxed{ a-bi } $$

is also a root.

Possible Real-Root Counts

A real quartic may have:

  • four distinct real roots;
  • three distinct real roots with one repeated;
  • two distinct real roots;
  • one distinct real root with higher multiplicity;
  • no real roots.

Counting multiplicity, the total number of complex roots remains $4$.

Multiplicity

A root $r$ has multiplicity $m$ when:

$$ \boxed{ (x-r)^m } $$

divides the polynomial but $(x-r)^{m+1}$ does not.

Maximum Root Multiplicity

A quartic root can have multiplicity at most $4$.

Example of Multiplicity Four

$$ \boxed{ P(x) = (x-2)^4 }. $$

Example of Two Double Roots

$$ \boxed{ P(x) = (x-1)^2(x+3)^2 }. $$

Graph End Behavior

Because quartic degree is even, the two ends of the real graph point in the same vertical direction.

If:

$$ a>0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ P(x)\to+\infty \quad \text{as } x\to\pm\infty }. $$

If:

$$ a<0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ P(x)\to-\infty \quad \text{as } x\to\pm\infty }. $$

Turning-Point Bound

A quartic polynomial can have at most three real turning points.

Why

Its derivative is cubic:

$$ \boxed{ P'(x) = 4ax^3+3bx^2+2cx+d }. $$

A cubic has at most three real zeros, so the quartic has at most three critical points arising from $P'(x)=0$.

Second Derivative

$$ \boxed{ P''(x) = 12ax^2+6bx+2c }. $$

Thus quartic concavity is governed by a quadratic expression.

Third Derivative

$$ \boxed{ P'''(x) = 24ax+6b }. $$

Fourth Derivative

$$ \boxed{ P^{(4)}(x) = 24a }. $$

A quartic therefore has constant nonzero fourth derivative.

Finite Differences

When sampled at equally spaced inputs, a quartic polynomial has constant fourth finite differences.

Unit-Spaced Fourth Difference

For:

$$ P(n)=an^4+\cdots, $$

the fourth finite difference at unit spacing is:

$$ \boxed{ 4!a = 24a }. $$

Symmetry Is Not Automatic

Not every quartic graph is symmetric about the $y$-axis.

For example:

$$ x^4+x $$

is quartic but not even.

Even Quartic

A quartic of form:

$$ \boxed{ ax^4+cx^2+e } $$

satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ P(-x)=P(x) } $$

and is symmetric about the $y$-axis.

Biquadratic Polynomial

A quartic containing only even powers:

$$ \boxed{ ax^4+cx^2+e } $$

is often called biquadratic because it is quadratic in $x^2$.

Biquadratic Substitution

Let:

$$ u=x^2. $$

Then:

$$ ax^4+cx^2+e=0 $$

becomes:

$$ \boxed{ au^2+cu+e=0 }. $$

Example

Solve:

$$ x^4-5x^2+4=0. $$

Let:

$$ u=x^2. $$

Then:

$$ u^2-5u+4=0. $$

Factor:

$$ (u-1)(u-4)=0. $$

Therefore:

$$ u=1 \quad\text{or}\quad u=4. $$

Returning to $x$:

$$ x^2=1 \quad\text{or}\quad x^2=4. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ x=\pm1, \quad x=\pm2 }. $$

Factoring by Grouping

Some quartics can be factored without a substitution.

For example:

$$ x^4+x^3-x-1. $$

Group:

$$ x^3(x+1)-1(x+1). $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ (x+1)(x^3-1) }. $$

Difference of Fourth Powers

$$ \boxed{ a^4-b^4 = (a^2-b^2)(a^2+b^2) } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ a^4-b^4 = (a-b)(a+b)(a^2+b^2) }. $$

Example

$$ x^4-16 = (x^2-4)(x^2+4) $$

and over the reals:

$$ \boxed{ x^4-16 = (x-2)(x+2)(x^2+4) }. $$

Perfect Fourth Power

$$ \boxed{ (x-a)^4 } $$

is quartic and has a root of multiplicity $4$ at $x=a$.

Binomial Expansion

$$ \boxed{ (x+a)^4 = x^4 + 4ax^3 + 6a^2x^2 + 4a^3x + a^4 }. $$

Pascal-Triangle Coefficients

$$ \boxed{ 1,\ 4,\ 6,\ 4,\ 1 }. $$

Rational Root Theorem

For an integer-coefficient quartic:

$$ ax^4+bx^3+cx^2+dx+e, $$

any rational root:

$$ \frac{p}{q} $$

in lowest terms must satisfy:

$$ \boxed{ p\mid e, \qquad q\mid a }. $$

Factor Theorem

If:

$$ P(r)=0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ x-r \text{ is a factor of }P(x) }. $$

Reducing Quartic Degree after Finding a Root

Once one linear factor is found, polynomial division reduces the remaining factor to a cubic.

Example Structure

$$ P(x) = (x-r)Q(x) $$

where:

$$ \boxed{ \deg Q=3 }. $$

Two Known Roots

If two linear factors are found, the remaining factor is quadratic and can be solved by the quadratic formula.

Quartic Formula

A general quartic equation can be solved by radicals. The resulting formulas are substantially more complicated than the quadratic formula and are rarely used directly in elementary computation.

Ferrari's Method

A classical general method for solving quartic equations is associated with Lodovico Ferrari. The method reduces a quartic to auxiliary lower-degree equations, including a resolvent cubic.

Depressed Quartic

The cubic term can be eliminated by a translation:

$$ \boxed{ x = y-\frac{b}{4a} }. $$

This converts:

$$ ax^4+bx^3+cx^2+dx+e $$

into a quartic in $y$ with no $y^3$ term.

Depressed Form

$$ \boxed{ y^4+py^2+qy+r=0 } $$

after normalization and translation.

Why Depressing the Quartic Helps

Removing the cubic term exposes a more symmetric algebraic structure and simplifies the factorization strategy used in general solution methods.

Solvability by Radicals

General polynomial equations of degrees $1$, $2$, $3$, and $4$ admit formulas by radicals.

Quintic Contrast

There is no formula by radicals that solves every general quintic polynomial. This sharp contrast gives quartics a special place in the historical development of algebra.

Vieta's Relations

If:

$$ P(x) = a(x-r_1)(x-r_2)(x-r_3)(x-r_4), $$

then the roots satisfy:

$$ \boxed{ r_1+r_2+r_3+r_4 = -\frac{b}{a} }. $$
$$ \boxed{ \sum_{1\leq i<j\leq4} r_ir_j = \frac{c}{a} }. $$
$$ \boxed{ \sum_{1\leq i<j<k\leq4} r_ir_jr_k = -\frac{d}{a} }. $$
$$ \boxed{ r_1r_2r_3r_4 = \frac{e}{a} }. $$

Monic Quartic

A quartic is monic when:

$$ \boxed{ a=1 }. $$

Its form is:

$$ x^4+bx^3+cx^2+dx+e. $$

Reciprocal or Palindromic Quartic

A symmetric coefficient pattern such as:

$$ \boxed{ ax^4+bx^3+cx^2+bx+a } $$

is often called reciprocal or palindromic.

Reciprocal Substitution

Dividing such an equation by $x^2$, where $x\neq0$, can produce:

$$ a \left( x^2+\frac{1}{x^2} \right) + b \left( x+\frac{1}{x} \right) + c = 0. $$

Using:

$$ u = x+\frac{1}{x} $$

and:

$$ x^2+\frac{1}{x^2} = u^2-2, $$

reduces the quartic to a quadratic in $u$.

Quartic as Composition

Some quartics arise from composing quadratics:

$$ \boxed{ P(x) = Q(R(x)) } $$

where both $Q$ and $R$ have degree $2$.

Since degrees multiply under nonconstant polynomial composition:

$$ \boxed{ \deg(Q\circ R) = 2\cdot2 = 4 }. $$

Example

$$ (x^2+1)^2 = x^4+2x^2+1. $$

Quartic Growth

For large $|x|$:

$$ \boxed{ P(x) \sim ax^4 }. $$

The leading quartic term dominates all lower-degree terms.

Comparison with Quadratic Growth

For large positive $x$:

$$ x^4 $$

grows faster than:

$$ x^2. $$

Comparison with Exponential Growth

Although quartic growth is faster than lower-degree polynomial growth, any fixed exponential function with base greater than $1$ eventually outgrows $x^4$.

Optimization

Quartic functions arise in optimization because they may have multiple local extrema.

Possible Three Critical Points

A quartic derivative can have three real roots, allowing two local minima separated by a local maximum, or the vertically reflected arrangement.

Double-Well Shape

A common example is:

$$ \boxed{ f(x) = x^4-2x^2 }. $$

Its derivative is:

$$ f'(x) = 4x^3-4x = 4x(x^2-1). $$

Critical points occur at:

$$ \boxed{ x=-1,\ 0,\ 1 }. $$

Quartic Potential Functions

Expressions involving fourth powers appear in simplified models of potential energy, stability, bifurcation, and nonlinear systems.

Probability and Statistics

Quartic terms can appear in loss functions, moment calculations, asymptotic expansions, and polynomial approximations, although the term quartic polynomial retains its ordinary degree-$4$ meaning.

Geometry

Equations involving quartic polynomials can define algebraic curves of degree $4$, called quartic curves.

Quartic Curve vs. Quartic Polynomial

A quartic polynomial is an algebraic expression of degree $4$. A quartic curve is a geometric set defined by a degree-$4$ polynomial equation in two variables.

Multivariable Quartic Polynomial

In several variables, a quartic polynomial has total degree $4$.

For example:

$$ \boxed{ x^4+x^2y^2+y^4 } $$

has total degree $4$.

Total Degree

The degree of a multivariable monomial is the sum of its exponents:

$$ \boxed{ \deg(x^2y^2)=2+2=4 }. $$

Quartic Form

A homogeneous polynomial of total degree $4$ may be called a quartic form.

For example:

$$ \boxed{ ax^4+bx^3y+cx^2y^2+dxy^3+ey^4 }. $$

Polynomial Division

Dividing a quartic by a linear factor produces a cubic quotient when the division is exact.

Synthetic Division

If a root $r$ is known, synthetic division can efficiently divide:

$$ P(x) $$

by:

$$ \boxed{ x-r }. $$

Repeated Root Test by Derivative

If $r$ is a repeated root of $P$, then:

$$ \boxed{ P(r)=0 \quad\text{and}\quad P'(r)=0 }. $$

Higher Multiplicity

If $r$ has multiplicity at least $3$, then:

$$ P(r)=P'(r)=P''(r)=0. $$

Multiplicity Four

For:

$$ P(x)=a(x-r)^4, $$

we have:

$$ \boxed{ P(r) = P'(r) = P''(r) = P'''(r) = 0 }. $$

Graph Behavior at Roots

At a root of odd multiplicity, the real graph generally crosses the $x$-axis. At a root of even multiplicity, it generally touches the axis and turns back.

Simple Root

Multiplicity $1$ usually produces a crossing.

Double Root

Multiplicity $2$ typically produces tangency without crossing.

Triple Root

Multiplicity $3$ produces a crossing with flattening.

Quadruple Root

Multiplicity $4$ produces higher-order contact with the axis without crossing.

Root Bounds

General polynomial root-bound techniques can be applied to quartics to estimate where real or complex roots may lie before exact or numerical solution.

Numerical Root Finding

In practice, complicated quartics are frequently solved numerically rather than with the full quartic formula.

Newton's Method

For:

$$ P(x)=0, $$

Newton iteration is:

$$ \boxed{ x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{ P(x_n) }{ P'(x_n) } }. $$

Convergence depends on the starting point and local behavior.

Exact vs. Numerical Solutions

A quartic may have an exact radical solution while a numerical approximation is still preferable for computation or interpretation.

Common Error: Calling Any Polynomial with an $x^4$ Term Quartic

If a higher-degree term survives, the polynomial is not quartic.

For example:

$$ x^5+x^4+1 $$

is quintic.

Common Error: Forgetting the Leading-Coefficient Condition

In:

$$ ax^4+bx^3+cx^2+dx+e, $$

one must require:

$$ \boxed{ a\neq0 }. $$

Common Error: Determining Degree before Simplification

Cancellation can remove apparent higher-degree terms.

Common Error: Assuming Every Quartic Has Four Distinct Roots

A quartic has four complex roots counting multiplicity, not necessarily four distinct roots.

Common Error: Assuming Every Quartic Has Four Real Roots

Some quartics have no real roots.

Example

$$ \boxed{ x^4+1 } $$

has no real zeros.

Common Error: Assuming Every Quartic Is Biquadratic

Only quartics involving even powers alone can be reduced directly using $u=x^2$.

Common Error: Using $u=x^2$ when Odd Powers Remain

In:

$$ x^4+x^3+x+1, $$

the substitution $u=x^2$ does not convert the expression into an ordinary polynomial in $u$.

Common Error: Forgetting $\pm$ after Solving for $x^2$

If:

$$ x^2=9, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ x=\pm3 }. $$

Common Error: Assuming the Quartic Formula Is the Normal First Method

Factoring, substitution, symmetry, rational-root testing, and numerical methods are often far simpler.

Common Error: Assuming a General Quartic Cannot Be Solved by Radicals

General quartics are solvable by radicals; the generic obstruction begins at degree $5$.

Common Error: Assuming All Quartic Graphs Have a W Shape

A positive-leading quartic may have zero, one, two, or three real critical points and need not display a pronounced W shape.

Common Error: Assuming Every Positive-Leading Quartic Has Two Minima

It may have only one minimum.

Example

$$ \boxed{ x^4 } $$

has a single minimum at $x=0$.

Common Error: Assuming Quartic Means Even Function

Degree parity and function parity are different concepts.

Example

$$ \boxed{ x^4+x^3 } $$

is quartic but neither even nor odd.

Common Error: Assuming Quartic Means Fourth Power

A quartic polynomial need not be of the form:

$$ (x-a)^4. $$

It merely has degree $4$.

Common Error: Confusing Quartic Polynomial with Quartic Equation

The polynomial:

$$ P(x)=x^4-5x^2+4 $$

is an algebraic expression or function rule, whereas:

$$ x^4-5x^2+4=0 $$

is an equation to solve.

Common Error: Confusing Quartic Polynomial with Quartic Curve

A quartic curve is a geometric locus defined by a degree-$4$ polynomial equation, often in two variables.

Common Error: Assuming Degree Is the Number of Terms

A quartic can have one term:

$$ \boxed{ 7x^4 }, $$

or many terms. Degree depends on exponent structure, not term count.

Common Error: Assuming All Real Quartics Factor into Four Real Linear Factors

Real quartics may require irreducible quadratic factors.

Example

$$ x^4+1 $$

has no real linear factors, though it factors over $\mathbb{R}$ into quadratic factors and over $\mathbb{C}$ into linear factors.

Common Misconception: A quartic polynomial is not simply an expression that visibly contains $x^4$, nor is it necessarily a fourth power, an even function, or a biquadratic. Its defining property is:

$$ \boxed{ \deg P=4 }. $$

Quartics can have complicated root and graph structures, but many special forms reduce through factoring, substitution, symmetry, or lower-degree equations.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$ax^4+bx^3+cx^2+dx+e$ "A x to the fourth plus b x cubed plus c x squared plus d x plus e."
$\deg P=4$ "P has degree four."
$x^4-5x^2+4=0$ "X to the fourth minus five x squared plus four equals zero."
$u=x^2$ "Let u equal x squared," for a biquadratic substitution.
$P(x)=a\prod_{k=1}^{4}(x-r_k)$ "P of x equals a times the product of x minus each of the four roots," counting multiplicity.
$x=y-\frac{b}{4a}$ "Translate x by negative b over four a to eliminate the cubic term."
$P^{(4)}(x)=24a$ "The fourth derivative of P is twenty-four a."

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"quartic polynomial"} } $$

means a polynomial whose highest surviving degree is exactly $4$.

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"quartic equation"} } $$

means an equation whose polynomial form has degree $4$.

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"biquadratic quartic"} } $$

usually refers to a quartic containing only even powers and therefore quadratic in $x^2$.

Defense Notation Advice: Simplify before assigning degree, and require a nonzero coefficient on the true fourth-degree term. Distinguish quartic from biquadratic, even, reciprocal, and perfect-fourth-power special forms. When solving a biquadratic by $u=x^2$, remember to return to the original variable and include every valid square-root branch. For general quartics, prefer structural factoring or substitution before invoking a full radical formula.

  • Core Interpretation: A quartic polynomial is a polynomial of exact degree $4$: $$ \boxed{ P(x) = ax^4+bx^3+cx^2+dx+e, \qquad a\neq0 }. $$ By the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra it has four complex roots counting multiplicity. Its derivative is cubic, so a real quartic can have at most three turning points, while its even degree forces both graph ends in the same direction. Special quartics may factor directly, reduce through substitutions such as $u=x^2$, or exploit reciprocal symmetry. General quartics are solvable by radicals, making degree $4$ the highest polynomial degree for which a universal radical solution formula exists.
