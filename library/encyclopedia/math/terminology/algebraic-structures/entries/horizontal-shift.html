Horizontal Shift

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

A horizontal shift is a transformation that moves the graph of a function left or right without changing its basic shape.

If the original function is:

$$ y=f(x), $$

then a horizontal shift by $h$ units is represented by:

$$ \boxed{ y=f(x-h) }. $$

When $h>0$, the graph moves $h$ units to the right. When $h<0$, the graph moves $|h|$ units to the left.

The adjective horizontal refers to movement parallel to the horizontal coordinate axis, ordinarily the $x$-axis. The word shift indicates a translation: every point moves the same distance in the same direction.

$$ \boxed{ (x,y) \mapsto (x+h,y) }. $$

The central notational feature is that the sign inside the function appears opposite to the direction of motion:

$$ \boxed{ f(x-h) \rightarrow \text{right by }h } $$
$$ \boxed{ f(x+h) \rightarrow \text{left by }h }. $$

Operational Nuance

Basic Transformation Rule

Starting with:

$$ y=f(x), $$

replacing $x$ by $x-h$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ y=f(x-h) }. $$

This shifts the graph horizontally by $h$ units.

Right Shift

For $h>0$:

$$ \boxed{ y=f(x-h) } $$

is the graph of $f$ shifted $h$ units to the right.

Left Shift

For $h>0$:

$$ \boxed{ y=f(x+h) } $$

is the graph of $f$ shifted $h$ units to the left.

Why the Sign Appears Reversed

Suppose the original graph contains:

$$ (a,f(a)). $$

On the shifted graph:

$$ y=f(x-h), $$

the same output $f(a)$ occurs when:

$$ x-h=a. $$

Therefore:

$$ x=a+h. $$

So:

$$ \boxed{ (a,f(a)) \mapsto (a+h,f(a)) }. $$

Every point moves $h$ units to the right.

Coordinate Transformation

A horizontal translation by $h$ units acts on every graph point by:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y) \mapsto (x+h,y) }. $$

Shape Preservation

Horizontal translation does not alter:

  • distances between points;
  • angles;
  • curvature as a geometric shape;
  • relative vertical positions;
  • the overall graph form.

Translation Is a Rigid Motion

Geometrically, horizontal shifting is a translation and therefore an isometry of the plane.

Example: Quadratic Parent Function

Begin with:

$$ f(x)=x^2. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ g(x)=(x-3)^2 } $$

is the graph of $y=x^2$ shifted $3$ units to the right.

Quadratic Left Shift

$$ \boxed{ g(x)=(x+4)^2 } $$

shifts $y=x^2$ four units to the left.

Vertex Movement

The vertex of:

$$ y=x^2 $$

is $(0,0)$. The vertex of:

$$ y=(x-h)^2 $$

is:

$$ \boxed{ (h,0) }. $$

General Quadratic Vertex Form

$$ \boxed{ y=a(x-h)^2+k }. $$

Here $h$ controls horizontal shift and $k$ controls vertical shift.

Absolute-Value Function

Starting with:

$$ f(x)=|x|, $$

the function:

$$ \boxed{ |x-5| } $$

shifts the graph right by $5$ units.

Square-Root Function

Starting with:

$$ f(x)=\sqrt{x}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ g(x)=\sqrt{x-2} } $$

shifts the graph right by $2$ units.

Domain Shift

Since:

$$ \operatorname{Dom}(\sqrt{x})=[0,\infty), $$

the shifted function:

$$ \sqrt{x-2} $$

has domain:

$$ \boxed{ [2,\infty) }. $$

General Domain Transformation

If $D$ is the domain of $f$, then the domain of:

$$ g(x)=f(x-h) $$

is:

$$ \boxed{ D+h = \{x+h:x\in D\} }. $$

Range Preservation

A pure horizontal shift does not change the range:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Range}(f(x-h)) = \operatorname{Range}(f) }. $$

Only the input locations producing those output values change.

Zeros Shift Horizontally

If:

$$ f(r)=0, $$

then:

$$ g(x)=f(x-h) $$

has a zero at:

$$ \boxed{ x=r+h }. $$

Example with Zeros

The function:

$$ f(x)=x^2-1 $$

has zeros $-1$ and $1$.

The shifted function:

$$ g(x)=f(x-3) $$

has zeros:

$$ \boxed{ 2,\ 4 }. $$

Intercepts May Change

Although the entire graph is translated rigidly, its intersections with the coordinate axes may change because the axes themselves remain fixed.

$y$-Intercept after Horizontal Shift

For:

$$ g(x)=f(x-h), $$

the $y$-intercept is:

$$ \boxed{ g(0)=f(-h) }. $$

Horizontal Asymptotes

A pure horizontal shift preserves horizontal asymptote values.

If:

$$ y=L $$

is a horizontal asymptote of $f$, then it remains a horizontal asymptote of:

$$ f(x-h). $$

Vertical Asymptotes Shift

If $f$ has a vertical asymptote at:

$$ x=a, $$

then $f(x-h)$ has a vertical asymptote at:

$$ \boxed{ x=a+h }. $$

Reciprocal Function Example

Starting with:

$$ f(x)=\frac{1}{x}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ g(x)=\frac{1}{x-4} } $$

shifts the graph right by $4$ units.

Its vertical asymptote moves from $x=0$ to:

$$ \boxed{ x=4 }. $$

Exponential Function

Starting with:

$$ f(x)=b^x, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ g(x)=b^{x-h} } $$

is a horizontal shift right by $h$.

Equivalent Algebraic Scaling in Exponentials

Since:

$$ b^{x-h} = b^{-h}b^x, $$

a horizontal shift of an exponential graph can also appear algebraically as a vertical scaling.

Transformation Equivalence

For exponential functions:

$$ \boxed{ b^{x-h} = b^{-h}b^x }. $$

This illustrates that different transformation descriptions can sometimes produce the same function.

Logarithmic Function

Starting with:

$$ f(x)=\log_b x, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ g(x)=\log_b(x-h) } $$

shifts the graph right by $h$.

Logarithmic Domain

For:

$$ \log_b(x-h), $$

the argument must satisfy:

$$ x-h>0. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ x>h }. $$

Trigonometric Functions

A horizontal shift of a periodic function is often called a phase shift.

Sine Example

$$ \boxed{ y=\sin(x-h) } $$

shifts $y=\sin x$ to the right by $h$.

Cosine Example

$$ \boxed{ y=\cos(x+h) } $$

shifts $y=\cos x$ to the left by $h$.

Period Preservation

A horizontal translation does not change the period of a periodic function.

If:

$$ f(x+T)=f(x), $$

then:

$$ g(x)=f(x-h) $$

has the same period $T$.

General Sinusoidal Form

$$ \boxed{ y=A\sin(B(x-h))+D }. $$

In this form, $h$ is the horizontal shift.

Phase Shift in Expanded Form

A sinusoid may instead be written:

$$ y=A\sin(Bx-C)+D. $$

Factoring $B$ from the input:

$$ Bx-C = B\left( x-\frac{C}{B} \right). $$

Therefore the horizontal shift is:

$$ \boxed{ h=\frac{C}{B} } $$

when $B\neq0$.

Important Scaling Interaction

In:

$$ f(Bx-C), $$

the shift is not generally $C$. One must first factor:

$$ Bx-C = B\left( x-\frac{C}{B} \right). $$

General Input Transformation

For:

$$ g(x)=f(b(x-h)), $$

the parameter $h$ controls horizontal translation while $b$ controls horizontal scaling and, when negative, reflection.

Horizontal Reflection Interaction

If:

$$ b<0, $$

then $f(b(x-h))$ includes a horizontal reflection as well as scaling and shifting.

Order of Transformations

When reading:

$$ f(b(x-h)), $$

it is safest to factor the entire input into transformation form before describing the horizontal effects.

Composition Interpretation

Define the translation map:

$$ T_h(x)=x-h. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ g=f\circ T_h }. $$

Thus a horizontal shift can be viewed as precomposition of $f$ with an input translation.

Why Horizontal Transformations Act Inside the Function

Horizontal transformations modify the input before the function evaluates it. That is why they occur inside the argument of $f$.

Vertical vs. Horizontal Shift

Compare:

$$ \boxed{ f(x-h) \rightarrow \text{horizontal shift} } $$
$$ \boxed{ f(x)+k \rightarrow \text{vertical shift} }. $$

Point Mapping Comparison

Horizontal shift:

$$ (x,y) \mapsto (x+h,y). $$

Vertical shift:

$$ (x,y) \mapsto (x,y+k). $$

Combined Translation

The function:

$$ \boxed{ g(x)=f(x-h)+k } $$

shifts the graph right by $h$ and vertically by $k$.

Combined Point Mapping

$$ \boxed{ (x,y) \mapsto (x+h,y+k) }. $$

Inverse Transformation

A shift right by $h$ is undone by a shift left by $h$.

$$ \boxed{ f(x-h) \xrightarrow{\text{shift left }h} f(x) }. $$

Composition of Horizontal Shifts

Shifting right by $h_1$ and then right by $h_2$ produces:

$$ \boxed{ f(x-h_1-h_2) }. $$

The total horizontal shift is:

$$ \boxed{ h_1+h_2 }. $$

Opposite Shifts Cancel

A right shift by $h$ followed by a left shift by $h$ returns the original graph.

Translation Group Structure

Horizontal translations compose by addition of their shift parameters:

$$ \boxed{ T_{h_1}\circ T_{h_2} = T_{h_1+h_2} }. $$

Identity Shift

When $h=0$:

$$ \boxed{ f(x-0)=f(x) }. $$

Shifted Equations

Horizontal shifts can also be applied to relations that are not functions.

For example, the circle:

$$ x^2+y^2=r^2 $$

shifted right by $h$ becomes:

$$ \boxed{ (x-h)^2+y^2=r^2 }. $$

Conic Sections

Horizontal translation appears naturally in standard forms such as:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{(x-h)^2}{a^2} + \frac{(y-k)^2}{b^2} = 1 }. $$

Here $h$ gives the horizontal coordinate of the ellipse's center.

Parabola Standard Form

$$ \boxed{ y=a(x-h)^2+k }. $$

The horizontal coordinate of the vertex is $h$.

Absolute-Value Standard Form

$$ \boxed{ y=a|x-h|+k }. $$

The vertex lies at $(h,k)$.

Coordinate-System Interpretation

Instead of moving the graph, one may equivalently think of translating the coordinate description. This distinction is useful in analytic geometry.

Graph Translation vs. Axis Translation

Moving an object relative to fixed axes and moving the coordinate axes relative to a fixed object produce opposite-looking substitution rules. The reference frame must therefore be stated carefully.

Data Alignment

In applied mathematics, horizontally shifting a graph can represent delaying or advancing a signal, event, response curve, or time-dependent model.

Time Delay

If $f(t)$ represents a signal, then:

$$ \boxed{ f(t-\tau) } $$

represents a delay of $\tau$ time units when $\tau>0$.

Time Advance

Similarly:

$$ \boxed{ f(t+\tau) } $$

shifts the signal earlier by $\tau$ units.

Signal Processing

Horizontal shifts are fundamental in signal analysis because translating a function in time changes when a signal occurs without changing its intrinsic waveform.

Probability Distributions

Translating a probability density horizontally changes its location while preserving its shape.

Location Families

A family of densities may have the form:

$$ \boxed{ f(x-\mu) }, $$

where $\mu$ acts as a location parameter.

Continuity Preservation

If $f$ is continuous, then $f(x-h)$ is continuous on the correspondingly shifted domain.

Differentiability Preservation

If $f$ is differentiable, then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}f(x-h) = f'(x-h) }. $$

Critical Points Shift

If $f'(c)=0$, then:

$$ g(x)=f(x-h) $$

has a corresponding critical point at:

$$ \boxed{ x=c+h }. $$

Extrema Shift

Local and absolute extrema move horizontally with the rest of the graph, while their output values remain unchanged under a pure horizontal shift.

Concavity Pattern Preservation

Horizontal translation preserves the pattern of concavity; locations of inflection behavior move by the shift amount.

Integral Translation

Under an appropriate change of variables:

$$ \int_a^b f(x-h)\,dx = \int_{a-h}^{b-h}f(u)\,du. $$

This reflects the same horizontal translation at the level of accumulated area.

Common Error: Reading the Sign Literally

Students often interpret $f(x-3)$ as left by $3$ because of the minus sign. The actual shift is right by $3$.

Common Error: Treating $f(x+h)$ as Right Shift

The graph of $f(x+h)$ moves left by $h$ when $h>0$.

Common Error: Changing Output Coordinates

A horizontal shift changes $x$-coordinates, not $y$-coordinates:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y) \mapsto (x+h,y) }. $$

Common Error: Confusing Horizontal and Vertical Shifts

$$ f(x-h) \neq f(x)-h. $$

The first is horizontal; the second is vertical.

Common Error: Ignoring an Inner Coefficient

In:

$$ f(2x-6), $$

one must factor:

$$ 2x-6 = 2(x-3). $$

The horizontal shift is $3$, not $6$.

Common Error: Assuming Domain Never Changes

The domain moves horizontally with the graph. Only its shape or interval pattern is preserved.

Common Error: Assuming the $y$-Intercept Is Preserved

A horizontal shift changes which original input lands at $x=0$, so the $y$-intercept generally changes.

Common Error: Assuming Every Algebraic Rewrite Has a Unique Transformation Story

For some function families, especially exponentials, a horizontal shift may be algebraically equivalent to a vertical scaling.

Common Misconception: The minus sign in $f(x-h)$ does not mean the graph moves left. Horizontal transformations act on inputs, so the point that originally occurred at $x=a$ occurs at $x=a+h$. Therefore $f(x-h)$ shifts right by $h$, while $f(x+h)$ shifts left by $h$.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$f(x-h)$ "The graph of f shifted h units to the right," when $h>0$.
$f(x+h)$ "The graph of f shifted h units to the left," when $h>0$.
$(x,y)\mapsto(x+h,y)$ "Translate every point h units horizontally to the right."
$y=(x-3)^2$ "Shift the basic parabola three units to the right."
$y=\sin(x-\pi/2)$ "Shift the sine graph pi over two units to the right."
$f(b(x-h))$ "Apply a horizontal shift by h together with the horizontal effect determined by b."
$f(x-h)+k$ "Shift the graph h units horizontally and k units vertically."

The phrases horizontal shift, horizontal translation, and sometimes simply translation in the $x$-direction describe the same basic operation.

In trigonometry and signal analysis, a horizontal shift is often called a phase shift or time shift, depending on context.

Defense Notation Advice: Put the input expression into factored transformation form before naming the shift. Read $f(x-h)$ as right by $h$ and $f(x+h)$ as left by $h$. For expressions such as $f(bx-c)$, factor the input as $b(x-c/b)$ before identifying the horizontal shift. Distinguish carefully between transformations applied inside the function and those applied outside it.
