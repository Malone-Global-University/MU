Factor Theorem

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

The Factor Theorem takes its name from the algebraic idea of a factor: an expression that divides another expression exactly. If a polynomial $f(x)$ can be written:

$$ f(x)=g(x)h(x), $$

then $g(x)$ and $h(x)$ are factors of $f(x)$.

The theorem establishes a fundamental correspondence between factors of the form:

$$ x-r $$

and zeros of a polynomial. It tells us that determining whether $r$ makes the polynomial equal to zero is equivalent to determining whether $x-r$ divides the polynomial with no remainder.

The term zero emphasizes the function:

$$ f(r)=0, $$

while root emphasizes the equation:

$$ f(x)=0. $$

In polynomial algebra, both viewpoints connect directly to linear factors through the Factor Theorem.

Operational Nuance

Let $f(x)$ be a polynomial. The Factor Theorem states:

$$ \boxed{ f(r)=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x-r\text{ is a factor of }f(x) } $$

Equivalently:

$$ \boxed{ f(r)=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad (x-r)\mid f(x) } $$

The symbol $\Longleftrightarrow$ means if and only if. The theorem therefore works in both directions.

If:

$$ f(r)=0, $$

then:

$$ f(x)=(x-r)q(x) $$

for some polynomial $q(x)$.

Conversely, if:

$$ f(x)=(x-r)q(x), $$

then substituting $x=r$ gives:

$$ f(r) = (r-r)q(r) = 0. $$

For example, consider:

$$ f(x)=x^3-4x^2+x+6. $$

To determine whether $x-2$ is a factor, evaluate:

$$ f(2) = 2^3-4(2^2)+2+6. $$

Therefore:

$$ f(2) = 8-16+2+6 = 0. $$

Since:

$$ f(2)=0, $$

the Factor Theorem guarantees:

$$ x-2 $$

is a factor.

Polynomial division then gives:

$$ f(x) = (x-2)(x^2-2x-3). $$

The quadratic factors further:

$$ x^2-2x-3 = (x-3)(x+1). $$

Hence:

$$ f(x) = (x-2)(x-3)(x+1). $$

The corresponding zeros are:

$$ \boxed{ 2,\ 3,\ -1 }. $$

Notice the sign relationship:

$$ x-r \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad r. $$

Thus:

$$ x+5=x-(-5) $$

corresponds to the zero:

$$ -5, $$

not $5$.

Common Misconception: If $f(r)=0$, the associated factor is $x-r$, not $x+r$. For example, if $3$ is a zero, the corresponding factor is:

$$ x-3. $$

If $-3$ is a zero, the corresponding factor is:

$$ x+3. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$f(r)=0$ "R is a zero of f," or "f of r equals zero."
$(x-r)\mid f(x)$ "X minus r divides f of x."
$f(x)=(x-r)q(x)$ "F of x equals x minus r times q of x."
$f(r)=0\Longleftrightarrow(x-r)\mid f(x)$ "F of r equals zero if and only if x minus r is a factor of f of x."
$x+4=x-(-4)$ "X plus four is x minus negative four."
$r$ is a root of $f(x)=0$ "R is a root of the polynomial equation f of x equals zero."

The phrase divides exactly means that polynomial division leaves remainder:

$$ 0. $$

Thus the following statements are equivalent:

$$ r\text{ is a zero of }f, $$ $$ r\text{ is a root of }f(x)=0, $$ $$ x-r\text{ is a factor of }f(x), $$ $$ f(x)\div(x-r)\text{ has remainder }0. $$

These are different ways of describing the same algebraic relationship.

Defense Notation Advice: Pay special attention to negative roots. If:

$$ r=-2, $$

then:

$$ x-r = x-(-2) = x+2. $$

The sign appearing in the factor is therefore opposite the sign of the zero when written in the form $x-r$.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Testing Factors: To test whether $x-r$ is a factor of $f(x)$, calculate: $$ f(r). $$ If: $$ f(r)=0, $$ then $x-r$ is a factor. If: $$ f(r)\neq0, $$ it is not a factor.
  • Finding Zeros: If a polynomial has already been factored: $$ f(x)=(x-a)(x-b)(x-c), $$ then the Factor Theorem immediately identifies the zeros: $$ a,\ b,\ c. $$
  • Remainder Theorem: The Factor Theorem is a direct special case of the Remainder Theorem. The Remainder Theorem states that when $f(x)$ is divided by $x-r$, the remainder is: $$ f(r). $$ Therefore: $$ f(r)=0 $$ precisely when the remainder is zero, which is precisely when $x-r$ is a factor.
  • Remainder Theorem to Factor Theorem: The logical chain is: $$ \text{remainder}=f(r) $$ $$ f(r)=0 $$ $$ \Longrightarrow $$ $$ \text{remainder}=0 $$ $$ \Longrightarrow $$ $$ x-r\text{ divides }f(x). $$
  • Rational Zero Theorem: The Rational Zero Theorem generates possible rational zeros. The Factor Theorem tests those candidates: $$ \text{candidate }r \longrightarrow f(r). $$ If $f(r)=0$, then $r$ is an actual zero and $x-r$ is an actual factor.
  • Synthetic Division: Synthetic division provides an efficient computational method for testing and extracting a linear factor. If synthetic division by $r$ produces final remainder $0$, then: $$ x-r $$ is a factor.
  • Repeated Factors: A zero can occur more than once. If: $$ f(x)=(x-2)^3(x+1), $$ then: $$ r=2 $$ is a zero of multiplicity $3$. The Factor Theorem still applies, but repeated division reveals the repeated factor.
  • Multiplicity: More generally, $r$ is a zero of multiplicity $m$ if: $$ (x-r)^m\mid f(x) $$ but: $$ (x-r)^{m+1}\nmid f(x). $$ This refines the basic zero-factor correspondence.
  • Graphical Interpretation: If $r$ is a real zero, then: $$ f(r)=0, $$ so the graph contains the point: $$ (r,0). $$ Thus real zeros correspond to $x$-intercepts, although even-multiplicity zeros may cause the graph to touch the axis rather than cross it.
  • Complex Zeros: The Factor Theorem is not restricted to real numbers. If: $$ f(a+bi)=0, $$ then: $$ x-(a+bi) $$ is a factor when the polynomial is considered over the complex numbers.
  • Conjugate Pair Theorem: If $f(x)$ has real coefficients and: $$ a+bi $$ is a nonreal zero, then: $$ a-bi $$ is also a zero. By the Factor Theorem, both: $$ x-(a+bi) $$ and: $$ x-(a-bi) $$ are factors over $\mathbb{C}$.
  • Quadratic Factors from Conjugates: Multiplying the conjugate factors gives: $$ [x-(a+bi)][x-(a-bi)] = (x-a)^2+b^2, $$ a quadratic factor with real coefficients.
  • Fundamental Theorem of Algebra: The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra guarantees that a degree-$n$ polynomial over the complex numbers has $n$ zeros counted with multiplicity. The Factor Theorem converts those zeros into linear factors: $$ f(x) = a_n (x-r_1)(x-r_2)\cdots(x-r_n), $$ with multiplicities included.
  • Polynomial Division: The Factor Theorem may be viewed through the polynomial division algorithm: $$ f(x) = (x-r)q(x)+R, $$ where $R$ is constant. Setting $x=r$ gives: $$ R=f(r). $$ Therefore $R=0$ exactly when $x-r$ divides $f(x)$.
  • Terminological Contrast: A zero is a number $r$ satisfying $f(r)=0$. A root is a solution of the equation $f(x)=0$. A factor is an expression that divides the polynomial exactly. The Remainder Theorem identifies the remainder after division by $x-r$, while the Factor Theorem identifies exactly when that remainder is zero.
