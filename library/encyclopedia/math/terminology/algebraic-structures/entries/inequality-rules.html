Inequality Rules

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

Inequality rules are the algebraic laws governing how order relations behave when expressions are transformed. They determine which operations preserve an inequality, which reverse its direction, and which require additional conditions before they can be used safely.

The word inequality refers to an ordered comparison such as:

$$ \boxed{ a<b,\qquad a>b,\qquad a\leq b,\qquad a\geq b }. $$

The word rule identifies a dependable transformation principle. Thus an inequality rule states how one valid comparison changes when the same operation or function is applied to both sides.

The central distinction is between order-preserving and order-reversing transformations.

$$ \boxed{ \text{order-preserving} \Longrightarrow \text{inequality direction stays the same} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{order-reversing} \Longrightarrow \text{inequality direction reverses} }. $$

These rules are foundational to solving inequalities, comparing expressions, establishing bounds, manipulating formulas, and proving more advanced inequalities.

Operational Nuance

Core Addition Rule

Adding the same real quantity to both sides preserves an inequality:

$$ \boxed{ a<b \Longrightarrow a+c<b+c }. $$

The same principle applies to $\leq$, $>$, and $\geq$.

Subtraction Rule

Subtracting the same real quantity from both sides also preserves order:

$$ \boxed{ a<b \Longrightarrow a-c<b-c }. $$

Why Addition Preserves Order

Adding the same amount translates both numbers by the same distance on the number line. Their relative left-right position therefore remains unchanged.

Example

$$ 3<8. $$

Adding $5$ gives:

$$ \boxed{ 8<13 }. $$

Multiplication by a Positive Number

If $c>0$, multiplying both sides by $c$ preserves order:

$$ \boxed{ a<b \Longrightarrow ac<bc }. $$

Division by a Positive Number

If $c>0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ a<b \Longrightarrow \frac{a}{c} < \frac{b}{c} }. $$

Positive Scaling Interpretation

Multiplication by a positive number stretches or compresses the number line without reversing its direction.

Multiplication by a Negative Number

If $c<0$, multiplying by $c$ reverses the inequality:

$$ \boxed{ a<b \Longrightarrow ac>bc }. $$

Division by a Negative Number

If $c<0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ a<b \Longrightarrow \frac{a}{c} > \frac{b}{c} }. $$

Why Negative Multiplication Reverses Order

Multiplication by a negative number includes reflection across zero. Reflection reverses left and right on the number line.

$$ 2<7 $$

becomes:

$$ \boxed{ -2>-7 }. $$

Negation Rule

Multiplying by $-1$ gives the general negation rule:

$$ \boxed{ a<b \iff -a>-b }. $$

Example in Solving

Solve:

$$ -4x\leq12. $$

Dividing by $-4$ reverses $\leq$ to $\geq$:

$$ \boxed{ x\geq-3 }. $$

Do Not Reverse after Addition or Subtraction

The inequality direction changes because of negative multiplication or division, not merely because a negative number appears somewhere in the calculation.

Unknown-Sign Multiplier

If the sign of a multiplier is unknown, multiplying both sides by it cannot be done safely without considering cases.

Suppose:

$$ a<b. $$

For an unknown real $c$:

  • if $c>0$, then $ac<bc$;
  • if $c<0$, then $ac>bc$;
  • if $c=0$, then $ac=bc=0$.

Division by an Unknown Expression

Dividing an inequality by an expression such as $x-2$ requires knowing whether that expression is positive, negative, or zero.

Division by Zero Is Forbidden

$$ \boxed{ \text{never divide by }0 }. $$

If a possible divisor can equal zero, that case must be handled separately.

Transitive Rule

Order is transitive:

$$ \boxed{ a<b \text{ and } b<c \Longrightarrow a<c }. $$

Non-Strict Transitivity

$$ \boxed{ a\leq b \text{ and } b\leq c \Longrightarrow a\leq c }. $$

Mixed Transitivity

If at least one link is strict:

$$ \boxed{ a<b\leq c \Longrightarrow a<c } $$

and similarly:

$$ \boxed{ a\leq b<c \Longrightarrow a<c }. $$

Trichotomy

For real numbers $a$ and $b$, exactly one of the following is true:

$$ \boxed{ a<b,\qquad a=b,\qquad a>b }. $$

Antisymmetry of Non-Strict Order

If:

$$ a\leq b \qquad\text{and}\qquad b\leq a, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a=b }. $$

Adding Two Inequalities

If:

$$ a<b \qquad\text{and}\qquad c<d, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a+c<b+d }. $$

Non-Strict Addition

$$ \boxed{ a\leq b,\ c\leq d \Longrightarrow a+c\leq b+d }. $$

Subtraction of Two Inequalities Requires Care

One cannot generally subtract inequalities side by side in the same orientation without first accounting for the order reversal caused by negation.

If:

$$ a\leq b \qquad\text{and}\qquad c\leq d, $$

then $-d\leq-c$, so:

$$ \boxed{ a-d\leq b-c }. $$

Product Rule for Positive Quantities

If:

$$ 0\leq a\leq b \qquad\text{and}\qquad 0\leq c\leq d, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ ac\leq bd }. $$

The nonnegativity conditions matter.

Products without Sign Conditions

Product inequalities cannot be inferred safely from factor inequalities unless signs are controlled.

Reciprocal Rule for Positive Numbers

If:

$$ 0<a<b, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{1}{a} > \frac{1}{b} }. $$

Why Reciprocals Reverse Positive Order

The function:

$$ f(x)=\frac{1}{x} $$

is strictly decreasing on $(0,\infty)$.

Reciprocal Rule for Negative Numbers

On $(-\infty,0)$, the reciprocal function is also strictly decreasing.

Thus if:

$$ a<b<0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{1}{a} > \frac{1}{b} }. $$

Reciprocals across Zero

No simple direct reversal rule can cross zero because the reciprocal is undefined at $0$ and changes sign across it.

Squaring Rule for Nonnegative Numbers

If:

$$ 0\leq a<b, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a^2<b^2 }. $$

Squaring Is Not Globally Order-Preserving

Without nonnegativity:

$$ -5<2 $$

but:

$$ \boxed{ 25>4 }. $$

Odd Powers

For odd positive integers $n$, the function $x^n$ is strictly increasing over all real numbers:

$$ \boxed{ a<b \Longrightarrow a^n<b^n \qquad (n\text{ odd}) }. $$

Even Powers

For even powers, sign information is necessary because $x^n$ is not strictly increasing over all real numbers.

Positive Roots

For nonnegative inputs:

$$ \boxed{ 0\leq a\leq b \Longrightarrow \sqrt[n]{a}\leq\sqrt[n]{b} } $$

for positive integers $n$ where the real roots are defined.

Odd Roots

An odd-root function is increasing on all real numbers:

$$ \boxed{ a<b \Longrightarrow \sqrt[3]{a}<\sqrt[3]{b} }. $$

Monotonic Function Rule

If $f$ is strictly increasing on the relevant domain:

$$ \boxed{ a<b \Longrightarrow f(a)<f(b) }. $$

Strictly Decreasing Function Rule

If $f$ is strictly decreasing:

$$ \boxed{ a<b \Longrightarrow f(a)>f(b) }. $$

Nondecreasing Functions

If $f$ is nondecreasing:

$$ \boxed{ a\leq b \Longrightarrow f(a)\leq f(b) }. $$

Exponential Rule with Base Greater Than One

If $b>1$:

$$ \boxed{ u<v \iff b^u<b^v }. $$

Exponential Rule with Base between Zero and One

If $0<b<1$, exponentiation reverses order:

$$ \boxed{ u<v \iff b^u>b^v }. $$

Logarithm Rule with Base Greater Than One

If $b>1$ and $u,v>0$:

$$ \boxed{ u<v \iff \log_bu<\log_bv }. $$

Logarithm Rule with Base between Zero and One

If $0<b<1$:

$$ \boxed{ u<v \iff \log_bu>\log_bv }. $$

Domain Conditions for Logarithms

$$ \boxed{ u>0,\qquad v>0 }. $$

These conditions must be retained while solving logarithmic inequalities.

Equivalent Transformations

A valid inequality-solving step should preserve exactly the same solution set.

$$ \boxed{ I_1(x) \iff I_2(x) }. $$

Example

$$ 3x+4<10 $$

is equivalent to:

$$ 3x<6 $$

and therefore:

$$ \boxed{ x<2 }. $$

Conditional Transformations

Some transformations are valid only under extra assumptions.

For example, multiplying:

$$ \frac{1}{x}>2 $$

by $x$ requires knowing the sign of $x$.

Safer Strategy for Rational Inequalities

Instead of multiplying through by an unknown-sign denominator, move everything to one side and perform sign analysis.

Example

$$ \frac{1}{x}>2 $$

becomes:

$$ \frac{1-2x}{x}>0. $$

Critical values are:

$$ x=0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad x=\frac{1}{2}. $$

Sign analysis then determines the valid intervals.

Compound Inequality Rule

A chain:

$$ a<x<b $$

means:

$$ \boxed{ a<x \text{ and } x<b }. $$

Performing the Same Operation on All Three Parts

For:

$$ 2<x+3\leq8, $$

subtract $3$ from all three parts:

$$ \boxed{ -1<x\leq5 }. $$

Multiplying a Three-Part Inequality by a Negative Number

If:

$$ a<x<b, $$

multiplying every part by $-1$ reverses both comparisons:

$$ -a>-x>-b. $$

Reordering from least to greatest gives:

$$ \boxed{ -b<-x<-a }. $$

Absolute-Value Rule for Less Than

For $r>0$:

$$ \boxed{ |x|<r \iff -r<x<r }. $$

Absolute-Value Rule for Less Than or Equal To

$$ \boxed{ |x|\leq r \iff -r\leq x\leq r }. $$

Absolute-Value Rule for Greater Than

$$ \boxed{ |x|>r \iff x<-r \text{ or } x>r } $$

for $r\geq0$.

Absolute-Value Rule for Greater Than or Equal To

$$ \boxed{ |x|\geq r \iff x\leq-r \text{ or } x\geq r } $$

for $r\geq0$.

Shifted Absolute Value

For $r>0$:

$$ \boxed{ |x-a|<r \iff a-r<x<a+r }. $$

This expresses a distance condition around $a$.

Boundary Inclusion Rule

If the original inequality is strict, equality points are excluded.

If it is non-strict, zeros of defined expressions may be included.

Denominator Exception

A denominator zero is excluded regardless of whether the inequality symbol is strict or non-strict.

Example

In:

$$ \frac{x-1}{x+2}\geq0, $$

$x=1$ may be included because the numerator becomes zero, but:

$$ \boxed{ x=-2 } $$

must be excluded because the denominator is zero.

Polynomial Sign Rule

For a factored polynomial:

$$ P(x) = c(x-r_1)^{m_1}\cdots(x-r_k)^{m_k}, $$

the sign can change only at its real zeros.

Odd-Multiplicity Root

The sign normally changes when crossing a root of odd multiplicity.

Even-Multiplicity Root

The sign normally remains the same when crossing a root of even multiplicity.

Rational Sign Rule

For:

$$ \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}, $$

sign changes are analyzed around zeros of both $P$ and $Q$.

Intersection Rule for AND

If two inequalities must both hold, their solution sets are intersected:

$$ \boxed{ S=S_1\cap S_2 }. $$

Union Rule for OR

If either condition may hold:

$$ \boxed{ S=S_1\cup S_2 }. $$

Interval Translation Rule

Strict boundaries correspond to parentheses:

$$ \boxed{ a<x<b \iff x\in(a,b) }. $$

Inclusive Interval Rule

$$ \boxed{ a\leq x\leq b \iff x\in[a,b] }. $$

Mixed Endpoint Rule

$$ \boxed{ a<x\leq b \iff x\in(a,b] }. $$

Infinity Endpoint Rule

Infinity is never included as a real endpoint:

$$ \boxed{ x\geq a \iff x\in[a,\infty) }. $$

Interval Translation Rule

Adding $c$ to every value in an interval translates the interval:

$$ \boxed{ [a,b]+c = [a+c,b+c] }. $$

Positive Interval Scaling

If $c>0$:

$$ \boxed{ c[a,b] = [ca,cb] }. $$

Negative Interval Scaling

If $c<0$, endpoint order reverses:

$$ \boxed{ c[a,b] = [cb,ca] }. $$

Bounds under Addition

If:

$$ a\leq x\leq b \qquad\text{and}\qquad c\leq y\leq d, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a+c \leq x+y \leq b+d }. $$

Bounds under Subtraction

Under the same assumptions:

$$ \boxed{ a-d \leq x-y \leq b-c }. $$

Bounds under Multiplication

Multiplication of bounded intervals is more complicated because signs matter. In general, all endpoint products may need comparison.

$$ \boxed{ xy \in [ \min(ac,ad,bc,bd), \max(ac,ad,bc,bd) ] } $$

when $x\in[a,b]$ and $y\in[c,d]$.

Comparing Fractions by Cross Multiplication

If $b>0$ and $d>0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{a}{b} < \frac{c}{d} \iff ad<bc }. $$

Positive denominator assumptions are essential.

Negative Denominators

If denominator signs are not known, cross multiplication can reverse the comparison or require case analysis.

Comparing with Zero

Sign analysis often simplifies inequalities by moving all terms to one side:

$$ \boxed{ L(x)<R(x) \iff L(x)-R(x)<0 }. $$

Zero as a Reference Boundary

Factored expressions can then be classified by positive, negative, or zero sign.

Preserving Domain

Every inequality transformation must preserve not only order but also the domain of the original expressions.

Radical Domain Rule

For an even root:

$$ \sqrt{g(x)}, $$

require:

$$ \boxed{ g(x)\geq0 }. $$

Logarithmic Domain Rule

$$ \boxed{ \log_b(g(x)) \text{ requires } g(x)>0 }. $$

Rational Domain Rule

$$ \boxed{ \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} \text{ requires } Q(x)\neq0 }. $$

Checking Candidate Solutions

Candidate intervals or boundary points should be verified against the original inequality when transformations may have introduced restrictions or lost information.

Squaring Both Sides

Squaring both sides can be safe under suitable nonnegative conditions.

If:

$$ 0\leq A<B, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ A^2<B^2 }. $$

Squaring without Sign Control

Without knowing the signs, squaring can alter the solution set.

Taking Even Roots

If:

$$ 0\leq A\leq B, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt{A} \leq \sqrt{B} }. $$

Absolute Value as Magnitude

For all real $x$:

$$ \boxed{ |x|\geq0 }. $$

Basic Absolute-Value Bound

$$ \boxed{ -|x| \leq x \leq |x| }. $$

Triangle Inequality

$$ \boxed{ |x+y| \leq |x|+|y| }. $$

Reverse Triangle Inequality

$$ \boxed{ \bigl||x|-|y|\bigr| \leq |x-y| }. $$

Order of Real Numbers

The ordinary inequality rules rely on the ordered-field structure of $\mathbb{R}$.

Ordered Field Principle

Real-number order is compatible with addition and multiplication:

$$ \boxed{ a<b \Longrightarrow a+c<b+c } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ 0<c,\ a<b \Longrightarrow ac<bc }. $$

Complex-Number Caution

The complex numbers do not support the same compatible total order as the real numbers. Ordinary rules involving $<$ and $>$ therefore do not transfer directly to arbitrary complex numbers.

Matrix Inequality Caution

Matrix inequalities may use specialized order relations such as positive semidefinite order rather than entrywise real-number order.

Vector Inequality Caution

Vector comparisons likewise require a specified ordering convention.

Equivalent vs. Implicational Steps

Some inequality manipulations give true equivalences:

$$ \boxed{ A<B \iff A+C<B+C }. $$

Other transformations may yield only one direction unless additional conditions are imposed.

Proof Context

Inequality rules are not only solving tools. They also support proofs of bounds and estimates.

Bounding by Known Quantities

If:

$$ |f(x)|\leq M $$

and $|g(x)|\leq N$, then:

$$ \boxed{ |f(x)g(x)| \leq MN }. $$

Squeeze-Style Reasoning

If:

$$ g(x)\leq f(x)\leq h(x), $$

information about the outer bounds constrains $f(x)$.

Common Error: Reversing the Sign after Subtraction

Subtracting the same quantity from both sides preserves order.

Common Error: Failing to Reverse after Negative Division

Division by a negative quantity reverses every inequality symbol involved.

Common Error: Reversing Merely Because a Negative Number Appears

The sign reverses only when the operation itself multiplies or divides both sides by a negative quantity, or when an order-reversing function is applied.

Common Error: Cross Multiplying without Checking Denominator Signs

Cross multiplication is safe in its simplest form only when denominator signs are known.

Common Error: Multiplying by a Variable without Sign Analysis

A variable expression may be positive, negative, or zero.

Common Error: Squaring Both Sides Automatically

Squaring is not globally order-preserving over the real numbers.

Common Error: Ignoring Domain Restrictions

Algebraically plausible values may still be invalid because of denominators, logarithms, or radicals.

Common Error: Including an Undefined Boundary

A denominator zero is never included even under $\leq$ or $\geq$.

Common Error: Forgetting All Parts of a Compound Inequality

The same legal operation must be applied consistently throughout a three-part inequality.

Common Error: Confusing AND with OR

Interior inequalities usually produce intersections, while exterior absolute-value inequalities often produce unions.

Common Error: Treating Every Function as Order-Preserving

Some functions increase, some decrease, and some are neither monotonic over the entire domain.

Common Misconception: Inequality rules are not identical to equation rules with a different symbol. Addition and subtraction behave similarly, but multiplication and division require sign awareness. Negative scaling reverses order, unknown-sign factors require case analysis, and nonlinear operations such as squaring, reciprocation, exponentiation, and logarithms preserve or reverse order only under specific domain and monotonicity conditions.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$a<b\Rightarrow a+c<b+c$ "Adding the same quantity to both sides preserves the inequality."
$a<b,\ c>0\Rightarrow ac<bc$ "Multiplication by a positive quantity preserves order."
$a<b,\ c<0\Rightarrow ac>bc$ "Multiplication by a negative quantity reverses the inequality."
$0<a<b\Rightarrow 1/a>1/b$ "For positive numbers, taking reciprocals reverses order."
$a<b\Rightarrow f(a)<f(b)$ "A strictly increasing function preserves order."
$a<b\Rightarrow f(a)>f(b)$ "A strictly decreasing function reverses order."
$a<x\leq b$ "X is greater than a and less than or equal to b."

The common classroom phrase flip the inequality sign refers to reversing the comparison direction:

$$ \boxed{ <\leftrightarrow>, \qquad \leq\leftrightarrow\geq }. $$

A more precise description is reverse the inequality or reverse the order relation.

Defense Notation Advice: Before multiplying, dividing, taking reciprocals, applying powers, or using logarithms, identify the sign and domain conditions that control order. Keep strict and non-strict boundaries distinct, never divide by zero, and use case analysis whenever an expression's sign is unknown. Treat every step as a claim about preserving the exact solution set.

  • Core Interpretation: Inequality rules describe how mathematical order changes under operations. Addition and subtraction preserve order: $$ \boxed{ a<b \Longrightarrow a+c<b+c }. $$ Positive scaling preserves it: $$ \boxed{ c>0,\ a<b \Longrightarrow ac<bc }. $$ Negative scaling reverses it: $$ \boxed{ c<0,\ a<b \Longrightarrow ac>bc }. $$ More advanced transformations depend on monotonicity, sign, and domain.
