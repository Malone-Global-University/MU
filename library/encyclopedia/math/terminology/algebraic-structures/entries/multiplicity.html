Multiplicity

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

Multiplicity measures how many times a mathematical object occurs when repeated occurrences are counted rather than collapsed into one distinct item. In elementary algebra, the term most commonly describes how many times a factor or root is repeated in a polynomial.

If a polynomial contains the factor:

$$ \boxed{ (x-r)^m }, $$

but is not divisible by $(x-r)^{m+1}$, then $r$ is a root of multiplicity $m$.

$$ \boxed{ (x-r)^m\mid P(x) \quad\text{but}\quad (x-r)^{m+1}\nmid P(x) } $$

The word multiplicity comes from the Latin root associated with multiple and many-fold. Its mathematical meaning preserves that linguistic idea: an object may occur once, twice, three times, or with still higher repetition.

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \text{multiplicity} = \text{count of repeated occurrence in a specified structure} }. $$

The phrase is context-sensitive. A root of a polynomial, an eigenvalue of a matrix, an intersection point, or an element of a multiset can each have a multiplicity, but the rule for determining that multiplicity depends on the field.

Operational Nuance

Polynomial Root Multiplicity

Let $P(x)$ be a polynomial. A number $r$ has multiplicity $m$ as a root if:

$$ \boxed{ P(x) = (x-r)^mQ(x) } $$

with:

$$ \boxed{ Q(r)\neq0 }. $$

Multiplicity One

If:

$$ P(x) = (x-r)Q(x) $$

and $Q(r)\neq0$, then $r$ has multiplicity:

$$ \boxed{ 1 }. $$

Such a root is commonly called a simple root.

Multiplicity Two

If:

$$ P(x) = (x-r)^2Q(x), \qquad Q(r)\neq0, $$

then $r$ has multiplicity:

$$ \boxed{ 2 }. $$

This is commonly called a double root.

Multiplicity Three

A root of multiplicity $3$ is a:

$$ \boxed{ \text{triple root} }. $$

Higher Multiplicity

Beyond small cases, one ordinarily says "a root of multiplicity $m$" rather than inventing special names for every value of $m$.

Example

Consider:

$$ P(x) = (x-2)^3(x+1)^2(x-5). $$

Then:

  • $x=2$ has multiplicity $3$;
  • $x=-1$ has multiplicity $2$;
  • $x=5$ has multiplicity $1$.

Total Multiplicity

For the factored polynomial above:

$$ \boxed{ 3+2+1=6 }. $$

This equals the polynomial's degree.

Fundamental Theorem of Algebra Connection

A nonconstant polynomial of degree $n$ over the complex numbers has exactly $n$ roots when roots are counted according to multiplicity.

$$ \boxed{ \deg P=n \Longrightarrow n\text{ complex roots counted with multiplicity} }. $$

Distinct Roots vs. Roots Counted with Multiplicity

A polynomial can have fewer than $n$ distinct roots while still having $n$ roots counted with multiplicity.

For example:

$$ P(x)=(x-1)^4 $$

has one distinct root:

$$ x=1, $$

but that root has multiplicity $4$.

Factor Exponent Interpretation

In a fully factored polynomial, multiplicity is read directly from the exponent on the corresponding linear factor.

$$ \boxed{ (x-r)^m \longrightarrow r\text{ has multiplicity }m }. $$

Multiplicity and Derivatives

A root $r$ has multiplicity at least $2$ exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ P(r)=0 \quad\text{and}\quad P'(r)=0 }. $$

General Derivative Criterion

A root $r$ has multiplicity exactly $m$ when:

$$ \boxed{ P(r) = P'(r) = \cdots = P^{(m-1)}(r) = 0 } $$

but:

$$ \boxed{ P^{(m)}(r)\neq0 }. $$

Simple Root Derivative Test

If:

$$ P(r)=0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad P'(r)\neq0, $$

then $r$ is a simple root.

Multiplicity and Graph Behavior

For real polynomial graphs, multiplicity strongly influences local behavior near a real zero.

Odd Multiplicity

A real polynomial generally changes sign across a root of odd multiplicity.

$$ \boxed{ m\text{ odd} \Longrightarrow \text{graph crosses the }x\text{-axis locally} }. $$

Even Multiplicity

At a root of even multiplicity, the polynomial generally retains the same sign on both sides.

$$ \boxed{ m\text{ even} \Longrightarrow \text{graph touches and turns locally} }. $$

Multiplicity One Graph

A simple root typically crosses the axis with nonzero slope.

Multiplicity Two Graph

A double root typically touches the axis and reverses vertical direction.

Multiplicity Three Graph

A triple root crosses the axis but is flatter than a simple root.

Higher Multiplicity and Flatness

Higher multiplicity usually produces increasing local flatness because more derivatives vanish at the root.

Local Model

Near a root $r$ of multiplicity $m$:

$$ P(x) = (x-r)^mQ(x), \qquad Q(r)\neq0. $$

The factor $(x-r)^m$ controls the basic crossing or touching pattern.

Zeros of General Functions

The idea of multiplicity extends beyond polynomials. A sufficiently differentiable function $f$ can have a zero of order $m$ at $a$ when:

$$ \boxed{ f(a) = f'(a) = \cdots = f^{(m-1)}(a) = 0 } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ f^{(m)}(a)\neq0 }. $$

Order of a Zero

In analysis and complex analysis, order of the zero is often used interchangeably with multiplicity of the zero.

Analytic Function Form

If $f$ is analytic near $z_0$ and has a zero of multiplicity $m$, then:

$$ \boxed{ f(z) = (z-z_0)^m g(z) } $$

where:

$$ \boxed{ g(z_0)\neq0 }. $$

Root Multiplicity and Factorization

Multiplicity is fundamentally a factor-counting concept. If:

$$ P(x) = c \prod_{j=1}^{k} (x-r_j)^{m_j}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \deg P = \sum_{j=1}^{k}m_j }. $$

Repeated Factor

A factor $(x-r)$ is repeated exactly when its exponent exceeds $1$.

Square-Free Polynomial

A polynomial is square-free if it has no repeated irreducible factors.

Over a field of characteristic zero, a polynomial is square-free exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(P,P')=1 } $$

up to multiplication by nonzero constants.

Repeated Root Detection

If $P$ and $P'$ share a nonconstant factor, then $P$ has a repeated root over a suitable splitting field.

Discriminant Connection

For many polynomial families, the discriminant detects repeated roots.

A polynomial has a repeated root precisely when its discriminant is zero, under the standard discriminant construction.

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Disc}(P)=0 \Longleftrightarrow P\text{ has a repeated root} } $$

Quadratic Case

For:

$$ ax^2+bx+c, $$

the discriminant is:

$$ \Delta=b^2-4ac. $$

If:

$$ \boxed{ \Delta=0 }, $$

the quadratic has one distinct root of multiplicity $2$.

Eigenvalue Multiplicity

In linear algebra, eigenvalues have two important notions of multiplicity:

  • algebraic multiplicity;
  • geometric multiplicity.

Algebraic Multiplicity

The algebraic multiplicity of an eigenvalue $\lambda$ is its multiplicity as a root of the characteristic polynomial:

$$ \boxed{ p_A(t) = \det(tI-A) }. $$

If:

$$ p_A(t) = (t-\lambda)^m q(t), \qquad q(\lambda)\neq0, $$

then $\lambda$ has algebraic multiplicity $m$.

Geometric Multiplicity

The geometric multiplicity of an eigenvalue $\lambda$ is the dimension of its eigenspace:

$$ \boxed{ \dim\ker(A-\lambda I) }. $$

Multiplicity Inequality

For an eigenvalue:

$$ \boxed{ 1 \leq \text{geometric multiplicity} \leq \text{algebraic multiplicity} }. $$

Diagonalizability Connection

A matrix is diagonalizable when it has enough linearly independent eigenvectors. Over a splitting field, this is equivalent to every eigenvalue having geometric multiplicity equal to its algebraic multiplicity.

Repeated Eigenvalue

An eigenvalue with algebraic multiplicity greater than $1$ is a repeated eigenvalue.

Repeated Eigenvalue Does Not Automatically Prevent Diagonalization

A repeated eigenvalue can still have a full-dimensional eigenspace.

Example: Diagonal Matrix

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0\\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}. $$

The eigenvalue $2$ has algebraic multiplicity $2$ and geometric multiplicity $2$.

Example: Defective Matrix

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1\\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}. $$

The eigenvalue $2$ has algebraic multiplicity $2$ but geometric multiplicity $1$.

Jordan Form

In Jordan canonical form, algebraic multiplicity counts the total size of Jordan blocks for an eigenvalue, while geometric multiplicity counts the number of Jordan blocks associated with that eigenvalue.

Multisets

A multiset permits repeated elements. The multiplicity of an element is the number of times it appears.

For the multiset:

$$ \{1,1,1,2,2,5\}, $$

the multiplicities are:

  • $1$ has multiplicity $3$;
  • $2$ has multiplicity $2$;
  • $5$ has multiplicity $1$.

Set vs. Multiset

Ordinary sets ignore repetition:

$$ \{1,1,2\} = \{1,2\} $$

as sets.

A multiset retains multiplicity information.

Prime Factorization Multiplicity

In integer factorization, the exponent of a prime can be understood as its multiplicity.

For:

$$ 360 = 2^3\cdot3^2\cdot5, $$

the prime factors occur with multiplicities $3$, $2$, and $1$ respectively.

$p$-Adic Valuation Connection

The exponent of a prime $p$ in an integer factorization is written:

$$ \boxed{ v_p(n) } $$

and can be viewed as the multiplicity of $p$ as a prime factor of $n$.

Intersection Multiplicity

In algebraic geometry, curves can intersect with multiplicity greater than one. Tangential contact can produce higher intersection multiplicity than a simple transverse crossing.

Simple Intersection

A transverse intersection often has multiplicity $1$.

Tangent Intersection

Tangency can increase intersection multiplicity because the curves agree to higher order at the contact point.

Multiplicity in Counting Roots

The phrase:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"counted with multiplicity"} } $$

means repeated occurrences must be counted individually.

Example

The roots of:

$$ (x-2)^2(x+3) $$

counted with multiplicity are:

$$ \boxed{ 2,\ 2,\ -3 }. $$

Counted Without Multiplicity

The distinct roots are simply:

$$ \boxed{ 2,\ -3 }. $$

Multiplicity Is Always Contextual

A statement such as "the multiplicity is $2$" is incomplete unless one knows what is being counted and in which structure.

Multiplicity vs. Frequency

In elementary data analysis, frequency is usually preferred for counting repeated observed data values. In algebraic contexts, multiplicity is more common for repeated structural occurrences.

Multiplicity vs. Cardinality

Cardinality counts distinct elements of an ordinary set. Multiplicity counts repetition of a particular object within a structure where repetitions are meaningful.

Multiplicity vs. Degree

A root's multiplicity is not automatically the polynomial's degree.

For:

$$ P(x) = (x-1)^2(x+4)^3, $$

the degree is $5$, while the root multiplicities are $2$ and $3$.

Multiplicity vs. Number of Distinct Roots

A degree-$n$ polynomial can have anywhere from one to $n$ distinct complex roots, while still having exactly $n$ roots counted with multiplicity.

Multiplicity vs. Exponent in an Unrelated Expression

An exponent indicates multiplicity only when it appears on a factor whose repeated occurrence is being counted.

Example

In:

$$ (x-3)^4, $$

the exponent $4$ is the multiplicity of the root $3$.

In:

$$ x^4+1, $$

the exponent $4$ is the degree of the monomial term $x^4$, not automatically the multiplicity of any root.

Common Error: Counting a Repeated Root Only Once

If the instruction says "counting multiplicity," a double root contributes $2$, not $1$, to the total root count.

Common Error: Confusing Multiplicity with Root Value

In:

$$ (x-5)^3, $$

the root is:

$$ 5, $$

while its multiplicity is:

$$ \boxed{ 3 }. $$

Common Error: Reading the Wrong Sign

The factor:

$$ (x+4)^2 $$

gives root:

$$ \boxed{ x=-4 } $$

with multiplicity $2$.

Common Error: Using the Factor Exponent as the Root

In $(x-r)^m$, $r$ is the root and $m$ is the multiplicity.

Common Error: Assuming All Repeated Roots Have Multiplicity Two

Repeated roots can have multiplicity $2$, $3$, $4$, or any larger positive integer.

Common Error: Assuming Even Multiplicity Means a Positive Root

Multiplicity concerns repetition, not the sign of the root.

Common Error: Assuming Odd Multiplicity Means an Odd Root Value

"Odd multiplicity" refers to the integer count $m$, not the parity of the root itself.

Common Error: Assuming the Graph Always Crosses at Every Zero

Even-multiplicity roots generally touch and turn rather than cross.

Common Error: Assuming a Repeated Root Must Be a Local Extremum

A triple root is repeated but usually crosses the axis rather than producing an extremum.

Common Error: Assuming Repeated Eigenvalues Are Automatically Defective

Algebraic multiplicity greater than one does not by itself prevent diagonalizability.

Common Error: Confusing Algebraic and Geometric Multiplicity

Algebraic multiplicity counts characteristic-polynomial repetition; geometric multiplicity counts eigenspace dimension.

Common Error: Assuming Geometric Multiplicity Can Exceed Algebraic Multiplicity

For an eigenvalue:

$$ \boxed{ \text{geometric multiplicity} \leq \text{algebraic multiplicity} }. $$

Common Error: Ignoring Context

Root multiplicity, eigenvalue multiplicity, prime-factor multiplicity, and multiset multiplicity are related ideas but have different formal definitions.

Common Misconception: Multiplicity does not mean merely that an object is "important" or appears visually more than once. It is a precise count of repetition within a specified mathematical structure. For a polynomial root $r$, multiplicity $m$ means that $(x-r)^m$ divides the polynomial but $(x-r)^{m+1}$ does not. This repeated-factor interpretation then extends naturally to repeated eigenvalues, zeros of analytic functions, prime factors, multisets, and intersection theory.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$(x-r)^m$ "The root r has multiplicity m," when this is the exact highest repeated factor.
$(x-2)^3$ "Two is a root of multiplicity three," or "two is a triple root."
$P(r)=P'(r)=0$ "R is at least a double root," assuming the polynomial context.
$P^{(m)}(r)\neq0$ "The first nonzero derivative at r occurs at order m."
$p_A(t)=(t-\lambda)^m q(t)$ "Lambda has algebraic multiplicity m."
$\dim\ker(A-\lambda I)$ "The geometric multiplicity of lambda."
$v_p(n)=k$ "The prime p occurs with multiplicity k in n," or "the p-adic valuation of n is k."

The phrase "counted with multiplicity" means repeated occurrences are included separately in the count.

Thus for:

$$ P(x) = (x-1)^2(x+4), $$

one may say:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"The polynomial has three roots counted with multiplicity."} } $$

but only two distinct roots.

In linear algebra, the unqualified phrase multiplicity of an eigenvalue can be ambiguous. Careful writing should specify algebraic multiplicity or geometric multiplicity.

Defense Notation Advice: For polynomial roots, identify the exact exponent of the repeated factor $(x-r)^m$. Do not confuse the root $r$ with its multiplicity $m$. When eigenvalues are involved, specify whether multiplicity means characteristic-polynomial repetition or eigenspace dimension. When a problem says "count with multiplicity," include repeated occurrences in the total rather than listing only distinct values.

  • Double Root: Multiplicity $2$.
  • Triple Root: Multiplicity $3$.
  • Higher Root: Described as "multiplicity $m$."
  • Degree Connection: Root multiplicities sum to the degree after complete factorization over a splitting field.
  • Fundamental Theorem of Algebra: Degree-$n$ complex polynomial has $n$ roots counted with multiplicity.
  • Distinct Root Count: Can be smaller than the degree.
  • Derivative Criterion: Vanishing derivatives detect repeated roots.
  • Exact Multiplicity: First nonzero derivative occurs at order $m$.
  • Simple-Root Test: $P(r)=0$ and $P'(r)\neq0$.
  • Repeated Root: $P(r)=P'(r)=0$ implies multiplicity at least $2$.
  • Odd Multiplicity: Real polynomial typically crosses the axis.
  • Even Multiplicity: Real polynomial typically touches and turns.
  • Higher Multiplicity: Produces increased local flatness.
  • Order of Zero: Common analytic synonym for multiplicity of a zero.

  • Repeated Factor: Factor exponent greater than one.
  • Square-Free Polynomial: Polynomial without repeated irreducible factors.
  • Derivative GCD Test: Repeated factors are related to nontrivial $\gcd(P,P')$.
  • Discriminant: Zero discriminant signals repeated polynomial roots under the standard construction.
  • Quadratic: Zero discriminant produces a double root.
  • Eigenvalue: Can possess algebraic and geometric multiplicity.
  • Algebraic Multiplicity: Multiplicity as a root of the characteristic polynomial.
  • Geometric Multiplicity: Dimension of the eigenspace.
  • Eigenvalue Inequality: Geometric multiplicity does not exceed algebraic multiplicity.
  • Diagonalizability: Requires sufficient geometric multiplicity across eigenvalues.
  • Repeated Eigenvalue: Algebraic multiplicity greater than one.
  • Jordan Form: Algebraic multiplicity counts total Jordan-block size for an eigenvalue.
  • Jordan Blocks: Geometric multiplicity counts the number of Jordan blocks for an eigenvalue.

  • Prime Factorization: Exponent of a prime records its multiplicity.
