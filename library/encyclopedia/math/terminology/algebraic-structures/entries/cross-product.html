Cross Product

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

The cross product, also called the vector product, is a binary operation on vectors in three-dimensional Euclidean space that produces another vector.

For vectors $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$, their cross product is written:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} }. $$

The resulting vector is perpendicular to both original vectors, with magnitude:

$$ \boxed{ \|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\| = \|\mathbf{a}\| \|\mathbf{b}\| \sin\theta }, $$

where $\theta$ is the angle between $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$.

The word cross refers to the multiplication symbol $\times$ commonly used in the notation. The alternative term vector product emphasizes that the result is a vector, contrasting it with the dot product, whose result is a scalar.

The cross product encodes both geometric magnitude and spatial orientation. Its direction is chosen according to the right-hand rule in a standard right-handed coordinate system.

Operational Nuance

Coordinate Definition

Let:

$$ \mathbf{a} = \langle a_1,a_2,a_3\rangle $$

and:

$$ \mathbf{b} = \langle b_1,b_2,b_3\rangle. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} = \left\langle a_2b_3-a_3b_2,\; a_3b_1-a_1b_3,\; a_1b_2-a_2b_1 \right\rangle }. $$

Determinant Mnemonic

The coordinate formula is often remembered using the formal determinant:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k}\\ a_1 & a_2 & a_3\\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \end{vmatrix} }. $$

Expanding along the first row gives:

$$ \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} = (a_2b_3-a_3b_2)\mathbf{i} - (a_1b_3-a_3b_1)\mathbf{j} + (a_1b_2-a_2b_1)\mathbf{k}. $$

The middle component is frequently a source of sign errors.

Example

Let:

$$ \mathbf{a}=\langle1,2,3\rangle, \qquad \mathbf{b}=\langle4,5,6\rangle. $$

Then:

$$ \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} = \left\langle 2(6)-3(5),\; 3(4)-1(6),\; 1(5)-2(4) \right\rangle. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} = \langle-3,6,-3\rangle }. $$

Perpendicularity

The cross product is orthogonal to each original vector:

$$ \boxed{ (\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b})\cdot\mathbf{a}=0 } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ (\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b})\cdot\mathbf{b}=0 }. $$

Therefore, when $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ are nonparallel, $\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}$ is a normal vector to the plane containing them.

Magnitude

If $\theta$ is the angle between the vectors:

$$ 0\leq\theta\leq\pi, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\| = \|\mathbf{a}\| \|\mathbf{b}\| \sin\theta }. $$

The magnitude is greatest when the vectors are perpendicular and zero when they are parallel or antiparallel.

Parallel Vectors

If $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ are parallel, then:

$$ \theta=0 \quad\text{or}\quad \theta=\pi. $$

Since:

$$ \sin0=\sin\pi=0, $$

one obtains:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0} }. $$

Parallelism Test

For nonzero vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0} \iff \mathbf{a}\parallel\mathbf{b} }. $$

Direction and the Right-Hand Rule

The magnitude formula alone does not determine which of the two possible perpendicular directions should be chosen.

In a right-handed coordinate system, curl the fingers of the right hand from $\mathbf{a}$ toward $\mathbf{b}$ through the smaller angle. The thumb points in the direction of:

$$ \boxed{\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}}. $$

Order Matters

The cross product is anticommutative:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} = -(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{a}) }. $$

Reversing the order preserves the magnitude but reverses the direction.

Basis Vector Products

In the standard right-handed basis:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{i}\times\mathbf{j} = \mathbf{k} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{j}\times\mathbf{k} = \mathbf{i} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{i} = \mathbf{j} }. $$

Reversing each order changes the sign:

$$ \mathbf{j}\times\mathbf{i}=-\mathbf{k}, $$ $$ \mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{j}=-\mathbf{i}, $$ $$ \mathbf{i}\times\mathbf{k}=-\mathbf{j}. $$

Self-Cross Product

Any vector crossed with itself gives the zero vector:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0} }. $$

This follows because the angle between a vector and itself is zero.

Zero Vector

For every vector $\mathbf{a}$:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}\times\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0} }. $$

Distributive Property

The cross product distributes over vector addition:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a}\times(\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{c}) = \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} + \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{c} }. $$

Similarly:

$$ (\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b})\times\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{c} + \mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c}. $$

Scalar Multiplication

For scalar $\lambda$:

$$ \boxed{ (\lambda\mathbf{a})\times\mathbf{b} = \lambda(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}) = \mathbf{a}\times(\lambda\mathbf{b}) }. $$

Not Associative

The cross product is generally not associative:

$$ \boxed{ (\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b})\times\mathbf{c} \neq \mathbf{a}\times(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c}) }. $$

Parentheses therefore matter in iterated cross products.

Vector Triple Product

One important identity is:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a}\times(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c}) = \mathbf{b}(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{c}) - \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}) }. $$

This is sometimes remembered by the pattern:

$$ \text{BAC}-\text{CAB}. $$

Area of a Parallelogram

The magnitude of the cross product equals the area of the parallelogram determined by $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$:

$$ \boxed{ A_{\text{parallelogram}} = \|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\| }. $$

Area of a Triangle

The corresponding triangle has half that area:

$$ \boxed{ A_{\triangle} = \frac12 \|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\| }. $$

Normal Vector to a Plane

If two nonparallel vectors $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ lie in a plane, then:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{n} = \mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v} } $$

is perpendicular to the plane and may be used as a normal vector.

Cross Product vs. Dot Product

The dot product produces a scalar:

$$ \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = \|\mathbf{a}\| \|\mathbf{b}\| \cos\theta, $$

whereas the cross product produces a vector:

$$ \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} = \|\mathbf{a}\| \|\mathbf{b}\| \sin\theta\,\mathbf{n}, $$

where $\mathbf{n}$ is an appropriately oriented unit normal vector.

Common Misconception: The cross product is not ordinary componentwise multiplication. For example:

$$ \langle a,b,c\rangle \times \langle d,e,f\rangle $$

does not equal:

$$ \langle ad,be,cf\rangle. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}$ "A cross b."
$\|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\|$ "The magnitude of a cross b."
$\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{0}$ "A cross b equals the zero vector."
$\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}=-(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{a})$ "A cross b equals the negative of b cross a."
$\mathbf{i}\times\mathbf{j}=\mathbf{k}$ "I cross j equals k."
$\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}$ "U cross v."
$\|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\|=\|\mathbf{a}\|\|\mathbf{b}\|\sin\theta$ "The magnitude of a cross b equals the magnitude of a times the magnitude of b times sine theta."

The symbol:

$$ \times $$

is read cross when used between vectors in this operation, rather than simply times.

Thus:

$$ \mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v} $$

is read:

$$ \text{"u cross v."} $$

Vector notation should be preserved because the output is itself a vector. For example:

$$ \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} = \langle3,-2,5\rangle $$

is fundamentally different from a scalar equation such as:

$$ \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=7. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Preserve the order of the vectors. Since:

$$ \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} = -\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{a}, $$

reversing the operands changes the sign and therefore reverses the direction of the resulting vector.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Coordinate Formula: For: $$ \mathbf{a} = \langle a_1,a_2,a_3\rangle, \qquad \mathbf{b} = \langle b_1,b_2,b_3\rangle, $$ $$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} = \langle a_2b_3-a_3b_2,\; a_3b_1-a_1b_3,\; a_1b_2-a_2b_1 \rangle }. $$
  • Magnitude Formula: $$ \boxed{ \|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\| = \|\mathbf{a}\| \|\mathbf{b}\| \sin\theta }. $$
  • Direction: The direction of $\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}$ is perpendicular to both vectors and determined by the right-hand rule in a right-handed system.
  • Anticommutativity: $$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} = -\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{a} }. $$
  • Self Product: $$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0} }. $$
  • Parallelism: For nonzero vectors: $$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0} \iff \mathbf{a}\parallel\mathbf{b} }. $$
  • Perpendicular Vectors: If: $$ \theta=\frac{\pi}{2}, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ \|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\| = \|\mathbf{a}\|\|\mathbf{b}\| }. $$
  • Orthogonality: $$ (\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b})\cdot\mathbf{a}=0 $$ and: $$ (\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b})\cdot\mathbf{b}=0. $$
  • Standard Basis: $$ \boxed{ \mathbf{i}\times\mathbf{j}=\mathbf{k},\qquad \mathbf{j}\times\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{i},\qquad \mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{i}=\mathbf{j} }. $$
  • Reverse Basis Products: $$ \mathbf{j}\times\mathbf{i}=-\mathbf{k}, $$ $$ \mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{j}=-\mathbf{i}, $$ $$ \mathbf{i}\times\mathbf{k}=-\mathbf{j}. $$
  • Distributivity: $$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a}\times(\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{c}) = \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} + \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{c} }. $$
  • Scalar Compatibility: $$ (\lambda\mathbf{a})\times\mathbf{b} = \lambda(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}). $$
  • Nonassociativity: In general: $$ \boxed{ (\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b})\times\mathbf{c} \neq \mathbf{a}\times(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c}) }. $$
  • Vector Triple Product: $$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a}\times(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c}) = \mathbf{b}(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{c}) - \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}) }. $$
  • Scalar Triple Product: The expression: $$ \mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c}) $$ is a scalar whose absolute value equals the volume of the parallelepiped determined by the three vectors.
  • Parallelogram Area: $$ \boxed{ A= \|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\| }. $$
  • Triangle Area: $$ \boxed{ A= \frac12 \|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\| }. $$
  • Normal Vector: For two nonparallel vectors lying in a plane: $$ \boxed{ \mathbf{n} = \mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v} } $$ gives a normal direction.
  • Plane Equation: If $\mathbf{n}$ is obtained from a cross product and $\mathbf{r}_0$ is a point on a plane, then: $$ \boxed{ \mathbf{n}\cdot(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_0)=0 } $$ gives a vector equation of the plane.
  • Three Points and a Plane: Given noncollinear points $A$, $B$, and $C$, vectors: $$ \overrightarrow{AB} \quad\text{and}\quad \overrightarrow{AC} $$ lie in the plane, so: $$ \boxed{ \overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC} } $$ is normal to the plane.
  • Collinearity Test: Points $A$, $B$, and $C$ are collinear when: $$ \overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC} = \mathbf{0}. $$
  • Distance Geometry: Cross products can be used to derive distances from points to lines in three-dimensional space.
  • Distance from a Point to a Line: If a line has direction $\mathbf{v}$ and passes through $A$, then the distance from point $P$ to the line is: $$ \boxed{ d= \frac{ \|\overrightarrow{AP}\times\mathbf{v}\| }{ \|\mathbf{v}\| } }. $$
  • Torque: In mechanics: $$ \boxed{ \boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F} }, $$ where $\mathbf{r}$ is a position vector and $\mathbf{F}$ is force.
  • Torque Magnitude: $$ \|\boldsymbol{\tau}\| = rF\sin\theta. $$
  • Angular Momentum: For linear momentum $\mathbf{p}$: $$ \boxed{ \mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{p} }. $$
  • Magnetic Force: The magnetic portion of the Lorentz force is: $$ \boxed{ \mathbf{F} = q\,\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B} }. $$
  • Vector Calculus: The curl of a vector field is symbolically written: $$ \boxed{ \nabla\times\mathbf{F} }. $$ The cross-product symbol reflects the orientation-dependent structure of curl, although $\nabla$ is a differential operator rather than an ordinary vector.
  • Surface Normals: For a parametrized surface: $$ \mathbf{r}(u,v), $$ a normal vector is obtained from: $$ \boxed{ \mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v }. $$
  • Surface Area: The area element of a parametrized surface is: $$ \boxed{ dS= \|\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v\|\,du\,dv }. $$
  • Orientation: Reversing the order: $$ \mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v \longrightarrow \mathbf{r}_v\times\mathbf{r}_u $$ reverses the chosen surface normal.
  • Computational Geometry: Cross-product signs can determine whether an oriented turn in a plane is clockwise or counterclockwise when planar vectors are embedded in three-dimensional space.
  • Planar Version: For: $$ \mathbf{a}=\langle a_x,a_y\rangle, \qquad \mathbf{b}=\langle b_x,b_y\rangle, $$ the scalar: $$ \boxed{ a_xb_y-a_yb_x } $$ is often called the two-dimensional cross product or signed area determinant, although the standard vector cross product formally produces a vector in three-dimensional space.
  • Orientation in the Plane: The sign of: $$ a_xb_y-a_yb_x $$ indicates the orientation of the ordered pair under the usual Cartesian convention.
  • Dot Product Contrast: $$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} \in\mathbb{R} } $$ while: $$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} \in\mathbb{R}^3 }. $$
  • Cosine vs. Sine: The dot product involves: $$ \cos\theta, $$ while the cross-product magnitude involves: $$ \sin\theta. $$
  • Dimension: The familiar cross product is fundamentally associated with oriented three-dimensional Euclidean vector space. Related algebraic constructions exist in other dimensions, but the ordinary elementary cross product does not generalize as the same binary vector-valued operation to arbitrary $\mathbb{R}^n$.
  • Exterior Algebra: In higher mathematics, the wedge product: $$ \mathbf{a}\wedge\mathbf{b} $$ provides a dimension-independent way to encode oriented area information. In three dimensions, the cross product is closely related to the wedge product through the Hodge star.
  • Orientation Dependence: Unlike the dot product, the direction of the cross product depends on the chosen orientation of the coordinate system.
  • Right-Handed Coordinates: In the standard orientation: $$ \mathbf{i}\times\mathbf{j}=\mathbf{k}. $$ A reversal of spatial orientation reverses the corresponding cross-product direction convention.
  • Terminological Contrast: The cross product or vector product produces a vector perpendicular to its inputs. The dot product or scalar product produces a scalar measuring directional alignment.
  • Core Interpretation: The cross product combines area, perpendicularity, and orientation: $$ \boxed{ \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} = \|\mathbf{a}\| \|\mathbf{b}\| \sin\theta\,\mathbf{n} }. $$ Its magnitude measures the oriented pair's area scale, while its direction supplies a perpendicular orientation according to the right-hand rule.
