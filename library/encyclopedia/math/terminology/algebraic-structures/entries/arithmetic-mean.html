Arithmetic Mean

Subfield: Probability & Statistical Inference

Etymology & Roots

The arithmetic mean is a measure of central tendency obtained by adding a collection of numerical values and dividing their total by the number of values.

For values:

$$ x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n, $$

their arithmetic mean is:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{x_1+x_2+\cdots+x_n}{n} }. $$

In everyday language, the arithmetic mean is frequently called the average. The word average, however, is broader and may refer to other measures of center depending on context.

The modifier arithmetic distinguishes this mean from other kinds of means, including the geometric mean, harmonic mean, quadratic mean, and weighted mean.

Operational Nuance

Basic Formula

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i }. $$

This is equivalent to:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{x_1+x_2+\cdots+x_n}{n} }. $$

Example

For the values:

$$ 4,\quad7,\quad9,\quad10, $$

the total is:

$$ 4+7+9+10=30. $$

Since there are four values:

$$ \bar{x} = \frac{30}{4} = \boxed{7.5}. $$

Equal-Sharing Interpretation

The arithmetic mean can be understood as the value each member would receive if the total quantity were redistributed equally while preserving the same total.

If:

$$ S=x_1+x_2+\cdots+x_n, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ S=n\bar{x} }. $$

Thus $n$ equal quantities of size $\bar{x}$ have exactly the same total as the original data.

Recovering the Total from the Mean

If the mean and number of values are known:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i = n\bar{x} }. $$

This relationship is useful in missing-value and combined-average problems.

Finding a Missing Value

Suppose five numbers have mean $12$. Their total must be:

$$ 5(12)=60. $$

If four of the values are:

$$ 8,\quad10,\quad13,\quad14, $$

their subtotal is:

$$ 45. $$

Therefore the missing value is:

$$ \boxed{ 60-45=15 }. $$

Balance-Point Interpretation

The arithmetic mean is also the balance point of the data on a number line.

The signed deviations from the mean satisfy:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i-\bar{x}) = 0 }. $$

Values above the mean contribute positive deviations, while values below it contribute negative deviations. These deviations balance exactly.

Proof of the Zero-Deviation Property

$$ \sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\bar{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i - \sum_{i=1}^{n}\bar{x}. $$

Since $\bar{x}$ appears $n$ times:

$$ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\bar{x}=n\bar{x}. $$

But:

$$ \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i=n\bar{x}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\bar{x})=0 }. $$

Sample Mean

In statistics, the arithmetic mean of sample observations is commonly written:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i }. $$

The symbol $\bar{x}$ is read x-bar.

Population Mean

For a finite population with values:

$$ x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_N, $$

the population mean is often denoted by the Greek letter $\mu$:

$$ \boxed{ \mu = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N}x_i }. $$

Sample Statistic versus Population Parameter

In statistical usage:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \text{sample statistic} } $$

while:

$$ \boxed{ \mu = \text{population parameter} }. $$

A sample mean is frequently used to estimate an unknown population mean.

Mean of a Frequency Table

Suppose value $x_i$ occurs with frequency $f_i$. Then:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{ \sum f_i x_i }{ \sum f_i } }. $$

Each value is counted according to the number of times it occurs.

Weighted Arithmetic Mean

If observations receive weights $w_i$, the weighted mean is:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x}_w = \frac{ \sum_{i=1}^{n}w_ix_i }{ \sum_{i=1}^{n}w_i } } $$

provided the total weight is nonzero.

The ordinary arithmetic mean is the special case in which all weights are equal.

Weighted-Mean Example

Suppose a course grade consists of:

$$ 80\text{ at }20\%, \qquad 90\text{ at }30\%, \qquad 70\text{ at }50\%. $$

Then:

$$ \bar{x}_w = 0.20(80) + 0.30(90) + 0.50(70). $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x}_w=78 }. $$

Mean of Two Numbers

For two numbers $a$ and $b$:

$$ \boxed{ A= \frac{a+b}{2} }. $$

This value lies exactly halfway between $a$ and $b$ on the real number line.

Midpoint Connection

The midpoint of two real coordinates $a$ and $b$ is their arithmetic mean:

$$ \boxed{ m= \frac{a+b}{2} }. $$

Likewise, the midpoint of points $(x_1,y_1)$ and $(x_2,y_2)$ is found by taking arithmetic means coordinate by coordinate:

$$ \boxed{ \left( \frac{x_1+x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1+y_2}{2} \right) }. $$

Arithmetic Mean in an Arithmetic Sequence

If $a$, $m$, and $b$ are consecutive terms of an arithmetic sequence, then:

$$ m-a=b-m. $$

Therefore:

$$ 2m=a+b. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ m= \frac{a+b}{2} }. $$

Thus the middle term is the arithmetic mean of its two neighboring terms.

Inserting Arithmetic Means

To insert one arithmetic mean between $a$ and $b$, use:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{a+b}{2} }. $$

To insert several arithmetic means, construct an arithmetic sequence connecting the endpoints.

Combined Mean

Suppose one group has:

$$ n_1 \text{ values with mean } \bar{x}_1 $$

and another has:

$$ n_2 \text{ values with mean } \bar{x}_2. $$

Their totals are:

$$ n_1\bar{x}_1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad n_2\bar{x}_2. $$

Therefore the combined mean is:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{ n_1\bar{x}_1+n_2\bar{x}_2 }{ n_1+n_2 } }. $$

Why Means Cannot Usually Be Averaged Directly

In general:

$$ \frac{\bar{x}_1+\bar{x}_2}{2} $$

is the combined mean only when the two groups have equal sizes.

Otherwise the group means must be weighted by their group counts.

Adding a Constant to Every Value

Suppose:

$$ y_i=x_i+c. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{y} = \bar{x}+c }. $$

Shifting every observation by the same amount shifts the mean by that amount.

Multiplying Every Value by a Constant

If:

$$ y_i=cx_i, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{y} = c\bar{x} }. $$

Linear Transformation of the Data

More generally, if:

$$ y_i=ax_i+b, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{y} = a\bar{x}+b }. $$

This linearity is one of the arithmetic mean's most useful algebraic properties.

Mean of Sums

If paired data $x_i$ and $y_i$ are combined term by term:

$$ z_i=x_i+y_i, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{z} = \bar{x}+\bar{y} }. $$

Mean of Differences

$$ \boxed{ \overline{x-y} = \bar{x}-\bar{y} }. $$

Mean of Products Is Different

In general:

$$ \boxed{ \overline{xy} \ne \bar{x}\bar{y} }. $$

The arithmetic mean is linear but does not generally distribute through nonlinear operations such as multiplication, squaring, reciprocation, or exponentiation.

Mean of a Constant

If every value equals $c$:

$$ c,c,\ldots,c, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x}=c }. $$

Mean Lies between the Minimum and Maximum

For a finite nonempty set of real observations:

$$ \boxed{ \min(x_i) \le \bar{x} \le \max(x_i) }. $$

Equality at one endpoint occurs only when all observations equal that endpoint.

The Mean Need Not Be an Observed Value

For:

$$ 1,\quad2,\quad8, $$

the mean is:

$$ \frac{11}{3}. $$

That number does not appear in the original data.

$$ \boxed{ \text{the mean need not be a member of the data set} }. $$

Sensitivity to Extreme Values

The arithmetic mean uses every numerical value directly. Consequently, unusually large or small observations can strongly affect it.

For example:

$$ 2,\ 3,\ 3,\ 4,\ 100 $$

has mean:

$$ \boxed{ 22.4 }. $$

Although four of the five observations lie between $2$ and $4$, the extreme value $100$ pulls the mean upward.

Arithmetic Mean versus Median

The median is the middle value after ordering the observations, whereas the arithmetic mean uses their numerical total.

In skewed data or data containing strong outliers, the median may better represent a typical central location.

Arithmetic Mean versus Mode

The mode is the most frequently occurring value.

A data set may have:

  • one mode,
  • multiple modes,
  • no repeated value.

The arithmetic mean does not depend on which value occurs most often except through its contribution to the total.

Arithmetic Mean versus Geometric Mean

For positive numbers $x_1,\ldots,x_n$, the geometric mean is:

$$ \boxed{ G= \left( x_1x_2\cdots x_n \right)^{1/n} }. $$

The arithmetic mean is additive in structure, while the geometric mean is multiplicative.

Arithmetic-Geometric Mean Inequality

For nonnegative real numbers:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{x_1+\cdots+x_n}{n} \ge \sqrt[n]{x_1x_2\cdots x_n} }. $$

Equality occurs precisely when all the values are equal.

Two-Number AM-GM

For $a,b\ge0$:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{a+b}{2} \ge \sqrt{ab} }. $$

Arithmetic Mean versus Harmonic Mean

For positive numbers, the harmonic mean is:

$$ \boxed{ H= \frac{n}{ \frac1{x_1}+ \frac1{x_2}+ \cdots+ \frac1{x_n} } }. $$

The harmonic mean is particularly useful for averaging rates under appropriate equal-quantity conditions.

Mean Inequality Chain

For positive data:

$$ \boxed{ H\le G\le A }. $$

Here $H$, $G$, and $A$ denote the harmonic, geometric, and arithmetic means.

Arithmetic Mean and Least Squares

The arithmetic mean has an important optimization property. For real values $x_1,\ldots,x_n$, the function:

$$ Q(c)= \sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-c)^2 $$

is minimized at:

$$ \boxed{ c=\bar{x} }. $$

Thus the arithmetic mean is the constant that minimizes the total squared deviations from the observations.

Deriving the Least-Squares Property

Differentiate:

$$ Q'(c) = -2 \sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-c). $$

Setting this equal to zero gives:

$$ \sum x_i-nc=0. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ c= \frac{\sum x_i}{n} = \bar{x} }. $$

Mean and Variance

Statistical variance is based on deviations from the arithmetic mean.

For a finite population:

$$ \boxed{ \sigma^2 = \frac1N \sum_{i=1}^{N} (x_i-\mu)^2 }. $$

The mean therefore serves as the center around which squared dispersion is measured.

Mean of a Discrete Random Variable

In probability, the expected value is a probability-weighted arithmetic mean. For a discrete random variable $X$:

$$ \boxed{ E[X] = \sum_x xP(X=x) }. $$

The probabilities act as weights.

Mean of a Continuous Random Variable

For a continuous random variable with density $f(x)$:

$$ \boxed{ E[X] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} xf(x)\,dx } $$

when the expected value exists.

Expectation as a Generalized Mean

The ordinary arithmetic mean assigns equal weight:

$$ \frac1n $$

to every observation. Expected value allows unequal weights determined by probability.

Running Mean

If the mean of the first $n$ values is $\bar{x}_n$ and a new value $x_{n+1}$ arrives, then:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x}_{n+1} = \frac{ n\bar{x}_n+x_{n+1} }{ n+1 } }. $$

This permits updating a mean without recomputing the entire original sum.

Removing an Observation

If $n$ observations have mean $\bar{x}$ and one observation $a$ is removed, the remaining $n-1$ values have mean:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x}_{\text{new}} = \frac{n\bar{x}-a}{n-1} }. $$

Replacing an Observation

If one value $a$ is replaced by $b$, the total changes by:

$$ b-a. $$

Therefore the new mean is:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x}_{\text{new}} = \bar{x} + \frac{b-a}{n} }. $$

Rounding the Mean

A mean need not have the same numerical form as the original observations. Integer data can have a fractional or decimal mean.

If rounding is required, it should generally be performed at the end of the calculation unless the application specifies otherwise.

Units

The arithmetic mean has the same units as the observations.

If the data are measured in meters:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x}\text{ is measured in meters} }. $$

If the observations are temperatures in degrees Celsius, the arithmetic mean is expressed in degrees Celsius.

Common Misconception

The arithmetic mean is not found by dividing by the largest value or by the number of spaces between observations.

The denominator is the number of observations:

$$ \boxed{ n }. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\bar{x}=\frac{x_1+\cdots+x_n}{n}$ "X-bar equals the sum of the observations divided by n."
$\bar{x}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n x_i$ "X-bar equals one over n times the sum from i equals one to n of x sub i."
$\mu=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N x_i$ "Mu equals the population total divided by capital N."
$\sum(x_i-\bar{x})=0$ "The sum of the deviations from the mean is zero."
$\bar{x}_w=\frac{\sum w_ix_i}{\sum w_i}$ "The weighted mean is the weighted sum divided by the total weight."
$H\le G\le A$ "The harmonic mean is at most the geometric mean, which is at most the arithmetic mean."

The everyday word average often means arithmetic mean, but mathematical writing is clearer when the specific measure is named. For example, arithmetic mean, median, and geometric mean are not interchangeable.

Defense Notation Advice: Keep the sample notation $\bar{x}$ and population notation $\mu$ distinct when doing statistics. When combining groups, reconstruct their totals using count × mean rather than averaging group means without regard to group size.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Formula: $$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{x_1+\cdots+x_n}{n} }. $$
  • Summation Form: $$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i }. $$
  • Equal-Sharing Interpretation: The mean is the common value obtained by redistributing the same total equally among all observations.
  • Total from Mean: $$ \boxed{ \sum x_i=n\bar{x} }. $$
  • Balance Property: $$ \boxed{ \sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\bar{x})=0 }. $$
  • Sample Mean: $$ \boxed{ \bar{x} } $$ commonly denotes a sample arithmetic mean.
  • Population Mean: $$ \boxed{ \mu = \frac1N \sum_{i=1}^{N}x_i }. $$
  • Frequency Mean: $$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{\sum f_ix_i}{\sum f_i} }. $$
  • Weighted Mean: $$ \boxed{ \bar{x}_w = \frac{\sum w_ix_i}{\sum w_i} }. $$
  • Two-Number Mean: $$ \boxed{ \frac{a+b}{2} }. $$
  • Midpoint Connection: The arithmetic mean of two coordinates gives their midpoint coordinate.
  • Arithmetic-Sequence Connection: A middle term of three consecutive arithmetic-sequence terms is the arithmetic mean of its neighbors.
  • Combined Mean: $$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{ n_1\bar{x}_1+n_2\bar{x}_2 }{ n_1+n_2 } }. $$
  • Adding a Constant: $$ \boxed{ \overline{x+c} = \bar{x}+c }. $$
  • Multiplying by a Constant: $$ \boxed{ \overline{cx} = c\bar{x} }. $$
  • Linear Transformation: $$ \boxed{ \overline{ax+b} = a\bar{x}+b }. $$
  • Mean of Sums: $$ \boxed{ \overline{x+y} = \bar{x}+\bar{y} }. $$
  • Nonlinear Caution: $$ \boxed{ \overline{xy} \ne \bar{x}\bar{y} } $$ in general.
  • Range Property: $$ \boxed{ \min(x_i) \le \bar{x} \le \max(x_i) }. $$
  • Observed-Value Caution: The arithmetic mean need not itself occur in the original data.
  • Outlier Sensitivity: Extreme observations can substantially change the arithmetic mean.
  • Median Contrast: Median depends on ordered position; arithmetic mean depends on numerical total.
  • Mode Contrast: Mode depends on frequency; arithmetic mean depends on all numerical values.
  • Geometric Mean: $$ \boxed{ G= (x_1x_2\cdots x_n)^{1/n} } $$ for positive data.
  • AM-GM: $$ \boxed{ A\ge G } $$ for nonnegative real values under the usual hypotheses.
  • Harmonic Mean: $$ \boxed{ H= \frac{n}{ \sum 1/x_i } } $$ for positive nonzero values.
  • Mean Inequality: $$ \boxed{ H\le G\le A }. $$
  • Least-Squares Property: The arithmetic mean minimizes: $$ \boxed{ \sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-c)^2 } $$ over real $c$.
  • Variance Connection: Variance measures squared deviations from the arithmetic mean.
  • Expected Value: $$ \boxed{ E[X]=\sum_x xP(X=x) } $$ is a probability-weighted mean for a discrete random variable.
  • Continuous Expectation: $$ \boxed{ E[X] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} xf(x)\,dx } $$ when the integral exists.
  • Running Mean: $$ \boxed{ \bar{x}_{n+1} = \frac{ n\bar{x}_n+x_{n+1} }{ n+1 } }. $$
  • Removing a Value: $$ \boxed{ \bar{x}_{\text{new}} = \frac{n\bar{x}-a}{n-1} }. $$
  • Replacing a Value: $$ \boxed{ \bar{x}_{\text{new}} = \bar{x} + \frac{b-a}{n} }. $$
  • Units: The arithmetic mean has the same units as the observations.
  • Common Error: Do not divide by the number of gaps between observations; divide by the number of observations.
  • Common Error: Group means cannot generally be averaged directly when the groups contain different numbers of observations.
  • Common Error: Average does not always uniquely mean arithmetic mean unless the context establishes that convention.
  • Terminological Contrast: Arithmetic mean uses addition followed by equal division. Median uses ordered position. Mode uses frequency. Geometric mean uses multiplication and roots. The arithmetic mean is especially important because it represents equal sharing, a balance point, and the least-squares center of numerical data.
