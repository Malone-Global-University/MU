Degree of a Term

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

The degree of a term measures the total exponent carried by the variable factors of a nonzero algebraic term.

In one variable, a term of the form:

$$ ax^n,\qquad a\neq0, $$

has degree:

$$ \boxed{n}. $$

In several variables, a monomial such as:

$$ ax^py^qz^r $$

has total degree:

$$ \boxed{p+q+r}. $$

The numerical coefficient does not contribute to the degree. Degree records the exponent structure of the variable part of the term.

Thus:

$$ 7x^4 $$

has degree $4$, while:

$$ -12x^2y^3 $$

has total degree:

$$ \boxed{2+3=5}. $$

The concept is fundamental because the degree of a polynomial is determined from the degrees of its nonzero terms.

Operational Nuance

One-Variable Term

For a nonzero term:

$$ ax^n,\qquad a\neq0, $$

the degree is:

$$ \boxed{\deg(ax^n)=n}. $$

Example

The term:

$$ 5x^7 $$

has degree:

$$ \boxed{7}. $$

The coefficient $5$ does not affect the degree.

Negative Coefficients

The sign of the coefficient is irrelevant to degree.

For example:

$$ -9x^6 $$

has degree:

$$ \boxed{6}. $$

Fractional Coefficients

Likewise:

$$ \frac34x^8 $$

has degree:

$$ \boxed{8}. $$

Constant Terms

A nonzero constant may be written:

$$ c=cx^0. $$

Therefore every nonzero constant term has degree:

$$ \boxed{0}. $$

For example:

$$ \deg(11)=0. $$

The Zero Term

The expression:

$$ 0x^n=0 $$

is simply the zero term. Its degree is not determined by the displayed exponent because:

$$ 0x^2=0x^{100}=0. $$

In elementary algebra, the degree of the zero term is therefore usually treated as undefined.

$$ \boxed{ \deg 0\text{ is undefined} } $$

Some advanced contexts assign:

$$ \deg0=-\infty $$

by convention.

Several Variables

For a nonzero monomial:

$$ ax_1^{n_1}x_2^{n_2}\cdots x_k^{n_k}, $$

its total degree is:

$$ \boxed{ n_1+n_2+\cdots+n_k }. $$

Two-Variable Example

The term:

$$ 4x^3y^5 $$

has total degree:

$$ \boxed{3+5=8}. $$

Three-Variable Example

The term:

$$ -2x^2y^4z^3 $$

has total degree:

$$ \boxed{2+4+3=9}. $$

Variables with Implied Exponent One

A variable written without an exponent has exponent $1$.

Therefore:

$$ 6x^2yz^4 $$

has degree:

$$ \boxed{2+1+4=7}. $$

Degree with Respect to One Variable

In multivariable algebra, one may distinguish total degree from degree in a selected variable.

For:

$$ 3x^4y^7, $$

the degree with respect to $x$ is:

$$ \boxed{4}, $$

the degree with respect to $y$ is:

$$ \boxed{7}, $$

and the total degree is:

$$ \boxed{11}. $$

Product of Terms

Multiplication adds corresponding exponents, so degrees add.

For example:

$$ (2x^3)(5x^4) = 10x^7. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ 3+4=7 }. $$

Multivariable Product

Consider:

$$ (x^2y^3)(x^4y). $$

The product is:

$$ x^6y^4. $$

The original term degrees are:

$$ 2+3=5 \qquad\text{and}\qquad 4+1=5. $$

The product has degree:

$$ \boxed{6+4=10}. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{5+5=10}. $$

Powers of a Term

If a term of degree $d$ is raised to the positive integer power $m$, its degree becomes:

$$ \boxed{md}. $$

For example:

$$ (x^2y^3)^4 = x^8y^{12}, $$

whose degree is:

$$ 8+12 = \boxed{20}. $$

Degree of a Term vs. Degree of a Polynomial

The degree of a term concerns one monomial. The degree of a nonzero polynomial is the greatest degree among its nonzero terms.

For:

$$ p(x)=4x^5-2x^3+7x-9, $$

the term degrees are:

$$ 5,\qquad3,\qquad1,\qquad0. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{\deg p=5}. $$

Multivariable Polynomial Example

Consider:

$$ f(x,y) = 2x^4y^2+5x^3y^5-y^6+4. $$

The term degrees are:

$$ 4+2=6, $$ $$ 3+5=8, $$ $$ 6, $$ $$ 0. $$

Therefore the total degree of the polynomial is:

$$ \boxed{8}. $$

Polynomial Terms Require Nonnegative Integer Exponents

In an ordinary polynomial, variable exponents must be nonnegative integers:

$$ \boxed{ 0,1,2,3,\ldots }. $$

Thus:

$$ x^{-2}, \qquad x^{1/2} $$

are not polynomial terms in $x$ under the standard definition.

Coefficient Independence

Provided the coefficient is nonzero:

$$ 2x^5,\qquad -100x^5,\qquad \frac17x^5 $$

all have degree:

$$ \boxed{5}. $$

Common Misconception: For a multivariable term, degree is not the largest individual exponent. The term $x^3y^5$ has total degree $8$, not $5$. The exponents of all variable factors are added.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$5x^7$ "Five x to the seventh; the term has degree seven."
$3x^2y^4$ "Three x squared y to the fourth; the term has total degree six."
$x^2yz^3$ "X squared y z cubed; the term has degree six."
$\deg_x(x^4y^2)=4$ "The degree with respect to x is four."
$\deg_y(x^4y^2)=2$ "The degree with respect to y is two."
$\deg(x^4y^2)=6$ "The total degree of the term is six."
$\deg 7=0$ "The degree of the nonzero constant term seven is zero."

In one variable, the phrase:

$$ \text{"degree of the term"} $$

usually means the exponent of the variable.

In several variables, it usually means the total degree:

$$ \boxed{ \deg(x^ay^bz^c) = a+b+c }. $$

If only one variable is being considered, the language should specify:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"degree with respect to }x\text{"} }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: In multivariable expressions, distinguish clearly between total degree and degree with respect to a particular variable. For $x^2y^7$, the total degree is $9$, the degree in $x$ is $2$, and the degree in $y$ is $7$.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • One-Variable Definition: $$ \boxed{ \deg(ax^n)=n, \qquad a\neq0 }. $$
  • Coefficient: A nonzero numerical coefficient does not contribute to the degree.
  • Sign: Positive and negative coefficients do not change term degree.
  • Constant Term: Every nonzero constant term has: $$ \boxed{\deg=0}. $$
  • Linear Term: $$ ax,\qquad a\neq0 $$ has degree: $$ \boxed{1}. $$
  • Quadratic Term: $$ ax^2 $$ has degree: $$ \boxed{2}. $$
  • Cubic Term: $$ ax^3 $$ has degree: $$ \boxed{3}. $$
  • Zero Term: In elementary algebra: $$ \boxed{ \deg0\text{ is undefined} }. $$
  • Advanced Zero Convention: Some algebraic treatments assign: $$ \boxed{\deg0=-\infty}. $$
  • Two Variables: $$ \boxed{ \deg(ax^py^q)=p+q }. $$
  • Three Variables: $$ \boxed{ \deg(ax^py^qz^r)=p+q+r }. $$
  • General Monomial: $$ \boxed{ \deg \left( ax_1^{n_1}\cdots x_k^{n_k} \right) = n_1+\cdots+n_k }. $$
  • Implied Exponent: A variable written as $x$ contributes: $$ \boxed{1} $$ to the degree.
  • Absent Variable: A variable absent from a monomial may be regarded as having exponent: $$ \boxed{0}. $$
  • Example: $$ 7x^3y^2 $$ has degree: $$ \boxed{5}. $$
  • Example with Three Variables: $$ 2a^4b^2c $$ has degree: $$ \boxed{4+2+1=7}. $$
  • Degree in One Variable: For: $$ x^4y^9, $$ $$ \boxed{ \deg_x=4, \qquad \deg_y=9 }. $$
  • Total Degree: For the same term: $$ \boxed{ \deg=4+9=13 }. $$
  • Products: The degree of a product of nonzero monomials equals the sum of their degrees.
  • Product Example: $$ (x^2y)(x^3y^4) = x^5y^5, $$ so: $$ \boxed{ 3+7=10 }. $$
  • Power of a Term: If a monomial has degree $d$, then its $m$th power has degree: $$ \boxed{md}. $$
  • Polynomial Degree: The degree of a nonzero polynomial is the greatest degree among its nonzero terms.
  • Polynomial Example: In: $$ 4x^6-3x^2+1, $$ the term degrees are: $$ 6,\;2,\;0, $$ so the polynomial degree is: $$ \boxed{6}. $$
  • Multivariable Polynomial: The total degree of a polynomial is the maximum total degree of its nonzero monomial terms.
  • Homogeneous Polynomial: A polynomial is homogeneous of degree $n$ when every nonzero term has total degree: $$ \boxed{n}. $$
  • Homogeneous Example: $$ x^3+2x^2y+xy^2+y^3 $$ is homogeneous of degree: $$ \boxed{3}. $$
  • Nonhomogeneous Example: $$ x^3+x^2+1 $$ contains terms of degrees: $$ 3,\;2,\;0. $$
  • Polynomial Requirement: Ordinary polynomial terms use nonnegative integer exponents.
  • Negative Exponents: $$ x^{-1} $$ is not a polynomial term in $x$.
  • Fractional Exponents: $$ x^{1/2} $$ is not a polynomial term in $x$ under the standard polynomial definition.
  • Variable in Denominator: $$ \frac1{x^2}=x^{-2} $$ is not a polynomial term.
  • Radicals: $$ \sqrt{x}=x^{1/2} $$ is not a polynomial term in $x$.
  • Like Terms: Like terms have identical variable factors and therefore the same degree.
  • Unlike Terms: Terms may have the same total degree without being like terms.
  • Example: $$ x^3y^2 \qquad\text{and}\qquad xy^4 $$ both have total degree: $$ \boxed{5}, $$ but they are not like terms.
  • Degree Does Not Determine Term Identity: Many different monomials can have the same total degree.
  • Coefficient Independence: $$ x^5,\quad 100x^5,\quad -\frac32x^5 $$ all have degree: $$ \boxed{5}. $$
  • Scaling: Multiplying a nonzero term by a nonzero scalar does not change its degree.
  • Differentiation: In one variable over characteristic zero: $$ \frac{d}{dx}(ax^n) = anx^{n-1}, $$ so differentiation lowers a positive term degree by: $$ \boxed{1}. $$
  • Partial Differentiation: Differentiating: $$ x^py^q $$ with respect to $x$ gives: $$ px^{p-1}y^q, $$ lowering total degree by $1$ when $p>0$ and the resulting coefficient is nonzero.
  • Integration: In one variable, integrating a nonzero polynomial term: $$ ax^n $$ ordinarily produces a term of degree: $$ \boxed{n+1}. $$
  • Ordering Polynomial Terms: In one variable, polynomial terms are often written in descending order of degree.
  • Leading Term: The nonzero term of highest degree in a polynomial is its leading term after terms are ordered and combined.
  • Algebraic Geometry: Total degree helps classify polynomial equations and the algebraic sets they define.
  • Graded Algebra: Polynomial rings can be decomposed according to homogeneous degree, making term degree part of a broader grading structure.
  • Terminological Contrast: The degree of a term concerns one monomial. The degree of a polynomial is the greatest degree among its nonzero terms.
  • Total-Degree Contrast: In: $$ x^2y^5, $$ the largest individual exponent is $5$, but the total degree is: $$ \boxed{2+5=7}. $$
  • Core Interpretation: Degree measures the exponent weight of the variable portion of a nonzero term: $$ \boxed{ \deg \left( ax_1^{n_1}\cdots x_k^{n_k} \right) = n_1+\cdots+n_k, \qquad a\neq0 }. $$ In one variable this reduces simply to the exponent of that variable.
