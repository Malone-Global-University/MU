Side of an Equation

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

A side of an equation is one of the two expressions separated by the equality sign.

$$ \boxed{ A=B } $$

Here $A$ is the left-hand side, commonly abbreviated LHS, and $B$ is the right-hand side, commonly abbreviated RHS.

$$ \boxed{ \underbrace{A}_{\text{LHS}} = \underbrace{B}_{\text{RHS}} }. $$

The word side comes from the visual placement of expressions on opposite sides of the equals sign. The terminology describes position, not mathematical superiority or direction.

Operational Nuance

Each Side Is an Expression

In:

$$ 3x+5=17, $$

the left-hand side is:

$$ \boxed{ 3x+5 } $$

and the right-hand side is:

$$ \boxed{ 17 }. $$

The Equality Sign Relates the Sides

An equation asserts that its two sides have equal values under the relevant conditions:

$$ \boxed{ \text{LHS}=\text{RHS} }. $$

Operating on Both Sides

Equation solving relies on applying equality-preserving operations to both sides. For example:

$$ x+4=9 $$

subtracting $4$ from both sides gives:

$$ x+4-4=9-4, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ x=5 }. $$

Balance Interpretation

An equation is often compared to a balanced scale. If the same valid operation is performed on both sides, equality is preserved.

$$ A=B \Longrightarrow A+c=B+c $$
$$ A=B \Longrightarrow cA=cB. $$

Reversing operations may require additional restrictions. For example, dividing both sides by $c$ requires $c\neq0$.

Simplifying One Side

It is valid to simplify one side internally if the new expression is equivalent to the original expression. For example:

$$ 2(x+3)=10 $$

may be rewritten as:

$$ 2x+6=10 $$

because the left-hand expressions are equivalent.

Moving Terms Across the Equals Sign

The phrase move a term to the other side is shorthand. Terms do not literally cross the equality sign and change sign by themselves.

For example:

$$ x+7=12 $$

becomes:

$$ x=12-7 $$

because $7$ was subtracted from both sides.

Either Side May Be Complicated

An equation need not have a variable on only one side:

$$ \boxed{ 4x-3=2x+9 }. $$

Both sides may contain variables, functions, fractions, radicals, matrices, or other mathematical expressions.

Function Equations

In:

$$ f(x)=g(x), $$

$f(x)$ is the left-hand side and $g(x)$ is the right-hand side. Solving the equation means finding inputs for which the two sides agree.

Equation in Zero Form

An equation can often be rewritten with zero on one side:

$$ f(x)=g(x) \Longleftrightarrow f(x)-g(x)=0. $$

This form is especially useful for factoring, root finding, and numerical methods.

Identities

In an identity, the two sides are equal for every admissible value of the variables. For example:

$$ \boxed{ (a+b)^2 = a^2+2ab+b^2 }. $$

The two sides may look different while representing the same quantity throughout the stated domain.

Conditional Equations

In a conditional equation, the two sides are equal only for particular values. For example:

$$ x+2=7 $$

is true only when:

$$ \boxed{ x=5 }. $$

Dimensions and Units

In applied mathematics, valid equations ordinarily require compatible dimensions on both sides. An equation such as:

$$ d=vt $$

equates a distance on the left with speed multiplied by time on the right.

Common Misconception

The equals sign does not mean "the answer comes next." It asserts equality between the complete expression on its left and the complete expression on its right.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$A=B$ "The left-hand side equals the right-hand side."
LHS "Left-hand side."
RHS "Right-hand side."
$3x+2=11$ "Three x plus two is the left-hand side; eleven is the right-hand side."
$f(x)-g(x)=0$ "Subtract the right-hand side from the left-hand side and set the result equal to zero."
$A+c=B+c$ "Add c to both sides of the equation."

The phrases left side and right side are common informally, while left-hand side and right-hand side are the conventional formal expressions.

Defense Notation Advice: Treat each side as a complete expression. When solving, describe the actual operation performed on both sides rather than relying only on shortcuts such as "move it across." This makes sign changes, division restrictions, and equivalence preservation explicit.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: One of the two expressions separated by an equality sign.
  • Left-Hand Side: Expression appearing to the left of $=$.
  • Right-Hand Side: Expression appearing to the right of $=$.
  • Abbreviations: LHS and RHS.
  • Equation Solving: Equality-preserving operations are commonly applied to both sides.
  • Internal Simplification: One side may be replaced by an equivalent expression.
  • Transposition Language: "Moving" a term across the equals sign abbreviates an operation performed on both sides.
  • Variables: May occur on either or both sides.
  • Zero Form: Many equations are rewritten so that one side is zero.
  • Functions: $f(x)=g(x)$ compares two function values at the same input.
  • Identities: Both sides agree for every admissible value.
  • Conditional Equations: Both sides agree only for particular solutions.
  • Linear Algebra: Matrix and vector equations also have left- and right-hand sides, as in $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$.
  • Differential Equations: One side may contain derivatives while the other contains functions or forcing terms.
  • Applied Mathematics: Dimensions and units on both sides must be compatible in a physically meaningful equation.
  • Common Error: Treating the equals sign as an instruction to compute rather than a relation.
  • Common Error: Changing only one side during an equation-solving step without replacing it by an equivalent expression.
  • Common Error: Saying a term "changes sign when moved" without recognizing the equality operation that causes the change.
  • Common Error: Applying an inverse operation without checking restrictions such as division by zero.
  • Core Interpretation: Every equation has two sides: $$ \boxed{ \underbrace{A}_{\text{left-hand side}} = \underbrace{B}_{\text{right-hand side}} }. $$ The equality sign asserts that these expressions have equal values under the equation's conditions. Solving an equation consists largely of transforming these sides through valid equivalence-preserving operations until the desired information is exposed.
