Linear

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

Linear is a mathematical adjective whose precise meaning depends on context. In elementary algebra, it commonly describes equations, functions, and relationships involving variables only to the first power and producing straight-line graphs. In linear algebra, however, the word has a stricter structural meaning based on preservation of addition and scalar multiplication.

The word derives from Latin linea, meaning a line or thread. Its elementary geometric association is therefore literal:

$$ \boxed{ y=mx+b } $$

graphs as a straight line in the Cartesian plane.

The modern mathematical word has grown far beyond visible straight lines. A transformation $T$ between vector spaces is linear when:

$$ \boxed{ T(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}) = T(\mathbf{u})+T(\mathbf{v}) } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ T(c\mathbf{v}) = cT(\mathbf{v}) }. $$

These properties together imply preservation of linear combinations:

$$ \boxed{ T(a\mathbf{u}+b\mathbf{v}) = aT(\mathbf{u})+bT(\mathbf{v}) }. $$

Consequently, the everyday classroom phrase "linear function" can refer to two related but technically different conventions: an affine straight-line function $f(x)=mx+b$, or, in linear algebra, a genuinely linear map that must send zero to zero.

Operational Nuance

Elementary Algebra Meaning

In elementary algebra, an equation or expression is commonly called linear in a variable when that variable occurs only to the first power and is not multiplied by another variable of the same unknown set.

$$ \boxed{ ax+b } $$

is linear in $x$.

Linear Equation in One Variable

A standard form is:

$$ \boxed{ ax+b=0, \qquad a\neq0 }. $$

Its unique solution is:

$$ \boxed{ x=-\frac{b}{a} }. $$

Linear Equation in Two Variables

A common form is:

$$ \boxed{ Ax+By=C } $$

where $A$ and $B$ are not both zero.

Its solution set is a line in the coordinate plane.

Slope-Intercept Form

$$ \boxed{ y=mx+b }. $$

Here $m$ is the slope and $b$ is the vertical intercept.

Constant Rate of Change

A fundamental property of an elementary linear relationship is constant rate of change:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}=m } $$

whenever $\Delta x\neq0$.

Slope between Any Two Points

For points $(x_1,y_1)$ and $(x_2,y_2)$ on a nonvertical line:

$$ \boxed{ m = \frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1} } $$

when $x_2\neq x_1$.

First-Degree Polynomial

A polynomial:

$$ \boxed{ P(x)=ax+b, \qquad a\neq0 } $$

has degree $1$ and is called a linear polynomial.

Constant Function Caveat

A constant polynomial:

$$ f(x)=b $$

has degree $0$, not degree $1$. Nevertheless, its graph is a horizontal straight line, so some graph-oriented discussions may group it with linear-looking functions.

Vertical Line Caveat

The equation:

$$ \boxed{ x=c } $$

is a linear equation in two variables even though it cannot be written as a function $y=f(x)$.

Linear Function in School Algebra

Many elementary textbooks call:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=mx+b } $$

a linear function because its graph is a line.

Strict Linear-Algebra Definition

In linear algebra, a map $T:V\to W$ is linear if it preserves vector addition and scalar multiplication.

$$ \boxed{ T(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}) = T(\mathbf{u})+T(\mathbf{v}) } $$
$$ \boxed{ T(c\mathbf{v}) = cT(\mathbf{v}) }. $$

Linearity of Linear Combinations

$$ \boxed{ T(c_1\mathbf{v}_1+\cdots+c_n\mathbf{v}_n) = c_1T(\mathbf{v}_1)+\cdots+c_nT(\mathbf{v}_n) }. $$

Zero-Preservation Requirement

Every linear transformation satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ T(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0} }. $$

Why Zero Must Map to Zero

$$ T(\mathbf{0}) = T(0\mathbf{v}) = 0T(\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{0}. $$

Affine vs. Linear

The map:

$$ f(x)=mx+b $$

with $b\neq0$ is not linear in the strict linear-algebra sense because:

$$ f(0)=b\neq0. $$

It is instead an affine map.

Strictly Linear Scalar Function

A linear map $\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ has the form:

$$ \boxed{ f(x)=mx }. $$

Direct Variation

The equation:

$$ \boxed{ y=kx } $$

represents both direct variation and a linear transformation of $\mathbb{R}$ in the strict sense.

Affine Relationship

The more general:

$$ \boxed{ y=mx+b } $$

combines a linear scaling $mx$ with a translation $b$.

Why School Terminology Differs

Elementary algebra historically emphasizes the geometric fact that $y=mx+b$ graphs as a line. Linear algebra emphasizes preservation of vector-space structure. Both usages are entrenched, so context is essential.

Linear Combination

A linear combination of vectors $\mathbf{v}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{v}_n$ is an expression:

$$ \boxed{ c_1\mathbf{v}_1+\cdots+c_n\mathbf{v}_n }. $$

The scalars $c_1,\ldots,c_n$ are coefficients.

Linear Span

The span of a collection of vectors is the set of all their linear combinations:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{span} \{\mathbf{v}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{v}_n\} = \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^{n}c_i\mathbf{v}_i \right\} }. $$

Linear Independence

Vectors $\mathbf{v}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{v}_n$ are linearly independent when:

$$ c_1\mathbf{v}_1+\cdots+c_n\mathbf{v}_n = \mathbf{0} $$

implies:

$$ \boxed{ c_1=\cdots=c_n=0 }. $$

Linear Dependence

A nontrivial coefficient choice producing the zero vector establishes linear dependence.

Linear System

A linear system consists of linear equations considered simultaneously.

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} a_{11}x_1+\cdots+a_{1n}x_n &= b_1,\\ \vdots \qquad\qquad & \vdots\\ a_{m1}x_1+\cdots+a_{mn}x_n &= b_m \end{aligned} } $$

Matrix Form

$$ \boxed{ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b} }. $$

Homogeneous Linear System

$$ \boxed{ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0} }. $$

Every homogeneous linear system has at least the trivial solution $\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$.

Nonhomogeneous Linear System

$$ \boxed{ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}, \qquad \mathbf{b}\neq\mathbf{0} }. $$

Linear Transformation and Matrices

Every linear transformation between finite-dimensional vector spaces can be represented by a matrix once bases are chosen.

$$ \boxed{ T(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x} }. $$

Matrix Multiplication Is Linear in a Vector

$$ A(c\mathbf{x}+d\mathbf{y}) = cA\mathbf{x}+dA\mathbf{y}. $$

Linear Operator

A linear transformation from a vector space to itself:

$$ \boxed{ T:V\to V } $$

is often called a linear operator.

Linear Functional

A linear map from a vector space to its scalar field:

$$ \boxed{ \varphi:V\to\mathbb{F} } $$

is called a linear functional.

Dot Product with a Fixed Vector

For fixed $\mathbf{a}$:

$$ \boxed{ T(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{x} } $$

is a linear functional.

Linear Form

An expression:

$$ \boxed{ a_1x_1+\cdots+a_nx_n } $$

is called a linear form.

Affine Linear Equation

An equation:

$$ a_1x_1+\cdots+a_nx_n=b $$

describes an affine hyperplane when the coefficient vector is nonzero.

Linear Subspace

A subspace must be closed under linear combinations:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in W, \ a,b\in\mathbb{F} \Longrightarrow a\mathbf{u}+b\mathbf{v}\in W }. $$

Subspaces Pass through the Origin

A genuine linear subspace contains:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{0} }. $$

A translated line or plane not containing the origin is affine rather than a linear subspace.

Geometric Line through the Origin

In $\mathbb{R}^2$, a one-dimensional subspace has form:

$$ \boxed{ \{t\mathbf{v}:t\in\mathbb{R}\} } $$

for some nonzero vector $\mathbf{v}$ and is a line through the origin.

Affine Line

A general line can be expressed:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{p}+t\mathbf{v} }. $$

This is affine unless $\mathbf{p}$ can be taken as $\mathbf{0}$.

Linear vs. Nonlinear Terms

The expression:

$$ 3x+2y $$

is linear in $x$ and $y$.

The following are nonlinear:

$$ x^2, \qquad xy, \qquad \sqrt{x}, \qquad \frac{1}{x}, \qquad e^x. $$

Why $xy$ Is Nonlinear in $(x,y)$

The variables multiply one another. A linear form contains variables only as first-power terms multiplied by scalar coefficients.

Linear in One Variable but Not Another Interpretation

The expression:

$$ xy $$

is linear in $x$ when $y$ is held fixed as a coefficient, and linear in $y$ when $x$ is held fixed. But it is not jointly linear as a function of the pair $(x,y)$.

Separate Linearity

A function linear in each argument separately may be called bilinear when it has two vector arguments.

Bilinear Example

The dot product:

$$ \boxed{ (\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) \mapsto \mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y} } $$

is bilinear over the real numbers.

Linear Polynomial vs. Linear Map

A first-degree polynomial:

$$ ax+b $$

may be called linear in school algebra even when $b\neq0$, whereas a linear map must satisfy $T(0)=0$.

Linear Growth

A quantity exhibits linear growth when equal changes in the independent variable produce equal additive changes in the dependent variable.

$$ \boxed{ y=y_0+rt }. $$

Linear vs. Exponential Growth

Linear growth adds a constant amount per step:

$$ y_{n+1}=y_n+d, $$

whereas exponential growth multiplies by a constant factor:

$$ y_{n+1}=ry_n. $$

Arithmetic Sequences

An arithmetic sequence:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d } $$

depends affinely on the index $n$ and is commonly described as exhibiting linear change.

Linear Interpolation

Between points $(x_0,y_0)$ and $(x_1,y_1)$, linear interpolation estimates values along the joining line:

$$ \boxed{ y = y_0 + \frac{x-x_0}{x_1-x_0} (y_1-y_0) } $$

when $x_1\neq x_0$.

Affine Combination Form

With:

$$ t = \frac{x-x_0}{x_1-x_0}, $$

interpolation can be written:

$$ \boxed{ (1-t)y_0+ty_1 }. $$

Linear Extrapolation

Extending the same constant-rate line beyond the observed interval is linear extrapolation.

Linear Regression

A simple linear regression model is commonly written:

$$ \boxed{ y=\beta_0+\beta_1x+\varepsilon }. $$

The model is linear in the predictor $x$ and in the parameters $\beta_0,\beta_1$.

Linear in Parameters

In statistics, the phrase linear model often means linear in its unknown coefficients, even when predictors are transformed.

For example:

$$ \boxed{ y = \beta_0+\beta_1x+\beta_2x^2+\varepsilon } $$

is nonlinear in $x$ as a polynomial curve but is still a linear regression model in the parameters $\beta_0,\beta_1,\beta_2$.

Design-Matrix Form

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{y} = X\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon} }. $$

Linear Programming

Linear programming optimizes a linear objective subject to linear equality or inequality constraints.

$$ \boxed{ \text{maximize or minimize } \mathbf{c}^T\mathbf{x} } $$

subject to conditions such as:

$$ \boxed{ A\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{b} }. $$

Feasible Region

Linear inequalities create half-spaces whose intersections form convex feasible regions.

Linear Differential Equation

A differential equation is linear in the unknown function when that function and its derivatives occur only to the first power and are not multiplied together.

First-Order Linear Differential Equation

$$ \boxed{ y'+p(x)y=q(x) }. $$

Second-Order Linear Differential Equation

$$ \boxed{ a_2(x)y'' + a_1(x)y' + a_0(x)y = g(x) }. $$

Homogeneous Linear Differential Equation

$$ \boxed{ L[y]=0 }. $$

Superposition Principle

If $L$ is a linear differential operator and:

$$ L[y_1]=0, \qquad L[y_2]=0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ L[c_1y_1+c_2y_2]=0 }. $$

Nonlinear Differential Equation

Equations containing terms such as:

$$ y^2, \qquad yy', \qquad \sin y $$

are generally nonlinear in $y$.

Linear Operator in Calculus

Differentiation is linear:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx} \left( af+bg \right) = a f' + b g' }. $$

Integration Is Linear

$$ \boxed{ \int (af+bg)\,dx = a\int f\,dx + b\int g\,dx } $$

up to the usual handling of constants of integration for indefinite integrals.

Definite Integral Linearity

$$ \boxed{ \int_a^b \left( cf(x)+dg(x) \right)\,dx = c\int_a^b f(x)\,dx + d\int_a^b g(x)\,dx }. $$

Expectation Is Linear

In probability:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{E}[aX+bY] = a\mathbb{E}[X] + b\mathbb{E}[Y] }. $$

This property does not require $X$ and $Y$ to be independent.

Covariance Caveat

Other statistical operations need not be linear in the same way. The word linear should therefore be attached to a specific operator or relationship.

Linear Approximation

A differentiable function near $x=a$ can be approximated by its tangent line:

$$ \boxed{ f(x) \approx f(a)+f'(a)(x-a) }. $$

Local Linearization

The function:

$$ L(x) = f(a)+f'(a)(x-a) $$

is an affine approximation to $f$ near $a$ and is commonly called the linearization.

Higher-Dimensional Linearization

For differentiable $F:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$:

$$ \boxed{ F(\mathbf{x}) \approx F(\mathbf{a}) + DF(\mathbf{a})(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{a}) }. $$

The derivative $DF(\mathbf{a})$ is a linear map.

Tangent Map

In multivariable calculus and differential geometry, derivatives are fundamentally linear approximations to local change.

Linearization vs. Original Function

A nonlinear function can have a useful local linear approximation without itself being linear.

Example

The function:

$$ f(x)=x^2 $$

is nonlinear, but near $x=1$:

$$ \boxed{ f(x) \approx 1+2(x-1) = 2x-1 }. $$

Piecewise Linear Function

A function is piecewise linear when its domain can be divided into regions on which the graph consists of line segments or rays.

Absolute Value Example

$$ |x| = \begin{cases} -x, & x<0,\\ x, & x\geq0. \end{cases} $$

The function is piecewise linear but is not a single affine function on all of $\mathbb{R}$.

Linear Trend

In data analysis, a linear trend means that a straight-line model captures the dominant pattern of change.

Linear Correlation

Linear correlation measures the degree of straight-line association between variables. It should not be confused with exact linear dependence.

Linear Relationship vs. Causation

A linear statistical association does not by itself establish a causal relationship.

Linear Scale

A linear axis or scale uses equal physical spacing for equal additive numerical differences.

Linear vs. Logarithmic Scale

On a linear scale:

$$ 10\to20 $$

occupies the same spacing as:

$$ 20\to30, $$

whereas logarithmic scales emphasize equal multiplicative ratios.

Linear Order

In order theory, linear has another specialized meaning. A linear order, also called a total order, compares every pair of elements.

$$ \boxed{ x\leq y \quad\text{or}\quad y\leq x } $$

for every pair $x,y$ in the ordered set.

Linear Order Is Unrelated to Straight-Line Graphs

This usage derives from arranging objects in a single sequence rather than from first-degree algebra.

Linear Search

In algorithms, linear search examines items sequentially. Its name refers to proceeding through a list in order, not to solving a linear equation.

Linear-Time Complexity

An algorithm has linear time when its work grows proportionally to input size:

$$ \boxed{ T(n)=O(n) }. $$

Here linear refers to growth order rather than an exact straight-line formula.

Linear Recurrence

A recurrence is linear when each sequence term appears to the first power and is combined linearly with coefficients.

Example

$$ \boxed{ a_n = 3a_{n-1} - 2a_{n-2} }. $$

Nonhomogeneous Linear Recurrence

$$ \boxed{ a_n = 3a_{n-1} - 2a_{n-2} + f(n) }. $$

Nonlinear Recurrence

A recurrence such as:

$$ a_n=a_{n-1}^2+1 $$

is nonlinear.

Linear Homogeneous Polynomial

A homogeneous polynomial of degree $1$:

$$ \boxed{ a_1x_1+\cdots+a_nx_n } $$

is genuinely linear as a map to the coefficient field.

Affine Constant Term

Adding a nonzero constant:

$$ a_1x_1+\cdots+a_nx_n+b $$

produces an affine expression rather than a homogeneous linear form.

Superposition as a Diagnostic

In many advanced contexts, the quickest test for linearity is:

$$ \boxed{ T(a\mathbf{x}+b\mathbf{y}) \stackrel{?}{=} aT(\mathbf{x})+bT(\mathbf{y}) }. $$

Example of a Linear Map

Let:

$$ T(x,y) = (2x-y,\ 3x+4y). $$

This is linear because it is matrix multiplication:

$$ \boxed{ T \begin{pmatrix} x\\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1\\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x\\ y \end{pmatrix} }. $$

Example of a Nonlinear Map

The map:

$$ T(x,y) = (x^2,y) $$

is nonlinear because squaring does not preserve scalar multiplication:

$$ (cx)^2=c^2x^2 $$

rather than generally $cx^2$.

Translation Is Not Linear

The transformation:

$$ T(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b} $$

with $\mathbf{b}\neq\mathbf{0}$ is affine, not linear, because:

$$ T(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{b}. $$

Rotation about the Origin

A rotation about the origin is linear:

$$ \boxed{ R_\theta = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix} }. $$

Reflection through a Linear Subspace

Reflections through lines or planes passing through the origin are linear transformations.

Translation Followed by Rotation

A rotation about a point other than the origin is generally affine rather than linear when expressed in ordinary coordinates.

Scaling

Uniform or coordinate scaling through the origin is linear:

$$ \boxed{ T(\mathbf{x})=c\mathbf{x} }. $$

Shear

A shear represented by matrix multiplication is linear.

Projection

Projection onto a linear subspace is a linear transformation when defined in the standard vector-space setting.

Linear Combination of Linear Maps

If $S$ and $T$ are linear and $a,b$ are scalars, then:

$$ \boxed{ aS+bT } $$

is linear.

Composition of Linear Maps

If $S$ and $T$ are linear, then:

$$ \boxed{ S\circ T } $$

is linear.

Inverse of a Linear Isomorphism

If a linear transformation is bijective, its inverse is also linear.

Kernel

For linear $T:V\to W$:

$$ \boxed{ \ker T = \{\mathbf{v}\in V:T(\mathbf{v})=\mathbf{0}\} }. $$

The kernel is a linear subspace.

Image

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{im}T = \{T(\mathbf{v}):\mathbf{v}\in V\} } $$

is likewise a linear subspace.

Rank-Nullity

For a linear transformation on a finite-dimensional domain:

$$ \boxed{ \dim V = \operatorname{rank}T + \operatorname{nullity}T }. $$

Linear Algebra Is Not Merely the Study of Lines

Linear algebra studies vector spaces, linear transformations, matrices, systems, eigenstructure, subspaces, and linear combinations. Straight lines are only one geometric manifestation of the underlying structure.

Common Error: Assuming Every Straight-Line Function Is Linear in the Strict Sense

The function:

$$ f(x)=2x+3 $$

has a straight-line graph but is affine, not linear as a vector-space map.

Common Error: Assuming Every Linear Map Has a One-Dimensional Graph

Linear maps may act between spaces of any finite or infinite dimension.

Common Error: Thinking Linear Means Horizontal or Vertical

Linear concerns algebraic structure, constant first-degree relationships, or superposition depending on context—not a particular visual orientation.

Common Error: Treating $xy$ as Linear in Two Variables

Because the variables multiply each other, $xy$ is not a linear form in the pair $(x,y)$.

Common Error: Treating $x^2$ as Linear

Squaring changes the degree and destroys the ordinary linearity conditions.

Common Error: Confusing Linear Growth with Proportional Growth

A model:

$$ y=mx+b $$

has constant additive rate of change but is directly proportional only when:

$$ \boxed{ b=0 }. $$

Common Error: Confusing Linear with Constant

A constant function has zero rate of change and degree $0$. Whether it is included under a broad "linear function" classroom convention depends on the local definition.

Common Error: Assuming a Linear Regression Must Graph as a Straight Line in the Original Predictor

Statistical "linear model" often refers to linearity in parameters, not necessarily linearity in the raw predictor variables.

Common Error: Assuming a Nonlinear Function Cannot Be Linearized

Differentiable nonlinear functions often possess useful local linear approximations.

Common Error: Confusing Piecewise Linear with Globally Linear

A function made of line segments may be piecewise linear without satisfying one global linear or affine formula.

Common Error: Ignoring the Zero Test

In linear algebra:

$$ \boxed{ T(\mathbf{0})\neq\mathbf{0} \Longrightarrow T \text{ is not linear} }. $$

Common Error: Thinking the Zero Test Alone Proves Linearity

Passing through the origin is necessary but not sufficient. A function such as:

$$ f(x)=x^3 $$

satisfies $f(0)=0$ but is not linear.

Common Error: Failing to State Which Variable the Expression Is Linear In

In multivariable and parameterized expressions, linearity may depend on which symbols are treated as variables and which are treated as fixed coefficients.

Common Error: Assuming "Linear" Has One Universal Definition across All Fields

Linear can mean first-degree, straight-line, superposition-preserving, total-order, sequential, or proportional growth depending on the mathematical context.

Common Misconception: "Linear" does not always mean exactly "forms a straight line." In elementary algebra, $y=mx+b$ is commonly called linear because its graph is a line. In linear algebra, however, a map must preserve addition and scalar multiplication, which forces $T(\mathbf{0})$ to equal $\mathbf{0}$. Under that stricter definition, $f(x)=mx$ is linear while $f(x)=mx+b$ with $b\neq0$ is affine. Accurate interpretation therefore depends on the field and the object being described.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$y=mx+b$ "A linear equation in slope-intercept form," under the elementary algebra convention.
$Ax+By=C$ "A linear equation in two variables."
$f(x)=mx$ "A linear map from the real numbers to themselves," in the strict linear-algebra sense.
$T(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v})=T(\mathbf{u})+T(\mathbf{v})$ "T preserves addition."
$T(c\mathbf{v})=cT(\mathbf{v})$ "T preserves scalar multiplication."
$T(a\mathbf{u}+b\mathbf{v})=aT(\mathbf{u})+bT(\mathbf{v})$ "T preserves linear combinations."
$A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ "A linear system in matrix form."

The phrase linear function is especially context-sensitive. In introductory algebra it often means:

$$ y=mx+b. $$

In linear algebra, a function called linear must satisfy the superposition laws and therefore must map zero to zero.

The word affine provides a useful precision distinction:

$$ \boxed{ \text{linear} \quad \mathbf{x}\mapsto A\mathbf{x} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{affine} \quad \mathbf{x}\mapsto A\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b} }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Determine the mathematical context before interpreting the word linear. In elementary algebra, state whether the convention includes $mx+b$. In linear algebra, test preservation of linear combinations and remember that a nonzero translation destroys strict linearity. In multivariable, statistical, differential-equation, and algorithmic settings, specify what object or variables the word "linear" refers to.

  • Vertical Line: $x=c$ is linear as an equation but not a function $y=f(x)$.
  • Constant Function: Degree $0$, though its graph is a straight horizontal line.
  • School-Algebra Convention: Often calls $f(x)=mx+b$ linear.

  • Homogeneity: $T(cv)=cT(v)$.

  • Zero Preservation: $T(0)=0$.
  • Strict Scalar Linear Function: $f(x)=mx$.
  • Affine Function: $f(x)=mx+b$ with nonzero $b$ in the strict structural terminology.
  • Direct Variation: $y=kx$.
  • Linear Combination: $c_1v_1+\cdots+c_nv_n$.
  • Span: Set of all linear combinations of given vectors.
  • Linear Independence: Only the trivial coefficient combination produces zero.
  • Linear Dependence: A nontrivial combination produces zero.
  • Linear System: Collection of simultaneous linear equations.
  • Matrix Form: $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$.
  • Homogeneous System: $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$.

  • Matrix Representation: $T(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x}$ in finite-dimensional coordinates.

  • Linear Functional: Linear map from a vector space to its scalar field.

  • Origin Requirement: Every linear subspace contains zero.
  • Affine Subspace: Translation of a linear subspace.
  • Linear Terms: Variables appear only to first power and are combined with scalar coefficients.

  • Bilinear: Linear separately in each of two vector arguments.

  • Core Interpretation: In elementary algebra, linear commonly describes a first-degree relationship: $$ \boxed{ y=mx+b }, $$ whose graph is a straight line and whose rate of change is constant: $$ \boxed{ \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}=m }. $$ In linear algebra, the term is stricter. A map is linear when: $$ \boxed{ T(a\mathbf{u}+b\mathbf{v}) = aT(\mathbf{u})+bT(\mathbf{v}) }, $$ which forces: $$ \boxed{ T(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0} }. $$ Therefore a map of the form $\mathbf{x}\mapsto A\mathbf{x}$ is linear, while $\mathbf{x}\mapsto A\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}$ with $\mathbf{b}\neq\mathbf{0}$ is affine. The word's precise meaning must always be read from its mathematical context.
