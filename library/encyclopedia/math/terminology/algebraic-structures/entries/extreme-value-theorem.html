Extreme Value Theorem

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

The term Extreme Value Theorem refers to the existence of extreme values of a function—its largest and smallest attained values on a specified domain. The adjective extreme comes from a Latin root meaning "outermost" or "furthest," which fits the mathematical idea of values lying at the upper or lower edge of a function's range.

In modern calculus, the theorem states that a continuous real-valued function on a closed, bounded interval must attain both an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum. In real analysis, this is understood as a special case of a more general compactness principle: a continuous real-valued function on a compact set attains its extrema.

The theorem is closely associated with the development of rigorous real analysis in the nineteenth century, where ideas such as continuity, boundedness, compactness, maxima, minima, and limits were given precise definitions. It is sometimes grouped with other foundational continuity theorems such as the Intermediate Value Theorem.

Operational Nuance

Let:

$$ f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R} $$

be continuous on the closed interval $[a,b]$. Then the Extreme Value Theorem states that there exist points:

$$ c,d\in[a,b] $$

such that:

$$ f(c)\le f(x)\le f(d) $$

for every:

$$ x\in[a,b]. $$

Equivalently:

$$ \boxed{ f(c)=\min_{x\in[a,b]}f(x) } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ f(d)=\max_{x\in[a,b]}f(x) }. $$

The theorem therefore guarantees two things:

  • the function is bounded above and below on the interval;
  • its greatest and least values are actually attained at points in the interval.

The phrase attained is crucial. Consider:

$$ f(x)=x $$

on:

$$ (0,1). $$

The function has values arbitrarily close to $1$, but no point in the interval satisfies:

$$ f(x)=1. $$

Thus:

$$ \sup f((0,1))=1, $$

but there is no absolute maximum. Likewise, values approach $0$ without ever attaining it, so there is no absolute minimum.

The failure occurs because $(0,1)$ is not closed.

Now consider the same function on:

$$ [0,1]. $$

The endpoints are included, so:

$$ \min f=0 \qquad\text{at }x=0 $$

and:

$$ \max f=1 \qquad\text{at }x=1. $$

Continuity is also essential. Define:

$$ f(x)= \begin{cases} x, & 0\le x<1,\\ 0, & x=1. \end{cases} $$

on the closed interval $[0,1]$. The function is not continuous at $x=1$. Its values approach $1$, but the function never equals $1$. Therefore it has no absolute maximum.

Common Misconception: A closed interval by itself does not guarantee extrema, and continuity by itself does not guarantee extrema on an arbitrary domain. The classical theorem requires both:

$$ \boxed{ \text{continuity} + \text{closed and bounded interval} } $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}$ "F maps the closed interval a to b into the real numbers."
$c,d\in[a,b]$ "C and d belong to the interval from a to b."
$f(c)\le f(x)\le f(d)$ "F of c is less than or equal to f of x, which is less than or equal to f of d."
$\min_{x\in[a,b]}f(x)$ "The minimum value of f on the interval a to b."
$\max_{x\in[a,b]}f(x)$ "The maximum value of f on the interval a to b."
$[a,b]$ "The closed interval from a to b."

The words absolute and global are frequently used interchangeably in this context. Thus:

$$ \text{absolute maximum} = \text{global maximum} $$

and:

$$ \text{absolute minimum} = \text{global minimum}. $$

These should be distinguished from local or relative extrema. A local maximum need only be larger than nearby values, whereas an absolute maximum must be at least as large as every value on the entire domain under consideration.

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish the point where an extremum occurs from the extreme value itself. If:

$$ f(2)=7 $$

is the absolute maximum, then:

  • $x=2$ is the location of the maximum;
  • $7$ is the maximum value.

Saying "the maximum is $x=2$" is common informal speech, but mathematically it mixes the input with the function value.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Closed Interval Method: In elementary calculus, the Extreme Value Theorem justifies the standard method for finding absolute extrema of a continuous function on $[a,b]$: find critical points in $(a,b)$, evaluate the function there, evaluate the endpoints, and compare all resulting values.
  • Critical Points: If an absolute extremum occurs at an interior point where the function is differentiable, then that point must usually satisfy: $$ f'(c)=0. $$ However, the Extreme Value Theorem itself does not identify the location of the extremum; it guarantees only that extrema exist.
  • Endpoints: Absolute extrema may occur at endpoints. For example: $$ f(x)=x $$ on: $$ [0,1] $$ has its minimum at $0$ and maximum at $1$, even though neither endpoint is an interior critical point.
  • Local vs. Absolute Extrema: A function may have several local maxima and minima but only one largest value and one smallest value, or several points may share the same absolute extreme value. The theorem guarantees extreme values, not unique locations.
  • Multiple Locations: If: $$ f(x)=x^2 $$ on: $$ [-1,1], $$ then the absolute maximum value is: $$ 1, $$ attained at both: $$ x=-1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad x=1. $$ Thus attainment does not imply uniqueness.
  • Boundedness: A continuous function on a closed bounded interval cannot grow without bound. The theorem guarantees real numbers $m$ and $M$ such that: $$ m\le f(x)\le M $$ for every $x$ in the interval.

  • Minimum vs. Infimum: Similarly, an infimum is the greatest lower bound and need not be attained. On $(0,1)$: $$ \inf\{x:0
  • Compactness: In real analysis, the theorem is generalized from closed bounded intervals to compact sets. If $K$ is compact and: $$ f:K\to\mathbb{R} $$ is continuous, then $f$ attains both its maximum and minimum on $K$.
  • Heine-Borel Connection: In $\mathbb{R}^n$, a set is compact exactly when it is closed and bounded. Thus the familiar elementary condition: $$ [a,b]\text{ is closed and bounded} $$ is the one-dimensional manifestation of compactness.
  • Continuous Images of Compact Sets: A deeper formulation says that the continuous image of a compact set is compact. Since compact subsets of $\mathbb{R}$ are closed and bounded, the range: $$ f(K) $$ contains its largest and smallest elements.
  • Optimization: The theorem underlies many optimization arguments. Before searching for an optimal value, one often first proves that an optimum must exist. The Extreme Value Theorem provides that existence guarantee under continuity and compactness.
  • Several Variables: If: $$ f:D\to\mathbb{R} $$ is continuous and $D$ is a closed and bounded subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$, then $f$ attains an absolute maximum and minimum on $D$. This is the multivariable form commonly used in calculus.
  • Relationship to Fermat's Theorem: The Extreme Value Theorem guarantees that extrema exist under appropriate conditions. Fermat's theorem on stationary points helps identify possible interior locations by stating that if $f$ has a local extremum at an interior point $c$ and is differentiable there, then: $$ f'(c)=0. $$ The two theorems therefore serve different roles: existence versus location.
  • Relationship to the Intermediate Value Theorem: Both results depend fundamentally on continuity. The Intermediate Value Theorem says that continuous functions take every intermediate value between two attained function values. The Extreme Value Theorem says that on a compact interval they also attain their highest and lowest values.
  • Terminological Contrast: An absolute maximum is the greatest attained function value on the entire domain under consideration; an absolute minimum is the least. A supremum or infimum need not be attained. A local extremum compares only nearby values. The Extreme Value Theorem guarantees attained absolute extrema for continuous functions on closed, bounded intervals.
