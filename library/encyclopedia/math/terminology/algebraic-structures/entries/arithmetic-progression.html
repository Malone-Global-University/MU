Arithmetic Progression

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

An arithmetic progression is an ordered sequence of numbers in which the difference between each term and the preceding term is constant.

The word arithmetic ultimately derives from Greek language associated with number and numerical calculation. The word progression refers to an ordered movement or advance from one term to the next. Together, the expression describes a numerical sequence that advances by repeatedly adding the same amount.

In modern mathematics, arithmetic progression and arithmetic sequence usually refer to the same concept. The word sequence is especially common in contemporary American textbooks, while progression remains widely used internationally and in older mathematical terminology.

The adjective arithmetic distinguishes this pattern from a geometric progression. An arithmetic progression is generated by repeated addition, whereas a geometric progression is generated by repeated multiplication.

Operational Nuance

A sequence:

$$ a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots $$

is an arithmetic progression if there exists a constant $d$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ a_{n+1}-a_n=d } $$

for every applicable value of $n$.

The constant $d$ is called the common difference.

For example:

$$ 5,\ 8,\ 11,\ 14,\ 17,\ldots $$

is an arithmetic progression because:

$$ 8-5=3, $$ $$ 11-8=3, $$ $$ 14-11=3. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{d=3}. $$

Beginning with the first term $a_1$, the sequence can be written:

$$ a_1,\quad a_1+d,\quad a_1+2d,\quad a_1+3d,\quad\ldots $$

Therefore the general, or nth-term, formula is:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d }. $$

The factor $n-1$ appears because no common difference has yet been added at the first term. To reach the second term, $d$ is added once; to reach the third term, it is added twice; and to reach the nth term, it is added $n-1$ times.

For the progression:

$$ 5,\ 8,\ 11,\ 14,\ldots, $$

we have:

$$ a_1=5, \qquad d=3. $$

Hence:

$$ a_n = 5+3(n-1). $$

Simplifying:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=3n+2 }. $$

To find the tenth term:

$$ a_{10} = 5+(10-1)(3) $$ $$ =5+27 $$ $$ =32. $$

Arithmetic progressions can increase, decrease, or remain constant.

If:

$$ d>0, $$

the sequence increases. If:

$$ d<0, $$

it decreases. If:

$$ d=0, $$

every term is equal.

Common Misconception: The common difference is found by subtracting consecutive terms, not by dividing them. Division of consecutive terms is associated with geometric progressions and their common ratio.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$a_1$ "A sub one," or "the first term."
$a_n$ "A sub n," or "the nth term."
$d$ "The common difference."
$a_{n+1}-a_n=d$ "Each term minus the preceding term equals the common difference."
$a_n=a_1+(n-1)d$ "The nth term equals the first term plus n minus one times the common difference."
$5,8,11,14,\ldots$ "Five, eight, eleven, fourteen, and so on."

An arithmetic progression may be described recursively:

$$ a_{n+1}=a_n+d. $$

This means:

$$ \text{next term} = \text{current term} + \text{common difference}. $$

It may instead be described explicitly:

$$ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d. $$

A recursive formula tells how to obtain one term from the preceding term, whereas an explicit formula allows the nth term to be calculated directly.

Defense Notation Advice: Some sequences are indexed beginning with $0$ instead of $1$. If:

$$ a_0 $$

is the initial term, then the corresponding formula becomes:

$$ a_n=a_0+nd. $$

The formulas:

$$ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d $$

and:

$$ a_n=a_0+nd $$

describe the same underlying pattern under different indexing conventions.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Arithmetic Sequence: In ordinary modern usage: $$ \boxed{ \text{arithmetic progression} = \text{arithmetic sequence} }. $$ The two terms are generally synonymous.
  • Common Difference: The defining feature is: $$ a_{n+1}-a_n=d. $$ The same value $d$ must occur between every pair of consecutive terms.
  • Increasing Progression: If: $$ d>0, $$ then: $$ a_1
  • Decreasing Progression: If: $$ d<0, $$ the sequence decreases by the same amount at every step. For example: $$ 20,\ 16,\ 12,\ 8,\ldots $$ has: $$ d=-4. $$
  • Constant Progression: If: $$ d=0, $$ then: $$ a_1=a_2=a_3=\cdots. $$ A constant sequence is therefore a special arithmetic progression.
  • Finding the Common Difference: Given consecutive terms: $$ a_n,\ a_{n+1}, $$ calculate: $$ d=a_{n+1}-a_n. $$
  • Finding a Missing Term: If: $$ 7,\ \square,\ 15 $$ are three consecutive terms of an arithmetic progression, then the middle term must be: $$ 11, $$ because: $$ 11-7=15-11=4. $$
  • Arithmetic Mean: If $a$, $m$, and $b$ are consecutive terms of an arithmetic progression, then: $$ m-a=b-m. $$ Therefore: $$ 2m=a+b $$ and: $$ \boxed{ m=\frac{a+b}{2} }. $$ The middle term is the arithmetic mean of its two neighbors.
  • Equally Spaced Terms: More generally, any term centered between two equally distant terms satisfies: $$ a_n = \frac{a_{n-k}+a_{n+k}}{2}. $$ This reflects the linear structure of an arithmetic progression.
  • Arithmetic Series: An arithmetic progression is a sequence: $$ a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n. $$ An arithmetic series is the sum of those terms: $$ a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n. $$ The sequence and its sum should not be confused.
  • Sum of an Arithmetic Progression: If: $$ S_n=a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ S_n=\frac{n}{2}(a_1+a_n) }. $$
  • Alternative Sum Formula: Since: $$ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d, $$ the sum may also be written: $$ \boxed{ S_n= \frac{n}{2} \left[ 2a_1+(n-1)d \right] }. $$
  • Gauss Pairing Method: The arithmetic-series formula can be understood by pairing the first and last terms, second and next-to-last terms, and so forth. Every pair has the same sum: $$ a_1+a_n. $$ This symmetry leads directly to: $$ S_n=\frac{n}{2}(a_1+a_n). $$
  • Linear Functions: The explicit formula: $$ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d $$ is linear in $n$. It can be rewritten: $$ a_n=dn+(a_1-d). $$ Thus arithmetic progressions are discrete analogues of linear functions.
  • Graphical Interpretation: Plotting the ordered pairs: $$ (n,a_n) $$ places all terms of an arithmetic progression on a straight line. The common difference $d$ corresponds to the slope of that discrete linear pattern.
  • Finite vs. Infinite Progressions: An arithmetic progression may contain finitely many terms: $$ 4,\ 9,\ 14,\ 19 $$ or continue indefinitely: $$ 4,\ 9,\ 14,\ 19,\ldots. $$ The defining constant-difference property is the same.
  • Infinite Arithmetic Series: An infinite arithmetic sequence is perfectly valid, but an infinite arithmetic series generally does not converge unless the terms are all zero. If $d\neq0$, the terms themselves do not approach zero.
  • Relationship to Geometric Progressions: Arithmetic progressions use a constant difference: $$ a_{n+1}-a_n=d. $$ Geometric progressions use a constant ratio: $$ \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}=r $$ whenever the quotient is defined.
  • Real-World Modeling: Arithmetic progressions model situations in which the same amount is repeatedly added or removed: fixed annual salary increases, regularly increasing seat counts, equally spaced measurements, or quantities changing by a constant additive amount each period.
  • Terminological Contrast: An arithmetic progression is the ordered list of terms. Its common difference is the constant change between successive terms. An arithmetic series is the sum of the terms, while an arithmetic mean is the value halfway between two numbers in the additive sense.
