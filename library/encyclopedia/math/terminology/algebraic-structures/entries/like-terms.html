Like Terms

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

Like terms are algebraic terms having the same variable part, meaning the same variables raised to the same corresponding exponents. Their numerical coefficients may differ.

$$ \boxed{ ax^m \text{ and } bx^m \text{ are like terms} } $$

because both contain the same variable factor $x^m$.

More generally:

$$ \boxed{ a x_1^{r_1}x_2^{r_2}\cdots x_n^{r_n} \quad\text{and}\quad b x_1^{r_1}x_2^{r_2}\cdots x_n^{r_n} } $$

are like terms because their variable-power structure is identical.

The adjective like here means alike in algebraic form, not equal in numerical value. Thus:

$$ 3x^2 \qquad\text{and}\qquad -7x^2 $$

are like terms even though they are usually not equal.

The word term refers to an additive component of an expression. In:

$$ 4x^2-3x+8, $$

the terms are:

$$ 4x^2, \qquad -3x, \qquad 8. $$

The notion of likeness allows algebraic expressions to be simplified by collecting terms that represent scalar multiples of the same variable expression.

Operational Nuance

Basic Definition

Two terms are like terms when their variable parts are identical.

$$ \boxed{ 5x \text{ and } -2x \text{ are like terms} }. $$

Coefficients May Differ

The coefficients do not need to match.

$$ 8x^3 \qquad\text{and}\qquad -\frac{1}{2}x^3 $$

are like terms because both have variable part $x^3$.

Signs Belong to Coefficients

In:

$$ -4x^2, $$

the coefficient is $-4$. The negative sign does not make the term unlike $7x^2$.

$$ \boxed{ -4x^2 \text{ and } 7x^2 \text{ are like terms} }. $$

Variable Names Must Match

The terms:

$$ 3x \qquad\text{and}\qquad 3y $$

are not like terms because $x$ and $y$ are different variables.

Exponents Must Match

The terms:

$$ 5x^2 \qquad\text{and}\qquad 5x^3 $$

are not like terms because their exponents differ.

Multiple Variables

Terms can contain several variables.

$$ \boxed{ 3x^2y^4 \text{ and } -9x^2y^4 \text{ are like terms} }. $$

Variable Order Does Not Matter in Commutative Multiplication

Because ordinary variable multiplication is commutative:

$$ xy=yx. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ 4xy \text{ and } -7yx \text{ are like terms} }. $$

Reordering Variables

To compare terms, it can help to place variables in a consistent order:

$$ 5yx^2 = 5x^2y. $$

Thus:

$$ 5yx^2 \qquad\text{and}\qquad 3x^2y $$

are like terms.

Constants Are Like Terms

All ordinary constant terms are like terms because they have no variable part.

$$ \boxed{ 4,\ -7,\ \frac{3}{2} \text{ are mutually like terms} }. $$

Constant as a Power-Zero Term

A constant can be viewed as:

$$ c = cx^0 $$

because $x^0=1$ for $x\neq0$ in the ordinary exponent-law setting. In polynomial notation, constants constitute the degree-$0$ terms.

Combining Like Terms

Like terms can be combined by adding or subtracting their coefficients while preserving the common variable part.

$$ \boxed{ ax^m+bx^m = (a+b)x^m }. $$

Example

$$ 3x+5x = \boxed{8x}. $$

Subtraction Example

$$ 9x^2-4x^2 = \boxed{5x^2}. $$

Negative Coefficients

$$ -6y+2y = \boxed{-4y}. $$

Fractional Coefficients

$$ \frac{1}{3}x + \frac{5}{6}x = \left( \frac{1}{3} + \frac{5}{6} \right)x = \boxed{ \frac{7}{6}x }. $$

Decimal Coefficients

$$ 2.5x^2+1.4x^2 = \boxed{3.9x^2}. $$

Why Combining Works

Combining like terms is an application of the distributive law:

$$ \boxed{ au+bu = (a+b)u }. $$

Here the common variable expression acts as the shared factor $u$.

Example through Distribution

$$ 4x^3+7x^3 = (4+7)x^3 = 11x^3. $$

Collecting Like Terms

The phrase collect like terms means to group and combine all terms having identical variable parts.

Example

Simplify:

$$ 3x+7+2x-4. $$

Group like terms:

$$ (3x+2x)+(7-4). $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ 5x+3 }. $$

Several Variable Classes

Simplify:

$$ 4x^2+3x-7+5x^2-2x+9. $$

Collect by variable structure:

$$ (4x^2+5x^2) +(3x-2x) +(-7+9). $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ 9x^2+x+2 }. $$

Unlike Terms Cannot Be Combined Additively

The expression:

$$ 3x+4x^2 $$

cannot be simplified to a single ordinary monomial because $x$ and $x^2$ are different variable parts.

Do Not Add Exponents When Adding Terms

In general:

$$ \boxed{ x^2+x^3 \neq x^5 }. $$

Exponents are added when multiplying like bases, not when adding unlike terms.

Multiplication Uses Different Rules

Unlike terms can still be multiplied.

$$ x^2\cdot x^3 = \boxed{x^5}. $$

The requirement of likeness applies to addition and subtraction simplification, not to multiplication.

Example with Different Variables

$$ 3x\cdot4y = \boxed{12xy}. $$

The terms $3x$ and $4y$ are unlike as additive terms, but multiplication is still valid.

Parentheses and Distribution

Expressions may need to be expanded before all like terms become visible.

For example:

$$ 3(x+2)+4x. $$

Distribute:

$$ 3x+6+4x. $$

Combine:

$$ \boxed{ 7x+6 }. $$

Subtraction of Parenthetical Expressions

Simplify:

$$ 5x-(2x-3). $$

Distribute the negative sign:

$$ 5x-2x+3. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ 3x+3 }. $$

Terms after Expansion

Expanding products can create like terms that were not obvious beforehand.

For example:

$$ (x+2)(x+3) = x^2+3x+2x+6. $$

Then:

$$ 3x+2x=5x, $$

giving:

$$ \boxed{ x^2+5x+6 }. $$

Polynomial Standard Form

Combining like terms ensures that each power of the variable occurs at most once in standard polynomial form:

$$ \boxed{ a_nx^n+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_1x+a_0 }. $$

Coefficient Collection

A polynomial can be understood as collecting one coefficient for each distinct monomial.

Zero Coefficient

Like terms may cancel completely:

$$ 7x^2-7x^2 = \boxed{0}. $$

Cancellation Removes the Term

If the resulting coefficient is zero, the term disappears from the simplified expression.

Degree Can Drop after Combining

Consider:

$$ 4x^3-4x^3+2x. $$

After combining:

$$ \boxed{ 2x }. $$

The apparent cubic terms cancel, so the resulting polynomial has degree $1$.

Like Terms in Several Variables

The terms:

$$ 3x^2yz^4 \qquad\text{and}\qquad -8x^2yz^4 $$

are like terms.

However:

$$ 3x^2yz^4 \qquad\text{and}\qquad 3xy^2z^4 $$

are not like terms because the exponents attached to $x$ and $y$ differ.

Exponent Pattern Must Match Variable by Variable

Compare:

$$ x^2y^3 \qquad\text{and}\qquad x^3y^2. $$

Both have total degree $5$, but they are not like terms.

Same Total Degree Is Not Enough

$$ \boxed{ \deg(x^2y^3) = \deg(x^3y^2) = 5 } $$

does not imply that the monomials are alike.

Literal Coefficients

Context determines which symbols are variables and which are coefficients.

If $a$ and $b$ are parameters while $x$ is the polynomial variable, then:

$$ ax^2 \qquad\text{and}\qquad bx^2 $$

are like terms in $x$.

Coefficient Domain Matters

In a polynomial ring such as:

$$ \mathbb{R}[x], $$

real numbers act as coefficients and powers of $x$ determine the term classes.

Polynomial in One Variable with Symbolic Coefficients

In:

$$ (a+b)x^2+cx^2, $$

the terms are like with respect to $x$ and combine to:

$$ \boxed{ (a+b+c)x^2 }. $$

Different Choice of Variables

An expression can be interpreted differently depending on the algebraic context. If both $a$ and $x$ are treated as variables, then the full monomial structure must be compared.

Functional Expressions

The same distributive principle can apply to repeated identical functional factors.

$$ 3f(x)+5f(x) = \boxed{ 8f(x) }. $$

Such terms behave like terms because they contain the same expression $f(x)$ as a common factor.

Radical Expressions

Like-radical terms can be combined when the radical parts are identical.

$$ 3\sqrt{2} + 5\sqrt{2} = \boxed{ 8\sqrt{2} }. $$

Radicals May Need Simplification First

Consider:

$$ \sqrt{8}+3\sqrt{2}. $$

Since:

$$ \sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{2}, $$

the expression becomes:

$$ 2\sqrt{2}+3\sqrt{2} = \boxed{ 5\sqrt{2} }. $$

Unlike Radicals

In simplified form:

$$ \sqrt{2}+\sqrt{3} $$

does not combine into a single like-radical term.

Complex Numbers

Terms involving the same basis element can be collected:

$$ 3i+7i = \boxed{ 10i }. $$

Vectors

Scalar multiples of the same vector can be collected:

$$ 4\mathbf{v}-2\mathbf{v} = \boxed{ 2\mathbf{v} }. $$

This reflects the same distributive structure:

$$ a\mathbf{v}+b\mathbf{v} = (a+b)\mathbf{v}. $$

Linear Algebra Analogy

Collecting like terms resembles collecting coefficients of the same basis vector. If:

$$ 3\mathbf{e}_1 + 5\mathbf{e}_1 - 2\mathbf{e}_2, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ 8\mathbf{e}_1 - 2\mathbf{e}_2 }. $$

Structural Interpretation

Like terms are terms occupying the same algebraic basis position. Their coefficients can therefore be combined without changing the underlying variable structure.

Monomial Interpretation

In polynomial algebra, two terms are like exactly when they are scalar multiples of the same monomial.

$$ \boxed{ au \text{ and } bu } $$

are like whenever $u$ is the same monomial.

Coefficient Extraction

Once like terms are collected, the resulting coefficient is the sum of the original coefficients.

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{coeff}_u \left( a_1u+\cdots+a_ku \right) = a_1+\cdots+a_k }. $$

Equation Simplification

Like terms should often be combined on each side of an equation before solving.

For example:

$$ 3x+5+2x=20. $$

Combine:

$$ 5x+5=20. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ x=3 }. $$

Inequality Simplification

The same collection rule applies within inequalities:

$$ 4x+2x-3<9 $$

becomes:

$$ \boxed{ 6x-3<9 }. $$

Expressions vs. Equations

Combining like terms simplifies an expression. It does not by itself "solve" an expression because expressions do not have solution sets unless placed in an equation, inequality, or other condition.

Combining Is Not Factoring

From:

$$ 3x+5x, $$

combining gives:

$$ 8x. $$

Factoring instead displays the shared structure:

$$ x(3+5). $$

The two viewpoints are connected by the distributive law.

Collecting Like Terms as Reverse Distribution

$$ \boxed{ ax+bx \longrightarrow (a+b)x } $$

can be understood as using distribution backward.

Distribution Creates Terms

$$ a(b+c) = ab+ac. $$

Collection Recombines Matching Terms

$$ ab+cb = (a+c)b. $$

Like Terms after Substitution

Symbolic terms that are unlike may evaluate to equal numerical values after a particular substitution, but that does not make them like terms algebraically.

For example:

$$ x \qquad\text{and}\qquad x^2 $$

both equal $1$ when $x=1$, but they remain unlike terms as symbolic monomials.

Likeness Is Structural, Not Pointwise

Like-term classification depends on algebraic form, not on accidental equality at selected input values.

Commutative-Algebra Caveat

Reordering factors such as:

$$ xy=yx $$

assumes a commutative multiplication setting. In noncommutative algebra, factor order may matter.

Noncommutative Example

For matrices $A$ and $B$, generally:

$$ AB\neq BA. $$

Therefore matrix words $AB$ and $BA$ should not automatically be treated as the same algebraic term.

Polynomial Ring Interpretation

In a commutative polynomial ring, monomials provide natural classes for collecting coefficients.

Canonical Form

Combining all like terms gives a canonical polynomial representation in which each monomial appears at most once with a single coefficient.

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{\alpha} c_{\alpha}x^{\alpha} } $$

where each exponent pattern $\alpha$ occurs once.

Multivariable Exponent Vector

A monomial:

$$ x_1^{a_1}\cdots x_n^{a_n} $$

can be identified by its exponent vector:

$$ \boxed{ (a_1,\ldots,a_n) }. $$

Two polynomial terms are like precisely when these exponent vectors match.

Example with Exponent Vectors

The terms:

$$ 3x^2y^5 \qquad\text{and}\qquad -4x^2y^5 $$

share exponent vector:

$$ \boxed{ (2,5) }. $$

Unlike Exponent Vectors

The terms:

$$ x^2y^5 \qquad\text{and}\qquad x^5y^2 $$

have exponent vectors $(2,5)$ and $(5,2)$ and are therefore unlike.

Total Degree Is Secondary

Both of those monomials have total degree $7$, yet their exponent patterns differ.

Computer Algebra

Computer-algebra systems routinely normalize expressions by sorting monomials and adding coefficients attached to identical monomial keys.

Data-Structure Analogy

Computationally, one can imagine storing each monomial as a key and its coefficient as a value. Combining like terms means adding values associated with the same key.

Common Error: Adding Coefficients of Unlike Terms

In general:

$$ \boxed{ 3x+4y \neq 7xy }. $$

Common Error: Adding Exponents

From:

$$ 2x^2+3x^2, $$

the correct result is:

$$ \boxed{ 5x^2 } $$

not $5x^4$.

Common Error: Multiplying Coefficients when Adding

The coefficients are added:

$$ 2x+3x = 5x, $$

not $6x$.

Common Error: Ignoring Different Exponents

$x^2$ and $x^3$ are unlike even though the variable symbol is the same.

Common Error: Ignoring Different Variables

$x^2$ and $y^2$ are unlike even though their exponents match.

Common Error: Using Total Degree Alone

$x^2y$ and $xy^2$ both have degree $3$ but are unlike.

Common Error: Treating Signs as Variable Structure

A negative sign belongs to the coefficient. Therefore $-3x$ and $5x$ are like.

Common Error: Forgetting Constant Terms Are Like

All constants belong to the same degree-$0$ term class.

Common Error: Combining before Distributing

Parentheses may hide terms that must first be expanded correctly.

Common Error: Losing a Negative Sign

In:

$$ 7x-(-2x), $$

subtraction of a negative coefficient gives:

$$ \boxed{ 9x }. $$

Common Error: Changing the Variable Part

Combining:

$$ 4x^3+2x^3 $$

changes only the coefficient:

$$ \boxed{ 6x^3 }. $$

Common Error: Assuming Equal Values Mean Like Terms

Symbolic structure, not equality at one input, determines whether terms are like.

Common Error: Assuming Factor Order Never Matters

Reordering variables is valid in ordinary commutative polynomial algebra but not automatically in noncommutative systems.

Common Misconception: Like terms do not need to have the same coefficient, sign, or numerical value. They need the same algebraic variable structure. Thus $3x^2y$ and $-8x^2y$ are like terms, while $3x^2y$ and $3xy^2$ are not. Combining like terms changes only their coefficients; it does not alter the common variable part.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$3x+5x$ "Three x and five x are like terms."
$3x+5x=8x$ "Combine the like terms by adding their coefficients."
$4x^2$ and $-7x^2$ "These are like terms because they have the same variable part, x squared."
$x^2$ and $x^3$ "These are unlike terms because their exponents differ."
$x^2y$ and $yx^2$ "These are like terms in ordinary commutative algebra."
$ax^m+bx^m=(a+b)x^m$ "Factor the common variable part and add the coefficients."
$x^2y^3$ and $xy^4$ "These are unlike because their exponent patterns do not match."

Common instructional phrases include combine like terms, collect like terms, group like terms, and simplify by combining like terms.

The phrase same variable part means more than merely containing the same letters. Corresponding exponents must also match.

Thus:

$$ x^2y^3 \qquad\text{and}\qquad 7x^2y^3 $$

are like, whereas:

$$ x^2y^3 \qquad\text{and}\qquad x^3y^2 $$

are not.

Defense Notation Advice: Compare terms only after simplifying their factors into a consistent order. Separate the coefficient from the variable part, and compare every variable exponent individually. When combining like terms, operate only on the coefficients and copy the common variable part unchanged. Do not import multiplication rules, such as adding exponents, into addition or subtraction.
