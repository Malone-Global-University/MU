Vertical Shift

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

A vertical shift is a transformation that moves every point of a graph upward or downward by the same amount without changing its horizontal coordinates.

$$ \boxed{ g(x)=f(x)+k } $$

The word vertical refers to motion parallel to the $y$-axis, while shift indicates a translation rather than a change in the graph's basic shape.

Operational Nuance

Upward Shift

If $k>0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ g(x)=f(x)+k } $$

moves the graph of $f$ upward by $k$ units.

Downward Shift

If $k<0$, the graph moves downward by $|k|$ units.

$$ \boxed{ g(x)=f(x)-3 } $$

represents a vertical shift downward by $3$ units.

Point Mapping

Every point:

$$ (x,y) $$

on the original graph is translated to:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y+k) }. $$

Domain

A pure vertical shift does not change the inputs for which the function is defined:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f+k)=\operatorname{Dom}(f) }. $$

Range

The range is translated vertically:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Range}(f+k) = \{y+k:y\in\operatorname{Range}(f)\} }. $$

Example

$$ f(x)=x^2, \qquad g(x)=x^2+4. $$

The graph of $g$ is the graph of $f$ shifted upward $4$ units. Its vertex moves from $(0,0)$ to $(0,4)$.

Horizontal Asymptotes

A vertical shift also moves horizontal asymptotes. If $f(x)$ has horizontal asymptote $y=L$, then $f(x)+k$ has:

$$ \boxed{ y=L+k }. $$

Vertical Asymptotes

A pure vertical shift does not change the $x$-locations of vertical asymptotes, because the input variable is unchanged.

Difference from Vertical Scaling

A vertical shift adds a constant:

$$ f(x)+k, $$

while vertical scaling multiplies the output:

$$ Af(x). $$

These are different transformations.

Common Misconception

Adding a constant outside the function changes the outputs, not the inputs. Therefore $f(x)+k$ produces a vertical shift, whereas $f(x-k)$ generally produces a horizontal shift.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$g(x)=f(x)+4$ "Shift the graph of f upward four units."
$g(x)=f(x)-3$ "Shift the graph of f downward three units."
$(x,y)\mapsto(x,y+k)$ "Keep x fixed and add k to every y-coordinate."
$k>0$ "The vertical shift is upward."
$k<0$ "The vertical shift is downward."
$y=L+k$ "The horizontal asymptote shifts vertically by k."

In transformation notation, vertical shifts are often represented by the final additive constant in forms such as:

$$ g(x)=Af(B(x-C))+D, $$

where $D$ controls the vertical translation.

Defense Notation Advice: Constants added outside the function act on output values. Keep this distinct from constants placed inside the function's input, which control horizontal transformations.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Form: $g(x)=f(x)+k$.
  • Positive Shift: $k>0$ moves the graph upward.
  • Negative Shift: $k<0$ moves the graph downward.
  • Point Transformation: $(x,y)\mapsto(x,y+k)$.
  • Domain: Unchanged by a pure vertical shift.
  • Range: Translated by the same vertical amount.
  • Intercepts: $y$-values shift, while $x$-intercepts may change or disappear.
  • Vertices: Vertex $y$-coordinates shift by $k$.
  • Horizontal Asymptotes: Shift vertically by $k$.
  • Vertical Asymptotes: Their $x$-locations remain unchanged under a pure vertical shift.
  • Transformations: Vertical shift is one component of the general form $Af(B(x-C))+D$.
  • Trigonometric Functions: The shift determines the midline of transformed sine and cosine graphs.
  • Modeling: A vertical shift can represent a baseline, offset, equilibrium level, or fixed background quantity.
  • Common Error: Reversing the direction of an outside additive constant.
  • Common Error: Confusing $f(x)+k$ with $f(x+k)$.
  • Common Error: Treating a vertical shift as a vertical stretch or compression.
  • Core Interpretation: A vertical shift changes every output by the same constant: $$ \boxed{ f(x)\longmapsto f(x)+k }. $$ Geometrically, the graph retains its shape while every point moves parallel to the $y$-axis by the same signed distance.
