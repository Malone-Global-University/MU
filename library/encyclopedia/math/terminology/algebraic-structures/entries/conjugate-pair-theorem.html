Conjugate Pair Theorem

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

The Conjugate Pair Theorem is also commonly called the Complex Conjugate Root Theorem or Complex Conjugate Zero Theorem. The word conjugate comes from a Latin root meaning "joined together" or "yoked together," an idea that fits the mathematical relationship: certain complex numbers naturally occur as a matched pair.

If:

$$ z=a+bi, $$

where $a,b\in\mathbb{R}$, then its complex conjugate is:

$$ \overline{z}=a-bi. $$

The two numbers have the same real part and imaginary parts of opposite sign. In the complex plane, they are reflections of one another across the real axis.

The phrase conjugate pair therefore refers to:

$$ a+bi \qquad\text{and}\qquad a-bi. $$

The theorem describes the special role these pairs play among the zeros of polynomials whose coefficients are real numbers.

Operational Nuance

Let:

$$ P(x) = a_nx^n+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_1x+a_0 $$

be a polynomial whose coefficients are all real:

$$ a_0,a_1,\ldots,a_n\in\mathbb{R}. $$

If a nonreal complex number:

$$ a+bi, \qquad b\neq0, $$

is a zero of $P$, then its complex conjugate:

$$ a-bi $$

must also be a zero.

$$ \boxed{ P(a+bi)=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad P(a-bi)=0 } $$

For example, if a real-coefficient polynomial has:

$$ 3+2i $$

as a zero, then it must also have:

$$ 3-2i $$

as a zero.

The theorem follows from a basic property of complex conjugation. For a polynomial with real coefficients:

$$ P(\overline{z}) = \overline{P(z)}. $$

If:

$$ P(z)=0, $$

then taking complex conjugates gives:

$$ \overline{P(z)} = \overline{0} = 0. $$

Therefore:

$$ P(\overline{z})=0. $$

The requirement that the coefficients be real is essential. It allows each coefficient to remain unchanged under conjugation:

$$ \overline{a_k}=a_k \qquad \text{when }a_k\in\mathbb{R}. $$

If a polynomial has nonreal complex coefficients, its zeros do not generally need to occur in conjugate pairs.

Common Misconception: The theorem does not say that every complex polynomial has conjugate-paired zeros. The condition:

$$ \text{all coefficients are real} $$

is indispensable. The pairing phenomenon is a consequence of the polynomial being unchanged when its coefficients are complex-conjugated.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$z=a+bi$ "Z equals a plus b i."
$\overline{z}=a-bi$ "Z-bar equals a minus b i," or "the complex conjugate of z is a minus b i."
$P(z)=0$ "Z is a zero of P."
$P(\overline{z})=0$ "The conjugate of z is also a zero of P."
$P(\overline{z})=\overline{P(z)}$ "P of z-bar equals the conjugate of P of z."
$a+bi,\ a-bi$ "A plus b i and a minus b i form a conjugate pair."

The horizontal bar in:

$$ \overline{z} $$

is read "z-bar" and denotes the complex conjugate of $z$ in this context. If:

$$ z=4-7i, $$

then:

$$ \overline{z}=4+7i. $$

The real part remains unchanged while the sign of the imaginary part reverses.

Defense Notation Advice: Do not change the sign of the entire complex number. The conjugate of:

$$ 5+3i $$

is:

$$ 5-3i, $$

not $-5-3i$. Only the sign of the imaginary component changes.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Polynomial Zeros: If $P(x)$ has real coefficients and: $$ a+bi $$ is a nonreal zero, then: $$ a-bi $$ must also be a zero. Nonreal zeros therefore occur in conjugate pairs.
  • Polynomial Factors: If $a+bi$ and $a-bi$ are zeros, then the corresponding linear factors are: $$ x-(a+bi) $$ and: $$ x-(a-bi). $$ Their product is: $$ [x-(a+bi)][x-(a-bi)]. $$ Simplifying gives: $$ (x-a)^2+b^2 $$ and therefore: $$ x^2-2ax+(a^2+b^2). $$ This quadratic has real coefficients.
  • Example of Conjugate Factors: If: $$ 2+3i $$ and: $$ 2-3i $$ are zeros, then: $$ [x-(2+3i)][x-(2-3i)] $$ becomes: $$ (x-2)^2+9 $$ $$ =x^2-4x+13. $$ Thus the conjugate pair combines into a real quadratic factor.
  • Constructing Polynomials: If a problem asks for a polynomial with real coefficients and specifies the zero: $$ 1+4i, $$ the zero: $$ 1-4i $$ must also be included. The corresponding quadratic factor is: $$ (x-1)^2+16 = x^2-2x+17. $$
  • Multiplicity: Conjugate roots occur with the same multiplicity. Thus, if: $$ a+bi $$ is a root of multiplicity $m$ of a real-coefficient polynomial, then: $$ a-bi $$ also has multiplicity $m$.
  • Real Roots: Every real number is its own complex conjugate: $$ \overline{a}=a \qquad (a\in\mathbb{R}). $$ The theorem therefore imposes no requirement that a distinct second root accompany a real zero.
  • Odd-Degree Polynomials: By the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra, a degree-$n$ polynomial has $n$ complex zeros counted with multiplicity. Since the nonreal zeros of a real polynomial occur in pairs, an odd-degree real polynomial cannot consist entirely of nonreal zeros. It must have at least one real zero.
  • Even Number of Nonreal Zeros: Because every nonreal zero is paired with a distinct conjugate zero, the total number of nonreal zeros of a real polynomial, counted with multiplicity, is even.
  • Fundamental Theorem of Algebra: The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra guarantees the existence of complex zeros; the Conjugate Pair Theorem adds additional structure when the coefficients are real. Together they imply that a real polynomial can be factored over $\mathbb{R}$ into linear factors and irreducible quadratic factors.
  • Factoring over the Real Numbers: A conjugate pair: $$ a\pm bi $$ gives the real quadratic: $$ x^2-2ax+(a^2+b^2). $$ Since: $$ (-2a)^2-4(a^2+b^2) = -4b^2<0 $$ for $b\neq0$, this quadratic has no real zeros and is irreducible over $\mathbb{R}$.
  • Factoring over the Complex Numbers: Over $\mathbb{C}$, the same quadratic separates into two linear factors: $$ x^2-2ax+(a^2+b^2) = [x-(a+bi)][x-(a-bi)]. $$ Thus the factorization of a polynomial depends on the number system in which factorization is being considered.
  • Complex Plane: The zeros: $$ a+bi \qquad\text{and}\qquad a-bi $$ are mirror images across the real axis. Therefore the nonreal zero set of any real-coefficient polynomial is symmetric with respect to the real axis.
  • Real Matrices: A related principle applies to eigenvalues of real matrices. Since the characteristic polynomial of a real matrix has real coefficients, its nonreal eigenvalues occur in complex conjugate pairs.
  • Terminological Contrast: A complex conjugate reverses the sign of the imaginary part of a complex number. The Conjugate Pair Theorem states that nonreal zeros of real-coefficient polynomials occur in conjugate pairs. The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra guarantees the total number of complex zeros counted with multiplicity, but does not by itself state the conjugate-pair property.
