Argument of a Function

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

An argument of a function is a value or mathematical object supplied to a function as input.

In:

$$ \boxed{ f(x) }, $$

the symbol $x$ is the argument of the function $f$, while $f(x)$ is the corresponding function value or output.

$$ \boxed{ x \xrightarrow{\ f\ } f(x) }. $$

More generally, if:

$$ f:D\to C, $$

then any admissible element $x\in D$ may serve as an argument of $f$.

The term is therefore closely related to input, but mathematical usage can be broader: an argument need not be a number. It may be a vector, matrix, function, set, ordered tuple, or another mathematical object accepted by the function.

Operational Nuance

Basic Function Notation

Suppose:

$$ f(x)=x^2+1. $$

If the argument is $3$, then:

$$ f(3)=3^2+1=10. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ 3=\text{argument}, \qquad 10=\text{function value} }. $$

Argument versus Variable

In the formula:

$$ f(x)=x^2+1, $$

$x$ is often called the independent variable. When a particular value is substituted into the function, that supplied value is an argument.

For example:

$$ f(7), $$

has argument $7$, even though the defining rule was originally written using the variable $x$.

The Argument Can Be an Expression

An argument need not be a single variable or number.

If:

$$ f(x)=x^2, $$

then in:

$$ f(a+b), $$

the entire expression:

$$ \boxed{ a+b } $$

is the argument.

Therefore:

$$ f(a+b)=(a+b)^2. $$

Parentheses Indicate Function Application

In:

$$ f(x), $$

the parentheses do not ordinarily mean multiplication.

Instead, they indicate that the function $f$ is being applied to the argument $x$:

$$ \boxed{ f(x) = \text{the value of }f\text{ at }x }. $$

Evaluation Is Substitution into the Rule

If:

$$ f(x)=2x^2-3x+4, $$

then:

$$ f(t) = 2t^2-3t+4. $$

The argument $t$ replaces every occurrence of the input variable in the rule.

Expression Arguments Require Grouping

If:

$$ f(x)=x^2-2x, $$

then:

$$ f(x+h) = (x+h)^2 - 2(x+h). $$

The entire argument $x+h$ must replace $x$:

$$ \boxed{ f(x+h) \ne x+h^2-2x+h }. $$

Arguments Must Belong to the Domain

A function may accept only certain arguments.

For:

$$ f(x)=\sqrt{x}, $$

regarded as a real-valued function, the domain is:

$$ [0,\infty). $$

Therefore $4$ is an admissible real argument:

$$ f(4)=2, $$

while $-1$ is not an argument in that real domain.

Rational-Function Restrictions

For:

$$ f(x)=\frac{1}{x-3}, $$

the argument $x=3$ is excluded because:

$$ x-3=0. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ 3\notin\operatorname{Dom}(f) }. $$

Logarithmic Restrictions

For the real natural logarithm:

$$ f(x)=\ln x, $$

the argument must satisfy:

$$ \boxed{ x>0 }. $$

The Argument of a Trigonometric Function

In:

$$ \sin x, $$

$x$ is the argument of the sine function.

In:

$$ \sin(3x+1), $$

the entire expression:

$$ \boxed{ 3x+1 } $$

is the argument of sine.

Outer and Inner Functions

In:

$$ \sin(x^2), $$

$x^2$ is the argument of the sine function.

At the same time, $x$ is the argument of the squaring function:

$$ x \mapsto x^2. $$

This layered input structure is the basis of function composition.

Function Composition

For functions $f$ and $g$:

$$ \boxed{ (f\circ g)(x) = f(g(x)) }. $$

Here $g(x)$ becomes the argument supplied to $f$.

$$ \boxed{ x \xrightarrow{\ g\ } g(x) \xrightarrow{\ f\ } f(g(x)) }. $$

Composition Example

Suppose:

$$ f(u)=u^2, \qquad g(x)=x+3. $$

Then:

$$ f(g(x)) = f(x+3) = (x+3)^2. $$

The output of $g$ becomes the argument of $f$.

Argument versus Function Value

If:

$$ f(2)=7, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ 2=\text{argument} } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ 7=\text{function value} }. $$

Confusing these reverses the input-output relationship.

Argument versus Image

If:

$$ y=f(x), $$

then $y$ may be called the image of $x$ under $f$:

$$ \boxed{ x\mapsto y }. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ x=\text{argument}, \qquad f(x)=\text{image of }x }. $$

Argument versus Preimage

If:

$$ f(x)=y, $$

then $x$ is a preimage of $y$ under $f$.

A function value may have more than one preimage if the function is not one-to-one.

Example of Multiple Preimages

Let:

$$ f(x)=x^2. $$

Then:

$$ f(2)=4 \qquad\text{and}\qquad f(-2)=4. $$

Thus $2$ and $-2$ are distinct arguments producing the same function value.

Functions of Several Arguments

A function may accept more than one input component:

$$ \boxed{ f(x,y) }. $$

For example:

$$ f(x,y)=x^2+y^2. $$

At:

$$ f(3,4), $$

the inputs are $3$ and $4$.

Formally, depending on how the function is defined, one can regard the input as the ordered pair:

$$ \boxed{ (3,4) } $$

or speak informally of the function as having two arguments.

Multivariable Functions

More generally:

$$ f(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n) $$

may be described as a function of $n$ arguments or as a function whose argument is an $n$-tuple:

$$ \boxed{ (x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n) }. $$

Vector Arguments

A function may take a vector as its argument:

$$ f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R. $$

One may write:

$$ \boxed{ f(\mathbf{x}) } $$

where:

$$ \mathbf{x} = (x_1,\ldots,x_n). $$

Matrix Arguments

Functions can also accept matrices:

$$ \det(A), \qquad \operatorname{tr}(A). $$

In these expressions, the matrix $A$ is the argument.

Functions as Arguments

In higher mathematics, a function itself may be an argument of another function or functional.

For example:

$$ \boxed{ I(f)=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx }. $$

Here the input to $I$ is the function $f$ itself.

Functional

A function whose inputs are themselves functions is often called a functional.

$$ \boxed{ J[f] } $$

is common notation in areas such as calculus of variations.

Parameters versus Arguments

Consider:

$$ f(x;a)=ax^2. $$

Here $x$ may be treated as the varying argument while $a$ is treated as a parameter selecting a member of a family of functions.

The distinction is contextual rather than purely typographical.

Parameter Can Become an Argument

If the same expression is instead interpreted as a function:

$$ F(x,a)=ax^2, $$

then both $x$ and $a$ are components of the input.

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \text{argument versus parameter depends on how the function is organized} }. $$

Dummy Variables

The input-variable name used in a function definition is often arbitrary.

These define the same rule:

$$ f(x)=x^2+1 $$

and:

$$ f(t)=t^2+1. $$

The name of the placeholder changes, but the function does not.

Lambda-Style Interpretation

The rule:

$$ f(x)=x^2+1 $$

can be interpreted structurally as:

$$ \boxed{ x\mapsto x^2+1 }. $$

This notation emphasizes the transformation from argument to value.

Inverse Functions

If $f$ is invertible:

$$ y=f(x) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x=f^{-1}(y). $$

The original output $y$ becomes the argument of the inverse function.

$$ \boxed{ f^{-1}(f(x))=x }. $$

Argument in a Difference Quotient

In:

$$ \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}, $$

the arguments of $f$ are:

$$ \boxed{ x+h \qquad\text{and}\qquad x }. $$

Recognizing this is essential when expanding difference quotients correctly.

Derivative Evaluation

If:

$$ f'(x) $$

is the derivative function, then in:

$$ f'(3), $$

the argument of the derivative function is $3$.

Definite Integrals and Function Arguments

In:

$$ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx, $$

$x$ is the variable supplied as the argument to $f$ throughout the integration.

Changing the dummy integration variable does not change the value:

$$ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \int_a^b f(t)\,dt }. $$

Argument in Sequences

A sequence can be treated as a function whose arguments are integers:

$$ a:\mathbb N\to\mathbb R. $$

The notation:

$$ a_n $$

corresponds functionally to:

$$ \boxed{ a(n) }. $$

Thus the index $n$ acts as the argument.

Piecewise Functions

In a piecewise-defined function, the argument determines which rule applies.

$$ f(x)= \begin{cases} x^2, & x\ge0,\\ -x, & x<0. \end{cases} $$

To evaluate $f(-3)$, the argument $-3$ determines that the second branch applies:

$$ f(-3)=3. $$

Programming and Computer Science Usage

Mathematical terminology strongly influences programming language. A function declaration may contain parameters, while a particular function call supplies arguments.

Conceptually:

$$ f(x)=x^2 $$

uses $x$ as a placeholder, while:

$$ f(5) $$

supplies $5$ as an actual argument.

Formal Input as an Ordered Tuple

For:

$$ f:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R, $$

a formally precise interpretation is that the function receives one ordered-pair argument:

$$ \boxed{ (x,y)\in\mathbb R^2 }. $$

Informally, mathematicians still commonly say that $f(x,y)$ is a function of two arguments or two variables.

Partial Application

If:

$$ f(x,y)=xy, $$

fixing one argument can produce a new one-variable function.

For example, fixing $y=3$ gives:

$$ g(x)=f(x,3)=3x. $$

Argument versus Argument of a Complex Number

The word argument has another important mathematical meaning.

For a complex number:

$$ z=re^{i\theta}, $$

$\theta$ may be called an argument of the complex number.

This is completely different from calling $x$ the argument of the function $f$ in:

$$ f(x). $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \text{function argument} = \text{input}, \qquad \text{complex argument} = \text{angle} }. $$

Argument of the Argument Function

The two meanings can even occur in the same expression:

$$ \operatorname{Arg}(z). $$

Here $z$ is the function argument supplied to the principal-argument function $\operatorname{Arg}$, while $\operatorname{Arg}(z)$ is an angle.

Common Misconception

In:

$$ f(2x+1), $$

the argument is not merely $x$.

The argument supplied directly to $f$ is:

$$ \boxed{ 2x+1 }. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$f(x)$ "F of x," or "the value of f at x."
$f(3)$ "F of three," with three as the argument.
$f(x+h)$ "F of x plus h," where the entire expression x plus h is the argument.
$f(g(x))$ "F of g of x," where g of x is the argument supplied to f.
$f(x,y)$ "F of x comma y," commonly described as a function of two arguments or two variables.
$f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R$ "F maps R n to R."

The word argument emphasizes function application rather than merely the symbol used in the definition. In $f(x+1)$, for example, $x$ is still a variable, but the argument passed to $f$ is the entire expression $x+1$.

Defense Notation Advice: When substituting a compound expression into a function, place it in parentheses everywhere the original input variable occurred. This prevents many sign, exponent, and distribution errors.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: An argument is an input supplied to a function.
  • Basic Form: $$ \boxed{ f(x) } $$ has argument $x$.
  • Function Value: $$ \boxed{ f(x) } $$ is the output corresponding to argument $x$.
  • Mapping View: $$ \boxed{ x\xmapsto{\ f\ }f(x) }. $$
  • Domain Requirement: An admissible argument must belong to the function's domain.
  • Expression Argument: In: $$ \boxed{ f(2x+1) }, $$ the argument is $2x+1$.
  • Composition: In: $$ \boxed{ f(g(x)) }, $$ $g(x)$ is the argument supplied to $f$.
  • Composition Rule: $$ \boxed{ (f\circ g)(x)=f(g(x)) }. $$
  • Trigonometric Argument: In: $$ \boxed{ \sin(3x) }, $$ $3x$ is the argument of sine.
  • Logarithmic Argument: In: $$ \boxed{ \ln(x-2) }, $$ $x-2$ is the argument of the logarithm.
  • Real Logarithm Restriction: $$ \boxed{ x-2>0 } $$ is required for $\ln(x-2)$ as a real-valued expression.
  • Inverse Function: In: $$ \boxed{ f^{-1}(y) }, $$ $y$ is the argument of the inverse function.
  • Difference Quotient: $$ \boxed{ \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} } $$ contains the arguments $x+h$ and $x$.
  • Multiple Arguments: $$ \boxed{ f(x,y) } $$ may be described informally as a function of two arguments.
  • Tuple Interpretation: Formally, $f:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R$ may be viewed as receiving the ordered pair $(x,y)$ as one input.
  • Vector Argument: $$ \boxed{ f(\mathbf{x}) } $$ may take an entire vector as its argument.
  • Matrix Argument: In $\det(A)$ or $\operatorname{tr}(A)$, the matrix $A$ is the argument.
  • Function Argument: A functional may take an entire function as input.
  • Functional Example: $$ \boxed{ I(f)=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx }. $$
  • Parameter Contrast: A parameter may be held fixed while another quantity is treated as the varying argument.
  • Context Dependence: A quantity treated as a parameter in one formulation may become an argument in another.
  • Dummy Variable: $$ \boxed{ f(x)=x^2 } $$ and: $$ \boxed{ f(t)=t^2 } $$ can define the same function.
  • Sequence Interpretation: A sequence can be regarded as a function whose arguments are integer indices.
  • Piecewise Function: The argument determines which branch of a piecewise definition applies.
  • Programming Usage: In many programming contexts, a parameter is a placeholder in a function definition and an argument is the actual value supplied in a call.
  • Input-Output Contrast: $$ \boxed{ \text{argument}\to\text{function}\to\text{value} }. $$
  • Image Contrast: If $y=f(x)$, then $x$ is an argument and $y$ is its image under $f$.
  • Preimage Contrast: If $f(x)=y$, then $x$ is a preimage of $y$.
  • Complex-Number Contrast: Argument of a function means input; argument of a complex number means direction angle.
  • Nested Meaning: In: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Arg}(z) }, $$ $z$ is the function argument, while the result is the principal argument angle of the complex number.
  • Common Error: Function parentheses do not ordinarily indicate multiplication.
  • Common Error: In $f(x+h)$, replace every occurrence of the original input variable with the complete expression $x+h$.
  • Common Error: The argument is not the same thing as the output.
  • Terminological Contrast: Argument is the supplied input. Domain is the set of admissible inputs. Function value is the resulting output. Image is the output associated with an input. Parameter is often a quantity held fixed while a function is studied with respect to another input.
