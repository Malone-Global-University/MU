Gaussian Integer

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

A Gaussian integer is a complex number whose real and imaginary parts are both ordinary integers. It has the form:

$$ \boxed{ a+bi, \qquad a,b\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$

The set of all Gaussian integers is written:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{Z}[i] = \{a+bi:a,b\in\mathbb{Z}\} }. $$

The name honors Carl Friedrich Gauss, whose work on complex integers and higher reciprocity helped establish these numbers as fundamental objects of number theory rather than merely convenient notation.

The notation $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ means the smallest ring containing the ordinary integers and the imaginary unit $i$, where:

$$ \boxed{i^2=-1}. $$

Gaussian integers therefore extend the ordinary integers from a one-dimensional number line into a two-dimensional integer lattice in the complex plane.

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Z}[i] \subset \mathbb{C} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Basic Form

Every Gaussian integer has the form:

$$ z=a+bi, \qquad a,b\in\mathbb{Z}. $$

Examples include:

$$ 3+2i, \qquad -5+i, \qquad 7, \qquad -4i. $$

Ordinary Integers Are Gaussian Integers

Every ordinary integer $n$ can be written:

$$ n=n+0i. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{Z}\subset\mathbb{Z}[i] }. $$

Pure Imaginary Gaussian Integers

Numbers of the form:

$$ bi, \qquad b\in\mathbb{Z}, $$

are also Gaussian integers.

Addition

Gaussian integers are added componentwise:

$$ \boxed{ (a+bi)+(c+di) = (a+c)+(b+d)i }. $$

Addition Example

$$ (3+2i)+(5-7i) = \boxed{8-5i}. $$

Subtraction

$$ \boxed{ (a+bi)-(c+di) = (a-c)+(b-d)i }. $$

Multiplication

Multiply as ordinary binomials and use $i^2=-1$:

$$ \boxed{ (a+bi)(c+di) = (ac-bd)+(ad+bc)i }. $$

Multiplication Example

$$ (2+3i)(4-i) = 8-2i+12i-3i^2 = \boxed{11+10i}. $$

Closure

Addition, subtraction, and multiplication of Gaussian integers always produce Gaussian integers.

$$ \boxed{ z,w\in\mathbb{Z}[i] \Longrightarrow z\pm w,\ zw\in\mathbb{Z}[i] }. $$

Division Is Different

Gaussian integers are not closed under ordinary division.

For example:

$$ \frac{1}{2}\notin\mathbb{Z}[i]. $$

Thus $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is a ring, not a field.

Complex Conjugate

The conjugate of:

$$ z=a+bi $$

is:

$$ \boxed{ \overline{z}=a-bi }. $$

The conjugate of a Gaussian integer is again a Gaussian integer.

Norm

The Gaussian norm is:

$$ \boxed{ N(a+bi) = a^2+b^2 }. $$

It is always a nonnegative ordinary integer.

Norm and Conjugation

$$ \boxed{ N(z) = z\overline{z} }. $$

For $z=a+bi$:

$$ (a+bi)(a-bi) = a^2+b^2. $$

Norm Example

$$ N(3+4i) = 3^2+4^2 = \boxed{25}. $$

Norm vs. Complex Modulus

The ordinary complex modulus is:

$$ |a+bi| = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ N(z)=|z|^2 }. $$

Multiplicative Norm

A key property is:

$$ \boxed{ N(zw)=N(z)N(w) }. $$

This makes the norm extremely useful in divisibility and factorization arguments.

Zero Norm

$$ \boxed{ N(z)=0 \iff z=0 }. $$

Units

A Gaussian integer is a unit if it has a multiplicative inverse that is also a Gaussian integer.

The units are exactly:

$$ \boxed{ 1,\ -1,\ i,\ -i }. $$

Norm Characterization of Units

$$ \boxed{ z\text{ is a unit} \iff N(z)=1 }. $$

The integer solutions of:

$$ a^2+b^2=1 $$

produce exactly the four Gaussian units.

Associates

Two Gaussian integers are associates if one differs from the other by multiplication by a unit.

$$ \boxed{ z\sim w \iff z=uw \text{ for some } u\in\{1,-1,i,-i\} }. $$

Example of Associates

The Gaussian integers:

$$ 1+i,\quad -1-i,\quad -1+i,\quad 1-i $$

are all associates.

Geometric Meaning of Associates

Multiplication by the units rotates a complex number by multiples of $90^\circ$ or reverses its direction.

Divisibility

For $\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}[i]$:

$$ \boxed{ \alpha\mid\beta \iff \exists\,\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}[i] \text{ such that } \beta=\alpha\gamma }. $$

Norm Divisibility Consequence

If:

$$ \alpha\mid\beta, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ N(\alpha)\mid N(\beta) } $$

in the ordinary integers.

The converse need not hold.

Gaussian Prime

A nonzero nonunit $\pi\in\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is a Gaussian prime if its only Gaussian divisors are units and associates of $\pi$.

Prime vs. Ordinary Prime

An ordinary prime integer need not remain prime in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$.

For example:

$$ 5 = (2+i)(2-i). $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ 5 \text{ is not Gaussian prime} }. $$

Two Also Factors

In $\mathbb{Z}[i]$:

$$ 2 = -i(1+i)^2. $$

Therefore $2$ is not Gaussian prime.

Ordinary Primes Congruent to $1$ mod $4$

If an ordinary prime satisfies:

$$ p\equiv1\pmod4, $$

then it factors nontrivially in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$:

$$ \boxed{ p=(a+bi)(a-bi) } $$

for suitable integers $a,b$ with:

$$ p=a^2+b^2. $$

Example: $13$

$$ 13 = 3^2+2^2 = (3+2i)(3-2i). $$

Ordinary Primes Congruent to $3$ mod $4$

If an ordinary prime satisfies:

$$ p\equiv3\pmod4, $$

then $p$ remains prime in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$.

Examples include:

$$ \boxed{ 3,\ 7,\ 11,\ 19,\ldots } $$

The Prime $2$

The prime $2$ is exceptional:

$$ \boxed{ 2=-i(1+i)^2 }. $$

It is said to ramify in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$.

Classification of Gaussian Primes

Up to multiplication by units, Gaussian primes fall into three main types:

  • $1+i$ and its associates;
  • ordinary primes $p\equiv3\pmod4$;
  • $a+bi$ with both $a$ and $b$ nonzero and $a^2+b^2$ an ordinary prime.

Example of a Nonreal Gaussian Prime

Consider:

$$ 2+i. $$

Its norm is:

$$ N(2+i)=5. $$

Since $5$ is an ordinary prime:

$$ \boxed{ 2+i \text{ is Gaussian prime} }. $$

Prime Norm Criterion

If:

$$ N(z) $$

is an ordinary prime, then $z$ is Gaussian prime.

The converse is false for real-axis Gaussian primes such as $3$, whose norm is:

$$ N(3)=9. $$

Euclidean Division

The Gaussian integers possess a division algorithm based on the norm.

For $\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}[i]$ with $\beta\neq0$, there exist:

$$ q,r\in\mathbb{Z}[i] $$

such that:

$$ \boxed{ \alpha=\beta q+r, \qquad N(r)<N(\beta) }. $$

Choosing the Quotient

Compute the complex quotient:

$$ \frac{\alpha}{\beta} $$

and choose a Gaussian integer $q$ whose real and imaginary parts are nearest integers to the corresponding coordinates of the quotient.

Euclidean Domain

Because the norm provides a Euclidean function:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{Z}[i] \text{ is a Euclidean domain} }. $$

Principal Ideal Domain

Every Euclidean domain is a principal ideal domain, so:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{Z}[i] \text{ is a PID} }. $$

Unique Factorization Domain

Every principal ideal domain is a unique factorization domain. Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{Z}[i] \text{ is a UFD} }. $$

Unique Factorization

Every nonzero nonunit Gaussian integer can be factored into Gaussian primes, and this factorization is unique up to order and multiplication of factors by units.

$$ \boxed{ z = u\pi_1\pi_2\cdots\pi_k } $$

where $u$ is a unit and each $\pi_j$ is Gaussian prime.

Uniqueness Is Up to Associates

Because:

$$ \pi,\ -\pi,\ i\pi,\ -i\pi $$

are associates, unique factorization cannot distinguish among them as genuinely different prime factors.

Greatest Common Divisor

The Euclidean algorithm can be used in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ to find greatest common divisors.

A Gaussian GCD is unique only up to multiplication by a unit.

Gaussian Euclidean Algorithm

Repeatedly divide:

$$ \alpha=\beta q_1+r_1, $$
$$ \beta=r_1q_2+r_2, $$

with strictly decreasing nonzero norms until a zero remainder occurs.

Bézout Identity

For Gaussian integers $\alpha$ and $\beta$, a greatest common divisor $d$ can be written:

$$ \boxed{ d=x\alpha+y\beta } $$

for some $x,y\in\mathbb{Z}[i]$.

Geometric Lattice Interpretation

Each Gaussian integer:

$$ a+bi $$

corresponds to the lattice point:

$$ \boxed{ (a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}^2 }. $$

Gaussian Integer Lattice

In the complex plane, $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ forms the square lattice:

$$ \boxed{ \{(a,b):a,b\in\mathbb{Z}\} }. $$

Norm as Squared Distance

Geometrically:

$$ \boxed{ N(a+bi) = a^2+b^2 } $$

is the square of the Euclidean distance from $(a,b)$ to the origin.

Conjugation as Reflection

Complex conjugation:

$$ a+bi\mapsto a-bi $$

reflects the lattice across the real axis.

Multiplication by $i$ as Rotation

$$ \boxed{ i(a+bi) = -b+ai }. $$

Geometrically this rotates the corresponding lattice point by $90^\circ$ counterclockwise.

Units as Quarter-Turn Symmetries

Multiplication by:

$$ 1,\ i,\ -1,\ -i $$

rotates by:

$$ 0^\circ,\ 90^\circ,\ 180^\circ,\ 270^\circ. $$

Sum of Two Squares

Gaussian integers provide a natural framework for studying representations:

$$ \boxed{ n=a^2+b^2 }. $$

Since:

$$ a^2+b^2=(a+bi)(a-bi), $$

sums of two squares become factorization statements in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$.

Fermat's Two-Square Theorem

An ordinary prime $p$ can be written:

$$ p=a^2+b^2 $$

exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ p=2 \text{ or } p\equiv1\pmod4 }. $$

General Two-Square Criterion

A positive integer $n$ is expressible as a sum of two integer squares exactly when every prime congruent to $3$ modulo $4$ occurs to an even exponent in the ordinary prime factorization of $n$.

Example

Since:

$$ 65=5\cdot13, $$

and both primes are $1$ modulo $4$, one obtains representations such as:

$$ \boxed{ 65=1^2+8^2=4^2+7^2 }. $$

Ordinary Prime Factorization Changes in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$

An ordinary integer can factor more deeply when the ambient ring is enlarged.

Example:

$$ 25 = 5^2 = (2+i)^2(2-i)^2 $$

up to regrouping and associates.

Irreducibility Depends on the Ring

Whether an element is irreducible or prime depends on the ambient algebraic structure.

Thus:

$$ 5 $$

is prime in $\mathbb{Z}$ but reducible in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$.

Ring Extension

The Gaussian integers are formed by adjoining $i$ to $\mathbb{Z}$:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{Z}[i] }. $$

This is an example of a ring extension.

Quadratic Integer Ring

Gaussian integers are one of the simplest examples of algebraic integers in an imaginary quadratic number field.

They lie inside:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{Q}(i) }. $$

Gaussian Rational

The field of fractions of $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{Q}(i) = \{a+bi:a,b\in\mathbb{Q}\} }. $$

Polynomial Description

The ring may also be described as:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^2+1) \cong \mathbb{Z}[i] }. $$

This expresses the relation $i^2+1=0$ algebraically.

Integral Domain

Since $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is contained in the field $\mathbb{C}$:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{Z}[i] \text{ has no zero divisors} }. $$

Not a Field

Not every nonzero Gaussian integer is invertible within $\mathbb{Z}[i]$.

For example, $2$ has inverse $1/2$ in $\mathbb{C}$ but:

$$ \frac12\notin\mathbb{Z}[i]. $$

Common Error: Assuming Every Complex Number Is Gaussian

A complex number is Gaussian only when both components are integers.

Thus:

$$ 3+\frac12i \notin \mathbb{Z}[i]. $$

Common Error: Confusing Gaussian Integers with Gaussian Numbers in Statistics

The adjective Gaussian also appears in probability and statistics in connection with the normal distribution. A Gaussian integer has no direct definitional connection to Gaussian probability distributions.

Common Error: Assuming Ordinary Primes Stay Prime

Primality depends on the ring. Some ordinary primes split in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$.

Common Error: Using the Modulus Instead of the Norm

The Euclidean function for Gaussian integer arithmetic is normally:

$$ N(z)=|z|^2, $$

an integer-valued quantity, rather than $|z|$ itself.

Common Error: Forgetting Units in Unique Factorization

Gaussian prime factorization is unique only up to multiplication by the four units and reordering of factors.

Common Error: Treating Associates as Distinct Prime Types

The numbers:

$$ 2+i,\quad -2-i,\quad -1+2i,\quad 1-2i $$

are associates rather than fundamentally different prime factors.

Common Error: Assuming Composite Norm Means Composite Gaussian Integer

A Gaussian prime can have composite norm. For example:

$$ N(3)=9, $$

yet $3$ is Gaussian prime because $3\equiv3\pmod4$.

Common Misconception: Gaussian integers are not merely complex numbers with an unusual name. They form a distinct arithmetic system $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ in which addition, multiplication, divisibility, primes, greatest common divisors, and unique factorization can all be studied. Enlarging $\mathbb{Z}$ to $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ changes which elements count as prime, while the norm $a^2+b^2$ links the algebra directly to geometry and classical number theory.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$a+bi\in\mathbb{Z}[i]$ "A plus b i is a Gaussian integer."
$\mathbb{Z}[i]$ "Z adjoin i," or "the Gaussian integers."
$N(a+bi)=a^2+b^2$ "The norm of a plus b i is a squared plus b squared."
$\overline{a+bi}=a-bi$ "The complex conjugate of a plus b i is a minus b i."
$\alpha\mid\beta$ "Alpha divides beta in the Gaussian integers."
$z=u\pi_1\cdots\pi_k$ "Z factors into a unit times Gaussian primes."
$p\equiv1\pmod4$ "P is congruent to one modulo four."

The notation:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{Z}[i] } $$

should not be read as an interval, array, or function evaluation. The square brackets indicate adjoining the element $i$ to the ring $\mathbb{Z}$.

In elementary writing, one often says "Gaussian prime" for a prime element of $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ and "ordinary prime" or "rational prime" when contrasting it with a prime integer in $\mathbb{Z}$.

Defense Notation Advice: Always specify the ambient ring when primality or divisibility might be ambiguous. A statement such as "5 is prime" is true in $\mathbb{Z}$ but false in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$. Use $N(z)$ for the integer-valued Gaussian norm, distinguish it from $|z|$, and remember that Gaussian factorizations are unique only up to units and ordering.

  • Zero Norm: Occurs only at $z=0$.
  • Units: $1,-1,i,-i$.
  • Unit Criterion: $N(z)=1$.
  • Associates: Elements differing by multiplication by a unit.
  • Geometric Associates: Related by quarter-turn rotations around the origin.
  • Divisibility: $\alpha\mid\beta$ if $\beta=\alpha\gamma$ for some $\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}[i]$.
  • Norm Divisibility: $\alpha\mid\beta$ implies $N(\alpha)\mid N(\beta)$.

  • Euclidean Division: $\alpha=\beta q+r$ with $N(r)<N(\beta)$.
  • Euclidean Function: The norm.
  • Euclidean Domain: $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is Euclidean.
  • Principal Ideal Domain: Every ideal of $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is principal.
  • Unique Factorization Domain: Every nonzero nonunit factors uniquely into Gaussian primes up to associates and order.
  • Prime Factorization: $$ z=u\pi_1\cdots\pi_k. $$
  • GCD: Can be computed using the Euclidean algorithm.
  • GCD Uniqueness: Unique only up to multiplication by a unit.
  • Bézout Identity: A Gaussian GCD can be expressed as a Gaussian integer linear combination.
  • Lattice: $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is the square integer lattice in the complex plane.
  • Coordinate Form: $a+bi\leftrightarrow(a,b)$.
  • Norm Geometry: $a^2+b^2$ is squared distance from the origin.

  • General Two-Square Theorem: Prime factors congruent to $3$ modulo $4$ must have even exponent.
  • Factorization of $5$: $5=(2+i)(2-i)$.

  • Prime $3$: Remains prime in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$.

Their arithmetic explains why some ordinary primes split, why others remain prime, and why sums of two squares are naturally connected to factorization in the complex plane.
