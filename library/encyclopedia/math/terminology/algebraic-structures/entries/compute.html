Compute

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

In mathematics, compute means to determine a mathematical result by carrying out an appropriate sequence of operations, transformations, or algorithmic steps.

$$ \boxed{ \text{compute} = \text{obtain a result by mathematical procedure} }. $$

The word comes from Latin roots associated with reckoning or calculating together. Historically, computation was strongly associated with arithmetic calculation, but modern mathematics uses the word much more broadly.

One may compute:

  • a numerical value,
  • a derivative,
  • an integral,
  • a determinant,
  • a probability,
  • a matrix product,
  • a greatest common divisor,
  • an approximation,
  • or the output of an algorithm.

Thus compute does not mean merely "do arithmetic." It refers more generally to producing a specified mathematical object or value through an explicit process.

Operational Nuance

Computing a Numerical Value

A basic use of compute asks for the value of an arithmetic expression.

For example:

$$ 7+3(4). $$

Computing the expression gives:

$$ \boxed{ 7+3(4)=19 }. $$

Compute Usually Implies a Procedure

To compute something is generally to obtain it through a sequence of legitimate mathematical steps.

$$ \boxed{ \text{input} \longrightarrow \text{procedure} \longrightarrow \text{output} }. $$

The procedure may be mental arithmetic, symbolic manipulation, a formula, a numerical algorithm, or computer-assisted calculation.

Computing a Function Value

If:

$$ f(x)=x^2+3x-1, $$

then computing $f(4)$ means substituting $4$ for $x$ and carrying out the required operations:

$$ f(4) = 4^2+3(4)-1 = 16+12-1 = \boxed{27}. $$

Compute versus Evaluate

Evaluate often emphasizes determining the value of an expression or function for specified inputs.

Compute is broader and may ask for an object that is not merely a substituted numerical value.

$$ \boxed{ \text{evaluate} \subseteq \text{common computational tasks} } $$

For example, one may evaluate $f(3)$, but one may also compute the derivative $f'(x)$, which may remain symbolic.

Computing a Derivative

Given:

$$ f(x)=x^3-5x, $$

computing the derivative gives:

$$ \boxed{ f'(x)=3x^2-5 }. $$

The result is an expression rather than a single number.

Computing an Integral

For example:

$$ \int_0^2 x^2\,dx $$

may be computed exactly:

$$ \int_0^2x^2\,dx = \left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^2 = \boxed{\frac83}. $$

Computing a Matrix Product

If:

$$ A= \begin{pmatrix} 1&2\\ 3&4 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad B= \begin{pmatrix} 2&0\\ 1&5 \end{pmatrix}, $$

then computing $AB$ means carrying out matrix multiplication:

$$ AB = \begin{pmatrix} 4&10\\ 10&20 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Computing a Determinant

For:

$$ A= \begin{pmatrix} a&b\\ c&d \end{pmatrix}, $$

the determinant is computed as:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A)=ad-bc }. $$

Computing a Probability

Suppose a fair six-sided die is rolled and $E$ is the event of obtaining an even number.

Then:

$$ E=\{2,4,6\}. $$

Computing its probability gives:

$$ \boxed{ P(E)=\frac36=\frac12 }. $$

Exact Computation

An exact computation produces a mathematically exact result.

For example:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt{8}=2\sqrt2 } $$

is exact.

Approximate Computation

A computation may instead produce a numerical approximation:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt8\approx2.828427 }. $$

The symbols $=$ and $\approx$ should not be interchanged.

Numerical Computation

Numerical computation generally produces numerical approximations using arithmetic procedures or algorithms.

For example, Newton's method may compute successive approximations to a root:

$$ \boxed{ x_{n+1} = x_n-\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)} }. $$

Symbolic Computation

Symbolic computation manipulates mathematical expressions in symbolic form.

For example:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}(x^5+2x) = 5x^4+2 }. $$

No decimal approximation is required.

Computing by Formula

A known formula can serve as an algorithm for computation.

The area of a circle is computed from:

$$ \boxed{ A=\pi r^2 }. $$

If $r=5$, then:

$$ A=25\pi. $$

This may remain exact or be approximated numerically.

Computing by Algorithm

Some mathematical objects are obtained by systematic algorithms rather than one closed formula.

The Euclidean algorithm computes the greatest common divisor of two integers.

For example:

$$ \begin{aligned} 252&=2(105)+42,\\ 105&=2(42)+21,\\ 42&=2(21)+0. \end{aligned} $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \gcd(252,105)=21 }. $$

Finite Computation

A finite computation reaches its result after finitely many steps.

$$ \boxed{ \text{finite input} \to \text{finite sequence of steps} \to \text{output} }. $$

Iteration in Computation

Some computations repeatedly apply the same rule.

$$ \boxed{ x_{n+1}=F(x_n) }. $$

The process may stop after a chosen number of iterations or when a specified error tolerance is reached.

Computational Error

Approximate computation introduces questions of error.

If $x$ is exact and $\widetilde{x}$ is an approximation, the absolute error is:

$$ \boxed{ |x-\widetilde{x}| }. $$

When $x\ne0$, relative error may be measured by:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{|x-\widetilde{x}|}{|x|} }. $$

Rounding Error

Computers and calculators commonly represent real numbers with finite precision. Rounding can therefore affect computed results.

A displayed decimal should not automatically be interpreted as an exact value unless the representation is known to be exact.

Computational Complexity

In algorithms and computer science, it is not enough to ask whether something can be computed. One may also ask how much time or memory the computation requires.

This leads to measures such as:

$$ \boxed{ O(n),\quad O(n\log n),\quad O(n^2) }. $$

These expressions describe how resource requirements grow with input size under specified models.

Computable Objects

In mathematical logic and theoretical computer science, the adjective computable has a more technical meaning. Roughly, it identifies an object or function that can be generated by an effective algorithm under a formal model of computation.

This technical concept is related to, but more precise than, the everyday classroom instruction compute the value.

Compute versus Calculate

Calculate and compute frequently overlap in ordinary mathematical usage.

However, calculate often suggests numerical arithmetic, while compute comfortably extends to symbolic and algorithmic results.

$$ \boxed{ \text{calculate} \approx \text{compute} \quad\text{in many elementary contexts} }. $$

Compute versus Solve

To solve usually means determine values or objects satisfying a condition.

For example:

$$ x^2-9=0 $$

is solved by:

$$ \boxed{ x=\pm3 }. $$

One may perform computations while solving, but compute and solve identify different goals.

Compute versus Simplify

To simplify means rewrite an expression into an equivalent form that is simpler or more useful under the relevant conventions.

For example:

$$ \sqrt{50}=5\sqrt2 $$

is a simplification.

Computing $\sqrt{50}$ numerically might instead produce:

$$ \sqrt{50}\approx7.071. $$

Compute versus Prove

To prove a proposition is to establish logically that it must be true from accepted assumptions, definitions, and prior results.

Computation can provide evidence or intermediate steps but does not automatically constitute proof.

$$ \boxed{ \text{many successful examples} \ne \text{proof of a universal claim} }. $$

Compute versus Estimate

To estimate generally means determine an approximate magnitude or value, often with less precision than a formal numerical computation.

For example:

$$ \pi\approx3.14 $$

may be used in an estimate, whereas a computation might retain more digits or use $\pi$ exactly.

Compute versus Derive

To derive a formula emphasizes establishing how it follows from other relationships. To compute using that formula emphasizes applying it to obtain a result.

For example, one may first derive:

$$ A=\pi r^2 $$

and later use it to compute the area of a particular circle.

Manual Computation

A result may be computed manually using written or mental mathematical procedures. The word does not imply the use of a machine.

Calculator-Assisted Computation

A calculator may perform numerical computation, but the mathematical interpretation of the input and output remains essential.

For example, entering:

$$ \sin^{-1}(0.5) $$

may produce different displayed numbers depending on whether the calculator is in degree or radian mode.

Computer-Assisted Computation

Computer algebra systems and numerical software can compute symbolic expressions, large matrix operations, numerical approximations, statistical quantities, and many other mathematical results.

Machine output still depends on assumptions, input syntax, algorithms, precision, and domain conventions.

Computation Does Not Replace Interpretation

A numerical output may be computationally correct while still being inappropriate as an answer if units, domain restrictions, or physical meaning have been ignored.

$$ \boxed{ \text{computed value} + \text{mathematical interpretation} = \text{usable result} }. $$

Units in Computation

Quantities with units must be computed dimensionally as well as numerically.

For example:

$$ 60\frac{\text{mi}}{\text{h}}\cdot2\text{ h} = \boxed{120\text{ mi}}. $$

The units are part of the mathematical result.

Domain Restrictions in Computation

A computation must respect the domain of the expressions involved.

Over the real numbers:

$$ \sqrt{-4} $$

is not a real-valued computation. Over the complex numbers:

$$ \boxed{ \sqrt{-4}=2i } $$

when the principal square root is intended.

Order of Operations in Computation

A symbolic expression must be parsed according to its grouping and operation conventions before it can be computed correctly.

For example:

$$ 2+3\cdot4 $$

gives:

$$ \boxed{ 14 }, $$

not $20$, because multiplication is performed before addition under the conventional order of operations.

Common Misconception: Compute Always Means Give a Decimal

A computed answer may be exact and symbolic:

$$ \boxed{ \int_0^1x^2\,dx=\frac13 }. $$

Converting to $0.333\ldots$ is unnecessary unless approximation is requested.

Common Misconception: Compute Means Show No Work

The verb identifies the requested mathematical goal, not the expected amount of explanation. In instructional settings, a computation may need to show enough steps to demonstrate method, reasoning, or error checking.

Common Misconception: Compute and Solve Are Synonyms

A problem may ask:

$$ \text{Compute }\det(A) $$

without asking for any unknown to be solved. Conversely, solving an equation may require more than simply evaluating an expression.

Common Misconception: A Computer Makes a Result Mathematically Certain

Computer output can be affected by incorrect input, finite precision, unsuitable algorithms, overflow, rounding, or misinterpreted assumptions.

Computational tools increase capability but do not eliminate the need for mathematical verification.

Common Misconception: More Digits Mean More Accuracy

Displaying many decimal digits does not guarantee that all are meaningful. Accuracy depends on the underlying data, algorithm, precision, and error propagation.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Written Instruction or Notation Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\text{Compute }7+3(4)$ "Compute seven plus three times four."
$\text{Compute }f(4)$ "Compute f of four."
$\text{Compute }\det(A)$ "Compute the determinant of A."
$\text{Compute }\int_0^2x^2\,dx$ "Compute the integral from zero to two of x squared with respect to x."
$\text{Compute }AB$ "Compute the matrix product A B."
$x\approx2.718$ "x is approximately two point seven one eight."

The instruction compute ordinarily expects a result rather than merely a restatement of a formula. The amount of supporting work expected depends on context. In a proof-oriented course, the computational steps may need justification; in a calculator exercise, the principal goal may be numerical output.

Phrases such as compute exactly, compute numerically, compute to three decimal places, or compute symbolically narrow the type of result requested.

Defense Notation Advice: Preserve exact forms unless an approximation is requested or useful. Use $=$ only for exact equality and $\approx$ for approximation. State units when the quantity has units, respect domain restrictions, and distinguish instructions to compute from instructions to solve, simplify, estimate, derive, or prove. When using software, identify relevant precision or algorithmic assumptions if they materially affect the result.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Meaning: To compute is to obtain a mathematical result by carrying out an appropriate procedure.
  • Arithmetic: Compute may mean perform numerical operations.
  • Functions: Compute $f(a)$ means determine the output corresponding to input $a$.
  • Algebra: One may compute products, powers, remainders, coefficients, or other symbolic quantities.
  • Calculus: Derivatives, integrals, limits, and numerical approximations may be computed.
  • Linear Algebra: Matrix products, determinants, inverses, ranks, eigenvalues, and related objects may be computed.
  • Number Theory: Algorithms may compute greatest common divisors, modular inverses, factorizations, and other arithmetic objects.
  • Probability: Probabilities, expectations, variances, and distributions may be computed.
  • Statistics: Sample statistics, estimates, test statistics, and fitted quantities may be computed from data.
  • Exact Computation: Produces an exact symbolic or numerical result.
  • Approximate Computation: Produces a numerical approximation with finite precision.
  • Symbolic Computation: Manipulates expressions rather than only decimal values.
  • Numerical Computation: Uses numerical algorithms to approximate quantities.
  • Algorithmic View: Computation maps inputs to outputs through prescribed executable steps.
  • Computer Science: Computation may refer broadly to algorithmic information processing.
  • Computability Theory: Computable has a formal meaning tied to effective procedures and models of computation.
  • Complexity Theory: One studies not only whether a result can be computed, but how many resources the computation requires.
  • Calculate Contrast: Calculate often overlaps with compute, especially for numerical tasks.
  • Evaluate Contrast: Evaluate usually emphasizes finding the value of an expression or function at specified inputs.
  • Solve Contrast: Solve seeks objects satisfying an equation, inequality, system, or other condition.
  • Simplify Contrast: Simplify seeks a more useful equivalent form, not necessarily a numerical value.
  • Estimate Contrast: Estimate seeks an approximate value or magnitude.
  • Derive Contrast: Derive emphasizes obtaining a formula or relation from prior principles.
  • Prove Contrast: Prove establishes logical necessity; computation alone does not ordinarily prove a general proposition.
  • Common Error: Do not assume a computed result must be decimal.
  • Common Error: Do not replace exact equality with approximate equality or vice versa.
  • Common Error: Do not ignore units or domain restrictions during computation.
  • Common Error: Do not assume calculator or computer output is automatically exact.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse computational evidence with mathematical proof.
  • Terminological Contrast: Compute means obtain a mathematical result through an appropriate procedure. Calculate often emphasizes numerical arithmetic and frequently overlaps with compute. Evaluate usually means determine the value of a specified expression or function. Solve means determine values or objects satisfying stated conditions. Simplify means rewrite an expression in an equivalent but more useful form. Estimate means obtain an approximate value. Derive emphasizes establishing a relation from prior principles. Prove establishes a proposition by logically valid reasoning.
