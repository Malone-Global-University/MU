Explicit Formula of a Sequence

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

An explicit formula of a sequence gives the value of a sequence term directly from its index, without requiring the preceding terms to be computed first.

A sequence is commonly written:

$$ \boxed{ a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots }. $$

An explicit formula has the general form:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=f(n) }, $$

where $n$ is the index and $f$ is a rule that determines the corresponding term directly.

For example, the sequence:

$$ 3,5,7,9,11,\ldots $$

has the explicit formula:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=2n+1, \qquad n\geq1 }. $$

Substituting $n=50$ immediately gives:

$$ a_{50}=2(50)+1=\boxed{101}. $$

The adjective explicit emphasizes that the requested term is expressed directly in terms of the index. This contrasts with a recursive formula, which defines terms using one or more preceding terms.

Operational Nuance

General Form

The simplest conceptual form is:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=f(n) }. $$

The index $n$ identifies which term is requested, and the formula produces that term directly.

Sequence as a Function

A sequence can be viewed as a function whose domain is a set of consecutive integers, often:

$$ \boxed{ \{1,2,3,\ldots\} } $$

or:

$$ \boxed{ \{0,1,2,3,\ldots\} }. $$

Thus:

$$ n\longmapsto a_n $$

behaves like ordinary function notation with a discrete input domain.

Indexing Convention Matters

The same sequence pattern may have different explicit formulas depending on whether indexing begins at $0$ or $1$.

For example:

$$ 2,4,6,8,\ldots $$

indexed beginning at $n=1$ has:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=2n }. $$

If the same displayed terms are indexed beginning at $n=0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=2n+2 }. $$

Arithmetic Sequence

An arithmetic sequence has a constant difference $d$:

$$ a_{n+1}-a_n=d. $$

If the first term is $a_1$, then its explicit formula is:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d }. $$

Arithmetic Example

For:

$$ 7,11,15,19,\ldots, $$

we have:

$$ a_1=7, \qquad d=4. $$

Therefore:

$$ a_n = 7+4(n-1) = \boxed{4n+3}. $$

Checking the Formula

For $n=1$:

$$ a_1=4(1)+3=7. $$

For $n=2$:

$$ a_2=4(2)+3=11. $$

These match the sequence.

Arithmetic Formula with a Different Starting Index

If an arithmetic sequence begins at index $n=n_0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=a_{n_0}+(n-n_0)d }. $$

This form prevents indexing errors when the sequence does not begin with $a_1$.

Geometric Sequence

A geometric sequence has a constant ratio $r$:

$$ \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}=r $$

where the ratio is defined.

If the sequence begins with $a_1$, then:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=a_1r^{\,n-1} }. $$

Geometric Example

For:

$$ 5,15,45,135,\ldots, $$

the first term is $5$ and the common ratio is $3$.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=5\cdot3^{n-1} }. $$

Zero-Based Geometric Formula

If a geometric sequence begins with $a_0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=a_0r^n }. $$

Explicit vs. Recursive Formula

An explicit formula directly computes $a_n$:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=3n+2 }. $$

A recursive description of the same arithmetic sequence could be:

$$ \boxed{ a_1=5, \qquad a_n=a_{n-1}+3 \quad(n\geq2) }. $$

Computational Difference

To compute $a_{100}$ explicitly, substitute:

$$ a_{100}=3(100)+2=302. $$

A recursive calculation, unless simplified or optimized, would conceptually move through preceding terms.

Explicit Does Not Mean Simple

An explicit formula may be complicated.

For example:

$$ \boxed{ a_n = \frac{n^3-2n+5}{n^2+1} }. $$

It is still explicit because $a_n$ is determined directly from $n$.

Polynomial Sequences

A sequence may have an explicit polynomial formula such as:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=n^2+n+1 }. $$

This produces:

$$ 3,7,13,21,31,\ldots $$

for $n=1,2,3,4,5,\ldots$.

Finite Differences

Polynomial explicit formulas can often be detected using finite differences.

A degree-$m$ polynomial sequence has constant $m$th finite differences when sampled at consecutive integer indices.

Quadratic Sequence Example

Consider:

$$ 1,4,9,16,25,\ldots. $$

The explicit formula is:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=n^2 }. $$

Alternating Sequence

Powers of $-1$ are often used in explicit formulas to alternate signs.

For:

$$ -1,1,-1,1,-1,\ldots, $$

beginning at $n=1$:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=(-1)^n }. $$

Alternating Magnitudes

The sequence:

$$ -1,2,-3,4,-5,\ldots $$

has:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=(-1)^n n }. $$

Piecewise Explicit Formula

An explicit formula need not consist of a single algebraic expression. A sequence can be defined explicitly by cases:

$$ \boxed{ a_n= \begin{cases} n, & n\text{ even},\\ -n, & n\text{ odd}. \end{cases} } $$

Each term is still determined directly from $n$.

Floor and Ceiling Functions

Explicit sequence formulas may use discrete functions such as floor and ceiling:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=\left\lfloor\frac n2\right\rfloor }. $$

Absolute Value

An explicit sequence formula may also use:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=|n-5| }. $$

Trigonometric Explicit Formulas

Sequences can be defined with trigonometric functions:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=\sin\left(\frac{n\pi}{2}\right) }. $$

This generates a periodic sequence.

Exponential Explicit Formula

Exponential sequences have formulas such as:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=2^n }. $$

Factorial Explicit Formula

The sequence:

$$ 1,2,6,24,120,\ldots $$

can be represented:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=n! }. $$

Closed Form

The terms explicit formula and closed form often overlap, but they are not perfectly synonymous.

An explicit formula merely requires direct dependence on the index rather than preceding sequence terms. Closed form usually additionally suggests an expression built from a specified collection of familiar operations or functions without an unevaluated recurrence, sum, or limiting process.

Explicit Formula with Sigma Notation

Depending on convention, a formula such as:

$$ a_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}k $$

may be regarded as explicit because it depends directly on $n$, though it may not be considered a closed form under a stricter convention.

It can be rewritten:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=\frac{n(n+1)}2 }. $$

Fibonacci Sequence

The Fibonacci sequence is naturally recursive:

$$ F_n=F_{n-1}+F_{n-2}. $$

Yet it also has an explicit formula known as Binet's formula:

$$ \boxed{ F_n = \frac{\varphi^n-\psi^n}{\sqrt5} } $$

where:

$$ \varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2}, \qquad \psi=\frac{1-\sqrt5}{2}, $$

under the convention $F_0=0$ and $F_1=1$.

A Sequence Can Have Multiple Explicit Formulas

Different-looking formulas may define the same sequence.

For example:

$$ a_n=2n+2 $$

and:

$$ a_n=2(n+1) $$

are equivalent explicit formulas.

The Starting Index Is Part of the Definition

The formula:

$$ a_n=\frac1n $$

cannot use $n=0$ because division by zero is undefined.

A proper specification is:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=\frac1n, \qquad n=1,2,3,\ldots }. $$

Explicit Formula for a Finite Sequence

Finite sequences can also have explicit formulas, provided the permitted index set is stated.

$$ \boxed{ a_n=n^2, \qquad 1\leq n\leq10 }. $$

Explicit Formula and Graphs

Since a sequence has a discrete domain, the graph of an explicit sequence consists of isolated points:

$$ \boxed{ (n,a_n) }. $$

Connecting these points with a continuous curve can be useful visually but may suggest values that are not actually sequence terms.

Finding an Explicit Formula from Data

Common clues include:

  • constant first differences for arithmetic sequences,
  • constant ratios for geometric sequences,
  • constant higher finite differences for polynomial sequences,
  • alternating signs suggesting powers of $-1$,
  • periodic values suggesting modular or trigonometric rules, and
  • known combinatorial or number-theoretic patterns.

Pattern Recognition Does Not Guarantee Uniqueness

A finite list of terms does not uniquely determine an infinite sequence. Infinitely many different explicit formulas can agree with any finite set of listed values and diverge later.

Therefore an explicit formula inferred from a finite pattern should be understood as a plausible rule unless additional structure or conditions make it uniquely determined.

Verification

A proposed explicit formula should be checked against:

  • the stated starting index,
  • several known terms,
  • the structural pattern of the sequence, and
  • any recurrence or conditions the sequence is known to satisfy.

Explicit Formula from a Recurrence

Converting a recurrence into an explicit formula may require algebraic, matrix, generating-function, characteristic-equation, or other methods.

First-Order Linear Recurrence Example

Suppose:

$$ a_1=4, \qquad a_n=a_{n-1}+3. $$

Repeated addition gives:

$$ a_n = 4+3(n-1), $$

hence:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=3n+1 }. $$

Geometric Recurrence Example

If:

$$ a_1=7, \qquad a_n=2a_{n-1}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=7\cdot2^{n-1} }. $$

Term Number vs. Term Value

In:

$$ a_n=4n-1, $$

$n$ is the term number or index, while $a_n$ is the corresponding term value.

Subscript Is Not Multiplication

The notation:

$$ a_n $$

does not mean:

$$ a\cdot n. $$

The subscript identifies which sequence term is being referenced.

Explicit Formula vs. General Term

In elementary sequence terminology, the general term, $n$th-term formula, and explicit formula often refer to essentially the same object:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=f(n) }. $$

Explicit Formula vs. Sequence Sum

The explicit formula for the terms must not be confused with a formula for the partial sums:

$$ S_n=a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n. $$

The term formula $a_n$ and the partial-sum formula $S_n$ describe different sequences.

Recovering Terms from Partial Sums

If $S_n$ is known, then:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=S_n-S_{n-1} } $$

for appropriate $n$.

Applications

Explicit formulas are useful whenever direct access to a particular indexed quantity is valuable, including:

  • financial growth schedules,
  • population models,
  • algorithmic iteration counts,
  • discrete physical models,
  • combinatorial sequences,
  • number-theoretic patterns, and
  • computer-generated data.

Common Misconception: An explicit formula is not merely a formula that displays several terms. It must determine the $n$th term directly from the index. Also, the starting index matters: formulas such as $a_n=a_1+(n-1)d$ assume that the first listed term is indexed by $1$.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$a_n=f(n)$ "A sub n equals f of n."
$a_n=a_1+(n-1)d$ "The nth term equals the first term plus n minus one times the common difference."
$a_n=a_1r^{n-1}$ "The nth term equals the first term times the common ratio raised to the n minus one."
$a_n=(-1)^n n$ "A sub n equals negative one to the nth power times n."
$a_n=n^2$ "The nth term is n squared."
$n=1,2,3,\ldots$ "N ranges over the positive integers beginning with one."
$a_{50}=f(50)$ "The fiftieth term is obtained by evaluating the explicit formula at fifty."

Common synonymous instructions include:

$$ \boxed{ \text{"find an explicit formula"} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{"find the general term"} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \text{"write an }n\text{th-term formula"} }. $$

In elementary sequence work, these usually ask for a rule of the form:

$$ \boxed{ a_n=f(n) }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Always state or infer the starting index before constructing an explicit formula. In particular:

$$ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d $$

and:

$$ a_n=a_0+nd $$

can describe the same arithmetic pattern under different indexing conventions. The difference is not cosmetic; it changes which term corresponds to each integer index.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Definition: An explicit formula gives a sequence term directly from its index.
  • General Form: $$ \boxed{ a_n=f(n) }. $$
  • Index: $n$ identifies the position of a term.
  • Term Value: $a_n$ is the value assigned to index $n$.
  • Function Interpretation: A sequence is a function with a discrete integer domain.
  • Common Domain: $$ \{1,2,3,\ldots\}. $$
  • Alternative Domain: $$ \{0,1,2,3,\ldots\}. $$
  • Indexing Caution: Starting at $0$ instead of $1$ generally changes the explicit formula.
  • Arithmetic Sequence: $$ \boxed{ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d }. $$
  • Arithmetic Difference: $$ \boxed{ a_{n+1}-a_n=d }. $$
  • General Starting Index: $$ \boxed{ a_n=a_{n_0}+(n-n_0)d }. $$
  • Geometric Sequence: $$ \boxed{ a_n=a_1r^{n-1} }. $$
  • Zero-Based Geometric Sequence: $$ \boxed{ a_n=a_0r^n }. $$
  • Geometric Ratio: $$ a_{n+1}=ra_n. $$
  • Direct Calculation: An explicit formula can compute a distant term without listing all preceding terms.
  • Recursive Contrast: A recursive formula defines terms using earlier terms.
  • Recursive Example: $$ a_n=a_{n-1}+d. $$
  • Explicit Arithmetic Equivalent: $$ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d. $$
  • Recursive Geometric Example: $$ a_n=ra_{n-1}. $$
  • Explicit Geometric Equivalent: $$ a_n=a_1r^{n-1}. $$
  • Polynomial Sequence: An explicit formula may be polynomial in $n$.
  • Quadratic Example: $$ \boxed{ a_n=n^2 }. $$
  • Cubic Example: $$ \boxed{ a_n=n^3 }. $$
  • Finite Differences: Constant higher differences can signal a polynomial explicit formula.
  • Alternating Signs: Powers of $-1$ commonly encode alternating behavior.
  • Alternating Example: $$ \boxed{ a_n=(-1)^n }. $$
  • Alternating Magnitude: $$ \boxed{ a_n=(-1)^nn }. $$
  • Periodic Sequence: Explicit formulas may involve modular arithmetic or trigonometric functions.
  • Trigonometric Example: $$ a_n=\sin(n\pi/2). $$
  • Exponential Sequence: $$ a_n=b^n $$ is explicit.
  • Factorial Sequence: $$ a_n=n! $$ is explicit.
  • Rational Formula: $$ a_n=\frac{n}{n+1} $$ is explicit.
  • Piecewise Formula: Explicit sequence definitions may use cases.
  • Floor Function: Floor and ceiling functions can appear in explicit formulas.
  • Absolute Value: Absolute-value formulas can encode symmetric discrete patterns.
  • Finite Sequence: Explicit formulas also apply to sequences with finite index sets.
  • Domain Restriction: The formula should be accompanied by the allowed values of $n$ when they are not obvious.
  • Division Restriction: If the formula contains a denominator, index values making it zero must be excluded.
  • Square-Root Restriction: Real-valued sequence formulas involving radicals must satisfy the relevant domain restrictions.
  • Logarithmic Restriction: Real logarithmic formulas require positive arguments.
  • General Term: Often synonymous with explicit formula in elementary sequence contexts.
  • $n$th-Term Formula: Another common name for an explicit sequence formula.
  • Closed Form: Often overlaps with explicit formula but may impose a stricter expectation about the types of operations allowed.
  • Explicit vs. Closed Form: An explicit finite sum may qualify as explicit while some authors would not call it a closed form.
  • Sigma Example: $$ a_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}k $$ depends directly on $n$.
  • Closed-Form Equivalent: $$ \boxed{ a_n=\frac{n(n+1)}2 }. $$
  • Fibonacci Sequence: A naturally recursive sequence can also possess an explicit formula.
  • Binet's Formula: Provides a classical explicit expression for Fibonacci numbers.
  • Multiple Representations: A sequence can have more than one equivalent explicit formula.
  • Formula Equivalence: Algebraically equivalent expressions define the same sequence when used on the same index domain.
  • Discrete Graph: Sequence graphs consist of points $(n,a_n)$ at permitted integer indices.
  • Graphing Caution: Connecting sequence points can visually imply nonsequence values.
  • Tables: An explicit formula can generate a sequence table efficiently.
  • Pattern Recognition: Tables and differences often help identify candidate formulas.
  • Finite-Data Ambiguity: A finite number of sequence terms never uniquely determines an arbitrary infinite continuation without further assumptions.
  • Verification: Test a proposed formula against known sequence terms.
  • First-Term Check: Verify that the formula produces the stated first term at the stated first index.
  • Pattern Check: Confirm that differences, ratios, or other defining structure agree with the proposed formula.
  • Recurrence Check: If a recurrence is given, substitute the explicit formula into it to verify compatibility.
  • Initial-Condition Check: A recurrence solution must also satisfy its stated initial conditions.
  • Sequence vs. Series: A sequence is an ordered list of terms; a series is a sum of sequence terms.
  • Term Formula: $$ a_n $$ describes individual terms.
  • Partial-Sum Formula: $$ S_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k $$ describes accumulated sums.
  • Recovering a Term from Sums: $$ \boxed{ a_n=S_n-S_{n-1} }. $$
  • Index vs. Multiplication: $a_n$ is subscript notation and does not mean $a\cdot n$.
  • Index Variable: Letters other than $n$ may be used, such as $a_k$, $u_m$, or $x_j$.
  • Programming: An explicit formula allows direct indexed computation without necessarily iterating through every earlier term.
  • Algorithmic Complexity: Direct formulas can be computationally advantageous over naive recurrence evaluation for distant terms.
  • Modeling: Explicit formulas express discrete quantities directly as functions of time step, position, generation, or another integer index.
  • Finance: Compound-growth schedules often produce geometric explicit formulas.
  • Population Models: Constant proportional growth across discrete time steps yields geometric sequences.
  • Combinatorics: Explicit sequence formulas may count arrangements or selections of size $n$.
  • Number Theory: Explicit formulas can describe figurate numbers, powers, divisor-related sequences, and many other integer patterns.
  • Triangular Numbers: $$ \boxed{ T_n=\frac{n(n+1)}2 }. $$
  • Square Numbers: $$ \boxed{ a_n=n^2 }. $$
  • Odd Positive Integers: $$ \boxed{ a_n=2n-1 } $$ for $n\geq1$.
  • Even Positive Integers: $$ \boxed{ a_n=2n } $$ for $n\geq1$.
  • Index-Shift Principle: Replacing $n$ with $n-c$ or $n+c$ can adjust a formula to a different starting index.
  • Arithmetic Index Shift: The factor $n-1$ in $a_1+(n-1)d$ counts how many common-difference steps separate $a_n$ from $a_1$.
  • Geometric Index Shift: The exponent $n-1$ in $a_1r^{n-1}$ counts how many multiplications by $r$ separate $a_n$ from $a_1$.
  • Core Interpretation: An explicit formula of a sequence turns the term index directly into the term value: $$ \boxed{ a_n=f(n) }. $$ Unlike a recursive rule, it does not require earlier terms to define the requested term. Arithmetic, geometric, polynomial, exponential, alternating, piecewise, and many other sequences can be represented explicitly, provided the formula and its index domain are stated correctly.
