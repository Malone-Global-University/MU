Consistent System of Equations

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

A consistent system of equations is a system for which at least one solution satisfies every equation simultaneously.

$$ \boxed{ \text{consistent system} \iff \text{at least one common solution exists} }. $$

The adjective consistent carries its ordinary sense of being mutually compatible. The equations do not impose contradictory requirements on the unknowns.

Because a system is interpreted conjunctively, a solution must satisfy equation one and equation two and every additional equation in the system.

$$ \boxed{ E_1\land E_2\land\cdots\land E_n } $$

A consistent system therefore has a nonempty common solution set.

$$ \boxed{ S\ne\varnothing }. $$

Operational Nuance

System of Equations

A system of equations is a collection of equations involving common unknowns that are required to hold simultaneously.

For example:

$$ \begin{cases} x+y=5,\\ x-y=1. \end{cases} $$

A solution must satisfy both equations.

Solution of a System

Solving the preceding system gives:

$$ x=3,\qquad y=2. $$

Checking:

$$ 3+2=5 $$

and:

$$ 3-2=1. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ (3,2) } $$

is a common solution and the system is consistent.

Basic Classification

A system of equations can have:

  • exactly one solution,
  • more than one solution, including infinitely many solutions, or
  • no solution.

The first two possibilities are consistent. The third is inconsistent.

Number of Solutions Classification
Exactly one Consistent
More than one Consistent
None Inconsistent

Consistent Independent System

In common elementary linear-algebra terminology, a linear system having exactly one solution is called consistent and independent.

$$ \boxed{ \text{one solution} \Rightarrow \text{consistent independent} } $$

Consistent Dependent System

A linear system having infinitely many solutions is commonly called consistent and dependent.

$$ \boxed{ \text{infinitely many solutions} \Rightarrow \text{consistent dependent} }. $$

This terminology is especially common in elementary treatments of two linear equations. In more advanced linear algebra, dependence is more precisely attributed to rows, equations, or vectors rather than used as the primary classification of an entire system.

Graphical Interpretation in Two Variables

Each linear equation in two variables represents a line. A common solution is a point belonging to every line in the system.

$$ \boxed{ \text{solution} = \text{common intersection point} } $$

One Intersection Point

Two distinct nonparallel lines meet once. Therefore the corresponding system has one solution and is consistent.

$$ \boxed{ \text{intersecting lines} \Rightarrow \text{one solution} }. $$

Coincident Lines

If two equations describe the same line, every point on that line satisfies both equations.

$$ \boxed{ \text{same line} \Rightarrow \text{infinitely many solutions} }. $$

The system is therefore consistent.

Parallel Distinct Lines

Distinct parallel lines have no common point.

$$ \boxed{ \text{distinct parallel lines} \Rightarrow \text{no solution} \Rightarrow \text{inconsistent} }. $$

Slope-Intercept Classification

Consider:

$$ y=m_1x+b_1 $$

and:

$$ y=m_2x+b_2. $$

Then:

Condition Geometric Relationship Solution Classification
$m_1\ne m_2$ Intersecting lines One solution; consistent
$m_1=m_2,\ b_1=b_2$ Same line Infinitely many solutions; consistent
$m_1=m_2,\ b_1\ne b_2$ Distinct parallel lines No solution; inconsistent

Example with One Solution

Consider:

$$ \begin{cases} y=2x+1,\\ y=-x+7. \end{cases} $$

Setting the expressions for $y$ equal:

$$ 2x+1=-x+7. $$

Then:

$$ 3x=6,\qquad x=2. $$

Substituting:

$$ y=5. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ (2,5) } $$

is the unique solution.

Example with Infinitely Many Solutions

Consider:

$$ \begin{cases} 2x+4y=8,\\ x+2y=4. \end{cases} $$

The first equation is exactly twice the second:

$$ 2(x+2y=4) \Rightarrow 2x+4y=8. $$

They describe the same line, so:

$$ \boxed{ \text{infinitely many solutions} }. $$

The system is consistent and dependent in elementary terminology.

Example of an Inconsistent System

For contrast:

$$ \begin{cases} x+2y=4,\\ 2x+4y=10. \end{cases} $$

Doubling the first equation would give:

$$ 2x+4y=8, $$

not:

$$ 2x+4y=10. $$

The equations therefore impose incompatible conditions.

Substitution Method

In substitution, one equation is solved for one variable and the resulting expression is substituted into another equation.

$$ \boxed{ \text{solve} \rightarrow \text{substitute} \rightarrow \text{solve remaining variable} }. $$

What Substitution Can Reveal

Substitution may lead to:

  • a specific variable value, indicating a unique solution in a typical two-variable linear system,
  • a true identity such as $0=0$, indicating redundant information and potentially infinitely many solutions, or
  • a contradiction such as $0=5$, proving inconsistency.

Elimination Method

Elimination combines equations so that one variable cancels.

For:

$$ \begin{cases} x+y=5,\\ x-y=1, \end{cases} $$

adding the equations gives:

$$ 2x=6, $$

hence:

$$ x=3. $$

This leads to the unique solution $(3,2)$.

Elimination to an Identity

For equivalent equations, elimination may produce:

$$ \boxed{0=0}. $$

This does not by itself mean that every ordered pair is a solution. It means one equation supplied no new restriction after combination. The remaining equations still determine the solution set.

Elimination to a Contradiction

An inconsistent linear system may reduce to:

$$ \boxed{0=c,\qquad c\ne0}. $$

For example:

$$ 0=2. $$

Since no values of the variables can make this true, the original system has no solution.

Augmented Matrix Representation

A linear system:

$$ \begin{cases} a_{11}x_1+\cdots+a_{1n}x_n=b_1,\\ \vdots\\ a_{m1}x_1+\cdots+a_{mn}x_n=b_m \end{cases} $$

can be represented by the augmented matrix:

$$ \boxed{ [A\mid\mathbf b] }. $$

Row Operations Preserve the Solution Set

The elementary row operations are:

  • interchanging two rows,
  • multiplying a row by a nonzero scalar, and
  • adding a multiple of one row to another.

These operations produce an equivalent system with the same solution set.

Row-Echelon Evidence of Inconsistency

A row of the form:

$$ \boxed{ [\,0\quad0\quad\cdots\quad0\mid c\,], \qquad c\ne0 } $$

represents:

$$ 0=c, $$

and therefore proves the system inconsistent.

Row-Echelon Evidence of Consistency

If row reduction produces no contradictory row of the form:

$$ [\,0\ \cdots\ 0\mid c\,], \qquad c\ne0, $$

then a linear system is consistent.

Pivot Variables

In row-echelon form, a variable corresponding to a pivot column is a pivot variable or basic variable.

Free Variables

Variables whose columns contain no pivot are free variables. In a consistent linear system, free variables generate families of solutions.

$$ \boxed{ \text{consistent} + \text{at least one free variable} \Rightarrow \text{infinitely many solutions} }. $$

Unique Solution in Row-Reduced Form

For a system with $n$ unknowns, a unique solution occurs when the system is consistent and every variable is a pivot variable.

Rank Criterion

For a linear system:

$$ A\mathbf x=\mathbf b, $$

the system is consistent exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{rank}(A) = \operatorname{rank}([A\mid\mathbf b]) }. $$

This is the consistency criterion appearing in the Rouché-Capelli theorem.

Rank and Unique Solutions

If there are $n$ unknowns, then:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{rank}(A) = \operatorname{rank}([A\mid\mathbf b]) = n } $$

gives a unique solution.

Rank and Infinitely Many Solutions

If:

$$ \operatorname{rank}(A) = \operatorname{rank}([A\mid\mathbf b])

then the system is consistent but contains free variables and therefore has infinitely many solutions.

Rank and Inconsistency

If:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{rank}(A) < \operatorname{rank}([A\mid\mathbf b]) } $$

then the system is inconsistent.

Square Systems and Determinants

For a square system:

$$ A\mathbf x=\mathbf b, $$

if:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A)\ne0 } $$

then $A$ is invertible and the system has the unique solution:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf x=A^{-1}\mathbf b }. $$

Thus such a system is necessarily consistent.

Zero Determinant Does Not Automatically Mean Inconsistent

If:

$$ \det(A)=0, $$

the coefficient matrix is singular. The system may then have either infinitely many solutions or no solutions.

$$ \boxed{ \det(A)=0 \not\Rightarrow \text{inconsistent} }. $$

Cramer's Rule

When $A$ is square and:

$$ \det(A)\ne0, $$

Cramer's Rule gives:

$$ \boxed{ x_i = \frac{\det(A_i)}{\det(A)} }. $$

The nonzero determinant guarantees a unique consistent solution.

Homogeneous Systems

A homogeneous linear system has form:

$$ \boxed{ A\mathbf x=\mathbf0 }. $$

It is always consistent because:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf x=\mathbf0 } $$

is always a solution.

Trivial Solution

The zero vector solution:

$$ \mathbf x=\mathbf0 $$

is called the trivial solution of a homogeneous system.

Nontrivial Solutions

A homogeneous system has nontrivial solutions when its null space contains vectors other than $\mathbf0$.

For an $n$-variable homogeneous system:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{rank}(A)

Null Space

The complete solution set of:

$$ A\mathbf x=\mathbf0 $$

is the null space:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Null}(A) = \{\mathbf x:A\mathbf x=\mathbf0\} }. $$

Column-Space Interpretation

For:

$$ A\mathbf x=\mathbf b, $$

a solution exists exactly when $\mathbf b$ lies in the column space of $A$.

$$ \boxed{ A\mathbf x=\mathbf b \text{ is consistent} \iff \mathbf b\in\operatorname{Col}(A) }. $$

Linear-Combination Interpretation

If the columns of $A$ are $\mathbf a_1,\ldots,\mathbf a_n$, then:

$$ A\mathbf x = x_1\mathbf a_1+\cdots+x_n\mathbf a_n. $$

Thus consistency means:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf b \text{ can be expressed as a linear combination of the columns of }A }. $$

Geometric Interpretation in Three Variables

A linear equation in three variables typically represents a plane. A system is consistent whenever all relevant planes share at least one common point.

Three Planes with One Common Point

Three planes may intersect at a single point. The corresponding system has a unique solution.

Three Planes Sharing a Line

Several planes may share a common line. Every point on that line satisfies the system, giving infinitely many solutions.

Coincident Planes

If some equations represent the same plane, those equations may be redundant. The system can still be consistent.

Pairwise Intersection Does Not Guarantee Common Intersection

In three dimensions, planes can intersect pairwise without all sharing one common point. Therefore consistency concerns the intersection of all equations simultaneously.

$$ \boxed{ S = S_1\cap S_2\cap\cdots\cap S_m }. $$

Set-Theoretic Interpretation

Let $S_i$ be the solution set of equation $E_i$. Then the system solution set is:

$$ \boxed{ S = \bigcap_{i=1}^{m}S_i }. $$

The system is consistent exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ \bigcap_{i=1}^{m}S_i\ne\varnothing }. $$

Logical Interpretation

If each equation is viewed as a predicate $E_i(\mathbf x)$, then consistency means:

$$ \boxed{ \exists\mathbf x\, \big[ E_1(\mathbf x) \land E_2(\mathbf x) \land\cdots\land E_m(\mathbf x) \big] }. $$

Thus consistency can be expressed as an existential claim about a conjunction of conditions.

Redundant Equations

A system can contain equations that add no new restrictions.

For example:

$$ \begin{cases} x+y=4,\\ 2x+2y=8. \end{cases} $$

The second equation is redundant because it follows from the first.

Redundancy Does Not Mean Inconsistency

Redundant equations may produce infinitely many solutions relative to the number of variables, but they do not create a contradiction.

Adding an Equation Can Change Consistency

A consistent system may become inconsistent when a new incompatible equation is added.

$$ \boxed{ \text{additional constraint} \Rightarrow \text{solution set can shrink} }. $$

Since:

$$ S_{\text{new}} = S_{\text{old}}\cap S_{\text{additional}}, $$

the new intersection may become empty.

Removing an Equation

Removing a constraint cannot eliminate an existing solution, though it can enlarge the solution set.

Parameters and Consistency

Some systems are consistent only for particular parameter values.

Consider:

$$ \begin{cases} x+y=2,\\ 2x+2y=k. \end{cases} $$

Doubling the first equation gives:

$$ 2x+2y=4. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ k=4 \Rightarrow \text{consistent with infinitely many solutions} } $$

while:

$$ \boxed{ k\ne4 \Rightarrow \text{inconsistent} }. $$

Nonlinear Systems

The term consistent is not restricted to linear systems. Any system of equations is consistent if at least one common solution exists.

For example:

$$ \begin{cases} x^2+y^2=25,\\ y=3. \end{cases} $$

gives:

$$ x^2+9=25, $$

hence:

$$ x=\pm4. $$

The system therefore has two solutions:

$$ \boxed{ (4,3),\quad(-4,3) }. $$

It is consistent even though it has neither exactly one solution nor infinitely many solutions.

Important Linear versus Nonlinear Nuance

For finite systems of linear equations over ordinary fields, a consistent system has either one solution or infinitely many solutions.

$$ \boxed{ \text{linear consistent system} \Rightarrow \text{one or infinitely many solutions} }. $$

Nonlinear systems may instead have two, three, or any other finite number of solutions.

Why Linear Systems Cannot Have Exactly Two Isolated Solutions

If a linear system has two distinct solutions $\mathbf x_1$ and $\mathbf x_2$, then appropriate affine combinations between them are also solutions to the same nonhomogeneous linear system.

Consequently, two distinct solutions lead to infinitely many.

Affine Structure of the Solution Set

If $\mathbf x_p$ is one solution of:

$$ A\mathbf x=\mathbf b, $$

then every solution has form:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf x = \mathbf x_p+\mathbf v, \qquad \mathbf v\in\operatorname{Null}(A) }. $$

Thus a consistent nonhomogeneous linear system has an affine solution set obtained by translating the null space.

Unique Solution and Trivial Null Space

If the system is consistent, its solution is unique exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Null}(A)=\{\mathbf0\} }. $$

Consistency versus Solvability

In systems terminology, consistent and solvable are often effectively synonymous:

$$ \boxed{ \text{consistent} \iff \text{has at least one solution} }. $$

Consistency versus Uniqueness

Consistency does not mean that a solution is unique.

$$ \boxed{ \text{consistent} \not\Rightarrow \text{unique} }. $$

Consistency versus Independence

Consistency asks whether the equations can be satisfied simultaneously. Independence concerns whether equations, rows, vectors, or constraints contain nonredundant information.

These concepts are related in elementary classifications but are not identical.

Consistency versus Compatibility

Some mathematical literature uses compatible system as a synonym for consistent system.

$$ \boxed{ \text{compatible} \approx \text{consistent} } $$

in that context.

Consistency versus Identity

A system need not consist of identities to be consistent. It only needs at least one common solution.

Consistency versus Equivalent Systems

Two systems are equivalent when they have exactly the same solution set.

Both may be consistent, both may be inconsistent, or row operations may be used to transform one into the other while preserving their shared consistency status.

Numerical Approximation

In applied computation, an exact mathematical system may be consistent even when numerical algorithms produce only approximate solutions due to floating-point arithmetic.

Approximation error should not be confused with mathematical inconsistency.

Overdetermined Systems

An overdetermined system has more equations than unknowns. Such a system can still be consistent if the equations are mutually compatible.

$$ \boxed{ \text{more equations than unknowns} \not\Rightarrow \text{inconsistent} }. $$

Underdetermined Systems

An underdetermined system has fewer independent equations than unknowns. If consistent, it typically has free variables and infinitely many solutions.

Least Squares Is Different

An inconsistent linear system:

$$ A\mathbf x=\mathbf b $$

may be approximated by a least-squares solution minimizing:

$$ \boxed{ \|A\mathbf x-\mathbf b\| }. $$

This does not make the original system consistent. It finds the best approximation under the chosen norm.

Checking a Claimed Solution

A proposed solution must be substituted into every original equation.

$$ \boxed{ \text{solution} \Rightarrow \text{satisfies every equation} }. $$

Satisfying only some equations is insufficient.

Common Misconception: Consistent Means Exactly One Solution

A system with infinitely many solutions is also consistent because solutions exist.

Common Misconception: 0 = 0 Means Every Ordered Pair Is a Solution

A row reducing to $0=0$ merely shows that one equation has become redundant. Other remaining equations may still restrict the solution set.

Common Misconception: Zero Determinant Means No Solution

A zero determinant only says that a square coefficient matrix is singular. The system may be inconsistent or may have infinitely many solutions.

Common Misconception: More Equations than Variables Means Inconsistent

Extra equations can be compatible or redundant. Consistency depends on whether a common solution exists.

Common Misconception: Fewer Equations than Variables Means Inconsistent

An underdetermined system can be perfectly consistent and may have infinitely many solutions.

Common Misconception: Two Solutions Are Possible for an Ordinary Linear System

A linear system over a field that has two distinct solutions automatically has infinitely many solutions.

Common Misconception: A Graph Must Show One Intersection for Consistency

Coincident lines provide infinitely many common points and therefore form a consistent system.

Common Misconception: Every Equation Must Contribute New Information

A consistent system may contain redundant equations.

Common Misconception: Approximate Failure to Match Means Inconsistency

Rounding and measurement errors can cause approximate numerical discrepancies. Mathematical consistency refers to the exact equations being modeled.

$A\mathbf x=\mathbf b$ "A x equals b."

$\mathbf b\in\operatorname{Col}(A)$ "b belongs to the column space of A."

$0=c,\ c\ne0$ "Row reduction has produced a contradiction, so the system is inconsistent."

In elementary algebra, a system may be described as consistent independent when it has exactly one solution and consistent dependent when equivalent equations produce infinitely many solutions. In advanced linear algebra, it is often clearer to state directly that a system is consistent with a unique solution or consistent with infinitely many solutions, while discussing linear dependence separately.

The phrase no solution means that no single assignment of the unknowns satisfies all equations simultaneously. It does not mean that the individual equations themselves lack solutions.

Defense Notation Advice: Define consistency by existence: a system is consistent exactly when its common solution set is nonempty. Do not use consistent as a synonym for unique. When row reducing, distinguish a redundant row $0=0$ from a contradictory row $0=c$ with $c\ne0$. For square systems, remember that $\det(A)\ne0$ guarantees a unique solution, whereas $\det(A)=0$ requires further analysis. In nonlinear systems, do not force the elementary one-or-infinitely-many linear classification onto systems that can have several isolated solutions.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: A consistent system has at least one common solution.
  • Solution-Set Form: $$ \boxed{ S\ne\varnothing }. $$
  • Logical Form: $$ \boxed{ \exists\mathbf x\, [E_1(\mathbf x)\land\cdots\land E_m(\mathbf x)] }. $$
  • Set-Theoretic Form: $$ \boxed{ \bigcap_iS_i\ne\varnothing }. $$
  • Unique Solution: A system with exactly one solution is consistent.
  • Multiple Solutions: Any system with more than one common solution is also consistent.
  • Linear-System Special Case: A finite linear system over a field has either no solution, one solution, or infinitely many solutions.
  • Nonlinear-System Nuance: A consistent nonlinear system may have two or any other finite number of isolated solutions.
  • Consistent Independent: Common elementary term for a linear system with one solution.
  • Consistent Dependent: Common elementary term for a linear system with infinitely many solutions due to dependent constraints.
  • Graphical One-Solution Case: Distinct nonparallel lines intersect once.
  • Graphical Infinite-Solution Case: Coincident lines share every point.
  • Graphical Inconsistent Case: Distinct parallel lines have no common point.
  • Substitution: May reveal a specific solution, an identity, or a contradiction.
  • Elimination: Combines equations while preserving the common solution set.
  • Redundant Equation: May reduce to $0=0$ without creating inconsistency.
  • Contradictory Row: $$ \boxed{ 0=c,\quad c\ne0 } $$ proves inconsistency.
  • Augmented Matrix: A linear system may be represented by $[A\mid\mathbf b]$.
  • Rank Criterion: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{rank}(A) = \operatorname{rank}([A\mid\mathbf b]) } $$ exactly when the linear system is consistent.
  • Unique Rank Case: If the common rank equals the number of unknowns, the consistent system has a unique solution.
  • Free-Variable Case: If the common rank is less than the number of unknowns, the consistent linear system has infinitely many solutions.
  • Column-Space Criterion: $$ \boxed{ \mathbf b\in\operatorname{Col}(A) } $$ exactly when $A\mathbf x=\mathbf b$ is consistent.
  • Invertible Square Matrix: $\det(A)\ne0$ guarantees a unique consistent solution.
  • Singular Matrix: $\det(A)=0$ does not distinguish between infinitely many solutions and no solution by itself.
  • Homogeneous System: $A\mathbf x=\mathbf0$ is always consistent because $\mathbf x=\mathbf0$ is a solution.
  • Affine Solution Set: For a consistent linear system, $\mathbf x=\mathbf x_p+\mathbf v$ with $\mathbf v\in\operatorname{Null}(A)$.
  • Overdetermined Systems: Can be consistent despite having more equations than unknowns.
  • Underdetermined Systems: If consistent, commonly possess free variables and infinitely many solutions.
  • Parameter Dependence: Consistency may depend on particular parameter values.
  • Least-Squares Contrast: A least-squares solution approximates an inconsistent system rather than making the original equations consistent.
  • Solvable Contrast: Solvable is commonly synonymous with consistent for systems of equations.
  • Compatible Contrast: Some literature uses compatible as a synonym for consistent.
  • Inconsistent Contrast: An inconsistent system has no common solution.
  • Unique Contrast: Consistency guarantees existence, not uniqueness.
  • Dependent Contrast: Dependence concerns redundancy or linear dependence and should not be confused with existence of solutions.
  • Equivalent-System Contrast: Equivalent systems have exactly the same solution set; consistency is merely the question of whether that set is empty.
  • Common Error: Do not equate consistency with exactly one solution.
  • Common Error: Do not conclude that $0=0$ means every possible tuple solves the original system.
  • Common Error: Do not conclude that $\det(A)=0$ automatically means no solution.
  • Common Error: Do not assume an overdetermined system must be inconsistent.
  • Common Error: Do not assume an underdetermined system must be inconsistent.
  • Common Error: Do not use the one-or-infinitely-many classification for arbitrary nonlinear systems.
  • Terminological Contrast: A consistent system of equations has at least one simultaneous solution. An inconsistent system has no simultaneous solution. A consistent independent system is elementary terminology for a linear system with exactly one solution. A consistent dependent system commonly describes a linear system with infinitely many solutions arising from dependent equations. An equivalent system has exactly the same solution set as another system. A homogeneous system has zero right-hand side and is always consistent. A least-squares solution is an approximation to an incompatible linear system and is not an exact solution of the original inconsistent equations.
