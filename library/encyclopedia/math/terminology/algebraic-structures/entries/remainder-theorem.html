Remainder Theorem

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

The Remainder Theorem takes its name from the remainder left after polynomial division. The English word remainder ultimately comes through French from Latin remanere, meaning "to remain" or "to stay behind." Its mathematical use therefore preserves the ordinary idea of something left after a division has been carried out.

The word theorem comes through Latin from Greek theōrēma, meaning a proposition or something considered or demonstrated. The phrase Remainder Theorem therefore names a proved statement describing precisely what remains when a polynomial is divided by a particular kind of linear polynomial.

In many textbooks the result is called the Polynomial Remainder Theorem to distinguish it from other mathematical results involving remainders. In particular, it should not be confused with the Chinese Remainder Theorem, which concerns simultaneous congruences in number theory.

Operational Nuance

The Remainder Theorem states that when a polynomial $f(x)$ is divided by a linear divisor of the form:

$$ x-a, $$

the remainder is:

$$ f(a). $$

In compact form:

$$ \boxed{ \text{remainder when }f(x)\text{ is divided by }x-a=f(a) } $$

For example, suppose:

$$ f(x)=x^3-4x+7 $$

is divided by:

$$ x-2. $$

Instead of performing polynomial long division, evaluate:

$$ f(2) = 2^3-4(2)+7 = 8-8+7 = 7. $$

Therefore the remainder is:

$$ 7. $$

The theorem follows directly from the polynomial division algorithm. When $f(x)$ is divided by $x-a$, there exist a quotient polynomial $q(x)$ and a remainder $r$ such that:

$$ f(x) = (x-a)q(x)+r. $$

Because the divisor $x-a$ has degree $1$, the remainder must have degree less than $1$. Therefore the remainder is simply a constant.

Now substitute:

$$ x=a. $$

Then:

$$ f(a) = (a-a)q(a)+r $$ $$ f(a)=0+r $$ $$ f(a)=r. $$

This short argument explains why evaluation at $a$ reveals the remainder without requiring the quotient to be calculated.

A particularly important sign issue occurs when the divisor is written:

$$ x+a. $$

Since:

$$ x+a=x-(-a), $$

the Remainder Theorem requires evaluation at:

$$ x=-a. $$

Thus, if $f(x)$ is divided by $x+3$, the remainder is:

$$ f(-3), $$

not $f(3)$.

Common Misconception: The theorem does not say that $f(a)$ is the quotient. It gives only the remainder. The quotient polynomial generally requires polynomial division, synthetic division, or another algebraic method if it is also needed.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$f(x)\div(x-a)$ "F of x divided by x minus a."
$r=f(a)$ "The remainder equals f of a."
$f(x)=(x-a)q(x)+r$ "F of x equals x minus a times q of x, plus the remainder r."
$f(3)=5$ "F of three equals five."
$f(a)=0$ "F of a equals zero."
$x-a\mid f(x)$ "X minus a divides f of x."

A typical verbal application sounds like:

$$ \text{"To find the remainder upon division by }x-4, \text{ evaluate }f(4)."} $$

The number substituted into the polynomial is obtained by setting the divisor equal to zero:

$$ x-a=0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x=a. $$

This is particularly useful when the divisor contains a plus sign. For example:

$$ x+5=0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x=-5. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Identify the value of $a$ from the complete form $x-a$. If the divisor is $x+6$, then $a=-6$. The sign appearing in the divisor is therefore opposite the number substituted into the polynomial.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Polynomial Division: If: $$ f(x)=(x-a)q(x)+r, $$ then: $$ r=f(a). $$ The theorem allows the remainder to be determined without explicitly finding $q(x)$.
  • Factor Theorem: The Factor Theorem is the zero-remainder case of the Remainder Theorem. If: $$ f(a)=0, $$ then the remainder upon division by $x-a$ is zero, so: $$ x-a\mid f(x). $$ Conversely, if $x-a$ is a factor of $f(x)$, then: $$ f(a)=0. $$
  • Testing Factors: To determine whether $x-3$ is a factor of a polynomial, evaluate $f(3)$. If: $$ f(3)=0, $$ then $x-3$ is a factor. If: $$ f(3)\neq0, $$ it is not.
  • Finding Polynomial Zeros: The theorem links zeros of a polynomial with its linear factors: $$ f(a)=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x-a\text{ is a factor of }f(x). $$ This relationship is central to factoring and solving polynomial equations.
  • Synthetic Division: Synthetic division provides a compact algorithm for dividing a polynomial by $x-a$. The final entry produced by synthetic division is the remainder, and the Remainder Theorem guarantees that this value is: $$ f(a). $$
  • Polynomial Evaluation: Synthetic division can therefore also be used as an efficient method of evaluating a polynomial at a particular number. The resulting remainder is the desired function value.
  • Divisors of the Form $x+a$: If the divisor is: $$ x+a, $$ rewrite it conceptually as: $$ x-(-a). $$ The remainder is therefore: $$ f(-a). $$
  • More General Linear Divisors: If a polynomial is divided by: $$ bx-c, \qquad b\neq0, $$ then the divisor vanishes at: $$ x=\frac{c}{b}. $$ Evaluating the polynomial there gives the constant remainder: $$ r=f\left(\frac{c}{b}\right). $$ This is the same underlying principle expressed for a nonmonic linear divisor.
  • Polynomial Congruences: In algebraic notation, the Remainder Theorem can be expressed as: $$ f(x)\equiv f(a)\pmod{x-a}. $$ This means that $f(x)-f(a)$ is divisible by $x-a$.
  • Difference of Function Values: An equivalent consequence is: $$ x-a\mid f(x)-f(a). $$ This identity provides another compact algebraic expression of the theorem and frequently appears in more advanced polynomial reasoning.
  • Proof Technique: The theorem is a direct application of the polynomial division algorithm: $$ f(x)=d(x)q(x)+r(x), $$ where: $$ \deg r<\deg d. $$ Taking $d(x)=x-a$ forces the remainder to be constant, which makes evaluation at $x=a$ especially powerful.
  • Terminological Contrast: The Remainder Theorem determines the remainder when a polynomial is divided by a linear factor $x-a$. The Factor Theorem determines whether that remainder is zero and hence whether $x-a$ is an actual factor. The Polynomial Division Algorithm is the broader result that guarantees the existence and uniqueness of a quotient and remainder for polynomial division.
