Domain

Subfield: Foundations & Logic

Etymology & Roots

In mathematics, the domain of a function or relation is the set of input values for which that mathematical object is defined.

If:

$$ f:A\to B, $$

then $A$ is the domain of $f$:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f)=A }. $$

The term comes from Latin roots associated with a territory, region, or field of control. In mathematics, the domain is the collection of admissible inputs over which a function, relation, expression, or other rule is being considered.

In elementary function notation:

$$ y=f(x), $$

the domain is the set of allowed values of $x$.

The term must be distinguished from the range, which concerns outputs actually produced, and from the codomain, which is the declared target set of a function.

Operational Nuance

Domain of a Function

A function may be written:

$$ \boxed{ f:A\to B }. $$

Here:

  • $A$ is the domain,
  • $B$ is the codomain, and
  • $f(x)$ is the output assigned to each $x\in A$.

Function Requirement

Every element of the domain must be assigned exactly one output:

$$ \boxed{ x\in\operatorname{Dom}(f) \Longrightarrow \text{exactly one }f(x) }. $$

Natural Domain

When a formula is given without an explicitly stated domain, the natural domain usually means the largest standard set of inputs for which the formula is meaningful in the number system being used.

For example:

$$ f(x)=x^2+3x-1 $$

has natural real domain:

$$ \boxed{\mathbb{R}}. $$

Polynomial Domain

Every real polynomial is defined for every real input:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(p)=\mathbb{R} }. $$

Rational Function Domain

A rational function:

$$ f(x)=\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} $$

is undefined wherever:

$$ Q(x)=0. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f) = \{x\in\mathbb{R}:Q(x)\neq0\} }. $$

Example: Rational Function

For:

$$ f(x)=\frac{x+1}{x-4}, $$

the denominator cannot be zero:

$$ x-4\neq0. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f) = \mathbb{R}\setminus\{4\} }. $$

Restrictions Survive Cancellation

Consider:

$$ f(x)=\frac{x^2-4}{x-2}. $$

Factoring gives:

$$ f(x) = \frac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x-2}. $$

Although the expression simplifies to:

$$ x+2, $$

the original function remains undefined at:

$$ \boxed{x=2}. $$

Thus its domain is:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb{R}\setminus\{2\} }. $$

Even Roots

Over the real numbers, an even root requires a nonnegative radicand:

$$ \sqrt{g(x)} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad g(x)\geq0. $$

Example: Square Root

For:

$$ f(x)=\sqrt{x-3}, $$

require:

$$ x-3\geq0. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f)=[3,\infty) }. $$

Odd Roots

Odd roots such as:

$$ \sqrt[3]{x} $$

are defined for every real number:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(\sqrt[3]{x}) = \mathbb{R} }. $$

Radical in a Denominator

If an even radical appears in a denominator, its radicand must be strictly positive rather than merely nonnegative.

For:

$$ f(x)=\frac1{\sqrt{x-2}}, $$

require:

$$ x-2>0. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f)=(2,\infty) }. $$

Logarithmic Domain

For a real logarithm:

$$ \log_b(g(x)), $$

the argument must satisfy:

$$ \boxed{g(x)>0}. $$

Example: Logarithm

For:

$$ f(x)=\ln(x+5), $$

require:

$$ x+5>0. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f)=(-5,\infty) }. $$

Exponential Functions

For:

$$ f(x)=a^x, \qquad a>0, $$

the standard real domain is:

$$ \boxed{\mathbb{R}}. $$

Trigonometric Functions

The basic sine and cosine functions have domain:

$$ \boxed{\mathbb{R}}. $$

Tangent is undefined where $\cos x=0$:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(\tan) = \mathbb{R} \setminus \left\{ \frac{\pi}{2}+k\pi: k\in\mathbb{Z} \right\} }. $$

Reciprocal Trigonometric Functions

Since:

$$ \csc x=\frac1{\sin x}, $$

its domain excludes:

$$ \boxed{x=k\pi}. $$

Similarly, $\sec x$ excludes zeros of cosine and $\cot x$ excludes zeros of sine.

Inverse Trigonometric Domains

The domain of:

$$ \arcsin x $$

is:

$$ \boxed{[-1,1]}, $$

while:

$$ \arctan x $$

has domain:

$$ \boxed{\mathbb{R}}. $$

Domain from a Graph

Graphically, the domain consists of all horizontal-coordinate values for which the graph contains at least one point.

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(R) = \{x:\exists y\text{ such that }(x,y)\in R\} }. $$

Domain of a Relation

For a relation:

$$ R\subseteq A\times B, $$

its domain is the set of first components that actually occur:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(R) = \{a\in A:\exists b\in B,\;(a,b)\in R\} }. $$

Example: Relation

If:

$$ R= \{(1,4),(2,5),(2,8),(7,9)\}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(R)=\{1,2,7\} }. $$

Domain vs. Range

For a relation:

$$ \boxed{ \text{domain} = \text{first coordinates} } $$

while:

$$ \boxed{ \text{range} = \text{second coordinates that actually occur} }. $$

Domain vs. Codomain

In:

$$ f:A\to B, $$

$A$ is the domain and $B$ is the codomain.

The range is:

$$ \boxed{ f(A)=\{f(x):x\in A\} } $$

and satisfies:

$$ \boxed{ f(A)\subseteq B }. $$

Declared Domain vs. Natural Domain

A formula does not by itself always determine the mathematical function.

For example:

$$ f(x)=x^2 $$

could be declared as:

$$ f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R} $$

or:

$$ f:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty). $$

The formulas agree, but the domains differ.

Domain Restrictions

A function may intentionally be restricted to a smaller domain.

For example, restricting:

$$ f(x)=x^2 $$

to:

$$ \boxed{x\geq0} $$

makes the function one-to-one and permits the inverse:

$$ \boxed{f^{-1}(x)=\sqrt{x}}. $$

Domain of a Composite Function

For:

$$ (f\circ g)(x)=f(g(x)), $$

the input must first belong to the domain of $g$, and the resulting value $g(x)$ must belong to the domain of $f$:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f\circ g) = \{x\in\operatorname{Dom}(g): g(x)\in\operatorname{Dom}(f)\} }. $$

Example: Composite Domain

Let:

$$ f(u)=\sqrt{u}, \qquad g(x)=x-5. $$

Then:

$$ (f\circ g)(x)=\sqrt{x-5}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f\circ g)=[5,\infty) }. $$

Domain of a Sum of Functions

For:

$$ (f+g)(x)=f(x)+g(x), $$

the natural domain is ordinarily:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f+g) = \operatorname{Dom}(f) \cap \operatorname{Dom}(g) }. $$

Domain of a Product of Functions

Similarly:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(fg) = \operatorname{Dom}(f) \cap \operatorname{Dom}(g) }. $$

Domain of a Quotient

For:

$$ \frac{f}{g}, $$

require both functions to be defined and:

$$ g(x)\neq0. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}\left(\frac fg\right) = \{x\in \operatorname{Dom}(f)\cap\operatorname{Dom}(g): g(x)\neq0\} }. $$

Piecewise Functions

The domain of a piecewise function is the union of the input sets on which its branches are defined.

For example:

$$ f(x)= \begin{cases} x^2, & x<0,\\ \sqrt{x}, & x\geq0, \end{cases} $$

has domain:

$$ \boxed{\mathbb{R}}. $$

Discrete Domain

A domain need not be an interval or all real numbers.

A sequence:

$$ a_n=2n+1 $$

may have domain:

$$ \boxed{\mathbb{N}} $$

or another specified set of integer indices.

Finite Domain

A function can have a finite domain:

$$ f:\{1,2,3\}\to\mathbb{R}. $$

Multivariable Domain

For a function of several variables:

$$ f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}, $$

the domain consists of admissible ordered $n$-tuples.

Example: Two Variables

For:

$$ f(x,y)=\sqrt{9-x^2-y^2}, $$

the real domain requires:

$$ 9-x^2-y^2\geq0, $$

or:

$$ \boxed{ x^2+y^2\leq9 }. $$

Thus the domain is the closed disk of radius $3$ centered at the origin.

Domain Depends on the Number System

The expression:

$$ \sqrt{x} $$

has real domain:

$$ [0,\infty), $$

but complex square root can be considered on larger sets once a branch or multivalued interpretation is specified.

Therefore domain questions must be interpreted relative to the mathematical setting.

Domain in Differential Equations

A solution to a differential equation is defined on an interval or other set over which the required functions and derivatives exist.

Thus a formula may solve a differential equation only on a particular domain.

Domain in Integration

In multivariable calculus, domain may also refer to a region of integration, such as:

$$ D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^2 $$

in:

$$ \iint_D f(x,y)\,dA. $$

Here $D$ is the region over which integration occurs.

Common Misconception: The domain is not simply "all $x$-values that appear in the formula." It is the set of inputs for which the function or relation is actually defined. Denominators, radicals, logarithms, explicit restrictions, composition, and the chosen number system can all restrict the domain.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\operatorname{Dom}(f)$ "The domain of f."
$f:A\to B$ "F maps A into B," or "f is a function from A to B."
$x\in\operatorname{Dom}(f)$ "X belongs to the domain of f."
$\mathbb{R}\setminus\{4\}$ "All real numbers except four."
$[3,\infty)$ "The interval from three inclusive to infinity."
$(-5,\infty)$ "All real numbers greater than negative five."
$\{x\in\mathbb{R}:x\neq2\}$ "The set of real x such that x is not equal to two."

Domain may be written in several equivalent forms. For:

$$ f(x)=\frac1{x-2}, $$

one may write:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f) = \mathbb{R}\setminus\{2\} } $$

or:

$$ \boxed{ \{x\in\mathbb{R}:x\neq2\} } $$

or in interval notation:

$$ \boxed{ (-\infty,2)\cup(2,\infty) }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish explicitly among domain, codomain, and range. In:

$$ f:A\to B, $$

$A$ is the domain and $B$ is the codomain. The range is the subset of $B$ actually attained:

$$ \boxed{ f(A)\subseteq B }. $$

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Basic Definition: The domain is the set of allowable inputs of a function or relation.
  • Function Notation: $$ \boxed{ f:A\to B } $$ identifies $A$ as the domain.
  • Domain Notation: $$ \boxed{\operatorname{Dom}(f)}. $$
  • Input Interpretation: In $y=f(x)$, the domain consists of permitted $x$-values.
  • Function Requirement: Every domain element must have exactly one assigned output.
  • Polynomial: A real polynomial has domain: $$ \boxed{\mathbb{R}}. $$
  • Rational Function: Exclude zeros of the denominator.
  • Rational Example: $$ \operatorname{Dom} \left( \frac1{x-3} \right) = \boxed{\mathbb{R}\setminus\{3\}}. $$
  • Cancellation: Values excluded by the original denominator remain excluded after algebraic cancellation.
  • Square Root: Over $\mathbb{R}$: $$ \sqrt{g(x)} $$ requires: $$ \boxed{g(x)\geq0}. $$
  • Even Root: An even-indexed real radical requires a nonnegative radicand.
  • Odd Root: An odd-indexed real radical accepts every real radicand.
  • Radical Denominator: $$ \frac1{\sqrt{g(x)}} $$ requires: $$ \boxed{g(x)>0}. $$
  • Logarithm: $$ \log_b(g(x)) $$ requires: $$ \boxed{g(x)>0}. $$
  • Exponential Function: Standard real exponential functions have domain $\mathbb{R}$.
  • Sine: $$ \boxed{\operatorname{Dom}(\sin)=\mathbb{R}}. $$
  • Cosine: $$ \boxed{\operatorname{Dom}(\cos)=\mathbb{R}}. $$
  • Tangent: Exclude zeros of cosine.
  • Cosecant: Exclude zeros of sine.
  • Secant: Exclude zeros of cosine.
  • Cotangent: Exclude zeros of sine.
  • Arcsine: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(\arcsin)=[-1,1] }. $$
  • Arccosine: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(\arccos)=[-1,1] }. $$
  • Arctangent: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(\arctan)=\mathbb{R} }. $$
  • Graphical Domain: The domain consists of horizontal coordinates represented by points of the graph.
  • Relation Domain: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(R) = \{x:\exists y,\;(x,y)\in R\} }. $$
  • Ordered Pairs: For a finite relation, collect the distinct first coordinates.
  • Range Contrast: Domain concerns inputs; range concerns outputs actually produced.
  • Codomain Contrast: The codomain is the declared target set, which may contain values never attained.
  • Range Inclusion: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Range}(f) \subseteq \operatorname{Codomain}(f) }. $$
  • Natural Domain: The largest ordinary set on which a formula is meaningful, unless another domain is explicitly specified.
  • Declared Domain: A function's domain can be intentionally smaller than the natural domain of its formula.
  • Restricted Domain: Restrictions can make a non-injective formula injective.
  • Inverse Functions: The domain of $f^{-1}$ is the range of $f$.
  • Inverse Range: The range of $f^{-1}$ is the domain of $f$.
  • Composition: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f\circ g) = \{x\in\operatorname{Dom}(g): g(x)\in\operatorname{Dom}(f)\} }. $$
  • Function Sum: The natural domain of $f+g$ is ordinarily the intersection of their domains.
  • Function Difference: The natural domain of $f-g$ is likewise the domain intersection.
  • Function Product: The natural domain of $fg$ is ordinarily the domain intersection.
  • Function Quotient: The denominator function must additionally be nonzero.
  • Piecewise Function: The domain is determined by the combined branch conditions.
  • Finite Domain: A function may be defined on only finitely many inputs.
  • Discrete Domain: Sequences commonly have domains contained in $\mathbb{Z}$ or $\mathbb{N}$.
  • Continuous Domain: Real-valued functions may have interval domains.
  • Disconnected Domain: A domain may be a union of separated intervals.
  • Example: $$ \frac1{x^2-1} $$ has domain: $$ \boxed{ (-\infty,-1)\cup(-1,1)\cup(1,\infty) }. $$
  • Singleton Domain: A function may have a domain containing exactly one element.
  • Empty Domain: In set-theoretic formulations, a function may have empty domain.
  • Multivariable Function: A domain may be a subset of: $$ \boxed{\mathbb{R}^n}. $$
  • Two-Variable Radical: $$ \sqrt{1-x^2-y^2} $$ has real domain: $$ \boxed{ x^2+y^2\leq1 }. $$
  • Geometry: A multivariable domain may form a disk, rectangle, annulus, half-plane, or more complicated region.
  • Complex Analysis: A domain often means an open connected subset of $\mathbb{C}$, a more specialized usage than the general function-domain meaning.
  • Topology: Function domains may be arbitrary sets or topological spaces depending on context.
  • Differential Equations: The domain of a solution is the interval or region on which the solution and required derivatives are defined.
  • Integration: A domain of integration is the set or region over which an integral is taken.
  • Probability: A random variable has a sample space as its function domain.
  • Computer Functions: In computational settings, accepted input types or values play a role analogous to a mathematical domain.
  • Domain Restriction as Assumption: Mathematical statements may explicitly restrict a domain to ensure an operation, theorem, or inverse is valid.
  • Equation Solutions: Candidate solutions lying outside the original domain must be rejected.
  • Extraneous Solutions: Domain restrictions are a common reason an algebraically generated candidate fails to solve the original equation.
  • Domain and Holes: A removable discontinuity may correspond to an input excluded from the domain despite cancellation in a simplified formula.
  • Domain and Vertical Asymptotes: Inputs causing a noncanceled denominator zero are excluded and may correspond to vertical asymptotes.
  • Domain and Endpoints: Interval endpoints are included or excluded according to whether the function is defined there.
  • Interval Notation: Parentheses denote excluded finite endpoints; brackets denote included finite endpoints.
  • Infinity: $\infty$ and $-\infty$ are never included as real-number endpoints, so interval notation always uses parentheses with them.
  • Set-Builder Notation: Domains may be written: $$ \boxed{ \{x\in\mathbb{R}:\text{condition on }x\} }. $$
  • Terminological Contrast: The domain is the set of permitted inputs; the range is the set of attained outputs; the codomain is the declared target set.
  • Core Interpretation: The domain identifies where a mathematical rule is allowed to receive inputs: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Dom}(f) = \{x:f(x)\text{ is defined under the stated conditions}\} }. $$ It may be determined by explicit declaration, algebraic restrictions, graph structure, composition, or the mathematical setting itself.
