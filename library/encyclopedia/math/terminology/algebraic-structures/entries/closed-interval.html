Closed Interval

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

A closed interval is an interval that contains both of its endpoints.

If $a\le b$, the closed interval from $a$ to $b$ is written:

$$ \boxed{ [a,b] }. $$

In inequality form:

$$ \boxed{ [a,b] = \{x\in\mathbb R:a\le x\le b\} }. $$

The square brackets indicate that the endpoints $a$ and $b$ are included.

The word closed has both an elementary interval-notation meaning and a deeper topological meaning. For ordinary real intervals, these meanings agree: $[a,b]$ is a closed set in $\mathbb R$.

Degenerate Closed Interval

If:

$$ a=b, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ [a,a]=\{a\} }. $$

Thus a closed interval can collapse to a single point.

What If a > b?

Under the standard ordered-interval convention, $[a,b]$ is ordinarily used with $a\le b$. If $a>b$, the condition:

$$ a\le x\le b $$

has no real solution.

Length of a Closed Interval

For $a\le b$, the length is:

$$ \boxed{ b-a }. $$

Including or excluding finitely many endpoints does not change interval length.

Midpoint

The midpoint of $[a,b]$ is:

$$ \boxed{ m= \frac{a+b}{2} }. $$

Radius about the Midpoint

The interval can be rewritten symmetrically around its midpoint.

Let:

$$ m=\frac{a+b}{2}, \qquad r=\frac{b-a}{2}. $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ [a,b] = [m-r,m+r] }. $$

Absolute-Value Form

The same interval can be described by:

$$ \boxed{ |x-m|\le r }. $$

Thus closed intervals connect naturally with distance and absolute value.

Boundedness

Every finite closed interval $[a,b]$ is bounded above by $b$ and bounded below by $a$.

$$ \boxed{ a\le x\le b \quad \text{for every }x\in[a,b] }. $$

Minimum and Maximum

A nonempty closed interval contains its least and greatest elements:

$$ \boxed{ \min[a,b]=a, \qquad \max[a,b]=b }. $$

Infimum and Supremum

$$ \boxed{ \inf[a,b]=a, \qquad \sup[a,b]=b }. $$

For a closed interval, the infimum and supremum are actually members of the set.

Closed as a Topological Property

In $\mathbb R$ with its usual topology, $[a,b]$ is a closed set.

One way to express this is that its complement is open:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb R\setminus[a,b] = (-\infty,a)\cup(b,\infty) }. $$

Closed Sets Contain Their Limit Points

Another characterization is that every limit point of $[a,b]$ belongs to $[a,b]$.

Compactness

In the real numbers, every closed and bounded set is compact.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ [a,b] \text{ is compact} }. $$

This is the Heine-Borel property for subsets of $\mathbb R$.

Why Compactness Matters

Compactness is one reason closed intervals appear throughout calculus and analysis. Continuous functions on closed intervals have particularly strong properties.

Extreme Value Theorem

If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, then $f$ attains both an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum on that interval.

$$ \boxed{ f\text{ continuous on }[a,b] \Rightarrow f\text{ attains max and min} }. $$

Intermediate Value Theorem

If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, every value between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ is achieved somewhere on the interval.

Mean Value Theorem

If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$, then there exists some $c\in(a,b)$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ f'(c) = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} }. $$

This theorem deliberately uses the closed interval for continuity and the open interval for differentiability.

Definite Integrals

A definite integral is commonly taken over a closed interval:

$$ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx }. $$

Changing the inclusion of isolated endpoints ordinarily does not alter the value of a standard Riemann integral, but $[a,b]$ remains the conventional notation for the interval of integration.

Function Domains

A function may be defined specifically on a closed interval:

$$ \boxed{ f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R }. $$

This states that both endpoint inputs belong to the domain.

Range as a Closed Interval

A function's range may also be a closed interval.

For example:

$$ f(x)=\sin x $$

has range:

$$ \boxed{ [-1,1] }. $$

Intersection of Closed Intervals

For example:

$$ [1,5]\cap[3,8] = \boxed{[3,5]}. $$

Intervals Touching at One Endpoint

Because closed intervals include endpoints:

$$ \boxed{ [a,b]\cap[b,c] = \{b\} }. $$

Union of Touching Closed Intervals

If $a\le b\le c$, then:

$$ \boxed{ [a,b]\cup[b,c] = [a,c] }. $$

Closed Interval in Extended Real Notation

Expressions involving infinity require care because $+\infty$ and $-\infty$ are not ordinary real endpoints.

Thus in standard real interval notation one writes:

$$ [a,\infty) $$

rather than:

$$ [a,\infty]. $$

Infinity is not included as a real-number endpoint.

Common Misconception: Closed Means Finite

In topology, a set can be closed without being bounded or finite.

For example:

$$ \boxed{ [0,\infty) } $$

is a closed subset of $\mathbb R$, even though it is unbounded.

Common Misconception: Closed Means No Gaps

The word closed does not mean merely "continuous" or "without gaps." It refers to endpoint inclusion in elementary interval notation and, more generally, to a topological property.

Common Misconception: Parentheses and Brackets Are Decorative

They encode membership information.

$$ [a,b] \ne (a,b). $$

Common Misconception: Infinity Can Be Included with a Square Bracket

In ordinary real interval notation, infinity is not a real number and cannot be included as an endpoint.

Common Misconception: Closed Intervals Always Have Positive Length

The degenerate interval:

$$ [a,a] $$

is a valid closed interval with length $0$.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$[a,b]$ "The closed interval from a to b."
$a\le x\le b$ "x is between a and b, inclusive."
$x\in[a,b]$ "x belongs to the closed interval a b."
$[a,a]=\{a\}$ "The degenerate closed interval a to a contains only a."
$\operatorname{int}[a,b]=(a,b)$ "The interior of the closed interval is the corresponding open interval."

The phrase between a and b inclusive is a common spoken equivalent of $[a,b]$. The word inclusive is important because ordinary conversational uses of "between" can be ambiguous about endpoints.

Defense Notation Advice: Use square brackets only when the corresponding finite endpoint is included. Translate immediately between $[a,b]$ and $a\le x\le b$ when checking interval notation, and remember that infinity always receives a parenthesis in ordinary real-number interval notation.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: A closed interval contains both endpoints.
  • Interval Notation: $$ \boxed{ [a,b] }. $$
  • Inequality Form: $$ \boxed{ a\le x\le b }. $$
  • Set-Builder Form: $$ \boxed{ \{x\in\mathbb R:a\le x\le b\} }. $$
  • Endpoints: Both $a$ and $b$ belong to the interval.
  • Length: $$ \boxed{ b-a }. $$
  • Midpoint: $$ \boxed{ \frac{a+b}{2} }. $$
  • Absolute-Value Form: $$ \boxed{ |x-m|\le r } $$ describes a closed interval centered at $m$ with radius $r$.
  • Minimum and Maximum: A nonempty finite closed interval contains both.
  • Topology: $[a,b]$ is closed in $\mathbb R$.
  • Compactness: Every finite closed interval in $\mathbb R$ is compact.
  • Calculus: Closed intervals appear in the Extreme Value, Intermediate Value, and Mean Value Theorems.
  • Definite Integration: Integrals are conventionally written over intervals such as $[a,b]$.
  • Open-Interval Contrast: $(a,b)$ excludes both endpoints.
  • Half-Open Contrast: $[a,b)$ and $(a,b]$ include exactly one endpoint.
  • Degenerate Case: $$ \boxed{ [a,a]=\{a\} }. $$
  • Common Error: Do not confuse square brackets with parentheses.
  • Common Error: Do not place a square bracket at infinity in ordinary real interval notation.
  • Common Error: Do not assume every closed set is bounded merely because finite closed intervals are bounded.
  • Terminological Contrast: Closed interval includes both finite endpoints. Open interval excludes both endpoints. Half-open interval includes exactly one endpoint. Endpoint is a boundary value of an interval. Interior of $[a,b]$ is $(a,b)$ when $a<b$. Closure of $(a,b)$ is $[a,b]$. Bounded interval has finite lower and upper bounds. Compact interval in $\mathbb R$ is equivalently a closed and bounded interval.
