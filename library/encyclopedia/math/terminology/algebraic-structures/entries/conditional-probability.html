Conditional Probability

Subfield: Probability & Statistical Inference

Etymology & Roots

Conditional probability is the probability assigned to an event after restricting attention to outcomes satisfying some specified condition. The most common notation is:

$$ \boxed{ P(A\mid B) = \frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}, \qquad P(B)>0 }. $$

The expression $P(A\mid B)$ is read “the probability of $A$ given $B$.” The event $B$ is the condition.

The adjective conditional carries its ordinary mathematical meaning: the probability is being evaluated under an additional requirement. Rather than asking how likely $A$ is throughout the entire probability space, one asks how likely $A$ is among the outcomes for which $B$ has occurred.

$$ \boxed{ \text{conditioning on }B = \text{restricting attention to outcomes in }B }. $$

The vertical bar $\mid$ is therefore not division and is not a logical implication symbol. In probability notation it means given or conditional on.

Operational Nuance

Basic Definition

For events $A$ and $B$ with $P(B)>0$:

$$ \boxed{ P(A\mid B) = \frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)} }. $$

The numerator measures outcomes satisfying both $A$ and $B$. The denominator measures all outcomes satisfying the condition $B$.

Restricting the Sample Space

Suppose the original sample space is $\Omega$. Once $B$ is known to have occurred, $B$ becomes the effective sample space for the conditional calculation.

$$ \boxed{ \Omega \longrightarrow B } $$

Probabilities are then renormalized within $B$ so that:

$$ \boxed{ P(B\mid B)=1 }. $$

Finite Equally Likely Outcomes

If the relevant outcomes are equally likely, conditional probability may be computed by counting:

$$ \boxed{ P(A\mid B) = \frac{|A\cap B|}{|B|} }. $$

This works because conditioning discards outcomes outside $B$ before the favorable outcomes are counted.

Example: Rolling a Die

Let a fair six-sided die be rolled. Define:

$$ A=\{\text{roll greater than }3\}=\{4,5,6\} $$

and:

$$ B=\{\text{roll is even}\}=\{2,4,6\}. $$

Then:

$$ A\cap B=\{4,6\}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ P(A\mid B)=\frac23 }. $$

The outcome $5$ no longer matters after conditioning on the die being even.

Why Conditional Probability Can Differ from Ordinary Probability

Before learning that the roll is even:

$$ P(A)=\frac36=\frac12. $$

After learning that the roll is even:

$$ P(A\mid B)=\frac23. $$

New information has changed the relevant probability.

Conditional Probability Is Directional

In general:

$$ \boxed{ P(A\mid B) \ne P(B\mid A) }. $$

Conditioning on $B$ restricts the sample space to $B$, while conditioning on $A$ restricts it to $A$. These are different operations.

Example of Directionality

Suppose:

$$ P(A)=0.20,\qquad P(B)=0.50,\qquad P(A\cap B)=0.10. $$

Then:

$$ P(A\mid B)=\frac{0.10}{0.50}=0.20, $$

while:

$$ P(B\mid A)=\frac{0.10}{0.20}=0.50. $$

Multiplication Rule

Rearranging the definition gives:

$$ \boxed{ P(A\cap B) = P(A\mid B)P(B) }. $$

Reversing the roles of the events also gives:

$$ \boxed{ P(A\cap B) = P(B\mid A)P(A) }. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ P(A\mid B)P(B) = P(B\mid A)P(A) }. $$

Chain Rule

The multiplication rule extends to several events:

$$ \boxed{ P(A_1\cap A_2\cap\cdots\cap A_n) = P(A_1) P(A_2\mid A_1) P(A_3\mid A_1\cap A_2) \cdots P(A_n\mid A_1\cap\cdots\cap A_{n-1}) }. $$

This decomposition is fundamental in probability trees, sequential models, Bayesian methods, stochastic processes, and statistical likelihood calculations.

Example: Drawing without Replacement

Suppose a container holds $5$ red objects and $3$ blue objects. Two objects are drawn without replacement.

Let $R_1$ denote red on the first draw and $R_2$ red on the second.

Then:

$$ P(R_1)=\frac58. $$

Given that the first was red, only $4$ red objects remain among $7$ total:

$$ P(R_2\mid R_1)=\frac47. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ P(R_1\cap R_2) = \frac58\cdot\frac47 = \frac5{14} }. $$

Without Replacement Creates Dependence

In the preceding example:

$$ P(R_2)=\frac58 $$

before the first result is known, but:

$$ P(R_2\mid R_1)=\frac47. $$

Since these differ, the events are dependent.

Independence

Events $A$ and $B$ are independent when occurrence of one does not change the probability of the other.

If $P(B)>0$:

$$ \boxed{ P(A\mid B)=P(A) } $$

is one characterization of independence.

Equivalently:

$$ \boxed{ P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B) }. $$

Independent Does Not Mean Mutually Exclusive

Two nonzero-probability mutually exclusive events cannot be independent.

If:

$$ A\cap B=\varnothing, $$

then:

$$ P(A\cap B)=0. $$

Independence would require:

$$ 0=P(A)P(B), $$

which is impossible if both probabilities are positive.

Conditional Complement

Within a condition $B$, an event and its complement still exhaust the conditioned space:

$$ \boxed{ P(A^c\mid B) = 1-P(A\mid B) }. $$

Conditional Addition Rule

For events $A$, $C$, and conditioning event $B$ with $P(B)>0$:

$$ \boxed{ P(A\cup C\mid B) = P(A\mid B) + P(C\mid B) - P(A\cap C\mid B) }. $$

Thus the ordinary probability laws remain valid within the conditioned probability space.

Partition of the Sample Space

Suppose $B_1,B_2,\ldots,B_n$ are mutually exclusive events whose union is the whole sample space:

$$ B_1\cup\cdots\cup B_n=\Omega. $$

Then the events form a partition.

Law of Total Probability

If $B_1,\ldots,B_n$ form a partition with positive probabilities, then:

$$ \boxed{ P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A\mid B_i)P(B_i) }. $$

This reconstructs an unconditional probability by combining conditional probabilities across all possible cases.

Bayes' Theorem

Conditional probability can be reversed using Bayes' theorem:

$$ \boxed{ P(B\mid A) = \frac{P(A\mid B)P(B)}{P(A)} } $$

whenever $P(A)>0$.

Bayes' Theorem with a Partition

If $B_1,\ldots,B_n$ form a partition, then:

$$ \boxed{ P(B_j\mid A) = \frac{ P(A\mid B_j)P(B_j) }{ \sum_{i=1}^{n}P(A\mid B_i)P(B_i) } }. $$

This form is central to Bayesian inference.

Prior and Posterior Probability

In Bayesian terminology:

  • $P(B)$ is a prior probability,
  • $P(A\mid B)$ describes how compatible the observed evidence is with $B$, and
  • $P(B\mid A)$ is the resulting posterior probability.
$$ \boxed{ \text{prior} + \text{evidence} \longrightarrow \text{posterior} }. $$

Base Rates Matter

Conditional probability problems can produce misleading intuition when the base probability $P(B)$ is ignored.

Bayes' theorem shows explicitly that:

$$ P(B\mid A) $$

depends not only on $P(A\mid B)$ but also on the prior probability $P(B)$ and the overall probability of the observed event $A$.

Example: Diagnostic Testing

Suppose a condition occurs in $1\%$ of a population:

$$ P(D)=0.01. $$

Suppose a test is positive for $95\%$ of individuals with the condition:

$$ P(+\mid D)=0.95, $$

and has a $5\%$ false-positive probability:

$$ P(+\mid D^c)=0.05. $$

The total probability of a positive result is:

$$ P(+) = (0.95)(0.01) + (0.05)(0.99) = 0.059. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ P(D\mid +) = \frac{(0.95)(0.01)}{0.059} \approx0.161 }. $$

A $95\%$ sensitivity does not mean that a positive result implies a $95\%$ probability of having the condition.

Contingency Tables

Conditional probabilities can be read naturally from contingency tables.

Suppose data are organized as:

$A$ $A^c$ Total
$B$ $n(A\cap B)$ $n(A^c\cap B)$ $n(B)$
$B^c$ $n(A\cap B^c)$ $n(A^c\cap B^c)$ $n(B^c)$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ P(A\mid B) = \frac{n(A\cap B)}{n(B)} } $$

when the table represents equally weighted observations.

Conditioning Changes the Denominator

A useful operational rule is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{the condition determines the denominator} }. $$

In $P(A\mid B)$, the denominator concerns $B$, not $A$.

Tree Diagrams

Tree diagrams represent sequential conditional probabilities. Probabilities along a path are multiplied.

If one branch corresponds to $A$ followed by $B$, then:

$$ \boxed{ P(A\cap B) = P(A)P(B\mid A) }. $$

Alternative mutually exclusive paths leading to the same outcome are then added.

Conditioning and Information

Conditional probability formalizes how probabilities change when information is supplied.

$$ \boxed{ \text{prior information state} \longrightarrow \text{new information} \longrightarrow \text{updated probability} }. $$

The update is mathematical rather than merely verbal: the probability measure is restricted and normalized relative to the information supplied.

Conditional Probability as a New Probability Measure

For fixed $B$ with $P(B)>0$, define:

$$ P_B(A)=P(A\mid B). $$

Then $P_B$ satisfies the probability axioms on the conditioned space.

In particular:

$$ P_B(B)=1. $$

Thus conditioning can be interpreted as constructing a new probability measure relative to the information $B$.

Conditional Probability of an Event Given Itself

If $P(A)>0$:

$$ \boxed{ P(A\mid A)=1 }. $$

Conditioning on the Whole Sample Space

Since $P(\Omega)=1$:

$$ \boxed{ P(A\mid\Omega)=P(A) }. $$

Conditioning on no additional restriction leaves the original probability unchanged.

Conditioning on an Impossible Event

The elementary formula:

$$ P(A\mid B) = \frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)} $$

requires:

$$ \boxed{ P(B)>0 }. $$

If $P(B)=0$, the quotient is undefined. More advanced probability theory can define conditional distributions in settings involving probability-zero events through regular conditional probabilities, densities, or other measure-theoretic constructions, but the elementary event formula cannot simply divide by zero.

Continuous Random Variables

For continuous random variables, an exact event such as:

$$ Y=y $$

often has probability zero. Conditional densities therefore replace the elementary finite-event ratio.

If $X$ and $Y$ have joint density $f_{X,Y}(x,y)$ and $f_Y(y)>0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y) = \frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_Y(y)} }. $$

Conditional Distribution

The conditional distribution of $X$ given $Y=y$ describes the probabilistic behavior of $X$ once the value of $Y$ is treated as known.

In a discrete setting:

$$ \boxed{ P(X=x\mid Y=y) }. $$

Conditional Expectation

The concept of conditioning extends from events to expected values.

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb E[X\mid Y] } $$

represents the expected value of $X$ given information supplied by $Y$.

More generally:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb E[X\mid\mathcal G] } $$

denotes conditional expectation relative to a $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal G$ of available information.

Conditional Variance

One may similarly define:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Var}(X\mid Y) }. $$

This measures the remaining variation in $X$ after conditioning on information about $Y$.

Law of Total Expectation

Conditional expectation gives the important identity:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbb E[X] = \mathbb E\!\left[\mathbb E[X\mid Y]\right] }. $$

This is sometimes called the law of iterated expectation or tower property in appropriate forms.

Law of Total Variance

Conditional variance yields:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb E[\operatorname{Var}(X\mid Y)] + \operatorname{Var}(\mathbb E[X\mid Y]) }. $$

The total variation is decomposed into expected within-condition variation and variation between conditional means.

Conditional Independence

Two events or random variables may become independent after conditioning on a third quantity even when they are not independent unconditionally.

Events $A$ and $B$ are conditionally independent given $C$ when:

$$ \boxed{ P(A\cap B\mid C) = P(A\mid C)P(B\mid C) } $$

whenever the relevant conditional probabilities are defined.

Conditional Independence Is Different from Independence

Neither implication holds automatically:

$$ \boxed{ A\perp B \not\Rightarrow A\perp B\mid C } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ A\perp B\mid C \not\Rightarrow A\perp B }. $$

Simpson's Paradox

Conditional and unconditional relationships can differ dramatically when data are separated according to a third variable. In some cases a trend visible within every subgroup may weaken, disappear, or reverse when the groups are combined.

This phenomenon, known as Simpson's paradox, illustrates why the conditioning variable must be understood rather than treated as a purely mechanical denominator choice.

Sampling without Replacement

Conditional probability is especially important when successive draws alter the composition of the population.

After each observation, the probabilities for later observations must be updated using the changed condition.

Sampling with Replacement

If the sampled object is replaced and the system returns to its original state, then successive draws may be independent.

For example, with replacement:

$$ \boxed{ P(R_2\mid R_1)=P(R_2) } $$

under the usual identical random-draw assumptions.

Conditional Relative Frequency

In observed data, conditional probability is often estimated by conditional relative frequency:

$$ \boxed{ \widehat{P}(A\mid B) = \frac{\text{number with both }A\text{ and }B} {\text{number with }B} }. $$

Conditioning in Statistics

Statistical procedures may condition on observed data, sufficient statistics, covariates, treatment groups, strata, or model variables.

Conditional probability therefore forms part of the mathematical foundation of regression, Bayesian inference, likelihood methods, graphical models, survival analysis, and many other statistical tools.

Conditional Probability Is Not Necessarily Causal

The statement:

$$ P(A\mid B)>P(A) $$

means that $A$ is more probable among cases satisfying $B$. It does not by itself prove that $B$ causes $A$.

$$ \boxed{ \text{conditional association} \ne \text{causation} }. $$

Conditional Probability Is Not Logical Implication

The probability notation:

$$ P(A\mid B) $$

is not the same as the logical statement:

$$ B\Rightarrow A. $$

Conditional probability measures uncertainty after conditioning; implication asserts a logical relation.

Probability One Does Not Always Mean Logical Necessity

In continuous probability spaces, an event can have probability $1$ without being logically unavoidable in the set-theoretic sense.

Thus:

$$ P(A\mid B)=1 $$

should not automatically be translated into a formal logical implication without considering the probability model.

Common Misconception: P(A|B) Equals P(B|A)

These probabilities condition on different events and generally have different denominators.

$$ \boxed{ P(A\mid B) = \frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)} } $$

whereas:

$$ \boxed{ P(B\mid A) = \frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(A)} }. $$

Common Misconception: The Vertical Bar Means Division

The bar in $P(A\mid B)$ is read given. Division appears in the definition, but the notation itself represents conditioning.

Common Misconception: Independence Means Mutually Exclusive

Independence means one event does not change the probability of the other. Mutual exclusivity means the events cannot occur together.

Common Misconception: A Positive Test Gives the Sensitivity as the Posterior Probability

$P(+\mid D)$ and $P(D\mid+)$ reverse the conditioning. Bayes' theorem is required to connect them.

Common Misconception: The Condition Is Added to the Numerator Only

Conditioning changes the entire reference population. The denominator becomes the probability of the conditioning event.

Common Misconception: Conditional Probability Always Makes an Event More Likely

Conditioning may increase, decrease, or leave unchanged the probability of an event.

$$ \boxed{ P(A\mid B) \begin{cases} >P(A),\\ =P(A),\\

are all possible.

Common Misconception: Conditioning Proves Causation

A conditional relationship may arise from causal influence, common causes, selection effects, sampling structure, or other forms of statistical association.

Common Misconception: Conditioning on Probability Zero Uses the Same Formula

The elementary quotient requires a positive-probability conditioning event. Probability-zero conditioning requires more advanced constructions.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$P(A\mid B)$ "The probability of A given B."
$P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}$ "The probability of A given B equals the probability of A intersect B divided by the probability of B."
$P(A\cap B)=P(A\mid B)P(B)$ "The probability of A and B equals the probability of A given B times the probability of B."
$P(A\mid B)=P(A)$ "Conditioning on B does not change the probability of A."
$P(B\mid A)=\frac{P(A\mid B)P(B)}{P(A)}$ "The probability of B given A is obtained by Bayes' theorem."
$\mathbb E[X\mid Y]$ "The conditional expectation of X given Y."
$f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)$ "The conditional density of X given Y equals y."

The bar $\mid$ should normally be spoken as given in probability expressions. Thus $P(A\mid B)$ is not naturally read as “A divided by B,” “A such that B,” or “A implies B.”

Order matters in speech as much as in notation. “Probability of $A$ given $B$” means $P(A\mid B)$, while “probability of $B$ given $A$” means $P(B\mid A)$. Reversing the spoken phrase reverses the conditioning.

Defense Notation Advice: Identify the conditioning event before calculating and use it as the denominator in the elementary formula. Preserve the order of $A$ and $B$ in spoken and written forms. Distinguish conditional probability from logical implication, causal claims, and ordinary intersection probability. When $P(B)=0$, do not use the elementary quotient formula. In tables and counting problems, restrict to the conditioned population first and then count the outcomes satisfying the event of interest.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: $$ \boxed{ P(A\mid B) = \frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}, \qquad P(B)>0 }. $$
  • Core Interpretation: Conditioning on $B$ restricts the effective sample space to $B$.
  • Finite Counting Form: For equally likely outcomes, $P(A\mid B)=|A\cap B|/|B|$.
  • Directionality: $P(A\mid B)$ and $P(B\mid A)$ are generally different.
  • Multiplication Rule: $$ \boxed{ P(A\cap B)=P(A\mid B)P(B) }. $$
  • Chain Rule: Joint probabilities can be decomposed into a product of successive conditional probabilities.
  • Independence: If $A$ and $B$ are independent and $P(B)>0$, then $P(A\mid B)=P(A)$.
  • Intersection Form of Independence: $$ \boxed{ P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B) }. $$
  • Complement: $P(A^c\mid B)=1-P(A\mid B)$.
  • Total Probability: $$ \boxed{ P(A) = \sum_iP(A\mid B_i)P(B_i) } $$ for an appropriate partition $\{B_i\}$.
  • Bayes' Theorem: $$ \boxed{ P(B\mid A) = \frac{P(A\mid B)P(B)}{P(A)} }. $$
  • Bayesian Interpretation: Conditioning updates prior probabilities in light of observed information.
  • Base Rates: Posterior probabilities depend on prior prevalence as well as conditional evidence probabilities.
  • Contingency Tables: The denominator is the total count satisfying the conditioning event.
  • Tree Diagrams: Multiply probabilities along conditional paths and add probabilities across mutually exclusive paths.
  • Without Replacement: Later probabilities generally depend on earlier outcomes.
  • With Replacement: Repeated trials may remain independent when the probability structure is fully restored after each draw.
  • Conditional Distribution: Describes the distribution of one random variable after information about another is supplied.
  • Conditional Density: $$ \boxed{ f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y) = \frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_Y(y)} } $$ where the relevant denominator is positive.
  • Conditional Expectation: $\mathbb E[X\mid Y]$ describes expected value under supplied information.
  • Conditional Variance: $\operatorname{Var}(X\mid Y)$ measures remaining variability after conditioning.
  • Total Expectation: $$ \boxed{ \mathbb E[X] = \mathbb E[\mathbb E[X\mid Y]] }. $$
  • Conditional Independence: $A$ and $B$ may be independent relative to a condition $C$ even when they are not independent unconditionally.
  • Statistics: Conditional probability underlies Bayesian inference, regression, likelihood, stratification, graphical models, and many other methods.
  • Probability-Zero Condition: The elementary quotient formula is undefined when $P(B)=0$.
  • Causation Contrast: $P(A\mid B)$ describes probabilistic association under a condition and does not by itself establish that $B$ causes $A$.
  • Implication Contrast: $P(A\mid B)$ is not the logical statement $B\Rightarrow A$.
  • Intersection Contrast: $P(A\cap B)$ measures joint occurrence; $P(A\mid B)$ renormalizes that joint occurrence relative to $B$.
  • Mutual-Exclusivity Contrast: Mutually exclusive events cannot both occur, whereas independent events do not probabilistically affect one another.
  • Common Error: Do not reverse $P(A\mid B)$ and $P(B\mid A)$.
  • Common Error: Do not use $P(A)$ as the denominator when computing $P(A\mid B)$.
  • Common Error: Do not interpret the vertical bar as division.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse independence with mutual exclusivity.
  • Common Error: Do not ignore base rates when interpreting diagnostic or classification probabilities.
  • Common Error: Do not infer causation merely from a conditional association.
  • Common Error: Do not apply the elementary formula when the conditioning event has probability zero.
  • Terminological Contrast: Conditional probability is the probability of an event under specified information. Joint probability is the probability that events occur together. Marginal probability is an unconditional probability obtained without fixing another event. Independence means conditioning on one event does not change the probability of the other. Mutual exclusivity means the events cannot occur together. Bayes' theorem relates probabilities conditioned in opposite directions. Conditional expectation extends conditioning from events to expected values of random variables.
