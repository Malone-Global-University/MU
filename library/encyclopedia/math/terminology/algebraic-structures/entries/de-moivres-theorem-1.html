De Moivre’s Theorem

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

De Moivre’s Theorem, also commonly called De Moivre’s Formula, relates powers of complex numbers in polar form to multiples of their arguments.

In its standard form:

$$ \boxed{ (\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^n = \cos(n\theta)+i\sin(n\theta), \qquad n\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$

More generally, if a nonzero complex number is written in polar form:

$$ z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta), $$

then for every integer $n$:

$$ \boxed{ z^n = r^n \left[ \cos(n\theta)+i\sin(n\theta) \right] }. $$

The theorem is named for Abraham de Moivre, an eighteenth-century mathematician whose work contributed substantially to the development of complex-number methods, probability, and analysis.

Conceptually, the theorem expresses a remarkably simple rule: raising a complex number to an integer power raises its modulus to that power and multiplies its angle by the same integer.

$$ \boxed{ r\longrightarrow r^n, \qquad \theta\longrightarrow n\theta }. $$

Operational Nuance

Polar Form of a Complex Number

A nonzero complex number:

$$ z=x+iy $$

can be expressed as:

$$ \boxed{ z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta) }, $$

where:

$$ r=|z|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2} $$

is the modulus and $\theta$ is an argument of $z$.

Core Formula

For integer $n$:

$$ \boxed{ [r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)]^n = r^n [\cos(n\theta)+i\sin(n\theta)] }. $$

The theorem therefore separates exponentiation into two simpler operations:

  • raise the modulus to the power $n$, and
  • multiply the argument by $n$.

Example: Squaring a Complex Number

Let:

$$ z=2\left( \cos\frac{\pi}{6} + i\sin\frac{\pi}{6} \right). $$

Then:

$$ z^2 = 2^2 \left( \cos\frac{2\pi}{6} + i\sin\frac{2\pi}{6} \right). $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ z^2 = 4\left( \cos\frac{\pi}{3} + i\sin\frac{\pi}{3} \right) }. $$

Example: Third Power

Let:

$$ z=\cos\frac{\pi}{4} + i\sin\frac{\pi}{4}. $$

Then:

$$ z^3 = \cos\frac{3\pi}{4} + i\sin\frac{3\pi}{4}. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ z^3 = -\frac{\sqrt2}{2} + i\frac{\sqrt2}{2} }. $$

Negative Integer Powers

The theorem also holds for negative integers when $z\neq0$.

For example:

$$ [r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)]^{-1} = \frac1r [\cos(-\theta)+i\sin(-\theta)]. $$

Since:

$$ \cos(-\theta)=\cos\theta, \qquad \sin(-\theta)=-\sin\theta, $$

this becomes:

$$ \boxed{ z^{-1} = \frac1r (\cos\theta-i\sin\theta) }. $$

Why the Formula Works

Complex multiplication in polar form multiplies moduli and adds arguments:

$$ r_1(\cos\alpha+i\sin\alpha) \cdot r_2(\cos\beta+i\sin\beta) $$

equals:

$$ \boxed{ r_1r_2 [ \cos(\alpha+\beta) + i\sin(\alpha+\beta) ] }. $$

Repeated multiplication of the same complex number therefore adds the same angle repeatedly:

$$ \theta+\theta+\cdots+\theta=n\theta. $$

Connection with Euler’s Formula

Euler’s formula states:

$$ \boxed{ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta }. $$

Therefore:

$$ (e^{i\theta})^n=e^{in\theta}. $$

Converting back to trigonometric form yields:

$$ \boxed{ (\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^n = \cos(n\theta)+i\sin(n\theta) }. $$

Finding Complex Roots

De Moivre’s Theorem can be reversed to find the $n$th roots of a complex number.

Suppose:

$$ z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta). $$

The solutions of:

$$ w^n=z $$

are:

$$ \boxed{ w_k = r^{1/n} \left[ \cos\left( \frac{\theta+2\pi k}{n} \right) + i\sin\left( \frac{\theta+2\pi k}{n} \right) \right], \quad k=0,1,\ldots,n-1 }. $$

Why There Are $n$ Roots

The argument of a complex number is not unique:

$$ \theta,\; \theta+2\pi,\; \theta+4\pi,\ldots $$

represent the same direction.

Dividing these arguments by $n$ produces $n$ distinct directions before the pattern repeats.

Roots of Unity

The solutions of:

$$ z^n=1 $$

are:

$$ \boxed{ z_k = \cos\frac{2\pi k}{n} + i\sin\frac{2\pi k}{n}, \qquad k=0,1,\ldots,n-1 }. $$

These are the $n$th roots of unity.

Geometric Arrangement of Roots

The $n$th roots of a nonzero complex number all have modulus:

$$ r^{1/n} $$

and are separated by the constant angular spacing:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{2\pi}{n} }. $$

Thus they form the vertices of a regular $n$-gon centered at the origin in the complex plane.

Cube Roots of Unity

For:

$$ z^3=1, $$

the roots are:

$$ \boxed{ 1,\qquad -\frac12+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i,\qquad -\frac12-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i }. $$

They lie $120^\circ$ apart on the unit circle.

Trigonometric Identities from De Moivre

Expanding:

$$ (\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^n $$

algebraically and comparing real and imaginary parts with:

$$ \cos(n\theta)+i\sin(n\theta) $$

produces multiple-angle identities.

Double-Angle Example

For $n=2$:

$$ (\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^2 = \cos2\theta+i\sin2\theta. $$

Expanding the left side:

$$ \cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta + 2i\sin\theta\cos\theta. $$

Comparing real parts:

$$ \boxed{ \cos2\theta = \cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta }. $$

Comparing imaginary parts:

$$ \boxed{ \sin2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta }. $$

Triple-Angle Example

For $n=3$, comparison of real parts gives:

$$ \boxed{ \cos3\theta = \cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta\sin^2\theta }. $$

Using:

$$ \sin^2\theta=1-\cos^2\theta $$

gives:

$$ \boxed{ \cos3\theta = 4\cos^3\theta-3\cos\theta }. $$

Similarly:

$$ \boxed{ \sin3\theta = 3\sin\theta-4\sin^3\theta }. $$

Integer Exponents vs. Fractional Exponents

The standard theorem is stated cleanly for:

$$ \boxed{n\in\mathbb{Z}}. $$

Extending the notation directly to noninteger powers requires care because complex arguments are multivalued.

For example, writing:

$$ (\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^{1/n} $$

as though there were only one result hides the existence of $n$ distinct complex roots.

Common Misconception: De Moivre’s Theorem does not say that an $n$th root has only one complex value. Integer powers are single-valued, but reversing the operation to solve $w^n=z$ generally produces $n$ complex roots when $z\neq0$.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^n$ "Cosine theta plus i sine theta, raised to the nth power."
$\cos(n\theta)+i\sin(n\theta)$ "Cosine n theta plus i sine n theta."
$z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)$ "Z equals r times cosine theta plus i sine theta."
$z^n=r^n(\cos n\theta+i\sin n\theta)$ "Z to the nth power equals r to the nth power times cosine n theta plus i sine n theta."
$r^{1/n}$ "The nth root of r," or "r to the one over n power."
$\frac{\theta+2\pi k}{n}$ "Theta plus two pi k, divided by n."
$e^{i\theta}$ "E to the i theta."

The surname De Moivre is conventionally retained in the theorem’s name, and mathematical texts may call the result either:

$$ \boxed{ \text{De Moivre’s Theorem} } $$

or:

$$ \boxed{ \text{De Moivre’s Formula} }. $$

The expression:

$$ r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta) $$

is often shortened in some textbooks using the notation:

$$ \operatorname{cis}\theta = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta. $$

With this notation, De Moivre’s Theorem becomes:

$$ \boxed{ (\operatorname{cis}\theta)^n = \operatorname{cis}(n\theta) }. $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ (r\operatorname{cis}\theta)^n = r^n\operatorname{cis}(n\theta) }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: When finding roots, include the $2\pi k$ term before dividing the argument by $n$:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\theta+2\pi k}{n} }. $$

Omitting $2\pi k$ produces only one branch and loses the other complex roots.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Standard Statement: $$ \boxed{ (\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^n = \cos(n\theta)+i\sin(n\theta), \qquad n\in\mathbb{Z} }. $$
  • Polar Form: $$ \boxed{ [r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)]^n = r^n[\cos(n\theta)+i\sin(n\theta)] }. $$
  • Modulus Rule: $$ \boxed{ |z^n|=|z|^n }. $$
  • Argument Rule: If $\theta$ is an argument of $z$, then: $$ \boxed{ n\theta } $$ is an argument of $z^n$, modulo $2\pi$.
  • Integer Exponents: The elementary theorem applies directly and unambiguously for integer powers.
  • Zero Exponent: For nonzero $z$: $$ z^0=1, $$ consistent with: $$ \cos0+i\sin0=1. $$
  • Negative Powers: For $z\neq0$: $$ z^{-n}=\frac1{z^n}. $$
  • Complex Multiplication: Multiplication in polar form multiplies moduli and adds arguments.
  • Repeated Multiplication: De Moivre’s Theorem is the repeated-product version of the polar multiplication rule.
  • Euler Form: Since: $$ e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta, $$ De Moivre’s relation corresponds to: $$ \boxed{ (e^{i\theta})^n=e^{in\theta} }. $$
  • Power Computation: Polar form often makes high powers of complex numbers substantially easier to compute than repeated rectangular-form multiplication.
  • Root Formula: The $n$th roots of: $$ r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta) $$ are: $$ \boxed{ r^{1/n} \left[ \cos\left(\frac{\theta+2\pi k}{n}\right) + i\sin\left(\frac{\theta+2\pi k}{n}\right) \right] } $$ for: $$ k=0,\ldots,n-1. $$
  • Number of Roots: A nonzero complex number has exactly: $$ \boxed{n} $$ distinct $n$th roots.
  • Angular Separation: Consecutive $n$th roots are separated by: $$ \boxed{\frac{2\pi}{n}}. $$
  • Common Modulus of Roots: Every $n$th root of a number with modulus $r$ has modulus: $$ \boxed{r^{1/n}}. $$
  • Geometric Interpretation: The roots form a regular $n$-gon centered at the origin in the complex plane.
  • Roots of Unity: $$ \boxed{ z_k=e^{2\pi ik/n}, \qquad k=0,\ldots,n-1 }. $$
  • First Root of Unity: $$ \boxed{z_0=1}. $$
  • Cube Roots of Unity: $$ \boxed{ 1,\quad -\frac12+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i,\quad -\frac12-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i }. $$
  • Fourth Roots of Unity: $$ \boxed{ 1,\;i,\;-1,\;-i }. $$
  • Polynomial Connection: The $n$th roots of unity are precisely the roots of: $$ \boxed{ z^n-1=0 }. $$
  • Factorization: Over $\mathbb{C}$: $$ \boxed{ z^n-1 = \prod_{k=0}^{n-1} \left( z-e^{2\pi ik/n} \right) }. $$
  • Trigonometric Identities: Comparing real and imaginary parts of De Moivre expansions generates multiple-angle formulas.
  • Double-Angle Cosine: $$ \boxed{ \cos2\theta = \cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta }. $$
  • Double-Angle Sine: $$ \boxed{ \sin2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta }. $$
  • Triple-Angle Cosine: $$ \boxed{ \cos3\theta = 4\cos^3\theta-3\cos\theta }. $$
  • Triple-Angle Sine: $$ \boxed{ \sin3\theta = 3\sin\theta-4\sin^3\theta }. $$
  • Binomial Expansion: Expanding: $$ (\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^n $$ with the binomial theorem and equating components gives polynomial expressions for $\cos(n\theta)$ and $\sin(n\theta)$.
  • Real Part: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Re} [(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^n] = \cos(n\theta) }. $$
  • Imaginary Part: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Im} [(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^n] = \sin(n\theta) }. $$
  • Complex Plane Geometry: Raising a unit complex number to the $n$th power multiplies its angle by $n$ while leaving its modulus equal to $1$.
  • General Geometric Scaling: For modulus $r$, exponentiation changes radial distance from $r$ to: $$ r^n. $$
  • Rotation Interpretation: Multiplying by: $$ \cos\theta+i\sin\theta $$ corresponds to rotation through $\theta$ in the complex plane.
  • Repeated Rotation: Raising a unit complex number to the $n$th power corresponds to adding the same angular rotation $n$ times.
  • Complex Conjugation: $$ \overline{\cos\theta+i\sin\theta} = \cos\theta-i\sin\theta = \cos(-\theta)+i\sin(-\theta). $$
  • Reciprocal on the Unit Circle: For: $$ |z|=1, $$ $$ \boxed{ z^{-1}=\overline z }. $$
  • Cis Notation: If: $$ \operatorname{cis}\theta = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ (\operatorname{cis}\theta)^n = \operatorname{cis}(n\theta) }. $$
  • Exponential Notation: $$ \boxed{ (re^{i\theta})^n = r^ne^{in\theta} } $$ for integer $n$.
  • Branch Caution: Complex fractional powers require choices of argument or consideration of multiple values; they should not be treated as naively single-valued extensions of the integer-power theorem.
  • Principal Argument: A chosen principal argument can define a principal branch of a complex power, but this selects one value from a generally multivalued relation.
  • Algebra: De Moivre’s Theorem provides an efficient bridge between rectangular and polar treatments of complex-number powers.
  • Trigonometry: The theorem provides a systematic derivation of multiple-angle identities.
  • Geometry: It interprets complex exponentiation as simultaneous radial scaling and angular multiplication.
  • Polynomial Theory: The root formula gives explicit geometric descriptions of solutions to equations of the form: $$ z^n=a. $$
  • Fourier Analysis: Roots of unity and complex exponentials derived from the same structure play central roles in discrete Fourier methods.
  • Terminological Contrast: De Moivre’s Theorem usually refers to the integer-power identity, while the associated root formula is obtained by applying the same polar-angle reasoning in reverse.
  • Core Interpretation: De Moivre’s Theorem reduces complex exponentiation in polar form to a power of the modulus and multiplication of the argument: $$ \boxed{ [r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)]^n = r^n [\cos(n\theta)+i\sin(n\theta)] }. $$ It is therefore a central connection among complex multiplication, trigonometry, powers, roots, and rotation in the complex plane.
