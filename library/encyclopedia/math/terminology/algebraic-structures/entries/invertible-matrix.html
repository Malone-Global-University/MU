Invertible Matrix

Subfield: Algebraic Structures

Etymology & Roots

An invertible matrix is a square matrix that possesses a multiplicative inverse. If $A$ is invertible, there exists a matrix $A^{-1}$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I }. $$

Here $I$ is the identity matrix of the same size.

The adjective invertible comes from the broader mathematical idea of an inverse: an object that reverses another operation and restores the relevant identity. For matrices, the operation being reversed is matrix multiplication.

$$ \boxed{ A \xrightarrow{\ A^{-1}\ } I }. $$

The term is therefore structural rather than merely computational. An invertible matrix represents a reversible linear transformation: no information is collapsed, every output has exactly one input, and the original vector can be recovered.

Traditional synonyms include:

  • nonsingular matrix;
  • regular matrix in some older or specialized texts;
  • invertible linear operator when the matrix is viewed as a transformation.

The standard contrasting term is singular matrix, meaning a square matrix with no ordinary multiplicative inverse.

Operational Nuance

Core Definition

A square matrix $A$ is invertible if there exists a square matrix $B$ of the same size satisfying:

$$ \boxed{ AB=BA=I }. $$

That matrix $B$ is unique and is denoted:

$$ \boxed{ A^{-1} }. $$

Square-Matrix Requirement

An ordinary two-sided matrix inverse exists only for square matrices.

Thus an $m\times n$ matrix with $m\neq n$ cannot possess an ordinary inverse satisfying both:

$$ AB=I \qquad\text{and}\qquad BA=I $$

with identity matrices of compatible dimensions.

Uniqueness of the Inverse

Suppose both $B$ and $C$ are inverses of $A$. Then:

$$ B = BI = B(AC) = (BA)C = IC = C. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \text{an inverse matrix, when it exists, is unique} }. $$

Two-by-Two Formula

For:

$$ A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b\\ c & d \end{pmatrix}, $$

the determinant is:

$$ \det(A)=ad-bc. $$

If:

$$ \boxed{ ad-bc\neq0 }, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad-bc} \begin{pmatrix} d & -b\\ -c & a \end{pmatrix} }. $$

Why the Determinant Must Be Nonzero

The two-by-two inverse formula requires division by $\det(A)$. If the determinant is zero, the formula fails because division by zero is undefined.

General Determinant Criterion

For an $n\times n$ matrix over a field:

$$ \boxed{ A \text{ invertible} \iff \det(A)\neq0 }. $$

Singular Matrix

$$ \boxed{ \det(A)=0 \iff A \text{ is singular} }. $$

Identity Matrix

The identity matrix is invertible and is its own inverse:

$$ \boxed{ I^{-1}=I }. $$

Diagonal Matrix

A diagonal matrix:

$$ D = \operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_n) $$

is invertible exactly when every diagonal entry is nonzero.

$$ \boxed{ D^{-1} = \operatorname{diag} \left( \frac{1}{d_1}, \ldots, \frac{1}{d_n} \right) }. $$

Triangular Matrix

A triangular matrix is invertible exactly when all of its diagonal entries are nonzero.

$$ \boxed{ A \text{ triangular and invertible} \iff a_{11}a_{22}\cdots a_{nn}\neq0 }. $$

Permutation Matrix

Every permutation matrix is invertible.

$$ \boxed{ P^{-1}=P^T }. $$

Orthogonal Matrix

A real matrix $Q$ is orthogonal when:

$$ Q^TQ=I. $$

Therefore every orthogonal matrix is invertible and:

$$ \boxed{ Q^{-1}=Q^T }. $$

Unitary Matrix

Over the complex numbers, a unitary matrix $U$ satisfies:

$$ U^{*}U=I. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ U^{-1}=U^{*} }. $$

Row Reduction Criterion

A square matrix is invertible exactly when it row reduces to the identity matrix:

$$ \boxed{ A \sim I }. $$

Reduced Row-Echelon Form

For a square matrix:

$$ \boxed{ A \text{ invertible} \iff \operatorname{rref}(A)=I }. $$

Computing the Inverse by Gauss-Jordan Elimination

Form the augmented matrix:

$$ \boxed{ [A\mid I] }. $$

Apply elementary row operations until the left side becomes $I$:

$$ \boxed{ [A\mid I] \longrightarrow [I\mid A^{-1}] }. $$

Failure during Gauss-Jordan Elimination

If the left block cannot be reduced to $I$, then $A$ is singular and no ordinary inverse exists.

Elementary Matrices

Every elementary row operation corresponds to multiplication by an elementary matrix.

Every elementary matrix is invertible because every elementary row operation can be reversed.

Inverse of an Elementary Matrix

The inverse is the elementary matrix corresponding to the inverse row operation.

Product of Invertible Matrices

If $A$ and $B$ are invertible, then $AB$ is invertible.

$$ \boxed{ (AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1} }. $$

Reverse Order Rule

The order reverses because inverses undo operations in reverse sequence.

$$ (AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = A(BB^{-1})A^{-1} = I. $$

Longer Product

$$ \boxed{ (ABC)^{-1} = C^{-1}B^{-1}A^{-1} }. $$

Inverse of an Inverse

$$ \boxed{ (A^{-1})^{-1} = A }. $$

Inverse of a Scalar Multiple

For nonzero scalar $c$ and invertible $A$:

$$ \boxed{ (cA)^{-1} = \frac{1}{c}A^{-1} }. $$

Inverse of a Power

For integer $n>0$:

$$ \boxed{ (A^n)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^n }. $$

Negative Matrix Powers

If $A$ is invertible:

$$ \boxed{ A^{-n} = (A^{-1})^n }. $$

Transpose of an Inverse

$$ \boxed{ (A^{-1})^T = (A^T)^{-1} }. $$

Adjoint of an Inverse

For complex matrices:

$$ \boxed{ (A^{-1})^{*} = (A^{*})^{-1} }. $$

Determinant of an Inverse

$$ \boxed{ \det(A^{-1}) = \frac{1}{\det(A)} }. $$

Adjugate Formula

If $\det(A)\neq0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(A)} \operatorname{adj}(A) }. $$

This formula is theoretically important, though direct row-reduction or numerical factorization is often preferable computationally for larger matrices.

Linear-System Interpretation

Consider:

$$ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}. $$

If $A$ is invertible, multiply by $A^{-1}$:

$$ A^{-1}A\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b} }. $$

Unique Solution for Every Right-Hand Side

A square matrix $A$ is invertible exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b} \text{ has exactly one solution for every }\mathbf{b} }. $$

Homogeneous System Criterion

A square matrix is invertible exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0} } $$

has only the trivial solution:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0} }. $$

Null-Space Criterion

$$ \boxed{ A \text{ invertible} \iff \operatorname{Null}(A) = \{\mathbf{0}\} }. $$

Nullity Criterion

$$ \boxed{ A \text{ invertible} \iff \operatorname{nullity}(A)=0 }. $$

Rank Criterion

For an $n\times n$ matrix:

$$ \boxed{ A \text{ invertible} \iff \operatorname{rank}(A)=n }. $$

Pivot Criterion

An $n\times n$ matrix is invertible exactly when it has a pivot in every row and every column.

Column Independence

$$ \boxed{ A \text{ invertible} \iff \text{columns of }A \text{ are linearly independent} }. $$

Column Span

$$ \boxed{ A \text{ invertible} \iff \operatorname{Col}(A) = \mathbb{F}^n }. $$

Basis Criterion

The columns of an invertible $n\times n$ matrix form a basis for $\mathbb{F}^n$.

Row Independence

The rows of an invertible square matrix are likewise linearly independent and form a basis of the corresponding row space.

Linear Transformation Interpretation

A square matrix $A$ defines:

$$ T(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x}. $$

The matrix is invertible exactly when $T$ is bijective.

$$ \boxed{ A \text{ invertible} \iff T \text{ is one-to-one and onto} }. $$

Injectivity

If $A$ is invertible:

$$ A\mathbf{x}_1 = A\mathbf{x}_2 $$

implies:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x}_1 = \mathbf{x}_2 }. $$

Surjectivity

For every vector $\mathbf{b}$, there exists:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b} } $$

satisfying $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$.

Geometric Meaning

An invertible linear transformation may stretch, shear, rotate, reflect, or otherwise transform space, but it does not collapse the dimension.

No Collapse to a Lower Dimension

A singular matrix can flatten a plane into a line or a three-dimensional region into a plane. An invertible matrix cannot.

Volume Scaling

The absolute determinant gives the factor by which an invertible matrix scales $n$-dimensional volume:

$$ \boxed{ \text{volume scale} = |\det(A)| }. $$

Nonzero Volume Factor

Invertibility corresponds geometrically to:

$$ \boxed{ |\det(A)|>0 }. $$

Orientation

For real matrices:

  • $\det(A)>0$ preserves orientation;
  • $\det(A)<0$ reverses orientation.

Both cases are invertible because the determinant is nonzero.

Eigenvalue Criterion

A square matrix is invertible exactly when $0$ is not an eigenvalue.

$$ \boxed{ A \text{ invertible} \iff 0 \text{ is not an eigenvalue of }A }. $$

Why Zero Eigenvalue Causes Singularity

If:

$$ A\mathbf{v} = 0\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0} $$

for some nonzero $\mathbf{v}$, then the null space is nontrivial and $A$ cannot be invertible.

Eigenvalues of the Inverse

If:

$$ A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v} $$

with $\lambda\neq0$, then:

$$ \boxed{ A^{-1}\mathbf{v} = \frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbf{v} }. $$

Thus eigenvalues invert multiplicatively.

Invertible Matrix Theorem

Many conditions for a square matrix are equivalent. Common versions of the Invertible Matrix Theorem state that the following are equivalent:

  • $A$ is invertible;
  • $\operatorname{rref}(A)=I$;
  • $A$ has a pivot in every row and column;
  • $\operatorname{rank}(A)=n$;
  • $\operatorname{Null}(A)=\{\mathbf{0}\}$;
  • the columns of $A$ are linearly independent;
  • the columns span $\mathbb{F}^n$;
  • the columns form a basis of $\mathbb{F}^n$;
  • $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ has a unique solution for every $\mathbf{b}$;
  • $\det(A)\neq0$;
  • $0$ is not an eigenvalue of $A$;
  • the associated linear transformation is bijective.

Invertibility and Change of Basis

A change-of-basis matrix must be invertible because coordinates must be recoverable in both directions.

$$ \boxed{ [\mathbf{v}]_{\mathcal{C}} = P^{-1} [\mathbf{v}]_{\mathcal{B}} } $$

for an appropriate invertible change-of-basis matrix $P$.

Similarity Transformations

Matrices $A$ and $B$ are similar when:

$$ \boxed{ B=P^{-1}AP } $$

for some invertible matrix $P$.

Invertibility under Similarity

Similar matrices are either both invertible or both singular.

Diagonalization

If:

$$ A=PDP^{-1}, $$

then $P$ must be invertible because its columns form a basis of eigenvectors.

Inverse of a Diagonalization

If $A$ is invertible and:

$$ A=PDP^{-1}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ A^{-1} = PD^{-1}P^{-1} }. $$

Invertibility and LU Factorization

When:

$$ A=LU, $$

and both triangular factors are invertible:

$$ \boxed{ A^{-1} = U^{-1}L^{-1} }. $$

Solving Systems without Explicitly Computing the Inverse

Although:

$$ \mathbf{x}=A^{-1}\mathbf{b} $$

is mathematically correct, numerical computation often solves $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ through elimination or matrix factorization rather than explicitly forming $A^{-1}$.

Why Explicit Inversion May Be Avoided Numerically

Direct factorization is often more efficient and may reduce unnecessary numerical error.

Conditioning

An invertible matrix may nevertheless be close to singular.

Such a matrix can be ill-conditioned, meaning small changes in data may cause large changes in the computed solution.

Condition Number

In a compatible matrix norm, a common condition number is:

$$ \boxed{ \kappa(A) = \|A\| \|A^{-1}\| }. $$

Well-Conditioned vs. Ill-Conditioned

A smaller condition number generally indicates more stable inversion, while a very large condition number indicates sensitivity.

Invertible Does Not Mean Numerically Easy

Exact invertibility and numerical stability are different questions.

Inverse Depends Continuously Away from Singularity

Small perturbations of a well-conditioned invertible matrix generally produce small changes in its inverse, while behavior can become unstable near the singular boundary.

General Linear Group

The set of all invertible $n\times n$ matrices over a field $\mathbb{F}$ forms the general linear group:

$$ \boxed{ GL(n,\mathbb{F}) }. $$

Group Operation

The operation is matrix multiplication.

Identity in the General Linear Group

$$ \boxed{ I_n }. $$

Inverse in the General Linear Group

$$ \boxed{ A^{-1} }. $$

Closure

Products of invertible matrices remain invertible, so $GL(n,\mathbb{F})$ is closed under matrix multiplication.

Noncommutativity

In general:

$$ AB\neq BA. $$

Therefore inversion must respect order:

$$ \boxed{ (AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1} }. $$

Left Inverse and Right Inverse for Square Matrices

For a square matrix over a field, if a matrix $B$ satisfies:

$$ BA=I, $$

then $A$ is invertible and:

$$ \boxed{ B=A^{-1} }. $$

Likewise, a right inverse is enough to imply ordinary invertibility for square matrices.

Rectangular One-Sided Inverses

Rectangular matrices may possess left or right inverses under appropriate rank conditions, even though they cannot possess an ordinary two-sided inverse.

Left Inverse of a Tall Matrix

If an $m\times n$ matrix has full column rank with $m\geq n$, a left inverse may exist.

Right Inverse of a Wide Matrix

If an $m\times n$ matrix has full row rank with $m\leq n$, a right inverse may exist.

Pseudoinverse

A rectangular or singular matrix may have a Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse:

$$ \boxed{ A^{+} }. $$

The pseudoinverse is a generalized inverse and should not be confused with the ordinary inverse $A^{-1}$.

Invertibility over Different Number Systems

Whether a matrix is invertible can depend on the scalar system.

For example, a matrix may be invertible over $\mathbb{R}$ but its inverse may not have integer entries, so it is not invertible as a matrix over the ring $\mathbb{Z}$ in the same sense.

Integer Matrices

An integer matrix has an inverse with integer entries exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A)=\pm1 }. $$

Such matrices are called unimodular.

Finite Fields

Over a finite field, the determinant criterion remains:

$$ \boxed{ \det(A)\neq0 } $$

where nonzero is interpreted within that field.

Polynomial Matrices

In more advanced algebra, invertibility may be considered over polynomial rings, rational-function fields, or other coefficient structures. The appropriate notion of a unit in the coefficient ring becomes important.

Geometric Transformations

Rotation, reflection, shear, and nonzero scaling transformations in matching dimensions are represented by invertible matrices when they preserve dimension.

Rotation Matrix

$$ R_\theta = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}. $$

Since:

$$ \det(R_\theta)=1, $$

every planar rotation matrix is invertible.

Inverse Rotation

$$ \boxed{ R_\theta^{-1} = R_{-\theta} = R_\theta^T }. $$

Scaling Matrix

A diagonal scaling matrix:

$$ S = \begin{pmatrix} s_x & 0\\ 0 & s_y \end{pmatrix} $$

is invertible exactly when:

$$ \boxed{ s_x\neq0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad s_y\neq0 }. $$

Zero Scaling Destroys Invertibility

If one scale factor is zero, an entire dimension is collapsed and the original vectors cannot be recovered uniquely.

Shear Matrix

A standard planar shear:

$$ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & k\\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} $$

has determinant $1$ and is invertible for every scalar $k$.

Computer Graphics

Invertible transformation matrices allow coordinate transformations to be undone, which is essential when converting between world, object, camera, and other coordinate systems.

Change of Coordinates

If:

$$ \mathbf{y}=A\mathbf{x}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{x}=A^{-1}\mathbf{y} } $$

whenever $A$ is invertible.

Cryptography

Some matrix-based ciphers require a key matrix that is invertible modulo a chosen integer, allowing decryption by multiplying by the modular inverse matrix.

Control Theory

Invertible matrices appear in coordinate transformations, state-space models, and transformations preserving system equivalence.

Statistics

Matrix inverses appear in covariance calculations, linear models, and least-squares formulas when the relevant matrices are nonsingular.

Normal Equations

In full-column-rank least squares:

$$ \boxed{ \hat{\mathbf{x}} = (A^TA)^{-1}A^T\mathbf{b} }. $$

This requires $A^TA$ to be invertible, which occurs when the columns of $A$ are linearly independent.

Covariance Matrix

A positive-definite covariance matrix is invertible. Its inverse is often called the precision matrix.

Positive-Definite Matrices

Every real symmetric positive-definite matrix is invertible because all of its eigenvalues are positive.

Positive-Semidefinite Caveat

A positive-semidefinite matrix need not be invertible because zero eigenvalues are permitted.

Inverse and Adjugate

The classical adjugate identity is:

$$ \boxed{ A\operatorname{adj}(A) = \operatorname{adj}(A)A = \det(A)I }. $$

When $\det(A)\neq0$, division by $\det(A)$ yields the inverse.

Common Error: Assuming Every Square Matrix Is Invertible

Square shape is necessary but not sufficient. A square matrix can still be singular.

Common Error: Assuming Every Matrix Has an Inverse

Rectangular matrices do not possess ordinary two-sided inverses, and singular square matrices possess none.

Common Error: Confusing Reciprocal with Matrix Inverse

In general:

$$ \boxed{ A^{-1} \neq \left( \frac{1}{a_{ij}} \right) }. $$

Matrix inversion is not performed by taking reciprocals of individual entries.

Common Error: Forgetting Product Order

$$ \boxed{ (AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1} } $$

rather than $A^{-1}B^{-1}$ in general.

Common Error: Using the Two-by-Two Formula when the Determinant Is Zero

Division by $ad-bc$ is valid only when $ad-bc\neq0$.

Common Error: Confusing Nonzero Determinant with Positive Determinant

A negative determinant still gives an invertible matrix.

Common Error: Treating Row Equivalence as Equality

Row reducing $A$ to $I$ proves invertibility, but the matrices encountered during row reduction are not generally equal to $A$.

Common Error: Computing an Explicit Inverse When Only a System Solution Is Needed

Although valid algebraically, forming $A^{-1}$ may be less efficient than solving the system directly.

Common Error: Confusing Invertibility with Good Numerical Conditioning

An invertible matrix can still be extremely ill-conditioned.

Common Error: Assuming Nonzero Entries Guarantee Invertibility

A matrix may have every entry nonzero and still have linearly dependent rows or columns.

Common Error: Assuming a Zero Entry Makes a Matrix Singular

Many invertible matrices contain zero entries, including the identity matrix.

Common Error: Confusing One-Sided and Two-Sided Inverses

Rectangular matrices may have a left or right inverse without possessing an ordinary two-sided inverse.

Common Error: Confusing the Pseudoinverse with the Ordinary Inverse

$A^{+}$ is a generalized inverse with different defining properties from $A^{-1}$.

Common Error: Assuming Determinant Alone Measures Numerical Stability

A nonzero determinant establishes exact invertibility, but numerical sensitivity is better analyzed using conditioning and singular values.

Common Misconception: An invertible matrix is not merely a square matrix for which someone happens to know an inverse formula. Invertibility is a structural property equivalent to full rank, trivial null space, nonzero determinant, linear independence of columns, unique solvability of $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ for every $\mathbf{b}$, and bijectivity of the associated linear transformation. All of these descriptions express the same central fact: the transformation loses no information and can be reversed uniquely.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$A^{-1}$ "A inverse; the inverse matrix of A."
$AA^{-1}=A^{-1}A=I$ "A times A inverse and A inverse times A both equal the identity."
$\det(A)\neq0$ "The determinant of A is nonzero, so A is invertible."
$\operatorname{rank}(A)=n$ "A has full rank."
$\operatorname{Null}(A)=\{\mathbf{0}\}$ "The null space of A contains only the zero vector."
$[A\mid I]\to[I\mid A^{-1}]$ "Use Gauss-Jordan elimination to compute the inverse."
$(AB)^{-1}=B^{-1}A^{-1}$ "The inverse of a product reverses the order of the factors."

The phrases invertible matrix and nonsingular matrix are ordinarily synonymous for square matrices over a field.

The notation:

$$ A^{-1} $$

means inverse under matrix multiplication. It does not mean entrywise reciprocation.

The phrase full rank must be interpreted relative to dimensions. For a square $n\times n$ matrix, full rank means rank $n$, which is equivalent to invertibility.

Defense Notation Advice: Verify that the matrix is square before discussing an ordinary two-sided inverse, and establish invertibility before writing $A^{-1}$. Use $\det(A)\neq0$, full rank, row reduction, or another equivalent criterion as appropriate. Never compute the inverse by taking reciprocals entry by entry, and remember that inverses of products reverse factor order.
