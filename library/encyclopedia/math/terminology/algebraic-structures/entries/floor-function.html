Floor Function

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

The floor function assigns to each real number the greatest integer that is less than or equal to that number.

The word floor uses a spatial metaphor. If a real number lies between two consecutive integers, its floor is the integer immediately below it on the number line. Thus the "floor" acts like the lower integer boundary beneath the number.

The function is written using special brackets:

$$ \lfloor x\rfloor. $$

It is also historically and pedagogically called the greatest integer function, although that phrase can be less descriptive when first encountered. The defining property is:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor = \text{greatest integer }n\text{ such that }n\le x }. $$

The floor function should be distinguished from ordinary rounding. It does not select the nearest integer. Instead, it always selects the greatest integer that does not exceed the input.

Operational Nuance

Formally:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor=n \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad n\le x

for some integer:

$$ n\in\mathbb{Z}. $$

For example:

$$ \lfloor 4.7\rfloor=4, $$ $$ \lfloor 9.001\rfloor=9, $$ $$ \lfloor 6\rfloor=6. $$

If the input is already an integer, the floor leaves it unchanged:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor n\rfloor=n \qquad (n\in\mathbb{Z}) }. $$

Negative Inputs

Negative values are where the floor function is most often misunderstood. Consider:

$$ x=-2.3. $$

The integers less than or equal to $-2.3$ include:

$$ \ldots,-5,-4,-3. $$

The greatest of these is:

$$ -3. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor-2.3\rfloor=-3 }. $$

It is not $-2$, because:

$$ -2>-2.3, $$

so $-2$ does not satisfy the defining requirement.

Interval Behavior

For each integer $n$:

$$ \lfloor x\rfloor=n $$

throughout the half-open interval:

$$ \boxed{ [n,n+1) }. $$

Thus:

$$ \lfloor x\rfloor=2 \qquad \text{for }2\le x<3. $$

At $x=3$, the function jumps to:

$$ \lfloor3\rfloor=3. $$

The graph therefore consists of horizontal steps.

Fundamental Inequality

For every real number $x$:

$$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor \le x < \lfloor x\rfloor+1 }. $$

Equivalently:

$$ \boxed{ x-1<\lfloor x\rfloor\le x }. $$

Common Misconception: The floor function is not "drop the decimal part." That shortcut works for positive numbers but fails for negative nonintegers. For instance:

$$ \lfloor-5.8\rfloor=-6, $$

not $-5$.

$n\le x "N is less than or equal to x, and x is less than n plus one."

Floor brackets:

$$ \lfloor\ \rfloor $$

have horizontal bars along the bottom. This visually distinguishes them from the ceiling brackets:

$$ \lceil\ \rceil, $$

which have horizontal bars along the top.

Defense Notation Advice: Ordinary square brackets:

$$ [x] $$

have been used in some contexts for greatest-integer notation, but that notation can conflict with interval notation, equivalence classes, matrices, and other uses. Modern floor brackets:

$$ \lfloor x\rfloor $$

are substantially clearer.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Greatest Integer Function: The floor function is often called the greatest integer function because: $$ \lfloor x\rfloor = \max\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:n\le x\}. $$
  • Domain & Range: The floor function has domain: $$ \mathbb{R} $$ and range: $$ \mathbb{Z}. $$ Thus: $$ \lfloor\cdot\rfloor: \mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{Z}. $$
  • Step Function: The floor function is constant on each interval: $$ [n,n+1) $$ and jumps by $1$ at every integer.
  • Discontinuities: The floor function is discontinuous at every integer and continuous at every noninteger real number.
  • Right Continuity: At each integer $n$, the floor function is right-continuous: $$ \lim_{x\to n^+}\lfloor x\rfloor=n, $$ but: $$ \lim_{x\to n^-}\lfloor x\rfloor=n-1. $$
  • Monotonicity: If: $$ x\le y, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor \le \lfloor y\rfloor }. $$ Thus the floor function is nondecreasing.
  • Integer Translation: For any integer $n$: $$ \boxed{ \lfloor x+n\rfloor = \lfloor x\rfloor+n }. $$
  • Idempotence: Since $\lfloor x\rfloor$ is already an integer: $$ \boxed{ \left\lfloor\lfloor x\rfloor\right\rfloor = \lfloor x\rfloor }. $$
  • Fractional Part: The fractional part of $x$ is defined by: $$ \boxed{ \{x\} = x-\lfloor x\rfloor }. $$ It satisfies: $$ 0\le\{x\}<1. $$
  • Negative Fractional Parts: Under this definition: $$ \{-2.3\} = -2.3-\lfloor-2.3\rfloor $$ $$ = -2.3-(-3) $$ $$ =0.7. $$ Thus the fractional part is still nonnegative.
  • Ceiling Function: The ceiling function: $$ \lceil x\rceil $$ gives the least integer greater than or equal to $x$.
  • Floor-Ceiling Relationship: A useful identity is: $$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor = -\lceil-x\rceil }. $$ Equivalently: $$ \lceil x\rceil = -\lfloor-x\rfloor. $$
  • When x Is Not an Integer: If: $$ x\notin\mathbb{Z}, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ \lceil x\rceil = \lfloor x\rfloor+1 }. $$
  • When x Is an Integer: If: $$ x=n\in\mathbb{Z}, $$ then: $$ \lfloor n\rfloor = \lceil n\rceil = n. $$
  • Floor of a Sum: In general: $$ \lfloor x+y\rfloor $$ is not always equal to: $$ \lfloor x\rfloor+\lfloor y\rfloor. $$ However: $$ \boxed{ \lfloor x\rfloor+\lfloor y\rfloor \le \lfloor x+y\rfloor \le \lfloor x\rfloor+\lfloor y\rfloor+1 }. $$
  • Counting Multiples: For positive integers $n$ and $d$, the number of positive multiples of $d$ not exceeding $n$ is: $$ \boxed{ \left\lfloor\frac nd\right\rfloor }. $$ For example, the number of multiples of $7$ not exceeding $100$ is: $$ \left\lfloor\frac{100}{7}\right\rfloor=14. $$
  • Integer Division: For positive integers $a$ and $b$: $$ \left\lfloor\frac ab\right\rfloor $$ gives the integer quotient in Euclidean division: $$ a=bq+r, $$ where: $$ q=\left\lfloor\frac ab\right\rfloor $$ and: $$ 0\le r
  • Computer Science: Floor operations appear in indexing, discretization, pagination, bucket assignment, pixel calculations, and algorithms that convert continuous or scaled quantities into integer categories.
  • Number Theory: Floor expressions occur extensively in formulas involving divisibility, counting lattice points, prime-factor exponents, sums over integers, and asymptotic estimates.
  • Legendre's Formula: The exponent of a prime $p$ in $n!$ is: $$ \boxed{ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \left\lfloor\frac{n}{p^k}\right\rfloor }. $$ Only finitely many terms are nonzero.

  • Terminological Contrast: The floor function returns the greatest integer not exceeding $x$. The ceiling function returns the least integer not below $x$. Ordinary rounding typically selects a nearest integer, while truncation usually removes digits after a chosen place and therefore behaves differently from floor for negative numbers.
