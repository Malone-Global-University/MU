Modulo n

Subfield: Algebraic & Analytic Structures

Etymology & Roots

Modulo $n$ describes arithmetic performed with respect to a fixed positive integer $n$, called the modulus. Two integers are congruent modulo $n$ when they differ by an integer multiple of $n$.

The standard notation is:

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv b\pmod n }. $$

This is read:

$$ \text{"a is congruent to b modulo n."} $$

The word modulo belongs to the terminology of modular arithmetic. The number $n$ is the modulus, and the equivalence relationship created by that modulus partitions the integers into a finite collection of residue classes.

Carl Friedrich Gauss systematically introduced congruence terminology and the symbol:

$$ \equiv $$

in his 1801 Disquisitiones Arithmeticae. His notation made it possible to treat divisibility relationships algebraically rather than repeatedly stating that one integer divides the difference of two others.

The phrase modulo $n$ should be distinguished from the computational expression:

$$ a\bmod n. $$

In many contexts, $a\bmod n$ denotes a particular remainder, while $a\equiv b\pmod n$ expresses a relation between two integers. These ideas are closely connected but not syntactically identical.

Operational Nuance

Definition of Congruence Modulo n

Let $n$ be a positive integer. Then:

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv b\pmod n \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad n\mid(a-b) }. $$

In words, $a$ and $b$ are congruent modulo $n$ precisely when $n$ divides their difference.

Equivalently, there exists an integer $k$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ a-b=kn }. $$

Example

Consider:

$$ 17\equiv5\pmod{12}. $$

This is true because:

$$ 17-5=12 $$

and:

$$ 12\mid12. $$

Likewise:

$$ 29\equiv5\pmod{12} $$

because:

$$ 29-5=24=2(12). $$

Remainder Interpretation

For integers $a$ and positive $n$, the division algorithm gives:

$$ a=qn+r, $$

where:

$$ 0\le r

Therefore:

$$ a-r=qn, $$

so:

$$ \boxed{ a\equiv r\pmod n }. $$

Thus two integers are congruent modulo $n$ exactly when they have the same canonical remainder after division by $n$.

Example: Modulo 5

Since:

$$ 23=4(5)+3, $$

we have:

$$ 23\bmod5=3 $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ 23\equiv3\pmod5 }. $$

Negative Integers

The congruence relation works naturally with negative numbers. For example:

$$ -2\equiv3\pmod5 $$

because:

$$ -2-3=-5 $$

is divisible by $5$.

Under the common mathematical convention that the remainder lies in:

$$ \{0,1,2,\ldots,n-1\}, $$

we therefore write:

$$ -2\bmod5=3. $$

Some programming languages adopt different conventions for remainder operations with negative operands, so mathematical modulo notation and programming-language operators should not automatically be assumed to behave identically.

Clock Arithmetic

A familiar model is a twelve-hour clock. Advancing $5$ hours from $10$ o'clock gives:

$$ 10+5=15. $$

But:

$$ 15\equiv3\pmod{12}. $$

Therefore the clock reads:

$$ 3. $$

Common Misconception: The statement:

$$ 17\equiv5\pmod{12} $$

does not mean that $17=5$. It means that the two integers belong to the same congruence class modulo $12$.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$a\equiv b\pmod n$ "A is congruent to b modulo n."
$a\bmod n$ "A mod n," commonly meaning the remainder of a upon division by n.
$n\mid(a-b)$ "N divides a minus b."
$a-b=kn$ "A minus b equals k times n for some integer k."
$[a]_n$ "The residue class of a modulo n."
$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ "The integers modulo n," or "Z mod n Z."

There are two closely related uses of mod notation.

In a congruence:

$$ 17\equiv5\pmod{12}, $$

the phrase:

$$ \pmod{12} $$

specifies the modulus under which the congruence is being interpreted.

In an operation:

$$ 17\bmod12=5, $$

mod behaves like a binary operation producing a remainder or canonical representative.

Defense Notation Advice: The symbol:

$$ \equiv $$

should not be casually replaced by:

$$ =. $$

Ordinary equality and congruence modulo $n$ express different relationships.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Divisibility Definition: The fundamental equivalence is: $$ \boxed{ a\equiv b\pmod n \iff n\mid(a-b) }. $$
  • Same-Remainder Definition: Two integers are congruent modulo $n$ if they produce the same remainder upon division by $n$ under the standard remainder convention.
  • Residues: Every integer is congruent modulo $n$ to exactly one integer in: $$ \boxed{ 0,1,2,\ldots,n-1 }. $$ These are the standard least nonnegative residue representatives.
  • Residue Classes: The class: $$ [a]_n $$ consists of all integers congruent to $a$ modulo $n$: $$ [a]_n = \{a+kn:k\in\mathbb{Z}\}. $$
  • Example of a Residue Class: Modulo $4$: $$ [1]_4 = \{\ldots,-7,-3,1,5,9,13,\ldots\}. $$ Every member differs from every other by a multiple of $4$.
  • Partition of the Integers: Modulo $n$, the integers separate into exactly $n$ residue classes: $$ [0]_n,[1]_n,\ldots,[n-1]_n. $$
  • Equivalence Relation: Congruence modulo $n$ is reflexive: $$ a\equiv a\pmod n, $$ symmetric: $$ a\equiv b\pmod n \Longrightarrow b\equiv a\pmod n, $$ and transitive: $$ a\equiv b\pmod n,\quad b\equiv c\pmod n \Longrightarrow a\equiv c\pmod n. $$
  • Addition: If: $$ a\equiv b\pmod n $$ and: $$ c\equiv d\pmod n, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ a+c\equiv b+d\pmod n }. $$
  • Subtraction: Under the same hypotheses: $$ \boxed{ a-c\equiv b-d\pmod n }. $$
  • Multiplication: Congruence is preserved under multiplication: $$ \boxed{ ac\equiv bd\pmod n }. $$
  • Powers: If: $$ a\equiv b\pmod n, $$ then for every nonnegative integer $k$: $$ \boxed{ a^k\equiv b^k\pmod n }. $$
  • Polynomial Substitution: If: $$ a\equiv b\pmod n, $$ then for any polynomial $P(x)$ with integer coefficients: $$ \boxed{ P(a)\equiv P(b)\pmod n }. $$
  • Reduction Before Computation: Large values may be replaced by congruent smaller residues. For example: $$ 103\equiv3\pmod{10} $$ and: $$ 87\equiv7\pmod{10}, $$ so: $$ 103\cdot87 \equiv 3\cdot7 \equiv 21 \equiv 1 \pmod{10}. $$
  • Last Digit: Modulo $10$ determines the final decimal digit of an integer: $$ N\bmod10. $$
  • Last Two Digits: Modulo $100$ determines the final two decimal digits.
  • Parity: Modulo $2$ separates integers into: $$ [0]_2 $$ and: $$ [1]_2. $$ These correspond to even and odd integers.
  • Divisibility: An integer $a$ is divisible by $n$ exactly when: $$ \boxed{ a\equiv0\pmod n }. $$
  • Cancellation Caution: From: $$ ac\equiv bc\pmod n, $$ one cannot always cancel $c$. Cancellation is valid in the familiar form when: $$ \gcd(c,n)=1. $$ For example: $$ 2\cdot1\equiv2\cdot4\pmod6 $$ because: $$ 2\equiv8\pmod6, $$ but: $$ 1\not\equiv4\pmod6. $$
  • Multiplicative Inverse: An integer $a$ has a multiplicative inverse modulo $n$ precisely when: $$ \boxed{ \gcd(a,n)=1 }. $$ In that case there exists $b$ such that: $$ ab\equiv1\pmod n. $$
  • Example of an Inverse: Modulo $7$: $$ 3\cdot5=15\equiv1\pmod7, $$ so: $$ 3^{-1}\equiv5\pmod7. $$
  • Division in Modular Arithmetic: Division is performed by multiplying by a modular inverse when that inverse exists. It is therefore not automatically available for every nonzero residue.
  • The Ring of Integers Modulo n: The residue classes form the algebraic structure: $$ \boxed{ \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} }. $$ Addition and multiplication are performed modulo $n$.
  • Prime Modulus: If $p$ is prime, then: $$ \boxed{ \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} } $$ is a field. Every nonzero residue class therefore has a multiplicative inverse.
  • Composite Modulus: If $n$ is composite, $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ contains zero divisors and is not a field.
  • Zero Divisor Example: Modulo $6$: $$ 2\cdot3\equiv0\pmod6, $$ even though neither: $$ 2\equiv0\pmod6 $$ nor: $$ 3\equiv0\pmod6. $$
  • Linear Congruences: Equations such as: $$ ax\equiv b\pmod n $$ are called linear congruences. Their solvability depends on: $$ \gcd(a,n). $$
  • Fermat's Little Theorem: If $p$ is prime and: $$ p\nmid a, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p }. $$
  • Euler's Theorem: If: $$ \gcd(a,n)=1, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ a^{\varphi(n)} \equiv1\pmod n }, $$ where $\varphi$ is Euler's totient function.
  • Chinese Remainder Theorem: Systems such as: $$ x\equiv a\pmod m $$ and: $$ x\equiv b\pmod n $$ can often be combined into a single congruence when the moduli satisfy suitable coprimality conditions.
  • Cryptography: Modern public-key cryptographic systems, including RSA, rely heavily on modular exponentiation, modular inverses, prime moduli or composite moduli, and the arithmetic of residue classes.
  • Computer Science: Modulo operations are used for cyclic indexing, hash tables, circular buffers, scheduling, pseudorandom generators, parity checks, and wrapping values into a fixed numerical range.
  • Angles: Angles differing by a whole number of complete turns represent the same direction: $$ \theta \equiv \theta+2\pi k \pmod{2\pi}. $$ Thus modular thinking naturally appears in periodic geometry and trigonometry.
  • Clock Arithmetic: Hours on a twelve-hour clock are naturally modeled modulo $12$, while minutes and seconds are commonly modeled modulo $60$.
  • Calendar Cycles: Weekdays form a seven-day cycle, so calculations involving shifts by whole weeks can be modeled modulo $7$.
  • Terminological Contrast: The modulus $n$ specifies the cycle or equivalence relation. Congruence modulo $n$ compares integers by divisibility of their difference. A residue is a representative of a congruence class, while the expression $a\bmod n$ commonly denotes the selected remainder of $a$ upon division by $n$.
