Average

Subfield: Probability & Statistical Inference

Etymology & Roots

In mathematics, average is a broad term for a value intended to summarize, represent, or describe the center or typical magnitude of a collection of values.

In ordinary mathematical conversation, an unspecified average most commonly means the arithmetic mean:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{x_1+x_2+\cdots+x_n}{n} }. $$

The word itself, however, is broader than this one formula. Depending on context, an average may be a:

  • arithmetic mean,
  • weighted mean,
  • geometric mean,
  • harmonic mean,
  • median or other measure of center in informal language,
  • expected value,
  • average rate,
  • average value of a function,
  • moving average.

Therefore the word average should not always be treated as the name of one uniquely determined mathematical operation.

$$ \boxed{ \text{average} = \text{context-dependent representative value} }. $$

Operational Nuance

Arithmetic Mean as the Default Average

When no other qualification is given, average usually refers to the arithmetic mean.

$$ \boxed{ \text{average} = \frac{\text{sum of values}}{\text{number of values}} }. $$

For values $x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n$:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i }. $$

Basic Example

For:

$$ 4,\ 7,\ 9,\ 12, $$

the arithmetic average is:

$$ \bar{x} = \frac{4+7+9+12}{4} = \frac{32}{4} = \boxed{8}. $$

Equal-Sharing Interpretation

The arithmetic average can be interpreted as the amount each observation would have if the total were redistributed equally.

If:

$$ x_1+\cdots+x_n=S, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ S=n\bar{x} }. $$

Thus the same total can be represented by $n$ equal quantities of size $\bar{x}$.

Average as a Balance Point

The arithmetic mean also balances the signed deviations of the observations:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\bar{x})=0 }. $$

Values above the mean balance values below it.

Median as a Different Center

The median is the middle observation after the data are ordered. It is not calculated by adding all observations and dividing by their count.

For:

$$ 2,\ 3,\ 4,\ 5,\ 100, $$

the median is:

$$ \boxed{4}, $$

while the arithmetic mean is:

$$ \boxed{22.8}. $$

This illustrates why the intended meaning of average matters.

Mode

The mode is the most frequently occurring value or category. It measures frequency rather than numerical balance.

For:

$$ 1,\ 2,\ 2,\ 2,\ 7, $$

the mode is:

$$ \boxed{2}. $$

In technical writing, it is better to say mode rather than simply average.

Weighted Average

When observations do not contribute equally, a weighted average may be appropriate.

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x}_w = \frac{ \sum_{i=1}^{n}w_ix_i }{ \sum_{i=1}^{n}w_i } }. $$

The weights $w_i$ determine the relative contribution of each value.

Weights that Sum to One

If:

$$ \sum_{i=1}^{n}w_i=1, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x}_w = \sum_{i=1}^{n}w_ix_i }. $$

Grade Average

Suppose assignments count for $20\%$, exams for $50\%$, and a project for $30\%$. If the corresponding scores are $90$, $80$, and $100$, then:

$$ \bar{x}_w = 0.20(90) + 0.50(80) + 0.30(100). $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x}_w=88 }. $$

Frequency Average

When value $x_i$ appears $f_i$ times:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{\sum f_ix_i}{\sum f_i} }. $$

Frequency is acting as weight.

Combined Average

Suppose a group of $n_1$ observations has average $\bar{x}_1$ and another group of $n_2$ observations has average $\bar{x}_2$.

The combined average is:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{ n_1\bar{x}_1+n_2\bar{x}_2 }{ n_1+n_2 } }. $$

Average of Averages

It is generally incorrect to compute:

$$ \frac{\bar{x}_1+\bar{x}_2}{2} $$

unless the two groups contain the same number of observations or are deliberately intended to receive equal weight.

The correct combined calculation uses the group sizes as weights.

Recovering a Total from an Average

If $n$ observations have arithmetic average $\bar{x}$:

$$ \boxed{ \text{total} = n\bar{x} }. $$

Finding a Missing Value

Suppose six values have average $10$. Their total is:

$$ 6(10)=60. $$

If five known values total $47$, the missing value is:

$$ \boxed{ 60-47=13 }. $$

Adding a Constant to Every Value

If:

$$ y_i=x_i+c, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{y} = \bar{x}+c }. $$

Scaling Every Value

If:

$$ y_i=cx_i, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{y} = c\bar{x} }. $$

Linear Transformation

More generally:

$$ y_i=ax_i+b $$

gives:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{y} = a\bar{x}+b }. $$

The Arithmetic Average Lies within the Data Range

For a finite nonempty collection of real numbers:

$$ \boxed{ \min(x_i) \le \bar{x} \le \max(x_i) }. $$

The Average Need Not Be an Observed Value

For:

$$ 1,\ 2,\ 6, $$

the arithmetic average is:

$$ \boxed{ 3 }. $$

The value $3$ need not have appeared among the original observations.

Sensitivity to Outliers

The arithmetic mean uses every value and is therefore sensitive to very large or very small observations.

The median is generally less sensitive to extreme observations.

Consequently, asking for the "best average" is really asking which summary measure best represents the data and the purpose of the analysis.

Geometric Mean

For positive values:

$$ \boxed{ G= \sqrt[n]{x_1x_2\cdots x_n} }. $$

The geometric mean is particularly useful for multiplicative growth factors and compounded proportional changes.

Harmonic Mean

For positive values:

$$ \boxed{ H= \frac{n}{ \frac1{x_1}+\frac1{x_2}+\cdots+\frac1{x_n} } }. $$

The harmonic mean often appears when averaging rates under equal-distance or equivalent reciprocal-weight conditions.

Quadratic Mean

The quadratic mean, also called the root-mean-square or RMS, is:

$$ \boxed{ x_{\mathrm{RMS}} = \sqrt{ \frac{x_1^2+\cdots+x_n^2}{n} } }. $$

It emphasizes magnitude and is especially useful when signs or oscillations would otherwise cancel.

Expected Value as a Probability-Weighted Average

For a discrete random variable $X$:

$$ \boxed{ E[X] = \sum_x xP(X=x) }. $$

The probabilities act as weights.

Expected value is therefore a weighted average of possible outcomes, although it need not itself be an outcome that can occur.

Continuous Expected Value

If a continuous random variable has probability density $f(x)$:

$$ \boxed{ E[X] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} xf(x)\,dx } $$

when the integral exists.

Average Speed

Average speed is not generally the arithmetic mean of several speeds.

It is defined by:

$$ \boxed{ v_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{\text{total distance}} {\text{total time}} }. $$

Why Speeds Cannot Always Be Averaged Directly

Suppose a traveler moves for one hour at $30$ km/h and three hours at $60$ km/h.

Total distance is:

$$ 30(1)+60(3)=210\text{ km}. $$

Total time is:

$$ 4\text{ h}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ v_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{210}{4} = 52.5\text{ km/h} }. $$

The simple arithmetic mean:

$$ \frac{30+60}{2}=45 $$

would incorrectly give the two speeds equal time weights.

Average Velocity

Average velocity is:

$$ \boxed{ \mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{\text{displacement}} {\text{elapsed time}} }. $$

Because displacement is directional, average velocity and average speed need not be equal.

Average Rate of Change

For a function $f$ on the interval $[a,b]$, the average rate of change is:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} }. $$

Geometrically, this is the slope of the secant line through:

$$ (a,f(a)) \qquad\text{and}\qquad (b,f(b)). $$

Average Rate versus Average Value

The average rate of change:

$$ \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} $$

measures how rapidly the function changes across an interval.

The average value of the function instead measures the function's average vertical value across that interval.

Average Value of a Function

For an integrable function $f$ on $[a,b]$:

$$ \boxed{ f_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{1}{b-a} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx }. $$

This is the continuous analogue of adding values and dividing by how much input range is represented.

Geometric Interpretation of Average Function Value

If:

$$ f_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{1}{b-a} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ f_{\mathrm{avg}}(b-a) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx }. $$

Thus a rectangle of width $b-a$ and height $f_{\mathrm{avg}}$ has the same signed area as the region represented by the integral.

Moving Average

A moving average computes averages over successive overlapping windows of data.

For a window of $k$ observations:

$$ \boxed{ M_n = \frac{ x_{n-k+1}+\cdots+x_n }{k} }. $$

Moving averages are commonly used to smooth short-term variation in time-series data.

Running Average

If $\bar{x}_n$ is the average of the first $n$ observations and a new value $x_{n+1}$ arrives:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x}_{n+1} = \frac{ n\bar{x}_n+x_{n+1} }{ n+1 } }. $$

This permits an average to be updated without recomputing the entire original sum.

Average Percentage

Percentages should not always be averaged directly. If percentages come from groups with different sizes, their denominators determine appropriate weights.

For example, if one class has $10$ students with an $80\%$ pass rate and another has $90$ students with a $60\%$ pass rate, the overall pass percentage is not:

$$ \frac{80\%+60\%}{2}=70\%. $$

Instead, use the actual counts:

$$ \frac{ 10(0.80)+90(0.60) }{100} = \frac{62}{100}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ 62\% }. $$

Units of an Arithmetic Average

An arithmetic average has the same physical units as the observations.

If:

$$ x_i\text{ is measured in meters}, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x}\text{ is measured in meters} }. $$

Averaging Unlike Quantities

Values generally should not be combined into a simple arithmetic average unless they represent compatible quantities or a meaningful normalization has been defined.

For example, directly averaging:

$$ 5\text{ meters} \quad\text{and}\quad 8\text{ seconds} $$

has no ordinary interpretation.

Rounding an Average

Intermediate values should ordinarily retain sufficient precision, with rounding delayed until the final result unless a problem specifies otherwise.

Premature rounding can alter the reported average.

Average and Typical Are Not Identical

A numerical average is a precisely defined summary once its method is specified. The word typical is more interpretive.

A mean may be mathematically correct yet poorly represent what is typical when data are strongly skewed or contain extreme outliers.

Average and Expected Are Not Identical

An expected value is an average defined through probabilities. It does not mean the outcome expected to occur on any single trial.

For a fair six-sided die:

$$ E[X] = \frac{1+2+3+4+5+6}{6} = \boxed{3.5}. $$

A single die roll can never equal $3.5$.

Common Misconception

The instruction "find the average" is not always mathematically complete. If context does not clearly establish the arithmetic mean, the intended averaging method should be identified.

$$ \boxed{ \text{always ask what kind of average the context requires} }. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\bar{x}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i$ "X-bar is the arithmetic average of the observations."
$\bar{x}_w=\frac{\sum w_ix_i}{\sum w_i}$ "The weighted average equals the weighted total divided by the total weight."
$E[X]=\sum_x xP(X=x)$ "The expected value of X is the probability-weighted average of its possible values."
$v_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{d_{\mathrm{total}}}{t_{\mathrm{total}}}$ "Average speed equals total distance divided by total time."
$\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$ "The average rate of change from a to b."
$f_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac1{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ "The average value of f on the interval from a to b."

In everyday speech, average often functions as shorthand for arithmetic mean. In technical writing, the specific statistic should be named whenever multiple interpretations are plausible.

Defense Notation Advice: Do not mechanically add quantities and divide by how many numbers are visible. First determine what is being weighted: observations, time, distance, probability, population size, interval length, or some other quantity.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Broad Meaning: An average is a representative or central value whose precise definition depends on context.
  • Default Mathematical Meaning: Unqualified average usually means arithmetic mean.
  • Arithmetic Mean: $$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i }. $$
  • Equal-Sharing Interpretation: The arithmetic average redistributes the same total equally across the observations.
  • Total from Average: $$ \boxed{ \sum x_i=n\bar{x} }. $$
  • Balance Property: $$ \boxed{ \sum(x_i-\bar{x})=0 }. $$
  • Median: A positional measure of center rather than a sum-and-divide operation.
  • Mode: The most frequently occurring value or category.
  • Weighted Average: $$ \boxed{ \bar{x}_w= \frac{\sum w_ix_i}{\sum w_i} }. $$
  • Frequency Average: Frequencies may serve as weights.
  • Combined Average: $$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac{ n_1\bar{x}_1+n_2\bar{x}_2 }{ n_1+n_2 } }. $$
  • Average-of-Averages Caution: Group averages should not normally be averaged without accounting for group sizes.
  • Geometric Mean: $$ \boxed{ G= (x_1x_2\cdots x_n)^{1/n} }. $$
  • Harmonic Mean: $$ \boxed{ H= \frac{n}{\sum 1/x_i} }. $$
  • Quadratic Mean: $$ \boxed{ x_{\mathrm{RMS}} = \sqrt{\frac1n\sum x_i^2} }. $$
  • Expected Value: $$ \boxed{ E[X] = \sum_x xP(X=x) } $$ for a discrete random variable.
  • Average Speed: $$ \boxed{ v_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{\text{total distance}}{\text{total time}} }. $$
  • Average Velocity: $$ \boxed{ \mathbf v_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{\text{displacement}}{\text{elapsed time}} }. $$
  • Average Rate of Change: $$ \boxed{ \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} }. $$
  • Average Function Value: $$ \boxed{ f_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac1{b-a} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx }. $$
  • Moving Average: An average calculated over successive windows of observations.
  • Running Average: $$ \boxed{ \bar{x}_{n+1} = \frac{n\bar{x}_n+x_{n+1}}{n+1} }. $$
  • Linear Translation: $$ \boxed{ \overline{x+c} = \bar{x}+c }. $$
  • Scaling: $$ \boxed{ \overline{cx} = c\bar{x} }. $$
  • Range Property: The arithmetic mean of real observations lies between their minimum and maximum.
  • Observed-Value Caution: An average need not occur among the original observations.
  • Outlier Sensitivity: The arithmetic mean is sensitive to extreme values; other measures of center may be more robust.
  • Percentage Caution: Percentages from unequal-sized groups generally require weighted combination.
  • Units: An arithmetic average retains the units of the quantities being averaged.
  • Compatibility Caution: Unlike physical quantities cannot ordinarily be averaged meaningfully without a defined model or conversion.
  • Common Error: Average speed is not generally the simple arithmetic mean of several speeds.
  • Common Error: An expected value need not be an outcome that can actually occur.
  • Common Error: Average and typical are related ideas but are not mathematically identical.
  • Common Error: Do not assume every occurrence of the word average means arithmetic mean when the surrounding mathematics defines another averaging process.
  • Terminological Contrast: Average is the broad umbrella term. Arithmetic mean is its most common numerical interpretation. Median uses ordered position. Mode uses frequency. Weighted mean allows unequal contributions. Average rate summarizes accumulated change relative to the quantity over which that change occurs.
