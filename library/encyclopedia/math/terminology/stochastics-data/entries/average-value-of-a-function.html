Average Value of a Function

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

The average value of a function is a single constant that represents the mean height of an integrable function across an interval.

For a function $f$ on $[a,b]$, where $a

$$ \boxed{ f_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{1}{b-a} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx }. $$

This definition is the continuous counterpart of the arithmetic mean:

$$ \bar{x} = \frac{x_1+x_2+\cdots+x_n}{n}. $$

In a finite data set, the total is divided by the number of observations. For a function defined across a continuous interval, the accumulated value is divided by the interval length:

$$ \boxed{ \text{average value} = \frac{\text{accumulated function value}} {\text{interval length}} }. $$

The word average therefore retains its equal-sharing interpretation: the function's total signed accumulation is redistributed uniformly across the interval.

Operational Nuance

Core Formula

If $f$ is integrable on $[a,b]$ and $a

$$ \boxed{ f_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{1}{b-a} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx }. $$

Why Divide by b - a?

The definite integral measures the accumulated value of $f$ across the interval. Dividing by:

$$ b-a $$

normalizes that accumulation by the length of the interval.

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = (b-a)f_{\mathrm{avg}} }. $$

Geometric Interpretation

The value $f_{\mathrm{avg}}$ is the height of a rectangle having width $b-a$ and the same signed area as the definite integral of $f$.

$$ \boxed{ \text{rectangle area} = (b-a)f_{\mathrm{avg}} = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx }. $$

Signed Area Matters

The definite integral is a signed accumulation. Portions of the graph below the $x$-axis contribute negatively.

Therefore average value is not necessarily:

$$ \frac{\text{total geometric area}}{b-a}. $$

If geometric area rather than signed accumulation is intended, the relevant expression is instead:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{1}{b-a} \int_a^b |f(x)|\,dx }. $$

Basic Example

Find the average value of:

$$ f(x)=x^2 $$

on:

$$ [0,3]. $$

Apply the formula:

$$ f_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac13 \int_0^3x^2\,dx. $$

Since:

$$ \int_0^3x^2\,dx = \left[ \frac{x^3}{3} \right]_0^3 = 9, $$

we obtain:

$$ \boxed{ f_{\mathrm{avg}}=3 }. $$

Constant Functions

If:

$$ f(x)=c, $$

then:

$$ f_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{1}{b-a} \int_a^b c\,dx. $$

Since:

$$ \int_a^b c\,dx = c(b-a), $$

it follows that:

$$ \boxed{ f_{\mathrm{avg}}=c }. $$

Linear Functions

For:

$$ f(x)=mx+c, $$

the average value over $[a,b]$ is:

$$ \boxed{ f_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{f(a)+f(b)}{2} }. $$

This endpoint-average shortcut works because a linear graph changes uniformly.

Derivation for a Linear Function

Begin with:

$$ f_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{1}{b-a} \int_a^b(mx+c)\,dx. $$

Integrating:

$$ f_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{ \frac{m}{2}(b^2-a^2)+c(b-a) }{b-a}. $$

Since:

$$ b^2-a^2=(b-a)(a+b), $$

this simplifies to:

$$ f_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{m(a+b)}{2}+c = \frac{f(a)+f(b)}{2}. $$

Average Value Is Not Usually the Endpoint Average

For nonlinear functions:

$$ \boxed{ f_{\mathrm{avg}} \ne \frac{f(a)+f(b)}{2} } $$

in general.

For example, $f(x)=x^2$ on $[0,3]$ has:

$$ f_{\mathrm{avg}}=3, $$

while:

$$ \frac{f(0)+f(3)}{2} = \frac{0+9}{2} = 4.5. $$

Mean Value Theorem for Integrals

If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, then there exists at least one $c\in[a,b]$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ f(c) = \frac{1}{b-a} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx }. $$

In other words:

$$ \boxed{ f(c)=f_{\mathrm{avg}} }. $$

A continuous function therefore actually attains its average value somewhere on the interval.

Integral Form of the Mean Value Theorem

The same theorem is often written:

$$ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = f(c)(b-a) } $$

for some $c\in[a,b]$.

Bounds on the Average Value

If:

$$ m\le f(x)\le M $$

throughout $[a,b]$, then:

$$ \boxed{ m \le f_{\mathrm{avg}} \le M }. $$

Thus an ordinary average value cannot lie outside the function's range bounds on the interval.

Positive Functions

If:

$$ f(x)\ge0 $$

throughout $[a,b]$, then:

$$ \boxed{ f_{\mathrm{avg}}\ge0 }. $$

Functions with Positive and Negative Values

Positive and negative portions can cancel.

For example:

$$ f(x)=x $$

on:

$$ [-1,1] $$

has:

$$ \int_{-1}^{1}x\,dx=0. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ f_{\mathrm{avg}}=0 }. $$

Odd-Function Symmetry

If $f$ is odd:

$$ f(-x)=-f(x), $$

then on a symmetric interval $[-a,a]$:

$$ \int_{-a}^{a}f(x)\,dx=0. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ f_{\mathrm{avg}}=0 }. $$

Even-Function Symmetry

If:

$$ f(-x)=f(x), $$

then:

$$ \int_{-a}^{a}f(x)\,dx = 2\int_0^a f(x)\,dx. $$

Since the interval length is $2a$:

$$ \boxed{ f_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac1a \int_0^a f(x)\,dx }. $$

Average Value over Adjacent Intervals

Suppose:

$$ a

If the average values over $[a,c]$ and $[c,b]$ are $A_1$ and $A_2$, then the average over $[a,b]$ is:

$$ \boxed{ A = \frac{ (c-a)A_1+(b-c)A_2 }{ b-a } }. $$

The interval lengths act as weights.

Average of Interval Averages

It is generally incorrect to write:

$$ \frac{A_1+A_2}{2} $$

unless the two subintervals have equal lengths or equal weighting is intentionally desired.

Translation of a Function

If:

$$ g(x)=f(x)+c, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ g_{\mathrm{avg}} = f_{\mathrm{avg}}+c }. $$

Scaling a Function

If:

$$ g(x)=cf(x), $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ g_{\mathrm{avg}} = c f_{\mathrm{avg}} }. $$

Linearity

If:

$$ h(x)=\alpha f(x)+\beta g(x), $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ h_{\mathrm{avg}} = \alpha f_{\mathrm{avg}} + \beta g_{\mathrm{avg}} }. $$

This follows directly from linearity of the definite integral.

Discrete Analogue

For discrete values:

$$ x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n, $$

the arithmetic mean is:

$$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i }. $$

Compare this with:

$$ \boxed{ f_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac1{b-a} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx }. $$

The sum becomes an integral, and the number of equally weighted observations is replaced by the length of the continuous interval.

Riemann-Sum Interpretation

Divide $[a,b]$ into $n$ equal subintervals of width:

$$ \Delta x= \frac{b-a}{n}. $$

A Riemann approximation gives:

$$ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \approx \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i^*)\Delta x. $$

Divide by $b-a=n\Delta x$:

$$ f_{\mathrm{avg}} \approx \frac{ \sum f(x_i^*)\Delta x }{ n\Delta x }. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ f_{\mathrm{avg}} \approx \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_i^*) }. $$

As the partition becomes finer, the discrete sample average approaches the continuous average under suitable conditions.

Weighted Average Value

Sometimes different input locations should not contribute equally. With a nonnegative weight function $w(x)$, a weighted average value may be defined as:

$$ \boxed{ f_{\mathrm{wavg}} = \frac{ \int_a^b f(x)w(x)\,dx }{ \int_a^b w(x)\,dx } } $$

provided the denominator is nonzero.

Probability Connection

If $f_X(x)$ is a probability density function, then:

$$ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_X(x)\,dx=1. $$

The expected value:

$$ \boxed{ E[X] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x f_X(x)\,dx } $$

is a probability-weighted continuous average of possible values of $X$.

Average Temperature

If $T(t)$ gives temperature over a time interval $[a,b]$, then:

$$ \boxed{ T_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac1{b-a} \int_a^b T(t)\,dt }. $$

This represents the time-average temperature over the interval.

Average Velocity from Velocity Function

If $v(t)$ is velocity, its average value over $[t_1,t_2]$ is:

$$ v_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac1{t_2-t_1} \int_{t_1}^{t_2}v(t)\,dt. $$

Since:

$$ \int_{t_1}^{t_2}v(t)\,dt = s(t_2)-s(t_1), $$

this becomes:

$$ \boxed{ v_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{ s(t_2)-s(t_1) }{ t_2-t_1 } }. $$

Thus average velocity can be viewed either as the average value of the velocity function or as the average rate of change of the position function.

Average Speed from a Speed Function

If $v(t)$ is signed velocity, speed is $|v(t)|$. Therefore average speed is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{average speed} = \frac1{t_2-t_1} \int_{t_1}^{t_2}|v(t)|\,dt }. $$

Average Power

If instantaneous power is $P(t)$ over a time interval $[a,b]$, then:

$$ \boxed{ P_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac1{b-a} \int_a^b P(t)\,dt }. $$

Periodic Functions

If $f$ has period $T$, its average value over one full period is:

$$ \boxed{ f_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac1T \int_{a}^{a+T}f(x)\,dx }. $$

For a periodic integrable function, this average is independent of which complete period is selected.

Sine over a Full Period

Since positive and negative portions cancel:

$$ \int_0^{2\pi}\sin x\,dx=0. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ (\sin x)_{\mathrm{avg}}=0 } $$

over any complete period.

Cosine over a Full Period

Similarly:

$$ \boxed{ (\cos x)_{\mathrm{avg}}=0 } $$

over a complete period.

Average Value of sin²x

Using:

$$ \sin^2x = \frac{1-\cos2x}{2}, $$

the cosine term averages to zero over a complete period. Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ (\sin^2x)_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac12 }. $$

Average Value versus Average Rate of Change

These terms are related linguistically but represent different mathematical ideas.

Average value:

$$ \boxed{ \frac1{b-a} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx } $$

measures the mean height of the function.

Average rate of change:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} } $$

measures net output change per unit input.

Units

The average value of a function has the same units as the function itself.

If $f(x)$ is measured in meters, then:

$$ \boxed{ f_{\mathrm{avg}} \text{ is measured in meters} }. $$

The integral has units of:

$$ (\text{function units})(\text{input units}), $$

and dividing by interval length removes the input units.

Improper Intervals

On an unbounded interval, the ordinary finite-interval formula does not apply directly because the interval length is infinite.

A long-run average may instead be defined, when the limit exists, by expressions such as:

$$ \boxed{ \lim_{T\to\infty} \frac1T \int_0^T f(x)\,dx }. $$

This is a distinct limiting concept and should not be assumed to exist for every function.

Common Misconception

The average value of a function is not generally obtained by averaging only its two endpoint values:

$$ \frac{f(a)+f(b)}{2}. $$

The correct general formula accounts for the entire interval:

$$ \boxed{ f_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac1{b-a} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx }. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$f_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac1{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ "The average value of f on the interval from a to b."
$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=(b-a)f_{\mathrm{avg}}$ "The integral equals the interval length times the average value."
$f(c)=f_{\mathrm{avg}}$ "At c, the function attains its average value."
$\frac1{b-a}\int_a^b|f(x)|\,dx$ "The average magnitude of f over the interval."
$\frac{\int_a^b f(x)w(x)\,dx}{\int_a^b w(x)\,dx}$ "The weighted average value of f with weight function w."
$\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$ "The average rate of change, not the average value."

The notation $f_{\mathrm{avg}}$ is descriptive rather than universally mandatory. Authors may use other symbols as long as the quantity is clearly defined.

Defense Notation Advice: Distinguish carefully between average value and average rate of change. Average value requires a definite integral divided by interval length; average rate of change requires an endpoint difference quotient.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Formula: $$ \boxed{ f_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac1{b-a} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx }. $$
  • Accumulation Form: $$ \boxed{ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = (b-a)f_{\mathrm{avg}} }. $$
  • Geometric Meaning: The average value is the constant height of a rectangle with the same signed integral over the interval.
  • Signed-Area Caution: Values below the $x$-axis contribute negatively.
  • Average Magnitude: $$ \boxed{ \frac1{b-a} \int_a^b|f(x)|\,dx } $$ averages magnitude rather than signed value.
  • Constant Function: If $f(x)=c$, then $f_{\mathrm{avg}}=c$.
  • Linear Function: $$ \boxed{ f_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{f(a)+f(b)}{2} } $$ for linear $f$.
  • Nonlinear Caution: Endpoint averaging is not valid in general.
  • Mean Value Theorem for Integrals: For continuous $f$, some $c\in[a,b]$ satisfies: $$ \boxed{ f(c)=f_{\mathrm{avg}} }. $$
  • Range Bound: If $m\le f(x)\le M$, then: $$ \boxed{ m\le f_{\mathrm{avg}}\le M }. $$
  • Odd Symmetry: An odd function has average value zero on a symmetric interval.
  • Even Symmetry: $$ \boxed{ f_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac1a \int_0^a f(x)\,dx } $$ on $[-a,a]$ for even $f$.
  • Adjacent Intervals: Interval averages combine using interval lengths as weights.
  • Translation: If $g=f+c$, then: $$ \boxed{ g_{\mathrm{avg}} = f_{\mathrm{avg}}+c }. $$
  • Scaling: If $g=cf$, then: $$ \boxed{ g_{\mathrm{avg}} = cf_{\mathrm{avg}} }. $$
  • Linearity: Average values respect linear combinations.
  • Discrete Analogue: $$ \boxed{ \bar{x} = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i }. $$
  • Riemann-Sum Connection: Continuous average value arises as the limit of averages of increasingly dense function samples under appropriate conditions.
  • Weighted Average: $$ \boxed{ \frac{ \int_a^b f(x)w(x)\,dx }{ \int_a^b w(x)\,dx } }. $$
  • Probability Connection: Expected value is a weighted continuous average using probability density.
  • Average Temperature: Time-average temperature is obtained by integrating temperature and dividing by elapsed time.
  • Average Velocity: The average value of the velocity function equals displacement divided by time.
  • Average Speed: Average speed uses the average value of $|v(t)|$ rather than signed velocity.
  • Periodic Function: $$ \boxed{ f_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac1T \int_a^{a+T}f(x)\,dx } $$ over a full period.
  • Sine and Cosine: Both have average value zero over any complete period.
  • Sine Squared: $$ \boxed{ (\sin^2x)_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac12 } $$ over a complete period.
  • Units: Average value has the same units as the function itself.
  • Average-Rate Contrast: $$ \frac1{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx $$ measures mean function height, while: $$ \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} $$ measures net change per unit input.
  • Long-Run Average: On an unbounded domain, a limiting average such as: $$ \boxed{ \lim_{T\to\infty} \frac1T\int_0^T f(x)\,dx } $$ may be considered when the limit exists.
  • Common Error: Do not forget to divide the definite integral by the interval length $b-a$.
  • Common Error: Do not replace the integral with the average of only the endpoint values unless the function is known to be linear or another argument specifically justifies it.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse signed average value with average magnitude.
  • Terminological Contrast: Average value of a function is the mean function height over an interval. Definite integral gives the accumulated signed value. Average rate of change gives endpoint output change per input change. Mean Value Theorem for Integrals guarantees that a continuous function attains its average value somewhere on the interval.
