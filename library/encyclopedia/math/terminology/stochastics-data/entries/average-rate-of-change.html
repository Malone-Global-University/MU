Average Rate of Change

Subfield: Calculus & Mathematical Analysis

Etymology & Roots

The average rate of change of a function measures how much the function's output changes, on average, for each unit of change in the input over a specified interval.

For a function $f$ on an interval from $x=a$ to $x=b$, with $a\ne b$, the average rate of change is:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} }. $$

The numerator:

$$ f(b)-f(a) $$

measures the change in output, while the denominator:

$$ b-a $$

measures the change in input.

$$ \boxed{ \text{average rate of change} = \frac{\text{change in output}} {\text{change in input}} }. $$

The phrase average is important because the function need not change at a constant rate throughout the interval. The formula summarizes the net change across the entire interval with a single rate.

Operational Nuance

Core Formula

For $a\ne b$:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{AROC}_{[a,b]}(f) = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} }. $$

The abbreviation AROC is sometimes used informally for average rate of change, though it is not universal notation.

Delta Notation

The same relationship can be written:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} }. $$

where:

$$ \Delta x=b-a $$

and:

$$ \Delta y=f(b)-f(a). $$

Secant-Line Interpretation

On the graph $y=f(x)$, consider the two points:

$$ P=(a,f(a)) $$

and:

$$ Q=(b,f(b)). $$

The average rate of change is the slope of the secant line through $P$ and $Q$:

$$ \boxed{ m_{\text{sec}} = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} }. $$

Basic Example

Let:

$$ f(x)=x^2. $$

Find the average rate of change from $x=1$ to $x=4$.

First evaluate the endpoints:

$$ f(1)=1, \qquad f(4)=16. $$

Then:

$$ \frac{16-1}{4-1} = \frac{15}{3} = \boxed{5}. $$

Interpretation of the Example

The function $x^2$ does not increase at a constant rate from $1$ to $4$. However, across that entire interval, its output increases by an average of $5$ units for each one-unit increase in $x$.

Difference Quotient Form

If the interval begins at $x$ and ends at $x+h$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{ f(x+h)-f(x) }{h} } $$

provided:

$$ h\ne0. $$

This is the standard difference quotient.

Why h Cannot Be Zero

If $h=0$, the denominator becomes zero:

$$ \frac{f(x)-f(x)}{0} = \frac00, $$

which is undefined.

Thus an average rate of change requires two distinct input values.

Linear Functions

For:

$$ f(x)=mx+b, $$

the average rate of change on any interval is:

$$ \boxed{ m }. $$

To see this:

$$ \frac{ [mb+c]-[ma+c] }{b-a} = \frac{ m(b-a) }{b-a} = m. $$

Therefore a linear function has constant average rate of change.

Nonlinear Functions

For nonlinear functions, the average rate of change generally depends on the chosen interval.

For $f(x)=x^2$:

$$ \operatorname{AROC}_{[0,1]}=1, $$

while:

$$ \operatorname{AROC}_{[1,2]}=3. $$

The changing secant slope reflects the curve's nonlinear behavior.

Positive Average Rate of Change

If:

$$ \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}>0, $$

then the function's net change agrees in sign with the change in input.

For $b>a$, this means:

$$ \boxed{ f(b)>f(a) }. $$

Negative Average Rate of Change

If $b>a$ and:

$$ \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}<0, $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ f(b)

The function has decreased overall across the interval.

Zero Average Rate of Change

If:

$$ f(b)=f(a), $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}=0 }. $$

This does not mean the function was constant throughout the interval; it means only that the endpoint outputs are equal.

Example of Zero Net Change

For:

$$ f(x)=x^2 $$

on $[-2,2]$:

$$ f(-2)=4, \qquad f(2)=4. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{AROC}_{[-2,2]}=0 }. $$

Yet the function decreases and then increases within the interval.

Reversing the Interval

Interestingly:

$$ \frac{f(a)-f(b)}{a-b} = \frac{-(f(b)-f(a))}{-(b-a)} = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}. $$

Thus reversing both endpoints leaves the numerical average rate of change unchanged:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{AROC}_{[a,b]}(f) = \operatorname{AROC}_{[b,a]}(f) }. $$

Units

Average rate of change has units:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\text{output units}} {\text{input units}} }. $$

If distance is measured in kilometers and time in hours, the rate has units of:

$$ \boxed{ \text{kilometers per hour} }. $$

Motion Interpretation

If $s(t)$ represents position, then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{s(t_2)-s(t_1)}{t_2-t_1} } $$

is the average velocity from $t_1$ to $t_2$.

Average Velocity versus Average Speed

Average velocity uses displacement:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\text{displacement}} {\text{elapsed time}} }. $$

Average speed uses total distance traveled:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\text{total distance}} {\text{elapsed time}} }. $$

These can differ whenever motion reverses direction.

Population Growth

If $P(t)$ denotes population, then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{P(t_2)-P(t_1)}{t_2-t_1} } $$

gives the average population change per unit time over the interval.

Average Cost Change

If $C(q)$ denotes production cost for quantity $q$, then:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{C(q_2)-C(q_1)}{q_2-q_1} } $$

measures the average additional cost per additional unit of production across the interval.

Temperature Change

If $T(t)$ denotes temperature:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{T(t_2)-T(t_1)}{t_2-t_1} } $$

describes the average temperature change per unit time.

Average Rate of Change from a Table

Suppose a table gives:

$$ f(2)=7, \qquad f(5)=19. $$

Then:

$$ \frac{19-7}{5-2} = \frac{12}{3} = \boxed{4}. $$

A formula for $f$ is not necessary if the endpoint values are known.

Average Rate of Change from a Graph

Read the coordinates of two points on the graph and calculate:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1} }. $$

This is exactly the slope formula.

Average Rate of Change and Slope

For a line, slope and average rate of change are the same constant everywhere.

For a curve, average rate of change is the slope of a secant line over a chosen interval.

$$ \boxed{ \text{AROC} = \text{secant slope} }. $$

Average versus Instantaneous Rate of Change

Average rate of change uses two distinct inputs:

$$ \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}. $$

Instantaneous rate of change concerns behavior at one input and is represented by the derivative:

$$ \boxed{ f'(a) }. $$

Derivative as a Limit of Average Rates

The derivative arises by shrinking the interval:

$$ \boxed{ f'(x) = \lim_{h\to0} \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} } $$

when the limit exists.

Thus instantaneous rate of change is built from average rates of change over increasingly small intervals.

Secant Line to Tangent Line

As $b$ approaches $a$, the secant line through:

$$ (a,f(a)) \qquad\text{and}\qquad (b,f(b)) $$

may approach the tangent line at $x=a$.

Correspondingly:

$$ \boxed{ \text{secant slope} \longrightarrow \text{tangent slope} }. $$

Mean Value Theorem Connection

If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$, the Mean Value Theorem guarantees at least one $c\in(a,b)$ such that:

$$ \boxed{ f'(c) = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} }. $$

In other words, at some interior point the instantaneous rate of change equals the average rate of change over the entire interval.

Quadratic Shortcut

For:

$$ f(x)=x^2, $$

the average rate of change from $a$ to $b$ is:

$$ \frac{b^2-a^2}{b-a}. $$

Factor the difference of squares:

$$ \frac{(b-a)(b+a)}{b-a} = \boxed{a+b}. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{AROC}_{[a,b]}(x^2) = a+b }. $$

Power Functions

For $f(x)=x^n$:

$$ \frac{b^n-a^n}{b-a} $$

can often be simplified using the factorization:

$$ b^n-a^n = (b-a) \left( b^{n-1} +b^{n-2}a +\cdots+ a^{n-1} \right). $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{b^n-a^n}{b-a} = b^{n-1} +b^{n-2}a +\cdots+ a^{n-1} }. $$

Discrete Sequences

A sequence $a_n$ can also have an average rate of change between indices $m$ and $n$:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{a_n-a_m}{n-m} }. $$

For an arithmetic sequence, this equals the common difference.

Arithmetic Sequence Connection

If:

$$ a_n=a_1+(n-1)d, $$

then:

$$ \frac{a_n-a_m}{n-m} = \boxed{d}. $$

Thus the common difference is the discrete constant rate of change of an arithmetic sequence.

Average Rate of Change under Vertical Translation

If:

$$ g(x)=f(x)+c, $$

then:

$$ \frac{g(b)-g(a)}{b-a} = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \text{vertical translation does not change average rate of change} }. $$

Vertical Scaling

If:

$$ g(x)=cf(x), $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{AROC}(g) = c\,\operatorname{AROC}(f) }. $$

Sum of Functions

If:

$$ h(x)=f(x)+g(x), $$

then:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{AROC}(h) = \operatorname{AROC}(f) + \operatorname{AROC}(g) } $$

over the same interval.

Average Rate versus Average Value

The average rate of change:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} } $$

measures change in output per unit input.

The average value of a function:

$$ \boxed{ \frac1{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx } $$

measures the average height or function value across the interval.

These are fundamentally different quantities.

Average Rate versus Percentage Change

Average rate of change uses an absolute change:

$$ f(b)-f(a). $$

Percentage change instead compares change to a reference value:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{f(a)}\times100\% } $$

when $f(a)\ne0$.

These should not be confused.

Average Rate versus Relative Growth Rate

A relative growth rate scales change by the size of the quantity itself. Average rate of change does not automatically perform that normalization.

Units Matter

If $x$ is measured in seconds and $f(x)$ in meters, then average rate of change is measured in meters per second.

If $x$ is years and $f(x)$ dollars, the rate is dollars per year.

Therefore the fraction should be interpreted dimensionally, not merely numerically.

Common Misconception

The average rate of change is not:

$$ \frac{f(a)+f(b)}{2}. $$

That expression averages the endpoint output values.

Average rate of change instead uses:

$$ \boxed{ \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} }. $$

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$ "The average rate of change of f from a to b."
$\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$ "Change in y divided by change in x."
$\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$ "The difference quotient of f from x to x plus h."
$m_{\text{sec}}=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$ "The secant slope equals the average rate of change."
$f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$ "The derivative is the limit of the average rates of change as h approaches zero."
$\frac{s(t_2)-s(t_1)}{t_2-t_1}$ "Average velocity from time t one to time t two."

The phrase from $a$ to $b$ identifies the input interval. It does not mean that the function itself necessarily increases from $f(a)$ to $f(b)$; the resulting rate may be positive, negative, or zero.

Defense Notation Advice: Subtract endpoint values in the same order in numerator and denominator. If you write $f(b)-f(a)$ on top, write $b-a$ below. Reversing only one subtraction changes the sign incorrectly.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Formula: $$ \boxed{ \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} }. $$
  • Delta Form: $$ \boxed{ \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} }. $$
  • Difference Quotient: $$ \boxed{ \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} } $$ for $h\ne0$.
  • Geometric Meaning: Average rate of change is the slope of the secant line joining the endpoint points of the graph.
  • Linear Functions: The average rate of change equals the constant slope on every interval.
  • Nonlinear Functions: The average rate generally depends on the selected interval.
  • Positive Rate: For $b>a$, a positive rate means $f(b)>f(a)$.
  • Negative Rate: For $b>a$, a negative rate means $f(b)
  • Zero Rate: $$ \boxed{ f(a)=f(b) } $$ gives zero average rate, even if the function changes between the endpoints.
  • Units: $$ \boxed{ \frac{\text{output units}}{\text{input units}} }. $$
  • Motion: Position change divided by time change gives average velocity.
  • Population: Population change divided by elapsed time gives average population growth per unit time.
  • Economics: Cost, revenue, or profit change divided by production change gives an average change per unit of output.
  • Table Use: Only the two endpoint input-output pairs are required.
  • Graph Use: Read two points and compute the secant slope.
  • Sequence Use: $$ \boxed{ \frac{a_n-a_m}{n-m} } $$ gives an average discrete rate of change.
  • Arithmetic Sequence: The average rate between any two indexed terms equals the common difference.
  • Instantaneous-Rate Contrast: Average rate uses an interval; instantaneous rate uses the derivative at one point.
  • Derivative Connection: $$ \boxed{ f'(x) = \lim_{h\to0} \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} }. $$
  • Mean Value Theorem: Under its hypotheses, some instantaneous rate inside the interval equals the average rate over the interval.
  • Vertical Translation: Adding a constant to $f$ does not change average rate of change.
  • Vertical Scaling: Multiplying $f$ by $c$ multiplies its average rate of change by $c$.
  • Sum of Functions: Average rates add across the same interval.
  • Average-Value Contrast: $$ \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} $$ measures change per unit input, while $$ \frac1{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx $$ measures average function value.
  • Percentage-Change Contrast: Percentage change divides by a reference output value; average rate of change divides by the change in input.
  • Common Error: Do not calculate: $$ \frac{f(a)+f(b)}{2} $$ when asked for average rate of change.
  • Common Error: Do not reverse the subtraction order in only the numerator or only the denominator.
  • Common Error: Zero average rate does not imply that the function was constant throughout the interval.
  • Terminological Contrast: Average rate of change is net output change divided by input change. Secant slope is its geometric interpretation. Difference quotient is its algebraic form over an increment $h$. Instantaneous rate of change is the limiting rate represented by a derivative.
