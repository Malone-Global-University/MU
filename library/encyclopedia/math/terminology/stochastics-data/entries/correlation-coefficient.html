Correlation Coefficient

Subfield: Probability & Statistical Inference

Etymology & Roots

A correlation coefficient is a numerical statistic that measures the direction and strength of an association between two variables. In elementary statistics, the term most commonly refers to the Pearson product-moment correlation coefficient, usually written as $r$ for a sample or $\rho$ for a population.

The word correlation combines the prefix co-, meaning "together," with relation, meaning a connection or relationship. The word therefore describes quantities whose values vary together in some systematic way.

A coefficient is a numerical factor or quantity used to characterize a mathematical relationship. In this context, the coefficient compresses information about the association between two variables into a single number.

The modern statistical theory of correlation developed during the nineteenth century through the work of researchers including Francis Galton and Karl Pearson. Pearson formalized the product-moment correlation coefficient that remains one of the most widely used measures of statistical association.

Although the phrase correlation coefficient is often used as though it referred to one specific statistic, there are actually several correlation coefficients designed for different kinds of data and different forms of association.

Operational Nuance

A correlation coefficient assigns a numerical value to the association between two variables. For the Pearson correlation coefficient, the value always lies between:

$$ \boxed{-1\le r\le1}. $$

The sign indicates the direction of the linear association, while the absolute value indicates its strength.

  • $r>0$ indicates a positive linear correlation.
  • $r<0$ indicates a negative linear correlation.
  • $r$ near $0$ indicates little or no linear correlation.
  • $r=1$ indicates perfect positive linear correlation.
  • $r=-1$ indicates perfect negative linear correlation.

Positive Correlation

A positive correlation means that larger values of one variable tend to occur with larger values of the other variable.

$$ r>0 $$

For example, if a dataset comparing hours studied with examination scores has:

$$ r=0.82, $$

the data exhibit a relatively strong positive linear association.

Negative Correlation

A negative correlation means that larger values of one variable tend to occur with smaller values of the other.

$$ r<0. $$

A value such as:

$$ r=-0.76 $$

indicates a substantial negative linear association.

Zero Correlation

A Pearson correlation coefficient of:

$$ r=0 $$

means that the variables have no measured linear correlation in the sample. It does not necessarily mean that the variables are unrelated.

For example, points arranged around a parabola can exhibit a strong nonlinear relationship while having a Pearson correlation near zero.

The Sample Pearson Correlation Coefficient

For paired observations:

$$ (x_1,y_1),(x_2,y_2),\ldots,(x_n,y_n), $$

the sample Pearson correlation coefficient may be written:

$$ \boxed{ r= \frac{ \sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y}) }{ \sqrt{ \sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\bar{x})^2 } \sqrt{ \sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\bar{y})^2 } } }. $$

Here:

  • $x_i$ and $y_i$ are paired observations,
  • $\bar{x}$ is the sample mean of the $x$-values,
  • $\bar{y}$ is the sample mean of the $y$-values, and
  • $n$ is the number of paired observations.

The numerator measures how the variables vary together, while the denominator standardizes that joint variation according to the variability of each variable.

Correlation and Covariance

Pearson correlation can also be understood as a standardized form of covariance.

$$ \boxed{ r= \frac{s_{xy}}{s_xs_y} }. $$

Here $s_{xy}$ represents sample covariance, while $s_x$ and $s_y$ represent the sample standard deviations of the two variables.

Covariance indicates whether two variables tend to vary in the same or opposite directions, but its magnitude depends on the units of measurement. Correlation removes those units through standardization.

Population Correlation

For population random variables $X$ and $Y$, the Pearson population correlation coefficient is conventionally written with the Greek letter rho:

$$ \boxed{ \rho_{X,Y} = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)} {\sigma_X\sigma_Y} }. $$

Thus:

$$ -1\le\rho_{X,Y}\le1. $$

Perfect Correlation

A Pearson correlation of:

$$ r=1 $$

occurs when all observations lie exactly on a straight line with positive slope. Similarly:

$$ r=-1 $$

occurs when all observations lie exactly on a straight line with negative slope.

In general, for variables with nonzero variation, a perfect Pearson correlation occurs when one variable is an exact affine function of the other:

$$ y=a+bx. $$

If $b>0$, the correlation is $1$; if $b<0$, the correlation is $-1$.

Strength of Correlation

Values of $|r|$ closer to $1$ indicate a stronger linear association, while values closer to $0$ indicate a weaker linear association.

However, there is no universal rule assigning labels such as "weak," "moderate," or "strong" to fixed intervals of $r$. Appropriate interpretations depend on the field, type of data, sample size, measurement precision, and research context.

Correlation Does Not Imply Causation

A large correlation coefficient does not establish that one variable causes another. Correlation measures association, not causal mechanism.

Two variables may be correlated because:

  • one variable influences the other,
  • the second variable influences the first,
  • a third variable influences both,
  • selection or measurement processes create the association, or
  • the observed correlation arose partly through random variation.

Common Misconception: A high correlation coefficient does not prove causation, and a correlation near zero does not prove that no relationship exists.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$r$ "R," referring to the sample Pearson correlation coefficient.
$\rho$ "Rho," referring to the population correlation coefficient.
$r=0.82$ "R equals zero point eight two."
$r=-0.76$ "R equals negative zero point seven six."
$r=1$ "R equals one; there is perfect positive linear correlation."
$r=-1$ "R equals negative one; there is perfect negative linear correlation."
$r\approx0$ "R is approximately zero; there is little linear correlation."
$-1\le r\le1$ "R is between negative one and one, inclusive."
$\rho_{X,Y}$ "The correlation of X and Y," or "rho sub X Y."
$r^2$ "R squared."

In statistical writing, the symbol $r$ usually denotes a correlation calculated from sample data, while $\rho$ usually denotes the corresponding population parameter.

A typical written statement is:

$$ r=0.68. $$

This may be expressed verbally as:

$$ \text{"The variables have a positive linear correlation of }0.68\text{."} $$

When reporting correlation professionally, it is useful to identify the specific coefficient when ambiguity is possible:

  • "The Pearson correlation coefficient is $r=0.68$."
  • "The Spearman rank correlation is $\rho_s=0.71$."
  • "Kendall's tau is $\tau=0.54$."

Defense Notation Advice: Do not assume that every symbol $r$, $\rho$, or $\tau$ refers to the same statistical procedure. State the type of correlation coefficient when the context does not make it clear.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Pearson Correlation Coefficient: The most common correlation coefficient for measuring linear association between two quantitative variables: $$ \boxed{ r= \frac{ \sum (x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y}) }{ \sqrt{\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2} \sqrt{\sum(y_i-\bar{y})^2} } }. $$
  • Sample vs. Population: Sample correlation is conventionally represented by: $$ r, $$ while population correlation is conventionally represented by: $$ \rho. $$
  • Population Pearson Correlation: For random variables $X$ and $Y$: $$ \boxed{ \rho_{X,Y} = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)} {\sigma_X\sigma_Y} }. $$
  • Standardization: Correlation is dimensionless. Changing from centimeters to meters, for example, does not change the magnitude of Pearson correlation when the transformation uses a positive scale factor.
  • Positive Linear Rescaling: If: $$ U=a+bX $$ and: $$ V=c+dY $$ with $b>0$ and $d>0$, then: $$ \operatorname{Corr}(U,V) = \operatorname{Corr}(X,Y). $$
  • Negative Rescaling: Multiplying exactly one variable by a negative constant reverses the sign of the Pearson correlation while preserving its magnitude.
  • Scatterplots: Correlation should often be examined alongside a scatterplot. A single coefficient cannot reveal every important feature of the data, including curvature, clusters, gaps, or unusual observations.
  • Outliers: Pearson correlation can be strongly affected by extreme observations. A single unusual point may substantially increase, decrease, or reverse the calculated value of $r$.
  • Nonlinear Relationships: Pearson correlation measures linear association. A strong curved relationship may therefore produce: $$ r\approx0. $$ This does not imply that the variables are unrelated.
  • Independence: If two random variables are independent and the relevant moments exist, then they are uncorrelated: $$ X\perp Y \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \rho_{X,Y}=0. $$ The converse is generally false.
  • Uncorrelated Does Not Mean Independent: Two variables can satisfy: $$ \rho_{X,Y}=0 $$ while still having a systematic nonlinear dependence.
  • Spearman Rank Correlation: Spearman's rank correlation coefficient measures the strength and direction of a monotonic relationship using ranked values rather than the original measurements. It is commonly written: $$ \rho_s $$ or: $$ r_s. $$
  • Kendall Rank Correlation: Kendall's tau measures ordinal association by comparing concordant and discordant pairs. It is usually written: $$ \tau. $$
  • Point-Biserial Correlation: The point-biserial correlation is used when one variable is quantitative and the other is genuinely dichotomous. It is mathematically closely related to Pearson correlation applied to suitable numerical coding of the binary variable.
  • Phi Coefficient: For two binary variables represented in a $2\times2$ contingency table, the phi coefficient: $$ \phi $$ provides a measure of association closely related to Pearson correlation.
  • Correlation Matrix: When several variables are studied simultaneously, their pairwise correlations can be arranged in a matrix: $$ R= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & r_{12} & \cdots & r_{1p}\\ r_{21} & 1 & \cdots & r_{2p}\\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\ r_{p1} & r_{p2} & \cdots & 1 \end{bmatrix}. $$
  • Diagonal of a Correlation Matrix: Every variable is perfectly correlated with itself, so the diagonal entries of an ordinary correlation matrix are: $$ 1. $$
  • Symmetry: Correlation is symmetric: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Corr}(X,Y) = \operatorname{Corr}(Y,X) }. $$ Thus the correlation of $X$ with $Y$ is the same as the correlation of $Y$ with $X$.
  • Regression: In simple linear regression with an intercept, the sample Pearson correlation coefficient is closely related to the slope of the fitted line. The signs of the regression slope and $r$ are the same.
  • Coefficient of Determination: In simple linear regression with an intercept: $$ R^2=r^2. $$ This special relationship should not be generalized automatically to every regression model.
  • Finance: Correlation coefficients are used to compare asset returns. Assets with lower correlations may provide diversification benefits because their returns tend not to move together as closely.
  • Economics: Economists use correlation to study associations among variables such as income, consumption, employment, prices, interest rates, production, and economic growth.
  • Science: Correlation coefficients are widely used to quantify relationships among experimental and observational measurements in biology, chemistry, physics, environmental science, medicine, and other fields.
  • Data Science: Correlation coefficients are frequently used during exploratory data analysis to identify related variables, potential predictors, redundant features, and patterns that warrant further investigation.
  • Statistical Significance: A correlation coefficient and a significance test answer different questions. The coefficient describes the observed association, while inferential procedures evaluate evidence concerning a population correlation under specified assumptions.
  • Sample Size: The same observed value of $r$ can have different inferential implications in small and large samples. Correlation strength should therefore not be confused with statistical significance.
  • Restricted Range: Restricting observations to a narrow range of one or both variables can reduce an observed correlation even when a broader population exhibits a substantial association.
  • Aggregated Data: Correlations calculated from grouped or aggregated data may differ substantially from correlations among individuals. Interpretation must therefore match the level at which the data were measured.
  • Spurious Correlation: Two variables may display a substantial correlation without a meaningful direct relationship. Shared trends, confounding variables, selection effects, or chance can create apparent associations.
  • Correlation vs. Causation: The statement: $$ |\rho|\approx1 $$ indicates strong association under the relevant correlation measure. It does not establish: $$ X\longrightarrow Y. $$ Causal conclusions require additional reasoning and evidence.
  • Terminological Contrast: Correlation refers broadly to statistical association. A correlation coefficient is a numerical statistic designed to quantify some particular form of that association.
