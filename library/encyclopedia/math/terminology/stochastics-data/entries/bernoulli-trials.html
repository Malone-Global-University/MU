Bernoulli Trials

Subfield: Probability & Statistical Inference

Etymology & Roots

A Bernoulli trial is a random experiment whose possible outcomes are grouped into exactly two categories, conventionally called success and failure.

If the probability of success is $p$, then the probability of failure is:

$$ \boxed{ q=1-p }. $$

The words success and failure are mathematical labels rather than judgments of desirability. A "success" is simply the outcome being counted or designated as the event of interest.

$$ \boxed{ P(\text{success})=p, \qquad P(\text{failure})=1-p }. $$

When the plural term Bernoulli trials is used for a repeated experiment, the standard model typically assumes that the trials are independent and share the same success probability $p$.

Operational Nuance

Two Outcome Classes

A Bernoulli trial has two outcome categories:

$$ \boxed{ \{\text{success},\text{failure}\} }. $$

These need not correspond to literally two physical outcomes. Several raw outcomes may be grouped into one category.

Example: Rolling a Die

Suppose a die is rolled and define:

  • success = rolling a 6,
  • failure = rolling anything else.

Then:

$$ \boxed{ p=\frac16, \qquad 1-p=\frac56 }. $$

Although the die has six physical outcomes, the experiment becomes a Bernoulli trial because the outcomes have been classified into two categories.

Bernoulli Random Variable

A Bernoulli trial is often represented by a random variable $X$ defined as:

$$ \boxed{ X= \begin{cases} 1,&\text{success},\\ 0,&\text{failure}. \end{cases} } $$

Then:

$$ \boxed{ P(X=1)=p, \qquad P(X=0)=1-p }. $$

Bernoulli Distribution

A random variable with this probability law is said to have a Bernoulli distribution with parameter $p$:

$$ \boxed{ X\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p) }. $$

Probability Mass Function

For $x\in\{0,1\}$:

$$ \boxed{ P(X=x) = p^x(1-p)^{1-x} }. $$

When $x=1$, this gives $p$. When $x=0$, it gives $1-p$.

Expected Value

For a Bernoulli random variable:

$$ \boxed{ E[X]=p }. $$

This makes intuitive sense because $X$ equals $1$ on success and $0$ on failure.

Variance

The variance is:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Var}(X) = p(1-p) }. $$

Standard Deviation

$$ \boxed{ \sigma = \sqrt{p(1-p)} }. $$

Repeated Bernoulli Trials

Suppose:

$$ X_1,X_2,\ldots,X_n $$

represent repeated Bernoulli trials.

In the standard repeated-trial model:

$$ \boxed{ P(X_i=1)=p } $$

for every trial $i$, and the trials are mutually independent.

Independence

Independence means that knowing the outcome of one trial does not change the modeled probability distribution of another trial.

For two trials:

$$ \boxed{ P(X_1=a,X_2=b) = P(X_1=a)P(X_2=b) } $$

for $a,b\in\{0,1\}$.

Constant Probability

Standard Bernoulli trials also use the same success probability on every trial:

$$ \boxed{ p_1=p_2=\cdots=p_n=p }. $$

If the probability changes from trial to trial, the individual experiments may still each be Bernoulli trials, but the sequence is no longer the usual identically distributed Bernoulli-trial model.

Counting Successes

Let:

$$ S_n=X_1+X_2+\cdots+X_n. $$

Since each $X_i$ is $0$ or $1$, $S_n$ counts the number of successes in $n$ trials.

Binomial Distribution

If $n$ independent Bernoulli trials have common success probability $p$, then:

$$ \boxed{ S_n\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n,p) }. $$

Probability of Exactly k Successes

$$ \boxed{ P(S_n=k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k(1-p)^{n-k} }. $$

Here:

$$ \binom nk $$

counts the number of ways to place $k$ successes among $n$ trials.

Probability of One Particular Outcome Sequence

Consider one specified sequence containing $k$ successes and $n-k$ failures. Independence gives:

$$ \boxed{ p^k(1-p)^{n-k} }. $$

The binomial coefficient is not included until all sequences containing the same number of successes are counted.

Example: Three Coin Tosses

For a fair coin, let heads be success:

$$ p=\frac12. $$

The probability of the particular sequence:

$$ HHT $$

is:

$$ \left(\frac12\right)^3 = \boxed{\frac18}. $$

Exactly Two Heads

There are:

$$ \binom32=3 $$

sequences containing exactly two heads:

$$ HHT,\quad HTH,\quad THH. $$

Therefore:

$$ P(S_3=2) = 3\left(\frac12\right)^3 = \boxed{\frac38}. $$

Expected Number of Successes

If:

$$ S_n=X_1+\cdots+X_n, $$

then:

$$ E[S_n] = E[X_1]+\cdots+E[X_n]. $$

For common success probability $p$:

$$ \boxed{ E[S_n]=np }. $$

Variance of the Number of Successes

Under independence:

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Var}(S_n) = np(1-p) }. $$

Sample Proportion

The fraction of successful trials is:

$$ \boxed{ \hat p = \frac{S_n}{n} }. $$

This quantity is fundamental in statistical estimation of an unknown Bernoulli probability $p$.

Expected Sample Proportion

$$ \boxed{ E[\hat p]=p }. $$

Variance of the Sample Proportion

$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Var}(\hat p) = \frac{p(1-p)}{n} }. $$

Bernoulli Trial versus Binomial Experiment

A Bernoulli trial is one two-outcome experiment. A binomial experiment consists of a fixed number $n$ of independent Bernoulli trials with a common success probability $p$.

$$ \boxed{ \text{one Bernoulli trial} \longrightarrow \text{one binary outcome} } $$
$$ \boxed{ n\text{ Bernoulli trials} \longrightarrow \text{binomial count} }. $$

Bernoulli Trial versus Bernoulli Distribution

The trial is the random experiment. The Bernoulli distribution is the probability distribution assigned to its $0$-$1$ numerical encoding.

Bernoulli Process

An infinite sequence of independent Bernoulli trials with common success probability $p$ is called a Bernoulli process.

$$ \boxed{ X_1,X_2,X_3,\ldots \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \operatorname{Bernoulli}(p) }. $$

Waiting for the First Success

If repeated Bernoulli trials continue until the first success, the number of trials required is modeled by a geometric distribution.

If $T$ is the trial number of the first success:

$$ \boxed{ P(T=k) = (1-p)^{k-1}p }. $$

Waiting for Multiple Successes

Continuing Bernoulli trials until a specified number of successes has occurred leads to the negative binomial family of distributions.

Applications

Bernoulli models appear whenever an observation is naturally or deliberately classified into two categories, such as:

  • pass or fail,
  • defective or nondefective,
  • click or no click,
  • purchase or no purchase,
  • positive or negative test result,
  • vote for a specified option or not,
  • event occurs or does not occur.

Success Need Not Be Desirable

If the event of interest is "machine failure," then machine failure may be labeled a success for purposes of the probability model.

$$ \boxed{ \text{success} = \text{designated event}, \text{ not necessarily a good event} }. $$

Physical Independence versus Modeled Independence

Independence is a mathematical modeling assumption. Repeated observations are not automatically independent merely because they occur at different times.

For example, sampling without replacement from a small population generally changes the probability of later outcomes.

Sampling with Replacement

If an item is selected, recorded, and replaced before the next selection, repeated trials can often preserve the same outcome probabilities and may reasonably be modeled as independent.

Sampling without Replacement

Without replacement, the composition of a finite population changes after each draw. Therefore:

$$ p_1,p_2,p_3,\ldots $$

may not remain equal, and the draws are generally dependent.

Changing Probability

Suppose each trial still has two outcomes but:

$$ p_i=P(X_i=1) $$

varies with $i$.

Each $X_i$ may individually be Bernoulli-distributed, but the total count is not generally $\operatorname{Binomial}(n,p)$ for one common $p$.

Independence versus Equal Probabilities

These are separate conditions.

Trials may:

  • be independent but have different success probabilities,
  • have equal marginal success probabilities but still be dependent.

Standard Bernoulli trials used in the binomial model require both conditions.

Complement Rule

Because success and failure exhaust the possible outcomes:

$$ \boxed{ P(F)=1-P(S) }. $$

At Least One Success

For $n$ independent Bernoulli trials:

$$ P(\text{at least one success}) = 1-P(\text{no successes}). $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ P(\text{at least one success}) = 1-(1-p)^n }. $$

No Successes

$$ \boxed{ P(S_n=0) = (1-p)^n }. $$

All Successes

$$ \boxed{ P(S_n=n) = p^n }. $$

Common Misconception: Bernoulli Means 50-50

A Bernoulli trial does not require:

$$ p=\frac12. $$

Any probability:

$$ \boxed{ 0\le p\le1 } $$

is mathematically possible.

Common Misconception: There Must Be Literally Two Raw Outcomes

What matters is that the outcomes are classified into two mutually exclusive categories for the question being studied.

Common Misconception: Repeated Trials Are Automatically Binomial

Repeating a two-outcome experiment does not by itself create a binomial model. One must also verify a fixed number of trials, independence, and a common success probability.

Common Misconception: Independence Means Different Outcomes

Independent trials may produce the same observed result repeatedly. Independence concerns probabilities, not whether successive observed outcomes happen to differ.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$X\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p)$ "X has a Bernoulli distribution with success probability p."
$P(X=1)=p$ "The probability of success is p."
$P(X=0)=1-p$ "The probability of failure is one minus p."
$S_n=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i$ "S sub n counts the number of successes in n Bernoulli trials."
$S_n\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n,p)$ "The number of successes has a binomial distribution with parameters n and p."
$P(S_n=k)=\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$ "The probability of exactly k successes in n independent Bernoulli trials."

In ordinary mathematical speech, one may say "a Bernoulli trial with probability $p$," although more precisely $p$ is the probability of the outcome designated as success. For repeated experiments, phrases such as independent Bernoulli trials and independent and identically distributed Bernoulli variables make the assumptions explicit.

Defense Notation Advice: Define what counts as success before assigning $p$. Then verify separately whether repeated trials are independent and whether the same $p$ applies to every trial before using a binomial formula.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Definition: A Bernoulli trial is a random experiment with two designated outcome classes.
  • Outcome Labels: Success and failure.
  • Success Probability: $$ \boxed{ P(S)=p }. $$
  • Failure Probability: $$ \boxed{ P(F)=1-p }. $$
  • 0-1 Encoding: Success is commonly encoded as $1$ and failure as $0$.
  • Bernoulli Distribution: $$ \boxed{ X\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p) }. $$
  • Probability Mass Function: $$ \boxed{ P(X=x)=p^x(1-p)^{1-x}, \qquad x\in\{0,1\} }. $$
  • Expected Value: $$ \boxed{ E[X]=p }. $$
  • Variance: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Var}(X)=p(1-p) }. $$
  • Standard Repeated-Trial Assumption: Trials are independent and have the same success probability $p$.
  • Success Count: $$ \boxed{ S_n=X_1+\cdots+X_n }. $$
  • Binomial Connection: $$ \boxed{ S_n\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n,p) } $$ under independent trials with common probability $p$.
  • Exactly k Successes: $$ \boxed{ P(S_n=k) = \binom nkp^k(1-p)^{n-k} }. $$
  • Expected Number of Successes: $$ \boxed{ E[S_n]=np }. $$
  • Variance of Success Count: $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Var}(S_n)=np(1-p) }. $$
  • At Least One Success: $$ \boxed{ 1-(1-p)^n }. $$
  • No Successes: $$ \boxed{ (1-p)^n }. $$
  • All Successes: $$ \boxed{ p^n }. $$
  • Sample Proportion: $$ \boxed{ \hat p=\frac{S_n}{n} }. $$
  • Bernoulli Process: An infinite sequence of independent, identically distributed Bernoulli trials.
  • Geometric Distribution Connection: Counts trials until the first success.
  • Negative Binomial Connection: Counts trials or failures associated with reaching a specified number of successes.
  • Binary Classification: Several physical outcomes may be grouped into success versus failure.
  • Independence Caution: Repetition alone does not imply independence.
  • Constant-p Caution: Individual trials may each be Bernoulli even when their success probabilities differ.
  • Sampling without Replacement: Repeated draws from a small finite population are generally dependent and may have changing probabilities.
  • Success Terminology: "Success" means the designated event of interest, not necessarily a desirable outcome.
  • Common Error: Do not assume a Bernoulli trial must have probability $1/2$.
  • Common Error: Do not use the binomial distribution merely because each observation has two categories; independence and constant $p$ must also be justified.
  • Common Error: Do not confuse one particular sequence probability $p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$ with the probability of all sequences having $k$ successes, which also requires $\binom nk$.
  • Terminological Contrast: Bernoulli trial is one two-category random experiment. Bernoulli random variable numerically encodes its outcome as $0$ or $1$. Bernoulli distribution is the probability law of that variable. Bernoulli trials commonly refers to repeated independent trials with a common success probability. Binomial distribution describes the number of successes in a fixed number of such trials. Bernoulli process is an infinite sequence of independent identically distributed Bernoulli trials.
