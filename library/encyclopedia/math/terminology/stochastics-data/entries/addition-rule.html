Addition Rule

Subfield: Probability & Statistical Inference

Etymology & Roots

In probability, the Addition Rule gives the probability that at least one of two events occurs. It is fundamentally a rule about the union of events.

For events $A$ and $B$, the event:

$$ A\cup B $$

means that $A$ occurs, $B$ occurs, or both occur.

The general Addition Rule is:

$$ \boxed{ P(A\cup B) = P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B) }. $$

The subtraction term is necessary because outcomes belonging to both $A$ and $B$ are included once in $P(A)$ and once again in $P(B)$. Without correction, those outcomes would be counted twice.

The word addition refers to combining the probabilities of alternatives, while the complete rule incorporates the logic of inclusion-exclusion: add the individual event probabilities, then remove the duplicated overlap.

In elementary probability, Addition Rule may refer either to this general formula or to its simpler special case for mutually exclusive events. Context should determine which version is intended.

Operational Nuance

General Addition Rule

For any two events $A$ and $B$:

$$ \boxed{ P(A\cup B) = P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B) }. $$

The rule does not require the events to be independent or mutually exclusive.

Why the Intersection Is Subtracted

Suppose an outcome belongs to both $A$ and $B$. When computing:

$$ P(A)+P(B), $$

that shared outcome contributes to both probabilities. It has therefore been included twice.

Subtracting:

$$ P(A\cap B) $$

removes one copy of that double-counted overlap.

Mutually Exclusive Events

Events $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive when they cannot occur together:

$$ \boxed{ A\cap B=\varnothing }. $$

Therefore:

$$ P(A\cap B)=0. $$

The general Addition Rule then becomes:

$$ \boxed{ P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B) }. $$

Example with Overlapping Events

Suppose:

$$ P(A)=0.55, $$ $$ P(B)=0.40, $$ $$ P(A\cap B)=0.20. $$

Then:

$$ P(A\cup B) = 0.55+0.40-0.20 $$ $$ = \boxed{0.75}. $$

Simply adding $0.55+0.40=0.95$ would incorrectly count the shared $0.20$ twice.

Example with Mutually Exclusive Events

For one roll of a standard die, let:

$$ A=\{\text{roll a }1\}, $$ $$ B=\{\text{roll a }6\}. $$

These events cannot occur simultaneously. Thus:

$$ P(A\cap B)=0. $$

Therefore:

$$ P(A\cup B) = \frac16+\frac16 = \boxed{\frac13}. $$

Inclusive OR

The union:

$$ A\cup B $$

corresponds to the inclusive mathematical meaning of or:

$$ \boxed{ A\text{ or }B\text{ or both} }. $$

If a problem means exactly one event but not both, it is asking for a different event, commonly called the symmetric difference.

Three-Event Addition Rule

Inclusion-exclusion extends the Addition Rule to three events:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} P(A\cup B\cup C) ={}& P(A)+P(B)+P(C)\\ &-P(A\cap B) -P(A\cap C) -P(B\cap C)\\ &+P(A\cap B\cap C). \end{aligned} } $$

The pairwise intersections are subtracted because they were counted twice. The three-way intersection must then be added back because it was removed too many times.

Common Misconception: The Addition Rule does not say $P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)$ in every problem. That shorter formula is valid only when $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$A\cup B$ "A union B," or "A or B."
$A\cap B$ "A intersection B," or "A and B."
$P(A\cup B)$ "The probability of A or B."
$P(A\cap B)$ "The probability of A and B."
$A\cap B=\varnothing$ "A and B are mutually exclusive," or "A and B are disjoint."
$P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)$ "The probability of A or B equals the probability of A plus the probability of B minus the probability of A and B."

In probability language, the words or and and usually correspond to:

$$ \boxed{ \text{or}\longleftrightarrow\cup } $$

and:

$$ \boxed{ \text{and}\longleftrightarrow\cap }. $$

Defense Notation Advice: In mathematical probability, A or B normally includes the possibility that both occur. Do not interpret the word or as exclusive unless the wording explicitly says "but not both," "exactly one," or something equivalent.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • General Addition Rule: $$ \boxed{ P(A\cup B) = P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B) }. $$
  • Mutually Exclusive Special Case: If: $$ A\cap B=\varnothing, $$ then: $$ \boxed{ P(A\cup B) = P(A)+P(B) }. $$
  • Disjoint Events: Disjoint and mutually exclusive describe the same set-theoretic condition: $$ A\cap B=\varnothing. $$
  • Inclusive OR: $$ A\cup B $$ contains outcomes in $A$, outcomes in $B$, and outcomes belonging to both.
  • Intersection Correction: The term: $$ -P(A\cap B) $$ corrects for double counting.
  • Complementary Events: Since: $$ A\cup A^c=S $$ and: $$ A\cap A^c=\varnothing, $$ one obtains: $$ \boxed{ P(A)+P(A^c)=1 }. $$
  • Complement Rule: Therefore: $$ \boxed{ P(A^c)=1-P(A) }. $$
  • Entire Sample Space: If: $$ A\cup B=S, $$ then: $$ P(A\cup B)=1. $$
  • Independent Events: Independence does not mean mutual exclusivity. If independent events $A$ and $B$ satisfy: $$ P(A)>0,\qquad P(B)>0, $$ then: $$ P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)>0, $$ so they are not mutually exclusive.
  • Addition Rule with Independence: If $A$ and $B$ are independent: $$ \boxed{ P(A\cup B) = P(A)+P(B)-P(A)P(B) }. $$
  • Conditional Probability Form: Since: $$ P(A\cap B) = P(A)P(B\mid A), $$ the Addition Rule may be written: $$ \boxed{ P(A\cup B) = P(A)+P(B)-P(A)P(B\mid A) } $$ when $P(A)>0$.
  • Counting Analogue: For finite sets: $$ \boxed{ |A\cup B| = |A|+|B|-|A\cap B| }. $$ The probability rule mirrors this inclusion-exclusion counting identity.
  • Venn Diagrams: The Addition Rule can be visualized by treating $A$ and $B$ as overlapping regions. The overlap belongs to both circles but must contribute only once to the union.
  • Survey Problems: If people may belong to two categories simultaneously, the overlap must be subtracted when determining how many belong to at least one category.
  • At Least One: Phrases such as: $$ \text{"A or B"} $$ and: $$ \text{"at least one of A and B"} $$ typically indicate a union.
  • Exactly One: The probability that exactly one of $A$ and $B$ occurs is: $$ \boxed{ P(A)+P(B)-2P(A\cap B) }. $$
  • Neither Event: The probability that neither $A$ nor $B$ occurs is: $$ \boxed{ 1-P(A\cup B) }. $$
  • De Morgan's Law: $$ \boxed{ (A\cup B)^c = A^c\cap B^c } $$ connects the Addition Rule with complementary-event strategies.
  • Three Events: $$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} P(A\cup B\cup C) ={}& P(A)+P(B)+P(C)\\ &-P(A\cap B)-P(A\cap C)-P(B\cap C)\\ &+P(A\cap B\cap C). \end{aligned} } $$
  • Pairwise Mutually Exclusive Events: If $A$, $B$, and $C$ are pairwise disjoint: $$ \boxed{ P(A\cup B\cup C) = P(A)+P(B)+P(C) }. $$
  • Finite Additivity: More generally, for finitely many pairwise disjoint events: $$ A_1,\ldots,A_n, $$ $$ \boxed{ P\left( \bigcup_{k=1}^{n}A_k \right) = \sum_{k=1}^{n}P(A_k) }. $$
  • Countable Additivity: Probability measures satisfy the stronger axiom that for countably many pairwise disjoint events: $$ \boxed{ P\left( \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}A_k \right) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}P(A_k) }. $$
  • Upper Bound: Since: $$ P(A\cap B)\ge0, $$ the Addition Rule gives: $$ \boxed{ P(A\cup B) \le P(A)+P(B) }. $$
  • Union Bound: More generally: $$ \boxed{ P\left( \bigcup_i A_i \right) \le \sum_iP(A_i) }. $$ This is also known as Boole's inequality.
  • Probability Cannot Exceed One: If an incorrect application of simple addition produces a probability greater than $1$, that is a warning that overlap or some other setup error has been ignored.
  • Not the Multiplication Rule: The Addition Rule concerns: $$ A\cup B, $$ while the Multiplication Rule is used to obtain probabilities involving: $$ A\cap B. $$
  • Terminological Contrast: The Addition Rule finds the probability of a union. The Multiplication Rule helps find the probability of an intersection. Mutually exclusive means events cannot occur together, while independent means occurrence of one does not change the probability of the other.
