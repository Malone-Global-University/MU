Correlation

Baseline Definition

Correlation is a statistical relationship between two variables in which changes in one variable are associated with changes in the other. Correlation describes the direction and, when measured numerically, the strength of an association.

If larger values of one variable tend to occur with larger values of another, the variables have a positive correlation. If larger values of one variable tend to occur with smaller values of the other, they have a negative correlation. If no consistent relationship is apparent, the variables may have little or no correlation.

Correlation describes association rather than necessarily explaining why the association exists. In particular, correlation does not by itself establish causation.

1. Etymology & Roots

The word correlation comes from the prefix co-, meaning “together,” and relation, referring to a connection or relationship. The term therefore literally suggests things that are related together.

In mathematics and statistics, the word developed a more precise meaning: a measurable tendency for two quantities to vary together. This relationship may be direct, inverse, strong, weak, linear, nonlinear, or absent depending on the data and the method used to study it.

The modern statistical study of correlation was developed extensively during the nineteenth century and became particularly associated with the work of Francis Galton, Karl Pearson, and later statisticians who formalized numerical measures of association.

2. Operational Nuance

Positive Correlation

A positive correlation occurs when larger values of one variable tend to be associated with larger values of another variable, while smaller values tend to occur together as well.

For example, in many populations, height and body mass exhibit positive correlation: taller individuals tend, on average, to have greater body mass than shorter individuals.

Negative Correlation

A negative correlation occurs when larger values of one variable tend to be associated with smaller values of another.

For example, if all other factors are held reasonably constant, the speed of completing a fixed-distance trip and the time required for that trip have an inverse relationship: greater speed is associated with less travel time.

Zero or Weak Correlation

Two variables have little or no correlation when changes in one variable do not show a consistent association with changes in the other under the measure being used.

A correlation near zero does not necessarily mean that the variables are completely unrelated. A strong nonlinear relationship can sometimes produce a linear correlation close to zero.

Correlation Coefficient

A correlation coefficient is a numerical measure used to describe an association. One of the most common is the Pearson correlation coefficient, usually written as r.

For Pearson correlation,

−1 ≤ r ≤ 1

  • r = 1 represents perfect positive linear correlation.
  • r = −1 represents perfect negative linear correlation.
  • r = 0 represents no linear correlation.
  • Values closer to 1 or −1 indicate stronger linear association.
  • Values closer to 0 indicate weaker linear association.

The sign of the coefficient indicates the direction of the relationship, while its magnitude indicates the strength of the linear relationship.

Perfect Correlation

A perfect positive correlation occurs when every observed point lies exactly on a straight line with positive slope. A perfect negative correlation occurs when every point lies exactly on a straight line with negative slope.

Real-world data rarely produce perfect correlations because measurements are commonly affected by variation, measurement error, uncontrolled variables, and other influences.

Correlation Is Not Causation

One of the most important cautions in statistics is that correlation does not imply causation. Two variables may move together without either one causing the other.

For example, ice cream sales and incidents of sunburn may both increase during the summer. This does not mean that buying ice cream causes sunburn. A third factor—warmer, sunnier weather—influences both variables.

A correlation may arise because one variable affects another, because both are affected by another variable, because the association is indirect, because of the way the data were selected, or simply because of chance.

Correlation and Scatterplots

Correlation is often examined visually with a scatterplot. Each observation is represented as an ordered pair. The overall arrangement of the points can reveal the direction, strength, shape, and unusual features of the relationship.

A narrow upward-sloping pattern suggests strong positive correlation, while a narrow downward-sloping pattern suggests strong negative correlation. A widely scattered cloud of points usually indicates a weaker linear relationship.

3. Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Written Usage

In statistical writing, correlation is commonly described using the variables involved, the direction of association, and sometimes a numerical correlation coefficient.

Examples include:

  • “The variables have a strong positive correlation.”
  • “There is a weak negative correlation between the two measurements.”
  • “The Pearson correlation coefficient is r = 0.82.”
  • “The data show little linear correlation.”

When a numerical coefficient is supplied, it is important to identify the type of correlation coefficient when the context does not make this clear. Different statistical measures capture different kinds of association.

Spoken Usage

The word correlation is pronounced approximately “kor-uh-LAY-shun.”

The notation r = 0.82 may be read aloud as:

“r equals zero point eight two.”

A value such as r = −0.7 may be described as a moderately strong or strong negative correlation depending on the conventions and context of the field.

Association vs. Equality

Correlation should not be read as an equation connecting two variables. Saying that x and y are correlated does not mean that one can necessarily be calculated exactly from the other.

Correlation describes a pattern of association among observations rather than an exact algebraic identity.

4. Case Usage & Field Variations

Statistics

In statistics, correlation is used to quantify and investigate relationships between variables. Pearson correlation is commonly used for linear relationships between quantitative variables, while other measures may be more appropriate for rankings, nonlinear associations, categorical data, or data that do not satisfy particular statistical assumptions.

Probability

In probability theory, correlation describes dependence between random variables through quantities related to their covariance and variability. Two independent random variables are uncorrelated when their relevant moments exist, but the converse is not generally true: uncorrelated variables need not be independent.

Data Science

In data science, correlation matrices are commonly used to examine relationships among many variables simultaneously. Analysts may use them to identify redundant features, possible predictors, unexpected associations, and variables that warrant further investigation.

Economics and Business

Correlation is frequently used to study relationships among prices, interest rates, sales, economic indicators, investment returns, consumer behavior, and other economic quantities.

In finance, the correlation between asset returns is particularly important because assets that do not move closely together can affect portfolio diversification and risk.

Science

Scientists use correlation to identify patterns that may motivate further investigation. For example, researchers may study correlations among environmental conditions, biological measurements, physical variables, or observed outcomes.

A detected correlation may support the development of a hypothesis, but establishing a causal mechanism generally requires additional evidence, experimental design, theoretical justification, or other forms of analysis.

Linear and Nonlinear Relationships

The ordinary Pearson correlation coefficient measures linear association. Consequently, a low Pearson correlation does not prove that no meaningful relationship exists.

For example, if one variable is approximately the square of another, the data may follow a strong curved pattern even when their linear correlation is small.

Correlation and Covariance

Correlation is closely related to covariance. Covariance indicates whether two variables tend to vary in the same or opposite directions, but its magnitude depends on the units in which the variables are measured.

Correlation standardizes this relationship, producing a dimensionless measure that is easier to interpret and compare across different datasets.

Interpretive Summary

Correlation answers a fundamental question: To what extent do two variables vary together?

The concept is useful because relationships among quantities are central to mathematics, statistics, science, economics, and data analysis. Correlation provides a systematic way to describe those relationships while preserving an essential distinction between association and causal explanation.
