Combination Formula

Subfield: Probability & Statistical Inference

Etymology & Roots

The combination formula counts the number of ways to select $r$ objects from $n$ distinct objects when the order of selection does not matter.

$$ \boxed{ \binom{n}{r} = \frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!} } $$

The expression $\binom{n}{r}$ is read n choose r and is also called a binomial coefficient.

For the ordinary finite counting interpretation:

$$ \boxed{ n,r\in\mathbb Z, \qquad 0\le r\le n }. $$

The formula is specifically designed to remove the duplicate orderings that occur when the same selected group can be arranged in several ways.

Operational Nuance

Core Formula

$$ \boxed{ \binom{n}{r} = \frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!} }. $$

Meaning of n

The quantity $n$ is the total number of distinct objects available.

Meaning of r

The quantity $r$ is the number of objects selected.

Meaning of the Factorials

The factorial:

$$ n! = n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots2\cdot1 $$

counts ordered arrangements of $n$ distinct objects.

In the combination formula, the factors $r!$ and $(n-r)!$ remove orderings that should not be counted as distinct combinations.

Derivation from Permutations

First count ordered selections of $r$ objects from $n$:

$$ P(n,r) = \frac{n!}{(n-r)!}. $$

Every selected group of $r$ objects can be internally arranged in:

$$ r! $$

different orders.

Since those orderings represent the same combination, divide by $r!$:

$$ \binom nr = \frac{P(n,r)}{r!}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \binom nr = \frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!} }. $$

Why Division by r! Is Necessary

Suppose the selected objects are $A$, $B$, and $C$.

The ordered arrangements are:

$$ ABC,\ ACB,\ BAC,\ BCA,\ CAB,\ CBA. $$

There are:

$$ 3!=6 $$

such arrangements, but all represent the same unordered selection:

$$ \boxed{ \{A,B,C\} }. $$

Example: Choose 3 from 8

$$ \binom83 = \frac{8!}{3!5!}. $$

Cancel the common $5!$:

$$ \binom83 = \frac{8\cdot7\cdot6}{3\cdot2\cdot1}. $$

Thus:

$$ \boxed{ \binom83=56 }. $$

Efficient Cancellation

It is usually unnecessary to calculate large factorials in full.

For example:

$$ \binom{20}{3} = \frac{20!}{3!17!} = \frac{20\cdot19\cdot18}{3\cdot2\cdot1}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \binom{20}{3}=1140 }. $$

Choose the Smaller Side

The symmetry identity:

$$ \boxed{ \binom nr = \binom n{n-r} } $$

can simplify arithmetic.

For example:

$$ \binom{100}{98} = \binom{100}{2}. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ \binom{100}{98} = \frac{100\cdot99}{2} = 4950 }. $$

Why the Symmetry Identity Works

Choosing the $r$ objects to include uniquely determines the $n-r$ objects that are excluded.

$$ \boxed{ \text{choose what enters} \longleftrightarrow \text{choose what remains out} }. $$

Choosing Zero Objects

$$ \boxed{ \binom n0=1 }. $$

There is exactly one zero-element selection: the empty set.

Choosing All Objects

$$ \boxed{ \binom nn=1 }. $$

Choosing One Object

$$ \boxed{ \binom n1=n }. $$

Choosing n - 1 Objects

$$ \boxed{ \binom n{n-1}=n }. $$

Choosing all but one object is equivalent to choosing which single object is left out.

Combination Formula versus Permutation Formula

Situation Formula Order Matters?
Combination $\displaystyle \binom nr=\frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}$ No
Permutation $\displaystyle P(n,r)=\frac{n!}{(n-r)!}$ Yes

Relationship to the Permutation Formula

$$ \boxed{ P(n,r) = r!\binom nr }. $$

A permutation count is therefore a combination count multiplied by the number of ways to order each selected group.

Committee Example

A committee of $5$ people is selected from $12$ candidates.

Since no positions are assigned:

$$ \binom{12}{5} = \frac{12!}{5!7!}. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ \binom{12}{5}=792 }. $$

When Roles Are Assigned

If the same five people are instead chosen for five distinct offices, the problem is ordered and the combination formula alone is not sufficient.

$$ \boxed{ P(12,5) = 5!\binom{12}{5} }. $$

Pascal's Identity

$$ \boxed{ \binom nr = \binom{n-1}{r-1} + \binom{n-1}{r} }. $$

This gives a recursive method for computing combinations.

Combinatorial Meaning of Pascal's Identity

Choose one particular object. Every $r$-object combination either:

  • contains that object, leaving $r-1$ objects to choose from the other $n-1$, or
  • does not contain it, requiring all $r$ objects to come from the other $n-1$.

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ \binom{n-1}{r-1} + \binom{n-1}{r} = \binom nr }. $$

Pascal's Triangle

Repeated use of Pascal's identity creates Pascal's Triangle, whose entries are combination counts.

$$ \begin{array}{ccccccccc} &&&&1\\ &&&1&&1\\ &&1&&2&&1\\ &1&&3&&3&&1\\ 1&&4&&6&&4&&1 \end{array} $$

Binomial Theorem

The same combination formula produces the coefficients in binomial expansion:

$$ \boxed{ (x+y)^n = \sum_{r=0}^{n} \binom nr x^{n-r}y^r }. $$

Why Combinations Appear in the Binomial Theorem

In multiplying:

$$ (x+y)(x+y)\cdots(x+y), $$

a term containing $r$ copies of $y$ is produced by choosing which $r$ of the $n$ factors contribute $y$.

The number of such choices is:

$$ \boxed{ \binom nr }. $$

Total Number of Subsets

An $n$-element set has:

$$ \boxed{ 2^n } $$

subsets.

Grouping those subsets by size gives:

$$ \boxed{ \sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nr = 2^n }. $$

Probability Applications

The combination formula frequently appears when equally likely outcomes are unordered selections.

Card-Hand Example

A $5$-card hand from a $52$-card deck can be selected in:

$$ \boxed{ \binom{52}{5} = 2{,}598{,}960 } $$

ways.

The order in which the cards were dealt does not change the final hand.

Binomial Probability Formula

The number of ways to choose which $k$ of $n$ Bernoulli trials are successes is:

$$ \binom nk. $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{ P(X=k) = \binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k} }. $$

Hypergeometric Probability

Suppose $N$ objects contain $K$ successes and $N-K$ failures. Selecting $n$ objects without replacement and obtaining exactly $k$ successes gives the favorable count:

$$ \boxed{ \binom Kk \binom{N-K}{n-k} }. $$

The total number of unordered samples is:

$$ \binom Nn. $$

Hence:

$$ \boxed{ P(X=k) = \frac{ \binom Kk \binom{N-K}{n-k} }{ \binom Nn } }. $$

Combinations with Repetition

The ordinary combination formula assumes no repeated selection of the same distinct object.

If repetition is allowed and $r$ objects are selected from $n$ types, the count becomes:

$$ \boxed{ \binom{n+r-1}{r} }. $$

This is a different formula and should not be confused with $\binom nr$.

Example with Repetition

Choosing $4$ scoops from $3$ flavors with repetition allowed and order ignored gives:

$$ \binom{3+4-1}{4} = \binom64 = \boxed{15}. $$

Connection to Stars and Bars

The combinations-with-repetition formula also counts nonnegative integer solutions to:

$$ x_1+x_2+\cdots+x_n=r. $$

The number of solutions is:

$$ \boxed{ \binom{n+r-1}{r} }. $$

Alternative Notations

The combination count may also appear as:

$$ \boxed{ {}_nC_r, \qquad C(n,r), \qquad \binom nr }. $$

The notation $\binom nr$ is especially common in higher mathematics because the same numbers are binomial coefficients.

Common Misconception: n Choose r Is a Fraction

Although $\binom nr$ is written vertically, it is not ordinary division. It is a special symbol for a binomial coefficient.

Common Misconception: Order Matters a Little

In an ordinary combination, order does not matter at all.

$$ \boxed{ \{A,B,C\} = \{C,A,B\} }. $$

Common Misconception: Divide by n!

Only the orderings of the selected $r$ objects are redundant, so the permutation count is divided by $r!$, not by $n!$.

Common Misconception: The Formula Allows Repetition Automatically

The standard formula $\binom nr$ counts selections without repetition from $n$ distinct objects.

Common Misconception: n - r Has No Interpretation

The quantity $n-r$ is the number of objects not selected. This interpretation explains both the factorial term $(n-r)!$ and the symmetry identity.

Common Misconception: Combination and Permutation Formulas Are Interchangeable

They answer different questions. The deciding issue is whether changing the order produces a different outcome.

Written vs. Spoken Syntax

Symbolic Notation (Written) Professional Articulation (Spoken)
$\binom nr$ "n choose r."
$\binom nr=\frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}$ "n choose r equals n factorial divided by r factorial times n minus r factorial."
$\binom nr=\binom n{n-r}$ "n choose r equals n choose n minus r."
$P(n,r)=r!\binom nr$ "The permutation count equals r factorial times the combination count."
$\binom{n+r-1}{r}$ "The combinations-with-repetition formula."

In spoken problem solving, phrases such as choose, select a group, form a committee, and make a hand often signal combinations when no ordering or distinct roles are involved.

Defense Notation Advice: Before substituting into the formula, explicitly identify $n$ as the total available objects and $r$ as the number chosen. Then ask whether order matters and whether repetition is allowed. If either condition differs from ordinary unordered sampling without replacement, another counting formula may be required.

Case Usage & Field Variations

  • Core Formula: $$ \boxed{ \binom nr = \frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!} }. $$
  • Meaning: Counts selections of $r$ distinct objects from $n$ distinct objects when order does not matter.
  • Standard Conditions: $n,r$ are nonnegative integers with $0\le r\le n$.
  • Read As: "$n$ choose $r$."
  • Permutation Relationship: $$ \boxed{ \binom nr = \frac{P(n,r)}{r!} }. $$
  • Symmetry: $$ \boxed{ \binom nr = \binom n{n-r} }. $$
  • Boundary Values: $$ \boxed{ \binom n0 = \binom nn = 1 }. $$
  • Pascal Identity: $$ \boxed{ \binom nr = \binom{n-1}{r-1} + \binom{n-1}{r} }. $$
  • Binomial Theorem: $\binom nr$ gives the coefficient of $x^{n-r}y^r$ in $(x+y)^n$.
  • Subset Counting: $\binom nr$ counts $r$-element subsets of an $n$-element set.
  • Probability: Used in card hands, committees, binomial probabilities, hypergeometric probabilities, and other unordered selections.
  • Repetition Allowed: $$ \boxed{ \binom{n+r-1}{r} } $$ is the standard combinations-with-repetition count.
  • Common Error: Do not use the formula when order distinguishes outcomes.
  • Common Error: Do not treat $\binom nr$ as ordinary division.
  • Common Error: Do not assume repetition is allowed in the ordinary formula.
  • Common Error: Do not expand enormous factorials unnecessarily; cancel common factorials first.
  • Terminological Contrast: Combination formula counts unordered selections without repetition. Combination is one such unordered selection. Binomial coefficient is the numerical value $\binom nr$. Permutation formula counts ordered selections. Combination with repetition permits repeated types and uses a different counting formula. Factorial supplies the multiplicative counting structure from which the combination formula is derived.
