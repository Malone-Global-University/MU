# William Grant Still

**William Grant Still (May 11, 1895 – December 3, 1978)** was an American composer, conductor, instrumentalist, arranger and orchestrator whose career joined the commercial music industry of the Jazz Age to the institutions of American concert music. His works encompassed symphonies, operas, ballets, chamber music, art songs, choral compositions and music for broadcasting and motion pictures. He is particularly associated with his *Afro-American Symphony*, first performed in 1931, and *Troubled Island*, produced by the New York City Opera in 1949.

Still participated in the development of African American musical theater, worked in the emerging Black recording industry and studied with composers whose approaches ranged from established European concert traditions to radical modernism. His professional achievement cannot be adequately described as a transition from popular music to classical music: his knowledge of both remained fundamental to his creative identity. He became a pioneering figure in the struggle for access to American orchestras and opera companies while continuing to develop an independent musical language.

## 1. Birth, family background and childhood in Arkansas

Still was born in Woodville, Mississippi, to William Grant Still Sr. and Carrie Lena Fambro. His parents were educators, and his father had musical experience. The elder Still died while his son was an infant. His mother subsequently established their home in Little Rock, Arkansas, where she worked as a teacher. She later married Charles Shepperson.

The family's educational background is important to understanding the composer's development. The late nineteenth-century South offered Black citizens severely restricted access to many public institutions, but African American schools, churches, professional households and community organizations sustained intellectual and artistic activity. Still grew up within that institutional world rather than outside it. His musical development was supported by family members who encouraged his interest in instruments and introduced him to different kinds of music.

The Arkansas environment also placed him in contact with traditions that later informed his compositions. Religious music, vernacular songs, instrumental entertainment and music circulated through recordings and live performance. These were not identical traditions, and Still would eventually draw upon them selectively rather than regard them as an undifferentiated folk inheritance.

His childhood helps explain why Southern cultural experience remained important even after decades spent in northern and western cities. Woodville was his birthplace, but Little Rock was the principal setting of his formative years. [1, 2, 3]

## 2. Musical education and Wilberforce University

Still entered Wilberforce in Ohio in 1911. The historically Black institution provided an educational environment in which he could pursue academic studies and participate in musical life. His initial course of study was directed toward medicine rather than a professional career in composition. Music nevertheless became increasingly central to his activities.

At Wilberforce he gained practical experience with ensembles and arranging. Such work involves more than the ability to play an instrument: a musician must adapt existing material to the number and abilities of available performers, understand instrumental ranges and organize rehearsable parts. These skills subsequently became commercially valuable.

His early education did not follow the uninterrupted course usually associated with a conservatory-trained composer. Work, marriage and military service competed with formal study. Still married Grace Bundy in 1915, and the couple eventually had four children. Supporting a family made paid employment an immediate consideration.

The distinction between formal credentials and actual musical preparation is especially relevant here. Still acquired knowledge through institutions, private teachers and professional practice, but did not complete the conventional conservatory degree pathway. His later honorary degrees recognized achievements established through his compositions and professional life. [1, 2, 4]

## 3. W. C. Handy and the commercial blues industry

Still's association with W. C. Handy began during the period in which the blues was becoming an increasingly important part of American commercial publishing. By 1916 he had worked with Handy in Memphis. Later employment with the Pace and Handy publishing enterprise brought him further experience in the business of musical arrangements.

Handy's enterprise occupied an important position between regional performance traditions and the national sheet-music market. Publishing made it possible for compositions to circulate far beyond the places where musicians first performed them. An arranger helped convert a song into forms suitable for various ensembles, while a publisher coordinated the distribution and exploitation of the work.

The process involved questions of artistic identity as well as business. Blues techniques existed in communities before commercial publication, and the popularity of published blues did not necessarily mean that the musicians who developed the underlying styles received financial recognition. Handy's own success demonstrated both the commercial potential of Black musical traditions and the importance of institutional control over their circulation.

Still learned to work inside that system. His later symphonic use of blues language was grounded in professional familiarity with the idiom, not simply in a concert composer's observation of an unfamiliar popular genre. [2, 5, 6]

## 4. Oberlin, naval service and the return to civilian music

Still entered Oberlin Conservatory in 1917. At Oberlin he had access to more systematic training in composition and musical theory, complementing the practical skills acquired earlier. His studies were interrupted by American participation in World War I.

He served in the United States Navy, where racial restrictions shaped the kinds of positions available to Black servicemen. Biographical accounts describe his service as including mess duties and violin performance for officers. Such assignments illustrate the distinction between military participation and equal professional opportunity: the armed forces could make use of a person's abilities without extending the same occupational pathways to all personnel.

Following his discharge, Still returned to Oberlin for a time. He ultimately left without completing a degree and moved into the increasingly active musical economy of New York in 1919.

Oberlin would remain connected to his reputation long after this period. Its archives now preserve material documenting his career, and the institution awarded him an honorary doctorate in 1947. His experience demonstrates the cumulative character of professional education: the study of theory, the demands of paid performance and the experience of working in ensembles were mutually reinforcing. [2, 4, 7]

## 5. New York, Harlem and the Great Migration

Still arrived in New York at a consequential time in American cultural history. The Great Migration was transforming the population of northern cities, while Harlem was becoming a center of Black publishing, politics, literature and entertainment. The postwar recording business and the demand for dance music created new opportunities, although discrimination remained embedded in hiring, theatrical access and ownership.

The Harlem Renaissance is frequently described through its poets and novelists, but its musical economy included many less visible workers: copyists, arrangers, pit musicians, accompanists, recording supervisors and touring instrumentalists. These workers supplied the infrastructure that allowed performers to appear before large audiences.

Still belonged to this professional community. His commercial assignments paid for daily life and brought him into contact with musicians from different backgrounds. His subsequent concert works were therefore products of an artist who had participated directly in metropolitan entertainment.

He also encountered intellectual arguments about the cultural value of Black creative production. Alain Locke and other writers associated with the New Negro movement treated the arts as a means of asserting independent cultural identity. Still's work shared that concern, although his aesthetic positions evolved over the course of his career and were not reducible to any single intellectual program. [5, 8, 9]

## 6. *Shuffle Along* and the Broadway orchestra

One of Still's most important early theatrical engagements was the original Broadway production of *Shuffle Along*, which opened in 1921. The musical was created by Noble Sissle, Eubie Blake, Flournoy Miller and Aubrey Lyles. It became a landmark in the history of Black American musical theater and helped extend the visibility of African American performers on Broadway.

The Internet Broadway Database identifies Still as an oboist in the original production. The orchestra also included Hall Johnson on viola and Eubie Blake at the piano. The surviving production credits provide a firmer basis for identifying Still's actual function than retrospective statements that vaguely associate him with the show.

The production connects Still directly to the careers of [Florence Mills](florence-mills.md) and [Josephine Baker](josephine-baker.md), both of whom emerged from the theatrical network associated with the musical. Mills became one of the show's celebrated performers; Baker's connection developed through its touring and chorus work. Their individual experiences should not be collapsed into a claim that every participant held the same role or appeared in the same cast at the same time.

Still's employment also reveals the technical resources behind successful Black musical comedy. The music required trained instrumentalists capable of performing precisely in conjunction with singers, dancers and stage action. The boundary between theatrical music and concert musicianship was considerably more porous than later genre classifications might suggest. [9, 10]

## 7. Black Swan Records and African American cultural enterprise

Still became involved with Black Swan Records, established by Harry Pace in 1921 after his publishing partnership with Handy ended. The new company sought to create a recording platform for African American talent at a time when major firms exercised disproportionate control over access to the market.

Still worked in recording supervision and arranging, and also performed as an instrumentalist. These functions placed him at the intersection of artistic preparation, technical execution and commercial distribution. The arrangements had to suit the artists, the available instrumentalists and the constraints of early recording technology.

Black Swan's ambition extended beyond the simple sale of records. Its catalog challenged the assumption that Black artists should be confined to a narrow range of commercially prescribed musical genres. The company pursued both popular and cultivated musical repertories, even though the latter were often difficult to market profitably.

The business encountered intense competition and financial problems, and its independent operation was short-lived. Its historical significance, however, cannot be assessed solely through its survival as a company. It demonstrated an alternative model of musical ownership, artistic selection and representation. For Still, the experience provided additional knowledge of the recording business, and his later difficulties in securing performance and publication would make the question of institutional access an enduring one. [5, 6, 11]

## 8. George Whitefield Chadwick and Edgard Varèse

Still's musical training included study with George Whitefield Chadwick and Edgard Varèse, two figures whose approaches help explain the breadth of his subsequent work. Chadwick, associated with the New England Conservatory, represented a strong tradition of American composition grounded in European formal and harmonic practices. Varèse, a French-born modernist active in New York, explored unconventional sonorities, new instrumental combinations and alternatives to established compositional expectations.

The two traditions were not simply successive stages from which Still selected a final allegiance. Chadwick's approach offered a foundation in the manipulation of established musical structures, while Varèse encouraged experimentation with the character of sound itself. Still could apply lessons from both without adopting the complete aesthetic program of either teacher.

Varèse's International Composers' Guild also provided opportunities for the presentation of new music that did not readily fit conventional concert programming. The Guild's activities placed Still's work in a context where departures from standard harmonic and instrumental practice could be heard as intentional artistic choices.

Still eventually favored a more openly melodic and culturally grounded idiom than that usually identified with Varèse. This did not erase the value of his modernist apprenticeship. His later clarity of expression was the product of selection among available resources rather than ignorance of the more experimental possibilities. [1, 5, 8]

## 9. Early concert compositions and *Darker America*

Still's concert output developed substantially during the 1920s. *Darker America*, composed in 1924–1925, is among the important works of this formative period. Other examples include *From the Land of Dreams*, *From the Journal of a Wanderer* and *From the Black Belt*.

The titles suggest an interest in the ability of instrumental composition to communicate character, experience and cultural meaning. *Darker America* is especially associated with the representation of Black experience in the United States. It employs orchestral contrasts rather than a spoken argument, allowing musical tension, changes of texture and the development of themes to carry expressive weight.

The work also belongs to a broader problem facing composers who wished to engage national or ethnic identity. A piece might use familiar idioms without reproducing a particular song, or refer to a historical experience without adopting a literal narrative. The listener should therefore distinguish musical references from the assumption that every passage depicts a specific event.

These early works were not merely incomplete preparations for the later symphony. They document a changing relationship among Still's experimental training, his professional musical experience and his efforts to establish a recognizable compositional identity. [2, 8, 12]

## 10. Florence Mills and *Levee Land*

In 1925 Still composed *Levee Land*, a suite for soprano and instrumental accompaniment that brought his concert interests into direct contact with the performing traditions of the Black musical stage. Florence Mills performed the work at an International Composers' Guild concert on January 24, 1926.

Mills was already celebrated for her singing, dancing and theatrical expression. Her participation in a modern-music concert challenges a narrow categorization of her as solely a Broadway or revue entertainer. The performance also demonstrates that Still could use the expressive abilities of a popular singer within a deliberately composed concert work.

The music juxtaposes blues-related material with other compositional textures. Its significance lies in the integration of those resources rather than in treating vernacular singing as a decorative interruption of serious music. The work offers a particularly useful example of how concert compositions and popular performance could interact during the Harlem Renaissance.

The surviving documentation of Mills's voice is limited. Consequently, the written music and historical accounts of the performance cannot fully reconstruct the nuances she brought to the occasion. Modern performances may illuminate the composition, but they should not be represented as exact reproductions of her interpretation.

For Mills's career and her influence on later entertainers, see the related [Florence Mills](florence-mills.md) and [Baby Esther](baby-esther.md) biographies. [12, 13]

## 11. The Paul Whiteman orchestra and commercial arranging

Still's work for Paul Whiteman linked him to one of the most influential commercial orchestras of the interwar period. Whiteman's organization employed arrangers who transformed popular songs into carefully coordinated orchestral presentations for recordings, broadcasts and public performance.

Still traveled to California in connection with Whiteman's activities in 1929. The engagement formed part of a wider professional network that included radio and theater assignments. He also worked with performers and bandleaders such as Sophie Tucker, Willard Robison and Artie Shaw over the course of his commercial career.

The arranger's responsibilities differed from those of the composer of an original song. An arrangement might change the instrumentation, introduction, harmonic background, transitions, instrumental solos or conclusion of a composition while retaining the source melody. The distinction matters because an arranger's musical contribution can be substantial even when the underlying composition belongs to another writer.

In an industry where many such contributions were not prominently credited, surviving contracts, scores and recording records are essential to precise attribution. Still's employment demonstrates his command of orchestral organization, but not every work issued by an ensemble that employed him can automatically be identified as his arrangement. [1, 2, 8]

## 12. *Africa*, *Sahdji* and the symphonic imagination

Still explored African and African diasporic subjects through works such as the orchestral suite *Africa* and the choral ballet *Sahdji*. These compositions emerged at a time when African themes occupied an important place in debates about Black cultural identity and artistic independence.

A concert work bearing an African title does not necessarily constitute an ethnographic transcription of African music. Composers frequently constructed imagined musical settings using the resources of Western orchestras, their knowledge of other traditions and the cultural expectations of the period. The difference between imaginative representation and direct documentation is important to any historical assessment of such works.

*Sahdji* also illustrates the collaborative nature of ballet. A score is only one component of a staged production, alongside choreography, scenario, design and performance. Its association with African subject matter should be examined through those components rather than understood as a purely instrumental declaration.

Howard Hanson's support at Rochester helped provide a setting in which works of this kind could be performed. Still's growing visibility as a concert composer resulted not only from the creation of manuscripts but from relationships with conductors, performers and institutions willing to present new American music. [8, 12, 14]

## 13. The *Afro-American Symphony* (1930–1931)

Still completed his first symphony in 1930. The Rochester Philharmonic Orchestra, conducted by Howard Hanson, presented the work in October 1931. The Boston Symphony's historical note identifies October 29 as the first performance; scholarship based on Still's chronology also records performances on October 28 and 29. The essential milestone is the Rochester presentation in October 1931.

It is widely recognized as the first symphony by an African American composer performed by a major American orchestra. The qualification matters: Black composers had written symphonies and orchestral works before Still, and the milestone concerns institutional performance rather than the invention of Black symphonic composition.

The symphony uses an original blues-inflected theme as a recurring musical resource. Instead of presenting blues as a brief interlude, Still integrates its melodic and harmonic characteristics into a multi-movement concert structure. The work also draws on the poetry of Paul Laurence Dunbar, whose verses frame the emotional character of the movements.

The score calls for a large orchestra that includes a tenor banjo. The presence of this instrument is particularly audible in the scherzo, where its association with American vernacular entertainment enters an orchestral setting. Still's achievement is not dependent upon claiming that no earlier symphony used such an instrument; it rests on the musical function assigned to it. [8, 14, 15, 16]

## 14. Musical structure and the meaning of the blues

The four movements of the *Afro-American Symphony* are commonly associated with the expressive designations Longing, Sorrow, Humor and Aspiration. They suggest a progression of emotional experience rather than a single uninterrupted portrait of suffering.

| Movement | Expressive designation | Principal listening focus |
|---|---|---|
| I | Longing | Establishment and development of the blues-related thematic material |
| II | Sorrow | A more contemplative treatment of melody and orchestral color |
| III | Humor | Rhythmic energy, contrasting themes and vernacular instrumental associations |
| IV | Aspiration | Expansion toward a concluding statement of confidence and possibility |

A recurring theme can unify a symphony even when the mood, tempo and orchestration of each movement differ. Still's use of thematic transformation illustrates the compatibility of the blues with large-scale musical organization. The work also complicates the expectation that music representing Black American experience must focus exclusively on hardship.

The Dunbar epigraphs provide a literary frame but do not prescribe a single mandatory interpretation for all listeners. Analysis of the score, comparison of recordings and study of Still's compositional sketches can reveal further relationships between text and music. Such analysis is distinct from assigning a specific historical event to each theme without documentary support.

The symphony remains a productive introduction to Still's music, but its popularity should not obscure his later orchestral and dramatic works. [9, 15, 16]

## 15. National recognition, Howard Hanson and orchestral access

The Rochester premiere was followed by performances that extended Still's reputation beyond the original institutional setting. The New York Philharmonic performed the *Afro-American Symphony* at Carnegie Hall on November 20, 1935. Other conductors and ensembles incorporated his compositions into their programs during the 1930s and 1940s.

Howard Hanson was an important advocate for American composers, and his willingness to program Still's work supplied a crucial link between manuscript composition and public performance. In concert music, institutional gatekeeping can occur at several stages: a conductor may select a score, an orchestra may authorize rehearsal time, a publisher may produce parts, and a presenter may decide how to market the resulting concert.

A composer who gains access to one of these stages has not necessarily secured the others. Even a successful premiere does not guarantee future performances, publication or recordings. This distinction is useful in understanding Still's later efforts to maintain the circulation of his works.

The broader significance of his success lies partly in the expansion of the possible repertory of American orchestras. By performing a major work explicitly grounded in Black musical culture, these institutions confronted assumptions about which traditions could sustain symphonic composition. [2, 8, 14]

## 16. California, fellowships and the Hollywood Bowl

Still relocated permanently to Los Angeles in 1934. The city offered commercial music opportunities associated with broadcasting and the motion picture industry, alongside a growing concert culture. Fellowship support during the 1930s helped create additional time for original composition, although commercial employment remained significant.

On July 23, 1936, he conducted the Los Angeles Philharmonic at the Hollywood Bowl in a program that included music from his own compositions. The orchestra and Bowl identify the event as the first occasion on which an African American conducted a major American orchestra. The performance was a major institutional breakthrough, particularly in light of the exclusion of Black musicians from many established orchestral positions.

Still's appearance did not mean that these barriers disappeared. A guest conducting engagement and appointment as the permanent music director of an orchestra are distinct achievements. His milestone should be understood on its own terms, without implying that he became the orchestra's regular conductor or that the event immediately transformed its hiring practices.

The California years also brought him into contact with artists and writers whose professional collaboration became important to his subsequent career. [17, 18]

## 17. Verna Arvey and the creative partnership

Still married Verna Arvey in Mexico in February 1939 following his divorce from Grace Bundy. California then prohibited marriages between people classified as white and Black under its racial marriage laws. Their marriage outside the state reflected a legal barrier, not merely a preference for an international wedding. Still and Arvey had two children together.

Arvey was a pianist, journalist and writer who became a major creative and professional collaborator. Her work included texts for vocal and dramatic compositions, the completion of libretti, publicity and the management of aspects of Still's career. Her own literary and musical training made this more than a conventional arrangement in which a spouse simply assisted an established artist.

The partnership is particularly important in the study of Still's operas. A libretto determines how dramatic action, characterization and language are organized for musical performance. A composer and librettist may revise a work repeatedly as the practical demands of staging become apparent. Arvey's contribution deserves independent attribution wherever the surviving documentation establishes it.

After Still's death, she published *In One Lifetime*, a biography that remains an important resource. Its proximity to the subject is valuable, but it also reflects the perspective of a participant in his professional struggles. Independent scholarship and archival materials can supplement and sometimes challenge that account. [19, 20, 21]

## 18. Hollywood, racial representation and *Stormy Weather*

Still's Hollywood employment involved composition and arrangement for motion pictures, often without prominent screen credit. Studio music departments relied on teams of composers, arrangers, orchestrators, copyists and performers. The name appearing in a film's principal music credit does not necessarily account for every musician who worked on the production.

One especially revealing episode involved the 1943 musical *Stormy Weather*. The film brought together major Black entertainers, including Lena Horne, Bill Robinson and Cab Calloway. Still worked on arrangements but withdrew from the production. In an account published in the *Chicago Defender*, he explained his objections to the treatment of Black people by the studio and to its handling of the musical material.

His protest complicates the film's legacy. A production may provide unusually extensive screen opportunities for performers who face discrimination while still relying on stereotyped characters, unequal working relationships or restrictive decisions made by studio executives. Still's account supplies evidence about his own experience and position; it is not sufficient by itself to establish the motives or experiences of every participant in the film.

The dispute also demonstrates his willingness to challenge the conditions of commercial employment. It belongs alongside his concert career in any evaluation of his professional choices. For the musical and nightclub background of several of the film's performers, see the [Cotton Club](../../art/music/music-history/cotton-club.md) and [Cab Calloway](cab-calloway.md) articles. [1, 22]

## 19. *Song of a City* and the 1939 New York World's Fair

Still composed *Song of a City* for the 1939 New York World's Fair. It accompanied Democracity, a model of an imagined future community presented inside the Perisphere, one of the fair's principal architectural attractions. The assignment required a composition designed to coordinate with a timed exhibition rather than an autonomous concert work.

The commission illustrates the range of Still's professional capabilities. Music intended for a continuously operating public attraction must accommodate the pacing of the visual presentation and the experience of visitors entering a carefully controlled exhibition environment. His background in film and radio provided relevant technical experience.

The work is sometimes described as the official theme of the entire fair. That characterization is imprecise. The Library of Congress's account distinguishes the music for Democracity from other songs associated with the exposition and examines the racial politics surrounding its selection and publicity. Still's commission was a significant achievement without requiring that it be assigned a broader official status than the evidence supports.

The exhibition also raises a historical contradiction: a Black composer provided music for a public vision of American progress at a time when legal segregation and racial exclusion continued to limit participation in that society. The artistic achievement and the social circumstances are both relevant to its interpretation. [23]

## 20. Anti-lynching compositions and wartime citizenship

Still addressed American racial violence and citizenship through several works for voice, chorus and orchestra. Among the most consequential is *And They Lynched Him on a Tree*, composed to a text by Katherine Garrison Chapin and presented in New York on June 24, 1940.

The work brings together collective voices and instrumental music to confront the practice of lynching. Its dramatic design permits contrasting social perspectives to appear within the same composition rather than reducing the subject to an abstract orchestral mood. The treatment is notable because it places a subject of immediate political and moral urgency inside a large-scale concert work.

Other compositions from this period include *Plain-Chant for America*, also associated with Chapin, and *The Colored Soldiers Who Died for Democracy*. The latter's title identifies the contradiction between the defense of democratic principles overseas and racial inequality at home. Such works demonstrate that Still's engagement with American national identity included explicit criticism of the nation's failures.

His art should not, however, be reduced to political statements. Musical form, poetic language and performance conditions remain essential to understanding the effectiveness of these compositions. His output ranged from works confronting social injustice to pieces concerned with domestic life, religious reflection, dance and instrumental expression. [2, 21, 24]

## 21. *Troubled Island* and the Haitian Revolution

Still's opera *Troubled Island* deals with the Haitian Revolution and the turbulent rule of Jean-Jacques Dessalines, a central figure in the establishment of independent Haiti. Langston Hughes initiated the libretto; Verna Arvey contributed to its completion. The work developed over a prolonged period before reaching the stage, and sources assign different dates to its initial completion and subsequent revisions.

The historical subject offered an opportunity to place Black political leadership, revolution and sovereignty at the center of an American opera. It was also dramatically challenging. The transformation of a complex revolution into a stage work requires the selection of particular characters, conflicts and events. The opera should therefore be approached as an artistic interpretation, not as a substitute for a history of Haiti.

On March 31, 1949, the New York City Opera presented the world premiere at City Center. The production is widely identified as the first opera by an African American composer staged by a major United States opera company. The distinction concerns the scale and status of the producing institution. African American composers had created operas and participated in operatic life long before 1949.

The occasion represented a breakthrough for Still, Hughes and Arvey, but also for the larger history of access to American opera. Its limited subsequent production history illustrates the difference between a landmark premiere and the establishment of a durable place in the repertoire. [20, 25, 26]

## 22. Critical reception, controversy and the limits of the breakthrough

*Troubled Island* received a mixed critical response. Some observers recognized the importance of its production, while others criticized elements of the drama and musical execution. A contemporary review in *The New Yorker* was unfavorable; *Time* also reported on the circumstances surrounding the premiere.

Still and Arvey subsequently attributed some of the opera's difficulties to hostile political networks. Their concerns must be understood in the context of the Cold War, when accusations of communist affiliation could affect artistic employment and public reputation. They also had direct experience with exclusion from musical institutions. Nevertheless, a claim that an organized campaign caused the opera's reception or limited run requires evidence beyond the fact of unfavorable reviews.

The distinction is especially important when assessing a career shaped by demonstrable discrimination. Evidence of racial barriers in one setting does not automatically establish the specific explanation for every later professional setback. Conversely, the existence of legitimate musical criticism does not disprove the influence of institutional prejudice.

Still's later political views and his relationship to other Black intellectuals were complex. His commitment to racial equality coexisted with anti-communist convictions that brought him into conflict with some contemporaries. The scholarship collected in Catherine Parsons Smith's *William Grant Still: A Study in Contradictions* and the 2003 *Troubled Island* symposium provides useful frameworks for examining these disputes without treating partisan allegations as settled facts. [8, 26, 27, 28, 29]

## 23. Later symphonies, chamber music and the breadth of his output

Still continued to compose extensively after the period in which his first symphony established his reputation. His five numbered symphonies demonstrate the persistence of his interest in large-scale orchestral expression. Their numbering is not a simple chronological sequence: the work now designated Symphony No. 5 was begun before the later numbered Sunday Symphony and underwent revision.

| Symphony | Title or identifying designation | Date or context |
|---|---|---|
| No. 1 | *Afro-American* | 1930; Rochester premiere, 1931 |
| No. 2 | *Song of a New Race* | 1937; premiered by the Philadelphia Orchestra under Leopold Stokowski |
| No. 3 | *The Sunday Symphony* | 1958 |
| No. 4 | *Autochthonous* | 1947; premiered in 1951 |
| No. 5 | *Western Hemisphere* | 1945; subsequently revised and renumbered |

*Autochthonous* is concerned with the combination of musical traditions in North America, extending his exploration of national identity beyond the particular program of the first symphony. His chamber and solo works demonstrate a similarly broad range of interests.

The *Suite for Violin and Piano* (1943) draws inspiration from visual artworks; its three movements are titled *African Dancer*, *Mother and Child* and *Gamin*. *Danzas de Panama* (1948) brings Panamanian dance material into string ensemble writing. The piano collection *Seven Traceries*, the vocal cycle *Songs of Separation*, and works such as *Ennanga* offer additional alternatives to the orchestral repertoire.

The numerical size of his output varies among reference sources because arrangements, revisions and unfinished works can be counted differently. It is more informative to identify the range of genres and consult a work-by-work catalog than to treat one aggregate count as definitive. [7, 30, 31, 32, 33]

## 24. Later life, death and posthumous performances

Still's later decades included further compositions, honorary degrees and ongoing efforts to have his music performed. The gap between the extent of his surviving catalog and the relatively small number of works regularly performed remained a challenge. Arvey and other family members continued to promote his music and preserve documentation of his life.

His health declined during the 1970s. He died in Los Angeles on December 3, 1978, aged eighty-three. His remains were cremated, and his ashes were scattered over the Pacific Ocean, according to the Encyclopedia of Arkansas biography.

One notable posthumous milestone occurred in 1981, when his opera *A Bayou Legend* was televised nationally. The performance underscores the unusually long interval that could separate the composition of a work from broad public access to it. It also shows why the chronology of an artist's reputation does not end at death: works can enter new media and reach new audiences decades after they were conceived.

Archival preservation has been essential to this process. Duke University, the University of Arkansas, Oberlin College and other repositories hold scores, correspondence, programs and related materials. The family-associated publishing and preservation enterprise has also played an important role in sustaining access to his compositions. [2, 4, 7, 12, 20]

## 25. Cultural significance and historical legacy

Still's career resists the separation of American music into isolated histories of classical composition, jazz, popular song and theatrical entertainment. His early engagements supplied skills that remained valuable in the concert hall, while his concert training informed his work as an arranger and orchestrator. The resulting career provides a useful example of musical knowledge moving between institutions that are often studied separately.

He is frequently identified by his pioneering achievements in orchestral conducting, symphonic composition and opera. These milestones are historically important, but an exclusive emphasis on being the first Black person to accomplish a particular task risks making racial barriers the sole measure of his artistic value. The music itself also deserves assessment through its formal organization, melodic invention, dramatic structure, expressive range and treatment of instrumental color.

His contemporaries included other composers confronting related problems of institutional access, among them Florence Price, William Levi Dawson and Margaret Bonds. Their careers illuminate different responses to questions of national identity, musical inheritance and professional opportunity. None should be treated as interchangeable with Still.

For the history of the Harlem Renaissance, Still also provides a direct connection among Broadway, Black-owned recording enterprises, literary modernism, experimental concert societies and major orchestras. His collaboration with Florence Mills and his employment in *Shuffle Along* are particularly instructive examples. For the study of American music more generally, his work demonstrates the expressive possibilities of combining musical resources without subordinating one tradition to another.

The recovery of his less frequently performed compositions remains an important part of evaluating his legacy. A fuller understanding requires attention not only to his best-known symphony but also to his operas, chamber music, songs, ballets and commercial arrangements.

## Historical evidence and common misconceptions

| Claim | Historical assessment |
|---|---|
| Still was the first African American ever to compose a symphony. | Misleading. His 1931 distinction concerns performance by a major American orchestra, not the first act of composition. |
| He was solely a classical musician. | Incorrect. His professional history includes theater, recording, radio, popular arrangements and film. |
| He became the permanent conductor of the Los Angeles Philharmonic in 1936. | Incorrect. His historic appearance was a conducting engagement, not appointment as music director. |
| *Troubled Island* was the first opera ever written by a Black composer. | Incorrect. Its landmark status concerns production by a major American opera company. |
| All unfavorable reviews of *Troubled Island* resulted from a coordinated conspiracy. | An allegation advanced by Still and Arvey; not established simply by the critical reception or limited subsequent staging. |
| *Song of a City* was the official theme of every part of the 1939 fair. | Imprecise. It was commissioned for the Democracity presentation. |
| Every film made by a studio employing Still contains identifiable music by him. | Unsupported without production-specific records or surviving scores. |
