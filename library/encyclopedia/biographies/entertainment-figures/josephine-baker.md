# Josephine Baker (1906–1975)

**Josephine Baker** (born Freda Josephine McDonald; June 3, 1906 – April 12, 1975), also known in France as **Joséphine Baker**, was an American-born French dancer, singer, actress, recording artist, wartime intelligence auxiliary, and civil rights advocate. She became an international celebrity after moving to Paris in 1925, where her combination of physical comedy, jazz dancing, theatrical spectacle, and musical performance made her one of the most recognizable entertainers of the interwar period. During the Second World War she assisted French intelligence and served in the Free French war effort. After the war she used her celebrity to oppose racial segregation and established an international adoptive family at her estate in southwestern France.

Baker's career connects the history of African American musical theater to French music hall, cinema, colonial representation, wartime resistance, transnational citizenship, and the American civil rights movement. Her extraordinary public success did not eliminate the racial restrictions, financial difficulties, or contradictions that shaped her life. On November 30, 2021, France inducted her symbolically into the Panthéon in Paris, while her remains stayed in Monaco.

## 1. Childhood in St. Louis

Baker was born into a poor family in St. Louis, an industrial city on the Mississippi River whose expanding population lived within a sharply unequal racial and economic order. Her mother, Carrie McDonald, worked to support her household. Eddie Carson, a musician and performer, is traditionally identified as Baker's father, although biographical accounts differ over his paternity. The available evidence does not justify treating alternative claims concerning her biological father as established fact.

Childhood employment in domestic service exposed Baker to the unequal relations between Black workers and white employers. Retrospective accounts describe hardship, mistreatment, and periods of insecure housing. These experiences should be understood within a labor system that offered Black girls few opportunities for secure work or prolonged schooling. Her subsequent fame was not the predictable result of a privileged artistic education; it developed through precarious work, informal training, and access to traveling entertainment.

The racial violence that surrounded the St. Louis metropolitan area also formed part of her early experience. The 1917 East St. Louis racial massacre occurred across the Mississippi in Illinois when Baker was eleven. In later autobiographical narratives she associated her childhood with such violence. The precise details of her individual experience are harder to establish than the historical reality of the massacre itself. [1, 2]

## 2. Early marriage and entry into entertainment

Baker married Willie Wells while still very young; the marriage was short-lived. She subsequently married William Howard Baker in 1921. The second husband's surname became the name under which she achieved international fame, long after the marriage had ended. These early marriages preceded the financial independence she later acquired through the stage.

Her route into entertainment followed an existing network of Black touring companies rather than a direct move into mainstream Broadway stardom. She worked with performers associated with the Dixie Steppers and developed the practical skills needed to survive in a variety company: keeping time, remembering choreography, adjusting to small stages, traveling with limited resources, and making an audience respond even when she was not the featured performer.

The early years demonstrate the difference between talent and occupational access. A young entertainer could have considerable dance ability without being permitted a major role. Touring work provided a comparatively accessible, if unstable, route into professional employment. Rehearsals, substitutions, and backstage duties offered chances to demonstrate skills that formal casting practices might otherwise have excluded. [1, 2, 3]

## 3. The Black musical theater network and *Shuffle Along*

The musical *Shuffle Along*, which opened on Broadway in 1921, was written by Flournoy Miller and Aubrey Lyles, with music by Eubie Blake and lyrics by Noble Sissle. Its success enlarged the commercial possibilities of Black musical theater and provided employment for performers whose access to white-controlled stages remained restricted.

Baker's participation must be dated carefully. The Internet Broadway Database describes her employment as a dresser with the touring company in 1922. During a Boston engagement she obtained a chorus opportunity when another performer became unavailable. It is therefore misleading to describe her as a member of the original Broadway opening-night cast merely because she subsequently worked in the production. [3, 4]

Her stage identity developed through deliberate comic disruptions of conventional chorus behavior. A chorus dancer was expected to maintain alignment and synchronize with the ensemble. Baker could turn apparent mistakes, facial expressions, and unusual bodily movements into individual attention without abandoning the underlying rhythm. The contrast between orderly choreography and a performer who appeared to break its rules became one of her distinctive resources.

[Florence Mills](florence-mills.md) rose to prominence through *Shuffle Along* and related theatrical networks before Baker became a star in France. Their careers illustrate how the same institutional breakthrough could lead to different international trajectories. Mills established herself as a featured musical revue performer in London; Baker became closely associated with Parisian music hall. The relationship is historical and institutional, not evidence that Mills personally trained Baker.

## 4. *The Chocolate Dandies* and New York

Baker appeared in *The Chocolate Dandies*, a Broadway production that ran from September 1 to November 22, 1924. The production reunited creative personnel associated with the earlier wave of successful Black musicals. For Baker it represented a move beyond the backstage and touring employment through which she had entered the profession. [3, 5]

The New York entertainment industry included theaters, cabarets, restaurants, booking agencies, rehearsal rooms, and publishing offices. Performers could move between these institutions, but each had its own hierarchy. A dancer who attracted attention at a nightclub might acquire the connections needed for a larger theatrical engagement. Conversely, a Broadway performer could continue working in clubs between productions.

Baker's work in Harlem, including at the Plantation Club, placed her in a commercial environment where the music and dance associated with Black modernity were becoming increasingly valuable. White audiences consumed Black entertainment even as discriminatory practices constrained Black performers and patrons. The resulting market was both a source of employment and a reminder that popularity did not necessarily produce equal treatment.

The European demand for American jazz opened a second market. Rather than regarding Paris as an inevitable destination, Baker's departure should be understood as the result of a concrete recruitment opportunity created by producers interested in exporting a Black American revue to France. [2, 6]

## 5. Paris and *La Revue Nègre* (1925)

In 1925 Baker joined an ensemble recruited for *La Revue Nègre*, a production that opened at the Théâtre des Champs-Élysées in Paris on October 2. Caroline Dudley Reagan was instrumental in assembling the company. The show brought American jazz musicians and dancers into a city already attracted to syncopated music, imported dance styles, and the cultural innovations of Black Americans. The saxophonist Sidney Bechet was among the ensemble's notable artists. [6, 7]

Baker's appearance in the revue made her an immediate sensation. Her dancing combined rapid changes of direction, exaggerated movement, comic timing, and a willingness to exploit the theatrical conventions of the production. In a featured sequence commonly known as the *Danse sauvage*, she danced with Joe Alex. Its presentation drew on European ideas of supposedly primitive movement, rather than representing an unmediated African dance tradition.

The production was simultaneously modern and exoticizing. Audiences encountered new American music and choreography through a spectacle marketed partly through fantasies about Blackness. The apparent contradiction was central to the commercial environment in which Baker operated. Her performance could challenge conventional European ballet and social manners while still being framed by racial stereotypes.

Her success also demonstrates the significance of international labor mobility. The same dancer who had occupied a marginal position within American musical theater acquired a radically different commercial standing when placed before French audiences. This did not mean France was free from racism. It meant that its entertainment market and racial conventions offered her a different combination of opportunities and constraints. [7, 8]

## 6. The Folies Bergère and the banana costume

Baker joined the Folies Bergère in 1926. The music hall specialized in elaborately staged revues, in which dance, comedy, fashionable costumes, and visual effects were more important than a continuous dramatic plot. In this setting she developed the image most often associated with her early fame: a dance costume incorporating a belt or skirt of artificial bananas. The Bibliothèque nationale de France dates the costume's introduction at the Folies Bergère to 1926; widely reproduced photographs and subsequent versions are also associated with later revues. [9]

It is important not to collapse all her early appearances into this single image. The 1925 *Revue Nègre* breakthrough and the 1926 Folies Bergère costume belonged to separate productions. The surviving publicity photographs are not necessarily records of the exact choreography, setting, or costume used on opening night.

The costume worked as a stage device because its movement amplified the dancer's rhythmic gestures. It was also an instantly reproducible visual symbol that circulated through photography, posters, and journalism. Its meanings remain contested. It could be interpreted as a commercial exploitation of colonial stereotypes, an ironic performance of those stereotypes, or a creative negotiation in which Baker used the limited images available to her to gain professional power.

None of these interpretations alone explains the whole performance. The costume was part of an industry whose managers and audiences had considerable influence over what was presented. Baker's distinctive presence made it possible for her to turn that material into a recognizable individual brand, but the existence of a lucrative brand did not make the surrounding racial assumptions harmless. [8, 9, 10]

## 7. Comedy, choreography, and the modern body

Baker's performance style drew its effectiveness from more than speed or physical flexibility. She could juxtapose apparently uncontrolled movement with precise coordination, then abruptly change expression or posture. The resulting comedy depended on mastery of timing. A movement that appeared accidental had to be placed accurately against the music and the reactions of other performers.

Her style emerged in the context of jazz dancing and the commercial circulation of the Charleston and related forms. Dance movements passed between theaters, social gatherings, clubs, and instructional publications. It is rarely possible to assign an individual movement to a single inventor. Baker's distinction lay in her combination of inherited vocabulary, theatrical exaggeration, personality, and control of audience attention.

The visual arts helped transmit her reputation beyond the theater. Paul Colin's poster and lithographic work associated the revue with the streamlined forms and energetic silhouettes characteristic of the era's graphic design. Photographers turned transient gestures and costumes into collectible images. These media enabled people who had never seen a performance to recognize Baker as a symbol of modern Paris.

The importance of this circulation was not merely aesthetic. Posters sold theater tickets, studio portraits supported publicity, and fashion photography changed the audience's perception of the performer. Baker's transition from comic dancer to elegant singer depended in part on the ability of visual media to present new versions of her identity. [8, 10, 11]

## 8. International celebrity, management, and commercial identity

As her reputation grew, Baker traveled through European entertainment markets and confronted sharply different receptions. Some audiences treated her as a representative of American modernism; others viewed her through moral or racial prejudices. International touring required managers to negotiate contracts, theater schedules, publicity, travel, and local restrictions.

Pepito Abatino, an Italian-born manager and companion, was an important figure in the commercial organization of Baker's early European career. Their relationship has been described in different terms by biographers, and his role should not be confused with an independently verified legal marriage. He helped manage the transition from sensational revue performer to internationally marketable celebrity. [6, 12]

Her public persona incorporated luxury, fashion, and unusual publicity devices. The cheetah associated with Baker, frequently identified as Chiquita, became part of the imagery surrounding her celebrity. Accounts of the animal's behavior vary and are often repeated for their entertainment value. The existence of photographs and publicity involving a cheetah does not authenticate every anecdote told about it.

The French Ministry of Culture's survey of surviving collections emphasizes the role of fashion and the designer Paul Poiret in Baker's evolving appearance. Her transformation into an elegant Parisian figure was not a repudiation of dance but an expansion of her commercial repertoire. The performer who could dominate a stage through comedy could also command attention through restraint, sophisticated clothing, and song. [11]

## 9. *Siren of the Tropics* (1927)

Baker's feature-film debut, *La Sirène des tropiques* (*Siren of the Tropics*), was released in France in 1927. She played Papitou, a woman from a Caribbean setting whose relationship with a French engineer leads into a narrative of danger, romance, migration, and theatrical success. The silent film showcased Baker's screen presence and dancing rather than her recorded singing voice. [13, 14]

The story illustrates the opportunities and constraints of her film career. Baker was not confined to a fleeting background appearance: she was the central attraction of a substantial commercial motion picture. Yet the narrative depended on colonial adventure conventions and exotic representations. Her character's ability to act independently coexisted with a story shaped by assumptions about racial difference and European civilization.

The shift from stage to film also transformed the performance medium. A live dancer could adjust her timing in response to an audience. A silent-film performer had to communicate through framing, gesture, editing, and physical expression, while musical accompaniment could differ between theaters. Baker's experience as a comic dancer provided resources especially well suited to this environment.

The film is often described as a landmark in Black female screen stardom. Claims that it was the absolute first film ever to star a Black woman require qualifications about national industries, distribution, and the meaning of a major motion picture. Its historical importance does not depend on excluding earlier Black filmmaking traditions. [13, 14, 15]

## 10. The Casino de Paris and *J'ai deux amours*

By 1930 Baker was expanding her identity as a French-language singer. At the Casino de Paris she cultivated a repertoire that could circulate independently of her dancing. Her signature song, *J'ai deux amours*, was composed by Vincent Scotto with lyrics by Géo Koger and Henri Varna. The Bibliothèque nationale de France catalogs the published music with a 1930 copyright. [16]

The song's emotional subject is attachment to more than one place. In Baker's case, it became closely associated with her American origins and affection for Paris. Its popularity helped translate the image of the foreign-born dancer into that of an artist whose relationship to France formed an essential part of her public identity. It should not be treated as a complete statement of her private feelings or as evidence that she had ceased to identify with African Americans.

Sound recordings offered a new kind of durability. The visual spectacle of a music-hall revue reached those who attended it; a commercial record could travel between countries, be replayed at home, and remain accessible after the production closed. Baker's recording career thus expanded both her audience and the surviving historical record.

Other songs associated with her repertoire include *La Petite Tonkinoise*, *Si j'étais blanche*, and *Haïti*. Their subjects range across different emotional and cultural settings. The surviving discographic evidence needs to be read carefully: recording dates, first issues, reissues, and compilation release dates are distinct. [16, 17]

## 11. *Zouzou* (1934)

With the transition to synchronized sound, film could preserve Baker's singing as well as her movement. Marc Allégret's *Zouzou* (1934), in which she appeared opposite Jean Gabin, is one of the most important surviving examples of this phase. Baker plays the title character, whose circumstances lead from ordinary work to the opportunity to become a stage performer. [15, 18]

The film uses the familiar musical narrative of a performer whose talent emerges when established theatrical arrangements break down. For Baker, such a plot resonated with her own history of moving from subordinate employment to prominent roles. The cinematic character, however, is a fictional construction and should not be treated as an autobiographical reconstruction.

The film's songs and staging provide a record of her more mature performance style. The audience is invited to attend to her voice, emotional expression, and theatrical presentation, not exclusively to the comic movement of the 1920s. This is important for assessing a career often reduced to the banana costume.

The picture also reveals the limitations of romantic recognition within mainstream cinema of the period. A Black woman could be presented as a remarkable performer while the narrative maintained conventions about which relationships were permissible or likely to be fulfilled. The contradiction between centrality as an entertainer and restriction as a romantic protagonist is a useful theme for studying Baker's films. [15, 18]

## 12. *Princesse Tam-Tam* (1935) and colonial fantasy

In Edmond T. Gréville's *Princesse Tam-Tam* (1935), Baker plays Aouina, a North African woman caught up in a European writer's project of transforming her into an apparent aristocrat. The film incorporates a story within a story, complicating the relationship between what is depicted and what the writer imagines. [15, 18]

The transformation narrative is significant because it dramatizes the act of constructing an exotic celebrity. Clothing, manners, and social settings become evidence through which other characters classify Aouina. The supposed distinction between a civilized European world and an uncivilized outsider is both used and exposed by the film's narrative machinery.

Baker's dancing works within this structure as a moment in which the constructed aristocratic identity gives way to a spectacle presented as authentic. Yet the dance itself is a professionally staged performance. The apparent return to a natural identity is therefore another theatrical construction, not documentary evidence of the character's culture.

The film can be studied alongside *Siren of the Tropics* and *Zouzou* as a series of star vehicles that made a Black woman central to French commercial cinema while retaining colonial assumptions. Baker's individual achievement and the ideological restrictions of her scripts are not mutually exclusive. [14, 18]

## 13. The 1936 American return

Baker returned to Broadway in the *Ziegfeld Follies of 1936*, a major revue featuring music by Vernon Duke and lyrics by Ira Gershwin. The Internet Broadway Database records her participation in the production, which ran from January 30 to May 9 in its first engagement. The George Balanchine Foundation's catalog also identifies her singing and dancing in the choreographic repertory of the show. [3, 19]

Her American reception was considerably less favorable than the acclaim she had experienced in France. Reviews included racialized assessments of her appearance and performance, and her international celebrity did not secure unrestricted access to American hotels or restaurants. The return exposed the difference between fame abroad and the practical privileges attached to racial status at home.

It would nevertheless be misleading to interpret the production as proof that Americans uniformly rejected her or that she never acquired an American audience. The theatrical engagement itself was evidence of considerable commercial interest. Her successful postwar appearances demonstrated that public reception could change, even while discrimination persisted. Baker's experience also depended on the venue, local management, press coverage, and the conditions under which a performance was booked.

## 14. French citizenship and personal relationships

On November 30, 1937, Baker married Jean Lion, a French industrialist and aviator of Jewish background. Through the marriage she acquired French nationality. The French National Archives documents the marriage and records her later assistance to Lion and members of his family in escaping the dangers of wartime Europe. [20]

Citizenship had consequences beyond personal sentiment. It established a formal legal connection with the country in which she had built her career and would soon perform wartime service. It also complicated her subsequent relationship with the United States, where immigration authorities could treat her as a foreign national rather than simply an American entertainer returning home.

Her marriages to Wells, Baker, and Lion were followed in 1947 by marriage to Jo Bouillon, an orchestra leader who became her partner in the development of Les Milandes and the raising of their adopted children. In her later years she was associated with the American artist Robert Brady. Accounts differ in how they characterize their private ceremony and its legal effect; it should not be presented as equivalent to a civil marriage without supporting documentation.

Baker's intimate life has also attracted attention from biographers interested in relationships with women. Retrospective testimony and biographical interpretations support discussion of these relationships, but the details of particular alleged affairs vary in evidentiary quality. Her public career and political commitments are substantially better documented than every aspect of her private life. [2, 12, 21]

## 15. The outbreak of war and intelligence recruitment

When war began in Europe in September 1939, Baker placed her celebrity and access to elite social circles at the service of French intelligence. Her wartime work began before the German occupation of Paris and before the Free French movement existed in its post-armistice form. It is therefore important to distinguish her initial intelligence assistance from her subsequent association with Free France.

Her status as an entertainer offered opportunities unavailable to ordinary travelers. Diplomatic receptions, international tours, and meetings with influential people formed part of her professional environment. French intelligence could make use of information acquired through those contacts. Jacques Abtey, a French intelligence officer, became closely associated with her wartime activities.

The surviving files held by the Service historique de la Défense include records relating to her air force career and Resistance service. France's military intelligence directorate has noted that the archival dossier does not establish the exact content of all the information she transmitted. Claims that Baker individually prevented a particular military operation or decisively changed the outcome of the war should therefore be treated with caution. [22, 23]

## 16. Occupied France, North Africa, and Free French service

The military defeat of France in 1940 divided the country and transformed the conditions under which Baker could work. She continued supporting the French war effort rather than accepting the political order associated with German occupation and collaboration. Her estate in the Dordogne and her professional travels became part of a broader wartime existence involving intelligence contacts, relief work, and performance.

Baker traveled to North Africa during the war and spent a considerable period there, including a serious illness that required medical care. As her health permitted, she resumed work supporting the Allied cause. Her performances helped provide entertainment for troops and supported relief and fundraising activities. These public activities were significant contributions in their own right, apart from the more secret aspects of her service.

Stories about concealed messages in musical scores and the use of invisible ink are widely repeated in accounts of Baker's espionage. They appear in institutional histories as well as popular biographies. However, the survival of a general account of intelligence work does not establish the precise details of every reported mission. Her documented service should be distinguished from retrospective embellishments. [21, 22, 24]

In 1944 Baker formally served as a junior officer in the women's auxiliary of the French air force, with the rank of sous-lieutenant. During the liberation period she performed for military personnel and civilians. Her position combined public communication, morale-building, and service to the institutions of a France being restored after occupation. [22, 23, 25]

## 17. Military recognition and the Légion d'honneur

Baker's wartime decorations provide an unusually valuable means of checking her biography against administrative records. They also illustrate the difference between a recommendation, a formal award, and the later presentation of insignia.

| Recognition | Historical significance |
|---|---|
| Resistance Medal with rosette | Recognition of her contribution to the Resistance; awarded in 1946 |
| Croix de guerre 1939–1945 with palm | Recognition of wartime service |
| Chevalier of the Légion d'honneur | Appointment in 1957, under a civil rather than military designation |
| Presentation at Les Milandes | General Martial Valin presented the Legion of Honour and Croix de guerre insignia on August 18, 1961 |

The French military archives describe a lengthy administrative discussion over the classification of her service. The eventual compromise recognized Baker through a civil appointment to the Legion of Honour while also granting the Croix de guerre with palm. This distinction is important: the award's civil classification does not negate the documented intelligence and air auxiliary service, but neither should it be silently converted into a military Legion of Honour award. [22, 23, 26]

Her wartime contribution was recognized by institutions that possessed records of her work. The full extent of her intelligence impact remains more difficult to evaluate, particularly where operational documentation is incomplete. A responsible historical assessment can recognize her commitment and service without attributing to her accomplishments beyond the evidence.

## 18. Postwar touring and resistance to segregation

Baker resumed an international entertainment career after the war. Her French reputation now included not only her prewar celebrity but also recognition of her service to the country. The American civil rights struggle increasingly became a central part of her public activities.

During American engagements she challenged segregated seating and discriminatory treatment of Black customers. Rather than treating entertainment as a separate realm from civil rights, she used the economic leverage attached to her appearances to demand more equal conditions. A booking contract could become a practical instrument for changing the rules of admission at a particular venue.

This strategy depended on her commercial standing. An entertainer without a substantial audience could be replaced if she demanded changes to venue policy; Baker's ability to attract paying customers gave her a stronger negotiating position. Even so, contractual leverage could not eliminate the discrimination she experienced outside the theater. Her experience at the Stork Club in New York became a particularly visible example of those limitations. [1, 27]

## 19. The Stork Club controversy (1951)

On October 16, 1951, Baker visited the Stork Club, an elite Manhattan nightclub and restaurant associated with celebrities, journalists, and influential New Yorkers. A dispute developed over the service she received. Baker publicly attributed the treatment to racial discrimination and challenged the conduct of the syndicated columnist Walter Winchell, whom she said had failed to intervene.

The controversy extended well beyond the original restaurant incident. The NAACP organized opposition to discriminatory treatment, and the press became an arena for competing accounts of what had happened. Winchell rejected Baker's characterization of his conduct and attacked her publicly. His considerable media influence made their dispute an important example of the relationship between celebrity, journalism, and political reputation.

The Library of Congress has examined this controversy through archival newspaper and photographic material. The resulting historical record supports the existence of the confrontation and the public campaign that followed, but individual witnesses disputed aspects of the restaurant's conduct and the sequence of events. An encyclopedia account should distinguish Baker's allegations from the responses of the establishment and the columnist rather than inventing a definitive reconstruction of every exchange. [28]

The Stork Club episode also demonstrates the risks of using professional visibility for political purposes. Baker had acquired an American audience after years of international success, but her willingness to challenge an influential establishment exposed her to hostile coverage. The episode deserves an independent article because it connects the history of celebrity dining establishments with organized civil rights activism and the power of syndicated newspapers.

## 20. Cold War scrutiny and American immigration

Baker's civil rights activities occurred during a period in which American authorities treated association with left-wing organizations as a potential security issue. Her international travel and political contacts brought her under federal scrutiny. The FBI's released records concerning Baker cover the years 1951–1966 and include material on immigration, security, and alleged associations with Communist proponents or related organizations. [29]

Such records require particular care. An intelligence or law-enforcement file may preserve allegations, surveillance reports, correspondence, and third-party claims without establishing their truth. Inclusion in an FBI file does not establish membership in a political party, unlawful activity, or loyalty to a foreign government.

Her French nationality added a practical dimension to the controversy. She could encounter restrictions on admission to the United States that would not apply in the same way to an American citizen. Immigration scrutiny, unfavorable publicity, and venue-level discrimination could reinforce one another. It would be an oversimplification to explain every difficulty in her American career through a single agency or individual.

Her later return to the United States for the March on Washington demonstrated that the difficulties of the early 1950s did not terminate her relationship with the American movement for racial equality. The intervening years nevertheless reveal how national security institutions and immigration policy could affect an internationally mobile entertainer. [28, 29, 30]

## 21. Les Milandes and the Rainbow Tribe

In 1937 Baker began renting the Château des Milandes, a historic property in the Dordogne region of southwestern France; she purchased it in 1947. With Jo Bouillon she developed an ambitious household and public enterprise at the estate. It became the setting for her adoption of twelve children from varied national, cultural, and religious backgrounds. [31, 32]

Baker called the family her *tribu arc-en-ciel*, or Rainbow Tribe. Her stated purpose was to demonstrate that children of different backgrounds could live together as members of a common family. The project made an argument about racial equality through domestic organization rather than exclusively through speeches or performances.

Les Milandes also functioned as a tourist destination and commercial enterprise. Its public attractions, staffing, property maintenance, and associated facilities required substantial expenditure. Baker sought to support these activities with entertainment income and visitor revenue. The estate thus combined a private household, philanthropic project, and business operation, each with different financial requirements.

The children were individuals, not simply representatives of their assigned national or religious origins. Later accounts of their experiences vary, and some biographers have questioned the extent to which the symbolic demands of the project affected family life. Baker's intentions and the experiences of the children should be examined separately. The adoption enterprise cannot be judged solely from its favorable publicity or solely from retrospective criticism. [12, 31, 32]

## 22. The March on Washington (1963)

On August 28, 1963, Baker addressed the crowd gathered in Washington, D.C., for the March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom. She wore her French military uniform and decorations, visually connecting her wartime service and French citizenship with her participation in an American civil rights demonstration.

Her remarks drew upon the contrasting treatment she had experienced in the United States and abroad. She discussed the personal consequences of racial discrimination and encouraged continued collective action. The speech was especially distinctive because it came from an entertainer who had spent much of her adult life outside the United States but continued to identify with the struggle against American segregation.

The event's speaking arrangements are sometimes simplified into the claim that Baker was the only woman who spoke. The John F. Kennedy Presidential Library's archival account places her remarks before the official proceedings and documents the participation of other women, including the tribute to women civil rights leaders. It is more accurate to identify Baker as one of the relatively few women who addressed the gathering, with a substantial speaking role, rather than to erase other women's participation. [30, 33]

Her appearance also demonstrates the international dimensions of the movement. The audience saw a Black American-born French citizen wearing decorations earned in the fight against fascism, speaking about discrimination in the country of her birth. The symbolism drew its force from the concrete experiences of her life, not simply her celebrity.

## 23. Financial collapse, loss of the château, and Monaco

The financial obligations of Les Milandes eventually exceeded the resources available to sustain it. The estate's upkeep, staff, public attractions, debts, and family expenses created demands that continued touring could not reliably meet. Baker's deteriorating financial position was made more visible by the contrast between the glamorous public image of her earlier career and the loss of the property most closely associated with her postwar aspirations.

The château was lost and Baker was evicted in March 1969. The event attracted international attention, including fundraising appeals and expressions of support from other entertainers. The French presidential address at her Panthéon ceremony specifically acknowledges Brigitte Bardot's public appeal and the assistance later provided by Princess Grace of Monaco. [32, 34]

Grace helped Baker and her family secure accommodation in the vicinity of Monaco. Baker continued performing rather than withdrawing permanently from public life. Her later circumstances demonstrate a vulnerability common among entertainers whose lifetime earnings depend on irregular contracts: high income during successful years does not necessarily provide sustainable capital for an expensive institution or household.

No complete audited accounting of the estate's finances is presented here. Its failure should not be attributed to a precise sum of debt, a particular instance of fraud, or an individual family member without documentary evidence. The known result was the loss of the estate despite repeated efforts to preserve it.

## 24. The final Paris revue, death, and funeral

Baker's last major return to the Paris stage occurred in April 1975 with *Joséphine à Bobino*, a revue celebrating five decades of her entertainment career. The production offered a retrospective of her life in the city that had made her an international star. It brought renewed public attention to an artist whose financial troubles had not extinguished her theatrical reputation.

Shortly after the opening, Baker suffered a cerebral hemorrhage. She died in Paris on April 12, 1975, aged sixty-eight. Her death came just as the retrospective had reestablished her in a prominent public position. [1, 2, 34]

Her funeral on April 15 included French military honors and a ceremony at the Church of La Madeleine in Paris. She was subsequently buried in Monaco. The military recognition attached to her funeral reflected the unusual conjunction of entertainment and national service that defined her public life. It was not merely a tribute to commercial success.

The funeral also revealed the many constituencies that claimed Baker's legacy: audiences familiar with her early revues, members of the Resistance generation, civil rights supporters, fellow entertainers, and people associated with her family project. These groups remembered different parts of a career that could not adequately be represented by a single photograph or performance.

## 25. Panthéon induction and historical legacy

On November 30, 2021, France inducted Baker into the Panthéon, its national monument honoring selected figures of French history. She became the first Black woman honored there. The ceremony occurred on the anniversary of her 1937 marriage to Jean Lion, through which she had acquired French nationality. Her remains were not transferred: a cenotaph was placed in the monument, incorporating soil from places associated with her life. Her burial in Monaco was preserved. [31, 32, 34]

The ceremony represented an official interpretation of Baker as a figure of French national history. It emphasized her service during the war, her commitment to racial equality, and her attachment to France. As with any national commemoration, the ceremony is evidence of how a later society chose to remember a historical person; it should be distinguished from contemporary evidence about her individual actions.

Baker's artistic legacy is equally significant. Her career provides a case study in the circulation of African American music and dance through European commercial institutions. Her movies preserve an early example of Black female screen stardom in an industry that frequently confined Black performers to marginal roles. Her recordings document a transformation from American musical comedy and dance into a French-language international singing career.

The tensions in her life remain relevant to cultural history: she encountered racial stereotypes and used the stage imagery associated with them, acquired international celebrity without attaining permanent financial security, and sought to demonstrate universal equality while navigating the unequal power relations of public life. Recognizing these tensions is not a denial of her accomplishments. It places them in the institutional and historical conditions in which they occurred.

Josephine Baker's significance consequently extends beyond her status as a Jazz Age icon. She links several histories often treated separately: Black American theater, French popular entertainment, cinema and visual culture, wartime intelligence, international citizenship, and the movement against racial discrimination. Her career illustrates the capacity of an entertainer to carry cultural influence across national boundaries and to employ public recognition in projects far removed from the stage.

## Historical evidence and frequently repeated claims

| Claim | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Baker was in the original 1921 Broadway opening cast of *Shuffle Along*. | The IBDB instead documents her entry through the 1922 touring production. |
| She wore the famous banana costume at her 1925 Paris debut. | The BnF places its introduction at the Folies Bergère in 1926, after that debut. |
| France offered a society entirely free of racism. | Her French success was real, but existed alongside racial stereotypes and colonial inequality. |
| The precise impact of each of her espionage missions is known. | French military archives establish service, but do not document the exact content of all transmitted intelligence. |
| She received the Legion of Honour in 1961. | The appointment was in 1957; the presentation was in 1961. |
| She was the only woman to speak at the 1963 March on Washington. | Archival accounts document additional women's participation; her remarks preceded the formal program. |
| Her remains were moved to the Panthéon in 2021. | A symbolic cenotaph was installed; her remains stayed in Monaco. |
