# Ada “Bricktop” Smith

**MGU Encyclopedia — Biographies — Entertainment Figures**

Ada Beatrice Queen Victoria Louise Virginia Smith (August 14, 1894 – January 31, 1984, commonly dated February 1 in some reference works), professionally known as **Bricktop**, was an African American singer, dancer, vaudeville performer, cabaret hostess, and nightclub proprietor. She became a central participant in the international entertainment culture of the Jazz Age, operating establishments in Paris, Mexico City, and Rome and building a professional network that crossed the boundaries of music, literature, high society, and cinema.

Unlike many performers whose reputations rest on recordings or motion pictures, Bricktop achieved her greatest influence through live entertainment and the management of social spaces. Her nightclubs combined music, dancing, hospitality, and the careful cultivation of repeat customers. They supplied employment to musicians, connected artists with influential patrons, and gave Black American performers an important foothold in European cultural life.

Her early career intersected with those of Florence Mills and Duke Ellington; in Paris, she became closely associated with Cole Porter and Mabel Mercer. The Second World War interrupted her French enterprise, but she subsequently established herself in Mexico and returned to Europe. Her Roman nightclub attracted a new generation of international celebrities during the rise of the Italian film industry. In old age she recorded oral histories, appeared in films, and published an autobiography with James Haskins. [1–6]

## 1. Birth and the transition from West Virginia to Chicago

Smith was born in Alderson, a small West Virginia community, during a period in which the legal gains of Reconstruction were being systematically undermined across the United States. Her long birth name survives in archival catalogs and biographical reference works. She was the youngest child of Thomas and Hattie Smith, according to a published biographical account. Following her father's death, the family relocated to Chicago while she was still young. [1, 7]

That relocation placed her in a much larger market for musical entertainment. Chicago's population and commercial activity had expanded rapidly during the nineteenth century. By Smith's childhood the city contained theaters, dance halls, neighborhood saloons, touring companies, and a growing Black cultural and business community. These establishments offered different paths into paid performance, though access was constrained by racial discrimination and the unequal resources available to Black entrepreneurs.

Smith's subsequent recollections stressed her childhood attraction to places where people gathered to sing and socialize. Her interest in the saloon was not simply an adult invention attached to a glamorous reputation. It reflected the environment in which she learned to think about entertainment as a form of social activity. Her professional ambition eventually united the two roles that she had observed as a child: the person providing the music and the person responsible for the room in which it was heard.

The West Virginia Encyclopedia reports a childhood stage appearance in a production of *Uncle Tom's Cabin* in Chicago. That recollection belongs to the history of theatrical adaptations of Harriet Beecher Stowe's novel, which circulated widely in American popular theater. The specific production and its cast have not been independently identified here. [1]

## 2. Vaudeville and the acquisition of professional skills

Around 1910, at approximately sixteen years of age, Smith entered traveling entertainment. Vaudeville was then a major commercial form of American popular culture. A typical variety bill could place vocalists, dancers, comedians, acrobats, instrumentalists, and specialty performers before the same audience. Acts were expected to attract attention quickly and maintain a reliable performance under changing theatrical conditions.

For Black performers, touring required navigating not only the artistic demands of the stage but also discriminatory booking practices, accommodation restrictions, and the uncertain availability of suitable venues. A successful engagement could generate another contract; a poorly managed tour could leave a performer without income far from home. The trade therefore rewarded adaptability and the ability to establish dependable professional contacts.

Smith's early affiliations included a touring company associated with Flournoy Miller and Aubrey Lyles. These two entertainers later became important figures in the history of Black musical comedy. Her training in variety entertainment consequently placed her within a network from which major theatrical productions would emerge. [2]

The skills she acquired extended beyond singing and dancing. She learned how to judge an audience, alter the pace of a performance, improvise around interruptions, and maintain working relationships with other artists. These abilities became especially important when she moved from formal theaters into small rooms where the performer could interact directly with customers.

## 3. The Panama Trio and Florence Mills

Smith's early group work included the Oma Crosby Trio and the Panama Trio, the latter associated with Florence Mills and Cora Green. The Schomburg Center identifies these affiliations in its archival descriptions. [2]

A small singing and dancing ensemble offered practical advantages. It could provide variety within a single act, divide musical and comic responsibilities among its members, and travel without the expense of a complete theatrical company. It also provided a setting in which performers developed closely coordinated timing and stage discipline.

Mills and Smith followed different professional trajectories. Mills achieved international fame through elaborate theatrical revues, while Smith became most closely identified with the cabaret and nightclub. Their common apprenticeship illustrates the flexibility of the African American entertainment network: the same training could lead toward Broadway, international music halls, or the operation of an independent entertainment business.

Mills died in 1927, at the height of her reputation, whereas Smith continued working for decades. Their different surviving records also reveal a challenge for historians. The history of a live ensemble may be scattered across theatrical advertisements, personal recollections, contracts, and photographs rather than preserved in a complete body of recordings.

See also: [Florence Mills](florence-mills.md).

## 4. Chicago, Jack Johnson, and the saloon economy

Smith's career brought her into contact with heavyweight boxing champion Jack Johnson. In a later account of her life she recalled working at his Café de Champion, also rendered Café Champ. Johnson's involvement in nightlife demonstrates how sports celebrity could be converted into an independent commercial enterprise. [8]

The nightclub and saloon were important businesses because their revenue did not depend solely upon the sale of admission to a theatrical production. Food, drinks, repeated customer visits, and special events could contribute to income. A proprietor who became famous might attract patrons who wished to experience the personality of the owner as well as the entertainment.

Smith was able to observe this commercial structure from the position of an employee before becoming an operator herself. She was also working in a milieu where informal introductions could influence the future of a musician or dancer. Personal reputation mattered because the same performers and employers often encountered one another across multiple establishments.

A 1917 photograph in the New York Public Library's collection places Smith with Jelly Roll Morton and others at the Cadillac Café in Los Angeles. It is a useful independent illustration of the geographic breadth of her early career. It does not establish the duration of each artist's engagement, but it documents a connection between Smith and another important figure in early jazz. [3]

Johnson later established the Harlem establishment from which the Cotton Club developed. Smith's career and the history of that venue thus intersect through a shared entertainment and entrepreneurial environment, rather than through an ownership interest by Smith in the Cotton Club.

## 5. Harlem and the origin of the name Bricktop

In New York, Smith performed at establishments associated with Harlem nightclub proprietor Barron Wilkins. Wilkins is credited with giving her the name Bricktop in reference to the color of her hair. Smith discussed the incident herself in an interview with Studs Terkel decades later. [9]

The nickname supplied a short and distinctive professional identity. Vaudeville advertising and nightclub publicity depended on names that could be remembered and readily communicated. A memorable name also allowed an entertainer to build continuity across changing employers and cities.

Smith's identification with Harlem coincided with the emergence of a new entertainment economy shaped by Black migration, artistic experimentation, urban leisure, and, after 1920, Prohibition. Nightclubs became important sites for musical development and public encounters across social groups. They were not uniformly integrated, however. Their ownership arrangements and admission practices varied considerably.

Barron's Exclusive Club formed part of this environment. It is important to distinguish that establishment from the Cotton Club, even though the histories of the venues overlap through the wider Harlem musical community. Smith's experience in such rooms prepared her for a later career in which reputation and personal introductions became essential resources.

## 6. Duke Ellington and the importance of early bookings

One of the most frequently repeated episodes from Smith's American career concerns her assistance to the group of Washington musicians that included the young Duke Ellington. In 1923, Ellington and his colleagues were seeking opportunities in New York. The ensemble associated with Elmer Snowden secured work at Barron Wilkins's establishment during that year. [10]

Smith remembered encouraging the engagement. Her account is particularly significant because it illustrates the power exercised by people who were neither the formal leader of the orchestra nor necessarily the owner of the venue. A trusted performer could recommend an unfamiliar act, persuade a proprietor to take a chance, and provide the musicians with an opportunity to demonstrate their abilities.

The episode should not be interpreted as proof that Smith alone discovered Ellington or was responsible for his entire subsequent career. Ellington already possessed musical training, experience in Washington, and connections with other musicians. His later achievements resulted from sustained work by himself and his orchestras, along with the contributions of numerous collaborators.

Nevertheless, access to the New York market represented an important step in his development. Smith's recollection, read alongside the established chronology of the Washingtonians, places her within the professional relationships that made such a step possible. Her contribution exemplifies the often underdocumented work of introducing and recommending artists.

See also: [Cab Calloway](cab-calloway.md) and the history of the Cotton Club.

## 7. Arrival in Paris in 1924

Smith traveled to France in 1924 and began working at Le Grand Duc, a small nightclub in the Montmartre entertainment district. This was an important transition from the American touring and cabaret market into an international setting. [1, 8]

Paris after the First World War attracted writers, artists, wealthy travelers, and musicians from numerous countries. Black American performers were among those who contributed to its developing jazz culture. The international reputation of American music created opportunities for artists whose commercial prospects at home were restricted by segregation.

Montmartre contained establishments of different sizes and social reputations. The intimacy of a small club could be beneficial to an entertainer who was adept at talking with patrons and shaping a room's atmosphere. It permitted the development of a regular clientele whose loyalty might extend beyond the appeal of any single song.

Smith entered an existing community rather than introducing jazz to France by herself. Black American soldiers, touring musicians, theatrical productions, and earlier expatriates had already contributed to the reception of American popular music. Her achievement was to become a prominent individual operator within this evolving market.

A photograph of Smith outside Le Grand Duc in 1925 is preserved in the Schomburg Center's photographic collection. [3] It provides a visual point of reference for this initial phase of her French career.

## 8. Montmartre and the expatriate entertainment network

The Black American entertainment community in interwar Paris included artists who arrived under theatrical contracts, musicians who traveled with orchestras, and individuals who settled for longer periods. Clubs created relationships among these groups and their French and foreign audiences.

For the visiting entertainer, a familiar establishment could offer information about upcoming engagements and introductions to employers. For the regular patron, the club supplied a recognizable social setting within a foreign city. For the proprietor, attracting both groups was a means of sustaining the business.

Smith's role in this network was distinctive because she could move between the professional world of the performers and the social world of the patrons. Her customers did not come solely to observe a stage act. They came to participate in a setting in which the presence of other customers was itself part of the attraction.

The Parisian experience did not abolish racial prejudice. Colonial attitudes, stereotypes about Black sexuality and musical ability, and the economic privileges of affluent tourists shaped the environment. An American entertainer could enjoy professional opportunities in France that were difficult to obtain at home while still encountering racialized expectations abroad.

This distinction is important in interpreting the contrast between American segregation and European expatriate life. Smith benefited from particular circumstances and personal relationships; the success of her establishment cannot be generalized into a claim that French society was free of discrimination.

## 9. The Music Box and the creation of Chez Bricktop

Smith advanced from performing for others to developing an establishment of her own. The West Virginia Encyclopedia dates the opening of the Music Box to 1926, with Bricktop's following it. Other reference works compress this sequence or attach different dates to the named club. [1, 11]

The variations are understandable because an entertainment business may retain a proprietor's name while moving premises or changing its commercial arrangements. The opening of an initial business and the opening of a subsequent venue are not necessarily the same event. A date attached to Chez Bricktop may refer to a particular site rather than the first occasion on which Smith operated a nightclub.

The development of her enterprise marked a significant change in her professional position. As a performer she was responsible primarily for delivering entertainment according to the requirements of an employer. As an operator she also had to arrange staffing, select entertainment, build a customer base, and respond to changes in the commercial environment.

Her personal identity remained at the center of this new enterprise. The name Bricktop functioned simultaneously as a stage name, a business identity, and an assurance of the kind of hospitality that a visitor might expect. The brand could therefore travel with her even when the physical establishment changed.

The NYPL photographic archive contains an image of a later Paris venue with the name Brick Top's displayed beneath the Monico marquee. It is a valuable reminder that the history of the business involved multiple locations and should not be reduced to a single address.

## 10. The nightclub as a business and cultural institution

Bricktop's enterprises provide an instructive example of a personality-led hospitality business. The following describes their general operating model, not a reconstruction of audited accounts.

| Business function | Role within the nightclub |
|---|---|
| Entertainment programming | Selection and coordination of musicians and performers |
| Hospitality | Greeting guests, arranging seating, maintaining the atmosphere |
| Customer retention | Building a regular clientele through personal relationships |
| Promotion | Reputation, social introductions, press coverage, and the proprietor's public identity |
| Operating costs | Premises, staffing, supplies, and payments to performers |
| Business exposure | Seasonal demand, travel restrictions, changing tastes, and dependence on the proprietor |

The distinction between a club and a formal theater affected the product being sold. A theater commonly presented a defined show to an audience at an appointed time. A nightclub could provide an evening of flexible duration, interspersed with conversation, musical performances, dancing, and unexpected interactions.

Smith's ability to manage the experience was itself a form of labor. Maintaining a successful room required more than persuading famous people to attend an opening night. The operator had to sustain a standard of service and entertainment capable of bringing customers back.

Her repeated attempts to establish the business in different countries also reveal its limitations. A celebrated proprietor might carry an international reputation into a new market, but the local conditions necessary to make that reputation profitable could not be transported automatically. Her unsuccessful postwar Paris reopening is a particularly clear example.

## 11. Cole Porter and the circulation of American popular song

Cole Porter became one of Smith's most important friends and patrons. His presence connected the nightclub to the world of American musical composition and affluent expatriate society. Porter combined a career in popular songwriting with extensive personal contacts among international elites.

In a cabaret, a song might acquire new associations outside the theatrical production for which it had originally been written. A performer could transform a composition into a signature number through repeated interpretation. Audiences might associate a particular song with an establishment or singer even when many other performers recorded it.

Smith's relationship with Porter demonstrates this distinction between composition and interpretation. She did not need to be the credited writer of a song to become closely associated with its performance. Her room also provided a setting in which new material could circulate among performers, musicians, and prospective listeners.

The friendship has generated numerous anecdotes, including stories about Porter introducing affluent friends to her performances and composing material for her. Her own recollections are important testimony to the relationship, although specific private conversations generally cannot be independently reconstructed. [9, 12]

## 12. Miss Otis Regrets and the problem of musical attribution

The Cole Porter song *Miss Otis Regrets* became closely identified with Bricktop. She maintained that Porter had brought the composition to her as something he had written for her to perform. That account appears in her recorded interview with Studs Terkel. [9]

The composition is a sophisticated example of the American popular song. Its narrative juxtaposes formal social manners with a disturbing story of violence and its consequences. The restrained wording and musical presentation enable a performer to create a striking contrast between the outward politeness of the narrator and the events being described.

Histories of the song offer differing accounts of its initial inspiration. Some connect it to Smith; others emphasize circumstances involving different members of Porter's social circle. The song entered wider commercial circulation in 1934, including a London theatrical performance. [13]

The following distinctions prevent a common historical confusion:

- Cole Porter was the songwriter.
- Smith was an important interpreter and associated the composition with her personal relationship with Porter.
- An assertion that it was written specifically for Smith is a claim about the circumstances of composition, not a claim to songwriting credit.
- A definitive account of its first private performance would require more direct contemporary documentation.

The association remains historically meaningful regardless of which origin anecdote is accepted. It demonstrates how a nightclub performer could become part of the public identity of an enduring composition.

## 13. The Charleston, the Black Bottom, and society dance instruction

Dance formed a second major element of Smith's appeal. The Charleston and the Black Bottom became widely fashionable during the 1920s, transmitting African American movement traditions into commercial theater and international social life. Their diffusion involved numerous performers, choreographers, dancers, and dance instructors.

Smith taught fashionable customers popular dances at private gatherings and social events. The Schomburg archival descriptions identify her participation in such gatherings among wealthy expatriates and European society figures. Her recollections also associate her with the Prince of Wales, later Edward VIII. [2, 8]

Dance instruction had economic as well as social value. A performer capable of teaching a fashionable dance could work beyond the nightclub's normal hours and create relationships with clients who might later attend her establishment. This provided a connection between the public world of commercial performance and the private world of society entertainment.

The movement of dances across racial and class boundaries did not always result in equitable recognition of their origins. Fashionable adoption might make a dance globally familiar while the individual Black performers who developed or transmitted it remained less well documented. Smith was an unusually visible participant in this process because her personal name continued to be associated with her work as an instructor and entertainer.

The precise details of particular lessons to titled clients are best understood as recollections unless corroborated by contemporary records. They are not necessary to establish her broader role in the transmission of American dance culture.

## 14. Josephine Baker and the female expatriate network

Josephine Baker arrived in Paris in 1925 with *La Revue Nègre*, a year after Smith's arrival. Baker's extraordinary success in the French theatrical market brought a new level of international attention to Black American performance. Smith was developing her own position in the same city through a different form of entertainment.

The two women's careers intersected through the expatriate social and professional network. Their association is reflected in the photographic and personal collections of the Schomburg Center. Accounts of Smith's life describe her as a supporter of Baker, although Baker's independent accomplishments should not be treated as the product of a single mentor. [2, 3]

The differences between their professional models are revealing. Baker's work in large-scale revues and films required elaborate production arrangements and extensive publicity. Smith's establishments depended more directly on the experience of individual patrons and the personal authority of the hostess. Baker became an internationally recognizable stage and screen star; Smith became internationally recognizable as the center of a social institution.

Both demonstrated that Black women could exercise authority in entertainment businesses as well as perform within them. Their successes nevertheless depended on particular markets, patrons, and the wider cultural labor of musicians, dancers, theater workers, and managers. Neither career offers evidence that the structural obstacles facing Black entertainers had disappeared.

See also: [Josephine Baker](josephine-baker.md).

## 15. Mabel Mercer and the art of cabaret

The expansion of Smith's Paris operation in the early 1930s brought Mabel Mercer into a particularly important professional relationship with Bricktop. Mercer was an English-born singer of mixed Black and white parentage who had already acquired experience in European entertainment. She became a featured vocalist at Smith's establishment and remained associated with its Parisian period until the approach of war. [14, 15]

Cabaret singing allowed Mercer to develop a distinctive relationship with words. Rather than relying exclusively on vocal projection, the singer could make a song intelligible as a small dramatic narrative. The intimacy of the room supported attention to phrasing, pauses, conversational delivery, and the interpretation of individual lines.

Smith and Mercer were not identical performers. Smith's reputation included dancing, conversation, comedy, and hospitality; Mercer's later reputation rested particularly on her interpretive approach to song. Their collaboration demonstrates the advantages of complementary skills within a small entertainment enterprise.

Mercer went on to a distinguished American nightclub career. Her receipt of the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1983 and the subsequent establishment of the Mabel Mercer Foundation illustrate the enduring recognition of the cabaret tradition. Smith's Paris room was an important stage in that development, though Mercer's subsequent artistic achievements were her own. [15]

The relationship is also a reminder that the history of entertainment cannot be reconstructed solely from the biographies of major recording stars. Proprietors and venues helped sustain artists whose distinctive contributions developed in live performance.

## 16. Marriage, private life, and public identity

In 1929, Smith married Peter Ducongé, a saxophonist. Photographs from that year document the couple in Paris. Her married surname appears in numerous archival records, especially those concerned with her later life. [3, 7]

A published biographical account dates their separation to 1933 and reports that they did not divorce. The available evidence does not warrant a detailed reconstruction of their private disagreements. [7]

Marriage did not displace Smith's public identity as Bricktop. The stage name remained the foundation of her professional reputation, while her married name was used in other contexts. This separation between a legal or personal name and a business identity was a practical feature of many entertainment careers.

Smith's position as a woman operating a nightlife business also shaped the expectations placed upon her. She had to exercise commercial authority in a field that involved late hours, close contact with strangers, and public scrutiny. The reputation of the establishment was often interpreted through the personality and conduct of its owner.

Biographies of nightclub figures can easily become collections of conjecture about private relationships. In Smith's case, the strongest approach is to distinguish her documented marriage, professional partnerships, friendships, and public persona from unsupported claims about her intimate life.

## 17. Literature, photography, and the social circle of Paris

Bricktop's reputation spread beyond the musical profession. Writers and artists participated in the social environment that surrounded her establishments. The archive contains material associated with major literary and entertainment figures, and her autobiography recounts numerous encounters with prominent visitors.

F. Scott Fitzgerald and Ernest Hemingway are commonly associated with the expatriate culture in which she moved. Their names have contributed to the mythology of interwar Paris, but the historical importance of the nightclub should not be measured exclusively by the prestige of white American writers who entered it. The establishment was also a workplace for Black musicians and an independent business developed by a Black woman.

Visual sources provide another perspective. Man Ray made a portrait of Smith around 1928, now held by the Centre Pompidou. Carl Van Vechten photographed her in Paris on June 22, 1934; prints and related catalog records survive at several institutions. [16–18]

Such images document the circulation of her public identity within modern photographic culture. They also reveal the selective character of the archive. A carefully composed portrait can establish appearance, authorship, and the circumstances recorded by a catalog, but it cannot provide a complete record of how an individual conducted a business.

The literary anecdotes likewise require attention to their origins. A story repeated in several memoirs may ultimately derive from one participant's recollection. Independent repetition should not automatically be mistaken for independent confirmation.

## 18. The 1930s: expansion, radio, and mounting international uncertainty

Smith enlarged her business during the early 1930s and also undertook seasonal activity in Biarritz. Her enterprise was becoming a recognized destination for international travelers rather than merely a neighborhood entertainment venue. [7]

The economic depression of the period complicated the operation of luxury businesses. Nightclubs catering to affluent customers were not insulated from falling travel demand, exchange-rate changes, or the difficulties experienced by artists seeking employment. The ability to maintain an attractive program became especially important when customers had many alternatives competing for their discretionary spending.

Smith's association with radio broadcasting in France during 1938 and 1939 illustrates another route by which a live entertainer could reach audiences outside the club. [1] Broadcasting could extend public recognition without reproducing the entire social experience of the establishment.

The political situation was increasingly threatening. Nazi Germany's expansion during the late 1930s raised the likelihood of a European war, affecting foreign residents and their plans for continued business. An enterprise serving travelers and expatriates was particularly exposed to international instability.

Smith's departure from France occurred in 1939, before the German military campaign against France in May 1940. This distinction matters: references to her fleeing during the German invasion may compress two separate events in the European war chronology.

## 19. Departure from France and wartime America

Smith left Paris as the Second World War began. Her later recollections credited influential friends, including the Duchess of Windsor and Elsie de Wolfe, with helping her arrange passage. [8]

The departure represented more than a change of residence. It interrupted a business built around a specific international clientele and a particular urban culture. The people who supported the establishment were themselves dispersing, and the political conditions under which the enterprise had developed were disappearing.

Returning to the United States meant confronting a familiar but materially different entertainment market. Her French reputation offered a commercial advantage, but the segregation of American nightlife complicated attempts to reproduce the Parisian experience. A venue whose appeal depended upon bringing disparate social groups together could not easily operate under exclusionary admission practices and discriminatory expectations.

One account identifies the Brittwood in Harlem in 1940 among her American business ventures. [7] The exact duration and legal ownership arrangements of individual wartime establishments would require further research in local business records and advertisements.

Smith's wartime difficulties are especially instructive in understanding the distinction between personal celebrity and a sustainable business. A famous operator cannot necessarily recreate a profitable enterprise in an unfamiliar market simply by retaining its name or previous style.

## 20. Mexico City and the North American expatriate circuit

During the war Smith moved her business to Mexico City. Sources place the beginning of this period in 1943 or 1944; the difference may reflect the distinction between her arrival and the opening of the club. Contemporary reporting associates the enterprise with assistance from the American heiress Doris Duke. [6, 7]

Mexico City provided a new cultural environment in which local artists, international residents, diplomats, and travelers could interact. For an operator accustomed to an international clientele, the capital presented an alternative to wartime Europe and to the more restrictive aspects of the American market.

Her Mexican period also extended the geographic reach of the Bricktop name. The enterprise was no longer simply a Paris institution temporarily relocated to the United States. It became part of a broader history of entertainment across national borders.

Smith's relationship with Mexico illustrates the practical importance of residency and immigration arrangements to international entrepreneurs. Her mobility depended not only on demand for her work but on the legal ability to live and conduct business in each country. A nightclub cannot function if its operator cannot remain in the market.

The surviving historical record does not provide a comprehensive reconstruction of the Mexican establishment's finances, staff, or programming. The period deserves research in Mexican newspapers, commercial directories, immigration records, and surviving expatriate correspondence, rather than reliance exclusively on American recollections.

## 21. Catholicism, charity, and the later social network

Smith converted to Roman Catholicism during her Mexican period. Religion became a significant element of her later public and private life. Her papers include correspondence connected with Fulton J. Sheen, while the photographic collection documents charitable work and audiences with successive popes. [2, 3, 7]

Her involvement in charitable activity demonstrates that her professional network extended beyond the entertainment industry. The skills that helped a nightclub operator mobilize guests and maintain personal relationships could also support fundraising and community activities.

The evidence should be understood in terms of specific documented actions rather than an assumption that every commercial decision was motivated by religious convictions. Smith's business interests, friendships, and faith were all important aspects of a life that continued to encompass multiple social environments.

Photographic records identify audiences with Pius XII in 1957, John XXIII in 1961, and Paul VI in 1964. These images supply chronological anchors for her activities in Italy. [3] They also help explain why her Roman years should be studied as more than an appendix to the glamorous history of Via Veneto.

## 22. Postwar Paris and the difficulties of reopening

Smith returned to Europe after the war and reopened her Paris enterprise in 1950. The new establishment initially attracted attention, and the photographic record includes a number of prominent performers and patrons from this period. [3, 7]

The reopening did not reproduce the success of the interwar business. European cities had experienced immense political and economic upheaval. The patterns of tourism, expatriate residence, spending, and nightlife on which her earlier enterprise had depended were no longer the same.

The case illustrates the importance of timing in entertainment. A successful venue may embody a particular historical moment as well as a particular operating concept. Once that moment has passed, the business must compete for the attention of a different public. Nostalgia can attract customers but does not necessarily provide sufficient demand to sustain an establishment indefinitely.

Smith subsequently redirected her efforts toward Italy. The transition was a response to changing commercial circumstances, rather than a simple continuation of her prewar French career. Her capacity to attempt another market after the Paris disappointment is an important feature of her long entrepreneurial history.

## 23. Rome, Via Veneto, and the emergence of film celebrity

By the early 1950s Smith was operating in Rome. The city had become an important center of international filmmaking, particularly through the expansion of Italian and foreign productions associated with Cinecittà. A new kind of international celebrity culture developed around actors, directors, journalists, and the establishments that catered to them.

Bricktop's Roman club on Via Veneto became part of that environment. Surviving photographs from the 1950s and early 1960s document encounters with people associated with music and cinema, including Billy Wilder, Orson Welles, and Pearl Bailey. [3]

The transition from Paris to Rome changed the composition of the clientele. Whereas literary expatriates and wealthy society figures had been central to the mythology of her French years, the Italian period attracted greater attention from the film industry. The proprietor had to adapt to the expectations of a public increasingly accustomed to celebrity journalism and photographic coverage.

One of the most publicized episodes concerned reports in 1962 about Elizabeth Taylor and Richard Burton visiting the club during the period of their highly scrutinized relationship. Smith objected to press accounts suggesting that the establishment facilitated their meetings. According to historian Tamara J. Walker's account of contemporary reporting, she threatened legal action but did not proceed with it. [5]

The incident illustrates a conflict between two forms of publicity. Celebrity attendance could help promote a nightclub, but intrusive or sensational reporting could damage its reputation as a place where patrons might expect discretion. The more famous the customers, the more difficult it became for the proprietor to control the public narrative surrounding the business.

The club's closing date is reported inconsistently: some biographical accounts give 1961, whereas Walker identifies contemporary coverage of a closing in 1964, which is also consistent with other accounts of Smith's Roman career. [5, 6] The latter date is preferable for the final closure of the Roman enterprise, pending examination of additional contemporary business records.

## 24. Return to America, oral history, and screen appearances

Smith spent additional time in Italy after withdrawing from nightclub ownership. Walker dates her final departure from Rome to 1970 and her settlement in New York to 1972. [5] These dates should be distinguished from the earlier termination of the nightclub business.

Her later American activities included personal appearances and the recording of her recollections. Her commercial recording *So Long, Baby*, associated with Cy Coleman, is generally dated to the early 1970s; published sources differ between 1970 and 1972. [1, 19] The description of this as her only commercial record should not be extended to mean that no other audio recordings of her voice survive.

The Studs Terkel radio archive, for example, preserves a substantial interview in which Smith discussed her career and musical colleagues. [9] Such recordings are valuable because they preserve the rhythm of her speech and the manner in which she presented her own history.

She also appeared in the American feature film *Honeybaby, Honeybaby* (1974) and in Woody Allen's *Zelig* (1983). The American Film Institute catalogs both appearances. [20, 21] The latter is a fictional mock documentary; its use of real historical personalities does not make its narrative about the invented Leonard Zelig historical evidence.

In 1983, Atheneum published *Bricktop*, her autobiography written with James Haskins. The work became an essential source for her career, particularly for encounters and episodes that left little other documentation. A memoir written late in life, however, necessarily reflects the author's selection and interpretation of events. It should be read together with photographs, contracts, contemporary journalism, and the surviving accounts of other participants.

## 25. Death, recognition, and historical significance

Smith died in New York City in early 1984 at the age of eighty-nine. Contemporary reporting published on February 2 described her death as occurring early Tuesday, which corresponds to January 31. The West Virginia Encyclopedia likewise gives January 31, though some archival descriptions use February 1. [1, 6, 11] The discrepancy should remain visible until the underlying vital record is examined. She is reported to have been buried at Woodlawn Cemetery in the Bronx. [7]

In 2013 she was inducted into the West Virginia Music Hall of Fame, recognizing the international achievements of an entertainer whose life began in that state. [22]

Smith's importance can be assessed in several related dimensions. As a performer, she transmitted traditions of American variety entertainment into international cabaret culture. As an entrepreneur, she developed a recognizable hospitality business across multiple countries. As a professional intermediary, she helped connect musicians to opportunities, patrons, and audiences. As a historical witness, she preserved recollections of entertainment networks that are difficult to reconstruct from recordings alone.

Her life also illuminates the limits of celebrity as a business resource. Reputation enabled her to move between markets, but did not shield her establishments from war, shifting tastes, changes in travel, or legal and commercial obstacles. The successes and disappointments of her enterprise are equally relevant to an understanding of her career.

Bricktop should therefore be remembered not merely as an acquaintance of famous people but as an entertainer and businesswoman whose own work helped create the social institutions in which many of those people gathered. Her achievement demonstrates the significance of hospitality, live performance, and personal networks within the history of twentieth-century popular culture.

## Historical evidence and limitations

| Claim | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Birth in Alderson on August 14, 1894 | Consistently supported by institutional biographies. |
| Early association with Florence Mills | Supported by archival descriptions of the Panama Trio. |
| Assistance to Duke Ellington | Her recollection is consistent with the documented 1923 engagement; sole-discovery claims are excessive. |
| Porter composed *Miss Otis Regrets* specifically for Smith | Her own account supports the claim; alternate origin accounts exist. |
| Continuous operation of one Paris club from 1924 onward | Misleading: multiple venues and wartime interruption must be distinguished. |
| Final closure in 1964 | Supported by historical research using contemporary reporting; some sources report 1961. |
| Exact death date | January 31 is supported by contemporary reporting; February 1 appears in archival metadata. |
| Detailed club profits and ownership arrangements | Not established by the sources examined. |

## Further research

The principal archival priorities are the reconstruction of the successive Paris premises, the employment and partnership arrangements of individual clubs, the Mexican enterprise's commercial history, and the chronology of the Roman closing. The Schomburg Center and Emory collections contain material capable of supplementing the published memoir. Contemporary French, Mexican, Italian, and American newspaper archives may independently establish opening dates, engagements, customer accounts, and business difficulties.

This article relies on accessible catalog descriptions, published historical accounts, and identified oral-history resources. The original vital record, complete memoir, unpublished correspondence, nightclub contracts, and financial ledgers were not examined. Some archival catalog pages were accessible only through indexed descriptions. Relative MGU links reflect the designated repository structure; live publication and rendering have not been verified.
