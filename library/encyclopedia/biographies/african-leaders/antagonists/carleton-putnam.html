Carleton Putnam

Airline pioneer, Theodore Roosevelt biographer, and northern segregationist writer whose Race and Reason supplied a widely circulated intellectual defense of racial hierarchy

Overview

Carleton Putnam (1901-1998) was an American aviation entrepreneur, lawyer, biographer, airline executive, and one of the most prominent northern intellectual defenders of racial segregation during the civil-rights era. A Princeton graduate with a Columbia law degree, Putnam founded the airline that became Chicago and Southern Air Lines, helped build it into a major carrier, and became chairman of Delta Air Lines after the two companies merged in 1953. He later wrote a well-regarded biography of Theodore Roosevelt before redirecting much of his public writing toward race.

Beginning in the late 1950s, Putnam argued that Brown v. Board of Education and subsequent integration efforts rested on a false assumption of racial equality. He presented himself as a “racial realist” and attempted to give segregation a biological and scientific foundation. His 1961 book Race and Reason: A Yankee View argued for continued racial separation and inherent racial inequality. It circulated widely through Citizens' Council and segregationist networks, sold more than 150,000 copies by the time he published a sequel in 1967, and became influential among southern politicians and later white-supremacist activists.

Putnam's historical importance lies in the translation of white supremacy into polished elite argument. He was not a Klan commander, violent enforcer, or southern officeholder. He brought the credentials of Princeton, Columbia, aviation leadership, historical scholarship, and a northern background to a movement that needed to portray segregation as intellectually serious rather than simply prejudiced. His use of anthropological and intelligence-testing literature, his correspondence with scientists such as his relative Carleton S. Coon, and his ties to Pioneer Fund and Citizens' Council networks helped bridge scientific racism, segregationist propaganda, and political resistance to Black civil rights.

Family and Education

Putnam was born in New York City into a family with deep New England roots. He later made ancestry part of his political self-presentation, stressing that his defense of segregation came from a Yankee rather than from a southerner protecting local custom.

He graduated from Princeton in 1924 after studying history and politics and received his law degree from Columbia in 1932. Those credentials mattered decades later. Putnam could present himself to readers as a cultivated businessman and scholar rather than as a professional racial agitator.

Aviation Becomes His First Career

Putnam became an aviation enthusiast while commercial flight was still a young industry. Instead of practicing law, he chose the far less predictable work of building an airline.

In 1933 he founded Pacific Seaboard Air Lines in California. Delta Flight Museum records its first service on June 25 of that year along the California coast between Los Angeles and San Francisco.

Building Chicago and Southern Air Lines

The small California carrier pursued federal airmail opportunities and shifted toward Mississippi Valley routes. It eventually became Chicago and Southern Air Lines.

Putnam was its founder and first president and later chairman. The company grew into a substantial carrier serving the Midwest and South, with routes eventually extending through New Orleans to international destinations.

A Genuine Airline Pioneer

Putnam's aviation accomplishments were real. Building an airline in the 1930s required capital, route strategy, equipment decisions, regulatory work, and organizational leadership.

His later racial politics should not erase that competence. In fact, the credibility earned in aviation helped make his later racial arguments more influential.

High Journey

Putnam published High Journey: A Decade in the Pilgrimage of an Air Line Pioneer in 1945, recounting the airline's development.

At this stage he was known principally as an aviation entrepreneur and writer.

Merger with Delta

Chicago and Southern merged with Delta Air Lines on May 1, 1953. Delta's own historical records identify Putnam as C&S's founder and first president and state that he became chairman of Delta's board after the merger.

He stepped down as chairman in January 1955 but remained a director for decades.

The Theodore Roosevelt Project

After leaving day-to-day airline leadership, Putnam increasingly devoted himself to historical writing. He moved to Virginia in part to work near the Library of Congress and began an ambitious multivolume biography of Theodore Roosevelt.

The first volume appeared in 1958 as Theodore Roosevelt: The Formative Years, 1858-1886.

A Respected Biographer

Princeton's memorial to Putnam described the Roosevelt book as critically acclaimed, and later Roosevelt specialists continued to value the depth of its research. He never completed the planned later volumes.

This matters because Putnam's segregationist writing did not emerge from an obviously unserious public figure. He had demonstrated patience with archives, command of prose, and the ability to produce a substantial historical study.

Brown v. Board of Education

The Supreme Court's 1954 decision in Brown v. Board of Education declared state-mandated public-school segregation unconstitutional.

Putnam did not immediately become a national anti-integration spokesman. His public racial campaign developed several years later, as federal courts pressed southern states toward actual desegregation.

The Putnam Letter

In the late 1950s Putnam wrote President Dwight D. Eisenhower attacking the legal and intellectual assumptions behind compulsory school integration. After publication and reprinting, the document became known as the “Putnam Letter.”

Citizens' Council networks helped circulate it across the South. Thousands of supportive responses followed.

Why the Yankee Identity Mattered

Putnam's northern identity was politically valuable to segregationists.

A Mississippi politician defending segregation could be dismissed as protecting local power. A Princeton-educated airline founder from the North could be presented as independent confirmation that the southern position was reasonable.

Beyond States Rights

Putnam did not rest his case on federalism. He wanted to challenge the assumption that racial equality itself was true.

If Black and white populations differed innately in intelligence, temperament, or social capacity, he argued, then compulsory integration could be portrayed as contrary to nature rather than merely contrary to local preference.

The Language of Racial Realism

Putnam preferred the posture of a reluctant truth-teller. He described himself and like-minded writers as racial realists.

The term was rhetorically useful: hierarchy could be presented as acceptance of evidence instead of prejudice.

Putnam Was Not a Scientist

Putnam had no professional training in genetics, anthropology, psychology, neurology, or statistics.

His method was synthetic and polemical. He selected research produced by others, interpreted it for a general audience, and drew political conclusions from it.

Carleton S. Coon

Physical anthropologist Carleton S. Coon was a relative of Putnam and became an important source within his racial campaign. Princeton archival research reports that Coon privately sent Putnam scientific material that Putnam used to support claims about Black inferiority even when Coon's public statements were more guarded.

Putnam later promoted Coon's evolutionary race theories aggressively.

Audrey Shuey and Intelligence Testing

Putnam also relied heavily on psychologist Audrey M. Shuey's The Testing of Negro Intelligence, a compilation of intelligence-test literature.

Segregationists treated such testing as evidence that Black-white differences were inherited. Critics argued that the tests could not be separated from environment, education, poverty, discrimination, and culture.

Henry E. Garrett

Henry E. Garrett, a former president of the American Psychological Association and outspoken opponent of integration, wrote the foreword to Race and Reason.

Garrett supplied professional psychological prestige. Putnam supplied readability, business stature, and a mass audience.

Wesley Critz George

Anatomist Wesley Critz George belonged to the same scientific-segregationist network and argued that biological race differences justified separation.

Putnam helped turn technical-sounding claims from such figures into political arguments that could circulate outside laboratories and universities.

The Pioneer Fund Network

The Pioneer Fund, created by Wickliffe Draper, financed hereditarian race research and segregationist intellectual work. Historical scholarship identifies Putnam as a Pioneer-supported segregationist figure.

The relationship helps explain the division of labor within the movement: Draper supplied private capital; Putnam turned racial science into persuasive public prose.

Race and Reason

Race and Reason: A Yankee View appeared in 1961.

It became Putnam's defining work.

What the Book Argued

Putnam attacked what he regarded as an “equalitarian” ideology and argued that heredity explained important average differences between racial groups. From that premise he defended continued segregation and opposed forced integration.

The conclusion was political even when the vocabulary was scientific.

Why It Looked Persuasive

Race and Reason was short, footnoted, direct, and written in a restrained style.

It did not look like a Klan handbill.

That distinction was one of its greatest propaganda advantages.

Citizens Councils Adopt the Book

Citizens' Council networks embraced Putnam because he gave their program an intellectual vocabulary. The University of Mississippi preserves material relating to Race and Reason in its Citizens' Council Collection.

Economic intimidation and political organizing could now be accompanied by claims that segregation was supported by biology.

Race and Reason Day

Mississippi Governor Ross Barnett proclaimed October 26, 1961, “Race and Reason Day.” Putnam appeared in Jackson before a segregationist audience.

The event made the relationship between Putnam's writing and public power explicit: a state government fighting desegregation celebrated his book as a political resource.

Louisiana Public Education

Princeton University Archives records that Race and Reason was made required reading for teachers and advanced students in Louisiana.

A polemic defending inherent racial inequality had moved from private publication into a state educational system.

A Large Audience

By 1967 the book had sold more than 150,000 copies, according to Princeton archival research.

For a racial-segregation argument presented as popular science, that was substantial reach.

Scientific Criticism

Putnam's political success did not mean his scientific interpretation was accepted. In 1963, sociologist Melvin Tumin and other specialists produced Race and Intelligence: An Evaluation in response to Putnam, Shuey, and similar writers.

Princeton's archival summary notes that the evaluators were in substantial agreement that Putnam's central racial claims lacked substantial scientific support.

Environment and Heredity

Putnam portrayed environmental explanations as ideological excuses. Many scientists working on intelligence at the time considered background, schooling, culture, social environment, and discrimination central to measured racial differences.

The dispute mattered because it changed where responsibility was placed. Heredity naturalized inequality; environment implicated institutions.

Carleton Coon and The Origin of Races

When Coon published The Origin of Races in 1962, Putnam and allied segregationists promoted it as evidence for deep biological racial differences.

Historians of anthropology have documented how aggressively segregationists attempted to appropriate the book for political purposes.

Speaking at Princeton

Putnam returned to Princeton on May 13, 1964, to speak at Whig Hall. He defended segregation in areas with large Black populations and argued that compulsory integration ignored biological incompatibility.

The appearance generated controversy at his own alma mater.

The Paternalist Defense

Putnam sometimes argued that segregation could be better for Black people themselves.

This paternalism allowed exclusion to be framed as protection rather than domination.

The National Putnam Letters Committee

Supporters developed the National Putnam Letters Committee to circulate his writings and maintain an organized correspondence campaign.

The lone letter writer had become the center of a propaganda network.

Race: A Reply

Putnam joined Henry Garrett and Wesley Critz George in 1964 to answer scientific critics of hereditarian race claims.

The objective was larger than defending one author. Their network was trying to establish an alternative body of expertise that segregationists could cite against mainstream scientific opinion.

Race and Reality

Putnam published Race and Reality: A Search for Solutions in 1967.

By then the Civil Rights Act, Voting Rights Act, and federal court enforcement had badly weakened the legal structure of Jim Crow. His work increasingly served a post-segregation ideology of racial difference rather than a practical blueprint for maintaining every old law.

From Jim Crow to Later White Identity Politics

The portability of Putnam's arguments gave them a long afterlife. Claims about innate racial difference could be redirected from defending segregated schools toward opposition to affirmative action, interracial marriage, immigration, and demographic change.

The legal battlefield changed. The underlying hierarchy could survive.

Influence on David Duke

Princeton University Archives records David Duke's later statement that reading Race and Reason as a young person was pivotal in his movement toward white-supremacist politics.

Putnam did not determine Duke's choices, but the example demonstrates how segregationist literature could recruit readers long after the specific crisis of Brown.

Princeton and Demographic Change

Putnam remained preoccupied with race into the 1970s. Princeton archival records preserve correspondence in which he complained that the university's changing student body no longer represented the hereditary “stock” of its founders.

His concern had expanded far beyond southern public schools.

Race as Ancestry

Putnam linked institutions and national culture to biological descent. In his worldview, changing the ancestry of the people inside an institution could alter the institution itself.

This was a theory of civilization rooted in bloodline.

The Dissident Persona

Putnam often portrayed himself as a dissident silenced by liberal academic orthodoxy.

There was an irony in the posture. He attacked elite institutions while using his own elite credentials, scholarly contacts, and business reputation as evidence that he deserved to be heard.

How Putnam Opposed Black People

Putnam opposed Black equality through ideas rather than street violence. He argued that Black people were innately inferior in important abilities, defended racial separation, resisted compulsory school integration, supplied segregationist organizations with scientific-looking arguments, and helped politicians portray resistance to civil rights as a rational response to biological difference.

His task was not to beat demonstrators. It was to explain why institutions should remain unequal.

Intellectual Cover

Segregation needed more than police, courts, and economic coercion.

It needed explanations.

Putnam supplied them.

What He Did Not Do

Putnam should not be assigned personal responsibility for Klan bombings, murders, or every act committed by segregationists who admired his work.

His documented role was ideological and propagandistic. Precision makes that role clearer rather than smaller.

Comparison with Wickliffe Draper

Draper was primarily a financier. Putnam was primarily a communicator.

Draper's capital sustained projects and researchers; Putnam's name and prose carried their conclusions into public debate.

Comparison with Robert B. Patterson

Robert “Tut” Patterson organized Citizens' Councils around businessmen, employers, bankers, and local political power. Putnam gave Council supporters something different: a compact argument that racial separation rested on biological reality.

Comparison with Thomas Pickens Brady

Thomas Pickens Brady approached massive resistance largely through constitutional, legal, and civilizational argument.

Putnam moved further into heredity and race science. Both sought respectable intellectual foundations for the same segregated order.

Comparison with Sam Bowers

Sam Bowers organized clandestine terror and authorized murder. Putnam did not.

Bowers tried to stop civil rights with fear. Putnam tried to stop them with legitimacy.

Comparison with Henry Garrett

Garrett had professional psychological credentials. Putnam had business prestige, literary skill, and public reach.

The alliance was mutually useful.

Was Putnam a Scientist?

No. He was a lawyer, businessman, historian, and political writer who interpreted selected scientific material.

Was Putnam a White Supremacist?

Yes, in the substantive historical sense. He defended segregation and asserted inherent racial hierarchy, including claims of Black inferiority.

His preferred label of racial realism did not change the political content.

Was He a Southerner?

No.

His northern identity was one reason segregationists valued him so highly.

Was Race and Reason Scientifically Accepted?

No. It was politically influential but faced substantial criticism from relevant scientists and social scientists.

Its success should be measured in propaganda and politics, not scientific consensus.

Did He Advocate Racial Violence?

Putnam's major documented program was segregation, racial hierarchy, and intellectual resistance to integration rather than organization of physical terror.

Violence by allied movements should not be attributed to him without specific evidence.

Two Reputations

Putnam left behind two sharply different reputations.

In aviation history he was an entrepreneur who helped build a successful airline. In civil-rights history he was a sophisticated propagandist who tried to make racial hierarchy scientifically respectable.

Death

Carleton Putnam died of pneumonia at his home in Charlottesville, Virginia, on March 5, 1998.

He was ninety-six.

Historical Significance

Carleton Putnam matters because he demonstrates how segregation adapted when open appeals to prejudice were losing national legitimacy. He brought heredity, anthropology, intelligence testing, individual liberty, and academic dissent into a movement fighting Black equality. His biography made the strategy unusually powerful: readers encountered not an anonymous extremist but an airline founder, Princeton and Columbia alumnus, and serious historical writer.

His career also reveals a division of labor within white resistance. Governors supplied state power. Citizens' Councils applied economic pressure. Klan groups supplied terror. Donors such as Wickliffe Draper supplied capital. Putnam supplied an argument telling educated white Americans that racial hierarchy could be reasonable, scientific, and modern.

Impact on African Americans

Putnam's work helped legitimize exclusion from equal schools and institutions by portraying group inequality as inherited. That framing diverted attention from discriminatory law, unequal resources, disfranchisement, poverty, and generations of coercion.

The effects were not confined to books. Southern political leaders celebrated his work, Citizens' Council networks circulated it, and Louisiana incorporated it into education. Ideas became institutional tools.

A Balanced Assessment

Putnam's nonracial accomplishments were substantial. He was a genuine aviation pioneer, successful executive, trained lawyer, capable writer, and serious Theodore Roosevelt biographer. These achievements are not incidental; they explain why audiences granted him authority.

They do not validate his racial conclusions. His central weakness was the transfer of prestige from fields in which he had demonstrated competence into scientific questions for which he lacked professional training. He selectively assembled disputed research to defend a political program of racial separation. Balance requires preserving both truths: the competence was real, and so was the segregationist purpose to which he applied it.

Legacy

Putnam's airline career became part of Delta's corporate history, and his Roosevelt biography retained value in presidential scholarship. His racial books travelled through a different network—Citizens' Council libraries, segregationist education campaigns, hereditarian debate, and later white-power movements.

His life is therefore a case study in credential transfer. Success in business or historical research can generate public trust that readers then extend into unrelated scientific subjects. Putnam used that trust to give racial hierarchy a voice that sounded calm, educated, and modern while defending an unequal social order.

Geographic Scope

New York and New Jersey formed Putnam's educational and early professional world. California was the birthplace of his airline, while Chicago and Southern eventually centered its routes in the Mississippi Valley and South. Putnam later lived in Virginia, where he worked near the Library of Congress and ultimately died in Charlottesville.

His segregationist influence was strongest across the American South, especially Mississippi and Louisiana, but his northern identity and appearances at Princeton gave the campaign a national dimension.

Legal and Constitutional Context

Putnam's racial campaign developed around Brown v. Board of Education, Cooper v. Aaron, the Fourteenth Amendment, federal enforcement of school desegregation, freedom-of-association arguments, and the collapse of state-sponsored racial separation.

He possessed no authority to enforce law. His strategy was to change public opinion so that courts, legislatures, constitutional amendments, local governments, or political resistance might preserve racial separation.
