Hiram Wesley Evans

Dallas dentist, documented participant in anti-Black Klan violence, Imperial Wizard of the second Ku Klux Klan, and architect of its transformation into a national white-Protestant political movement

Overview

Hiram Wesley Evans (1881-1966) was an Alabama-born dentist, Dallas Ku Klux Klan leader, white-supremacist organizer, writer, businessman, and Imperial Wizard of the second Ku Klux Klan from the early 1920s until 1939. He joined the Klan in 1920, rose quickly through the Texas hierarchy, participated directly in the abduction and torture of a Black hotel employee, and then helped force founder William Joseph Simmons from practical control of the national organization.

Evans transformed the second Klan from a rapidly growing fraternity into a more disciplined political movement. He emphasized what he called native-born white Protestant supremacy, promoted immigration restriction, anti-Catholicism, antisemitism, racial separation, Prohibition, and political “Americanism,” and tried to convert millions of members into an electoral bloc. Under his leadership the Klan marched openly in Washington, influenced local and state elections, supported candidates such as Texas senator Earle Mayfield, and became a major force in political disputes from school policy to the 1928 presidential election.

Evans tried to distinguish national Klan leadership from uncontrolled vigilantism because public violence damaged the organization’s respectability, but his own earlier conduct undermined that claim. In 1921 he led Klansmen who abducted Black bellhop Alex Johnson and burned or etched Klan initials into his forehead with acid. His later political strategy did not abandon white supremacy; it attempted to make racial hierarchy more organized, respectable, and politically effective. Membership collapsed amid scandals, factionalism, public opposition, and the Great Depression, and Evans eventually resigned in 1939 before spending his later years in highway-material businesses and legal controversies.

Birth in Alabama and Childhood in Texas

Evans was born in Ashland, Alabama, on September 26, 1881, to Hiram Martin Evans and Georgia Striplin Evans. His family moved to Texas while he was young, and he graduated from high school in Hubbard, a small central Texas community.

His formative years therefore bridged the post-Reconstruction South and the rapidly developing Southwest. Both regions preserved strong traditions of white racial hierarchy even as cities, industry, migration, and electoral politics created new social tensions.

Dental Training

Evans studied dentistry at Vanderbilt University but left before completing a degree. He nevertheless obtained a Texas dental license in 1900 and established a practice in Dallas.

Dentistry gave him the professional standing that later distinguished him from caricatures of Klansmen as purely rural or marginal. Evans became an example of the educated, urban, middle-class leadership that made the second Klan politically formidable.

Dallas Dentist

Evans practiced dentistry in Dallas for roughly two decades. Dallas was becoming a major commercial city, attracting migrants, immigrants, new industries, and competing political movements during the Progressive Era and World War I years.

Those changes helped create the atmosphere in which the revived Ku Klux Klan could appeal to white Protestants who felt that their social position, moral authority, or political influence was being threatened by a changing urban America.

The Second Ku Klux Klan

William Joseph Simmons had revived the Ku Klux Klan in Georgia in 1915. Unlike the first Reconstruction-era Klan, the second Klan was organized as a national fraternal order with elaborate ranks, dues, uniforms, initiation rituals, paid recruiters, publications, and legal corporate structures.

The organization retained anti-Black white supremacy at its foundation while adding aggressive anti-Catholicism, antisemitism, hostility to immigrants, Prohibition enforcement, and a broader claim that native-born white Protestants were the proper guardians of American civilization.

Joining the Klan in 1920

Evans joined the Ku Klux Klan in 1920, just as professional recruiting was causing the organization to expand rapidly beyond Georgia. He soon devoted most of his time to Klan work rather than dentistry.

His rapid rise suggests both ambition and organizational skill. Within a year he was leading the Dallas local chapter and developing a reputation as one of the strongest Klan organizers in Texas.

Exalted Cyclops of Dallas

By 1921 Evans had become the “Exalted Cyclops,” or chief officer, of the Dallas Klavern. The title placed him at the head of a local organization whose membership included men with substantial community influence.

The Dallas Klan operated during a period when the second Klan was expanding directly into municipal politics, policing, elections, and informal social regulation. Evans’s authority was therefore more than ceremonial.

Alex Johnson

One of the clearest episodes in Evans’s record involved Alex Johnson, a Black bellhop employed at Dallas’s Adolphus Hotel. The Texas State Historical Association records that Evans led a group of Klansmen who forcibly removed Johnson from the hotel in 1921.

The men abducted Johnson and used acid to mark the letters “K.K.K.” on his forehead. The assault demonstrated that Evans’s later preference for political respectability did not arise from a consistent personal rejection of racial vigilantism.

The Meaning of the Johnson Assault

The attack on Johnson was not a symbolic political disagreement. A Black worker was seized by organized white men, taken outside ordinary legal process, physically tortured, and permanently marked with the initials of a white-supremacist organization.

The act was designed to establish racial authority through fear. Whatever specific accusation Klansmen used to justify the attack, they claimed for themselves the power to abduct and punish a Black man without trial.

Direct Responsibility for Racial Violence

Evans’s participation in the Johnson assault is especially important because it provides documented evidence of direct involvement in racial violence rather than merely ideological leadership. He should not be described only as an administrator who inherited violent followers.

Later in his career he sought to suppress unsanctioned violence because it damaged Klan politics, but his own rise had included precisely the kind of extralegal racial punishment he later portrayed as inconsistent with respectable Klan leadership.

Expansion of the Texas Klan

The Klan grew rapidly in Texas during the early 1920s. Local chapters organized social events, political endorsements, moral-policing campaigns, and intimidation, while statewide leaders worked to translate membership into influence over Democratic primaries.

Because the Democratic Party dominated Texas politics, influence inside its primaries could often determine who actually held office. Evans understood that organized white Protestant voters could become far more powerful than isolated vigilantes.

Great Titan of Texas

When the Klan formally organized its Texas “Realm” in 1922, Evans became a Great Titan, a district leadership position. His advancement placed him above local chapter administration and gave him responsibility for broader organizational development.

He quickly became part of the national Klan hierarchy and moved from Dallas politics toward control of the entire Invisible Empire.

Imperial Kligrapp

William Joseph Simmons appointed Evans Imperial Kligrapp, or national secretary, in 1922. The office gave Evans direct access to the national organization’s administration and intensified his involvement in internal power struggles.

The founder had created the rituals and structure of the second Klan, but by this point other leaders believed Simmons lacked the discipline and political strategy required to manage an organization that had grown explosively.

The Campaign to Remove William Joseph Simmons

Evans became part of the group that pushed William Joseph Simmons out of practical control of the Klan. The transition occurred through internal maneuvering rather than a democratic membership election and became the subject of bitter disputes and later litigation.

Evans and his allies presented the change as necessary professionalization. Simmons regarded it as a betrayal by subordinates who had taken control of the organization he founded.

Becoming Imperial Wizard

Evans seized practical leadership in late 1922 and was formally established as Imperial Wizard during the subsequent national reorganization. He would remain the Klan’s dominant national leader until his resignation in 1939.

His long tenure makes him more important to the second Klan’s political history than Simmons, even though Simmons deserves credit for creating the revived organization itself.

From Fraternity to Political Machine

Evans wanted the Klan to become a disciplined political force rather than an organization defined primarily by local vigilantism and initiation spectacle. He encouraged members to vote as an organized Protestant bloc and sought influence over candidates, primaries, school policy, immigration, and law enforcement.

This did not moderate the Klan’s racial ideology. It shifted white supremacy toward electoral and institutional power, where the organization could affect government without every action carrying a hood or flaming cross.

Native, White, Protestant Supremacy

Evans’s own writings stated the Klan ideal with unusual clarity. In his 1926 essay on Americanism he summarized the movement around native-born, white, Protestant supremacy and argued that racial loyalty was a foundational element of authentic American identity.

The statement shows that race was not incidental to his politics. Evans defined the nation hierarchically and placed white racial identity alongside Protestant religion and native ancestry as qualifications for full cultural belonging.

White Supremacy as a Worldview

Evans argued not merely that white people should retain particular privileges in the South, but that the white race should remain supreme nationally and internationally. He treated racial hierarchy as a natural and necessary condition of civilization.

Such arguments gave ideological legitimacy to excluding Black citizens from political equality, social integration, and the national identity Evans reserved for what he called the pioneer stock.

Racial Purity

Evans warned against racial mixture and treated the maintenance of white “racial integrity” as a sacred obligation. He used pseudo-biological comparisons to suggest that mixing socially distinct peoples would weaken the nation.

This rhetoric made opposition to Black equality appear larger than a dispute about schools, voting, or jobs. Integration itself could be presented as a threat to the biological future of white Americans.

Opposition to Black Citizenship

The Klan under Evans did not need a single national platform calling for repeal of every Black constitutional right. Its cultural program assumed that white Protestants should dominate public institutions and that challenges to that dominance threatened America.

Local Klan activity therefore often reinforced segregation, political exclusion, employment discrimination, and intimidation of Black citizens attempting to claim greater social or political power.

The Great Migration

The Great Migration carried hundreds of thousands of African Americans into northern and midwestern cities during and after World War I. Black workers competed for industrial employment, Black families entered new neighborhoods, and Black voters gained political leverage in cities where disfranchisement laws were weaker than in the South.

The second Klan used these changes to nationalize white racial fear. Under Evans, white supremacy could be marketed not simply as a southern tradition but as a nationwide defense against changing demographics and Black mobility.

Anti-Catholicism

Evans also made anti-Catholicism central to Klan politics. He argued that Catholic loyalty to the Church hierarchy and pope threatened independent American citizenship and repeatedly portrayed Catholic institutions as political competitors to Protestant America.

This hostility became especially important in the 1928 presidential election, when Democratic nominee Al Smith’s Catholic faith allowed the Klan to combine partisan politics with religious prejudice.

Antisemitism

The Klan under Evans also trafficked in antisemitism. Evans sometimes denied being personally antisemitic while simultaneously describing Jewish influence in finance, politics, the New Deal, or urban culture as threatening.

That contradiction reflected a common rhetorical tactic: reject the label of prejudice while continuing to circulate generalized claims portraying Jews as an alien or destabilizing political force.

Immigration Restriction

Evans wrote extensively against modern immigration, arguing that large numbers of newcomers from southern and eastern Europe threatened the cultural cohesion and racial character of the United States. His 1923 book The Menace of Modern Immigration made this one of his signature themes.

The Klan supported the restrictive immigration climate that culminated in the Immigration Act of 1924, although that law resulted from a much broader national nativist coalition rather than from Evans or the Klan alone.

Prohibition and Moral Regulation

Evans’s Klan also presented itself as a defender of Prohibition, sexual morality, public schools, Protestant family life, and political honesty. These causes allowed members to describe the organization as a moral reform movement rather than only a racial organization.

The reform language broadened recruitment, but moral policing could become coercive when Klansmen assumed extralegal authority to investigate, threaten, or punish neighbors whose behavior they considered immoral.

Public Schools and Protestant Culture

Klan defense of public schools often carried an anti-Catholic purpose because Catholic parochial education was portrayed as a competing loyalty system. In some states Klan supporters backed efforts to make public schooling compulsory.

Thus an apparently universal institution—public education—could become part of a campaign to reinforce Protestant cultural dominance while marginalizing Catholic communities.

Respectable Membership

Evans sought members among ministers, professionals, merchants, police officers, civic leaders, farmers, and middle-class white Protestants. The movement’s power came partly from the fact that it could recruit people already embedded in ordinary institutions.

For Black citizens and other targeted minorities, this created uncertainty about where Klan influence ended. The Klansman behind a hood might also be an employer, officer, teacher, banker, or elected official.

The Klan and Texas Politics

Texas became one of the states where the second Klan exercised substantial electoral influence. Klan endorsements affected local and statewide Democratic primaries, and candidates both courted and denounced the organization.

Evans’s experience in Texas helped convince him that electoral organization could produce more lasting power than isolated acts of vigilantism.

Earle B. Mayfield

The Klan strongly supported Democrat Earle B. Mayfield in the 1922 Texas Senate election. His opponent later challenged the result and charged the Klan with extensive involvement, prompting a Senate investigation.

Official Senate history records that the investigation acknowledged Klan participation and found evidence of voter intimidation in local primary contests, although it concluded that the irregularities had not changed the Senate result and did not connect Mayfield personally to intimidation.

Political Intimidation

The Mayfield election controversy demonstrates that Klan power extended beyond ordinary endorsement. Senate investigators considered allegations involving intimidation, election irregularities, and organized Klan intervention in Democratic politics.

The episode illustrates why Evans’s political Klan remained threatening even when national leaders publicly discouraged mob violence. A secret fraternity with large membership could influence political behavior without leaving a simple paper trail.

National Headquarters and Washington

Evans increasingly oriented the Klan toward national politics and established a major presence in Washington, D.C. Moving closer to Congress and federal institutions symbolized his ambition to make the organization more than a southern protest movement.

The nation’s capital also gave the Klan a stage on which it could demonstrate that hooded white Protestant supremacy considered itself part of mainstream American political life.

The 1925 Washington March

In August 1925 tens of thousands of Klansmen and their families marched down Pennsylvania Avenue in Washington. Smithsonian collections preserve images of the procession, which included white-robed participants moving through the ceremonial heart of the national capital.

The march was an extraordinary display of political normalization. An organization dedicated to racial and religious hierarchy was able to parade openly before national monuments rather than functioning only as a secret night-riding order.

The Meaning of the Washington Spectacle

The Washington march communicated power to both supporters and targets. To members, it demonstrated national reach and respectability; to Black Americans, Catholics, Jews, immigrants, and political opponents, it displayed the size of a movement that explicitly questioned their equal place in the nation.

Public spectacle thus became a form of intimidation even in the absence of direct physical attack.

The 1926 Washington Parade

The Klan returned to Washington for another major parade in September 1926. A Library of Congress photograph shows Evans himself marching in full Klan regalia at the head of the procession.

By this point, however, declining membership and worsening scandals meant that such demonstrations increasingly served as attempts to prove that the Invisible Empire remained powerful.

1924 National Politics

The Klan became a major political issue during the 1924 election cycle. Democratic divisions over whether the party should condemn the organization reflected the Klan’s strength among important constituencies, particularly in parts of the South and Midwest.

Evans sought to demonstrate that candidates could not ignore Klan voters. Even politicians who disliked the organization had to calculate the electoral consequences of openly attacking it.

Klan Political Strategy

Evans preferred influence rather than creation of a formal Klan political party. Members could remain Democrats or Republicans while supporting candidates who aligned with Klan positions.

This flexibility made the organization harder to isolate. Klan power could appear inside ordinary party structures instead of announcing itself through a separate ballot line.

Women and Klan Politics

The 1920s movement developed affiliated organizations for white Protestant women. Women participated in parades, political campaigns, educational activism, charity, and anti-Catholic or nativist organizing even though the original male Klan structure had excluded them.

Women’s participation extended Klan influence into schools, churches, families, and community reform movements, giving Evans’s political project a broader social base.

Conflict with D. C. Stephenson

Indiana Klan leader D. C. Stephenson built one of the strongest state political organizations in the country and increasingly resisted national control. Evans and Stephenson clashed over money, authority, women’s organizing, and political power.

The conflict exposed a basic weakness in the Klan’s national structure: leaders who controlled large state memberships could become nearly independent political bosses.

The Stephenson Scandal

Stephenson’s 1925 conviction in the kidnapping, rape, and death of Madge Oberholtzer devastated the Klan’s claims to moral guardianship. The scandal received national attention and helped persuade many members that Klan leaders were corrupt or hypocritical.

Stephenson’s crimes were his own and should not be attributed personally to Evans. But the scandal damaged the organization Evans led because it exposed the gap between Klan moral rhetoric and the conduct tolerated within its power structure.

Publicly Discouraging Vigilantism

As Imperial Wizard, Evans increasingly discouraged unauthorized floggings, kidnappings, and other forms of vigilante violence. His motivation included concern that scandals and criminal acts would destroy the political legitimacy he was trying to build.

This policy represented a tactical change rather than a rejection of racial hierarchy. Evans wanted white Protestant supremacy exercised through organized politics, institutions, and controlled social pressure instead of uncontrolled violence that embarrassed national leadership.

The Contradiction of Evans's Own Violence

Evans’s effort to present himself as a responsible opponent of vigilantism was undermined by his personal role in the Alex Johnson assault. He had participated directly in precisely the kind of extralegal racial punishment he later warned could damage the organization.

The contradiction is central to evaluating his later claims that violence represented only irresponsible local misconduct rather than something connected to Klan culture.

The Klan and Law Enforcement

Klan chapters often included police officers, sheriffs, deputies, and other officials. Where law-enforcement personnel belonged to the organization, targeted citizens could reasonably question whether authorities would protect them from Klan intimidation.

This overlap between secret fraternity and public power made the second Klan more dangerous than a purely private social club. Racial hierarchy could be reinforced both formally and informally.

The Klan and Churches

Evans wrapped the organization in Protestant Christianity and sought the support of ministers and congregations. Klan delegations sometimes appeared at churches, donated money, or publicly honored sympathetic clergy.

Such activity helped establish moral legitimacy among white Protestants while simultaneously defining Catholics, Jews, and Black citizens as outside the movement’s preferred national community.

The Klan's Fight for Americanism

Evans’s 1926 essay “The Klan’s Fight for Americanism” is one of the clearest primary statements of his ideology. He argued that authentic Americanism rested on loyalty to the white race, inherited national traditions, and Protestantism.

The essay matters because it strips away later claims that the Klan was simply a generic law-and-order group. Evans himself placed racial supremacy at the center of the movement’s definition of America.

Old-Stock Americans

Evans portrayed the descendants of earlier northern and western European settlers as “old-stock” Americans whose cultural and racial authority was being undermined by immigration, urbanization, religious diversity, and racial change.

The category turned ancestry into a political credential. Citizens could be legally American yet still be treated by Evans as less authentically American because of race, religion, or family origin.

The Politics of Fear

Evans’s speeches frequently portrayed established white Protestant society as a threatened majority being displaced by aggressive minorities, immigrants, political machines, liberal elites, and changing cultural values.

This reversal was politically useful. A movement defending racial and religious privilege could present itself as a victim seeking protection rather than as an organization attempting to preserve unequal power.

Al Smith and the Election of 1928

Evans strongly opposed Democratic presidential nominee Al Smith in 1928. Smith was Catholic, a New Yorker, an opponent of Prohibition, and a symbol of the urban immigrant political culture the Klan had spent years attacking.

Klan propaganda emphasized Smith’s religion and suggested that a Catholic president would be influenced by Rome. The campaign became one of the clearest examples of Evans’s anti-Catholicism entering national presidential politics.

Herbert Hoover

Many Klan voters moved toward Republican Herbert Hoover in 1928 because of opposition to Smith, although Evans later exaggerated the Klan’s responsibility for Hoover’s victory. The electorate was shaped by many issues beyond Klan influence.

The episode nevertheless demonstrated how Evans could mobilize religious prejudice inside a national election without fielding a Klan candidate.

Declining Membership

By the late 1920s the Klan was shrinking rapidly. Internal factionalism, corruption, public scandals, political defeats, financial disputes, and disillusionment caused many members to stop paying dues or participating.

Evans repeatedly predicted renewed growth, but the organization never regained the mass membership that had made it a national political phenomenon earlier in the decade.

The Great Depression

The Great Depression intensified the Klan’s financial problems after 1929. Membership dues and organizational revenue collapsed as millions of Americans faced unemployment, bank failures, and severe economic insecurity.

A movement that had depended on paid membership and elaborate organizational structures could not easily survive when former members lacked both money and enthusiasm.

Franklin D. Roosevelt

Some Klansmen initially supported Franklin D. Roosevelt in 1932, but Evans became a fierce opponent of the New Deal. He portrayed the expanding federal state as a threat to individual freedom, traditional Americanism, and the social authority of the people he considered the country’s authentic stock.

His opposition also increasingly blended anti-government arguments with antisemitic insinuations about Jewish officials and advisers.

Henry Morgenthau Jr. and Antisemitic Politics

Evans singled out Treasury secretary Henry Morgenthau Jr., who was Jewish, while attacking Roosevelt administration policy. His rhetoric suggested that Jewish influence was connected to economic and political changes he opposed.

Although Evans sometimes denied antisemitism, the repeated framing of Jewish officials and communities as alien political influences places his arguments firmly within the antisemitic tradition of the interwar far right.

Anti-Communism

During the 1930s Evans emphasized anti-Communism more heavily. He warned that communist ideas were entering government, labor movements, and American institutions and increasingly connected Klan identity with opposition to organized radicalism.

Anti-Communism gave the shrinking Klan a new language for attacking political enemies after the movement’s older anti-immigrant and Prohibition themes had lost some of their power.

Opposition to Organized Labor

Evans attacked industrial unionism, particularly the Congress of Industrial Organizations, as a source of disorder and radical influence. He portrayed militant labor organization as a threat to property, capitalism, and established American social relations.

This orientation brought the Klan closer to some conservative business interests even while Evans sometimes criticized corporations for importing cheap immigrant labor.

Capitalism and Corporate Power

Evans defended capitalism but accused wealthy corporate elites of undermining American workers by encouraging immigration and prioritizing cheap labor. His economic worldview therefore combined defense of private enterprise with hostility toward particular business practices.

The argument allowed him to present white Protestant nationalism as a defense of ordinary native-born workers against both radical labor and cosmopolitan corporate elites.

Huey Long

Evans also became involved in a highly publicized dispute with Louisiana senator Huey P. Long during the 1930s. Evans considered campaigning against Long as the Louisiana populist contemplated a possible national challenge to Roosevelt.

Long responded with characteristically aggressive ridicule, and Evans abandoned the proposed Louisiana campaign. The episode demonstrated how far Klan power had fallen from the period when politicians had been reluctant to confront its leadership openly.

Business Career in Asphalt

As Klan income declined, Evans became involved in the emulsified-asphalt business used in highway construction. The Texas State Historical Association records that he dealt in highway asphalt while continuing to serve as Imperial Wizard.

His business activities eventually created legal problems and added another chapter to a career already marked by disputes over the relationship between private profit and organizational or political influence.

Georgia Highway Department Connections

Evans became associated with highway-material purchasing and the supply of asphalt to public road projects in Georgia. These business relationships later drew scrutiny from state authorities.

The controversy further damaged the moral image of a man who had spent years claiming that the Klan existed to purify public life and fight corruption.

Antitrust Litigation

Georgia brought litigation alleging price fixing and suppression of competition in the asphalt market. The dispute eventually reached the U.S. Supreme Court in Georgia v. Evans in 1942 on the legal question of whether a state could sue for treble damages under the Sherman Act.

The Supreme Court held that a state could qualify as a “person” entitled to seek damages when injured as a purchaser by an antitrust violation. The ruling addressed federal antitrust law rather than deciding Evans’s broader moral character.

Fraud Proceedings

The Digital Library of Georgia also preserves photographs from 1942 proceedings in which Evans, then a former Imperial Wizard and a purchasing agent connected to the State Highway Department, stood accused of fraud involving highway purchases.

These later legal controversies belong in his biography because they complicate the Klan’s longstanding public claim to represent clean, moral government.

Resignation as Imperial Wizard

Evans resigned as Imperial Wizard in 1939 after approximately seventeen years at the head of the national Klan. By then the organization was only a shadow of the movement that had mobilized millions during the mid-1920s.

James A. Colescott succeeded him and inherited an organization struggling with financial problems, damaged reputation, and shrinking membership.

Sale of the Klan's Atlanta Headquarters

Shortly before leaving national leadership, Evans sold the Klan’s Peachtree Street headquarters property in Atlanta to the Catholic Church. The transaction carried obvious symbolic irony given the organization’s long campaign against Catholic political and religious influence.

It also reflected the Klan’s weakened financial and institutional condition as Evans’s era came to an end.

Marriage and Family

Evans married Bam Hill on February 5, 1923, during the period of his rapid rise to national Klan leadership. The couple had three children.

His private family life received far less public attention than his political activities, and historians know him overwhelmingly through the institutional record of the Klan and his own publications.

The Menace of Modern Immigration

Evans published The Menace of Modern Immigration in 1923, placing immigration restriction near the center of his national political message. The book treated demographic change as a threat to the cultural character of the United States.

It illustrates how the second Klan connected anti-Black white supremacy with a broader campaign against immigrants and religious pluralism.

The Klan of Tomorrow

In The Klan of Tomorrow, published in 1924, Evans argued for a more disciplined and future-oriented movement. He wanted the Klan to become a permanent political and cultural institution rather than a temporary eruption of postwar fear.

The title itself reflected his ambition to modernize the organization and separate its national strategy from the uncontrolled vigilantism that threatened public legitimacy.

Alienism in the Democracy

Evans’s 1927 book Alienism in the Democracy continued his argument that citizenship alone did not make every American equally trustworthy within the national culture. He distinguished between legal membership and the racial-cultural identity he considered authentically American.

This framework turned pluralism itself into a political problem and justified continued suspicion of immigrants, Catholics, Jews, and racial minorities.

The Rising Storm

Evans published The Rising Storm in 1929 as the political power of the Klan was already declining. His continued writing shows that he regarded the movement as part of a larger ideological struggle rather than a temporary fraternal fad.

His books remain important primary sources for understanding the worldview that national Klan leadership wanted members to adopt.

Later Life

After leaving Klan leadership, Evans remained in Atlanta and continued business activity. His public prominence declined sharply compared with the period when newspapers treated him as one of the country’s most powerful movement leaders.

The Klan itself fragmented further during World War II, and the centralized second Klan eventually collapsed before later independent Klan organizations emerged during the postwar civil-rights era.

Death

Evans died in Atlanta in September 1966, during the height of the modern civil-rights revolution. By then the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and Voting Rights Act of 1965 had directly attacked many of the racial arrangements his Klan had sought to defend.

He therefore lived long enough to see federal law move decisively toward a conception of equal citizenship fundamentally opposed to his ideology of white Protestant supremacy.

Who Evans Was Historically

Evans was more than a ceremonial Klan leader. He was the organizer who took control after William Joseph Simmons and attempted to convert an expanding hate fraternity into a national political movement with electoral influence, ideological publications, public demonstrations, and relationships with established politicians.

His significance lies in political institutionalization: he tried to move white supremacy from secret ritual and local vigilantism into mainstream campaigns, public policy, and a national definition of American identity.

How His Opposition to Black People Was Carried Out

Evans opposed Black equality through both direct and institutional means. He personally led the 1921 assault on Alex Johnson, promoted an ideology in which whites were to remain supreme, organized Klan voting power, normalized racial intimidation, and led an organization whose local chapters enforced racial hierarchy through political pressure and extralegal coercion.

His later discouragement of unauthorized violence did not abandon those goals. It sought a more politically effective method for maintaining white control while reducing the scandals that threatened the Klan’s respectability.

Violence and Politics Were Not Opposites

Evans’s career demonstrates that racial violence and respectable politics could reinforce one another. The fear created by the Klan made political organization more powerful, while political influence could protect or normalize local Klansmen.

The organization did not have to choose between the ballot and intimidation. In many communities, the possibility of Klan retaliation formed part of the environment in which elections and public debate occurred.

The Meaning of Respectability

Evans wanted Klan members to look like disciplined citizens defending law, morality, Protestant faith, and national tradition. That presentation attracted middle-class supporters who might have rejected an organization openly defined as a terrorist society.

Respectability therefore expanded rather than necessarily reduced the Klan’s capacity for harm by placing white-supremacist politics inside ordinary institutions.

Comparison with William Joseph Simmons

William Joseph Simmons created the revived Klan’s ritual system and national structure. Evans proved more effective at turning the organization into a political machine and displaced the founder once the movement had become too large for Simmons’s style of leadership.

Simmons was the founder; Evans became the principal political executive of the second Klan’s peak era.

Comparison with D. C. Stephenson

D. C. Stephenson became a state political boss in Indiana and represented the Klan’s capacity to penetrate mainstream government. Evans remained the national leader who attempted to coordinate such state organizations.

Stephenson’s criminal scandal helped destroy the Klan’s moral image, while Evans struggled unsuccessfully to maintain national discipline and distance the organization from such abuses.

Comparison with Robert B. Patterson

Robert “Tut” Patterson later organized the Citizens’ Council movement in Mississippi after Brown v. Board of Education. Patterson preferred open economic pressure by bankers, employers, planters, and professionals and explicitly presented the Council as a respectable alternative to Klan secrecy.

Evans likewise valued respectability, but he remained the leader of a hooded secret fraternity whose history and symbols were inseparable from vigilantism and racial terror.

Comparison with George Wallace

George Wallace later demonstrated how racial backlash could be translated into national electoral appeals emphasizing states’ rights, law and order, and resentment of federal civil-rights enforcement. Evans had pursued an earlier version of that strategy through the Klan.

Both sought to broaden racial politics beyond the Deep South, although Wallace operated as an elected public official while Evans led a private white-supremacist organization.

Was Evans a White Supremacist?

Yes. His own published writing explicitly placed white supremacy at the center of the Klan’s ideology. He did not merely inherit a racist organization whose principles he privately rejected.

Evans argued that racial identity determined authentic Americanism and that white political and cultural supremacy should be preserved.

Was Evans Personally Violent?

Yes, at least in the documented Alex Johnson case. The Texas State Historical Association records that Evans led the Klansmen who abducted Johnson and marked his forehead with acid.

This documented episode distinguishes Evans from leaders whose relationship to local Klan violence is only organizational or ideological.

Did Evans Later Oppose Klan Violence?

He publicly discouraged uncontrolled vigilantism once he became national leader, largely because it damaged the political organization he wanted to build. He preferred disciplined action and often claimed that criminal violence violated Klan principles.

That position represented tactical restraint, not racial moderation. Evans continued promoting white supremacy and exclusion while seeking methods less likely to create legal and public-relations disasters.

Did Evans Make the Klan More Powerful?

Yes, politically. Membership had already expanded dramatically before he took full control, but Evans helped direct the large organization toward elections, lobbying, public demonstrations, national publications, and a more explicit political ideology.

He also inherited severe structural weaknesses—local autonomy, factional ambition, commercialized recruitment, and scandal—that eventually helped destroy the national movement.

Did Evans Create the Klan's Peak Membership?

No single leader deserves sole credit for the second Klan’s enormous growth. Simmons founded the organization, Edward Young Clarke and Elizabeth Tyler professionalized recruitment, local organizers built chapters, and Evans inherited a movement already expanding rapidly.

His distinct contribution was attempting to turn that mass membership into durable political power.

Historical Significance

Hiram Wesley Evans was one of the most important leaders of the second Ku Klux Klan because he presided over its transformation into a national political force. He helped bring Klan influence into elections, public demonstrations, national ideological debate, immigration politics, religious conflict, and mainstream civic institutions.

His career also shows the relationship between overt racial violence and institutional white supremacy. Evans personally participated in the brutal assault on Alex Johnson, then later sought to make the same racial hierarchy sustainable through disciplined politics and respectable public leadership.

Impact on African Americans

For African Americans, Evans’s Klan represented organized opposition to mobility and equal citizenship during the Great Migration and the early development of Black urban political power. The organization treated Black advancement as a threat to white authority and reinforced segregation, discrimination, and intimidation.

Evans’s personal attack on Alex Johnson provides a direct example of that harm. At the national level, his more enduring impact came from legitimizing a mass organization that taught millions of white Americans to interpret Black equality as incompatible with authentic Americanism.

A Balanced Assessment

Evans was an effective organizer, political strategist, writer, and institutional leader who understood that mass movements required disciplined messaging, electoral participation, national symbolism, and relationships with respectable institutions. Those abilities help explain why the second Klan became more than a short-lived southern fraternity.

He used those skills in service of white supremacy, religious bigotry, nativism, and political exclusion. His later attempts to suppress uncontrolled Klan violence were preferable to encouraging more vigilantism, but they were intended to protect a racist political project rather than to recognize equal Black citizenship.

Legacy

Evans’s Klan ultimately failed as a permanent mass organization, but it demonstrated that white supremacy could become a nationwide middle-class political movement rather than remaining confined to southern night riders. Hooded parades in Washington and Klan influence in elections revealed how deeply exclusionary politics could enter mainstream American life.

His longer legacy lies in the political language he helped normalize: claims that racial and religious hierarchy represented patriotism, that dominant groups were actually victims of threatening minorities, and that “Americanism” could be defined by ancestry and race rather than equal citizenship.

Geographic Scope

Ashland, Alabama was Evans’s birthplace, while Hubbard and Dallas, Texas shaped his education, dentistry, and early Klan career. Atlanta, Georgia became the center of his national leadership and later business activity.

Under Evans, Klan influence extended throughout the South, Midwest, West, and parts of the Northeast. Washington, D.C. became the movement’s most powerful symbolic stage when tens of thousands of Klansmen marched publicly through the national capital.

Legal and Constitutional Context

Evans’s career intersected with criminal vigilantism, freedom of association, state election law, voting intimidation, immigration restriction, religious liberty, Prohibition enforcement, federal antitrust law, and the constitutional protections of citizenship targeted indirectly by Klan political power.

The 1925 Senate review of the Earle Mayfield election and the 1942 Supreme Court case Georgia v. Evans demonstrate the unusual range of legal controversies surrounding a movement that tried simultaneously to operate as a secret fraternity, political organization, moral regulator, and business network.
