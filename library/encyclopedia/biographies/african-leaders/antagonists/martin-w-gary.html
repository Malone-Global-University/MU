Martin W. Gary

South Carolina lawyer, Confederate brigadier general, Edgefield political leader, architect of the 1876 Edgefield Plan, and Red Shirt organizer

Overview

Martin Witherspoon Gary (1831-1881) was a South Carolina lawyer, state legislator, Confederate brigadier general, Democratic organizer, Red Shirt leader, and one of the most explicit architects of the campaign that overthrew Reconstruction-era Republican power in South Carolina.

Gary's public career had two principal phases. During the Civil War he rose from captain in Hampton's Legion to command a Confederate cavalry brigade. After the war he returned to Edgefield, resumed law and business, and became an uncompromising opponent of Republican Reconstruction and Black political equality.

His greatest historical significance comes from the election of 1876. Gary developed what became known as the “Edgefield Plan,” a detailed strategy calling for armed Democratic organization, disruption of Republican meetings, intimidation and purchase of Black votes, and the political subordination of African Americans. Red Shirt organizations helped implement that strategy while Wade Hampton III presented the more conciliatory public face of the Democratic campaign.

Family Background

Gary was born to Dr. Thomas Reeder Gary and Mary Ann Porter Gary in Cokesbury, then part of Abbeville District.

He grew up within the social and political culture of the South Carolina upcountry at a time when slavery, plantation agriculture, states' rights, and sectional politics increasingly shaped white public life.

Cokesbury

Cokesbury was the center of Gary's childhood and became important again at the end of his life. The community lay in the old Abbeville District and is now in Greenwood County.

Education

Gary attended local schooling before enrolling at South Carolina College in Columbia.

He later continued his education at Harvard University and graduated in 1854.

South Carolina College

Gary's time at South Carolina College exposed him to an institution that educated many members of the state's political and professional elite.

He did not complete his degree there, instead finishing his education at Harvard.

Harvard University

Gary graduated from Harvard University in 1854.

His northern education is worth noting because his later racial and sectional politics did not develop from complete isolation from northern institutions.

Legal Training

After returning to South Carolina, Gary read law in Edgefield.

He was admitted to the bar and established a legal practice there.

Edgefield as Political Base

Edgefield became the central location of Gary's professional and political life.

The district later became one of the most important centers of violent white Democratic resistance to Reconstruction, producing figures including Gary, Matthew C. Butler, and Benjamin Tillman.

Antebellum Political Environment

Gary entered politics in a state dominated by white slaveholding leadership and increasingly radical sectional debate.

His later campaign against Black citizenship developed from a political culture that had previously excluded African Americans from legal political power through slavery and racial restrictions.

Election to the South Carolina House

Gary was elected to the South Carolina House of Representatives in 1860.

His first period in state office was brief because South Carolina seceded and the Civil War transformed political life almost immediately.

Secession

Gary supported the secessionist cause and committed himself to the Confederate war effort once South Carolina left the Union.

Unlike older figures such as Robert Barnwell Rhett, he was not a decades-long national theorist of disunion; his historical prominence initially came through military service.

Confederate Enlistment

Gary entered Confederate service in June 1861 as a captain in Hampton's Legion.

The Legion had been organized by Wade Hampton III, who later became both Gary's political ally and political rival.

Hampton's Legion

Hampton's Legion was a mixed Confederate command whose components included infantry and cavalry.

Gary began in its infantry and served in the major engagements involving the command during the war.

First Manassas

Gary fought at the First Battle of Manassas in July 1861.

When senior officers of Hampton's Legion were killed, wounded, or disabled, command responsibility temporarily devolved upon Gary, helping establish his early military reputation.

Rise in Rank

Gary rose from captain to field officer as the war continued.

By 1862 he was lieutenant colonel of the infantry element of Hampton's Legion and later became its colonel.

Colonel of Hampton's Legion

Gary's promotion to colonel placed him in sustained command of the Legion's infantry regiment.

His rise reflected actual battlefield experience rather than political status alone.

Virginia Campaigns

Gary and Hampton's Legion participated in campaigning with the Army of Northern Virginia.

The unit saw service in the major eastern-theater operations through the first half of the war.

Maryland Campaign

Gary served during the Maryland Campaign of 1862, including operations around Sharpsburg, or Antietam.

The campaign ended Robert E. Lee's first major invasion of Union territory.

Fredericksburg

Gary's command also served in the Fredericksburg campaign.

By this period he had become an established Confederate field commander rather than the relatively inexperienced captain of 1861.

Transfer West

Hampton's Legion later moved west with Confederate forces associated with James Longstreet's corps.

The transfer brought Gary into the western theater during the campaigns around Chattanooga and East Tennessee.

Chickamauga

Gary and his command participated in the Confederate victory at Chickamauga in September 1863.

The battle was one of the Confederacy's most important western battlefield victories, although it failed to produce a lasting strategic reversal.

East Tennessee

Gary also served during the East Tennessee operations that followed Chickamauga.

The campaigns exposed his command to a different theater before its eventual return east.

Return to Virginia

By 1864 Gary had returned to Virginia as the Confederate military situation deteriorated under sustained Union pressure.

Conversion to Mounted Service

Elements of Hampton's Legion were converted to mounted service in 1864.

This transition helped move Gary from infantry leadership into the cavalry command for which he became known late in the war.

Promotion to Brigadier General

Gary was appointed brigadier general on May 19, 1864.

He received command of a cavalry brigade responsible in part for operations connected with the defense of Richmond.

Gary's Brigade

Gary's cavalry brigade eventually included the 7th South Carolina Cavalry, Hampton Legion cavalry, 7th Georgia Cavalry, and 24th Virginia Cavalry.

National Park Service records list the brigade within the Confederate cavalry order of battle during the Appomattox Campaign.

Defense of Richmond

Gary's brigade served in the military defense of Richmond and surrounding communications during the final year of the war.

The Confederate capital faced increasing pressure as Union armies tightened their operations around Petersburg and central Virginia.

Petersburg Campaign

Gary's mounted troops participated in the broader Petersburg Campaign.

The long struggle gradually exhausted Confederate manpower, transportation, supplies, and strategic mobility.

Army of Northern Virginia Cavalry

By the final campaign Gary's brigade formed part of the cavalry forces attached to the Army of Northern Virginia.

The National Park Service places Gary's brigade in the Appomattox order of battle under Confederate cavalry command.

Evacuation of Richmond

Richmond and Petersburg were evacuated in early April 1865 after Union forces broke the Confederate defensive system.

Gary's command joined the retreat westward.

Appomattox Campaign

Gary's brigade participated in the Appomattox Campaign as Robert E. Lee's army attempted to escape Union forces and reach supplies farther west or south.

Appomattox Court House

On April 9, 1865, Lee agreed to surrender the Army of Northern Virginia to Ulysses S. Grant.

National Park Service records confirm that Gary's brigade remained part of the Confederate order of battle at Appomattox.

Gary and the Surrender

South Carolina historical summaries state that Gary refused to surrender with Lee and left Appomattox with part of his command.

Because some later researchers have questioned details of the traditional narrative surrounding his movements after Appomattox, the safest conclusion is that Gary did not end his Confederate service through the ordinary Appomattox parole process.

Jefferson Davis Tradition

Widely repeated South Carolina accounts state that Gary and roughly two hundred men later joined the fleeing Confederate leadership and assisted Jefferson Davis's party during its movement through the Carolinas.

The broad story forms part of Gary's historical reputation, though precise details of his movements after Appomattox are less firmly documented than his presence in the brigade order of battle itself.

End of Confederate Service

Gary's Confederate service ended with the collapse of organized resistance in the spring of 1865.

He returned to South Carolina carrying the prestige of a brigadier general and veteran of four years of combat.

Military Reputation

Gary's wartime record earned him considerable respect among former Confederate soldiers and white South Carolinians.

That military prestige became one of his most important political assets during Reconstruction.

The 'Bald Eagle of Edgefield'

Gary became known as the “Bald Eagle of Edgefield.”

South Carolina historical summaries describe him as volatile, forceful, and prone to profanity—characteristics that fit the aggressive political style he later developed.

Return to Law

After the Civil War Gary resumed law practice in Edgefield.

He also participated in cotton planting and various business ventures.

Emancipation

The Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery throughout the United States except as punishment for crime.

The end of slavery permanently altered the labor system and racial order that had structured antebellum South Carolina.

Black Citizenship

The Fourteenth Amendment established national citizenship and equal-protection principles, while the Fifteenth Amendment prohibited racial discrimination in voting rights.

These constitutional changes placed Black political participation at the center of South Carolina's Reconstruction struggle.

Reconstruction South Carolina

Because African Americans formed a majority of South Carolina's population, Black male suffrage created the possibility of genuine Black political power.

Black citizens became voters, legislators, judges, local officials, militia members, and participants in the state's constitutional government.

Gary's Opposition to Reconstruction

Gary became an outspoken opponent of Republican Reconstruction.

His opposition extended beyond complaints about taxation or corruption to rejection of the political equality of African Americans.

White Political Supremacy

Gary believed that white South Carolinians should control the state's political institutions.

His surviving campaign plan treated Black voters not as independent citizens whose support should simply be persuaded, but as a population whose political behavior could legitimately be controlled.

Rejection of Black Political Equality

Gary's politics rested on an explicit racial hierarchy.

His 1876 campaign instructions described African Americans as naturally subordinate to white people and treated Black political independence as a threat to the social order he wanted restored.

Reconstruction Republicans

Gary and his allies commonly referred to Republicans as “Radicals.”

In South Carolina, the Republican coalition included formerly enslaved Black citizens, free people of color, white southern Unionists, northern migrants, and other supporters of Reconstruction.

Black Majority Politics

The central strategic problem for white Democrats was numerical: a unified white electorate could not automatically defeat a Republican coalition backed by a large Black voting majority.

Gary's answer was not merely persuasion. It was organized coercion designed to alter turnout and political behavior.

Earlier White Terror

Ku Klux Klan organizations had already used clandestine violence against Black voters and Republican officials during the late 1860s and early 1870s.

Federal Enforcement Acts and prosecutions weakened portions of that underground movement.

From Secret Terror to Open Paramilitary Politics

By the middle of the 1870s, white conservative organization shifted toward more open rifle clubs and Red Shirt groups.

Lowcountry Digital History Initiative materials describe this as a transition from clandestine Klan methods toward overt military-style political intimidation closely connected to the Democratic Party.

Mississippi as a Model

South Carolina Democrats studied the success of white Democrats in Mississippi, where intimidation and violence had helped overthrow Republican political control in 1875.

Gary adapted that experience to South Carolina conditions.

The Edgefield Plan

Gary's strategy became known as the Edgefield Plan, Mississippi Plan, or in some accounts the shotgun policy.

Its goal was to overcome Republican numerical strength by combining white political unity, armed organization, disruption of opposition meetings, pressure on Black voters, and aggressive control of election-day conditions.

A Written Plan

Gary did not leave historians merely indirect evidence of his intentions.

His surviving “Plan of the Campaign of 1876” provides direct documentation of the tactics he wanted Democrats to use.

Controlling Black Votes

The plan instructed each Democrat to take responsibility for controlling at least one Black vote.

The methods Gary listed included intimidation, purchase, or keeping the voter away from the polls.

Armed Attendance at Republican Meetings

Gary instructed Democrats to attend Republican meetings in large numbers and to go armed.

The purpose was not neutral observation: Democrats were to confront Republican speakers and seize opportunities to address Black audiences themselves.

Disrupting Republican Speech

The Edgefield Plan encouraged Democrats to challenge Republican speakers publicly and undermine their credibility before Black voters.

This strategy made free political assembly difficult even when a meeting did not end in direct violence.

Fear as a Political Instrument

Gary explicitly treated fear as a legitimate instrument of political persuasion.

His plan assumed that African American political behavior could be changed through intimidation rather than ordinary democratic argument.

Racial Subordination

Gary's written instructions stated that Black people should be shown that their proper social position was subordinate to white people.

This makes the racial objective of the campaign unusually explicit.

Black Democratic Clubs

Gary's plan also proposed organizing—or in some cases pretending to have organized—Black Democratic clubs.

The tactic could create the appearance of a larger African American movement toward the Democrats than actually existed.

Political Theater

The plan called for disciplined processions, banners, club organization, and displays designed to create an imposing spectacle.

Political theater and physical intimidation therefore worked together.

Rifle Clubs

Local rifle clubs provided an existing organizational base for Democratic paramilitary activity.

Many of these organizations evolved into or cooperated with Red Shirt clubs during the 1876 campaign.

Red Shirts

Red Shirts became the most visible armed Democratic force in South Carolina politics during 1876.

Members rode in groups, displayed weapons, attended rallies, disrupted Republican gatherings, and projected the ability to use violence if political opponents resisted.

Gary as Red Shirt Organizer

South Carolina Public Radio's historical summary identifies Gary as the organizer of white Democratic clubs known as the Red Shirts to implement his plan.

His role was therefore strategic as well as rhetorical.

Scale of Paramilitary Organization

University of South Carolina historical research reports that by the end of the 1876 campaign approximately 290 rifle or Red Shirt clubs existed, involving roughly 18,000 members.

The scale demonstrates that white paramilitary activity was not merely a collection of isolated local incidents.

Matthew C. Butler

Gary worked closely with fellow Edgefield Confederate veteran Matthew C. Butler.

University of South Carolina research identifies the two men as principal leaders of the coercive component of the Democratic campaign.

Wade Hampton III

Democrats nominated former Confederate lieutenant general Wade Hampton III for governor.

Hampton possessed statewide prestige and presented a public message of conservative order, reconciliation, and efforts to win some Black Democratic support.

Two Sides of the Hampton Campaign

The Hampton campaign combined two approaches that were sometimes in tension.

Hampton publicly condemned violence and cultivated an image of paternalistic moderation, while Gary and Butler developed and implemented intimidation aimed at reducing Republican Black voting strength.

Gary's View of Hampton's Moderation

Gary regarded Hampton's attempts to win substantial Black support as unrealistic.

He believed coercive control of voting behavior was more effective than appeals based on persuasion alone.

Hampton's Dependence on the Red Shirts

Whatever Hampton's personal objections to uncontrolled violence, Red Shirt organizations openly supported his candidacy and accompanied Democratic campaigning across the state.

The intimidation associated with those organizations materially benefited the Democratic ticket.

Hamburg Massacre

The July 1876 Hamburg Massacre became one of the defining acts of racial violence of the campaign.

Armed whites attacked a Black militia company, and captured African American militiamen were later executed.

Gary and Hamburg

Matthew C. Butler played the more direct documented leadership role in the Hamburg confrontation itself.

Gary's importance lay in converting the wider political lesson of such armed mobilization into a systematic statewide campaign strategy rather than in being identified as the individual commander of the Hamburg killings.

The Red Shirt Symbol

The Hamburg aftermath helped popularize the red shirt as a Democratic political symbol.

As the campaign expanded, that symbol came to represent militant white Democratic solidarity.

Ellenton Violence

Political and racial violence also erupted around Ellenton and other parts of the state during the campaign.

Such incidents reinforced the atmosphere of danger surrounding Black Republican political participation.

Cainhoy

Armed political conflict at Cainhoy near Charleston showed that violence could also occur when Black Republicans resisted white Democratic intervention.

The broader pattern was a statewide political environment in which armed organizations increasingly replaced ordinary electoral competition.

Economic Coercion

Gary's strategy did not depend only on guns.

Employers, landlords, merchants, creditors, and other economically powerful white Democrats could pressure Black workers and tenants through threats involving jobs, housing, credit, or access to local resources.

Voter Purchase

Gary's own written plan contemplated purchase of votes alongside intimidation.

This demonstrates that his strategy included both coercive and corrupt electoral methods.

Keeping Voters Away

Preventing Republican voters from reaching the polls could be as politically effective as forcing them to vote Democratic.

The Edgefield Plan explicitly treated nonparticipation produced by pressure as a desirable outcome.

Election Day Strategy

Armed Democratic organizations sought to dominate the physical environment surrounding polling places.

Even without a shooting at every poll, the visible presence of organized armed men could change whether citizens felt safe enough to vote.

The Election of 1876

South Carolina's November 1876 election became one of the most disputed elections in the state's history.

Wade Hampton and Republican governor Daniel H. Chamberlain both claimed victory.

Edgefield County Returns

Edgefield County became central to the dispute.

Reported Democratic voting totals were extraordinarily high, and Republican leaders charged both fraud and suppression of Black voting.

Laurens County Returns

Laurens County produced similarly contested results.

The returns from Edgefield and Laurens became critical because Hampton's statewide claimed margin was narrow.

Fraud and Violence as Separate Issues

Historical evaluation should distinguish ballot fraud from voter intimidation.

Both could affect an election, but armed coercion attacked the citizen's ability to participate freely even before ballots were counted.

Gary's Election to the State Senate

Gary was elected to the South Carolina Senate from Edgefield during the same 1876 political transformation.

His victory returned him to formal state office after years of political organization outside government.

Lawrence Cain

Gary succeeded Lawrence Cain, an African American Republican who had served in the South Carolina House and Senate and studied law at the integrated University of South Carolina.

The transition from Cain to Gary symbolized the broader transfer of political authority from Reconstruction-era Black Republican leadership to white Democratic control.

Black Political Losses

The Democratic victory did not immediately eliminate every Black officeholder or voter.

It did, however, fundamentally change the direction of state power and begin a sustained contraction of Republican and African American political influence.

Two Rival Governments

After the election, Hampton and Chamberlain maintained competing claims to the governorship.

The state crisis unfolded simultaneously with the disputed presidential contest between Rutherford B. Hayes and Samuel J. Tilden.

Federal Troops

Federal troops remained an important factor because Republican leaders depended on federal authority to prevent armed Democrats from simply taking physical control of government institutions.

April 1877

In April 1877 federal troops were withdrawn from their political support role in Columbia.

Chamberlain abandoned his claim, and Hampton gained undisputed control of the state government.

End of Reconstruction in South Carolina

Hampton's assumption of undisputed power is generally treated as the effective end of Reconstruction government in South Carolina.

Gary's Edgefield strategy had been one of the principal forces making that political transformation possible.

Meaning of 'Redemption'

White Democrats described their victory as the “Redemption” of South Carolina from Republican misgovernment.

For Black Republicans, the same transition meant the destruction of the most substantial experiment in biracial democratic government South Carolina had yet experienced.

Gary Versus Hampton

The alliance between Gary and Hampton did not survive the Democratic victory.

Once white Democrats controlled the state, differences over patronage, racial policy, public finance, and political leadership became increasingly visible.

Hampton's Magnanimity Policy

Hampton sought to preserve a public reputation for relative fairness toward Black South Carolinians and appointed some African Americans to government positions.

Gary opposed what South Carolina historical summaries describe as Hampton's policy of magnanimity toward Black citizens.

Gary's Harder Racial Politics

Gary favored a more explicit restoration of white political control and had little patience with Hampton's attempts to cultivate an enduring Black Democratic constituency.

The disagreement was tactical as well as ideological.

State Debt

Gary also opposed payment of portions of South Carolina's Reconstruction-era public debt.

Debt policy became a major conflict among Democrats after they regained control.

Public School Taxation

South Carolina historical summaries state that Gary opposed tax funding for public schools during his later political career.

This position placed him at odds with parts of Hampton's more institutionally conservative program.

Public Education Context

Reconstruction had expanded public education as a state responsibility, including schooling for African American children.

Debates over taxation therefore involved both fiscal conservatism and the future structure of the post-emancipation public order.

U.S. Senate Ambition

Gary sought election to the United States Senate after Democratic restoration.

He failed to obtain the seat, and the contests deepened his hostility toward Hampton and the governor's allies.

Failure to Reach the U.S. Senate

Gary's military prestige and role in the 1876 campaign did not translate into the national office he wanted.

His inability to reach the Senate became one of the major frustrations of his final political years.

Break with Wade Hampton

Gary and Hampton became political enemies after having served as Confederate comrades and partners in the 1876 Democratic victory.

The break demonstrates that the Redeemer coalition contained significant internal divisions once the common Republican opponent had been defeated.

State Senate Career

Gary remained in the South Carolina Senate through the end of his life.

His legislative career was much shorter and less consequential than his role as political strategist in 1876.

The Limits of Gary's Political Success

Gary succeeded dramatically at helping overthrow Reconstruction government but failed to become South Carolina's dominant post-Reconstruction statesman.

Hampton and other conservatives retained greater institutional influence.

Relationship to Benjamin Tillman

Gary died before Benjamin Tillman rose to statewide dominance.

Yet Edgefield's later populist white-supremacist politics developed within a political order that Gary had helped create by destroying Republican power and legitimizing aggressive racial mobilization.

Paramilitary Politics After 1876

The immediate Red Shirt campaign gradually gave way to more formal mechanisms of Democratic control.

Election administration, polling rules, economic pressure, fraud, and eventually constitutional disfranchisement reduced the need for constant open paramilitary mobilization.

From Violence to Institutional Control

Gary's career therefore belongs to a transitional moment.

Armed coercion helped seize political power; later Democratic governments could use state institutions themselves to entrench that power.

Connection to Jim Crow

Gary died before the full Jim Crow system and the 1895 South Carolina disfranchising constitution.

Nevertheless, the political defeat of Reconstruction that he helped engineer created essential conditions for those later developments.

Not the Sole Architect of Redemption

Gary did not overthrow Reconstruction alone.

Matthew C. Butler, Wade Hampton III, local rifle clubs, Red Shirt leaders, Democratic committees, economic elites, newspaper editors, and thousands of white voters all contributed to the campaign.

Gary's Distinctive Role

What distinguished Gary was the explicitness and organization of his strategy.

His surviving written plan provides unusually direct evidence that intimidation and racial subordination were deliberate political tools rather than accidental by-products of an otherwise ordinary campaign.

Was Gary a Confederate Major General?

No. Reliable modern historical sources identify Gary's final Confederate rank as brigadier general.

Some local commemorative materials have described him as a major general, but this biography follows the documented brigadier-general rank.

Was Gary a Fire-Eater?

Gary was a secessionist and Confederate officer, but he is less accurately classified among the classic antebellum Fire-Eaters whose national fame came from decades of disunion agitation.

His principal importance arose first from military service and then from Reconstruction-era white-supremacist political organization.

Was Gary a Ku Klux Klan Leader?

The strongest sources used for this biography establish Gary's leadership of rifle clubs and Red Shirt politics rather than formal command of the earlier Ku Klux Klan.

Assigning unsupported Klan membership is unnecessary because his documented Red Shirt program is itself extensive and explicit.

Was the Edgefield Plan Only About Persuasion?

No. Gary included political argument and attempts to recruit Black Democrats, but his written instructions expressly incorporated intimidation, vote purchase, voter exclusion, armed attendance at opposition meetings, and racial subordination.

Did Gary Personally Commit Every Act of 1876 Violence?

No evidence supports attributing every Red Shirt assault or killing personally to Gary.

His responsibility is better described as strategic and organizational: he designed and promoted a system in which intimidation and the credible threat of violence were deliberate campaign instruments.

Did Hampton Approve Everything Gary Proposed?

No. Hampton publicly rejected uncontrolled violence and disagreed with Gary's contempt for efforts to win Black votes.

That disagreement does not erase the fact that Gary's Red Shirt organizations supported Hampton's candidacy and helped create the conditions for Democratic victory.

Did Gary Accept Black Political Equality?

No. His surviving plan explicitly envisioned African Americans as subordinate to white political leadership.

His objection was not simply to Republican corruption or taxation; it extended to independent Black political power itself.

Was Every Black Democratic Vote Coerced?

No. Some African Americans genuinely supported Hampton or local Democratic candidates for reasons including personal relationships, economic interests, dissatisfaction with Republicans, or hopes for peace.

Gary's plan nevertheless makes clear that Democrats also intended to manufacture or compel Black support through intimidation, purchase, and exclusion.

Was the 1876 Election Only About Race?

No. Public debt, taxation, corruption, economic depression, local government, and party patronage also mattered.

Race was nevertheless fundamental because Republican electoral strength depended heavily on newly enfranchised Black citizens and Gary's strategy specifically targeted their political independence.

Death

Martin W. Gary died on April 9, 1881, at Cokesbury, South Carolina.

He was fifty years old.

Burial

Gary was buried in Tabernacle Cemetery at Cokesbury.

Oakley Park and Memory

Gary became closely associated with Oakley Park in Edgefield, later remembered locally as an important site connected with Red Shirt organization.

The preservation of Gary-related sites illustrates how white southern memory long celebrated the Redeemer movement as heroic rather than examining its effects on Black citizenship.

Confederate Memory

Gary's reputation was also shaped by Confederate commemoration.

His military courage, Appomattox defiance tradition, and nickname often overshadowed his Reconstruction political program in older memorial narratives.

Redemption Memory

For white Democrats, Gary could be remembered as a defender of “home rule” who helped restore local government after Reconstruction.

For African American political history, that same restoration represented the use of organized coercion to break Black electoral power.

Modern Reassessment

Modern scholarship places Gary's own campaign document at the center of interpretation.

Because his instructions survive, historians need not infer whether intimidation was merely accidental: the plan itself identifies coercion as a method of political control.

Comparison with Wade Hampton III

Hampton was the public candidate and symbol of Democratic Redemption; Gary was one of the principal architects of its coercive machinery.

Hampton sought a paternalistic image of reconciliation and some Black support. Gary considered that approach insufficient and relied more explicitly on intimidation and white solidarity.

Comparison with Matthew C. Butler

Butler and Gary were fellow Edgefield lawyers, Confederate veterans, and key organizers of the 1876 campaign.

Butler played a direct documented role in the escalation at Hamburg, while Gary left the clearest surviving written blueprint for the broader statewide coercive strategy.

Comparison with Robert Barnwell Rhett

Rhett helped normalize secession before the Civil War. Gary helped normalize paramilitary suppression of Black Republican politics after the war.

Their careers therefore represent different stages in the defense of white southern political power.

Comparison with Benjamin Tillman

Tillman rose after Gary's death and attacked the old planter establishment while embracing an even more openly demagogic white-supremacist politics.

Gary's Edgefield Plan helped establish the political environment in which later white-only Democratic competition could replace biracial Republican-Democratic competition.

Comparison with Lawrence Cain

Lawrence Cain represented the political possibilities created by Reconstruction: a formerly enslaved African American could become educated, practice law, and serve in the state senate.

Gary's 1876 victory over Cain symbolized the reversal of that transformation as armed white Democratic organization recovered control of Edgefield politics.

Historical Significance

Gary is historically significant because his career provides unusually clear evidence of intentional voter suppression during the overthrow of Reconstruction.

His “Plan of the Campaign of 1876” documented a strategy for converting white demographic minority status in South Carolina into political power through organization, intimidation, fraud, economic pressure, and control of Black voting behavior.

Impact on African Americans

The political system Gary helped destroy had serious weaknesses and corruption, but it also represented an unprecedented expansion of African American citizenship.

Black men voted, held office, attended integrated public institutions, served in militias, and exercised direct influence over state government. Gary's campaign helped sharply reduce that independence and restore government to white Democratic control.

A Balanced Assessment

Gary was educated at Harvard, developed a successful legal career, displayed courage and competence during four years of Confederate military service, rose to brigadier general, and possessed substantial organizational and political skill.

Those abilities made his Reconstruction role more consequential. He deliberately applied military-style organization to electoral politics, rejected independent Black political equality, and authored a campaign program that treated intimidation and exclusion as legitimate means of restoring white Democratic rule.

Legacy

Gary's legacy is inseparable from the end of Reconstruction in South Carolina. He was not merely a beneficiary of spontaneous racial violence; he was a strategist who sought to coordinate coercion toward a defined political result.

His surviving plan is therefore one of the clearest primary documents for understanding how white “Redemption” could operate: electoral politics, racial ideology, armed organization, fraud, economic pressure, and violence were not separate stories but mutually reinforcing parts of the same campaign.

Geographic Scope

Cokesbury was Gary's birthplace and the setting of his final years. Columbia was the location of South Carolina College and the state government.

Edgefield became his legal and political base and gave its name to the Edgefield Plan. His Confederate service took him through Virginia, Maryland, Pennsylvania, Tennessee, Georgia, and North Carolina.

Appomattox marked the collapse of Lee's army, while the postwar political struggle centered especially on Edgefield, Hamburg, Ellenton, Charleston, and Columbia.

Legal and Constitutional Context

Gary's career crossed the transition from a slaveholding republic to constitutional emancipation, Black citizenship, and Black male suffrage.

The Thirteenth, Fourteenth, and Fifteenth Amendments fundamentally changed the legal status of African Americans. Gary's 1876 strategy sought to defeat the practical political consequences of those constitutional changes without formally restoring slavery.
