William Joseph Simmons

Alabama-born preacher and fraternal organizer who founded the second Ku Klux Klan, created its ritual system, and helped turn white Protestant supremacy into a national mass movement

Overview

William Joseph Simmons (1880-1945) was an Alabama-born Methodist preacher, fraternal organizer, professional recruiter, and founder of the second Ku Klux Klan. In 1915 he revived the name and mythology of the Reconstruction-era Klan, created a new ritual system and hooded uniform, obtained a Georgia charter, and led a ceremonial cross burning on Stone Mountain that announced the rebirth of the organization. He then declared himself Imperial Wizard of what he called the “Invisible Empire.”

Simmons’s revived Klan was built around white Protestant supremacy. It excluded Black people, Catholics, Jews, immigrants, and other groups from its conception of full American belonging and presented native-born white Protestant men as the proper guardians of the nation. Simmons initially struggled to build membership, but in 1920 he hired publicity specialists Edward Young Clarke and Elizabeth Tyler, who created a commission-based national recruiting system. Within a few years the Klan grew from a small southern fraternity into a mass organization with hundreds of thousands and eventually millions of members, substantial political influence, and local chapters across the United States.

His opposition to Black people was carried out primarily by creating and legitimizing the organization through which others could intimidate, exclude, and politically subordinate them. The second Klan promoted white supremacy, racial separation, and the defense of white Protestant control, while local Klans participated in threats, kidnappings, beatings, economic pressure, political intimidation, and other forms of vigilantism. Simmons publicly denied that violence represented the organization’s purpose and defended the Klan before Congress as a patriotic Christian fraternity, but the movement he created helped make organized racial coercion nationally respectable. He lost operational control to Hiram Wesley Evans in 1922 and never regained his former influence.

Not the Founder of the Original Ku Klux Klan

Simmons did not create the first Ku Klux Klan. The original Klan emerged in Tennessee after the Civil War and became a violent counterrevolutionary movement devoted to overturning Reconstruction and suppressing Black citizenship. Federal prosecutions and political change weakened that first movement by the early 1870s.

Simmons’s importance came half a century later. He resurrected the Klan name, adapted its mythology to the twentieth century, and built a new organization whose membership and political reach became much larger than the fragmented Reconstruction-era secret society.

Birth in Harpersville, Alabama

Simmons was born in Harpersville, Alabama, in 1880 and grew up in the post-Reconstruction South. The region’s white political culture increasingly romanticized the Confederacy and the first Klan while Black citizens faced disfranchisement, segregation, and racial violence.

That historical environment mattered because Simmons later treated the original Klan not as a terrorist organization that had attacked Black voters and Reconstruction governments, but as a heroic model of white southern self-defense.

Religious Background

Simmons entered religious work through the Methodist Episcopal Church, South. He preached and taught for a period but did not establish a lasting successful ministry, and biographical accounts describe his church career as troubled before he moved more fully into fraternal organizing.

Religious language nevertheless remained central to his later Klan leadership. He repeatedly presented the organization as Christian, surrounded its ceremonies with Bibles and Protestant symbolism, and fused white supremacy with a claim to moral and religious legitimacy.

Fraternal Organizations

Before founding the Klan, Simmons became deeply involved in the flourishing world of American fraternal societies. He belonged to numerous orders and worked as a recruiter for the Woodmen of the World, learning how secret rituals, special titles, initiation ceremonies, insurance benefits, and local lodges could bind members together.

Those experiences were essential to the Klan he later created. The second Klan was not simply a political club; it was constructed as a secret fraternity with elaborate ceremonies, passwords, ranks, uniforms, and a hierarchy that made membership feel like entry into an exclusive brotherhood.

Professional Organizing Experience

Simmons also learned that fraternal organizing could be a profession. Recruitment required public speaking, personal salesmanship, local contacts, ceremonial excitement, and constant creation of new chapters. He later applied those same skills to the Ku Klux Klan.

His greatest talent was not original political theory. It was combining racial mythology, fraternal ritual, patriotic language, and organizational structure into a product that other recruiters could sell.

Atlanta and the Search for a New Order

By the 1910s Simmons was living and organizing in Atlanta, a rapidly growing southern city where Confederate memory, industrial expansion, immigration anxieties, racial segregation, and fraternal culture intersected. He became fascinated with the possibility of creating a new order modeled on the legendary image of the Reconstruction Klan.

Atlanta offered an ideal setting because it was both a modern city and a center of Lost Cause commemoration. Simmons could present his new movement as simultaneously ancient, southern, patriotic, and modern.

Thomas Dixon and the Romanticized Klan

Popular novels by Thomas Dixon portrayed Reconstruction as a period of Black misrule and represented the Ku Klux Klan as heroic white rescuers. These works helped reshape public memory of the original Klan by obscuring its campaign of racial terror.

Simmons inherited that mythology. Instead of beginning from the historical record of attacks on Black voters and officeholders, he began from a fictional tradition that celebrated the Klan as a defender of white civilization.

The Birth of a Nation

D. W. Griffith’s 1915 film The Birth of a Nation, adapted from Dixon’s writing, gave that mythology unprecedented visual power. The film depicted Black political participation during Reconstruction as dangerous and portrayed hooded Klansmen as saviors of the South.

New Georgia Encyclopedia identifies the film as a major inspiration for Simmons. Its enormous popularity demonstrated that a romanticized Klan could attract audiences far beyond traditional Confederate circles.

The Leo Frank Case

The 1913 conviction and 1915 lynching of Jewish factory superintendent Leo Frank also shaped the environment in which Simmons revived the Klan. After Georgia governor John Slaton commuted Frank’s death sentence, an organized group of armed men abducted Frank from prison and lynched him near Marietta.

New Georgia Encyclopedia identifies the antisemitism surrounding the Frank case, together with the racism encouraged by The Birth of a Nation, as major influences on Simmons’s decision to establish the new Klan.

Knights of Mary Phagan

Some men involved in Frank’s lynching organized themselves as the Knights of Mary Phagan. Several participants in Simmons’s later Stone Mountain ceremony were connected to that circle, linking the new Klan symbolically to the extrajudicial killing that had electrified white Georgia.

The connection demonstrates that the second Klan emerged from more than abstract historical nostalgia. Its founding environment included living networks willing to use racial and religious vigilantism outside formal law.

Securing a Georgia Charter

Simmons incorporated the new Knights of the Ku Klux Klan under Georgia law, giving the organization a formal legal identity. This was one of the important differences between a mere secret club and a durable institution capable of owning property, collecting dues, issuing charters, and establishing subordinate chapters.

Legal incorporation also helped Simmons present the Klan as a legitimate fraternal association rather than an outlaw conspiracy, even though its name deliberately invoked the violent Reconstruction organization.

Designing the Uniform

New Georgia Encyclopedia credits Simmons with designing the second Klan’s hooded uniform. White robes and pointed hoods became among the most recognizable visual symbols of American white supremacy during the twentieth century.

The uniform served several purposes at once: it evoked the fictional Klan celebrated in popular culture, created dramatic public spectacle, erased individual identity inside the group, and gave members the psychological power of appearing as part of a larger anonymous force.

Creating the Ritual System

Simmons composed elaborate rituals, invented titles, and developed a ceremonial vocabulary for the new order. Members entered local “Klaverns,” organizers became “Kleagles,” and national officers received grandiose titles designed to make the movement seem mysterious and historic.

This ritualism was not superficial decoration. It helped transform prejudice into membership identity. A recruit was no longer simply a white man who opposed Black equality; he became part of an exclusive “Invisible Empire” with duties, oaths, symbols, and fellow initiates.

Stone Mountain

Stone Mountain outside Atlanta became the symbolic birthplace of Simmons’s revived Klan. The mountain was already associated with Confederate memory and later became the site of the enormous carving depicting Jefferson Davis, Robert E. Lee, and Stonewall Jackson.

Using the mountain linked the organization visually and emotionally to the Lost Cause. Simmons presented the ceremony as a rebirth of white southern guardianship on a landscape increasingly devoted to Confederate commemoration.

Thanksgiving Night, 1915

On Thanksgiving evening, November 25, 1915, Simmons and sixteen other members climbed Stone Mountain and formally proclaimed the revived Knights of the Ku Klux Klan. New Georgia Encyclopedia records that several of the men were also associated with the Knights of Mary Phagan.

The ceremony was deliberately theatrical. Simmons wanted a founding story powerful enough to be repeated to future recruits as the sacred origin of a new national brotherhood.

The Burning Cross

At Stone Mountain the founders ignited a flaming cross, combining Christian imagery with the racial mythology popularized by The Birth of a Nation. Cross burning became a defining symbol of the twentieth-century Klan and later a method of intimidation directed at Black families, civil-rights activists, religious minorities, and others.

Simmons presented the cross as a sacred emblem rather than a symbol of terror. In practice, the Klan’s use of burning crosses converted a Christian symbol into a warning that organized white supremacists were present and watching.

Declaring an Invisible Empire

Simmons called the organization the Invisible Empire of the Knights of the Ku Klux Klan and styled himself Imperial Wizard. The language elevated a newly created fraternity into an imagined sovereign order with authority extending across state lines.

That grandiosity helped recruitment. Members could believe they were joining something larger than a local political club—a hidden nationwide brotherhood charged with determining who truly belonged in America.

Who Could Join

The new Klan initially restricted membership to white, native-born Protestant men. New Georgia Encyclopedia identifies those requirements explicitly. The membership rule made racial, religious, and national-origin exclusion part of the organization’s basic structure before any local chapter took further action.

Black Americans were excluded by race; Catholics and Jews by religion; many immigrants by national origin. Simmons therefore built hierarchy directly into the definition of membership.

White Protestant Nationalism

Simmons’s Klan presented native-born white Protestant men as the authentic core of the American nation. It treated their cultural dominance as something requiring organized defense against racial equality, immigration, Catholic influence, Jewish communities, and radical politics.

This ideology made anti-Black racism part of a larger politics of exclusion. The Klan promised members that many forms of social change could be explained as threats to a supposedly endangered Protestant America.

Opposition to Black Equality

For African Americans, the second Klan represented a renewed organization explicitly committed to white supremacy. It opposed the expansion of Black social, political, and economic equality and celebrated an earlier Klan remembered precisely for defeating Black political power during Reconstruction.

Simmons did not need to publish a detailed legislative program denying each individual right. The organization’s identity itself taught members that white racial control was legitimate and that challenges to that control were threats to the nation.

The Great Migration

During and after World War I, large numbers of African Americans moved from the rural South to northern and midwestern cities. The Great Migration altered labor markets, neighborhoods, schools, elections, and public culture in communities where many white residents had never before confronted significant Black political or economic competition.

The national Klan later exploited these tensions. White supremacy was no longer presented only as a southern issue; it could be marketed to northern and western whites anxious about demographic change.

World War I and 'Americanism'

World War I intensified demands for cultural conformity and suspicion of people portrayed as insufficiently American. Simmons’s Klan wrapped itself in flags, military language, Protestantism, and patriotic ceremony and claimed to represent uncompromising loyalty to the United States.

This “Americanism” was racially and religiously exclusive. It treated the cultural identity of one group—native-born white Protestants—as the standard by which everyone else’s loyalty was judged.

Slow Early Growth

Despite the dramatic Stone Mountain founding, the Klan initially grew slowly. During its first several years it remained largely concentrated in parts of Georgia and Alabama and numbered only thousands rather than the millions later associated with the 1920s movement.

Simmons had created a compelling ritual package but lacked the organizational machinery required for national mass recruitment.

The Need for Professional Publicity

By 1920 Simmons recognized that the Klan needed a different recruitment model. He turned to Edward Young Clarke and Elizabeth Tyler, professional fundraisers and publicity entrepreneurs associated with the Southern Publicity Association.

The partnership transformed the movement. Clarke and Tyler treated recruitment as a sales operation, creating paid organizers with direct financial incentives to sign new members.

Edward Young Clarke

Clarke brought experience in promotion, fundraising, and fraternal organizing. Under the publicity arrangement, he helped divide the country into organizational territories and built a hierarchy of professional recruiters.

His role is essential because Simmons’s small regional fraternity became a national mass movement only after this commercialization of recruitment.

Elizabeth Tyler

Elizabeth Tyler was an unusually powerful woman within the otherwise male-centered Klan world. She co-ran the publicity operation, helped shape the movement’s national marketing, and became one of the most important architects of its rapid expansion.

Her involvement also illustrates the difference between official Klan membership and Klan power. Women initially could not join Simmons’s male order, yet Tyler exercised enormous influence over how it was sold to the public.

The Ten-Dollar Initiation Fee

New recruits generally paid a ten-dollar initiation fee. The publicity and recruiting hierarchy divided the money among local recruiters, higher organizers, Clarke and Tyler’s operation, and Simmons.

This commission system gave recruiters a direct economic incentive to find new members. Racial and religious prejudice became not only an ideology but a profitable sales product.

Kleagles

Paid Klan recruiters were known as Kleagles. They entered communities, identified local grievances, organized meetings, and emphasized whichever fears were likely to produce the strongest response among native-born white Protestants.

In one community they might stress Black migration; in another Catholic schools, immigrants, labor radicalism, bootlegging, or political corruption. The message was flexible, while the organization’s white Protestant identity remained constant.

Targeted Recruitment

The professional recruiting system helped the Klan adapt to different parts of the country. Simmons’s original southern racial mythology could be combined with local resentments, allowing the movement to become influential in states far removed from the former Confederacy.

This flexibility explains why the second Klan cannot be understood solely as a southern organization. It turned white supremacy into one element of a nationwide movement of Protestant nativism.

Anti-Catholicism

Under the expanded recruitment campaign, anti-Catholicism became one of the second Klan’s central themes. Klansmen portrayed Catholics as politically controlled by the pope and therefore supposedly incapable of undivided loyalty to the United States.

The Klan attacked Catholic schools, religious institutions, and political influence. This broadened the organization’s hate beyond race while reinforcing its claim that only a particular kind of Protestant identity represented authentic American citizenship.

Antisemitism

The revived Klan also promoted antisemitism, drawing on prejudices already intensified in Georgia by the Leo Frank case. Jewish Americans were portrayed as alien, financially threatening, culturally subversive, or insufficiently assimilated.

Although anti-Black racism remained fundamental to Klan identity, antisemitism helped make the second Klan a broader movement of ethnoreligious exclusion.

Anti-Immigrant Politics

The organization advocated immigration restriction and hostility toward newcomers from southern and eastern Europe. These immigrants were often Catholic or Jewish and were portrayed as changing the racial, religious, and political character of the United States.

The Klan’s nativism connected local racial hierarchy to federal immigration politics, making Simmons’s organization attractive to white Protestants who might never have viewed Reconstruction memory as personally important.

Prohibition and Moral Regulation

The Klan also promoted Prohibition, public morality, traditional family life, and stricter law enforcement. Recruiters denounced gambling, bootlegging, sexual behavior they considered immoral, and political corruption.

These issues gave the Klan a respectable reform language capable of attracting people who did not initially think of themselves as joining a hate movement. Moral reform and racial exclusion were packaged together as a single defense of community standards.

Public Schools

The second Klan often claimed to support public education. In practice, this position could be directed against Catholic parochial schools and used to strengthen a Protestant civic culture.

The Klan’s educational politics therefore illustrate its broader pattern: apparently universal civic causes could be mobilized selectively against religious and ethnic minorities.

Rapid National Expansion

After Clarke and Tyler professionalized recruitment, Klan membership increased dramatically. Contemporary estimates vary because the organization was secretive and often exaggerated its own strength, but by the early 1920s it had hundreds of thousands of members and was moving rapidly toward the multi-million-member peak reached after Simmons lost control.

The key historical fact is the scale of transformation. Simmons’s obscure 1915 fraternity became one of the largest political and social movements in the United States.

The Klan as a Social Organization

Local Klans held parades, picnics, church appearances, initiations, charity events, and large public ceremonies. These activities allowed the organization to become woven into ordinary white community life rather than existing only as a clandestine band.

Social normalization increased its power. A movement that included neighbors, ministers, police officers, merchants, and elected officials could make white supremacy appear like mainstream civic participation.

Political Influence

As membership grew, Klan chapters entered local and state politics. Candidates sought Klan endorsements, members ran for office, and organized voting blocs could influence primaries and municipal elections.

New Georgia Encyclopedia notes that by the 1920s Klan influence reached state governments across several regions. Much of that political peak came under Hiram Evans, but Simmons created the organization that made such power possible.

How the Klan Opposed Black Political Power

In communities where Black citizens gained voting strength or economic visibility, the Klan’s white-supremacist message legitimized efforts to preserve white political control. Local intimidation, social pressure, and political organization could discourage Black participation while mobilizing white voters around racial solidarity.

The exact methods varied by locality, so not every act should be attributed personally to Simmons. His direct responsibility was for building and promoting the national institution that encouraged members to see Black equality as a danger requiring organized action.

Vigilantism

Local Klan activity during the 1920s included extralegal punishment and vigilantism directed at people accused of violating racial, religious, sexual, or moral norms. Victims could be threatened, abducted, beaten, whipped, tarred, or otherwise abused by men acting under Klan authority or imagery.

Simmons publicly insisted that such conduct violated official Klan principles. The distinction between national policy and local violence should be preserved, but the organization’s secrecy and self-appointed moral authority created conditions in which vigilantism could flourish.

Direct Violence Versus Organizational Responsibility

There is no sound basis for saying Simmons personally ordered every assault or murder associated with the second Klan. He often denied authorizing mob violence and presented the organization as law-abiding.

His responsibility was nevertheless substantial. He deliberately revived the name, symbols, secrecy, and mythology of an organization historically associated with racial terror and built a hierarchy that claimed authority to police the behavior and identity of American communities.

Cross Burning as Intimidation

Cross burning became one of the second Klan’s most enduring contributions to American racial intimidation. A burning cross could be used ceremonially at Klan gatherings, but when placed near a Black family’s home, a Catholic institution, or the property of a political opponent, its meaning was unmistakably threatening.

Simmons helped institutionalize the symbol through the revived Klan. It would remain associated with white-supremacist terrorism long after his own leadership ended.

Masks, Hoods, and Anonymity

Klan robes and hoods could conceal individual identity during night activity and create uncertainty about who belonged to the organization. A victim might reasonably fear that neighbors, employers, police officers, or local officials were hidden behind the same uniform.

This uncertainty magnified intimidation. The power of the Klan came partly from the possibility that its network extended into institutions that ordinarily claimed to protect citizens.

Christian Symbolism

Simmons constantly surrounded the Klan with Christian language, Bibles, prayers, crosses, chaplains, and claims of Protestant moral purpose. The movement portrayed itself as a defender of Christianity against perceived racial, religious, and ideological enemies.

This religious framing helped normalize white supremacy among churchgoing recruits. It allowed exclusion and vigilantism to be interpreted as moral guardianship rather than prejudice.

The Contradiction with Christian Equality

The Klan’s Christian self-image stood in obvious tension with its racial and religious exclusions. Black Christians were excluded from membership, Catholic Christianity was treated as suspect, and Jewish Americans were targeted by a movement claiming biblical legitimacy.

Simmons’s career therefore illustrates how religious symbols can be appropriated to sanctify a political hierarchy that denies equal membership to other believers and citizens.

The New York World Exposé

In 1921 the New York World and collaborating newspapers published a major investigative series examining Klan secrecy, finances, recruitment, and reports of violence. The coverage transformed the organization into a national political controversy.

Rather than destroying the movement immediately, the publicity increased public curiosity and helped make the Klan one of the most discussed organizations in the country.

Congressional Investigation of 1921

The controversy led the U.S. House Committee on Rules to hold hearings on the Ku Klux Klan in October 1921. Simmons appeared before Congress and spent days defending the organization.

A surviving Library of Congress photograph shows him seated during those hearings, visually capturing the moment when the founder of a secret white-supremacist fraternity was required to explain the movement before the national legislature.

Simmons's Defense Before Congress

Simmons portrayed the Klan as a patriotic, Christian, fraternal organization committed to law and morality. He distanced the national leadership from crimes committed by individual Klansmen and argued that wrongdoing should not define the organization as a whole.

The defense became a pattern later used by many extremist organizations: separate formal doctrine from local misconduct while continuing to benefit from the fear and solidarity produced by the broader movement.

No Immediate Federal Suppression

The 1921 House hearings produced no federal suppression comparable to the Enforcement Act campaign that had broken the first Klan during Reconstruction. The second Klan continued growing after the investigation.

This outcome demonstrated how effectively Simmons and his publicity partners had placed the organization inside the legal and political mainstream of the 1920s, despite its secrecy and discriminatory ideology.

Financial Controversies

Rapid recruitment generated large sums through initiation fees, organizer commissions, dues, and the sale of robes and other required materials. Critics accused Klan leadership of turning white Protestant fear into a lucrative commercial enterprise.

The financial structure also created internal tensions. The larger the organization became, the more valuable control of the national hierarchy became to ambitious Klan officials.

Commercializing White Supremacy

The recruiting system transformed prejudice into a revenue stream. Every new member produced money for people at several levels of the hierarchy, giving paid organizers a reason to intensify local racial, religious, or political fears.

This is one of the most distinctive features of the second Klan. Simmons founded an ideological movement, but its explosive growth depended on a sales model that rewarded successful marketing of exclusion.

Hiram Wesley Evans

Hiram Wesley Evans was a Texas dentist who joined the Klan in 1920 and rose rapidly through its hierarchy. By 1922 he had built support among officials dissatisfied with Simmons’s management and eager to turn the Klan into a more disciplined political machine.

Evans’s ascent demonstrated a weakness in Simmons’s leadership: the founder had created valuable symbols and structure but was less effective at controlling the enormous national organization that developed afterward.

Evans and Klan Violence

Evans himself had been involved in a notorious 1921 attack in Dallas in which Klansmen abducted a Black bellhop, Alex Johnson, and burned Klan initials into his forehead with acid. The Texas State Historical Association documents Evans’s leadership role in the group responsible.

That episode illustrates the kind of racial terror existing inside the movement that Simmons had built even as national leaders publicly denied that vigilantism represented Klan purposes.

The 1922 Leadership Coup

At the Klan’s 1922 national gathering in Atlanta, Simmons was displaced from day-to-day leadership. Hiram Evans became Imperial Wizard, while Simmons was elevated to the grander-sounding title of Emperor.

The arrangement allowed Simmons to save face, but practical authority had shifted. He soon discovered that his new title provided prestige without effective control of the movement he had founded.

Losing the Invisible Empire

Simmons resisted his loss of power and became embroiled in legal and organizational conflict with Evans and other Klan leaders. The dispute involved control of property, copyrights, finances, and claims to legitimate leadership.

His downfall was partly the consequence of the Klan’s success. A small fraternity could revolve around its founder; a wealthy national organization attracted rivals capable of pushing him aside.

Financial Settlement

Biographical accounts record that Simmons eventually accepted a substantial financial settlement in exchange for surrendering claims and copyrights connected to the Klan. The exact characterization of the settlement varies among sources, but the practical result was the end of his command over the main organization.

He had transformed a nearly penniless organizing project into an institution valuable enough to fight over financially.

The Klan After Simmons

Under Evans, the Klan reached the height of its political influence during the mid-1920s. Membership estimates vary widely, but the organization numbered in the millions and held significant power in state and local politics across the country.

Simmons therefore did not personally preside over the Klan at its absolute peak. His historical importance is foundational: he created the structure, symbols, identity, and organization that successors expanded.

D. C. Stephenson and Scandal

The second Klan’s reputation later suffered devastating damage from internal corruption and criminal scandal. Indiana leader D. C. Stephenson’s 1925 conviction in the kidnapping, rape, and death of Madge Oberholtzer became the most notorious example.

Stephenson’s crimes occurred after Simmons had lost national control, so they should not be attributed to Simmons personally. They nevertheless exposed the gap between the Klan’s moralistic public claims and the conduct of some of its most powerful leaders.

Decline of the Second Klan

Factional conflict, financial disputes, public exposure, corruption scandals, political defeats, and changing social conditions caused Klan membership to fall dramatically after the mid-1920s. By the 1930s the organization was a fraction of its former size.

The decline demonstrated that the movement’s enormous reach had depended on a temporary convergence of racial fear, nativism, religious conflict, fraternal culture, and aggressive commercial recruitment.

Knights of the Flaming Sword

After losing control of the Klan, Simmons attempted to build another white-supremacist fraternal organization, the Knights of the Flaming Sword. He again used dramatic biblical and fraternal symbolism and tried to attract former Klan supporters dissatisfied with Evans.

The new order never equaled the influence of the organization Simmons had lost. His ability to create the second Klan proved difficult to reproduce once the national Klan already occupied the political space he had opened.

Roy E. Davis

Evangelist and organizer Roy E. Davis became one of Simmons’s allies during his effort to rebuild influence outside Evans’s Klan. Davis travelled and recruited for Simmons’s new movement and helped retain some loyalty among former Klansmen.

The alliance again demonstrates Simmons’s reliance on religious language and itinerant organizing to mobilize white-supremacist networks.

Later Publications

Simmons continued writing and publishing defenses of his movement after losing control. His works explained Klan organization, presented its history from his perspective, and promoted his interpretation of “Americanism.”

These publications are valuable primary sources because they show how Simmons wanted the Klan understood: not as a hate organization, but as a moral and patriotic fraternity defending the nation from internal enemies.

The Klan Unmasked

In The Klan Unmasked, Simmons defended the Invisible Empire and its philosophy in his own voice. The book presented him as founder and ideological authority even after internal rivals had reduced his practical power.

For historians, such writings require critical reading. They reveal Simmons’s arguments and self-image but cannot be treated as neutral descriptions of Klan conduct.

How Simmons Wanted to Be Remembered

Simmons wanted to be seen as a Christian patriot who had created a fraternity devoted to law, morality, native-born Americanism, and social order. He rejected portrayals of the Klan as merely violent or criminal.

That self-description leaves out the central fact that his organization defined legitimate American identity racially and religiously and revived symbols historically associated with the violent suppression of Black citizenship.

Later Life

After his national influence declined, Simmons lived increasingly outside the center of Klan politics. The movement passed through new leaders and eventually fragmented, while Simmons remained remembered primarily as the founder whose 1915 ceremony had launched the second era.

He never again commanded an organization remotely comparable in size or power to the Klan he created.

Death in 1945

Simmons died in Atlanta on May 18, 1945. His death came near the end of World War II and before the postwar civil-rights movement transformed American law and politics.

The Klan organization he had founded was already far weaker than during the 1920s, but its symbols, rituals, and model of racial organizing survived and were later reused by new Klan groups opposing Black civil rights.

Who Simmons Was Historically

Simmons was not primarily a successful politician, military leader, or major theologian. His importance was entrepreneurial and organizational. He took an old name, romanticized history, Christian symbols, white racial fear, and American fraternal culture and combined them into a new mass-membership institution.

That creation changed the history of American white supremacy by giving it a national organizational form suitable to the twentieth century.

How His Opposition to Black People Was Carried Out

Simmons opposed Black equality by founding a national organization that excluded Black people from membership, glorified the Reconstruction Klan, preached white supremacy, normalized racial hierarchy, mobilized white voters, and gave local Klans a common identity through which to intimidate Black communities.

His role was often indirect but foundational. He created the institution, ritual, hierarchy, and recruiting system through which thousands of local members could translate racial ideology into political pressure, threats, exclusion, and violence.

Organizational Versus Personal Violence

It is important not to collapse organizational responsibility into unsupported claims of personal criminal direction. The historical record does not establish that Simmons personally ordered every local whipping, kidnapping, or killing attributed to Klansmen.

At the same time, a founder bears responsibility for deliberately constructing and legitimizing the organization in which such conduct repeatedly occurred, particularly when that organization celebrated a predecessor known for racial terror.

The Power of Myth

Simmons’s greatest ideological achievement was making myth politically useful. The first Klan’s attacks on Black political rights were transformed into a story of noble white rescue, and that story became the emotional foundation of the second Klan.

Historical distortion therefore functioned as a political weapon. If Reconstruction could be remembered falsely as Black tyranny, then renewed white organization could be sold as defensive rather than oppressive.

The Power of Ritual

Robes, titles, secret oaths, flaming crosses, initiation ceremonies, and dramatic language made racial politics emotionally compelling. They gave members a sense of belonging and heroic mission that ordinary party meetings could not easily reproduce.

Simmons understood that movements are strengthened not only by arguments but by symbols. Many of the symbols he created or institutionalized remained attached to the Klan for generations.

The Power of Respectability

The second Klan often attracted ministers, businesspeople, police officers, professionals, and elected officials rather than existing exclusively on the social fringe. Its public events and reform rhetoric could make membership appear respectable within many white communities.

That respectability expanded the harm. Black citizens were not necessarily confronting a handful of isolated extremists; in some places they faced an organization embedded within ordinary institutions of local power.

The Power of Commercial Recruitment

Simmons’s partnership with Clarke and Tyler revealed how modern marketing could accelerate ideological movements. Recruiters were financially rewarded for finding grievances and converting resentment into paid membership.

The result was a self-expanding system in which prejudice generated revenue and revenue financed further recruitment. The second Klan became both a political movement and a commercial enterprise.

Comparison with Nathan Bedford Forrest

Nathan Bedford Forrest became associated with leadership of the Reconstruction-era Klan, an organization that used violence to suppress Black voting and Republican government after the Civil War. Simmons belonged to a later generation and did not create that first movement.

His contribution was to appropriate the original Klan’s reputation and convert it into a twentieth-century mass organization operating through fraternal membership, publicity, electoral politics, and renewed vigilantism.

Comparison with Hiram Wesley Evans

Simmons was the founder and ritual creator; Evans became the more effective national political strategist. Evans displaced Simmons in 1922 and presided over the Klan during its greatest political influence.

The contrast shows that creating a movement and governing it are different skills. Simmons built the symbolic architecture, while Evans proved more capable of controlling the institution once it became enormous.

Comparison with D. C. Stephenson

D. C. Stephenson became one of the most powerful state Klan leaders and a symbol of the corruption and violence that helped destroy the organization’s reputation. His criminal downfall occurred after Simmons had already lost control.

Simmons therefore should not be assigned responsibility for Stephenson’s personal crimes, but Stephenson rose through the organizational world Simmons had created.

Comparison with Robert B. Patterson

Robert B. “Tut” Patterson founded the first Citizens’ Council in Mississippi almost forty years after Simmons revived the Klan. Patterson preferred open economic and civic pressure and deliberately presented the Council as a respectable alternative to Klan vigilantism.

Both men built white-supremacist organizations, but Simmons relied more heavily on secrecy, ritual, spectacle, and fraternal identity, while Patterson mobilized existing business and professional power.

Comparison with William J. Simmons of the Citizens’ Councils

William Joseph Simmons should not be confused with William James Simmons, the Mississippi businessman who led Citizens’ Councils of America during the civil-rights era. The two men were unrelated public figures from different periods.

William Joseph Simmons founded the second Ku Klux Klan in 1915; William James Simmons became a major Citizens’ Council administrator in the 1950s and 1960s.

Was Simmons a White Supremacist?

Yes. The organization he founded was explicitly restricted to white men and New Georgia Encyclopedia describes the second Klan as dedicated to white supremacy. Its public ideology treated white Protestant control as a legitimate national objective.

This conclusion does not depend on disputed private motives. It follows directly from the Klan’s membership requirements, mythology, rhetoric, and political program.

Was Simmons a Christian Minister?

Yes, Simmons had a Methodist religious background and served as a preacher and teacher before becoming primarily a fraternal organizer. His ministerial career was not his greatest professional success, but Christian imagery remained central to his public persona.

He used that background to represent the Klan as a Christian order even though its racial and religious exclusions contradicted the equal civic standing of millions of Americans.

Did Simmons Invent the Ku Klux Klan?

No. He revived the Klan name in 1915 after the original Reconstruction organization had disappeared decades earlier. His creation is therefore commonly called the second Ku Klux Klan.

What Simmons did invent or formalize were many of the rituals, titles, organizational practices, and visual symbols associated with the twentieth-century Klan.

Did Simmons Personally Invent Cross Burning?

The revived Klan institutionalized cross burning under Simmons and used it at the Stone Mountain founding ceremony. The image had been popularized in the fictional and cinematic Klan mythology that influenced him.

It is more precise to say Simmons made cross burning a defining ritual of the second Klan than to claim he independently invented the practice from nothing.

Did Simmons Order Klan Violence?

Simmons publicly denied that violence was authorized Klan policy, and the available evidence does not support attributing every local act of violence directly to his orders. Local chapters often acted with considerable autonomy.

His responsibility remains serious because he founded, armed symbolically, promoted, and profited from an organization whose white-supremacist secrecy and self-appointed authority repeatedly produced intimidation and vigilantism.

Did Congressional Hearings Destroy the Klan?

No. The 1921 hearings exposed the organization to scrutiny but did not suppress it. The publicity actually coincided with continued rapid growth.

The Klan declined later because of leadership conflict, corruption, criminal scandals, political backlash, and changing social conditions rather than a single congressional investigation.

Historical Significance

William Joseph Simmons was one of the most consequential organizers of American white supremacy in the twentieth century. He revived a largely defunct Reconstruction symbol and transformed it into a modern fraternity capable of mass recruitment, publicity, political organization, and nationwide expansion.

His importance extends beyond the Klan’s membership totals. The symbols he institutionalized—hooded robes, flaming crosses, the Invisible Empire, elaborate Klan titles, and public ritual—became enduring visual language for racial intimidation in the United States.

Impact on African Americans

Simmons’s organization contributed to a renewed climate of racial intimidation at a time when African Americans were migrating to cities, building political organizations, seeking employment, and challenging segregation. Klan chapters treated those changes as threats to white control and mobilized members around resistance.

Even when Simmons did not personally direct local violence, the national legitimacy and organizational identity he provided empowered white men who threatened, assaulted, and politically intimidated Black citizens. The second Klan helped make resistance to Black advancement a mass civic cause rather than merely the work of isolated vigilantes.

A Balanced Assessment

Simmons possessed genuine organizational imagination. He understood fraternal psychology, ritual, symbolism, recruitment, publicity, and the appeal of belonging to a supposedly heroic secret order. His creation eventually became far larger than anything his own managerial ability could control.

Those abilities were devoted to an exclusionary and destructive cause. He revived a name associated with racial terror, defined authentic American identity around white Protestant membership, helped normalize antisemitism and anti-Catholic nativism, and created the institutional framework through which racial intimidation could spread nationally.

Legacy

Simmons’s personal power was short-lived, but his organizational legacy was enormous. The Klan he founded became a multi-million-member movement, shaped elections and state governments, and carried white-supremacist organization far beyond the South before collapsing under factionalism and scandal.

Later Klan groups never fully reproduced the 1920s movement’s mainstream reach, but they inherited Simmons’s symbols, titles, robes, cross burnings, and myth of an Invisible Empire. His greatest historical legacy is therefore not a long personal reign but the durable architecture he gave modern American Klan politics.

Timeline

  • May 7, 1880: Born in Harpersville, Alabama.
  • Late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries: Pursues religious work and becomes deeply involved in fraternal organizations.
  • Early 1900s: Works as a Methodist preacher, teacher, lecturer, and fraternal recruiter.
  • 1915: Becomes increasingly interested in reviving the Ku Klux Klan amid the popularity of The Birth of a Nation and the aftermath of the Leo Frank case.
  • November 25, 1915: Leads sixteen other men to Stone Mountain, burns a cross, and formally establishes the second Ku Klux Klan.
  • 1915-1919: Builds the Klan slowly in Georgia, Alabama, and nearby areas.
  • 1917-1918: Klan promotes wartime Americanism and white Protestant nationalism during World War I.
  • 1920: Simmons hires Edward Young Clarke and Elizabeth Tyler to professionalize Klan recruitment.
  • 1920-1921: Commission-based Kleagle system produces rapid national membership growth.
  • 1921: New York World investigation exposes Klan finances, secrecy, and reports of violence.
  • October 1921: Simmons testifies before the U.S. House Committee on Rules during congressional investigation of the Klan.
  • 1921-1922: Klan expands rapidly throughout the South, Midwest, West, and other regions.
  • 1922: Hiram Wesley Evans displaces Simmons as Imperial Wizard; Simmons receives the title of Emperor but loses operational control.
  • 1923-1924: Simmons fights with Evans and attempts to retain loyal supporters.
  • 1924: Organizes the Knights of the Flaming Sword as a rival white-supremacist fraternity.
  • Mid-1920s: Main Klan reaches peak national influence under Evans while Simmons’s rival movement remains secondary.
  • 1925: D. C. Stephenson scandal accelerates national disillusionment with the Klan.
  • Late 1920s: Klan membership and political influence decline sharply.
  • 1930s: Simmons continues writing and defending his interpretation of Klan history and Americanism.
  • 1944: The once-powerful second Klan is greatly diminished and fragmented.
  • May 18, 1945: Simmons dies in Atlanta, Georgia.

Geographic Scope

Harpersville, Alabama was Simmons’s birthplace, while Atlanta, Georgia became the center of his fraternal career and the national headquarters of the organization he created. Stone Mountain became the symbolic site of the second Klan’s rebirth.

After 1920 the movement spread far beyond the Deep South. Klan chapters became influential in parts of the Midwest, West, Southwest, and Northeast, demonstrating Simmons’s success in turning a regional racial mythology into a nationwide politics of white Protestant nationalism.

Legal and Constitutional Context

Simmons’s Klan operated within the constitutional protections available to private associations while simultaneously challenging the equal citizenship of groups excluded from its definition of Americanism. Its activities raised questions involving free association, religious liberty, political intimidation, vigilantism, local law enforcement, and federal power.

The contrast with the Reconstruction Klan is especially important. Federal Enforcement Acts had been used against the first Klan because of its campaign against Black voting and constitutional government. The second Klan, operating decades later through a legally incorporated fraternal structure, proved much harder to suppress through a single federal response.
