Eugene Thomas

Bessemer steelworker, Ku Klux Klan operative, and federally convicted civil-rights conspirator who drove and helped instigate the 1965 Klan attack that killed Viola Gregg Liuzzo after the Selma-to-Montgomery voting-rights march

Overview

Eugene Thomas was a Bessemer, Alabama, steelworker, Ku Klux Klan member, and federally convicted civil-rights conspirator whose role in the March 25, 1965 pursuit and shooting of Viola Gregg Liuzzo placed him among the principal violent antagonists of the Selma voting-rights campaign. Federal evidence placed Thomas as the driver of the Klan automobile, an initiator and sustainer of the pursuit, the owner of the handgun passed to Collie Leroy Wilkins Jr., and the participant who urged continued gunfire. Alabama later acquitted Thomas of murder, but a federal jury convicted him under 18 U.S.C. § 241, and a 1983 federal civil court found that Wilkins shot Liuzzo with Thomas encouraging him.

Birth-Date Uncertainty

President Lyndon B. Johnson identified Thomas as forty-three years old on March 26, 1965, while some later summaries give forty-two. The strongest public sources therefore support a birth year around 1921 or 1922, but not a confidently documented exact birth date.

Death-Date Uncertainty

Reliable public sources consulted for this page establish that Thomas later died, but do not consistently supply a precise death date. This page leaves the date unresolved rather than inventing one.

Bessemer Residence

Thomas lived in Bessemer, Alabama, an industrial city southwest of Birmingham closely tied to steel production.

Steelworker

Contemporary reporting identified Thomas as a U.S. Steel employee and steelworker.

Klan Membership

Thomas was an active Ku Klux Klan member in the Birmingham-Bessemer area.

Veteran Klansman

Later historical accounts describe Thomas as an experienced or veteran Klansman rather than a casual or newly recruited member.

Private Extremist Role

Unlike George Wallace, Jim Clark, or other antagonists who wielded public office, Thomas exercised power through private white-supremacist organization and violence.

Selma Voting-Rights Context

The Selma campaign challenged systematic barriers that prevented Black citizens in central Alabama from registering and voting.

SNCC

The Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee had conducted sustained grassroots voting-rights work in Selma before the 1965 national crisis.

SCLC

The Southern Christian Leadership Conference joined the Selma campaign and helped organize mass demonstrations.

Dallas County Voters League

Local Black activists in the Dallas County Voters League provided essential long-term leadership before national organizations arrived.

Jimmie Lee Jackson

The fatal shooting of Jimmie Lee Jackson by an Alabama state trooper helped inspire the march from Selma to Montgomery.

Bloody Sunday

On March 7, 1965, Alabama state troopers and local forces attacked marchers at the Edmund Pettus Bridge.

Turnaround Tuesday

A second march attempt on March 9 ended when Martin Luther King Jr. turned demonstrators back rather than violate a federal order.

James Reeb

White minister James Reeb was fatally attacked by segregationists after travelling to Selma in support of the movement.

Williams v. Wallace

Federal Judge Frank M. Johnson Jr. ruled that the marchers had a constitutional right to proceed and required Alabama officials not to interfere.

March Protection Order

The March 17 federal order prescribed the route and required police protection for the Selma-to-Montgomery march.

March 21 Klan Protest

The Fifth Circuit found abundant proof that Thomas and Wilkins attended a Ku Klux Klan rally and parade in Montgomery protesting the federal march order.

Cramton Bowl

The Klan protest rally was held at Cramton Bowl in Montgomery.

Notice of Federal Protection

Thomas's participation in the protest helped prove that he knew the march was federally protected.

March 25 Phone Call

On March 25, Thomas telephoned FBI informant Gary Thomas Rowe Jr. and said he had to join a group of Klansmen going to Montgomery.

Imperial Office Instruction

A Klan official separately told Rowe that the Montgomery trip had been directed by the Klan's Imperial Office.

Rowe Contacts FBI

Rowe informed his FBI handler and was instructed to accompany Thomas and the others.

Four-Man Group

Thomas, Collie Leroy Wilkins Jr., William Orville Eaton, and Gary Thomas Rowe Jr. travelled together.

Thomas's Automobile

The four men travelled to Montgomery in Thomas's automobile.

Armed Group

The Fifth Circuit stated that Thomas, Eaton, and Rowe were armed, although the guns were initially left in the parked automobile.

Five Hours Near Capitol

The group spent roughly five hours near the Capitol while the voting-rights march concluded.

Harassment of Marchers

Thomas and his companions booed, shouted at, and argued with marchers and Black spectators.

Thomas Announces Selma Trip

After the Capitol program, Thomas announced that the group was going to Selma.

Things to Do

When Eaton asked why, Thomas said they had things to do and were going to get them done.

Hitchhiker Incident

The group considered harassing a roadside hitchhiker they thought might be a marcher.

Highway Patrol Stop

Thomas's automobile was stopped at a Highway 80 radar checkpoint.

Improper Muffler Warning

Thomas received a warning for an improper muffler, independently placing his car on the Selma-Montgomery corridor.

Silver Moon Cafe

The group stopped at the Silver Moon Cafe in Selma for approximately thirty to forty-five minutes.

Reeb-Killing Reference

The Fifth Circuit described an encounter at the cafe in which a man charged in the James Reeb killing encouraged Thomas and his companions to do their job. This is contextual evidence, not proof of a formal conspiracy between the two crimes.

Thomas Driving

After leaving the cafe, Thomas drove the Klan automobile.

Seating Arrangement

Eaton sat beside Thomas in front; Rowe sat behind Thomas; Wilkins sat behind Eaton.

First Pedestrian Target

Near a church the group saw Black pedestrians and discussed confronting them.

Thomas Slows

Thomas slowed his car while discussing having 'fun' with the pedestrians.

Gun Passed to Wilkins

During that encounter Thomas took a handgun from between the seats and handed it to Wilkins.

Army Truck

The group abandoned the first confrontation after Rowe noticed soldiers nearby.

Escalation Before Liuzzo

The pedestrian episode demonstrated that discussion of armed violence preceded the encounter with Viola Liuzzo.

Michigan Oldsmobile

The men later saw a light-colored Oldsmobile bearing Michigan plates.

Viola Liuzzo

The driver was Viola Gregg Liuzzo, a white civil-rights volunteer from Detroit.

Leroy Moton

The passenger was nineteen-year-old Black civil-rights worker Leroy Moton.

Interracial Pair

The sight of a white woman and Black man together triggered racist commentary from the Klan group.

Thomas Proposes Following

The Fifth Circuit record attributes the decision to follow Liuzzo's vehicle to Thomas.

Racial-Sexual Assumption

Thomas speculated that Liuzzo and Moton might be going somewhere private together, reflecting white-supremacist obsession with interracial intimacy.

Get Down

Thomas told men in the back seat to lower themselves below window level.

Take Them

Thomas repeatedly said the group was going to 'take' or 'get' the occupants of the Oldsmobile.

Brass Comment

Thomas suggested the occupants might be important civil-rights figures.

Craig Air Force Base

Rowe tried to persuade Thomas to abandon the pursuit near Craig Air Force Base.

Thomas Refuses

The federal record states that Thomas refused to break off the pursuit.

High-Speed Chase

Thomas pursued Liuzzo's Oldsmobile at approximately eighty to ninety miles per hour.

Thomas Asks About Ramming

Thomas asked whether he should bump the Oldsmobile to stop it.

Wilkins Rejects Ramming

Wilkins objected because transferred paint could connect the Klan car to the crime.

Evidence Awareness

The exchange demonstrated concern about forensic evidence and detection.

Pulling Alongside

Thomas maneuvered the Klan car alongside Liuzzo's Oldsmobile.

Thomas's Handgun

Wilkins was holding Thomas's handgun when the vehicles came alongside.

Wilkins Fires

The federal record states that Wilkins fired two shots into Liuzzo's vehicle.

Thomas Orders Gunfire

The Fifth Circuit record attributes to Thomas an order that everyone continue shooting.

Eaton Fires

The federal evidence stated that Eaton also fired.

Rowe's Account

Rowe maintained that he drew his gun but did not fire.

Liuzzo Killed

Viola Liuzzo was struck by gunfire and killed.

Moton Survives

Leroy Moton survived the attack.

Feigning Death

Moton remained motionless when the attackers returned, causing them to believe he was dead.

Shell Disposal

The Fifth Circuit stated that Wilkins and Eaton discarded cartridge casings from the car.

Alibi Planning

The group attempted to arrange alibis after returning toward Bessemer.

Thomas's Operational Role

Federal evidence placed Thomas as caller, driver, pursuer, gun owner, and instigator rather than as a passive passenger.

No Fatal Shot Attributed to Thomas

The strongest judicial findings identify Wilkins, not Thomas, as the person who fired the fatal shots.

Criminal Responsibility Without Trigger Pull

A conspirator can bear direct criminal responsibility for planned lethal violence without personally firing the fatal bullet.

March 26 Arrest

FBI agents arrested the Klan participants within roughly a day of the murder.

Johnson Announces Arrests

President Johnson announced the arrests on national television.

Age and Residence

Johnson identified Thomas as forty-three and from Bessemer.

Rowe Revealed

Rowe's status as an FBI informant soon transformed the prosecution.

Immunity

State and federal authorities promised Rowe protection in return for testimony.

Alabama Murder Charge

Thomas faced a state murder indictment arising from Liuzzo's death.

Wilkins Tried First

Alabama first tried Collie Wilkins.

First Wilkins Trial

Wilkins's first state trial ended in a hung jury.

Second Wilkins Trial

Wilkins's second state trial ended in acquittal.

Federal Trial Timing

Thomas was federally tried before his own Alabama murder case reached trial.

Federal Trial Dates

The federal conspiracy trial ran from November 29 to December 3, 1965.

Judge Frank Johnson

Judge Frank M. Johnson Jr. presided over the federal trial.

John Doar

Civil Rights Division attorney John Doar led the federal prosecution.

Section 241

The defendants were prosecuted under 18 U.S.C. § 241, the federal conspiracy-against-rights statute.

Reconstruction Origins

Section 241 descends from Reconstruction-era federal law designed in part to combat Klan interference with citizenship rights.

Narrowed Federal Theory

The jury ultimately considered a conspiracy to interfere with people participating in or supporting the protected Selma-to-Montgomery march.

Not Federal Murder

Thomas was not convicted in federal court of murder.

Shooting as Intent Evidence

The Fifth Circuit held that evidence surrounding Liuzzo's shooting was highly relevant to the conspirators' intent.

Federal Conviction

A federal jury convicted Thomas, Wilkins, and Eaton.

Maximum Sentence

Each received the statutory maximum ten-year sentence.

Federal Jury

Historical accounts identify the federal jury as all white.

Appeal

Thomas and Wilkins appealed the convictions.

Eaton Dies

Eaton died while the federal appeal was pending.

April 27, 1967

The Fifth Circuit affirmed Thomas's civil-rights conspiracy conviction.

Evidence More Than Ample

The appellate court said the evidence was more than ample to sustain the verdict.

National Citizenship

The Fifth Circuit held that participation in the march was an attribute of national citizenship protected from private conspiracy.

Names of Victims Not Required

The conspirators did not have to know individual marchers' names; targeting the protected class was sufficient.

Specific Intent

The government still had to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendants intended to interfere with a federal right.

Allen Charge

The federal jury received a supplemental instruction encouraging continued deliberation.

Allen Charge Upheld

The Fifth Circuit rejected the defense challenge to that instruction.

Supreme Court Review

The United States Supreme Court declined further review.

State Murder Trial

Thomas's Alabama murder trial occurred in late September 1966.

Hayneville

The state trial took place in Hayneville, Lowndes County.

Judge Thagard

Circuit Judge T. Werth Thagard presided.

Richmond Flowers

Alabama Attorney General Richmond Flowers personally prosecuted Thomas.

Racially Mixed Jury

The jury consisted of eight Black jurors and four white jurors.

Historic Jury Change

Contemporary reporting described it as the first racially mixed criminal jury in local history.

Federal Jury-Selection Intervention

The mixed jury followed federal intervention requiring Black citizens to be included in Lowndes County jury pools.

Ballistics Evidence

An FBI expert testified that a bullet removed from Liuzzo had been fired from a revolver owned by Thomas.

Gun Ownership Versus Shooter Identity

Ownership of the revolver connected Thomas strongly to the crime but did not establish that Thomas personally fired it.

Thomas Acquitted

The Alabama jury found Thomas not guilty of murder.

Short Deliberation

Contemporary accounts place deliberation at roughly eighty-five to ninety minutes.

Acquittal Finality

The state acquittal barred another Alabama prosecution of Thomas for the same murder.

Acquittal Does Not Identify Another Shooter

A not-guilty verdict does not itself prove that Rowe or anyone else was the shooter.

Federal Conviction Survives

Thomas's state acquittal did not invalidate his distinct federal civil-rights conviction.

Sawed-Off Shotgun Search

FBI agents searched Thomas's Bessemer home and found a sawed-off shotgun.

Bessemer Address

The Fifth Circuit firearms opinion identified his residence as 1519 12th Avenue North, Bessemer.

Shotgun Description

The firearm was a Newport double-barrel shotgun with a barrel length of approximately twelve and one-half inches.

Firearms Conviction

Thomas was initially convicted under federal firearms tax law.

Two-Year Consecutive Term

The shotgun conviction carried a two-year sentence consecutive to the ten-year civil-rights sentence.

Search-Warrant Defect

The Fifth Circuit held that the search-warrant affidavit failed to allege a federal crime.

Unreasonable Search

Because of that defect, the appellate court held the search unreasonable.

Chain and Bullwhip

Agents also seized a chain and bullwhip, which the Fifth Circuit called highly prejudicial and irrelevant to the firearm charge.

Firearms Conviction Reversed

The Fifth Circuit reversed the sawed-off-shotgun conviction.

Firearms Reversal Scope

The reversal did not affect Thomas's civil-rights conspiracy conviction.

Not a Final Gun Conviction

It is inaccurate to list the shotgun case as a final surviving conviction against Thomas.

Prison Time

Historical sources generally say Thomas served about six to seven years on the ten-year civil-rights sentence.

Release

Thomas eventually returned to civilian life in Alabama.

1970 Presentence Litigation

Thomas sought a copy of his federal presentence report for a contemplated collateral attack.

Presentence Request Denied

The Fifth Circuit affirmed denial of the request in 1970.

1979 Accusation Against Rowe

Thomas publicly alleged that FBI informant Gary Rowe had actually shot Liuzzo.

Borrowed Gun Claim

Thomas said Rowe borrowed Thomas's gun because Rowe's own gun did not work.

Thomas Explains Silence

Thomas said counsel had advised him not to testify in the original proceedings.

Shelton Appears

United Klans of America leader Robert Shelton appeared at the press event where Thomas made the accusation.

Rowe Denies

Rowe continued saying Wilkins fired the shots with Thomas's handgun.

Renewed Lowndes Investigation

The later accusations contributed to renewed investigation of Rowe.

Polygraphs

Thomas and Wilkins reportedly passed polygraph examinations, but polygraphs are not reliable substitutes for judicial proof.

Rowe Indicted

A Lowndes County grand jury indicted Rowe for murder in 1978.

Indictment Not Conviction

An indictment is an accusation based on probable cause, not a determination of guilt.

Rowe Immunity Litigation

Rowe argued that Alabama had promised him immunity in exchange for his testimony.

Federal Injunction

A federal court blocked Alabama from prosecuting Rowe.

Rowe v. Griffin

The Eleventh Circuit affirmed the injunction in 1982.

No Shooter Ruling

The Eleventh Circuit did not decide who fired the fatal shots.

Liuzzo Family Civil Suit

The Liuzzo family sued the United States over the FBI's management of Rowe.

1983 Bench Trial

Federal Judge Charles W. Joiner heard the civil case without a jury.

Thomas Repeats Rowe Claim

Thomas testified that Rowe had been the shooter.

Former Wife Impeachment

Thomas's former wife testified that a few weeks after the killing Thomas told her Wilkins shot Liuzzo at Thomas's direction.

Compelling Impeachment

Judge Joiner considered the former wife's testimony particularly compelling.

Thomas Testimony Rejected

The civil opinion stated that Thomas's testimony fell apart under the impeachment.

Civil Finding

Judge Joiner found that Wilkins shot Liuzzo and Thomas encouraged him.

Civil Standard

That factual finding was made under the preponderance-of-the-evidence standard.

Not Criminal Conviction

The 1983 civil finding did not reverse Thomas's Alabama acquittal or create a murder conviction.

Rowe Found Not Shooter

The same civil court found that Rowe did not fire the fatal shots.

Rowe's Other Violence

The court nevertheless believed Rowe had participated in other violent Klan acts and had not always reported himself truthfully.

FBI Informant Ethics

The case exposed serious ethical and operational problems in managing informants embedded in violent extremist groups.

Hoover-Era Smears

Historical evidence documents FBI efforts to discredit Viola Liuzzo after her death.

Victim Blaming Falsehoods

False insinuations about Liuzzo's morality or relationship with Moton had no legitimate bearing on her murder.

Voting Rights Act

Liuzzo's murder contributed to the national climate in which the Voting Rights Act passed in August 1965.

No Single-Cause Myth

The Voting Rights Act resulted from decades of Black organizing, litigation, protest, federal action, and congressional strategy.

Thomas Versus Wilkins

The strongest judicial findings cast Wilkins as shooter and Thomas as driver, gun owner, and instigator.

Thomas Versus Eaton

Eaton was another passenger whom Rowe said also fired during the attack.

Thomas Versus Rowe

Rowe was the FBI informant whose own violent history complicated every later interpretation of the case.

Thomas Versus Wallace

Wallace used governmental power to resist civil rights; Thomas used private Klan violence.

Thomas Versus Jim Clark

Clark used sheriff's authority; Thomas held no comparable public office.

Direct-Violence Category

Thomas belongs among antagonists who personally participated in clandestine racial terrorism.

Historical Record and Common Misconceptions

The central corrections are that Thomas was acquitted of state murder, convicted federally of civil-rights conspiracy, had his separate shotgun conviction reversed, and was later found in a civil case to have encouraged Wilkins to shoot.

Was Thomas Convicted of Murder?

No. Alabama acquitted him in 1966.

Was Thomas Convicted in Connection With Liuzzo?

Yes. A federal jury convicted him of conspiracy against federally protected civil rights.

Did Thomas Drive the Klan Car?

Yes, according to the Fifth Circuit's factual summary.

Did Thomas Own the Handgun?

Yes; the prosecution tied the fatal bullet to a revolver owned by Thomas.

Did Thomas Pull the Trigger?

The strongest later judicial finding says Wilkins fired the fatal shots.

Did Thomas Encourage the Shooting?

Yes according to Rowe's testimony credited by the federal jury and the 1983 civil court.

Was the Shotgun Conviction Final?

No. The Fifth Circuit reversed it.

Did Thomas Later Blame Rowe?

Yes.

Was Rowe Convicted?

No. Federal courts blocked his prosecution because of immunity promises.

Legacy

Thomas's record demonstrates the necessity of separating verdicts, evidentiary findings, and later allegations. His Alabama acquittal remains real; so does his federal conviction for intentionally conspiring against march supporters, and so does the later judicial finding that he encouraged Wilkins to shoot Liuzzo.

Geographic Scope

Bessemer and Birmingham formed Thomas's home and Klan environment. Montgomery was the march destination and federal trial site. Selma was the campaign center. U.S. Highway 80 became the route of the fatal pursuit. Lowndes County and Hayneville were the site of Liuzzo's death and Thomas's state murder trial.

Legal and Constitutional Context

Thomas's record includes a state murder acquittal, a federal Section 241 civil-rights conspiracy conviction, a separate firearms conviction later reversed because of an invalid warrant, and a later civil factual finding that he encouraged Wilkins to shoot. Each proceeding had different elements, burdens, and legal consequences.
