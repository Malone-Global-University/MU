Charles Sumner

Abolitionist senator, Radical Republican, and one of the nineteenth century's most persistent congressional advocates for racial equality and civil rights

Overview

Charles Sumner was an American lawyer, abolitionist, and United States senator from Massachusetts who became one of the most uncompromising congressional opponents of slavery and racial discrimination in nineteenth-century American politics. He served in the Senate from 1851 until his death in 1874, spanning the sectional crisis of the 1850s, the American Civil War, and most of Reconstruction.

Sumner believed opposition to slavery required more than preventing its territorial expansion. He increasingly demanded complete abolition, citizenship, voting rights, equal protection, integrated public institutions, and federal protection of African American civil rights.

His public career placed him in repeated conflict with political leaders who favored compromise over slavery or more limited approaches to racial equality. Before entering the Senate, Sumner participated in one of the earliest American legal challenges to racially segregated public education, arguing with Black attorney Robert Morris in Roberts v. City of Boston that separation on the basis of race violated equality.

Sumner became nationally famous after delivering his “Crime Against Kansas” speech in May 1856. In the speech, he condemned slavery, the Kansas-Nebraska Act, and proslavery senators, including Andrew Butler of South Carolina. Two days after Sumner completed the speech, Butler's relative, Representative Preston Brooks of South Carolina, attacked Sumner at his Senate desk and repeatedly struck him with a heavy cane.

The assault left Sumner seriously injured and unable to perform regular Senate duties for approximately three years. His empty Senate seat became a powerful Northern symbol of what antislavery Americans considered the violence of the slaveholding political system. In much of the South, meanwhile, Brooks was celebrated by supporters who believed Sumner had insulted their region and political leaders.

Sumner returned to active Senate service before the Civil War and became a major Radical Republican leader. During the war, he pressed President Abraham Lincoln and Congress toward emancipation, supported the enlistment of Black soldiers, advocated permanent constitutional abolition, and served as chairman of the Senate Committee on Foreign Relations.

After slavery was abolished, Sumner argued that emancipation alone was insufficient. He demanded equal citizenship and political and civil rights for formerly enslaved people. He opposed President Andrew Johnson's Reconstruction policies, supported Johnson's impeachment, defended Black suffrage, and sought extensive federal protection against racial discrimination.

In 1870, Sumner introduced a comprehensive civil rights proposal designed to prohibit racial discrimination in public accommodations and other areas of public life. He died in 1874 before the measure became law. Congress subsequently enacted a reduced version as the Civil Rights Act of 1875.

Although the Supreme Court later invalidated major provisions of that law, Sumner's conception of federal civil rights protection anticipated principles that would become central to American law during the twentieth-century Civil Rights Movement.

Early Life

Charles Sumner was born in Boston, Massachusetts, on January 6, 1811. His father, Charles Pinckney Sumner, was a lawyer who served for many years as sheriff of Suffolk County. His mother was Relief Jacob Sumner.

Sumner grew up on the north slope of Boston's Beacon Hill, an area that included an important free Black community. The neighborhood contained Black churches, schools, abolitionist organizations, businesses, and residences that later became associated with the Underground Railroad and Boston's antislavery movement.

His exposure to this community helped place Sumner in contact with African American reformers and abolitionists at a relatively early stage of his public life.

Education

Sumner attended Boston Latin School before enrolling at Harvard College. He continued his education at Harvard Law School, where he studied during a period when Supreme Court Justice Joseph Story was one of the institution's most influential legal scholars.

Sumner graduated from Harvard Law School in 1833 and was admitted to the Massachusetts bar the following year.

His early career reflected considerable intellectual ambition. He practiced law, contributed to legal publications, assisted with legal education, and developed relationships with prominent scholars, writers, reformers, and political figures.

European Travel

During the late 1830s, Sumner travelled extensively in Europe. He studied European legal institutions, attended lectures, observed governments and courts, and developed relationships within intellectual and political circles.

His travels strengthened his interest in international law, diplomacy, peace movements, and political reform. These interests later became important when he assumed leadership of the Senate Committee on Foreign Relations.

Sumner also observed racial attitudes outside the United States. Experiences in Europe contributed to his growing rejection of the racial hierarchy that remained deeply embedded in American law and society.

Legal Career and Reform

Sumner initially appeared positioned for a distinguished career within the legal profession rather than electoral politics. He practiced law in Boston and became associated with prominent intellectuals and reformers.

During the 1840s, however, he increasingly used public speeches and legal arguments to address broader moral and political issues. Opposition to war, slavery, and racial discrimination gradually became central to his public identity.

His reform commitments eventually alienated some members of Boston's conservative business and social establishment, particularly as Sumner became more aggressive in attacking the economic and political relationships that protected slavery.

Roberts v. City of Boston

Before entering the United States Senate, Sumner participated in one of the most important early American challenges to racial segregation in public education.

Boston maintained separate public schools for Black children. Black parents and community activists challenged the system, arguing that their children should have access to the same neighborhood schools available to white children.

Benjamin Roberts brought a lawsuit on behalf of his daughter Sarah Roberts after she was denied admission to a white public school closer to her home.

African American attorney Robert Morris and Charles Sumner served as counsel in the case. Sumner argued that Massachusetts law did not authorize racial discrimination in public schools and that separating children by race violated the principle of equality.

The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court rejected their argument in Roberts v. City of Boston. The ruling allowed Boston's segregated school system to continue.

The defeat was significant because the court's reasoning helped establish an early version of the concept later known as “separate but equal.”

Black activists nevertheless continued their campaign. In 1855, the Massachusetts legislature prohibited racial segregation in the state's public schools.

Sumner's argument that segregation itself created inequality anticipated principles that became central to later school desegregation litigation, culminating nationally in Brown v. Board of Education in 1954.

Growth of His Abolitionism

By the late 1840s, Sumner had become increasingly identified with the organized antislavery movement.

He condemned not only Southern slaveholders but also Northern financial, commercial, and political interests that benefited from or accommodated slavery.

Sumner rejected compromises that he believed strengthened the institution. His position increasingly aligned him with political abolitionists seeking to prevent slavery's expansion and ultimately eliminate it altogether.

He also developed close relationships with prominent African American abolitionists, including Frederick Douglass, and with white reformers such as William Lloyd Garrison, Wendell Phillips, Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, and Samuel Gridley Howe.

Free Soil Politics

Sumner helped develop the Free Soil movement in Massachusetts. The Free Soil Party opposed the expansion of slavery into western territories acquired by the United States.

The party brought together antislavery Democrats, former members of the Liberty Party, and antislavery Whigs.

Sumner's position went beyond that of many Free Soilers. Some politicians opposed expansion principally because they wanted western territories reserved for free white labor. Sumner's opposition increasingly rested on a broader moral condemnation of slavery and belief in racial equality.

Fugitive Slave Act of 1850

Passage of the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 intensified Sumner's political activism.

The law strengthened federal procedures for capturing people accused of escaping slavery and required assistance with enforcement even in free states.

Boston became an important battleground over enforcement. Abolitionists organized resistance, provided legal assistance, aided freedom seekers, and challenged federal attempts to return Black residents to slavery.

Sumner regarded the law as morally illegitimate and politically dangerous because it forced citizens of free states and the federal government to participate directly in maintaining slavery.

Election to the United States Senate

In 1851, the Massachusetts legislature elected Sumner to the United States Senate following a prolonged political contest. He entered the chamber as a Free Soil senator.

Sumner quickly became one of the most outspoken antislavery politicians in Congress.

His speeches attacked the Fugitive Slave Act, efforts to expand slavery into western territories, and the political influence exercised by slaveholding interests within the federal government.

His uncompromising style earned admiration among abolitionists while generating intense hostility among many Southern politicians and some Northern moderates.

Kansas-Nebraska Act

The Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854 became one of the central issues of Sumner's early Senate career.

The law permitted settlers in Kansas and Nebraska to determine whether slavery would be allowed under the principle of popular sovereignty. In doing so, it effectively repealed the Missouri Compromise restriction that had prohibited slavery in much of the Louisiana Purchase north of latitude 36°30′.

Antislavery and proslavery settlers subsequently competed for control of Kansas. Fraudulent elections, rival governments, armed organizations, raids, killings, and political intimidation produced the period known as Bleeding Kansas.

Sumner viewed the Kansas crisis as evidence that slaveholding interests were willing to use fraud and violence to extend slavery into territory where it had previously been restricted.

The Republican Party

The destruction of the Whig Party and backlash against the Kansas-Nebraska Act contributed to the formation of the Republican Party during the mid-1850s.

Sumner became an important antislavery figure within the emerging party. Republicans opposed the extension of slavery into the western territories, although members differed substantially over abolition and racial equality.

Sumner represented the more radical antislavery wing and would eventually become one of the leading figures associated with the Radical Republicans.

The Crime Against Kansas

In May 1856, Sumner delivered a lengthy Senate speech titled “The Crime Against Kansas.”

The speech condemned the effort to establish slavery in Kansas and accused proslavery politicians of responsibility for the violence and political fraud occurring in the territory.

Sumner attacked several political opponents by name, most notably Senator Andrew P. Butler of South Carolina and Senator Stephen A. Douglas of Illinois.

His language was deliberately provocative. Sumner combined constitutional arguments with moral denunciation, sarcasm, personal ridicule, and attacks on what he regarded as the political power of slavery.

The speech intensified already severe sectional hostility in Congress.

Caning of Charles Sumner

On May 22, 1856, Representative Preston Brooks of South Carolina, a relative of Senator Andrew Butler, entered the Senate chamber after the Senate had adjourned.

Sumner was seated at his desk when Brooks confronted him over the “Crime Against Kansas” speech.

Brooks then struck Sumner repeatedly with a heavy walking cane, hitting him on the head and upper body.

Sumner initially found it difficult to escape because his legs were trapped beneath a Senate desk secured to the floor. During the attack, he tore the desk loose as he attempted to rise and move away.

Brooks continued striking him until the cane broke. Sumner suffered serious physical injuries and psychological trauma that affected him for years.

The attack became one of the most notorious acts of political violence in congressional history.

Political Reaction to the Caning

Reactions to the assault demonstrated the widening sectional divide within the United States.

Across much of the North, Sumner was portrayed as a victim of the violent political system supporting slavery. The image of a senator being beaten at his desk for delivering an antislavery speech became powerful Republican propaganda.

Many Southern supporters of slavery viewed Brooks differently. He received public praise and expressions of support from admirers who believed he had defended Southern honor against Sumner's insults.

The radically different responses suggested that Americans were increasingly unable even to agree on the legitimacy of political violence arising from the slavery controversy.

Recovery

Sumner's injuries prevented him from regularly performing his Senate duties for approximately three years.

He travelled within the United States and Europe seeking medical treatment and periods of recovery. He returned to the Senate intermittently but was unable to resume a full workload.

Massachusetts nevertheless reelected him in 1857 while he was still largely absent from Washington.

His vacant desk in the Senate became a political symbol. Supporters deliberately left the seat effectively unfilled rather than replacing him, emphasizing that political violence had deprived Massachusetts of active representation.

Return to the Senate

Sumner returned to regular Senate activity in 1859.

Rather than moderating his political position following the assault, he resumed his campaign against slavery.

In 1860, he delivered another major antislavery speech, “The Barbarism of Slavery,” in which he attacked slavery as a system that damaged enslaved people, slaveholders, Southern institutions, and the nation.

By this time, the national political system was approaching collapse. Abraham Lincoln's election in November 1860 was followed by the secession of South Carolina and eventually ten additional slaveholding states.

American Civil War

The Civil War transformed Sumner from a member of an embattled antislavery minority into one of the most influential members of the Republican-controlled Senate.

Sumner argued early in the conflict that preservation of the Union and destruction of slavery were inseparable objectives.

He believed slavery provided economic resources, labor, social organization, and political motivation to the Confederate rebellion. Destroying the institution would therefore serve both a military and a moral purpose.

Sumner repeatedly pressed the Lincoln administration and Congress toward broader emancipation policies and opposed compromises that might preserve slavery in exchange for restoring the Union.

Senate Foreign Relations Committee

In 1861, Sumner became chairman of the Senate Committee on Foreign Relations, one of the most powerful positions in Congress. He remained chairman until 1871.

The position gave him substantial influence over diplomacy during the Civil War, when preventing European recognition or military support for the Confederacy was a major Union objective.

Britain and France possessed significant economic and strategic interests connected to the conflict. Sumner's knowledge of European politics and international law made him an important congressional participant in maintaining foreign relations during the crisis.

Trent Affair

One of the most dangerous diplomatic crises of the Civil War developed in late 1861 during the Trent Affair.

United States naval officers removed Confederate diplomats James Mason and John Slidell from the British mail vessel Trent.

Britain protested the seizure and demanded their release. For a period, observers feared that the dispute could lead to war between the United States and Britain.

Sumner used his position as chairman of the Foreign Relations Committee to support a diplomatic resolution rather than escalation. The Lincoln administration eventually released the Confederate envoys, helping prevent another major international conflict.

Emancipation

Sumner consistently pressed for national emancipation during the Civil War.

When Lincoln issued the Emancipation Proclamation effective January 1, 1863, Sumner welcomed the measure but regarded it as incomplete.

The proclamation rested on presidential war powers and applied principally to enslaved people in areas under Confederate control. Sumner believed slavery required permanent constitutional abolition throughout the entire United States.

He therefore continued pressing Congress for a constitutional amendment abolishing the institution.

Black Military Service

Sumner supported the enlistment of African American soldiers in the United States military.

Black military service challenged assumptions that the Civil War was exclusively a white conflict and strengthened demands that African American men who fought for the nation receive citizenship and political rights.

Massachusetts became particularly important through the formation of the 54th Massachusetts Volunteer Infantry, one of the most famous Black regiments of the war.

The service of Black soldiers reinforced Sumner's argument that the postwar United States could not legitimately deny equal citizenship to men who had helped preserve the Union.

Thirteenth Amendment

Sumner supported constitutional abolition and participated in the congressional movement that produced the Thirteenth Amendment.

He wanted language that would not merely eliminate slavery but affirm broader equality before the law. Other senators considered such language too expansive.

Congress ultimately adopted language prohibiting slavery and involuntary servitude, except as punishment for crime following conviction.

The Senate passed the proposed amendment in April 1864. The House approved it in January 1865, and the states ratified the Thirteenth Amendment in December 1865.

Sumner supported the amendment even though its language did not incorporate the broader equality principle he had preferred.

Relationship with Abraham Lincoln

Sumner and President Abraham Lincoln shared the objective of destroying slavery but frequently differed over strategy, timing, and political compromise.

Sumner generally pressed Lincoln toward more rapid and comprehensive action on emancipation and Black citizenship. Lincoln, responsible for holding together a broad wartime coalition, often moved more cautiously.

Despite these differences, the two developed a working political relationship. Sumner supported major emancipation policies and defended the necessity of transforming the war into a struggle against slavery.

Sumner was among those present at Lincoln's deathbed after the president was shot in April 1865.

Reconstruction

The end of the Civil War did not end Sumner's campaign for racial equality. He argued that abolition was only the first stage of creating a genuinely free political system.

Millions of formerly enslaved people needed legal protection, education, economic opportunity, political representation, and protection from violence.

Sumner rejected the idea that former Confederate states should immediately regain full political authority without guaranteeing the rights of Black citizens.

He became one of the leading Radical Republicans advocating congressional control over Reconstruction and strong federal protections for freedpeople.

Opposition to Andrew Johnson

Sumner became a major opponent of President Andrew Johnson's Reconstruction policies.

Johnson favored rapid restoration of former Confederate states and granted broad pardons to many former Confederates. Southern governments established under his approach enacted Black Codes restricting the rights and economic independence of African Americans.

Sumner and other Radical Republicans argued that Johnson's policies endangered emancipation and risked restoring political power to the white Southern elite that had supported secession.

Sumner therefore supported congressional Reconstruction and later supported Johnson's impeachment and removal from office.

The House impeached Johnson in 1868, but the Senate fell one vote short of the two-thirds majority required for conviction.

Black Suffrage

Sumner regarded voting rights as essential to protecting freedom.

Without political power, he argued, African Americans would remain vulnerable to state governments, employers, former slaveholders, and organized racial violence.

His position placed him ahead of many politicians who supported abolition but remained unwilling to endorse equal Black political participation.

The eventual ratification of the Fifteenth Amendment in 1870 prohibited governments from denying the right to vote on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude.

Sumner nevertheless continued arguing that voting rights alone could not provide equality without protection in education, transportation, public accommodations, jury service, and other elements of civic life.

Fourteenth Amendment

Sumner supported the central objective of establishing national citizenship and equal legal protection for African Americans, but he initially criticized the proposed Fourteenth Amendment because he believed it failed to guarantee Black voting rights explicitly.

He feared that political compromise would permit states to maintain racially restrictive voting systems.

Sumner ultimately voted for the amendment. Ratified in 1868, the Fourteenth Amendment established national citizenship and prohibited states from denying due process and equal protection of the laws.

Its Equal Protection Clause later became one of the principal constitutional foundations of federal civil rights law.

Equal Education

Sumner's commitment to integrated education continued from Roberts v. City of Boston through Reconstruction.

He rejected the argument that government could satisfy equality merely by providing separate institutions for different races. Segregation itself, in his analysis, placed a mark of inferiority on Black citizens.

Consequently, Sumner sought to include public schools within federal civil rights legislation after the Civil War.

This position encountered substantial resistance even among Republicans who supported voting rights and other Reconstruction measures.

Civil Rights Bill

In 1870, Sumner introduced what became the central legislative project of his final years: a comprehensive federal civil rights bill.

His proposal sought equal treatment regardless of race in public accommodations and other areas of civic life. Versions of the measure addressed access to facilities such as inns, transportation, theaters, schools, churches, cemeteries, and jury service.

Sumner viewed such legislation as necessary because the formal abolition of slavery had not eliminated racial discrimination.

For him, laws and customs enforcing racial separation represented remnants of the slave system and were incompatible with equal citizenship.

The bill repeatedly encountered congressional opposition during Sumner's lifetime.

Conflict with President Ulysses S. Grant

Sumner's relationship with President Ulysses S. Grant deteriorated sharply during the early 1870s.

The most important conflict involved Grant's effort to annex the Dominican Republic, then commonly referred to in American politics as Santo Domingo.

Sumner opposed annexation and used his position as chairman of the Senate Foreign Relations Committee to resist the administration's policy.

The dispute became personal as well as political. Grant's allies ultimately supported removing Sumner from the chairmanship of the Foreign Relations Committee in 1871.

Losing the chairmanship substantially reduced Sumner's institutional power and contributed to his growing isolation from the Republican leadership.

Opposition to Expansionism

Sumner's opposition to the annexation of Santo Domingo reflected a broader concern about territorial expansion and the exercise of American power abroad.

His position was shaped by international law, opposition to imperial expansion, and concern that powerful states should not absorb weaker nations through political pressure.

This dimension of Sumner's career connected his earlier involvement in peace reform with his later leadership in foreign policy.

Political Isolation

By the early 1870s, Sumner had become increasingly isolated within the Republican Party.

His uncompromising approach to civil rights frustrated colleagues seeking political reconciliation with former Confederates, while his conflict with Grant alienated members loyal to the presidential administration.

Sumner nevertheless refused to abandon the civil rights program that had defined much of his career.

He continued introducing and advocating his civil rights bill despite declining health and reduced political influence.

Relationship with Frederick Douglass

Frederick Douglass and Charles Sumner became important allies in the antislavery and civil rights struggles.

Their relationship reflected Sumner's unusually strong commitment, for a major white politician of his period, to treating African American leaders as political participants rather than merely subjects of reform.

Douglass respected Sumner's long defense of Black citizenship even though the two men did not agree on every political question.

Sumner's civil rights legislation remained a central concern during his final days, and Douglass was among those associated with the effort to preserve the measure after Sumner's death.

Relationship with Henry Wadsworth Longfellow

Poet Henry Wadsworth Longfellow was one of Sumner's closest lifelong friends.

Their friendship lasted for decades and provided Sumner with an important personal and intellectual relationship outside formal politics.

Sumner was a frequent visitor at Longfellow's Cambridge home, and the two exchanged extensive correspondence concerning politics, literature, reform, international affairs, and personal matters.

Longfellow supported Sumner during the political controversies and health crises that followed the 1856 caning.

Death

Charles Sumner died in Washington, D.C., on March 11, 1874, at the age of 63 following heart problems.

His civil rights bill remained unfinished at the time of his death. According to accounts preserved by the Senate, Sumner continued expressing concern that the measure not be allowed to fail.

His body lay in state in the United States Capitol Rotunda, an honor granted to relatively few Americans.

Thousands of people paid their respects in Washington and along the route by which his remains were returned to Massachusetts.

At the Massachusetts State House, members of the 54th Massachusetts Infantry, the celebrated African American Civil War regiment, participated in the ceremonies honoring him.

Sumner was buried at Mount Auburn Cemetery in Cambridge, Massachusetts.

Civil Rights Act of 1875

Sumner did not live to see Congress enact his civil rights proposal.

Following his death, supporters continued pressing for passage. Congress eventually approved a reduced version, and President Ulysses S. Grant signed the Civil Rights Act of 1875 on March 1, 1875.

The law prohibited racial discrimination in specified public accommodations and addressed exclusion from jury service.

Congress removed the provision requiring integrated public schools, one of the strongest components of Sumner's original program, before final passage.

In the Civil Rights Cases of 1883, the United States Supreme Court invalidated major portions of the act. The Court concluded that the Fourteenth Amendment restricted discriminatory action by states but did not provide Congress with the authority asserted in the statute to regulate many forms of private discrimination.

Federal legislation of comparable scope would not emerge again until the twentieth-century Civil Rights Movement.

Legacy

Charles Sumner occupies a distinctive place in American political history because his career connected the antebellum abolitionist struggle directly to Reconstruction-era civil rights politics.

Before the Civil War, his central objective was the destruction of slavery. After emancipation, he immediately turned toward the larger question of what freedom required.

Sumner's answer was unusually expansive for his era. He advocated citizenship, voting rights, equal legal protection, integrated education, access to public accommodations, jury service, and federal intervention when state governments failed to protect African Americans.

His critics regarded him as self-righteous, inflammatory, inflexible, and unwilling to accept political compromise. Even fellow Republicans sometimes found his demands politically unrealistic or his personal style antagonistic.

Those same characteristics also explain much of his historical importance. Sumner repeatedly demanded policies that initially appeared too radical for congressional majorities but later became fundamental components of American civil rights law.

The 1856 caning remains the most visually dramatic episode of his career, but it can obscure the larger significance of his work. Sumner served another fifteen years after returning to active duty and devoted much of that period to emancipation and racial equality.

His advocacy demonstrates that the struggle following slavery was not simply a dispute over whether formerly enslaved people were technically free. It concerned the deeper question of whether freedom would include equal membership in American political and civic life.

Historical Significance

Sumner's political career illustrates the evolution of American antislavery politics from opposition to slavery's expansion into a broader program of abolition and equal citizenship.

During the 1850s, he became one of the most visible symbols of the breakdown of political compromise over slavery. The attack by Preston Brooks demonstrated that sectional conflict had moved beyond legislative disagreement into physical violence within Congress itself.

During the Civil War, Sumner helped push national policy toward permanent abolition. During Reconstruction, he argued that the federal government possessed both the authority and obligation to protect African Americans against discriminatory state laws, racial violence, and exclusion from civic institutions.

His unsuccessful school-desegregation argument in the 1840s and his postwar civil rights program also connect the abolitionist era to legal principles that became central to the twentieth-century Civil Rights Movement.

Sumner therefore belongs not only to the history of abolition and the Civil War but also to the longer history of American civil rights law.

Geographic Scope

Sumner's life was centered principally in Boston, Cambridge, and Washington, D.C., but his work involved political questions extending across the United States and internationally.

Massachusetts provided the institutional base for his legal, abolitionist, and political career. Washington became the center of his work after his election to the Senate in 1851.

Kansas became symbolically important because the struggle over slavery there produced Sumner's “Crime Against Kansas” speech and indirectly triggered the assault that made him nationally famous.

His leadership of the Foreign Relations Committee also connected his career to Britain, France, the Caribbean, and the wider diplomatic environment of the Civil War and Reconstruction eras.

Legal Context

Sumner's career developed during a fundamental transformation in the constitutional status of slavery and African American citizenship.

Before the Civil War, federal law protected important slaveholding interests through measures including the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850. The Supreme Court's decision in Dred Scott v. Sandford in 1857 further denied national citizenship to African Americans and restricted congressional authority over slavery in federal territories.

The Civil War and Reconstruction produced a radically different constitutional system.

The Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery. The Fourteenth Amendment established national citizenship and equal protection. The Fifteenth Amendment prohibited racial discrimination in voting.

Sumner argued that these constitutional changes should support broad congressional authority to prohibit racial discrimination in public life.

The Supreme Court rejected substantial portions of this approach in the Civil Rights Cases of 1883, limiting federal power to regulate private discrimination under the Fourteenth Amendment.

The constitutional debate over federal civil rights enforcement would continue well into the twentieth century.
