Andrew Goodman

White Jewish civil-rights activist and Queens College student murdered with James Chaney and Michael Schwerner during Mississippi Freedom Summer in 1964

Overview

Andrew Goodman was a white Jewish American college student and civil-rights activist who volunteered for the 1964 Mississippi Freedom Summer campaign and was murdered by Ku Klux Klan members together with James Earl Chaney and Michael Henry Schwerner on June 21, 1964.

Goodman was born in New York City on November 23, 1943, to Robert and Carolyn Goodman. He grew up in a politically engaged household that encouraged social responsibility, racial integration, intellectual independence, and participation in public life.

From early childhood through high school, Goodman attended the progressive and integrated Walden School in Manhattan. As a teenager he participated in the 1958 Youth March for Integrated Schools in Washington, D.C., studied poverty in West Virginia coal communities, joined civil-rights demonstrations, and developed interests in theater, literature, anthropology, racial inequality, and economic justice.

Goodman briefly attended the University of Wisconsin before returning to New York. He later enrolled at Queens College, where he majored in anthropology. In spring 1964 he volunteered for the Mississippi Summer Project, commonly called Freedom Summer, a major campaign organized through the Council of Federated Organizations to challenge Black voter disfranchisement and build Freedom Schools and political institutions in Mississippi.

Freedom Summer deliberately recruited large numbers of northern college students, including white students, to work alongside experienced Black Mississippians and movement organizers. The strategy was controversial but purposeful: organizers hoped that the presence of northern volunteers would draw national media and federal attention to the violence and political repression Black Mississippians had endured for generations.

Goodman trained with other volunteers in Oxford, Ohio, in June 1964. There he joined Michael Schwerner, an experienced Congress of Racial Equality organizer, and James Chaney, a Black Mississippi CORE activist, on an emergency trip to investigate the Ku Klux Klan's burning of Mount Zion Methodist Church in Neshoba County and the beating of Black parishioners.

Goodman arrived in Mississippi on June 20, 1964. The next day, June 21, Chaney, Goodman, and Schwerner travelled from Meridian to the Mount Zion community, interviewed residents, and then began the return trip through Philadelphia, Mississippi.

Neshoba County deputy sheriff Cecil Ray Price arrested the three men, officially accusing Chaney of speeding while holding Goodman and Schwerner for investigation. Price was connected to the local Ku Klux Klan. The men were jailed until after dark and released at approximately 10:30 p.m.

Their release was part of a prearranged Klan conspiracy. Price and Klansmen pursued the station wagon, stopped the activists on a rural road, and delivered them to an armed Klan group. The three men were taken to Rock Cut Road and shot to death.

Their bodies were placed in the station wagon, transported to the Old Jolly Farm owned by Olen Burrage, and buried beneath an earthen dam under construction. The station wagon was then burned and abandoned.

The disappearance triggered a massive federal investigation known by the FBI code name MIBURN, short for Mississippi Burning. The burned station wagon was found on June 23, but the bodies remained missing for forty-four days.

On August 4, 1964, acting on information from a confidential source, federal agents excavated the dam and recovered the bodies of Chaney, Goodman, and Schwerner. Goodman was twenty years old.

The case became one of the most publicized acts of white-supremacist terrorism of the civil-rights era. The intense national attention also exposed an uncomfortable racial disparity: violence against Black Mississippians had often received little sustained national coverage, whereas the disappearance of two white northern volunteers alongside Chaney immediately mobilized national media and federal resources.

Mississippi did not prosecute the killers for murder in 1964. The federal government instead pursued civil-rights conspiracy charges because ordinary murder prosecution remained primarily a state responsibility. After major litigation culminating in United States v. Price, federal prosecutors brought the conspirators to trial in 1967.

Seven defendants were convicted at the 1967 federal trial, and participant James Jordan separately pleaded guilty, producing eight federal convictions connected to the conspiracy. Sentences were limited by the civil-rights statutes then available and ranged from three to ten years. No defendant was convicted of murder in that proceeding.

One important Klan organizer, Edgar Ray Killen, escaped conviction in 1967 when the jury failed to reach a verdict. Mississippi reopened the case decades later, and on June 21, 2005—forty-one years to the day after the murders—a state jury convicted Killen of three counts of manslaughter. He received three consecutive twenty-year sentences.

Goodman's parents, Robert and Carolyn Goodman, transformed grief into continued civic work. In 1966 they established the Andrew Goodman Foundation to carry forward the ideals of democratic participation, civil rights, and youth activism associated with their son.

Goodman, Chaney, and Schwerner were posthumously awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom by President Barack Obama in 2014.

Goodman's historical importance rests not on the length of his activist career—he was murdered almost immediately after reaching Mississippi—but on what his choice represented. He was a young northern student who voluntarily entered a dangerous movement led largely by Black Southerners and accepted personal risk in opposition to a political system that did not directly disfranchise him.

The most accurate way to remember Goodman is alongside Chaney and Schwerner and within the much larger Freedom Summer movement. Their murders became national symbols, but hundreds of local Black Mississippians had already endured firing, eviction, beatings, bombings, arrests, and murder for challenging white supremacy. Goodman's sacrifice became meaningful because it was part of that larger struggle rather than separate from it.

Birth in New York City

Andrew Goodman was born on November 23, 1943, in New York City.

He was the son of Robert Goodman, a writer and civil engineer associated with his family's construction business, and Carolyn Goodman, a psychologist and social activist.

The family possessed economic security and substantial access to education, giving Goodman opportunities very different from those available to most Black Mississippians he later sought to assist.

Robert and Carolyn Goodman

Robert and Carolyn Goodman created a household in which political discussion and social questions were ordinary parts of family life.

Carolyn had a long record of social activism and professional work in psychology.

The Goodmans supported progressive causes and encouraged their children to examine racial and economic injustice rather than remain insulated from it.

Jewish Identity and Social Justice

Goodman was raised in a Jewish family during a period when many American Jews were active in labor, civil-liberties, and civil-rights causes.

Jewish identity alone did not determine political views, and Goodman should not be treated as representative of all Jewish Americans.

In his case, however, family culture, education, and awareness of discrimination contributed to an ethical outlook that made racial inequality a personal concern.

The Walden School

Beginning at approximately age three, Goodman attended the Walden School in Manhattan and remained there through twelfth grade.

Walden was progressive, academically experimental, and racially integrated at a time when many American schools remained formally or informally segregated.

The school encouraged students to connect classroom study with contemporary social problems.

Early Exposure to Integration

Goodman's schooling placed him in sustained contact with classmates from different racial and social backgrounds.

That experience differed sharply from the legally segregated education system still operating across much of the South.

Integration was therefore part of his ordinary educational life rather than only an abstract political principle.

1958 Youth March for Integrated Schools

In 1958, Goodman participated in the Youth March for Integrated Schools in Washington, D.C.

The demonstration brought thousands of young people to the capital to demand implementation of the Supreme Court's school-desegregation decisions.

Goodman was still a teenager, but the event placed him within organized civil-rights activism several years before Freedom Summer.

Listening Across the Racial Divide

Accounts from Goodman's family and school years describe him deliberately seeking conversations about racial experience rather than assuming that his own political sympathies gave him complete understanding.

During travel to the 1958 youth march, he reportedly chose to sit beside a Black passenger and spent hours asking about the man's experiences with racism.

The anecdote became part of family memory because it reflected Goodman's curiosity and willingness to learn directly from people affected by discrimination.

West Virginia Poverty Study

As a high-school senior, Goodman and friend Ralph Engelman investigated poverty in West Virginia coal communities.

They interviewed miners, elected officials, and labor representatives.

Their project connected economic inequality to institutional structures and encouraged Goodman to think beyond charity toward questions of political and economic power.

Theater and Acting

Goodman was strongly interested in theater and performance.

In 1961, he appeared in an Off-Broadway production of Russian playwright Nikolai Evreinov's The Chief Thing.

For a period he considered drama as a possible professional direction.

University of Wisconsin

After high school, Goodman briefly attended the University of Wisconsin.

Illness interrupted his studies, and he returned to New York.

His academic path then shifted toward Queens College, where his political and intellectual interests became increasingly focused on anthropology and social inequality.

Queens College

Goodman enrolled at Queens College in New York City in 1962.

Queens College had a strong tradition of public education and growing student involvement in civil-rights activism.

Goodman's association with the college later became an important part of the institution's historical identity.

Anthropology

Goodman majored in anthropology at Queens College.

The discipline appealed to his interest in culture, inequality, human difference, and the relationship between institutions and everyday life.

Queens College's anthropology department later remembered his participation in Freedom Summer as an example of applying learning to public service.

Civil-Rights Study and Student Activism

Goodman read and wrote about race while in college and participated in demonstrations.

His surviving papers include academic work on the Black Muslim movement and other social questions.

The record shows that his decision to join Freedom Summer was not an impulsive act disconnected from earlier interests.

1964 World's Fair Protest

In April 1964, Goodman participated in a CORE demonstration connected to the New York World's Fair at Flushing Meadows.

Activists criticized the fair's optimistic theme of international understanding while racial segregation and civil-rights violence remained unresolved in the United States.

The protest placed Goodman in direct contact with organized CORE activism shortly before he applied for Freedom Summer.

Recruitment for Freedom Summer

Civil-rights organizers visited northern colleges in spring 1964 to recruit volunteers for the Mississippi Summer Project.

At Queens College, Goodman encountered movement speakers and recruitment appeals associated with Mississippi activists and national organizers.

Only a small number of students who attended one recruitment session applied; Goodman was among them.

Goodman's Criticism of Northern Apathy

Shortly before joining the project, Goodman criticized what he viewed as indifference among white Northerners toward the denial of Black civil rights.

His concern was that national political delay remained possible partly because people outside the South could treat racial oppression as someone else's problem.

Volunteering for Mississippi was one way of rejecting that distance.

Asking His Parents' Permission

Because Goodman was twenty years old, he sought his parents' permission to join Freedom Summer.

The project required volunteers to understand that they could face arrest, violence, and serious personal danger.

His parents agreed despite knowing that Mississippi civil-rights workers had already been beaten, jailed, threatened, and killed.

Freedom Summer

Freedom Summer, formally the Mississippi Summer Project, was a large 1964 campaign aimed at challenging Black voter suppression and the political isolation of Mississippi's Black communities.

The project involved voter registration, Freedom Schools, community centers, political education, legal support, and organizing connected to the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party.

It brought together local Black activists and hundreds of outside volunteers.

Council of Federated Organizations

Freedom Summer operated through the Council of Federated Organizations, commonly known as COFO.

COFO brought together major civil-rights groups operating in Mississippi, especially the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee, Congress of Racial Equality, NAACP, and Southern Christian Leadership Conference.

The coalition helped coordinate work in a state where local activists had already spent years building movement infrastructure.

Black Disfranchisement in Mississippi

Mississippi had one of the most effective systems of Black voter suppression in the country.

Registrars used literacy tests, arbitrary interpretation requirements, economic retaliation, intimidation, and violence to prevent eligible Black citizens from registering.

In many communities, attempting to register could cost a person employment, credit, housing, land access, or physical safety.

Why Recruit White Northern Students?

Movement leaders debated the decision to recruit large numbers of white northern students.

Supporters believed that their presence would attract journalists, lawyers, political pressure, money, and federal attention that Black Mississippians had repeatedly been denied.

Critics worried that white volunteers might receive attention at the expense of local Black organizers or unintentionally reproduce racial hierarchy within the movement.

The murders of Chaney, Goodman, and Schwerner demonstrated both sides of the strategy: national attention surged, but the disparity in attention itself exposed how white lives were often valued differently by the national press.

Training in Oxford, Ohio

Freedom Summer volunteers attended intensive training sessions at Western College for Women in Oxford, Ohio.

They learned about Mississippi politics, nonviolent discipline, voter registration, teaching, security, movement culture, and the likelihood of violent resistance.

Training did not remove the danger; it was designed to ensure volunteers understood it.

Michael Schwerner

Michael Schwerner was a twenty-four-year-old white Jewish CORE organizer from New York who had already been working in Mississippi.

He and his wife Rita had organized in Meridian and surrounding communities.

Schwerner had become a particular target of local Ku Klux Klan members because of his civil-rights work.

James Earl Chaney

James Earl Chaney was a twenty-one-year-old Black Mississippian from Meridian and an experienced local CORE activist.

He possessed knowledge of local roads, communities, racial customs, and political danger that outside volunteers could not acquire quickly.

Chaney's role is essential to understanding the trio: Goodman was a newcomer, Schwerner an experienced outside organizer, and Chaney a local Black activist working within his own state.

Mount Zion Methodist Church

Mount Zion Methodist Church in Neshoba County was a Black church whose members had discussed allowing civil-rights workers to use the building for movement activity and a Freedom School.

Schwerner and Chaney had worked with members of the congregation.

The church therefore became a target for white-supremacist retaliation.

The June 16 Klan Attack

On June 16, 1964, armed Klansmen went to Mount Zion looking for civil-rights workers.

When they did not find the organizers they sought, they beat Black parishioners.

The church was later burned.

Investigators concluded that some Klan members believed the attack might lure Schwerner back into Neshoba County.

Leaving Ohio for Mississippi

Schwerner and Chaney learned about the Mount Zion attack while involved in the Ohio training program.

Goodman joined them for the trip south.

The three reached Meridian by June 20, 1964.

Goodman's First Day in Mississippi

Goodman arrived in Mississippi on June 20.

He had almost no time to begin the long-term voter-registration and educational work for which he had volunteered.

He was killed the following night.

The Last Postcard Home

Goodman mailed a postcard to his parents from Mississippi that was postmarked June 21, the day he was murdered.

The card became one of the most poignant surviving primary documents from his life.

Its ordinary character emphasizes how little time separated his arrival from the lethal conspiracy already surrounding the movement.

June 21 Trip to Neshoba County

On June 21, Chaney drove Goodman and Schwerner from Meridian into Neshoba County to inspect the burned Mount Zion church and interview members of the community.

The men understood that the area was dangerous.

They intended to return to Meridian later that day and had movement contacts expecting them.

Philadelphia, Mississippi

Their return route passed through Philadelphia, the Neshoba County seat.

The town and surrounding county contained law-enforcement officials and civilians connected to the Ku Klux Klan.

The activists' presence was quickly noticed.

Deputy Sheriff Cecil Price

Neshoba County deputy sheriff Cecil Ray Price stopped and arrested the three activists.

Price was involved with the local Klan network.

The official traffic justification was speeding, with Chaney accused as the driver; Goodman and Schwerner were also detained for investigation.

Detention in the Neshoba County Jail

The three men were taken to the Neshoba County jail.

They were prevented from making the ordinary contacts that might have alerted colleagues immediately to their detention.

Keeping them in custody until nightfall gave Klansmen time to assemble.

A Murder Conspiracy Forms

While the activists were jailed, Klan organizers mobilized men from the Philadelphia and Meridian areas.

Edgar Ray Killen played a central organizational role in gathering participants.

The conspiracy depended upon cooperation between private Klansmen and local law enforcement.

Release After Dark

At approximately 10:30 p.m., Price released Chaney, Goodman, and Schwerner.

They drove toward Meridian in their station wagon.

The release did not free them from danger; it initiated the next phase of the prearranged attack.

The Chase

Klan participants followed the activists as they left Philadelphia.

Price also pursued them in his patrol vehicle.

The activists were stopped again after leaving town and placed under the control of the Klan group.

Rock Cut Road

The killers took Chaney, Goodman, and Schwerner to a secluded location on Rock Cut Road.

Federal evidence later reconstructed the participation of multiple Klansmen at the scene.

The three civil-rights workers were shot and killed.

The Shooting of Goodman

According to a confession later used by investigators, Klansman Alton Wayne Roberts shot Michael Schwerner and then Andrew Goodman.

Other testimony and confessions differed on some details of the murders, but the evidence consistently established an organized Klan execution.

Goodman died from gunfire at age twenty.

The Killing of James Chaney

Evidence from the federal investigation identified James Jordan as one of the men responsible for shooting Chaney, while accounts also described severe violence directed at Chaney.

The racial language attributed to participants underscored that the killings were explicitly white-supremacist.

Because some later popular accounts add details not uniformly supported by the surviving forensic and trial record, descriptions of Chaney's treatment should remain tied closely to documented evidence.

Disposal of the Bodies

The killers placed the three bodies in the activists' station wagon and transported them to the Old Jolly Farm outside Philadelphia.

The property belonged to Olen Burrage.

The bodies were buried beneath an earthen dam then under construction.

Burning the Station Wagon

The station wagon was later burned and abandoned away from the burial site.

The destruction was intended to conceal evidence and mislead searchers.

The burned vehicle was discovered on June 23.

The Missing-Person Alarm

COFO workers became alarmed when the three men failed to return to Meridian as scheduled.

Civil-rights representatives contacted federal officials and demanded an investigation.

Local law enforcement initially denied holding men matching their description despite Price's earlier arrest of the three.

Federal Intervention

The Department of Justice requested FBI involvement, and Attorney General Robert F. Kennedy directed an intensive federal search.

The federal response grew rapidly because the case had enormous national political implications and local law enforcement itself appeared suspect.

President Lyndon B. Johnson also pressed for an aggressive investigation.

MIBURN: Mississippi Burning

The FBI code-named the investigation MIBURN, short for Mississippi Burning.

Hundreds of federal agents, military personnel, investigators, and support staff became involved.

The case contributed to establishment of a permanent FBI field office in Jackson, Mississippi.

The Burned Car Is Found

On June 23, investigators located the charred station wagon used by Chaney, Goodman, and Schwerner.

No bodies were inside.

The discovery intensified fears that the men had been murdered.

Forty-Four Days Missing

The search continued through late June and July.

Swamps, roads, fields, and rural properties were examined while federal agents pressured Klan members and developed informants.

For the Goodman family, the forty-four-day period meant living without official confirmation of whether Andrew was alive or dead.

Other Black Victims and Unequal Attention

The search for the three activists drew attention to a larger landscape of racial violence.

Search efforts in Mississippi uncovered evidence connected to other missing or murdered Black people whose disappearances had not generated comparable national mobilization.

This contrast became one of the most important lessons of the case: Goodman and Schwerner's whiteness helped produce a level of national concern that Black victims of white-supremacist violence had often been denied.

Recognizing that disparity does not diminish Goodman or Schwerner. It places their deaths within the racial system they had gone south to oppose.

Discovery of the Bodies

A confidential source eventually provided reliable information that the bodies had been buried in a dam on the Old Jolly Farm.

Federal investigators obtained a search warrant and began excavation.

On August 4, 1964, the bodies of Chaney, Goodman, and Schwerner were recovered from deep beneath the earthen structure.

Identification and Return Home

The recovery ended weeks of uncertainty for the families.

Goodman's remains were returned to New York.

The discovery also transformed the case from a disappearance investigation into a publicly undeniable triple murder.

Goodman's Funeral

Goodman's funeral was held on August 9, 1964, at the meetinghouse of the New York Society for Ethical Culture.

Friends, family members, activists, and civic figures memorialized him as a young person who had chosen participation over indifference.

He was buried the same day.

Mount Judah Cemetery

Andrew Goodman was buried at Mount Judah Cemetery in Ridgewood, Queens, New York.

Cemetery records place his grave in Section 2.

His burial site became one of several physical memorials connecting New York to the Freedom Summer murders.

Hate Mail to the Goodman Family

The Goodman family received hate mail after Andrew's murder.

The letters demonstrated that racist hostility was not confined geographically to Mississippi.

The family's preservation of some of this material provides evidence of the antagonism faced even by relatives of murdered civil-rights workers.

Why Mississippi Did Not Prosecute Murder in 1964

Ordinary murder prosecution was primarily the responsibility of Mississippi state authorities.

The state's criminal-justice institutions had a long history of failing to convict white people for violence against civil-rights activists and Black victims.

No immediate state murder prosecution brought the killers to justice.

Federal Civil-Rights Charges

The federal government did not then possess a general murder statute that automatically covered the killings.

Federal prosecutors therefore used criminal civil-rights statutes, charging conspirators with depriving the victims of federal rights and conspiring with officials acting under color of law.

The legal theory reflected both the ingenuity and the limitations of federal civil-rights enforcement at the time.

United States v. Price

Federal indictments faced an important legal challenge over whether private Klansmen could be prosecuted under civil-rights statutes together with law-enforcement officers.

The Supreme Court held in United States v. Price that private participants could be prosecuted when they conspired with state officials to deprive victims of constitutional rights.

The ruling allowed the federal prosecution to proceed against the broader conspiracy.

The 1967 Federal Trial

The federal conspiracy trial began in Meridian in October 1967.

The government presented Klan insiders, law-enforcement evidence, confessions, records, and testimony reconstructing the plot.

The trial was historically significant because a Mississippi jury was being asked to convict white supremacists and local officials for violence against civil-rights workers.

Federal Convictions

Seven defendants were convicted at the 1967 trial: Cecil Price, Alton Wayne Roberts, H. D. Barnette, Billy Wayne Posey, Jimmy Arledge, Jimmy Snowden, and Klan leader Sam Bowers.

James Jordan, a participant and government witness, separately pleaded guilty.

Taken together, the federal effort produced eight convictions connected to the conspiracy.

Limited Sentences

Because the defendants were convicted under the federal civil-rights conspiracy law rather than for murder, sentences were limited.

The most severe sentences imposed at trial were ten years; others received six- or three-year terms, while Jordan received four years after pleading guilty.

The disparity between three murders and relatively short sentences became another symbol of the weakness of civil-rights enforcement during the period.

Edgar Ray Killen Escapes Conviction in 1967

Edgar Ray Killen was one of the principal organizers targeted by federal prosecutors.

The 1967 jury failed to reach a verdict against him.

Contemporary accounts later reported that a juror was unwilling to convict a Baptist preacher.

The Cold Case Reopens

Decades later, journalists, family members, civil-rights advocates, and government investigators continued pressing for accountability.

Investigative reporting by Mississippi journalist Jerry Mitchell helped revive public attention to unresolved civil-rights murders.

State and federal authorities reopened aspects of the Chaney-Goodman-Schwerner case.

Edgar Ray Killen's 2005 Trial

Mississippi indicted Killen on murder charges in 2005.

The trial began in Neshoba County on June 13.

The prosecution relied heavily on the historical record, witness testimony, Klan evidence, and statements attributed to Killen.

Manslaughter Convictions

On June 21, 2005, exactly forty-one years after the murders, the jury convicted Killen on three counts of manslaughter.

The jury did not convict him of murder.

The verdict represented the first state criminal conviction directly punishing an organizer for the deaths.

Sixty-Year Sentence

Killen received twenty years for each manslaughter conviction.

The sentences were ordered to run consecutively for a total of sixty years.

Given Killen's age, the sentence effectively ensured that he would spend the remainder of his life in prison.

End of the Criminal Investigation

Federal investigators continued reviewing the case under later civil-rights cold-case initiatives.

By 2016, authorities concluded that no further prosecution was realistically possible because potential defendants and critical witnesses had died or the remaining evidence could not meet prosecution standards.

The wider investigation was formally closed.

Killen's Death

Edgar Ray Killen died in Mississippi custody in January 2018.

His death ended the imprisonment of the only person convicted by the state in direct connection with the three killings.

Many participants in the conspiracy had died without facing murder charges.

Freedom Summer Continues After the Murders

The killings did not end Freedom Summer.

Volunteers continued arriving in Mississippi, and local activists continued voter registration, Freedom Schools, community organizing, and political work.

Movement leaders argued that abandoning the campaign would reward the terror intended to stop it.

Freedom Schools

Freedom Schools provided educational programs outside Mississippi's segregated and unequal public-school system.

Teachers and students studied Black history, civics, literature, political rights, and local organizing.

Although Goodman was killed before he could perform his assigned long-term work, he had volunteered for a project in which education was inseparable from political participation.

Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party

Freedom Summer supported the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party, which challenged the legitimacy of Mississippi's all-white regular Democratic delegation.

The MFDP took its challenge to the 1964 Democratic National Convention.

Its campaign exposed the contradiction between national Democratic support for civil rights and continued recognition of a segregated state party.

Civil Rights Act of 1964

The Civil Rights Act of 1964 was signed on July 2, less than two weeks after the three workers disappeared and before their bodies were recovered.

The legislation had already passed through a long national struggle before June 21, so it would be inaccurate to say the murders alone caused its passage.

The disappearance nevertheless intensified national attention to the violence sustaining segregation during the final stage of the legislative battle.

Voting Rights Act of 1965

Freedom Summer helped demonstrate that Black disfranchisement in Mississippi could not be solved simply by asking local officials to administer existing constitutional protections fairly.

The murders reinforced the evidence that voter suppression was maintained through violence and official complicity.

The Voting Rights Act of 1965 created stronger federal tools against discriminatory registration and election practices.

The Meaning of White Allyship

Goodman's life became an enduring example of white participation in a Black-led freedom struggle.

His role should not be described as rescuing Black Mississippians. Local Black communities had organized for years before he arrived and continued after his death.

His contribution was the decision to use the relative privileges available to him—education, mobility, northern connections, and white social status—in support of a movement whose leadership and primary burden were borne by Black Southerners.

Risk and Privilege

Goodman's whiteness gave him privileges denied to James Chaney, but it did not make him safe once he openly challenged Mississippi white supremacy.

The Klan treated white civil-rights workers as racial traitors and sometimes targeted them precisely because interracial organizing threatened the social order.

The case therefore demonstrates that privilege can reduce some forms of danger without eliminating the consequences of choosing to oppose an oppressive system.

James Chaney and the Problem of Unequal Memory

Popular retellings often present Chaney, Goodman, and Schwerner as an inseparable trio, but their social positions were not identical.

Chaney was a Black Mississippian confronting the racial system of his own home state; Goodman had been in Mississippi one day; Schwerner had been organizing there for months.

A complete account should honor all three while preserving those differences rather than converting them into interchangeable symbols.

The Term "Martyr"

Goodman is commonly described as a civil-rights martyr.

The term refers to the symbolic meaning of a person killed because of participation in a moral or political cause.

Goodman did not travel to Mississippi seeking death, and martyrdom should not romanticize the fact that his life was deliberately taken by a terrorist conspiracy.

The term is most useful when it emphasizes the cost imposed by white supremacy rather than portraying murder as necessary for progress.

The Andrew Goodman Foundation

In 1966, Robert and Carolyn Goodman founded the Andrew Goodman Foundation.

The organization was created to continue the spirit and purpose they associated with their son's life.

Carolyn continued its work after Robert's death in 1969.

Carolyn Goodman's Later Activism

Carolyn Goodman became a prominent keeper of Andrew's historical record and an advocate for civil rights and social justice.

She preserved family correspondence, legal materials, photographs, hate mail, and records relating to the murders and prosecutions.

Her archival work helped ensure that Andrew's life did not disappear behind the more famous label "Mississippi Burning."

David Goodman and the Next Generation

Andrew's younger brother David Goodman later became a major leader in preserving and extending the family's civic legacy.

Along with Sylvia Goodman, he helped expand the Andrew Goodman Foundation's work in youth voting and civic participation.

The family's activism transformed private bereavement into a multigenerational public institution.

Vote Everywhere

On the fiftieth anniversary of Freedom Summer, the Andrew Goodman Foundation formally launched the Vote Everywhere program.

The initiative organizes and trains college students around voter registration, voting access, civic participation, and related democracy issues.

The program intentionally links contemporary student activism to Goodman's decision to volunteer as a college student in 1964.

Presidential Medal of Freedom

President Barack Obama posthumously awarded Andrew Goodman, James Chaney, and Michael Schwerner the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2014.

Relatives accepted the honors at the White House.

The award formally placed all three among nationally recognized figures in the history of American civil rights.

Queens College Memory

Queens College has repeatedly commemorated Goodman as one of its students who placed public service above personal safety.

The college's Rosenthal Library clock tower honors Chaney, Goodman, and Schwerner.

The anthropology department remembers Goodman as an example of the college motto, "We learn so that we may serve."

Goodman Mountain

A mountain in New York's Adirondack region was named Goodman Mountain in his memory.

The memorial is connected to an area where the Goodman family had spent time.

The naming extended his remembrance beyond civil-rights museums and legal history into the physical landscape.

Freedom Place Memorial

New York City's Upper West Side contains a Freedom Place memorial honoring James Chaney, Andrew Goodman, and Michael Schwerner.

The memorial reflects the strong connection between the case and New York's civil-rights community.

It also reinforces the practice of commemorating the three together rather than isolating one victim from the others.

Mount Zion Memorial

Mount Zion Church in Mississippi maintains a memorial to Chaney, Goodman, and Schwerner.

The site connects the murdered activists directly to the Black congregation whose beating and burned church brought them into Neshoba County.

Remembering the church members is essential because their suffering preceded and triggered the trip that ended in the murders.

The Film Mississippi Burning

The 1988 film Mississippi Burning took its title from the FBI's MIBURN investigation.

The film brought the murders to a new generation but substantially fictionalized the investigation and centered heroic white FBI agents.

Civil-rights veterans and historians criticized the film for minimizing Black activists, local movement organizing, and the federal government's own uneven civil-rights record.

The movie is therefore useful as evidence of late twentieth-century memory, not as a reliable reconstruction of the actual investigation.

Media Attention and the Value of White Lives

The national press response to the disappearance remains one of the most revealing aspects of the story.

Violence against Black Mississippians had often been ignored or treated as local news.

The disappearance of Goodman and Schwerner—young white men from New York—made it much harder for national institutions to look away.

Civil-rights organizers were painfully aware that the federal attention they sought came partly from a racial hierarchy in whose destruction they were engaged.

The Murders as White-Supremacist Terrorism

The murders were not simply an impulsive act by a few violent individuals.

They involved an organized Ku Klux Klan network, a targeted civil-rights project, local surveillance, law-enforcement participation, coordinated detention and release, pursuit, execution, burial, and destruction of evidence.

Describing the killings as white-supremacist terrorism captures their political purpose: they were intended to intimidate an entire movement and preserve a racial political order.

Law Enforcement Complicity

Deputy sheriff Cecil Price's role demonstrated that civil-rights workers faced danger not only from unofficial mobs but from people exercising public authority.

The participation of law-enforcement personnel was critical to federal jurisdiction and to the constitutional theory later upheld in United States v. Price.

The case remains a major example of why federal civil-rights enforcement became necessary when local institutions themselves participated in racial violence.

Historical Significance

Andrew Goodman's biography connects northern student activism, Jewish participation in the civil-rights movement, Freedom Summer, Black voter suppression, Ku Klux Klan terrorism, federal civil-rights enforcement, media bias, youth activism, and the long struggle for voting rights.

His life is unusually brief in the documentary record because he died at twenty and was killed almost immediately after reaching Mississippi.

That brevity makes it especially important not to invent a longer activist career than he actually had.

His significance lies in the decision to participate, the movement he joined, the conspiracy that killed him, and the political and moral questions his death forced the country to confront.

Legacy

Goodman's legacy has endured because his death became connected to a larger democratic principle: citizens who possess political rights have obligations toward people deliberately denied them.

His example has been invoked to encourage youth voting, public service, racial solidarity, nonviolent activism, and resistance to political apathy.

At the same time, the strongest memorial practice does not make Goodman the central hero of Freedom Summer. It places him beside Chaney and Schwerner and behind the many Black Mississippians whose organizing created the movement he entered.

Remembered in that context, Goodman represents allyship at its most serious: not speaking over the people most affected, but choosing to join their work and accept the risks attached to it.

Historical Record and Common Misconceptions

Goodman was twenty years old, not twenty-one, when he was murdered.

He had been in Mississippi only about one day before his death; he did not spend the summer carrying out a long voter-registration assignment.

Freedom Summer was not created by Goodman, Schwerner, or northern white students. It grew from years of organizing by Black Mississippians and movement organizations coordinated through COFO.

Goodman was a new volunteer; James Chaney was a local Mississippi activist and Michael Schwerner was an experienced CORE organizer already working in the state.

The men were not murdered randomly after a routine traffic stop. The Klan had targeted civil-rights activity, Price's detention facilitated the conspiracy, and the release was coordinated with the killers.

The 1967 federal case did not convict the defendants of murder. It produced civil-rights conspiracy convictions because federal criminal jurisdiction was limited and Mississippi had failed to bring murder prosecutions.

Seven defendants were convicted by the jury in the 1967 federal trial, while James Jordan separately pleaded guilty, producing eight federal convictions associated with the conspiracy.

Edgar Ray Killen was not convicted of murder in 2005. He was convicted of three counts of manslaughter and sentenced to sixty years.

The murders did not single-handedly cause the Civil Rights Act of 1964, which had been the subject of a long legislative struggle and was signed before the bodies were discovered.

Goodman, Chaney, and Schwerner's disappearance did intensify public awareness of Mississippi racial violence and became part of the political context surrounding federal civil-rights and voting-rights enforcement.

The 1988 film Mississippi Burning is highly fictionalized and should not be used as a substitute for the historical record.

  • Late June 21: Price and Klan conspirators pursue, abduct, and murder Chaney, Goodman, and Schwerner on Rock Cut Road.
  • Late June 21-early June 22: Their bodies are buried beneath an earthen dam on the Old Jolly Farm and their station wagon is burned.

  • June 23, 1964: The burned station wagon is discovered.

  • August 4, 1964: Federal agents recover the bodies of Chaney, Goodman, and Schwerner beneath the earthen dam.

  • December 1964: Federal authorities arrest and charge participants in the civil-rights conspiracy.
  • 1965: Federal indictments proceed amid major litigation over the scope of civil-rights law.
  • August 6, 1965: President Johnson signs the Voting Rights Act of 1965.

  • 1966: Robert and Carolyn Goodman establish the Andrew Goodman Foundation.

  • 1988: The film Mississippi Burning dramatizes the FBI investigation while substantially fictionalizing the historical story.
  • 1989: A major twenty-fifth-anniversary memorial is held in New York.

  • June 21, 2005: Edgar Ray Killen is convicted on three counts of manslaughter, forty-one years to the day after the murders.
  • June 2005: Killen receives three consecutive twenty-year prison sentences, totaling sixty years.
  • 2007: Carolyn Goodman dies; David and Sylvia Goodman continue the foundation's work.

  • November 24, 2014: President Barack Obama posthumously awards the Presidential Medal of Freedom to Chaney, Goodman, and Schwerner.
  • 2014: The Andrew Goodman Foundation formally launches Vote Everywhere.
  • 2016: Authorities close the remaining federal investigation after concluding that further prosecutions are not feasible.
  • January 2018: Edgar Ray Killen dies in prison.
  • 2024: Queens College and civil-rights organizations commemorate the sixtieth anniversary of Freedom Summer and the murders.
  • 2026: The Andrew Goodman Foundation continues national youth civic-engagement and voting-rights work.

Geographic Scope

New York City shaped Goodman's childhood, education, political awareness, and family life.

Washington, D.C., became part of his early activism through the Youth March for Integrated Schools.

West Virginia was the site of his teenage investigation into coal-community poverty.

Oxford, Ohio, was the principal Freedom Summer training location where Goodman prepared to work in Mississippi.

Meridian, Philadelphia, Mount Zion Church, Rock Cut Road, and the Old Jolly Farm in Mississippi define the geography of his final days.

His burial and many of his memorials returned the story to New York, while his national legacy is preserved through campuses, civil-rights institutions, and voting-rights programs across the United States.

Legal Context

Goodman entered Mississippi when the Fifteenth Amendment formally prohibited racial discrimination in voting but state and local institutions had developed elaborate methods to evade that command.

Federal civil-rights law before 1964 was often too weak or too difficult to enforce against private racial terrorism and complicit local officials.

Because ordinary murder was principally a state crime, the federal government prosecuted the killers through statutes protecting constitutional rights and prohibiting conspiracies involving state action.

United States v. Price became a major constitutional decision because it confirmed that private Klansmen could be reached by federal law when they conspired with officials acting under color of state law.

The Civil Rights Act of 1964 and Voting Rights Act of 1965 later expanded federal authority to combat segregation and voting discrimination, although neither law alone eliminated racial terrorism.
