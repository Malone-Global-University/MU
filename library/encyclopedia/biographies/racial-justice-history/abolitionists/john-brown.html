John Brown

Militant abolitionist who fought slavery through armed resistance and led the 1859 raid on Harpers Ferry

Overview

John Brown was an American abolitionist who became one of the most consequential and controversial opponents of slavery in the years immediately preceding the American Civil War. Unlike abolitionists who relied primarily on speeches, publications, petitions, political organization, or moral persuasion, Brown ultimately concluded that slavery could be destroyed only through direct action and armed resistance.

Brown first achieved national prominence during the conflict known as Bleeding Kansas, where antislavery and proslavery settlers fought over whether Kansas would enter the United States as a free state or a slave state. Brown and several of his followers participated in violent confrontations with proslavery forces, including the 1856 Pottawatomie killings, in which five proslavery men were killed.

Brown later developed a much more ambitious plan to attack slavery within the slaveholding states themselves. On October 16, 1859, he and 21 followers seized the federal armory and arsenal at Harpers Ferry, Virginia, in an attempt to obtain weapons and initiate an armed liberation movement among enslaved people.

The raid failed within approximately thirty-six hours. United States Marines commanded by Army Colonel Robert E. Lee stormed the armory fire enginehouse where Brown and his surviving followers had taken refuge. Brown was wounded, captured, tried by the Commonwealth of Virginia, convicted of treason, murder, and conspiracy to incite an enslaved uprising, and executed by hanging on December 2, 1859.

Brown's execution transformed him into a powerful symbol. Many abolitionists regarded him as a martyr who willingly died while confronting an institution responsible for the enslavement of millions of people. Many white Southerners instead viewed the raid as evidence that militant Northern abolitionism threatened their lives, property, and social order.

Brown's place in American history remains contested. He employed lethal violence and deliberately attempted to initiate armed resistance against slavery, making his methods fundamentally different from those of many other abolitionists. At the same time, the institution he sought to destroy was itself maintained through systemic violence, coercion, forced labor, family separation, and legal repression.

His historical importance therefore extends beyond whether he is characterized as a hero, martyr, revolutionary, extremist, or terrorist. Brown forced Americans to confront a fundamental question that had increasingly divided the country: whether slavery could be eliminated peacefully or whether the institution would ultimately be destroyed through violence.

Early Life

John Brown was born on May 9, 1800, in Torrington, Connecticut. His parents, Owen Brown and Ruth Mills Brown, belonged to a deeply religious family whose beliefs strongly influenced his development.

When Brown was still a child, his family moved to Hudson, Ohio, then part of the rapidly developing Western Reserve. His father operated a tannery and became an outspoken opponent of slavery. The elder Brown's religious convictions and antislavery beliefs provided an important foundation for his son's later activism.

Brown later recalled that witnessing the mistreatment of an enslaved Black boy during his youth profoundly affected him. Whether treated as a single defining moment or as part of a broader upbringing, opposition to slavery became increasingly connected in Brown's mind with his understanding of Christian duty.

Brown briefly attempted to prepare for the ministry during his youth, but poor eyesight and limited finances prevented him from continuing his formal studies. He instead returned to Ohio and entered the tanning trade.

Family Life

Brown married Dianthe Lusk in 1820. They had seven children before her death in 1832.

In 1833, Brown married Mary Ann Day. They had thirteen children. Across his two marriages, Brown fathered twenty children, although several died during infancy or childhood.

Brown's family became deeply involved in his antislavery activities. Several of his sons accompanied him to Kansas, and multiple family members later participated directly in preparations for the Harpers Ferry operation.

His sons Owen, Watson, and Oliver were among those associated with the Harpers Ferry expedition. Watson and Oliver died from wounds received during the raid, while Owen escaped.

Business Career

Before becoming nationally known as an abolitionist, Brown spent decades attempting to support his large family through a succession of businesses and occupations.

He worked as a tanner, farmer, land surveyor, cattle dealer, wool merchant, and real-estate speculator. His family lived at various times in Ohio, Pennsylvania, Massachusetts, and New York.

Brown experienced repeated financial difficulties. He accumulated substantial debts and declared bankruptcy in the early 1840s. His later partnership in a wool business with Simon Perkins also failed to achieve the commercial success Brown expected.

These business failures contrasted sharply with the organizational discipline and determination he later displayed in the antislavery struggle. By the 1850s, abolitionism had increasingly become the central purpose of his life.

Religious Beliefs and Abolitionism

Brown's abolitionism was rooted strongly in his Christian faith. He regarded slavery not merely as a political problem or an undesirable economic institution but as a profound moral evil contrary to the commands of God.

His religious worldview also influenced his increasingly militant conclusions. Brown believed individuals possessed a moral duty to resist injustice even when governments and established institutions protected it.

Unlike many white Americans of his period who opposed the expansion of slavery while accepting racial hierarchy, Brown developed unusually egalitarian relationships with African Americans. He attended Black churches, worked alongside Black abolitionists, supported African American communities, and advocated social and political equality.

Brown's willingness to treat Black abolitionists as political partners distinguished him from many white reformers of the antebellum period.

Assistance to Freedom Seekers

Brown and members of his family assisted people escaping slavery long before the Harpers Ferry raid.

During the 1820s, Brown operated a tannery in Pennsylvania that reportedly included a concealed space used to shelter freedom seekers travelling northward.

His opposition to laws requiring the return of escaped enslaved people became still more militant following passage of the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850. The law strengthened federal mechanisms for capturing people who had escaped slavery and penalized individuals who assisted them.

Brown believed resistance to slave catchers was morally justified and helped organize Black residents in Springfield, Massachusetts, to defend themselves against attempted capture.

League of Gileadites

While living in Springfield, Massachusetts, Brown became closely connected to the city's African American community.

In response to the Fugitive Slave Act, he helped organize a self-defense group commonly known as the League of Gileadites. Its purpose was to resist attempts to seize Black residents under federal fugitive-slave law.

Brown advised members to organize collectively rather than permit an individual freedom seeker to be taken without resistance. The organization reflected a central feature of his political philosophy: oppressed people and their allies possessed the right to defend freedom by force when legal institutions supported enslavement.

North Elba and Timbuctoo

In 1849, Brown moved his family to North Elba in New York's Adirondack region.

Wealthy abolitionist Gerrit Smith had distributed parcels of land to Black New Yorkers in the area. Ownership of sufficient property could help Black men satisfy New York's discriminatory property requirement for voting.

The settlement became associated with a broader Black farming community sometimes called Timbuctoo.

Brown acquired land there and assisted neighboring Black families with farming, surveying, construction, and adapting to the difficult Adirondack environment.

North Elba later became Brown's final burial place and an important location in the historical memory of his abolitionist career.

Kansas-Nebraska Act

The Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854 intensified the national struggle over slavery by allowing settlers in the territories to determine whether slavery would be permitted through the principle of popular sovereignty.

Both antislavery and proslavery settlers moved into Kansas in an effort to influence its political future. Elections were accompanied by fraud, intimidation, competing governments, armed organizations, raids, and retaliatory violence.

Several of Brown's sons moved to Kansas as free-state settlers. Brown followed in 1855, bringing weapons and intending to help defend antislavery communities.

Kansas became the setting in which Brown moved decisively from militant rhetoric to organized armed action.

Bleeding Kansas

The conflict between proslavery and free-state factions became known as Bleeding Kansas. The territory experienced political instability and recurring episodes of organized violence during the mid-1850s.

Brown joined free-state militias and gained a reputation for determination, personal courage, and an unwillingness to compromise with supporters of slavery.

In his view, previous efforts to oppose slavery through moral persuasion and political compromise had repeatedly failed. Kansas reinforced his conviction that proslavery forces would use violence whenever necessary and therefore had to be met with organized resistance.

Sack of Lawrence

On May 21, 1856, a proslavery force entered the free-state town of Lawrence, Kansas. The attackers destroyed newspaper presses, damaged buildings, and burned the Free State Hotel.

The attack became one of the major episodes in the escalating territorial conflict.

Brown was outraged both by the destruction at Lawrence and by what he considered the inadequate response of free-state leaders. Within days, he participated in an act of violent retaliation that became the most controversial event of his Kansas career.

Pottawatomie Killings

On the night of May 24 and into the early hours of May 25, 1856, Brown and a small group that included several of his sons entered the Pottawatomie Creek area.

Five men associated with the proslavery cause were taken from their homes and killed with swords and firearms.

The victims were James Doyle, two of his sons, William Doyle and Drury Doyle, Allen Wilkinson, and William Sherman.

The killings were deliberate and extrajudicial. Brown regarded the action as part of a broader war against violent proslavery forces, but the men killed had not been tried for crimes, and the episode was condemned even by some antislavery settlers.

The Pottawatomie killings remain essential to evaluating Brown's historical legacy. They demonstrate that his use of violence was not limited to combat against armed soldiers during the Harpers Ferry raid. He was willing to authorize or participate in premeditated lethal action against men he regarded as participants in the proslavery system.

The killings also contributed to a cycle of retaliation that intensified warfare in eastern Kansas.

Battle of Black Jack

On June 2, 1856, Brown and a group of free-state fighters confronted proslavery forces led by Henry Clay Pate near Black Jack, Kansas.

After several hours of fighting, Pate and a number of his men surrendered.

The engagement strengthened Brown's reputation among militant free-state settlers and demonstrated his effectiveness as the leader of a small irregular fighting force.

Battle of Osawatomie

On August 30, 1856, a much larger proslavery force attacked Osawatomie, Kansas.

Brown led a smaller group of defenders in resistance before being forced to withdraw. His son Frederick Brown was killed during the fighting, and much of Osawatomie was burned.

The engagement further enhanced Brown's reputation among some antislavery supporters and contributed to the nickname “Osawatomie Brown.”

Development of an Armed Liberation Strategy

Brown's experiences in Kansas reinforced a strategy he had been contemplating for years: rather than merely defending free territories from slavery, abolitionists should attack slavery directly within the slave states.

Brown envisioned establishing a network of armed resistance in the Appalachian Mountains. Enslaved people who escaped plantations could join or receive assistance from these groups, which would conduct raids, disrupt slaveholding communities, liberate additional people, and make slavery increasingly difficult to maintain.

His plan did not necessarily depend upon defeating the United States Army in conventional warfare. Instead, Brown envisioned mobile guerrilla-style operations operating across mountainous terrain and gradually destabilizing slavery.

To implement that strategy, however, he needed supporters, weapons, money, secure bases, and cooperation from enslaved people themselves.

Relationship with Frederick Douglass

Brown developed a relationship with Frederick Douglass, the formerly enslaved abolitionist, writer, and orator.

Brown admired Douglass and discussed his plans for militant antislavery action with him. Douglass respected Brown's commitment to racial equality but did not agree with all of his strategic conclusions.

Shortly before the Harpers Ferry raid, Brown met Douglass near Chambersburg, Pennsylvania, and attempted to persuade him to participate.

Douglass refused. He believed attacking a federal armory in a town surrounded by mountains would leave Brown trapped and would likely end in disaster.

After Brown's execution, however, Douglass became one of the most important interpreters of his legacy and argued that Brown's attack struck directly at the institution of slavery in a way that had profound national consequences.

Black Allies

Brown's antislavery network included African American activists, freedom seekers, ministers, community leaders, and militants. His willingness to organize alongside Black Americans as partners was unusual among white reformers of his period.

Black participants in the Harpers Ferry expedition included Osborne Perry Anderson, John Anthony Copeland Jr., Lewis Sheridan Leary, Dangerfield Newby, and Shields Green.

Their participation demonstrates that the raid was not exclusively a project of white abolitionists acting on behalf of enslaved people. Black abolitionists themselves participated directly in Brown's armed campaign.

Dangerfield Newby, who had previously been enslaved, had a particularly personal motivation. His wife and children remained enslaved, and he hoped to obtain their freedom.

Harriet Tubman

Brown also developed a relationship with Harriet Tubman, who had repeatedly returned to slaveholding territory to guide enslaved people to freedom.

Brown deeply respected Tubman's experience, courage, knowledge of escape networks, and willingness to take extraordinary risks in opposition to slavery.

Tubman supported Brown's antislavery objectives and assisted with elements of his organizing, but she did not participate in the Harpers Ferry raid itself.

The Secret Six

Brown required financial support to develop his plans. A small group of prominent Northern abolitionists later became known as the Secret Six because of their financial or organizational assistance.

The group consisted of Thomas Wentworth Higginson, Samuel Gridley Howe, Theodore Parker, Franklin Benjamin Sanborn, Gerrit Smith, and George Luther Stearns.

Members differed in how much they understood about Brown's specific intentions, but their support helped provide money and resources for his militant antislavery activities.

Chatham Convention

In May 1858, Brown held a convention in Chatham, Canada West, now Ontario.

The meeting included Black and white supporters and was intended to establish a political framework for the liberation movement Brown hoped to create.

Participants adopted a document called the Provisional Constitution and Ordinances for the People of the United States.

The constitution proposed an alternative governmental structure for communities operating within Brown's revolutionary antislavery organization.

Brown was selected as commander in chief. The Chatham gathering illustrates that the Harpers Ferry operation was not conceived merely as a single attack on a government facility but as the opening stage of a much larger liberation campaign.

Missouri Raid

In December 1858, Brown led a raid from Kansas into Missouri. His party entered slaveholding properties and liberated a group of enslaved people.

Brown then undertook an extended journey to move the freedom seekers through hostile territory and eventually toward safety in Canada.

Eleven formerly enslaved people ultimately reached Canada in 1859.

The Missouri operation demonstrated on a smaller scale the type of direct liberation Brown hoped to expand through his planned Appalachian campaign.

Kennedy Farm

In the summer of 1859, Brown rented a small farm in Maryland near Harpers Ferry under the name Isaac Smith.

The property, known as Kennedy Farm, served as the secret staging area for the raid.

Brown's followers gradually assembled there along with firearms, ammunition, supplies, and approximately one thousand pikes intended for use by people who joined the uprising.

The location allowed Brown's group to remain relatively close to Harpers Ferry while concealing the preparations from local authorities.

Why Harpers Ferry?

Harpers Ferry was strategically attractive because the federal government operated an armory, arsenal, and rifle works there. Large quantities of weapons were stored at the facility.

The town was also located near the Appalachian Mountains, which Brown hoped would provide defensible terrain for an extended liberation campaign.

Brown intended to seize weapons, distribute them to enslaved people who joined him, withdraw into the mountains, and expand the movement through additional raids.

The strategy depended heavily on momentum. Brown needed enslaved people to receive information about the revolt, join quickly, and allow his small initial force to expand before state or federal military forces could respond.

Raid on Harpers Ferry

On the night of October 16, 1859, Brown and 21 followers began their attack on Harpers Ferry, Virginia, now West Virginia.

The raiders captured the federal armory complex and took several local residents hostage. Parties were sent into the surrounding countryside to bring enslaved people to Harpers Ferry.

Brown initially encountered relatively little organized resistance, but he failed to withdraw from the town while escape remained possible.

Local militia companies and armed residents began surrounding Brown's force. Railroad communication also carried news of the uprising beyond Harpers Ferry.

As resistance increased, Brown and several surviving followers withdrew into the armory's fire enginehouse with hostages. The structure later became known as John Brown's Fort.

Failure of the Raid

Brown's operational assumptions failed in several important ways. His force was extremely small, communications with enslaved communities were limited, and no large-scale uprising developed before military forces arrived.

Brown also remained at Harpers Ferry longer than his broader guerrilla strategy appears to have required. By the time he attempted to consolidate his position, escape routes had largely been closed.

President James Buchanan's administration dispatched United States Marines from Washington to suppress the raid.

Army Colonel Robert E. Lee commanded the federal force, while Lieutenant J. E. B. Stuart delivered a demand for Brown's surrender. Brown refused.

On the morning of October 18, Marines stormed the enginehouse. Brown was wounded and captured.

Casualties

The Harpers Ferry raid resulted in deaths among Brown's men as well as among local residents and people responding to the attack.

Ten of Brown's followers were killed during the operation or died from their wounds. Those killed included his sons Watson and Oliver. Several others escaped, and surviving raiders captured by Virginia authorities were subsequently tried.

The first person killed during the raid was Heyward Shepherd, a free Black railroad baggage master who was shot by one of Brown's men while attempting to alert others.

The death of Shepherd illustrates one of the ironies and tragedies of the raid: an armed operation intended to liberate Black Americans also caused the death of a free Black civilian.

Capture

Brown survived the Marine assault despite suffering serious wounds. He was captured alive on October 18, 1859.

Authorities interrogated Brown while he was still injured. Rather than denying his objectives, he openly defended his opposition to slavery and explained that he intended to free enslaved people.

His calm demeanor following capture became an important part of his public image. Newspaper reporters transmitted his statements throughout the country, transforming a failed military operation into a national political event.

Trial

Brown was tried in Charles Town, Virginia, rather than in federal court.

The Commonwealth of Virginia charged him with treason against the state, murder, and conspiring with enslaved people to rebel.

The proceedings moved rapidly. Brown's defense attorneys questioned whether a person who was not a citizen of Virginia could legally commit treason against the Commonwealth, but the argument did not prevent conviction.

On October 31, 1859, the jury found Brown guilty. He was sentenced to death on November 2.

Brown used the sentencing hearing to explain his motives. He denied seeking indiscriminate killing and argued that his objective had been to free enslaved people.

His conduct during the trial substantially altered public perceptions. Even some people who condemned the raid acknowledged his composure, personal courage, and willingness to accept death for his beliefs.

Execution

John Brown was hanged in Charles Town on December 2, 1859. Security surrounding the execution was extensive because Virginia officials feared an attempted rescue or additional abolitionist violence.

Among those present in military formations guarding the execution were individuals who would later become prominent during the Civil War.

Shortly before his death, Brown predicted that the nation's conflict over slavery would ultimately require bloodshed to resolve.

His body was returned to North Elba, New York, where he was buried near his family home.

Northern Reaction

Northern opinion toward Brown was divided.

Many Northerners condemned the raid as illegal, reckless, or dangerously provocative. Political leaders opposed to the expansion of slavery were particularly concerned that Southern politicians would portray the raid as representative of the entire Northern antislavery movement.

Radical abolitionists, however, increasingly celebrated Brown as a martyr. His willingness to die for the freedom of enslaved people gave him a moral stature among supporters that the military failure of Harpers Ferry had not produced.

Memorial meetings, sermons, speeches, and public discussions followed his execution throughout portions of the North.

Southern Reaction

The raid created intense alarm throughout the slaveholding South.

White Southerners had long feared organized revolts by enslaved people. Brown's attempt demonstrated that militant abolitionists were willing to enter a slaveholding state, seize federal weapons, and actively encourage armed resistance.

Particularly alarming was the discovery that Brown had received support from prominent Northern abolitionists.

Southern political leaders increasingly argued that the raid demonstrated a broader Northern threat to slavery and to the security of white Southern society.

Militia organizations expanded, sectional distrust intensified, and the political environment surrounding the 1860 presidential election became still more volatile.

Relationship to the Civil War

Brown did not cause the Civil War by himself. The sectional crisis had developed over decades through disputes involving slavery, western expansion, political representation, fugitive-slave laws, federal authority, economic interests, and competing constitutional interpretations.

The Harpers Ferry raid nevertheless accelerated the breakdown of trust between North and South.

To many white Southerners, the raid provided evidence that remaining within a Union containing a growing antislavery movement might eventually threaten the survival of slavery itself.

To many abolitionists, Brown's execution demonstrated that the American legal system would execute a man attempting to liberate enslaved people while continuing to protect the institution of slavery.

Less than eighteen months after Brown's execution, Confederate forces fired on Fort Sumter and the Civil War began.

John Brown's Body

Brown's memory entered Union military culture during the Civil War through the song “John Brown's Body.”

Union soldiers adapted a marching tune whose lyrics proclaimed that Brown's body lay in the grave while his spirit continued onward.

The song became one of the most recognizable Union marching songs and helped transform Brown from a controversial antebellum militant into a symbol associated with the war against slavery.

The melody was later used for Julia Ward Howe's “Battle Hymn of the Republic,” further connecting Brown's memory with the moral and religious interpretation of the Union war effort.

John Brown and African American Memory

Brown acquired a particularly important place in African American historical memory because he was a white abolitionist who not only denounced slavery but was willing to risk his life alongside Black men in an attempt to destroy it.

Frederick Douglass repeatedly reflected on Brown's significance. Other Black writers, activists, and intellectuals also treated him as evidence that racial solidarity could extend beyond sympathy to active sacrifice.

In 1909, W. E. B. Du Bois published a major biography titled John Brown. Du Bois situated Brown within the broader struggle of enslaved people themselves for freedom and presented him as an important participant in the historical destruction of American slavery.

The Niagara Movement, an early civil-rights organization led by Du Bois and others, held its second annual meeting at Harpers Ferry in 1906. Participants made a pilgrimage to locations associated with Brown, demonstrating the continuing importance of his memory within Black freedom movements.

Violence and Historical Debate

No assessment of Brown is complete without confronting his use of political violence.

His defenders emphasize that slavery was itself maintained through systematic violence. Enslaved people could be beaten, sexually exploited, separated from relatives, imprisoned, sold, or killed, while federal and state governments protected slaveholders' ownership claims.

From this perspective, Brown's decision to use force can be interpreted as resistance to an already violent system rather than the introduction of violence into an otherwise peaceful political dispute.

Critics respond that moral opposition to slavery does not automatically justify every method used to oppose it. The Pottawatomie killings involved the deliberate killing of five men without trial, while the Harpers Ferry raid endangered hostages, civilians, and Brown's own followers.

Historians therefore disagree not about whether Brown used violence but about how that violence should be interpreted within the moral, political, and legal conditions of a slaveholding society.

Was John Brown a Terrorist?

The application of the modern term “terrorist” to Brown is disputed. Some scholars and commentators use the term because Brown employed politically motivated violence and participated in deliberate killings intended to influence a larger conflict.

Others reject the classification or regard it as historically misleading. They distinguish Brown's stated objective of liberating enslaved people and attacking the institutions sustaining slavery from campaigns primarily designed to terrorize civilian populations.

The Pottawatomie killings complicate the distinction because Brown and his party intentionally killed civilians associated with the proslavery cause. The Harpers Ferry operation, by contrast, was directed principally at a federal military installation and was intended to acquire weapons for an insurrection.

Because modern definitions of terrorism differ, a biography is better served by describing Brown's actions precisely than by using a single contemporary label as a substitute for historical analysis.

Legacy

John Brown remains one of the most morally and politically challenging figures in United States history.

He failed militarily at Harpers Ferry. His force did not initiate the large-scale uprising he anticipated, most of his immediate followers were killed or captured, and he was executed within weeks.

Politically and symbolically, however, the raid had consequences far beyond its military dimensions. Brown placed slavery and armed resistance at the center of national debate at precisely the moment the political system was approaching collapse.

His execution created a martyr for militant abolitionists and a nightmare figure for defenders of slavery.

Brown also occupies an unusual position in the history of American racial politics. He advocated a degree of racial equality uncommon among white Americans of his period and repeatedly accepted Black Americans as colleagues, soldiers, political participants, and equals within his movement.

At the same time, admiration for his antislavery commitment cannot erase the lethal methods he employed. The five killings at Pottawatomie and the casualties at Harpers Ferry remain integral components of his record.

Brown's significance ultimately lies partly in the contradiction between the catastrophic failure of his immediate plan and the extraordinary influence of his death. The armed revolt he hoped to initiate did not occur in 1859. Yet within two years the United States entered a civil war that eventually mobilized millions of soldiers and resulted in the destruction of legal slavery.

Historical Significance

Brown's career demonstrates the increasing difficulty of separating the slavery question from the use of force during the 1850s.

The Kansas conflict showed that the political struggle over slavery's expansion could become organized warfare. Harpers Ferry demonstrated that abolitionist militancy could carry that conflict directly into a slaveholding state.

Brown also exposed a major contradiction within American political life. The federal system treated enslaved people as legally controlled property while Brown regarded assisting their liberation as obedience to a higher moral law.

His trial therefore became more than a criminal proceeding. It developed into a national argument about the legitimacy of laws protecting slavery, the right of resistance, the meaning of racial equality, and the limits of political compromise.

Those questions continued into the Civil War and remained central to the country's transformation during emancipation and Reconstruction.

Geographic Scope

Brown's life and activities connected several major centers of the nineteenth-century antislavery struggle.

Important locations included Torrington, Connecticut; Hudson, Ohio; Richmond, Pennsylvania; Springfield, Massachusetts; North Elba, New York; eastern Kansas; western Missouri; Chatham in Canada West; Chambersburg, Pennsylvania; Kennedy Farm in Maryland; Harpers Ferry, Virginia; and Charles Town, Virginia.

His activities also linked local antislavery networks to the international boundary with Canada, which served as an important destination for freedom seekers escaping the reach of United States fugitive-slave law.

Legal Context

Brown's career developed within a legal system that recognized and protected slavery in much of the United States.

The Constitution contained provisions protecting important slaveholding interests, while federal fugitive-slave laws required the return of people who escaped bondage.

The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 strengthened federal enforcement and directly influenced Brown's organization of resistance in Massachusetts.

The Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854 reopened the question of slavery's expansion into western territories and created the political conditions that produced Bleeding Kansas.

Following Harpers Ferry, Brown was prosecuted under Virginia law. His conviction for treason against Virginia was particularly controversial because Brown was not a Virginia resident.

The larger constitutional struggle over slavery was not resolved until the Civil War and the ratification of the Thirteenth Amendment in 1865.
