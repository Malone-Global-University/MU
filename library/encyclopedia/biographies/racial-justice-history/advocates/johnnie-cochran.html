Johnnie Cochran

American trial lawyer, civil-rights attorney, criminal defense lawyer, plaintiffs' advocate, and legal entrepreneur whose career reshaped public debates over police misconduct, race, wrongful conviction, and courtroom advocacy

Overview

Johnnie L. Cochran Jr. was an American trial lawyer, civil-rights attorney, criminal defense lawyer, plaintiffs' lawyer, prosecutor, author, and legal entrepreneur whose career became closely associated with police misconduct litigation, racial justice, celebrity criminal defense, and the transformation of trial lawyers into national media figures.

Cochran was born in Shreveport, Louisiana, in 1937 and grew up principally in Los Angeles after his family joined the larger twentieth-century movement of Black families from the South to the West. He was the great-grandson of enslaved people and the grandson of a sharecropper.

His family belonged to the expanding Black middle class of postwar Los Angeles. His father worked his way from shipyard employment into insurance management, and his parents emphasized church, education, debate, professional ambition, and disciplined upward mobility.

Cochran graduated from Los Angeles High School, earned a degree in business economics from UCLA in 1959, and received his law degree from Loyola Law School in 1962. During law school he became the first Black law clerk hired by the Los Angeles City Attorney's Office.

After admission to the California bar in 1963, Cochran became a deputy Los Angeles city attorney and worked as a prosecutor. One of his early cases involved comedian Lenny Bruce, whom Cochran unsuccessfully prosecuted for alleged obscenity. Decades later Cochran acknowledged that the prosecution conflicted with the free-speech principles he had come to embrace.

Cochran left government prosecution for private practice in 1965 as his concern about police violence against Black defendants grew. The 1966 police shooting of Leonard Deadwyler became a defining early case. Cochran represented Deadwyler's widow at the highly publicized coroner's inquest and later in civil litigation, helping establish his reputation as a lawyer willing to challenge the Los Angeles Police Department.

During the 1970s, Cochran combined criminal defense with civil-rights and police-misconduct work. His most painful defeat came in the 1972 conviction of Black Panther Party leader and Vietnam veteran Elmer "Geronimo" Pratt. Cochran remained involved in Pratt's case for decades and later regarded the overturning of the conviction in 1997 as the proudest achievement of his legal career.

Cochran returned to government in 1978 when Los Angeles County District Attorney John Van de Kamp recruited him as an assistant district attorney. He became the first African American to hold the senior position and worked on institutional reforms involving domestic violence and sexual assault before returning to private practice in the early 1980s.

His representation of the family of college football player Ron Settles, who died in Signal Hill police custody in 1981, reinforced his reputation as one of the country's most formidable police-misconduct attorneys. The Settles family received a substantial wrongful-death settlement after a coroner's jury rejected the official claim that Settles had killed himself.

By the early 1990s Cochran was a leading Los Angeles trial lawyer with major civil verdicts and a growing celebrity clientele. He represented or advised clients including Michael Jackson, Todd Bridges, Snoop Dogg, Sean Combs, Reginald Denny, Abner Louima, and numerous families alleging police abuse or wrongful death.

Cochran became internationally famous after joining the defense team of O.J. Simpson, who was charged with murdering his former wife Nicole Brown Simpson and Ronald Goldman. During the 1995 criminal trial, Cochran emerged as the defense team's dominant courtroom voice and framed the prosecution's case around reasonable doubt, evidence handling, and racial distrust of the Los Angeles Police Department.

His closing argument included the phrase "If it doesn't fit, you must acquit," referring most famously to the gloves that Simpson appeared unable to put on during a courtroom demonstration. The line was conceived by fellow defense attorney Gerald Uelmen, but Cochran's delivery made it one of the most famous phrases in American legal history.

Simpson was acquitted of murder in October 1995. The verdict made Cochran a hero to some Americans and a deeply polarizing figure to others. Critics accused the defense of exploiting racial division and attacking police institutions to distract from physical evidence. Cochran responded that race was relevant because the credibility and conduct of police witnesses were legitimately at issue and because the prosecution had failed to prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.

Cochran did not represent Simpson in the later civil wrongful-death trial. In 1997, a civil jury found Simpson liable for the deaths of Nicole Brown Simpson and Ronald Goldman and awarded substantial damages to their families. The different outcomes reflected different parties, evidence presentation, legal strategies, and standards of proof.

The Simpson trial did not end Cochran's civil-rights work. He helped secure Geronimo Pratt's release, participated in the civil case of Haitian immigrant Abner Louima after police tortured him in a Brooklyn precinct, expanded a national plaintiffs' practice, and joined attorney Cyrus Mehri in challenging discriminatory hiring practices involving Black coaches in the National Football League.

The Cochran-Mehri campaign contributed directly to the NFL's adoption of the Rooney Rule, which originally required teams hiring head coaches to interview at least one minority candidate. The policy became one of the most influential diversity rules in American professional sports.

Cochran also became a symbol of trial advocacy itself. Admirers emphasized his preparation, jury communication, command of narrative, and willingness to confront powerful institutions. Critics viewed his courtroom performances as overly theatrical or manipulative and argued that his fame sometimes encouraged media spectacle around serious legal proceedings.

Cochran rejected the idea that his practice was limited to celebrity clients. He repeatedly described his mission as representing both famous people and ordinary people who lacked power when confronting police departments, corporations, prosecutors, or other institutions.

Cochran was diagnosed with a brain tumor in December 2003. He underwent treatment and sharply reduced his public work. He died at his Los Angeles home on March 29, 2005, at age sixty-seven.

His legacy extends far beyond the Simpson trial. Cochran helped normalize aggressive civil litigation against police misconduct, contributed to the reversal of a major wrongful conviction, promoted diversity in the legal profession and professional sports, built a national plaintiffs' firm, and became one of the most recognizable Black lawyers in American history.

Birth in Shreveport

Cochran was born on October 2, 1937, in Shreveport, Louisiana.

He later described himself as the great-grandson of enslaved people and the grandson of a sharecropper.

His birth placed him within a generation of Black Americans whose families were moving from the rural and segregated South into new urban opportunities across the country.

Johnnie Cochran Sr. and Hattie Cochran

Cochran's parents were Johnnie L. Cochran Sr. and Hattie Cochran.

His father worked as a pipe fitter in California shipyards before moving into the insurance industry with Golden State Mutual, one of the most important Black-owned insurance companies in the West.

The elder Cochran eventually managed an insurance district office and became a model of professional advancement for his children.

Cochran's mother also contributed to the family's strong emphasis on church, education, discipline, and social mobility.

Migration to California

The Cochran family moved west during the 1940s as part of the larger migration of African Americans seeking industrial employment and greater opportunity outside the Jim Crow South.

The family lived for a period in Northern California before settling permanently in Los Angeles in 1949.

Los Angeles offered opportunities unavailable in Louisiana, but housing discrimination, police violence, employment barriers, and segregated social networks continued to shape Black life.

West Adams and Black Los Angeles

The family settled in the West Adams area of Los Angeles, a major center of the city's Black professional and middle-class community.

Cochran grew up surrounded by ministers, entrepreneurs, teachers, professionals, activists, and families pursuing upward mobility in a city undergoing rapid demographic change.

This environment helped shape his later ability to move comfortably between Black political institutions, elite legal circles, corporate clients, and national media.

Second Baptist Church

The Cochran family attended Second Baptist Church, one of Los Angeles's historically important Black churches.

The church provided religious formation as well as social networks, civic leadership, and connections to the Black professional community.

Cochran remained publicly identified with Christian faith throughout his life.

Los Angeles High School

Cochran attended Los Angeles High School and graduated in 1955.

He was one of a relatively small number of Black students at the school during that period.

He excelled academically and developed the confidence in argument and public speaking that would later become central to his legal career.

Interest in Debate and Law

Cochran later traced his attraction to law partly to a love of argument and debate.

Family discussions and school experiences allowed him to practice defending positions and persuading listeners.

The future trial lawyer learned early that style and logic were most powerful when combined with confidence and an understanding of the audience.

UCLA

Cochran attended the University of California, Los Angeles, and earned a degree in business economics in 1959.

At UCLA he became a member of Kappa Alpha Psi, a historically Black fraternity whose professional and civic networks remained important throughout his life.

His education gave him both analytical training and access to a growing Black professional network in Southern California.

Loyola Law School

Cochran entered Loyola Law School in Los Angeles and received his law degree in 1962.

During law school he became the first Black law clerk hired by the Los Angeles City Attorney's Office.

The clerkship gave him direct experience with government litigation before he entered the profession full time.

Admission to the California Bar

Cochran passed the California bar examination and entered practice in 1963.

He began his career at a time when Black lawyers remained underrepresented in prosecutors' offices, major firms, judgeships, and corporate practice.

His advancement therefore carried symbolic significance even before he became a nationally known civil-rights advocate.

Deputy Los Angeles City Attorney

Cochran became a deputy city attorney in the criminal division.

He prosecuted misdemeanor and other criminal matters and gained extensive courtroom experience.

Trial work appealed to his competitive instincts and gave him a practical education in witness examination, jury persuasion, evidence, and courtroom procedure.

The Lenny Bruce Obscenity Case

As a young prosecutor, Cochran participated in the 1964 prosecution of comedian Lenny Bruce on obscenity allegations in Los Angeles.

The prosecution failed to secure a conviction.

Cochran later acknowledged that pursuing Bruce for offensive speech was inconsistent with the First Amendment values he came to defend as a lawyer.

The episode demonstrates that Cochran's mature civil-liberties identity developed over time rather than existing fully formed at the beginning of his career.

Concerns About Police Conduct

While prosecuting cases, Cochran became increasingly disturbed by defendants who appeared to have been beaten or mistreated by police.

Cases involving resistance to arrest were particularly troubling because allegations against defendants sometimes existed alongside visible injuries sustained in custody.

The experience contributed to his decision to leave prosecution and become a private attorney willing to challenge police officers and government agencies.

Entering Private Practice

Cochran left the City Attorney's Office in 1965 and entered private practice.

He initially worked with attorney Gerald Lenoir and soon developed his own practice.

His cases combined criminal defense with civil litigation involving police shootings, brutality, wrongful death, and racial discrimination.

Cochran, Atkins & Evans

Cochran helped establish the firm Cochran, Atkins & Evans in Los Angeles.

Building a Black-led law practice was itself significant in a legal market where major commercial firms remained overwhelmingly white.

The firm became a platform from which Cochran could pursue police-misconduct cases that conventional practices might have viewed as politically difficult or financially risky.

Leonard Deadwyler

Cochran's breakthrough public case involved Leonard Deadwyler, a Black motorist shot and killed by Los Angeles police in May 1966.

Deadwyler had been driving rapidly while transporting his pregnant wife during what the family believed was a medical emergency.

An LAPD officer approached the stopped vehicle with his weapon drawn and shot Deadwyler at close range. The officer said the car lurched and caused an accidental discharge; Deadwyler's widow disputed that account.

The Deadwyler Inquest

Cochran represented Barbara Deadwyler during the televised coroner's inquest.

The case drew intense attention because it occurred less than a year after the Watts uprising and amid deep Black distrust of the LAPD.

The coroner's jury ultimately characterized the shooting as accidental.

Although Cochran did not prevail, his highly visible participation established him as an important legal advocate for Black Los Angeles.

Deadwyler Civil Litigation

Cochran also pursued civil claims arising from Leonard Deadwyler's death.

The litigation did not produce the victory Cochran sought.

He later regarded the case as professionally formative because it demonstrated both the difficulty of winning police cases and the importance of giving victims' families an effective public advocate.

Police Misconduct as a Career Focus

After Deadwyler, Cochran increasingly built his practice around cases alleging police abuse.

At the time, civil suits against officers and municipalities were harder to win than they later became, and public deference to law enforcement was strong.

Cochran's willingness to litigate these cases helped make police misconduct a recurring legal and political issue rather than solely an internal disciplinary matter.

Geronimo Pratt

One of the most important clients of Cochran's career was Elmer "Geronimo" Pratt, a Vietnam veteran and leader in the Black Panther Party.

Pratt was charged with the 1968 robbery and murder of Caroline Olsen in Santa Monica.

Cochran joined Pratt's defense and argued that his client was elsewhere when the crime occurred.

Pratt's 1972 Conviction

Pratt was convicted in 1972 and sentenced to prison.

Cochran regarded the verdict as one of the most painful defeats of his career.

The case later became more troubling when evidence emerged that a key prosecution witness had served as a government informant and that potentially exculpatory information had not been properly disclosed.

COINTELPRO and the Pratt Case

Pratt's case unfolded within the period when the FBI's COINTELPRO program targeted the Black Panther Party and other political organizations.

Later litigation and investigation showed extensive government surveillance and infiltration directed at Panther activists.

The Pratt case became one of the most important examples used by critics who argued that political policing corrupted ordinary criminal-justice procedures.

Continuing the Pratt Fight

Cochran did not abandon Pratt after the conviction.

Over the following decades he remained connected to the effort to overturn the judgment, working with other attorneys as new evidence and legal arguments emerged.

The persistence of that representation became central to Cochran's own understanding of his professional identity.

Criminal Trial Lawyer of the Year

By the late 1970s, Cochran had become one of the most respected criminal trial lawyers in Los Angeles.

The Los Angeles Criminal Courts Bar Association named him Criminal Trial Lawyer of the Year in 1977.

The award reflected professional recognition that preceded his later celebrity.

Return to Government

In 1978, Los Angeles County District Attorney John Van de Kamp recruited Cochran into the senior leadership of the district attorney's office.

Cochran accepted a large reduction in income to return to public service.

He became the first African American to hold his senior assistant district attorney position.

Reform from Inside the District Attorney's Office

Cochran said that returning to prosecution allowed him to attempt reform from within the criminal-justice system.

Loyola Law School credits his tenure with institutional initiatives including work involving domestic violence and sexual assault.

The experience broadened Cochran's understanding of prosecution management and gave him an insider's perspective that later benefited his defense work.

Return to Private Practice in the 1980s

Cochran returned to private practice in the early 1980s.

He increasingly focused on major civil litigation, wrongful death, police misconduct, personal injury, and high-profile criminal matters.

His practice became both commercially successful and politically influential.

Ron Settles

Ron Settles was a college football player at California State University, Long Beach, who was arrested for speeding in Signal Hill in 1981.

He was later found hanging in his jail cell. Police initially described the death as suicide.

Settles's family rejected that explanation and hired Cochran.

The Settles Inquest and Settlement

Cochran and attorney Mike Mitchell argued that Settles had died as the result of police violence, including possible use of a chokehold.

A coroner's jury concluded that Settles had not killed himself and had died "at the hands of another."

The family later received a settlement of approximately $760,000 in wrongful-death litigation.

The case became one of Cochran's defining victories and intensified scrutiny of police chokeholds.

Chokehold Reform

Cochran became involved in broader advocacy against dangerous police chokehold practices in Los Angeles.

He participated in efforts before the Police Commission that contributed to restrictions on the bar-arm chokehold.

The issue linked individual damage litigation with institutional reform, a model Cochran would repeatedly pursue.

Los Angeles Political Networks

Cochran developed close connections within Black Los Angeles politics and civic institutions.

Mayor Tom Bradley, a fellow member of Kappa Alpha Psi and an important mentor, appointed Cochran to the Los Angeles Airport Commission.

The appointment placed Cochran within a wider network of political leadership, business development, contracting, and civic administration.

Patty Diaz Police Misconduct Case

Cochran and law partner Eric Ferrer won a major jury verdict for Patty Diaz, a thirteen-year-old Latina who had been sexually assaulted by an LAPD officer.

The jury awarded $9.2 million, then one of the largest verdicts arising from LAPD misconduct.

The amount was later reduced through settlement, but the case demonstrated that police-misconduct litigation had become capable of producing significant financial accountability.

Todd Bridges

Cochran successfully represented actor Todd Bridges in a 1990 criminal case involving allegations that Bridges shot a drug dealer.

Bridges was acquitted.

The case strengthened Cochran's growing reputation as a lawyer capable of combining technical criminal defense with persuasive jury advocacy.

Celebrity Representation

By the early 1990s, Cochran had become a lawyer sought by entertainers, athletes, and other public figures.

His celebrity clients eventually included Michael Jackson, Tupac Shakur, Snoop Dogg, Sean Combs, Jim Brown, Riddick Bowe, and others.

Cochran insisted that celebrity work did not replace his civil-rights practice and often used the resources and visibility of high-profile cases to support a broader national law firm.

Michael Jackson

Cochran joined Michael Jackson's legal team in 1993 during civil litigation and criminal investigation arising from allegations of child sexual abuse.

The civil claim was later settled without an admission of criminal guilt.

Cochran's work for Jackson further established him as a lawyer to major entertainment figures before the Simpson trial.

Reginald Denny

Cochran's clients were not exclusively Black.

He represented Reginald Denny, the white truck driver brutally beaten during the 1992 Los Angeles uprising, in civil claims against the city.

The representation was important to Cochran's public argument that his criticism of police and government was based on abuse of power rather than hostility toward white clients or law enforcement as a category.

O.J. Simpson Is Charged with Murder

On June 12, 1994, Nicole Brown Simpson and Ronald Goldman were found stabbed to death outside Brown Simpson's condominium in Brentwood.

O.J. Simpson, a former football star, actor, and broadcaster, became the principal suspect and was charged with both murders.

The case immediately became one of the largest media events in American criminal-justice history.

Joining the Simpson Defense

Simpson initially relied heavily on attorney Robert Shapiro and other lawyers.

Cochran joined the defense team in 1994 after Simpson personally sought his assistance.

As trial preparation advanced, Cochran increasingly became the dominant courtroom strategist and public face of the defense.

The "Dream Team"

The Simpson defense assembled a group of prominent lawyers and experts that the media labeled the "Dream Team."

The group included Cochran, Robert Shapiro, F. Lee Bailey, Barry Scheck, Peter Neufeld, Alan Dershowitz, Gerald Uelmen, Robert Kardashian, Carl Douglas, and others.

The team combined criminal-defense experience, DNA expertise, appellate strategy, scientific challenges, and media sophistication.

The Prosecution Case

Prosecutors Marcia Clark and Christopher Darden presented substantial circumstantial and physical evidence linking Simpson to the killings.

The prosecution relied heavily on blood evidence, DNA testing, a trail of blood, gloves, Simpson's injuries, timeline evidence, and the history of domestic violence involving Nicole Brown Simpson.

The defense did not need to prove an alternative killer. Its legal task was to persuade jurors that the prosecution had failed to establish Simpson's guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.

DNA Evidence

The Simpson case introduced millions of Americans to DNA evidence.

Prosecutors argued that blood testing created powerful statistical links among Simpson, the victims, the crime scene, Simpson's vehicle, and his property.

Defense attorneys Barry Scheck and Peter Neufeld attacked collection procedures, contamination risks, laboratory practices, chain of custody, and the way scientific evidence had been handled.

Cochran incorporated those technical arguments into a larger narrative about reasonable doubt and institutional reliability.

Mark Fuhrman

LAPD detective Mark Fuhrman became one of the most damaging prosecution witnesses.

Fuhrman denied under oath that he had used a racial slur during the relevant period, but recordings later emerged in which he repeatedly used racist language.

The revelations severely damaged his credibility and strengthened the defense argument that jurors could not simply assume police witnesses were neutral and trustworthy.

Fuhrman later entered a no-contest plea to a perjury charge arising from his trial testimony.

Race and the Simpson Trial

Race became inseparable from the Simpson trial because it occurred only a few years after the videotaped beating of Rodney King, the acquittal of LAPD officers in state court, the 1992 Los Angeles uprising, and continuing allegations of police misconduct.

Cochran argued that racial bias mattered to the credibility of particular officers and to the possibility of a premature police conclusion that Simpson was guilty.

Critics accused him of exploiting Los Angeles racial tensions to distract jurors from physical evidence.

The dispute over whether the defense legitimately confronted racism or improperly played a "race card" became one of the trial's defining controversies.

The Gloves

A bloodstained glove was found at the Bundy crime scene and another on Simpson's property.

During trial, prosecutor Christopher Darden asked Simpson to try on the gloves in front of the jury.

Simpson appeared unable to pull them fully over his hands while wearing thin protective gloves underneath.

The courtroom demonstration became one of the most memorable moments in the trial and a major rhetorical opportunity for the defense.

"If It Doesn't Fit, You Must Acquit"

During closing argument, Cochran repeatedly used the phrase "If it doesn't fit, you must acquit."

The line is popularly attributed to Cochran because he delivered it, but fellow defense attorney and law professor Gerald Uelmen devised the phrase during defense strategy work.

Cochran used the rhyme not merely as a comment about the gloves but as a summary device for his larger claim that the prosecution's evidence did not fit together reliably enough to support conviction.

Cochran's Closing Argument

Cochran's closing argument combined factual criticism, legal standards, moral language, repetition, rhythm, and direct appeal to the jury's responsibility.

He attacked evidence handling, police credibility, inconsistencies, and what he presented as a rush to judgment.

Supporters regarded the closing as a model of jury advocacy. Critics saw it as an example of rhetorical skill overwhelming the weight of incriminating evidence.

The October 1995 Acquittal

On October 3, 1995, the jury found O.J. Simpson not guilty of both murders.

The verdict produced dramatically different public reactions and revealed major racial divisions in trust toward police and the criminal-justice system.

For Cochran, the acquittal created worldwide fame and permanently attached his name to the Simpson case.

Criminal Acquittal Versus Factual Innocence

A criminal acquittal means that prosecutors failed to prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt; it is not a formal judicial declaration that every allegation against the defendant was false.

Cochran maintained after the trial that he believed Simpson was innocent of the murder charges.

The legal significance of the verdict should therefore be distinguished from continuing public debate over who committed the killings.

The Later Civil Verdict

Cochran did not represent Simpson in the subsequent civil wrongful-death trial.

In 1997, a civil jury found Simpson liable for the deaths of Nicole Brown Simpson and Ronald Goldman and awarded compensatory and punitive damages.

Civil liability required proof by a lower standard than criminal conviction and involved a different presentation of evidence and legal strategy.

The civil verdict does not legally reverse the criminal acquittal; the two proceedings answered different legal questions under different standards.

Criticism from Robert Shapiro

After the criminal trial, former co-counsel Robert Shapiro criticized the defense's use of race and publicly distanced himself from portions of the strategy.

Cochran rejected the suggestion that Black jurors had been manipulated into ignoring evidence because Simpson was Black.

The dispute reflected real strategic and personal tensions within the defense team as well as the larger national conflict over the meaning of the verdict.

Was the Simpson Defense Merely Theatrical?

Cochran's style was undeniably theatrical in the sense that he understood presentation, timing, language, clothing, visual evidence, and jury psychology.

Trial advocacy, however, is inherently persuasive rather than purely academic. Lawyers are expected to organize evidence into competing narratives while remaining within ethical and evidentiary rules.

The legitimate historical question is therefore not whether Cochran used persuasion—every trial lawyer does—but whether particular arguments were supported by the record and whether racial framing clarified or distorted the evidence.

Reasonable assessments differ sharply on that question.

Media Celebrity

The Simpson trial transformed Cochran from a prominent Los Angeles lawyer into an international celebrity.

He became a television guest, public speaker, bestselling author, cultural reference, and frequent subject of parody.

Television programs and films used his name as shorthand for a powerful defense lawyer capable of winning seemingly impossible cases.

Journey to Justice

In 1996, Cochran published Journey to Justice with journalist Tim Rutten.

The memoir discussed his childhood, race, legal career, police-misconduct work, Geronimo Pratt, and the Simpson trial.

The book allowed Cochran to present his own interpretation of the racial controversies that followed the acquittal.

Geronimo Pratt's Conviction Is Vacated

In 1997, after years of litigation, a California judge vacated Geronimo Pratt's murder conviction.

The ruling was based on the prosecution's failure to disclose evidence relevant to the credibility and government-informant status of key witness Julius Butler.

Pratt was released after spending approximately twenty-seven years in prison.

Cochran's Proudest Legal Achievement

Cochran repeatedly described Pratt's release as the happiest or proudest moment of his legal career.

That assessment is important because it reveals how Cochran ranked his own work: the internationally famous Simpson acquittal was not, in his view, more significant than correcting what he believed was a profound wrongful conviction.

Loyola Law School later emphasized the Pratt case when memorializing Cochran's civil-rights legacy.

Pratt Civil Settlement

After his release, Pratt pursued civil claims alleging malicious prosecution and false imprisonment.

Cochran and other attorneys helped secure a settlement totaling approximately $4.5 million from Los Angeles and the federal government.

The settlement provided financial compensation but could not restore the decades Pratt had spent incarcerated.

Abner Louima

Cochran became part of the legal team representing Abner Louima, a Haitian immigrant who was brutally beaten and sexually assaulted by New York City police officers after his 1997 arrest.

Officer Justin Volpe pleaded guilty to the central assault and received a lengthy prison sentence.

The case became a national symbol of police brutality and the so-called blue wall of silence.

Louima Civil Settlement

Louima's civil litigation ended in 2001 with an $8.75 million settlement involving New York City and the police union.

At the time, the city's portion was the largest payment New York had made to a police-brutality victim.

Cochran was one of several attorneys involved in the representation; the case also produced disagreements among lawyers over strategy, control, and fees.

Amadou Diallo and the Limits of Cochran's Expanding Practice

Cochran's fame brought invitations to participate in a large number of civil-rights and police cases across the country.

The volume of his commitments created criticism that he sometimes spread himself too thin.

The mother of Amadou Diallo, an unarmed West African immigrant killed by New York police in 1999, retained Cochran but later dismissed him after concluding that he could not devote enough time to the matter.

The episode illustrates a genuine tension between national influence and the close client attention required in individual cases.

Snoop Dogg and Other Criminal Cases

Cochran continued to participate in major criminal-defense matters after Simpson.

His celebrity clientele included rapper Snoop Dogg and other prominent entertainers and athletes.

These cases reinforced his reputation as a lawyer who could operate effectively under extraordinary media pressure.

Sean Combs Trial

Cochran joined attorney Benjamin Brafman in defending music executive Sean Combs in a 2001 New York criminal trial arising from a nightclub shooting.

Combs faced weapons and bribery-related allegations and was acquitted.

Cochran later indicated that the case would be among his final major criminal trials as he shifted attention toward civil litigation and national institutional work.

A Lawyer's Life

Cochran published A Lawyer's Life, written with David Fisher, in 2002.

The book examined legal strategy, famous cases, professional growth, courtroom technique, and Cochran's philosophy of advocacy.

Together with Journey to Justice, it provides an important primary source for understanding how Cochran interpreted his own career.

The Cochran Firm

Cochran sought to build a national law firm capable of representing plaintiffs against corporations, hospitals, government agencies, and other powerful defendants.

During the late 1990s he joined with attorneys Jock Smith, Keith Givens, Samuel Cherry, and others in developing what became The Cochran Firm.

The firm expanded into multiple American cities and continued after Cochran's death.

A National Plaintiffs' Practice

The national practice focused heavily on personal injury, wrongful death, medical malpractice, civil rights, product liability, and other plaintiff-side litigation.

Cochran argued that a large national firm could give ordinary clients access to investigative and legal resources comparable to those available to institutions and insurers.

Critics of plaintiffs' litigation and tort law sometimes viewed this model as encouraging excessive lawsuits; Cochran regarded civil damages as an important mechanism of accountability.

Cochran, Neufeld & Scheck

Cochran also developed a professional relationship with Peter Neufeld and Barry Scheck, lawyers who had been part of the Simpson defense and were major figures in DNA-based innocence work.

Their collaboration focused on police misconduct and wrongful convictions.

The relationship connected Cochran's older civil-rights practice to the emerging innocence movement of the DNA era.

The NFL Coaching Discrimination Campaign

In 2002, civil-rights attorneys Johnnie Cochran and Cyrus Mehri challenged the National Football League over the small number of Black head coaches despite a heavily Black player population.

They commissioned and publicized analysis arguing that Black coaches received fewer opportunities and were treated differently in hiring and firing.

The lawyers threatened litigation if the league failed to adopt meaningful reforms.

The Rooney Rule

The pressure generated by Cochran, Mehri, former players, coaches, and other advocates contributed to the NFL's adoption of the Rooney Rule in 2003.

The original policy required teams searching for a head coach to interview at least one minority candidate.

The rule was named for Pittsburgh Steelers owner Dan Rooney, who chaired the NFL committee addressing the issue.

The policy was later expanded and modified and became a model frequently discussed in debates over diversity in hiring.

Race and Employment Opportunity

Cochran's work on NFL coaching extended his civil-rights practice beyond policing and criminal justice.

He argued that institutions could produce racial exclusion even when no written rule explicitly barred Black candidates.

The campaign therefore addressed opportunity structures, professional networks, and hiring practices rather than only overt discriminatory statements.

Courtroom Style

Cochran's courtroom style combined meticulous preparation with polished performance.

He was known for controlled pacing, memorable phrases, eye contact, carefully tailored appearance, humor, repetition, moral framing, and an ability to simplify complicated factual disputes for jurors.

His strongest advocates regarded those traits as the essence of elite trial practice.

Narrative and Jury Psychology

Cochran understood that jurors rarely experience a case as a collection of isolated exhibits.

He organized facts into narratives involving credibility, power, motive, fairness, and institutional behavior.

This method was especially effective in police-misconduct litigation because the legal dispute often depended upon whether jurors trusted official accounts over civilian testimony.

Criticism of Showmanship

Cochran's success also produced criticism that he relied too heavily on showmanship.

Opponents argued that memorable rhetoric could distract juries from unfavorable evidence and that media attention risked turning trials into public spectacle.

Cochran generally rejected the distinction between substance and presentation, arguing that lawyers must communicate evidence effectively if they expect jurors to understand it.

Race and the Legal System

Race was a central theme in Cochran's professional life, but his approach was not simply that every adverse outcome involving a Black defendant resulted from racism.

He focused repeatedly on institutional credibility, unequal policing, excessive force, prosecutorial disclosure, jury perception, and access to high-quality counsel.

His career reflected a broader civil-rights legal tradition in which individual cases served as windows into structural problems.

Relationship with Law Enforcement

Cochran's criticism of police misconduct led some opponents to portray him as anti-police.

He rejected that label and distinguished criticism of abusive officers from hostility toward law enforcement as a profession.

His son Jonathan became a California Highway Patrol officer, a fact Cochran sometimes cited when responding to claims that he opposed police categorically.

Personal Wealth and Public Image

Cochran's successful practice made him wealthy.

He became known for tailored suits, expensive automobiles, prominent offices, and an elegant public style.

To admirers, his success represented Black professional achievement in a field that had historically excluded African Americans. Critics sometimes saw the image as inconsistent with a lawyer who emphasized representation of the disadvantaged.

Cochran embraced both identities: he openly enjoyed success while arguing that wealth did not negate a commitment to civil rights.

Marriage and Family

Cochran married Barbara Berry while he was in law school. They had two daughters, Melodie and Tiffany, before the marriage ended.

He also had a son, Jonathan, from a relationship with Patricia Sikora.

In 1985 Cochran married Dale Mason, whom he had met several years earlier. She remained his wife until his death.

Professional Mentorship

Cochran mentored younger attorneys and used his firms to expand opportunities for Black lawyers and other lawyers from underrepresented backgrounds.

His national practice was intentionally designed around racially and professionally diverse teams.

Many lawyers who worked with him later became prominent trial attorneys, civil-rights advocates, and firm leaders.

Illness

Cochran was diagnosed with a brain tumor in December 2003.

He underwent treatment under neurosurgeon Keith Black and sharply reduced his public appearances.

His family and colleagues initially protected the privacy of his medical condition while planning for continuity within the national law practice.

Death

Johnnie L. Cochran Jr. died at his home in Los Angeles on March 29, 2005.

He was sixty-seven years old.

His death produced tributes from civil-rights leaders, lawyers, former clients, political officials, entertainers, and professional colleagues who emphasized both his famous courtroom victories and his less-publicized civil-rights work.

Funeral and Burial

Public memorial events in Los Angeles drew major figures from law, politics, entertainment, and civil rights.

Cochran was buried at Inglewood Park Cemetery in Inglewood, California.

The attendance of clients such as O.J. Simpson and Michael Jackson underscored the celebrity dimension of his career, while civil-rights advocates emphasized his police-misconduct and wrongful-conviction work.

Johnnie L. Cochran Jr. Chair in Civil Rights

Loyola Law School established the Johnnie L. Cochran Jr. Chair in Civil Rights in recognition of its alumnus.

The endowed chair memorializes his work in civil rights and trial advocacy rather than only his celebrity criminal cases.

Its creation reflects the legal academy's recognition of Cochran as an important figure in modern civil-rights litigation.

Johnnie L. Cochran Jr. Brain Tumor Center

Cedars-Sinai Medical Center later established a brain tumor center bearing Cochran's name.

The center was associated with neurosurgeon Keith Black, who had treated Cochran.

The memorial linked Cochran's public legacy to research into the disease that caused his death.

School and Street Naming

Los Angeles renamed Cochran's former middle school in his honor after his death.

A nearby street segment was also designated Johnnie Cochran Vista.

These local memorials reflect his deep association with Los Angeles and particularly with the city's Black civic and professional history.

Legacy

Johnnie Cochran remains one of the most famous trial lawyers in American history.

The O.J. Simpson case made him internationally recognizable, but focusing exclusively on Simpson obscures the civil-rights career that made him important in Los Angeles decades earlier.

His police-misconduct litigation helped families challenge official narratives at a time when such claims were exceptionally difficult to win.

His long representation of Geronimo Pratt demonstrated unusual persistence in a wrongful-conviction case, while his NFL work showed that he continued pursuing institutional racial questions near the end of his life.

His legacy is nonetheless contested. Admirers see an exceptional advocate who forced powerful institutions to answer for misconduct; critics see a lawyer whose extraordinary rhetorical ability sometimes amplified polarization and courtroom spectacle.

Both interpretations acknowledge the same central fact: Cochran changed the public image of the American trial lawyer and made questions of race, police credibility, and institutional accountability impossible to separate from some of the most watched legal proceedings of his era.

Historical Significance

Cochran's career connects the postwar Black migration, the rise of Black Los Angeles, the civil-rights era, police brutality litigation, the Black Panther period, wrongful-conviction advocacy, the Rodney King era, celebrity culture, televised trials, DNA evidence, and employment diversity in professional sports.

He also represents a transition in civil-rights law from constitutional test cases alone toward high-stakes tort litigation capable of imposing substantial financial costs on police departments and municipalities.

The Simpson trial demonstrated how changes in television, race relations, forensic science, and distrust of police could converge inside one criminal courtroom.

The Pratt and Rooney Rule campaigns show that Cochran's influence extended beyond celebrity criminal defense into institutional reform.

Historical Record and Common Misconceptions

Cochran was already a highly accomplished and prominent lawyer before O.J. Simpson hired him. Simpson made him internationally famous; the case did not create his legal career.

The phrase "If it doesn't fit, you must acquit" was delivered by Cochran but was devised by fellow defense attorney Gerald Uelmen.

Cochran did not represent Simpson in the later civil trial in which Simpson was found liable for the deaths of Nicole Brown Simpson and Ronald Goldman.

A criminal acquittal and a civil liability verdict are not legally contradictory because the proceedings use different standards of proof and procedural rules.

Cochran's criticism of LAPD racism was not invented solely for the Simpson case. Police misconduct had been a central part of his practice since the 1960s.

Geronimo Pratt—not Simpson—was the case Cochran repeatedly identified as his most important or proudest legal achievement.

Cochran was not exclusively a criminal defense attorney. Much of his career involved plaintiffs' civil litigation, wrongful death, police misconduct, personal injury, and civil rights.

His role in the Rooney Rule campaign is an important but often overlooked part of his later civil-rights work.

Geographic Scope

Cochran was born in Shreveport, Louisiana, but Los Angeles became the defining city of his life and career.

His work was closely connected to Black neighborhoods of South and West Los Angeles, the downtown criminal courts, Wilshire Boulevard legal practice, Los Angeles police institutions, and the city's entertainment industry.

As his reputation grew, his cases and law firm expanded to New York, other major American cities, and a national plaintiffs' practice.

His work on Abner Louima and Sean Combs made New York an important second center of his late career, while the Rooney Rule campaign gave his civil-rights work national institutional reach.

Legal Context

Cochran's career developed during a major expansion of federal civil-rights litigation and constitutional regulation of police conduct.

His police cases involved wrongful death, civil rights, tort law, municipal liability, evidence, and the difficult problem of proving misconduct when official witnesses controlled much of the initial record.

His criminal-defense work operated under the constitutional guarantees of counsel, confrontation, due process, exclusion of improperly obtained evidence, and proof beyond a reasonable doubt.

The Pratt litigation implicated prosecutorial disclosure duties and government informant activity, while the Simpson trial became a national lesson in forensic evidence, reasonable doubt, jury selection, police credibility, and racial context.

The NFL campaign involved employment discrimination and the threat of civil litigation as leverage for institutional reform.
