Thurgood Marshall

Civil-rights lawyer, NAACP Legal Defense Fund leader, federal judge, solicitor general, and first African American justice of the United States Supreme Court

Overview

Thurgood Marshall was an American civil-rights lawyer, federal judge, United States solicitor general, and associate justice of the Supreme Court whose career transformed the constitutional law of race and equality. Before joining the Court, he was the leading courtroom strategist in the legal campaign against Jim Crow segregation and argued more than thirty cases before the Supreme Court, winning twenty-nine of the thirty-two cases commonly credited to his advocacy.

Marshall was born in Baltimore, Maryland, in 1908. He attended Lincoln University in Pennsylvania and Howard University School of Law, where he studied under Charles Hamilton Houston and graduated first in his class in 1933.

Unable to attend the segregated University of Maryland School of Law, Marshall helped challenge that institution only a few years later. Working with Houston in Murray v. Pearson, he helped secure the admission of Donald Gaines Murray to the University of Maryland law school, an early victory in the strategy of attacking the legal foundations of separate-but-equal education.

Marshall joined the national legal staff of the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People and eventually became the chief lawyer of the NAACP Legal Defense and Educational Fund. He travelled throughout the country representing Black teachers, students, voters, criminal defendants, military personnel, homeowners, and communities confronting discrimination.

His legal strategy was cumulative. Rather than attacking every form of segregation at once, Marshall and the lawyers around him built a chain of precedents showing that supposedly equal segregated institutions were unequal in resources, opportunity, prestige, professional interaction, political power, or psychological effect.

Important victories included challenges to white primaries, exclusion from graduate and professional schools, racially restrictive housing covenants, discriminatory jury practices, and segregation in interstate transportation. Cases such as Smith v. Allwright, Shelley v. Kraemer, Sipuel v. Board of Regents, and Sweatt v. Painter weakened the constitutional foundations of Jim Crow before the decisive school-segregation cases reached the Court.

Marshall's most famous victory came in Brown v. Board of Education. On May 17, 1954, the Supreme Court unanimously held that state-imposed racial segregation in public schools violated the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment because separate educational facilities were inherently unequal.

The decision was not the work of Marshall alone. Brown represented a coordinated effort involving local Black parents and students, community organizers, state and local lawyers, social scientists, and an extraordinary legal team that included Robert L. Carter, Spottswood Robinson, Constance Baker Motley, Jack Greenberg, James Nabrit, George E. C. Hayes, and others. Marshall nevertheless served as the central national strategist and principal oral advocate.

After years of civil-rights litigation, Marshall entered the federal judiciary. President John F. Kennedy gave him a recess appointment to the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit in 1961. After prolonged Senate resistance, he was confirmed in 1962.

President Lyndon B. Johnson appointed Marshall solicitor general in 1965. As the federal government's principal Supreme Court advocate, Marshall argued nineteen cases and won fourteen. The position broadened his legal work beyond civil rights and demonstrated his capacity to represent the institutional interests of the United States.

Johnson nominated Marshall to the Supreme Court on June 13, 1967. The Senate confirmed him 69-11 on August 30, and Marshall became the first African American justice in the Court's history.

Marshall served on the Supreme Court from 1967 until 1991. He generally belonged to the Court's liberal wing and strongly defended racial equality, affirmative action, criminal defendants' rights, freedom of expression, access to courts, reproductive liberty, prisoners' rights, and constitutional protection for poor and politically marginalized people.

He also became one of the Court's most persistent opponents of capital punishment. After Furman v. Georgia temporarily invalidated existing death-penalty systems in 1972, Marshall concluded that the death penalty itself violated the Eighth Amendment. When the Court allowed revised capital statutes in 1976, he maintained that position for the remainder of his judicial career.

As the Court became more conservative during the 1970s and 1980s, Marshall increasingly wrote dissents. His opinions in cases involving school finance, metropolitan desegregation, affirmative action, criminal justice, and the death penalty drew upon both legal doctrine and his unusually extensive firsthand knowledge of how law operated against people without political power.

Marshall's historical perspective was especially visible in his 1987 remarks during the bicentennial of the Constitution. He rejected an uncomplicated celebration of the original 1787 document, emphasizing that it protected slavery and excluded most Americans from political participation. He argued that constitutional greatness emerged through later amendments, civil war, political struggle, and generations of people who forced the nation to broaden the meaning of freedom.

Marshall retired in 1991 after declining health. President George H. W. Bush nominated Clarence Thomas to succeed him. Marshall died of heart failure at the National Naval Medical Center in Bethesda, Maryland, on January 24, 1993, at age eighty-four. He was buried at Arlington National Cemetery.

Marshall's legacy is unusually broad. He was not merely the first Black Supreme Court justice. Before taking the bench, he had already helped dismantle the legal architecture of American apartheid. As a justice, he then spent nearly a quarter century applying a constitutional philosophy rooted in equal citizenship, practical experience, and skepticism toward government power exercised against minorities and the poor.

Birth and Family Background

Marshall was born in Baltimore on July 2, 1908, to William Canfield Marshall and Norma Arica Williams Marshall.

His ancestry included people who had experienced slavery, and his family history connected him directly to the long transition from slavery to Black urban professional life.

The household emphasized education, argument, self-respect, and political awareness.

William Canfield Marshall

Marshall's father, William Canfield Marshall, worked in service occupations including railroad and club employment.

He was deeply interested in current events and legal controversies despite lacking formal legal training.

Marshall later credited his father with teaching him to argue logically by challenging statements and requiring him to defend his conclusions.

Norma Arica Marshall

Marshall's mother, Norma Arica Williams Marshall, was a teacher.

She placed strong emphasis on education and helped finance her son's legal studies.

The combination of his mother's academic expectations and his father's argumentative style helped shape the future lawyer's confidence and discipline.

Baltimore and Jim Crow

Marshall grew up in a Baltimore governed by racial segregation in housing, education, public accommodations, employment, and professional opportunity.

Maryland was not part of the Deep South, but Jim Crow law and custom sharply restricted Black citizens.

Marshall's later legal career was therefore directed against institutions he had encountered personally rather than against an abstract system known only through books.

Frederick Douglass High School

Marshall attended Baltimore's Frederick Douglass High School and graduated in 1925.

He was known as a strong debater and sometimes as a mischievous student who nonetheless possessed unusual verbal ability.

His education occurred in a segregated school system whose constitutional legitimacy he would later help destroy.

Learning the Constitution

A frequently repeated account of Marshall's youth holds that school discipline sometimes required him to study or memorize portions of the United States Constitution.

Whether every later anecdotal detail is exact, Marshall clearly became familiar with constitutional language early and later joked that punishment had given him an education useful to his career.

The irony was profound: a Black child educated under segregation would later become one of the country's most important interpreters of the Fourteenth Amendment.

Lincoln University

Marshall entered Lincoln University in Pennsylvania, one of the country's oldest historically Black colleges.

He graduated with honors in 1930.

At Lincoln he participated in the varsity debating team and encountered an intellectually ambitious Black student body preparing for leadership in law, education, literature, medicine, religion, and public life.

Debate and Intellectual Development

Marshall spent four years on Lincoln's debate team and participated in interracial and international debates.

Competitive debate sharpened skills that later defined his courtroom practice: rapid analysis, concise response, understanding an opponent's strongest argument, and communicating complex principles in ordinary language.

He learned to combine technical preparation with humor and conversational ease.

Lincoln University Contemporaries

Marshall attended Lincoln during a period when the institution educated several people who later became nationally significant.

His contemporaries included poet Langston Hughes and future leaders in Black professional and cultural life.

The university exposed Marshall to debates over racial integration, Black nationalism, political strategy, and the responsibilities of an educated Black elite.

Marriage to Vivian Burey

Marshall married Vivian "Buster" Burey in 1929 while still a student.

Burey became an important partner during the difficult early years of his legal career.

Their marriage lasted until her death from cancer in 1955.

Exclusion from the University of Maryland

After Lincoln, Marshall wanted to study law closer to home.

The University of Maryland School of Law did not admit Black students because of segregation.

Marshall instead travelled to Washington, D.C., to attend Howard University School of Law.

The exclusion became personally significant when he later helped force the University of Maryland to admit a Black applicant.

Howard University School of Law

Marshall entered Howard University School of Law in 1930.

Howard had become one of the central institutions for training Black lawyers in the United States.

Marshall commuted from Baltimore to Washington while studying under a demanding faculty committed to using legal doctrine as an instrument of racial reform.

Charles Hamilton Houston

The most important professional influence on Marshall was Charles Hamilton Houston, vice dean and later dean of Howard Law School.

Houston believed lawyers should become "social engineers" capable of using law to reshape unjust institutions.

He imposed rigorous standards of research, preparation, writing, and courtroom performance on students who would later become the core of the twentieth-century civil-rights bar.

Graduating First in His Class

Marshall graduated from Howard in 1933 first in his class.

His academic performance established him as one of Houston's strongest students.

The training he received at Howard gave him the doctrinal foundation and strategic discipline that would guide his civil-rights litigation for the next three decades.

Opening a Baltimore Law Practice

After law school, Marshall opened a private practice in Baltimore.

The early practice was financially difficult because Black clients often had limited resources and white institutions were unlikely to hire a young Black attorney.

Marshall nevertheless became involved with the Baltimore branch of the NAACP and increasingly devoted his work to civil rights.

Donald Gaines Murray

Donald Gaines Murray was a Black Amherst College graduate who sought admission to the University of Maryland School of Law.

The school rejected him because of race and proposed that Maryland assist Black students attending law school outside the state.

Marshall and Charles Hamilton Houston challenged that arrangement as constitutionally inadequate.

Murray v. Pearson

In Murray v. Pearson, Marshall and Houston argued that Maryland could not satisfy equal protection by excluding Black students from the state's own law school and sending them elsewhere.

Maryland's highest court agreed and ordered Murray admitted.

The case was personally meaningful for Marshall because it attacked the same segregation policy that had prevented him from attending the University of Maryland.

The Incremental Strategy Against Segregation

Houston and Marshall recognized that the Supreme Court was unlikely to overturn Plessy v. Ferguson immediately.

They therefore built precedent through carefully selected cases in which segregated facilities were demonstrably unequal or no Black facility existed at all.

Each victory increased the financial, administrative, and constitutional difficulty of maintaining segregation.

Joining the NAACP National Legal Staff

Marshall joined the NAACP's national legal staff in 1936.

He travelled widely, often entering communities where Black lawyers faced intimidation, surveillance, and threats of violence.

The work demanded not only courtroom ability but coordination with local lawyers, branches, teachers, ministers, parents, and community activists.

Chief Legal Officer of the NAACP

By 1938 Marshall had become the NAACP's chief legal officer.

He inherited and expanded Houston's campaign against state-enforced racial inequality.

His responsibilities included education, voting, criminal justice, labor, transportation, housing, military discrimination, and protection of local activists.

Missouri ex rel. Gaines v. Canada

The NAACP's graduate-school campaign achieved a major Supreme Court victory in Missouri ex rel. Gaines v. Canada in 1938.

The Court held that Missouri could not exclude Lloyd Gaines from legal education within the state while offering to pay for his education elsewhere.

The ruling strengthened the principle that states had to provide genuinely equal educational opportunity within their own systems.

Dangerous Southern Litigation

Marshall's civil-rights work frequently placed him in personal danger.

He travelled through southern towns where white officials and mobs could threaten Black lawyers with little expectation of accountability.

Marshall later described episodes involving police harassment and fear of lynching, experiences that informed his lifelong skepticism toward claims that criminal procedure should simply defer to local authority.

Chambers v. Florida

In 1940, Marshall participated in the successful Supreme Court challenge in Chambers v. Florida.

The case involved Black defendants whose murder confessions had been obtained after prolonged coercive interrogation.

The Supreme Court reversed the convictions, holding that the coerced confessions violated due process.

The case was an early demonstration that Marshall's civil-rights practice included criminal justice as well as segregation.

Founding the NAACP Legal Defense and Educational Fund

In 1940, the NAACP created the Legal Defense and Educational Fund, commonly known as LDF, with Marshall as its first Director-Counsel.

The new organization gave civil-rights litigation a more specialized institutional base.

Marshall led LDF until entering the federal judiciary in 1961.

Black Teachers' Salary Cases

Marshall and the NAACP litigated cases challenging lower salaries paid to Black teachers in segregated school systems.

These suits exposed the contradiction of school districts claiming separate systems were equal while openly paying Black professionals less.

Salary litigation also helped build local organizational networks that later supported direct challenges to segregation itself.

White Primaries

In much of the South, Democratic Party primaries effectively determined election outcomes, while Black citizens were excluded from voting in those primaries.

The white-primary system allowed states and political parties to preserve Black disenfranchisement while claiming that the discrimination was private party activity rather than state action.

Marshall and the NAACP helped attack that structure.

Smith v. Allwright

In 1944, the Supreme Court decided Smith v. Allwright, striking down the Texas white primary.

Marshall argued that exclusion from the decisive stage of the electoral process violated the Fifteenth Amendment.

The ruling became one of the most important voting-rights victories before the Voting Rights Act of 1965.

Morgan v. Virginia

In 1946, the Supreme Court invalidated a Virginia law requiring racial segregation on interstate buses in Morgan v. Virginia.

The decision rested on the Commerce Clause rather than a broad ruling that all transportation segregation violated equal protection.

The case nevertheless weakened state control over segregation in interstate travel and became an important precursor to later freedom-ride activism.

Restrictive Covenants

Racially restrictive covenants were private agreements intended to prevent homes from being sold or occupied by Black people and other disfavored groups.

Although private discrimination raised difficult constitutional questions, enforcement of the covenants depended upon state courts.

Marshall and other civil-rights lawyers argued that judicial enforcement itself constituted state action.

Shelley v. Kraemer

In 1948, the Supreme Court held in Shelley v. Kraemer that courts could not constitutionally enforce racially restrictive housing covenants.

The decision did not outlaw every private discriminatory agreement, but it removed the power of state courts to enforce them.

The ruling was an important step in connecting the Fourteenth Amendment to housing discrimination.

Sipuel v. Board of Regents

Ada Lois Sipuel sought admission to the University of Oklahoma law school and was denied because she was Black.

Marshall argued her case before the Supreme Court in 1948.

The Court held that Oklahoma had to provide legal education to her on the same basis as it provided education to other qualified applicants.

Sweatt v. Painter

Heman Marion Sweatt applied to the University of Texas School of Law and was rejected because of race.

Texas attempted to preserve segregation by creating a separate law school for Black students.

Marshall argued that equality could not be measured only by buildings and books; professional education also depended on reputation, alumni networks, faculty, student interaction, and the wider intellectual environment.

In 1950, the Supreme Court agreed that the separate Black law school was constitutionally unequal and ordered Sweatt admitted to the University of Texas.

McLaurin v. Oklahoma State Regents

George McLaurin was admitted to graduate study at the University of Oklahoma but was forced to sit apart from white students in classrooms, libraries, and dining facilities.

In 1950, the Supreme Court held that those internal segregation practices impaired his ability to study and interact with other students.

Together with Sweatt, the decision moved the Court toward recognizing that racial segregation itself damaged educational equality.

From Equalization to Direct Attack

By the early 1950s, Marshall and LDF had accumulated precedents showing that separate institutions repeatedly failed constitutional scrutiny.

The legal campaign then shifted toward a direct argument that state-imposed segregation in public education was inherently unequal even if physical facilities could be equalized.

This strategic transition led to the cases consolidated as Brown v. Board of Education.

The Five School Segregation Cases

The Supreme Court's school-desegregation litigation combined cases from Kansas, South Carolina, Virginia, Delaware, and the District of Columbia.

The cases arose from different local circumstances but shared a central challenge to government-imposed racial separation in public education.

The District of Columbia case, Bolling v. Sharpe, arose under the Fifth Amendment because the Fourteenth Amendment applies directly to states rather than the federal government.

The Brown Legal Team

Marshall served as the central national strategist, but Brown was a collective legal achievement.

Important lawyers included Robert L. Carter, Spottswood Robinson III, Constance Baker Motley, Jack Greenberg, James Nabrit Jr., George E. C. Hayes, Oliver Hill, Harold Boulware, Louis Redding, Charles Scott, and others.

Local plaintiffs, parents, students, teachers, churches, and NAACP branches supplied the courage and factual foundation without which the litigation could not have existed.

Social Science Evidence

The school cases included social-science evidence concerning the psychological effects of legally imposed segregation on Black children.

Kenneth and Mamie Clark's work involving children's racial identification became especially famous.

The legal argument did not rest solely on psychology; Marshall's team also argued that racial classifications enforced by the state were constitutionally arbitrary and incompatible with equal citizenship.

First Brown Argument

The Supreme Court first heard argument in the school-segregation cases in December 1952.

The justices did not immediately decide the cases and later ordered reargument, including historical questions concerning the original understanding of the Fourteenth Amendment.

Marshall's team used the additional time to strengthen both the constitutional and historical arguments against segregation.

Reargument and Earl Warren

Before the cases were decided, Chief Justice Fred Vinson died and President Dwight Eisenhower appointed Earl Warren.

The Court heard reargument in December 1953.

Warren worked to secure a unanimous decision, believing that a divided ruling on school segregation would weaken the Court's authority and encourage resistance.

Brown v. Board of Education

On May 17, 1954, the Supreme Court unanimously ruled that racial segregation in public schools violated the Equal Protection Clause.

Chief Justice Warren wrote that separate educational facilities were inherently unequal.

The decision repudiated the application of the separate-but-equal principle to public education and became the central constitutional victory of Marshall's career.

What Brown Did and Did Not Do

Brown declared state-imposed school segregation unconstitutional, but it did not instantly integrate American schools.

Many school districts resisted, delayed, closed schools, redirected public funds, or used residential boundaries and private institutions to preserve separation.

The distinction between constitutional victory and actual implementation became one of the central lessons of the civil-rights era.

Brown II

In 1955, the Supreme Court addressed remedies in the school cases.

The Court ordered desegregation to proceed with what became the controversial phrase "all deliberate speed."

Marshall and other civil-rights lawyers later confronted years of resistance caused in part by the flexibility local officials drew from the remedial language.

Massive Resistance

Southern politicians organized a campaign of "Massive Resistance" against Brown.

States adopted pupil-placement laws, tuition grants, school-closing measures, and other mechanisms intended to avoid integration.

Marshall and LDF lawyers spent years returning to federal courts to enforce a ruling that had been unanimous in principle but fiercely resisted in practice.

Little Rock and Cooper v. Aaron

The 1957 crisis at Central High School in Little Rock, Arkansas, demonstrated the depth of resistance to Brown.

Federal troops were required to protect Black students entering the school after state officials opposed integration.

In Cooper v. Aaron in 1958, the Supreme Court unanimously rejected Arkansas officials' attempt to delay desegregation and reaffirmed that state officials were bound by the Court's constitutional rulings.

Marshall participated in the legal effort defending the students' rights.

Civil-Rights Lawyer as National Figure

By the late 1950s, Marshall had become one of the best-known lawyers in the United States.

His reputation rested not on a single case but on decades of litigation throughout the country.

He became known informally as "Mr. Civil Rights" and served as a bridge among local activists, national civil-rights organizations, courts, foundations, political leaders, and the press.

Relationship with Martin Luther King Jr. and Direct Action

Marshall supported the goals of the broader civil-rights movement but sometimes differed with activists who emphasized mass protest, civil disobedience, or rapid confrontation.

His professional instinct favored carefully prepared litigation and enforceable legal victories.

The movement ultimately depended on both strategies: direct action changed public and political conditions, while litigation translated constitutional claims into judicial orders.

Marriage to Cecilia Suyat

After Vivian Marshall died in 1955, Marshall married Cecilia "Cissy" Suyat later that year.

Suyat had worked at the NAACP and was of Filipino ancestry.

Their interracial marriage occurred years before the Supreme Court invalidated state bans on interracial marriage in Loving v. Virginia.

They had two sons, Thurgood Marshall Jr. and John W. Marshall.

Appointment to the Second Circuit

President John F. Kennedy selected Marshall for the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit.

Kennedy gave him a recess appointment on October 5, 1961.

The nomination faced prolonged opposition, particularly from segregationist senators, before the Senate confirmed him on September 11, 1962.

Second Circuit Service

Marshall served on the Second Circuit from 1961 until 1965.

The appellate judgeship required him to decide a broad range of federal cases rather than act as an advocate for a civil-rights organization.

The position demonstrated his ability to shift from strategic litigation to judicial decision-making within precedent and institutional constraints.

Solicitor General of the United States

In 1965, President Lyndon B. Johnson appointed Marshall solicitor general of the United States.

The solicitor general represents the federal government before the Supreme Court and helps determine the government's legal positions in appellate litigation.

Marshall became the first African American to hold the office.

Advocacy as Solicitor General

As solicitor general, Marshall argued nineteen cases before the Supreme Court and won fourteen.

His docket extended far beyond racial discrimination and required him to defend federal statutes, agencies, criminal prosecutions, immigration policy, labor regulation, and other government interests.

The office gave Marshall experience representing the United States as an institution rather than a particular civil-rights constituency.

Tom C. Clark's Retirement

A Supreme Court vacancy opened in 1967 when Justice Tom C. Clark retired.

Clark stepped down after his son, Ramsey Clark, became attorney general, avoiding a potential appearance of conflict involving Justice Department cases before the Court.

President Johnson used the vacancy to nominate Marshall.

Supreme Court Nomination

President Johnson nominated Marshall on June 13, 1967.

Johnson presented the appointment as both historically significant and justified by Marshall's extraordinary legal qualifications.

Marshall had already argued dozens of cases before the Court, served as a federal appellate judge, and served as solicitor general.

Confirmation Battle

Marshall's confirmation encountered opposition from senators who challenged his judicial philosophy, civil-rights record, and qualifications.

Some resistance reflected the same southern segregationist politics Marshall had fought as an advocate.

The Senate confirmed him by a vote of 69-11 on August 30, 1967.

First Black Supreme Court Justice

Marshall became the first African American to sit on the Supreme Court.

He took the constitutional oath in September and the judicial oath when the Court's new term began in October 1967.

His appointment carried enormous symbolic weight because the Supreme Court had once approved slavery, racial exclusion, and segregation and now included a justice who had spent his career dismantling those doctrines.

The Warren Court Context

Marshall joined the Court near the end of Chief Justice Earl Warren's tenure.

The Warren Court had expanded constitutional protections in racial equality, voting, criminal procedure, free speech, and other areas.

Marshall generally supported that rights-protective constitutional project while bringing unusual experience as a trial lawyer and civil-rights litigator.

Judicial Philosophy

Marshall's jurisprudence emphasized the practical consequences of legal rules for people with little political or economic power.

He did not regard constitutional interpretation as an exercise detached from history.

His opinions repeatedly examined how government power actually operated against racial minorities, criminal defendants, prisoners, poor litigants, workers, and unpopular groups.

Equality and the Fourteenth Amendment

Marshall viewed the Equal Protection Clause as a central constitutional guarantee of equal citizenship.

His experience with Jim Crow made him especially skeptical of laws that classified people by race or reproduced entrenched hierarchy.

He also believed that formal neutrality could be insufficient when government had created or maintained deep systems of exclusion.

Affirmative Action

Marshall supported constitutionally permissible race-conscious policies intended to remedy the consequences of historical discrimination.

He rejected the claim that every governmental use of race was morally or constitutionally equivalent.

For Marshall, a racial classification designed to maintain white supremacy differed fundamentally from a limited policy intended to open institutions from which Black citizens had long been excluded.

Regents of the University of California v. Bakke

In the 1978 Bakke case, the Court considered a medical-school admissions program that reserved places for disadvantaged minority applicants.

Marshall wrote separately to emphasize the long history of legally enforced racial exclusion in the United States.

He argued that the Constitution permitted carefully designed race-conscious measures aimed at overcoming the continuing effects of that history.

School Finance and San Antonio v. Rodriguez

In San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez in 1973, the Court rejected a federal equal-protection challenge to a Texas school-finance system that produced substantial disparities among districts.

Marshall dissented.

He objected to a constitutional framework that treated the relationship between wealth and access to public education too narrowly and warned that educational inequality could undermine meaningful opportunity.

Metropolitan Segregation and Milliken v. Bradley

In Milliken v. Bradley in 1974, the Court restricted the use of metropolitan-wide desegregation remedies in the Detroit area unless suburban districts themselves were shown to have committed constitutional violations.

Marshall dissented strongly.

He argued that limiting remedies at municipal boundaries would leave many urban Black children in racially isolated school systems and weaken the promise of Brown.

Capital Punishment

Marshall became one of the Supreme Court's most determined opponents of the death penalty.

His experience with racially discriminatory criminal justice contributed to his concern that capital punishment was arbitrary, irreversible, and disproportionately imposed on vulnerable defendants.

He eventually concluded that the death penalty violated the Eighth Amendment under all circumstances.

Furman v. Georgia

In 1972, the Court decided Furman v. Georgia, invalidating existing death-penalty systems because their application was constitutionally defective.

Marshall wrote a separate concurrence arguing more broadly against capital punishment.

The decision led states to redesign their capital statutes rather than permanently end the death penalty.

Gregg v. Georgia

In 1976, the Supreme Court upheld revised capital-punishment procedures in Gregg v. Georgia and related cases.

Marshall dissented, maintaining that the death penalty itself was cruel and unusual punishment.

For the rest of his career, he and Justice William Brennan repeatedly noted their opposition when the Court permitted executions to proceed.

Criminal Procedure

Marshall generally favored strong procedural protections for criminal defendants.

His views reflected decades of seeing coerced confessions, discriminatory juries, inadequate counsel, police abuse, and prosecutorial misconduct in civil-rights cases.

He regarded procedural rights not as technical obstacles to justice but as safeguards against the predictable abuse of concentrated government power.

Prisoners' Rights

Marshall rejected the older assumption that imprisonment placed people largely outside constitutional protection.

He supported judicial review of prison conditions and meaningful protection for prisoners' access to courts, religious liberty, due process, and freedom from cruel treatment.

His approach helped develop the broader modern understanding that incarceration restricts liberty but does not erase constitutional personhood.

Privacy and Stanley v. Georgia

Marshall wrote the Court's opinion in Stanley v. Georgia in 1969.

The Court held that the government could not criminalize the private possession of obscene material in a person's home merely because the state disapproved of what the individual chose to read or view.

The decision reflected Marshall's concern with intellectual freedom and limits on government intrusion into private life.

Reproductive Liberty

Marshall joined the Court's recognition of constitutional protection for reproductive decision-making during the 1970s.

He voted with the majority in Roe v. Wade and later opposed efforts he believed improperly burdened abortion access.

His approach connected reproductive liberty to privacy, equality, and the real-world burdens government regulation could impose on poor women.

Voting Rights

Marshall's Supreme Court work continued his lifelong concern with political participation.

He generally supported strong enforcement of voting rights and close scrutiny of electoral arrangements that diluted minority political power.

His experience litigating white-primary cases gave him historical perspective on how formally neutral election rules could preserve exclusion.

First Amendment and Expression

Marshall generally favored broad protection for speech, press, political association, and unpopular expression.

He understood that minority movements often depend on constitutional protection precisely when their views are unpopular with local majorities.

His First Amendment jurisprudence therefore overlapped with his civil-rights experience.

The Poor and Access to Justice

Marshall repeatedly emphasized that legal rights can become meaningless when people lack practical access to courts, counsel, education, or public institutions.

He was skeptical of rules that treated poverty as irrelevant when financial barriers determined whether a person could exercise a legal right.

This concern appeared across criminal procedure, education, welfare, and access-to-court cases.

Relationship with Justice William Brennan

Marshall often aligned with Justice William J. Brennan Jr., particularly on civil liberties, equal protection, and the death penalty.

As the Court moved rightward, Brennan and Marshall became the most consistent voices for several Warren Court principles.

Their repeated death-penalty dissents became a distinctive feature of the Court's later twentieth-century docket.

The Burger Court

Chief Justice Warren Burger succeeded Earl Warren in 1969.

Although the Burger Court did not immediately reverse the Warren Court's major precedents, its doctrine became more cautious in several areas.

Marshall increasingly found himself dissenting from decisions that limited school desegregation remedies, narrowed protections for defendants, or restricted equality claims.

The Rehnquist Court

William Rehnquist became chief justice in 1986.

During Marshall's final years, the Court became substantially more conservative on criminal justice, federalism, affirmative action, and several civil-rights questions.

Marshall's dissents increasingly served as defenses of doctrines he believed were being weakened.

The 1987 Constitution Bicentennial Speech

During the bicentennial of the United States Constitution in 1987, Marshall challenged celebratory accounts suggesting that the original document represented a completed triumph of liberty.

He emphasized that the 1787 Constitution tolerated slavery and excluded women, Black people, Indigenous people, and many poor citizens from meaningful political power.

Marshall argued that the Constitution Americans celebrated had been transformed by the Civil War amendments, political struggle, judicial interpretation, legislation, and movements demanding inclusion.

Marshall and Originalism

Marshall's constitutional thought differed sharply from theories that treat the intentions or expectations of the founding generation as the principal limit on modern constitutional meaning.

He believed the founding generation had left profound injustices embedded in the original constitutional order.

For Marshall, later amendments and historical struggles were not deviations from the Constitution but essential parts of the constitutional tradition.

Storytelling Inside the Court

Marshall was famous among colleagues and law clerks for telling stories from his years as a civil-rights lawyer.

The stories conveyed lessons about police power, racial violence, courts, courage, and the difference between legal doctrine on paper and law as experienced by ordinary people.

Justice Sandra Day O'Connor and others later emphasized that Marshall's life experience changed how colleagues understood cases before them.

Humor and Personality

Marshall combined formidable legal seriousness with humor, informality, storytelling, and a dislike of unnecessary pretension.

His public image as a solemn civil-rights icon can obscure the wit remembered by colleagues and friends.

Humor was also a professional tool that helped him disarm hostile audiences and maintain perspective during dangerous or frustrating litigation.

Law Clerks and Mentorship

Marshall's chambers trained generations of lawyers who later became judges, professors, government officials, and advocates.

Among his former clerks was Elena Kagan, who later became solicitor general and a justice of the Supreme Court.

Marshall often used stories and direct conversation rather than abstract lectures to teach clerks how legal decisions affected real people.

Ill Health and Retirement

Marshall's health declined during his later years on the Court.

He resisted retirement for as long as he believed he could continue performing the work.

On June 27, 1991, he announced that he would retire, and his retirement became effective October 1.

Clarence Thomas as Successor

President George H. W. Bush nominated Clarence Thomas to replace Marshall.

Thomas was also African American but held a substantially more conservative constitutional philosophy.

The succession demonstrated that racial identity did not determine a single judicial ideology and marked a dramatic shift in the jurisprudential direction of Marshall's former seat.

Death

Marshall died of heart failure on January 24, 1993, at the National Naval Medical Center in Bethesda, Maryland.

He was eighty-four years old.

His death prompted national tributes recognizing both his civil-rights career and his nearly twenty-four years on the Supreme Court.

Lying in Repose at the Supreme Court

Marshall's flag-draped casket lay in repose in the Great Hall of the Supreme Court.

He was only the second justice to receive that honor at the time.

Thousands of people came to the Court to pay respects to a man whose career had transformed the institution from outside before he joined it from within.

Funeral and Arlington National Cemetery

Marshall's funeral service was held at Washington National Cathedral.

Chief Justice William Rehnquist and civil-rights leaders paid tribute to his role in making the constitutional promise of equal justice more meaningful.

Marshall was buried at Arlington National Cemetery in Section 5, Grave 40-3.

Legacy

Thurgood Marshall is one of the most consequential American lawyers of the twentieth century.

His importance began long before his Supreme Court appointment. As an advocate, he helped dismantle white primaries, segregated higher education, restrictive covenants, coerced-confession practices, and ultimately public-school segregation.

His method demonstrated how constitutional change can be built through decades of coordinated local litigation rather than one dramatic lawsuit.

As a justice, Marshall brought the experience of a civil-rights trial lawyer into the Supreme Court's internal deliberations. He consistently asked how rules that appeared neutral in appellate language operated in police stations, prisons, poor neighborhoods, segregated schools, and institutions shaped by historical exclusion.

Marshall's jurisprudence did not prevail in every case and became increasingly dissenting as the Court changed. Those dissents nevertheless became important statements of an alternative constitutional tradition centered on equal citizenship and practical liberty.

His appointment also had symbolic importance that should not be confused with his substantive legal achievement. Being first mattered, but Marshall had already earned a central place in constitutional history before he ever put on a Supreme Court robe.

Historical Significance

Marshall's life connects Jim Crow, historically Black higher education, the professionalization of civil-rights litigation, the NAACP, the collapse of separate-but-equal doctrine, the modern federal judiciary, the Great Society era, and the transformation of the Supreme Court from the Warren Court through the Rehnquist Court.

His career also demonstrates the relationship between social movements and courts. Litigation succeeded because local citizens were willing to become plaintiffs and activists were willing to organize despite retaliation.

Marshall's work shows that constitutional law can be both conservative in method and revolutionary in effect: many of his greatest victories emerged from precedent, procedural discipline, and incremental argument rather than rhetorical rejection of the legal system.

At the same time, his later dissents reveal his awareness that legal victories are reversible, narrowable, or ineffective without political and institutional commitment.

Historical Record and Common Misconceptions

Marshall did not single-handedly create or win Brown v. Board of Education. He was the central national strategist and principal advocate within a large network of plaintiffs, local lawyers, NAACP branches, LDF attorneys, and social scientists.

The legal attack on school segregation did not begin with Brown. It developed through years of cases involving graduate education, teacher salaries, voting, housing, transportation, and other forms of state-enforced racial inequality.

Marshall was not simply an activist placed directly on the Supreme Court. Before his appointment he had served approximately four years on a federal court of appeals and two years as solicitor general.

He was the first Black Supreme Court justice, not the first Black federal judge.

Marshall's constitutional philosophy extended well beyond race. His opinions addressed criminal procedure, capital punishment, privacy, reproductive liberty, prisoners, voting, speech, poverty, administrative law, and federal power.

His opposition to the death penalty became categorical after Furman; he did not merely object to particular executions.

Marshall's 1987 constitutional criticism did not reject the Constitution itself. He argued that its greatness came from transformation through amendments and struggle rather than from treating the compromises of 1787 as morally complete.

The commonly cited record of 29 victories in 32 Supreme Court cases refers to his extraordinary advocacy career before becoming a justice and is one reason he was already a historic lawyer before his judicial appointments.

Geographic Scope

Baltimore and the state of Maryland shaped Marshall's early life, education, and first legal practice.

Washington, D.C., became central through Howard University, the NAACP's national litigation, the Department of Justice, and the Supreme Court.

Marshall's civil-rights work was national and particularly intensive throughout the South, where he challenged segregated schools, voting systems, criminal prosecutions, transportation rules, and state universities.

His Second Circuit service connected him institutionally to New York, Connecticut, and Vermont.

His legacy is national, reflected in courthouses, schools, law centers, archives, monuments, and civil-rights institutions across the United States.

Legal Context

Marshall's early career unfolded under Plessy v. Ferguson, the 1896 Supreme Court decision that permitted legally mandated racial separation under the doctrine of "separate but equal."

The NAACP strategy relied heavily on the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment, the Fifteenth Amendment's protection of voting rights, federal due process, the Commerce Clause, and doctrines governing state action.

The Civil War amendments had created constitutional tools against racial subordination, but those guarantees were weakened for decades by narrow judicial interpretation and violent political resistance.

Marshall's litigation helped revive those constitutional provisions as enforceable protections of equal citizenship.

As a justice, he worked within a later constitutional environment shaped by Brown, the Civil Rights Act of 1964, Voting Rights Act of 1965, modern criminal-procedure doctrine, and expanding federal civil-rights enforcement.
