Bull Connor

Birmingham segregationist politician and public-safety commissioner whose use of police power, fire hoses, mass arrest, and accommodation of white-supremacist violence made him one of the most notorious official opponents of the Civil Rights Movement

Overview

Theophilus Eugene "Bull" Connor was an Alabama politician, Birmingham commissioner of public safety, segregationist, and white supremacist whose use of municipal power made him one of the most notorious public antagonists of the modern Civil Rights Movement.

Connor was born in Selma, Alabama, on July 11, 1897. He never graduated from high school but learned telegraphy, worked in communications and sales, and became a popular Birmingham sports-radio announcer before entering politics.

His public career lasted more than four decades. He served in the Alabama House of Representatives, repeatedly won election to Birmingham's city commission, became Alabama's Democratic national committeeman, sought the governorship, participated in the 1948 Dixiecrat revolt, and later served as president of the Alabama Public Service Commission.

Connor's historical importance rests primarily on his long tenure as Birmingham's commissioner of public safety. Under the commission form of city government, his portfolio gave him administrative authority over the police and fire departments and influence across other public institutions. That authority allowed him to turn segregationist ideology into municipal policy.

To many white voters, Connor cultivated the image of a forceful defender of public order, low taxes, working people, and the racial status quo. To Black Birmingham residents, civil-rights organizers, and later national audiences, he became a symbol of government power used to enforce racial subordination.

Connor's segregationism was not confined to rhetoric. Birmingham maintained segregated public facilities, discriminatory employment practices, racially divided neighborhoods, unequal schools, and a political order that excluded Black residents from meaningful power. Connor repeatedly used police authority and municipal regulation to defend that system.

He also tolerated or protected violent white supremacists. Reliable Alabama historical accounts describe him as apparently not a formal Ku Klux Klan member but as an official who protected Klansmen responsible for racial violence and bombings.

In 1961, during the Freedom Rides, Connor ordered Birmingham police to remain away from the Trailways bus station while Klansmen attacked arriving riders. The assault became one of the most infamous episodes in the Freedom Rides and demonstrated the danger created when local law enforcement deliberately withheld protection from civil-rights activists.

Connor also supported closing public parks rather than allowing court-ordered integration. Birmingham ultimately closed dozens of parks, playgrounds, golf courses, and swimming pools rather than permit interracial use.

By the early 1960s, Connor's uncompromising segregationism had damaged Birmingham's national reputation and increasingly alienated segments of the city's white business leadership. In 1962, voters approved replacing the three-member city commission with a mayor-council form of government, a reform supported in part by people seeking to remove Connor's influence.

Connor ran for mayor under the new system but lost to Albert Boutwell in 1963. Connor and the other commissioners challenged the transition in court and remained in office during the opening weeks of the Birmingham Campaign.

That timing placed Connor at the center of one of the decisive confrontations of the Civil Rights Movement. The Alabama Christian Movement for Human Rights, led by Fred Shuttlesworth, and the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, led by Martin Luther King Jr., launched Project C—"C" for confrontation—against Birmingham segregation in April 1963.

The campaign used sit-ins, marches, economic boycotts, mass meetings, and deliberate arrests to pressure downtown merchants and city authorities. Connor initially relied heavily on arrests rather than spectacular violence.

Martin Luther King Jr. and Ralph Abernathy were arrested on Good Friday, April 12. King's detention led to his famous "Letter from Birmingham Jail," which defended nonviolent direct action against calls for patience and criticized white moderates who preferred order to justice.

When adult participation and bail funds weakened, SCLC organizer James Bevel proposed mobilizing students. On May 2, more than a thousand young Black demonstrators left school and gathered at Sixteenth Street Baptist Church. Hundreds were arrested in what became known as the Children's Crusade.

On May 3, more students and supporters returned. Connor ordered the fire department and police to use high-pressure water hoses and police dogs to disperse protesters and onlookers. Children and adults were knocked down, chased, bitten, and battered while cameras recorded the confrontation.

Images of police dogs and high-pressure streams striking Black demonstrators circulated nationally and internationally. Rather than restoring Birmingham's authority, Connor's tactics damaged the legitimacy of segregation and increased pressure on the Kennedy administration and local business leaders.

The movement and Birmingham's business representatives reached a desegregation agreement on May 10. Connor opposed the direction of change, but his control of Birmingham government was already ending. On May 23, the Alabama Supreme Court ordered the outgoing commissioners to vacate their offices.

Connor did not disappear from public life. In 1964 he won election as president of the Alabama Public Service Commission and served there into the early 1970s.

Unlike some segregationist politicians who later apologized or attempted to reinvent their racial records, Connor never meaningfully repudiated his defense of segregation.

He died after suffering a stroke on March 10, 1973, and was buried at Elmwood Cemetery in Birmingham.

Connor's legacy is paradoxical. He devoted much of his career to preserving segregation, yet the visibility of his tactics helped weaken the political system he defended. His response to nonviolent demonstrators supplied the Civil Rights Movement with some of its most powerful visual evidence that local segregation was maintained not by custom alone but by coercive government power.

That unintended consequence should not be confused with contribution. Connor did not advance civil rights by choice. The movement strategically exposed his methods, journalists documented them, demonstrators endured them, and national audiences reacted against them.

A complete historical treatment therefore understands Bull Connor neither as a cartoon villain acting alone nor as a neutral official trapped by his era. He was an elected political actor who repeatedly chose to use public authority to preserve white supremacy within a much larger Birmingham system supported by law, business practices, white voters, segregationist organizations, and tolerated racial violence.

Birth in Selma

Connor was born in Selma, Alabama, on July 11, 1897.

Selma later became famous as a voting-rights battleground, but Connor's early life preceded the modern civil-rights era by decades.

He was born into a South whose political order had already been reshaped by disfranchisement, segregation, and the suppression of Reconstruction-era Black political power.

Hugh King Connor

Connor's father, Hugh King Connor, worked as a railroad train dispatcher and telegraph operator.

Railroad employment required repeated movement between communities and states.

Eugene learned telegraphy from his father and later used communications skills in his own employment.

Molly Godwin Connor

Connor's mother, Molly Godwin Connor, died of pneumonia when he was about eight years old.

Her death disrupted the family.

Connor subsequently spent substantial periods with relatives while his father continued travelling for railroad work.

Childhood Mobility

Connor lived in several places during childhood, including Atlanta and communities in Alabama.

He spent time with relatives in Plantersville and Birmingham.

The instability contributed to an irregular education.

Limited Formal Education

Connor attended schools in Alabama but never graduated from high school.

His later political success therefore rested on practical skills, personal connections, radio fame, and an ability to communicate with voters rather than formal higher education.

His career illustrates that political influence in early twentieth-century municipal Alabama did not require elite academic credentials.

Telegraphy and Early Employment

Connor learned telegraphy and worked in communications-related jobs.

He also held sales positions.

Those occupations developed the public confidence and familiarity with local networks that later supported his political career.

Marriage to Beara Levens

In 1920, Connor married Beara Levens, whose family was connected to the lumber industry.

The couple settled permanently in Birmingham in the early 1920s.

Their marriage lasted through Connor's political career and death.

Birmingham in the 1920s

Birmingham was a rapidly developing industrial city shaped by steel, coal, railroads, manufacturing, and migration.

Its Black population formed a major part of the industrial workforce while remaining segregated in housing, public accommodations, education, politics, and many occupations.

White elites and working-class whites often differed over economics while sharing broad support for racial segregation.

Sports Broadcasting

Connor became a popular sports-radio announcer in Birmingham.

He was particularly associated with baseball broadcasting and developed substantial local name recognition.

Radio gave him a public persona before he became a politician.

Origin of the Nickname "Bull"

Connor's nickname did not arise from police brutality, as later memory sometimes implies.

Historical accounts trace it to friends who noticed a similarity between his name and a newspaper cartoon character called Dr. B. U. L. Conner.

The nickname nevertheless later seemed to fit the aggressive public image he cultivated.

Entering Politics

Connor entered electoral politics in 1934.

His familiarity to Birmingham radio audiences gave him an advantage.

He won election to the Alabama House of Representatives with relatively little conventional campaigning.

Alabama House of Representatives

Connor served in the Alabama legislature from 1935 to 1937.

His record combined economic populism, opposition to some tax increases, support for measures popular with white workers, and commitment to the racial order of Jim Crow.

That combination anticipated the politics he later used in Birmingham.

Labor Issues

Connor was not uniformly hostile to organized labor.

He opposed an anti-sedition measure that labor supporters viewed as a threat to union activity.

This part of his record complicates any attempt to classify his politics solely through race while doing nothing to reduce the centrality of segregation to his public career.

Poll Taxes and Political Power

Alabama's poll tax helped restrict political participation, especially among Black citizens and poor people.

Connor operated within a political system in which Black disfranchisement sharply reduced the electorate whose votes municipal politicians needed to win.

The racial structure of voting therefore shaped the incentives of Birmingham politics.

Election as Public Safety Commissioner

Connor won election to Birmingham's city commission in the late 1930s and became commissioner of public safety.

The position was unusually powerful under Birmingham's commission form of government.

It made Connor one of the most important municipal officials in Alabama.

The Commission Form of Government

Birmingham was governed by a small city commission rather than a modern mayor-council system.

Each commissioner controlled major administrative departments.

This concentrated authority allowed individual commissioners to exercise significant power with limited separation between policymaking and department oversight.

Authority Over Police and Fire

As public safety commissioner, Connor exercised administrative authority over the Birmingham Police Department and Fire Department.

Historical sources also identify responsibility or influence over schools, public health services, and libraries.

That portfolio made his racial policies operational rather than merely rhetorical.

Segregation as Municipal Policy

Birmingham enforced racial separation through law, regulation, policing, custom, licensing, public facilities, and economic pressure.

Connor defended that system openly.

His political identity depended heavily on presenting segregation as legitimate public order.

White Working-Class Appeal

Connor cultivated an image as a defender of ordinary white citizens.

He opposed tax increases and presented himself as a crime fighter.

His ability to combine racial conservatism with working-class rhetoric helped sustain his electoral success for decades.

Relationship with Business Interests

Connor also maintained important relationships with Birmingham's business establishment.

For much of his career, segregation and economic development coexisted comfortably for many white business leaders.

Only when Connor's tactics began damaging Birmingham's national image and commercial interests did significant parts of the white business community turn decisively against him.

Crime and Public Order

Connor promoted himself as an aggressive guardian of law and order.

The meaning of order in segregated Birmingham was racialized.

Activities that challenged discriminatory customs could be treated as threats to peace even when demonstrators themselves used nonviolent tactics.

Eleanor Roosevelt Confrontation

Connor became nationally known before the 1960s for segregationist confrontations with prominent visitors.

One of the most famous involved First Lady Eleanor Roosevelt during a Birmingham meeting at which seating arrangements were segregated.

The episode demonstrated Connor's willingness to enforce local racial customs even against nationally prominent figures.

Senator Glen Taylor

In 1948, Progressive Party vice-presidential candidate and U.S. senator Glen H. Taylor attempted to enter an interracial meeting in Birmingham.

Connor's police arrested Taylor after a confrontation over segregation.

The incident linked Birmingham's racial policies to Connor's wider use of anti-Communist rhetoric against interracial political organizations.

Anti-Communism

Connor regularly associated civil-rights and interracial organizing with Communism.

That tactic was common among segregationists during the early Cold War.

It allowed officials to present demands for racial equality as threats to national security rather than constitutional claims.

The 1948 Democratic Convention

Connor served as an Alabama delegate to the 1948 Democratic National Convention.

When the national Democratic Party adopted a stronger civil-rights position associated with President Harry Truman, Connor helped lead Alabama delegates out of the convention.

The walkout became part of the Dixiecrat revolt.

The Dixiecrat Revolt

Southern segregationists formed the States' Rights Democratic Party and nominated Strom Thurmond for president in 1948.

The movement defended state authority largely as a vehicle for preserving racial segregation.

Connor's participation placed him within a broader regional political resistance to federal civil-rights policy.

First Campaign for Governor

Connor sought the Alabama governorship but failed to win.

His statewide ambitions demonstrate that he was more than a municipal bureaucrat.

He attempted repeatedly to convert Birmingham name recognition and segregationist popularity into higher office.

1950s Political Difficulties

Connor did not remain continuously in Birmingham office.

Personal scandal and allegations involving police corruption weakened him during the early 1950s.

He temporarily left the commission before returning later in the decade.

Extramarital-Affair Scandal

Historical accounts describe an extramarital-affair scandal as one reason Connor did not seek immediate reelection during the 1950s.

The episode damaged his public image but did not end his political career.

White Birmingham voters later returned him to office.

Return to the City Commission

Connor returned to Birmingham's city commission in 1957.

His comeback coincided with the accelerating national struggle over school desegregation, public transportation, and Black voting rights.

He resumed office as civil-rights organizations were increasingly prepared to challenge Birmingham directly.

Fred Shuttlesworth

Reverend Fred Shuttlesworth became Connor's most persistent local civil-rights adversary.

Shuttlesworth helped establish the Alabama Christian Movement for Human Rights after Alabama effectively suppressed the NAACP within the state.

He repeatedly challenged Birmingham segregation through lawsuits, boycotts, demonstrations, and direct action despite bombings and physical assault.

Alabama Christian Movement for Human Rights

The ACMHR was a local Black-led organization rather than an outside group imported by Martin Luther King Jr.

It created the organizational base from which the Birmingham Campaign later emerged.

Understanding that local foundation is essential because Connor had been confronting Black Birmingham activism for years before SCLC arrived.

Bombingham

Birmingham became notorious for bombings directed at Black homes and institutions in areas undergoing racial residential change.

The nickname "Bombingham" reflected the frequency of racial terrorism.

Connor did not invent that violence, but historical accounts document his protection or accommodation of white supremacists responsible for racial attacks.

Connor and the Ku Klux Klan

No reliable evidence establishes Connor as a formal Ku Klux Klan member.

That distinction should be preserved.

It is equally important not to use the absence of formal membership to minimize his relationship with white-supremacist violence: contemporary and later historical accounts describe close ties, tolerance, and protection of Klansmen.

Government Protection of Private Terror

A segregationist government did not need every official to wear Klan robes in order to enable racial terrorism.

Police could fail to investigate, delay intervention, ignore threats, or treat victims as troublemakers.

Connor's Birmingham provides an important example of how public and private coercion could reinforce one another.

School Desegregation Resistance

After Brown v. Board of Education, Birmingham officials resisted integration of public education.

Connor supported measures intended to preserve segregated schools.

The city's resistance reflected both local policy and Alabama's broader campaign against federal desegregation orders.

Public Parks and Desegregation

Federal court orders threatened segregation in Birmingham's public recreational facilities.

Rather than operate integrated parks, city officials chose closure.

Connor became one of the principal public defenders of that approach.

Closing Birmingham's Parks

By late 1961, Birmingham eliminated funding for most of its recreational system and closed dozens of parks and playgrounds, golf courses, and swimming pools rather than integrate them.

The policy harmed Black residents most directly but also deprived white residents of public facilities.

It demonstrated the lengths segregationists were willing to go to preserve racial separation.

Freedom Rides

In 1961, interracial Freedom Riders travelled through the South to test federal rulings prohibiting segregation in interstate transportation facilities.

Alabama became the site of some of the most severe violence.

Birmingham's response placed Connor at the center of the crisis.

The Anniston Attack

Before reaching Birmingham, one Freedom Ride bus was firebombed near Anniston.

Passengers escaping the burning vehicle were assaulted.

The attack demonstrated that the riders were entering an environment in which organized white violence could become lethal.

Birmingham Trailways Station

A second group of riders arrived at Birmingham's Trailways station.

Klansmen were waiting.

The absence of meaningful police protection allowed a brutal attack to unfold.

Connor Orders Police Away

National Park Service and Alabama historical accounts state that Connor ordered Birmingham police to remain away from the station while Klansmen attacked the Freedom Riders.

Other historical accounts describe an arrangement giving attackers a period before police intervention.

The essential documented point is that the city knowingly withheld protection during foreseeable racial violence.

Police Non-Protection as State Action

Connor's role in the Freedom Rider attack illustrates that government oppression can operate through deliberate inaction as well as direct assault.

When officials know violence is imminent and intentionally remove protection, private attackers effectively gain space to act.

The event became a major embarrassment to local and state authorities.

Federal Intervention in the Freedom Rides

The Kennedy administration became increasingly involved as the Freedom Rides continued.

Federal marshals and Interstate Commerce Commission action eventually strengthened enforcement of desegregation rules.

Connor opposed this expansion of federal authority.

National Reputation of Birmingham

By the early 1960s, Birmingham had become internationally associated with racial repression.

Connor's confrontational public style intensified that reputation.

White business leaders increasingly feared that racial conflict was damaging investment, commerce, tourism, and the city's national standing.

1962 Vote to Change City Government

Birmingham voters approved replacing the three-member commission with a mayor-council system in 1962.

Government reform had multiple causes, but Connor's dominance and the city's civil-rights reputation were major factors.

The reform created a direct pathway to ending his control of police and fire.

1963 Mayoral Campaign

Connor ran for mayor under the new system.

His principal opponent in the runoff was Albert Boutwell, a segregationist by later standards but regarded as more moderate and less confrontational than Connor.

Boutwell won.

Albert Boutwell

Boutwell's victory did not represent the sudden conversion of white Birmingham to racial equality.

Many voters remained segregationist.

The election instead demonstrated that significant numbers of white citizens had concluded Connor's style was politically and economically damaging.

Connor Refuses to Leave Immediately

Connor and the other commissioners challenged the new mayor-council government in court.

They claimed their terms had not legally expired.

As a result, Birmingham temporarily had competing claims to municipal authority.

The Timing of the Birmingham Campaign

The civil-rights campaign began while Connor still controlled the public-safety apparatus.

Movement organizers understood his reputation and anticipated repression.

SCLC's strategy partly depended upon creating a confrontation that exposed the operation of segregation to national audiences.

Project C

The 1963 Birmingham Campaign was often called Project C, with the letter standing for confrontation.

The objective was not random disorder.

Organizers sought to create sustained economic and political pressure through nonviolent direct action.

Local and National Leadership

Fred Shuttlesworth and the ACMHR supplied the local organizing base.

Martin Luther King Jr., Ralph Abernathy, Wyatt Tee Walker, James Bevel, and SCLC brought national networks, fundraising, media attention, and strategic support.

Connor confronted a coalition rather than a single leader.

Economic Boycott

Black Birmingham residents withheld spending from downtown merchants that maintained segregation and discriminatory employment practices.

The Easter shopping season increased the boycott's economic leverage.

Business pressure became as important to the campaign as street demonstrations.

Sit-Ins and Marches

Beginning in early April, demonstrators held sit-ins, marches, and mass meetings.

Connor's police repeatedly arrested participants.

The campaign sought to fill jails and raise the political cost of continued segregation.

State Court Injunction

Birmingham obtained a court injunction restricting demonstrations.

Movement leaders decided to disobey the order, arguing that the injunction was being used to suppress constitutional protest.

The decision increased the likelihood of arrests.

Martin Luther King Jr.'s Arrest

On April 12, 1963, King and Ralph Abernathy deliberately submitted to arrest.

King was held in Birmingham jail.

Connor's public-safety machinery thus contributed directly to the circumstances surrounding one of the most famous texts of the Civil Rights Movement.

"Letter from Birmingham Jail"

While imprisoned, King responded to white Alabama clergy who criticized demonstrations as untimely.

The resulting "Letter from Birmingham Jail" defended direct action and argued that unjust systems rarely surrender privilege voluntarily.

The document's enduring influence extends far beyond Connor, but his Birmingham jail supplied its immediate setting.

Bail Crisis

The campaign began running short of money to post bail for arrested demonstrators.

Without a new source of participants, the strategy risked collapse.

James Bevel proposed recruiting schoolchildren and teenagers.

James Bevel and Youth Mobilization

Bevel argued that young people could participate in ways adults dependent on white employers sometimes could not.

The plan was controversial even within the Black community.

Parents had to weigh the political value of participation against the risk of exposing children to Connor's police.

The Children's Crusade

On May 2, 1963, more than a thousand Black students gathered at Sixteenth Street Baptist Church and attempted to march downtown.

Police arrested hundreds.

The scale of youth participation overwhelmed ordinary jail capacity.

May 2 Arrests

Historical sources differ slightly in precise arrest counts, but more than nine hundred young people were taken into custody on the first major day of the Children's Crusade.

National Park Service accounts describe approximately 1,200 children arrested by day's end.

The important point is the extraordinary scale of youth detention.

May 3 Escalation

On May 3, hundreds more young demonstrators returned.

Connor shifted from mass arrest alone toward physical dispersal.

He directed police and fire departments to use force.

High-Pressure Fire Hoses

Birmingham firefighters turned high-pressure streams on demonstrators and onlookers.

The force could knock people off their feet, roll them along pavement, strip bark from trees, and cause serious injury.

Television cameras captured the spectacle.

Police Dogs

Police dogs were used to chase, intimidate, and attack demonstrators.

Photographs of dogs lunging at Black citizens became among the most recognizable images of the movement.

The visual association between Connor and police dogs became permanent.

Children as Targets

Many of the people subjected to hoses, dogs, arrests, and police pressure were minors.

That fact intensified national outrage.

Segregation could no longer easily be presented as a peaceful regional custom when children confronting it were being blasted through the streets by municipal fire hoses.

Fred Shuttlesworth Injured

Shuttlesworth was struck by the force of a fire hose during the campaign and required hospitalization.

Connor reportedly responded contemptuously to news of the injury.

The incident reinforced the contrast between Shuttlesworth's long local resistance and Connor's long defense of segregation.

Onlookers and Bystanders

Force was not confined neatly to formal marchers.

Onlookers and people in surrounding areas could be caught in police and fire-department actions.

This broadened community anger and made crowd control more difficult.

The Role of Photographers

Photographers including Charles Moore captured police dogs and fire hoses in Birmingham.

Still photographs circulated through newspapers and magazines.

The images translated local coercion into a national visual language that required little explanation.

Television Coverage

Television gave the Birmingham confrontation unprecedented immediacy.

Americans who might never visit Alabama could watch municipal authorities use extreme force against demonstrators.

International broadcasting also damaged the United States' image during the Cold War.

Cold War Consequences

U.S. officials were concerned that racial violence undermined American claims to democratic leadership against the Soviet Union.

Birmingham therefore became more than a local political problem.

Connor's tactics created international propaganda costs for the federal government.

Connor's Strategic Failure

Connor intended force to suppress demonstrations.

Instead, force strengthened the movement's public argument.

The result is one of the clearest examples of political repression producing the opposite of its intended effect.

The Business Community Turns Toward Negotiation

Downtown merchants faced boycotts, negative publicity, and growing instability.

Prominent business representatives became more willing to negotiate.

Their motives included economic self-interest as well as concern about civic reputation.

Burke Marshall

Attorney General Robert Kennedy sent Assistant Attorney General Burke Marshall to Birmingham.

Marshall worked with Black leaders and white business representatives in negotiations.

Federal involvement increased as Connor's tactics made the crisis impossible to contain locally.

May 10 Agreement

On May 10, negotiators announced an agreement addressing public accommodations, employment, communication between Black and white representatives, and release of protesters on bond.

The agreement was incomplete and implementation remained contested.

It nevertheless marked a significant defeat for the rigid municipal segregation Connor defended.

Bombings After the Agreement

White supremacists bombed the Gaston Motel area and the home of A. D. King after the agreement.

The attacks triggered unrest and federal preparations for intervention.

Connor should not be described as personally responsible for those bombings without evidence, but they occurred within the violent segregationist environment his government had long tolerated.

Federal Troop Preparations

President John F. Kennedy ordered federal troops positioned near Birmingham and prepared to federalize the Alabama National Guard if necessary.

The prospect reflected the depth of the crisis.

Municipal segregation had become a federal constitutional and public-order issue.

End of Connor's Birmingham Authority

On May 23, 1963, the Alabama Supreme Court upheld the transition to the new municipal government and ordered Connor and the other commissioners out.

His long control over Birmingham public safety ended.

The timing meant that his final weeks in municipal authority became the most famous of his career.

Sixteenth Street Baptist Church Bombing

On September 15, 1963, Ku Klux Klan members bombed Sixteenth Street Baptist Church, killing four Black girls.

Connor was no longer public-safety commissioner when the bombing occurred.

That distinction matters: the crime belonged to the continuing white-supremacist terrorist environment of Birmingham but should not be falsely attributed to Connor as an operational participant.

The Four Girls

The bombing killed Addie Mae Collins, Denise McNair, Carole Robertson, and Cynthia Wesley.

The church had served as a major organizational center for the Birmingham Campaign.

Their deaths became another powerful symbol of the violence surrounding resistance to desegregation.

Connor After Birmingham

Losing municipal office did not end Connor's political viability in Alabama.

He soon sought statewide regulatory office.

Many Alabama voters continued to support him despite—or in some cases because of—his segregationist record.

Alabama Public Service Commission

Connor won election as president of the Alabama Public Service Commission in 1964.

The commission regulated utilities and other major public-service industries.

The victory demonstrated that national condemnation did not automatically translate into political defeat within Alabama.

Continued Political Popularity

Connor's later election complicates a simplified story in which Birmingham instantly rejected him after 1963.

White opinion in Alabama remained deeply divided over civil rights.

His public image retained appeal among voters who admired resistance to federal intervention and racial change.

Health Problems

Connor suffered significant health problems during his later years.

A major stroke in the 1960s impaired his mobility.

He nevertheless remained connected to public office for years afterward.

Final Years in Public Office

Connor served on the Public Service Commission into the early 1970s.

He was eventually defeated for another term.

His career thus extended well beyond the Birmingham confrontation for which he is remembered.

Death

Connor suffered another severe stroke in early 1973.

He died in Birmingham on March 10, 1973.

He was seventy-five years old.

Elmwood Cemetery

Connor was buried at Elmwood Cemetery in Birmingham.

The burial location places him in the city whose twentieth-century racial history he helped shape.

Modern public memory of Birmingham, however, is increasingly centered on the movement activists who opposed him.

Did Connor Ever Repent?

Unlike George Wallace, who later publicly apologized for his earlier segregationist politics, Connor never meaningfully repudiated his defense of segregation.

Historical accounts describe him as unrepentant.

This difference is important when assessing later reputation.

Connor Versus George Wallace

Connor and Wallace are often grouped together as Alabama segregationists.

Their offices and political trajectories differed.

Wallace exercised gubernatorial power and later attempted a public racial transformation; Connor's defining power was municipal and he did not undertake a comparable repudiation.

Connor Versus Jim Clark

Connor is also commonly compared with Dallas County sheriff Jim Clark.

Both became symbols of coercive law enforcement against civil-rights demonstrators.

Connor's authority over both police and fire departments made Birmingham's repression institutionally distinctive.

Was Connor a Police Chief?

Connor is sometimes called Birmingham's police chief in popular accounts.

That is inaccurate.

He was the elected commissioner of public safety with administrative authority over the police department; the department also had professional police chiefs.

Was Connor a Ku Klux Klan Member?

Historical sources do not establish Connor as a formal Klan member.

Some accounts describe him as having close Klan ties, and Alabama historians document his protection of Klansmen involved in racial violence.

The publication-safe conclusion is that Connor enabled and accommodated Klan violence without asserting unproven formal membership.

Did Connor Personally Hold Every Fire Hose?

Connor did not personally operate every hose or control every dog.

His responsibility was political and administrative.

He directed the departments, authorized or ordered the tactics, and publicly defended the segregation system those tactics protected.

The Institutional Nature of Responsibility

Focusing exclusively on Connor can create the false impression that Birmingham segregation was the work of one unusually cruel official.

The system depended on ordinances, police, courts, employers, school authorities, housing practices, white voters, business institutions, state officials, and private white-supremacist organizations.

Connor mattered because he concentrated and dramatized that larger structure.

The Myth of an Accidentally Helpful Connor

Civil-rights strategists understood that Connor's likely overreaction could create national sympathy.

That does not make him a secret ally or accidental civil-rights leader.

The movement converted his repression into political leverage; the moral and strategic credit belongs to the organizers and demonstrators who endured it.

SCLC Strategy and Connor

SCLC had learned from Albany, Georgia, that disciplined police restraint could make a protest campaign less dramatic and easier to contain.

Birmingham offered a different opponent.

Connor's reputation for confrontation made him strategically predictable.

Local Black Agency

Connor's story is often told as a duel with Martin Luther King Jr.

That framing minimizes Fred Shuttlesworth, the ACMHR, Black students, churches, women, workers, and families who had resisted Birmingham segregation for years.

The antagonist biography should therefore preserve the people whose organizing created the conflict.

The Children Who Confronted Connor

The young people of Birmingham transformed the campaign by volunteering for arrest despite fear of school discipline and police violence.

Their courage overwhelmed jail capacity and forced Connor to choose between allowing demonstrations and escalating repression.

His decision created the images that came to define him.

Segregation and Public Order

Connor consistently presented segregation as a normal legal order disrupted by agitators.

Civil-rights activists reversed that framing.

They argued that the unjust racial system itself constituted the disorder and that nonviolent protest exposed rather than created conflict.

Law and Morality

Many of Connor's actions relied on local ordinances, court orders, and official authority.

The legality of particular enforcement measures did not settle their morality or constitutionality.

The Birmingham struggle became a major national debate over the duty to obey laws that sustain injustice.

Federalism

Connor defended local and state authority against federal civil-rights intervention.

Segregationists frequently framed the issue as states' rights or municipal self-government.

Civil-rights advocates argued that local autonomy could not override federal constitutional guarantees.

White Moderate Opposition to Connor

Some white Birmingham leaders opposed Connor without embracing full racial equality.

They wanted reduced conflict, improved business conditions, and gradual change.

This distinction matters because Connor's electoral defeat did not mean white Birmingham had suddenly accepted the movement's complete program.

Business Pragmatism

Commercial elites increasingly viewed highly visible segregationist violence as economically costly.

Their negotiations with civil-rights leaders were partly pragmatic.

The interaction between moral protest and economic pressure was central to the campaign's success.

National Legislative Consequences

The Birmingham images helped change national opinion and increased pressure for federal civil-rights legislation.

President Kennedy soon called for major civil-rights legislation.

The bill developed through further political struggle and became the Civil Rights Act of 1964 after Kennedy's assassination.

Civil Rights Act of 1964

The Civil Rights Act prohibited major forms of discrimination in public accommodations, employment, and federally supported programs.

Birmingham was one of several events that shaped the political environment for the law.

It would be too simple to say Connor alone caused the Act, but his tactics became important evidence for the need for federal intervention.

International Legacy

Photographs from Birmingham circulated globally.

They became enduring evidence used in histories of American racism, policing, protest, and democratic reform.

Connor's image therefore became known far beyond Alabama.

Photography and Historical Memory

Modern memory often reduces Connor to three objects: a police dog, a fire hose, and a hard-faced segregationist official.

Those images are historically grounded but incomplete.

His earlier electoral career, labor politics, Dixiecrat role, Freedom Ride conduct, and later statewide office show a broader political life.

Public Memory in Birmingham

Birmingham now commemorates the Civil Rights Movement through institutions including Birmingham Civil Rights National Monument, the Birmingham Civil Rights Institute, Kelly Ingram Park, Sixteenth Street Baptist Church, and other sites.

Connor appears in this public history principally as an example of official resistance.

The city's commemorative landscape increasingly centers the people who challenged his authority.

Connor in Film and Television

Archival footage of Connor appears frequently in documentaries and historical programs about the Civil Rights Movement.

Dramatizations have also portrayed him as an archetype of segregationist authority.

Popular culture sometimes exaggerates or compresses events, so visual portrayals should be checked against the historical record.

Connor as Symbol

Connor's name became shorthand for racist law enforcement.

Later politicians and commentators have invoked "Bull Connor" when criticizing government repression or racial injustice.

That symbolic use reflects his enduring association with the Birmingham images of 1963.

The Danger of Symbolic Overuse

Using Connor only as an insult can flatten history.

His significance comes from specific institutions, policies, elections, orders, and events.

Historical comparison is most useful when those mechanisms are examined rather than when his name functions merely as a synonym for racism.

Responsibility for Birmingham's Racial Violence

Connor bore substantial responsibility for creating and enforcing a municipal environment hostile to Black civil rights.

That does not mean every racist attack in Birmingham was ordered by him.

Responsible history distinguishes direct orders, tolerated violence, structural policy, and crimes carried out independently by private terrorists.

Historical Significance

Bull Connor's biography connects Jim Crow government, municipal policing, anti-Communism, the Dixiecrat revolt, white working-class politics, Klan violence, Freedom Rides, public-facility segregation, the Birmingham Campaign, youth protest, mass media, federal intervention, and the Civil Rights Act era.

His career shows how racial inequality was maintained through elected government as well as extremist organizations.

He was repeatedly returned to office by white voters and operated inside ordinary municipal institutions.

That fact is historically more important than portraying him as an isolated monster outside mainstream politics.

Legacy

Connor intended to preserve Birmingham's segregated political and social order.

For much of his career, he succeeded in using municipal authority to reinforce that order.

His greatest historical failure came when coercive tactics that had long operated locally became visible to national and international audiences.

The Civil Rights Movement deliberately brought segregation into public confrontation, and Connor responded in ways that made the movement's argument visually undeniable.

He consequently became one of the clearest examples of an antagonist whose repression helped expose the system he sought to defend.

The lesson is not that injustice defeats itself automatically. Connor had held power for decades. Change required organized Black resistance, legal challenges, economic boycotts, strategic leadership, youth participation, journalism, federal pressure, and extraordinary personal courage.

Historical Record and Common Misconceptions

Connor's full name was Theophilus Eugene Connor, and he was born in Selma on July 11, 1897.

His nickname "Bull" predated the civil-rights confrontations and was not originally a reference to police brutality.

Connor was Birmingham's commissioner of public safety, not technically the city's police chief.

He exercised administrative control over police and fire services under Birmingham's commission form of government.

Reliable historical sources do not establish that Connor was formally a Ku Klux Klan member.

The absence of proven Klan membership does not mean he was distant from Klan violence; Alabama historical sources document his protection of Klansmen and his deliberate withdrawal of police protection during the 1961 Freedom Rider attack.

Connor did not personally create Birmingham's segregation system. He became one of its most powerful elected defenders.

The local Black freedom movement existed before Martin Luther King Jr. arrived in 1963; Fred Shuttlesworth and the Alabama Christian Movement for Human Rights had challenged Birmingham segregation for years.

Connor initially relied heavily on mass arrests during the Birmingham Campaign and escalated to hoses and dogs when the Children's Crusade strained jail capacity.

The precise number of children arrested on May 2 varies among historical sources, but all reliable accounts describe mass arrest on an extraordinary scale.

Connor authorized the use of police and fire power in May 1963; he did not personally handle every police dog or fire hose.

He lost the 1963 mayoral race before the most famous Birmingham demonstrations but remained in authority because the outgoing commission challenged the change in government.

The Alabama Supreme Court forced Connor and the old commissioners out on May 23, 1963.

Connor was no longer public-safety commissioner when the Sixteenth Street Baptist Church bombing occurred in September 1963.

No evidence supports claiming that Connor personally ordered that bombing.

Birmingham's 1963 campaign contributed to momentum for federal civil-rights legislation, but Connor alone did not cause the Civil Rights Act of 1964.

Connor later won statewide office on the Alabama Public Service Commission, demonstrating that his national notoriety did not eliminate his political support within Alabama.

Unlike George Wallace, Connor never made a major public repudiation of his segregationist record.

Timeline

  • July 11, 1897: Theophilus Eugene Connor is born in Selma, Alabama.
  • c. 1905: Connor's mother dies of pneumonia; his childhood becomes increasingly mobile.
  • 1900s-1910s: Lives with relatives in Alabama and learns telegraphy from his father.
  • 1920: Marries Beara Levens.
  • 1922: Settles in Birmingham.
  • 1920s-1930s: Works as a telegraph operator, salesman, and radio personality.
  • Early 1930s: Becomes a well-known Birmingham sports broadcaster.
  • 1934: Enters electoral politics and wins a seat in the Alabama House of Representatives.
  • 1935-1937: Serves in the Alabama legislature.
  • Late 1930s: Wins election to Birmingham's city commission and assumes the public-safety portfolio.
  • 1940s: Builds a durable political base as a law-and-order segregationist.
  • 1948: Participates in the walkout from the Democratic National Convention over the national party's civil-rights plank.
  • 1948: Supports the Dixiecrat revolt that nominates Strom Thurmond for president.
  • 1948: Birmingham police arrest Senator Glen Taylor during a confrontation involving segregated access to an interracial political meeting.
  • 1950: Connor unsuccessfully seeks the Alabama governorship.
  • Early 1950s: Personal scandal and allegations involving police corruption weaken his municipal position.
  • Mid-1950s: Temporarily remains outside the Birmingham commission.
  • 1957: Returns to office as Birmingham public-safety commissioner.
  • Late 1950s: Confronts Fred Shuttlesworth and the Alabama Christian Movement for Human Rights.
  • 1960: Becomes Alabama's Democratic national committeeman.
  • 1961: Freedom Riders enter Alabama amid organized white-supremacist violence.
  • May 1961: Connor orders Birmingham police away from the Trailways station while Klansmen attack Freedom Riders.
  • 1961: Birmingham officials choose to close public recreational facilities rather than permit integration.
  • Late 1961: Dozens of parks, playgrounds, pools, and other facilities are closed.
  • 1962: Birmingham voters approve replacing the commission system with a mayor-council government.
  • 1962: Connor again unsuccessfully seeks the Alabama governorship.
  • Early 1963: Connor campaigns for mayor under Birmingham's new governmental structure.
  • April 1963: Albert Boutwell defeats Connor in the mayoral runoff.
  • April 3, 1963: Birmingham Campaign begins with sit-ins, marches, mass meetings, and an economic boycott.
  • April 10, 1963: Birmingham obtains a court injunction restricting demonstrations.
  • April 12, 1963: Martin Luther King Jr. and Ralph Abernathy are arrested.
  • April 1963: King writes "Letter from Birmingham Jail."
  • May 2, 1963: Children's Crusade begins; hundreds of Black students are arrested.
  • May 3, 1963: Connor directs police and fire departments to use high-pressure hoses and police dogs against demonstrators and onlookers.
  • May 3-7, 1963: Images of Birmingham repression circulate nationally and internationally.
  • May 1963: Fred Shuttlesworth is injured by a fire hose and hospitalized.
  • May 1963: Kennedy administration sends Burke Marshall to assist negotiations.
  • May 10, 1963: Movement and business representatives announce a desegregation agreement.
  • May 11, 1963: White supremacists bomb the Gaston Motel area and A. D. King's home.
  • May 23, 1963: Alabama Supreme Court orders Connor and the old commissioners to vacate office.
  • September 15, 1963: Sixteenth Street Baptist Church is bombed by Klansmen; Connor is no longer commissioner.
  • 1964: Connor wins election as president of the Alabama Public Service Commission.
  • 1964: Civil Rights Act becomes federal law amid a political climate shaped in part by Birmingham.
  • Late 1960s: Connor suffers a major stroke but remains in public life.
  • 1968: Wins another term on the Alabama Public Service Commission.
  • 1972: Loses his bid for another term.
  • February 1973: Suffers another severe stroke.
  • March 10, 1973: Dies in Birmingham at age seventy-five.
  • Post-1973: Connor becomes an enduring symbol of official resistance to the Civil Rights Movement.
  • 1990s-2020s: Birmingham public-history institutions increasingly interpret Connor within the larger history of Black organizing and municipal segregation.

Geographic Scope

Selma, Alabama, was Connor's birthplace.

Birmingham became the defining city of his personal, broadcasting, and political career.

His political influence extended statewide through the Alabama legislature, Democratic Party, gubernatorial campaigns, and Public Service Commission.

The Freedom Rides connected Birmingham to an interstate national protest network.

The 1963 campaign transformed a local Alabama confrontation into an international civil-rights event.

Legal and Institutional Context

Connor's career developed within the Jim Crow legal system created after Reconstruction.

Black Alabamians faced segregation, poll taxes, discriminatory registration practices, unequal education, racially divided public facilities, employment discrimination, and widespread intimidation.

The Supreme Court's twentieth-century civil-rights rulings increasingly placed local segregation policies in conflict with federal constitutional law.

Connor generally responded by defending municipal and state authority and resisting federal enforcement.

His public-safety office illustrates how local government could turn racial hierarchy into practical enforcement through police deployment, arrests, permits, department policy, and selective protection.
