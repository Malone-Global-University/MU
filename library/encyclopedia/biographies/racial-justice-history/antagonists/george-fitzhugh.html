George Fitzhugh

Virginia lawyer, slaveholder, and proslavery social theorist who attacked free labor and defended hierarchical dependency in Sociology for the South and Cannibals All!

Overview

George Fitzhugh (1806-1881) was a Virginia lawyer, slaveholder, essayist, and social theorist who became one of the antebellum South's most aggressive intellectual defenders of slavery.

He is best known for Sociology for the South; or, The Failure of Free Society (1854) and Cannibals All! or, Slaves Without Masters (1857). Rather than defend slavery only as a racial institution or temporary necessity, Fitzhugh attacked free labor, free markets, individualism, and political equality and argued that some form of slavery or enforced dependency was the natural foundation of civilized society.

His significance lies primarily in ideology rather than public office. Fitzhugh helped push proslavery argument toward an explicit rejection of free society while simultaneously presenting racial slavery as protective and paternal. His writings remain important evidence of how some white southern intellectuals rationalized human bondage in the decade before the Civil War.

Birth-Date Discrepancy

Many twentieth-century reference works give Fitzhugh's birth date as November 4, 1806. The Library of Virginia, however, cites the Fitzhugh family Bible for November 9, 1806.

This biography uses November 9 because it is supported by the family record while noting the commonly repeated alternative date rather than presenting an uncertain detail as settled.

Early Life

Fitzhugh was born in Prince William County, Virginia, to physician George Fitzhugh and Lucy Stuart Fitzhugh. Around 1812 the family moved to a plantation in King George County.

His upbringing placed him within Virginia's plantation society during a period when slavery structured labor, property, politics, and social rank throughout much of the Tidewater region.

Education

Fitzhugh did not receive the extensive formal education associated with many elite southern political figures. His childhood schooling was irregular, but he read widely, learned Latin, and largely educated himself.

That self-directed intellectual background later contributed to his unusual style as a polemicist: broad, confident, provocative, and willing to challenge established economic and political thinkers.

Legal Training

Fitzhugh studied law and qualified as an attorney on October 4, 1827. Law provided his principal professional foundation before writing increasingly displaced legal practice as the center of his career.

Marriage and Family

On December 21, 1829, Fitzhugh married Mary Metcalf Brockenbrough. The couple settled in Port Royal, Caroline County, Virginia.

They had nine children—six daughters and three sons. Two of their children died in November 1844, one at age ten and another at age two.

Port Royal, Virginia

Port Royal became Fitzhugh's principal community during the most productive years of his legal and writing career. By 1842 he was in a law partnership with John T. Thornton.

Caroline County was also a slave society undergoing demographic and economic change. By mid-century African Americans formed a majority of the county's population, and most of that Black population was enslaved.

Slaveholding

Fitzhugh was himself a slaveholder, not merely an outside theorist of slavery. Census records summarized by Encyclopedia Virginia indicate that he held five enslaved people in 1830, eight in 1840, and twenty-seven in 1850.

By 1860 the household's recorded slaveholding had fallen to eight people, listed under his wife. The decline coincided with deterioration in Fitzhugh's finances as he devoted increasing time and resources to travel and publication.

The People He Enslaved

The census figures establish the scale of Fitzhugh's slaveholding at different points, but they do not by themselves tell the life stories of the individual people held in bondage.

That limitation matters. Fitzhugh's writings frequently reduced enslaved people to categories within a social theory, while slavery in practice governed real people's movement, labor, family security, legal status, and ability to make choices about their own lives.

Virginia's Changing Slave Society

Fitzhugh wrote during a period of substantial change in eastern Virginia. Enslaved people were being sold out of the region in large numbers as the domestic slave trade redirected labor toward the expanding cotton South, while many white Virginians also migrated westward and southward.

Instead of treating these changes as evidence of instability within slavery, Fitzhugh idealized the plantation order and blamed social problems on free labor and market competition.

From Lawyer to Polemicist

During the 1840s and 1850s Fitzhugh increasingly shifted his attention from legal practice toward writing about political economy, slavery, class relations, and social order.

He was not a systematic academic scholar in the modern sense. His importance came from polemical force: he deliberately framed extreme arguments that challenged northern antislavery assumptions and even conventional southern defenses of slavery.

Intellectual Influences

Fitzhugh read widely in British political economy and conservative social criticism. John Taylor of Caroline influenced his economic thinking, while the Scottish writer Thomas Carlyle influenced both his criticism of liberal society and his rhetorical style.

He also read writers he opposed. His arguments emerged from sustained engagement with debates over Adam Smith, John Locke, capitalism, abolitionism, Christianity, and the meaning of liberty.

Slavery Justified

In 1850 Fitzhugh published Slavery Justified; by a Southerner. It marked his emergence as a distinctive proslavery writer.

His method was already clear: instead of merely answering abolitionist criticism, he attacked the moral legitimacy of free society itself and insisted that competitive labor markets exposed weaker people to exploitation.

What Shall Be Done With the Free Negroes

In 1851 Fitzhugh published essays under the title What Shall Be Done With the Free Negroes. He advocated the enslavement of free African Americans.

This position demonstrates that his paternalist rhetoric did not imply support for Black autonomy. He regarded Black freedom as socially dangerous and argued for imposing legal dependency on people who were already free.

Racial Ideology

Fitzhugh's broader theory of dependency sometimes extended beyond race, but his writings remained deeply white supremacist. He repeatedly portrayed Black people as incapable of successfully managing freedom and used those assertions to justify racial bondage.

His argument therefore combined two claims: a general attack on free labor and a specifically racial assertion that African Americans were particularly suited to enslavement.

Slavery as Protection

Fitzhugh portrayed slavery as a protective relationship in which masters supposedly assumed responsibility for food, shelter, care, and support when enslaved people were young, sick, or old.

This paternalist model excluded the fundamental fact that the enslaved person could not choose the relationship. The slaveholder's claimed duty of care existed alongside legal ownership, compulsory labor, restricted movement, sale, punishment, and vulnerability to family separation.

Attack on Free Society

The defining feature of Fitzhugh's mature thought was that he did not stop at defending the South. He argued that so-called free society was itself fundamentally defective.

Free competition, in his view, allowed stronger people to exploit weaker people without assuming responsibility for their survival. He presented wage labor as a disguised form of slavery without the protections he attributed to legal slaveholding.

Critique of Capitalism

Fitzhugh attacked laissez-faire capitalism for separating capital from labor. He believed employers could extract labor while abandoning workers when they were no longer profitable.

His critique resembled some contemporary attacks on industrial exploitation, but his proposed solution was radically different from abolitionist, labor-reform, socialist, or democratic alternatives. He advocated hierarchy, dependency, and forms of slavery rather than greater freedom or worker power.

Rejection of Liberal Individualism

Fitzhugh rejected major assumptions associated with liberal political thought, including individual autonomy, natural equality, free contract, and the idea that people should generally be left to pursue their own interests.

He argued instead that society required guardianship and hierarchy because individuals differed in ability and strength. This placed him in direct opposition to the egalitarian language of the Declaration of Independence and to antislavery appeals to natural rights.

Sociology for the South

Fitzhugh published Sociology for the South; or, The Failure of Free Society in 1854. Encyclopedia Virginia identifies it as the first American imprint to use the relatively new word sociology in its title.

The book argued that free trade and free labor produced antagonism rather than harmony. Fitzhugh challenged Adam Smith's political economy and John Locke's social-contract tradition while presenting slavery as a system of reciprocal obligations.

Why Sociology for the South Mattered

The book made Fitzhugh nationally known because it did not frame slavery as a regrettable southern exception. It instead placed slavery at the center of a broad attack on modern free society.

That inversion was provocative: abolitionists argued that slavery contradicted American liberty, while Fitzhugh increasingly suggested that liberty itself was the problem.

Universal Slavery

Fitzhugh eventually argued for slavery in the abstract rather than defending only the enslavement of Black people. He believed societies inevitably divided into people who governed and people who labored under dependency.

He therefore sometimes suggested that poor or vulnerable white people could also benefit from forms of enslavement or compulsory guardianship. This made his theory more radical than conventional racial defenses of southern slavery, although it did not diminish the white supremacy of his actual arguments about African Americans.

Not a Theory of Equality

Fitzhugh's willingness to imagine white slavery can superficially appear less race-specific than other proslavery arguments. It should not be mistaken for egalitarianism.

He opposed equality itself. His universal theory expanded the category of people he thought should be subordinated rather than extending freedom to Black people.

Religious and Scientific Disagreements

Fitzhugh did not simply repeat every common southern defense of slavery. He disagreed with aspects of the theological defense advanced by James Henley Thornwell and rejected some of the racial ethnology associated with physician Josiah Nott.

Those disagreements demonstrate the diversity of proslavery thought. Southern intellectuals could disagree sharply about why slavery was legitimate while remaining committed to preserving the institution.

Correspondence with Abolitionists

Fitzhugh corresponded with ideological opponents including William Lloyd Garrison and Gerrit Smith. He even subscribed to Garrison's antislavery newspaper, The Liberator.

His engagement with abolitionists reflected intellectual combat rather than movement toward abolition. He sought to test, publicize, and sharpen his own arguments by confronting opponents directly.

William Lloyd Garrison

Garrison represented nearly the opposite political position from Fitzhugh: immediate abolition grounded in the moral illegitimacy of human bondage.

Fitzhugh's willingness to correspond with him is useful evidence that Fitzhugh understood abolitionist arguments and deliberately chose to answer them by attacking the foundations of free society rather than by ignoring them.

Wendell Phillips Debate

In March 1855 Fitzhugh was invited to New Haven, Connecticut, to debate abolitionist Wendell Phillips. The two spoke on alternating evenings.

The trip was Fitzhugh's first journey north of the Potomac River. After travelling through portions of New York and New England, he returned to Virginia more committed to his critique of northern society.

Northern Travel

Fitzhugh's northern travel gave him direct exposure to the industrial and commercial society he criticized. He interpreted poverty, labor insecurity, and urban inequality as confirmation of his belief that free labor exploited workers.

He did not give comparable weight to the legal freedom northern workers possessed to change employers, organize families, move, own themselves, or participate in civic life—rights categorically denied to enslaved people.

Cannibals All!

In 1857 Fitzhugh published Cannibals All! or, Slaves Without Masters, his most famous work. The book intensified his attack on capitalism and free labor.

The title captured his central metaphor: people who lived from the labor of others were, in his rhetoric, economic cannibals. He contended that northern capitalists exploited workers while avoiding the responsibilities imposed on southern slaveholders.

The 'Slaves Without Masters' Argument

Fitzhugh described wage workers as slaves without masters because employers could purchase their labor temporarily without accepting permanent responsibility for their welfare.

He contrasted that system with southern slavery, where the financial value of an enslaved person supposedly gave an owner an incentive to provide long-term care. The argument treated ownership as protection and omitted the enslaved person's lack of consent and legal independence.

What the Argument Concealed

Fitzhugh's comparison between wage labor and chattel slavery depended on collapsing very different forms of exploitation into the same category.

A badly exploited wage worker remained legally a person rather than property. An enslaved person could be bought, sold, inherited, forcibly moved, denied wages, and subjected to a body of law designed to preserve another person's ownership claim.

National Attention

Fitzhugh became one of the country's best-known proslavery extremists during the 1850s. Southern supporters praised the boldness of his arguments, while abolitionists and antislavery northerners worried that his writings exposed the logical direction of the slaveholding South.

His notoriety exceeded his formal political power. He never held a national elected office comparable to John C. Calhoun, James Henry Hammond, Alexander Stephens, or Robert Toombs.

De Bow's Review and the Southern Literary Messenger

Between 1855 and 1867 Fitzhugh published more than ninety articles and essays in De Bow's Review and the Southern Literary Messenger.

His subjects extended beyond slavery to political economy, history, foreign affairs, travel, culture, the Civil War, and Reconstruction. The volume of this writing made him a sustained participant in southern intellectual life rather than the author of only two controversial books.

George Frederick Holmes

Fitzhugh developed an important correspondence with George Frederick Holmes, an academic who favorably reviewed Sociology for the South and later taught at the University of Virginia.

The correspondence is historically valuable because Fitzhugh sometimes expressed private reservations that were less visible in his published polemics.

Private Doubts and Public Extremism

Encyclopedia Virginia notes that Fitzhugh privately told Holmes that he was not personally a friend of slavery or the slave trade and considered them necessary evils, while also indicating that he did not think he should admit that position publicly.

This contradiction is important. His published works often presented uncompromising defenses of slavery, while private correspondence suggests a more complicated personal assessment. Historical interpretation should preserve both records rather than allowing either to erase the other.

Financial Strain

Fitzhugh's writing career came at a financial cost. He neglected portions of his law practice, spent money on publication and travel, and by the end of the 1850s possessed substantially less property than he had earlier in the decade.

The decline in recorded slaveholding—from twenty-seven enslaved people in 1850 to eight listed under his wife in 1860—may have reflected sales made to support his activities.

Washington Appointment

In 1857 Fitzhugh sought government employment in President James Buchanan's administration and obtained a temporary salaried position in the attorney general's office in Washington, D.C.

The appointment underscores that his principal public influence came from writing and patronage networks rather than from an electoral constituency.

Lecture Tour of 1860

In 1860 Fitzhugh undertook a lecture tour advocating greater southern economic self-sufficiency and attacking northern editors and northern influence.

By then the sectional crisis had moved beyond abstract debate over slavery toward the immediate question of whether slave states would remain in the Union after a Republican presidential victory.

Secession

Fitzhugh supported secession. His mature political thought portrayed the slaveholding South as a social order fundamentally different from, and superior to, the liberal and commercial society developing in the North.

Secession therefore fit the trajectory of his writings: if free society was corrupt and hostile to slavery, separation could be presented as preservation rather than revolution.

The 'Conservative Revolution'

During the Civil War Fitzhugh described the Confederate cause as a conservative revolution. He argued that the South was repudiating dangerous principles of individual liberty and equality associated with the Enlightenment and the American Revolution.

This was an unusually explicit statement of the ideological break between his social theory and the egalitarian claims contained in the nation's founding rhetoric.

Confederate Treasury Department

During the Civil War Fitzhugh worked as a clerk in the Confederate Treasury Department in Richmond.

He did not become a major Confederate policymaker or military leader. His wartime significance remained chiefly ideological: he continued writing in defense of the social order the Confederacy was fighting to preserve.

The Collapse of Slavery

Union victory and emancipation destroyed the legal institution Fitzhugh had spent more than a decade defending. The Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery throughout the United States except as punishment for crime.

The postwar period therefore forced Fitzhugh to address a society in which formerly enslaved African Americans were legally free and federal policy sought to define their citizenship and civil rights.

Freedmen's Bureau Court

After the Civil War Fitzhugh served as an associate judge in a Richmond court connected with the Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands, commonly known as the Freedmen's Bureau.

The position is striking given his prewar defense of slavery, but it did not indicate conversion to racial equality. His postwar writings continued to denigrate African Americans and oppose Reconstruction policies intended to expand their opportunities.

Postwar Views of African Americans

Fitzhugh continued to assert that Black Americans lacked the capacity for autonomous citizenship and argued that education could make freedpeople less suited to manual labor.

He opposed federal Reconstruction intervention and maintained that southern whites should control race relations without outside interference.

Reversal on Capitalism

Fitzhugh's postwar thought changed in one significant respect. Before the war he had attacked concentrated capital and free-market competition as destructive to civilization.

After emancipation he increasingly defended property concentration and eventually argued that monopoly of capital by a minority could advance civilization. Historians have treated this as an important reversal in his political economy.

Why His Economic Reversal Matters

The reversal demonstrates that Fitzhugh's thought was not a fixed economic doctrine independent of slavery. When the slave system disappeared, parts of his earlier attack on capitalism became less useful to his social goals.

His deeper commitment was to hierarchy and controlled labor, and his economic arguments changed as the institutions available to preserve hierarchy changed.

Later Writing

Fitzhugh continued publishing after the Civil War in periodicals including Lippincott's Magazine and the Southern Magazine. He wrote about Reconstruction, race relations, labor, and property.

He ceased publishing in 1872.

Death of Mary Fitzhugh

Mary Metcalf Brockenbrough Fitzhugh died in 1877. After her death, Fitzhugh left Virginia and lived increasingly with his adult children.

Kentucky

Fitzhugh moved to Frankfort, Kentucky, to live with one of his sons. His later life was far removed from the period of national notoriety he had experienced during the 1850s.

Texas

In 1880 Fitzhugh moved to Huntsville, Texas, to live with one of his daughters. By this time he was nearly blind.

Death

George Fitzhugh died in Huntsville on July 30, 1881. He was buried in Oakwood Cemetery.

He died sixteen years after the destruction of American chattel slavery, leaving writings that continued to be studied as unusually explicit statements of the antebellum proslavery worldview.

Was Fitzhugh a Major Politician?

No. Fitzhugh's historical importance did not come from elected office, executive authority, or military command.

He belongs in the history of white antagonism toward African American freedom primarily as an ideologue: a writer who supplied aggressive intellectual arguments for slavery, racial dependency, and rejection of free Black citizenship.

Comparison with John C. Calhoun

John C. Calhoun defended slavery through constitutional theory, minority rights for slaveholding states, and national political power. Fitzhugh went further in attacking the premises of free society itself.

Calhoun's importance came from both office and ideology; Fitzhugh's came overwhelmingly from ideology.

Comparison with James Henry Hammond

James Henry Hammond combined plantation wealth, elected office, and proslavery theory. Fitzhugh possessed less wealth and little formal political power, but his critique of free labor was more sweeping.

Both argued that slavery supposedly protected laborers better than northern wage systems, but Fitzhugh developed that comparison into a broader rejection of liberal society.

Comparison with Alexander Stephens

Alexander H. Stephens articulated white supremacy and slavery as foundational to the Confederacy while serving as its vice president. Fitzhugh did not hold comparable office.

His role was to help construct an intellectual climate in which explicit rejection of racial equality and free-labor ideology could be presented as a coherent southern social philosophy.

Historical Significance

Fitzhugh is historically significant because he exposed the radical possibilities within proslavery thought. Instead of apologizing for slavery, he attacked liberty, equality, free contract, and free labor as dangerous or fraudulent principles.

His writings show that the antebellum debate was not simply about whether slavery should expand geographically. At its deepest level, it involved competing definitions of freedom, labor, citizenship, human equality, and legitimate political authority.

Legacy

Modern historians generally study Fitzhugh not as a credible sociologist whose prescriptions should be revived, but as a revealing spokesman for an extreme proslavery intellectual tradition.

His work remains especially valuable because its bluntness strips away some of the euphemisms surrounding slavery. By insisting that hierarchy and dependency were preferable to individual liberty, Fitzhugh made explicit the authoritarian assumptions underlying his defense of bondage.

Geographic Scope

Prince William County was Fitzhugh's birthplace; King George County was the plantation environment of much of his childhood.

Port Royal and Caroline County became the center of his adult legal practice, slaveholding, and writing career. Washington, D.C. provided temporary federal employment, while Richmond was the center of his Confederate and immediate postwar government work.

In later life he moved first to Frankfort, Kentucky, and then to Huntsville, Texas, where he died.

Legal and Constitutional Context

Fitzhugh's work emerged from the antebellum struggle over slavery, free Black status, abolitionism, federalism, territorial expansion, and the relationship between property rights and human liberty.

Unlike constitutional politicians who concentrated on the legal protections of slaveholding states, Fitzhugh frequently challenged the ideological foundations of liberal constitutionalism itself. His writings attacked equality and free contract as social principles, thereby moving beyond conventional states' rights argument into a broader defense of hierarchy.
