Andrew Johnson

Seventeenth president of the United States, Southern Unionist, architect of Presidential Reconstruction, opponent of federal protections for Black citizenship, and first U.S. president to be impeached

Overview

Andrew Johnson (1808-1875) was a Tennessee tailor, self-made politician, Southern Democrat, slaveholder, Unionist during the Civil War, military governor, vice president under Abraham Lincoln, and the seventeenth president of the United States. Born into poverty and lacking formal schooling, Johnson built an extraordinary political career by presenting himself as a champion of ordinary white working men against wealthy planters. His refusal to join Tennessee's secession made him nationally famous and eventually placed him on Lincoln's 1864 National Union ticket. When Lincoln was assassinated only six weeks after Johnson became vice president, Johnson inherited the presidency at the precise moment the United States had to decide how the defeated South would be restored and what freedom would mean for approximately four million formerly enslaved people.

Johnson's presidency became defined by that decision. He moved rapidly to restore former Confederate states under civilian governments, issued thousands of pardons to former Confederates, returned large amounts of confiscated or abandoned land to pardoned Southern owners, opposed Black suffrage, vetoed an expansion of the Freedmen's Bureau, vetoed the Civil Rights Act of 1866, opposed the Fourteenth Amendment, and later vetoed the Reconstruction Acts through which Congress placed much of the former Confederacy under military supervision and required stronger protections for Black citizenship. Congress repeatedly overrode him. Johnson's policies did not create the Southern Black Codes, but the speed and terms of his restoration program allowed white Southern governments to return to power while freedpeople remained politically vulnerable, helping produce the confrontation that led congressional Republicans to seize control of Reconstruction.

The conflict eventually became constitutional. After Congress passed the Tenure of Office Act over Johnson's veto, Johnson attempted to remove Secretary of War Edwin M. Stanton, a congressional ally who supported military Reconstruction. The House impeached Johnson in 1868. The Senate fell one vote short of the two-thirds majority needed to convict and remove him. Johnson completed his term with little political authority, left office in 1869, and later achieved the unusual distinction of returning to the U.S. Senate in 1875. He died only months later. His legacy remains inseparable from Reconstruction: he preserved loyalty to the Union when secession threatened the country, but as president he used executive power to resist the federal reconstruction of Southern society around equal Black citizenship.

Early Life, Tailoring, and the Rise of a Working-Class Politician

Johnson was born in Raleigh, North Carolina, on December 29, 1808. His father, Jacob Johnson, died when Andrew was young, leaving the family in severe poverty. Johnson never attended school in the conventional sense. As a boy he was apprenticed to a tailor, an arrangement that provided a trade but little social status. He eventually left North Carolina and settled in Greeneville, Tennessee, where he opened a tailor shop and married Eliza McCardle in 1827. Eliza, who had more education than her husband, assisted his continuing self-education and helped him improve his reading, writing, arithmetic, and public presentation.

Johnson's tailor shop became a political space as well as a business. Customers discussed local and national affairs while he worked, giving him practice in argument and exposure to the political culture of east Tennessee. He entered public life as a Greeneville alderman in 1829, became mayor, entered the Tennessee legislature, and later advanced to the state senate. In 1843 he won election to the U.S. House of Representatives. His rise was remarkable because he lacked the education, inherited property, and family network that usually accompanied high office in the antebellum South.

He built his political identity around hostility to entrenched privilege. Johnson portrayed wealthy planters as an aristocracy and cast himself as the representative of farmers, mechanics, and laboring white men. Yet his economic populism did not extend to racial equality. Like many white Southern Democrats, he accepted a political order in which Black people were enslaved or subordinated while white male political participation expanded. This combination—class hostility toward elite whites and racial hierarchy over Black people—remained central to his worldview.

Slaveholding and Racial Worldview

Johnson became a slaveholder as his economic position improved. National Park Service records document that he purchased Sam, a thirteen-year-old boy, in 1842 for $541 and purchased Sam's half-sister Dolly the following year. In 1857 he bought another thirteen-year-old boy, Henry, for $1,015. Sam and Dolly had families whose children were born into slavery. Johnson also hired Sam's labor to other people for work around Greeneville, receiving income or services from that labor.

Johnson was not a major plantation owner, and his political career often placed him against the wealthiest slaveholding elite. That distinction matters for understanding his class politics, but it did not make him an opponent of slavery during most of his antebellum career. He defended the constitutional rights of slaveholders and accepted slavery as part of Southern society. His resentment of planter aristocracy was primarily about class power among white people, not a rejection of racial bondage.

His position changed during the Civil War, principally because he came to view slavery as subordinate to preservation of the Union. As military governor of Tennessee he eventually supported emancipation in the state, and family tradition holds that the Johnson household freed the people it enslaved in 1863. Tennessee abolished slavery during Johnson's tenure as military governor. This was a genuine change from his antebellum position, but emancipation did not produce a corresponding commitment to political equality. Johnson could support the end of slavery while still believing that political authority should remain overwhelmingly in white hands. That distinction became decisive once he became president.

Congressman, Governor, Senator, and Southern Unionist

Johnson served five terms in the U.S. House of Representatives beginning in 1843. He championed homestead legislation intended to make western land available to small farmers and continued attacking concentrated economic privilege. In 1853 he became governor of Tennessee and served two terms. The Tennessee legislature elected him to the U.S. Senate in 1857.

The secession crisis transformed him from a regional Democrat into a national figure. Johnson supported slavery and had opposed many Republican policies, but he rejected the doctrine that a state could simply leave the Union after losing a presidential election. When Tennessee seceded in 1861, Johnson refused to abandon his Senate seat. He became the most prominent Southern senator to remain loyal to the United States while his state joined the Confederacy.

That decision involved real political and personal risk. Johnson was denounced as a traitor by Confederate supporters, and much of his property and political base lay within a state at war with the government he continued to serve. President Abraham Lincoln rewarded his loyalty in March 1862 by appointing him military governor of Tennessee. Johnson's task was to restore federal authority, suppress Confederate political power, and rebuild a loyal state government under wartime conditions.

Military Governor, Emancipation, and the 1864 National Union Ticket

As military governor, Johnson governed Tennessee under extraordinary wartime authority. He initially remained cautious about emancipation, but the progress of the war changed his position. By 1864 he was publicly arguing that preserving the Union was more important than preserving slavery. Tennessee, which was excluded from Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation because it was already substantially under Union control, moved toward state abolition under Johnson's military governorship. Voters approved a new state constitution abolishing slavery in February 1865.

Lincoln chose Johnson as his running mate in 1864 partly because Johnson symbolized Southern Unionism. The Republican Party temporarily campaigned as the National Union Party, seeking to unite Republicans, War Democrats, and loyal Southerners. Replacing Vice President Hannibal Hamlin with a Tennessee Democrat communicated that the war was for restoration of the Union as well as emancipation.

Johnson took office as vice president on March 4, 1865. His vice-presidential inaugural appearance became an embarrassment after he delivered a rambling address while visibly impaired. It might have remained a minor historical episode. Six weeks later it became a footnote to a national catastrophe: John Wilkes Booth assassinated Lincoln, and on April 15 Johnson was sworn in as president.

Succession after Lincoln and the Choice of Presidential Reconstruction

Johnson inherited no completed blueprint for Reconstruction. Lincoln had favored relatively rapid restoration of loyal governments but had also moved toward limited Black suffrage shortly before his death. Congress contained moderates and Radicals with different expectations, and the former Confederate states were socially and economically devastated. The central questions were unresolved: Who would govern the South? What political disabilities would former Confederates face? Would freedpeople receive land, federal protection, citizenship, and voting rights? How much power would Congress exercise over readmission?

Johnson initially spoke harshly about Confederate leaders, which led some Radical Republicans to believe he would impose a tougher settlement than Lincoln. Instead, he quickly pursued restoration under presidential control. Beginning in May 1865 he appointed provisional governors and established procedures by which Southern states could reorganize civil governments, repudiate secession, accept the destruction of slavery, and resume ordinary political life. He excluded certain Confederate leaders and wealthy rebels from automatic amnesty, but these men could apply directly to him for individual pardons.

The pardon process became one of the most consequential features of his presidency. Johnson issued more than thirteen thousand pardons and later broadened amnesty further. Many former Confederate politicians and members of the prewar governing class regained property and political standing. Southern electorates chose numerous former Confederates for office, including men who had served the Confederate government. To Johnson, rapid restoration vindicated the principle that secession had been illegal and the states had never truly left the Union. To congressional Republicans, it appeared that the defeated ruling class was returning before the federal government had secured the freedom of the people it had formerly enslaved.

Land Restoration, Black Codes, and the Meaning of Freedom

The conflict was not only political. It concerned land and labor. During the war the federal government had taken control of abandoned and confiscated property in parts of the South, and some freed families occupied land under wartime programs. Johnson's pardons restored property rights to many former Confederates. As pardoned owners recovered land, freedpeople who had hoped that emancipation would bring an independent economic base were frequently displaced or pushed toward labor contracts with white landowners.

At the same time, newly restored Southern legislatures enacted Black Codes. The details varied by state, but the laws sought to regulate Black labor, movement, contracts, family life, weapons possession, and other aspects of freedom. Vagrancy laws and labor provisions could force freedpeople into highly coercive employment arrangements. The codes demonstrated that abolishing slavery did not automatically produce equal citizenship.

Johnson did not personally write the Black Codes, and it is important not to attribute state legislation directly to him. His responsibility operated through the structure of restoration. He returned political authority to white Southern governments without requiring Black suffrage or strong federal civil-rights guarantees as a condition of readmission. When those governments restricted freedpeople, Johnson continued to insist that most questions of civil status belonged primarily to the states and that the Southern states should be restored rapidly to normal representation.

Freedmen's Bureau, Civil Rights Act of 1866, and the Break with Congress

The Freedmen's Bureau became one of the first major tests. Created in 1865, the bureau provided food, medical assistance, labor-contract supervision, education, legal help, and other services to freedpeople and refugees. Congress passed legislation in 1866 to extend and strengthen the agency. Johnson vetoed it in February, arguing that the federal program exceeded constitutional authority, interfered with state responsibility, and created special treatment for one class of people. Congress initially failed to override that veto.

The next battle was larger. The Civil Rights Act of 1866 declared U.S.-born persons citizens without regard to race, with important protections for equal civil rights. Johnson vetoed the bill on March 27. His veto message objected to what he considered federal intrusion into state law and complained that the measure conferred advantages on Black people while large numbers of immigrants had not yet acquired citizenship. Congress overrode him—the first time in American history that Congress overrode a presidential veto of major legislation of this kind.

A revised Freedmen's Bureau bill followed. Johnson vetoed that measure as well, but this time Congress overrode him. The political relationship was now fundamentally broken. Johnson believed congressional Republicans were revolutionizing the federal system and degrading white self-government in the South. Republicans increasingly believed the president was obstructing the minimum national guarantees necessary to make emancipation real.

Black Suffrage, the Fourteenth Amendment, and the 1866 Political Crisis

Johnson's opposition to Black political equality was explicit. He resisted proposals for federal Black suffrage and argued that voting qualifications should remain primarily under state control. In the immediate postwar South, where the restored states were controlled by white electorates, that position meant that millions of freedmen remained politically powerless while former Confederates regained influence.

Congress responded by proposing the Fourteenth Amendment in 1866. The amendment established national birthright citizenship, prohibited states from denying due process or equal protection, altered representation rules, and restricted certain former Confederates from office. Johnson opposed it and encouraged Southern states to reject it. Tennessee nevertheless ratified the amendment and was readmitted to congressional representation.

The political environment grew more violent. White attacks on Black communities and Unionists in Memphis and New Orleans in 1866 convinced many Northerners that presidential restoration had failed to protect freedpeople. Johnson then embarked on his “Swing Around the Circle,” an unprecedented speaking tour intended to rally voters behind his Reconstruction policy in the congressional elections. He attacked leading congressional opponents and argued with hecklers in performances widely regarded as undignified. Rather than rebuilding support, the tour helped Republicans portray the president as reckless and strengthened the congressional majority determined to take Reconstruction out of his hands.

Congressional Reconstruction and Johnson's Resistance

After the Republican victory in the 1866 elections, Congress moved decisively. The Reconstruction Act of 1867 divided most of the former Confederacy into five military districts and required new political arrangements before the states could regain full representation. The process included ratification of the Fourteenth Amendment and expanded voting rights for Black men. Johnson vetoed the legislation. Congress overrode him.

Johnson also vetoed supplementary Reconstruction measures and continued trying to limit the congressional program through appointments, removals, interpretations of executive authority, and resistance within the administration. Congress repeatedly overrode his vetoes. The result was an extraordinary period in which Congress effectively designed a major national policy in open conflict with the president charged with executing federal law.

For Black Southerners, Congressional Reconstruction opened political possibilities that Johnson had opposed. Black men registered to vote, participated in constitutional conventions, held local and state offices, and entered Congress. These gains remained vulnerable to white violence, but they represented a fundamentally different reconstruction of Southern government from the white-controlled restoration Johnson had favored.

Impeachment and the Tenure of Office Crisis

Congress sought to protect its Reconstruction program from presidential interference. One major step was the Tenure of Office Act of 1867, passed over Johnson's veto. The law restricted the president's ability to remove certain federal officials without Senate approval. Its constitutionality and precise application were disputed, but Congress intended in part to protect Secretary of War Edwin M. Stanton, who supported Congressional Reconstruction and worked closely with the military officials administering it.

Johnson suspended Stanton in August 1867 and placed Ulysses S. Grant temporarily in the War Department. When the Senate rejected the suspension, Stanton returned and Grant stepped aside. In February 1868 Johnson attempted to remove Stanton again and named Lorenzo Thomas as secretary of war ad interim. The House treated the action as a direct challenge to federal law and executive accountability.

On February 24, 1868, the House voted 126 to 47 to impeach Johnson. Eleven articles followed, most centered on the Stanton removal and alleged violations of the Tenure of Office Act, while others accused Johnson of attempting to undermine congressional authority. Chief Justice Salmon P. Chase presided over the Senate trial. On May 16 the Senate voted 35 guilty to 19 not guilty on one article—one vote short of the two-thirds majority required for conviction. Two other articles later produced the same result. Johnson remained president.

The acquittal did not vindicate his Reconstruction program. Several senators who voted against conviction opposed Johnson politically but feared that removal on the charges presented would weaken the constitutional independence of the presidency or turn impeachment into a parliamentary vote of no confidence. The episode therefore belongs simultaneously to the history of Reconstruction, civil rights, executive power, and impeachment.

Foreign Affairs, Alaska, and the Rest of the Presidency

Reconstruction dominated Johnson's presidency, but his administration also oversaw important foreign-policy developments. Secretary of State William H. Seward remained in office after Lincoln's death and negotiated the purchase of Alaska from Russia for $7.2 million in 1867. The Senate approved the treaty, Johnson signed it, and Alaska was formally transferred to the United States in October 1867. The acquisition expanded U.S. territory and later proved strategically and economically significant.

The administration also dealt with French intervention in Mexico, relations with Britain after the Civil War, and other questions inherited from the Lincoln years. Johnson's foreign policy was generally less disruptive than his domestic presidency because Seward provided continuity and because Congress and the president shared some expansionist and diplomatic objectives.

By 1868 Johnson had little realistic chance of winning another term. Democrats did not nominate him for president, and Republican Ulysses S. Grant won the election. Johnson left office on March 4, 1869. He declined to attend Grant's inauguration, an outward sign of the hostility that had developed between the two men and between Johnson and the political order that replaced him.

How Andrew Johnson Opposed Black Americans

Johnson's opposition to Black equality was exercised primarily through presidential authority rather than through a private racist organization. He opposed Black suffrage at the moment when voting rights would determine who controlled the reconstructed South. He restored political rights to thousands of former Confederates through pardons, resisted making strong federal civil-rights protections a condition of restoration, vetoed legislation expanding the Freedmen's Bureau, vetoed the Civil Rights Act of 1866, opposed the Fourteenth Amendment, and vetoed Congressional Reconstruction measures that protected Black voting and placed the former Confederate states under military supervision.

His actions mattered because timing mattered. The period immediately after emancipation was the moment when the federal government possessed extraordinary leverage over the defeated South. Johnson chose to use much of that leverage to accelerate restoration of white-controlled state governments rather than to make Black political participation a prerequisite for readmission. Southern governments then enacted Black Codes and tolerated or failed to suppress widespread racial violence. Johnson did not create every discriminatory measure that followed, but his reconstruction policy strengthened the governments and social forces resisting Black autonomy.

Johnson's racial politics also reveal an important nineteenth-century distinction. He had come to support emancipation and could recognize individual Black people as industrious or deserving, yet he rejected the proposition that emancipation required equal political citizenship. Freedom, in his framework, did not necessarily include the vote, federal protection against discriminatory state law, or equal participation in governing the South. That gap between freedom from slavery and equality before the state is central to understanding his presidency.

Comparison with Abraham Lincoln, Congressional Republicans, and Ulysses S. Grant

Johnson often claimed continuity with Lincoln, and there was some basis for the claim. Lincoln had favored relatively rapid restoration and had resisted the harshest congressional proposals during the war. But Lincoln's final position was still evolving when he was assassinated. In his last public speech he endorsed limited Black suffrage for educated Black men and Black veterans. Johnson moved in the opposite direction by opposing Black suffrage and resisting national civil-rights guarantees.

Congressional Republicans were themselves divided. Moderates and Radicals disagreed over confiscation, suffrage, military government, and the proper conditions for readmission. Johnson's persistent vetoes and the behavior of restored Southern governments helped unify these factions around stronger federal intervention. In that sense Johnson did not merely lose the Reconstruction debate; his resistance helped produce a more aggressive congressional program than might otherwise have emerged.

Grant inherited a different political landscape in 1869. Under Grant, the federal government enforced the Fifteenth Amendment and used Enforcement Acts against the Ku Klux Klan and other violent opponents of Reconstruction. Federal protection remained uneven and eventually weakened, but Grant's administration accepted a national role in defending Black political participation that Johnson had consistently resisted.

Return to Tennessee Politics and the United States Senate

Johnson did not retire quietly. He remained determined to vindicate himself and repeatedly sought a return to public office in Tennessee. He failed in campaigns for the U.S. Senate and House before finally winning a Senate seat in January 1875 after a prolonged contest in the Tennessee legislature.

His return was historically unique. On March 5, 1875, Andrew Johnson took the oath as a United States senator in the same chamber that had come within one vote of removing him from the presidency seven years earlier. He remains the only former U.S. president to serve subsequently in the Senate.

The comeback was brief. After a short special session he returned to Tennessee. Johnson suffered a stroke and died on July 31, 1875, at the age of sixty-six. He was buried in Greeneville, wrapped in an American flag with a copy of the Constitution, a burial arrangement that reflected how strongly he continued to identify his political career with preservation of the Union and his own interpretation of constitutional government.

Historical Significance

Andrew Johnson's importance comes from the collision of two very different legacies. During the secession crisis he showed unusual courage in remaining loyal to the Union when Tennessee joined the Confederacy. As military governor he eventually supported emancipation and helped restore loyal government in his state. Those actions explain why Lincoln placed him on the 1864 national ticket and why contemporaries initially believed he might become a forceful postwar president.

Yet Johnson's presidency became one of the great missed opportunities of Reconstruction. The United States had defeated the Confederacy, abolished slavery, and temporarily possessed the military and political power to restructure Southern institutions. Johnson prioritized rapid restoration of state authority and white political control over enforceable guarantees of Black equality. The resulting struggle pushed Congress toward the Civil Rights Act of 1866, Fourteenth Amendment, Military Reconstruction Acts, and ultimately a new constitutional definition of citizenship that developed largely in opposition to the president.

His impeachment was also historically foundational. The Johnson trial established early precedents about the difference between political disagreement and impeachable misconduct, the independence of the presidency, the Senate's role as a court of impeachment, and the dangers of using removal as an ordinary weapon in a separation-of-powers dispute.

Impact on African Americans

Johnson's presidency had immediate consequences for formerly enslaved people. His restoration policy strengthened white Southern governments before Black men possessed secure voting rights. His pardons helped restore property and political influence to former Confederates. His resistance to the Freedmen's Bureau and Civil Rights Act attempted to limit the federal institutions Congress was constructing to protect freedpeople from discriminatory state action and economic coercion.

The political reaction to Johnson also had an indirect and paradoxical effect. His obstruction convinced many congressional moderates that stronger national protections were necessary. Measures Johnson opposed became central foundations of American citizenship: the Civil Rights Act of 1866, the Fourteenth Amendment, and Congressional Reconstruction. Black political participation expanded dramatically after Congress displaced his restoration program.

For that reason, Johnson's impact cannot be measured only by the policies he successfully enacted. His presidency became the negative case against which a more national understanding of citizenship and equal protection was constructed.

A Balanced Assessment

Johnson possessed qualities that explain his extraordinary rise. He was self-educated, resilient, politically courageous during secession, an effective stump speaker for much of his career, and deeply committed to the survival of the Union. His loyalty in 1861 was not inevitable for a Tennessee Democrat and slaveholder. As military governor he administered a state divided by civil war and moved from defending slavery to supporting its abolition.

Those strengths coexisted with severe limitations. Johnson's racial assumptions prevented him from understanding emancipation as a transformation of citizenship rather than simply the termination of legal slavery. His rigid constitutionalism often served an existing racial order because the states to which he wanted to return authority were governed by white men determined to limit Black freedom. His temperament made compromise more difficult: he personalized political conflict, publicly attacked opponents, and frequently treated disagreement as evidence of disloyalty or conspiracy.

A balanced assessment therefore does not require choosing between “Union hero” and “failed Reconstruction president.” He was both a consequential Southern Unionist and a president whose policies obstructed the early construction of equal Black citizenship. The contradiction is the biography.

Legacy

Johnson left office politically isolated, and historians have generally judged his presidency harshly because of his Reconstruction record. The constitutional system he resisted outlasted him. The Fourteenth Amendment established national citizenship and equal protection; the Fifteenth Amendment prohibited racial discrimination in voting; and later civil-rights movements repeatedly invoked these Reconstruction amendments against state systems of racial exclusion.

The impeachment legacy is more complicated. Johnson's acquittal has often been interpreted as preserving a degree of executive independence even though the policies that produced the confrontation were deeply damaging to freedpeople. Later impeachment debates repeatedly returned to 1868 when asking whether removal should require criminality, abuse of office, defiance of Congress, or some broader breach of constitutional trust.

Johnson is also remembered as a warning about political transition after civil conflict. Ending a war and abolishing an institution are not the same as constructing a new social order. His presidency demonstrates how quickly old power structures can reconstitute themselves when legal emancipation is not accompanied by secure political rights, enforcement institutions, and access to economic independence.

Geographic Scope

North Carolina shaped Johnson's impoverished childhood, while Greeneville, Tennessee became his adult home, business base, and political identity. His state and national career connected him to Nashville, where he served as military governor during the Civil War, and to Washington, D.C., where he served in both houses of Congress, the vice presidency, and the presidency.

His greatest impact fell across the former Confederate South. Presidential Reconstruction affected the restoration of governments from Virginia to Texas, the status of former Confederates, Black political rights, land disputes, and federal occupation policy. His administration also enlarged the national geographic scope through the 1867 purchase of Alaska.

Legal and Constitutional Context

Johnson's presidency sits at the center of the constitutional transformation produced by the Civil War. The Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery, while the Civil Rights Act of 1866 and Fourteenth Amendment developed national definitions of citizenship and equal civil status. Johnson opposed much of that expansion of federal authority, arguing for a quicker restoration of state control and a narrower understanding of national power.

The Reconstruction Acts raised additional constitutional questions about congressional authority over defeated states, military government, suffrage, and conditions for representation. The Tenure of Office Act and Johnson's impeachment then turned Reconstruction into a separation-of-powers crisis. His acquittal did not settle every issue, but it became a major precedent in the American law and politics of impeachment.
