Gary Thomas Rowe Jr.

FBI informant, Ku Klux Klan operative, participant in civil-rights-era violence, and principal insider witness in the 1965 prosecution arising from the murder of Viola Gregg Liuzzo

Overview

Gary Thomas Rowe Jr. was an American Federal Bureau of Investigation informant, Ku Klux Klan member, and participant in violent Klan activity in Alabama during the civil-rights era.

Between 1960 and 1965, Rowe infiltrated the Birmingham-area Klan while reporting to the FBI.

His historical significance is unusually complex because he was simultaneously a source for federal investigators and an active participant in the organization he was assigned to penetrate.

Rowe became one of the FBI's most valuable informants inside the Alabama Klan, but later judicial and congressional records also established that he sometimes participated in violence while undercover.

That dual role places him at the center of major questions about informant management, government responsibility, undercover participation in criminal organizations, and the limits of intelligence gathering.

Rowe was born on August 13, 1933, in Savannah, Georgia, to Gary Thomas Rowe and Alma Ann Sellars.

He left school after the eighth grade and later served in the Georgia National Guard and United States Marine Corps Reserve.

After his military discharge in March 1957, he moved to Birmingham, Alabama.

Rowe wanted a career in law enforcement but lacked the educational qualifications required for some positions.

He worked as a bouncer at a Veterans of Foreign Wars club in Birmingham, a location frequented by Ku Klux Klan members.

Local Klansmen encouraged him to join the organization.

At roughly the same time, the FBI was attempting to penetrate violent white-supremacist organizations in Alabama.

FBI agent Barrett G. Kemp recruited Rowe as an informant in 1960.

Rowe joined the Eastview Klavern in Birmingham and began providing information about Klan personnel, meetings, plans, and violent activity.

He was paid by the FBI for his services and expenses.

His ability to gain Klan trust made him exceptionally valuable to the Bureau.

Rowe rose rapidly within the Klan and became associated with an elite action group involved in violent operations.

A later federal court found that the FBI encouraged him to work his way upward because serious conspiratorial activity was often planned by small action squads.

Rowe eventually became a leader of an action squad.

When FBI headquarters learned of the leadership position, it directed that he resign because informants were not to direct, lead, or instigate violent acts.

Rowe was also instructed that when illegal acts were expected, he should avoid genuine participation where possible and attempt to fake or minimize his involvement.

The historical record shows that those restrictions did not prevent him from participating in violence.

In 1983, a federal district court concluded that there were times when Rowe committed violent acts with other Klansmen and was not fully truthful to the FBI about his own participation.

The same court found that his handlers must have understood that he sometimes engaged in violence because he repeatedly remained accepted among violent Klansmen.

Later Justice Department oversight materials similarly described Rowe as an informant who participated in violent Klan acts while the FBI attempted to preserve his cover.

Rowe's conduct during the May 1961 Freedom Rider crisis became one of the clearest examples.

He warned the FBI in advance that Klan members were planning violence against arriving Freedom Riders.

Despite that warning, the attacks in Birmingham proceeded.

Rowe later acknowledged participating in violence during Klan actions in order to preserve his undercover identity.

These episodes became central to later criticism of FBI informant policy.

The controversy is not simply whether the Bureau had a source inside the Klan.

The deeper issue is how much criminal participation the government tolerated, anticipated, or failed to control while using that source.

Rowe's informant work nevertheless produced substantial intelligence.

He provided names, organizational information, reports of planned violence, and information concerning past crimes.

A later federal court described him as perhaps the best Klan informant in the Birmingham area.

His access also made him especially dangerous if not controlled because the same traits that gave him credibility with violent Klansmen could place him directly inside violent events.

Some later accounts suspected Rowe of involvement in the May 1963 bombings connected to the Gaston Motel and the home of Martin Luther King Jr.'s brother.

Those suspicions should be distinguished from established findings.

Likewise, Rowe was later suspected of having knowledge of or involvement in the September 1963 Sixteenth Street Baptist Church bombing.

A later Justice Department investigation did not establish that Rowe participated in that bombing.

Historical treatment therefore must separate documented violence, admitted conduct, government findings, allegations, and unresolved claims.

By March 1965 Rowe remained an FBI informant inside the Klan.

The immediate political context was the Selma voting-rights campaign and the federally protected march from Selma to Montgomery.

On March 25, 1965, Klan leadership directed Rowe to join Eugene Thomas, Collie Leroy Wilkins Jr., and William Orville Eaton on a trip connected to the march.

Before leaving, Rowe contacted his FBI handler and reported the proposed trip.

His handler authorized or encouraged him to go because the Bureau wanted information on the Klan's response to the march.

The four men travelled together from the Birmingham-Bessemer area to Montgomery.

They spent hours around the state capitol observing and heckling civil-rights participants and Black spectators.

Afterward, the group drove toward Selma and continued along the Highway 80 corridor.

Their conduct escalated from observation and harassment to discussion of violence.

Later that night they encountered a light-colored Oldsmobile driven by Viola Gregg Liuzzo, a white civil-rights volunteer from Detroit.

Leroy Moton, a Black civil-rights worker, was riding with her.

The Klan car pursued Liuzzo's vehicle at high speed.

Gunfire from the Klan car struck Liuzzo, killing her.

Moton survived by remaining motionless and later obtaining help.

Rowe was physically present in the Klan car throughout the pursuit and shooting.

That fact is undisputed.

What Rowe did during the shooting became the subject of decades of controversy.

Rowe consistently maintained that he did not fire at Liuzzo's car and that Collie Wilkins fired the fatal shots while Eugene Thomas encouraged the attack.

Wilkins and Thomas later accused Rowe of being the shooter.

Those competing accounts became central to a 1978 Alabama murder indictment against Rowe and to later civil litigation.

The strongest later judicial finding rejected the accusation that Rowe shot Liuzzo.

In 1983, after an eight-day federal civil trial examining the FBI's management of Rowe, Judge Charles W. Joiner found that Rowe did not shoot Liuzzo and did not encourage Wilkins to shoot.

The court found that Wilkins shot Liuzzo and that Thomas encouraged him.

The court also found that Rowe had attempted to defuse or divert the confrontation without exposing his informant status.

That finding concerned the Liuzzo killing specifically.

It did not exonerate Rowe from his documented participation in other Klan violence.

Immediately after the murder, Rowe reported the crime to his FBI contact.

He identified Thomas, Wilkins, and Eaton and helped locate evidence.

Federal authorities arrested the four men within approximately fifteen hours.

President Lyndon B. Johnson publicly announced the arrests on March 26, 1965.

Johnson identified Rowe as a thirty-one-year-old Birmingham resident and publicly described all four arrested men as members of the United Klans of America.

At that moment the administration did not disclose that Rowe was an FBI informant.

Rowe's undercover status soon became known as prosecutors prepared to use him as a witness.

State and federal officials promised Rowe immunity in exchange for his cooperation and testimony.

That promise later became legally decisive.

Rowe testified before state and federal grand juries and at state and federal trials.

His testimony was central to the cases against Wilkins, Thomas, and Eaton.

The federal government prosecuted Wilkins, Thomas, and Eaton under civil-rights law.

On December 3, 1965, a federal jury convicted the three men of conspiracy against federally protected rights.

Each received a ten-year federal sentence.

Rowe himself was not a defendant in that federal conspiracy trial because authorities were using him as the cooperating insider witness.

After his testimony, the federal government relocated Rowe and gave him a new identity.

He lived under the name Thomas Neil Moore.

His post-1965 protection reflected the danger created by his exposure as an FBI source against the Klan.

Rowe's relationship with the FBI later deteriorated.

In 1975, congressional investigations of domestic intelligence and FBI informant practices returned attention to his conduct.

The Church Committee examined what the Bureau knew about his participation in Klan violence and what instructions agents had given him.

Rowe testified that he and other Klansmen had beaten people, attacked bus passengers, and used weapons during violent actions.

He also asserted that the FBI sometimes received advance warning of planned violence without successfully preventing it.

The committee record helped expose the operational dilemma at the center of Rowe's career.

The FBI wanted intelligence from the most violent portions of the Klan, but remaining credible inside those circles could require proximity to and sometimes participation in criminal conduct.

FBI headquarters formally prohibited Rowe from directing or instigating violence.

Yet the practical management of his undercover role allowed repeated contact with men committing serious assaults.

Rowe published an autobiography in 1976 titled My Undercover Years with the Ku Klux Klan.

The book reinforced his public image as both a government source and a participant in the violent world he described.

By the late 1970s, surviving defendants and journalists were reexamining the Liuzzo case.

In September 1978, a Lowndes County grand jury indicted Rowe for Liuzzo's murder.

District Attorney Jesse Bryan pursued the case after new allegations and polygraph-related material suggested to him that Rowe might have fired the fatal shots.

Polygraph results themselves were not admissible proof of guilt, and the later federal appellate record emphasized the more fundamental problem: Rowe had already been promised immunity.

Rowe sought federal relief to stop the Alabama prosecution.

In 1980, a federal district court permanently enjoined further prosecution.

The Eleventh Circuit affirmed that injunction in Rowe v. Griffin on May 17, 1982.

The appellate court held that state and federal authorities had promised Rowe transactional immunity in exchange for his testimony and cooperation.

The court concluded that the government could not accept the benefits of that bargain and then prosecute him for the same episode.

That ruling did not decide whether Rowe was factually innocent of every allegation made against him.

It enforced the government's immunity promise.

The distinction is crucial.

Immunity is not the same thing as exoneration.

Likewise, an indictment is not the same thing as a conviction.

Rowe was indicted for Liuzzo's murder in 1978, but the prosecution was barred before a trial on the merits.

A separate 1983 federal civil case produced the most important judicial factual finding on the shooting itself.

In Liuzzo v. United States, the court examined whether the federal government could be held liable for the FBI's use and supervision of Rowe.

The court found that Rowe was acting as an FBI informant when he joined the March 25 trip.

It also found that he had a history of violent conduct and that his agents likely knew he sometimes participated in violence.

Nevertheless, the court rejected the claim that Rowe shot Liuzzo or encouraged her killing.

The court found that Wilkins fired the fatal shots at Thomas's urging.

The court further found that Rowe's rapid report was the principal reason the murder was solved so quickly.

The United States therefore was not held liable for Liuzzo's death on the theory presented in that case.

This legal result did not erase the broader scandal surrounding FBI handling of Rowe.

His career remained an important case study in the risks of using informants inside violent extremist organizations.

Rowe's record shows both the investigative value and institutional danger of deep infiltration.

His information helped identify violent Klansmen and supported successful federal prosecutions.

At the same time, his own violent participation exposed the danger that an informant may become entangled in the crimes he is assigned to observe.

His biography therefore cannot be reduced to either 'heroic undercover agent' or 'secret Klan criminal.'

Both descriptions omit essential evidence.

He was an FBI source who materially assisted investigations.

He was also a genuine Klan member whose acceptance inside violent circles was not merely theatrical and whose undercover work included participation in violent acts.

The exact boundaries of his culpability varied from incident to incident.

For the Liuzzo murder, the strongest later judicial finding concluded that he neither fired the fatal shots nor encouraged them.

For other Klan violence, courts and congressional oversight materials found or recorded direct participation.

That mixed record makes precise wording essential.

Rowe should not be falsely described as convicted of Liuzzo's murder.

He was never convicted of that crime.

He should also not be described as an uninvolved witness who merely happened to observe Klan violence.

His own testimony and government findings establish participation in violent Klan activity.

Rowe spent much of his later life under an assumed identity.

He died of a heart attack in Savannah, Georgia, on May 25, 1998, at age sixty-four.

His death was not widely reported until several months later.

By then, his career had already shaped debates about FBI informants, Klan infiltration, government accountability, and civil-rights enforcement.

For MGU's antagonist series, Rowe requires a separate analytical category from ordinary Klan perpetrators.

He was both embedded in the violent white-supremacist movement and employed by the federal government to report on it.

That relationship does not cancel his responsibility for violence in which he personally participated.

Nor does his violent record justify disregarding judicial findings that rejected specific later accusations against him.

His historical importance lies in the collision between those two realities.

Gary Thomas Rowe Jr. demonstrates how an intelligence operation can penetrate an extremist organization while simultaneously becoming compromised by the conduct required to maintain access.

Historical Significance

Rowe's biography connects Klan violence, FBI infiltration, informant crime, congressional oversight, immunity law, civil-rights prosecution, and government accountability.

Legacy

Geographic Scope

Savannah, Georgia, was Rowe's birthplace and the city in which he later lived under federal protection and died in 1998.

Birmingham, Alabama, was the center of his principal FBI informant work and the violent Klan network he infiltrated.

Selma and Montgomery formed the political geography of the 1965 voting-rights campaign.

Lowndes County became the site of the Liuzzo murder investigation, the later 1978 indictment of Rowe, and the state prosecution controversy.

Legal and Constitutional Context

Rowe's legal history differs sharply from that of the three Klansmen prosecuted in federal court after the Liuzzo murder.

State and federal authorities used Rowe as a cooperating witness and promised him immunity in exchange for his testimony.

When Alabama later indicted him for murder in 1978, federal courts enforced that promise rather than allow the prosecution to proceed.

Rowe v. Griffin therefore turned primarily on due process, enforceability of prosecutorial promises, equitable immunity, and federal intervention in a pending state prosecution.

The Eleventh Circuit treated the immunity as transactional: Rowe had been promised that he would not later be prosecuted for his activity in the Liuzzo episode if he cooperated and testified.

The decision did not transform immunity into a factual finding of innocence.

A separate civil action, Liuzzo v. United States, addressed the FBI's responsibility for its use and supervision of Rowe.

That court made the strongest later judicial findings about the actual shooting, concluding that Rowe did not shoot Liuzzo and did not encourage the shooting.

The same opinion nevertheless recognized that Rowe had participated in other violent Klan acts and had not always been fully truthful with his handlers about his own conduct.

Rowe's legal record therefore demonstrates why indictment, immunity, civil findings, criminal conviction, and historical responsibility must be treated as different categories.
