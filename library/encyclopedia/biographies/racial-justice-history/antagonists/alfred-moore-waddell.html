Alfred Moore Waddell

North Carolina lawyer, Confederate officer, U.S. representative, white-supremacy orator, and central leader of the 1898 Wilmington coup

Overview

Alfred Moore Waddell (1834-1912) was a North Carolina lawyer, newspaper editor, Confederate officer, United States representative, author, political orator, mayor of Wilmington, and a central public leader of the white-supremacist Wilmington coup of 1898.

His career was marked by political transformation. Waddell opposed secession in 1860 as a Constitutional Unionist and edited a Unionist newspaper, but after the outbreak of the Civil War he joined the Confederacy and rose to lieutenant colonel. After the war he returned to law, addressed Black Wilmingtonians in 1865 in favor of a formally race-neutral property or educational voting qualification, and later served four terms in Congress.

By the 1890s, however, Waddell had become one of the most incendiary speakers in North Carolina's white-supremacy campaign. In 1898 he publicly threatened mass racial violence, chaired the meeting that issued the “Wilmington Declaration of Independence,” led the armed march against the Black-owned Daily Record, participated in forcing the elected biracial city government from office, and accepted the mayoralty created by the coup.

Family Background

Waddell was the son of Hugh and Susan Moore Waddell and came from a wealthy and prominent North Carolina family with deep Lower Cape Fear connections.

Revolutionary-Era Ancestry

His ancestry included Hugh Waddell, Francis Nash, and Alfred Moore, giving him family ties to several prominent Revolutionary-era figures.

Education

Waddell attended Bingham's School and Caldwell Institute in Hillsborough before graduating from the University of North Carolina in 1853.

Legal Career

He studied law, was admitted to the bar in 1855, and established himself in Wilmington by 1856.

Marriage and Family

Waddell married Julia Savage in 1857. They had two children. After Julia's death he married her sister Ellen Savage in 1878, and after Ellen's death he married Gabrielle de Rosset in 1896.

Clerk and Master in Equity

Waddell's first public office was Clerk and Master in Equity for New Hanover County, a position he held from 1858 until 1861.

American Party Politics

During the 1850s Waddell supported the American Party and participated in its political activities.

Constitutional Unionist

In 1860 he supported the Constitutional Union Party and attended its national convention as an alternate delegate from North Carolina.

Opposition to Secession

Waddell opposed secession in 1860. This is important because his later Confederate service represented a political change rather than lifelong disunionism.

The Wilmington Herald

He purchased and edited the Wilmington Herald in 1860 and 1861. The newspaper reflected his Unionist position during the final antebellum crisis.

Fort Sumter and Political Conversion

After visiting Charleston Harbor during the opening crisis at Fort Sumter and the outbreak of war, Waddell abandoned his Unionist position.

Confederate Service

Waddell resigned his county office and entered Confederate service. He eventually rose to lieutenant colonel in the 41st North Carolina Regiment, also known as the 3rd North Carolina Cavalry.

Ill Health and Departure from the Army

Ill health forced Waddell to leave Confederate service in 1864.

Return to Wilmington

After the Civil War he returned to Wilmington and reopened his law practice.

1865 Address to Black Citizens

In July 1865 Waddell delivered an address to Black residents of Wilmington at their request.

Voting-Rights Position in 1865

In that address Waddell endorsed a voting qualification based on intelligence, property, or both and stated that anyone meeting the standard should be able to vote regardless of race.

The proposal was restrictive by modern democratic standards but significantly more race-neutral than the political position he would adopt three decades later.

Political Evolution After the War

Waddell's later white-supremacist politics should not be projected backward unchanged onto 1865. His racial and electoral views hardened substantially during the postwar decades.

Conservative Party Nomination

In 1870 Waddell accepted the Conservative Party nomination for Congress and won election to the Forty-second Congress.

Fourteenth Amendment Eligibility Question

Because he had held a prewar public position and then joined the Confederacy, some members questioned whether Section Three of the Fourteenth Amendment barred him from Congress.

Admission to Congress

Congress ultimately permitted Waddell to take his seat after supporters argued that his earlier county post was clerical rather than the kind of office contemplated by the constitutional disability clause.

Oath of Allegiance

In 1871 Waddell took an oath of allegiance to the United States and began congressional service.

Opposition to the Enforcement Act

Waddell opposed the Enforcement Act, or Ku Klux Klan Act, as an excessive federal response to southern conditions.

Investigation of Southern Violence

At the same time, he voted to establish a House investigation into Ku Klux Klan activity and other southern political violence and served on the investigative committee.

Minority Report

Waddell signed a minority report acknowledging Ku Klux violence while arguing that Republican oppression and Reconstruction policy had helped produce it.

Four Terms in Congress

Waddell won repeated reelection and served in the U.S. House from 1871 through 1879.

Committee Work

His congressional assignments included printing, enrolled bills, the Library, election investigations, and postal affairs.

Post Office and Post Roads Committee

During his final term he chaired the House Committee on Post Office and Post Roads.

Postal Reform

Waddell supported additional postal routes, higher compensation for carriers, and development of a postal savings-bank concept.

Political Oratory

Waddell developed a broad reputation as a gifted public speaker. Admirers praised his eloquence, while Republican critics sometimes described his rhetoric as incendiary.

1876 Caning Incident

In 1876 Waddell struck a Republican newspaper editor with a cane after the paper published personal accusations against him. A court later fined Waddell for the assault.

Election Allegations

Republican opponents accused Waddell's 1876 reelection effort of fraud, intimidation, and corruption. These were partisan allegations and are best treated as allegations rather than settled judicial findings.

Defeat in 1878

Waddell lost his congressional seat in 1878 to Daniel L. Russell.

Law, Journalism, and Writing

After leaving Congress he resumed law, edited newspapers, delivered political and ceremonial speeches, and increasingly devoted himself to writing and history.

Confederate Memorial Activity

Waddell became a prominent speaker at Confederate commemorations and veteran events.

1895 Confederate Monument Address

At North Carolina's Confederate monument dedication in Raleigh in 1895, Waddell publicly defended the Confederate cause.

Lost Cause Memory

His commemorative work contributed to the Lost Cause tradition, which emphasized Confederate valor and constitutional arguments while minimizing slavery's central role in secession.

Fusion Politics

During the 1890s Republicans and Populists formed a Fusion coalition that successfully challenged Democratic control in North Carolina.

Black Political Power in Wilmington

Wilmington had a large and politically active African American population. Black residents owned businesses, held skilled jobs, worked in public employment, participated in civic life, and supported Republican-Fusion politics.

The 1898 White-Supremacy Campaign

North Carolina Democrats made white supremacy the central theme of the 1898 campaign, combining speeches, newspapers, racist imagery, sexual propaganda, Red Shirt intimidation, and election manipulation.

Furnifold Simmons

State Democratic chairman Furnifold M. Simmons organized the statewide campaign and deployed prominent speakers, including Waddell.

Waddell's Return to Political Prominence

Waddell's oratorical skill returned him to the center of public politics after nearly two decades outside elected national office.

Thalian Hall Speech

In October 1898 Waddell delivered one of the campaign's most inflammatory speeches at Thalian Hall in Wilmington.

Absolute White Supremacy

He explicitly defended the absolute supremacy of white people and portrayed Black political power as illegitimate.

Threat of Mass Violence

Waddell threatened extreme violence if necessary to restore white control, invoking the possibility of filling the Cape Fear with bodies.

The threat is central to evaluating his later leadership because it preceded the actual armed political violence of November.

Alex Manly

Alexander Manly was editor of the Black-owned Daily Record, one of the most important independent Black newspapers in North Carolina.

The Daily Record Editorial

Manly published an editorial challenging the white-supremacist claim that interracial sexual relationships resulted only from Black male assaults on white women. Democrats republished it statewide to inflame racial anger.

Election Day, November 8, 1898

Democrats swept the election amid documented intimidation, threats, and tampering with election returns in New Hanover County and elsewhere.

The Municipal Government Remained in Office

The election did not itself replace Wilmington's municipal government. The later removal of the mayor, aldermen, and police chief therefore was not a normal implementation of the election result.

November 9 Mass Meeting

White Democrats held a mass meeting on November 9. Waddell was selected to chair the gathering.

Wilmington Declaration of Independence

Waddell read the so-called “Wilmington Declaration of Independence,” which demanded major political and social concessions from the city's Black community.

Committee of Twenty-Five

He appointed a Committee of Twenty-Five to carry out the white leaders' demands.

Committee of Colored Citizens

Prominent Black Wilmingtonians were summoned and presented with the demands as an ultimatum rather than a negotiation.

Morning of November 10

On November 10 hundreds of armed white men assembled at the Wilmington Light Infantry armory.

Leadership of the Armed March

After other possible leaders declined, Waddell led the armed march to the Daily Record office.

Burning of the Daily Record

The white mob invaded and burned the building housing the Daily Record, destroying a central Black political and journalistic institution.

Expansion of Violence

Racial violence then spread through Wilmington as armed whites attacked Black residents and neighborhoods.

Black Deaths

The exact death toll remains uncertain. North Carolina's official historical account states that the 2006 state report settled on “as many as sixty,” with all reported victims Black.

Mass Flight

Large numbers of African Americans fled, hid outside the city, or permanently left Wilmington.

Economic Destruction

The coup damaged Black property ownership, businesses, employment networks, political officeholding, and accumulated wealth far beyond the immediate deaths.

Forced Resignations

Mayor Silas P. Wright, aldermen, the police chief, and other officials were forced to resign under armed political pressure.

Installation as Mayor

The new white-controlled municipal authorities selected Waddell as mayor on November 10.

A Coup d'État

North Carolina's Department of Natural and Cultural Resources now describes the event as a white-supremacist massacre and coup d'état, not merely a race riot.

Was Waddell the Sole Planner?

No. The coup involved a broad network of Democratic politicians, businessmen, newspaper leaders, militia members, Red Shirts, and secret organizers. NCpedia also notes evidence disputing claims that Waddell personally planned every stage.

Waddell's Central Responsibility

Whatever the planning chronology, Waddell publicly threatened violence, chaired the November 9 meeting, appointed the Committee of Twenty-Five, led the armed march, participated in removal of the government, and accepted the mayoralty created by the overthrow.

Attempt to Restore Order

Once in power, Waddell attempted to halt uncontrolled violence and restore public order.

Guarding the Jail

Waddell and other leaders guarded the Wilmington jail when armed whites threatened to lynch Black prisoners.

Proclamations for Peace

He issued proclamations promising protection and declaring that law would be enforced against disorder.

Banishment

At the same time, prominent Black and white political opponents were expelled or pressured to leave Wilmington. Some Black men protected from lynching were subsequently escorted out of the city under armed guard.

Protection After Revolution

His efforts to stop additional killing were real but limited. Waddell sought order after participating in the violent destruction of the existing political order.

Mayor After the Coup

Waddell won election to a regular mayoral term in 1899 and remained a major figure in city government for several years.

Black Political Displacement

The new regime sharply reduced African American participation in municipal authority and public employment.

Demographic Consequences

The coup accelerated the decline of Wilmington's Black population and permanently altered the city's political and economic balance.

Statewide Disfranchisement

The 1898 Democratic victory helped produce statewide voting restrictions, including literacy requirements and a grandfather clause designed to exclude most Black voters while protecting many white voters.

Attack on the Fifteenth Amendment

In 1899 Waddell described the Fifteenth Amendment's protection of Black voting rights as a grave political error and favored repeal or modification.

Contrast with 1865

This was a substantial departure from the formally race-neutral qualification he had advocated in his 1865 address to Black Wilmingtonians.

Later Mayoral Career

Waddell lost the mayoralty in 1903 but returned to office after reelection in 1905.

Author and Historian

His later publications included Some Memories of My Life (1908) and A History of New Hanover County and the Lower Cape Fear Region (1909).

Self-Representation of 1898

Waddell's later accounts described the Wilmington upheaval through the perspective of its white Democratic victors and contributed to the long tradition of calling it a revolution or riot rather than a coup.

Official Reassessment

North Carolina's state-authorized 1898 commission and later scholarship reconstructed the event as a coordinated white-supremacist political campaign culminating in massacre and overthrow of local government.

Death

Waddell died suddenly in Wilmington on March 17, 1912, reportedly after an attack of angina pectoris.

Burial

He was buried in Oakdale Cemetery in Wilmington.

Was Waddell Always a Secessionist?

No. In 1860 he was a Constitutional Unionist and Unionist newspaper editor. He changed course after the outbreak of the Civil War.

Was Waddell Always Opposed to Black Voting?

No. In 1865 he publicly supported a formally race-neutral property or educational qualification. By 1898-1899 he advocated absolute white supremacy and attacked the Fifteenth Amendment.

Did Waddell Personally Kill Black Residents?

The strongest institutional sources establish Waddell's leadership of the armed movement but do not establish that he personally shot individual Black victims.

Did Waddell Try to Stop Violence?

Yes. After the coup was underway he took steps to stop additional mob violence, including guarding Black prisoners from lynching. Those actions do not erase his leadership in creating the armed political crisis.

Historical Significance

Waddell's life demonstrates how a respected lawyer, congressman, author, Confederate veteran, and civic speaker could become a leader of an antidemocratic racial coup.

His political evolution also demonstrates that disfranchising white supremacy after Reconstruction was actively constructed by politicians who adapted to new constitutional conditions rather than merely preserving an unchanged antebellum order.

Impact on African Americans

Waddell's most consequential impact on African Americans came in 1898. His rhetoric and leadership contributed to the destruction of Black political participation, killing and terrorization of Black residents, destruction of a Black newspaper, forced migration, loss of public employment, and collapse of a thriving multiracial civic order.

A Balanced Assessment

Waddell was an educated lawyer, accomplished orator, experienced congressman, postal-policy legislator, author, historian, and municipal administrator. He opposed secession before the Civil War, investigated southern political violence while in Congress, and after taking power in 1898 acted to prevent some further mob killings.

Those facts coexist with the defining record of his later career: he became an explicit advocate of absolute white supremacy, threatened mass racial violence, chaired coercive demands against Wilmington's Black leadership, led the armed destruction of the Daily Record, participated in overthrowing an elected government, accepted office from the coup regime, and later attacked Black voting rights.

Legacy

For much of the twentieth century Waddell could be remembered locally as a Confederate veteran, congressman, mayor, historian, and civic leader while the violence of 1898 was minimized.

Modern scholarship and official North Carolina historical work place the coup at the center of his legacy. His biography is essential to understanding how post-Reconstruction white supremacy was enforced through propaganda, organized intimidation, massacre, forced exile, and direct seizure of government.

Geographic Scope

Hillsborough was Waddell's birthplace, Chapel Hill the site of his university education, and Wilmington the center of his legal, political, journalistic, and mayoral career.

Washington, D.C. was the center of his congressional service, while Charleston Harbor helped trigger his shift from Unionism to Confederate allegiance.

Legal and Constitutional Context

Waddell's career crossed secession, Confederate rebellion, Section Three of the Fourteenth Amendment, Reconstruction enforcement law, Black male suffrage, the Fifteenth Amendment, election violence, and later literacy tests and grandfather clauses.

His 1898 role is constitutionally significant because elected municipal government was displaced through armed coercion rather than ordinary electoral succession.
