Coleman Blease

South Carolina white-working-class populist, governor, prolific pardoning executive, segregationist, lynching defender, and U.S. senator

Overview

Coleman Livingston “Cole” Blease (1868-1942) was a South Carolina lawyer, legislator, mayor, two-term governor, United States senator, working-class populist, and one of the state's most openly white-supremacist political leaders of the early twentieth century.

Blease built his strongest following among poorer white farmers, textile-mill workers, and other voters who believed the older Democratic establishment treated them with contempt. As governor he supported common schools, a state tuberculosis sanatorium, public acquisition of the Medical College of Charleston, taxation of hydroelectric companies, and improved prison conditions. He also used the pardon power on an extraordinary scale, releasing or reducing punishment for roughly two thousand prisoners, including many poor and African American defendants whom he believed had been treated too harshly.

Those populist and humanitarian elements coexisted with virulent racial politics. Blease defended white supremacy, attacked Black education and officeholding, endorsed lynching rhetoric, removed Black educator Thomas E. Miller from the presidency of South Carolina State, and later carried similar attitudes into the U.S. Senate. His Senate career also included a major role in the 1929 law that first criminalized unauthorized entry into the United States, making his legacy important in both Jim Crow history and the history of federal immigration enforcement.

Family Background

Blease was born near Newberry to Henry Horatio Blease and Mary Ann Livingston Blease.

His family belonged to the white South Carolina community that experienced Reconstruction, Democratic Redemption, and the political transformation associated with Benjamin Tillman's rise.

Childhood in Newberry County

Newberry County remained the center of Blease's personal and political identity throughout his life.

He developed a conversational and highly social style that later translated effectively into courthouse, stump, and mill-town campaigning.

Education

Blease attended local schools, Newberry College, the University of South Carolina, and Georgetown University in Washington, D.C.

Georgetown Law Degree

He graduated from Georgetown University's law department in 1889.

Admission to the Bar

Blease was admitted to the South Carolina bar in 1889 and returned to Newberry to practice law with his brother.

Legal Practice

Criminal law became an important part of his practice.

His courtroom experience helped shape the combative speaking style and sympathy for defendants that later influenced his extensive use of gubernatorial clemency.

First Marriage

Blease married Lillie B. Summers. She remained his wife through most of his political career and died in 1934.

Second Marriage

He later married Caroline Floyd Hoyt. South Carolina State Library materials report that the marriage ended after roughly a year.

Neither marriage produced children.

Political South Carolina After Reconstruction

Blease entered politics after white Democrats had already regained state control and after the political power of Black Republicans had been sharply reduced.

The Tillman Era

Benjamin Tillman's rise in 1890 transformed white Democratic politics by challenging the old Hampton-aligned conservative establishment and mobilizing rural white farmers.

Blease as a Tillman Protégé

Blease initially belonged to the political generation shaped by Tillman's insurgency.

He adopted the language of class resentment and direct appeal to white farmers, but later built an independent following that Tillman increasingly distrusted.

Election to the South Carolina House

Blease entered the South Carolina House of Representatives in 1890.

Speaker Pro Tempore

During his early House service he rose to speaker pro tempore.

Loss of Legislative Office

Blease did not advance in a straight line. He lost his legislative position in the mid-1890s and failed in an attempt to return immediately.

Mayor of Helena

In 1897 Blease served as mayor of Helena, a small municipality near Newberry.

Return to the House

He returned to the South Carolina House for the 1899-1900 period.

City Attorney of Newberry

Blease served as city attorney of Newberry in the early twentieth century.

South Carolina Senate

He later entered the South Carolina Senate and served from 1905 through 1909.

President Pro Tempore

Blease served as president pro tempore of the state Senate during part of his tenure.

Early Campaigns for Governor

Blease made unsuccessful attempts to win the Democratic gubernatorial nomination before finally prevailing in 1910.

The One-Party Democratic System

By this period Black disfranchisement had made the Democratic primary the decisive election in most statewide contests.

Political competition remained vigorous among white Democrats even as African American electoral participation had been drastically reduced.

South Carolina Constitution of 1895

The 1895 state constitution used literacy rules, registration mechanisms, and other devices to suppress Black voting.

Blease rose to statewide power inside the political order created by that disfranchisement.

Working-Class White Base

Blease built an unusually strong following among white textile-mill workers and poorer Upcountry voters.

Mill Villages

Rapid textile industrialization had created mill communities whose white workers often felt socially inferior to established professionals, planters, and businessmen.

Blease presented himself as their defender.

Class Resentment

He attacked what supporters perceived as aristocratic contempt toward working whites.

His populism promised greater dignity inside the white electorate while leaving racial hierarchy intact.

White Equality Versus Racial Equality

Blease demanded social and political respect for poorer whites but emphatically rejected equality between Black and white South Carolinians.

This distinction was fundamental to his political coalition.

Race as Political Mobilization

Blease repeatedly used racial resentment to strengthen solidarity among economically divided white voters.

Mayor of Newberry

In 1910 Blease became mayor of Newberry shortly before his successful campaign for governor.

The 1910 Governor's Race

The Democratic gubernatorial contest placed Blease against several candidates, with Claudius C. Featherstone becoming his principal runoff opponent.

Runoff Victory

Blease won the Democratic runoff in September 1910 and faced no meaningful opposition in the general election.

Election as Governor

He entered the governor's office in January 1911.

Populist Governorship

Blease's administration was characterized by aggressive personal use of executive power, frequent conflict with state institutions, extensive vetoes, and constant direct communication with his political base.

The 'Little Man'

Supporters regarded Blease as a champion of people whom lawyers, judges, newspaper editors, college officials, and businessmen supposedly ignored or mocked.

Hostility from Conservative Elites

Many professional and business leaders considered Blease crude, reckless, and unfit for high office.

Elite denunciation often strengthened his appeal among supporters who already believed respectable society despised them.

The Blease Machine

Patronage, personal loyalty, local courthouse networks, mill communities, and direct campaigning created a political organization centered more heavily on Blease himself than on a coherent ideological program.

Reelection in 1912

Blease won reelection in 1912 despite intense opposition from much of the state's conservative press and from Benjamin Tillman.

Benjamin Tillman's Opposition

Tillman had once represented the insurgent challenge to aristocratic rule, but he came to regard Blease's methods as degrading even by the rough standards of South Carolina politics.

From Tillmanism to Bleaseism

Blease inherited Tillman's white-populist political structure while pushing its anti-elite, personal, and inflammatory style further.

Public Schools

Blease supported increased provision for common schools.

The National Governors Association and South Carolina State Library both identify education among the constructive areas of his administration.

White School Politics

His strongest educational appeals were directed toward white working-class families who wanted better access to schooling.

Black Education

Blease sharply opposed educational advancement that he believed would create independent Black professionals or challenge white supremacy.

Thomas E. Miller

Thomas Ezekiel Miller was a former Black congressman and the first president of the Colored Normal, Industrial, Agricultural and Mechanical College of South Carolina, later South Carolina State University.

Removal of Thomas E. Miller

The official U.S. House biography of Miller records that Blease forced him to resign from the college presidency in 1911 after Miller had opposed Blease in the 1910 campaign.

Political Retaliation and Black Higher Education

Miller's removal combined personal political retaliation with Blease's hostility toward independent Black educational leadership.

South Carolina State University Context

The institution had been created as a separate public college for African Americans during the Jim Crow era.

Even within segregation, control over Black institutions remained politically contested.

Medical College of Charleston

During Blease's governorship the Medical College of Charleston became a state institution.

Public Health

Blease supported expansion of public-health institutions.

Tuberculosis Sanatorium

South Carolina established a state tuberculosis sanatorium during the Blease era.

Tuberculosis was a major public-health threat, particularly in poor communities with crowded housing and limited medical access.

Hydroelectric Tax

His administration supported a special tax on hydroelectric companies.

The measure fit his broader willingness to make powerful economic interests contribute more heavily to state revenue.

Corporate Power

Blease's economic rhetoric often targeted large corporations and institutions seen as distant from ordinary workers.

Labor Politics

He presented himself as sympathetic to working people and supported some labor-oriented measures.

Limits of Labor Reform

His labor record was inconsistent. Blease could champion workers rhetorically while opposing regulatory measures that he believed intruded too far into private decisions or industrial management.

Penitentiary Hosiery Mill

Blease supported abolition of the state penitentiary hosiery mill because of unhealthy working conditions.

Prison Conditions

His concern with prisons became most visible through his extraordinary use of pardons and paroles.

The Pardoning Governor

Blease became nationally famous for exercising executive clemency far more frequently than his predecessors.

Scale of Pardons and Paroles

The National Governors Association estimates that he granted pardons or paroles to roughly two thousand prisoners during his governorship.

Other historical summaries give somewhat lower counts because they classify pardons, paroles, commutations, and restorations differently.

Why Blease Pardoned Prisoners

Blease argued that courts, prosecutors, and prison systems often dealt too harshly with poor defendants.

He portrayed clemency as a democratic check on legal institutions dominated by social elites.

Poor Prisoners

Many beneficiaries lacked wealth, influence, or the ability to hire prominent attorneys.

This feature helped make clemency popular among voters suspicious of the justice system.

African American Prisoners

Historical scholarship on Blease's pardon policy emphasizes that many Black prisoners also benefited.

This is an important complication in the record of an otherwise extreme white supremacist.

Racism and Clemency

Blease's clemency toward individual Black defendants did not signal support for racial equality.

He could view a particular prisoner as unfairly punished while continuing to insist on collective white political supremacy.

George Washington Murray

Former Black congressman George Washington Murray had been convicted in a disputed South Carolina prosecution and fled the state.

Blease later pardoned Murray, another example of the tension between his racial ideology and willingness to use clemency for individual Black defendants.

Criticism of the Pardon Policy

Judges, prosecutors, newspaper editors, and political opponents accused Blease of undermining the criminal-justice system by releasing dangerous offenders.

Blease's Defense of Clemency

Blease answered that executive pardon power existed precisely because courts could make mistakes and punishment could become excessive.

Law-and-Order Paradox

His governorship combined extraordinary mercy toward convicted prisoners with public tolerance of extralegal racial violence.

This is one of the sharpest contradictions in his political record.

Lynching

Blease became notorious for rhetoric defending lynching.

He treated mob killing as legitimate in cases where white communities claimed a Black man had sexually assaulted a white woman.

Sexualized Racial Propaganda

His lynching rhetoric drew on the familiar Jim Crow image of Black men as inherent threats to white women.

Such claims frequently served to justify racial terror regardless of whether accusations had been tested in court.

Promise of Clemency to Lynchers

Blease publicly indicated that he would use the pardon power to protect white men convicted for participating in lynching under circumstances he approved.

Executive Duty and Mob Violence

By legitimizing mob action, Blease undermined the same legal system whose harshness toward prisoners he criticized.

Racial Terror

Lynching functioned not merely as punishment of accused individuals but as collective intimidation of Black communities.

Black Citizenship

Blease rejected the idea that African Americans should exercise social or political equality with whites.

Segregation

He defended the Jim Crow system of racial separation.

Chain Gangs

Blease supported racial separation within penal labor systems and the broader segregation of public institutions.

White Supremacy as Governing Principle

His racial position was not merely coded in states' rights language. Blease spoke openly about white supremacy as a legitimate political objective.

The 1912 Campaign and Race

Racial rhetoric remained an important part of Blease's successful reelection campaign.

Conflict with Newspapers

Blease had an intensely hostile relationship with many South Carolina newspapers.

He accused editors of representing corporate and aristocratic interests; they portrayed him as a dangerous demagogue.

Press and Populism

The conflict helped Blease portray criticism as proof that the political establishment feared the voters who supported him.

Conflict with the Judiciary

Blease repeatedly clashed with courts and judges over pardons, executive authority, and political power.

Conflict with State Officials

He also fought with other state officials and frequently ignored requests from legislators who expected conventional patronage influence.

Extensive Vetoes

Blease used the gubernatorial veto more aggressively than earlier South Carolina governors.

The National Governors Association describes him as having vetoed more legislation than any of his predecessors.

Efficiency and Economy

Despite his chaotic political reputation, historical summaries credit parts of his administration with improving governmental efficiency and attempting to restrain waste and extravagance.

Militia Controversy

Near the end of his governorship Blease entered a major dispute with federal authorities concerning the condition and efficiency of the South Carolina militia.

Disbanding the Militia

One week before leaving office he disbanded the South Carolina militia rather than accept federal pressure over its condition.

Resignation Before Term End

Blease resigned several days before his second term formally expired in January 1915.

Charles Aurelius Smith

Lieutenant Governor Charles Aurelius Smith briefly succeeded him as governor.

Why the Early Resignation Mattered

The resignation was characteristic of Blease's highly personal approach to executive authority and his preference for dramatic confrontation over ceremonial political convention.

After the Governorship

Blease remained an active candidate and Democratic factional leader for many years after leaving the governor's office.

1914 Senate Campaign

He sought a U.S. Senate seat while still governor but failed to win the Democratic nomination.

Later Governor Campaigns

Blease repeatedly attempted to return to the governorship during the years between his state and federal service.

Persistent Personal Following

Even after defeats, his base among poorer white voters remained large enough to make him a continuing force in state politics.

World War I Era

Blease remained active in Democratic politics during the First World War and the immediate postwar period.

Postwar Race Politics

The postwar era brought renewed Black demands for voting rights, racial violence across the United States, and the revival of the Ku Klux Klan.

Blease continued to defend the racial order of the Jim Crow South.

The Second Ku Klux Klan Context

The revived Klan became politically important during the 1920s, combining white supremacy with anti-Catholic, anti-Jewish, and anti-immigrant politics.

The 1924 Senate Race

Blease sought the Senate seat held by Nathaniel B. Dial in 1924.

James F. Byrnes

James F. Byrnes emerged as one of Blease's principal rivals in the Democratic primary.

Religious Prejudice in the Campaign

The 1924 campaign unfolded during a period of intense anti-Catholic politics.

Rumors concerning Byrnes's Catholic upbringing damaged him among some Protestant voters and became part of the political environment from which Blease benefited.

Election to the U.S. Senate

Blease won election to the United States Senate and began service on March 4, 1925.

Senate Term

He served one six-year term, through March 3, 1931.

From State Demagogue to National Senator

Senate service brought Blease's South Carolina political style onto the national stage.

Race in the Senate

Blease continued openly racist rhetoric while serving in Washington.

Attack on Black Education

He publicly argued that white citizens should not finance advanced education for African Americans and denied the value of Black professional education.

Interracial Relationships

Blease proposed federal measures intended to reinforce racial separation and stigmatize interracial relationships.

The White House Poem Incident

In 1929 Blease introduced and read on the Senate floor a racist poem attacking racial inclusion at the White House.

Other senators objected, and the offensive material was excluded from the printed Congressional Record.

Senate Objections

Republican senators including Hiram Bingham protested that the material was indecent and insulting to Black citizens and to American constitutional principles.

Blease's Response

Blease withdrew the immediate resolution but did not repudiate the racial beliefs behind it.

Immigration Politics

The 1920s were also an era of intense nativism and restrictive immigration policy.

National Origins Quotas

The Immigration Act of 1924 sharply restricted immigration from much of Europe and excluded immigration from Asia through a racially structured quota system.

Western Hemisphere Exception

Immigration from Mexico and much of the Western Hemisphere was not subjected to the same quota structure because agricultural and industrial employers depended heavily on Mexican labor.

Pressure to Restrict Mexican Immigration

Nativist lawmakers nevertheless sought methods to restrict Mexican migration without imposing the same numerical quota system.

Undesirable Aliens Act of 1929

Blease played an important role in the legislation commonly known as the Undesirable Aliens Act of 1929 or Blease's Law.

Criminalizing Unauthorized Entry

The law made unlawful entry into the United States a federal misdemeanor and made certain forms of unlawful reentry after deportation a felony.

Legislative Compromise

Blease helped broker a compromise among restrictionists and economic interests that wanted continued access to Mexican labor.

Racial Context of the 1929 Law

Modern legal and historical scholarship has documented that the legislative debate surrounding the 1929 law contained explicit racial and eugenic arguments directed particularly at Mexican migration.

Blease and Immigration Race Politics

Blease's established record of white supremacy does not by itself prove the motive of every legislator who supported the act.

It does, however, form part of the broader racial and nativist context in which he helped advance the legislation.

Modern Sections 1325 and 1326

Federal illegal-entry and illegal-reentry provisions were later reenacted and reorganized in the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952 as 8 U.S.C. §§ 1325 and 1326.

The modern statutes therefore have a legislative lineage extending back to the 1929 act, although later Congresses amended and reenacted them.

Why the Immigration Legacy Matters

Blease's influence thus extends beyond South Carolina history into a long-running national debate over criminal immigration enforcement.

Senate Record Beyond Race

Blease also participated in ordinary legislative work involving public lands, pensions, immigration administration, and regional concerns.

Limited National Influence

He never achieved the committee seniority or national legislative power later accumulated by figures such as James F. Byrnes or Strom Thurmond.

1930 Reelection Defeat

Blease failed to win renomination in 1930.

James F. Byrnes Wins

James F. Byrnes defeated him and began a political career that would eventually include service in the Senate, Supreme Court, State Department, and South Carolina governorship.

Political Generational Change

Blease's defeat signaled the declining effectiveness of the older style of highly personalized white-populist politics at the national level.

1932 Senate Campaign

Blease attempted another Senate comeback against Ellison D. Smith in 1932 but lost.

1934 Governor Campaign

He again sought the Democratic gubernatorial nomination in 1934 without success.

1938 Governor Campaign

Blease made another unsuccessful gubernatorial campaign in 1938.

Continued Democratic Activity

Despite repeated defeats, he remained active in the South Carolina Democratic Party.

State Democratic Convention

Blease served as president of the state Democratic convention in 1926.

Democratic National Convention

He later served as a delegate to the Democratic National Convention in 1936.

Unemployment Compensation Commission

In 1941 the South Carolina legislature elected Blease to the state unemployment compensation commission.

Late-Life Public Service

The appointment demonstrated that his political influence had not disappeared completely even a decade after leaving the Senate.

Death

Coleman Livingston Blease died in Columbia on January 19, 1942.

He was seventy-three years old.

Burial

Blease was buried in Rosemont Cemetery in Newberry.

Was Blease Simply Benjamin Tillman's Successor?

He inherited Tillman's white-populist political tradition but developed a distinct movement.

Tillman eventually considered Blease's style too reckless and opposed his reelection.

Was Blease a Labor Progressive?

Only partly. He championed working-class white voters and supported selected reforms, but his record on workplace regulation was inconsistent.

Did Blease Support Public Education?

Yes. His administration supported common-school funding and other educational measures.

His support was racially bounded: he opposed advanced Black educational development and removed Thomas E. Miller from leadership of South Carolina's Black public college.

Did Blease Support Public Health?

Yes. His administration is associated with a state tuberculosis sanatorium and the incorporation of the Medical College of Charleston into state government.

Did Blease Pardon Black Prisoners?

Yes. His extraordinary clemency program included many African American prisoners.

Individual mercy did not translate into support for collective Black civil or political equality.

Did Blease Pardon Nearly Two Thousand People?

Historical sources differ according to whether they count pardons alone or combine pardons, paroles, commutations, and related acts of clemency.

The National Governors Association summarizes the total as roughly two thousand pardons or paroles.

Was Blease Pro-Lynching?

Yes. He publicly defended lynching in racially charged circumstances and indicated willingness to pardon white participants.

Was Blease Anti-Black in Every Individual Case?

No. His pardons and occasional interventions for individual Black defendants show that personal legal sympathy could coexist with a deeply racist political ideology.

Did Blease Remove Thomas E. Miller?

Yes. The official U.S. House biography of Miller states that Blease forced the former Black congressman to resign as president of the state Black college in 1911.

Did Blease Personally Create Modern Section 1325?

Not by himself. Blease was an important sponsor and broker of the 1929 act that criminalized unauthorized entry, but modern 8 U.S.C. § 1325 was enacted within the later Immigration and Nationality Act framework.

It is therefore most accurate to describe the 1929 law as the legislative ancestor of the modern offense.

Was the 1929 Immigration Law Only About Race?

The law also reflected border administration, labor-market concerns, enforcement policy, and conflict between restrictionists and employers.

Racial and eugenic arguments were nevertheless documented components of the legislative environment surrounding its passage.

Comparison with Benjamin Tillman

Tillman created the mass white-farmer insurgency that displaced South Carolina's old planter establishment. Blease appealed to a later constituency that included textile-mill workers and other whites who believed Tillman's movement had not delivered enough social equality among whites.

Both defended white supremacy, but Blease used a more personal, theatrical, and erratic governing style.

Comparison with Wade Hampton III

Hampton represented aristocratic paternalism and Confederate military prestige. Blease represented the revolt of poorer whites against that older political culture.

The transition changed the class composition of white political leadership without dismantling white supremacy.

Comparison with Theodore Bilbo

Blease and Mississippi's Theodore Bilbo both fused economic populism with extreme racial politics, cultivated poor white constituencies, used combative personal rhetoric, and treated elite hostility as proof of authenticity.

Comparison with James K. Vardaman

Vardaman and Blease belonged to the same southern tradition of anti-corporate white populism.

Vardaman attacked Black voting and education in Mississippi; Blease used similar rhetoric in South Carolina while developing an unusually expansive clemency policy.

Comparison with Thomas E. Miller

Miller represented the Black political and educational leadership that emerged from Reconstruction. Blease represented a later white-populist order in which poorer whites demanded greater political respect while Black institutions remained subject to white political control.

Comparison with James F. Byrnes

Byrnes represented a more disciplined, institutionally sophisticated form of South Carolina Democratic politics.

His defeat of Blease in 1930 marked a shift away from Blease's highly personal stump-demagoguery toward a politician capable of building extensive national power.

Historical Significance

Blease is historically significant because he demonstrates how class insurgency and racial hierarchy could reinforce one another. He widened the sense of political belonging among many working-class whites while defining African Americans as outside the boundaries of equal citizenship.

His career also reveals a less predictable side of southern populism: a governor capable of vicious racist rhetoric could simultaneously distrust courts, pardon large numbers of Black prisoners, expand health institutions, tax powerful companies, and claim the criminal-justice system treated poor people unfairly.

Impact on African Americans

Blease's overall political impact on African Americans was strongly negative. He defended segregation and white supremacy, legitimated lynching, attacked Black education, and used executive authority against Thomas E. Miller and Black institutional autonomy.

His clemency record complicates but does not reverse that judgment. Many Black prisoners benefited from his belief that courts imposed excessive punishment, showing that racial domination and individual acts of mercy could operate simultaneously within Jim Crow government.

A Balanced Assessment

Blease was an effective lawyer, charismatic representative of working-class white voters, supporter of common schools and public health, critic of waste, advocate of selected taxes on powerful corporations, and unusually energetic user of executive clemency. His willingness to pardon poor and Black prisoners reflected a genuine distrust of harsh criminal punishment.

Those achievements coexist with an unmistakable record of racial extremism. He defended lynching, opposed Black educational advancement and political equality, removed a major Black educator from office, used racial prejudice as an electoral tool, and carried openly racist rhetoric into the United States Senate. His role in the 1929 immigration law further extends his significance into the history of racialized and nativist federal policy.

Legacy

For supporters, Blease was remembered as a fearless champion of the “little man” who was willing to fight judges, editors, colleges, corporations, legislators, and established politicians. His repeated electoral comebacks demonstrate that this image had genuine appeal.

Modern historical interpretation places the costs of that politics alongside its populist achievements. Blease broadened political dignity for some poor whites while reinforcing a racial order that denied equal citizenship to Black South Carolinians. His career is therefore a strong example of why populism and democracy are not synonymous: a movement can challenge elite power while defining political equality in explicitly racial terms.

Geographic Scope

Newberry County was Blease's birthplace, legal base, and central political stronghold. Columbia was the center of his gubernatorial administration and the site of his death.

Orangeburg and the state's Black higher-education institutions became important to the conflict involving Thomas E. Miller. Charleston figured in his public-health and medical-college legacy.

Washington, D.C. was the center of his Senate career and the 1929 immigration legislation that became part of his national legacy.

Legal and Constitutional Context

Blease's career intersected with South Carolina's 1895 disfranchisement constitution, Jim Crow segregation, gubernatorial clemency power, lynching and failures of criminal prosecution, federal-state militia relations, congressional immigration power, and the emerging criminalization of unauthorized border entry.

His clemency policy and racial politics reveal an unusual constitutional contradiction: he used formal executive power aggressively to mitigate criminal punishment while simultaneously legitimating extrajudicial racial violence outside the courts.
