Earnest Sevier Cox

Virginia white-supremacist writer, cofounder of the Anglo-Saxon Clubs of America, architect of racial-integrity legislation, and lifelong advocate of removing Black Americans to Africa

Overview

Earnest Sevier Cox (1880-1966) was a Tennessee-born writer, lecturer, former preacher, real-estate salesman, Army officer, white supremacist, and one of Virginia's most persistent advocates of racial separation during the first half of the twentieth century. After studying theology and sociology and travelling for five years through Africa, Asia, Australia, New Zealand, Central America, and South America, Cox reinvented himself as a self-described expert on the world's “color problem.” He concluded that Black and white people could not coexist permanently and spent the remainder of his life trying to build laws and political movements around that belief.

In Richmond, Cox joined pianist and composer John Powell in founding the Anglo-Saxon Clubs of America in 1922. The organization used eugenics and “racial integrity” rhetoric to demand strict racial boundaries. Cox and Powell helped persuade the Virginia General Assembly to enact the Racial Integrity Act of 1924, one of the country's most restrictive anti-miscegenation laws, and the 1926 Massenburg Bill requiring racial separation in places of public assembly. Cox's 1923 book White America supplied the broader ideology: he argued that interracial mixing destroyed white civilization and that segregation could never be secure until Black Americans were removed from the United States and resettled in Africa.

Cox pursued that “repatriation” program for more than forty years. He entered a strategic alliance with Marcus Garvey in 1924, later worked with the Black-led Peace Movement of Ethiopia, advised Mississippi senator Theodore Bilbo during the Greater Liberia Bill campaign, and testified for later repatriation proposals sponsored by Senator William Langer. The cooperation crossed racial and ideological lines but did not make Cox an advocate of Black equality; his goal remained the creation of a permanently white United States. Financiers including Wickliffe Draper supported his publications and lobbying, and after Brown v. Board of Education Cox returned to open defense of segregation. He died in Richmond from complications of emphysema on April 26, 1966.

Early Life and Education

Cox was born near Knoxville in Blount County, Tennessee, on January 24, 1880. His parents were Ann Maria Earnest Cox Cox and Samuel Thompson Cox, a farmer who sometimes preached in the Methodist tradition.

His father died when Cox was about twelve.

Cox attended private schools and received a bachelor of science degree in 1899 from Roane College, an unaccredited Tennessee institution.

His later publicity often encouraged the impression of a broadly educated expert, but his academic path was irregular and he never completed the advanced degrees he pursued.

Before settling on a career, Cox worked as a schoolteacher, loan officer, and newspaper reporter. He also took a business course and briefly considered studying law.

The pattern was restless. So was his education.

In 1902 Cox entered the Moody Bible Institute in Chicago, where he trained as a public speaker and street preacher.

Public speaking would become one of his most useful political skills long after theology ceased to be his primary vocation.

Cox moved to Nashville in 1903 and continued theological studies at Vanderbilt University. He remained for about three years but left without a degree.

Encyclopedia Virginia notes that his fundamentalist beliefs increasingly clashed with the faculty's teaching.

A chronic throat problem made it difficult for Cox to earn a living through preaching.

He changed direction rather than disappearing from public life.

University of Chicago and Racial Formation

In 1906 Cox entered graduate sociology coursework at the University of Chicago. The university credited his earlier study as equivalent to a bachelor's degree, but he again left without completing an advanced degree.

During these years, race became the subject that consumed the rest of his life.

Cox came to believe not merely that Black people were inferior—a prejudice common among many whites of his era—but that Black and white populations were fundamentally incapable of permanent coexistence.

That second proposition drove nearly everything that followed.

During the summers of 1907 through 1909, Cox travelled to cities including Chicago, Norfolk, and Pittsburgh to give lectures accompanying large cyclorama displays.

The work trained him to combine dramatic presentation, travel narrative, and sweeping historical claims.

Cox left Chicago in 1909 without a graduate degree.

He decided that travel could provide the authority academic credentials had not.

Five Years of International Travel

In February 1910 Cox sailed for Africa. Over roughly five years he travelled from Cape Town toward Cairo and later through Australia, New Zealand, Hong Kong, Singapore, the Philippines, Central America, and South America.

He worked along the way, including in South African mining districts.

The journey gave Cox a persona he used for decades: explorer, observer, ethnographer, and specialist in race relations.

Travel is not the same as scientific research. Cox nevertheless treated his personal observations of colonial societies as evidence for broad biological and political conclusions about entire peoples.

Cox studied how European colonial powers governed nonwhite populations and interpreted systems of racial domination as proof that strict racial separation preserved civilization.

He rarely treated colonial inequality as a problem to be overcome. He treated it as a model from which whites could learn.

Cox returned to the United States in 1915 and supported himself through lectures and newspaper writing about his travels.

By then the outlines of White America were forming.

World War I and the Army Reserve

Cox entered the United States Army in 1917 and reached the rank of captain. Encyclopedia Virginia records that a superior in France removed him from front-line service after concluding that his subordinates would not take orders from him.

He nevertheless received an honorable discharge in September 1919.

Cox remained in the reserves after the war, becoming a major in 1920 and lieutenant colonel in 1929.

Military rank became another credential he could attach to his public identity.

Richmond, John Powell, and Madison Grant

In January 1920 Cox moved to Richmond, Virginia, and began selling real estate.

The city became his permanent political base.

In Richmond, Cox met John Powell, a celebrated pianist and composer who shared his obsession with white racial purity.

Powell possessed cultural prestige. Cox possessed organizing energy and a ready-made racial doctrine.

Cox also established ties with Madison Grant, the New York eugenicist and author of The Passing of the Great Race.

Grant's Nordicist racial theories gave Cox a more elaborate vocabulary for arguments he had already begun developing from travel and sociology.

Cox did not remain a solitary pamphleteer.

He and Powell built an organization.

Founding the Anglo-Saxon Clubs of America

In September 1922 Cox and Powell founded the Anglo-Saxon Clubs of America in Richmond. The Library of Virginia describes the organization as committed to preserving an Anglo-Saxon racial and cultural order.

Membership was restricted to native-born white American men who met age, character, and voting qualifications.

The Anglo-Saxon Clubs did not present themselves as a secret terrorist order. Members came disproportionately from educated and socially prominent white Virginia circles.

That respectability was politically useful.

By 1923 Richmond newspapers reported more than four hundred members. By the middle of the decade, the organization claimed dozens of posts in Virginia and several in northern institutions and communities.

Cox had turned racial purity into a membership movement.

The clubs warned that interracial marriage and ancestry mixing would destroy what they called Anglo-Saxon civilization.

They treated race as inherited political destiny.

Walter Plecker and the Campaign for Racial Classification

Walter Ashby Plecker, Virginia's registrar of vital statistics, became the third major figure in the campaign. As a state official, Plecker possessed administrative power that Cox and Powell lacked.

The combination was formidable: activists outside government and a registrar inside it.

Cox, Powell, and Plecker used newspapers, public meetings, speeches, lobbying, and official statistics to persuade white Virginians that racial boundaries were being dangerously blurred.

The answer they proposed was law backed by state recordkeeping.

The Racial Integrity Act of 1924

Cox and Powell helped build the lobbying campaign that resulted in Virginia's Racial Integrity Act of 1924. The law required racial classification and prohibited marriage between people legally defined as white and people classified as nonwhite.

Encyclopedia Virginia describes it as probably the country's strictest anti-miscegenation statute of its time.

Virginia's final definition of whiteness included a narrow exception allowing small amounts of Native American ancestry, largely because elite white families claimed descent from Pocahontas.

The exception exposed the political construction behind supposedly scientific racial categories.

The Racial Integrity Act was built primarily to protect white supremacy and prevent Black-white marriage, but its classification machinery also caused severe harm to Virginia's Indigenous communities.

Native identities were frequently erased or recategorized in official records.

Cox believed private intimacy was too politically important to leave to individual choice.

Racial separation required control of marriage, ancestry, and reproduction.

The Massenburg Bill and Segregated Public Space

Two years later Cox and Powell successfully pressed for another measure, the Massenburg Bill, requiring racial separation in places of public assembly.

Their program had moved from marriage to public space.

White America

Cox self-published White America in 1923 after failing to find a conventional publisher.

The book made him locally famous.

Cox claimed that history showed white civilizations declining whenever whites mixed with nonwhite populations. He treated his travels as confirmation of a universal rule.

His conclusion was stark: whites could mix and disappear, or separate and survive.

Cox believed ordinary segregation could only postpone the outcome.

His permanent solution was removal.

Repatriation as Cox's End State

Cox argued that Black Americans should leave the United States for Africa with federal assistance. He consistently used terms such as “repatriation” or colonization rather than openly describing the proposal as expulsion.

The objective remained a United States without a substantial Black population.

Cox sometimes presented emigration as beneficial to Black people. But the premise of his program was that Black Americans did not belong permanently within American society on equal terms.

He wanted racial peace through racial removal.

The word “repatriation” suggested return to an ancestral homeland. Most Black Americans Cox wanted relocated had been born in the United States, as had their parents and grandparents.

Calling removal repatriation allowed him to describe exclusion as benevolence.

Let My People Go and Marcus Garvey

In 1925 Cox published Let My People Go, a short work advocating Black emigration.

He dedicated it to Marcus Garvey.

Garvey was a Jamaican-born Black nationalist and founder of the Universal Negro Improvement Association. His movement promoted Black pride, independent institutions, international African identity, and—among some followers—emigration to Africa.

That created an unusual point of tactical overlap with Cox.

Cox entered into a working relationship with Garvey in September 1924. Encyclopedia Virginia describes the alliance as rooted in their shared belief that Black emigration offered a solution to America's racial conflict.

The similarities should not erase the differences.

Garvey's separatism emerged from Black nationalism and the conviction that white America would not permit genuine equality. Cox's separatism emerged from white supremacy and the conviction that equality itself was undesirable.

They could agree on migration while disagreeing profoundly about who possessed moral and political authority.

The cooperation was more than a single photo opportunity. Cox wrote to Garveyites, promoted emigration legislation, and advocated Garvey's release while Garvey was imprisoned.

Yet Cox never became an advocate of Black civil equality in the United States.

The active partnership largely ended when federal authorities deported Garvey in 1927.

Cox continued looking for Black organizations willing to support federally financed emigration.

The Peace Movement of Ethiopia

Cox later worked with the Chicago-based Peace Movement of Ethiopia, a Black organization that promoted emigration to Africa.

The alliance gave him something white segregationists alone could not provide: Black witnesses willing to say publicly that they wanted federal assistance to leave the United States.

The Peace Movement of Ethiopia and Cox did not share the same view of Black humanity.

They shared a legislative objective.

In 1936 Cox successfully pressed the Virginia General Assembly to recommend federal assistance for Black emigration to Africa.

His idea had moved from private publication to formal state endorsement.

Theodore Bilbo and the Greater Liberia Bill

Mississippi senator Theodore G. Bilbo became the most notorious national politician associated with Cox's repatriation campaign.

Bilbo was an openly racist demagogue. Cox was the policy specialist.

Bilbo introduced legislation commonly known as the Greater Liberia Bill to provide federal support for Black migration to Liberia.

Encyclopedia Virginia notes that the bill unmistakably reflected the influence of White America.

Cox urged Bilbo to moderate his language when dealing with Black repatriation advocates. The advice was strategic, not evidence that Cox had abandoned white supremacy.

If Black organizations were necessary to pass the bill, insulting them publicly was counterproductive.

The campaign brought together white segregationists who wanted Black Americans gone and Black separatists who wanted an independent future beyond white domination.

That coalition was real. Its motives were not identical.

The proposal never became law. Congressional resistance, practical obstacles, foreign-policy complications, and the outbreak of World War II undermined the campaign.

Cox did not abandon the idea.

Wickliffe Draper and Financial Patronage

Wealthy eugenics patron Wickliffe Draper became one of the most important financial supporters of Cox's work.

Draper could provide what Cox rarely possessed in abundance: money.

A special 1937 edition of White America stated that a prominent citizen had financed copies for free distribution to legislators. Historical scholarship identifies Draper as the patron behind major reprint and lobbying support for Cox's repatriation campaign.

Ideas that had once struggled to find a publisher could now be mailed directly to lawmakers.

Cox produced the program and propaganda. Draper provided capital.

The relationship foreshadowed Draper's later use of the Pioneer Fund and private giving to support racial-hierarchy projects.

Lincoln's Negro Policy

Cox's 1938 Lincoln's Negro Policy attempted to enlist Abraham Lincoln in support of colonization and separation.

By doing so, Cox tried to detach repatriation from crude contemporary racism and give it the authority of a revered national figure.

William Langer and the Last Congressional Repatriation Campaign

After Bilbo's death, North Dakota senator William Langer introduced similar repatriation proposals.

Cox again became promoter, adviser, and congressional witness.

Encyclopedia Virginia records that Cox testified before congressional committees on repatriation at least twice during the Langer period.

He continued to present himself as an expert decades after his five-year journey had ended.

Langer's death in 1959 effectively ended the last serious congressional phase of Cox's long repatriation project.

Teutonic Unity and the Postwar Far Right

In 1951 Cox published Teutonic Unity, extending his racial nationalism beyond the Black-white divide in America toward a broader conception of Germanic or “Teutonic” racial solidarity.

The book placed him increasingly within the postwar international racialist right.

Cox's later career overlapped with younger segregationist intellectuals such as Carleton Putnam, who used race-and-intelligence arguments to resist integration.

Cox belonged to an older generation of racial theorists but remained useful to the post-Brown movement.

By the 1950s and 1960s, Cox's earlier writings circulated within newer white-supremacist and far-right networks.

His longevity made him a bridge between Progressive Era eugenics, interwar racial nationalism, Jim Crow segregation, and postwar white resistance.

Brown v. Board of Education and Unending Hate

The Supreme Court's 1954 ruling in Brown v. Board of Education struck directly at the legal segregation Cox had defended for decades.

He responded immediately.

Cox published Unending Hate in 1955 as an attack on the school-desegregation decision. Duke's archival guide describes the pamphlet as vilifying Brown and claiming that the ruling formed part of a federal effort to destroy white southern resistance.

At seventy-five, Cox was still fighting integration.

Cox's preferred long-term solution remained removal, but Brown forced him back into the immediate battle over segregation.

If Black Americans were not going to leave, he wanted the color line preserved.

Later Work and Retirement

For all his sweeping racial ambitions, Cox did not become wealthy. Real-estate work in Richmond provided a modest living for much of his adult life.

He retired from the business in 1958.

In 1963 Cox published Black Belt Around the World at the High Noon of Colonialism, an autobiographical account of his earlier travels.

He mailed copies to members of Congress and other federal officials, still trying to make a five-decade-old journey politically useful.

Cox never married.

His adult life revolved heavily around writing, correspondence, racial activism, travel memories, and political lobbying.

Death and the Collapse of Virginia's Racial-Mixing Laws

Earnest Sevier Cox died in a Richmond hospital on April 26, 1966, from complications of emphysema.

He was eighty-six.

Cox was buried at Arlington National Cemetery two days later, reflecting his military service rather than official endorsement of his racial politics.

Cox died before the Supreme Court's 1967 decision in Loving v. Virginia invalidated laws prohibiting interracial marriage.

The constitutional order he had helped build in 1924 collapsed almost completely within a year of his death.

How Cox Opposed Black People

Cox's opposition to Black equality was systematic. He helped build organizations enforcing a strict color line, lobbied for laws prohibiting interracial marriage and integrated public assembly, portrayed Black people as biologically inferior, attacked school integration, and spent decades arguing that Black Americans should ultimately leave the United States.

His politics treated Black citizenship as temporary or fundamentally incompatible with a white nation.

Cox did not merely dislike interracial relationships.

He wanted government to classify ancestry, police marriage, separate public life, and subsidize population removal.

Cox sometimes argued that physical separation would reduce racial conflict.

But his system was not “separate but equal” in any meaningful egalitarian sense. It began from belief in white superiority and sought to preserve white possession of the United States.

Black Separatist Allies and the Limits of the Alliance

Garveyites and Peace Movement of Ethiopia activists who supported emigration did not thereby validate Cox's claims of Black inferiority.

Their cooperation reflected their own disillusionment with American racism and their own nationalist projects.

Comparison with Marcus Garvey

Garvey sought Black power, dignity, independent institutions, and international African nationhood. Cox sought a white United States protected from racial mixture.

They sometimes agreed on separation. They did not agree on racial hierarchy.

Comparison with Theodore Bilbo and Wickliffe Draper

Bilbo used the Senate, racial invective, and populist demagoguery. Cox supplied a more continuous policy framework and cultivated Black separatist allies the Mississippi senator could not easily reach on his own.

One was the public politician. The other was the specialist.

Draper rarely wrote major public racial manifestos. Cox did.

Draper financed. Cox organized and argued.

Comparison with Carleton Putnam, Walter Plecker, and John Powell

Putnam defended segregation through elite business prestige and postwar race science. Cox's intellectual formation was older, rooted in eugenics, colonial observation, anti-miscegenation politics, and repatriation.

Both tried to give racial separation a respectable intellectual vocabulary.

Plecker possessed administrative power as Virginia's registrar. Cox possessed ideological and lobbying power outside the bureaucracy.

Cox helped create the racial-purity campaign; Plecker helped turn it into records, certificates, and enforcement.

Powell brought musical celebrity, elite social connections, and charismatic public performance to the Anglo-Saxon Clubs. Cox brought relentless writing, travel authority, and political organizing.

The movement needed both.

Was Cox a Scientist?

No. Cox presented himself as an expert on race, but he did not complete advanced scientific training and did not conduct systematic modern anthropological research.

His conclusions rested heavily on personal observation, eugenic theory, racial assumptions, and selective historical interpretation.

Was Cox a White Supremacist?

Yes. Encyclopedia Virginia explicitly describes him as a committed white supremacist, and his own political record supports the classification.

He argued for Black inferiority, anti-miscegenation law, rigid segregation, and eventual Black removal.

Voluntary Emigration, Removal, and Coercion

Cox usually advocated federally assisted “repatriation” and worked with Black groups that sought voluntary emigration. His rhetoric often emphasized assistance rather than immediate forcible deportation.

At the same time, his political objective was complete racial separation and a white United States. A careful biography should preserve both the formal legislative mechanism he promoted and the exclusionary end state he desired.

Did Cox Support Violence?

Cox's principal methods were legislation, lobbying, organization, publication, and propaganda rather than Klan-style terrorism.

He should not be assigned responsibility for violent acts without evidence. His documented impact came through racial law and ideology.

Historical Significance

Earnest Sevier Cox was one of the most persistent architects of racial separation in twentieth-century Virginia. He helped convert eugenic racial theory into organizations and statutes, then carried the argument further by insisting that Jim Crow itself was unstable unless Black Americans were ultimately removed from the country.

His career also reveals how strange political alliances can form around a shared tactic without shared moral principles. Cox could cooperate with Marcus Garvey and Black emigration organizations while remaining a committed white supremacist because both sides sometimes wanted geographic separation for entirely different reasons. That complexity makes Cox more historically instructive, not less.

Impact on African Americans

Cox helped build laws that restricted marriage, ancestry classification, public association, and citizenship on racial grounds. His repatriation campaign denied that Black Americans had an unconditional claim to remain within the country of their birth as equal citizens.

His influence also operated indirectly. Books, speeches, legislative testimony, and organizational networks gave white Virginians an ideological vocabulary for treating discrimination as “racial preservation” rather than domination.

A Balanced Assessment

Cox was energetic, widely travelled, persistent, capable of building unlikely alliances, and effective at turning ideas into lobbying campaigns. He served in the military, supported himself through ordinary work, and spent decades pursuing a political program with unusual consistency.

Those abilities were devoted overwhelmingly to maintaining racial hierarchy. His travels broadened his experience without broadening his racial sympathies; instead, he used colonial domination as evidence for white separation. His capacity to work respectfully with individual Black separatists also did not translate into belief in Black equality. A balanced assessment should recognize organizational skill without confusing tactical civility with egalitarianism.

Legacy

Cox's immediate legislative legacy largely collapsed. The anti-miscegenation structure he helped build was invalidated by Loving v. Virginia, public segregation became unconstitutional, and Congress never enacted his repatriation program.

His ideological legacy lasted longer. Later segregationists and white racialists continued circulating his books, and the network connecting Cox to Madison Grant, Wickliffe Draper, Carleton Putnam, and postwar racial organizations helped transmit older eugenic ideas into newer arguments about race, heredity, and national identity.

Geographic Scope

Tennessee shaped Cox's early life, while Chicago and Nashville shaped his religious and academic formation. His 1910-1915 travels carried him through large parts of Africa, Asia, Australia, New Zealand, and the Americas.

Richmond, Virginia became his permanent base and the center of the Anglo-Saxon Clubs, racial-integrity lobbying, and his publishing work. His repatriation campaign eventually reached Washington, D.C., Mississippi, Chicago, and Liberia through congressional politics and alliances with Black emigration organizations.

Legal and Constitutional Context

Cox's political career intersected with anti-miscegenation law, racial classification, public segregation, eugenics, the Fourteenth Amendment, Brown v. Board of Education, congressional power over emigration assistance, and ultimately Loving v. Virginia.

He illustrates how private ideological movements can become law. Cox had no formal legislative office, yet lobbying by the Anglo-Saxon Clubs helped write racial boundaries into Virginia statutes that governed marriage and public life for decades.
