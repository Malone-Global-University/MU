Roger B. Taney

Fifth Chief Justice of the United States, Jacksonian jurist, author of the Dred Scott decision, and a central figure in the constitutional history of slavery and the Civil War era

Overview

Roger Brooke Taney (1777-1864) was an American lawyer, Maryland politician, attorney general, treasury secretary, and the fifth Chief Justice of the United States. He led the Supreme Court from 1836 until his death in 1864, nearly three decades that encompassed the Bank War, westward expansion, intensifying sectional conflict over slavery, the collapse of the Second Party System, the secession crisis, and most of the Civil War. Taney was a major constitutional jurist whose Court modified some of the nationalist tendencies associated with Chief Justice John Marshall and gave greater latitude to state regulatory power. Yet his historical reputation is dominated by one decision: Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857), in which he wrote that Black people descended from enslaved Africans were not citizens within the meaning of the federal Constitution and that Congress lacked authority to prohibit slavery in the federal territories.

Taney's relationship to slavery and Black Americans was more complicated than the Dred Scott opinion alone might suggest, but it ultimately led to one of the most damaging racial decisions in Supreme Court history. Born into a wealthy Maryland slaveholding family, Taney himself owned enslaved people and later manumitted those he held. As a young lawyer he once defended an abolitionist Methodist minister and spoke of slavery as an evil. Over time, however, he became increasingly hostile to abolitionism and increasingly committed to constitutional protection for slaveholders. By the 1850s he viewed antislavery agitation as a threat to the Union and believed a sweeping Supreme Court ruling might settle the territorial slavery controversy. Instead, Dred Scott intensified it.

The decision went far beyond denying Dred Scott's individual freedom claim. Taney's opinion declared that Black people whose ancestors had been imported and enslaved could not be citizens of the United States for purposes of Article III, treated enslaved people as constitutionally protected property, and invalidated the Missouri Compromise's prohibition of slavery in certain federal territories. The ruling strengthened the legal position of slaveholders while denying federal political membership to an entire racial population. It was repudiated politically during the Civil War and constitutionally by the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments.

Taney's legacy nevertheless cannot be reduced entirely to Dred Scott. He played a central role in Andrew Jackson's attack on the Second Bank of the United States, became the first cabinet nominee formally rejected by the Senate when his treasury nomination failed, and later led a Court that issued important decisions on contracts, corporations, federalism, interstate commerce, admiralty, and state police powers. During the Civil War he also confronted Abraham Lincoln in Ex parte Merryman, arguing that the president could not unilaterally suspend the writ of habeas corpus. Taney therefore occupies an unusual place in American constitutional history: a jurist capable of defending civil liberty against executive detention while simultaneously authoring a racial decision that denied national citizenship to Black Americans.

Early Life, Catholic Maryland, and Legal Training

Taney was born on March 17, 1777, in Calvert County, Maryland, into a prosperous Catholic family whose wealth was tied to tobacco agriculture and enslaved labor. Maryland Catholics occupied an unusual social position. The Taneys were part of the colonial gentry, but the family also inherited memories of earlier political disabilities imposed on Catholics. That background contributed to Taney's enduring suspicion of concentrated political power and majoritarian interference with established legal rights, although those principles did not lead him toward racial equality.

At fifteen Taney entered Dickinson College in Pennsylvania. He graduated in 1795 and studied law in Annapolis under Judge Jeremiah Townley Chase. He was admitted to the Maryland bar in 1799 and quickly established himself as an able attorney. His career developed in Frederick, Maryland, where he built a profitable practice and became active in politics.

Taney initially identified with the Federalist Party. He served one term in the Maryland House of Delegates beginning in 1799 and later entered the Maryland Senate. Unlike many Federalists, however, he increasingly sympathized with the political coalition that developed around Andrew Jackson. By the 1820s, Taney had become a committed Jacksonian and an important Maryland ally of the future president.

Slaveholding, Manumission, and Early Views of Slavery

Taney's personal history with slavery is important because it complicates—but does not excuse—his later constitutional record. He inherited and owned enslaved people. Maryland archival records indicate that he owned at least eight and manumitted them through a series of actions beginning in 1818, with some manumissions taking legal effect later. By the mature phase of his national career, there is no clear evidence that he continued to own enslaved people.

In 1819 Taney defended Jacob Gruber, a Methodist minister prosecuted in Maryland after delivering an antislavery sermon. Taney argued that criticizing slavery was not itself criminal and described slavery in morally negative terms. The episode is sometimes used to depict a younger Taney as antislavery. That characterization should be used carefully. Taney did not become an abolitionist, did not embrace Black political equality, and remained embedded in a slaveholding society. His willingness to manumit people he personally held coexisted with a constitutional philosophy that later protected the broader slave system.

The shift was partly political. As national antislavery activism expanded in the 1830s and 1840s, Taney increasingly saw abolitionism as destabilizing and dangerous to the Union. Like many white border-state conservatives, he could regard slavery as unfortunate in the abstract while believing that slaveholders possessed constitutional rights that antislavery majorities could not disturb. That distinction became decisive in Dred Scott.

Maryland Attorney General and Rise with Andrew Jackson

Taney became attorney general of Maryland in 1827. His legal reputation, political loyalty, and increasingly close relationship with Andrew Jackson soon brought him into national government. Jackson appointed Taney attorney general of the United States in 1831. The appointment placed Taney at the center of one of the defining struggles of the Jackson presidency: the conflict over the Second Bank of the United States.

Taney strongly supported Jackson's campaign against the Bank. He shared the president's suspicion that a powerful federally chartered financial corporation concentrated economic and political privilege in private hands. When Treasury Secretary William Duane refused Jackson's order to remove federal deposits from the Bank, Jackson dismissed him and placed Taney in the Treasury Department during a recess appointment in 1833.

The Bank War and Senate Rejection

As acting secretary of the treasury, Taney carried out Jackson's order to remove federal deposits from the Second Bank of the United States and redirect government funds into selected state banks. The move became one of the most controversial exercises of executive power in the Bank War. Opponents charged Jackson and Taney with undermining congressional authority and destabilizing the financial system; supporters portrayed the action as a democratic attack on entrenched privilege.

When Jackson formally submitted Taney's nomination for treasury secretary in June 1834, the Senate rejected it, 28 to 18. Taney thereby became the first cabinet nominee in American history formally rejected by the Senate. The rejection was heavily political. The chamber contained a strong anti-Jackson coalition, and Taney had made himself a central participant in the president's attack on the Bank.

Jackson did not abandon him. In 1835 the president nominated Taney to the Supreme Court as an associate justice, but opposition again prevented confirmation before the Senate adjourned. Political conditions changed after Jacksonian Democrats strengthened their position. Following Chief Justice John Marshall's death in 1835, Jackson nominated Taney as chief justice. The Senate confirmed him in March 1836.

Chief Justice of the United States

Taney succeeded John Marshall, whose thirty-four-year chief justiceship had strengthened the institutional authority of the Supreme Court and articulated broad principles of federal power. Taney did not simply dismantle Marshall's constitutional structure. The Taney Court preserved judicial review, federal supremacy within constitutionally assigned spheres, and much of the institutional authority Marshall had established. But Taney and his colleagues were generally more receptive to state regulatory power and less inclined to treat older corporate charters as barriers to later democratic legislation.

One of the clearest examples was Charles River Bridge v. Warren Bridge (1837). The Court rejected an expansive interpretation of an older corporate charter that would have prevented Massachusetts from authorizing a competing bridge. Taney's reasoning favored public development and state regulatory flexibility over implied monopoly rights. The decision became associated with Jacksonian hostility toward entrenched corporate privilege.

The Taney Court also decided major cases involving banking, commerce, admiralty, federalism, and the authority of states over corporations operating across state lines. In decisions such as Briscoe v. Bank of Kentucky, Bank of Augusta v. Earle, and the License Cases, the Court developed constitutional doctrines that were neither simply “states' rights” nor uniformly nationalist. Taney's jurisprudence often tried to preserve a workable boundary between national constitutional authority and meaningful state governance.

Slavery Cases Before Dred Scott

Slavery repeatedly entered the Taney Court before 1857. The Constitution contained protections important to slaveholders, and the growth of abolitionism created conflicts over fugitives, interstate movement, maritime law, and federal authority. Taney generally approached these disputes from the position that slave property recognized by state law received substantial constitutional protection.

In Prigg v. Pennsylvania (1842), Justice Joseph Story wrote for the Court that the federal Fugitive Slave Clause and federal fugitive-slave legislation preempted certain state interference. Taney concurred in the result but argued that states could exercise more authority to assist in the recapture process. The case weakened northern personal-liberty laws while reinforcing the constitutional position of slaveholders seeking people who had escaped.

Taney's concern increasingly became not simply slavery where states chose to maintain it, but whether antislavery political majorities could restrict slaveholders in national territories. This territorial question became one of the most explosive issues in American politics after the Mexican War. By the time Dred Scott reached the Court, Taney believed the judiciary could provide a constitutional settlement.

Dred Scott's Freedom Suit

Dred Scott was an enslaved man who had lived with his enslaver, U.S. Army surgeon John Emerson, in Illinois and in the Wisconsin Territory, jurisdictions where slavery was prohibited. Scott and his wife Harriet later sued for freedom in Missouri, arguing that residence on free soil had made them free. Their litigation moved through the Missouri courts and eventually entered federal court after ownership passed into the Sanford family network. The federal case reached the Supreme Court as Dred Scott v. Sandford.

The Court could have resolved the dispute narrowly. Instead, the justices addressed broad questions about Black citizenship, congressional authority in the territories, and the constitutional status of enslaved property. The majority was divided in its reasoning, but Taney wrote the opinion that came to define the case.

The Dred Scott Decision

On March 6, 1857, Taney delivered his opinion. He concluded that Dred Scott could not invoke federal diversity jurisdiction as a citizen of Missouri because, in Taney's reading of the Constitution's history, Black people descended from Africans brought to America and enslaved were not included in the political community whose members formed the Constitution. The opinion did not merely say that an enslaved person lacked citizenship while enslaved. It attempted to exclude an entire class of Black people, including free Black people descended from enslaved Africans, from federal constitutional citizenship.

Taney then addressed the territorial question. He concluded that Congress could not prohibit slaveholders from taking enslaved people into federal territories because doing so would deprive owners of property without constitutionally sufficient authority. On that basis the Court treated the Missouri Compromise's restriction on slavery north of latitude 36°30′ in the Louisiana Purchase as unconstitutional. The ruling therefore expanded constitutional protection for slavery beyond the slave states themselves.

The opinion also rejected Scott's argument that his residence in free federal territory had made him permanently free. Missouri law, Taney reasoned, controlled his status after his return. The combined effect was sweeping: Scott lost his claim, federal Black citizenship was denied, and congressional power to exclude slavery from the territories was sharply restricted.

How Taney Opposed Black People

Taney's most consequential opposition to Black people was judicial rather than organizational. Unlike a segregationist governor, Klan leader, or racial propagandist, he did not create a mass movement against Black Americans. His power came from constitutional office. As chief justice, he authored an opinion that attempted to place Black Americans outside the national political community and to constitutionalize stronger protection for slaveholding expansion.

The citizenship holding was especially damaging. Free Black communities existed in several states at the founding and some Black men had participated in political life under state law. Taney nevertheless constructed a national historical argument that treated the relevant Black population as excluded from the federal citizenry. That reasoning subordinated actual variation in early American practice to a sweeping racial conclusion.

The property holding compounded the injury. By treating Congress's territorial restriction on slavery as an unconstitutional interference with slave property, Taney placed the rights of slaveholders above Congress's longstanding practice of regulating slavery in federal territories. The decision thus operated on two levels at once: it denied Black national citizenship while strengthening the constitutional claims of the people who enslaved them.

Taney did not advocate every form of racial oppression common in nineteenth-century America, and his earlier manumissions show that he could act against slavery at the personal level. But Dred Scott demonstrates why personal acts of manumission cannot substitute for analysis of institutional power. When Taney possessed the authority to define national constitutional membership, he used that authority in a way that sharply restricted Black rights.

The Infamous Citizenship Language

The most notorious passage in Taney's opinion described Black people as having been regarded by whites at the founding as an inferior order and associated that historical claim with the proposition that they possessed no rights that white people were bound to respect. The language was framed by Taney as a description of the Founding generation's assumptions, but the Court used that historical interpretation as a basis for a contemporary constitutional holding.

The distinction between describing eighteenth-century racism and endorsing its constitutional consequences matters, but it does not rescue the opinion. Taney treated the racial exclusions he attributed to the founding era as legally controlling in 1857 and used them to deny federal citizenship to Black Americans. The opinion therefore transformed a racist historical narrative into constitutional doctrine.

Reaction to Dred Scott

Many white Southerners and proslavery Democrats welcomed the decision because it appeared to protect slavery from congressional restriction. President James Buchanan publicly hoped the Court would settle the territorial issue. Instead, northern opposition hardened. Republicans denounced the decision as evidence that the federal government was being used to nationalize slavery, while abolitionists regarded it as confirmation that the constitutional order had become deeply compromised by slaveholding power.

Abraham Lincoln made Dred Scott a major subject of his political rise. He argued that decisions bound the immediate parties but rejected the idea that a single Supreme Court ruling should permanently dictate national political policy on a fundamental constitutional question. The decision became central to the Lincoln-Douglas debates and to Republican warnings about a possible broader effort to protect slavery throughout the nation.

Rather than ending sectional conflict, Dred Scott aggravated it. The ruling destroyed the possibility that the Missouri Compromise could serve as a constitutional model, undermined popular-sovereignty arguments, sharpened divisions inside the Democratic Party, and strengthened Republican claims that slavery threatened free political institutions.

Was Dred Scott Legally Necessary?

The breadth of Taney's opinion became controversial almost immediately. If Scott lacked standing as a citizen to bring the federal diversity suit, critics argued, the Court could have stopped there. Going on to invalidate the Missouri Compromise and address substantive territorial rights appeared unnecessary to resolve the jurisdictional issue.

Defenders of the majority contended that the Court needed to address the full record and the arguments presented. Critics responded that the Court deliberately reached out to settle a national political dispute that constitutional adjudication could not successfully resolve. The failure was not only moral; it was institutional. A decision intended to end controversy instead weakened public confidence in the Court and became a classic warning against attempting to settle explosive political conflicts through unnecessarily broad constitutional rulings.

The Election of Lincoln and the Secession Crisis

Taney remained chief justice as the political order around him collapsed. Abraham Lincoln won the presidency in 1860 on a platform opposing the expansion of slavery into the territories. Southern states began seceding before Lincoln took office. Taney, a Marylander whose state remained in the Union but contained substantial pro-Confederate sentiment, watched the crisis with deep alarm.

He administered the presidential oath of office to Lincoln on March 4, 1861. The two men represented profoundly different constitutional visions of slavery and national power, and within months they would clash over wartime civil liberties.

Ex parte Merryman and Habeas Corpus

After the Civil War began, Washington's security depended heavily on transportation routes through Maryland. Confederate sympathizers destroyed bridges and interfered with rail connections. President Lincoln authorized military officers to suspend the writ of habeas corpus along strategically important routes and detain suspected saboteurs without immediate civilian judicial review.

John Merryman, a Maryland militia officer accused of aiding secessionist activity, was arrested by military authorities and held at Fort McHenry. Acting in his capacity as a federal circuit judge, Taney issued a writ ordering the military to bring Merryman before the court. The commanding officer refused, citing presidential authorization.

In Ex parte Merryman (1861), Taney concluded that the Constitution placed the suspension power with Congress, not the president. He warned that allowing the executive and military to imprison civilians outside ordinary judicial process threatened government under law. Lincoln did not accept Taney's interpretation and continued wartime suspensions, later receiving congressional authorization.

Merryman is one reason Taney's constitutional legacy cannot be summarized as simple judicial deference to oppressive power. In that case he defended a restrictive view of executive authority and insisted on judicial protection against military detention. The tension is stark: the same chief justice who denied Black national citizenship in Dred Scott also articulated one of the nineteenth century's strongest judicial warnings against unilateral executive suspension of habeas corpus.

Taney, Lincoln, and the Civil War Constitution

The conflict between Taney and Lincoln continued throughout the war. Taney feared that military necessity was eroding constitutional limits; Lincoln believed extraordinary executive action was necessary to preserve the Union. Their dispute became part of a much broader question that has recurred in American history: how far presidential power may expand during rebellion or war.

Taney lived long enough to see the Emancipation Proclamation, Union military advances, and the beginning of slavery's destruction. He did not live to see final ratification of the Thirteenth Amendment or adoption of the Fourteenth Amendment's citizenship clause. Those Reconstruction Amendments would dismantle the constitutional foundation of his most famous ruling.

Death and Succession

Taney died in Washington, D.C., on October 12, 1864, at age eighty-seven. He had served as chief justice for more than twenty-eight years. President Lincoln nominated former Treasury Secretary Salmon P. Chase—an antislavery politician and one of the nation's best-known opponents of slavery—to succeed him.

The symbolism was striking. Taney had written the opinion denying federal Black citizenship and limiting congressional power over slavery. Chase would preside over the Court during Reconstruction, when constitutional amendments and federal legislation established emancipation, birthright citizenship, equal protection, and new federal authority to protect civil rights.

Dred Scott and the Reconstruction Amendments

The Civil War and Reconstruction repudiated the core constitutional propositions of Dred Scott. The Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery throughout the United States, eliminating the property regime on which much of the decision depended. The Civil Rights Act of 1866 declared national citizenship for persons born in the United States, subject to specified exceptions. The Fourteenth Amendment then constitutionalized national birthright citizenship and prohibited states from denying due process or equal protection.

The Citizenship Clause was, in part, a direct rejection of Dred Scott. Where Taney had attempted to exclude a racial class from national citizenship, the Fourteenth Amendment placed citizenship in constitutional text beyond the control of state racial classifications or the historical theory used in his opinion.

Comparison with Andrew Johnson

Taney and Andrew Johnson opposed Black equality through different institutions and at different constitutional moments. Taney's decisive power came from the judiciary before and during the Civil War. Johnson's came from the presidency during Reconstruction. Taney used constitutional interpretation to deny Black citizenship and protect slavery's territorial position; Johnson used executive power to resist congressional efforts to establish Black civil and political rights after slavery had been abolished.

Both men illustrate the importance of institutional authority. Neither needed to organize a private white-supremacist movement to produce enormous consequences. Courts and presidencies could shape citizenship, property, voting, civil rights, and the distribution of power across the entire nation.

Comparison with John C. Calhoun

John C. Calhoun developed an explicit political theory defending slavery as a positive good and emphasizing the constitutional rights of slaveholding states and minorities. Taney was less systematic as a proslavery theorist and had earlier expressed moral discomfort with slavery. Nevertheless, Dred Scott moved constitutional doctrine toward an important part of the proslavery constitutional position by treating enslaved people as protected property that Congress could not simply exclude from the territories.

Calhoun worked primarily through political theory, the Senate, and executive office. Taney worked through constitutional adjudication. Their arguments converged most clearly on the claim that federal territorial policy could not disregard slaveholders' asserted constitutional property rights.

Comparison with John Marshall

Taney's chief justiceship is frequently contrasted with that of John Marshall. Marshall is associated with strong national judicial authority, broad readings of federal power, and protection of contractual obligations. Taney preserved much of the Court's institutional strength but was more willing to accommodate state regulatory authority and Jacksonian suspicion of monopoly privilege.

The contrast should not be exaggerated. The Taney Court did not simply reverse Marshall. It adapted constitutional doctrine to a rapidly changing commercial democracy while maintaining a significant role for federal courts. That broader judicial record is why Taney remains important to constitutional historians even apart from Dred Scott.

Historical Significance

Roger B. Taney was one of the most consequential chief justices in American history. His Court governed constitutional law during a period of enormous economic expansion, state development, sectional crisis, and war. His decisions helped define the relationship between corporations and government, state and federal power, commercial regulation, and judicial authority after the Marshall era.

Yet Dred Scott overwhelms that achievement because it joined racial hierarchy to constitutional law at the highest judicial level. Taney did not merely uphold an existing state slave code. He attempted to define Black people as outside national citizenship and to remove a major antislavery policy option from Congress. Few Supreme Court opinions have been repudiated so completely by subsequent constitutional change.

Taney is therefore historically important both for what a chief justice can build and for what a Supreme Court can damage. His career demonstrates that legal sophistication and institutional stature do not guarantee justice. A powerful jurist can produce enduring doctrine in some areas while committing a catastrophic constitutional error in another.

Impact on African Americans

The immediate legal impact of Dred Scott was to deny Dred and Harriet Scott relief from the Supreme Court and to place free and enslaved Black Americans under a sweeping constitutional theory of exclusion. The Court's citizenship analysis suggested that even free Black people descended from enslaved Africans could not claim federal citizenship under the Constitution as Taney understood it.

The decision also strengthened slaveholders politically. By invalidating congressional restrictions on slavery in the territories, it threatened to open additional western land to slaveholding and weakened the ability of antislavery voters to use federal law to contain slavery's expansion.

Its longer-term impact was paradoxical. The ruling inflicted immediate constitutional harm but also became one of the strongest examples used by Republicans, abolitionists, and later Reconstruction lawmakers to demonstrate the need for explicit constitutional protection of Black citizenship. The Fourteenth Amendment's Citizenship Clause stands as a direct institutional repudiation of the exclusion Taney attempted to constitutionalize.

A Balanced Assessment

Taney was an able lawyer, an important Jacksonian statesman, and a chief justice of substantial intellectual and institutional capacity. His Court made durable contributions to American federalism, commercial law, corporate regulation, and the adaptation of constitutional doctrine to a changing economy. His stance in Ex parte Merryman reflected a serious commitment to limiting unilateral executive detention in wartime. His personal manumission of enslaved people also distinguishes his biography from that of politicians who spent their careers defending slavery without qualification.

Those facts cannot neutralize Dred Scott. Taney's greatest exercise of constitutional authority on race produced a sweeping denial of Black citizenship and an expansion of constitutional protection for slaveholding interests. The opinion was historically strained, politically disastrous, morally indefensible, and institutionally damaging. A balanced assessment therefore requires holding two propositions together: Taney was a major jurist of genuine ability, and his most famous decision was among the gravest failures in the history of the Supreme Court.

Legacy

For generations Taney was commemorated as a major Maryland jurist and chief justice, with statues, portraits, schools, streets, and public buildings carrying his name. Over time, Dred Scott became increasingly central to public evaluation of those honors. Debates over Taney monuments intensified during broader reconsideration of public memorials connected to slavery, segregation, and white supremacy.

His legal legacy remains more complex than his public memory. Constitutional scholars still study the Taney Court's federalism, corporate, commercial, and wartime decisions. Ex parte Merryman remains part of major debates over presidential emergency power and habeas corpus. But Dred Scott functions primarily as a negative precedent—a warning about racialized constitutional interpretation, judicial overreach, historically selective originalism, and attempts by courts to settle political crises through expansive rulings.

Taney's reputation ultimately illustrates how one decision can reorder the legacy of an otherwise consequential judicial career. He served nearly three decades as chief justice. Yet the constitutional exclusion of Black Americans in 1857 remains the act by which he is most widely remembered.

Geographic Scope

Maryland shaped Taney's personal, legal, political, and racial outlook. He was born in Calvert County, built his legal career in Frederick, served in state office, and remained deeply connected to a border-state society where slavery, free Black communities, Catholic history, and Unionist politics coexisted uneasily.

Washington, D.C. became the center of his national career as attorney general, treasury official, and chief justice. The constitutional controversies he decided reached across the entire United States—from corporate development in Massachusetts and banking disputes in Kentucky to slavery in Missouri, Illinois, and the federal territories, and Civil War military detention in Maryland.

Legal and Constitutional Context

Taney's career intersected with nearly every major constitutional controversy of the antebellum era: the scope of federal economic power, the Contract Clause, corporate charters, interstate commerce, state police powers, federalism, slavery, the Fugitive Slave Clause, congressional territorial authority, citizenship, due process, secession, habeas corpus, and presidential emergency power.

His racial legacy is especially connected to Article III citizenship, the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause, congressional power over federal territories, and the constitutional status of slavery before the Reconstruction Amendments. The Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments fundamentally transformed the constitutional landscape on which Dred Scott rested.
