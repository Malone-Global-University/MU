Robert Toombs

Georgia lawyer, slaveholding planter, U.S. representative and senator, secession leader, Confederate secretary of state and brigadier general, and postwar opponent of Reconstruction

Overview

Robert Augustus Toombs was a prominent nineteenth-century Georgia lawyer, planter, politician, and Confederate leader. He served in the Georgia legislature, the United States House of Representatives, and the United States Senate before helping lead Georgia out of the Union in 1861. He then became the Confederacy's first secretary of state and later a brigadier general in the Confederate Army.

Toombs's political career changed substantially over time. During the 1840s and much of the 1850s he sought to preserve the Union through sectional compromise, but his Unionism was tied to continued constitutional protection for slavery and slaveholder political power. By the presidential crisis of 1860-1861, he had become an outspoken advocate of secession.

His significance to African American history rests on his direct participation in slavery, his defense of slaveholder interests in national politics, his advocacy of secession, his service to the Confederacy, and his postwar resistance to Reconstruction. His life also intersects with that of Garland H. White, a man Toombs enslaved who escaped, became an abolitionist minister, and later served as a chaplain in the United States Colored Troops.

Early Life in Wilkes County

Toombs was born in Wilkes County, Georgia, on July 2, 1810. He grew up in a prosperous white family within a region whose agricultural economy and political order were deeply connected to slavery.

His father died when Toombs was still a child. The family's social position nevertheless gave him access to education and professional opportunities unavailable to most Georgians and categorically denied to enslaved African Americans.

Education

Toombs entered Franklin College, the institution that later became the University of Georgia, while still a teenager. After disciplinary difficulties, he continued his studies at Union College in Schenectady, New York, graduating in 1828.

He then studied law at the University of Virginia before returning to Georgia and entering legal practice.

Marriage and Family

In 1830 Toombs married Julia Ann DuBose. Their marriage lasted for more than five decades and became an important part of his personal life, as reflected in extensive surviving correspondence between them.

The couple experienced repeated family loss. Their son died in childhood, and both daughters died before Toombs, one in 1855 and the other in 1866.

Legal Career

Toombs was admitted to the Georgia bar in 1830 and developed a successful circuit-law practice. His courtroom skill, forceful personality, and reputation as an orator helped establish the public profile that carried him into politics.

Law remained an important source of wealth and influence throughout his life, including after the Civil War when formal political disabilities limited his eligibility for public office.

Planter and Slaveholder

Toombs was not only a political defender of slavery; he personally enslaved African Americans and accumulated wealth within Georgia's plantation economy. His political arguments about southern rights therefore had a direct material connection to his own economic interests.

Slavery placed the people he held under a legal regime in which their labor, movement, family security, and civil status were controlled by slaveholders and state law.

Garland H. White

One of the most important African American lives connected to Toombs was Garland H. White. Born enslaved in Virginia in 1829, White was sold as a child to Toombs and later spent substantial time with him in Washington, D.C.

White escaped slavery, made his way to Canada, entered the ministry, and returned to the United States during the Civil War. He became a chaplain in the 28th United States Colored Infantry and participated in the Union war effort against the Confederacy that Toombs had helped create.

White's Life as a Counterpoint

Garland White's biography provides an essential counterpoint to Toombs's own account of slavery and southern political rights. Toombs defended a system in which White was legally property; White escaped that system and later served in an army helping destroy it.

Their connected histories illustrate why biographies of slaveholding politicians should include the lives and agency of the people they enslaved rather than treating slavery only as an abstract political issue.

Entry into Georgia Politics

Toombs entered the Georgia House of Representatives in 1837. He served there during most of the period through 1843 while continuing his law practice.

He emerged as a gifted political speaker and developed relationships with other influential Georgia politicians, especially Alexander H. Stephens and Howell Cobb.

Political Partnership with Alexander H. Stephens

Toombs and Alexander H. Stephens formed one of the most consequential political friendships in antebellum Georgia. Both began as Whigs, emphasized constitutional arguments and states' rights, and sought to protect southern political interests within the Union for much of their prewar careers.

Their paths converged around the defense of slavery but diverged at important moments. Stephens opposed immediate secession at Georgia's January 1861 convention, while Toombs had become one of the state's leading advocates of withdrawal.

Election to the U.S. House

Toombs won election to the United States House of Representatives as a Whig and served from March 1845 through March 1853.

His House career coincided with escalating sectional disputes over territorial expansion, the Mexican-American War, slavery in newly acquired lands, fugitive slaves, and the political balance between free and slave states.

Whig Politics

As a Whig, Toombs did not initially fit the later image of the uncompromising Confederate secessionist. He often favored national political arrangements and compromise when he believed they could preserve both the Union and southern constitutional claims.

That distinction is important: Toombs's antebellum nationalism generally sought a Union that continued to recognize and protect slavery rather than a Union moving toward Black freedom or racial equality.

Compromise of 1850

Toombs supported the Compromise of 1850 and joined Alexander Stephens and Howell Cobb in opposing immediate southern secession during that crisis.

Their position helped shape the Georgia Platform, which accepted the compromise but warned that further federal restrictions affecting slavery could justify resistance. Toombs thus stood against disunion in 1850 without abandoning slaveholder political demands.

Georgia Platform

The Georgia Platform represented a conditional Unionism. Georgia would remain in the United States, but the settlement rested on continued recognition of southern claims involving slavery and the territories.

Toombs's support for the platform is one of the clearest examples of his earlier belief that slavery could be protected more effectively inside the Union than through immediate secession.

Collapse of the Whig Party

National sectional conflict eventually shattered the Whig Party. Toombs helped establish the short-lived Constitutional Union movement and later aligned with the Democratic Party as the old party structure disintegrated.

Party labels changed, but his central commitment to protecting southern slaveholding interests remained consistent.

United States Senate

Toombs entered the United States Senate in 1853 and remained there until Georgia's secession crisis in 1861. He became a nationally visible speaker on sectional issues and an increasingly forceful defender of slavery and southern political power.

His Senate career illustrates the transformation of a conditional southern Unionist into an advocate of disunion.

Proslavery Politics

Toombs defended the legal security and territorial future of slavery. He resisted attempts to exclude slavery from western territories and argued that southern slaveholders were entitled to equal protection for their property under the Constitution.

Such arguments translated the ownership of human beings into constitutional claims about property, federalism, and sectional equality.

Expansion of Slavery

By the late 1850s Toombs increasingly treated territorial restriction of slavery as a threat to the South's future. His rhetoric reflected a broader fear among proslavery leaders that confinement of slavery would eventually weaken slaveholder power.

This concern was central to the political movement that ultimately carried much of the Deep South toward secession.

Public Defense of Slavery

Toombs did not confine his proslavery views to southern audiences. He publicly defended the institution in national political debate and during speaking appearances outside the South.

By the eve of the Civil War, his speeches framed the preservation of slavery as a fundamental southern constitutional demand rather than a temporary policy dispute.

Election of 1860

During the presidential crisis of 1860, Toombs supported the southern Democratic candidacy of John C. Breckinridge. Abraham Lincoln's election convinced him that the political balance necessary to protect slavery within the Union had collapsed.

His response was no longer compromise. He began pressing Georgia toward secession.

From Unionist to Secessionist

Toombs's shift after Lincoln's election was dramatic but not entirely disconnected from his earlier politics. His Unionism had always depended on his belief that the national government would protect southern slaveholding rights.

When he concluded that those guarantees were no longer politically secure, he moved from conditional Unionism to active disunion.

Secession Campaign in Georgia

Toombs became one of the most influential advocates of immediate secession in Georgia. His stature among former Whigs was especially important because he could persuade voters and delegates who had previously resisted the more radical fire-eaters.

He participated in Georgia's sovereignty convention and supported the state's withdrawal from the United States.

Farewell to the U.S. Senate

In January 1861 Toombs delivered a final major Senate speech defending the southern case for separation. His rhetoric explicitly rejected Black political equality and citizenship while presenting southern resistance as a defense of constitutional rights.

The speech is important because it connects secession not merely to an abstract doctrine of states' rights but to specific demands concerning slavery and racial hierarchy.

Withdrawal from the Senate

Toombs withdrew from the United States Senate on February 4, 1861, after Georgia had seceded. The Senate later formally declared his seat vacant.

His withdrawal ended approximately sixteen years of continuous service in the federal Congress and marked his transition into Confederate government.

Confederate Leadership

Toombs participated in the formation of the Confederate States of America and served in its Provisional Congress. He hoped to play a leading role in the new government and was considered among the prominent southern politicians available for national Confederate leadership.

Jefferson Davis became president, while Toombs entered the cabinet as secretary of state.

First Confederate Secretary of State

Toombs became the first secretary of state of the Confederate States in February 1861. The office placed him at the center of the new government's attempts to establish diplomatic legitimacy and secure recognition abroad.

His cabinet service was brief. He became increasingly dissatisfied with Jefferson Davis and the direction of Confederate policy.

Conflict with Jefferson Davis

Toombs developed a contentious relationship with Confederate president Jefferson Davis. His independence, ambition, and criticism of central authority made prolonged cabinet cooperation difficult.

The conflict reflected a recurring tension within the Confederacy: leaders who had invoked states' rights against the United States often disagreed about how much centralized power their own wartime government should exercise.

Resignation from the Cabinet

Toombs resigned as Confederate secretary of state after only a few months. He then entered military service, seeking a more active role in the war.

His movement from senator to Confederate diplomat to general demonstrates how completely his political career became tied to the survival of the slaveholding republic he had helped establish.

Confederate Brigadier General

Toombs received a commission as a brigadier general in the Confederate Army and commanded Georgia troops in the eastern theater.

His military career was shorter and less politically successful than his congressional career, but it made him a direct participant in armed Confederate resistance to the United States.

Antietam

Toombs is particularly associated with the fighting at Antietam on September 17, 1862. Confederate troops under his command defended the crossing later known as Burnside Bridge against repeated attacks by a much larger Union force.

The tactical episode became one of the best-known events of his military career. It should be understood within the larger war in which Confederate military success served a government created to preserve a slaveholding social order.

Resignation from Confederate Army Service

Toombs became dissatisfied with his military prospects and Confederate leadership. After failing to secure the advancement he expected, he resigned his Confederate Army commission and returned to Georgia.

He later remained involved with Georgia's military affairs, but his principal national military role had ended.

Confederate Defeat

By 1865 the Confederacy was collapsing under Union military pressure, emancipation, destruction of its armies, and the breakdown of its political institutions.

For Toombs, defeat meant not only the loss of the Confederacy but also the destruction of the legal slave system whose security had been central to his antebellum politics.

Flight from Federal Authorities

At the end of the war, Toombs avoided arrest by federal authorities and went into hiding before leaving the United States. He travelled through Cuba and Europe during his exile.

His flight distinguished him from some former Confederate leaders who were captured and imprisoned immediately after the war.

Return to Georgia

Toombs returned to the United States in 1867 and resumed life in Washington, Georgia. He rebuilt his legal practice and recovered substantial influence despite remaining outside formal federal political office.

His return coincided with Reconstruction, when federal law and constitutional amendments were redefining citizenship, voting rights, and the legal status of formerly enslaved African Americans.

Refusal to Seek a Pardon

Toombs refused to ask the federal government for a pardon. He consequently remained under postwar political disabilities affecting his eligibility for public office.

His refusal became part of his postwar identity as an "unreconstructed" Confederate who did not repudiate the cause for which he had fought.

Opposition to Reconstruction

Toombs opposed Congressional Reconstruction and the new political order created after emancipation. He resisted Republican rule and the expansion of federal protection for African American citizenship and political participation.

Although he no longer held national office, his wealth, legal career, reputation, and political network gave him continued influence in Georgia Democratic politics.

African American Citizenship After the War

Reconstruction directly confronted the racial political order Toombs had defended before and during the Civil War. The Fourteenth Amendment established national citizenship and equal-protection principles, while the Fifteenth Amendment prohibited racial discrimination in voting rights.

Toombs's postwar political orientation stood against the Republican project of using federal power to secure those new constitutional rights in the former Confederacy.

Georgia Constitutional Convention of 1877

Toombs returned to formal state constitutional politics as a delegate to Georgia's constitutional convention of 1877. He was one of the convention's most influential participants and helped shape the state's post-Reconstruction governmental structure.

His role demonstrated that Confederate defeat and political disability did not eliminate the influence of former slaveholding elites within southern state politics.

Restored Political Influence Without Federal Office

Toombs never returned to the United States Congress and never reclaimed the national political career he had held before secession. Nevertheless, he remained a major figure in Georgia public life through law, political organization, constitutional advocacy, and personal influence.

His postwar career illustrates how former Confederate elites could regain substantial power even without returning to their prewar federal offices.

Relationship with Railroads and State Finance

In his later years Toombs became known for attacking railroad corporations, bond arrangements, and forms of state financial support he believed favored private interests.

These positions complicate any simple description of him as merely a planter aristocrat. They do not, however, alter his record on slavery, secession, or Reconstruction.

Personal Losses in Later Life

Toombs's final years were marked by major personal losses. Alexander H. Stephens, his longtime friend and political ally, died in 1883, and Toombs's wife Julia also died that year.

Contemporary and later biographical accounts describe the losses as deeply affecting the aging Toombs.

Death

Robert Toombs died in Washington, Georgia, on December 15, 1885. He was seventy-five years old.

He was buried in Rest Haven Cemetery in Washington, Georgia.

Was Toombs Always a Secessionist?

No. During the 1840s and the crisis of 1850, Toombs worked to preserve the Union and opposed immediate disunion. His earlier Unionism was nevertheless conditional upon protection of southern slaveholding interests.

By late 1860 he had concluded that those interests were no longer secure within the Union and became one of Georgia's strongest advocates of secession.

Did His Unionism Make Him Antislavery?

No. Toombs personally enslaved African Americans and consistently defended the political and constitutional security of slavery.

His conflict with earlier secessionists concerned strategy and timing, not a rejection of slaveholding itself.

Was Toombs a Major Confederate Leader?

Yes. He helped lead Georgia toward secession, participated in the Confederate provisional government, served as the Confederacy's first secretary of state, and became a brigadier general in its army.

His repeated disputes with Jefferson Davis reduced his long-term influence within the Confederate national government but did not make his role marginal.

Was Toombs a White-Supremacist Political Figure?

Yes. His politics defended slavery and explicitly rejected Black political equality. His secession-era rhetoric connected the preservation of southern political autonomy with maintenance of a racial order based on white supremacy and Black subordination.

Historical Significance

Toombs is historically significant because his life connects several major phases of nineteenth-century American conflict: antebellum Unionism, slaveholder constitutional politics, sectional compromise, secession, Confederate government, Civil War military service, Reconstruction resistance, and the restoration of former Confederate influence in southern politics.

He is especially useful for understanding why Unionism and proslavery politics were not opposites before the Civil War. For much of his career, Toombs wanted to preserve both the United States and slavery. Secession became his preferred strategy only when he concluded that the first could no longer reliably protect the second.

Legacy

Toombs was celebrated for generations in Georgia as a powerful statesman, lawyer, orator, Confederate leader, and symbol of resistance to Reconstruction. Counties, institutions, memorials, and historic sites preserved that public memory.

A fuller historical assessment must also account for the people he enslaved and the political order he defended. Garland H. White's escape from Toombs and later service in the United States Colored Troops provides a particularly important counter-memory: the man once treated as Toombs's property became an agent in the military destruction of American slavery.

Geographic Scope

Wilkes County and Washington, Georgia, formed the center of Toombs's personal, legal, plantation, and political life.

Washington, D.C., was the principal stage for his congressional career and also a place where Garland H. White lived while enslaved by him.

Montgomery, Alabama, and Richmond, Virginia, became important during the formation and administration of the Confederacy, while Maryland became central to his military reputation through the Battle of Antietam.

Cuba and Europe formed part of his postwar exile before his return to Georgia in 1867.

Legal and Constitutional Context

Toombs's career crossed two fundamentally different constitutional orders. Before the Civil War, slavery was protected by state law and recognized within the federal constitutional system. After the war, the Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery, the Fourteenth Amendment constitutionalized national citizenship and equal protection, and the Fifteenth Amendment prohibited racial discrimination in voting rights.

His political transformation and later opposition to Reconstruction cannot be understood apart from that constitutional revolution.
