Asa Earl Carter

Alabama white-supremacist organizer, Klan faction founder, segregationist speechwriter and political candidate who later reinvented himself as the western author “Forrest Carter”

Overview

Asa Earl Carter was an American sailor, radio commentator, white-supremacist propagandist, segregationist organizer, Ku Klux Klan faction founder, political candidate, speechwriter, and novelist who later reinvented himself publicly as the supposedly Cherokee western author Bedford Forrest "Forrest" Carter.

Carter was born on September 4, 1925, in the Oxford-Anniston area of Calhoun County, Alabama, to Ralph and Hermione Carter. He graduated from Calhoun County High School in 1943.

He entered the United States Navy during World War II, serving from May 1943 until March 1946. After military service he married his high-school sweetheart, India Thelma Walker, with whom he had four children.

Carter later studied journalism at the University of Colorado and returned to Alabama, where he entered broadcasting and political activism.

By the early 1950s he had become one of Birmingham's most aggressive public advocates of white supremacy. His radio broadcasts combined racial segregationism, antisemitism, anti-communism, and attacks on the emerging Civil Rights Movement.

He also published a white-supremacist periodical titled The Southerner and became involved in the Alabama Citizens' Council movement.

Carter was more extreme than some leaders of the mainstream Citizens' Council network. When other segregationists attempted to limit overt antisemitic rhetoric and emphasize resistance to Black integration, Carter broke away and built his own North Alabama organization.

He later helped found a militant Klan splinter organization generally identified as the Original Ku Klux Klan of the Confederacy.

Members of Carter's Klan organization participated in serious racial violence. In April 1956 members attacked Black singer Nat King Cole onstage during a Birmingham concert.

In September 1957 six members of the organization abducted Black handyman Judge Edward Aaron, castrated him, poured turpentine into his wounds, and abandoned him. Carter was not present for either the Cole assault or the Aaron mutilation according to the Encyclopedia of Alabama, and a responsible biography should not assign him personal participation in those attacks without evidence.

The distinction does not make Carter's institutional role incidental. He helped create and lead the organization from which the attackers came and promoted a political worldview in which racial equality was portrayed as a dangerous threat.

Carter himself also became involved in violent internal conflict. In 1957 he was charged in connection with the shooting of two members of his own Klan group during a dispute involving money. The charges were later dropped.

The absence of a conviction must be preserved. Historical sources establish the charge and internal dispute, but they do not justify describing Carter as criminally convicted of attempted murder.

Carter also participated directly in interstate segregationist agitation.

In 1956, after federal courts required Clinton High School in Tennessee to desegregate, outside segregationists descended on the community.

John Kasper of the Seaboard White Citizens Council became the best-known early outside agitator and was jailed for criminal contempt after defying a federal injunction.

Carter then joined the segregationist effort. The Tennessee Encyclopedia records that crowds continued growing after his arrival and that large-scale white rioting erupted during Labor Day weekend.

Cars were overturned, windows were smashed, Black travelers were threatened, Black neighborhoods faced intimidation, and officials called in the Tennessee National Guard.

It would be inaccurate to claim Carter alone caused the Clinton riot. Local participants, Kasper's earlier mobilization, additional segregationists, and the broader anti-integration campaign all contributed.

It is nevertheless historically significant that Carter travelled from Alabama to help sustain a campaign intended to prevent Black students from attending an integrated public high school after federal courts had ordered desegregation.

Carter's political ambitions repeatedly brought him into Birmingham and Alabama electoral politics.

He challenged Eugene "Bull" Connor for Birmingham's public-safety commission position and later ran for lieutenant governor of Alabama in 1958, finishing at the bottom of a five-candidate field.

These defeats illustrated an important boundary within white segregationist politics: Carter could be too extreme even for an electorate in which segregation remained overwhelmingly popular.

During the early 1960s Carter found a more influential role behind the scenes as a speechwriter associated with George C. Wallace.

Authoritative Alabama historical sources identify Carter as responsible for or centrally involved in drafting Wallace's January 1963 inaugural address, which contained the famous declaration "segregation now ... segregation tomorrow ... segregation forever."

The precise drafting history should be described with some caution. The Encyclopedia of Alabama and Birmingham Public Library archival finding aids identify Carter as a writer of Wallace's segregationist rhetoric, while other historical accounts describe him as one of multiple writers and Wallace publicly denied or minimized the association.

The larger point is not seriously disputed: Carter worked within Wallace's political orbit and helped supply rhetoric that transformed resistance to civil rights into a national political brand.

Carter also wrote speeches for Lurleen Wallace during the period in which she became governor of Alabama.

George Wallace had strong political incentives to benefit from Carter's rhetorical ability while distancing himself publicly from a man associated with Klan extremism and antisemitic agitation.

The relationship therefore illustrates how openly violent or fringe segregationist networks could overlap with electoral politics without becoming formally identical to them.

By the late 1960s Carter broke with Wallace, believing Wallace had moderated or diluted the hard-line racial message Carter wanted.

Carter entered the 1970 Democratic primary for governor of Alabama on an openly white-supremacist and anti-integration platform.

He finished far behind the leading candidates. Wallace returned to the governorship after defeating the more moderate incumbent Albert Brewer in the runoff.

The 1970 defeat effectively ended Asa Carter's conventional electoral career.

What followed made his biography one of the strangest cases of political and literary reinvention in twentieth-century America.

Carter left Alabama, spent time in Florida and Texas, adopted the name Bedford Forrest Carter, and presented himself as "Forrest Carter," a western storyteller with purported Cherokee heritage.

The chosen name itself complicates any claim that the new identity represented a straightforward repudiation of his racial past. Alabama historical sources state that he adopted Bedford Forrest Carter in honor of Confederate general and early Ku Klux Klan leader Nathan Bedford Forrest.

As Forrest Carter he produced fiction centered on dispossessed Confederates, Native American characters, resistance to centralized government, personal honor, revenge, kinship, and suspicion of institutional authority.

His first major western introduced Josey Wales, a former Confederate guerrilla who refuses to surrender after the Civil War and is pursued by forces associated with the victorious Union.

The work appeared under titles including The Rebel Outlaw: Josey Wales and Gone to Texas, with publication histories differing somewhat among editions and bibliographic sources.

Clint Eastwood adapted the Josey Wales story into the successful 1976 film The Outlaw Josey Wales.

Carter followed with another Josey Wales novel and with Watch for Me on the Mountain, a fictionalized treatment of Apache leader Geronimo.

The novels sometimes portray Native peoples and defeated Confederates as honorable outsiders confronting a cruel or overbearing federal government.

Those themes overlap significantly with Carter's earlier states' rights and anti-federal worldview, although literary fiction should not be treated as a transparent political manifesto.

Carter's most famous and controversial work became The Education of Little Tree, published in 1976.

The book was presented as the autobiographical story of a young orphan raised by Cherokee grandparents in the Appalachian mountains.

That specific autobiographical story was false.

Asa Carter had not been orphaned as a young child and raised by Cherokee grandparents. He had grown up in Alabama with his biological family, attended Alabama schools, served in the Navy, and spent the 1950s and 1960s in documented segregationist political activity.

Carter's broader claims of Cherokee ancestry have been disputed and should be distinguished from the demonstrably false childhood narrative. Some family members have claimed distant Indigenous ancestry, but the literary persona and specific upbringing described as autobiography were fabricated.

Scholars and Cherokee critics have also challenged the book's supposed Cherokee vocabulary, customs, and spiritual teachings as inaccurate, stereotyped, generalized, or invented.

The book's popularity nevertheless became substantial.

Readers praised its environmental themes, distrust of bureaucracy, celebration of family bonds, criticism of institutional cruelty, and apparent sympathy for Indigenous people.

The University of New Mexico Press later reissued the book and today openly identifies Forrest Carter as Asa Earl Carter, the Alabama segregationist who reinvented himself as a western novelist.

The contradiction between Carter's literary voice and political past generated a long debate over whether the books represented repentance, psychological compartmentalization, ideological continuity in a different form, commercial reinvention, or some combination.

The surviving evidence cannot establish a secret moral conversion.

Carter never publicly admitted during his lifetime that Forrest Carter and Asa Carter were the same person, never offered a public apology for his Klan and segregationist activities, and continued denying the identity connection after journalists exposed it.

In 1976 Alabama journalist Wayne Greenhaw publicly connected Forrest Carter to Asa Carter. The revelation received national attention after the release of The Outlaw Josey Wales.

Carter responded by maintaining the new persona. At times he referred to Asa Carter as though Asa were a different and disreputable relative.

A nationally televised interview with Barbara Walters intensified scrutiny because people who had known Asa Carter recognized him.

The identity controversy revived dramatically in 1991 when The Education of Little Tree became a major paperback bestseller and received a prominent booksellers' award.

Historian Dan T. Carter, who was not related to Asa Carter, published a widely discussed account documenting the transformation of the Alabama segregationist into the supposedly Cherokee author.

The book's classification and marketing became part of the controversy. A work promoted as autobiography or nonfiction was increasingly treated as fiction after the deception became impossible to ignore.

The literary scandal raises a separate question from the political one.

An author with a reprehensible political history can still produce fiction that some readers find emotionally effective or artistically valuable. Historical criticism does not require pretending the books had no literary appeal.

At the same time, the false claim to Cherokee identity and fabricated autobiography are relevant to evaluating the work because authenticity was part of how Little Tree was marketed and received.

The case therefore involves both racial politics and literary fraud.

Carter died on June 7, 1979, near Abilene, Texas, while travelling in connection with his writing and possible film projects.

Historical accounts agree that his death followed a physical altercation at or near his son's home and a fall or injury. Published accounts differ in some medical details concerning the immediate mechanism of death, so the biography avoids turning one disputed detail into certainty.

His body was returned to Alabama and buried at the cemetery of DeArmanville Methodist Church east of Oxford.

Carter died before The Education of Little Tree reached its greatest commercial success and before the identity controversy became a major national literary scandal.

His posthumous reputation therefore developed in two largely separate worlds that eventually collided.

Film audiences knew Josey Wales. Teachers, parents, environmentalists, and readers embraced Little Tree. Civil-rights historians and Alabama journalists remembered Asa Carter as a white-supremacist organizer and Wallace speechwriter.

Once those identities were conclusively connected, publishers, educators, critics, and readers had to decide how the political history should affect interpretation of the books.

For MGU's antagonist series, Carter is particularly important because he demonstrates how extremist ideas move between organizational forms.

His career passed through radio propaganda, Citizens' Councils, Klan organization, street agitation, electoral politics, gubernatorial speechwriting, western fiction, and invented ethnic identity.

He was not simply a Klansman who vanished after the 1950s, nor simply an author whose youthful politics were incidentally embarrassing.

The continuity of themes—states' rights, resistance to federal authority, reverence for Confederate memory, suspicion of institutions, outsider heroism, and self-mythologizing—makes the relationship between Asa Carter and Forrest Carter historically significant even where the moral meaning of that continuity remains open to interpretation.

A responsible biography must therefore avoid two opposite errors.

The first is to romanticize the literary persona as proof that Carter became an enlightened advocate for Indigenous peoples and racial reconciliation.

The second is to assume that every sentence he later wrote was simply coded segregationist propaganda.

The historical record supports a firmer conclusion: Carter built a public career promoting white supremacy and helping organize an extremist Klan faction, later concealed that identity, fabricated a Cherokee autobiography, and produced fiction whose artistic and ideological relationship to his earlier politics remains a legitimate subject of interpretation rather than a settled fact.

People who had known Asa Carter in Alabama recognized the supposedly mysterious western author.

Identity Was Not a Rumor by 1976

The connection was publicly reported during Carter's lifetime, even though later readers often forgot or never learned it.

Why 1991 Felt Like a New Revelation

The massive paperback audience for Little Tree largely encountered the book independently of the 1976 reporting.

Dan T. Carter

Historian Dan T. Carter wrote a major 1991 account documenting the transformation.

No Relation

Dan T. Carter was not related to Asa Earl Carter.

Transformation of a Klansman

The historian's work became central to public understanding of the double identity.

Academic Scrutiny

Literary scholars, historians, Native writers, and educators increasingly examined the political background of the text.

Documentary Film

The later documentary The Reconstruction of Asa Carter explored the conflict between his political and literary identities.

PBS and Public-History Attention

Documentary broadcasts and public-history discussions kept the controversy alive decades after Carter's death.

Oprah Controversy

The book was later removed from a prominent recommendation list after its author's background became more widely understood.

Reader Betrayal

Some readers felt deceived because they had believed both the autobiographical narrative and the claimed Cherokee identity.

Can the Art Be Separated From the Author?

Carter's case became a recurring example in debates over whether artistic value should be judged independently of an author's conduct.

Historian's Answer Is Different From Reader's Answer

Historical analysis can document authorship and deception without dictating a single emotional response readers must have toward the fiction.

Was Forrest Carter a Different Person Legally?

Historical sources primarily describe Forrest or Bedford Forrest Carter as an assumed identity or pseudonym; the precise paperwork of any formal legal name change is less important than the documented identity continuity.

Did Carter Become Cherokee?

No change of name or literary persona could itself make Carter a citizen or cultural representative of the Cherokee Nation.

Was He Completely Fabricating Every Indigenous Connection?

The specific autobiographical story was fabricated; broader family claims of distant Cherokee ancestry have been disputed and are not established strongly enough to support the author persona.

Did Native Critics Accept Little Tree as Accurate Cherokee Culture?

No. Cherokee and Native critics have challenged important language, custom, and stereotype problems.

Did Carter Publicly Repent of White Supremacy?

No documented public apology or confession comparable to George Wallace's later repudiation became part of Carter's record.

Did He Admit Being Asa Carter?

No. During his lifetime he continued resisting the identification.

Could the Fiction Represent Private Regret?

It is possible to interpret some literary themes that way, but private remorse cannot be established from the books alone.

Could the Fiction Represent Ideological Continuity?

The strong anti-federal, Confederate-sympathetic, and outsider-resistance themes make continuity plausible, but novels are not simple transcripts of an author's ideology.

Atonement Theory

Some readers and critics have wondered whether sympathetic Native characters represented an attempt at moral atonement.

Evidence Problem With Atonement

The theory remains inference because Carter neither admitted his old identity nor publicly repudiated the politics that made it notorious.

Commercial Reinvention Theory

The new identity also clearly created marketable mystique around a western storyteller.

Self-Mythologizing

Carter had already demonstrated talent for political mythmaking; the Forrest persona applied similar narrative skill to his own biography.

Identity and Marketing

Publishers and readers were encouraged to treat the author biography as part of the books' authenticity.

Fraud Beyond a Pen Name

Using a pseudonym alone is ordinary literary practice; falsely presenting a novel as personal Indigenous autobiography raises a different ethical issue.

Pseudonym Versus Hoax

The problem was not that Asa Carter wrote under another name, but that he used the new identity to support false autobiographical claims.

Literary Anti-Racism Paradox

A work that appears to condemn prejudice can still come from an author with a documented history of white-supremacist activism.

Psychological Motive Cannot Be Known

Historians can describe the contradiction without diagnosing Carter's inner psychology.

Political Reinvention

Carter's career shows that defeated extremists can seek new audiences rather than disappear.

Media Reinvention

The shift from radio demagogue to novelist changed audience and genre but retained his strength as a storyteller.

Nathan Bedford Forrest Symbolism

Choosing the name Forrest tied Carter's literary identity to Confederate memory even while the new persona claimed Indigenous ancestry.

Confederate and Indigenous Outsiders

His fiction often grouped defeated Confederates and Native peoples into a common anti-government moral universe.

Historical Problem With the Parallel

Confederate political goals and Indigenous resistance to dispossession were historically different, and treating them as equivalent can obscure slavery and conquest.

Josey Wales and Reconstruction Memory

The Josey Wales narrative fits a long cultural tradition depicting Reconstruction-era federal authority as oppressive toward white southerners.

Little Tree and Federal Institutions

The book similarly treats state institutional authority as threatening family, local autonomy, and traditional life.

Government as Villain

Suspicion of centralized power is one of the clearest thematic continuities between Carter's politics and fiction.

Why That Theme Is Not Inherently Racist

Anti-government thought has many ideological forms and is not inherently white supremacist; context matters.

Why Context Still Matters Here

Carter had explicitly used states' rights and anti-federal rhetoric earlier to defend racial segregation.

Death Journey

In June 1979 Carter was travelling between Texas and California in connection with writing and possible film work.

Visit to Son

He stopped near Abilene to visit a son.

Physical Altercation

Published accounts describe a fight or altercation during the visit.

Fall and Injury

Carter fell or was injured during the incident.

Immediate Cause Reports Vary

Sources describe airway obstruction in somewhat different terms, including choking or aspiration after the altercation.

Safest Death Description

The strongest common conclusion is that Carter died following the fight and resulting medical emergency rather than from a long-term illness.

He died on June 7, 1979.

Age Fifty-Three

Carter was fifty-three years old.

Return to Alabama

His body was returned to Alabama.

DeArmanville Methodist Cemetery

He was buried at DeArmanville Methodist Church cemetery east of Oxford.

Unfinished Work

Carter left planned literary and film projects unfinished.

Little Tree Sequel

He had reportedly begun work on a Little Tree sequel.

Poetry Project

Other literary projects were also incomplete at his death.

Posthumous Growth in Fame

His most controversial book achieved its largest readership only after he died.

1997 Little Tree Film

The Education of Little Tree was later adapted into a film.

Film Adaptation Does Not Authenticate Memoir

A movie adaptation reflects the popularity of the story, not proof of its autobiographical truth.

Educational Use

The book has appeared in classrooms, reading lists, and youth literature discussions.

Teaching Controversy

Educators debate whether the book should be discarded, taught only as fiction, or used specifically to examine authorship, fraud, stereotyping, and historical context.

Cherokee Perspectives

Any educational use claiming to teach Cherokee culture should include actual Cherokee-authored and Cherokee-vetted sources rather than treating Carter as an authority.

Historical Source Value

Carter's speeches, political publications, news coverage, archives, and literary works together provide evidence of how segregationist culture could reinvent itself.

Carter Versus George Wallace

Wallace possessed mass electoral power and later publicly repudiated segregation; Carter was a more ideologically rigid organizer who never made that public transformation.

Carter Versus Bull Connor

Connor controlled municipal police power; Carter's primary power came from propaganda, extremist organization, and speechwriting.

Carter Versus Jim Clark

Clark wielded a sheriff's office and posse; Carter never possessed comparable governmental law-enforcement authority.

Carter Versus Ross Barnett

Barnett controlled a state executive branch; Carter exercised influence largely from outside formal office.

Carter Versus Strom Thurmond

Thurmond converted segregationist politics into a decades-long institutional career; Carter repeatedly failed electorally and eventually abandoned conventional politics.

Carter Versus Nathan Bedford Forrest

Carter deliberately borrowed Forrest's name, but the two belonged to entirely different centuries and political contexts.

Why Carter Belongs in the Antagonist Series

His importance comes from ideological organization and propaganda rather than formal governmental rank.

Words as Instruments of Power

Political speechwriting can shape public policy by supplying slogans through which millions understand conflict.

Organizations as Instruments of Power

Founding extremist groups creates consequences even when the founder is absent from individual acts of violence.

Media as an Instrument of Power

Radio, print, speeches, and later literature allowed Carter to reach audiences far beyond face-to-face organizing.

Historical Responsibility Without Overclaim

Carter can be held responsible for documented leadership, rhetoric, and organization without attributing every crime committed by associates directly to him.

Historical Significance

Asa Earl Carter's life connects World War II veteran experience, Birmingham white supremacy, antisemitic radio propaganda, Citizens' Councils, Klan factionalism, Nat King Cole's assault, Judge Edward Aaron's mutilation, the Clinton desegregation crisis, Bull Connor-era politics, George Wallace, massive resistance rhetoric, western fiction, Confederate memory, Indigenous appropriation, literary hoax, and debates over art and authorship.

Legacy

Carter's most enduring historical significance is the ease with which one person moved between explicit extremist politics and respectable cultural production. The Forrest Carter persona did not erase Asa Carter; it concealed him. Recovering both identities together reveals how political mythology, literary storytelling, racial ideology, and self-invention can occupy the same life.

Geographic Scope

Oxford and Anniston, Alabama, formed Carter's childhood region and later burial area.

Birmingham became the center of his radio propaganda, segregationist organizing, Klan activity, and early political campaigns.

Clinton, Tennessee, became an important example of Carter's willingness to participate in interstate resistance to school desegregation.

Montgomery became central through his association with George and Lurleen Wallace and the 1963 inaugural address.

Florida and Texas became the principal geography of his post-political reinvention.

Sweetwater and Abilene, Texas, were important to his western-writing career and final years.

The fictional American West and Appalachia became the imaginative landscapes through which Forrest Carter constructed his new identity.

Legal and Institutional Context

Carter's career crossed several different legal environments: protected but controversial political speech, private extremist organization, criminal violence by associates, dropped criminal charges against Carter himself, school-desegregation litigation, electoral politics, and later literary deception.

Membership in or leadership of a racist political organization is not by itself equivalent to criminal responsibility for every crime committed by members. Historical attribution therefore requires distinguishing Carter's leadership from attacks he did not personally commit.

The Clinton desegregation crisis occurred under federal court orders implementing Brown v. Board of Education. Carter's political agitation sought to frustrate lawful school integration but did not give him formal governmental authority over Tennessee schools.

As a speechwriter Carter had political influence without executive authority; George Wallace remained legally and politically responsible for gubernatorial actions and public speeches he adopted.

The Little Tree controversy primarily concerns literary fraud, cultural representation, and false autobiographical claims rather than a criminal prosecution.
