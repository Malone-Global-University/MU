Byron De La Beckwith

White Citizens' Council activist, white-supremacist extremist, and convicted murderer who assassinated Mississippi NAACP field secretary Medgar Evers in 1963 and escaped a final verdict for more than three decades

Overview

Byron De La Beckwith was an American salesman, United States Marine veteran, White Citizens' Council activist, Ku Klux Klan associate, white-supremacist propagandist, explosives offender, political candidate, and convicted murderer who assassinated Mississippi NAACP field secretary Medgar Wiley Evers outside Evers's Jackson home on June 12, 1963.

Beckwith was born on November 9, 1920, in Colusa, California, to Susan Southworth Yerger and Byron De La Beckwith Sr.

His father died of pneumonia when Beckwith was five. Beckwith and his mother then moved to Greenwood, Mississippi, where her family lived.

His mother died of lung cancer when Beckwith was twelve, and he was raised thereafter by an uncle and other relatives.

Beckwith attended Greenwood public schools, the Webb School in Bell Buckle, Tennessee, Columbia Military Academy in Tennessee, and briefly Mississippi State University.

In 1942 he enlisted in the United States Marine Corps.

He served in the Pacific during World War II, including at Guadalcanal and Tarawa. He was wounded at Tarawa and received the Purple Heart along with other service decorations.

His genuine wartime service belongs in a complete biography. Military courage and later racial extremism are not mutually exclusive historical facts.

After the war Beckwith married Mary Louise Williams. Their relationship ended and resumed repeatedly before finally ending in divorce; they had a son.

He worked primarily as a travelling salesman, including in fertilizer sales for Delta Liquid Plant Food during the early 1960s.

Beckwith's documented segregationist activism intensified after the Supreme Court's 1954 decision in Brown v. Board of Education.

He joined a White Citizens' Council, part of the organized white resistance movement that sought to preserve racial segregation through political, economic, legal, and social pressure.

In 1956 he applied for work with the Mississippi State Sovereignty Commission, the state agency created to resist federal desegregation and monitor civil-rights activity.

The Commission did not hire him.

His application nevertheless provides direct evidence of his ideology. Mississippi archival records describe him as expressing an intense desire to defeat the NAACP and the forces of integration.

By the early 1960s Medgar Evers represented precisely the civil-rights leadership Beckwith opposed.

Evers was a World War II veteran, insurance salesman, voting-rights organizer, and the NAACP's first state field secretary in Mississippi.

He investigated racial killings, assisted desegregation litigation, organized voter registration, led boycotts, challenged segregated public facilities, and became one of the most visible Black civil-rights leaders in Mississippi.

Evers and his family lived under repeated threat.

The Evers home in Jackson had been attacked before the assassination, including by a firebomb.

On the evening of June 11, 1963, President John F. Kennedy delivered a nationally televised civil-rights address calling racial equality a moral issue.

Shortly after midnight, Evers returned home from movement work.

A rifleman concealed in vegetation roughly two hundred feet from the Evers home fired a single high-powered shot into Evers's back.

The bullet passed through his body, struck objects near or inside the home, and fragmented.

Evers was taken to the hospital but died from massive blood loss.

His wife Myrlie and their children were at home.

Investigators traced the firing position through flattened vegetation and a line of sight toward the house.

Police discovered a concealed, recently fired, sporterized Model 1917 Enfield .30-06 rifle in vegetation near the sniper position.

The rifle had a Golden Hawk telescopic scope.

A latent fingerprint on the scope was matched to Beckwith.

Later FBI testimony compared the latent print both with a modern Beckwith print and with the fingerprint card from his 1942 Marine Corps records.

The rifle itself was traced to Beckwith through prior ownership testimony.

A Mississippi farmer testified that he had traded the Enfield rifle to Beckwith in 1960.

A sporting-goods seller's preserved testimony established that Beckwith purchased the Golden Hawk scope and mount on May 12, 1963, one month before Evers was killed.

Firearms examination established that the spent cartridge in the recovered rifle had been fired by that weapon.

Forensic testimony found the bullet evidence consistent with a .30-06 Enfield rifle.

The FBI also reported evidence that Beckwith had been asking how to locate Evers's home before the assassination.

Investigators photographed a half-moon-shaped injury above Beckwith's eye after his arrest.

Prosecutors argued that the injury was consistent with recoil or contact from the rifle scope.

The defense countered with a witness who said he had already seen an injury around Beckwith's eye two days before the murder.

The physical evidence was therefore strong but not every individual item was uncontested.

Beckwith's principal defense was alibi.

Several Greenwood witnesses testified that they saw him in Greenwood at or around the time when the prosecution placed the assassin in Jackson.

Greenwood is approximately ninety to ninety-five miles from Jackson.

The reliability of the alibi became one of the central disputes in all three trials.

At the 1994 trial the State presented evidence that two Greenwood police officers who later supported Beckwith's alibi had failed to report these supposed sightings when investigators interviewed them shortly after the murder.

The prosecution argued that the late-emerging alibi was unreliable.

Beckwith was arrested on June 23, 1963, and indicted for Evers's murder.

His first state murder trial began in early 1964.

The prosecution presented the rifle, fingerprint, firearms evidence, motive, and other circumstantial proof.

The defense presented the Greenwood alibi and challenged the physical evidence.

The jury could not reach a unanimous verdict.

A mistrial was declared on February 7, 1964.

Mississippi tried Beckwith again in April 1964.

The second jury also deadlocked, and another mistrial was declared on April 17.

The two 1964 juries were all white and all male.

That composition reflected Mississippi's political system, in which Black citizens had been systematically excluded from voting and therefore from voter-based jury pools.

The mistrials were not acquittals.

Because neither jury reached a verdict, the Constitution's Double Jeopardy Clause did not permanently bar another trial.

The political atmosphere surrounding the 1964 trials was extraordinary.

Prominent segregationist politicians publicly showed sympathy toward Beckwith.

Former governor Ross Barnett famously appeared in court and greeted Beckwith during the proceedings.

Such conduct did not constitute legal evidence of innocence or guilt, but it demonstrated the social and political environment in which the juries deliberated.

The White Citizens' Council movement and other segregationists treated Beckwith as a cause célèbre.

Evidence discovered decades later revealed an even more serious institutional problem.

The Mississippi State Sovereignty Commission, an official state agency, secretly assisted Beckwith's defense during the earlier proceedings.

Commission files showed that state investigators gathered information on potential jurors for the benefit of the defense.

The 1997 Mississippi Supreme Court opinion emphasized that the prosecution had not known of this state assistance during the original trials.

The discovery became one of the major reasons prosecutors reconsidered whether the case could lawfully and practically be reopened.

Following the second mistrial, Beckwith was released on $10,000 bond.

No third trial followed during the 1960s.

In 1967 Beckwith ran in the Democratic primary for lieutenant governor of Mississippi.

He finished fifth among six candidates.

His candidacy demonstrated that notoriety as the accused killer of a civil-rights leader could be converted into a political identity, although it did not produce broad enough support to win statewide office.

In March 1969 the original Evers indictment was formally dismissed through a nolle prosequi requested by the succeeding district attorney.

That dismissal did not amount to an acquittal and did not constitutionally prevent a later indictment when additional evidence emerged.

During the years after the mistrials Beckwith became increasingly involved in the extremist white-right milieu.

Mississippi archival material documents correspondence, racist propaganda, Christian Identity material, and Klan-oriented writings associated with his later life.

Former Klansman and FBI informant Delmar Dennis testified that he heard Beckwith speak at a 1965 Klan meeting in Byram, Mississippi.

Dennis testified that Beckwith urged greater violence and made an incriminating statement about a killing.

Other witnesses later testified that Beckwith privately or publicly made statements they interpreted as admissions to killing Evers.

Mary Ann Adams testified that in 1966 she was introduced to Beckwith as the man who killed Evers and that Beckwith responded to her condemnation with a vicious racial comparison rather than a clear denial of having killed him.

Daniel Prince testified that Beckwith later said he had been tried twice for killing a Black man and that he had 'a job to do' and did it.

Peggy Morgan testified that Beckwith told her he had killed Evers and threatened further violence if the conversation became known.

Former FBI informant Dick Davis testified that Beckwith discussed 'selective killings' as a political tactic and said he would not ask someone to do what he had not done himself.

Former Angola correctional officer Mark Reiley later testified that Beckwith confessed to killing Evers while imprisoned in Louisiana.

Because these recollections were made years after the murder, the defense attacked credibility, delay, memory, and motive.

The 1994 jury was required to evaluate those witnesses rather than accept them automatically.

Beckwith's own writings also provided powerful evidence of motive.

The 1994 prosecution introduced racist correspondence and propaganda expressing intense hostility toward Black equality, the NAACP, civil-rights leadership, and perceived political enemies.

The Mississippi Supreme Court later held that such evidence was admissible to show motive and intent.

The letters should not be confused with a direct confession where they do not contain one.

Their evidentiary function was to establish racial motive and the ideological context of the assassination.

Beckwith's extremist activity was not limited to Mississippi.

In September 1973 law-enforcement officers stopped him as he entered the New Orleans area after receiving confidential information that he was bringing an explosive device to attack a civic leader.

Police found a loaded .45-caliber pistol on Beckwith and searched his vehicle.

Inside the car officers discovered a box containing sticks of dynamite, a timing device, wires, additional weapons, ammunition, and a marked New Orleans map.

The confidential information linked the threatened target to A. I. Botnick, a Jewish civil-rights advocate and regional Anti-Defamation League leader.

The exact criminal judgments must be stated carefully.

Beckwith was acquitted in the federal prosecution arising from the 1973 incident.

Louisiana separately prosecuted him under state explosives law.

The state trial judge expressly prevented the prosecution from trying him for an alleged intent to murder or bomb a person in that case.

The Louisiana jury convicted Beckwith specifically of knowingly transporting dynamite within the state without the required license or permit.

He received a five-year sentence.

The Louisiana Supreme Court affirmed the explosives conviction in 1977.

The court held that the police lawfully stopped and searched the vehicle after the informant's detailed predictions were corroborated by Beckwith's arrival, vehicle description, substituted license plate, weapon, and other details.

The court also emphasized that the jury was not deciding whether he intended to harm A. I. Botnick or any other person.

This distinction prevents a common historical error: the suspected assassination plan and the offense of conviction were not legally identical.

Beckwith served roughly three years in the Louisiana State Penitentiary at Angola from 1977 to 1980.

The 1997 Mississippi opinion notes that the Louisiana explosives judgment later became legally complicated; the safest biographical description is that he was convicted, imprisoned under that judgment, and served approximately three years while the later procedural history should not be confused with the separate Evers murder conviction.

While at Angola, Beckwith continued expressing racial and extremist political ideas.

He eventually moved to Tennessee and in 1983 married Thelma Lindsay Neff.

He lived in the Signal Mountain area near Chattanooga.

His later correspondence demonstrates continued engagement with Christian Identity and white-separatist ideology.

There is no credible basis for constructing a late-life racial-repentance narrative.

The Evers case was never forgotten.

Myrlie Evers continued pressing for accountability.

Civil-rights activists, journalists, historians, and prosecutors revisited the unanswered questions surrounding the two mistrials.

The Clarion-Ledger investigation was especially important.

In 1989 the newspaper reported evidence from Mississippi State Sovereignty Commission records showing that the state agency had assisted Beckwith's defense by investigating potential jurors.

That disclosure transformed the procedural history of the case.

Hinds County District Attorney Ed Peters and assistant district attorney Bobby DeLaughter began examining whether a new prosecution was possible.

The investigation searched for surviving physical evidence, witnesses, admissions, transcripts, and constitutional obstacles.

The old rifle survived.

The fingerprint evidence could still be examined.

A 1991 re-autopsy of Evers by forensic pathologist Michael Baden provided additional evidence concerning the wound and weapon type.

New witnesses were willing to testify about Beckwith's alleged admissions.

The State also reevaluated the old Greenwood alibi.

In December 1990 a Hinds County grand jury returned a new murder indictment.

Beckwith was then living in Tennessee.

He fought extradition.

After Tennessee proceedings, he was returned to Mississippi and jailed without bail.

Before trial Beckwith argued that another prosecution violated the constitutional guarantees of double jeopardy, speedy trial, and due process.

In 1992 the Mississippi Supreme Court rejected his argument that the two mistrials and later nolle prosequi permanently barred retrial.

The court emphasized that deadlocked juries are a recognized basis for mistrial and do not produce final acquittals.

The case proceeded toward a third trial.

Because of intense publicity, the court used a Panola County jury while trying the case in Hinds County.

The 1994 jury consisted of eight Black jurors and four white jurors.

That composition differed dramatically from the all-white, all-male juries of 1964.

The racial change in the jury should not be reduced to the claim that Beckwith was convicted simply because Black people finally sat on the jury.

The legal question remained whether the prosecution proved guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.

The changed jury composition instead reflected the enormous transformation of Mississippi voting and jury participation after the Civil Rights Act, Voting Rights Act, federal court decisions, and broader political change.

The prosecution reconstructed the physical evidence.

The rifle found near the sniper position was identified as the one transferred to Beckwith.

The scope was traced to a purchase Beckwith made shortly before the killing.

The fingerprint on the scope was again identified as Beckwith's.

The spent cartridge was matched to the recovered rifle.

Forensic testimony linked the wound to a high-powered .30-06-type weapon.

The State presented evidence of Beckwith's efforts to locate Evers, his racial ideology, his post-murder statements, and weaknesses in the Greenwood alibi.

The defense argued that evidence had been lost during the thirty-year delay, witnesses had died, recollections had deteriorated, and prosecution witnesses were unreliable.

It also argued that the rifle had been stolen and that Beckwith had been in Greenwood.

The passage of time genuinely made the case more difficult for both sides.

Some physical items had disappeared, including evidence discussed in earlier trial records.

Some witnesses could testify only through preserved transcripts.

Other witnesses were dead.

The Mississippi Supreme Court later examined these losses in detail when considering Beckwith's due-process claims.

On February 5, 1994, the jury convicted Byron De La Beckwith of the murder of Medgar Evers.

He was sentenced to life imprisonment.

Unlike the earlier mistrials, the 1994 verdict produced a final state murder conviction.

The conviction formally established what the historical and physical evidence had long indicated: Beckwith murdered Evers.

Beckwith appealed on numerous grounds.

He challenged the decades-long delay, the admission of racial writings, testimony concerning his statements, forensic evidence, jury selection, venue procedure, missing evidence, and other trial rulings.

On December 22, 1997, the Mississippi Supreme Court affirmed the murder conviction and life sentence.

The majority concluded that no reversible error required disturbing the verdict.

The opinion explicitly affirmed the jury's finding that Beckwith murdered Medgar Evers.

The court also treated the Sovereignty Commission's secret assistance to the defense as highly significant to understanding why prosecutors reconsidered the case.

The decision was not unanimous.

Chief Justice Dan Lee dissented and raised serious concerns about the extraordinary delay and prejudice to the defense.

Including the dissent matters because fair legal history should acknowledge that late prosecutions create difficult constitutional questions even when the defendant is ultimately guilty.

The majority nevertheless held that those constitutional concerns did not require dismissal.

Beckwith pursued further state and federal post-conviction challenges.

A federal district court rejected his habeas corpus claims in 2000.

His conviction remained intact.

Beckwith was incarcerated at the Central Mississippi Correctional Facility.

He died on January 21, 2001, at the University of Mississippi Medical Center in Jackson.

He was eighty years old.

He died while serving the life sentence for Evers's murder.

The assassination's historical consequences extend far beyond Beckwith's own biography.

Evers's death occurred during a critical week in the national civil-rights struggle.

Kennedy's June 11 civil-rights address had publicly reframed racial discrimination as a national moral problem.

Evers was murdered only hours later.

The assassination intensified national attention to violent white resistance in Mississippi.

Evers's funeral and burial at Arlington National Cemetery became major public events.

The murder also revealed the weakness of a justice system in which overwhelming evidence could confront juries drawn from a racially exclusionary political order.

The eventual conviction became one of the earliest and most symbolically important modern civil-rights cold-case prosecutions.

It preceded the renewed convictions of other civil-rights-era perpetrators such as Samuel Bowers and Edgar Ray Killen.

Journalist Jerry Mitchell's work on Mississippi cold cases became part of a broader movement to revisit crimes that had escaped punishment during the segregation era.

The case also illustrates the role of Myrlie Evers as more than a victim's widow.

She became an important civil-rights leader in her own right and persisted for decades in demanding that the state reopen the murder investigation.

The State Sovereignty Commission's involvement exposes a second layer of institutional responsibility.

The state did not merely fail to convict Beckwith in 1964.

An official state agency secretly provided investigative help to the defense of the man accused of murdering the state's leading NAACP official.

That fact demonstrates how resistance to civil rights could operate through administrative institutions rather than only through private extremists.

Beckwith's case also shows the distinction between public segregationist politics and direct racial assassination.

White Citizens' Councils, segregationist governors, state legislators, and local officials created the political environment of massive resistance.

Beckwith personally crossed from political white supremacy into intentional lethal violence.

For MGU's antagonist series he therefore belongs in the category of direct racial assassin rather than merely segregationist ideologue.

At the same time, describing him simply as an irrational lone killer would obscure the organized world that shaped and celebrated him.

He moved through White Citizens' Council politics, Klan circles, state anti-integration institutions, extremist religious networks, and public segregationist campaigning.

Prominent white officials treated him respectfully while he stood trial.

A state agency assisted his defense.

Witnesses later described him boasting among fellow extremists.

His crime was individual, but it occurred inside an institutional and ideological ecosystem.

Historical precision requires preserving the hierarchy of evidence.

The 1994 conviction establishes that Beckwith murdered Evers.

The rifle, fingerprint, scope purchase, firearms evidence, and post-crime statements formed major evidentiary pillars.

His White Citizens' Council activism and later Klan involvement establish extremist association and motive.

The suspected 1973 plot against A. I. Botnick is supported by law-enforcement intelligence and the explosives found in Beckwith's vehicle, but the state conviction itself was for unlicensed transportation of dynamite rather than attempted murder.

Reported admissions should be identified as witness testimony rather than silently transformed into verbatim historical fact independent of the trial.

Those distinctions make the case against Beckwith stronger because they separate what is conclusively established from what is contextual, testimonial, or alleged.

Byron De La Beckwith's life demonstrates how white supremacy could move from political organization to assassination, how racially exclusionary institutions could obstruct justice, and how decades-late prosecution could still produce a legally sustainable murder conviction.

Historical Significance

His case connects Citizens' Council politics, the Sovereignty Commission, Klan extremism, Medgar Evers's activism, racial assassination, jury exclusion, state interference, cold-case journalism, constitutional retrial questions, and the transformation of Mississippi's justice system.

Legacy

Beckwith escaped a final verdict in 1964 because two juries deadlocked in a system shaped by racial exclusion and political segregation. Thirty years later a different Mississippi jury convicted him. The delayed judgment did not undo the murder, but it permanently established his criminal responsibility and exposed the institutions that had helped justice fail the first time.

Legal and Constitutional Context

The Beckwith litigation involved murder law, mistrials, double jeopardy, speedy-trial rights, due process, lost evidence, change of venue, racial jury selection, evidentiary use of extremist writings, and habeas corpus review.

Two hung juries in 1964 did not produce acquittals, so retrial was not automatically barred by double jeopardy.

The 1969 nolle prosequi likewise did not transform the unresolved murder case into a final judgment of innocence.

The Mississippi Supreme Court ultimately concluded that the extraordinary delay and loss of some evidence did not constitutionally require dismissal of the renewed prosecution.

The Louisiana explosives case was separate. Beckwith's state conviction concerned unlicensed transportation of explosives, while the trial court prevented alleged intent to harm a civic leader from becoming the offense tried to that jury.
