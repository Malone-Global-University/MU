Robert Barnwell Rhett

South Carolina planter, slaveholder, congressman, senator, newspaper proprietor, Fire-Eater, and long-running architect of southern secession

Overview

Robert Barnwell Rhett (1800-1876), born Robert Barnwell Smith, was a South Carolina lawyer, planter, slaveholder, state legislator, attorney general, United States representative, United States senator, newspaper proprietor, secessionist organizer, and Confederate politician. He became one of the antebellum South's most persistent advocates of disunion and was widely identified as a leading “Fire-Eater.”

Rhett's political career evolved from nullification-era resistance to federal tariff policy into a broader campaign for an independent slaveholding South. Long before Abraham Lincoln's election, he argued that South Carolina should consider separate state action, helped organize the Bluffton Movement, opposed the Compromise of 1850, and promoted southern political organization outside the existing national party system.

His historical significance lies less in administrative achievement than in sustained agitation. Rhett spent decades trying to make secession politically conceivable, used the Charleston Mercury to amplify proslavery and separatist arguments, helped carry South Carolina out of the Union in 1860, and then participated in drafting the permanent Confederate Constitution before becoming a sharp critic of Jefferson Davis's wartime government.

Early Life

Rhett was born Robert Barnwell Smith in Beaufort, South Carolina, on December 21, 1800. He belonged to a politically connected Lowcountry family and grew up within the social world of South Carolina's planter elite.

The political culture surrounding him emphasized landed wealth, state sovereignty, plantation agriculture, and the racial slave system on which much of the region's economy depended.

Legal Education

Rhett received preparatory education, studied law, and was admitted to the South Carolina bar. The U.S. House historian records that he began practicing law in Beaufort in 1824.

Law became both his profession and an important foundation for the constitutional arguments he later used in defense of nullification, state sovereignty, slavery, and secession.

Marriage and Family

Rhett married Elizabeth Washington Burnet. Their family became deeply integrated into South Carolina's plantation and political world, and several of their sons later participated in Confederate military, political, or journalistic life.

After Elizabeth's death, Rhett married Catherine Herbert Dent.

Name Change

Rhett was born Robert Barnwell Smith. The official U.S. House biography records that he changed his surname to Rhett in 1838.

The new surname honored the Rhett line in his ancestry and permanently distinguished him from the many South Carolina politicians bearing the Smith name.

Planter and Slaveholder

Rhett was not only a political defender of slavery but a substantial slaveholder. South Carolina historic-preservation documentation reports that by 1850 he possessed approximately 190 enslaved people on two estates.

His political defense of slavery therefore coincided with a direct material interest in plantation agriculture and human property.

The Enslaved Population

The people enslaved on Rhett's plantations were the labor force underlying a significant portion of his wealth and planter status. Census-era slave schedules often recorded enslaved people by age, sex, and color rather than by name, making individual reconstruction more difficult.

A balanced biography must therefore avoid allowing Rhett's political papers to make the enslaved community invisible. His arguments about property and southern liberty concerned a system that denied those people legal ownership of themselves.

Plantation Wealth

Rhett's wealth grew through plantation investment as well as legal and political connections. Plantation ownership gave him economic independence and social authority within the political class whose interests he defended.

It also embedded his public ideology in the practical economy of coerced Black labor.

South Carolina House of Representatives

Rhett entered the South Carolina House of Representatives in 1826 and was reelected in 1828, 1830, and 1832.

His rise coincided with the intensifying southern reaction against protective tariffs and the emergence of constitutional theories asserting that states could resist federal laws considered unconstitutional.

Tariff Politics

Protective tariffs became one of Rhett's earliest major political causes. South Carolina planters argued that federal tariff policy benefited northern manufacturers while raising the cost of imported goods and distorting the southern export economy.

Rhett came to treat tariff resistance as part of a larger struggle over whether a federal majority could impose policies on a regional minority.

Nullification

During the Nullification Crisis, Rhett stood among South Carolina's most militant advocates of resistance. Nullifiers claimed that a state could declare a federal law unconstitutional and prevent its enforcement within the state's borders.

The immediate controversy concerned tariffs, but the constitutional logic later became increasingly linked to slavery as antislavery politics expanded.

Radicalism During the Nullification Crisis

Rhett's rhetoric during the crisis was often more radical than that of politicians seeking compromise. He treated the possibility of state resistance, confrontation, and even disunion as legitimate responses to what he regarded as unconstitutional federal power.

This early willingness to imagine separation distinguished him from many southern politicians who defended state sovereignty while still treating preservation of the Union as a primary objective.

Relationship with John C. Calhoun

John C. Calhoun became the dominant intellectual figure in South Carolina's states' rights politics, and Rhett generally moved within the constitutional world Calhoun helped construct.

Yet Rhett was often more impatient than Calhoun. Where Calhoun frequently sought coordinated southern action and constitutional safeguards inside the Union, Rhett became increasingly willing to contemplate unilateral South Carolina action and outright separation.

South Carolina Attorney General

In 1832 Rhett was elected attorney general of South Carolina. He held the office until 1837.

The position gave him statewide legal standing during the aftermath of the Nullification Crisis and before his entry into national politics.

Election to Congress

Rhett was elected as a Democrat to the U.S. House of Representatives and began service on March 4, 1837.

He remained in the House through March 3, 1849, serving six consecutive terms. This was the longest sustained period of formal federal office in his career.

Congressional Career

In Congress Rhett defended southern agricultural interests, opposed protective tariffs, supported slavery, and became increasingly distrustful of national compromises that required the slaveholding South to accept the decisions of northern majorities.

His congressional career transformed him from a South Carolina radical into a nationally visible sectional politician.

Tariff of 1842

The Tariff of 1842 renewed Rhett's hostility to protective economic policy. He regarded the measure as another example of federal power being used for the benefit of northern manufacturing interests.

The controversy helped produce the most important early secessionist campaign of his career.

The Bluffton Movement

On July 31, 1844, Rhett helped launch what became known as the Bluffton Movement in South Carolina's Lowcountry.

The movement demanded forceful state action against the tariff and placed secession or renewed nullification on the political agenda. Rhett's speech at Bluffton became an important milestone in his reputation as an advocate of separate state action.

Separate State Action

Rhett was more willing than many contemporaries to argue that South Carolina should act even without waiting for all other slaveholding states.

This position created a strategic divide among states' rights leaders. The issue was not whether southern interests required protection, but whether South Carolina should risk isolation by moving first.

Calhoun Rejects the Bluffton Strategy

Calhoun rejected the Bluffton strategy and feared that premature state action would divide southern Democrats and damage broader political objectives.

Without Calhoun's support, the Bluffton Movement lost momentum. The episode demonstrated that Rhett remained ahead of much of his own state's political establishment in openly contemplating disunion.

Why Bluffton Matters

The failure of the Bluffton Movement is as important as its existence. In 1844 Rhett could not yet persuade South Carolina's political leadership that secession was the proper response to sectional conflict.

Sixteen years later, the state unanimously adopted secession. Rhett's career spans much of the political transformation that made that change possible.

Slavery and Territorial Expansion

The acquisition of western territory after the Mexican-American War shifted sectional politics from tariffs toward the expansion of slavery.

Rhett opposed restrictions that would prevent slaveholders from taking enslaved people into federal territories and increasingly treated territorial exclusion as evidence that the existing Union could not protect the South's social system.

The Wilmot Proviso

The Wilmot Proviso proposed excluding slavery from territory acquired from Mexico. Although it never became permanent federal law, the proposal convinced southern radicals that a growing northern political coalition intended to confine slavery geographically.

For Rhett, restriction of slavery's expansion was not a peripheral policy dispute but a warning about the future balance of power between free and slave states.

Compromise of 1850

Rhett opposed the Compromise of 1850. The package attempted to settle disputes arising from western expansion through a series of measures affecting California, territorial government, Texas, the slave trade in Washington, D.C., and fugitive slaves.

Rhett believed continued compromise merely postponed a fundamental conflict between slaveholding and non-slaveholding sections.

Nashville Convention

Rhett participated in the Nashville Convention of 1850, where southern delegates gathered to discuss responses to restrictions on slavery and the growing sectional crisis.

He belonged to the radical wing that wanted stronger collective southern resistance and was disappointed when the convention failed to endorse disunion.

Southern National Party

After the Nashville Convention, Rhett joined William Lowndes Yancey and other radicals in attempting to organize a Southern National Party.

The project was short-lived, but it is important because it shows Rhett experimenting with a political structure explicitly organized around sectional rather than national interests.

Election to the U.S. Senate

In December 1850 the South Carolina legislature elected Rhett to the U.S. Senate to fill the vacancy that ultimately followed the death of John C. Calhoun.

He entered the Senate as one of the country's best-known southern-rights radicals.

Senate Service

Rhett served from December 18, 1850, until his resignation became effective on May 7, 1852.

His Senate tenure was brief because political developments inside South Carolina undermined his immediate strategy for secession.

The Cooperationist Victory

South Carolina divided between immediate secessionists and “Cooperationists,” who opposed acting alone and wanted coordinated action with other slave states.

The Cooperationists prevailed politically. Rhett's faction failed to secure immediate separate-state secession, and the defeat sharply reduced his influence inside formal state government.

Resignation from the Senate

After the defeat of the immediate-secession movement, Rhett resigned from the U.S. Senate in 1852.

The resignation marked a temporary retreat from elected office but not from political agitation. He increasingly relied on writing, newspaper influence, private networks, and convention politics.

The Charleston Mercury

Rhett and his family became closely associated with the Charleston Mercury, which developed into one of the leading newspapers of southern radicalism during the 1850s.

The National Park Service identifies Rhett as the paper's owner, while his son Robert Barnwell Rhett Jr. became its principal editor.

Newspaper as Political Instrument

The Mercury gave Rhett something electoral office could not: a continuing platform for attacking compromise, defending slavery, promoting southern unity, and normalizing the possibility of disunion.

It helped transform secession from an episodic protest into a sustained political program.

The Rhett Family Political Network

Rhett's political influence increasingly operated through family as well as formal office. His son Robert Barnwell Rhett Jr. became an important editor and political advocate, while other sons served the Confederate cause.

The family network linked plantation wealth, newspapers, politics, and military service.

Fire-Eater Leadership

By the 1850s Rhett was widely identified as one of the principal “Fire-Eaters,” the southern radicals who believed the slave states might need to leave the Union to secure their political and social system.

Unlike politicians who became secessionists only after Lincoln's election, Rhett had spent years trying to create a constituency for disunion.

Meaning of 'Father of Secession'

Rhett was later called the “Father of Secession” or the “Great Secessionist.” The titles do not mean that he single-handedly caused the Civil War or invented the doctrine of secession.

They reflect the extraordinary duration of his advocacy. He argued for increasingly radical state action long before secession became majority policy in South Carolina.

Defense of Slavery

Rhett's sectional politics consistently defended slavery. He regarded slaveholding as central to southern economic life, political independence, and social order.

His mature secession argument treated antislavery political power in the North as incompatible with the long-term security of slave states.

White Supremacy

Rhett's defense of slavery rested on a racial order in which white political authority and Black subordination were taken as foundational features of southern society.

He did not advocate a multiracial democracy outside the Union. The independent South he envisioned was explicitly a slaveholding political order.

African Slave Trade Debate

During the 1850s southern radicals debated whether the United States should reopen the transatlantic African slave trade, which federal law had prohibited beginning in 1808.

Rhett's papers include correspondence concerning the issue, demonstrating that reopening the trade was part of the political world surrounding his later proslavery activism.

Rhett and Reopening the Trade

Rhett was sympathetic to arguments for reopening the African slave trade, although historians have noted that his tactical position could vary according to political circumstances.

The issue illustrates how far portions of the southern radical movement had moved by the late 1850s: some advocates were no longer satisfied merely to protect existing slavery but discussed reviving legal overseas importation of enslaved Africans.

John Brown's Raid

John Brown's 1859 raid on Harpers Ferry intensified southern fears that antislavery activism could produce armed attacks and slave insurrection.

For Rhett and other long-standing separatists, the raid reinforced the argument that the cultural and political gap between North and South had become irreconcilable.

Republican Party

The rise of the Republican Party gave Rhett a powerful new political target. Republicans opposed the expansion of slavery into western territories, although the party did not initially campaign for immediate federal abolition inside states where slavery already existed.

Rhett nevertheless treated Republican national power as an existential danger to slavery.

Election of 1860

Abraham Lincoln's election in November 1860 created the political opening Rhett had anticipated for years.

Rather than seeking another national compromise, Rhett urged immediate separation and argued that continued participation in a Union governed by a sectional antislavery party would eventually destroy the slaveholding South.

South Carolina Secession Convention

Rhett was elected as a delegate to South Carolina's secession convention. On December 20, 1860, he joined the other delegates in the unanimous vote adopting the state's ordinance of secession.

For Rhett, this represented the culmination of a political campaign extending back decades.

Address to the Slaveholding States

Rhett wrote the convention's Address of the People of South Carolina, Assembled in Convention, to the People of the Slaveholding States of the United States.

The document urged other slave states to join South Carolina and form a new confederation. It provides unusually direct evidence of how Rhett connected constitutional complaints to protection of slavery.

A Slaveholding Confederacy

Rhett's 1860 address described the North and South as increasingly separate peoples and argued that slaveholding states could not safely remain politically subordinate to non-slaveholding states.

He expressly called for a confederacy of slaveholding states. This makes it historically inaccurate to separate his secession program from the institution of slavery.

Tariff and Slavery Together

Rhett's secession argument contained more than one grievance. He continued to condemn protective tariffs, federal spending patterns, and what he regarded as consolidation of national power.

At the same time, his own 1860 argument identified slavery as the crucial sectional institution threatened by northern political ascendancy. The two themes should be treated together rather than reducing his politics to either tariffs alone or slavery alone.

Formation of the Confederacy

After South Carolina and other Deep South states seceded, delegates met in Montgomery, Alabama, to create the Confederate States of America.

Rhett represented South Carolina in the Provisional Confederate Congress beginning in 1861.

Ambition for Confederate Leadership

Rhett had spent so many years advocating southern independence that he expected substantial influence in the new government.

He did not receive the presidency or another dominant executive position. Jefferson Davis emerged as president, leaving Rhett influential but dissatisfied.

Permanent Confederate Constitution

Rhett became chairman of the committee that reported the permanent Constitution of the Confederate States.

The role placed him at the center of translating decades of southern constitutional criticism into the institutional structure of the new government.

Constitutional Changes

The Confederate Constitution retained much of the structure and language of the United States Constitution while making several significant changes.

Among other provisions, it explicitly protected slave property in Confederate territories, restricted protective tariffs and certain internal-improvement spending, and established a single six-year presidential term.

Explicit Constitutional Protection of Slavery

The Confederate constitutional settlement provided stronger textual protection for slavery than the United States Constitution had used in its original euphemistic references to enslaved people.

That feature was consistent with Rhett's long-standing demand that a southern political system directly secure slaveholder property interests.

The Presidency

Rhett's failure to secure the Confederate presidency intensified his dissatisfaction with the new administration. He had spent decades advancing secession but found himself outside the highest level of executive power once separation occurred.

This gap between revolutionary agitation and post-secession governance became central to his Confederate career.

Jefferson Davis

Rhett became a severe critic of Confederate president Jefferson Davis. The disagreement involved personality, patronage, military policy, executive power, and fundamental questions about how centralized the Confederacy should become during war.

States' Rights Versus Wartime Centralization

The Civil War exposed a structural contradiction within the secessionist movement. A government created in the name of state sovereignty needed taxation, conscription, military coordination, economic regulation, and executive authority to wage a modern war.

Rhett believed Davis repeatedly concentrated too much power in the Confederate central government.

Departure from the Provisional Congress

Rhett served one year in the Provisional Confederate Congress and did not become a major officeholder in the permanent Confederate government.

His political influence during the remainder of the war operated chiefly through criticism and the Charleston Mercury.

The Mercury During the Civil War

The Charleston Mercury remained strongly committed to Confederate independence while frequently attacking the Davis administration.

This distinction is important: criticism of Davis did not make Rhett anti-Confederate. He believed the Confederate government was mishandling the cause he had spent decades promoting.

Criticism of Confederate Generals

The Mercury also criticized military leadership and wartime policy. Its editorial independence made it one of the most prominent internal critics of the Confederate administration.

Rhett's wartime politics thus combined unwavering commitment to southern independence with deep suspicion of the government attempting to achieve it.

Fort Sumter and the Rhett Family

Rhett's family was directly involved in the opening military confrontation at Charleston Harbor. His son Alfred M. Rhett served as a Confederate artillery officer in the defenses around Fort Moultrie.

The transition from political secession to armed war therefore touched Rhett's household as well as his public ideology.

Slavery During the Confederate War

The Confederate project Rhett supported depended heavily on enslaved labor throughout the war. Enslaved people worked on plantations, fortifications, transportation systems, and other parts of the southern economy while also escaping to Union lines in large numbers when opportunities arose.

Union military policy and Black self-emancipation progressively transformed the war into a struggle that destroyed the legal institution Rhett had sought to protect.

Emancipation

The Emancipation Proclamation of 1863 declared enslaved people free in areas under rebellion, subject to its legal and military limits. Black men also entered United States military service in large numbers.

The Thirteenth Amendment, ratified in 1865, abolished slavery throughout the United States except as punishment for crime.

Collapse of the Confederacy

The Confederacy collapsed in 1865. Union victory destroyed the separate slaveholding republic Rhett had worked for decades to create.

The defeat also devastated much of the wealth, political influence, and social order of the planter class with which he had been identified.

Flight from Charleston

Rhett family papers preserve accounts of the family's departure from Charleston as Union forces approached and Confederate control collapsed.

These family records provide a personal view of the destruction of the political system Rhett had helped build.

Postwar Move to Louisiana

In 1867 Rhett moved to St. James Parish, Louisiana.

His postwar life was far less politically influential than his antebellum career. He never regained the national prominence he had possessed as a secessionist organizer.

Reconstruction

Reconstruction established new constitutional conditions Rhett had spent his career opposing: slavery was abolished, Black citizenship was constitutionally recognized, and Black male voting rights received federal protection.

Rhett lived long enough to see the political system of the slaveholding South formally dismantled, even as white resistance to Reconstruction sought to restore racial domination through new means.

Memoir

In later life Rhett worked on autobiographical and historical recollections of secession and the Confederacy.

His surviving memoir is useful as a primary source but must be read critically. Like most retrospective political memoirs, it reflects memory, self-justification, personal rivalries, and an author's desire to shape how posterity understands his role.

Health

Rhett suffered serious health problems in later life, including a disfiguring cancerous growth affecting his face.

His physical condition deteriorated during the postwar years.

Death

Robert Barnwell Rhett died in St. James Parish, Louisiana, on September 14, 1876.

He was seventy-five years old.

Burial

Rhett was buried in Magnolia Cemetery in Charleston, South Carolina.

His burial in Charleston returned him symbolically to the city that had served as one of the most important centers of his secessionist political and journalistic activity.

Was Rhett the Sole 'Father of Secession'?

No. Secession emerged from decades of sectional conflict and involved many politicians, editors, planters, activists, and voters across the slave states.

Rhett's claim to the title rests on persistence rather than sole authorship: he advocated disunion earlier, longer, and more consistently than most nationally prominent southern politicians.

Was Rhett's Politics Mainly About Tariffs?

Tariffs were central to Rhett's early radicalization, particularly during the Nullification Crisis and Bluffton Movement.

By the sectional crises of the 1850s and 1860, however, his own speeches and writings explicitly treated the protection of slavery and the political security of slaveholding states as central reasons for separation.

Was Rhett a Slaveholder?

Yes. Rhett was a substantial plantation owner and slaveholder. South Carolina historic documentation places approximately 190 enslaved people on his two estates by 1850.

This direct economic relationship to slavery is essential context for evaluating his constitutional and political arguments.

Was Rhett a Confederate Executive Leader?

No. Despite his long secessionist career, Rhett did not become Confederate president, cabinet secretary, or a dominant wartime executive official.

His principal formal Confederate role was service in the Provisional Congress and chairmanship of the committee reporting the permanent constitution.

Was His Criticism of Davis Unionist?

No. Rhett's attacks on Jefferson Davis did not represent reconciliation with the United States.

He remained committed to Confederate independence but believed Davis's policies violated the political principles and strategic interests of the Confederacy.

Comparison with John C. Calhoun

Calhoun provided much of the constitutional language of southern minority rights, state sovereignty, and defense of slavery. Rhett inherited that intellectual environment but was often more willing to proceed rapidly toward separate state action.

Calhoun died in 1850 before secession became state policy. Rhett lived to help turn the theory of disunion into an actual Confederate government.

Comparison with William Lowndes Yancey

William Lowndes Yancey of Alabama and Rhett became two of the best-known Fire-Eaters. Both spent years attempting to make southern separation politically acceptable before 1860.

Yancey's greatest weapon was public oratory and Democratic convention politics; Rhett combined congressional experience, planter influence, convention activity, and the sustained editorial power of the Charleston Mercury.

Comparison with Robert Toombs

Robert Toombs remained committed to preserving the Union for much longer than Rhett, provided slavery received sufficient constitutional protection.

Rhett's willingness to embrace disunion earlier distinguishes him from the large group of proslavery leaders who became secessionists only during the final crisis after Lincoln's election.

Comparison with Alexander H. Stephens

Alexander H. Stephens initially opposed immediate secession in Georgia before accepting Confederate leadership after his state withdrew from the Union.

Rhett was nearly the reverse case: he spent years advocating secession but received less authority than he expected once the Confederacy actually existed.

Comparison with James Henry Hammond

James Henry Hammond became famous as a proslavery theorist and senator who articulated the “mudsill” and “King Cotton” arguments.

Rhett was less important as a systematic social theorist. His distinctive contribution was political agitation—turning proslavery sectional grievances into an organized program of disunion.

Historical Significance

Rhett is historically significant because he demonstrates that secession was not an automatic reaction produced suddenly by Lincoln's election. It required intellectual preparation, political organization, repeated failed campaigns, newspaper propaganda, convention strategy, and gradual normalization of disunion.

For African American history, his role is especially important because the political system he sought to protect rested on racial slavery. His own 1860 appeal for southern unity explicitly urged a confederation of slaveholding states.

Legacy

Rhett's long career links the Nullification Crisis of the 1830s to the Confederate experiment of the 1860s. He began by resisting federal tariff power, progressively made slavery and sectional sovereignty central to his politics, and eventually helped establish the government that attempted to make southern independence permanent.

His later conflict with Jefferson Davis also demonstrates the limits of revolutionary unity. Rhett was highly effective at attacking the old Union and promoting secession but substantially less successful at shaping the wartime government that followed.

Geographic Scope

Beaufort and the South Carolina Lowcountry formed Rhett's birthplace, early legal environment, and plantation base. Walterboro and Charleston became major centers of his political, residential, and newspaper activity.

Washington, D.C. was the arena for his long congressional service. Bluffton became associated with his 1844 separatist agitation, while Nashville hosted the 1850 southern convention in which he participated.

Montgomery, Alabama became the birthplace of the Confederate government in which Rhett served. After the Civil War he relocated to St. James Parish, Louisiana, where he died.

Legal and Constitutional Context

Rhett's career crossed several major constitutional controversies: protective tariffs, nullification, federal versus state sovereignty, slavery in the territories, fugitive-slave obligations, secession, and the design of the Confederate government.

His political development shows how constitutional arguments about minority protection and state sovereignty could become instruments for defending a racial slave system. By 1860 Rhett explicitly argued that slaveholding states required political independence from non-slaveholding majorities.
