Ross Barnett

Mississippi governor, segregationist, and massive-resistance leader who used state agencies, prisons, police power, and direct executive obstruction against civil-rights activists and James Meredith's integration of the University of Mississippi

Overview

Ross Robert Barnett was an American lawyer, Democratic politician, segregationist, and governor of Mississippi from 1960 through 1964 whose administration became one of the most visible state-level opponents of the modern Civil Rights Movement.

Barnett was born on January 22, 1898, at Standing Pine in Leake County, Mississippi. He grew up in a large rural family, served in the United States Army during World War I, worked his way through college, and became one of Mississippi's most successful trial lawyers.

He graduated from Mississippi College in 1922 and earned a law degree from the University of Mississippi in 1926. He established a law practice in Jackson and built a statewide reputation as an effective courtroom advocate, particularly in damage suits.

Barnett was not a career officeholder before becoming governor. The governorship was the only public elective office he ever held and the only office for which he repeatedly campaigned.

He ran unsuccessfully for governor in 1951 and 1955, won the office in 1959, and attempted a final comeback in 1967.

Barnett's governorship combined two records that must be preserved together. His administration promoted industrial recruitment, created industrial parks, amended workers' compensation law, enacted a right-to-work law, and claimed more than forty thousand new jobs during his term.

At the same time, Barnett made preservation of racial segregation one of the defining purposes of his administration. He aligned the governor's office with Mississippi's massive-resistance system and publicly promised that public schools would remain segregated while he was governor.

Barnett's segregationism operated through speeches, state agencies, police power, the Mississippi State Sovereignty Commission, cooperation with Citizens' Council networks, legislation, and direct resistance to federal court orders.

His administration inherited the Mississippi State Sovereignty Commission, a state agency created in 1956 to defend segregation and resist federal civil-rights enforcement. Barnett expanded its investigative and propaganda functions.

Under Barnett, Sovereignty Commission investigators travelled the state, collected information on civil-rights organizers, monitored institutions and individuals considered threats to segregation, used informants and private investigators, and developed public-relations campaigns defending Mississippi's racial order.

The Commission also provided taxpayer money to Citizens' Council organizations. State archival records document large grants during and after Barnett's administration, demonstrating an unusually direct financial relationship between state government and organized segregationist activism.

Barnett's first major national civil-rights confrontation came during the Freedom Rides of 1961.

Freedom Riders challenged segregation in interstate bus and rail facilities after federal court decisions had invalidated such discrimination. After riders were subjected to mob violence in Alabama, activists continued into Mississippi.

Mississippi officials adopted a different strategy from the uncontrolled mob attacks seen in Alabama: police arrested arriving Freedom Riders in Jackson on charges such as breach of peace.

Large numbers of riders refused bail and filled local jails. Barnett's administration transferred many of them to the Mississippi State Penitentiary at Parchman.

The transfer was intended to break the campaign through harsh confinement. Civil-rights historical sources preserve Barnett's instruction that prison officials should 'break their spirits, not their bones.'

Parchman's treatment of Freedom Riders included isolation, humiliating searches, deliberate deprivation, intimidation, and other abuses. Sending nonviolent protesters to the state's notorious prison system made Barnett an important antagonist of the Freedom Rides.

The arrests did not end the campaign. Continued rides helped force stronger federal enforcement against segregation in interstate transportation facilities.

Barnett's most famous confrontation came in 1962 when James Howard Meredith sought admission to the University of Mississippi.

Meredith was a Black Mississippi native, Air Force veteran, and student at Jackson State College who deliberately challenged the racial exclusion of the state's flagship public university.

He applied to the University of Mississippi in January 1961. University officials rejected his application through administrative explanations that federal courts eventually found to be racially discriminatory.

The NAACP Legal Defense Fund supported Meredith's litigation, with lawyers including Thurgood Marshall and Constance Baker Motley participating in the legal struggle.

In June 1962 the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit concluded that Meredith had been rejected because of race and ordered his admission.

Segregationist officials continued legal and administrative maneuvers to delay enforcement.

On September 10, 1962, Supreme Court Justice Hugo Black set aside remaining stays that interfered with Meredith's admission.

Three days later Barnett addressed Mississippi by television and radio, denouncing federal action and declaring that the state would not surrender to what he called illegal federal tyranny.

Barnett cast the Meredith dispute as a contest between Mississippi sovereignty and federal coercion. In constitutional reality, the conflict concerned whether a state could use public authority to deny a citizen equal access to a state university because of race after binding federal court orders required admission.

Mississippi officials created additional obstacles. Meredith was prosecuted on a voter-registration charge that the Fifth Circuit had characterized as frivolous, and the legislature enacted a measure designed to block enrollment by people convicted of crimes involving moral turpitude.

The state College Board gave Barnett authority to act as registrar so that he could personally obstruct Meredith.

On September 20, 1962, Meredith arrived at the University of Mississippi accompanied by federal officials. Barnett personally stood in his way and refused to allow registration.

Five days later Barnett again blocked Meredith, this time when federal officials attempted registration through the state College Board office.

The Fifth Circuit issued orders directing Barnett not to interfere and required him to explain why he should not be held in contempt.

Privately, Barnett was speaking with Attorney General Robert F. Kennedy in an effort to find a face-saving arrangement that would permit Meredith's admission without making the governor appear to surrender politically.

One proposed plan called for federal marshals to confront Barnett publicly, allowing him to step aside only after a dramatic display of federal force. The plan collapsed because of concerns about the growing segregationist crowd and the danger that staged political theater could become real violence.

The private negotiations reveal an important distinction between Barnett's public rhetoric and his practical understanding of federal power. He knew that the federal government could ultimately enforce the court order and sought a way to preserve his political standing while avoiding direct armed conflict.

On September 28 the Fifth Circuit held Barnett in civil contempt and threatened a fine of ten thousand dollars for each day he continued defying the court.

Lieutenant Governor Paul B. Johnson Jr. was also held in contempt after participating in resistance.

President John F. Kennedy issued a proclamation on September 29 ordering Mississippi officials and citizens obstructing federal law to cease and disperse.

Federal authorities prepared a large force of deputy United States marshals and special deputies to escort Meredith onto the Oxford campus.

On September 30, federal officials brought Meredith to the University of Mississippi and placed him under guard in a campus dormitory.

A large segregationist crowd gathered around the Lyceum. The gathering included students but increasingly drew armed outsiders and militant segregationists from beyond the university.

Mississippi Highway Patrol officers were present, but state authorities failed to prevent the crowd from escalating into a violent riot.

Rioters attacked federal marshals with rocks, bricks, bottles, fire, and gunfire. The marshals initially operated under orders designed to avoid lethal force and relied heavily on tear gas.

The violence killed two people and wounded scores of others, including many federal officers.

President Kennedy federalized the Mississippi National Guard and dispatched federal troops to restore order.

By the morning of October 1, military force had secured the campus.

James Meredith registered and began attending classes under federal protection.

For the following year deputy marshals provided extensive protective coverage while Meredith endured harassment and threats.

Meredith graduated from the University of Mississippi in August 1963 with a degree in political science.

The Ole Miss crisis was a defeat for Barnett's constitutional position. Mississippi did not possess lawful authority to nullify federal court orders requiring equal protection.

It was also a political spectacle deliberately shaped by Barnett's segregationist rhetoric and face-saving strategy. His public resistance encouraged supporters to believe Mississippi could or should block the federal government even when he privately understood that ultimate enforcement was likely.

The riot cannot responsibly be reduced to a claim that Barnett personally ordered individual rioters to kill or injure people. The armed mob contained actors beyond his direct command.

Barnett nevertheless bears substantial political and executive responsibility for escalating a lawful university admission into a confrontation between state and federal authority, repeatedly physically obstructing Meredith, mobilizing segregationist public opinion, and failing to create a reliable state security arrangement capable of preventing the final riot.

The federal courts pursued contempt proceedings against Barnett, but he ultimately did not serve a jail sentence or pay the threatened daily civil fine. Later proceedings were terminated after compliance and changed circumstances.

The absence of imprisonment should not be described as a legal victory establishing that Barnett's resistance had been constitutional.

The Fifth Circuit later stated plainly that Barnett had intended physically to obstruct the court's decree admitting Meredith.

Barnett's use of the Sovereignty Commission intensified during the same period. The agency promoted segregation through speakers, films such as Message from Mississippi, investigations, informants, and financial assistance to Citizens' Council activity.

The Commission's activities demonstrate that massive resistance in Mississippi was not merely private prejudice. The state government itself devoted public money and investigative resources to defending racial segregation and monitoring people who challenged it.

Barnett's administration also intersected with the case of Clyde Kennard, a Black Korean War-era veteran who had sought admission to Mississippi Southern College, now the University of Southern Mississippi.

Kennard had been convicted in 1960 on charges later shown to be false and was imprisoned at Parchman. While he suffered terminal cancer, civil-rights advocates and his family urged Barnett to release him.

Barnett resisted the campaign for months and finally suspended Kennard's sentence in early 1963 when his illness was already advanced.

Kennard died in July 1963. His conviction was later completely overturned after evidence demonstrated that the principal accusation had been fabricated.

Barnett's handling of Kennard is historically important because it occurred alongside the Meredith crisis and illustrates the broader system of state resistance surrounding Black access to higher education.

In June 1963 Mississippi NAACP field secretary Medgar Evers was assassinated outside his Jackson home by white supremacist Byron De La Beckwith.

No reliable evidence establishes that Barnett ordered Evers's murder, and the assassination should not be attributed to him as a criminal conspiracy without proof.

Barnett's administration nevertheless existed within a political system that used state surveillance, segregationist propaganda, economic pressure, and public resistance against the movement Evers represented.

Barnett also opposed proposed federal civil-rights legislation in congressional testimony in 1963.

His gubernatorial term ended in January 1964 because Mississippi governors at that time could not immediately succeed themselves.

Shortly after leaving office, Barnett appeared at one of Byron De La Beckwith's trials for the murder of Medgar Evers and publicly shook the defendant's hand in the courtroom. The gesture became one of the starkest symbols of elite white solidarity surrounding the accused assassin.

Both 1964 trials ended with all-white juries unable to reach verdicts. De La Beckwith was finally convicted of Evers's murder in 1994.

Barnett continued defending segregationist politics after leaving office and supported Barry Goldwater in the 1964 presidential election after Goldwater opposed the Civil Rights Act on constitutional grounds.

Barnett attempted a political comeback in the 1967 Mississippi governor's race but finished well behind the leading candidates.

He returned to law practice and remained a popular speaker at traditional Mississippi political gatherings such as the Neshoba County Fair.

Unlike some former segregationist governors who later issued public apologies or significantly repudiated their earlier positions, Barnett remained largely unapologetic about his role in the Ole Miss crisis.

Late in life, when asked about the confrontation, he continued to defend his actions and said he had no apology to make.

Barnett died in Jackson on November 6, 1987, at age eighty-nine.

He was buried near his birthplace in Leake County.

His name was placed on public works and civic sites during and after his lifetime, reflecting how many white Mississippians continued to honor his governorship even as national historical memory increasingly centered on his resistance to civil rights.

Ross Barnett's historical significance lies in the combination of conventional state governance and deliberate constitutional obstruction. He was a capable lawyer and successful industrial-recruitment governor who used those same political skills and the authority of the governor's office to defend racial segregation.

For MGU's antagonist series, Barnett is best understood as one of the clearest examples of segregation maintained not merely by private prejudice or mob violence but by coordinated state action: law, police, prisons, surveillance, propaganda, public money, and executive resistance to federal constitutional rights.

After leaving office, Barnett continued appearing before groups committed to segregationist or white conservative politics.

No Major Public Repudiation

Barnett did not become known for a late-life apology comparable to George Wallace's public repudiation of segregation.

1982 Ole Miss Anniversary Comments

Twenty years after the Meredith crisis, Barnett continued defending his actions rather than apologizing.

No Regrets Position

His late-life comments indicated that he still believed his conduct had been justified.

Death

Ross Barnett died in Jackson on November 6, 1987.

Age Eighty-Nine

He was eighty-nine years old.

Burial Near Standing Pine

Barnett was buried in Leake County near the rural community in which he had been born.

Ross Barnett Reservoir

A major reservoir near Jackson was named for Barnett, illustrating the civic honors attached to his governorship.

Public Memory

Barnett's name remains inseparable from debates over whether public honors should commemorate economic-development achievements while ignoring segregationist resistance.

Lawyer and Demagogue

Barnett's legal intelligence made his constitutional obstruction more significant: he was not unfamiliar with courts, orders, or procedural authority.

States' Rights as Racial Policy

Federalism can involve legitimate disputes unrelated to race, but Barnett's most famous states' rights claims were deployed specifically to preserve racial exclusion.

Segregation as State Policy

Mississippi's racial order during Barnett's governorship was supported by laws, educational policy, police, prisons, state surveillance, and public money.

Citizens' Council Versus Ku Klux Klan

The Citizens' Council presented itself as respectable and lawful rather than clandestine and violent, but it used economic coercion and political power to preserve white supremacy.

Barnett and the Klan

Barnett should not automatically be classified as a Ku Klux Klan operational leader merely because he was a segregationist. His documented institutional alliances centered more clearly on Citizens' Council and state massive-resistance structures.

Violence and Respectable Segregation

The Barnett era demonstrates how formally respectable officials and organizations could sustain conditions in which racial violence operated even when they did not personally commit every violent act.

Freedom Riders Versus Meredith

The Freedom Riders challenged segregation through mass direct action; Meredith challenged a university through individual application and litigation. Barnett used different state mechanisms against both.

Parchman as Political Weapon

The use of Mississippi's penitentiary against nonviolent protesters shows how ordinary criminal institutions could become instruments of racial-political control.

Sovereignty Commission as Political Intelligence

The Commission blurred the line between legitimate state administration and surveillance of lawful political dissent.

State Propaganda

Public money funded messaging designed to persuade Americans that segregation represented harmonious race relations rather than enforced inequality.

Mississippi's Closed Society

Contemporary critics described Mississippi as a closed society in which political, economic, journalistic, and institutional pressure narrowed the space for white as well as Black dissent.

Economic Progress Without Civil Equality

Barnett's industrial achievements demonstrate that modernization does not automatically produce equal citizenship.

Was Barnett Only a Figurehead?

No. He personally intervened in the Meredith case, directed the executive branch, shaped the Sovereignty Commission, and became the public face of resistance.

Was Barnett Always Governor During the Civil Rights Era?

No. His term covered 1960 through January 1964, an especially intense but limited period within the longer movement.

Did Barnett Serve Multiple Terms?

Mississippi then prohibited immediate gubernatorial succession. Barnett served one four-year term.

Was Barnett the 52nd or 53rd Governor?

Reference sources differ in ordinal numbering conventions for Mississippi governors. MGU follows Mississippi historical sources describing him as the fifty-third governor.

Was Barnett a Poorly Educated Politician?

No. He was a university and law-school graduate and one of Mississippi's most successful trial attorneys.

Did Barnett Personally Keep Every Mississippi School Segregated?

No. Segregation involved local boards, state law, courts, and multiple officials. Barnett made statewide preservation of the system an explicit political goal.

Did Barnett Personally Block James Meredith?

Yes. Barnett physically appeared and refused Meredith admission during registration attempts in September 1962.

Did Meredith Lose His Federal Case?

No. After an initial district-court loss, the Fifth Circuit held that race had caused the rejection and ordered his admission.

Did the Supreme Court Order Meredith Admitted?

Federal appellate orders were ultimately protected from further stays by Supreme Court Justice Hugo Black, making the admission mandate enforceable.

Did Barnett Go to Jail for Defying the Court?

No. He faced contempt sanctions but ultimately served no jail term.

Does That Mean Barnett Won the Case?

No. Meredith was admitted, federal law prevailed, and the later termination of contempt sanctions did not legalize Barnett's obstruction.

Did Barnett Pay a Ten-Thousand-Dollar Daily Fine?

No. The threatened civil fine was not ultimately collected.

Did Barnett Want a Shooting War with Washington?

His private negotiations indicate he wanted the political appearance of resistance while avoiding an uncontrollable armed confrontation.

Was the Ole Miss Riot Staged by Barnett?

No. Barnett engaged in political theater, but the final riot became real, uncontrolled violence involving armed participants from multiple places.

Did Barnett Order the Two Riot Deaths?

No reliable evidence establishes that he ordered the killings of Paul Guihard or Ray Gunter.

Did State Authorities Control the Crowd Effectively?

No. Federal accounts describe the state security response as inadequate and at critical moments abandoned or conflicted.

Did Kennedy Federalize the Guard?

Yes. The president placed the Mississippi National Guard under federal control and deployed regular military forces.

Was Meredith Protected Only for One Day?

No. Federal marshals continued protecting him extensively through his time at the university.

Did Meredith Graduate?

Yes. He earned a political-science degree from the University of Mississippi in August 1963.

Did Barnett Personally Sentence Freedom Riders?

No. Local courts imposed sentences, but Barnett's administration supported the state's arrest-and-imprisonment strategy and transfer to Parchman.

Was Parchman Chosen Merely Because Local Jails Were Full?

Capacity was part of the context, but the state's use of Parchman also functioned deliberately as a means of intimidating and punishing the riders.

Did Barnett Release Clyde Kennard Immediately When Illness Became Known?

No. The clemency campaign continued for months before Barnett suspended the sentence in 1963.

Was Kennard Actually Guilty?

Later evidence established that the central testimony against Kennard was false, and his conviction was ultimately vacated.

Did Barnett Order Medgar Evers's Murder?

No reliable evidence establishes that claim.

Did Barnett Support Beckwith After the Murder?

His highly public courtroom handshake with Beckwith during the 1964 prosecution demonstrated political sympathy toward the accused white supremacist.

Did Barnett Later Apologize for Ole Miss?

No major public repudiation comparable to George Wallace's later apologies became part of Barnett's record.

Was Barnett Only About Segregation?

No. He had a substantial legal career and an active economic-development agenda. Segregation nevertheless became the defining issue of his governorship because he chose to make state resistance a central executive mission.

Historical Significance

Barnett's life connects rural Mississippi politics, trial-law populism, massive resistance, Freedom Riders, Parchman Farm, the Sovereignty Commission, Citizens' Councils, James Meredith, federal marshals, presidential enforcement, Clyde Kennard, and Medgar Evers.

Legacy

Ross Barnett is remembered chiefly because he converted white resistance to integration into official state policy. His record demonstrates how constitutional rights can be obstructed not only by mobs but by educated officials using courts, legislatures, police, prisons, surveillance agencies, public relations, and executive power.

Geographic Scope

Standing Pine and Leake County shaped Barnett's rural childhood and final burial.

Clinton was the site of his undergraduate education at Mississippi College.

Oxford became central twice: first through Barnett's law education and later through his attempt to prevent James Meredith from attending the University of Mississippi.

Jackson became the center of Barnett's law practice, governorship, Freedom Rider arrests, Sovereignty Commission operations, and the assassination of Medgar Evers.

Parchman Penitentiary became an instrument of the state's response to Freedom Riders and other civil-rights prisoners.

Washington, D.C., became the site of Barnett's national confrontation with federal courts, the Kennedy administration, and proposed civil-rights legislation.

Legal and Constitutional Context

Barnett's governorship occurred after Brown v. Board of Education had held state-imposed educational segregation unconstitutional under the Fourteenth Amendment.

The Freedom Rides relied on federal constitutional and statutory restrictions against segregation in interstate transportation.

James Meredith's case reached the Fifth Circuit, which held that racial discrimination caused his exclusion from the University of Mississippi.

The Supremacy Clause and Fourteenth Amendment prevented Mississippi officials from nullifying binding federal court orders through declarations of state sovereignty.

Barnett's civil-contempt proceedings enforced judicial authority rather than creating a separate criminal conviction for segregationist beliefs.

The Mississippi State Sovereignty Commission illustrates how states could use administrative agencies and tax revenue to resist constitutional rights even without openly criminalizing every civil-rights activity.
