Josephus Daniels

North Carolina editor and Democratic strategist, architect of white-supremacy propaganda, Secretary of the Navy, Progressive reformer, and U.S. ambassador to Mexico

Overview

Josephus Daniels (1862-1948) was a North Carolina newspaper editor and publisher, Democratic Party strategist, secretary of the United States Navy, diplomat, author, and one of the most influential political journalists in the South from the 1890s through the New Deal era.

Daniels used the News and Observer both as a newspaper and as a political instrument. He supported agrarian and Progressive reforms including antitrust measures, railroad regulation, a graduated income tax, women's suffrage, workers' compensation, child-labor regulation, public education, and prohibition. At the same time, he made the paper a central propaganda organ of North Carolina's white-supremacy campaigns of 1898 and 1900, using racist editorials, sensational stories, and political cartoons to attack Black political participation and the Republican-Populist Fusion coalition.

His later national career was equally consequential. Daniels served as Woodrow Wilson's secretary of the Navy from 1913 to 1921, oversaw major naval expansion and World War I mobilization, improved educational opportunities for enlisted sailors, authorized the enlistment of women during World War I, and supported Wilson administration policies of federal racial segregation and U.S. intervention in the Caribbean. Franklin D. Roosevelt later appointed him ambassador to Mexico, where he served from 1933 to 1941 and became an important figure in the Good Neighbor era.

Family Background

Daniels was born in Washington, North Carolina, the fourth of five children of Josephus Daniels Sr. and Mary Cleaves Seabrook Daniels.

His Father's Unionism

Daniels's father was a Whig and Unionist before the Civil War. He worked as a ship carpenter and, during the conflict, worked in Confederate shipyards at Wilmington.

Death of His Father

On January 28, 1865, Josephus Daniels Sr. was killed when Confederate troops fired on a steamer in which he was travelling near Washington, North Carolina.

The unusual circumstances meant that the future Democratic white-supremacy editor grew up as the son of a man whose prewar loyalty had been to the Union and who died from Confederate fire.

Move to Wilson

After her husband's death, Mary Daniels moved the family to Wilson, North Carolina.

Mary Daniels as Postmistress

In December 1866 Mary Daniels became postmistress of Wilson. She supported the family and raised Josephus within a devout Methodist household.

Wilson Collegiate Institute

Daniels attended the Wilson Collegiate Institute, a private boarding school.

Entry into Journalism

He left school in 1880 to become local editor of the Wilson Advance.

This began a newspaper career that would span nearly seven decades.

Purchase of the Wilson Advance

In 1882, at about twenty years old, Daniels purchased the Wilson Advance.

Kinston Free Press

By 1885 Daniels and his brother Charles owned the Kinston Free Press, where Josephus also wrote editorials.

Rocky Mount Reporter

Daniels also became part owner of the Rocky Mount Reporter.

North Carolina Press Association

In 1885 he served as president of the North Carolina Press Association, evidence of his rapid rise within the state's newspaper profession.

Legal Study

Daniels considered combining journalism with the practice of law and studied at the University of North Carolina's law school in 1885.

Admission to the Bar

He passed the North Carolina bar examination in October 1885.

Although legally trained, journalism rather than courtroom practice remained his principal profession.

Julian S. Carr

Industrialist Julian S. Carr became an important financial supporter of Daniels's newspaper ambitions.

Carr's backing helped Daniels acquire a Raleigh weekly that combined the State Chronicle and Farmer and Mechanic.

State Chronicle

Daniels developed the State Chronicle into an increasingly influential Democratic publication.

State Printer

The North Carolina legislature selected Daniels as state printer in 1887, 1889, 1891, and 1893.

The appointments reflected the close relationship between partisan newspapers and party government in the nineteenth century.

Failure of the Daily State Chronicle

Daniels converted the State Chronicle into a daily newspaper in 1890, but the venture failed financially in 1892.

The North Carolinian

After disposing of the failed State Chronicle, Daniels established the weekly North Carolinian.

Watauga Club

Daniels joined Raleigh's Watauga Club in 1885. The organization promoted industrial development, popular education, and modernization in North Carolina.

Support for Public Education

Daniels supported expanded public education and federal aid to education during his early editorial career.

Agricultural and Mechanical Education

He supported establishment of the North Carolina College of Agriculture and Mechanic Arts, later North Carolina State University.

Economic Reform

Daniels distrusted concentrated corporate power and campaigned against the tobacco trust.

Railroad Regulation

He supported creation of a state railroad commission and other measures intended to regulate powerful transportation corporations.

Antitrust Politics

Daniels favored antitrust legislation and frequently cast himself as an opponent of monopolistic economic power.

Free Silver

Like many agrarian reformers of the 1890s, Daniels supported free coinage of silver as a means of expanding the money supply.

Graduated Income Tax

He favored a graduated income tax, a reform intended to shift more of the tax burden toward those with greater income.

Direct Election of Senators

Daniels supported direct election of United States senators before adoption of the Seventeenth Amendment.

Women's Suffrage

He was an early supporter of women's suffrage.

This reformist position existed alongside his campaign to deprive most Black men of effective voting rights.

Workers' Compensation

Daniels supported workers' compensation laws.

Child-Labor Regulation

He also supported legal regulation of child labor, an important issue in the increasingly industrialized South.

A Progressive and a Partisan

Daniels's reform politics did not make him politically independent. He remained an intensely partisan Democrat and used his newspapers to advance the party's interests.

Department of the Interior

After effective Democratic campaigning in the 1892 election, President Grover Cleveland appointed Daniels to a position in the United States Department of the Interior in 1893.

The News and Observer

In 1894 Daniels and allies associated with Julian Carr acquired the financially troubled Raleigh News and Observer.

Daniels began writing editorials for the paper while still in Washington and returned to Raleigh in 1895 to take full control.

Building the Newspaper

The paper had a circulation of only a few thousand when Daniels took control. He stabilized its finances, expanded its circulation, and eventually made it one of the South's most influential newspapers.

Newspaper as Party Organ

Under Daniels, the News and Observer was not committed to modern ideals of nonpartisan political journalism.

It functioned openly as a Democratic newspaper and, during crucial campaigns, as a major instrument of party strategy.

Fusion Politics

During the 1890s North Carolina Republicans and Populists created a Fusion coalition that defeated Democrats and broadened political opportunities for Black voters and officeholders.

Daniel L. Russell

Republican Daniel L. Russell won the governorship in 1896, demonstrating that Fusion could threaten long-term Democratic control of the state.

William Jennings Bryan

Daniels became an enthusiastic supporter of William Jennings Bryan and helped with Bryan's effort to secure the 1896 Democratic presidential nomination.

Democratic National Committee

In 1896 Daniels became North Carolina's Democratic national committeeman, a position he retained until 1916.

Strategy Against Fusion

Daniels worked to break the alliance between white Populists and Black Republicans.

Racial division became a particularly effective means of separating economically discontented white farmers from Black political allies.

Planning the 1898 Campaign

Modern University of North Carolina historical research identifies Daniels as one of the central strategists of the 1898 white-supremacy campaign.

In March 1898 he met with Furnifold M. Simmons and Charles B. Aycock in New Bern to help formulate a strategy for defeating Fusion.

Furnifold M. Simmons

Simmons supplied statewide Democratic organization while Daniels supplied a powerful newspaper platform.

Charles B. Aycock

Aycock, a close friend of Daniels, became one of the campaign's most effective speakers and later its gubernatorial beneficiary.

The 'Write, Speak, and Ride' Campaign

The Democratic strategy relied on complementary forms of political pressure: newspaper editors and cartoonists who could write, orators who could speak, and armed Red Shirt organizations that could ride.

The News and Observer as Propaganda

Daniels positioned the News and Observer as a central propaganda arm of the 1898 campaign.

The 'Negro Domination' Theme

The newspaper repeatedly warned white readers that Fusion government meant Black domination.

This framing exaggerated the extent of Black officeholding and treated any substantial Black political participation as inherently illegitimate.

Racist Political Cartoons

The paper used racist political cartoons to depict Black voters and officeholders as dangerous, corrupt, ignorant, or sexually threatening.

Norman E. Jennett

Cartoonist Norman E. Jennett produced some of the News and Observer's most influential images during the campaign.

The Incubus Image

One notorious cartoon portrayed Black political power as a monstrous incubus emerging from the Fusion ballot box and threatening white society.

The imagery connected voting rights to sexual fear and racial panic.

Sexualized Racial Propaganda

Democratic campaign rhetoric repeatedly portrayed Black men as sexual threats to white women.

Such claims were a major component of the broader ideology used to justify political intimidation and racial violence.

Sensational Reporting

Daniels later acknowledged that he had not always carefully verified sensational stories during the campaign.

His retrospective comments recognized that propagandistic effectiveness often took priority over factual reliability.

Black Officeholding

The campaign portrayed Black officeholding as evidence of misgovernment even though African Americans remained underrepresented relative to their share of the state's population in many institutions.

Red Shirts

Red Shirt organizations rode through communities, displayed weapons, disrupted political activity, and intimidated Black Republicans and white Populists.

Daniels and Red Shirt Violence

Daniels was not primarily a mounted Red Shirt commander. His distinctive role was editorial and strategic.

His newspaper helped legitimize and energize the political movement in which paramilitary intimidation operated.

Wilmington

Wilmington was North Carolina's most important center of Black urban political and economic power and became the most violent site of the 1898 campaign.

Alex Manly and the Daily Record

Alexander Manly edited the Black-owned Daily Record in Wilmington. His challenge to racist claims about interracial sexual relations became a major target of white-supremacist propaganda.

Amplifying the Manly Editorial

White Democratic newspapers and campaigners circulated Manly's editorial across the state to inflame racial anger.

The controversy became one of the most potent propaganda weapons of the campaign.

The Wilmington Massacre and Coup

On November 10, 1898, armed white supremacists in Wilmington destroyed the Daily Record, killed Black residents, drove others from the city, forced elected officials to resign, and replaced the municipal government.

Daniels's Responsibility for Wilmington

Daniels did not lead the armed march in Wilmington; Alfred Moore Waddell and local conspirators carried out the direct overthrow.

Daniels's responsibility lies in statewide planning and propaganda that helped create the racial panic and political mobilization surrounding the coup.

Not Merely a Newspaper Observer

Modern North Carolina historical research rejects the view that Daniels was merely reporting events from Raleigh.

He participated in planning the statewide campaign and deliberately used the paper to shape political behavior.

Democratic Victory in 1898

The 1898 campaign returned Democrats to control of the state legislature and broke much of the Fusion coalition's power.

The 1900 Campaign

Daniels then supported a second white-supremacy campaign built around the election of Charles B. Aycock and a constitutional suffrage amendment.

Disfranchisement

The amendment used literacy requirements, a poll tax, and a grandfather clause to remove most Black men from the electorate while protecting many illiterate white voters.

Daniels and the Suffrage Amendment

Daniels enthusiastically supported the amendment through the News and Observer.

North Carolina educational sources note that he celebrated its passage as a historic political victory.

Long-Term Political Consequences

Disfranchisement helped establish one-party Democratic control in North Carolina for decades.

Black citizens remained active in churches, businesses, education, civic organizations, and legal challenges, but their direct electoral influence was sharply curtailed.

Jim Crow

The political settlement of 1898-1900 developed alongside formal racial segregation and unequal public institutions.

Daniels and Public Education

At the same time Daniels remained a strong supporter of public education, including state financial support for the University of North Carolina.

Prohibition

He favored restriction of alcohol sales and strongly supported North Carolina's statewide prohibition referendum in 1908.

North Carolina Yearbook

Daniels founded the North Carolina Yearbook and Business Directory, expanding his publishing interests beyond daily journalism.

North Carolina Review

From 1909 to 1913 he published the North Carolina Review, a literary supplement.

Relationship with Woodrow Wilson

Daniels became an important supporter of Woodrow Wilson during Wilson's rise to national leadership.

Democratic Convention of 1912

Daniels helped smooth disputes among Democratic factions and played a role in Wilson's successful nomination in 1912.

Wilson Campaign Publicity

During the 1912 presidential campaign Daniels served as Wilson's publicity chief.

Secretary of the Navy

Wilson appointed Daniels secretary of the Navy in 1913.

He became one of only four cabinet members to remain throughout both Wilson administrations.

Franklin D. Roosevelt

Franklin D. Roosevelt served as assistant secretary of the Navy under Daniels.

Their working relationship developed into a long political friendship despite differences in temperament and policy.

Civilian Control of the Navy

Daniels strongly asserted civilian authority over the naval establishment and frequently clashed with senior officers who regarded him as an intrusive reformer.

Enlisted Sailors

He took an unusual interest in the education and living conditions of enlisted sailors.

Shipboard Schools

Daniels promoted schools aboard ships and at naval stations so enlisted personnel could improve literacy and technical skills.

Vocational Training

His approach treated naval service partly as an educational institution capable of preparing sailors for advancement during and after service.

General Order 99

In 1914 Daniels issued General Order 99 prohibiting alcoholic beverages aboard naval vessels and at Navy shore stations.

The 'Cup of Joe' Story

Popular folklore sometimes claims the expression “cup of joe” arose because Daniels's alcohol ban forced sailors to drink coffee instead.

The association is widely repeated but the etymology is uncertain, so it should not be treated as established historical fact.

Veracruz

In 1914 Daniels carried out President Wilson's naval orders during the United States occupation of Veracruz, Mexico.

The episode later complicated his reception when he returned to Mexico as ambassador.

Naval Oil Reserves

Daniels fought to protect federal naval petroleum reserves from private exploitation.

This policy later became particularly important during controversies over the reserves in the Harding administration.

Naval Expansion

As international tensions increased, Daniels supported a major naval construction program beginning in the middle of the 1910s.

Naval Consulting Board

In 1915 he established the Naval Consulting Board, headed by inventor Thomas Edison, to bring scientific and industrial expertise into naval preparedness.

Armor Plate Factory

Daniels supported creation of a government-operated armor plate factory, arguing that the Navy should not remain completely dependent on private suppliers.

Naval Aviation

His tenure coincided with rapid development of naval aviation and experimentation with new technologies as the service prepared for modern warfare.

Women in the Navy

In 1917 Daniels determined that women could be enrolled in the Naval Reserve under existing statutory language.

The decision opened the way for thousands of women, especially Yeomen (F), to serve in uniform during World War I.

Yeomen (F)

More than eleven thousand women ultimately served as Yeomen (F), performing clerical and other shore-based duties and becoming pioneers of women's enlisted service in the Navy.

World War I

After the United States entered World War I in April 1917, Daniels oversaw rapid expansion and mobilization of the Navy.

Naval Operations in European Waters

The Navy deployed destroyers, patrol craft, transports, mine forces, and other units to support the Allied war effort and protect transatlantic movement.

Troop Transportation

The naval establishment played a critical role in transporting and protecting American troops and supplies crossing the Atlantic.

Council of National Defense

Daniels served on the Council of National Defense, which coordinated major elements of wartime mobilization.

Committee on Public Information

He also participated in the Committee on Public Information, the Wilson administration's wartime propaganda and information organization.

Wartime Censorship

Daniels's wartime responsibilities intersected with censorship and public-information controls used to maintain support for the war effort.

Federal Segregation

The Wilson administration expanded racial segregation within parts of the federal government.

Modern University of North Carolina historical research identifies Daniels as an active supporter of this segregationist policy.

Race in the Federal Bureaucracy

Segregation damaged the careers of Black federal employees who had gained positions through civil-service systems and earlier Republican administrations.

Daniels's Racial Politics in National Office

Daniels did not leave his North Carolina racial ideology behind when he entered the cabinet.

His participation in federal segregation linked the white-supremacist politics of the post-Reconstruction South to national administrative policy.

Occupation of Haiti

In 1915 the Wilson administration began the United States occupation of Haiti.

As secretary of the Navy, Daniels headed the department responsible for the naval and Marine forces carrying out the intervention.

Haiti and U.S. Power

The occupation sought political and financial control in a period when the Wilson administration feared instability and foreign influence in the Caribbean.

Forced Labor and Abuse in Haiti

The occupation became associated with forced labor, racialized rule, summary violence, and serious abuses committed by U.S. forces and local constabularies.

Responsibility for particular abuses belonged to specific officers and units, but Daniels remained a cabinet-level defender and administrator of the broader intervention.

A Black Republic Under White Occupation

Haiti had been founded through a successful slave revolution and was the world's first independent Black republic.

The racial hierarchy imposed during the U.S. occupation made the intervention particularly significant in African diaspora history.

Dominican Republic and Caribbean Intervention

Daniels's Navy Department also operated within the broader Wilson-era pattern of United States military intervention and political control in the Caribbean and Central America.

World War I Naval Record

Daniels's naval record therefore combines substantial administrative modernization with racial segregation, imperial intervention, and wartime restrictions on civil liberties.

End of Cabinet Service

Daniels left the Navy Department in March 1921 when the Wilson administration ended.

Return to the News and Observer

He returned to Raleigh and resumed direct leadership of the News and Observer.

UNC Trustee

Daniels served as a trustee of the University of North Carolina from 1901 until his death.

University Consolidation

During the 1920s he advocated consolidation of the state-supported institutions at Chapel Hill, Raleigh, and Greensboro into a coordinated university system.

Frank Porter Graham

Daniels supported Frank Porter Graham's selection as president of the University of North Carolina in 1930.

Opposition to the Ku Klux Klan

During the 1920s Daniels opposed the revived Ku Klux Klan.

This later opposition to the Klan is a genuine change from the racial militancy of 1898, but it did not amount to acceptance of racial integration or federal civil-rights enforcement.

Democratic Politics in the 1920s

Daniels remained an active Democrat, supporting William G. McAdoo in 1924 and Cordell Hull in 1928 before ultimately backing the party's nominees.

Potential Gubernatorial Candidacy

Daniels generally avoided seeking elective office himself, although observers believed he might have accepted a gubernatorial nomination in 1932.

Automobile Accident

A serious automobile accident in 1932 weakened Daniels physically and helped remove the possibility of a gubernatorial campaign.

Support for Franklin D. Roosevelt

Daniels supported Franklin D. Roosevelt for the Democratic presidential nomination and presidency in 1932.

A Reversed Relationship

Two decades earlier Roosevelt had been Daniels's assistant at the Navy Department. In 1933 President Roosevelt became Daniels's appointing authority.

Ambassador to Mexico

Roosevelt appointed Daniels ambassador extraordinary and plenipotentiary to Mexico on March 17, 1933.

Daniels presented his credentials on April 24.

Reception in Mexico

His arrival carried historical baggage because he had been secretary of the Navy during the 1914 Veracruz occupation.

Nevertheless, he gradually developed strong working relationships within Mexico.

Good Neighbor Policy

Daniels's diplomacy formed part of Roosevelt's Good Neighbor Policy, which sought to reduce overt military intervention and improve relations with Latin American states.

Mexican Revolution Claims

In 1934 Daniels negotiated a settlement concerning special claims by U.S. citizens for losses connected to the Mexican Revolution.

Land Reform Disputes

His embassy dealt extensively with American claims arising from Mexican agrarian reform and expropriation of foreign-owned property.

Mexican Oil Expropriation

President Lázaro Cárdenas nationalized foreign oil properties in 1938.

Daniels played an important diplomatic role as the United States and Mexico worked through compensation and broader bilateral tensions.

Daniels and American Oil Companies

Daniels was less sympathetic to private oil companies than many American business leaders and favored negotiation rather than military or extreme economic coercion.

Relationship with Lázaro Cárdenas

Daniels developed a constructive relationship with President Cárdenas and came to admire aspects of Mexico's social and agrarian reform program.

Popular Reputation in Mexico

Despite the memory of Veracruz, Daniels eventually gained substantial goodwill in Mexico and was widely regarded as an effective representative of the Good Neighbor approach.

Departure from Mexico

Daniels left his ambassadorial post on November 9, 1941.

Reason for Resignation

His wife's severe arthritis was a major reason for returning to Raleigh.

Addie Worth Bagley Daniels

Daniels had married Addie Worth Bagley on May 2, 1888.

She came from a prominent North Carolina family and was the sister of naval officer Worth Bagley, who became a celebrated casualty of the Spanish-American War.

Children

Josephus and Addie Daniels had six children. Two daughters died in infancy; four sons survived to adulthood: Josephus, Worth Bagley, Jonathan Worth, and Frank Arthur Daniels.

Death of Addie Daniels

Addie Daniels died in 1943 after years of severe illness.

Return to Editorial Work

After returning from Mexico, Daniels resumed full-time editorial activity in Raleigh.

Nine-Month School Term

He supported a nine-month public-school term in North Carolina.

Public Health

Daniels also supported expanded public-health programs during his later years.

Opposition to Postwar Conscription

He opposed compulsory military service after World War II, continuing a strain of small-d democratic suspicion toward permanent militarization.

1944 Democratic Platform

In 1944 Daniels opposed efforts by some southern Democrats to place an explicit white-supremacy plank in the national party platform.

Later Views on Race

NCpedia records that Daniels came to feel shame over the extreme racism that had animated his politics in 1898 and 1900.

He increasingly advocated what he considered fair treatment of African Americans and continued to emphasize education.

Limits of His Racial Evolution

Daniels did not become a supporter of full racial integration or federal civil-rights enforcement.

He continued to fear social integration and defended substantial state control over racial policy.

Opposition to Federal Civil-Rights Legislation

He opposed federal civil-rights legislation even while criticizing some forms of racial injustice.

Truman's Committee on Civil Rights

In 1947 President Harry S. Truman's Committee on Civil Rights issued To Secure These Rights, recommending strong federal action against segregation and racial discrimination.

Daniels's 1947 Editorial

Daniels strongly attacked the committee's recommendations and argued that federal enforcement threatened southern state authority.

Return to Sexualized Racial Rhetoric

His 1947 criticism also returned to the long-standing southern trope linking lynching to accusations of Black sexual violence against white women.

This showed the limits of his later repentance over the propaganda methods of 1898.

A Partial but Incomplete Change

Daniels's racial views changed in significant but incomplete ways over his lifetime.

He rejected the Klan and the most explicit forms of partisan white-supremacy rhetoric while continuing to oppose the structural civil-rights program necessary to dismantle Jim Crow.

Author

Daniels wrote numerous books on politics, journalism, the Navy, Woodrow Wilson, diplomacy, and his own life.

Tar Heel Editor

His memoir Tar Heel Editor, published in 1939, covered his early newspaper career and North Carolina politics.

Editor in Politics

Editor in Politics, published in 1941, offered Daniels's retrospective account of his political journalism and campaigning.

Our Navy at War

Our Navy at War presented his account of naval mobilization and operations during World War I.

The Wilson Era

His multi-volume writings on Woodrow Wilson preserved extensive recollections of cabinet government and wartime policy.

Shirt-Sleeve Diplomat

Shirt-Sleeve Diplomat, published in 1947, recounted his years in Mexico.

Value and Limits of the Memoirs

Daniels's memoirs are valuable primary sources but must be read critically.

They preserve extensive firsthand observation while also reflecting retrospective self-justification, changing memory, and the perspective of a participant seeking to shape his own legacy.

The News and Observer After Josephus

Daniels's son Jonathan increasingly directed the newspaper in later years and moved its editorial position away from some of his father's earlier white-supremacist politics.

Later Civil-Rights Direction of the Newspaper

Under later leadership the News and Observer gradually became more supportive of civil rights and eventually endorsed major federal civil-rights legislation in the 1960s.

Death

Daniels's health deteriorated late in 1947. He continued visiting his office into January 1948.

He died of pneumonia at his Raleigh home on January 15, 1948.

Burial

Daniels was buried in Oakwood Cemetery in Raleigh.

USS Josephus Daniels

The United States Navy later named the guided-missile frigate USS Josephus Daniels in his honor.

The naming reflected the long-standing national emphasis on his Navy service rather than his North Carolina racial politics.

Statues and Building Names

Schools, buildings, and public memorials were named for Daniels during the twentieth century, including facilities connected to North Carolina universities.

UNC Daniels Building

The University of North Carolina named a building for Daniels in 1967.

Decades later the university's historical review concluded that his leadership in white-supremacy campaigns and federal segregation made continued honorific naming inappropriate.

Modern Name Removal

In the twenty-first century institutions removed Daniels's name from buildings and schools as historical interpretation placed greater emphasis on his role in disfranchisement and racial propaganda.

Family Removal of the Raleigh Statue

In 2020 Daniels's descendants removed a family-owned statue of him from downtown Raleigh amid renewed public debate over racial injustice and commemoration.

News and Observer Apology

In 2006 the News and Observer formally apologized for the newspaper's role in the white-supremacy campaigns of 1898 and 1900.

Why the Apology Matters

The apology represented an institutional acknowledgment that the newspaper had not merely reflected racial prejudice but had actively helped organize and legitimize a political campaign with violent and antidemocratic consequences.

Was Daniels a Newspaper Editor or Political Boss?

He was both an editor and a powerful political strategist. His newspaper gave him influence over elections, party organization, public policy, and political careers far beyond ordinary journalism.

Was Daniels a Populist?

No. He shared several Populist economic positions but remained a loyal Democrat and worked aggressively to destroy the Republican-Populist Fusion alliance.

Did Daniels Personally Lead the Wilmington Coup?

No. Alfred Moore Waddell and Wilmington organizers carried out the direct armed overthrow.

Daniels's role was statewide strategy, newspaper propaganda, racial incitement, and support for the movement that produced the coup.

Did Daniels Support Women's Voting Rights?

Yes. He supported women's suffrage while simultaneously campaigning to disfranchise most Black men.

The contrast demonstrates that the expansion and contraction of democracy could occur simultaneously for different groups.

Did Daniels Improve Conditions for Enlisted Sailors?

Yes. His Navy reforms included education and training for enlisted personnel and reflected a genuine belief that service should improve ordinary sailors' opportunities.

Did Daniels Open Naval Service to Women?

Under Daniels the Navy authorized women to enlist in the Naval Reserve during World War I, leading to service by more than eleven thousand Yeomen (F).

Did Daniels Desegregate the Navy?

No. His reformism toward enlisted sailors did not translate into racial egalitarianism. He supported the Wilson administration's broader segregationist policies.

Did Daniels Change His Views on Race?

Yes, to a degree. In later life he rejected the Ku Klux Klan, expressed shame about the extremism of 1898-1900, and opposed an explicit white-supremacy plank in the 1944 Democratic platform.

He nevertheless continued to support segregation, oppose federal civil-rights legislation, and resist rapid integration.

Was He a Successful Ambassador to Mexico?

Generally, yes. Despite the earlier Veracruz intervention, Daniels developed effective relationships with Mexican leaders and handled difficult claims and oil disputes during the Good Neighbor era.

Comparison with Charles B. Aycock

Aycock supplied much of the oratorical power and later became governor; Daniels supplied newspaper propaganda, political strategy, and continuing institutional influence.

Both combined reformist policies with deliberate Black disfranchisement.

Comparison with Alfred Moore Waddell

Waddell directly led the armed Wilmington movement that destroyed the Daily Record and displaced the elected city government.

Daniels helped create the statewide political and media environment that made the coup part of a broader Democratic strategy.

Comparison with Furnifold Simmons

Simmons organized the Democratic machine; Daniels provided its most powerful newspaper voice.

Their collaboration demonstrates the integration of party organization and media propaganda in the 1898 campaign.

Comparison with Benjamin Tillman

Both men combined economic reform with white supremacy. Tillman was a mass agrarian orator and elected officeholder; Daniels exercised enormous influence through journalism, party organization, cabinet service, and diplomacy.

Comparison with Franklin D. Roosevelt

Roosevelt began as Daniels's assistant secretary of the Navy and later became the president who appointed Daniels ambassador to Mexico.

Their relationship is one of the unusual long-term reversals of rank in American political history.

Historical Significance

Daniels is historically significant because he demonstrates the extraordinary political power of partisan journalism. He could shape not only opinion but party strategy, racial ideology, election campaigns, public institutions, and the careers of major politicians.

His national career also shows how southern white-supremacist political assumptions could enter federal administration while coexisting with Progressive reform, wartime modernization, and international diplomacy.

Impact on African Americans

Daniels's greatest negative impact on African Americans came through the 1898 and 1900 campaigns. His newspaper helped demonize Black voters and officeholders, legitimize intimidation, destroy the Fusion coalition, and build public support for constitutional disfranchisement.

His later support for federal segregation and defense of Jim Crow extended that racial politics beyond North Carolina. Although he moderated some views late in life and opposed the Ku Klux Klan, he never embraced full political and social equality.

A Balanced Assessment

Daniels was a gifted editor, institution-builder, economic reform advocate, supporter of women's suffrage, public education, workers' compensation and child-labor regulation, influential Navy secretary, supporter of enlisted education, pioneer in the wartime enlistment of women, defender of naval oil reserves, and effective ambassador to Mexico.

Those accomplishments coexist with deliberate antidemocratic actions of enormous consequence. Daniels helped design and publicize a white-supremacist movement, knowingly used sensational racial propaganda, supported Black disfranchisement, promoted federal segregation, and defended a racial order that denied African Americans equal citizenship. His later moderation was real but incomplete.

Legacy

For much of the twentieth century Daniels was remembered principally as the great editor of the News and Observer, Wilson's Navy secretary, Franklin Roosevelt's mentor, and an effective ambassador to Mexico. Those achievements explain why ships, schools, buildings, statues, and public institutions carried his name.

Modern scholarship has substantially widened that picture. Daniels now stands as a major example of how Progressive reform and white supremacy could reinforce one another: political participation could expand for women and many white citizens even as Black citizens were violently removed from electoral power. His legacy therefore includes both substantial public-service achievements and a central role in constructing North Carolina's Jim Crow political order.

  • April 1917: United States enters World War I; Daniels oversees rapid Navy mobilization.
  • 1917-1918: Serves in broader wartime mobilization and public-information institutions.
  • March 1921: Leaves Navy Department and returns to Raleigh.
  • 1920s: Opposes the revived Ku Klux Klan and remains active in Democratic politics and higher education.
  • 1930: Supports Frank Porter Graham for presidency of the University of North Carolina.
  • 1932: Seriously injured in automobile accident; strongly supports Franklin D. Roosevelt for president.
  • March 17, 1933: Appointed ambassador to Mexico.
  • April 24, 1933: Presents credentials in Mexico City.
  • 1934: Negotiates settlement of special claims related to the Mexican Revolution.
  • 1938: Handles major diplomatic issues after Mexican expropriation of foreign oil properties.
  • November 9, 1941: Leaves ambassadorial post.
  • 1943: Addie Daniels dies.
  • 1944: Opposes an explicit white-supremacy plank in the Democratic national platform.
  • 1947: Attacks major recommendations of Truman's Committee on Civil Rights.
  • January 15, 1948: Dies of pneumonia in Raleigh.
  • 2006: News and Observer formally apologizes for its role in the 1898 and 1900 white-supremacy campaigns.
  • 2020: Daniels family removes his statue from downtown Raleigh.

Geographic Scope

Washington and Wilson, North Carolina formed Daniels's childhood and early newspaper world. Raleigh became the center of his journalistic, political, educational, and later family life.

Wilmington was the principal site of the violent culmination of the 1898 campaign he helped organize. Washington, D.C. was the setting of his federal service, while his naval responsibilities extended to Mexico, Haiti, the Caribbean, and European waters.

Mexico City became his principal residence during his ambassadorship from 1933 to 1941.

Legal and Constitutional Context

Daniels's career crossed Black suffrage under the Fifteenth Amendment, state disfranchisement through literacy tests and grandfather clauses, Jim Crow segregation, federal civil-service policy, wartime censorship, U.S. military occupation abroad, women's military service, and later federal civil-rights proposals.

His record demonstrates how democratic reform for some groups could coexist with systematic exclusion of others within the same political program.
