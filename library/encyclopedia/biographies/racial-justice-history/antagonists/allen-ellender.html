Allen Ellender

Louisiana House speaker and U.S. senator, Long-era political ally, agricultural-policy chairman, appropriator, president pro tempore, and longtime segregationist

Overview

Allen Joseph Ellender (1890-1972) was a Louisiana lawyer, state legislator, Speaker of the Louisiana House, United States senator, New Deal agricultural supporter, appropriator, president pro tempore of the Senate, segregationist, and one of the most powerful agricultural legislators of the mid-twentieth century.

Ellender rose from a Terrebonne Parish farm family into the political world created by Huey P. Long. Initially independent of Long, he became an important legislative ally, helped defeat the 1929 impeachment effort against the governor, and later served as Speaker of the Louisiana House. In the U.S. Senate he supported major New Deal farm policies, chaired the Agriculture and Forestry Committee for eighteen years, helped shape commodity, conservation, school-lunch, and food-stamp legislation, and in his final years became chairman of Appropriations and president pro tempore.

His legislative achievements coexisted with an uncompromising defense of segregation. Ellender led a 1938 filibuster against federal anti-lynching legislation, signed the Southern Manifesto, opposed the Civil Rights Acts of 1957, 1960, 1964, and 1968, opposed the Twenty-Fourth Amendment and Voting Rights Act of 1965, and defended white supremacy in explicit terms. The Senate Historical Office also notes that agricultural policies he shaped routinely excluded African American farmers. He was personally popular among colleagues and occasionally liberal on education, housing, censorship, and church-state questions, making his record broader—but not less consequential on race—than a simple ideological label suggests.

Was Ellender a Huey Long Ally?

Yes, but not from the beginning and not without limits. He initially opposed Long, later became a major legislative ally, defended him during the impeachment crisis, and then broke with parts of the organization over questionable oil-leasing practices.

Was Ellender Huey Long's Political Heir?

No. He became an important Longite but was not selected as the principal state heir to Huey's machine.

Was Ellender Economically Conservative?

Generally, but with major exceptions. He strongly supported federal farm programs, selected education and housing measures, and food assistance while opposing many other expansions of federal power.

Was Ellender a New Dealer?

He supported major New Deal agricultural policies and some Roosevelt initiatives but was not a comprehensive New Deal liberal.

Did Ellender Lead an Anti-Lynching Filibuster?

Yes. Library of Congress records identify him as a leading speaker in the 1938 southern filibuster and note roughly twenty-eight hours of speechmaking over six days.

Did Ellender Support White Supremacy?

Yes. His opposition to anti-lynching legislation included explicit defense of white supremacy.

Did Ellender Sign the Southern Manifesto?

Yes.

Did Ellender Oppose the Civil Rights Act of 1964?

Yes.

Did Ellender Oppose the Voting Rights Act of 1965?

Yes.

Did Ellender Oppose the Twenty-Fourth Amendment?

Yes. He opposed the constitutional prohibition on poll taxes in federal elections.

Did Ellender Ever Support a Voting Reform Favored by Civil-Rights Groups?

He supported repeal of Louisiana's state poll tax, but that limited state-level position did not alter his broader opposition to federal civil-rights enforcement.

Did Ellender Help Create Food Stamps?

As Agriculture Committee chairman he played an important role in the Senate process that produced the Food Stamp Act of 1964.

Did Ellender Support School Lunches?

Yes. Child nutrition and school meals became important parts of the agricultural-policy system developed under his committee leadership.

Did Ellender's Farm Policies Help Black Farmers Equally?

No. The Senate Historical Office explicitly notes that his agricultural efforts routinely excluded African Americans.

Was Ellender Anti-McCarthy?

He became one of the earlier senators to criticize Joseph McCarthy while remaining strongly anti-Communist.

Was Ellender Against All Social Programs?

No. His record included support for public housing, education measures, food assistance, and agricultural subsidies.

Did Ellender Support Apartheid?

During a 1962 Africa trip newspapers reported remarks supportive of apartheid and skeptical of African self-government. Ellender denied important parts of the reporting, so the biography presents the controversy rather than treating every quotation as undisputed.

Was Ellender President Pro Tempore?

Yes. He served from 1971 until his death in 1972.

Was Ellender Appropriations Chairman?

Yes. He became chairman in 1971 and held the position until his death.

Comparison with Huey P. Long

Long was a mass political insurgent and national populist; Ellender became the legislative institutionalist who carried important elements of Longism into a long Senate career.

Comparison with Russell B. Long

Russell Long inherited the Long family name and became a powerful Finance Committee legislator. Ellender's strongest Senate specialization was agriculture and appropriations.

Comparison with James Eastland

Both were powerful segregationist committee chairmen. Eastland's principal instrument was Judiciary; Ellender's was Agriculture and later Appropriations.

Comparison with John Stennis

Stennis specialized in defense and institutional ethics. Ellender specialized in agriculture and appropriations. Both combined Senate respectability with opposition to major civil-rights protections.

Comparison with Richard Russell Jr.

Russell and Ellender were senior southern institutionalists who defended segregation while developing national expertise in major policy fields—defense for Russell, agriculture for Ellender.

Comparison with John Sparkman

Sparkman was more economically liberal across housing and development policy. Ellender's federal activism was concentrated more heavily in agriculture, food policy, and Louisiana infrastructure.

Comparison with J. William Fulbright

Fulbright became a major critic of Vietnam and an internationalist intellectual; Ellender was a more pragmatic agricultural senator and skeptical budget overseer.

Comparison with Theodore Bilbo

Bilbo used theatrical racial demagoguery. Ellender was less flamboyant but defended Bilbo during the contested 1946 election and maintained equally firm segregationist positions in Senate voting.

Comparison with Harry F. Byrd Sr.

Byrd emphasized fiscal conservatism and restricted government spending; Ellender was far more willing to use federal power to subsidize agriculture and fund public works while sharing Byrd's opposition to civil rights.

Historical Significance

Allen Ellender was one of the Senate's most consequential agricultural legislators. His eighteen years chairing Agriculture and Forestry placed him at the center of commodity supports, conservation, agricultural modernization, farm credit, school meals, and the emergence of modern federal food assistance. His later chairmanship of Appropriations and service as president pro tempore reflected an extraordinary accumulation of institutional power.

His career is equally important to African American history because it demonstrates how federal economic policy could expand while racial citizenship remained restricted. Ellender could endorse farm subsidies, food stamps, school lunches, housing, and educational programs while using Senate procedure and votes to resist anti-lynching, desegregation, voting-rights, and fair-housing laws.

Impact on African Americans

Ellender's impact on African Americans was strongly negative in civil-rights policy. He helped defeat federal anti-lynching legislation, signed the Southern Manifesto, opposed the major civil-rights acts of his era, opposed the Twenty-Fourth Amendment, and voted against the Voting Rights Act of 1965.

His agricultural influence also had racial consequences. Federal farm programs he supported could stabilize rural economies and improve agricultural productivity, but the Senate itself acknowledges that his efforts routinely excluded African American farmers. In the segregated South, access to federal agricultural resources was often mediated through white-controlled local institutions.

A Balanced Assessment

Ellender was a highly effective legislator, knowledgeable agricultural policymaker, defender of rural producers, supporter of conservation, food assistance, school nutrition, selected housing and education measures, and a skilled appropriator. His willingness to criticize Joseph McCarthy and his occasional civil-libertarian positions show a politician more complicated than a uniform reactionary.

Those achievements cannot be separated from his commitment to white supremacy. He used his considerable speaking ability and institutional influence to defeat anti-lynching legislation, resisted every major federal civil-rights breakthrough of the central civil-rights era, and presided over agricultural policies that did not distribute benefits equally across race. His personal warmth and cross-party popularity did not lessen those consequences.

Legacy

Ellender's legacy remains visible in Louisiana's agricultural and political history and in the institutional development of the modern Senate. He represented the transition from Huey Long's insurgent state politics to a seniority-based form of national legislative power centered on committees, appropriations, and policy specialization.

His biography also exposes the limits of economically interventionist southern politics under Jim Crow. Government could be mobilized aggressively to stabilize farm prices, build infrastructure, conserve soil, and feed poor families while the same senator opposed equal federal protection against racial violence and disfranchisement. That contradiction is central rather than incidental to understanding his career.

  • 1928: Becomes House floor leader and moves into Huey Long's political coalition.
  • 1929: Helps defeat the impeachment effort against Governor Huey Long.
  • 1932: Becomes Speaker of the Louisiana House.
  • 1935: Huey Long is assassinated.
  • 1936: Ellender wins election to the U.S. Senate after the death of Senate nominee Oscar K. Allen.
  • January 3, 1937: Begins Senate service.
  • 1937: Supports Roosevelt in major New Deal and Supreme Court political battles.
  • January 1938: Leads major portions of the southern filibuster against federal anti-lynching legislation.
  • 1939-1941: Supports major measures aiding countries resisting Axis expansion.
  • 1942: Wins reelection.
  • 1946: Participates in Senate consideration of Theodore Bilbo's contested Mississippi election.
  • 1948: Wins reelection.
  • 1951: Becomes chairman of Senate Agriculture and Forestry Committee.
  • 1953-1954: Republican Senate control temporarily interrupts chairmanship.
  • 1955: Returns as Agriculture chairman.
  • 1956: Signs the Southern Manifesto and helps shape Soil Bank-era agricultural policy.
  • 1957: Votes against Civil Rights Act.
  • 1960: Votes against another Civil Rights Act and wins reelection.
  • 1962: Africa trip produces international controversy over reported racial remarks.
  • 1964: Helps guide Food Stamp Act through Agriculture Committee and votes against Civil Rights Act.
  • August 31, 1964: President Johnson acknowledges Ellender's role when signing the Food Stamp Act.
  • 1965: Votes against the Voting Rights Act.
  • 1966: Wins final completed Senate reelection and continues leadership during expansion of child-nutrition programs.
  • 1968: Opposes the Civil Rights Act containing federal fair-housing protections.
  • 1971: Leaves Agriculture chairmanship after eighteen total years as chairman.
  • January 1971: Becomes chairman of Appropriations and president pro tempore.
  • 1972: Faces Democratic primary challenge from J. Bennett Johnston Jr.
  • July 27, 1972: Dies of a heart attack at Bethesda Naval Hospital while still in office.
  • July 31, 1972: Funeral services held in Houma with President and Mrs. Nixon in attendance.
  • August 1972: Elaine S. Edwards is appointed temporarily to his Senate seat.
  • November 1972: J. Bennett Johnston Jr. wins election to the seat.

Geographic Scope

Montegut, Houma, and Terrebonne Parish formed Ellender's personal and political base. His constituency was deeply connected to the agricultural and coastal economy of southern Louisiana, including sugarcane, waterways, flood control, and rural infrastructure.

Baton Rouge was the center of his Louisiana legislative career and alliance with Huey Long. Washington, D.C. became the center of his thirty-five-year Senate career, while his extensive congressional travels carried him across Europe, Asia, Africa, and other regions.

Legal and Constitutional Context

Ellender's career intersected with the Louisiana Constitution of 1921, Huey Long's impeachment crisis, New Deal agricultural law, anti-lynching legislation, Senate filibusters, school desegregation, the Southern Manifesto, the Civil Rights Acts, the Twenty-Fourth Amendment, the Voting Rights Act, federal food and agricultural legislation, and congressional appropriations power.

His record illustrates the political flexibility of federalism: Ellender accepted extensive national power to regulate and subsidize agriculture but resisted national power when it challenged southern racial institutions.
