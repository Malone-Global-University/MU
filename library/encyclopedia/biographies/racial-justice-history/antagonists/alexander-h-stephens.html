Alexander H. Stephens

Georgia politician, U.S. representative, slaveholder, defender of slavery, vice president of the Confederate States, postwar congressman, and governor of Georgia

Overview

Alexander Hamilton Stephens was a Georgia lawyer and politician whose public career stretched from the antebellum era through Reconstruction. He served in the Georgia legislature, represented Georgia in the United States House of Representatives before and after the Civil War, became the only vice president of the Confederate States of America, and ended his career as governor of Georgia.

Stephens was a committed defender of slavery and white political supremacy, but he was not an uncomplicated advocate of immediate secession. In January 1861 he warned Georgia against leaving the Union and voted against secession. Once Georgia seceded, however, he signed the ordinance, joined the Confederate government, and became one of its highest-ranking officials.

His historical importance to African American history rests especially on his public defense of slavery and racial hierarchy. In his March 1861 Cornerstone Speech, Stephens openly described African American inequality and enslavement as fundamental principles of the new Confederate political order. After the war, he returned to public office and opposed federal civil-rights legislation designed to protect formerly enslaved people.

Early Life in Georgia

Stephens was born on February 11, 1812, near Crawfordville in what became Taliaferro County, Georgia. His parents were Andrew Baskins Stephens and Margaret Grier Stephens.

Both parents died while he was still young, leaving him orphaned by age fourteen. Relatives and family friends helped support his education and early development.

The rural Georgia society in which Stephens grew up was shaped by plantation agriculture, slavery, and sharp distinctions of race and class. Those institutions formed the political environment in which he later built his career.

Education

Stephens attended Franklin College in Athens, the institution that later became the University of Georgia. He graduated in 1832.

After graduation he taught school for approximately eighteen months before studying law. He was admitted to the Georgia bar at Crawfordville in 1834.

Law provided Stephens with both an occupation and a route into public life. He developed a reputation for close argument, political calculation, and an unusually detailed command of constitutional questions.

Health and Personal Life

Stephens was physically frail for much of his life and suffered recurring illness. Contemporary observers frequently commented on his small stature and delicate health, although those conditions did not prevent him from sustaining a long political career.

He never married. Much of his adult life centered on politics, correspondence, legal work, reading, visitors, and the household at Liberty Hall.

Stephens also developed a reputation for helping some young men obtain an education. That personal generosity existed alongside his participation in a social system that denied enslaved African Americans control over their own lives and opportunities.

Liberty Hall

Stephens made his home at Liberty Hall in Crawfordville. The residence became closely associated with his public identity and later with the memory of his political career.

Liberty Hall was not simply a private retreat. It was a working household embedded in Georgia's slave society, and enslaved African Americans performed labor connected to the property. Historic documentation of the site includes surviving or recorded slave quarters.

Stephens's attachment to Liberty Hall later became part of his postwar image as the so-called Sage of Liberty Hall.

Slaveholding

Stephens personally held African Americans in slavery. His political defense of slavery therefore cannot be separated from his own material participation in the institution.

The number of people enslaved by Stephens changed over time, and surviving records do not always produce a single simple total for every year. The larger point is clear: his household and property operated within the legal regime of human bondage.

Stephens sometimes cultivated a paternal image of slaveholding, but paternalism did not alter the central legal reality. Enslaved people were treated as property under law and could not freely choose their residence, labor, movement, or legal status.

Entry into Georgia Politics

Stephens entered elective politics in the 1830s. He served in the Georgia House of Representatives from 1836 through 1841 and in the Georgia Senate in 1842.

He initially rose as a Whig, combining support for constitutional government and economic development with a firm commitment to the interests of the slaveholding South.

During these years he formed an important political partnership with Robert Toombs, another Georgia Whig who would later become a Confederate leader.

First Election to the U.S. House

In 1843 Stephens won election to the United States House of Representatives to fill a vacancy. He remained in the House until March 1859.

His prewar congressional career covered the central sectional controversies of the era: Texas annexation, the Mexican-American War, slavery in the territories, the Compromise of 1850, the Fugitive Slave Act, and the Kansas-Nebraska Act.

Stephens changed party labels as the national Whig coalition collapsed, but his commitment to protecting slavery and southern political power remained consistent.

Texas Annexation and Slavery

Stephens supported the annexation of Texas. The issue carried direct sectional implications because Texas entered the Union as a slave state and raised questions about the future balance of political power between free and slave states.

His position illustrates an important feature of his antebellum politics: he could oppose particular expansionist wars or immediate secession while still defending the expansion and security of slavery.

Mexican-American War

Stephens opposed the Mexican-American War, criticizing the conflict even while remaining committed to southern claims regarding slavery in territory acquired by the United States.

The territories gained from Mexico intensified the national argument over whether slavery could expand westward. Stephens opposed efforts such as the Wilmot Proviso that would have prohibited slavery in the new territories.

His opposition to the war should therefore not be mistaken for opposition to slavery. The two questions were distinct in his political thinking.

Compromise of 1850

Stephens became an important supporter of the Compromise of 1850, the package of measures intended to reduce sectional conflict over slavery and the territories.

He and Robert Toombs helped defend the settlement in Georgia. Their position contributed to the Georgia Platform, which accepted the compromise while warning that further restrictions on southern slavery interests could provoke resistance.

Stephens's support for compromise reflected his preference for preserving the Union so long as he believed slavery and southern constitutional claims remained protected within it.

The Fugitive Slave System

The Compromise of 1850 included a stronger federal Fugitive Slave Act. The law strengthened mechanisms for the capture and return of people alleged to have escaped slavery and limited procedural protections available to the accused.

Stephens defended the broader compromise that included the measure. For African Americans, the fugitive-slave regime made the federal government an active participant in enforcing slaveholders' property claims even in free states.

Kansas-Nebraska Act

Stephens supported the Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854 and helped move the legislation through the House of Representatives.

The act repealed the Missouri Compromise restriction on slavery north of the 36°30' line and allowed territorial settlers to decide the slavery question under popular sovereignty.

The law intensified sectional conflict and contributed to violence in Kansas. Stephens supported it because it removed a federal restriction on the possible expansion of slavery.

Political Defense of Slavery

Throughout his antebellum career Stephens defended slavery as a constitutionally protected institution and opposed federal measures that threatened slaveholder control.

He was less rhetorically radical than some southern fire-eaters during portions of the 1840s and 1850s, but moderation on timing or tactics did not make him an opponent of slavery.

His politics consistently treated the security of slavery as a major southern interest and as a legitimate subject of federal constitutional protection.

Violence in the 1848 Political Dispute

Stephens's political career included a notorious personal confrontation in 1848 with Georgia judge Francis H. Cone. The dispute grew out of intense arguments over sectional policy and Stephens's conduct in Congress.

Cone attacked and severely wounded Stephens with a knife. Stephens survived after a lengthy recovery.

The episode became part of Stephens's personal legend, but it should not obscure the underlying political context: disputes over slavery's expansion were already making southern politics unusually volatile years before secession.

Retirement from Congress in 1859

Stephens chose not to seek renomination in 1858 and left the House when his term ended in March 1859.

His retirement did not remove him from politics. The national party system was breaking down, the Republican Party was growing in the North, and southern political leaders were increasingly debating whether a Republican presidential victory would justify secession.

Stephens remained an influential Georgia voice during the approaching crisis.

Election of 1860 and Secession Crisis

Abraham Lincoln's election in November 1860 triggered immediate secession movements across the Deep South. South Carolina left the Union in December, and Georgia convened a special convention to decide its course.

Stephens opposed immediate secession. He argued that Lincoln's election by itself did not yet constitute a sufficient reason to abandon the Union and warned that secession could produce war.

His position placed him among Georgia cooperationists and conditional Unionists rather than the most aggressive secessionists.

Georgia Secession Convention

At Georgia's secession convention in January 1861, Stephens voted against the ordinance of secession.

The convention nevertheless approved secession by a wide margin. Once the state made that decision, Stephens did not remain a Union loyalist.

He signed the ordinance and accepted Georgia's decision as binding. This transition is central to understanding him: he opposed the timing and prudence of secession but subsequently gave the Confederacy his political service.

From Unionist to Confederate Leader

Stephens was selected as one of Georgia's delegates to the Provisional Confederate Congress meeting in Montgomery, Alabama.

Confederate leaders chose him as vice president of the provisional government in February 1861. His earlier reputation as a Unionist helped broaden the new government's appeal beyond the most radical secessionists.

He later won election as vice president under the permanent Confederate Constitution and remained in office until the Confederacy collapsed in 1865.

The Confederate Constitution

Stephens participated in the formation of the Confederate government and defended its constitution as an improvement over the United States Constitution.

The Confederate Constitution explicitly protected slavery in several ways and restricted the central government's ability to interfere with property in enslaved people.

Stephens presented these protections not as incidental provisions but as part of what he believed the new political order had resolved more clearly than the old Union.

The Cornerstone Speech

On March 21, 1861, Stephens delivered an extemporaneous address in Savannah that became known as the Cornerstone Speech.

He described slavery as the immediate cause of the sectional rupture and argued that the Confederacy rested on the proposition that Black people were not equal to white people and that racial subordination was their supposedly natural condition.

The speech is one of the clearest primary-source statements by a senior Confederate official connecting secession, slavery, and white supremacy. It is therefore essential evidence when evaluating later claims that slavery was peripheral to the creation of the Confederacy.

Racial Hierarchy

Stephens's proslavery ideology went beyond a defense of constitutional property rights. In the Cornerstone Speech he presented racial inequality as a basic social and political principle.

He rejected the egalitarian implications of the Declaration of Independence as applied across racial lines and portrayed white supremacy as consistent with what he regarded as science, nature, and social order.

For African Americans, this doctrine denied not only emancipation but equal citizenship. It supplied an ideological justification for a government designed to preserve their subordination.

Vice President of the Confederacy

Stephens initially had a meaningful role in the Confederate government and was consulted by President Jefferson Davis.

As the war continued, however, his influence diminished. He spent less time in Richmond and increasingly criticized centralizing wartime policies.

The vice presidency gave him high rank but relatively limited independent executive power. His historical importance came more from his political symbolism, speeches, constitutional arguments, and public disputes than from control of Confederate military strategy.

Relationship with Jefferson Davis

Stephens and Jefferson Davis developed a difficult working relationship. Stephens believed Davis and the Confederate government often exceeded proper constitutional limits.

He objected to policies including conscription, suspension of habeas corpus, and other wartime exercises of central authority. He aligned at times with Georgia governor Joseph E. Brown in resisting Confederate centralization.

These disputes reveal an internal contradiction within the Confederacy: leaders committed to creating an independent slaveholding nation still disagreed over how much power that nation's central government should possess.

States' Rights During the War

Stephens continued to emphasize state sovereignty even while serving as vice president of a government fighting a major war.

His criticism of Confederate conscription and emergency powers was based largely on constitutional and states' rights principles rather than opposition to the Confederacy's racial or slaveholding foundations.

Thus, opposition to Davis should not be confused with opposition to the Confederate project itself.

Peace Advocacy

As Confederate military prospects deteriorated, Stephens increasingly supported negotiation as a path toward ending the war.

His peace efforts reflected dissatisfaction with Davis's leadership, concern over Confederate losses, and a belief that political negotiation might achieve results that battlefield victory increasingly could not.

He did not, however, embrace unconditional restoration of the Union on the federal government's terms while Confederate independence remained a possible negotiating objective.

Hampton Roads Conference

In February 1865 Stephens headed a Confederate delegation that met President Abraham Lincoln and Secretary of State William H. Seward at Hampton Roads, Virginia.

The meeting reunited Stephens and Lincoln, who had known one another as Whig members of Congress in the 1840s.

Lincoln made clear that restoration of federal authority and the end of armed rebellion were essential conditions for peace. The conference produced no settlement, and the war continued for several more weeks.

Collapse of the Confederacy

By spring 1865 Confederate military resistance was collapsing. Richmond fell, Robert E. Lee surrendered the Army of Northern Virginia, and the Confederate government dispersed.

Stephens returned to his Georgia home rather than becoming a leader of prolonged guerrilla resistance.

Federal troops arrested him at Crawfordville on May 11, 1865.

Imprisonment at Fort Warren

After his arrest, Stephens was transported north and confined at Fort Warren in Boston Harbor.

He remained there for approximately five months, from late May until October 1865. His prison diary later became an important source for his reflections on defeat, confinement, politics, and the future.

Stephens was never tried for treason. He was released as President Andrew Johnson's administration moved toward a relatively lenient approach to restoring former Confederate states.

Attempted Return to the U.S. Senate

In 1866 the Georgia legislature selected Stephens for the United States Senate.

His selection produced national controversy. The former vice president of the Confederacy was being sent back to Congress before Georgia had been fully restored to congressional representation.

Stephens did not take a Senate seat. Congressional Republicans refused to recognize the attempted restoration of former Confederate leadership under the terms then being pursued by President Andrew Johnson.

A Constitutional View of the Late War

During his postwar period outside federal office, Stephens wrote A Constitutional View of the Late War Between the States, published in two volumes between 1868 and 1870.

The work interpreted the Civil War primarily through constitutional disputes over sovereignty, compact theory, and the relationship between states and the federal government.

That postwar framing differed sharply in emphasis from the Cornerstone Speech, where Stephens had explicitly identified African slavery and racial hierarchy as central to the Confederate revolution. The contrast became important in the later development of Lost Cause interpretations.

Return to the U.S. House

Stephens returned to the United States House of Representatives in 1873, fourteen years after his earlier congressional service had ended.

He served continuously from December 1873 until resigning in November 1882. His return demonstrated how rapidly some former Confederate leaders regained national political influence after the Civil War.

He chaired the House Committee on Coinage, Weights, and Measures during several Congresses.

Reconstruction and Black Citizenship

Stephens returned to Congress after the Thirteenth, Fourteenth, and Fifteenth Amendments had transformed the constitutional status of African Americans.

Slavery had been abolished, birthright citizenship and equal protection had been constitutionalized, and Black male voting rights had received explicit federal protection.

Stephens did not accept the expansive Reconstruction-era view that the federal government should actively guarantee equal civil rights against discriminatory state practices.

Opposition to the Civil Rights Bill

In January 1874 Stephens gave a lengthy House speech opposing the federal civil-rights legislation that eventually became the Civil Rights Act of 1875.

He framed the dispute as a conflict between constitutionalism and centralization and argued that many matters involving civil relations remained within state authority.

In practice, that position would have left African Americans heavily dependent on state governments at a time when racial discrimination and political violence were widespread across the former Confederacy.

Debate with Black Members of Congress

Black representatives directly challenged Stephens's constitutional position during the Civil Rights Act debates.

South Carolina Representative Robert B. Elliott argued that the Reconstruction Amendments empowered Congress to protect citizens against racial discrimination. Other Black lawmakers, including Alonzo Ransier and James T. Rapier, likewise rejected narrow interpretations that left equal rights dependent on hostile state governments.

The exchanges were historically striking: a former Confederate vice president who had publicly defended racial inequality was now debating Black federal legislators who held office under the constitutional order created by Union victory and Reconstruction.

Postwar Views and Lost Cause Memory

After the Confederacy's defeat, Stephens increasingly emphasized constitutional conflict and state sovereignty when explaining the war.

Those themes were real parts of his political thought, but treating them as substitutes for slavery distorts his own 1861 statements. His Cornerstone Speech explicitly connected the new Confederate order to slavery and racial inequality.

Stephens's postwar writings therefore became useful to later Lost Cause narratives that minimized slavery's central role in secession while emphasizing abstract constitutional disagreement.

Governor of Georgia

In 1882 Stephens left Congress after winning election as governor of Georgia.

He took office late in life and in declining health. His governorship lasted only a few months.

The election represented the final stage of an extraordinary political restoration: a former Confederate vice president had returned first to Congress and then to the chief executive office of his state.

Death

Stephens died in Atlanta on March 4, 1883, while serving as governor. He was seventy-one years old.

He was initially interred in Atlanta's Oakland Cemetery and was later reinterred at Liberty Hall near Crawfordville.

His home eventually became part of a Georgia state historic park, preserving both the residence associated with Stephens and material reminders of the slave society in which he lived.

Impact on African Americans

Stephens's importance to African American history is not limited to a single speech. Across decades, he helped defend a political system in which African Americans could be enslaved, excluded from equal citizenship, and subordinated by law.

Before the Civil War he supported policies that protected slavery and permitted its possible expansion. In 1861 he became vice president of a nation founded by seceding slave states and publicly described racial inequality as one of that nation's foundational principles.

After emancipation, he opposed federal civil-rights legislation intended to protect Black citizens from discriminatory treatment. His career therefore spans three distinct forms of antagonism: defense of slavery, leadership in the Confederacy, and resistance to expansive federal enforcement of Black civil equality.

Stephens as an Antagonist

Stephens belongs in the political and ideological category of antagonists of African American freedom rather than the category of direct racial terrorists.

He was not a Klan assassin, mob leader, or segregationist police official. His influence operated through legislation, constitutional argument, national office, political organization, and the Confederate government.

That distinction matters because white supremacy depended not only on direct violence but also on educated political leaders who supplied legal theories, institutional legitimacy, and public arguments for racial domination.

The Unionist Paradox

Stephens's opposition to immediate secession is one of the most important complications in his biography.

He believed southern interests, including slavery, could still be protected within the Union and feared the consequences of premature separation. His January 1861 vote against secession was therefore genuine.

But his Unionism had limits. Once Georgia left the Union, he signed the secession ordinance, joined the Confederate government, defended its founding principles, and remained its vice president through the war. Opposition to secession did not translate into opposition to slavery or loyalty to the United States after Georgia's decision.

Historical Significance

Alexander H. Stephens is historically significant because his life connects antebellum congressional politics, the defense of slavery, the secession crisis, Confederate nation-building, wartime constitutional conflict, Reconstruction, and the restoration of former Confederate political elites.

His Cornerstone Speech remains especially important because it provides unusually direct evidence from the Confederacy's second-highest official about the relationship between slavery, racial hierarchy, and the Confederate project.

His later career also demonstrates that defeat in the Civil War did not permanently remove former Confederate leaders from American public life.

Legacy

Stephens was remembered for generations in Georgia as a major statesman, and his name and image were incorporated into public memorials.

Modern historical interpretation gives greater weight to the enslaved people connected to Liberty Hall, to Stephens's defense of racial slavery, to his Confederate leadership, and to his postwar opposition to federal civil-rights enforcement.

A balanced assessment therefore requires holding several truths together: Stephens was intelligent, constitutionally sophisticated, personally capable of generosity, and genuinely opposed to immediate secession; he was also a slaveholder, a determined defender of slavery, the vice president of the Confederacy, an explicit theorist of racial inequality, and a postwar opponent of major federal civil-rights protections.

Geographic Scope

Crawfordville and Taliaferro County, Georgia, formed the center of Stephens's personal and political identity.

Washington, D.C., was the principal setting for his long U.S. congressional career before and after the Civil War.

Montgomery, Alabama, was the birthplace of the provisional Confederate government in which Stephens became vice president. Richmond, Virginia, later became the Confederate capital.

Savannah, Georgia, was the site of the Cornerstone Speech. Hampton Roads, Virginia, was the setting of the 1865 peace conference. Fort Warren in Boston Harbor was the site of Stephens's postwar imprisonment.

Legal and Constitutional Context

Stephens's career intersected with several major constitutional transformations: the antebellum debate over slavery in the territories, secession, Confederate constitutionalism, emancipation, and the Reconstruction Amendments.

Before the war he generally argued that slavery was protected by the constitutional rights of slaveholders and should not be excluded from federal territories by congressional prohibition.

After the war he interpreted the Reconstruction Amendments more narrowly than Black and Republican lawmakers who argued that Congress possessed broad authority to protect equal civil rights against discriminatory state action.
