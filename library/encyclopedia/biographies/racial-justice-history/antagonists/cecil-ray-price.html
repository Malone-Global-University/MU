Cecil Ray Price

Neshoba County chief deputy sheriff, White Knights Klansman, and federally convicted civil-rights conspirator who used state police authority in the 1964 kidnapping and murders of James Chaney, Andrew Goodman, and Michael Schwerner

Overview

Cecil Ray Price was an American salesman, fire chief, deputy sheriff, Ku Klux Klan member, federal civil-rights conspirator, and one of the central operational participants in the 1964 murders of civil-rights workers James Chaney, Andrew Goodman, and Michael Schwerner near Philadelphia, Mississippi.

Price was born on April 15, 1938, in Flora, Mississippi. He graduated from Flora High School in 1956.

Before becoming a Neshoba County law-enforcement officer, biographical accounts describe Price working as a dairy-supplies salesman and serving as a local fire chief.

By 1964 Price was the chief deputy sheriff of Neshoba County under Sheriff Lawrence Andrew Rainey.

He was also a member of the Philadelphia-area Klavern of the White Knights of the Ku Klux Klan of Mississippi.

That combination—sworn public officer and secret white-supremacist conspirator—made Price one of the most legally significant defendants in the Mississippi Burning case.

Price did not merely fail to protect Chaney, Goodman, and Schwerner.

Federal court records established that his official arrest, detention, calculated release, pursuit, recapture, and participation in the murder operation were integral parts of the conspiracy.

The Fifth Circuit later described the evidence against Price as overwhelming.

His use of government authority also made the case constitutionally important.

The Supreme Court's 1966 decision in United States v. Price held that private citizens who act jointly with state officials can themselves be treated as acting under color of law for purposes of federal civil-rights statutes.

The Court emphasized that the alleged conspiracy was made possible by state detention and calculated release of the victims.

Price therefore became the named defendant in one of the most important Reconstruction-statute civil-rights decisions of the twentieth century.

The immediate background was Freedom Summer.

Civil-rights organizations planned a massive 1964 campaign to challenge Mississippi's systematic suppression of Black voting, establish Freedom Schools, and build durable local political institutions.

Michael Schwerner and James Chaney were experienced movement workers based in Meridian. Andrew Goodman had arrived in Mississippi only shortly before the Neshoba trip.

Schwerner had become a particular target of the White Knights because of his work with Black communities in eastern Mississippi.

On June 16, 1964, Klansmen attacked Black parishioners at Mount Zion Methodist Church in Neshoba County and burned the church.

Chaney and Schwerner had worked with members of the congregation and hoped to use the church for Freedom Summer activity.

After learning of the attack while at volunteer training in Ohio, Chaney, Schwerner, and Goodman returned to Mississippi.

On Sunday, June 21, they travelled into Neshoba County to inspect the burned church and speak with local residents.

At about 3 p.m., Price stopped their 1963 Ford station wagon while they were returning toward Meridian.

The Department of Justice states that Price recognized Schwerner and identified the station wagon as belonging to the Council of Federated Organizations.

Price arrested all three men.

He booked Chaney, the Black Mississippi driver, for speeding and held Schwerner and Goodman 'for investigation,' ostensibly concerning the church arson.

He transported them to the Neshoba County jail in Philadelphia.

Price then contacted local Klan leader Edgar Ray Killen and informed him that Schwerner and the other two civil-rights workers were in custody.

That notification set the local Klan machinery into motion.

Killen travelled to Meridian, gathered Klansmen, instructed them to bring guns and gloves, and arranged for men from outside Neshoba County to participate.

The use of non-local Klansmen reduced the likelihood that victims or witnesses would identify the attackers.

Price kept the three activists in custody while the Klan assembled.

Justice Department trial records describe Chaney paying a $20 speeding fine before the men were finally released late that night.

Price ordered them to leave Neshoba County and initially escorted them toward the road to Meridian.

The release was not the end of Price's role.

Price drove his official patrol car to meet two carloads of Klansmen outside Philadelphia.

The group then pursued the civil-rights workers at high speed.

When the station wagon attempted to evade the pursuers, Price used his police authority to stop it again.

The three men were forced into Price's patrol car.

A Klansman took control of the CORE station wagon.

The convoy reversed direction and travelled to an isolated road south of Philadelphia.

There Chaney, Goodman, and Schwerner were assaulted and shot to death.

Price was not merely somewhere else while the murders occurred.

The Fifth Circuit expressly found that Price was present and participated in the murder of the three men and the disposal of their bodies.

That makes his role categorically different from Edgar Ray Killen, who organized the operation but arranged an alibi away from the killing site, and Sam Bowers, who authorized the Klan elimination from the state leadership level.

The victims' bodies were transported to an earthen dam on property associated with Olen Burrage.

Heavy equipment was used to bury them beneath approximately fifteen feet of earth.

The murder party then dispersed.

On June 22 the FBI confronted Price.

He admitted that he had arrested and jailed the three men but claimed that after releasing them he last saw them travelling toward Meridian.

Investigators became suspicious when records and interviews could not account for Price's whereabouts during the period after the release.

The missing men rapidly became the focus of a massive federal investigation.

The FBI used the code name MIBURN, short for Mississippi Burning.

The burned station wagon was discovered on June 23.

The three bodies were not found until August 4, when investigators located them beneath the earthen dam.

Price participated in the recovery operation after the bodies were found.

Secondary trial-history accounts report that investigators deliberately allowed him to assist while observing his reactions.

Mississippi did not bring ordinary murder charges against Price or the other principal conspirators during the 1960s.

That failure forced the federal government to rely on criminal civil-rights statutes descending from Reconstruction.

Federal prosecutors charged that the conspirators had deprived Chaney, Goodman, and Schwerner of life and liberty without due process of law.

Because Price was a deputy sheriff acting through official power, his participation supplied the state-action element that made the federal constitutional theory especially powerful.

Federal district judge William Harold Cox initially dismissed substantial portions of the indictments.

The Justice Department appealed directly to the Supreme Court.

On March 28, 1966, the Supreme Court unanimously reversed the dismissals in the major respects at issue.

The Court held that a private person who willfully joins state officials in unconstitutional conduct can act under color of law even without holding public office.

The Court described the alleged conspiracy as official lawlessness made possible by state detention and calculated release.

The case returned to Mississippi for trial.

Eighteen defendants went on trial in federal court in Meridian in October 1967.

Price was one of seven defendants convicted by the jury.

A separate conspirator, James Edward Jordan, had pleaded guilty and cooperated, bringing the total number successfully prosecuted federally to eight.

Price was convicted under federal civil-rights conspiracy law.

He was not convicted of state murder.

Judge Cox sentenced Price to six years in federal prison.

The sentence was notably shorter than the ten-year maximum imposed on Sam Bowers and Alton Wayne Roberts.

The sentencing disparities reflected the limited federal statutes available and Judge Cox's sentencing choices; they should not be mistaken for a ranking of each participant's moral seriousness.

Price appealed his conviction along with the other convicted defendants.

In 1969 the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit affirmed.

The appellate court stated that direct and circumstantial evidence of Price's guilt was overwhelming.

It specifically found overwhelming evidence that the conspirators planned for Price to arrest and hold the three men until the Klan could intercept them after release.

The court also found that Price and the other operational defendants were present at and participated in the murders and the burial of the bodies.

The United States Supreme Court declined further review of the convictions.

Price served his sentence at the federal prison in Sandstone, Minnesota.

Sources differ slightly on the exact amount of time he actually served. Contemporary obituary reporting says about four years; some later case histories say approximately four and a half years.

He was released during the 1970s and returned to Philadelphia, Mississippi.

After prison Price worked in a variety of ordinary jobs, including surveying, driving for an oil company, repairing watches in a jewelry store, and later truck-driving work.

He did not regain a law-enforcement career comparable to the one he had held in 1964.

Price's later racial attitudes are more difficult to evaluate than his 1964 conduct.

He generally refused to give detailed public accounts of the murder conspiracy.

A 1977 interview reported that Price said integration had become a reality that people had to accept.

Later secondary accounts also report that he told a Black minister he had repented and that he had cordial relationships with some Black residents.

Those reports deserve inclusion but should not be exaggerated into a documented public confession.

Price never became known for a detailed public acknowledgment of his role in the murders, a comprehensive apology to the Chaney, Goodman, and Schwerner families, or testimony identifying every remaining participant.

His later personal conduct therefore may show some degree of changed racial behavior without establishing full public accountability.

One frequently discussed later episode involved Marcus Dupree, the Black football star from Philadelphia.

Price's son Cecil Jr. and Dupree were friends, and later accounts describe Price assisting Dupree with obtaining a commercial driver's license after Dupree's professional football career.

That episode illustrates how local interpersonal relationships in later decades could become less racially rigid even while the unresolved historical crime remained.

It does not erase the fact that Price had used police authority in 1964 to deliver three civil-rights workers into a Klan murder conspiracy.

The 1988 film Mississippi Burning renewed public attention to the case.

The fictional deputy sheriff Clinton Pell, played by Brad Dourif, was substantially based on Price.

The movie should not be treated as a literal biography.

It altered names, compressed participants, invented scenes, and emphasized fictional FBI agents while reducing the visibility of local Black organizers and movement institutions.

Price's actual life is better reconstructed from federal trial records, appellate opinions, FBI files, Justice Department investigations, and contemporary journalism.

By the late 1990s Mississippi authorities began seriously considering state murder prosecutions in the old case.

Attorney General Mike Moore and Neshoba County prosecutors reviewed old files and interviewed witnesses.

Price became one of the most important surviving subjects of that renewed investigation.

He potentially occupied two roles: a principal murder defendant or a uniquely valuable cooperating witness capable of identifying the full conspiracy.

He died before prosecutors could resolve that question.

On May 3, 2001, while working at an equipment-rental business in Philadelphia, Price fell approximately thirteen feet from a lift and struck his head on a trailer.

He suffered a fractured skull.

Price died on May 6, 2001, at a hospital in Jackson, Mississippi.

He was sixty-three years old.

Mississippi Attorney General Mike Moore publicly described the death as a major blow to the renewed case, saying that Price could have become either a principal defendant or the prosecution's best witness.

Price was never prosecuted by Mississippi for murder.

That distinction must remain visible.

It is accurate to say that the Fifth Circuit found he participated in the murders and that the federal jury convicted him of the civil-rights conspiracy.

It is inaccurate to say that a Mississippi jury convicted him of murder.

His biography is particularly important to civil-rights law because the crime was not only private Klan terrorism.

Price placed the power of the sheriff's department at the service of the Klan.

The Supreme Court's opinion emphasized that state officers participated in the venture from detention and release through interception, assault, and murder.

This made the conspiracy a particularly stark form of official lawlessness.

For MGU's antagonist series, Cecil Ray Price is best understood as a law-enforcement antagonist who converted lawful state authority into a mechanism of racial terrorism.

He was not merely a sheriff's deputy who looked the other way.

He arrested the victims, controlled their detention, notified the Klan, released them when the murder party was ready, pursued them in an official patrol car, recaptured them through the authority of his badge, and participated in the fatal operation and burial.

His later life may contain evidence of some personal change, but no later conduct can alter the constitutional importance of what happened in 1964: public police power became part of a private white-supremacist murder conspiracy.

His operational role made him an obvious possible state murder defendant.

Potential Witness

His inside knowledge also made him potentially the strongest surviving witness against other conspirators.

Death Before Resolution

Price died before prosecutors could obtain either a state indictment or full cooperation.

May 3, 2001 Accident

Price fell from a lift while working at an equipment-rental store.

Thirteen-Foot Fall

Witnesses described a fall of approximately thirteen feet.

Head Injury

He struck his head and suffered a fractured skull.

Jackson Hospital

Price was taken to a hospital in Jackson.

May 6 Death

He died three days after the accident.

Age Sixty-Three

Price was sixty-three years old.

Attorney General Reaction

Mike Moore described Price's death as a serious blow to the renewed case.

Principal Defendant or Best Witness

Moore said Price could have been either a principal defendant or the state's best witness.

Investigation Continues

Authorities continued investigating after his death.

2005 Edgar Ray Killen Trial

Four years later Mississippi prosecuted Killen.

Killen Convicted of Manslaughter

Killen was convicted of three manslaughter counts, not murder.

Price Never Faced State Homicide Trial

Price therefore died without a Mississippi homicide verdict.

2016 DOJ Closure

Federal and state authorities eventually concluded that no additional prosecutable case could be established.

Nine Ultimately Prosecuted

DOJ counted eight successful federal prosecutions plus Killen's later state manslaughter conviction.

Price Included in Federal Eight

Price was one of the federal defendants who had actually been held criminally accountable.

Was Price Sheriff?

No. In 1964 he was chief deputy sheriff under Lawrence Rainey.

Did Price Run for Sheriff?

Yes. He ran in 1967 but lost.

Was Price a Klan Member?

Yes. The Department of Justice and Fifth Circuit identify him as a White Knights member.

Did Price Merely Arrest the Men?

No. Federal courts found that he participated in the planned release, interception, murders, and burial.

Were the Initial Arrests Unrelated to the Murders?

No. The federal appellate court found overwhelming evidence that the detention was part of the conspiracy.

Was Chaney Actually Charged With Speeding?

Yes. Federal records state that Price booked Chaney for speeding and collected a fine.

Were Goodman and Schwerner Charged With Arson?

They were held for 'investigation' concerning the church fire rather than convicted or lawfully punished for arson.

Did Price Release Them Freely?

The release was part of a calculated plan enabling the Klan to intercept them.

Did Price Follow Them After Release?

Yes. DOJ reconstruction and appellate findings place Price in the pursuit.

Did Price Use His Patrol Car?

Yes.

Did Price Recapture Them?

Yes. Federal records state that he stopped them again and forced them into his patrol car.

Did Price Personally Shoot Them?

The strongest records establish his presence and participation but more specifically identify other Klansmen as the direct shooters; the page therefore does not claim a proven fatal shot by Price.

Was Price at the Murder Site?

Yes according to the Fifth Circuit's evidentiary findings.

Did Price Help Dispose of the Bodies?

The Fifth Circuit included him among participants in the burial operation.

Was Price Convicted of Murder?

No.

Was Price Convicted of a Federal Crime?

Yes, conspiracy to deprive the victims of constitutional rights.

Why Is United States v. Price Important?

It established that private conspirators acting jointly with state officers may fall within federal color-of-law civil-rights liability.

Did the Supreme Court Convict Price?

No. The Supreme Court resolved legal questions allowing the prosecution to proceed; a federal jury later convicted him.

Did Price Serve Ten Years?

No. He received a six-year sentence.

Did He Serve the Full Six Years?

No. Sources place his actual imprisonment at roughly four to four and one-half years.

Was Price Acquitted on Appeal?

No. The Fifth Circuit affirmed his conviction.

Did Price Publicly Confess Later?

No comprehensive public confession became part of the record.

Did Price's Racial Attitudes Change?

Some later reports suggest more accepting personal behavior and statements, but the available evidence does not establish a full public reckoning with the murders.

Did Price Die Before the 2005 Killen Trial?

Yes. He died in 2001.

Was His Death Natural?

No. He died from injuries sustained in a workplace fall.

Was Price Still Under Investigation When He Died?

Yes. Mississippi prosecutors were actively reviewing possible state murder charges.

Why Price Matters Legally

His abuse of police authority transformed a private Klan plot into a constitutional case about official state lawlessness.

Why Price Matters Historically

His actions demonstrate how civil-rights terrorism could depend upon cooperation from sworn officers rather than operating wholly outside government.

Historical Significance

Cecil Ray Price's biography connects local policing, Ku Klux Klan organization, Freedom Summer, church burning, voter suppression, kidnapping, murder, federal civil-rights statutes, Reconstruction law, the Supreme Court's color-of-law doctrine, the Mississippi Burning trial, delayed state accountability, and the problem of evaluating later personal change without documented public confession.

Legacy

Price's central historical legacy is the corruption of public authority. The badge that should have protected prisoners became a tool for holding them until killers were ready. His conviction did not fully punish the murders as homicide, but United States v. Price helped strengthen the principle that state officers and private accomplices cannot escape federal civil-rights law when they jointly use official power to strip citizens of life and liberty.

Geographic Scope

Flora, Mississippi, was Price's birthplace and childhood community.

Philadelphia, Mississippi, became the center of his law-enforcement career, Klan activity, arrest of the three civil-rights workers, later civilian life, and fatal workplace accident.

Longdale and Mount Zion connected the victims' Neshoba County investigation to the Klan church attack.

Highway 19 and rural roads south of Philadelphia formed the route of the release, pursuit, recapture, and killing operation.

The earthen dam southwest of Philadelphia became the burial site.

Meridian became the movement base for Schwerner and Chaney and the site of the 1967 federal trial.

Sandstone, Minnesota, became the site of Price's federal imprisonment.

Jackson, Mississippi, became the location of Price's death after the 2001 workplace injury.

Legal and Constitutional Context

Price's case became a landmark because his official status connected private Klan violence directly to state action.

The Supreme Court held in United States v. Price that a private citizen who is a willful participant in joint activity with state officials can act under color of law for purposes of federal civil-rights statutes.

The Court also held that 18 U.S.C. § 241 protected Fourteenth Amendment rights, allowing federal prosecution of a conspiracy to deprive citizens of life and liberty without due process.

The Fifth Circuit later affirmed Price's conviction and concluded that overwhelming evidence established the planned arrest, detention, release, interception, killing, and disposal operation.

Mississippi never obtained a state homicide judgment against Price because he died before the renewed state investigation produced charges.
