Edward Young Clarke

Atlanta publicity executive, Imperial Kleagle, acting Imperial Wizard, and commercial organizer who helped transform the second Ku Klux Klan into a national mass movement

Overview

Edward Young Clarke (born about 1877; date of death uncertain) was an Atlanta businessman, professional fundraiser, advertising executive, and one of the most consequential organizers of the second Ku Klux Klan. William Joseph Simmons had revived the Klan in Georgia in 1915, but for several years it remained relatively small. Clarke and his business partner Mary Elizabeth “Bessie” Tyler changed that trajectory. Through their Southern Publicity Association, they applied commercial advertising, commissioned salesmanship, direct-mail techniques, newspaper publicity, fraternal recruiting, and a carefully structured hierarchy of paid organizers to the business of selling Klan membership. The result was explosive growth during the early 1920s.

Clarke's historical importance lies less in inventing the Klan's ideology than in making that ideology scalable. The second Klan promoted white supremacy while also attacking Catholics, Jews, immigrants, political radicals, and people accused of violating Protestant moral norms. Clarke and Tyler learned to tailor the organization's message to local grievances. In one community recruiters could emphasize Black political or social advancement; elsewhere they might emphasize Catholic influence, immigration, Prohibition enforcement, public morality, or antisemitic conspiracy theories. This broadened the Klan from a primarily southern white-supremacist revival into a national organization capable of recruiting native-born white Protestants far beyond the former Confederacy.

Clarke rose to the title of Imperial Kleagle, effectively placing him over the Klan's national recruiting apparatus, and in 1922 he served as acting Imperial Wizard during a period of internal struggle. His career also demonstrated the movement's contradictions. The Klan publicly advertised moral discipline while its leadership was repeatedly damaged by allegations of profiteering, sexual scandal, factional conflict, and violence by members. Clarke eventually broke with the organization and publicly criticized its political direction. In 1924 he pleaded guilty in federal court to a Mann Act violation after federal investigators used the interstate transportation charge to reach a prominent Klan leader whose organization was otherwise difficult for the federal government to prosecute. His later life was much less influential, but the recruitment machine he helped construct had already helped turn the 1920s Klan into one of the largest extremist mass movements in American history.

Early Life and Career in Publicity

Clarke's early life is less thoroughly documented than the careers of many politicians or public officials of the period. Census and contemporary records place his birth in Georgia around 1877, and his adult career became closely associated with Atlanta. He came of age as the city was becoming one of the South's principal commercial, transportation, newspaper, and promotional centers. Atlanta's business culture rewarded boosterism: organizers sold conventions, charities, civic campaigns, fraternal organizations, and political causes using many of the same techniques later associated with professional public relations.

Clarke developed experience in fraternal promotion and public events before entering Klan leadership. That background was crucial. He did not approach political organizing primarily as a theorist or elected official. He approached it as a salesman. Causes required slogans, lists of prospects, commissioned field representatives, publicity events, press attention, and a revenue structure capable of paying the people who generated new business.

Mary Elizabeth Tyler became his most important professional partner. Together they operated the Atlanta-based Southern Publicity Association, a promotional and fundraising firm that worked for civic, charitable, temperance, and organizational clients. Their pre-Klan work demonstrated that the same basic machinery could be used to promote very different causes. When they encountered William Joseph Simmons's struggling Ku Klux Klan, they recognized an organization with intense symbolism and emotional appeal but an inadequate sales system.

William J. Simmons and the Struggling Second Klan

William Joseph Simmons formally revived the Ku Klux Klan in Atlanta in 1915. Inspired partly by romanticized memories of the Reconstruction-era Klan, the Leo Frank case, fraternal ritual, and the enormous cultural impact of D. W. Griffith's The Birth of a Nation, Simmons created an organization restricted to native-born white Protestant men. He developed robes, titles, ceremonies, passwords, and a mythology portraying the Klan as guardian of white Protestant civilization.

The revival initially grew slowly. Ritual alone did not produce a national movement. Simmons lacked the financial and administrative infrastructure necessary to locate prospects, train recruiters, open chapters, collect initiation fees, and sustain publicity across dozens of states. By 1920, Clarke and Tyler's Southern Publicity Association offered a solution.

The arrangement transformed both parties. Simmons supplied the name, charter, racial mythology, fraternal ritual, and authority of the “Invisible Empire.” Clarke and Tyler supplied professional promotion. They received a financial interest in successful recruitment, giving the publicity operation a direct incentive to maximize membership. What had been an ideological fraternity increasingly functioned like a national sales organization.

The Southern Publicity Association and the Business of Recruitment

Clarke and Tyler reorganized Klan expansion around paid field organizers known as kleagles. Rather than depending primarily on volunteers who happened to be enthusiastic about the cause, the new system rewarded recruiters for producing paying members. Initiation fees were divided through the organizational hierarchy, with portions flowing to recruiters and higher officials. The financial structure made “kluxing”—selling Klan membership—a profession for some organizers.

The model created rapid replication. A successful recruiter could enter a community, identify grievances, cultivate ministers or businessmen, hold invitation-only meetings, initiate members, organize a local klavern, and then move to the next territory. Higher-level organizers supervised regions and received portions of the revenue generated beneath them. Robes, regalia, publications, and other Klan products added further commercial value.

Clarke eventually became Imperial Kleagle, the official most closely identified with national recruitment. Contemporary claims about total membership varied widely and Klan officials had strong incentives to exaggerate them, but historians agree on the direction and scale of the change. An organization that had numbered only in the thousands before the publicity campaign grew into the hundreds of thousands and then into a national movement claiming millions of adherents. By the middle of the decade, the Klan exercised substantial influence in municipal and state politics across several regions of the country.

Selling Different Prejudices to Different Communities

One of Clarke and Tyler's most important innovations was not a new doctrine but a new marketing method. The Klan did not have to lead with precisely the same grievance everywhere. Recruiters were encouraged to determine which perceived threat would most effectively mobilize native-born white Protestants in a particular community.

In much of the South, Black social advancement, voting, labor competition, interracial contact, and the preservation of white supremacy remained central themes. In northern and western communities with smaller Black populations, recruiters could place greater emphasis on Catholic political influence, Jewish immigrants, southern and eastern European immigration, public schools, Prohibition, supposed sexual immorality, labor radicalism, or fear of Bolshevism. The Klan packaged these grievances within the language of “100 percent Americanism.”

This flexibility was strategically powerful because it allowed different forms of prejudice to reinforce one another. A person attracted primarily by anti-Catholicism entered an organization committed to white supremacy. A segregationist entered an organization circulating antisemitic and anti-immigrant propaganda. A prohibitionist concerned about bootlegging could be introduced to racial and religious conspiracy theories. Clarke's recruiting system therefore created not merely more members but a network through which multiple prejudices could be consolidated into one political identity.

How Clarke Opposed Black Americans

Clarke's opposition to Black Americans operated chiefly through institution-building. He was not simply a man who held racist opinions in private. He helped create the recruitment machinery of an organization explicitly dedicated to white supremacy. The Klan defended segregation, opposed interracial relationships, resisted Black political and social equality, and portrayed white Protestant control of American institutions as legitimate and necessary.

Clarke's particular contribution was to make those ideas easier to organize around. His publicity network recruited large numbers of white Americans into local organizations where racial hierarchy was reinforced through ritual, propaganda, political endorsements, boycotts, intimidation, and community pressure. The second Klan's reach varied greatly by state and locality, but where it became powerful, Black citizens could face employment pressure, political exclusion, threats, surveillance, mob intimidation, and violence carried out in the name of preserving white control.

He also helped recast white supremacy as part of a broader moral and patriotic platform. Instead of presenting the Klan only as an organization hostile to Black people, publicity portrayed it as defender of schools, families, law enforcement, Protestantism, Prohibition, and national loyalty. That packaging made racial exclusion more socially marketable to whites who might not have joined an organization advertised only as a racial terror movement.

Violence, Intimidation, and Organizational Responsibility

The second Klan was not identical everywhere, and it is historically important to distinguish between crimes committed by particular members, violence ordered by local leaders, and actions directly authorized by national officials. Clarke should not automatically be assigned personal responsibility for every assault, kidnapping, whipping, or murder associated with a klavern merely because he helped build the organization.

At the same time, the national recruiting system cannot be separated from the coercive power that made Klan membership meaningful. In areas of high Klan strength, the organization's secrecy, numbers, robes, political connections, and reputation for extralegal punishment created intimidation even when no assault occurred. The FBI's later historical account of the Louisiana investigation describes a movement powerful enough that Governor John M. Parker appealed for federal assistance after kidnappings, torture, killings, and threats were attributed to Klan power in his state.

Clarke helped build the system that multiplied those local organizations. His documented historical responsibility therefore lies in scaling an institution whose ideology sanctioned white supremacy and whose presence repeatedly coincided with intimidation and violence, even where the evidentiary record does not show Clarke personally ordering a particular attack.

The 1921 Press Exposé and Congressional Scrutiny

Rapid growth produced intense scrutiny. In 1921 the New York World launched a major investigative series exposing the Klan's internal finances, leadership, secrecy, prejudice, and alleged violence. Newspapers around the country republished the investigation. Instead of remaining a mysterious southern fraternity, the Klan became a national political controversy.

The U.S. House of Representatives conducted hearings into the organization. Simmons defended the Klan and denied that national leadership authorized unlawful violence, while critics focused on the organization's secrecy, coercive local practices, and the substantial sums of money generated through initiation fees and regalia. Clarke and Tyler's commercial recruitment apparatus was central to the controversy because it demonstrated that Klan expansion was also a profitable business.

The exposure did not immediately destroy the organization. Public controversy sometimes generated additional curiosity and recruitment, and Klan organizers learned that hostile publicity could still function as publicity. Yet scrutiny deepened internal factionalism and exposed the gap between the movement's public claims of moral purification and the financial and personal behavior of some of its leaders.

Personal Scandal and the Problem of “Moral” Leadership

Clarke's own private conduct became a serious liability. Reports concerning his relationship with Tyler and an earlier arrest embarrassed an organization that publicly presented itself as guardian of sexual morality, family order, sobriety, and Protestant virtue. Opponents used the scandal to portray Klan leadership as hypocritical, while internal rivals used it in struggles for power.

The episode is historically important not because private scandal alone explains the Klan's politics, but because morality was part of the product Clarke sold. Recruiters promised that the Klan would discipline communities, suppress vice, punish adulterers and bootleggers, enforce traditional gender roles, and defend respectable family life. Conduct by senior officials that contradicted those claims therefore threatened the credibility of the entire recruitment model.

Acting Imperial Wizard and the Marcus Garvey Meeting

By 1922 Clarke had risen beyond recruitment management and temporarily exercised the authority of acting Imperial Wizard during a period when Simmons's control was weakening. His prominence produced one of the most controversial meetings in the history of early Black nationalism: his June 1922 encounter in Atlanta with Marcus Garvey, founder of the Universal Negro Improvement Association.

Garvey and Clarke represented fundamentally opposed racial movements. Clarke worked for an organization committed to white supremacy. Garvey sought Black racial pride, independent institutions, African redemption, and political separation from white domination. Yet both rejected interracial social integration and believed racial groups should organize separately. That limited overlap made a meeting possible.

Garvey later publicly described the encounter and argued that open white separatists were in some respects easier to understand than whites who professed equality while maintaining discrimination. The meeting triggered fierce condemnation from Black leaders including A. Philip Randolph and others who regarded any accommodation with the Klan as morally and politically unacceptable. The episode should not be interpreted as evidence that Clarke supported Black equality. The common ground was separation, reached from opposite sides of a racial hierarchy.

The Fall of Simmons and Rise of Hiram Wesley Evans

Success intensified factional conflict inside the Klan. Simmons had founded the revived order, but other executives increasingly controlled money, recruitment, and administration. Clarke's promotional machine had become more operationally important than Simmons's original fraternal vision. Rival factions competed over revenue, authority, and the future direction of the organization.

Hiram Wesley Evans ultimately emerged as the dominant leader. In 1922 he displaced Simmons as Imperial Wizard and attempted to transform the Klan from a rapidly expanding recruitment enterprise into a more disciplined national political force. Clarke left high Klan office during this transition. His departure did not undo what he had built: the network of chapters, recruiters, initiation-fee incentives, propaganda channels, and mass publicity continued to operate.

The transition also illustrates a recurring pattern in extremist movements. The entrepreneur who creates rapid growth does not necessarily control the institution once it becomes large enough to possess political value. Clarke helped build the machine; Evans inherited a movement capable of influencing elections.

Break with the Klan

After leaving national leadership, Clarke became publicly critical of the organization he had helped expand. By late 1923 newspapers were reporting statements in which he warned that the Klan's political tendencies threatened orderly government and described himself as prepared to oppose its existing direction. His criticism reflected genuine factional hostility and concern about the movement's evolution, but it did not erase his earlier role.

This distinction matters. A former executive can later oppose an organization without ceasing to be historically responsible for building it. Clarke's post-Klan criticism is part of his biography, but so is the fact that the political power he criticized was possible in large part because the recruitment system he and Tyler had created brought enormous numbers of people into the movement.

The Federal Investigation and Mann Act Case

Federal law-enforcement officials faced a jurisdictional problem when confronted with Klan violence in the early 1920s. Many assaults, kidnappings, threats, and murders were state crimes, and the federal Bureau of Investigation did not yet possess the broad statutory authority later federal agencies would use in civil-rights cases. Louisiana governor John M. Parker appealed directly to federal authorities in 1922, describing a Klan powerful enough to dominate portions of his state and linking it to serious violence.

Investigators examined Clarke because of his former national position and his role in the recruitment system. According to the FBI's later historical account, investigators also documented the large personal revenues he derived from Klan membership and regalia. They eventually found a federal charge unrelated to the Klan's racial program: the Mann Act, which prohibited certain interstate transportation for sexual purposes.

Clarke was arrested and in March 1924 pleaded guilty in federal court to violating the Mann Act. He avoided imprisonment but received a substantial fine. The case did not prosecute him for Klan violence or racial intimidation, but it became an early example of federal investigators using an available federal statute to reach a prominent leader of an organization whose more direct misconduct often fell outside federal jurisdiction at the time.

Later Business and Publishing Activity

Clarke's national importance declined sharply after the early 1920s. He returned to private business and later became associated with publishing, including Monarch Publishing. In 1939 that company published Thomas Dixon Jr.'s novel The Flaming Sword. Dixon had been one of the major literary figures responsible for romanticizing the Reconstruction-era Klan, especially through The Clansman, which became a source for The Birth of a Nation.

The connection is historically notable. Clarke had helped commercialize the real second Klan that emerged partly from the cultural mythology created by Dixon's fiction; years later he was involved in publishing another Dixon novel. It represented a continuing intersection between racial propaganda, publishing, and political mythology.

Records of Clarke's later decades are fragmentary compared with the extensive press coverage of his Klan period. He resurfaced periodically in news reports involving business and financial disputes, but he never again exercised anything close to the national political influence he possessed as the Klan's chief promoter.

Comparison with William Joseph Simmons

Simmons and Clarke performed different functions. Simmons was the revivalist. He supplied the Klan's name, mythology, ritual structure, titles, and symbolic connection to Reconstruction. Without Simmons there would have been no chartered second Klan for Clarke to market.

Clarke was the scaler. He took a struggling fraternal order and applied professional sales methods to it. If Simmons created the product, Clarke and Tyler built the distribution network. Their partnership explains why the second Klan should be understood not only as an ideological movement but also as an organizational and commercial enterprise.

Comparison with Mary Elizabeth Tyler

Clarke's achievements cannot be accurately described without Mary Elizabeth Tyler. She was not a marginal secretary or assistant. Historical scholarship identifies her as a major partner in the Southern Publicity Association and one of the central architects of the Klan's national expansion. She helped shape publicity, recruitment, fundraising, newspaper operations, and outreach to women.

Clarke occupied the formal male hierarchy of the Klan and acquired titles such as Imperial Kleagle and acting Imperial Wizard. Tyler operated in a movement that officially restricted its main membership to men yet exercised extraordinary informal influence. Their partnership demonstrates that the second Klan's growth depended on professional organizers as much as on ritual leaders and local demagogues.

Comparison with Hiram Wesley Evans

Clarke was strongest at expansion. Evans was stronger at consolidation. Clarke's system rewarded recruiters for acquiring new members and rapidly multiplying chapters. Evans inherited the resulting organization and sought to convert membership into durable political influence.

Under Evans, the Klan placed greater emphasis on elections, public policy, schools, immigration restriction, Protestant political identity, and alliances with officeholders. The difference helps explain the second Klan's evolution from commercial recruitment boom to political machine. Clarke made scale possible; Evans tried to turn scale into power.

Comparison with Later Klan Organizers

Later Klan leaders such as Samuel Green, Robert Shelton, and David Duke operated in a different political environment. By the postwar civil-rights era, Klan membership was far smaller and the name itself carried much greater social stigma. Those leaders struggled to recreate the broad social legitimacy the second Klan had temporarily enjoyed.

Clarke therefore belongs to a distinctive period in which extremist politics could be marketed through conventional civic and commercial techniques to a mass constituency. His career demonstrates how a hate movement can expand when professional recruitment, financial incentives, local grievance, social respectability, and ideological propaganda become integrated into a single operating system.

Historical Significance

Edward Young Clarke was one of the most important organizational entrepreneurs in the history of the Ku Klux Klan. He did not revive the second Klan, write its principal rituals, or lead it through most of its peak political period. His contribution was more operational: he helped discover how to sell it.

That contribution was decisive. The second Klan's enormous expansion after 1920 cannot be explained solely by racism, nativism, or postwar anxiety, because those forces existed before membership exploded. Clarke and Tyler created mechanisms that converted those sentiments into dues-paying organization: targeted messaging, paid recruiters, local chapters, financial commissions, press events, publicity campaigns, and a replicable expansion process.

His career therefore offers a broader lesson about extremist movements. Ideology matters, but organizational technology matters too. Prejudice becomes more politically consequential when someone builds institutions capable of identifying sympathizers, recruiting them, collecting money, maintaining loyalty, and converting grievance into coordinated action.

Impact on African Americans

Clarke's impact on Black Americans was principally indirect but substantial. He helped expand an organization whose foundational political purpose included maintenance of white supremacy. The resulting Klan network strengthened forces opposing Black civic equality at precisely the moment when the Great Migration, Black veterans' activism, the growth of the NAACP, Garveyism, and new urban Black communities were challenging older racial boundaries.

The Klan's presence amplified intimidation. In many communities, membership among police officers, judges, businessmen, ministers, politicians, or employers meant that Black citizens could not easily distinguish private prejudice from organized institutional power. Economic retaliation, political pressure, threats, masked demonstrations, and violence all became more potent when backed by an organization claiming thousands of local allies.

Clarke's role was to make that network larger, richer, and more connected. He therefore belongs in the history of opposition to Black equality even though he was not primarily a legislator like Theodore Bilbo, a courtroom figure like Roger Taney, or a later terrorist organizer. His instrument was organizational scale.

A Balanced Assessment

Clarke was a capable promoter, salesman, fundraiser, and organizational strategist. He understood incentives, publicity, market segmentation, and distributed recruitment at a level unusual among political organizers of his era. His methods helped create extraordinary growth from a weak institutional base.

Those abilities were directed toward a movement built on exclusion. The Klan he helped scale asserted that full American belonging was properly centered on native-born white Protestant identity and mobilized hostility toward Black people, Catholics, Jews, immigrants, and other targeted groups. Commercial ingenuity did not make the movement less ideological; it made the ideology easier to distribute.

His later break with the Klan deserves inclusion because people and organizations change. But historical assessment requires attention to sequence. Clarke criticized the political machine only after helping construct the recruitment infrastructure that made it powerful. His eventual opposition cannot be retroactively substituted for his earlier record.

Legacy

Clarke's name is less widely remembered than William J. Simmons, Hiram Wesley Evans, or later Klan figures, but his organizational legacy is arguably as important as many of theirs. The second Klan demonstrated that racial and religious extremism could be marketed with the same professional techniques used to promote charities, commercial products, political campaigns, and fraternal societies.

The commission system also exposed a structural weakness. When recruitment itself produces revenue, leaders have an incentive to exaggerate threats, create controversy, expand rapidly, and prioritize new members over institutional discipline. The same system that created explosive growth encouraged profiteering and internal conflict. Once scandals accumulated and political conditions changed, the organization proved far less stable than its membership claims suggested.

Clarke's legacy is therefore both ideological and organizational. He helped nationalize a movement committed to white supremacy, but he also provided an early American example of professionalized grievance marketing: identify fear, tailor the message, monetize recruitment, and turn customers into political members.

Geographic Scope

Atlanta, Georgia was the center of Clarke's business and Klan career. It was the birthplace of the second Klan, headquarters of Simmons's organization, home of the Southern Publicity Association, and site of Clarke's 1922 meeting with Marcus Garvey.

The recruitment system Clarke helped design spread far beyond Georgia. Kleagles organized throughout the South, Midwest, West, and parts of the Northeast, adapting Klan propaganda to different regional grievances. Clarke's legal troubles later involved federal authorities operating in Louisiana and interstate travel, while his later business activity appeared in several states.

Legal and Constitutional Context

Clarke's career occurred during an era when federal power to prosecute private racial violence was far narrower in practice than it would become during the modern civil-rights period. Many Klan assaults, kidnappings, threats, and homicides were prosecuted—if at all—under state law. Local officials could themselves be Klan members or sympathizers, further weakening enforcement.

The 1921 congressional investigation raised questions of political secrecy, private coercion, interstate organization, and the relationship between extremist associations and public officials. Clarke's eventual federal prosecution under the Mann Act demonstrates the jurisdictional limits of the period: investigators reached a former Klan leader through an interstate sexual-morality statute rather than through a comprehensive federal law against Klan intimidation.
