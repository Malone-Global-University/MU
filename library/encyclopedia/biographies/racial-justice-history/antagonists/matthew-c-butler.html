Matthew C. Butler

South Carolina lawyer, Confederate major general, Hamburg and Red Shirt leader, U.S. senator, and Spanish-American War major general

Overview

Matthew Calbraith Butler (1836-1909) was a South Carolina lawyer, state legislator, Confederate cavalry commander, Reconstruction-era Democratic organizer, United States senator, and later a major general of United States Volunteers during the Spanish-American War.

Butler's career crossed two sharply different political periods. After the Civil War he briefly participated in the Union Reform movement, which attempted to combine white conservatives with moderate Republicans and accept Black suffrage as a political reality. By 1876, however, he had become one of the principal leaders of South Carolina's “straight-out” Democratic campaign, closely associated with the Hamburg Massacre, Red Shirt paramilitary organization, intimidation of Black Republican voters, and Wade Hampton III's successful effort to overthrow Reconstruction government.

His later career added further complexity. Butler served eighteen years in the U.S. Senate, chaired important committees, advocated selected civil-service and infrastructure reforms, promoted a controversial federal program encouraging Black emigration to Africa, returned to United States military service in 1898, and participated in supervising Spain's withdrawal from Cuba.

Family Background

Butler was born into one of South Carolina's most politically prominent families. His father, William Butler, had served in Congress, while his uncle Andrew Pickens Butler became a powerful United States senator.

Another uncle, Pierce Mason Butler, served as governor of South Carolina and later died while commanding the Palmetto Regiment during the Mexican-American War.

Perry Family Connection

Butler's mother, Jane Tweedy Perry, came from a prominent naval family. She was a sister of Commodore Oliver Hazard Perry and Commodore Matthew Calbraith Perry.

Matthew Butler was named for his uncle Matthew Calbraith Perry, who later became famous for the United States naval expedition that opened formal relations with Japan.

Preston Brooks

Butler's extended family also included Representative Preston Brooks, whose 1856 assault on Senator Charles Sumner became one of the defining episodes of the antebellum sectional crisis.

Brooks attacked Sumner after the Massachusetts senator delivered an antislavery speech that mocked Senator Andrew Pickens Butler, Matthew Butler's uncle.

Childhood in South Carolina

Matthew Calbraith Butler was born near Greenville on March 8, 1836. His childhood unfolded within an elite southern political culture increasingly divided over slavery, territorial expansion, states' rights, and sectional power.

Arkansas Years

In 1848 Butler accompanied his father to Arkansas. After his father's death in 1850, Butler returned to South Carolina.

He then lived with his uncle Andrew Pickens Butler near Edgefield, placing him near the center of one of the state's most politically influential districts.

Edgefield

Edgefield became the central geographic base of Butler's adult life. The district produced an unusually large number of political leaders, Confederate officers, militant white Democrats, and later architects of the campaign to overthrow Reconstruction.

Its political culture became essential to Butler's postwar rise.

Slaveholding Political Environment

Butler grew up in and married into South Carolina's slaveholding political elite. His family, his uncle Andrew Pickens Butler, his Pickens in-laws, and the Edgefield planter class were deeply embedded in an economy and social order structured by slavery.

The strongest sources reviewed for this biography establish that environment clearly. Because they do not provide a sufficiently precise census-based count of enslaved people personally held by Butler, this page does not assign him an exact individual slaveholding total.

South Carolina College

Butler attended South Carolina College in Columbia during the 1850s. The institution educated a large portion of the state's antebellum political and professional elite.

He left college for legal study and was admitted to the South Carolina bar in 1857.

Legal Career

After admission to the bar, Butler began practicing law in Edgefield. Legal training became central to both his political career and his role in the events leading to the Hamburg Massacre.

Marriage to Maria Calhoun Pickens

On February 25, 1858, Butler married Maria Calhoun Pickens, daughter of Francis Wilkinson Pickens.

Francis Pickens became governor of South Carolina during the secession crisis, further connecting Butler to the state's highest political leadership.

Francis Wilkinson Pickens

Francis Pickens was an ardent defender of southern slaveholding interests and became governor as South Carolina seceded from the Union.

Butler's marriage therefore joined two major political families at the moment when sectional conflict was approaching its final crisis.

Election to the South Carolina House

Butler was elected to the South Carolina House of Representatives in 1860.

His legislative career was interrupted almost immediately by secession and civil war.

Secession Context

South Carolina became the first state to secede from the United States in December 1860.

Butler was not a nationally important ideological architect of secession comparable to Robert Barnwell Rhett, but he soon committed himself fully to Confederate military service.

Confederate Service Begins

Butler entered the Confederate Army as a cavalry captain in June 1861.

He served initially in Hampton's Legion, the command organized by wealthy planter and future political ally Wade Hampton III.

Hampton's Legion

Hampton's Legion combined infantry, cavalry, and artillery elements and became one of South Carolina's best-known early Confederate formations.

Butler's association with Hampton began in the military sphere and later became one of the most important political alliances of Reconstruction-era South Carolina.

Promotion to Major

Butler rose quickly in the mounted arm and became a major during the first year of the war.

His cavalry career ultimately lasted through nearly the entire conflict.

Second South Carolina Cavalry

As cavalry units were reorganized, Butler became colonel of the 2nd South Carolina Cavalry.

The regiment served in the Army of Northern Virginia's increasingly important mounted operations.

Cavalry Warfare

Confederate cavalry carried out reconnaissance, screening, raids, counter-reconnaissance, flank protection, and increasingly large mounted battles.

Butler developed into an experienced field commander in this environment rather than remaining simply a politically connected officer.

Brandy Station

Butler fought at the Battle of Brandy Station on June 9, 1863, the largest predominantly cavalry battle of the Civil War.

The engagement demonstrated the growing combat effectiveness of Union cavalry and inflicted a life-changing wound on Butler.

Loss of His Right Foot

At Brandy Station Butler was struck by artillery fire and so severely wounded that his right foot had to be amputated.

The injury did not end his military career.

Return to Service

Butler returned to field duty despite the amputation. His continued service contributed to his reputation among Confederates for personal courage and endurance.

Brigadier General

Butler was promoted to brigadier general in 1864.

He commanded a cavalry brigade in Wade Hampton's division and participated in some of the most intense mounted fighting of the war's final two years.

Trevilian Station

Butler played an important role in the Battle of Trevilian Station in June 1864.

The engagement was among the largest cavalry battles of the war and helped establish Butler as one of Hampton's more capable subordinate commanders.

Major General

Butler eventually attained the Confederate rank of major general.

When Hampton moved to higher cavalry command, Butler received broader divisional responsibilities.

Carolinas Campaign

Late in the war Butler returned south with Hampton to resist William T. Sherman's advance through the Carolinas.

The campaign brought Butler's military career back to the region where his political life had begun.

Monroe's Crossroads

Butler's cavalry participated in the March 1865 fighting at Monroe's Crossroads in North Carolina.

His command helped launch a surprise Confederate assault against Union cavalry during the final phase of the Carolinas Campaign.

Bentonville

Butler's division also participated in the Battle of Bentonville, the last major Confederate offensive battle of the Civil War.

Confederate forces could delay but not reverse Sherman's movement through North Carolina.

Confederate Defeat

By spring 1865 the Confederacy was collapsing. Butler's military service ended with the destruction of the government and army to which he had committed himself.

Return to Edgefield

After the war Butler returned to Edgefield and resumed the practice of law.

Like many former Confederate elites, he faced severe financial disruption and a political system transformed by emancipation.

Emancipation

The Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery throughout the United States except as punishment for crime.

The abolition of slavery destroyed the legal labor system that had underwritten the wealth and social authority of South Carolina's prewar planter class.

Reconstruction

Reconstruction posed a second transformation: formerly enslaved men acquired citizenship and voting rights, Black South Carolinians entered public office, and federal law attempted to protect the new political order.

Butler's political career after 1865 was largely defined by his response to these changes.

Return to the South Carolina House

Butler was again elected to the South Carolina House of Representatives in 1866.

His second legislative period occurred before Congressional Reconstruction fully reorganized the state's political system.

Congressional Reconstruction

Congress placed the former Confederate states under new requirements after southern governments resisted federal civil-rights policy.

South Carolina's 1868 constitution established Black male suffrage and produced a biracial Republican government in a state where African Americans formed a majority of the population.

Initial Postwar Moderation

Butler's Reconstruction record did not move immediately from Confederate service to Red Shirt politics.

He initially participated in a political experiment that attempted to unite white conservatives, moderate Republicans, and at least some African American voters.

Union Reform Movement

In 1870 Butler became the Union Reform candidate for lieutenant governor.

The movement sought to defeat Republican governor Robert K. Scott through a coalition that accepted Black suffrage and attempted to attract African American voters while campaigning against corruption and alleged misgovernment.

The 1870 Platform

The Union Reform coalition represented a tactical departure from conventional white Democratic politics.

Its leadership recognized that in a state with a Black voting majority, openly rejecting African American suffrage made statewide victory nearly impossible.

Butler and Black Suffrage in 1870

During the Union Reform campaign Butler operated within a platform that accepted the Fifteenth Amendment and Black voting rights as political realities.

This phase of his career is important because it demonstrates that his later embrace of coercive white Democratic politics was a political development rather than an unchanging strategy from 1865 onward.

Failure of Union Reform

The Union Reform ticket lost decisively in 1870. Most Black voters remained loyal to the Republican Party, whose record was associated with emancipation, citizenship, suffrage, and federal protection.

The failure deeply influenced white conservative strategy.

Butler's Reconstruction Testimony

In congressional testimony concerning South Carolina Reconstruction, Butler described white dissatisfaction with the new government and complained about political disabilities imposed on former Confederate leaders.

Lowcountry Digital History Initiative materials show him explicitly using “the people” to mean white people during parts of that testimony, revealing the racial assumptions underlying his political vocabulary.

From Fusion to 'Straight-Out' Politics

After the Union Reform experiment failed, Butler moved away from cooperation with moderate Republicans.

By 1876 he had become a key leader of the “straight-out” Democratic strategy that sought to overthrow Republican rule through unified white political mobilization.

Edgefield Political Culture

Edgefield became one of the principal laboratories for the new Democratic strategy.

Former Confederate officers including Butler and Martin W. Gary concluded that ordinary electoral competition would not by itself overcome the state's Black Republican majority.

Martin W. Gary

Martin W. Gary was Butler's fellow Edgefield lawyer, Confederate veteran, and political ally.

Gary became the most explicit theorist of the coercive “Edgefield Plan,” but Butler was a major partner in the broader campaign that operationalized armed white Democratic mobilization.

The Edgefield Plan

The Edgefield Plan called for disciplined white political organization, disruption of Republican meetings, armed presence at polls, economic pressure, intimidation of Black voters, and manipulation of election administration.

University of South Carolina historical research identifies Butler and Gary as leaders of the coercive half of Wade Hampton's 1876 campaign strategy.

Ku Klux Klan and a Shift in Tactics

Earlier Ku Klux Klan violence had demonstrated both the destructive capacity of white paramilitary terror and the possibility of strong federal response under the Enforcement Acts.

By the mid-1870s, Democratic organizers increasingly sought more public, politically coordinated forms of armed organization through rifle clubs and Red Shirt groups.

Rifle Clubs

White rifle clubs spread throughout South Carolina during the Reconstruction period.

They could present themselves as social or military organizations while functioning as a ready-made political force during elections.

Hamburg

Hamburg was a predominantly Black town in Aiken County located across the Savannah River from Augusta, Georgia.

Its Black militia company formed part of the South Carolina National Guard structure created under Reconstruction government.

July 4, 1876

On July 4, 1876, Hamburg's Black militia conducted a parade and drill. Two white men, Thomas Butler and Henry Getzen, became involved in a confrontation over passage along the road.

The militia eventually opened its ranks to let them pass, but the dispute did not end there.

Butler as Attorney

Thomas Butler and Henry Getzen retained Matthew C. Butler as their attorney.

Butler brought their complaint before Black trial justice Prince Rivers, and the matter was scheduled for a July 8 hearing.

Recruiting White Military Clubs

According to the Lowcountry Digital History Initiative, while the legal dispute was pending Matthew Butler began recruiting men from white military clubs in the surrounding area.

This moved the confrontation far beyond an ordinary dispute between two travelers and a militia company.

July 8, 1876

Hundreds of armed white men gathered around Hamburg for the scheduled hearing.

Butler represented the white complainants and demanded that the Black militia surrender its weapons.

Demand for the Militia's Arms

The Black militiamen refused the demand to surrender their arms.

Butler did not possess general governmental authority to disarm the state militia unit, making the demand part of an extralegal escalation surrounding the legal proceeding.

Armed Assault

White forces attacked the building where the Black militia had taken refuge. The attackers used small arms and a cannon.

One white attacker died during the exchange of gunfire.

James Cook

Hamburg town marshal James Cook, an African American man, was shot and killed while trying to escape the fighting.

His death was followed by a more deliberate episode of racial killing.

The 'Death Ring'

Armed whites captured roughly two dozen Black men after the militia position collapsed.

Several prisoners were selected and executed in what later became known as the “Death Ring.”

Five Prisoners Executed

Five captured Black militiamen were killed after they had become prisoners.

These killings distinguish the Hamburg Massacre from a battle in which all deaths occurred during an exchange of fire.

Number of Black Victims

Including James Cook and the prisoners executed after capture, multiple African American men were deliberately killed in the Hamburg violence.

Contemporary and later accounts sometimes vary in how they count particular victims, so this biography emphasizes the documented sequence rather than relying on a single simplified casualty number.

Robbery and Disorder

After the attack, armed whites also robbed members of the Hamburg community.

The violence therefore combined political intimidation, racial terror, killing, and destruction of local authority.

Butler's Responsibility at Hamburg

Historical evidence places Butler at the center of the escalation: he represented the white complainants, recruited men from white military clubs, participated in the demand that the Black militia surrender its arms, and stood as a leading political figure among the armed whites.

The record should nevertheless distinguish leadership in the confrontation from proof that Butler personally fired the shots that killed individual prisoners. The strongest sources reviewed do not establish that he personally executed the captured men.

No Meaningful Prosecution

The Hamburg killings produced national outrage, but state and federal authorities failed to secure meaningful punishment for the white perpetrators.

The absence of effective prosecution had major political consequences.

Political Meaning of Hamburg

Hamburg demonstrated that armed white Democrats could attack Black Republican institutions and produce lethal violence without decisive federal intervention.

Lowcountry Digital History Initiative interpretation identifies the massacre as a turning point that strengthened the straight-out Democratic strategy for the 1876 campaign.

The 'Bloody Shirt' Order

University of South Carolina research reports that Butler told white defendants connected with the Hamburg violence to wear red shirts during their court appearance.

The symbolism helped transform the red shirt into a visible uniform of South Carolina's Democratic paramilitary movement.

Red Shirts

The Red Shirts became the most recognizable armed Democratic organizations of the 1876 campaign.

They rode in organized groups, displayed weapons, attended Democratic rallies, disrupted Republican political activity, and projected the threat of force throughout the state.

Butler as Red Shirt Leader

Official U.S. House historical materials identify Butler as a Red Shirt leader.

His role was therefore not limited to serving as a lawyer during the Hamburg dispute; he became a major political organizer in the broader campaign to destroy Republican power.

Wade Hampton III

Democrats nominated former Confederate lieutenant general Wade Hampton III for governor in 1876.

Hampton's public strategy emphasized reconciliation, order, and attempts to attract some Black voters, while Butler and Gary directed a more coercive strategy on the ground.

Two-Pronged Democratic Campaign

University of South Carolina research characterizes the campaign as operating through two complementary approaches.

Hampton publicly condemned political violence and presented himself as a paternalistic moderate, while Butler and Gary implemented intimidation intended either to force Black voters into the Democratic column or prevent them from voting Republican.

Economic Pressure

Political coercion did not always require physical assault.

Black workers and tenants could face threats to employment, housing, credit, medical treatment, or economic relationships if they remained Republican.

Disrupting Republican Meetings

Red Shirt groups regularly appeared at Republican gatherings and attempted to overwhelm or disrupt speakers.

The tactic reduced the ability of Republican organizers to campaign freely and communicated that political participation could carry physical risk.

Robert Smalls

Robert Smalls was a formerly enslaved Civil War hero, Republican politician, and United States representative from South Carolina.

He became one of the most important Black political opponents of Red Shirt rule.

Edgefield Rally Against Robert Smalls

During the 1876 campaign Smalls travelled with Republican governor Daniel H. Chamberlain to a rally in Edgefield.

The official U.S. House biography of Smalls states that Butler and his Red Shirts overran the meeting and threatened Smalls's life.

Threat to a Black Congressman

The Edgefield episode is particularly significant because Smalls was a sitting member of the United States House of Representatives.

The incident demonstrates that Red Shirt intimidation reached not only ordinary voters but nationally elected Black officials.

Republican Response

Republicans argued that federal troops were essential to protecting elections because state and local authorities could not reliably restrain Red Shirt organizations.

Smalls warned Congress that private white militias were effectively making war on the Reconstruction government and freedpeople.

The 1876 Election

The South Carolina election of 1876 was extraordinarily violent and disputed.

Both parties faced accusations of electoral misconduct, but the Democratic campaign's use of organized paramilitary intimidation against Black Republican voters is extensively documented.

Edgefield County

Edgefield became one of the strongest centers of Democratic mobilization.

Election results from Edgefield and Laurens counties were so extreme that reported Democratic vote totals became major evidence in the dispute over the gubernatorial election.

Fraud and Intimidation

Historical accuracy requires distinguishing different forms of misconduct. Election fraud occurred, but armed suppression of political opposition constituted a separate and more serious assault on the ability of citizens to vote freely.

Butler's political significance lies heavily in his association with the latter.

Contested State Government

After the election, both Wade Hampton and Republican governor Daniel Chamberlain claimed victory.

For several months South Carolina effectively had rival governments while the national presidential election was also disputed.

End of Reconstruction in South Carolina

Federal troops were withdrawn from their political support role in Columbia in April 1877.

Chamberlain abandoned his claim, and Hampton took undisputed control of state government, effectively ending Reconstruction in South Carolina.

Butler's Senate Election

The Democratic-controlled South Carolina legislature elected Butler to the United States Senate in 1876 for the term beginning March 4, 1877.

His elevation to national office came directly out of the political transformation produced by the Redemption campaign.

Controversy Over His Senate Election

Butler's path to the Senate was controversial because of the violence surrounding South Carolina politics.

Congressional testimony and later historical accounts preserved accusations that he had threatened Black political opponents during the campaign.

Harrison N. Bouey

African American Edgefield leader Harrison N. Bouey accused Butler of threatening him and other Black men.

The allegations became part of the wider dispute over whether political power in South Carolina had been obtained through lawful electoral competition or coercion.

United States Senator

Butler served in the U.S. Senate from March 4, 1877, through March 3, 1895.

His eighteen years in the chamber gave him a substantially longer national career after Reconstruction than his earlier state political record might suggest.

Return to Federal Office

Butler's Senate service represented one of the central paradoxes of postwar reconciliation: a former Confederate major general and Red Shirt leader returned to a powerful position in the national government only twelve years after Confederate defeat.

Civil Service and Retrenchment Committee

During the Forty-sixth Congress Butler chaired the Senate Committee on Civil Service and Retrenchment.

This work placed him within national debates over federal administration, patronage, and reform.

Interstate Commerce Committee

During the Fifty-third Congress Butler chaired the Senate Committee on Interstate Commerce.

The committee dealt with the increasingly important federal regulation of railroads and national transportation networks.

Foreign Relations

Butler also served on the Senate Foreign Relations Committee during portions of his later Senate career.

This assignment broadened his work beyond sectional and southern political issues.

Military and Naval Policy

His committee work included military and naval affairs, subjects on which his long service as a cavalry officer gave him significant practical experience.

Civil Service Reform

Butler supported selected civil-service reform initiatives intended to reduce some forms of political patronage.

This reform record belongs alongside, rather than in place of, his much more consequential role in South Carolina's racial politics.

Modernization of the Navy

During his Senate years Butler supported efforts associated with modernization of the United States Navy.

The issue became increasingly important as the United States expanded its industrial and international ambitions in the late nineteenth century.

Cherokee Claims

Butler later participated as an attorney in litigation concerning claims of the Eastern Cherokees against the United States.

Federal records list him among counsel connected to the long-running claims litigation.

The 1890 Emigration Proposal

In 1890 Butler advanced a proposal for federal support of African American emigration to Africa.

The proposal reflected a segregationist solution to the country's racial conflict: rather than treating Black Americans as permanent equal citizens within a multiracial republic, it encouraged their physical removal.

African Colonization Tradition

Butler's proposal drew on a long American colonization tradition that had advocated moving free African Americans outside the United States.

By the 1890s most Black Americans rejected such programs because the United States was their country and citizenship, not expatriation, was the central political demand.

Why the Emigration Proposal Matters

The emigration proposal demonstrates that Butler's long Senate career did not represent a transition to racial egalitarianism.

His post-Reconstruction politics continued to seek solutions that maintained racial separation and white political dominance.

Federal Elections and Southern Control

During Butler's Senate era, southern Democratic governments steadily consolidated control over elections while Black voting strength declined through violence, administrative manipulation, and later formal disfranchisement.

Butler's national career unfolded within the political order that the 1876 campaign had helped create.

Benjamin Tillman

Benjamin Ryan Tillman had supported the Edgefield Democratic movement as a young man but later became the leader of an insurgent white-farmer faction hostile to the old conservative Democratic establishment.

By the 1890s Tillman challenged Butler's position as one of South Carolina's dominant senators.

Butler Versus Tillman

The conflict between Butler and Tillman was partly generational and partly class-based.

Butler represented Confederate-officer prestige, professional politics, and the older conservative elite; Tillman mobilized white farmers through populist rhetoric and more openly inflammatory racial appeals.

Loss of the Senate Seat

Butler failed to secure another Senate term in 1894.

Benjamin Tillman succeeded him when Butler's term ended in March 1895.

Meaning of the Tillman Victory

Butler's defeat did not represent a rejection of white supremacy in South Carolina.

It represented a transfer of leadership from one white-supremacist Democratic faction to another, more populist and openly demagogic movement.

Washington Law Practice

After leaving the Senate Butler resumed legal practice in Washington, D.C.

His long federal career had given him extensive national political and professional connections.

Spanish-American War

When the United States went to war with Spain in 1898, President William McKinley appointed Butler a major general of United States Volunteers.

The appointment was part of a broader policy of using former Confederate officers as symbols of sectional reconciliation.

Former Confederate in U.S. Uniform

Butler's return to American military service carried powerful symbolism.

A man who had commanded Confederate cavalry against the United States now accepted a general's commission from the federal government.

First Division, Second Army Corps

Federal records state that Butler commanded the First Division of the Second Army Corps during his Spanish-American War service.

Cuban Evacuation Commission

Butler was appointed to the commission responsible for arranging and supervising the evacuation of Spanish forces from Cuba after the war.

The assignment placed him within the transition from Spanish colonial rule to United States military occupation.

Evacuation of Cuba

The commission coordinated the withdrawal of Spanish troops and the transfer of governmental control.

Butler's role therefore connected his final military service to the emergence of the United States as an overseas imperial power.

Honorably Discharged

Butler was honorably discharged from United States volunteer service in April 1899.

His military career thus formally encompassed senior service in both the Confederate States Army and the United States forces.

Sectional Reconciliation

Butler's 1898 commission illustrates one form of national reconciliation after the Civil War: reconciliation between white Union and Confederate veterans.

That reconciliation often proceeded without an equivalent national commitment to protecting the Black civil and political rights that Reconstruction had attempted to secure.

Southern Historical Society

In later life Butler became associated with the Southern Historical Society and was elected one of its officers.

The organization helped preserve Confederate documents but also participated in shaping the historical memory commonly associated with the Lost Cause.

Lost Cause Memory

Lost Cause interpretation emphasized Confederate bravery, constitutional disputes, and sectional honor while frequently minimizing slavery's centrality and portraying Reconstruction as illegitimate.

Butler's postwar reputation as soldier, Redeemer, and senator fit comfortably within this memorial framework.

Mexico

In the early twentieth century Butler spent time in Mexico and became involved in mining enterprise.

This business career represented another late-life shift away from electoral office.

Second Marriage

After the death of his first wife, Maria, Butler later married Nannie Whitman.

His final years were divided among business, legal, historical, and family interests.

Death

Matthew Calbraith Butler died in Columbia, South Carolina, on April 14, 1909.

He was seventy-three years old.

Burial

Butler was buried in Willow Brook Cemetery in Edgefield.

His burial returned him to the community most closely associated with both his legal career and his Reconstruction-era political power.

Was Butler a Lifelong Opponent of Black Voting?

No. In 1870 Butler participated in the Union Reform movement, which formally accepted Black suffrage and attempted to attract African American voters.

That episode did not make him a racial egalitarian. It does show that his later embrace of the straight-out and Red Shirt strategy followed the failure of an earlier attempt at electoral coalition.

Was Butler a Ku Klux Klan Member?

The strongest sources reviewed for this biography do not establish Butler as a formal Ku Klux Klan member.

His documented role in Red Shirt organization and the 1876 coercive campaign is sufficient to assess his participation in white paramilitary politics without assigning unsupported Klan membership.

Was Butler Responsible for the Hamburg Massacre?

Butler held significant responsibility for the confrontation that became the Hamburg Massacre. He represented the white complainants, recruited armed men from military clubs, and demanded disarmament of the Black militia.

Historical precision nevertheless requires distinguishing political and operational leadership from proof that he personally killed any particular victim. The sources reviewed do not establish him as the individual executioner of the prisoners.

Was Hamburg Simply a Race Riot?

No. The initial dispute developed into an organized armed assault on a Black state militia unit, followed by the capture and execution of Black prisoners.

Calling the event merely a riot can obscure the asymmetry of the final violence and its political function within the campaign against Reconstruction.

Did Butler Support Wade Hampton's Moderate Strategy?

Butler supported Hampton's candidacy but represented a harder tactical wing of the Democratic campaign.

Hampton publicly emphasized conciliation and Black Democratic support, while Butler and Gary were more directly associated with coercion, rifle clubs, and Red Shirt mobilization.

Was Butler's Senate Career Only About Race?

No. Butler worked on civil service, transportation, foreign relations, military policy, naval policy, and other national issues during eighteen years in the Senate.

A balanced biography should include that legislative record while recognizing that his route to the Senate was inseparable from the violent overthrow of Reconstruction politics in South Carolina.

Was His 1898 U.S. Service Significant?

Yes. Butler's appointment as a U.S. major general was an important symbol of white sectional reconciliation three decades after the Civil War.

It also demonstrates how quickly former Confederate elites could be reintegrated into national institutions even as Black citizenship rights in the South were being systematically restricted.

Comparison with Wade Hampton III

Hampton and Butler served together militarily and politically. Hampton was Butler's senior Confederate commander and later the public face of the 1876 Democratic campaign.

Hampton cultivated a more conciliatory public image, while Butler became one of the campaign's principal coercive organizers.

Comparison with Martin W. Gary

Gary was more explicit than Butler in writing and articulating the most violent details of the Edgefield Plan.

Butler, however, was not a detached moderate. University of South Carolina research places the two men together as leaders of the intimidation strategy behind Hampton's campaign.

Comparison with Benjamin Tillman

Butler helped establish the white Democratic order within which Tillman later rose.

Tillman then displaced Butler's older political faction while making white supremacy even more openly central to populist Democratic mobilization.

Comparison with Robert Barnwell Rhett

Rhett's greatest historical role came before and during secession as an ideological and organizational advocate of disunion.

Butler's defining political role came later: the armed destruction of Reconstruction-era Republican power and the restoration of white Democratic government.

Comparison with Matthew Butler's Uncle Andrew

Andrew Pickens Butler belonged to the antebellum generation of national proslavery senators.

Matthew Butler inherited the family's political prestige but became historically important in a different era, when the principal struggle had shifted from preserving slavery to controlling the political consequences of emancipation.

Historical Significance

Butler's life links Confederate military leadership to the postwar overthrow of Reconstruction and then to reintegration into the federal political establishment.

For African American history, his most consequential role came in 1876. He helped transform white political frustration into organized paramilitary pressure against Black Republican citizenship, participated directly in the escalation at Hamburg, led Red Shirt activity, and helped create the conditions under which white Democrats recovered control of South Carolina.

A Balanced Assessment

Butler was an able cavalry officer who continued military service after losing a foot, an experienced lawyer, a long-serving senator, a participant in civil-service and infrastructure policy, and later a United States general entrusted with an international military transition in Cuba. His brief participation in the Union Reform movement also demonstrates that he did not initially embrace the most coercive possible Reconstruction strategy.

Those facts coexist with a record of enormous consequence for Black South Carolinians: after interracial electoral cooperation failed, Butler became a major leader of the straight-out movement, helped mobilize armed whites at Hamburg, participated in the demand to disarm Black militiamen, supported Red Shirt paramilitary politics, threatened or was accused of threatening Black Republican leaders, and helped destroy the biracial political order created during Reconstruction.

Legacy

For decades Butler was remembered primarily in white southern commemoration as a Confederate cavalry hero, senator, and symbol of national reconciliation. His later United States military service made that narrative especially attractive.

Modern scholarship places greater weight on the Reconstruction record. Butler's biography demonstrates that the end of Reconstruction was not merely a political bargain in Washington; in South Carolina it was produced through local organization, armed intimidation, racial violence, electoral manipulation, and the successful suppression of Black political power.

  • July 8, 1876: Armed confrontation escalates into the Hamburg Massacre; captured Black militiamen are executed.
  • Summer-Fall 1876: Butler emerges as a central Red Shirt and straight-out Democratic leader.
  • 1876: Butler and Martin W. Gary help direct the coercive side of Wade Hampton's gubernatorial campaign.
  • 1876: Butler and Red Shirts overrun a Republican meeting in Edgefield and threaten Representative Robert Smalls.
  • November 1876: South Carolina's gubernatorial election produces rival claims by Hampton and Daniel Chamberlain.
  • 1876: Democratic legislature elects Butler to the United States Senate.
  • March 4, 1877: Begins Senate service.
  • April 1877: Hampton gains undisputed control of South Carolina government, ending Reconstruction in the state.
  • 1879-1881: Butler chairs the Senate Committee on Civil Service and Retrenchment.
  • 1882: Reelected to the Senate.
  • 1888: Reelected to a third Senate term.
  • 1890: Promotes federal assistance for African American emigration to Africa.
  • 1891-1895: Serves on the Senate Foreign Relations Committee.
  • 1893-1895: Chairs the Senate Committee on Interstate Commerce.
  • 1894: Loses the political contest for another Senate term to the Tillman movement.
  • March 3, 1895: Senate service ends.
  • 1895-1898: Practices law in Washington, D.C.
  • May 22, 1898: Commissioned major general of United States Volunteers during the Spanish-American War.
  • 1898: Serves on commission supervising Spanish evacuation of Cuba.
  • April 1899: Honorably discharged from United States military service.
  • 1903: Elected to leadership in the Southern Historical Society.
  • 1904: Moves to Mexico and becomes involved in mining enterprise.
  • 1906: Marries Nannie Whitman.
  • April 14, 1909: Dies in Columbia, South Carolina.

Geographic Scope

Greenville was Butler's birthplace, but Edgefield became the central location of his legal career and Reconstruction politics. Columbia was the seat of state government and the site of his later death.

His Confederate career took him through Virginia, Maryland, Pennsylvania, North Carolina, and South Carolina. Brandy Station was the site of his disabling wound, while Trevilian Station, Monroe's Crossroads, and Bentonville became major points in his cavalry career.

Hamburg, South Carolina became the defining location of his Reconstruction-era record. His later federal career centered on Washington, D.C., while his final military service extended to Cuba.

Legal and Constitutional Context

Butler's life crossed the transition from slavery and Confederate rebellion to emancipation, Reconstruction, Black suffrage, federal civil-rights enforcement, Redemption, and the later consolidation of white Democratic government.

His career demonstrates how the central racial struggle changed after slavery ended. The postwar conflict no longer concerned legal ownership of African Americans; it concerned citizenship, voting, officeholding, militia power, federal enforcement, and whether Black citizens could exercise political authority without white permission.
