Orval Faubus

Six-term Arkansas governor and World War II veteran whose reform record was eclipsed by his use of state power to block the Little Rock Nine, resist federal desegregation orders, and close public high schools rather than accept integration

Overview

Orval Eugene Faubus was an American teacher, newspaper editor, Army intelligence officer, county official, highway commissioner, Democratic politician, and six-term governor of Arkansas whose career became permanently associated with the 1957 Little Rock school-integration crisis.

Faubus was born on January 7, 1910, in a rented log cabin on Greasy Creek in Madison County in the Ozark Mountains. His father, John Samuel Faubus, was a self-educated farmer and socialist who named Orval's middle name for Socialist Party leader Eugene V. Debs.

The young Faubus briefly attended Commonwealth College, a left-wing self-help institution, taught in rural schools, worked seasonal jobs, and entered Democratic politics during the New Deal era.

He won election as circuit clerk and recorder of Madison County in 1938 and served until entering the United States Army during World War II.

Faubus served as an Army intelligence officer in five major European campaigns, including the Battle of the Bulge, and attained the rank of major. His wartime service became an important credential in his postwar political rise.

After the war he returned to Huntsville, Arkansas, served as postmaster, and with his wife Alta purchased the weekly Madison County Record. His editorials promoted better education, healthcare, highways, and public services.

Faubus became an ally of Governor Sidney McMath, another World War II veteran associated with Arkansas's postwar reform movement. McMath appointed him to the Arkansas State Highway Commission and later brought him into the governor's office.

In 1954 Faubus mounted what initially appeared to be an unlikely challenge to incumbent governor Francis Cherry. His campaign mixed economic populism, attacks on utilities and elite interests, support for welfare recipients, and a disciplined rural campaign organization.

Cherry's attempt to portray Faubus as a communist because of his youthful connection to Commonwealth College backfired. Faubus defeated Cherry in the Democratic runoff and then won the general election by a landslide.

Faubus entered the governor's office in January 1955 with a reputation closer to New Deal populism and racial moderation than to the hard-line massive-resistance politics associated with governors such as Ross Barnett or George Wallace.

His administration increased teacher pay, expanded highways and state parks, improved state mental-health institutions, created the Arkansas Children's Colony, supported industrial development, and later helped preserve the Buffalo River from a proposed dam.

His administration also hired some Black employees, supported funding for historically Black institutions, and later joined efforts to replace Arkansas's discriminatory poll-tax registration system with a modern voter-registration process.

These facts are historically important because Faubus's later obstruction of school integration was not simply the predictable continuation of an unchanging extremist ideology.

The political environment changed rapidly after Brown v. Board of Education. Arkansas had initially experienced relatively peaceful school desegregation in places such as Charleston and Fayetteville, and state officials had generally avoided the most defiant Deep South response.

Hard-line segregationists, however, organized around figures such as James D. 'Justice Jim' Johnson. Johnson attacked Faubus from the right in the 1956 governor's race and helped make opposition to integration a major statewide electoral issue.

Faubus defeated Johnson by promising to protect segregated institutions without embracing all of Johnson's disorderly extremism. The competition nevertheless pushed the governor toward increasingly explicit resistance to federal desegregation.

Arkansas voters adopted Amendment 44 in 1956, an interposition measure declaring state opposition to the Supreme Court's school-desegregation rulings.

In 1957 Faubus signed legislation creating the Arkansas State Sovereignty Commission and related mechanisms intended to monitor or resist organizations associated with school integration.

The defining crisis began when the Little Rock School District prepared to implement a gradual desegregation plan at Central High School in September 1957.

The plan was limited and cautious. It would admit a small number of carefully selected Black students to one formerly all-white high school while broader integration was postponed.

Segregationist organizations, including the Capital Citizens' Council and Mothers' League of Central High, mobilized to stop the plan and predicted violence.

Faubus publicly claimed that he had to preserve order. On September 2, 1957, he placed the Arkansas National Guard around Central High School and directed the Guard to prevent Black students from entering.

On September 4, nine Black students attempted to attend Central High. National Guardsmen acting under Faubus's authority turned them away.

Fifteen-year-old Elizabeth Eckford arrived separately and was surrounded by a white mob that screamed insults and threats while soldiers denied her entrance. Photographs of her ordeal circulated around the world.

The nine students—Melba Pattillo, Minnijean Brown, Elizabeth Eckford, Ernest Green, Gloria Ray, Terrence Roberts, Jefferson Thomas, Carlotta Walls, and Thelma Mothershed—became known as the Little Rock Nine.

Faubus insisted that the Guard had been deployed to prevent violence rather than to defy integration. The actual orders and effect of the deployment, however, prevented the Black students from attending a school they were legally entitled to enter under federal court orders.

President Dwight D. Eisenhower warned Faubus that the federal Constitution would be upheld. Congressman Brooks Hays arranged a face-to-face meeting between the governor and president at Newport, Rhode Island, on September 14.

After the meeting, Eisenhower believed Faubus had agreed to respect federal court decisions and allow the Guard to preserve order rather than obstruct integration. Faubus's later conduct did not produce the cooperation Eisenhower expected.

On September 20, federal judge Ronald Davies issued an injunction barring Faubus and Guard commanders from using troops to interfere with the students' attendance.

Faubus withdrew the National Guard. He did not, however, create an adequate alternative security arrangement to protect the students from the white segregationist crowd gathering around the school.

On September 23 the students entered Central High, but an angry crowd outside attacked Black journalists and threatened to overwhelm local police. Authorities removed the students for their safety.

Little Rock mayor Woodrow Wilson Mann appealed to Eisenhower for federal assistance.

On September 23 Eisenhower issued a presidential proclamation ordering people obstructing federal court orders to disperse.

On September 24 Eisenhower issued Executive Order 10730, federalized the Arkansas National Guard, and sent soldiers of the 101st Airborne Division to Little Rock.

On September 25, under federal military escort, the Little Rock Nine entered Central High for their first full day of classes.

The crisis represented the most serious direct confrontation between state resistance and federal civil-rights enforcement since Reconstruction.

Faubus lost the immediate constitutional contest. Federal authority prevailed, the students entered the school, and the Supreme Court's desegregation rulings remained binding.

Politically, however, the confrontation transformed Faubus into an enormously popular figure among many white Arkansas voters who admired his resistance to Washington.

The Little Rock Nine endured harassment throughout the school year. Minnijean Brown was eventually expelled after retaliating against white students who had repeatedly tormented her.

Ernest Green became the first Black graduate of Central High School in May 1958.

Faubus and his allies responded to the defeat at Central High by developing new legal and administrative strategies designed to delay or evade desegregation.

In 1958 the Arkansas legislature passed Act 4, which allowed public schools facing integration to be closed, and Act 5, which attempted to redirect public funds toward alternative schools.

When the Supreme Court ordered Little Rock to continue desegregation in September 1958, Faubus signed the resistance legislation and closed all four public high schools in Little Rock.

The resulting 1958–59 academic year became known as the Lost Year.

Nearly four thousand students were displaced. White families had substantially greater access to private and alternative education, while approximately half of displaced Black students received no schooling at all.

Public high-school teachers continued reporting to empty buildings, while high-school football continued by order of the governor.

The school-closing episode demonstrates that Faubus's resistance went beyond one dramatic National Guard confrontation. He used state law and executive power to deny public education rather than accept even limited integration.

Other anti-integration measures targeted the NAACP and state employees. Act 10 required public employees to disclose organizational affiliations, while Act 115 barred NAACP members from state employment.

Faubus also signed a 1959 law requiring donated blood to be labeled by race.

The segregationist program damaged Little Rock's national reputation, frightened some prospective employers, divided the white business community, and created increasing opposition among white moderates as well as Black citizens.

The Women's Emergency Committee to Open Our Schools organized to defend public education, and a later recall election removed segregationist school-board members who had fired forty-four teachers and administrators.

Federal courts struck down the school-closing framework, and Little Rock's high schools reopened in August 1959 with token integration.

Faubus nevertheless remained electorally dominant. He won reelection four more times after the 1957 crisis and served six consecutive two-year terms from 1955 through 1967, longer than any other Arkansas governor.

His success depended on more than segregation. Faubus combined racial resistance with public spending, road building, education improvements, mental-health reform, industrial recruitment, rural patronage, and a highly effective statewide political organization.

He regularly presented himself as the defender of ordinary Arkansans against federal judges, outside elites, utility interests, newspaper critics, and political reformers.

The Arkansas Gazette became one of his most persistent institutional critics and won Pulitzer recognition for its coverage and editorial position during the Little Rock crisis.

Faubus also faced criticism over corruption, patronage, highway politics, and financial scandals such as Arkansas Loan and Thrift.

One of the darkest episodes of his governorship outside Central High was the March 1959 fire at the Arkansas Negro Boys Industrial School near Wrightsville.

Twenty-one Black boys died in a dormitory whose doors had been locked from the outside. The disaster exposed extreme inequality between segregated state institutions and conditions that reformers had documented years before the fire.

The fire was not an act personally ordered by Faubus, but it occurred under a state administration responsible for an institution whose conditions had long been known to be dangerously inferior.

By the 1960s Faubus continued anti-integration rhetoric while generally avoiding another direct military confrontation with the federal government.

His formula combined appeals to white backlash with modernization of Arkansas government.

He also opposed a proposed dam on the Buffalo River, helping preserve a natural resource that later became the Buffalo National River.

Faubus did not seek a seventh consecutive term in 1966 and left office undefeated in January 1967.

His political machine did not transfer smoothly to a successor. Republican Winthrop Rockefeller won the 1966 governorship, becoming Arkansas's first Republican governor since Reconstruction.

Faubus attempted political comebacks in 1970, 1974, and 1986 but failed each time.

After leaving office his finances and political influence declined. He briefly served as general manager of Dogpatch USA, a theme park in northern Arkansas.

His first marriage to Alta Haskins ended in divorce in 1969. Their only child, Farrell, died by suicide in 1976.

Faubus later married Elizabeth Westmoreland. She was murdered in Houston in 1983 while the marriage was breaking down. Faubus was not accused of involvement in the killing.

In 1986 he married teacher Jan Hines Wittenberg.

In his later years Faubus wrote memoirs and essays, commented on politics, moved increasingly toward conservative positions, and received his final public appointment as Arkansas director of veterans' affairs from Republican governor Frank White.

Faubus died in Conway, Arkansas, on December 14, 1994, from complications associated with prostate cancer.

He was buried in Combs Cemetery in Madison County above the Ozark landscape where his life had begun.

His legacy remains unusually paradoxical. His administration improved schools, roads, hospitals, parks, and public services, yet his defining use of state power was to obstruct Black students exercising constitutional rights.

Faubus did not need to possess the most extreme private racial beliefs in Arkansas to become a major segregationist antagonist. Political calculation, institutional power, and willingness to exploit racial fear were sufficient to produce serious constitutional harm.

For MGU's antagonist series, Faubus is therefore especially important as an example of how a politician with a reform background and comparatively moderate prior racial reputation can adopt segregationist resistance when electoral incentives reward it—and then convert that calculation into state policy.

The administration's tangible accomplishments helped explain why many voters continued supporting Faubus even after racial conflict subsided.

Faubus frequently invoked the Arkansas Children's Colony as evidence that critics threatened valuable public programs.

Faubusism

The governor himself used the term 'Faubusism' to refer to the combination of public services, populist rhetoric, political organization, and personal leadership associated with his administration.

Sid McMath Becomes an Opponent

Former ally Sidney McMath later challenged Faubus but failed to dislodge him.

Winthrop Rockefeller

Industrialist and reform-minded Republican Winthrop Rockefeller became one of the leading opponents of the Faubus political system.

Dale Alford

Congressman Dale Alford, elected during the segregationist backlash of 1958, also later became a Faubus rival.

Bruce Bennett

Attorney General Bruce Bennett was a major segregationist ally in constructing anti-integration legislation before later challenging Faubus politically.

Arkansas Gazette

The Arkansas Gazette became the most influential newspaper critic of Faubus's segregationist course.

Pulitzer Recognition

The Gazette's Little Rock crisis coverage and editorial stance received national recognition.

African American Opposition

Black Arkansans opposed Faubus through the NAACP, churches, litigation, journalism, electoral action, and local organizing.

White Moderate Opposition

Many white opponents focused first on law, education, civic reputation, and economic stability rather than immediate broad racial equality.

Racial Moderation and Political Responsibility

A politician does not need the most extreme private prejudice to produce segregationist policy when political incentives reward obstruction.

Hiring Black State Employees

Faubus's administration hired a number of Black people in state government.

Funding Historically Black Institutions

The administration provided state support to historically Black colleges and other institutions.

Poll Tax Reform

Faubus later supported efforts to replace Arkansas's discriminatory poll-tax registration system.

Amendment 51

Arkansas eventually adopted a modern voter-registration system that eliminated the poll tax as the central registration mechanism.

Why These Facts Matter

The existence of some racially moderate or beneficial actions makes Faubus's Central High decisions more clearly choices rather than unavoidable products of a fixed ideology.

Six Consecutive Terms

Faubus won six consecutive two-year terms, serving from 1955 through 1967.

Longest Arkansas Governorship

His twelve-year tenure remains the longest continuous governorship in Arkansas history.

No Seventh-Term Bid

Faubus did not seek another consecutive term in 1966.

1966 Political Transition

The absence of Faubus opened space for a realignment in Arkansas politics.

Winthrop Rockefeller Elected

Republican Winthrop Rockefeller won the governorship in 1966, ending nearly a century of continuous Democratic gubernatorial control since Reconstruction.

1967 Departure

Faubus left office in January 1967 without having been defeated for reelection as an incumbent.

Post-Governor Political Machine

Former allies attempted to preserve the Faubus organization after his departure, but Arkansas politics was changing.

1970 Comeback Attempt

Faubus sought the Democratic nomination for governor in 1970 but failed.

1974 Comeback Attempt

He tried again in 1974 and again lost.

1986 Comeback Attempt

At age seventy-six he made one final gubernatorial attempt in 1986.

Changing Electorate

Black enfranchisement, generational change, party realignment, and new political issues reduced the usefulness of the old Faubus coalition.

Dogpatch USA

Faubus briefly served as general manager of Dogpatch USA, a theme park based on the Li'l Abner comic strip.

Financial Decline

His personal financial position deteriorated after leaving the governor's office.

Divorce from Alta

Orval and Alta Faubus divorced in 1969 after decades of marriage.

Elizabeth Westmoreland

Faubus married Elizabeth Westmoreland after his first divorce.

Elizabeth's 1983 Murder

Elizabeth Faubus was murdered in Houston in 1983 while the couple was separating. Orval Faubus was not charged or identified as the killer.

Farrell's Death

Faubus's only son, Farrell, died by suicide in 1976, a major personal tragedy.

Jan Hines Wittenberg

Faubus married teacher Jan Hines Wittenberg in 1986.

Memoirs

He published autobiographical volumes titled Down from the Hills and Down from the Hills II.

Shaping His Own Legacy

Faubus's memoirs defended his interpretation of the Central High crisis and broader political career.

Increasing Conservatism

In later years he moved toward more conservative political positions and sometimes encouraged Republican candidates.

Continued Democratic Identity

Faubus nevertheless insisted that he remained personally loyal to the Democratic Party.

Frank White

Republican governor Frank White appointed Faubus state director of veterans' affairs.

Veterans' Affairs

The appointment became Faubus's final major public position.

Later Years in Conway

Faubus lived in Conway with Jan during his final years.

Prostate Cancer

His health declined from complications associated with prostate cancer.

Death

Faubus died on December 14, 1994, at age eighty-four.

Combs Cemetery

He was buried in Combs Cemetery in Madison County near the Ozark community of his childhood.

Did Faubus Personally Hate Black People?

Historical evidence cannot reliably reduce his actions to a simple private-emotion diagnosis. Arkansas historians generally describe a politician who had previously shown racial moderation but chose segregationist resistance when it became electorally advantageous.

Was Faubus Always a Segregationist Hard-Liner?

No. He supported segregation within the existing Arkansas system, but he did not begin his governorship with the hard-line profile he acquired after 1956 and 1957.

Was Faubus a Communist?

No credible evidence establishes that Faubus was a Communist Party leader or operative. His youthful Commonwealth College attendance became a Red Scare campaign attack.

Was Faubus a Socialist?

He was raised by a socialist father and briefly attended a left-wing college, but his adult political career was firmly rooted in the Democratic Party rather than the Socialist Party.

Did Faubus Integrate Arkansas Before Little Rock?

Several Arkansas communities desegregated while Faubus was governor and without state interference. That does not mean Faubus personally led an integration campaign.

Did Faubus Call the Guard Only to Prevent Violence?

Preventing disorder was his public justification, but his orders and the Guard's actual conduct physically prevented Black students from entering Central High.

Did Faubus Defy a Supreme Court Order Directly on September 4?

The immediate operational orders flowed through federal district-court litigation implementing Brown. The broader constitutional obligation originated in the Supreme Court's desegregation rulings.

Did Eisenhower Immediately Send Troops on September 4?

No. Eisenhower first pursued warnings, legal action, and negotiation. Federal troops were deployed only after the September 23 mob crisis.

Did Faubus and Eisenhower Reach an Agreement at Newport?

They issued conciliatory statements, but their understandings diverged and no durable operational settlement resulted.

Did Faubus Refuse the September 20 Federal Injunction?

He ultimately removed the Guard after Judge Davies enjoined further interference.

Why Did Federal Troops Become Necessary?

After the Guard left, local police could not control the violent segregationist crowd when the students entered on September 23.

Did Faubus Command the 101st Airborne?

No. Eisenhower deployed the 101st under federal authority.

Why Federalize the Arkansas Guard?

Federalization removed the Guard from Faubus's control and turned it from an obstruction force into part of the federal enforcement structure.

Was the Little Rock Nine Crisis Only Symbolic?

No. The students lost instructional time, endured physical and psychological abuse, and required military protection to exercise a constitutional right.

Did the Little Rock Nine Attend Together as a Group?

They attended the same school but generally did not share classes and were intentionally separated through the school day.

Did Faubus Win the Legal Battle?

No. Federal courts rejected state obstruction and integration proceeded.

Did Faubus Win Politically?

In the short term, yes. His resistance greatly strengthened his popularity among many white Arkansas voters and helped sustain four more reelections.

Did Faubus Close Only Central High in 1958?

No. He closed all four public high schools in the Little Rock district.

Did Closing the Schools Affect Only Black Students?

No. Thousands of white students were also displaced, although white families had much greater access to private-school alternatives.

Was the Lost Year a Local School-Board Decision Alone?

No. State legislation championed and signed by Faubus provided the mechanism, and the governor invoked it to close the schools.

Did the Lost Year End Because Faubus Voluntarily Reversed Course?

No. Local political resistance, school-board changes, economic pressure, and federal court rulings combined to end the closure system.

Was the Arkansas State Sovereignty Commission as Active as Mississippi's?

No. Arkansas's commission received sweeping legal authority but appears to have met only twice and never developed the same extensive operational machinery.

Did Faubus Personally Cause the Wrightsville Fire?

No evidence establishes that he caused or ordered the fire. His administration nevertheless bore responsibility for a segregated state institution whose dangerous conditions had been documented.

Did Faubus Have Genuine Policy Accomplishments?

Yes. Education funding, mental-health reform, roads, parks, institutional development, and preservation of the Buffalo River form an important part of his record.

Do Those Accomplishments Reduce His Civil-Rights Responsibility?

No. Government officials can produce beneficial policies in one area while violating constitutional rights in another.

Was Faubus More Moderate Than George Wallace?

Before the Little Rock crisis his record was more moderate than Wallace's later segregationist image. Both men, however, chose to use executive power against school integration.

Faubus Versus Bull Connor

Connor had long built his identity around municipal segregation and police power. Faubus's most notorious racial turn emerged from statewide electoral calculation and a constitutional confrontation over school integration.

Faubus Versus Ross Barnett

Barnett entered the Mississippi governorship as an outspoken segregationist. Faubus's prior moderation makes his political shift especially instructive.

Faubus Versus Jim Johnson

Justice Jim Johnson pressured Faubus from the segregationist right and helped create the electoral incentives that pushed the governor toward resistance.

Faubus and Eisenhower

Their conflict was less a personal feud than a constitutional contest over whether state executive authority could obstruct federal court orders.

Faubus and Daisy Bates

Daisy Bates represented the local Black civic organization and legal persistence that Faubus's resistance attempted to defeat.

Faubus and Elizabeth Eckford

Eckford's September 4 ordeal became the human image of a policy decision made at the governor's level.

Faubus and Ernest Green

Green's graduation demonstrated the ultimate failure of Faubus's attempt to keep Central High completely segregated.

Faubus and the Arkansas Gazette

The newspaper's criticism showed that white Arkansas opinion was never uniformly behind the governor.

Faubus and the Women's Emergency Committee

The Lost Year produced organized white opposition centered on preservation of public education and civic stability.

Political Ambition and Racism

Faubus's record demonstrates that racist outcomes can arise from opportunistic political incentives even when a politician's private ideology is more complicated.

Institutional Responsibility

Faubus's significance lies not only in words but in his control of the National Guard, legislative agenda, executive agencies, patronage, and public communication.

Constitutional Significance

Little Rock became a defining example of the supremacy of federal constitutional rights over state resistance.

Civil-Rights Significance

The crisis demonstrated that Black children sometimes required federal military protection simply to attend a public school.

Cold War Significance

Little Rock exposed the contradiction between American democratic rhetoric abroad and racial exclusion at home.

Political Significance

Faubus helped demonstrate the electoral power of white backlash and anti-federal rhetoric in postwar southern politics.

Arkansas Significance

His twelve years in office reshaped the state's institutions, highways, party organization, racial politics, and public image.

Historical Significance

Faubus connects Ozark socialism, New Deal populism, World War II veteran politics, postwar modernization, massive resistance, the Little Rock Nine, federalism, school closure, civil-rights law, and southern political realignment.

Legacy

Orval Faubus remains one of the clearest examples of a politician whose most infamous act was not inevitable from his early biography. He possessed genuine reform instincts and had previously tolerated limited racial change, yet he chose to turn state military and legislative power against Black students when segregationist pressure threatened his political position. That choice became the defining fact of his public life.

Geographic Scope

Madison County and the Ozark Mountains shaped Faubus's childhood, rural political identity, and final burial.

Huntsville became the center of his newspaper and early postwar political career.

Little Rock became the center of his governorship and the international civil-rights crisis that defined him.

Newport, Rhode Island, became the site of his failed effort to reach an understanding with President Eisenhower.

Central High School became the principal physical symbol of Faubus's constitutional confrontation with the federal government.

Wrightsville became associated with another racial tragedy during his administration through the 1959 Negro Boys Industrial School fire.

Legal and Constitutional Context

Faubus governed during the implementation of Brown v. Board of Education, which held state-mandated public-school segregation unconstitutional under the Fourteenth Amendment.

Federal district and appellate courts approved and enforced Little Rock's gradual desegregation plan.

State interposition and sovereignty measures could not lawfully override the Supremacy Clause of the United States Constitution.

The federal injunction against Faubus, Eisenhower's use of military authority, and the Supreme Court's decision in Cooper v. Aaron together established that state officials could not obstruct constitutional rights by invoking local opposition or political disagreement.

Later litigation protecting freedom of association also undermined Arkansas efforts to penalize NAACP membership and lawful political organization.
