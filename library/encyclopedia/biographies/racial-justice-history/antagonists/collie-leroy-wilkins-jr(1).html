Collie Leroy Wilkins Jr.

Alabama Klansman, participant in the armed pursuit that killed Viola Liuzzo, and federal civil-rights conspirator convicted for attacking supporters of the Selma voting-rights march

Overview

Collie Leroy Wilkins Jr. (1943-1994) was an Alabama automobile mechanic, Ku Klux Klan member, and participant in the March 25, 1965 attack that killed Detroit civil-rights volunteer Viola Liuzzo after the Selma-to-Montgomery voting-rights march. At twenty-one, Wilkins was the youngest of four Klansmen travelling together that day: Eugene Thomas, William Orville Eaton, Wilkins, and Gary Thomas Rowe Jr., who was secretly a paid FBI informant inside the Klan.

Wilkins's opposition to Black equality was direct rather than merely ideological. The federal appellate record established that he had attended a Klan rally protesting the Selma march, travelled with the group to Montgomery and Selma on the march's final day, harassed march participants and Black spectators, discussed attacking suspected marchers, joined the high-speed pursuit of Liuzzo's car after the Klansmen saw her travelling with Black activist Leroy Moton, and participated in the shooting attack. The Fifth Circuit's account of evidence credited by the federal jury stated that Wilkins fired multiple shots into Liuzzo's automobile.

Alabama did not convict Wilkins of murder. His first state trial ended in a hung jury, and a later state proceeding resulted in acquittal. The federal government instead prosecuted Wilkins, Eaton, and Thomas for conspiring to injure, oppress, threaten, and intimidate citizens exercising federally protected rights connected to the Selma march. All three were convicted in December 1965 and sentenced to the ten-year statutory maximum. Wilkins served years in federal prison, later joined Eugene Thomas in accusing Rowe of being Liuzzo's actual shooter, and died in Birmingham on December 23, 1994.

Birth and Early Life

Wilkins was born in Alabama in 1943. The surviving easily accessible public record is much fuller about his Klan activity and criminal cases than about his childhood, education, or family background.

By March 1965 he was twenty-one years old, single, living in Fairfield near Birmingham, and working as an automobile mechanic. The Civil Rights Digital Library also records that he had prior convictions before the Liuzzo case.

Fairfield and the Birmingham Industrial Area

Fairfield lay within the heavily industrialized Birmingham metropolitan area, where steel mills, manufacturing, working-class white neighborhoods, Black communities, segregationist politics, and organized Klan activity all existed in close proximity.

Wilkins's entry into Klan violence therefore occurred within one of the most conflict-ridden racial environments in the country. Birmingham had already witnessed attacks on Freedom Riders, repeated bombings, mass civil-rights demonstrations, and the 1963 bombing of Sixteenth Street Baptist Church.

Automobile Mechanic

Contemporary sources described Wilkins as a mechanic, with some calling him self-employed and others unemployed at the time of Liuzzo's murder. He had no major public office or recognized leadership career.

His historical importance comes instead from operational participation in Klan violence. He represents the rank-and-file or lower-level militant whose actions translated segregationist ideology into an armed attack on civil-rights workers.

The Alabama Ku Klux Klan

The Klan in Alabama during the early 1960s was divided among organizations and local chapters, but Birmingham-area Klansmen developed a reputation for exceptional violence. Some members participated in attacks on Freedom Riders, bombings, intimidation, and assaults on people challenging segregation.

Wilkins belonged to this broader Klan culture rather than merely expressing private racist beliefs. His later federal conviction established participation in an organized conspiracy aimed at people supporting the Selma voting-rights movement.

Eastview Klavern No. 13

National Park Service accounts identify the four men involved in the Liuzzo attack as belonging to Birmingham's Eastview Klavern No. 13 network. Eastview was also associated with some of the most violent Klan activity of the Birmingham civil-rights era.

Membership placed Wilkins inside a social organization that treated racial separation and resistance to civil rights as collective duties rather than private opinions.

Klan 'Missionary Squad'

The National Park Service describes Wilkins, Eaton, and Thomas as members of a Klan “missionary squad,” with Rowe travelling alongside them as an undercover informant. The phrase referred to a mobile group prepared to harass or attack people associated with civil-rights activity.

This was not religious missionary work. It was Klan terminology for extending the organization's presence and enforcement beyond a single meeting hall or neighborhood.

Selma and Black Voting Rights

In early 1965 Selma became the center of a national campaign against the systematic exclusion of Black Alabamians from voter registration. Demonstrators faced arrests, beatings, economic retaliation, and official resistance from Dallas County sheriff Jim Clark and other segregationists.

The Klan understood voting rights as a threat to white political control. A larger Black electorate could reshape sheriffs' offices, juries, city councils, legislatures, and statewide elections that had long operated under overwhelmingly white political dominance.

Jimmie Lee Jackson

In February 1965 Alabama state trooper James Bonard Fowler shot Black voting-rights demonstrator Jimmie Lee Jackson in Marion. Jackson died several days later.

His death intensified the movement's determination to march from Selma to Montgomery and helped connect local Black voter suppression with a national demand for federal voting-rights legislation.

Bloody Sunday

On March 7, 1965, state troopers and sheriff's deputies attacked peaceful marchers attempting to cross the Edmund Pettus Bridge. Television images of beatings and tear gas produced national outrage.

For civil-rights supporters, Bloody Sunday demonstrated the need for federal protection. For white supremacists, the expanding movement and national intervention represented a growing challenge to Alabama's racial order.

Federal Court Protection for the March

Federal Judge Frank M. Johnson Jr. issued an order on March 17 permitting a protected Selma-to-Montgomery march. The Fifth Circuit later emphasized that the march concerned grievances over Black voter registration and was conducted under federal judicial protection.

This legal context became crucial in Wilkins's federal prosecution because the government charged that the Klan group conspired to attack people exercising rights associated with the federally protected march.

The Klan Rally of March 21

On March 21, the day the final Selma-to-Montgomery march began, the Ku Klux Klan held a rally and parade at Cramton Bowl in Montgomery. The parade permit expressly described the event as a protest against the federal court order allowing the civil-rights march.

The Fifth Circuit found abundant evidence that Wilkins and Eugene Thomas attended. The court treated their presence not as a crime by itself but as evidence that they knew about the march and were acting together in opposition to it.

A Lawful Protest Becomes Evidence of Intent

The federal trial judge told jurors that Wilkins and the others had a right to attend the Klan protest rally. Political expression, even racist political expression, was not itself the offense charged.

The rally mattered because later conduct showed the same men travelling together on March 25 to harass and attack march supporters. Their earlier organized opposition helped establish intent and knowledge in the conspiracy case.

Viola Liuzzo

Viola Fauver Gregg Liuzzo was a thirty-nine-year-old white Detroit mother of five who travelled to Alabama to assist the voting-rights movement after seeing the violence of Bloody Sunday. She participated in logistical support for the Selma-to-Montgomery march.

Her work included transporting people connected to the demonstration. That volunteer driving placed her on U.S. Highway 80 with Black activist Leroy Moton on the night she was killed.

Leroy Moton

Leroy Moton was a young Black civil-rights volunteer who rode with Liuzzo on March 25. The interracial pairing of a white woman and Black man attracted the attention of the Klansmen and activated powerful segregationist taboos surrounding interracial social contact.

Moton survived the shooting and resulting crash. His presence is central to understanding the racial motive: the men were not pursuing a random vehicle but a car visibly carrying a white civil-rights volunteer and Black activist together.

March 25, 1965

The Selma-to-Montgomery march reached the Alabama Capitol on March 25. Thousands gathered as Martin Luther King Jr. and other leaders concluded the demonstration demanding voting rights.

Wilkins, Thomas, Eaton, and Rowe travelled into Montgomery that morning. Their activities over the following hours formed the factual basis of the federal conspiracy conviction.

Gary Thomas Rowe Jr.

Gary Thomas Rowe Jr. was a Birmingham-area Klansman who had secretly worked as a paid FBI informant for years. The other men in the car did not know of his federal relationship.

His dual role became one of the most controversial aspects of the Liuzzo case. Rowe supplied the government's eyewitness account, but his history of participation in Klan violence later created intense debate about both his credibility and the FBI's management of violent informants.

Eugene Thomas

Eugene Thomas was an older Klansman who drove the automobile carrying the four men for much of March 25. The Fifth Circuit record depicts him as repeatedly pushing the group toward confrontation and announcing that they had work to accomplish in Selma.

Thomas and Wilkins were later the surviving appellants whose federal conspiracy convictions were affirmed by the Fifth Circuit.

William Orville Eaton

William Orville Eaton was another Klan participant in the group. The federal record states that he was armed and later fired during the attack on Liuzzo's car.

Eaton was convicted with Wilkins and Thomas in federal court but died in March 1966 before beginning his prison sentence.

Orders from the Klan's Imperial Office

The Fifth Circuit record states that on March 25 a Klan official telephoned Rowe and instructed him to travel to Montgomery, saying the order came from the Klan's Imperial Office. Rowe then contacted his FBI handler and was told to accompany the group.

This evidence placed the men's trip within organized Klan activity rather than portraying four acquaintances as coincidentally travelling together on the day of the march.

Meeting Near the Bessemer Klan Hall

Rowe met Thomas, Wilkins, and Eaton approximately two blocks from a Klan meeting hall in Bessemer. The four then travelled together to Montgomery in Thomas's automobile.

The meeting point and common purpose reinforced the federal jury's conclusion that the defendants were operating collectively rather than engaging in independent spontaneous conduct.

Armed for the Trip

The federal record states that Rowe, Eaton, and Thomas had firearms in the vehicle. Wilkins later handled Thomas's pistol during confrontations and ultimately during the attack on Liuzzo's automobile according to evidence credited by the jury.

The availability of weapons made the group's repeated discussions of “taking” march supporters immediately dangerous rather than rhetorical bravado alone.

Five Hours Near the Capitol

The four men reached Montgomery around 10:00 a.m. and remained near the Capitol area for hours as the civil-rights march concluded. They positioned themselves close enough to watch participants and spectators.

Their prolonged presence gave them opportunities to identify people associated with the movement and reinforced the conclusion that the journey had been undertaken because of the march.

Harassing Marchers

The Fifth Circuit summarized eyewitness evidence that Wilkins and the others harassed marchers, booed and shouted at them, and argued with Black spectators near the Capitol.

This conduct directly expressed the group's hostility toward people exercising voting-rights protest. It also provided a factual bridge between their Klan opposition to the march and the armed pursuit later that night.

Decision to Go to Selma

After the Capitol program ended, Thomas announced that the group was going to Selma because they had things to do. The men ate and then drove west on Highway 80 toward the city from which the march had begun.

The federal court treated the continuing trip as part of the same anti-march conspiracy. The men were not simply returning home after a political demonstration.

The Hitchhiker Incident

On the road the group saw a hitchhiker and wondered whether he had participated in the march. The Fifth Circuit record attributes to Wilkins a suggestion that they approach and, if he were a marcher, give him a threatening “surprise.”

They decided the man was not a marcher and left him alone. The episode is important because it shows that the group was actively searching for potential movement targets before encountering Liuzzo.

Arrival in Selma

The Klansmen eventually reached Selma and spent time in a cafe before driving toward an area where marchers had gathered. Their movements continued to center on people associated with the voting-rights demonstration.

What happened next showed that their earlier threats were capable of becoming armed action whenever they believed a vulnerable target was available.

Encounter with Black Pedestrians

Near a church, the group noticed Black pedestrians walking along a dirt street. The federal record states that Thomas slowed the automobile and that Wilkins told Rowe to prepare because they were going to “take” them.

The planned approach stopped only when Rowe noticed nearby soldiers. Thomas's pistol, which had been handed to Wilkins, was returned between the seats when the men recognized the risk of witnesses and intervention.

What the Pedestrian Incident Shows

No attack occurred against those pedestrians, but the episode was powerful evidence of intent. The group was considering armed violence against Black people in the area of the civil-rights mobilization before Liuzzo's vehicle ever appeared.

The decision to abandon the attempt because soldiers were nearby suggested concern about being caught rather than abandonment of the racial objective.

Liuzzo's Oldsmobile

After leaving the area, the Klansmen stopped near the Alabama River bridge and noticed a light-colored Oldsmobile with Michigan license plates. Liuzzo was driving, and Moton was seated beside her.

The combination of an out-of-state plate, a white woman, and a Black male passenger immediately drew the group's attention. Their conversation, as reconstructed at trial, became explicitly focused on following and attacking the pair.

Interracial Travel as a Klan Provocation

Segregationist culture treated interracial social relationships as threats to white racial boundaries. Klan rhetoric routinely sexualized or criminalized ordinary interracial contact, especially contact involving a white woman and Black man.

The men's response to Liuzzo and Moton reflected that ideology. They interpreted two civil-rights volunteers sharing an automobile as a situation that justified pursuit and punishment.

The Pursuit Begins

Thomas followed Liuzzo's Oldsmobile as the group discussed “taking” the occupants. The men in the back seat initially lowered themselves below window level to avoid being seen.

Hiding from view indicated awareness that the pursuit was not legitimate. The group wanted to approach the victims without being recognized before they had an opportunity to attack.

High-Speed Chase

Liuzzo accelerated, and Thomas pursued at speeds the federal record described as reaching approximately eighty to ninety miles per hour. The chase continued along Highway 80 between Selma and Montgomery.

Wilkins participated actively in the discussion about how the group should force the Oldsmobile to stop while minimizing evidence that might later connect the two vehicles.

Avoiding Physical Evidence

When Thomas suggested bumping Liuzzo's automobile, the federal record states that Wilkins objected that exchanging paint between the vehicles could allow investigators to identify the attackers. He instead urged Thomas to pull alongside.

This evidence was particularly damaging because it suggested consciousness of criminal risk and an effort to avoid leaving trace evidence before the shooting began.

The Shooting

According to evidence recited and credited in the federal appellate decision, Thomas pulled alongside Liuzzo's Oldsmobile and Wilkins extended Thomas's pistol from the window. As Liuzzo looked toward the pursuing car, Wilkins fired into the front of her automobile.

Eaton also began shooting. The attack was therefore carried out from a moving Klan vehicle during a pursuit rather than as a confrontation in which the victims threatened the Klansmen.

Wilkins as the Alleged Trigger Man

At the 1965 Alabama murder trial, prosecutors alleged that Wilkins was the principal trigger man. The National Park Service similarly states that Wilkins opened fire into Liuzzo's vehicle, while the Fifth Circuit recounts testimony that he fired the initial shots and continued firing.

That evidence supported the federal conspiracy conviction. The separate state murder proceedings, however, did not result in a murder conviction and must remain part of the legal history.

Liuzzo Is Killed

Bullets struck Liuzzo in the head, killing her. Her Oldsmobile left the road and crashed after the attack.

The killing removed a volunteer who had travelled hundreds of miles to assist Black Alabamians seeking the vote. It was therefore simultaneously a homicide and an act of political terror directed against the civil-rights movement.

Leroy Moton Survives

Moton survived the attack and crash. The Klansmen believed that both occupants had been killed, allowing Moton to escape further immediate assault.

His survival ensured that the Black civil-rights volunteer whom the group had intended to terrorize remained a witness to the consequences of the attack.

Statements After the Shooting

The federal appellate opinion recounts Wilkins expressing confidence that the occupants were dead after Rowe suggested the shots might have missed. The court omitted racial slurs from its published text but preserved the substance of the statement.

The comment, if credited as the jury did, indicated both intent and satisfaction rather than shock at an accidental discharge.

Discarding Cartridge Casings

The federal record states that Wilkins and Eaton threw cartridge casings from the vehicle after the shooting. The group later attempted to create alibis when it returned to the Birmingham area.

These acts were evidence of consciousness of guilt. They suggest that participants understood immediately that investigators would search for physical evidence and reconstruct their movements.

Rowe Reports to the FBI

Because Rowe was an FBI informant, he reported the attack to federal agents quickly. His presence in the car allowed investigators to identify the suspected perpetrators within hours.

The speed of the arrests became a major public-relations success for the FBI, even as Rowe's own involvement later produced serious questions about how the Bureau managed an informant embedded in violent Klan activity.

President Lyndon Johnson Announces the Arrests

Within twenty-four hours President Lyndon B. Johnson announced that federal authorities had arrested four Ku Klux Klan members in connection with Liuzzo's death. The president praised the FBI and used the killing to emphasize the danger posed by organized racial terror.

The arrests transformed Wilkins from a largely unknown young mechanic into a nationally identified Klan defendant.

State Murder Charges

Alabama prosecutors charged Wilkins with Liuzzo's murder. Rowe, whose FBI role had become central to the government's case, testified against his former Klan associates after receiving immunity.

The case was tried in Hayneville, the Lowndes County seat, in a region where white juries had long been reluctant to convict white defendants for racial violence.

Matthew Murphy

Wilkins was represented by attorney Matthew Hobson Murphy Jr., himself a high-ranking Klan legal officer. The defense attacked Rowe as a traitor to his Klan oath and attempted to shift blame onto Liuzzo for travelling at night with a Black man.

The argument illustrates how segregationist sexual stereotypes were used in court to reduce sympathy for a murder victim and portray interracial civil-rights cooperation as morally suspect.

The First State Trial

Wilkins's first Alabama trial began in May 1965. The prosecution portrayed him as the shooter, while the defense concentrated on undermining Rowe's credibility and exploiting local racial attitudes.

The jury could not reach a unanimous verdict, producing a hung jury. This was neither conviction nor acquittal and left Alabama free to try Wilkins again.

The Later State Acquittal

A subsequent Alabama proceeding ended with Wilkins acquitted of the murder charge. The Library of Congress summarizes the state cases by noting that Wilkins, Eaton, and Thomas were ultimately cleared in Alabama murder proceedings.

The acquittal means that a biography should not describe Wilkins as having been legally convicted of Liuzzo's murder. His later federal conviction involved a civil-rights conspiracy, a distinct offense.

Why the Federal Government Prosecuted

State failure did not end the case because federal officials viewed the attack as interference with rights protected by national law. The men had targeted people who participated in or assisted a federally protected voting-rights march.

The federal indictment therefore focused on conspiracy to injure, oppress, threaten, and intimidate citizens exercising those rights rather than attempting to retry the defendants for state-law murder.

18 U.S.C. § 241

The federal government charged Wilkins, Eaton, and Thomas under 18 U.S.C. § 241, a Reconstruction-era civil-rights conspiracy statute descended from federal enforcement legislation enacted after the Civil War.

The law made it a crime for two or more persons to conspire to interfere with rights or privileges secured by the Constitution or federal law. Its Reconstruction origin was especially significant in a prosecution aimed at modern Klan violence.

The Federal Conspiracy Theory

Prosecutors alleged that the defendants planned to harass, threaten, pursue, and assault people who had participated in or supported the Selma-to-Montgomery march. The indictment tied the conspiracy to the federally protected protest over Black voter registration.

This approach allowed federal courts to address organized Klan intimidation even though ordinary murder prosecution remained primarily a matter of Alabama law.

Federal Jury Convicts

On December 3, 1965, a federal jury convicted Wilkins, Eaton, and Thomas. The Library of Congress notes that the jury was all white and all male, making the result particularly notable in the context of southern racial prosecutions.

The verdict established beyond a reasonable doubt that Wilkins intentionally participated in a conspiracy against people exercising federally protected rights connected to the march.

Ten-Year Maximum Sentence

The federal court imposed the statutory maximum term of ten years. The sentence was substantial for the conspiracy statute but did not approximate the punishment that could have followed a state murder conviction.

The gap demonstrates a recurring problem in civil-rights-era prosecutions: federal law could sometimes reach a conspiracy when local systems failed, but available penalties were often limited.

Federal Appeal

Wilkins and Eugene Thomas appealed their convictions to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit. Their case required the court to examine what federal rights the Klan conspiracy had targeted and whether the evidence supported the verdict.

The resulting 1967 opinion provides one of the clearest judicial narratives of Wilkins's conduct on March 25 because it summarizes evidence the federal jury had accepted as true.

The Fifth Circuit Affirms

The Fifth Circuit affirmed the convictions. The judges concluded that the evidence was sufficient to establish intentional conspiracy against citizens participating in the march and petitioning government over voting-rights grievances.

The appellate decision did not overturn the state acquittal or convert the federal conviction into a murder conviction. It upheld a separate federal civil-rights judgment.

The Federal Court's Factual Record

The appellate court's narrative is especially important because it did more than state that Wilkins was a Klansman. It described his participation in harassment, discussions about attacking marchers, possession and use of a pistol during the pursuit, the shooting, disposal of evidence, and efforts to arrange alibis.

The judges stated that the record contained extensive corroboration beyond the summary reproduced in the opinion.

Prison

Wilkins served years in federal custody after his appeals failed. Later summaries commonly report that he served approximately seven years of the ten-year sentence.

His imprisonment represented a rare federal penalty imposed on an Alabama Klansman for violence connected directly to the voting-rights struggle.

Firearms Trouble

While the federal case continued, Wilkins also faced firearms-related legal trouble. Later historical summaries note convictions arising from firearms violations during the period surrounding his appeal.

These offenses were separate from the central § 241 conspiracy conviction and should not be confused with the Alabama murder case.

Life After Prison

Publicly accessible historical sources provide far less detail about Wilkins's life after incarceration than about 1965. He did not return to national prominence as a Klan leader or major political figure.

His historical identity remained defined by the Liuzzo attack, the federal civil-rights conviction, and the later controversy over Gary Rowe's role.

The Rowe Controversy

Rowe's status as an FBI informant became increasingly controversial during the 1970s. Evidence emerged that he had participated in serious Klan violence while supplying information to the Bureau and that FBI officials had sometimes tolerated dangerous conduct to preserve his usefulness as an informant.

This history did not erase the federal convictions of Wilkins and Thomas, but it reopened questions about the exact actions of each man during Liuzzo's murder.

Wilkins Accuses Rowe

When Wilkins and Eugene Thomas were later called before a Lowndes County grand jury, they insisted that Rowe—not Wilkins—had fired the shots that killed Liuzzo. Encyclopedia of Alabama reports that both men passed lie-detector examinations during that later inquiry.

Polygraph results are not definitive proof and are generally treated cautiously by courts and historians. Their claims nevertheless became part of the evidentiary dispute surrounding Rowe.

Statements Attributed to Rowe

Encyclopedia of Alabama also records that two Birmingham police officers later said Rowe had bragged to them that he had killed Liuzzo. Alabama authorities indicted Rowe for murder in 1978.

The charge was dismissed because federal authorities had granted him immunity in exchange for his original testimony. As a result, no later murder trial conclusively resolved the competing shooter claims.

How to Reconcile the Competing Accounts

The strongest legally established facts are that Wilkins joined the armed Klan pursuit and was federally convicted of the conspiracy. The Fifth Circuit's account of evidence credited by that jury states that he fired repeatedly into Liuzzo's car.

The separate state murder case did not convict him, and later evidence generated claims that Rowe may have been the shooter. A careful biography should present all three facts instead of turning uncertainty over the trigger into uncertainty over Wilkins's proven participation in the violent conspiracy.

FBI Responsibility and Informant Management

Rowe's presence created a separate institutional question: what responsibility did the FBI bear for deploying a violent informant without preventing the attack? Liuzzo's family later sued the federal government, arguing that the Bureau's handling of Rowe contributed to her death.

A federal court rejected the family's claim of government legal liability. The controversy nonetheless became a major example of the dangers involved when law enforcement allows informants to participate deeply in violent extremist organizations.

The Smear Campaign Against Liuzzo

After the killing, FBI Director J. Edgar Hoover spread or encouraged false and defamatory insinuations about Liuzzo's morality, family life, drug use, and relationship with Moton. Encyclopedia of Alabama describes these efforts as an attempt to deflect criticism from the Bureau's relationship with Rowe.

These attacks victimized Liuzzo's family a second time and show how racial and sexual stereotypes could be used to discredit a white woman who crossed segregationist boundaries in support of Black civil rights.

President Johnson and the Klan

Liuzzo's murder helped persuade President Johnson to intensify federal attention to Ku Klux Klan violence. The administration treated the killing as evidence that private racial terrorism could undermine constitutional rights even when formal state barriers were being challenged successfully.

The attack therefore accelerated scrutiny of organizations such as the United Klans of America and other Klan factions active in the Deep South.

Voting Rights Act of 1965

Liuzzo was killed while supporting a campaign whose central objective soon became federal law. President Johnson signed the Voting Rights Act on August 6, 1965.

The law transformed political participation across Alabama by giving the federal government powerful tools against discriminatory registration practices. The Klan violence meant to protect white political control ultimately occurred on the losing side of that constitutional transformation.

Political Meaning of Liuzzo's Murder

The murder was not simply a roadside crime between strangers. Wilkins and his companions had spent the day around a voting-rights demonstration, had discussed attacking marchers, and selected Liuzzo and Moton because they appeared connected to the movement and violated racial taboos.

The attack was therefore political terrorism: violence intended to punish civil-rights participation and frighten others from assisting Black citizens seeking equal political power.

The Interracial Dimension

The presence of Moton beside Liuzzo was central to the group's reaction. The federal record preserves Thomas speculating about the pair in sexualized racial terms before deciding to follow them.

Such assumptions reflected a long white-supremacist tradition of portraying interracial contact as illicit or sexually threatening. The stereotype transformed ordinary civil-rights cooperation into a supposed racial offense deserving punishment.

How Wilkins Opposed Black People

Wilkins opposed Black equality through direct participation in organized Klan intimidation. On March 25 he harassed Black spectators, discussed attacking suspected marchers, prepared for an armed assault on Black pedestrians, joined the pursuit of Liuzzo and Moton, and participated in the shooting attack according to evidence upheld in federal court.

His actions targeted the infrastructure of Black political advancement. The people he sought to intimidate were marchers, volunteers, and supporters demanding the right of Black Alabamians to register and vote.

Why Rank-and-File Militants Matter

White supremacy depended on more than governors, sheriffs, senators, and Imperial Wizards. Movements of intimidation required ordinary members willing to follow cars, carry guns, threaten strangers, assault activists, burn property, or provide enforcement for racial rules.

Wilkins's biography demonstrates this lower operational level. He did not design Alabama segregation, but he helped make resistance physically dangerous for people challenging it.

Comparison with Eugene Thomas

Eugene Thomas was older and played a leading role in directing the group's movements on March 25. The federal record repeatedly describes Thomas pushing toward confrontation while driving the car used in the pursuit.

Wilkins nevertheless acted independently within that collective plan, including advising how to approach the victims and handling Thomas's pistol during the attack according to the evidence credited by the jury.

Comparison with William Orville Eaton

Eaton travelled with Wilkins throughout the day and was armed. The federal court record states that Eaton also fired at Liuzzo's car during the attack.

Both men were convicted federally, although Eaton died before serving his sentence. Their joint participation illustrates the group character of the assault rather than a crime attributable to one isolated gunman.

Comparison with Gary Thomas Rowe Jr.

Rowe was both a Klan participant and a paid FBI informant. His immediate report gave federal investigators an eyewitness inside the attack car and enabled rapid arrests.

His later history also complicated the case profoundly. Allegations of violent misconduct, government tolerance, and competing claims about who fired the fatal shots made Rowe both an essential witness and a deeply controversial figure.

Comparison with Robert M. Shelton

Robert M. Shelton led the United Klans of America from Tuscaloosa and helped build the largest Klan faction of the civil-rights era. Wilkins possessed no comparable leadership authority.

Shelton represents organizational command; Wilkins represents the militant member who participated directly in street-level enforcement of white-supremacist objectives.

Comparison with Bull Connor

Bull Connor used official Birmingham police authority against civil-rights demonstrators, while Wilkins acted as a private Klansman. The two represent different mechanisms through which Black activism faced coercion.

State violence could operate through arrest and police force; private Klan violence could pursue activists beyond formal demonstrations and attack them on highways, at homes, or in churches.

Comparison with Sam Bowers

Sam Bowers commanded Mississippi's White Knights through a secret hierarchy capable of authorizing murder. Wilkins was not a commander and did not occupy Bowers's strategic position.

The comparison clarifies responsibility: Bowers is important for authorizing and organizing terror from above, while Wilkins is important for personally joining an armed operation on the ground.

Comparison with Collie Wilkins and Viola Liuzzo

Liuzzo travelled to Alabama voluntarily to help strangers exercise rights guaranteed by the Constitution. Wilkins travelled with fellow Klansmen to oppose and intimidate those same people.

The collision between their choices gives the case its enduring significance: one citizen crossed racial and regional boundaries to assist Black voting rights, while another participated in violence meant to punish that assistance.

Was Wilkins a Ku Klux Klansman?

Yes. Contemporary records, federal court proceedings, the National Park Service, and the Civil Rights Digital Library all identify Wilkins as a Klan member.

His affiliation is not based on rumor or later inference. He travelled with other Klansmen on organized Klan activity and was prosecuted on evidence arising directly from that association.

Was Wilkins Convicted of Murder?

No. Alabama did not obtain a murder conviction against him. His first trial ended in a hung jury, and a later state proceeding resulted in acquittal.

He was convicted in federal court of conspiracy to violate federally protected civil rights and received the maximum ten-year sentence for that offense.

Did Wilkins Fire at Liuzzo's Car?

The Fifth Circuit's 1967 opinion recounts evidence credited by the federal jury that Wilkins fired Thomas's pistol into Liuzzo's automobile and continued firing as the Klan vehicle passed. The National Park Service similarly identifies Wilkins as the shooter.

Later, Wilkins and Thomas claimed that FBI informant Gary Rowe had fired the fatal shots. Because Alabama never convicted Wilkins of murder and Rowe was later accused himself, the precise shooter controversy should be acknowledged even though Wilkins's armed participation in the conspiracy was federally established.

Why Was Wilkins Federally Convicted After a State Acquittal?

The two prosecutions involved different sovereigns and different offenses. Alabama prosecuted homicide; the United States prosecuted conspiracy against federally protected rights under 18 U.S.C. § 241.

At the time, the constitutional doctrine of dual sovereignty allowed separate state and federal prosecutions when the same conduct violated distinct state and federal laws.

Did the Federal Conviction Depend Only on Rowe?

Rowe was the central eyewitness, but the Fifth Circuit stated that the record contained extensive detailed corroboration supporting his account. The appellate judges found the evidence more than sufficient to sustain the conspiracy conviction.

Later questions about Rowe's credibility therefore complicate the historical reconstruction without automatically nullifying the final federal judgment.

Was the Attack Specifically About Black Voting?

Yes, in the broader political sense. The Selma-to-Montgomery march existed to protest the denial of Black voter registration, and Wilkins had participated in a Klan rally opposing the court-protected march before joining the group that attacked its supporters.

The federal case explicitly tied the conspiracy to people participating in or supporting that voting-rights demonstration.

Death in 1994

Wilkins died in Birmingham on December 23, 1994, at age fifty-one. Contemporary Associated Press reporting described him as a Klansman convicted in one of the civil-rights era's most notorious slayings, referring to the federal conviction arising from Liuzzo's death.

He died before many of the later civil-rights cold-case prosecutions that brought renewed criminal accountability to other former Klansmen during the 1990s and 2000s.

Historical Significance

Collie Leroy Wilkins Jr. is historically significant as a direct participant in the Klan attack that killed Viola Liuzzo and as one of the defendants in an important federal civil-rights conspiracy prosecution. His case illustrates how Reconstruction-era federal law was used a century later against modern Klan violence aimed at Black voting rights.

The case also demonstrates the difference between historical, factual, and legal responsibility. The federal jury found his conspiratorial participation beyond a reasonable doubt and the Fifth Circuit affirmed; Alabama did not convict him of murder; later evidence raised a competing claim about Rowe's role as shooter. Keeping those distinctions clear produces a stronger historical account than simplifying the case into a single label.

Impact on African Americans

Wilkins's actions contributed to an environment in which Black Alabamians and their allies could be followed, threatened, assaulted, or killed for supporting voting rights. The terror was designed to reach beyond the immediate victims by warning anyone considering participation in the movement.

The intended political result was continued white control. If Black citizens could be frightened away from marches, registration offices, interracial organizing, and assistance from outside volunteers, the formal right to vote could remain difficult to exercise in practice.

A Balanced Assessment

Wilkins was a young mechanic rather than a major intellectual, politician, or Klan commander, and the accessible record offers little evidence of noteworthy public accomplishments outside the episode that made him infamous. His biography is therefore necessarily narrower than that of a governor, judge, military officer, or organizational founder.

That limited public profile should not minimize his significance. White-supremacist systems required people willing to perform enforcement at the ground level, and the federal record portrays Wilkins actively participating in an armed mission against civil-rights supporters. At the same time, historical accuracy requires stating that he was acquitted of state murder rather than falsely describing his federal conspiracy conviction as a murder judgment.

Legacy

Wilkins's legacy is bound to the final night of the Selma voting-rights campaign and to Viola Liuzzo's death. The murder became a national symbol of the danger faced not only by Black activists but by white Americans who violated segregationist expectations by working alongside them.

The federal prosecution also helped demonstrate that private Klan conspiracies could fall within national civil-rights enforcement when they attacked citizens exercising federally protected political rights. The unresolved controversy surrounding Gary Rowe additionally left the case as a warning about the risks created when law-enforcement agencies place informants deeply inside violent extremist groups.

Timeline

  • 1943: Born in Alabama.
  • Early 1960s: Lives in Fairfield in the Birmingham metropolitan area and works as an automobile mechanic.
  • Early 1960s: Becomes involved in Birmingham-area Ku Klux Klan activity.
  • March 7, 1965: Bloody Sunday attack on voting-rights marchers occurs in Selma.
  • March 17, 1965: Federal court authorizes a protected Selma-to-Montgomery march.
  • March 21, 1965: Wilkins participates in a Klan rally and parade in Montgomery protesting the federal march order.
  • March 21-25, 1965: Selma-to-Montgomery voting-rights march proceeds.
  • March 25, 1965: Wilkins travels with Eugene Thomas, William Eaton, and FBI informant Gary Rowe to Montgomery and Selma.
  • March 25, 1965: Group harasses march supporters and searches for people associated with the movement.
  • Night of March 25, 1965: Klansmen pursue Viola Liuzzo and Leroy Moton on Highway 80 and fire into Liuzzo's car, killing her.
  • March 26, 1965: Federal authorities arrest the four men; President Lyndon Johnson publicly announces the arrests.
  • Spring 1965: Alabama indicts Wilkins on a murder charge.
  • May 1965: First state murder trial ends in a hung jury.
  • 1965: Later Alabama proceedings fail to produce a murder conviction; Wilkins is acquitted.
  • December 3, 1965: Federal jury convicts Wilkins, Eaton, and Thomas of conspiracy against federally protected civil rights.
  • December 1965: Wilkins receives the statutory maximum sentence of ten years.
  • March 1966: William Orville Eaton dies before serving his federal sentence.
  • April 27, 1967: Fifth Circuit affirms the federal convictions of Wilkins and Thomas.
  • Late 1960s-1970s: Wilkins serves years in federal custody.
  • Late 1970s: Wilkins and Thomas tell a Lowndes County grand jury that Gary Rowe was Liuzzo's actual shooter.
  • 1978: Alabama indicts Rowe for murder; charge is later dismissed because of federal immunity.
  • 1980s: Litigation over FBI responsibility and Rowe's informant role continues.
  • December 23, 1994: Wilkins dies in Birmingham at age fifty-one.

Geographic Scope

Fairfield and Birmingham formed Wilkins's home and Klan environment. Montgomery was the destination of the voting-rights march and the location of the Klan rally and march-day harassment documented in the federal case.

Selma and Lowndes County were central to the pursuit and murder of Liuzzo. U.S. Highway 80 connected the movement's two major cities and became the landscape on which Klan opposition to Black voting rights turned into lethal violence.

Legal and Constitutional Context

Wilkins's prosecution involved 18 U.S.C. § 241, the federal civil-rights conspiracy statute; federally protected assembly and petition rights; the right to participate in a court-protected voting-rights march; dual state and federal prosecutions; state homicide law; and federal appellate review.

The Fifth Circuit's decision helped explain how a private conspiracy aimed at participants in a federally protected political demonstration could fall within national criminal jurisdiction. The case belongs to the broader revival of Reconstruction-era civil-rights enforcement during the 1960s.
