William Orville Eaton

Bessemer steelworker, Ku Klux Klan operative, and federally convicted civil-rights conspirator who fired during the 1965 Klan attack that killed Viola Gregg Liuzzo after the Selma-to-Montgomery voting-rights march

Overview

William Orville Eaton was an American retired steelworker, Ku Klux Klan member, federal civil-rights conspirator, and direct armed participant in the March 25, 1965 Klan pursuit and shooting that killed civil-rights volunteer Viola Gregg Liuzzo after the Selma-to-Montgomery voting-rights march.

Eaton lived in Bessemer, Alabama, southwest of Birmingham. President Lyndon B. Johnson identified him as forty-one years old when announcing the arrests on March 26, 1965.

Public records differ somewhat on Eaton's exact year of birth. Some later family and biographical records give 1923, while other secondary records place his birth slightly earlier. Because the strongest contemporary official source gives his age rather than a birth date, this biography treats the exact year with caution.

Contemporary sources described Eaton as a retired steelworker.

The Civil Rights Digital Library identifies him as married and the father of five children.

By 1965 Eaton was a member of the Ku Klux Klan and part of the same Birmingham-Bessemer extremist network that included Eugene Thomas, Collie Leroy Wilkins Jr., and FBI informant Gary Thomas Rowe Jr.

Eaton was not a government official.

His role in civil-rights violence therefore differed from figures such as George Wallace, Jim Clark, and Bull Connor, who wielded state or municipal authority.

Eaton operated instead through private white-supremacist organization and direct armed violence.

The immediate context was the Selma voting-rights campaign.

Black citizens of Dallas County and surrounding areas had faced systematic barriers to voter registration and political participation.

Local activists, the Dallas County Voters League, the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee, and the Southern Christian Leadership Conference intensified their campaign during early 1965.

On February 18, 1965, Alabama state trooper James Bonard Fowler shot Jimmie Lee Jackson during a voting-rights confrontation in Marion.

Jackson died on February 26.

Movement leaders responded by planning a march from Selma to the Alabama state capitol in Montgomery.

On March 7, 1965, approximately six hundred marchers attempted to cross the Edmund Pettus Bridge.

Alabama state troopers and local forces attacked them in the event known as Bloody Sunday.

Televised images of the attack created national outrage.

A second march attempt on March 9 ended when Martin Luther King Jr. turned demonstrators back rather than violate a federal injunction.

Later that day white segregationists attacked Unitarian minister James Reeb.

Reeb died two days later.

Federal District Judge Frank M. Johnson Jr. subsequently ruled that the marchers had a constitutional right to demonstrate.

His March 17 order in Williams v. Wallace restrained state officials from interfering with the march and required them to provide protection.

The final march began on March 21 and reached Montgomery on March 25.

The Ku Klux Klan openly opposed the federally protected demonstration.

On March 21 Klan members held a rally at Cramton Bowl in Montgomery and a public parade specifically protesting the federal court order allowing the Selma-to-Montgomery march.

The Fifth Circuit later found abundant evidence that the defendants associated with the Liuzzo conspiracy attended the protest rally and parade.

That participation became important evidence of notice and intent in the later federal prosecution.

On March 25, FBI informant Gary Thomas Rowe Jr. received a telephone call from Eugene Thomas instructing him to join Klansmen travelling to Montgomery.

A Klan official separately told Rowe to make the trip and said the instruction came from the Klan's Imperial Office.

Rowe contacted his FBI handler before joining the group.

He then met Eugene Thomas, Collie Leroy Wilkins Jr., and William Orville Eaton near the Klan meeting hall in Bessemer.

All four men travelled to Montgomery in Thomas's automobile.

The Fifth Circuit specifically noted that Eaton, Thomas, and Rowe were armed.

The men left the weapons in the parked car while spending roughly five hours near the Capitol.

During that period the group harassed civil-rights marchers and Black spectators by booing, shouting, and arguing.

After the speeches ended, all four returned to the automobile.

Thomas announced that they were going to Selma.

Eaton asked why.

Thomas replied that they had things to do and intended to get them done.

That exchange is important because it places Eaton inside the conversation that immediately preceded the group's movement toward Selma.

After leaving a restaurant, the men saw a hitchhiker.

Eaton asked whether the man might be one of the marchers.

Wilkins suggested slowing down to determine whether the hitchhiker was associated with the movement and spoke of giving him a surprise.

They decided he did not appear to be a marcher and left him alone.

The episode did not result in violence, but it helped the federal jury understand the group's purpose and hostility toward people they associated with the march.

The Klan car continued toward Selma on Highway 80.

Alabama Highway Patrol officers stopped the vehicle at a radar checkpoint.

Thomas received a warning ticket for an improper muffler.

That stop later served as independent corroboration that the four men were travelling together on the Selma-Montgomery corridor.

In Selma the men stopped at the Silver Moon Cafe for approximately thirty to forty-five minutes.

The Fifth Circuit later described an encounter in which a man charged in the James Reeb killing encouraged Thomas and the other men to do their job.

This encounter is historically revealing but should not be stretched into proof of a formal joint conspiracy between the Reeb killing and the later Liuzzo attack.

After leaving the cafe, Thomas drove.

Eaton sat in the front passenger seat beside him.

Rowe sat behind Thomas.

Wilkins sat behind Eaton.

Near a church where movement participants had gathered, the men noticed Black pedestrians.

Thomas slowed the car and spoke of having fun with them.

Wilkins told Rowe to prepare because they were going to take the pedestrians.

The group abandoned the confrontation after Rowe noticed an Army truck with soldiers nearby.

During that episode Thomas passed his handgun to Wilkins and then returned it between the seats when the group withdrew.

The incident showed that armed intimidation was already under discussion before Liuzzo's car appeared.

The four men then drove through Selma toward the Alabama River bridge.

At a traffic light they saw a light-colored Oldsmobile bearing Michigan plates.

Viola Gregg Liuzzo, a white civil-rights volunteer from Detroit, was driving.

Leroy Moton, a nineteen-year-old Black civil-rights worker, was with her.

Liuzzo had travelled to Alabama after Bloody Sunday and volunteered to transport march participants between Selma and Montgomery.

Moton had also worked with movement transportation.

The sight of a white woman and Black man travelling together provoked racist commentary from the Klansmen.

Thomas proposed following them.

He told the men in the rear seat to lower themselves below window level and repeatedly said that the group was going to get the occupants.

The Klan car began pursuing Liuzzo's Oldsmobile.

Thomas drove at speeds estimated by the Fifth Circuit at eighty to ninety miles per hour.

Rowe attempted to persuade Thomas to end the pursuit near Craig Air Force Base.

Thomas refused.

Thomas asked whether he should ram Liuzzo's car.

Wilkins objected because contact might transfer paint and provide evidence linking the vehicles.

Instead, Thomas pulled the Klan automobile alongside Liuzzo's Oldsmobile.

Wilkins held Thomas's pistol.

According to the evidence summarized by the Fifth Circuit, Wilkins extended his arm from the car and fired two shots into Liuzzo's vehicle.

At that point Thomas ordered everyone to shoot.

The Fifth Circuit then described Eaton beginning to fire his own .22-caliber pistol.

The court stated that Eaton's pistol was loaded with shaved bullets.

Eaton leaned out of the moving car and continued trying to fire while the vehicles passed one another.

This is the strongest judicially summarized evidence of Eaton's direct physical role in the attack.

It distinguishes him from a passive passenger or merely present conspirator.

The available appellate record does not identify Eaton's shots as the bullets that caused Liuzzo's death.

The strongest later judicial findings instead identify Wilkins as the principal fatal shooter.

That distinction does not reduce Eaton's direct participation.

He intentionally fired a handgun from the pursuing Klan vehicle during the attack on Liuzzo and Moton.

After the shooting Liuzzo's Oldsmobile left the highway.

Liuzzo died from gunshot wounds.

Moton survived.

The attackers returned toward the disabled car.

Moton remained motionless and pretended to be dead.

After the Klan car left, he sought assistance.

The Fifth Circuit stated that Wilkins and Eaton threw their cartridge casings out of the window after the attack.

Discarding the casings constituted evidence of consciousness of detection and an effort to reduce physical evidence.

When the men returned to Bessemer, they attempted to arrange alibis.

Rowe soon reported the murder to his FBI contact.

He identified Thomas, Wilkins, and Eaton as the other men in the Klan car.

The FBI moved rapidly.

Within roughly twenty-four hours federal agents arrested the four men.

President Lyndon Johnson announced the arrests on national television on March 26.

Johnson identified Eaton as forty-one years old and from Bessemer.

Johnson also publicly identified all four men as members of the United Klans of America, Inc., Knights of the Ku Klux Klan.

The President condemned the killing as Klan terrorism and called on members to leave the organization.

Rowe's status as an FBI informant was initially concealed from the public.

He was soon separated from the other suspects and became the government's principal insider witness.

Alabama and federal authorities promised Rowe immunity in exchange for cooperation.

Rowe later testified before state and federal grand juries and in multiple trials.

Eaton, Thomas, and Wilkins were indicted for Liuzzo's murder under Alabama law.

They also faced a federal civil-rights prosecution.

The two proceedings involved different offenses and should not be conflated.

Alabama's case concerned murder.

The federal case concerned conspiracy to interfere with federally protected rights.

Collie Wilkins was tried first by Alabama.

His first murder trial began in May 1965 in Hayneville.

The jury deadlocked and a mistrial was declared.

A second Alabama jury acquitted Wilkins in October 1965.

Eaton did not receive a final Alabama murder verdict.

Some later secondary summaries loosely state that all three non-informant Klansmen were acquitted in Alabama.

That is inaccurate as applied to Eaton.

His murder charge remained pending when he died in March 1966.

Eugene Thomas was not tried by Alabama until September 1966, after Eaton's death.

Thomas was then acquitted.

Eaton's legal position was therefore distinct: he was indicted for murder but died before that state charge was adjudicated.

The federal prosecution moved more quickly.

The Justice Department charged Eaton, Wilkins, and Thomas under 18 U.S.C. § 241.

The indictment alleged a conspiracy to injure, oppress, threaten, and intimidate citizens participating in or supporting the Selma-to-Montgomery march.

The federal prosecution did not charge ordinary murder.

The shooting of Liuzzo nonetheless became central evidence of the conspiracy's criminal intent.

The trial began in federal court in Montgomery on November 29, 1965.

Judge Frank M. Johnson Jr. presided.

John Doar of the Justice Department Civil Rights Division led the federal prosecution.

Former Birmingham mayor Arthur Hanes represented the defendants.

The government relied heavily on Rowe's eyewitness account.

The prosecution also presented extensive corroboration concerning the Klan protest rally, the March 25 trip, the Highway Patrol stop, the defendants' movements, the weapons, the pursuit, and the effort to dispose of evidence.

The federal jury was instructed that the defendants had a constitutional right to attend the Klan rally and protest the march.

Their political expression by itself was not criminal.

The rally became relevant because it demonstrated notice of the federally protected march and helped establish intent when the same men later travelled to Montgomery and Selma.

This is an important legal distinction.

Racist belief, Klan membership, and anti-march protest were not themselves substitutes for proof of the federal conspiracy.

The criminal case rested on the defendants' later conduct directed at march participants and supporters.

On December 3, 1965, the federal jury found Eaton, Wilkins, and Thomas guilty.

Each man received the statutory maximum sentence of ten years in federal prison.

Eaton was therefore a convicted federal civil-rights conspirator.

He was not convicted of murder.

Nor should his later death erase the fact that a jury had already returned a valid guilty verdict against him on the federal charge.

Eaton and the others appealed.

The appeal eventually produced the major Fifth Circuit decision commonly captioned under Wilkins and Thomas because Eaton died while it was pending.

The Fifth Circuit expressly noted in a footnote that Eaton died during the pendency of the appeal and that his case was dismissed as moot.

Because Eaton died before appellate review concluded, the Fifth Circuit's formal affirmance applied to the surviving appellants Wilkins and Thomas.

The court nevertheless summarized the evidence involving Eaton in detail as part of the common trial record.

That record described Eaton as armed from the beginning of the March 25 trip.

It recorded his question about whether a hitchhiker was one of the marchers.

It placed him in the front passenger seat during the later attack.

It stated that he began firing a .22 pistol after Thomas ordered everyone to shoot.

It described him leaning out of the car trying to fire.

It stated that Eaton and Wilkins threw cartridge casings out of the window afterward.

Those details provide a far more precise picture of his participation than the generic statement that he was merely 'in the car.'

Eaton remained free pending appeal rather than beginning his ten-year sentence immediately.

The Civil Rights Digital Library states that he had earlier been released on $50,000 bond.

He therefore died without entering prison on the federal civil-rights sentence.

On March 10, 1966, contemporary newspaper reports announced that William Orville Eaton had died in Bessemer after a heart attack.

He was approximately forty-two years old.

The death occurred only a few months after his federal conviction.

It also occurred before Alabama tried him on the pending murder indictment.

His death mooted his federal appeal.

No state murder jury ever acquitted Eaton.

No state murder jury ever convicted him.

No later tribunal could impose the ten-year federal sentence because he was dead.

That procedural history is one of the most important corrections required in writing his biography.

The later controversy over FBI informant Gary Rowe continued long after Eaton's death.

In the late 1970s surviving defendants Wilkins and Thomas accused Rowe of being the actual shooter.

A Lowndes County grand jury indicted Rowe for murder in 1978.

Federal courts later blocked Alabama from prosecuting him because state and federal authorities had promised him immunity in exchange for his 1965 testimony.

The Eleventh Circuit's decision in Rowe v. Griffin did not determine who fired the fatal shots.

Its central issue was whether the government could withdraw its immunity promise after Rowe had relied upon it.

Because Eaton had died in 1966, he never participated in the late-1970s effort by Wilkins and Thomas to shift shooter responsibility to Rowe.

This absence matters.

There is no later Eaton testimony accusing Rowe comparable to the statements made by the two surviving defendants.

The Liuzzo family later sued the United States over the FBI's management of Rowe.

In 1983 a federal court conducted an extensive factual inquiry into the killing.

The judge found that Rowe did not fire the fatal shots.

The court concluded that Wilkins shot Liuzzo and Eugene Thomas encouraged him.

That later civil finding does not specifically assign a fatal bullet to Eaton.

It is consistent, however, with the earlier federal evidence that Eaton also fired during the attack.

The strongest historical description of Eaton is therefore direct but precise.

He was an armed Klan participant in the pursuit.

He was not merely present.

He fired his .22 pistol during the attack and helped discard evidence afterward according to testimony credited by the federal jury and summarized by the Fifth Circuit.

The available record does not establish that Eaton fired the particular bullet or bullets that killed Liuzzo.

He was convicted of federal conspiracy against civil rights.

He was indicted for murder but died before a state murder verdict.

Eaton's family status also deserves careful treatment.

Contemporary biographical summaries described him as married and the father of five children.

Some accounts of the 1965 courtroom proceedings describe Eaton bringing his children to hearings or trials.

His family relationships are part of his biography but should not be used either to soften or sensationalize his criminal conduct.

Ordinary family life can coexist with participation in extremist violence.

Eaton's background as a steelworker likewise demonstrates that organized white-supremacist terrorism was not limited to public officials or professional political extremists.

The Klan drew participants from ordinary workplaces and neighborhoods.

The Liuzzo conspiracy therefore illustrates how a violent political organization could mobilize men from everyday civilian life to attack protected political activity.

The timing of the murder is especially significant.

Liuzzo was killed only hours after a federal court-protected march for voting rights successfully reached Montgomery.

The defendants had attended a Klan protest against the federal march order.

They then travelled along the same corridor, harassed supporters, and eventually attacked an interracial pair they associated with the movement.

That sequence gave the federal government a powerful theory under Reconstruction-era law.

Section 241 traces its roots to federal efforts after the Civil War to protect citizenship rights from conspiratorial violence.

Nearly a century later, prosecutors used the same statutory tradition against Klan members attacking voting-rights supporters in Alabama.

The Fifth Circuit ultimately held that participation in the Selma-to-Montgomery march was an attribute of national citizenship protected from private conspiracy.

Although Eaton died before that appellate judgment formally resolved his own appeal, his conviction arose from the same trial and evidence.

For MGU's antagonist series, Eaton belongs in the category of direct violent white-supremacist operative.

He differs from ideological propagandists such as Asa Earl Carter.

He differs from institutional segregationists such as Harry F. Byrd Sr.

He differs from law-enforcement antagonists such as Jim Clark.

He is closer to Collie Wilkins, Robert Chambliss, Bobby Frank Cherry, and other men who personally participated in armed racial violence.

Even within that category, the precise roles should remain distinct.

The strongest record identifies Wilkins as the man who fired the first shots with Thomas's pistol.

Thomas drove, pursued, supplied the weapon, and urged everyone to shoot.

Eaton was armed with his own .22 pistol, fired during the attack, leaned from the moving car while trying to shoot, and helped discard cartridge casings afterward.

Rowe was the FBI informant whose presence made rapid identification possible but created major later controversies over federal informant management.

The victim-centered historical record remains essential.

Viola Liuzzo was a thirty-nine-year-old mother of five who had travelled from Detroit to assist a voting-rights movement.

She was murdered because the Klan group interpreted her interracial movement work as a challenge to white supremacy.

Leroy Moton survived the attack and became an indispensable eyewitness to the violence from the victim vehicle.

The crime should not be reduced to the reputations of the men who attacked them.

Liuzzo's death became part of the national political climate surrounding the Voting Rights Act of 1965.

Her murder did not single-handedly cause the legislation.

The Act grew from decades of Black organizing, litigation, political pressure, federal action, and the cumulative crisis of Selma.

Her killing nevertheless exposed the lethal danger faced by people challenging racial disfranchisement and increased demands for federal protection.

William Orville Eaton's life is therefore historically important not because he was a major public leader, but because he demonstrates how organized racial terror functioned operationally.

He joined an armed Klan expedition, participated in harassment of movement supporters, rode in the pursuing car, fired during the lethal attack, helped dispose of evidence, and was convicted by a federal jury of conspiring against protected civil rights.

His death prevented both imprisonment and a final Alabama murder adjudication.

Historical accuracy requires preserving both facts.

Yes. A federal jury convicted him of conspiracy against civil rights.

Did Eaton Go to Prison?

No. He died before beginning the federal prison sentence.

Was Eaton's Federal Appeal Decided on the Merits?

No. His appeal was dismissed as moot after his death.

Was Eaton Acquitted by Alabama?

No. He died before his pending state murder case reached a verdict.

Was He a Klan Member?

Yes.

Was He a Steelworker?

Yes. Contemporary sources describe him as a retired steelworker.

Did He Have Children?

Yes. Archival biographical summaries identify five children.

Historical Significance

Eaton's biography links ordinary industrial life, organized Klan membership, armed intimidation of march supporters, direct gunfire in the Liuzzo attack, federal civil-rights prosecution, and the procedural consequences of a defendant dying before state trial and federal appeal.

Legacy

William Orville Eaton illustrates why precise legal history matters. He was not acquitted of murder; he died before trial. He was not merely present in the Klan car; the federal record says he fired during the attack and discarded evidence afterward. And although he never served his federal sentence, a jury had already convicted him of intentionally conspiring against federally protected civil-rights activity.

Geographic Scope

Bessemer, Alabama, was Eaton's home, workplace community, and principal Klan environment.

Montgomery was the destination of the voting-rights march, the site of the Klan protest rally, and the location of the federal conspiracy trial.

Selma was the center of the voting-rights campaign and the place from which the Klan group began the final pursuit.

U.S. Highway 80 between Selma and Montgomery became the route of the chase and shooting.

Lowndes County was the site of Liuzzo's murder and the pending Alabama murder charge against Eaton.

Birmingham and the surrounding industrial communities supplied much of the social geography of the Alabama Klan network.

Legal and Constitutional Context

Eaton's legal history involved a pending Alabama murder indictment and a completed federal conspiracy-against-rights conviction.

He died before the state murder case reached trial, so he was neither convicted nor acquitted of murder.

The federal prosecution under 18 U.S.C. § 241 was distinct from ordinary homicide law and focused on intentional interference with people exercising federally protected rights through the Selma-to-Montgomery march.

Because Eaton died while his federal appeal was pending, the Fifth Circuit dismissed his portion of the appeal as moot rather than affirming or reversing it on the merits.

The surviving defendants' convictions were affirmed, and the appellate opinion preserved a detailed factual account of Eaton's role in the common conspiracy.
