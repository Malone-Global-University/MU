Wade Hampton III

South Carolina planter and major slaveholder, Confederate cavalry commander, Redeemer governor, U.S. senator, and central figure in the overthrow of Reconstruction

Overview

Wade Hampton III (1818-1902) was a South Carolina planter, slaveholder, state legislator, Confederate cavalry commander, governor, United States senator, and one of the most influential conservative Democratic leaders of Reconstruction-era South Carolina.

Before the Civil War, Hampton belonged to one of the wealthiest slaveholding families in the United States and personally controlled a vast plantation estate worked by hundreds of enslaved African Americans. Although he opposed reopening the transatlantic slave trade and was less eager for immediate secession than South Carolina's most radical Fire-Eaters, he ultimately supported secession and raised the Hampton Legion for Confederate service at his own expense.

After the war, Hampton became the public face of South Carolina's Democratic “Redemption” movement. He publicly urged restraint and sought some Black Democratic support, but his 1876 gubernatorial campaign operated alongside Red Shirt paramilitary organizations that used intimidation, fraud, and racial violence to suppress Republican political power. His victory and assumption of office in 1877 marked the effective end of Reconstruction government in South Carolina.

Family Background

Hampton was born into a family whose wealth, political influence, and military reputation stretched back generations. His grandfather Wade Hampton I had been a Revolutionary-era soldier, politician, land speculator, and one of the country's wealthiest plantation owners.

His father, Wade Hampton II, inherited and expanded extensive agricultural holdings in South Carolina and the Mississippi Valley.

Birth and Childhood

Wade Hampton III was born in Charleston on March 28, 1818, to Wade Hampton II and Ann Fitzsimmons Hampton. He grew up primarily within the world of the South Carolina planter elite.

The wealth surrounding his childhood was inseparable from slavery: the family's plantations depended upon the coerced labor of large enslaved communities.

South Carolina College

Hampton attended South Carolina College, now the University of South Carolina, and graduated in 1836.

He later maintained a long institutional relationship with the college as a trustee and, as governor, participated in its reopening after Reconstruction-era political changes.

Study of Law

After college Hampton studied law but did not establish a sustained legal practice. His financial position allowed him to devote himself primarily to plantation management, public affairs, hunting, and eventually politics.

Marriage to Margaret Preston

On October 10, 1838, Hampton married Margaret Frances Preston. They had five children before her death in 1852.

The marriage connected Hampton to another prominent southern family and further embedded him in the planter aristocracy.

Second Marriage

Hampton married Mary Singleton McDuffie on January 27, 1858. They had four children.

His second wife was also drawn from an influential South Carolina family, reinforcing the dense family networks that structured elite political and economic life in the antebellum state.

The Hammond Family Connection

Hampton's family was connected to James Henry Hammond, the South Carolina governor, senator, planter, and proslavery theorist.

Hammond sexually abused four of Hampton's sisters when they were teenagers. Hampton remained close to his sisters and helped support them financially after the death of their father.

Inheritance

When Wade Hampton II died in 1858, Hampton III assumed management of much of the family's enormous landholding empire.

These properties included plantations in South Carolina and Mississippi and interests elsewhere in the South.

Millwood Plantation

Millwood, near Columbia, became one of the residences most closely associated with Hampton. The plantation house and surrounding property symbolized the scale of his prewar wealth.

The estate was destroyed during the Civil War as Union forces moved through South Carolina.

Mississippi Plantations

The Hampton family possessed extensive cotton lands in Mississippi, where plantation agriculture was especially dependent on large enslaved labor forces.

Hampton spent substantial time managing these properties and deriving income from cotton production.

Nearly One Thousand Enslaved People

University of South Carolina historical research reports that at the height of Hampton's property ownership he controlled nearly one thousand enslaved people.

This placed him among the largest slaveholders in the United States and makes slavery a central, rather than incidental, component of his biography.

Plantation Income

Historical research cited by the University of South Carolina estimates that Hampton's plantations produced nearly $200,000 annually at their peak, an extraordinary nineteenth-century income.

The production underlying that wealth depended overwhelmingly on enslaved labor.

The Enslaved Communities

The people enslaved by the Hampton family formed large communities across multiple plantations. They worked in agricultural, domestic, skilled, transportation, and supervisory roles while living under a legal system that treated them as property.

A biography centered only on Hampton's wealth risks reproducing the perspective of the slaveholder's records. His financial success represented work performed by people who were denied wages, freedom of movement, and control over their family and legal status.

Slaveholder Paternalism

Like many members of the planter elite, Hampton later described slavery through paternalistic language, arguing that white slaveholders had cared for and guided enslaved African Americans.

That interpretation treated domination as guardianship while obscuring the fundamental coercion of slavery: the enslaved person did not choose the relationship and could not legally leave it.

Opposition to Reopening the African Slave Trade

In 1859 Hampton publicly opposed proposals to reopen the transatlantic slave trade, calling the trade odious.

This position distinguished him from some southern radicals who advocated renewed African importation during the 1850s.

Opposition to the Trade Was Not Abolitionism

Hampton's opposition to reopening the international slave trade did not amount to opposition to slavery. He continued to own hundreds of enslaved people and did not emancipate them before the Civil War.

His position was therefore consistent with defending the existing domestic slave system while rejecting renewed overseas importation.

Entry into Politics

Hampton entered formal politics in the 1850s after years primarily devoted to plantation management.

His wealth and family standing gave him political independence and substantial social authority before he ever held elected office.

South Carolina House of Representatives

Hampton served in the South Carolina House of Representatives from 1852 to 1858.

His antebellum legislative career was comparatively conventional for an elite South Carolina Democrat and did not initially place him among the state's most radical secessionist agitators.

South Carolina Senate

In 1858 Hampton entered the South Carolina Senate, where he served until the beginning of the Civil War.

By then the national political system was breaking apart over slavery, territorial expansion, and the rise of the Republican Party.

A Conservative Within South Carolina Politics

Compared with Fire-Eaters such as Robert Barnwell Rhett, Hampton was relatively conservative during the final secession debate.

He did not spend decades organizing for disunion and initially preferred a less precipitous response to sectional conflict.

Secession

Once South Carolina seceded in December 1860, Hampton supported his state and the emerging Confederacy.

His decision demonstrates an important distinction between hesitation over the timing of secession and opposition to the Confederate cause itself.

Why Slavery Matters to the Secession Context

Hampton's political world was deeply bound to slavery. The Republican Party's opposition to slavery's territorial expansion threatened the long-term political position of the slaveholding elite to which Hampton belonged.

Although Hampton was not one of the ideological architects of secession, the social order he entered the Confederate war to defend was a slaveholding one.

Hampton's Legion

At the outbreak of war Hampton used his personal wealth to organize and equip a mixed Confederate command known as Hampton's Legion.

The National Park Service records that the Legion contained infantry and cavalry components that later served separately.

No Formal Military Training

Hampton had no West Point education and little formal professional military preparation when the war began.

His initial military authority reflected wealth, status, organizational ability, and political influence as much as prior training.

From Private to Colonel

Hampton initially enrolled in South Carolina military service as a private but was quickly elevated to command.

Despite the unusual route into senior command, he proved more militarily capable than many politically connected officers appointed during the war.

First Manassas

Hampton's Legion fought at the First Battle of Manassas in July 1861. Hampton was wounded during the battle.

The performance of his troops and his personal conduct established an early reputation for battlefield courage.

Transition to Cavalry Command

Although Hampton's Legion included both infantry and cavalry, Hampton increasingly became associated with mounted operations.

His abilities as a cavalry commander eventually made him one of the highest-ranking mounted officers in the Confederate army.

Brigadier General

Hampton was promoted to brigadier general in 1862.

He commanded cavalry within the Army of Northern Virginia under J. E. B. Stuart.

Army of Northern Virginia Cavalry

Hampton became a major brigade commander in Stuart's cavalry organization. The cavalry screened Confederate movements, gathered intelligence, guarded flanks, raided communications, and fought increasingly large mounted engagements.

Brandy Station

Hampton's command participated in the Battle of Brandy Station in June 1863, the largest predominantly cavalry engagement of the Civil War.

The fighting demonstrated the growing effectiveness of Union cavalry and marked a change in the mounted balance between the two armies.

Gettysburg Campaign

Hampton led Confederate cavalry during the Gettysburg Campaign. National Park Service records place his brigade within Stuart's cavalry division.

He fought heavily around Gettysburg and was seriously wounded during the campaign.

Battlefield Wounds

Hampton was wounded multiple times during the Civil War. His willingness to expose himself in combat contributed significantly to his reputation among Confederate soldiers.

That personal bravery can be acknowledged independently of the political cause for which he fought.

Major General

Hampton rose to major general as his cavalry responsibilities expanded.

His promotions reflected a genuine record of effective field command rather than status alone.

Death of J. E. B. Stuart

J. E. B. Stuart died in May 1864 after being mortally wounded at Yellow Tavern.

His death created a major leadership problem in the cavalry arm of Robert E. Lee's Army of Northern Virginia.

Trevilian Station

Hampton played a leading role in the Battle of Trevilian Station in June 1864, one of the war's largest all-cavalry battles.

The engagement strengthened his position in the competition to succeed Stuart as the principal Confederate cavalry commander in Virginia.

Commander of Lee's Cavalry

By August 1864 Hampton had become commander of the cavalry of the Army of Northern Virginia.

This placed a planter with no prewar professional military education in one of the Confederacy's most important mounted commands.

Beefsteak Raid

In September 1864 Hampton's cavalry carried out the raid commonly known as the Beefsteak Raid, capturing large numbers of cattle intended for Union forces around Petersburg.

The operation became one of the most celebrated Confederate cavalry raids of the late war.

Lieutenant General

Hampton ultimately reached the rank of lieutenant general. He was among the relatively small number of Confederate officers who attained such rank without having attended the United States Military Academy.

Return to South Carolina

As Union armies advanced into the Carolinas in 1865, Hampton returned to defend his home state.

His cavalry operated against forces under General William T. Sherman during the final months of Confederate resistance.

Sherman's Carolina Campaign

Sherman's march through South Carolina destroyed infrastructure, military resources, plantations, railroads, and substantial private property.

For Hampton, the campaign brought the destruction of both the Confederacy he served and much of the plantation world from which his wealth had come.

Destruction of Millwood

Union forces destroyed Hampton's Millwood residence and other family property near Columbia in 1865.

The physical destruction became an enduring part of Hampton's postwar interpretation of the conflict and of the Lost Cause memory surrounding him.

End of the Confederacy

Hampton was reluctant to accept Confederate defeat and initially considered continued resistance.

Organized Confederate military power nevertheless collapsed during the spring of 1865, ending the government and army for which he had fought.

Emancipation

The destruction of slavery transformed Hampton's economic position as fundamentally as Confederate defeat transformed his political position.

Nearly one thousand people previously subject to the Hampton family's ownership claims were legally free.

Financial Collapse

Hampton's wealth was devastated by war, destruction of property, falling land values, and the end of enslaved labor. University of South Carolina research notes that he declared bankruptcy in 1868.

His postwar political prominence therefore coexisted with substantial personal financial decline.

The 1865 Governor's Race

Hampton remained extraordinarily popular among white South Carolinians after the war. Despite saying he was not seeking the office, he nearly won the governorship in 1865.

His military reputation gave conservative whites a symbol around whom they could organize after Confederate defeat.

Advice to Confederate Veterans

In an 1866 speech to Confederate veterans, Hampton defended the honor of the Confederate cause and bitterly criticized federal Reconstruction policy.

At the same time, he declared that he did not wish to see slavery restored and advised white southerners to treat freedpeople justly and kindly.

Acceptance of Emancipation

Hampton's statement accepting emancipation is an important part of his postwar evolution. He said he did not want the abolition of slavery reversed.

Yet his accompanying language remained paternalistic, portraying slavery as having civilized and benefited African Americans rather than acknowledging enslaved people's own resistance, aspirations, and claims to freedom.

Racial Paternalism After Slavery

Hampton argued that former slaveholders were especially qualified to guide freedpeople in freedom. He attributed many postwar racial problems to northern intervention and the Freedmen's Bureau.

This position accepted legal emancipation while seeking to preserve white southern leadership over Black social and political life.

Hostility Toward Black Union Troops

Hampton's private and public writings could contain openly racist hostility. In correspondence with President Andrew Johnson, he used dehumanizing language to condemn the presence and conduct of Black United States troops in the South.

The language demonstrates that his later rhetoric of kindness toward freedpeople coexisted with strong beliefs in white racial superiority.

Opposition to Congressional Reconstruction

Hampton strongly opposed Congressional Reconstruction and the political changes imposed after former Confederate governments rejected or resisted federal civil-rights requirements.

He objected especially to Black political equality and to federal authority over the reconstructed southern states.

The 1868 South Carolina Constitution

South Carolina's 1868 constitution established universal male suffrage and expanded civil and political rights for Black citizens.

Hampton attacked the new order and complained that political power was passing from what he described as the superior race to the inferior.

Limited Black Suffrage

Hampton's position on Black voting evolved tactically. By the late 1860s he accepted that some African American men would vote and urged white Democrats to cultivate Black political support.

His acceptance of limited Black suffrage did not represent acceptance of equal interracial political power. He continued to envision white leadership as the natural governing authority.

Making Black Voters Democrats

Hampton advised white southerners to win the confidence of African Americans and turn Black voters away from the Republican Party.

This strategy distinguished his public approach from politicians who relied only on exclusion or direct racial terror, although it ultimately operated beside more coercive Democratic strategies.

Ku Klux Klan Era

During the late 1860s and early 1870s Ku Klux Klan organizations and related groups used terror against Black voters, Republican officials, teachers, and white Unionists throughout parts of South Carolina.

The violence triggered federal investigations, Enforcement Act prosecutions, and military intervention.

Bail for Suspected Klansmen

University of South Carolina research reports that Hampton contributed to bail funds for men suspected of participation in Ku Klux Klan racial violence.

This involvement complicates his later public posture as an opponent of uncontrolled political violence.

Public Opposition to Klan Violence

Hampton eventually called for an end to Ku Klux Klan violence. His reasons included concern for public order and fear that continued terrorism would provoke stronger federal intervention.

The shift was meaningful but did not amount to endorsement of Republican Reconstruction.

Federal Pardon

Hampton's political disabilities arising from Confederate service were removed during the Reconstruction era, and by 1872 he had regained eligibility for full political participation.

This cleared the way for his return to formal electoral leadership.

The Redeemers

White conservative Democrats seeking to overthrow Republican Reconstruction governments often called themselves “Redeemers.” Hampton became South Carolina's most important public symbol of this movement.

To supporters, Redemption meant restoring honest local government and white conservative rule. To many Black Republicans, it meant the destruction of the political gains secured through emancipation and Reconstruction.

Republican South Carolina

South Carolina presented a distinctive Reconstruction political environment because African Americans constituted a majority of the population and a majority of eligible male voters.

Black citizens elected legislators, held state offices, participated in constitutional government, and formed the core of the Republican electorate.

Criticism of Republican Government

Hampton and Democratic conservatives attacked Republican state government for corruption, taxation, debt, and administrative abuses.

Some criticism reflected genuine problems within Reconstruction government, but Democratic rhetoric often merged complaints about corruption with opposition to Black political power itself.

The 1876 Democratic Nomination

In 1876 South Carolina Democrats selected Hampton as their candidate for governor against Republican incumbent Daniel H. Chamberlain.

Hampton's Confederate prestige, aristocratic reputation, and comparatively moderate public style made him useful to a party attempting to attract conservative whites and a portion of Black voters.

Two Democratic Strategies

The 1876 Democratic campaign operated through two related but different strategies. Hampton publicly emphasized order, interracial conciliation, and the promise of impartial government.

At the same time, figures such as Martin W. Gary and Matthew C. Butler promoted aggressive pressure against Republican voters and organizations.

Martin W. Gary

Martin W. Gary was one of the principal architects of the coercive Democratic campaign. His “Edgefield Plan” called for organized control of Black voting through intimidation, economic pressure, disruption of Republican meetings, and other forms of coercion.

Gary considered Hampton's efforts to persuade Black voters insufficient by themselves.

Matthew C. Butler

Matthew C. Butler, Hampton's former Confederate subordinate and later political ally, also became a leading figure in the Democratic campaign.

Butler's political rise was closely associated with the Hamburg Massacre and the Red Shirt mobilization that followed.

The Hamburg Massacre

In July 1876 a confrontation at Hamburg, South Carolina, ended in the killing of Black militiamen by armed white men after some of the African Americans had surrendered.

The massacre occurred before Hampton's election and became a powerful signal of the violence surrounding the campaign.

Hampton and Hamburg

Hampton did not personally order the Hamburg killings and publicly attempted to distance his campaign from uncontrolled violence.

Nevertheless, leading participants in the Democratic movement used the event politically, and the white paramilitary mobilization that followed became a major component of Hampton's campaign environment.

Red Shirts

The Red Shirts were Democratic paramilitary organizations that became highly visible during the 1876 campaign. Many originated in local rifle clubs.

They rode in organized formations, attended Democratic rallies, displayed weapons, confronted Republican organizations, and projected white Democratic power throughout the state.

Scale of the Red Shirt Movement

University of South Carolina historical research reports approximately 290 rifle or Red Shirt clubs involving roughly 18,000 members by the end of the 1876 campaign.

The scale made them more than isolated vigilantes; they formed a statewide political mobilization supporting Hampton's candidacy.

Red Shirt Intimidation

Red Shirt tactics included disruption of Republican meetings, armed presence at political events, economic threats, voter intimidation, and in some areas direct violence.

The National Park Service describes the 1876 campaign as violent and corrupt and identifies suppression of Black and Republican voters as a Red Shirt objective.

Hampton's Public Position on Violence

Hampton repeatedly denounced political violence in public and did not openly endorse the most extreme elements of Gary's strategy.

This distinction is important: Hampton's public political style differed from the explicit violent rhetoric of some of his lieutenants.

Hampton and the Red Shirt Processions

Despite his public calls for peace, Hampton travelled and campaigned with large groups of Red Shirt supporters.

Their armed and uniformed presence supplied visual intimidation even where no violence occurred directly in Hampton's presence.

Responsibility and Historical Interpretation

Historians therefore distinguish between direct responsibility for particular violent acts and political responsibility for a campaign that benefited from a statewide paramilitary movement.

The evidence does not require portraying Hampton as personally ordering every attack, but neither does it support separating his candidacy from the coercive machinery that helped produce Democratic victory.

Black Supporters of Hampton

Not every African American voter supported the Republican Party in 1876. Black Democratic clubs existed, and some freedmen genuinely supported Hampton's promises of peace, local stability, or economic cooperation.

This complicates a purely binary description of the election.

Coercion and Black Democratic Support

At the same time, Black Democratic support existed within an environment of severe economic and physical pressure. Employers could threaten workers, landlords could influence tenants, and Republican voters faced intimidation in many communities.

Historical analysis therefore cannot assume that every Black Democratic vote represented either coercion or free ideological choice; the evidence supports both phenomena.

Ellenton Violence

Racial violence near Ellenton in 1876 killed numerous African Americans and at least one white person.

The violence further shaped the political environment in Edgefield and nearby counties, where suppression of Black Republican voting became especially severe.

Election Day

The gubernatorial election took place in November 1876 amid allegations of intimidation, ballot manipulation, and fraud by competing political organizations.

South Carolina's result became one of the most contested state elections in American history.

Edgefield and Laurens Counties

Democratic totals in Edgefield and Laurens counties became especially controversial because reported votes exceeded plausible numbers of eligible male residents.

The state canvassing dispute over these counties became central to determining whether Hampton or Chamberlain had won.

Fraud on Both Sides

Contemporary investigations and later historians have documented election irregularities associated with both political parties.

The existence of Republican fraud, however, does not negate the independently documented Democratic use of paramilitary intimidation and voter suppression.

Two Governments

After the election, Hampton and Chamberlain both claimed the governorship. Rival political authorities operated in Columbia while the state and national governments confronted simultaneous electoral crises.

The Presidential Election of 1876

The South Carolina dispute unfolded alongside the contested presidential election between Republican Rutherford B. Hayes and Democrat Samuel J. Tilden.

Federal negotiations over the national result became intertwined with the future of Republican governments in South Carolina and Louisiana.

Withdrawal of Federal Troops

President Rutherford B. Hayes withdrew federal troops from their political support role in Columbia in April 1877.

Without military backing, Chamberlain concluded that he could not maintain his government and withdrew.

Hampton Takes Undisputed Office

Hampton took effective control of the governorship on April 11, 1877.

For many historians, this date marks the practical end of Reconstruction in South Carolina.

Meaning of 'Redemption'

Hampton's supporters described his victory as the redemption of South Carolina from corrupt Republican rule.

Black Republicans experienced the same transition as the collapse of a biracial political order and the restoration of white Democratic control.

Governor

As governor, Hampton pursued fiscal retrenchment, administrative reorganization, and restoration of Democratic authority.

He simultaneously attempted to maintain his image as a conservative capable of governing without indiscriminate racial violence.

Public Debt

South Carolina's public debt and taxation were major issues during Hampton's governorship. Democrats had campaigned heavily against Reconstruction-era expenditures and alleged corruption.

Fiscal reform became one of the principal administrative priorities of the new government.

Removal of Republican Officials

Hampton demanded the resignation of Republican officials whom Democrats accused of holding office through fraudulent elections.

The struggle over appointments and officeholding helped dismantle Republican institutional power throughout the state.

Charleston Legislative Seats

The Democratic-controlled House refused to seat a number of elected Republican representatives from Charleston and declared their positions vacant.

The episode demonstrated that the transfer of power involved not merely executive control but restructuring of legislative representation.

Ballot Stuffing Admission

University of South Carolina research reports that Hampton later acknowledged Democratic ballot stuffing during a special election involving disputed legislative representation.

This admission is important when evaluating claims that Redemption represented only a clean correction of Republican electoral corruption.

Education Policy

Hampton supported public education and increased attention to state educational institutions.

His administration illustrates the possibility of institutional investment alongside the simultaneous contraction of Black political power.

Reopening South Carolina College

During Hampton's governorship South Carolina College was reopened under restored conservative white control.

The institution, which had been integrated during Reconstruction, was resegregated.

Black Higher Education

Hampton's government also supported a state institution for Black higher education.

This policy reflected his paternalistic approach: he could support separate educational opportunity for African Americans while rejecting interracial political equality and integrated institutions.

African American Appointments

Hampton appointed some African Americans to state positions and emphasized these appointments as evidence that his government would not entirely exclude Black citizens.

University of South Carolina research identifies 117 African American appointments among approximately 1,400 nominations.

Black Democrats in Office

The Black men favored by Hampton were generally Democrats or politically compatible conservatives rather than representatives of the Republican majority among Black voters.

Appointments therefore functioned partly as a strategy for building a Black Democratic constituency.

Election Law Changes

Under Democratic control, South Carolina changed election procedures in ways that reduced independent supervision and weakened Republican voting strength.

Polling arrangements and administrative control became major instruments for consolidating one-party Democratic power.

Reduced Polling Access

Changes in polling locations imposed especially heavy burdens in areas of Republican strength, requiring some voters to travel long distances.

Because Black voters remained concentrated in the Republican electorate, these policies had significant racial consequences.

The 1878 Election Law

Legislation associated with Martin W. Gary and signed by Hampton altered polling arrangements and separated state and federal ballot procedures in ways that weakened federal oversight.

The measure became part of the institutional transition from paramilitary campaigning toward more durable mechanisms of Democratic electoral control.

Election of 1878

Hampton won reelection in 1878. The campaign occurred after Democrats had gained control over much of the machinery of state government.

Political intimidation and electoral irregularities remained serious features of the election.

Harder Racial Rhetoric in 1878

Hampton's rhetoric hardened during the 1878 campaign. He warned African Americans against maintaining a political “color line” through continued Republican voting.

The language revealed the limits of his earlier image as a purely conciliatory racial moderate.

Acknowledgment of Fraud and Terror

Hampton later acknowledged serious fraud and terror in the 1878 election while explaining them as products of Reconstruction-era political conditions.

His explanation did not deny that abuses occurred; it justified or contextualized them as part of a struggle over control of the state.

Hunting Accident

Shortly after his reelection, Hampton suffered a severe hunting accident.

Complications required amputation of one of his legs.

Election to the U.S. Senate

While still governor, Hampton was elected by the South Carolina legislature to the United States Senate.

He left the governorship and began Senate service on March 4, 1879.

United States Senator

Hampton served in the U.S. Senate from 1879 until 1891.

His return to federal office represented a striking restoration of political status for a former Confederate lieutenant general only fourteen years after the Civil War.

Senate Style

Hampton was generally less active in Senate debate than he had been as a Reconstruction-era political symbol.

Age, health, and the changing structure of South Carolina politics reduced his public dominance.

Fiscal Conservatism

Hampton's Senate politics reflected conservative Democratic positions on federal spending, currency, and governmental authority.

He supported the gold standard and generally resisted expansion of federal power.

Opposition to the Federal Elections Bill

Near the end of his Senate career Hampton opposed the Federal Elections Bill, commonly called the Force Bill by opponents.

The legislation sought stronger federal protection and supervision of congressional elections, especially where Black voting rights faced suppression in the South.

Meaning of Force Bill Opposition

Opposition to the bill fit Hampton's long-standing resistance to federal supervision of southern elections.

For Black citizens facing disfranchisement and intimidation, however, reduced federal oversight weakened one of the few institutions capable of challenging state-level suppression.

Rise of Benjamin Tillman

By the late 1880s Hampton's conservative planter leadership faced a populist challenge from Benjamin Tillman and his supporters.

Tillman attacked the old Bourbon elite even while embracing a more openly aggressive white-supremacist politics.

Loss of the Senate Seat

Hampton lost his Senate position amid the rise of the Tillman movement. John L. M. Irby, aligned with Tillman's faction, succeeded him.

The defeat symbolized the decline of the old planter aristocracy's control over South Carolina Democratic politics.

Difference Between Hampton and Tillman

Hampton and Tillman both defended white Democratic political supremacy, but their styles and constituencies differed.

Hampton represented aristocratic paternalism and the prestige of Confederate military leadership; Tillman mobilized white farmers through populist rhetoric and far more openly inflammatory racial politics.

United States Railroad Commissioner

President Grover Cleveland appointed Hampton a United States railroad commissioner. He served from 1893 to 1897.

This became his final significant public office.

Later Financial Problems

Hampton never regained the enormous wealth he had possessed before the Civil War. His final years included continuing financial difficulties.

Supporters eventually helped provide him with housing after another residence burned near the end of his life.

Confederate Veterans

Hampton remained active in Confederate veterans' organizations and commemorative activities.

He became an important living symbol of both Confederate military memory and the overthrow of Reconstruction government.

Lost Cause Memory

The Lost Cause tradition portrayed the Confederacy as honorable, minimized slavery's role in secession, emphasized southern military valor, and often presented Reconstruction as corrupt or illegitimate.

Hampton's reputation became deeply embedded in this memorial culture.

Hampton's Own Lost Cause Role

Hampton was not merely commemorated by later Lost Cause advocates; he actively participated in constructing Confederate memory during his lifetime.

His speeches defended the motives and honor of former Confederates and portrayed federal Reconstruction as an unjust occupation.

The 'Hero of 1876'

For generations of white South Carolina Democrats, Hampton's greatest political achievement was not his Civil War command but the 1876 campaign that returned Democrats to power.

He was celebrated as the “Hero of 1876” and as the man who restored white conservative government.

The 'Rightful Rulers' Language

In later commemorative rhetoric Hampton celebrated the withdrawal of federal troops as the moment when South Carolinians he regarded as the state's rightful rulers resumed political authority.

The language reflected the racial and class assumptions underlying Redemption: political control was treated as a hereditary entitlement of white native conservatives.

Monuments

South Carolina erected numerous memorials, streets, schools, and institutions honoring Hampton.

A major equestrian monument stands on the South Carolina State House grounds in Columbia.

Wade Hampton College

The University of South Carolina named a women's dormitory Wade Hampton College in 1940, decades after Hampton's death.

A university historical commission later recommended removal of the name after reviewing his slaveholding, Confederate service, Reconstruction politics, and Red Shirt associations.

Historical Memory and Reassessment

Modern reassessment of Hampton has shifted attention from battlefield heroism and paternalist political imagery toward slavery, voter suppression, racial violence, and the destruction of Reconstruction democracy.

This does not require ignoring his military skill, public calls for restraint, Black Democratic supporters, or educational policies. It requires placing those facts alongside the coercive political system from which his postwar power emerged.

Was Hampton a Fire-Eater?

Not in the classic sense. Hampton did not spend the antebellum decades demanding immediate secession as Robert Barnwell Rhett and William Lowndes Yancey did.

He was comparatively cautious during the secession crisis but ultimately chose the Confederacy once South Carolina withdrew from the Union.

Was Hampton a Major Slaveholder?

Yes. At his economic peak Hampton controlled nearly one thousand enslaved people across extensive plantation holdings.

He was therefore among the largest individual slaveholders in the country.

Did Hampton Oppose Slavery?

No. His opposition to reopening the African slave trade should not be confused with abolitionism.

He continued to own enslaved people until emancipation destroyed the legal institution and later defended slavery through paternalistic arguments.

Did Hampton Personally Order Red Shirt Violence?

The surviving record does not establish that Hampton personally ordered every violent Red Shirt action, and he publicly condemned political violence.

However, his gubernatorial campaign was materially supported by Red Shirt organizations, he appeared with them in mass political demonstrations, and his victory benefited from intimidation and suppression carried out by Democratic paramilitary forces.

Was Every Black Hampton Voter Coerced?

No. Some African Americans joined Black Democratic clubs and supported Hampton for reasons that included dissatisfaction with Republican leadership, hope for peace, personal relationships, employment concerns, or genuine political preference.

At the same time, coercion and economic pressure were widespread enough that individual Black Democratic votes cannot automatically be treated as evidence of a free and nonviolent campaign environment.

Did Hampton Support Any Black Civil Rights?

Hampton accepted emancipation, eventually accepted Black male voting as a political reality, appointed some African Americans to office, and supported separate Black education.

He nevertheless rejected equal Black political control, defended white racial leadership, and presided over a Democratic system that progressively restricted Republican and Black electoral power.

Was Hampton More Moderate Than Tillman?

Yes in political style and in several policy respects. Hampton used paternalistic and conciliatory language more often, cultivated some Black Democratic support, and lacked Tillman's openly incendiary populist racism.

That relative moderation should not be confused with racial egalitarianism. Both operated within a political order dedicated to white Democratic supremacy.

Comparison with Robert Barnwell Rhett

Rhett spent decades building the ideological and organizational case for secession. Hampton entered the Confederate cause later and was more soldier than secession theorist.

After the war their historical roles diverged further: Rhett faded politically, while Hampton became one of the principal architects of conservative Democratic restoration.

Comparison with William Lowndes Yancey

Yancey used oratory and party organization to promote disunion before 1860. Hampton's antebellum politics were less radical.

Hampton's greater historical importance came during military service and Reconstruction rather than in the ideological origins of secession.

Comparison with James Henry Hammond

James Henry Hammond was Hampton's uncle by marriage and a major proslavery theorist whose personal abuse profoundly harmed Hampton's sisters.

Hammond's importance lay heavily in proslavery ideology, while Hampton's lay in plantation power, military command, and Reconstruction-era political leadership.

Comparison with Benjamin Tillman

Hampton represented the old planter elite and the politics of aristocratic white paternalism. Tillman represented an insurgent white-farmer movement hostile to the old Bourbon establishment.

The shift from Hampton to Tillman marked a change in who led white supremacy in South Carolina, not the end of white-supremacist political control.

Historical Significance

Hampton is historically significant because his life connects three major systems of nineteenth-century southern power: plantation slavery, Confederate military nationalism, and postwar white Democratic Redemption.

For African American history, the final stage is especially consequential. The 1876 movement that elevated Hampton ended the period in which Black South Carolinians exercised their greatest nineteenth-century share of state political power and helped establish the one-party Democratic order that later moved toward systematic disfranchisement and Jim Crow.

A Balanced Assessment

Hampton was personally courageous in battle, developed into a skilled cavalry commander, supported public education, accepted the permanence of emancipation, publicly discouraged some political violence, cultivated African American supporters, and made limited Black appointments after becoming governor.

Those facts coexist with an equally substantial record: he was one of America's largest slaveholders, fought at the highest levels of the Confederacy, expressed explicit beliefs in white racial superiority, opposed Reconstruction's biracial political order, benefited from a campaign of Red Shirt intimidation and violence, signed election measures that weakened Black Republican voting power, and later opposed renewed federal protection of southern elections.

Legacy

For much of the twentieth century Hampton was celebrated in South Carolina as the ideal Confederate soldier and Redeemer statesman. That interpretation reflected the political triumph of the very movement he helped lead.

Modern scholarship presents a more complex figure: neither simply a murderous paramilitary commander nor a benign racial moderate, but an elite slaveholder and accomplished soldier who publicly preferred controlled conservative leadership to chaotic racial terror while nonetheless helping dismantle Reconstruction democracy and restore durable white political supremacy.

  • 1893-1897: Serves as United States railroad commissioner.

Geographic Scope

Charleston and Columbia, South Carolina were central to Hampton's family, political, and commemorative life. Millwood near Columbia became one of his best-known plantation residences.

The Hampton plantation system also extended deeply into Mississippi, where slave-grown cotton formed a major source of family wealth.

His military career took him through Virginia, Maryland, Pennsylvania, North Carolina, and South Carolina. His Reconstruction political career was statewide, with particularly intense conflict in Edgefield, Laurens, Hamburg, Ellenton, Charleston, and Columbia.

Legal and Constitutional Context

Hampton's life crossed several transformations in American law: antebellum protection of slavery, secession and civil war, emancipation, the Thirteenth Amendment, Reconstruction Acts, Black male suffrage, federal Enforcement legislation, the withdrawal of federal military support for Reconstruction governments, and later southern restrictions on elections.

His postwar career demonstrates how white Democratic leaders adapted from defense of slavery to resistance against federal protection of Black political power after slavery itself had become constitutionally impossible to restore.
