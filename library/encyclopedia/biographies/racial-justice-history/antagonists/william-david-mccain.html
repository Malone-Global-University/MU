William David McCain

Mississippi archivist, World War II preservation officer, major general, Southern Miss president, institution builder, Confederate-heritage leader, and segregationist who obstructed Clyde Kennard's enrollment

Overview

William David McCain (1907-1993) was a Mississippi historian, archivist, military officer, preservation specialist, genealogist, Confederate-memory organizer, and university president. He directed the Mississippi Department of Archives and History, served overseas during World War II, participated in the Allied effort to protect and recover European archives and cultural records, rose to major general in the Mississippi National Guard, and transformed Mississippi Southern College into the much larger University of Southern Mississippi during a twenty-year presidency.

McCain was also a committed segregationist who used his institutional authority to resist Black access to higher education. His most consequential racial action involved Clyde Kennard, a Black Army veteran and University of Chicago student who repeatedly sought admission to Mississippi Southern. McCain acknowledged privately that Kennard's academic record was above average yet withheld assistance that would have allowed him to satisfy an admissions requirement, cooperated with the Mississippi State Sovereignty Commission, joined Governor James P. Coleman in pressuring Kennard to withdraw, and later blocked another attempt to register on an administrative technicality.

By 1965 McCain and other university officials recognized that continued exclusion could not be sustained. Southern Miss then admitted Raylawni Branch and Gwendolyn Elaine Armstrong without the violence that had accompanied James Meredith's admission to the University of Mississippi. That peaceful transition was preferable to violent resistance, but it came only after McCain had spent years working to prevent integration. His biography therefore combines major achievements in archives, military preservation, university development, and Mississippi history with a documented role in the state's institutional campaign against Black equality.

Correct Identity

The segregation-era university president relevant to this series was William David McCain, not William J. McCain. He is sometimes listed simply as William D. McCain and was widely called “General McCain” because of his long National Guard career.

He should also not be confused with Arizona senator John McCain or other members of the broader McCain family. William David McCain's career was centered in Mississippi history, archives, military service, Confederate commemoration, and the University of Southern Mississippi.

Birth in Bellefontaine

McCain was born in Bellefontaine, Mississippi, on March 29, 1907. He grew up during the Jim Crow era, when Mississippi law and political practice systematically separated Black and white citizens and sharply restricted Black voting, education, and public participation.

His later historical and educational career developed inside that social order. Unlike many segregationist politicians, however, McCain's first professional identity was scholarly rather than electoral.

Education at Delta State

McCain attended what became Delta State University and developed an early interest in Mississippi history. His education prepared him for work in historical research, teaching, genealogy, and archives rather than conventional law or business.

He later maintained close ties to Mississippi historical scholarship and became one of the state's most prolific institutional advocates for preserving documentary records.

University of Mississippi Graduate Work

McCain continued his education at the University of Mississippi, where he completed graduate work in history. His master's research included a study of the Populist movement in Mississippi, reflecting an early scholarly interest in state political history.

This academic background gave him substantial authority when he later became state archivist and university president. His segregationist politics therefore coexisted with genuine training as a professional historian.

Doctoral Study at Duke University

McCain later completed doctoral work at Duke University. The degree placed him within an emerging generation of professionally trained southern historians and archivists who were helping formalize historical institutions during the twentieth century.

His education also widened his professional network beyond Mississippi and helped establish the scholarly reputation that led to positions in federal and state archival institutions.

Early Teaching

Before his long archival career, McCain taught history at several colleges and universities, including institutions in Mississippi. Teaching reinforced his interest in regional history and connected him to the state's developing higher-education system.

His later presidency at Mississippi Southern therefore represented a return to higher education after nearly two decades centered on archives, preservation, and public history.

Morristown National Historical Park

McCain worked as a historian, genealogist, and archivist at Morristown National Historical Park in New Jersey. The work involved preserving and interpreting historical records connected to one of the major sites of the American Revolutionary era.

This experience helped establish McCain as a professional archivist at a time when archival administration was becoming a distinct field in the United States.

Assistant Archivist at the National Archives

McCain subsequently worked as an assistant archivist at the U.S. National Archives in Washington. The National Archives itself was a relatively new federal institution, and McCain participated in the professionalization of records preservation during its formative period.

Federal archival work exposed him to large-scale administration, cataloguing, preservation, and the relationship between documentary records and public institutions.

Society of American Archivists

McCain was among the early figures associated with the Society of American Archivists and later served as its president. His professional standing in the archival field was substantial and deserves separate recognition from his later racial politics.

He helped represent a generation that transformed archives from repositories maintained largely through tradition into institutions increasingly governed by professional standards and trained specialists.

Director of the Mississippi Department of Archives and History

In 1938 McCain became director of the Mississippi Department of Archives and History. He remained in that position until 1955, apart from wartime military service, and became one of the most influential custodians of Mississippi historical records.

His directorship strengthened his relationships with governors, legislators, historians, genealogists, Confederate-heritage organizations, and other members of Mississippi's political establishment. Those networks later helped him as a university president.

Genealogy and Mississippi History

McCain wrote and compiled genealogical studies of several families and contributed to the documentation of Mississippi history. He became known as an energetic researcher who valued documentary preservation and family history.

His historical interests were broad, but Confederate and post-Civil War history became especially important to his later public identity and work with heritage organizations.

The Story of Jackson

McCain contributed substantially to documenting the history of Jackson and other aspects of Mississippi's past. His historical publications and archival work made him a recognized authority on state and local history.

That scholarly legacy remains useful to researchers even though modern historians approach some of the racial assumptions and Confederate-memory traditions of McCain's generation much more critically.

Joining the Mississippi National Guard

McCain joined the Mississippi National Guard as a young man in 1924. Military service became a second major institutional strand of his life alongside history and archives.

He remained connected to the Guard for decades and eventually rose to the rank of major general, a status that reinforced the commanding style for which he later became known as a university president.

World War II Service

During World War II McCain served with the U.S. Army in the Mediterranean theater. He initially worked with an antiaircraft artillery unit before being reassigned to historical and archival duties as Allied forces advanced through North Africa, Sicily, and Italy.

His combination of military training and professional archival experience made him unusually well qualified for the preservation work that followed.

Military Historian in Italy

In late 1943 McCain was assigned as a military historian documenting the U.S. Fifth Army's operations. Military historians collected records, reports, maps, photographs, and firsthand information so that wartime operations could later be reconstructed accurately.

This role connected his civilian profession directly to the military effort and prepared him for work protecting endangered cultural records.

Monuments, Fine Arts, and Archives Work

In 1944 McCain joined the Allied Monuments, Fine Arts, and Archives program in Italy. He served as a regional archivist in Lombardy and worked with Italian officials and Allied specialists to locate and protect endangered books, manuscripts, government records, and library collections.

The Monuments Men and Women Foundation credits McCain with helping restore damaged libraries and coordinate the return of evacuated Italian government records to Rome. This was an important and constructive part of his wartime service.

Protecting European Cultural Records

War threatened cultural heritage through bombing, theft, emergency evacuation, and administrative collapse. McCain's archival expertise helped safeguard documentary collections whose destruction would have permanently damaged the historical record of Italian institutions.

This work stands among the clearest positive achievements of his life and demonstrates that his professional skills had value far beyond Mississippi.

Return to Mississippi Archives

After returning from Europe in late 1945, McCain resumed leadership of the Mississippi Department of Archives and History. His wartime reputation strengthened an already important career in public history.

For the next decade he continued building archival collections and relationships throughout the state while also becoming increasingly active in Confederate heritage organizations.

Sons of Confederate Veterans

McCain became a major national leader in the Sons of Confederate Veterans during the early 1950s, when the organization had declined greatly from its earlier prominence. Under his leadership the SCV expanded its membership, revived its publication, accumulated financial resources, and strengthened its national organization.

His leadership should be understood in the context of his deep interest in Confederate history and memory, but also in the racial politics of the period. The revival occurred just as southern resistance to civil-rights change was intensifying.

Reviving Confederate Veteran

McCain helped revive Confederate Veteran, the SCV's historic publication. The journal provided an institutional vehicle for Confederate commemoration, genealogy, battlefield history, and interpretations of the Civil War and Reconstruction.

Confederate historical preservation was not automatically identical to segregationist activism, but in McCain's own career the two worlds overlapped strongly. He simultaneously promoted Confederate memory and defended Mississippi's contemporary racial order.

Expanding the SCV

Under McCain's long involvement, the Sons of Confederate Veterans grew dramatically from the weakened organization he encountered in the early 1950s. He helped establish stronger national headquarters, recruitment efforts, publications, and financial stability.

The achievement showed the same administrative strengths he later brought to Southern Miss: fundraising, organization building, institutional loyalty, and sustained attention to infrastructure.

Becoming President of Mississippi Southern College

In 1955 McCain left the state archives to become president of Mississippi Southern College in Hattiesburg. He arrived with substantial political connections, military stature, administrative experience, and a reputation for forceful leadership.

His presidency lasted twenty years and became the most important institutional period of his life. During it, the college grew from a regional teachers' institution into a much larger university.

An Institution Builder

McCain approached the presidency as an aggressive expansion project. The Monuments Men and Women Foundation credits his tenure with increased enrollment, an expanded athletic program, major fundraising, and construction of numerous academic, housing, and athletic facilities.

He cultivated legislators and state officials effectively, making Southern Mississippi a stronger competitor for public resources within Mississippi higher education.

Campus Construction

McCain's administration added numerous buildings and facilities while modernizing an institution that had begun as a teachers' college. His presidency helped create much of the physical campus associated with the later University of Southern Mississippi.

This construction record became central to the university's later commemoration of him and helps explain why his name remained attached to the archival library despite later controversy over his racial record.

Enrollment Growth

Enrollment increased dramatically during McCain's presidency as southern higher education expanded after World War II. Veterans, economic growth, population changes, and growing demand for professional education all contributed to the university's development.

McCain proved adept at using those trends to enlarge the institution's academic and political importance.

Athletics and Campus Identity

McCain also invested heavily in athletics and campus identity. Facilities such as Reed Green Coliseum and improvements to the football stadium reflected his belief that a growing university required visible institutions capable of attracting students and statewide support.

Athletic development helped Southern Miss become a more recognizable regional university rather than remaining primarily a teachers' college.

A Commanding Leadership Style

McCain's military rank shaped how colleagues perceived his administrative style. He was often addressed as “General,” and accounts of his presidency portray him as forceful, centralized, and demanding.

That leadership style could be effective in construction and lobbying, but it also meant that controversial decisions—including decisions about race, student life, and faculty conduct—were closely associated with the president himself rather than a distant bureaucracy.

Brown v. Board and Mississippi Higher Education

McCain became president one year after Brown v. Board of Education. Although Brown addressed public schools, its equal-protection reasoning placed increasing pressure on segregated public colleges and universities as Black applicants challenged exclusion.

Mississippi's white political establishment responded through delay, legal maneuvering, political pressure, the Citizens' Councils, and eventually the State Sovereignty Commission. McCain became part of that institutional resistance.

McCain as a Segregationist

The University of Southern Mississippi's own Center for Black Studies describes McCain as a staunch segregationist and notes his membership in the Citizens' Council. His racial position was therefore not limited to quiet administrative caution about campus unrest.

He defended the continuation of a segregated educational and political system and cooperated with organizations created specifically to prevent civil-rights advances.

The Mississippi State Sovereignty Commission

Mississippi established the State Sovereignty Commission in 1956 after Brown. The Mississippi Department of Archives and History explains that the agency possessed broad investigative powers and was created to resist perceived federal encroachment while preserving racial separation.

The Commission investigated civil-rights activists, used informants, coordinated propaganda, and advised officials on ways to resist integration. McCain worked with this agency during the Clyde Kennard controversy and participated in its public-relations campaign.

Clyde Kennard

Clyde Kennard was a Black Army veteran who had served in Germany and Korea and later attended the University of Chicago. After his stepfather died, Kennard returned to the Hattiesburg area to help his mother operate the family farm and sought to complete his college degree close to home.

Mississippi Southern was only a short drive from the family farm, but it was an all-white public institution. Kennard's repeated effort to enroll became one of the defining civil-rights controversies of McCain's presidency.

Kennard's Academic Qualifications

Mississippi History Now records that McCain privately told the Sovereignty Commission that Kennard's academic record was “above average” and that he had satisfied the college's requirements except for obtaining recommendations from five alumni in his home county.

This evidence is important because it undermines any suggestion that McCain opposed Kennard principally because he lacked academic ability. Race was the central problem confronting the white establishment.

The Five-Alumni Requirement

Kennard's initial application was blocked because he could not produce recommendations from five Mississippi Southern alumni in his county. He asked McCain for a list of local alumni so he could contact people capable of satisfying the requirement.

McCain told Kennard that such a list was unavailable, even while privately acknowledging to state segregation officials that the recommendations were the remaining barrier. The administrative rule therefore became an effective mechanism for maintaining racial exclusion.

Administrative Neutrality Versus Racial Purpose

On paper, an alumni-reference rule did not explicitly mention race. In practice, it placed a Black applicant seeking entry into an all-white institution at the mercy of white alumni during a period of organized massive resistance.

McCain's handling of the rule illustrates how segregation could be preserved through facially neutral administrative procedures instead of a written statement saying that Black students were forbidden.

Kennard's 1958 Public Letter

Kennard continued pressing for admission and in December 1958 wrote publicly to the Hattiesburg American. He argued that people should be judged by their abilities rather than skin color and announced his intention to enter Mississippi Southern.

His public persistence transformed what administrators hoped would remain a quiet application problem into a visible challenge to the state's higher-education system.

Sovereignty Commission Investigation of Kennard

After Kennard renewed his effort, Sovereignty Commission investigators searched for damaging information that might discredit or disqualify him. Mississippi History Now documents inquiries into his finances, personal life, employment, and background.

The investigation demonstrates that state authorities were not simply waiting to see whether Kennard satisfied ordinary admissions rules. They were actively looking for a means to stop a qualified Black applicant from challenging segregation.

McCain and State Officials

McCain communicated with the Sovereignty Commission and state political leaders about Kennard's application. The matter involved not only university administration but the broader white political establishment, which feared that successful enrollment would establish a precedent for other Black applicants.

McCain's role was therefore institutional and coordinated. He was the university official controlling access to the campus while state agencies supplied political pressure and intelligence.

Governor James P. Coleman

Governor James P. Coleman preferred quieter segregationist tactics than his successor Ross Barnett. MDAH describes Coleman's Sovereignty Commission as seeking to manage racial challenges “quietly and effectively” rather than through constant public confrontation.

The Kennard case fit that approach. State officials hoped to persuade him to withdraw voluntarily so Mississippi Southern could remain segregated without the spectacle of a federal court battle.

Pressure to Withdraw

In early 1959 Coleman met personally with Kennard and told him that the time was not appropriate for him to attend Mississippi Southern. Mississippi History Now records that both Coleman and McCain placed pressure on Kennard, who temporarily withdrew his application.

This was a clearer form of racial obstruction than an impersonal admissions technicality. A qualified Black veteran was being asked to defer his education because white officials were unwilling to integrate a public college.

Kennard Reapplies in 1959

Kennard soon reconsidered and informed McCain in August 1959 that he intended to apply again. He followed with a detailed letter to admissions director Aubrey Lucas challenging the standard arguments for segregation and indicating that he was prepared to go to federal court.

His determination confronted McCain with the possibility that quiet pressure would no longer be sufficient to preserve the institution's racial exclusion.

September 15, 1959 Registration Attempt

On September 15, 1959, Kennard attempted to register at Mississippi Southern. McCain blocked the effort on the ground that the university had not received an academic transcript from the University of Chicago, a claim Kennard disputed.

Once again, the immediate mechanism was administrative rather than an explicit racial ban. The broader context, however, was the documented effort by McCain and state officials to prevent Kennard from becoming the first Black student.

Kennard's Arrest After Leaving McCain's Office

After leaving McCain's office that day, Kennard was arrested by local constables on charges involving driving and illegal possession of whiskey. Mississippi historians later concluded that the charges were fabricated or seriously tainted, and Kennard's friends emphasized that he was a devout Baptist who did not drink.

The timing makes the arrest one of the most disturbing episodes in the case, but McCain's direct role in arranging the arrest has not been established. The biography should not turn institutional suspicion into a criminal allegation unsupported by evidence.

No Proven McCain Role in Fabricating the Criminal Charges

McCain bears clear responsibility for opposing Kennard's admission and cooperating with the segregationist state apparatus. That is different from proving that he instructed constables, witnesses, or prosecutors to fabricate criminal evidence.

The surviving record does not justify attributing the false prosecutions directly to him. Maintaining this distinction makes the documented case against his educational discrimination stronger rather than weaker.

Kennard's Final Prosecution

In 1960 Kennard was arrested again after a young employee implicated him in the theft of chicken feed from a cooperative. Kennard was convicted as an accessory to burglary and sentenced to seven years in prison.

Decades later the principal witness recanted, and in 2006 a Mississippi judge declared Kennard innocent. The case is now recognized as a major miscarriage of justice connected to the state's effort to suppress his challenge to segregation.

Kennard at Parchman

Kennard was sent to the Mississippi State Penitentiary at Parchman, where he performed harsh agricultural labor despite declining health. He later developed severe colon cancer and was released only after a campaign for clemency.

He died in 1963 without ever being allowed to attend Mississippi Southern. His treatment became one of the clearest examples of the human cost of Mississippi's resistance to higher-education integration.

McCain's Responsibility in the Kennard Story

McCain's documented responsibility centers on the university side of the case: he opposed integration, handled Kennard's application personally, refused useful assistance with the alumni-reference barrier, communicated with the Sovereignty Commission, participated in pressure for withdrawal, and later used another administrative basis to prevent registration.

Those actions materially contributed to keeping Kennard out of a public institution for which he was academically qualified. They are significant even without attributing the later criminal frame-up personally to McCain.

Public Defense of Segregation

McCain did not restrict his segregationist activity to private university administration. He became a public spokesman for Mississippi's racial position and participated in speaking efforts associated with the State Sovereignty Commission.

His public advocacy demonstrates that resistance to Kennard reflected a broader political conviction rather than a one-time concern about campus management.

The 1960 Chicago Speech

In 1960 McCain travelled to Chicago on a speaking trip sponsored by the Sovereignty Commission and defended Mississippi's segregated social and educational order. Contemporary records and later historical accounts preserve his explicit support for continued separation and white political control.

The speech is particularly revealing because it occurred while McCain was actively working to keep Kennard out of Mississippi Southern. His public ideology and administrative practice therefore aligned closely.

Black Voting and White Political Control

McCain's recorded segregationist speeches extended beyond school enrollment into political power. He acknowledged that Mississippi was not encouraging Black voting and defended continued white control of government.

This places his politics within the wider Jim Crow system rather than a narrow debate over campus admissions. Educational segregation, disfranchisement, and white political authority were mutually reinforcing institutions.

Citizens' Council Connections

The University of Southern Mississippi's Center for Black Studies identifies McCain as a member of the segregationist Citizens' Council. The Councils organized businesspeople, professionals, and public officials to resist integration through political and economic means.

For a university president, Council affiliation linked campus policy to a broader network of organized white resistance operating throughout Mississippi.

Cold War Anti-Communism and Civil Rights

Like many Mississippi segregationists, McCain connected civil-rights activism with Cold War fears about communist subversion. State resistance organizations routinely portrayed integration movements as externally manipulated rather than as demands made by Black Mississippians themselves.

This framing made racial equality easier to treat as a security problem. A Black student seeking admission could be represented as part of a political threat rather than simply an applicant asserting constitutional rights.

Mississippi Southern Becomes a University

McCain was highly successful in raising Mississippi Southern's institutional status. With support from state political leaders, including Governor Ross Barnett, the college became the University of Southern Mississippi in 1962.

The change recognized the institution's expanding academic mission and student body and stands as one of McCain's most important administrative achievements.

Relationship with Ross Barnett

Ross Barnett was one of Mississippi's most confrontational segregationist governors. McCain worked within the same political establishment and benefited from Barnett's support in advancing Mississippi Southern to university status.

Their roles differed: Barnett became the public face of state defiance during the James Meredith crisis, while McCain exercised authority primarily through higher education, historical institutions, and organizational networks.

James Meredith and Ole Miss

James Meredith's successful effort to enter the University of Mississippi in 1962 produced violent resistance, federal intervention, and international embarrassment for the state. The crisis demonstrated that massive resistance could no longer reliably prevent higher-education integration when the federal government enforced court orders.

For McCain and Southern Miss officials, Ole Miss became a warning about what could happen if university integration were handled through open defiance rather than controlled preparation.

The University Could Not Remain Segregated Indefinitely

By the mid-1960s federal court decisions, the Civil Rights Act of 1964, and the experience at Ole Miss made continued exclusion of Black students increasingly untenable. University officials recognized that Southern Miss would eventually have to admit qualified Black applicants.

The strategic question shifted from whether integration could be prevented permanently to whether it could occur without the violence and chaos Mississippi had experienced in Oxford.

Preparing for Integration in 1965

Southern Miss administrators quietly prepared for the admission of Black students in 1965. The university planned security, monitored campus conditions, and sought cooperation from student and institutional leaders so that integration would not produce a public crisis.

This preparation represented a real change in administrative behavior. The same institution that had worked to keep Kennard out now planned how to admit Black students safely.

Raylawni Branch

Raylawni Branch was a twenty-four-year-old Hattiesburg civil-rights activist with experience in organizations including COFO, SNCC, and SCLC. She agreed to enroll at Southern Miss alongside the younger Elaine Armstrong so that Armstrong would not face the campus alone.

Branch later became an important source on the university's integration and eventually returned to Southern Miss for graduate study.

Gwendolyn Elaine Armstrong

Gwendolyn Elaine Armstrong was an eighteen-year-old graduate of Hattiesburg's Black Rowan High School. Along with Branch, she became one of the first two African American students to enroll at the University of Southern Mississippi.

Their enrollment fulfilled the basic right Clyde Kennard had sought years earlier and demonstrated how much the legal and political environment had changed.

September 10, 1965

Branch and Armstrong began attending Southern Miss on September 10, 1965. University archival materials record that security protection accompanied them as administrators worked to prevent harassment or violence.

The admission occurred without the riot that had accompanied Meredith at Ole Miss. That peaceful outcome was important, but it should be understood as a controlled surrender to legal reality rather than evidence that the university had always welcomed integration.

Peaceful Integration

McCain deserves some credit for ensuring that the 1965 admission did not become another violent Mississippi university crisis. Once the decision to integrate had been made, his administration prioritized order and the physical safety of the new students.

That achievement is compatible with acknowledging his earlier obstruction. A person can resist change for years and later administer the unavoidable transition more responsibly than other segregationist officials did.

From Resistance to Managed Compliance

The transition from Kennard to Branch and Armstrong shows a significant shift in McCain's institutional role. During the late 1950s he used administrative and political influence to prevent integration; by 1965 he used administrative power to control and stabilize the integration process.

It was adaptation rather than a documented conversion to racial egalitarianism. The evidence does not show that he repudiated the segregationist beliefs underlying his earlier actions.

Black Students After 1965

Admission of the first two Black students did not instantly create an equal campus. Integration is a longer process than simply permitting enrollment, and Black students continued confronting a university culture shaped by decades of white-only traditions.

Southern Miss nevertheless moved steadily from formal exclusion toward a racially integrated student body during the remainder of McCain's presidency.

Campus Culture and Confederate Identity

McCain's strong Confederate historical interests existed alongside university traditions saturated with “Dixie” imagery and Lost Cause symbolism. Such symbols were common across white southern campuses during the segregation era and often communicated who institutions imagined as their traditional constituency.

Modern Southern Miss debates over names, traditions, and memorials have forced the university to reconsider how that historical culture affected Black students.

Authoritarian Campus Governance

McCain's forceful administrative style extended beyond race. Accounts of his presidency describe centralized control over faculty, students, discipline, and public controversy.

Supporters viewed this as decisive leadership that enabled rapid institutional growth. Critics saw a command structure that left limited room for dissent, especially as the national university culture became more politically active in the 1960s.

Faculty Dissent

As Southern Miss expanded, some faculty members challenged the administration on academic freedom, campus governance, race, and political expression. McCain's centralized management increasingly clashed with the more open intellectual culture emerging on American campuses.

These conflicts formed part of the larger transformation of Southern Miss from a tightly controlled regional college into a modern university with a more diverse faculty and student body.

Campus Policing of Sexuality

McCain's administration also conducted campaigns against students and employees suspected of homosexuality, reflecting both the prejudices of the era and his broader emphasis on institutional discipline. His surviving journals have become important sources for historians studying these purges.

This aspect of his presidency is separate from his opposition to Black integration but reinforces the picture of an administration that closely policed conformity and personal behavior.

1960s Campus Change

By the late 1960s Southern Miss confronted many of the same changes affecting universities nationwide: civil-rights activism, challenges to administrative authority, student political organizations, changing sexual norms, and debates over the Vietnam War.

McCain, whose governing style had been formed in the military and mid-century Mississippi establishment, found these changes increasingly difficult to control through older command methods.

After the Jackson State Killings

The 1970 killings of Black students at Jackson State College intensified Black student activism throughout Mississippi. At Southern Miss, Black students pressed university administrators for greater representation, changes in campus policing, recognition of Black organizations, and a more inclusive faculty and campus culture.

These demands showed that simple admission was no longer enough. By 1970 the question had shifted from whether Black students could attend to whether they would possess an equal place inside university life.

McCain and Black Student Demands

McCain met Black student activism through negotiation as well as administrative control. The university's later racial development during his presidency was therefore more complicated than the complete resistance that had marked the Kennard period.

Even so, the need for students to demand basic representation demonstrated how slowly institutions built under segregation adjusted to genuine inclusion.

Federal Civil-Liberties Challenges

McCain's administration also faced federal court challenges over student political activity and civil liberties. The changing legal culture of the late 1960s and early 1970s placed limits on the broad discretionary authority university presidents had once exercised.

These cases were part of a national transformation in which public universities increasingly had to recognize constitutional protections for students and campus organizations.

Appropriations and Political Skill

One of McCain's strongest abilities was obtaining state support for Southern Miss. He cultivated legislators and governors effectively and competed aggressively with the University of Mississippi and Mississippi State for money, facilities, and institutional status.

His political skill contributed directly to the physical and academic expansion that supporters remember as the defining success of his presidency.

Twenty Years of Institutional Growth

When McCain became president in 1955, Mississippi Southern was substantially smaller and more narrowly focused than the university he left in 1975. During his tenure enrollment expanded, graduate education grew, facilities multiplied, and the institution's statewide role increased.

Any balanced assessment has to acknowledge that the modern university was shaped heavily by his administrative ambition and ability to secure resources.

Retirement in 1975

McCain retired from the presidency in 1975 after twenty years. The university granted him president emeritus status, and the archival library was named in his honor.

He continued research from offices in the library, returning in retirement to the archival and historical interests that had defined the first half of his career.

McCain Library and Archives

The William David McCain Library and Archives became the home of Southern Miss special collections, including major materials on Mississippi history and the civil-rights movement. The irony is significant: an archival building named for a segregationist university president now preserves extensive evidence documenting the movement that challenged the system he defended.

The library also holds materials associated with McCain himself, allowing later generations to study both his achievements and his failures.

President Emeritus and Historical Research

After retirement McCain continued writing, genealogy, Confederate research, and archival work. His identity as a historian remained strong until the end of his life.

He belonged to a generation for whom preservation of southern history often overlapped with reverence for the Confederacy, a tradition that contemporary historians now examine with much greater attention to slavery and Black historical experience.

Later Sons of Confederate Veterans Leadership

McCain remained deeply involved with the Sons of Confederate Veterans after leaving Southern Miss. The organization credited him with increasing membership, strengthening finances, restoring publications, and establishing a more durable national headquarters.

His long SCV career demonstrates that Confederate commemoration was not a passing hobby. It was a major institutional commitment parallel to his university and archival work.

Confederate Memory and Race

Commemoration of Confederate ancestors can take many forms, but McCain's own Confederate advocacy cannot be separated completely from his segregationist politics because the two commitments overlapped chronologically and institutionally during the civil-rights era.

He helped revitalize Confederate heritage organizations at the same time he publicly defended white political control and resisted Black university enrollment.

Death in 1993

William David McCain died on September 5, 1993. By then Southern Miss had become a substantially larger and more diverse university than the segregated Mississippi Southern College he had taken over in 1955.

His death left behind a divided legacy: an institution-builder and respected archivist whose name remained prominent on campus, and a segregationist administrator whose role in the Clyde Kennard case became increasingly difficult for the university to ignore.

Modern Reassessment

As universities across the United States reassessed buildings named for segregationists and defenders of white supremacy, Southern Miss students and faculty began debating McCain's commemoration more directly. The university's Center for Black Studies has publicly documented his segregationist history and central role in excluding Kennard.

The debate reflects a broader distinction between preserving history and conferring honor. McCain's historical importance is unquestioned; whether institutional names should continue to function as honors is a different question.

2018 Renaming Discussion

In 2018 Southern Miss student government discussed removing McCain's name from the university library because of his segregationist record. The proposal drew attention to the contrast between the modern university's diverse student body and the racial policies defended by the man commemorated on one of its major buildings.

State monument and memorialization law complicated the possibility of renaming because McCain was also a significant military figure.

Who McCain Was Historically

McCain was unusual among the figures in this biography series because his greatest public achievements were not primarily electoral or ideological. He was an archivist, preservation specialist, military officer, historian, university builder, and institutional executive.

That professional competence made his segregationist actions more consequential. He possessed precisely the kind of administrative power capable of turning racial preference into admissions policy and coordinated state action.

How He Opposed Black Equality

McCain opposed Black equality most clearly through higher-education administration and public advocacy. He worked to keep Mississippi Southern segregated, cooperated with the State Sovereignty Commission, pressured Clyde Kennard to abandon his application, invoked administrative barriers to prevent enrollment, spoke publicly for segregation, and participated in organizations defending the racial status quo.

His opposition was therefore institutional rather than primarily street-level. He controlled access to a public university and used that authority in coordination with a broader state apparatus committed to white supremacy.

Administrative Barriers as Segregation

The Kennard affair demonstrates how discrimination can operate without a rule stating plainly that an applicant is rejected because he is Black. Alumni recommendations, transcript disputes, delays, and procedural requirements can become tools of exclusion when administrators apply them in a racial political context.

This form of discrimination was often harder to challenge publicly because officials could describe each decision as ordinary administration while privately acknowledging the racial objective.

Institutional Coordination

McCain's actions also show that Jim Crow was not maintained by isolated individuals acting separately. University administrators, governors, investigators, local officials, business leaders, and segregationist organizations communicated and reinforced one another.

Black applicants therefore confronted more than a college admissions office. They confronted an interconnected political system designed to make integration fail before a court could compel it.

The Importance of Distinguishing Direct and Indirect Responsibility

McCain's documented actions are serious enough without exaggeration. The evidence supports direct responsibility for resisting Kennard's admission and cooperating with the state segregation apparatus. It does not establish that McCain personally ordered the false criminal testimony or fabricated the burglary case.

Separating those levels of responsibility preserves historical accuracy and prevents a strong institutional case from becoming dependent on allegations the evidence cannot prove.

World War II Preservation Versus Domestic Segregation

One of the deepest contradictions in McCain's life is the contrast between protecting endangered European cultural institutions during World War II and later denying a qualified Black veteran equal access to a public educational institution in Mississippi.

Historical figures often contain such contradictions. Recognizing McCain's valuable wartime preservation work does not require minimizing Kennard, and recognizing Kennard does not require pretending McCain accomplished nothing of value elsewhere.

Military Service Versus Black Veteran Citizenship

Kennard's own military service sharpens that contradiction. Both men had served the United States, yet McCain operated within a political system in which a white veteran could rise to university president while a Black veteran could be pressured and obstructed when he sought to finish his education.

The Kennard case therefore illustrates the unequal citizenship that Black veterans repeatedly challenged after World War II.

Comparison with Ross Barnett

Ross Barnett used gubernatorial power in dramatic public defiance of James Meredith's federal court orders. McCain's style was quieter and administrative, particularly during the Kennard case.

Both defended segregation, but Barnett embodied confrontation while McCain demonstrated how university procedures and coordinated institutional pressure could achieve similar objectives without an immediate riot.

Comparison with James Eastland

James Eastland defended segregation through Senate power and the Judiciary Committee. McCain operated in a different institutional arena, controlling access to a public university and participating in Mississippi's historical, military, and civic establishment.

Their careers show how white resistance functioned at multiple levels simultaneously—from congressional obstruction to admissions administration.

Comparison with Robert B. Patterson

Robert “Tut” Patterson built the Citizens' Council movement by organizing white business and civic elites. McCain belonged to the same broader segregationist world but possessed direct control over a state educational institution.

Patterson mobilized pressure from outside government; McCain could translate the racial order into university decisions from inside a public institution.

Comparison with William J. Simmons

William J. Simmons became the Citizens' Council's principal administrator, editor, and broadcaster. McCain was a university president and historian rather than a full-time segregationist organizer.

Both nevertheless used professional credibility and institutional authority to make resistance to Black equality appear orderly and respectable rather than simply violent.

Comparison with Laurie Pritchett

Laurie Pritchett used disciplined policing and administrative preparation to obstruct civil-rights protest without creating images of spectacular violence. McCain similarly illustrates a bureaucratic form of racial resistance.

In both cases the methods could appear professional and restrained while still serving institutions committed to denying immediate Black equality.

Comparison with Clyde Kennard

McCain and Kennard represented radically unequal positions within the same educational conflict. McCain was the powerful white administrator with access to governors, state investigators, alumni networks, and institutional discretion; Kennard was a Black veteran trying to use a nearby public college.

The imbalance of power is central to understanding why Kennard's application became a civil-rights issue rather than a routine admissions dispute.

Comparison with James Meredith

Kennard's challenge to Southern Miss preceded James Meredith's successful integration of Ole Miss. Meredith obtained federal court enforcement and survived a violent state confrontation that made higher-education segregation increasingly impossible to sustain.

By the time Branch and Armstrong entered Southern Miss in 1965, McCain had learned from the Meredith crisis that controlled compliance was preferable to spectacular resistance.

Was McCain a Segregationist?

Yes. Southern Miss's own Center for Black Studies identifies him as a staunch segregationist, and his conduct in the Kennard affair, Citizens' Council affiliation, Sovereignty Commission cooperation, and public speeches all support that conclusion.

This characterization does not depend on inference from Confederate interests alone; it rests on documented political and administrative actions during the civil-rights era.

Did McCain Frame Clyde Kennard?

The evidence establishes that the state campaign against Kennard included fabricated criminal charges and that McCain was part of the segregationist political network opposing his admission. It does not establish that McCain personally engineered the false arrests, witness testimony, or burglary prosecution.

The biography therefore says that he obstructed Kennard's education and cooperated with the segregationist state apparatus, while treating direct responsibility for the criminal frame-up as unproven.

Did McCain Know Kennard Was Qualified?

Yes. McCain privately told the Sovereignty Commission that Kennard's grades were above average and that he had met the college's requirements except for the alumni recommendations. That makes the later obstruction especially significant.

The dispute was not fundamentally about whether Kennard could perform college work; it was about whether Mississippi Southern would admit a Black student.

Did McCain Later Integrate Southern Miss Peacefully?

Yes. Under McCain's presidency, Raylawni Branch and Gwendolyn Elaine Armstrong entered Southern Miss in September 1965 without a violent campus crisis. The administration planned carefully for their safety and for campus stability.

This is a genuine positive aspect of his later record, but it followed years of active opposition and should be understood as managed compliance after the legal battle for complete exclusion had been lost.

Did Peaceful Integration Erase the Kennard Record?

No. Responsible administration in 1965 does not retroactively remove McCain's role in denying Kennard access during the 1950s. The two episodes should be read together because they show institutional change over time.

McCain moved from active resistance to controlled implementation, not from a documented early commitment to integration to consistent support for Black equality.

Was McCain Only a Racist University President?

No. Reducing him to a single dimension would obscure major parts of his life, including professional archival leadership, military preservation work, historical scholarship, National Guard service, fundraising, construction, and the transformation of Southern Miss.

Balance, however, does not mean treating racial exclusion as a minor flaw. The Kennard affair was a major exercise of presidential authority with life-changing consequences for a Black citizen.

Historical Significance

William David McCain helped shape several important twentieth-century institutions. He was a significant early American archivist, contributed to preservation of European cultural records during World War II, strengthened Mississippi historical collections, revived a major Confederate heritage organization, and transformed Mississippi Southern into the University of Southern Mississippi.

He is equally significant as an example of institutional segregation. McCain shows how educated and professionally accomplished administrators—not only demagogues or violent extremists—could use ordinary bureaucracy, political relationships, and public authority to block Black constitutional rights.

Impact on African Americans

McCain's most direct documented harm to African Americans was his role in keeping Clyde Kennard out of Mississippi Southern. The university's resistance denied a qualified Black veteran access to nearby public higher education and helped place him in confrontation with a much larger segregationist state apparatus.

More broadly, McCain publicly defended a segregated society and white political control at a time when Black Mississippians were challenging exclusion from education and voting. His later peaceful administration of integration reduced the likelihood of violence in 1965 but did not undo the years of exclusion that came before it.

A Balanced Assessment

McCain was capable, energetic, disciplined, and highly effective at institution building. His archival work, World War II cultural-preservation service, university expansion, fundraising, historical research, and military career were substantial accomplishments. Southern Miss became a much larger and more important university under his leadership.

The same administrative effectiveness was used in defense of segregation. McCain knew Clyde Kennard was academically capable, yet cooperated with state officials to prevent his admission and publicly supported racial separation and white political dominance. His later controlled integration of the university matters, but it cannot be treated as a repudiation of the earlier record without evidence that he actually renounced those beliefs.

Legacy

McCain's legacy remains unusually visible because the university archival library bears his name while also preserving collections documenting the civil-rights struggle and Clyde Kennard. The building itself has become a site where institutional memory, historical preservation, and debates over public honor intersect.

His biography is most useful when neither side is erased. McCain helped preserve history and build a university; he also used that university to exclude a Black veteran because Mississippi's white establishment was determined to maintain segregation. Both realities belong to the same life.

  • 1935: Works at Morristown National Historical Park.
  • Mid-1930s: Works as assistant archivist at the U.S. National Archives.
  • 1938: Becomes director of the Mississippi Department of Archives and History.
  • World War II: Serves in the U.S. Army in the Mediterranean theater.
  • 1943: Reassigned as military historian with the U.S. Fifth Army.
  • 1944: Begins Monuments, Fine Arts, and Archives work in northern Italy.
  • 1945: Returns to Mississippi and resumes state archival leadership.
  • Early 1950s: Becomes a major leader in the revival of the Sons of Confederate Veterans.
  • 1955: Becomes president of Mississippi Southern College.
  • 1955: Clyde Kennard first seeks admission to Mississippi Southern.
  • 1956: Mississippi creates the State Sovereignty Commission.
  • 1958: Kennard publicly renews his campaign to attend Mississippi Southern.
  • Early 1959: McCain and Governor James P. Coleman pressure Kennard to withdraw his application.
  • September 15, 1959: Kennard attempts to register; McCain rejects registration on a transcript issue. Kennard is arrested later that day on false or tainted local charges.
  • 1960: Kennard is prosecuted in a separate burglary case and sentenced to seven years.
  • 1960: McCain participates in a Sovereignty Commission-sponsored speaking trip defending Mississippi segregation.
  • 1962: Mississippi Southern becomes the University of Southern Mississippi.
  • 1962: James Meredith integrates the University of Mississippi amid violent resistance.
  • 1963: Clyde Kennard dies of cancer after release from prison.
  • 1964: Civil Rights Act increases federal authority against segregated public institutions.
  • September 10, 1965: Raylawni Branch and Gwendolyn Elaine Armstrong become the first Black students at Southern Miss.
  • Late 1960s: Southern Miss continues desegregating while campus conflicts over governance and social change intensify.
  • 1970: Black students press the administration for greater inclusion after the Jackson State killings.
  • 1975: McCain retires as university president after twenty years.
  • 1975: University archival library is named for McCain.
  • 1975-1993: Continues historical, genealogical, Confederate, and archival work as president emeritus.
  • September 5, 1993: Dies in Mississippi.
  • 2006: A Mississippi circuit judge formally declares Clyde Kennard innocent of the criminal charge that sent him to prison.
  • 2018: Southern Miss student government discusses removing McCain's name from the library because of his segregationist record.

Geographic Scope

Bellefontaine and Mississippi formed McCain's cultural and historical base, while Jackson became the center of his archival career and state political relationships. Hattiesburg became the central location of his university presidency and civil-rights controversy.

His professional career also extended far beyond Mississippi. He worked in Washington, D.C., at Morristown, New Jersey, and throughout Italy during World War II, where his archival work became part of the Allied cultural-preservation effort.

Legal and Constitutional Context

McCain's career intersected with Brown v. Board of Education, the Fourteenth Amendment, public-university desegregation, state sovereignty doctrine, the Civil Rights Act of 1964, student civil liberties, and the constitutional limits imposed on state institutions receiving public funds.

The Kennard case is especially important because it shows how constitutional exclusion could continue after formal legal doctrine began changing. Administrative discretion and coordinated state pressure could delay equal access even without an explicit statute saying that a Black applicant must be rejected.
