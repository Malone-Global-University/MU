Philip “Rusty” Rastelli (1918–1991)

Bonanno crime family boss, federal racketeering convict, and leading figure in the family’s turbulent 1970s–1980s succession, internal conflicts, and labor-racketeering prosecutions.

This article gives priority to adjudicated outcomes and distinguishes claims, testimony, and historical interpretation. [1] [2] [4] [6] [7]

1. Historical Overview

Philip “Rusty” Rastelli occupied the center of the Bonanno crime family’s most unstable postwar leadership period. His career traversed a commercial lunch-wagon extortion racket, lengthy incarceration, a struggle with Carmine Galante, the 1981 violence inside the family, an undercover FBI penetration, and the labor-racketeering prosecutions of the mid-1980s. Contemporary officials called him the family’s boss, but the historical challenge is to distinguish that title from practical command over crews, money and decisions during his extended imprisonment. [1] [3] [4] [6] [10]

Rastelli’s biography is unusually well anchored in court records. The Second Circuit affirmed his 1976 antitrust and Hobbs Act convictions; a later federal judge reviewed parole proceedings in detail; a 1986 district-court opinion described the Local 814 case; and the Commission-trial appeal discussed the factional conflict surrounding Galante’s killing. These records do not produce a complete account of every private Mafia decision, but they give historians a sound basis for identifying what a jury found, what authorities alleged and what remained unresolved. [1] [2] [3] [4]

2. Name, Birth, and Identification

Rastelli is generally identified as Philip Rastelli, nicknamed “Rusty.” A profile submitted for the 1983 Senate organized-crime hearings lists his date of birth as 31 January 1918. Contemporary obituaries report that he died on 24 June 1991 at age seventy-three, which is consistent with that date. The best-grounded description of his death is that liver cancer brought about his removal from a federal prison hospital and death in a New York hospital. [6] [10] [11]

Details that appear in popular biographical summaries—precise birthplace, early childhood addresses, schooling or an exact date of Mafia induction—require more scrutiny than the available court opinions support. A federal criminal case may mention family ties, addresses or associations without establishing a complete civil biography. Consequently this page does not present unverified family-origin stories as settled historical facts. A subsequent edition could test them against birth, census, marriage and death records. [2] [6] [10]

3. The Bonanno Family After Joseph Bonanno

The organization that Rastelli eventually led emerged from decades of upheaval. Joseph Bonanno had commanded the family built from the old Maranzano organization, but his confrontation with fellow leaders and the internal fighting of the 1960s fragmented its authority. Rival claimants, shifting factions and the rise and fall of figures including Gaspar DiGregorio, Paul Sciacca and Natale Evola made leadership more complicated than a clean succession list would suggest. [4] [6]

The distinction between family membership and recognized leadership matters throughout Rastelli’s career. Other Mafia families could attempt to settle a dispute or withhold recognition; a person with recognition could lack operational reach; and imprisonment could leave subordinates to conduct business without the boss appearing in public. This environment made Rastelli’s eventual promotion possible but also created the pressures that complicated his tenure. [4] [6] [15]

4. Early Criminal Affiliations and His Rise

The 1983 Senate record portrays Rastelli as a Bonanno caporegime in 1971 and describes grand-jury resistance and a 1972 loansharking conviction. Because this summary was compiled as investigative material, its claims about early conduct should be attributed to that document rather than automatically treated as individually examined court judgments. By the early 1970s, however, it is clear that government investigators considered him a senior figure rather than a marginal associate. [6]

These years also show the difference between reputation and documents. Mafia leadership stories often attribute promotions to clandestine meetings and private approvals that left little public paper trail. The source-supported conclusion is narrower: Rastelli was recognized as an important Bonanno leader in the early 1970s; later sources offer different timelines for when he became acting, recognized or effective boss. That distinction remains important even when a simplified index assigns his leadership to 1973 or 1974. [4] [6] [10]

5. Connie Rastelli and the Limits of Personal-Life Reporting

Rastelli’s marriage entered public coverage through sensational accounts of conflict with his wife, Connie. Obituaries published after his death describe her threatening to communicate information about him to federal investigators, an episode in which she allegedly shot and wounded him, and her fatal shooting in 1962. These are statements reported by journalists relying on sources available at the time, not a substitute for a certified homicide case file or a trial verdict on the identity of her killer. [10] [11]

A responsible biography records Connie as a person whose death matters in its own right, rather than using it merely to dramatize Rastelli’s personality. The available obituary accounts are sufficient to explain why the marriage appeared in later profiles; they do not establish who ordered her death, whether a particular allegation about narcotics was true, or the exact sequence of each confrontation. Those questions require primary police, court and contemporaneous documentary corroboration. [10] [11]

6. Commercial Racketeering Before the 1980s

Rastelli’s criminal career cannot be reduced to Mafia titles. His most clearly adjudicated 1970s enterprise involved an ordinary part of New York’s working economy: mobile lunch vendors serving factory and industrial workers. Rather than controlling one spectacular commodity, the defendants used a vendor association to impose payments and exclude competition from profitable locations. Small businesses bore the costs and lost freedom to compete for customers. [1]

The records describe a recurrent technique of organized racketeering: create or capture an institution that appears to serve legitimate business interests, then turn access to its relationships or customers into a source of involuntary revenue. An extorted supplier might continue to conduct everyday transactions with the same people responsible for the coercion. The court emphasized that apparently cordial dealing did not disprove fear of economic loss. [1]

7. The Workman’s Mobile Lunch Association

The Second Circuit described the Workman’s Mobile Lunch Association, formed in 1966 under Carl Gary Petrole, as the central structure of the racket. Members paid dues, and the group ostensibly discussed services such as insurance and purchasing discounts. According to the court’s account of the evidence, promised benefits largely failed to materialize while payments were extracted from suppliers and competitive routes were policed through threats or pressure. [1]

A crucial detail is that Rastelli held no formal office in the association. The government nevertheless proved that he controlled it indirectly and participated in the proceeds. His nephew Louis Rastelli succeeded Petrole as association president. That combination—an apparent legitimate trade body, subordinate officeholders and hidden criminal control—makes this litigation an important case study in the difference between registered authority and effective domination. [1]

8. The 1976 Lunch-Truck Convictions

On 23 April 1976 a federal jury convicted Rastelli and two codefendants of a Sherman Act restraint-of-trade count, a Hobbs Act extortion conspiracy, and two substantive Hobbs Act counts. The Second Circuit affirmed the judgments on 18 March 1977. Because these outcomes were adjudicated and affirmed, they support a stronger factual statement than the many untested accusations attached to Rastelli’s public reputation. [1]

The appellate opinion explained that the enterprise used suppliers’ dependence on association members to demand kickbacks and that threats could drive unprotected operators away from valuable stops. The economic harm is central: legitimate businesses, their workers and consumers operated under distorted competition. The case makes clear why labor and commercial racketeering were strategically important to organized crime even when an individual extortion payment attracted little press coverage. [1]

9. A Ten-Year Federal Sentence and Extended Incarceration

A 1985 federal habeas opinion gives a precise sentencing history: on 27 August 1976, Rastelli received ten years in federal prison, consecutive to a four-year state criminal sentence. He left state confinement on 15 June 1977 and began serving the federal term. That sequence is clearer than popular biographies that collapse the conviction, sentencing, and start of federal confinement into a single unspecified year. [2]

His imprisonment proved decisive for Bonanno governance. A prison sentence could preserve a reputation and access to loyal intermediaries while sharply limiting direct meetings, communications and disciplinary control. Historical accounts therefore need to distinguish Rastelli’s nominal position from the decisions subordinates made while he was absent. His imprisonment is not proof that he personally approved every crime attributed to members of the family during those years. [2] [4] [6]

10. The Disputed Date of His Accession

The commonly stated dates for Rastelli’s accession are not uniform across the source record. A Senate hearing compilation published in 1983 says that he served as acting boss in 1974 while Carmine Galante was imprisoned, and it also describes his elevation following Galante’s 1979 death. The Commission-trial appellate record discusses an earlier position for Rastelli and a later challenge by Galante. These are different formulations of an evolving struggle rather than proof that one date can capture every aspect of authority. [4] [6]

For MGU’s leadership index, a cautious formulation is that Rastelli was a major Bonanno boss-level claimant and recognized leader during the mid-1970s, while Galante exercised or asserted competing authority after his return from prison. Rastelli’s position was consolidated in the wake of the 1979 murder. The record supports authority as a historical process with competing centers, not a ceremonial baton passed instantly on a universally agreed date. [4] [5] [6]

11. Carmine Galante’s Return and Rival Authority

Carmine Galante emerged from federal imprisonment in 1974, reactivated old relationships and increasingly challenged the balance of power within the Bonanno organization. Sources characterize him as a dominant figure or rival boss, while others portray Rastelli as the recognized incumbent. The Second Circuit’s later review of the Commission prosecution explicitly treats the family as riven by the contest between these men. [4] [5]

The conflict involved more than personal dislike. Rival leaders competed for loyalty, revenue, respect from other families and access to criminal markets. Continued instability invited intervention by the Mafia Commission. An encyclopedic biography should avoid deciding the winner retrospectively on the basis of a single timeline: Galante’s reach was real, Rastelli’s claim was real, and the relationship changed abruptly when Galante was killed. [4] [6]

12. The Galante Assassination of 12 July 1979

On 12 July 1979, gunmen killed Carmine Galante, Leonard Coppola and Giuseppe Turano at Joe and Mary’s restaurant in Brooklyn. Galante’s death became a major turning point for Rastelli’s position within the family. The later appellate record of the Mafia Commission trial described evidence that a multi-family decision had facilitated the killing and that the Commission subsequently reorganized Bonanno authority under Rastelli. [4]

The limits of that conclusion are as important as the conclusion itself. The relevant appeal examined whether the trial evidence supported Commission enterprise convictions; it was not a murder trial of Rastelli as defendant for personally shooting, directing or ordering the deaths. It permits historians to explain the interests served by the assassination and the later restoration of authority, but it should not be rewritten as a personal homicide conviction that Rastelli never received in that case. [4] [12]

13. Commission Recognition and the Cost of Outside Control

The Commission acted as a governing forum among New York’s Mafia families. In the 1989 appellate decision, testimony and other evidence indicated that Bonanno instability had subjected the family to particularly close Commission supervision and that the killing of Galante led to renewed recognition of Rastelli and a measure of restored internal autonomy. Such evidence helps explain why one family’s leadership conflict mattered to the wider criminal organization. [4]

Outside arbitration came with a structural cost. When the Bonanno family could not settle its own succession, other families gained leverage over its internal politics. Rastelli’s nominal success therefore did not solve the fundamental problem of weak cohesion. Fragmented crews, incarceration and further violence continued to limit the institution’s ability to operate as a unified enterprise. [4] [15]

14. Running a Family Through Intermediaries

During prolonged imprisonment, Rastelli’s authority depended upon subordinates who could meet openly, collect proceeds, relay requests and represent the family. Later court and government documents identify figures including Nicholas Marangello, Joseph Massino and other senior members in the Bonanno leadership structure. Their documented roles do not imply that every action they took must be attributed automatically to a direction from prison. [3] [7] [8]

The difference between a formal boss and practical management is particularly visible in the 1983 Senate material, which named Salvatore Ferrugia as acting boss while Rastelli was incarcerated. Other accounts emphasize Massino in later years. Rather than a single seamless acting-boss list, the historical record suggests overlapping temporal and functional descriptions that must be tied to a date and source. [6] [7] [8]

15. The Three Captains Killings of 1981

In May 1981, Alphonse “Sonny Red” Indelicato, Philip “Philly Lucky” Giaccone and Dominick “Big Trin” Trinchera were killed in the course of the Bonanno family’s factional conflict. Later federal cases and historical reporting connected the murders with the struggle between the Rastelli-aligned leadership and rival captains. The killings were consequential not only as crimes against three individuals but as a demonstration that formal succession had not ended lethal internal disputes. [4] [15]

Massino and other participants were subjects of later prosecution and testimony, but a biographer must distinguish evidence against them from a separate judicial finding about Rastelli’s own role. Later witnesses and reporters described a pro-Rastelli faction and the killings’ political effect; that does not establish a particular unrecorded command by Rastelli to murder each captain. For a fuller attribution study, the underlying Massino trial transcripts and judgments would be needed. [15]

16. The Donnie Brasco Penetration

FBI agent Joseph D. Pistone operated undercover as “Donnie Brasco” beginning in 1976. He built relationships with men in and around the Bonanno family, notably Benjamin Ruggiero and later Dominick Napolitano. In a 1984 appellate decision reviewing convictions of other Bonanno defendants, the court described Pistone’s assignment, the crew structures he learned and the succession from Galante to Rastelli as understood within the organization. [5] [9]

The FBI later reported that the long-running infiltration generated evidence leading to more than one hundred federal convictions. That outcome is a measure of the operation as a whole, not a claim that all of those convictions were of Rastelli’s direct subordinates or rested on his own criminal acts. His tenure is inseparable from the security failure revealed by an operative gaining extensive access to family members over several years. [5] [9]

17. Aftermath of Exposure and Violence

Pistone’s identity was revealed to the underworld in 1981. The FBI’s historical account records the shock of the Bonanno crew that had considered inducting him. A federal appeals opinion also notes that police subsequently confirmed the murder of Dominick Napolitano, one of the Bonanno figures tied to Pistone’s infiltration. The exposed relationship helped transform an investigative success into a crisis of organizational trust. [5] [9]

Later narratives sometimes use the operation as a comprehensive explanation for the Bonanno family’s loss of prestige and its exclusion from Commission deliberations. Such claims need a date-specific source because Commission standing had already been affected by earlier instability. It is more defensible to say that Donnie Brasco deepened an existing legitimacy and security crisis than to identify it as the sole origin of all of the family’s problems. [4] [5] [9]

18. Mandatory Release in April 1983

On 21 April 1983 Rastelli was mandatorily released from federal confinement after receiving the statutory good-time credit applicable to his sentence. The 1985 habeas opinion states that the conditions of the release required supervision through December 1986. The term “mandatory release” is a technical legal category; it did not mean that the underlying antitrust and extortion convictions had been vacated. [2]

After release, federal and state law-enforcement officers monitored him. Their surveillance and later parole proceedings became a revealing test of the difference between association, criminal intelligence and proof of a release-condition violation. That episode is historically significant because it shows how law-enforcement assumptions about organized-crime membership interacted with procedural protections in a judicially reviewable setting. [2]

19. The August 1984 Arrest and Parole Allegations

Rastelli was arrested on a parole warrant on 16 August 1984. The Parole Commission initially alleged that he had associated with people holding criminal records and with people said to be involved in criminal activity. A first proceeding produced administrative findings and an extended recommitment decision. Rastelli challenged that decision through habeas proceedings rather than simply accepting the agency’s characterization of his conduct. [2]

The court record is an unusually detailed reminder that a suspected organized-crime figure does not forfeit procedural rights. Officials may scrutinize associations; nevertheless, the legal question was what release condition existed, what conduct actually occurred, and what evidence the Commission could fairly rely upon. The case should not be shortened to “convicted of new racketeering in 1984,” because those parole allegations were not themselves a new federal trial conviction. [2]

20. The 1985 Parole Due-Process Rulings

In November 1985, Judge Edward Weinfeld reviewed the renewed parole-revocation proceeding. The court criticized the evidentiary basis for the Commission’s findings, including reliance on an unintelligible tape recording and inadequate proof that certain individuals were engaged in criminal activity on the dates of contact. He found the action lacked a rational foundation and failed to meet due-process requirements. This is a distinct decision from the criminal cases against Rastelli. [2]

An important lesson lies in the standard of evidence: the government’s belief that an individual belonged to an organized-crime group was not necessarily proof of an actual criminal act at a particular meeting. Historians must apply an analogous discipline. The allegation that a person associated with Mafiosi can be documented; the conclusion that he committed a specified crime on that occasion requires additional evidence. [2]

21. The 1985 Mafia Commission Indictment

Federal prosecutors named Rastelli among reputed leaders in the broad 1985 case against the New York Mafia Commission. The indictment concerned the Commission as a cross-family enterprise and included allegations surrounding construction extortion and murders. Because Rastelli was also facing the major Bonanno-linked commercial moving prosecution, his Commission case was severed rather than culminating in the same jury verdict that convicted the remaining defendants. [4] [12]

The distinction corrects a common mistake: being named in the 1985 Commission indictment is not equivalent to being convicted in the famous November 1986 Commission verdict. Federal reporting specifies that Rastelli had been removed from the joint trial and, separately, convicted in October 1986 in the Local 814 case. The two prosecutions must remain separate in a scholarly chronology. [12] [14]

22. The Local 814 Enterprise: Union, Employers and Mafia

Teamsters Local 814 represented workers in New York’s commercial moving and storage industry. A 1986 district-court opinion described federal allegations that Bonanno family figures, local union officials and moving companies constituted an association-in-fact enterprise. Prosecutors alleged they secured payments in exchange for labor peace, favored employers, manipulated bidding and exploited employee benefit funds. [3] [7]

This scheme was more institutional than the earlier lunch-truck association: it involved a large union, employer relationships, government moving contracts and pension and welfare arrangements. The workers whose representation was compromised and employers excluded from fair competition were the principal casualties of the alleged and later prosecuted corruption. The criminal case illustrates the broader economic mechanisms by which organized crime sought durable revenue from legitimate commercial infrastructure. [3] [7] [8]

23. The 1985 Local 814 Indictment

A grand jury returned the Local 814 indictment on 10 June 1985. The charging document contained sixty-four counts against seventeen defendants, according to the district court’s February 1986 opinion. Named defendants included Philip Rastelli, Joseph Massino, Nicholas Marangello and other individuals alleged to occupy positions in the Bonanno family, Local 814 or related companies. [3] [14]

A charging document states the government’s claims; it does not, standing alone, establish that each defendant committed every act listed. The later trial and sentencing history are therefore treated separately. The indictment is still highly valuable to historians because it identifies alleged enterprise relationships, types of transactions, a charged period reaching back to 1964, and the government’s theory of how criminal influence moved between union officials and business owners. [3] [14]

24. 1986 Conviction and 1987 Twelve-Year Sentence

Contemporary reporting places Rastelli’s Local 814 conviction in October 1986, after a long trial. The 1989 House oversight record describes the successful prosecution of Bonanno leaders and other defendants for RICO and related crimes concerning the moving-and-storage industry. A government conviction table lists Rastelli’s RICO sentence as twelve years in 1987. This conviction is different from his earlier lunch-truck case and from the Commission trial. [7] [12]

The case marked a major blow against the family’s exploitation of organized labor. Federal prosecutors traced racketeering conduct through company contracts, union power and employee-benefit arrangements rather than relying exclusively on testimony about Mafia ritual or rank. For Rastelli personally, the sentence ensured that he would spend his final years largely outside ordinary public life and further weakened his practical leadership. [3] [7] [10]

25. The 1987–1988 Civil RICO Campaign

The Justice Department filed a civil RICO case against the Bonanno organized-crime family, Rastelli and others in August 1987. The DOJ’s later Civil RICO manual lists amended pleadings in April and October 1988 and describes claims directed at the family’s alleged domination of Local 814, its officials and benefit funds. The complaint sought remedies ranging from restrictions on participation in union affairs to control of potentially tainted business interests. [8] [13]

Civil RICO litigation serves a different purpose from criminal sentencing. Rather than incarcerating a defendant for a past offense alone, it can seek institutional changes that prevent further exploitation. The government’s description of Rastelli as a Bonanno boss in such pleadings is important evidence of its position; the complaint’s wider allegations must not be reported as if every pleaded fact were individually adjudicated in the civil action. [8] [13]

26. Relatives and Commercial Connections

Family relationships appear in the formal records without needing sensational embellishment. The 1977 appellate opinion identifies Louis Rastelli as Philip’s nephew and as an officer of the mobile lunch-wagon association. The 1986 Local 814 indictment named Carmine Rastelli alongside people alleged to be involved in union and commercial moving affairs; the 1991 obituary identified Carmine as Philip’s brother. [1] [3] [10]

Those connections are historically relevant because family businesses and kinship networks could become channels of access or trust within criminal enterprises. Yet the presence of a shared surname must never replace proof about an individual’s own conduct. The people and legal proceedings are named separately here; no unverified guilt is imputed to an entire family or to all workers and businesses associated with the same institutions. [1] [3] [10]

27. Joseph Massino and the Future Leadership

Joseph Massino emerged as a central Bonanno figure in the 1980s and was named with Rastelli in the 1985 Local 814 indictment. A 1989 congressional account listed Massino among the Bonanno leaders convicted in that prosecution. Histories of the family describe him as a Rastelli-aligned figure whose influence grew as older factions were displaced and whose later career reshaped the organization. [3] [7] [15]

Massino eventually became Rastelli’s successor, but the transition should not be flattened into a single unchallenged change of office. He exercised significant authority before Rastelli died and faced his own years of imprisonment; the death in June 1991 ended Rastelli’s tenure, while Massino’s subsequent rise developed within a still damaged organization. A separate Massino biography should provide the detailed record of his trials, later cooperation and leadership. [7] [15]

28. Illness, Release and Death in June 1991

By early summer 1991, Rastelli was gravely ill with liver cancer. Contemporary Associated Press reporting states that he had been released from a federal medical facility in Springfield, Missouri, where he was serving a twelve-year sentence, and transferred to a New York hospital. He died on 24 June 1991, aged seventy-three. He therefore did not complete the final years of his sentence in ordinary release or return to sustained active command. [10] [11]

The obituaries described a long prison history and deteriorating family coherence. Those judgments reflect the perceptions of officials and journalists writing at the time, but the broader record supports the structural interpretation: repeated convictions and incarceration deprived Rastelli of immediate control while rival leadership centers, killings and undercover investigations strained the family’s operating capacity. [2] [7] [10]

29. Public Memory and the Problems of the “Boss” Label

Rastelli rarely receives the kind of sensational attention attached to figures such as Galante or John Gotti. His significance is instead partly administrative: prosecutors, courts and police repeatedly identified him as a key link between criminal leadership and otherwise ordinary trade and union institutions. The story of the mobile lunch-wagon association and Local 814 explains how organized-crime power could persist without constant public displays of violence. [1] [3] [7]

The word “boss” needs a date and source attached to it. A Senate intelligence chart, an undercover informant’s description, a defendant’s statement and an appellate court’s historical summary may assign titles differently. Some discrepancies are real; others result from changing alliances. This biography therefore describes Rastelli as a recognized Bonanno boss and significant leadership claimant without claiming that every colleague accepted his orders throughout 1974–1991. [4] [5] [6]

30. Historical Significance

Rastelli’s career illuminates three processes: criminal capture of commercial associations, recurrent attempts to preserve organizational command during imprisonment, and the growing federal capacity to prosecute racketeering as an interconnected enterprise. The first appears in the 1976 lunch-truck judgment, the second in the Galante dispute and later acting-leadership arrangements, and the third in the Local 814 and civil RICO proceedings. [1] [3] [4] [7] [8]

His record also shows that Mafia history is not solely a procession of individual strongmen. Workers, small vendors, business competitors, murder victims, undercover agents, prosecutors and courts all belong to the story. Institution-level exploitation and the collapse of trust within criminal organizations had measurable human and economic consequences. The surviving record warrants a sober evaluation rather than either romanticized notoriety or an uncritical compilation of law-enforcement allegations. [1] [5] [7] [9]

31. Outstanding Research Questions and Source Criticism

A fuller critical edition should compare Rastelli’s birth and death certificates, early arrest dockets, the entire 1972 loansharking case record, certified 1976 and 1987 sentencing documents, and the Local 814 trial transcript. Those sources could clarify early career dates, the separate 1972 criminal case, the exact count-by-count 1986 verdict, and disputed promotions. The 1983 Senate crime profile is useful but remains an intelligence compilation rather than an all-purpose factual certificate. [1] [3] [6] [7]

The Galante dispute and 1981 killings require separate careful treatment of trial testimony, later cooperating-witness statements and the legal role of each alleged participant. The available Commission appeal explains what evidence supported its enterprise conclusions; it is not an individual criminal judgment against Rastelli for the murders. The resulting biography is a sourced first-edition synthesis, not an exhaustive review of unpublished FBI files, every witness deposition, or every original transcript. [4] [15]

Sources and Further Reading

Judicial decisions take priority for convictions, sentence dates and procedural rulings; government hearing records establish what authorities testified or reported; journalism gives contemporaneous context rather than unreviewable proof of contested acts. Source numbers correspond to the references in the article.

  1. United States v. Rastelli, 551 F.2d 902 (2d Cir. 1977), March 18, 1977. Primary appellate ruling affirming antitrust and Hobbs Act convictions arising from the mobile lunch-truck protection business.
  2. Rastelli v. Warden, Metropolitan Correctional Center, 622 F. Supp. 1387 (S.D.N.Y. 1985), November 23, 1985. Primary judicial decision documenting the 1976 prison sentence, 1983 release, 1984 parole arrest, and due-process defects in parole proceedings.
  3. United States v. Rastelli, 653 F. Supp. 1034 (E.D.N.Y. 1986), February 27, 1986. Primary district-court opinion detailing the 1985, 64-count Local 814 indictment and the alleged moving/storage enterprise; an indictment is not a conviction.
  4. United States v. Salerno, 868 F.2d 524 (2d Cir. 1989), January 31, 1989. Primary appellate examination of the Bonanno leadership dispute, 1979 Galante murders, and evidence relating to Commission involvement.
  5. United States v. Ruggiero, 726 F.2d 913 (2d Cir. 1984), January 18, 1984. Primary appellate reconstruction of FBI undercover agent Joseph Pistone’s encounters with Bonanno members and the organization’s shifting leadership.
  6. U.S. Senate Committee on the Judiciary, Organized Crime in America: Hearings, S. Hrg. 98-184, Part 1 (1983), pp. 263–265. Contemporary state-police and federal-era intelligence profile giving Rastelli’s birth date, criminal investigations and leadership characterization. Allegations are distinguished from verdicts.
  7. U.S. House Judiciary Subcommittee, Oversight Hearing on Organized Crime Strike Forces, June 20, 1989 (1989). Congressional oversight record summarizing the Local 814 prosecutions and listing Rastelli’s 1987 twelve-year RICO sentence.
  8. U.S. Department of Justice, Civil RICO: A Manual for Federal Attorneys (October 2007), Appendix B, Bonanno Family Case. Justice Department retrospective of the 1987–1988 civil RICO complaints concerning the Bonanno family and Teamsters Local 814.
  9. Federal Bureau of Investigation, “Joe Pistone, Undercover Agent,” historical case study. Official FBI summary of the six-year Donnie Brasco operation and its role in Bonanno criminal prosecutions.
  10. Associated Press, “Organized Crime Boss Philip Rastelli, 73, Dies,” The Washington Post, June 25, 1991. Near-contemporaneous obituary documenting death, imprisonment, personal life and late-stage illness. Some marriage allegations are attributed rather than proven.
  11. “Philip (Rusty) Rastelli; Head of the Bonanno Crime Family,” Los Angeles Times, July 1, 1991. Retrospective obituary corroborating death in June 1991 and describing public reporting about Connie Rastelli.
  12. United Press International, “Eight convicted in Mafia ‘commission’ trial,” November 20, 1986. Contemporary source distinguishing Rastelli’s severance from the Commission trial from his separate October 1986 Local 814 conviction.
  13. The Washington Post, “U.S. Files Suit in N.Y. to Break Up Mob Family,” August 27, 1987. Contemporary reporting on the civil RICO action targeting the Bonanno organization and Local 814.
  14. United States v. Mariani, 851 F.2d 595 (2d Cir. 1988). Appellate history placing the 1985 Local 814 indictment in the chronology of a federal investigation begun in 1982.
  15. Richard Corliss and Simon Crittle, “The Last Don,” Time, March 29, 2004. Secondary synthesis of later Joseph Massino prosecution, 1981 three-captains killings, and retrospective Rastelli factional alignments.
  16. Selwyn Raab, Five Families: The Rise, Decline, and Resurgence of America’s Most Powerful Mafia Empires (Thomas Dunne, 2005). Secondary book-length history of New York organized crime; included for further reading but no individual pages collated for this first edition.
  17. Anthony M. DeStefano, King of the Godfathers: Joseph Massino and the Fall of the Bonanno Crime Family (Citadel, 2006). Secondary book on the Rastelli-to-Massino transition, offered as further reading; exact passages are not used as stand-alone proof.

Research and Publication Limitations

This first-edition biography synthesizes selected published judicial decisions, a congressional law-enforcement profile, government prosecution overviews, institutional history and contemporaneous journalism. It is not a complete original-trial transcript study. Specific birth and marriage certificates, the underlying 1972 case, all 1986 verdict forms, all Commission-era transcripts and every historical book page have not been exhaustively collated.

Government investigations, charges, informant descriptions and trial summaries are labeled according to what they actually prove. Neither a Commission-trial indictment nor a theory of the 1979 Galante assassination is treated as a personal murder conviction of Rastelli. No externally sourced portrait has been embedded because image licensing and archival identification have not been verified. Live deployment and server component loading remain untested.
