Gaspar DiGregorio (1905–1970)

Contested Bonanno-family leader during the Banana War, longtime associate of Joseph Bonanno, and central figure in the Mafia succession crisis of the 1960s.

1. Historical Overview

Gaspar, also written Gaspare or Gasperino, DiGregorio occupies an unusually revealing position in the history of New York’s Italian-American Mafia. He was a longtime associate of Joseph Bonanno, a member of the same interlocking Sicilian-American world, and in the mid-1960s the man recognized by rival underworld authorities as Bonanno’s successor. His position, however, did not produce uncontested leadership. The organization divided into opposing camps, and the ensuing conflict entered popular history as the “Banana War.” DiGregorio’s biography is therefore less a conventional rise-to-power story than a study of what happens when organizational legitimacy, personal loyalty, and control on the street diverge. [1] [2] [3]

Contemporary evidence leaves little doubt that DiGregorio was publicly identified as a replacement leader during the dispute. In April 1966 the Congressional Record, discussing federal organized-crime investigations, reported that he had been named boss of the former Bonanno organization, yet specifically acknowledged that some members remained loyal to Joseph and Salvatore “Bill” Bonanno. A March 1969 account entered into the same official record described DiGregorio as the man who first succeeded Bonanno and was then replaced by Paul Sciacca. These reports establish the sequence of outside recognition; they do not establish unanimous obedience within the family. [1] [2]

The most useful historical question is consequently not simply whether DiGregorio was “the boss.” It is who recognized his authority, which crews cooperated with him, which refused, and why outside recognition could not end the fighting. This entry uses that distinction throughout. Alleged violence is attributed to factions or to the reporting source where individual criminal responsibility has not been established. [3] [4]

2. Names, Birth, and Vital-Record Questions

The name appears in historical accounts as Gaspar DiGregorio, Gaspare DiGregorio, Gaspare Di Gregorio, and Gasperino DiGregorio. Different spellings may reflect transcription, Italian and English name forms, newspaper conventions, or an informal diminutive. They should not automatically be treated as separate individuals. For MGU cataloging, the canonical display name is Gaspar DiGregorio; cross-references preserve the other forms so searches of period records are easier. [3] [10]

Historical compilations give 1905 as his birth year and associate him with western Sicily, particularly Castellammare del Golfo in the province of Trapani. Some accounts identify Trapani as his birthplace, whereas a more specialized historical roster identifies Castellammare del Golfo. The distinction may involve a town-versus-province shorthand, but the original Italian civil registration has not been collated for this edition. More precise birth dates in online summaries should be treated as provisional until matched to a civil-register entry. [3] [11]

His death is much more securely fixed. A Federal Bureau of Investigation records list, reproduced from the Bureau’s deceased-person index, records Gaspare DiGregorio as born in 1905 and deceased on 11 June 1970. The occasional June 15 date in modern summaries conflicts with that record and with independent historical rosters. This entry follows June 11 and expressly identifies the discrepancy. [5] [3]

3. Western Sicily and the Castellammarese World

The Castellammare del Golfo district figures prominently in the background of several New York Mafia leaders, including Salvatore Maranzano and Joseph Bonanno. Migration carried families, business connections, marriages, and personal rivalries from that part of Sicily into Brooklyn, Buffalo, and other North American communities. Such networks mattered because underworld alliances were often embedded within relationships that predated any modern organizational chart. Shared origin, however, is context rather than proof that every migrant entered a criminal group. [3] [12]

DiGregorio’s placement within this history is supported by multiple organized-crime chronologies and retrospective accounts, but a detailed, independently verified reconstruction of his Sicilian childhood remains unavailable in the sources examined. Claims about childhood friendships, early criminal acts, or exact initiation dates should not be inserted as established biography without contemporary corroboration. It is more defensible to say that he became part of a Brooklyn-based Sicilian-American network later associated with the organization led by Joseph Bonanno. [3] [4]

This source gap affects interpretation. A biography of DiGregorio does not require an invented origin scene or a dramatized apprenticeship. His historically documented significance arises from the offices and loyalties at stake in the 1960s, and the available evidence is much stronger for that period than for his early life. [1] [2]

4. Migration, Settlement, and Civilian Enterprise

Secondary biographies report that DiGregorio entered the United States in the early 1920s and established himself in the New York area. One commonly repeated account specifies arrival from Canada in 1921. This edition has not matched that claim to an individual passenger manifest or border-crossing record, so the year and route remain subjects for archival confirmation rather than definitive migration facts. [11] [3]

Later historical profiles describe him as involved in clothing manufacturing. That description is consistent with the important place of garment-related enterprises in New York’s Italian-American commercial world, but it does not by itself show whether a particular business was lawful, unlawful, or used for laundering criminal proceeds. No authenticated business registration or full tax record for DiGregorio was reviewed here. The biography therefore treats the garment-business description as reported occupational background, not as proof of a specific racketeering scheme. [4] [11]

Careful separation of civilian and alleged criminal enterprises matters in biographical writing. A person could be identified by law enforcement as an organized-crime member while also operating real businesses. Each criminal allegation requires its own source and legal status; an occupational label should not stand in for an indictment or judgment. [7] [11]

5. Marriage and the Magaddino Connection

DiGregorio’s relationship with Stefano Magaddino, the influential Buffalo Mafia figure, is central to nearly every explanation of his later claim to command. Historical rosters identify DiGregorio as Magaddino’s brother-in-law through marriage to a woman in the Magaddino family. A March 1969 report reproduced in the Congressional Record independently uses the brother-in-law description. This is a significant point of corroboration because it places the relationship in contemporaneous public reporting as well as later Mafia histories. [2] [3]

A detailed modern narrative names his first wife as Maria Magaddino and reports that she died in 1927. Other accounts indicate DiGregorio remarried. The names and dates are useful leads for genealogy, but this edition has not collated marriage licenses, death certificates, or family probate records. They should remain attributed reported details pending civil-record verification. [4] [11]

Kinship did not automatically produce a single political position. Bonanno, DiGregorio, and Magaddino could share family ties while becoming adversaries over authority. Their entanglement demonstrates why organized-crime disputes often crossed the categories of personal allegiance, marriage alliance, and jurisdictional rivalry. [3] [8]

6. Friendship with Joseph Bonanno and the Godfather Relationship

DiGregorio was not merely a distant functionary recruited when Bonanno disappeared. Historical accounts describe a longstanding friendship between the two men. He is identified as best man at Joseph Bonanno’s 1931 wedding to Fay Labruzzo and as godfather to Bonanno’s first son, Salvatore Vincent, known as Bill. These associations are reported in detailed historical rosters and in reconstructions of the family dispute. They establish the intimacy that made his subsequent rivalry especially striking. [3] [4]

The 1931 wedding followed the death of Salvatore Maranzano and the emergence of Bonanno as leader of the Brooklyn-based organization. For DiGregorio, proximity to the new leader meant access to a network built upon more than a transactional relationship. It was not necessarily proof of a guaranteed succession right, and no verified document supports the claim that Joseph Bonanno formally designated him as heir. [3] [8]

Later recollections of the conflict can make DiGregorio’s change of allegiance look like an abrupt personal betrayal. A fuller explanation must also consider Commission politics, disputes over Bill Bonanno’s advancement, and the desire of outside bosses to challenge Joseph Bonanno’s independence. Several causal forces operated at once; no single memoir can conclusively establish DiGregorio’s private motives. [8] [9]

7. Service in the Bonanno Organization

During Joseph Bonanno’s long tenure, DiGregorio is commonly described as a captain or senior leader with his own following. Specialized historical accounts place him within the early command structure; retrospective histories connect his position to the former Castellammarese faction. The specific date of his initiation and the complete roster of men in his crew have not been authenticated in this edition. [3] [4]

In the language of La Cosa Nostra, a captain or caporegime generally supervises a group of inducted members and associates below the family administration. A later Department of Justice description of the Bonanno organization explains the hierarchy of boss, underboss, consigliere, captains, soldiers, and associates. That modern legal description is included to clarify organizational vocabulary, not to prove the personnel or boundaries of DiGregorio’s 1950s crew. [7]

His leadership prospects derived partly from personal standing and partly from the absence of an uncontested successor in the crisis of the 1960s. The popular image of a sudden elevation from obscurity obscures the more important fact that he already possessed enough organizational recognition for other leaders to advance him as a credible alternative. [2] [3]

8. The Mafia Commission and Questions of Authority

The Mafia Commission was the interfamily forum through which major New York and other American Mafia leaders sought to manage disputes and preserve their influence. Its authority was neither a court of law nor an elected public institution. The extent of compliance rested on armed power, personal standing, relationships, and the willingness of local crews to accept decisions. This distinction is essential to understanding the DiGregorio period. [7] [12]

Joseph Bonanno had been one of the important leaders in the post-1931 American Mafia system. By the early 1960s, his relations with several peers deteriorated. The immediate dispute is often explained through allegations that Bonanno and Joseph Magliocco favored violence against rival bosses, together with questions about the elevation of Bill Bonanno. Sources differ on exactly which figures ordered or approved particular acts and on how to characterize Bonanno’s intentions. [3] [8]

External leaders could endorse DiGregorio as a replacement, but they could not force every Bonanno loyalist to recognize him. That institutional weakness explains why an apparently settled succession became a protracted contest. A leadership chart that inserts DiGregorio as a simple one-line successor risks erasing the continued resistance of the Bonanno faction. [1] [2]

9. The Early-1960s Fracture

Joseph Bonanno’s long rule had created stability for some participants but also concentrated power within a circle closely tied to his relatives. The public record of the early 1960s shows official scrutiny of the New York Mafia increasing and competing leadership assessments emerging. Retrospective accounts emphasize that Bonanno’s opponents viewed his ambitions and his son’s advancement as threats to an established distribution of power. [3] [12]

The commonly repeated story of an assassination conspiracy against Carlo Gambino and Thomas Lucchese rests heavily on later recollections and law-enforcement interpretations. It is historically important because it shaped relations among Mafia leaders, but it should not be presented as an adjudicated conspiracy charge against DiGregorio. No verified criminal judgment places him as a participant in a plot of that kind. [3] [8]

For DiGregorio, the moment represented both opportunity and danger. Supporting the outside leaders placed him in contention for the administration of the family. It also put him at odds with men who had followed Bonanno for decades and with the son to whom he had personal godfather ties. That contradiction became the central problem of his administration. [3] [4]

10. Bill Bonanno and the Succession Question

Salvatore “Bill” Bonanno became an especially contentious figure because he combined family inheritance expectations with a leadership role inside the organization. FBI material released through the National Archives includes an early-1964 assessment that Bill had been designated consigliere and that leadership arrangements were being discussed. Those intelligence assessments show the instability of titles even before the celebrated 1964 disappearance. [6] [9]

It is inaccurate to reduce the dispute to a simple father-to-son transfer of the boss title. Bill’s recognized or claimed role varied among sources and moments. There were also other proposed acting leaders, including relatives and senior aides. The underlying controversy concerned who could speak for Joseph Bonanno, who could bind the organization, and whether a regional or national interfamily body could set aside a family’s established boss. [3] [6]

DiGregorio’s appeal to opponents of Bonanno had a structural logic: he was an experienced insider, yet he was willing to operate without Joseph’s approval. The same quality that made him acceptable to external bosses made him unacceptable to many Bonanno loyalists. [1] [3]

11. The October 1964 Disappearance of Joseph Bonanno

Joseph Bonanno disappeared in Manhattan on 21 October 1964, shortly before a scheduled federal grand-jury appearance. Contemporary reports described an alleged armed abduction. Bonanno later asserted that he had been detained through a Magaddino-associated intervention, while government investigators and subsequent commentators disputed aspects of that explanation and questioned whether some of his absence was voluntary. [1] [12]

The uncertainty surrounding that disappearance should not obscure its practical effects. The family could not rely on regular public contact with its longstanding leader. Rival groups asserted different arrangements for managing business, representing members, and controlling the organization’s future. This was the environment in which DiGregorio’s selection gained momentum. [1] [3]

DiGregorio should not be automatically described as responsible for the alleged kidnapping. Historical proximity and the political advantage he gained from the absence do not establish participation in the event. The available sources for this edition do not sustain such an individual attribution. [8] [12]

12. DiGregorio’s Installation and the Disputed Date

Many historical leadership tables place DiGregorio’s effective accession in 1965, while some accounts begin the contested takeover in late 1964 after Bonanno’s disappearance. The difference reflects more than imprecise recordkeeping. One date may represent the onset of claims to leadership, another recognition by the Commission or influential bosses, and a third the time when a particular faction began to operate under his direction. [1] [3]

The strongest contemporary public anchor is the April 1966 Congressional Record, which expressly identifies DiGregorio as having been named boss and notes persistent Bonanno loyalists. An FBI-filed press excerpt and later official references also describe the transfer from Bonanno through DiGregorio to Paul Sciacca. These are substantial bases for including DiGregorio in a succession chronology with a disputed or factional authority notation. [1] [10]

This article uses the broad working period approximately 1965–1966/68 for his claim, with the precise transition to Sciacca left qualified. It would be misleading either to erase his recognition or to record him as the universally accepted boss of an intact family until 1968. [2] [3]

13. Stefano Magaddino’s Role

Accounts consistently identify Magaddino as a supporter of DiGregorio’s elevation, and a March 1969 statement published in the Congressional Record explicitly refers to DiGregorio as Magaddino’s brother-in-law. Magaddino’s stature in Buffalo made his support consequential, especially because leadership controversies within one Mafia family often became matters for related organizations. [2] [3]

That support must nevertheless be described precisely. It is not proof that Magaddino personally ordered each act of violence during the Banana War, or that DiGregorio operated as his subordinate in every decision. Historical sources sometimes compress a complex factional alignment into a one-sentence claim that an outside boss “installed” someone; the mechanisms are harder to reconstruct. [4] [8]

What can be said is that DiGregorio’s position involved more than a local Brooklyn quarrel. It became a test of the reach of regional Mafia alliances and of how much authority one boss could exercise over another family’s succession. [3] [12]

14. One Organization, Two Competing Administrations

The Banana War was an internal struggle, not a formal permanent division into two separately chartered New York Mafia families. Members and associates identified with rival claimants while retaining overlapping histories, neighborhoods, and business relationships. Public agencies and journalists sometimes used shorthand labels such as the former Bonanno family, the DiGregorio faction, and Bonanno loyalists to capture the unstable situation. [1] [12]

DiGregorio’s faction pursued recognition as the legitimate administration. Joseph Bonanno’s supporters insisted that their original leader had not properly surrendered his position and that his son or aides could act on his behalf. Both claims drew from different ideas of continuity: the authority of the senior boss on one side and recognition by the Commission and other powerful figures on the other. [3] [8]

The coexistence of the two claims had practical consequences. Meetings could not always be trusted as neutral, commanders faced competing orders, and associates might be exposed to retaliation because of perceived loyalties. The record must be read with this contested situation in mind rather than with hindsight from later organized-crime charts. [1] [4]

15. Escalating Violence and Public Exposure

Published histories attribute a series of shootings and killings in the mid-1960s to the Bonanno factional struggle. The aggregate conflict is well documented, but the evidentiary standing of individual incident accounts varies considerably. Some were reported contemporaneously; others became better known through participant memoirs or much later retrospective stories. A case-by-case reconstruction would require police reports, charging documents, and court outcomes for each event. [4] [12]

The distinction between “a member of this faction was shot” and “DiGregorio ordered this shooting” is critical. The first describes an event and group association. The second accuses a named individual of directing violence. Even when plausible as a theory, the latter requires stronger evidence than proximity to the faction or a later author’s narrative assumption. [4] [11]

Public shootings increased federal interest in the dispute. A national account in the April 1966 Congressional Record grouped the feud with ongoing grand-jury investigations of the New York Mafia. The attention made the succession dispute a question not only of underground power but also of federal enforcement and public safety. [1]

16. The Troutman Street Shooting, 28 January 1966

The defining confrontation took place on the night of 28 January 1966 in the Bushwick section of Brooklyn. Bill Bonanno and associates attended or approached a purported reconciliation meeting connected with DiGregorio’s side of the split. Gunfire erupted in the area of Troutman Street. Bill Bonanno survived, and the incident quickly became the most recognizable symbol of the Banana War. [1] [4]

The strongest concise contemporary reference is the April 1966 Congressional Record, which states that an attempt was made on Bill Bonanno and associates during a gun battle on Brooklyn streets. Thom L. Jones’s longer reconstruction supplies a detailed scene from retrospective accounts, including a disputed meeting arrangement and varying estimates of the number of shots. Such reconstruction is valuable for identifying research leads but must not be confused with a complete contemporaneous police finding. [1] [4]

Crucially, even though DiGregorio was associated with the faction on the other side of the conflict, the cited official summary does not establish that he personally planned, ordered, attended, or fired during the attack. This biography describes an ambush or attempted ambush reported in accounts of the feud without assigning him individual criminal liability not proved by the accessible evidence. [1] [4]

17. How the Troutman Street Story Was Retold

Later narrative treatments often turn Troutman Street into a cinematic assassination trap with carefully positioned gunmen and dramatic escapes. The broad event—a meeting connected with the war followed by a street gunfight—is supported by a contemporary federal-era account. The exact sequence of arrivals, telephone calls, gun positions, ammunition expended, and identities of attackers is less secure. [1] [4]

Bill Bonanno’s own memoir is especially relevant because he was a participant, but memoir testimony cannot be its own independent corroboration. It may preserve firsthand observations while also reflecting later memory, self-protection, and a desire to frame his family’s actions favorably. The same methodological caution applies to accounts defending DiGregorio or the rival faction. [9] [4]

For an encyclopedia biography, the most useful outcome was political rather than theatrical: a purported path toward reconciliation had become a public firefight, and DiGregorio’s claim to unify the organization was no closer to uncontested acceptance. [1] [2]

18. Joseph Bonanno Reappears in May 1966

Joseph Bonanno returned to public view in May 1966 after an extended period out of sight. Contemporary and later sources agree that the return did not automatically restore orderly family leadership. Disputed loyalties remained, as did the outside recognition already given to rival figures. In political terms, the return sharpened the question of whether a contested boss could reclaim practical authority merely by reappearing. [6] [12]

For DiGregorio, this produced a difficult strategic position. He had gained standing partly because Joseph Bonanno was absent, yet Bonanno’s continued personal prestige with part of the membership made an uncontested resolution unlikely. DiGregorio could not assume that the people who accepted his selection as an emergency measure would continue to support him when the original leader was visibly present. [3] [4]

Accounts of Bonanno’s reappearance also differ in whether they emphasize surrender to federal judicial authorities, withdrawal from hiding, or a political return to family affairs. These are separate dimensions of the event and should not be conflated in a precise chronology. [6] [8]

19. The Failure of a Durable Settlement

DiGregorio’s brief prominence illustrates the difference between acquiring a title and consolidating an organization. He appears to have possessed support from influential external figures and a portion of the Bonanno membership, but he could not eliminate the rival claim. Continuing violence and investigations reduced the value of nominal recognition. [1] [2]

Some later histories describe him as personally reluctant to wage an expansive war against longtime friends. Others emphasize the unwillingness of the Commission to tolerate an ineffective administration. Neither portrait by itself explains the conflict. The available contemporaneous material supports a narrower conclusion: other figures eventually sought a new leader, and DiGregorio’s claim diminished before a durable peace had taken hold. [2] [4]

The case cautions against reading the Commission as an omnipotent government. Its leaders could withdraw endorsement, but sustaining command still depended on actual crews, local relationships, and the ability to prevent violence. The failure of those conditions destabilized the entire succession. [7] [12]

20. Paul Sciacca and the Transfer of Recognition

Paul Sciacca emerged as the successor to DiGregorio’s leadership claim. A March 1969 organized-crime account reproduced in the Congressional Record reported speculation that Sciacca had become boss and explained that DiGregorio had become ill and was replaced. This is a contemporaneous account of the transfer, although the phrasing itself signals that its authors did not present the entire succession as a judicially established fact. [2]

In a separate FBI-released file containing a period press report, a Justice Department attribution describes Bonanno family leadership as having been transferred first to DiGregorio and later to Sciacca. That statement independently supports the broad sequence, while not supplying one definitive appointment date. Reference works consequently differ over whether DiGregorio should be assigned 1965–1966, 1965–1968, or another approximate span. [10] [3]

The responsible editorial solution is to state plainly that Sciacca supplanted DiGregorio during the later phase of the conflict, and to keep the date qualified unless a dated contemporaneous installation record becomes available. The apparent precision of a succession table should not exceed the sources that support it. [2] [3]

21. Health, Withdrawal, and the Final Years

The 1969 congressional account links DiGregorio’s replacement to illness and an inability to withstand the pressure of the struggle. Subsequent narratives mention heart trouble. Specific descriptions of attacks, injuries, or medical treatment are difficult to corroborate from the sources examined; no hospital chart has been reviewed. It is therefore reasonable to describe declining health as a contemporary explanation for his exit, without asserting a precise private diagnosis during the succession crisis. [2] [4]

Accounts generally place his remaining years with family on Long Island. He was no longer the publicly recognized central claimant to Bonanno leadership, and later succession histories move to Paul Sciacca and then the figures who followed him. The quiet final phase contrasts with the violence of the preceding contest. [3] [11]

Some modern summaries state that DiGregorio himself was wounded by gunfire during the last phase of the war. This edition does not adopt that assertion as an established event because a suitably specific contemporary investigative or medical record was not independently located. That omission is deliberate rather than accidental. [4] [11]

22. Death, Date Discrepancy, and Burial

Gaspar DiGregorio died on 11 June 1970 in Smithtown, Long Island, according to the FBI deceased-person index and specialist historical rosters. The reported cause was lung cancer. Secondary histories associate his final illness with St. John’s Episcopal Hospital in Smithtown and list St. Charles Cemetery in Farmingdale as his burial place. Those details are consistent across several retrospective accounts but were not independently checked against original hospital and cemetery registers for this edition. [5] [3] [11]

A persistent inconsistency in internet biographies labels the death “15 June 1970,” even while citing a newspaper obituary dated 13 June 1970 and elsewhere stating June 11. The 15 June date is chronologically suspect in that context and conflicts with the indexed FBI date. MGU therefore uses June 11, explicitly acknowledging that final reconciliation with a certified death record would be preferable. [5] [11]

He was sixty-four years old if the frequently reported 1905 birth year is correct. Describing him as a former contested leader of the Bonanno organization is more precise than the unqualified phrase “former boss,” which may imply a period of undisputed rule that the contemporaneous record does not demonstrate. [1] [2]

23. Historical Interpretation: Legitimacy Without Control

DiGregorio’s case shows that Mafia leadership depended on at least three different resources: local obedience, approval by other major criminal leaders, and continuity with a family’s own customs and alliances. Joseph Bonanno possessed long tenure and loyalists; DiGregorio obtained alternative endorsement and his own adherents; Sciacca was later recognized as a possible route out of the impasse. None of these elements operated in isolation. [1] [2] [3]

It also reveals the limits of kinship as a political instrument. DiGregorio was bound to Joseph Bonanno through close personal associations and to Stefano Magaddino by marriage. Those relationships did not prevent competing allegiances or remove the risks associated with a disputed succession. Family ties could make a claim credible and simultaneously make a rupture more bitter. [2] [4]

Finally, DiGregorio’s less-visible civilian history and the concentration of evidence around one crisis illustrate a broader challenge in criminal biography. Well-documented institutional roles do not automatically supply reliable detail about every decade of a person’s life. Historical writing should preserve what is known, make uncertainties visible, and resist filling gaps with colorful but undocumented episodes. [3] [12]

24. Evidence, Attribution, and Research Method

This biography distinguishes four kinds of evidence. Contemporaneous government-era reporting is useful for public chronology and official beliefs, but may contain unproven allegations. Federal investigative material documents what investigators recorded, not necessarily what courts established. Specialist histories help reconstruct succession and relationships but sometimes inherit errors from earlier compilations. Participant memoirs provide crucial first-person perspectives and require independent comparison. [1] [2] [6]

The most consequential unresolved questions are the exact Italian civil-registration details; the immigration route and business documentation; the dates and mechanisms of Commission recognition; the full police and prosecutorial record of the 1966 Troutman Street shooting; and the precise moment Sciacca replaced DiGregorio in practice. Future archival research should prioritize those items before adding claims about discrete criminal offenses. [3] [4] [5]

No individual conviction is asserted against DiGregorio in this article because the cited sources do not supply a verified judgment establishing one. That absence of verified adjudication is not an affirmative claim that he had no arrests, convictions, or other legal proceedings. Equally, designation as a Mafia leader is not a finding of guilt for every violent act committed during the war. [1] [4]

Research Limitations and Next Steps

This first-edition article is an evidence-based synthesis, not a complete archival edition. It does not claim to have inspected original Sicilian birth or marriage records, certified U.S. death or burial registrations, immigration manifests, clothing-business filings, original police evidence exhibits from the Troutman Street shooting, or the entirety of the recently released FBI DiGregorio files. It does not reproduce uncross-checked memoir scenes as established fact.

Recommended archival priorities: confirm 1905 birth details in Trapani-province civil registrations; reconcile the DiGregorio–Magaddino marriage; obtain the 1970 obituary and death certificate; locate contemporary police case materials and any prosecutions tied to Troutman Street; compare the full FBI DiGregorio release with congressional reporting; and document a dated record of Commission recognition and the Sciacca transition. A final editorial review should also audit how family leadership appears across all MGU timelines.
