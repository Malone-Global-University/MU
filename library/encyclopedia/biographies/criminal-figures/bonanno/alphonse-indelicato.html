1. Historical Overview

Alphonse “Sonny Red” Indelicato was a captain in New York’s Bonanno crime family whose murder in May 1981 marked a decisive stage in a struggle over leadership. He was killed together with fellow captains Philip “Philly Lucky” Giaccone and Dominick “Big Trin” Trinchera. Their deaths became the centerpiece of major federal organized-crime investigations and, much later, prosecutions of Joseph Massino and Vito Rizzuto. The case is therefore both a biography of an individual captain and a study of how organized-crime succession disputes produced violence inside an already divided organization. [1] [2] [8] [10] [12]

The most trustworthy public record does not support a fully reconstructed childhood-to-death narrative. It does, however, establish Indelicato’s rank, his alliance with Giaccone and Trinchera, his opposition to the faction supporting Philip Rastelli, the recovery of his body in Queens, and the government’s investigation of those murders. This article prioritizes judicial decisions and contemporary reporting, distinguishes legal findings from allegations, and treats later cooperating-witness accounts as evidence requiring attribution rather than unquestioned personal recollection. [1] [2] [4] [12]

2. Name, Alias, and Identity

The subject is Alphonse Indelicato, usually called “Sonny Red”; some sources abbreviate the name to “Al,” while historical records sometimes use a spelling such as “Alfonzo.” His alias helps distinguish him from Dominick “Sonny Black” Napolitano, another Bonanno captain who belonged to the opposing faction. The men were contemporaries but not interchangeable; confusion between them can substantially alter an account of the 1981 killings and the Donnie Brasco operation. [1] [4] [5] [15]

A further distinction is essential: Alphonse’s son was Anthony “Bruno” Indelicato. The prominent federal appellate decision United States v. Indelicato, 865 F.2d 1370 (1989), concerns Anthony’s convictions in relation to the July 1979 murder of Carmine Galante and two associates, not a prosecution of his father. In that opinion Anthony is repeatedly described as having a father who was a Bonanno captain. A biography of Alphonse must not transfer the son’s acts, convictions, courtroom defenses, or sentence to the father. [2] [3] [14]

3. Birth, Early Life, and Unverified Details

Popular short biographies disagree about the year of Indelicato’s birth, sometimes supplying 1927 and sometimes 1931. The appellate opinions examined for this article do not supply a certified birth date. Accordingly, his date of birth and age at death are not asserted as confirmed civil-register facts here. The identification of 1981 as the year of death, in contrast, is firmly established by the investigation, recovery of his body, and the subsequent homicide prosecutions. [1] [2] [4] [15]

Claims concerning exact childhood addresses, schooling, marriages, employment, a particular date of Mafia induction, or the origin of the “Sonny Red” nickname circulate in retellings but were not established by the legal materials assessed here. Such details should be added only after checking birth, marriage and census documents or reliable, identified first-person sources. The absence of those details is an editorial boundary, not evidence that they did not exist. [1] [2] [15]

4. The Bonanno Family He Entered

The Bonanno family emerged from the faction led by Salvatore Maranzano and then developed for decades under Joseph Bonanno. Its later decades included the Banana War of the 1960s and a contested succession in which recognition, imprisonment, and personal alliances often mattered as much as a formal boss title. The history of these disputes helps explain why several captains in 1981 believed that the existing leadership could be challenged rather than simply obeyed. [1] [3] [14] [16]

By the late 1970s the Bonanno family had also lost ordinary representation on the interfamily Mafia Commission for part of the period. The 1989 appellate account in Anthony Indelicato’s case describes Commission intervention in the family’s affairs and the succession crisis surrounding Carmine Galante. That ruling is useful background for understanding the environment in which Alphonse became a senior figure; it is not proof of every particular business deal attributed to him in later folklore. [3] [14]

5. Indelicato’s Role as a Captain

The federal courts identified Alphonse Indelicato as one of the Bonanno captains, or caporegimes, involved in the 1981 leadership confrontation. In the organizational structure described by the federal judiciary, captains supervised crews of initiated members and associates, while leaders above them held boss, underboss and consigliere functions. A captain was not merely a street-level subordinate: his ability to command followers could make him a consequential political participant in a succession crisis. [1] [2] [3]

The available record does not establish a complete membership list for Indelicato’s crew, a definitive territory, or a reliable accounting of his personal criminal earnings. It would be inaccurate to turn the general activities of the Bonanno organization—narcotics, gambling, loansharking and commercial racketeering—into specific convictions or profits attributable to him without distinct supporting evidence. His documented historical role is most securely tied to the 1981 faction and its fatal outcome. [1] [2] [13]

6. Carmine Galante and the 1979 Leadership Crisis

Carmine Galante exercised formidable influence in the late 1970s, while Philip Rastelli’s supporters contested his position. Galante and two men at his table, Giuseppe Turano and Leonard Coppola, were killed in a Brooklyn restaurant on 12 July 1979. The 1989 Second Circuit opinion reconstructs a larger Commission-centered plan and describes how that violence reshaped the Bonanno hierarchy. [3] [14]

The Galante case matters for the elder Indelicato chiefly because his son Anthony was drawn into the successful prosecution arising from the shooting. The younger man’s connection to the Galante killing and subsequent promotion as described in the court’s historical account created family ties across the factional landscape. Yet a father–son relationship alone does not prove that Alphonse personally organized, approved or executed the Galante murder. The court’s evidence and criminal judgment concern Anthony. [3] [14]

7. The Anthony “Bruno” Indelicato Relationship

Anthony Indelicato was an active Bonanno member and Alphonse’s son, a relationship expressly described in federal appellate records. The Commission-trial appeal says Anthony was involved in the 1979 Galante murders and was later promoted to captain. After Alphonse was killed in 1981, Anthony became a separate target in a plot to eliminate surviving opponents of the Rastelli-aligned faction. [2] [3] [5] [6]

Family relationships in organized crime had operational consequences: kin might share loyalties, inherit enemies, or become vulnerable to retaliation for decisions they had not made. But this reality also creates a methodological danger. A historian must distinguish what the father did, what the son did, and what others feared the son might do. The separate court record relating to Anthony should therefore be cross-referenced, not silently merged into the present biography. [2] [3] [6]

8. The Succession Problem After Galante

Galante’s death did not end the succession struggle. Rastelli was recognized by influential members of the organization, yet spent important periods in federal custody. This created an opening for competing captain-level coalitions to bargain over leadership, the future of their crews and their standing with other Mafia families. The court in Ruggiero later identified the central blocs involved in the May 1981 confrontation rather than describing a stable, unanimous chain of command. [1] [2] [3]

The struggle should not be reduced to a contest between two charismatic individuals. The state of the wider Bonanno organization, internal violence, imprisonment, Commission relationships and the movement of younger members between factions all helped determine who could give effective orders. Indelicato’s role was significant because he was among the senior captains willing to oppose the Rastelli-aligned group at a moment when the family’s leadership remained vulnerable. [1] [12]

9. The Two Factions of Five Captains

The 1984 Second Circuit decision identified five captains as especially important to the conflict. Dominick “Sonny Black” Napolitano and Joseph Messina remained loyal to Rastelli. Opposing them were Alphonse Indelicato, Philip Giaccone and Dominick Trinchera, who sought to take control away from the Rastelli faction. The five-captains description comes from the evidence introduced through the Donnie Brasco investigation and should be understood as the court’s summary of a particular moment, not as an exhaustive list of every Bonanno capo. [1] [2]

The three aligned captains were not an informal friendship circle alone. Federal charges identified a struggle for control of the family’s organization, and the later Massino prosecution connected this struggle to leadership decisions and lethal violence. Calling Indelicato a “rebel captain” is a useful shorthand, but it is relative to the Rastelli position; it does not make either side’s criminal violence legitimate, inevitable or historically justified. [1] [2] [8] [12]

10. Philip “Philly Lucky” Giaccone

Philip Giaccone, another Bonanno captain, was one of Indelicato’s two principal allies. The 1989 Commission-case ruling separately mentions Giaccone in the aftermath of the Galante shooting, when Anthony Indelicato visited a Manhattan social club alongside his father and others. By 1981 Giaccone was part of the faction that opposed Rastelli’s supporters. The relationship connects otherwise separate moments in the family’s political history. [1] [3]

Giaccone’s fate illustrates the difficulty of reconstructing an underground murder case when victims’ bodies are concealed. Contemporary 1981 and 1982 court records said his body had not been found. Later searches at the Queens burial site recovered remains believed to be his and those of Trinchera. This later physical evidence should not be retroactively inserted into a quotation or account from an earlier investigation as though investigators possessed it in 1981. [1] [4] [11]

11. Dominick “Big Trin” Trinchera

Dominick Trinchera was the third captain in the anti-Rastelli coalition. He appears in the court record of the Galante assassination in connection with Anthony Indelicato and other Bonanno participants. By the following years, however, he belonged to the same faction as Alphonse and Giaccone. His trajectory helps demonstrate that alignments within the family changed following Galante’s death rather than remaining fixed around the 1979 shooting. [1] [3] [12]

Trinchera was killed in the same 1981 operation that ended Indelicato’s life. The evidence for the shared event comes from later judicial review and numerous legal proceedings; accounts of each man’s final movements and precise placement during the shooting are more dependent on particular cooperating witnesses. This page therefore presents the joint murder as established but does not dramatize unverified last words or minute-by-minute choreography. [1] [2] [12]

12. Escalation and Suspected Coup Plans

Later prosecutorial descriptions of the three-captains case portrayed the Indelicato faction as preparing to seize the family from Rastelli and as acquiring weapons. A 2010 federal prosecution filing recounted witness information about threats and a decision by the opposing coalition to act first. Those retrospective accounts help historians explain the perceived threat, but the claim that every named individual had agreed on an exact coup plan remains dependent on the witnesses and evidence behind the filing. [12]

The appellate narrative supports the broader fact of a struggle for control and the murders that followed. It does not provide a complete transcript of every secret meeting between the two camps. Describing a planned coup as an allegation or interpretation is therefore more defensible than treating its alleged dates, participants and proposed targets as incontrovertibly settled. Escalation is evident; the private intentions of each participant are less fully documented. [1] [2] [8]

13. The Wider Mafia Commission Context

Other New York crime families had a stake in the Bonanno dispute. The Commission was an interfamily institution whose recognition and interventions could alter the balance of power inside a member organization. The 1989 appellate record describes intervention in the 1979 Galante affair; later prosecutors alleged that outside support and opposition also influenced the split that led to the 1981 three-captains killings. [3] [12]

The distinctions between outside endorsement, active participation and authorizing a particular murder should remain explicit. A historical account that says “the Commission ordered the hit” without identifying which proceeding or witness established that claim can create an illusion of certainty. The available legal materials provide a strong account of a family struggle with interfamily ramifications, but personal responsibility for individual decisions must be assigned from specific evidence. [1] [3] [12]

14. The Rastelli-Aligned Coalition

The loyalist side included Napolitano and Messina, and later prosecution records placed Joseph Massino centrally among those protecting Rastelli’s authority. Massino’s eventual prominence must not be projected backward as though he was the universally accepted boss in 1981. Rather, the 1981 conflict was one stage in his ascent, in which the elimination of rival captains helped consolidate the side with which he was aligned. [1] [10] [12]

The case demonstrates how a criminal organization can respond to contested authority not through an orderly leadership vote but through clandestine negotiations followed by attacks on internal competitors. This was not a dispute without victims: three men were killed, another was hunted, and the organization became subject to an increasingly extensive set of surveillance and racketeering investigations. [1] [5] [8]

15. The Meeting of 5 May 1981

The court in Ruggiero states that on 5 May 1981 the three opposing captains were lured to a meeting and murdered. Indelicato, Giaccone and Trinchera appear consistently as the victims. The location and narrative details of the actual shooting are described with varying degrees of specificity in later testimony, secondary histories and press accounts; the core date and identities of the victims are secure in the federal legal record. [1] [2] [12]

The term “Three Captains murders” has become conventional because the victims held the same rank and were aligned in a single faction. It should not obscure the essential fact that the episode involved the planned killing of three specific people. The murder changed the family’s leadership balance, but it did not produce a legitimate succession procedure or necessarily settle all internal rivalries. [1] [8] [10]

16. What the Evidence Establishes About the Murders

The 1984 appeal says the evidence strongly implicated Napolitano, Ruggiero, Anthony Rabito and Messina but explicitly acknowledged that it did not conclusively determine who physically carried out the murders. Undercover conversations and subsequent surveillance described in the opinion formed an important part of the government’s case. Those observations and recordings were powerful investigative material, but their historical interpretation and each defendant’s criminal verdict require separate discussion. [1]

Later proceedings developed substantially more evidence. The 2004 conviction of Joseph Massino and Vito Rizzuto’s 2007 guilty plea connected different people to the same 1981 crimes. Those later adjudicated results should be recognized without rewriting what the 1982 jury found or implying that every person originally named in the case was convicted of personally firing a weapon. This distinction is central to responsible crime-history publication. [2] [8] [9] [10] [12]

17. Burial and Recovery of Indelicato’s Body

Contemporaneous United Press International reporting states that Indelicato’s body was found on 24 May 1981 in a shallow grave in a Queens vacant lot. The 1984 appellate opinion confirms that his body was discovered while Giaccone and Trinchera were still missing. Later narratives often give a May 28 date, potentially confusing initial discovery with subsequent public reporting. The initial recovery date is consequently identified as May 24 on the strength of the near-contemporaneous account, with the discrepancy disclosed. [1] [4]

The concealment of a body mattered to investigators because it delayed identification of the murder’s physical circumstances and could frustrate proof in court. Recovery gave the case a concrete victim and confirmed homicide in a way that witness talk alone could not. Descriptions of the grave that add cinematic detail—precise discoverer identities, dramatic quotations or invented weather and lighting—are omitted unless corroborated. [1] [4] [11]

18. The Other Two Victims and the 2004 Search

Giaccone and Trinchera remained missing when federal judges reviewed the original Donnie Brasco cases. More than two decades later, investigators searched a Queens vacant lot associated with organized-crime burials. October 2004 reporting described the recovery of human remains and personal items that authorities believed were connected to the two missing captains; early reports treated identification as preliminary. [1] [11]

This later development helps explain why a case discussed as involving two missing bodies in 1984 could be revisited with new forensic and witness evidence. It does not justify presenting the 2004 discovery as an event personally witnessed by Indelicato, who had died in 1981. The proper sequence is murder in 1981, contemporary criminal investigations, later trials and testimony, and subsequent forensic recovery. [1] [8] [11]

19. The Plot Against Anthony “Bruno” Indelicato

Violence did not stop with the three captains. The 1985 Eleventh Circuit decision states that the 1982 indictment included a separate conspiracy to murder Anthony “Bruno” Indelicato, expressly identifying him as Alphonse’s son. Joseph Pistone testified that Napolitano had instructed him to locate and kill the younger man, who had escaped the fate of the three captains. Contemporary reporting of the 1982 trial gives the same essential account. [2] [5] [6]

The distinction between a criminal plot and a completed homicide is indispensable: Anthony survived the 1981 episode. The murder conspiracy also shaped the undercover investigation’s end. A federal agent who is asked to kill a target faces a different operational and ethical boundary from an agent collecting information about bookmaking or other rackets. The threatened killing became one of the defining moments of the case. [5] [6] [7]

20. Donnie Brasco and the Federal Undercover Record

FBI Special Agent Joseph Pistone operated as “Donnie Brasco,” first entering organized-crime circles in 1976 and gaining the confidence of Bonanno members over several years. His undercover contacts with Benjamin Ruggiero and Dominick Napolitano gave federal investigators unusual access to internal discussions. The Ruggiero decision describes wiretaps, surveillance and recorded conversations in which the 1981 leadership battle and murders were discussed. [1] [7]

This evidence is particularly valuable because it was recorded near the event rather than assembled solely from retrospective memoirs. It has limits: a participant may boast, omit his own actions, misunderstand another crew’s decisions, or deliberately mislead an associate. Courts may properly use a recording as evidence while historians still ask whose voice it captures, what claim was made, and whether the statement was independently corroborated. [1] [5] [7]

21. July 1981: The Undercover Identity Exposed

The FBI dates the exposure of Pistone’s cover to 26 July 1981. The undercover operation’s termination came only weeks after the killing of Indelicato and the threats against Anthony. When Bonanno members learned that “Donnie Brasco” was a federal agent, the organization faced not only the consequences of its internal struggle but also the prospect that sensitive meetings, transactions and threats could be presented in court. [1] [7]

Later films and books have emphasized the personal drama of this infiltration. In the source-based historical account, its central institutional significance lies in the transformation of previously closed Mafia conversations into evidence that could be tested in federal trials. The case weakened the organization’s assumption that trusted associates could not themselves be investigators. [1] [7] [16]

22. The 1981–1982 Federal Charges

Federal authorities publicly announced charges related to the three murders in November 1981. The subsequent New York indictment of 7 July 1982 alleged RICO conspiracies and substantive crimes involving multiple defendants, including the murders of Indelicato, Giaccone and Trinchera and the separate plan to kill Anthony. A charge reports the government’s accusation; it is not by itself a finding that the accused committed the alleged act. [2] [4] [13]

The prosecutions also addressed narcotics, gambling and other rackets in addition to the internal violence. This breadth matters historically: the killings were treated not merely as a local homicide case but as part of a continuing criminal enterprise. The RICO framework allowed prosecutors to link different crimes and personnel through a claimed organizational structure, subject to the legal standards and defenses applied at trial. [1] [2] [13]

23. What the 1982 Trial Did—and Did Not—Prove

The Second Circuit’s 1984 opinion records that Benjamin Ruggiero was convicted of RICO conspiracy but acquitted on a substantive RICO count; Nicholas Santora was convicted of the two RICO counts and narcotics conspiracy but acquitted of a separate substantive narcotics count; Anthony Rabito and Antonio Tomasulo received narrower verdicts; and John Cerasani was acquitted of all counts tried against him. These are differentiated criminal results, not a blanket conviction of everyone originally suspected of the three murders. [1] [2]

The court also explained that the record strongly implicated particular men in the murders but did not conclusively identify every actual killer. It would therefore be inaccurate to write that the 1982 proceeding independently proved every detailed story about who pulled each trigger. Later guilty pleas and convictions further developed the case; each belongs to its own procedural period. [1] [2] [8]

24. Joseph Massino and the 1987–2004 Prosecution History

Joseph Massino’s legal history is unusually important to the later reconstruction of the 1981 murders. A 2004 district-court decision described his 1987 acquittal on earlier RICO charges, including a finding that certain murder-conspiracy predicates had not been proven in that case. That ruling then considered and rejected his effort to bar the newer prosecution. The 1987 acquittal must not be hidden or retroactively erased by later events. [8]

After a later and more expansive trial, a federal jury convicted Massino in July 2004 of racketeering that included the murders of Indelicato, Giaccone and Trinchera among the predicate offenses, as contemporaneous trial reporting and Department of Justice retrospectives record. Thus a proper biography can say both that the earlier proceeding ended in acquittal on its own charges and that the later jury reached a different adjudicated result on the subsequently charged offenses. [8] [9] [10] [12]

25. Vito Rizzuto’s Later Guilty Plea

The three-captains murder case had consequences beyond New York. The 2010 federal prosecution memorandum concerning Salvatore Vitale states that Vito Rizzuto, a Montreal-connected Bonanno figure, pleaded guilty on 4 May 2007 to racketeering conspiracy that included the murder-conspiracy and murder predicates for the three captains, and received a ten-year term on 25 May 2007. This was a guilty plea in a later case, not a verdict against every other individual named in earlier investigations. [12]

The fact that a decades-old New York homicide surfaced in litigation involving a figure associated with Canadian organized crime illustrates the family’s wider networks. It does not mean that Indelicato’s personal biography should be transformed into a general history of Montreal rackets. Those networks deserve their own sourced articles and should be linked here only where they help explain adjudicated responsibility for the 1981 murders. [12] [17]

26. Violence, Power, and the Limits of Mafia Loyalty

Indelicato’s story is often narrated as a morality tale about betrayal. The federal record gives a more useful account of rival captains competing for control of a hierarchical criminal enterprise, with alliances that changed after the 1979 Galante killing. The three-captains attack eliminated an important faction and strengthened those allied with Rastelli, but the cost was homicide, retaliation threats and the destruction of trust within the organization. [1] [3] [8] [12]

The case also exposes the limits of titles. A boss in prison might retain recognition while losing direct control; a captain might command meaningful influence without broad authorization from the Commission; and an associate deeply trusted by a crew might actually be an undercover federal agent. These differences are the structural story behind the dramatic individual deaths. [1] [3] [7]

27. Popular Retellings and the Historical Record

The “Three Captains” murders gained lasting visibility through coverage of the Donnie Brasco investigation, later criminal trials and screen portrayals of organized crime. Such accounts can lead readers to believe that every meeting, line of dialogue and weapon used at the attack is settled. In fact, the surviving evidence comes in layers: near-contemporaneous recordings, indictments, jury verdicts, appellate summaries, cooperating-witness testimony and secondary reconstructions. [1] [5] [7] [8] [12]

In particular, narrative retellings sometimes blend Alphonse “Sonny Red” with Dominick “Sonny Black,” and may confuse the father with Anthony “Bruno” during discussion of the Galante murder. Each carries its own biography, position, prosecution history and date of death. Editorial cross-references should preserve those identities rather than privileging a memorable dramatic presentation. [1] [3] [14]

28. Historical Significance

Indelicato’s historical significance rests primarily on his role in one of the most consequential internal Bonanno power struggles of the late twentieth century. The murder of three captains demonstrated the lethal stakes of a contested hierarchy. The threats against his son revealed the prospect of continued retaliatory violence. The Donnie Brasco case showed how those same secretive decisions could become evidence through a sustained federal infiltration. [1] [2] [5] [7]

Subsequent Massino and Rizzuto cases then demonstrated that murder accountability could reach back decades. For the MGU Crime Encyclopedia, this makes Indelicato a useful point of entry to the linked histories of Rastelli, Napolitano, Massino, Anthony Indelicato, Giaccone, Trinchera, and federal RICO prosecutions. The emphasis belongs on source-backed causation and victims rather than mythologizing criminal status. [8] [9] [10] [12]

29. Research Questions and Evidentiary Limitations

Important gaps remain. The birth year and details of Indelicato’s private life call for verified civil documents; a definitive roster of his crew requires contemporaneous investigative records rather than association by rumor; and the exact sequence of the 5 May 1981 attack should be tested against complete trial transcripts and authenticated exhibits. Separate records of initial body recovery would help reconcile the May 24 and May 28 discovery dates found in different sources. [1] [4] [12] [15]

This first-edition account does not claim to have collated every FBI teletype, the full original Massino or Rizzuto plea proceedings, the handwritten burial-site case file, or certified vital records. Where a federal filing is encountered as reproduced text, it is identified as such. These constraints justify narrow wording, not unsupported invention. Subsequent editions can expand the source register while retaining the established division between allegations, adjudications and historical interpretation. [1] [8] [12] [15]

Sources and Further Reading

The citations separate appellate findings, original government allegations, defendant-specific verdicts, contemporary trial reporting, and later secondary interpretation. Readers should consult the linked full decisions and the underlying trial exhibits when making claims more precise than those stated here.

  1. United States v. Ruggiero, 726 F.2d 913 (2d Cir. 1984), decided 18 January 1984. Controlling published appellate reconstruction of Bonanno factions, the three-captains murders, Joseph Pistone recordings, investigation, and 1982 verdicts.
  2. United States v. Ruggiero, 754 F.2d 927 (11th Cir. 1985), decided 5 March 1985. Appellate summary of the 1982 New York indictment, three murdered captains, the distinct plot targeting Anthony “Bruno” Indelicato, and Ruggiero/Cerasani dispositions.
  3. United States v. Indelicato, 865 F.2d 1370 (2d Cir. 1989) (en banc), decided 13 January 1989. Court case concerns ANTHONY Indelicato, son of Alphonse: Galante triple homicide and RICO convictions, not a conviction of Alphonse.
  4. United Press International, “Six alleged mobsters, including four charged with killing three…,” 24 November 1981. Contemporaneous reporting on charges and recovery of the elder Indelicato’s body in Queens on May 24, 1981; criminal accusations here are not verdicts.
  5. United Press International, John F. Rhodes, “The slaying of Mafia captain Alphonse ‘Sonny Red’ Indelicato…,” 4 August 1982. Contemporaneous coverage of undercover testimony, death threats against Anthony, and distinctions between the factions.
  6. Joyce Wadler, “Undercover FBI Agent Testifies Mob Assigned Him to Kill Rival Leader,” Washington Post, 4 August 1982. Near-contemporaneous court reporting on Pistone’s testimony about the planned killing of Anthony “Bruno” Indelicato.

  10. Anthony M. DeStefano, “Mobster found guilty of seven murders,” Newsday via Spokesman-Review, 1 August 2004. Contemporary account that the July 2004 jury found Joseph Massino responsible for the three captains’ murders.

Research and Publication Limitations

This scholarly first edition draws on published judicial opinions, selected contemporary newspaper accounts, an FBI institutional case study, and a reproduced federal prosecution memorandum. It does not purport to be an exhaustive transcript-by-transcript archival edition. Civil-registration data, individual FBI files, every original taped conversation, complete Massino and Rizzuto plea and trial transcripts, and full forensic case reports remain uncollated.

The birth year is left unresolved because incompatible dates circulate without a verified vital record. The 1981 body-recovery date is based on contemporaneous reporting and is explicitly distinguished from later versions. A 1989 appellate case about Anthony “Bruno” Indelicato must not be confused with the elder Alphonse. No external photograph is embedded because the pictured subject’s identity and usage rights have not been independently verified. Remote assets, sitewide CSS and live server integrations have not been tested.
