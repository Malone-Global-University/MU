1. Historical Overview

Natale Joseph Evola, usually called “Joe Diamond” or “Joe Diamonds,” was a senior member of the New York organization known as the Bonanno crime family. His career is unusually well documented at its middle: a detailed New York State investigative profile identifies his transport businesses in 1958; two federal appellate decisions explain the disposition of separate criminal cases; and 1970 newspaper reporting places him amid the organization’s disputed succession. His later designation as boss is widely accepted, although the date on which he acquired exclusive authority is not. [1] [2] [3] [4] [6]

The principal historical issue is not whether Evola was important. He moved at the intersection of garment delivery, organized-crime associations, national narcotics prosecution and the Bonanno family’s attempt to recover from a long leadership conflict. The issue is how to distinguish documented acts—business offices, court judgments and reports of his role—from less verifiable claims about what every member accepted behind closed doors. This biography therefore treats “boss” as a historical classification whose timing and practical scope require qualification. [1] [2] [4] [6]

Evola’s death in August 1973 ended a comparatively short period of leadership. Philip “Rusty” Rastelli is generally identified as his successor, but accounts of a preceding leadership committee and Rastelli’s growing authority show why a neat one-name-per-year chart is inadequate. [6] [7] [12]

2. Identity, Name, and Vital Records

The strongest readily available contemporary identification is the New York State commissioner’s April 1958 report, which calls the subject “Natale Joseph Evola” and states that he was born in New York City on 22 February 1907. The report records an address in the Bay Ridge area of Brooklyn. These are data entered by investigators, not a certified birth-certificate transcription; they nonetheless carry more documentary weight than undated repetitions on commercial biography sites. [1]

Public accounts most commonly call him “Joe Diamond”; “Joe Diamonds” and “Diamond Joe” also appear in retellings. A nickname should not be taken as a legal surname or evidence of a particular offense. Biographical metadata should record the variants as aliases while retaining the full name from the 1958 state record. [1] [4] [7]

The conventional date of death is 28 August 1973, at age sixty-six. Contemporary obituary coverage exists, but the precise hospital or location of death is inconsistently repeated in derivative accounts. This edition does not choose between conflicting hospital descriptions without obtaining the obituary image or a vital record. [6] [7]

3. Brooklyn and Castellammarese Connections

Evola grew up in a community with enduring connections to western Sicily, especially Castellammare del Golfo. Subsequent writers identify his parents as Filippo and Francesca and describe a Brooklyn household with several siblings. Those details are plausible but presently derive from later biographies rather than a verified household census and immigration documentation. For a definitive first-generation family history, birth, marriage and census records would need to be checked independently. [7] [13]

The historical importance of the Castellammarese connection lies in social networks, not in the premise that an ethnic origin entails criminal activity. Among a much larger immigrant community, a small group of men formed durable ties through businesses, kinship and neighborhood acquaintance. Evola became associated with members of the organization that Joseph Bonanno led after Salvatore Maranzano’s death in 1931. The available contemporary record confirms his later association with Bonanno, but it does not support reconstructing every private meeting during his youth. [1] [6] [13]

Accounts that place Evola among Bonanno’s close companions in the early 1930s are useful as historical leads. However, treating attendance at a wedding or an acquaintance from a neighborhood as conclusive proof of rank would be poor methodology. The date and function of any alleged ceremonial role should be reconciled with original wedding documentation before being made an exact biographical milestone. [7] [13]

4. Early Police Contacts and Their Outcomes

The state’s 1958 profile lists an August 1931 New York City arrest under a Penal Law provision and notes that the matter was discharged several days later. It also lists a coercion arrest in June 1932 with acquittal in September of that year. These entries are valuable precisely because they include dispositions. “Arrested” and “convicted” are different categories, and neither a discharge nor an acquittal should be silently converted into a finding of criminal conduct. [1]

Such entries do not, by themselves, establish Evola’s role in the Castellammarese War or prove a string of violent crimes in the 1930s. Later accounts sometimes use the early arrest record to imply a larger history of “strong-arm” enforcement; where that inference relies solely on a dropped or unsuccessful prosecution, it remains unproven. An encyclopedia should present the state profile accurately and identify any further claims as historical allegations. [1]

This distinction becomes particularly important because Evola later sustained a genuine federal narcotics conviction. Combining the two early cases, the Apalachin case, and the narcotics case under a generic label such as “many convictions” would obscure what the courts actually decided. [1] [2] [3]

5. The Garment District Economy

The commercial garment industry depended on moving clothing, fabric and components among cutting rooms, sewing contractors, showrooms and warehouses. A relatively small number of delivery companies could become strategically important in a dense district where time-sensitive shipments and street access determined whether manufacturers met orders. Transport created legitimate economic opportunities as well as points at which coercion, labor intimidation or anticompetitive arrangements could be imposed. [1] [8] [10]

In Evola’s case the business connection is not simply a later folklore assertion. The 1958 New York State record identified him as an officer of two garment delivery firms. That establishes a specific, contemporary commercial footprint. It does not establish that every route, truck, client or transaction of the firms was illicit. Investigative conclusions about influence over associations must be reported as allegations unless corroborated by adjudication or documentary business records. [1]

The industry setting helps explain why men with senior positions in organized-crime networks developed relationships across the formal boundaries of Mafia families. Suppliers, haulers, union officials and customers formed overlapping commercial systems. Those networks were not equivalent to Mafia membership, and an ordinary employee’s work for a company should not be presented as proof that the employee belonged to an illegal organization. [8]

6. Belmont, Amity, and Other Business Interests

The Reuter report lists Evola as president-treasurer of Belmont Garment Delivery Company and president of Amity Garment Delivery Company, both given at 242 West 37th Street, Manhattan. It also describes him as operating Frateli Berio Distributing Company. These are concrete historical identifications suitable for targeted verification in incorporation records, commercial directories, labor filings and property records. [1]

The same document reported a belief that Evola exercised influence over the shoulder-pad trade with the support of figures identified as Joseph Stracci and Harry Strasser. This was an agency assessment, not a finding in a final civil or criminal judgment presented in the report. It deserves attribution and should not be transformed into a factual claim that every shoulder-pad maker paid him or participated in criminal agreements. [1]

A later scholarly study of organized crime’s New York business holdings discusses Evola’s interests in Amity and other trucking operations. Taken together, the primary profile and the secondary business history suggest that garment transport was an enduring source of organizational leverage. The exact legal ownership and management of individual firms at different dates remain questions for corporate-file research. [1] [8]

7. Apalachin, November 1957

On 14 November 1957 a large gathering of organized-crime figures was interrupted at the residence of Joseph Barbara near Apalachin in upstate New York. The publicity that followed made the meeting a watershed in public understanding of nationwide Mafia associations. Evola was identified among those encountered by authorities. The 1958 state report provides individual details beyond the generic observation that he attended. [1] [3] [9]

That profile says Evola was encountered near the property in a Cadillac registered to Alfred Angelicola and was then with Frank Cucchiara of Boston. It also contains hotel details and a supplementary account of travel with other participants. These contemporaneous observations establish how investigators reconstructed his presence, although the inconsistencies in travel descriptions should not be smoothed into a falsely exact minute-by-minute itinerary. [1]

The event’s historical significance is clear, but police discovery of an association does not itself establish an unlawful purpose for the meeting. The subsequent federal litigation made precisely that distinction, and the final appellate result is a defining part of Evola’s legal record. [3]

8. Investigation, Indictment, and Evidentiary Burden

Following the gathering, investigators sought to learn who had attended, who arranged travel, and what participants discussed. Federal prosecutors eventually brought a conspiracy case accusing a group of men of obstructing justice and giving false or evasive accounts about the meeting. Evola was one of the defendants. The prosecution proceeded against an entire alleged agreement, not merely the isolated question of his presence on a road. [3]

Contemporaneous state investigation, grand-jury questioning and the later indictment arose at different times and under different legal powers. This matters because the existence of state investigative suspicion cannot substitute for proof of an agreement violating federal law. The trial jury initially convicted defendants in the federal case, but appellate review was decisive. [1] [3]

A biographical entry should therefore distinguish the celebrated “Apalachin raid” from the separate court prosecution over responses to investigators. Evola’s eventual narcotics conviction also proceeded from a different indictment and evidentiary record. Confusing those two proceedings creates an inaccurate account of what he was punished for. [2] [3]

9. The 1960 Reversal of the Apalachin Conviction

On 28 November 1960 the Second Circuit reversed the judgments in United States v. Bufalino, 285 F.2d 408. The court concluded that prosecutors had not adequately proved a common agreement among defendants to lie about Apalachin and had failed to establish necessary evidence that the participants anticipated the later sworn inquiries when the alleged conspiracy supposedly began. These deficiencies required reversal, not simply an administrative reduction in sentence. [3]

The opinion also stressed that the government had not proved an illicit purpose for the meeting itself. It did not proclaim that every attendee had lawful business dealings or that organized crime was imaginary; it held that the specific criminal conspiracy alleged had not been proved. The difference between broad historical inference and the elements of a criminal offense is central to a legally accurate encyclopedia. [3]

Evola consequently should not be listed as having a surviving federal conviction for the alleged Apalachin cover-up. When that case is mentioned, the correct disposition is “convicted at trial; conviction reversed on appeal in 1960.” His narcotics conviction, by contrast, survived appellate review. [2] [3]

10. Federal Narcotics Investigation, 1955–1958

A parallel federal investigation pursued a multistate narcotics conspiracy involving figures from several New York Mafia networks. The trial record summarized in United States v. Aviles described transport, distribution, financing, and planning over a period beginning in the mid-1950s. The eventual indictment named dozens of people, and seventeen stood trial; government witnesses testified about deliveries and meetings linked to several principal participants, including Evola. [2] [9] [11]

The appellate opinion describes conversations concerning proposed narcotics movements between Miami, Mexico, Puerto Rico, Philadelphia and New York. The testimony did not have to show Evola physically carrying every shipment. Prosecutors argued that persons who planned markets and allocation could participate in a conspiracy even when other actors moved the packages. This feature of the case would later be critical to the affirmance. [2]

Newspaper reporting from June 1958 called Evola a Brooklyn garment trucker and identified him as one of the persons named in the new federal case. That contemporary item proves the public status of the allegation then, but the court opinion is the proper source for the final disposition. [2] [11]

11. What the 1960 Appellate Opinion Actually Described

The central government witness was Nelson Silva Cantellops, whose extensive testimony recounted meetings and transactions with various defendants. The Second Circuit summarized his account of a 1956 discussion about expanding narcotics distribution in the East Bronx, with Evola among the men at the meeting. The opinion also referred to Evola being present for discussions of delivery arrangements. [2]

The court assessed those statements in the context of other witnesses and the entire trial record rather than assuming that a single meeting made every participant guilty. Its analysis expressly identified Evola among the central conspirators supported by sufficient proof and explained that planning and strategy could establish participation in the illegal enterprise. That is a judicial finding in the appellate review of a specific conviction. [2]

Historians should nevertheless avoid embellishing the judgment. The opinion does not show that Evola personally imported every consignment, controlled all narcotics arriving from Sicily, or held the same position in the Genovese family as Vito Genovese. The case establishes the conspiracy conviction and its evidentiary basis, not a complete org chart for every criminal enterprise involved. [2]

12. Sentence and Affirmance

On 17 April 1959, following the federal narcotics trial, Evola received a ten-year prison sentence and a $20,000 fine. The judgment was among the sentences reviewed in United States v. Aviles, decided on 12 January 1960. The court affirmed the convictions of Evola and most appellants, reversing the judgment of Benjamin Rodriquez alone. [2]

The separation of appellate outcomes is fundamental: the narcotics case was affirmed in January 1960, whereas the Apalachin obstruction conspiracy was overturned in November of the same year. These holdings are not contradictory. They were different prosecutions, involving different charges, witnesses, trials and legal questions. [2] [3]

Biographical accounts sometimes call the narcotics matter a ten-year “heroin trafficking conviction.” The legally precise description is conspiracy to violate the federal narcotics statutes as described in the court’s opinion. A prison term in a judgment also does not by itself establish a verified date of release or the exact amount of time actually served; those require correctional records or reliable contemporary reporting. [2]

13. Bonanno Ties and Cross-Family Relationships

The 1958 state profile lists Joseph Bonanno and Carmine Galante among Evola’s reputed associates, alongside figures identified with other parts of the Mafia landscape. That list supports the conclusion that investigators placed Evola in a broad network; it does not independently prove that every contact was a co-conspirator in every event. [1]

At the narcotics trial, the named participants came from several organizations later conventionally described as separate Mafia families. Organizational affiliation in a mid-century investigative document is not always consistent with later succession charts. The historian should not conflate participation in a narcotics conspiracy with permanent transfer of a member from the Bonanno family to a different family. [2] [9]

For the Bonanno organization, such cross-family ties were useful and controversial. They could facilitate access to money and clients while also making senior figures susceptible to pressure from powerful external bosses when Joseph Bonanno’s leadership was contested. The documentary record permits analysis of this structural problem; it does not permit assigning every private negotiation to Evola without specific evidence. [1] [6] [13]

14. The Banana War and Joseph Bonanno’s Exit

During the 1960s Joseph Bonanno’s authority was contested by rival leaders and by opponents associated with the Mafia Commission. Gaspar DiGregorio was backed as a replacement, the family split, and Paul Sciacca became the leading figure of a successor faction. The family’s conflict entailed violent incidents and a prolonged inability to settle leadership in a way all senior members accepted. [6] [13] [14]

Joseph Bonanno’s withdrawal from New York leadership in 1968 did not mean that his entire prior organization immediately became an orderly hierarchy. Several senior members had already chosen sides or attempted to avoid open commitment. Evola’s position is often interpreted as less directly identified with the worst of the factional conflict, a factor that may have made him an acceptable compromise after other leaders’ influence weakened. [6] [13] [14]

The interpretation that Evola “healed” the organization should be tested against later developments: the persistence of leadership committees and disputes under Philip Rastelli and Carmine Galante suggests that the structural tensions survived Evola’s lifetime. He may have offered a degree of continuity without creating a permanently settled succession. [6] [13] [14]

15. Paul Sciacca and the Interim Leadership Arrangement

Published lists commonly give Paul Sciacca a period ending around 1971, followed by Evola as boss through August 1973. This is a useful shorthand for an encyclopedia succession table, but it cannot be treated as an exact chronology of a public appointment. Contemporary sources complicate it. [4] [6]

The Associated Press report of 12 June 1970 describes Sciacca as a figure the Justice Department had identified as head of the former Bonanno family; in the same dispatch it says published accounts regarded Evola and Philip Rastelli as two of three successors to Sciacca. The report is therefore direct evidence that a successor-panel narrative circulated publicly before 1971. It cannot determine which of the three held exclusive command on any particular day. [4]

Specialist histories similarly describe a period of management by changing senior panels after Joseph Bonanno’s departure and emphasize Evola and Rastelli as influential figures. The most defensible formulation is that Evola emerged as a leading successor around 1969–1971 and is conventionally listed as the family’s boss from 1971 until his death in 1973. [4] [6] [14]

16. Leadership, Influence, and the Meaning of Boss

The title “boss” in organized-crime history can denote three distinct things: a recognized office, the day-to-day control of a body of members, or the practical ability to resolve conflicts and command revenue-producing relationships. These functions did not necessarily coincide during the Bonanno family’s unsettled transition. A member may have been accepted by outside leaders yet unable to compel unanimous compliance inside the family. [4] [6]

Evola’s garment business history, longstanding personal contacts, and absence from the most visible leadership feuds provided reasons why he may have seemed a workable senior choice. That assessment rests on synthesis, not a located transcript of his induction or appointment as boss. It should not be cast as an eyewitness account of an election. [1] [6] [13]

Unlike Joseph Bonanno, Evola did not leave a widely circulated memoir that defines his authority from his own perspective. The historian must therefore reconstruct the office primarily through investigators’ reports, press observations and later accounts. This makes ambiguity a feature of the evidence, not a problem to conceal with an artificially precise accession date. [4] [5] [6]

17. Garment-District Operations in the Early 1970s

Evola’s continuity in trucking positioned him within a district in which access to loading space, business associations, drivers and delivery relationships had substantial economic value. These areas could support racketeering, coercion, illicit loans or patronage when controlled by criminal actors. At the same time, the ordinary garment economy contained many legitimate firms and workers; the presence of organized-crime figures did not make every commercial transaction unlawful. [1] [8] [10]

New York’s Joint Legislative Committee on Crime studied organized crime’s penetration of legitimate business in this period. Its publication record offers an avenue for identifying hearings, documents and witnesses. Because the committee materials have not been fully collated for this biography, no specific quotation is placed in Evola’s mouth and no finding from an unseen transcript is treated as established. [10]

Later research connects Evola to multiple garment trucking interests, while the older state profile supplies two corporate titles and a third enterprise. The analytical lesson is that business offices were not an incidental cover story: they could be part of a durable local structure that outlived the changing personnel of an illegal organization. The extent of his influence in any single year still requires business and case-file corroboration. [1] [8]

18. The 1970 Brooklyn Grand-Jury Indictment

In June 1970 a Brooklyn grand jury investigation produced criminal-contempt indictments against dozens of reputed organized-crime figures who had refused to answer inquiries into the infiltration of legitimate businesses. An Associated Press dispatch published on 12 June included Natale Evola by name, noted his association with Apalachin, and linked the inquiry to a broad law-enforcement sweep. [4]

The report also mentioned Paul Sciacca and Philip Rastelli in the same leadership context. This juxtaposition is useful because it catches the organization in transition rather than reconstructing it from later retrospective labels. It does not, however, demonstrate that those named coordinated the refusals to testify or acted as a single criminal group in the 1970 case. [4]

The newspaper establishes that Evola was indicted as reported at the time, not that he was ultimately convicted of contempt. A final docket entry, plea, dismissal, trial verdict or appellate disposition would be needed before an encyclopedia could state a final result. This biography keeps the charge separate from the affirmed 1959 narcotics sentence. [2] [4]

19. Organized Crime, Narcotics, and Public Policy

Evola’s career overlapped a period when police and federal investigators increasingly treated organized crime as a national network rather than a collection of isolated local gangs. Apalachin, the Federal Bureau of Narcotics prosecutions, and televised investigations each helped shape this public understanding, but different agencies and proceedings produced different kinds of evidence. [1] [2] [3] [9]

The narcotics judgment is a formal judicial outcome. The 1958 state commissioner’s report is an investigative profile. The 1970 press item describes criminal charges and reported leadership. All three are valuable, yet they should not be flattened into an undifferentiated “government proof” category. [1] [2] [4]

The era also brought competing narratives inside organized-crime circles about whether senior members permitted, discouraged or personally benefited from narcotics sales. Evola’s affirmed conviction makes the personal legal record concrete, but it does not by itself establish the official policy of every Bonanno administration over the following decades. [2] [9] [13]

20. Philip Rastelli and the Next Succession

The usual summary names Philip “Rusty” Rastelli as Evola’s successor following the August 1973 death. A specialist leadership history explicitly states that Rastelli took control in 1973, after having already been prominent in earlier successor arrangements. This provides the most useful broad sequence: Sciacca’s disputed leadership, Evola’s short term, Rastelli’s assumption of control. [6]

A complication is that material placed in the Congressional Record in February 1973 already described Rastelli as an acting Bonanno boss and Evola as another senior boss. Such language is evidence of contemporary reporting, not an official congressional determination about internal Mafia rank. It supports the interpretation that authority could be divided or differently described depending on who was speaking. [12]

Carmine Galante’s emergence after release from prison in 1974 also destabilized later administration. Evola had died before that later struggle unfolded. Assigning him operational responsibility for events after his death would be an obvious anachronism; his relevance is that the unsettled structure he inherited became part of the context for his successors. [6] [13]

21. Final Illness and Death, 1973

Evola died on 28 August 1973, in New York, according to the standard historical chronology. He was sixty-six years old. Contemporary obituary reporting attributes the death to cancer. The exact place of death is not consistently recorded in derivative biographies, and this edition does not select a hospital or borough without collating the original newspaper image and vital-registration material. [6] [7]

The death altered the succession problem because the candidate who had provided a degree of continuity was no longer available. Rastelli is commonly shown as the next boss, though the transition should be understood against evidence that Rastelli had exercised influence before 1973. [4] [6] [12]

Public memorial records list an interment at Calvary Cemetery in Queens, but a burial entry alone is not proof of the internal events of a criminal organization. If the cemetery detail is retained in a later editorial revision, a verified interment record should be cited; it is not necessary for the central historical argument of this page. [7]

22. Historical Significance and Legacy

Evola is important in three different ways. As a participant in the 1950s federal narcotics conspiracy, he is connected to a case with a substantial surviving judicial record. As a garment delivery company officer, he illustrates the interface between legitimate commercial structures and alleged racketeering influence. As a post-Banana-War senior figure, he exemplifies the difference between formal succession lists and practical authority in an unsettled organization. [1] [2] [4] [6] [8]

His biography also demonstrates an uncommon legal contrast: one federal conspiracy conviction was affirmed while another, arising from the inquiry into Apalachin, was reversed. Those outcomes must stand together. Selecting only the conviction or only the acquittal-like result would distort both the legal record and the public history of organized crime. [2] [3]

Evola did not achieve the name recognition of Joseph Bonanno or Carmine Galante, but the surviving government documents make him an unusually useful subject for evidence-based biography. For the MGU collection he is a bridge between the original leadership, the mid-century national prosecutions, and the more unstable years that preceded Philip Rastelli’s administration. [1] [2] [4] [6]

23. Source Criticism and Further Research Priorities

A rigorous biography should preserve the origin of each statement. A court judgment establishes the final result of the case and the record of evidence the judges evaluated. A government intelligence report can establish what officials believed or were told at a particular time. A newspaper can establish what was reported publicly. A later historian may interpret patterns, but interpretation must not be silently elevated into a judicial finding. [1] [2] [3] [4]

Next steps would include examining the full FBI Vault file; obtaining the actual 1973 New York Times obituary image; checking 1930–1932 police dispositions against certified court records; reconstructing the incorporation histories of Belmont and Amity; locating the final disposition in the 1970 criminal-contempt matter; and finding leadership reports from 1969 through 1973 that name Evola and Rastelli together. [1] [4] [5] [7] [10]

Until those steps are completed, the best account is precise in what it knows and explicit about what it cannot settle. The core facts—that Evola was a garment transportation executive, attended Apalachin, had a narcotics conviction affirmed in 1960, was named among Sciacca’s successors by 1970, and died in August 1973—are well supported. The exact moment of exclusive succession remains an interpretive problem. [1] [2] [3] [4] [6] [7]

Research and Publication Limitations

This first-edition biographical article is a documented historical synthesis, not a complete original-file transcription. The 1958 New York State profile and 1960 appellate opinions have been checked for the claims attributed to them. The FBI Vault collection is identified, but its scanned file has not been comprehensively read; the 1973 newspaper obituary facsimile and the complete 1970 contempt docket have not been obtained. Full 1971 committee transcript testimony, corporate ownership books and the person’s certified vital records are also outside this edition.

Inferences about family policy, leadership selection, narcotics networks and control of the garment industry have been limited accordingly. All alleged violent conduct and all company-to-organized-crime relationships require individual source corroboration. The page does not claim to have confirmed the precise hospital or place of death, or an uncontested date on which Evola became sole boss.

Next documentary priorities: inspect the complete FBI Vault case file; obtain New York City vital records and the 1973 obituary facsimile; trace corporate filings for Belmont and Amity; verify 1970 grand-jury case outcomes; and reconcile dated 1968–1973 surveillance and intelligence records for the Evola–Rastelli succession.
