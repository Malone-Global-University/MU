1. Historical Overview

Joseph Gregory Gallo (April 7, 1929–April 7, 1972), nicknamed “Crazy Joe,” was an American organized-crime figure associated with the Brooklyn-based Profaci crime family, subsequently called the Colombo crime family. Alongside his brothers Lawrence (“Larry”) and Albert (“Kid Blast”) Gallo, he became a leading figure in an insurrection against boss Joseph Profaci beginning in 1961. Unlike the crime-family leaders who sought recognition through the Mafia Commission, Gallo became famous for publicly defying his own organization’s hierarchy. [2][6][12] His adult career divides into a documented 1961 criminal prosecution and long imprisonment, a spectacular public reemergence in 1971, and a fatal shooting at a Little Italy restaurant in April 1972. The most secure primary material identifies him and traces his extortion case, while contemporary journalism records the police investigation into his death. Claims that he personally murdered rival bosses, organized Joseph Colombo’s shooting or was killed by Frank Sheeran require a different and much lower level of confidence. [1][2][4][7][9] Gallo’s outsized place in Mafia history also reflects the press’s appetite for an intellectual, flamboyant, self-conscious outlaw. His literary and entertainment connections are real subjects of study, but fame should not obscure the intimidation, extortion and factional violence underlying his notoriety. [6][9][10]

2. Identification and the Two Joseph Gallos

A July 19, 1967 FBI memorandum, prepared in response to a Royal Canadian Mounted Police inquiry, explicitly identifies Joseph Gregory (“Crazy Joe”) Gallo as a member of the Joseph Colombo family, with FBI number 120842A. It separately identifies Joseph N. Gallo, FBI number 244809, as a member of the Carlo Gambino family, adding that investigators knew of no family relationship between them. The first is the subject of this biography; the second was a different, older Mafia figure. [2] This primary distinction is vital for search indexes, photograph captions and succession chronologies. A historical page should not silently substitute Joseph N. Gallo’s career, photograph or alleged offices for Joseph Gregory Gallo’s. Nor should the convenient label “boss” be applied to Joseph Gregory without explaining that he led a dissident faction rather than a Commission-recognized Mafia family. [2][3][4]

3. Birth, Family and Brooklyn Childhood

Gallo’s generally reported birth date is April 7, 1929, in Brooklyn, New York. Family-history records identify Umberto Silvio Gallo Sr. and Maria Nunziato as his parents. The familial identification is consistent with the broader biographical tradition, though this edition has not directly examined an original birth certificate. Because he died on April 7, 1972, he was killed on his forty-third birthday. [6][8][10] The Gallo name became closely associated with Red Hook and adjacent South Brooklyn crime networks. Some accounts distinguish his Red Hook origins from periods when the family lived farther inland. Readers should not mistake the neighborhood of a gang’s principal base for a documented address occupied by every family member at every stage. [6][8][12]

4. Lawrence, Albert and the Gallo Family Faction

Lawrence Gallo, known as Larry, and Albert Gallo, known as Kid Blast, were Joseph’s brothers and fellow members of the faction associated with their family name. Their relative seniority, operational roles and relationships shifted as incarceration and violence removed individual members from the street. References to “the Gallo brothers” do not always imply that all three were present or directing a specific event. [3][6][12] Contemporary FBI reporting in June 1963 focused on Larry’s efforts to seek outside assistance and peace mediation. That document demonstrates that the faction’s leadership and bargaining could continue while Joseph was in state prison; it does not make Joseph the personal author of every subsequent action. [1][3]

5. Entry into the Profaci Organization

By the 1950s Gallo and the people around him operated within the underworld environment connected with Joseph Profaci, whose family controlled important Brooklyn rackets. Secondary histories describe gambling, loan-sharking and enforcement activities involving Gallo’s circle. The exact command decisions behind individual crimes are unevenly recorded, and an affiliation alone does not prove personal responsibility for every offense associated with the family. [6][12] The Profaci organization’s internal patronage helped produce the dispute. Bosses allocated opportunities and enforced payments through their own networks; ambitious younger members could view those practices as extraction at their expense. Historians often interpret the Gallo rebellion as both a fight over money and a challenge to Profaci’s right to govern. [6][12]

6. Organized-Crime Structure and the Meaning of Rebellion

The term crime family describes an organized group, not a straightforward biological family. Boss, underboss, consigliere and captain denote reported positions, while “crew” may refer to an informal operating group and “faction” to a coalition challenging recognized leadership. Joseph Gallo’s reputation grew from his role in an insurgent faction inside or against the Profaci organization, not from a documented election as the undisputed head of the Colombo family. [2][3][6] The dispute had an institutional dimension. A boss had to maintain credibility with his own members and the larger Mafia leadership, while rebels could survive by holding neighborhood bases and recruiting or negotiating with other groups. Documentary evidence of a single meeting or reported alliance cannot settle the question of who commanded the entire organization. [3][12]

7. The 1957 Albert Anastasia Assassination Allegation

Albert Anastasia was murdered in a Manhattan hotel barbershop in October 1957. Later Mafia histories and museum accounts identify Gallo as suspected or believed to have played a role in the operation. That characterization is a statement about later attribution, not the finding of a criminal court that Gallo fired a weapon or issued an order. [6][11] The story is especially prone to embellished detail because the killing was visually dramatic and became a reference point in later screen depictions of organized crime. The present first edition does not assign Gallo a proved role in Anastasia’s death; that would require stronger original investigative or trial evidence. [6][11]

8. Public Hearing and the Cultivation of an Image

A famous photograph of Gallo wearing dark sunglasses during a 1958 Senate inquiry into labor racketeering helped establish his public image. The Mob Museum describes his appearance and invocation of constitutional protections. A hearing appearance should not be confused with a conviction; witnesses may appear because investigators believe they possess information, not because guilt has been established. [6] Gallo’s publicity differed from the secrecy commonly associated with senior Mafia leaders. Later writers emphasized his appearance, verbal confidence and taste for theatrical effect. Those traits are pertinent to the history of the mass-mediated Mafia, yet contemporary images also helped construct a persona that could exaggerate what he personally controlled. [6][10]

9. Profit Distribution and the Abbatemarco Dispute

The 1959 killing of gambling operator Frank Abbatemarco is described by academic and organized-crime histories as a turning point in tensions between Profaci and the Gallo group. Secondary accounts say that the brothers expected a larger share of the victim’s numbers business and became dissatisfied when Profaci distributed the proceeds elsewhere. This explanation is plausible as a motive in the historical literature, but the precise financial promises and their participants are not authenticated here from original ledgers or sworn witnesses. [12] Such disagreements illustrate a defining feature of criminal enterprises: market control depended on violence and protection relationships, not enforceable commercial contracts. Internal conflict could therefore become armed conflict when competing factions disputed the right to allocate revenues and territory. [6][12]

10. The February 1961 Kidnappings

In early 1961 the Gallo faction abducted several prominent Profaci associates to force concessions from leadership. Accounts commonly date the action to February and include the detention of high-ranking figures; individual lists and descriptions vary in later retellings. The episode became the rebellion’s most memorable affront to Mafia hierarchy. [6][11][12] Profaci survived the episode without accepting permanent subordination. Reported agreements over money and control failed to end the mistrust. The rebellion’s importance was not simply the detention of particular men but the challenge to the premise that a family boss could exercise uncontested authority inside his organization. [6][12]

11. The First Gallo–Profaci War

Violence escalated across 1961 as loyalists and Gallo faction members struggled over revenue streams, neighborhood authority and survival. Shootings, ambushes and disappearances featured in the contemporary reporting and later history. A single figure for the war’s fatalities is difficult to sustain because accounts differ in dates, attributed participants and whether particular killings belonged to this dispute. [6][12] The vocabulary of a “war” should not imply two regular armies or an unbroken series of formally announced battles. These were clandestine organizations whose acts were often alleged by rivals, police or reporters before any court determined responsibility. The conflict can be reliably identified; the command responsibility for each incident requires incident-specific verification. [4][12]

12. Attack on Larry Gallo and the Persico Allegation

Accounts of an August 1961 attempt to strangle Larry Gallo at a Brooklyn lounge describe a turning point in the first war. Academic histories attribute a leading role to Carmine Persico and others; Larry survived after police intervention. The narrative has become central to the later reputation of Persico as a particularly ruthless Colombo figure. [12] That account is a historical attribution rather than a substitute for contemporaneous arrest paperwork and a resulting criminal judgment. It also concerns Larry Gallo, not an attack on Joseph himself. Chronological and personal precision matters in a history populated by repeated nicknames and overlapping family members. [3][12]

13. President Street, Red Hook and the Crew Headquarters

The Gallo faction used a headquarters on President Street in Brooklyn, described in secondary sources as a fortified neighborhood base during the confrontation. Its location near police facilities later became a frequent anecdote about the surreal proximity of the police and the gang’s ongoing activities. This location is historically meaningful as a neighborhood organizing site, but it was not a sovereign enclave outside New York law. [12] A responsible local history should attend to the experience of residents and nearby businesses, who faced intimidation, fear and uncertain public safety. Popular treatments sometimes focus on the crew’s swagger while minimizing people who had no role in the criminal organizations but lived within their sphere of violence. [6][12]

14. May 1961 Indictment in an Extortion Case

The separate criminal case that led to Gallo’s prolonged imprisonment can be traced through a federal judicial opinion. Judge Edward Weinfeld’s decision in United States ex rel. Gallo v. Follette records that Gallo was indicted on May 26, 1961. The charges concerned conspiracy and attempted extortion. This is a concrete legal chronology, unlike informal accounts that speak only of his being “locked up for the war.” [1] The opinion also describes threats and assaults alleged against the complaining witness and the unusual expense of protecting that witness. Those passages present the prosecution’s account of why the state pressed for trial and why further postponement was contested; they should not be copied into an unqualified list of separate convictions. [1]

15. The 1961 Trial and December Convictions

The trial proceedings were repeatedly postponed as Gallo’s chosen counsel became unavailable and attorneys litigated venue and publicity. The federal court reconstructed an October–November 1961 sequence in which the judge refused additional lengthy delays, substitute counsel attended, and Gallo did not cooperate with those lawyers. Later retellings often omit the complexity of this contested representation. [1] The state trial ended in convictions for conspiracy and attempt to commit extortion. The 1967 federal opinion establishes that the sentencing range was seven years and three months to fourteen years and six months. Some intelligence summaries rounded it to “seven and a half to fourteen and a half years”; the published judicial wording is the more precise formulation for a reference biography. [1][2]

16. The 1967 Habeas Corpus Decision

While confined at Green Haven Prison, Gallo sought federal habeas corpus relief under the Sixth and Fourteenth Amendments. He argued, among other things, that the state trial’s handling of his chosen attorney and the selection of an initial juror had denied him due process. The resulting opinion, 270 F. Supp. 507, is among the best surviving records of his formal legal history. [1] Judge Weinfeld rejected Gallo’s counsel-of-choice argument and also concluded that the prejudicial-publicity contention had not been properly exhausted in state proceedings. This opinion should not be described as a new trial for murder or as proof about Gallo’s role in rival mob killings; its subject was the earlier extortion case and the procedural rights Gallo asserted. [1]

17. Prison Years and Internal Faction Continuity

Incarceration removed Joseph from day-to-day Brooklyn operations for much of the 1960s, but did not eliminate the wider Gallo faction. His prison experience later became central to the story of his transformed public persona: accounts describe extensive reading, painting, language and contact with a more diverse range of prisoners. These details are substantially from reporting and reminiscence rather than from comprehensive prison registers in this edition. [1][6][9] The distinction matters because secondary narratives sometimes imply that Joseph directed every move in the family war from prison. The most direct federal material instead shows other participants negotiating and maneuvering on the street during his confinement. [3]

18. The June 1963 FBI Record of Mediation

A June 18, 1963 FBI teletype described intelligence that Larry Gallo had approached Nicholas Bianco in Providence to seek help from Raymond Patriarca. The same message referred to Charles Lo Cicero, a former acting consigliere in the Magliocco–Profaci organization, as a figure who had been attempting to mediate between the Gallo and Magliocco camps for approximately two years. [3] This is important primary evidence that the conflict was neither a simple personality contest between Joseph and Profaci nor a war that ended automatically with Profaci’s death. Its source was confidential police and FBI information. Accordingly, the named conversations and threats remain intelligence reports unless supported by independently verified testimony or records. [3]

19. Profaci’s Death and the Magliocco Transition

Joseph Profaci died in June 1962. His longtime underboss Joseph Magliocco took effective control but struggled to secure full recognition from the Mafia Commission. The family’s leadership was therefore disputed at a time when the Gallo faction had not yet been fully reconciled with the organization. [2][3][12] Some narratives collapse these developments into a neat succession of Profaci, Magliocco and Colombo. In practice the family experienced internal negotiation, competing claims and changing recognition by powerful outsiders. For Gallo’s biography, those ambiguities explain the persistence of his factional grievances after the founding boss’s death. [3][12]

20. Joseph Colombo’s Rise and the Name Change

Joseph Colombo emerged as the family leader during the 1963–1964 transition, after Magliocco’s loss of authority. The organization would thereafter generally be called the Colombo crime family. The 1967 FBI document identifies Joseph Gregory Gallo as connected to the Colombo organization, providing a contemporary official naming reference. [2][3] A changed family name did not mean that every existing grievance disappeared. The Gallo faction’s hostility originated in the Profaci era and persisted through successor regimes. Hence the First Gallo–Profaci War and later Colombo-era conflict belong to a continuing organizational history, although particular negotiations and assassinations must be dated separately. [3][6]

21. Race, Prison Associations and Public Image

One strand of Gallo’s post-prison celebrity concerned the claim that he had formed friendships with Black prisoners and rejected some Mafia prejudices. Reporters portrayed him as unusually cosmopolitan for an organized-crime figure. Evidence of personal relationships does not mean he operated a racially egalitarian organization, and descriptions of prison experiences should be attributed to those who reported them. [6][9] A particularly serious analytical error is to treat such associations as sufficient proof that Gallo orchestrated the 1971 shooting of Joseph Colombo, whose assassin was Black. Race is not evidence of command, and no sound historical account should manufacture a conspiracy by association. [6][7]

22. Release in 1971

Gallo was released from imprisonment in 1971 after spending much of the 1960s in custody. The precise computation of time served, parole adjustments and earlier detention is more complicated than the widespread phrase “ten years in prison.” The 1967 court ruling independently establishes his incarceration during that earlier year, while later reporting records his return to public life. [1][6][9] He emerged into a transformed New York: downtown arts circles, popular fascination with gangster stories and new public campaigns about Italian-American identity provided opportunities for a figure who courted publicity. This visibility was substantial but could also create serious personal-security vulnerabilities. [6][9]

23. Writers, Actors and the Manhattan Social World

During 1971–1972, Gallo associated with journalists, writers and entertainers, including actor Jerry Orbach. Retrospective accounts describe his interest in a possible prison-based screenplay and his presence in Manhattan nightlife and cultural venues. It is not necessary to suppose that he had become a legitimate artist, or had abandoned criminal relationships, in order to recognize the distinct cultural environment in which he moved. [6][9] The celebrity relationships were reciprocal: Gallo gained access and a transformed image, while observers acquired a sensational subject who could speak about violence, crime and books. Such relationships should be documented without allowing an articulate self-presentation to stand in for a verified change of occupation. [6][10]

24. Marriage, Household and the Final Weeks

Reports from April 1972 identify Gallo’s new wife as Sina and describe their marriage as roughly three weeks old at the time of his death. They record her young daughter Lisa among the guests at the final restaurant outing. Genealogical records give the marriage in March 1972. Contemporary stories may call Lisa Gallo’s “daughter” or “stepdaughter” without consistent precision; she was Sina’s daughter and present with the group. [4][8][10] This household setting shaped public reaction to the murder. The shooting was not an isolated underworld confrontation in an empty place: people dining with him, workers and other patrons were exposed to gunfire and the bodyguard was wounded. [4][9][10]

25. Joseph Colombo’s 1971 Shooting: What Is Known

Colombo was shot and catastrophically injured at a public gathering at Columbus Circle in June 1971. Reports identified the immediate assailant as Jerome Johnson, who was killed moments after the attack. Authorities and later writers examined possible organized-crime motives, but there is no cited court judgment establishing that Joseph Gallo planned or ordered the shooting. [6][7] The suspicion is historically important because the family was already divided and because Colombo’s incapacity opened another struggle over succession. Still, the proposition “Gallo was suspected” must not be rewritten as “Gallo ordered.” The difference between an investigative theory and a proven conspiracy is indispensable in this entry. [6][7][11]

26. Return to Brooklyn and Continuing Tensions

The aftermath of Gallo’s release included new disputes about old territory, autonomy and recognition. Joseph Yacovelli and other Colombo figures appear in contemporary descriptions of the organization after Colombo was incapacitated. A report of someone serving as acting head does not necessarily mean that all captains or dissident crews accepted the designation. [4][6] Gallo’s public exposure, unresolved factional claims and proximity to the Colombo family’s instability have led many observers to interpret his killing as retaliation or preventive violence. That interpretation is plausible within the context, but intent and the identity of order-givers require separate evidence. [4][7][10]

27. The Night of April 6–7, 1972

Gallo celebrated his forty-third birthday in Manhattan on the night of April 6. Accounts describe an evening at the Copacabana followed by a late-night stop at Umberto’s Clam House in Little Italy. His wife Sina, her daughter, his sister Carmella and bodyguard Peter “Pete the Greek” Diapoulas were among the party. Witness-based reports vary somewhat over exactly who sat where and which entertainers joined or declined the restaurant visit. [4][9][10] These discrepancies should not obscure the firmly established setting: early morning, April 7, 1972, at a restaurant on Mulberry Street. Gallo was not killed in a private residence or at a Mafia Commission meeting. His death occurred in a public dining establishment while relatives and employees were nearby. [4][5][10]

28. Shooting at Umberto’s Clam House

A gunman or gunmen entered the restaurant and fired at Gallo, striking him and wounding bodyguard Diapoulas. Contemporary police reporting described a man using a .38-caliber handgun; later witness and informant stories alleged the participation of additional accomplices. Accounts agree Gallo staggered out of the restaurant and died in the vicinity of Hester Street in the early hours of April 7, 1972. [4][6][9][10] The precise number of shooters, entrance route and sequence of every shot differ in source traditions. It would be irresponsible to convert every detail from a later cinematic recreation into historical fact. The fatal event, date, general location and bodyguard’s injury are far more secure than the complete reconstruction of the shooting team. [4][7][10]

29. Immediate Police Investigation

On April 13, 1972, journalist Nicholas Gage reported that detectives were seeking two men with reported ties to the Colombo family and Joseph Yacovelli’s circle. The report included Chief of Detectives Albert Seedman’s physical description of a suspected gunman: a middle-aged man of relatively short stature, dark hair and thinning hair at the front. Investigators at that stage were reportedly distinguishing a suspected shooter from a getaway driver. [4] The article was published during an active investigation and explicitly described suspects rather than convicted offenders. It is a high-value record of the direction police were looking within a week of the homicide, not a final judicial finding. [4]

30. Informant Joseph Luparelli and the DiBiase Attribution

Subsequent reports and legal commentary describe Joseph Luparelli’s account that he helped assemble or participated in a group connected with Gallo’s killing, naming associates including Carmine “Sonny Pinto” DiBiase and Philip Gambino. Jack Goldsmith’s evidentiary review explains that the account informed investigative and grand-jury work in 1972. This is a named, attributable theory supported by more contemporary investigative context than later entertainment narratives. [7][11] Yet a participant’s statement can still require corroboration and does not automatically constitute a final homicide conviction. This biography presents DiBiase as a suspect identified through informant and investigative accounts, rather than as a person adjudicated guilty in a cited homicide trial. Exact membership in the shooting party is left to the evidence. [4][7]

31. Frank Sheeran and the Later Competing Confession

Decades after Gallo’s death, accounts attributed to Frank Sheeran claimed that he alone shot Gallo. The idea entered popular culture through I Heard You Paint Houses and its film adaptation, The Irishman. This is not contemporaneous police identification; it is a late-life claim that conflicts with significant investigative descriptions and accounts from people present. [7][11][13][14] Jack Goldsmith and other commentators questioned the confession’s plausibility, particularly the discrepancy between Sheeran’s height and appearance and the description of the suspected gunman. The case should be taught as a demonstration of how a dramatized crime can acquire the status of a remembered event even where the evidence remains contested. [4][7][13]

32. Was the Killing Ordered as Retaliation?

A leading theory views Gallo’s homicide as retaliation for the earlier shooting of Joseph Colombo or as an effort to end his faction’s destabilizing challenge. The proximity of the events and Gallo’s history of conflict are grounds for investigating the theory, not proof that it was the actual motive. Some Colombo-linked suspects appeared in the contemporary investigation; rival accounts differ about who authorized the killing. [4][6][7] This entry therefore does not assign an unqualified order to Joseph Yacovelli, Vincent Aloi, Carmine Persico or any other figure without the necessary original evidence. Organizational suspicion, informant allegations, grand-jury testimony and a criminal conviction are distinct evidentiary states. [4][7]

33. The April 10 Funeral and Burial

Gallo was buried at Brooklyn’s Green-Wood Cemetery following a funeral on April 10, 1972. Eric Pace’s contemporaneous report documents a procession of limousines, relatives at the graveside and Peter Diapoulas attending despite his gunshot injury. The funeral occurred amid other gangland-style killings that alarmed investigators and journalists. [5][8] Some contemporary headlines framed those deaths as reprisals, even as police cautioned that not all incidents had been connected. Their juxtaposition demonstrates the risk of inferring a single coordinated murder campaign simply because violent incidents happened within days of one another. [5]

34. Aftermath of the Gallo Faction

Joseph’s death removed the most publicly recognizable figure from the insurgent camp, but the faction’s history was neither created nor extinguished by one man. His surviving relatives, former associates and Colombo adversaries continued to operate within an organization whose leadership remained unstable during the 1970s. [3][6] The long-term consequences were organizational rather than merely personal: Mafia factions had demonstrated that a recognized boss could be challenged internally for years and that press attention could intensify conflicts otherwise managed through secrecy. A precise post-1972 succession among Gallo associates belongs in separate biographies grounded in the later records. [4][6][12]

35. Gallo and the Myth of the Intellectual Gangster

Retrospectives emphasize Gallo’s reading, artistic interests and theatrical manner. Such qualities were unusual enough to attract journalists who saw him as a countercultural or literary figure, despite a career marked by coercion and violent factional conflict. The resulting image is an important artifact of 1970s New York, but does not make him morally or politically representative of the communities among whom he lived. [6][9] The phrase “intellectual gangster” can help explain why writers were fascinated by him; it is a weak substitute for evidence about specific criminal conduct. A biographical encyclopedia should document both the cultivated image and the independently verifiable case record, without allowing one to erase the other. [1][6][9]

36. Literature, Film and Music

Jimmy Breslin’s novel The Gang That Couldn’t Shoot Straight and its film adaptation became closely associated with satirical portrayals of the Gallo-era New York mob. Later representations and Bob Dylan’s “Joey” reinforced the romantic image of a complex outlaw. These works demonstrate Gallo’s afterlife in popular culture; fiction and song cannot resolve historical questions about particular homicides. [6][11] The 2019 film The Irishman presents one version of Gallo’s death as part of Frank Sheeran’s purported career. Its representation deserves discussion because many readers encounter the character first through film, but the article’s chronology is grounded in primary court material and contemporary 1972 reporting instead. [7][13][14]

37. Evidence Hierarchy and Source Criticism

The core source hierarchy in this biography is straightforward. A published federal opinion establishes what the courts held about the extortion case. An FBI memorandum resolves a potentially consequential mistaken identity. A 1963 teletype shows what investigators were told about mediation. Newspapers reveal how the 1972 investigation was reported at the time. Retrospective books and film scholarship explain how competing stories emerged. [1][2][3][4][7] None of these classes is infallible. Courts may recount disputed allegations before determining procedural rights; informant files may transmit false or incomplete claims; journalists may report police hypotheses that later change; memoirists may reshape memories. The article’s assertions are therefore calibrated to the record they actually rest on. [1][3][4][7]

38. Historical Assessment and Research Priorities

Gallo remains a significant figure because the First Gallo–Profaci War exposed the fragility of criminal leadership, the 1961 extortion prosecution created a long and unusually well-documented custodial period, and his 1972 death became an enduring public symbol of Mafia violence. His career connects gangland history, criminal justice, neighborhood conflict and the construction of celebrity. [1][4][6][12] Further scholarship should seek original New York trial transcripts, parole and corrections files, full police homicide files, contemporary sworn testimony identifying suspects, and contemporaneous records of the faction’s financing. Those materials are more likely to refine the historical picture than repeating a late memoir’s confident identification of the killer. [1][4][7]

Research and Publication Limitations

Identification and court records. The 1967 federal district opinion and National Archives FBI memorandum were inspected for the identity distinction, trial chronology and sentence. These do not establish every alleged criminal activity in the 1950s or 1960s. No original birth certificate, 1961 state trial transcript or full corrections file was obtained.

Gallo family war. The 1963 FBI message is contemporaneous but reports confidential-source intelligence, not sworn findings. Detailed descriptions of the February kidnappings, Abbatemarco revenue division and Larry Gallo’s strangling rely partly on secondary historical synthesis. Precise casualty figures and the authorship of individual acts are not asserted as proven.

1972 shooting. The April 14 and April 11 1972 Observer editions containing licensed reprints of New York Times reports were located and reviewed, including page images. Later Luparelli, DiBiase and Sheeran narratives are compared, but no original criminal homicide judgment identifying the triggerman was located for this article. The number of gunmen, ordering authority and motive remain attributed rather than settled.

Birth and family dates. Genealogical compilations were consulted for family relationships and marriage, but original civil registration records were not separately authenticated. Small differences in news reports regarding companions, table position and shot counts are preserved as source variation when material.

Scholarly classification. This biography is historical analysis of organized crime and prosecution, not an endorsement of criminal activity. Nicknames and terms such as “boss,” “war” and “hitman” are explained in their evidentiary and organizational context.

Media and deployment. No portrait is embedded because image identity, reuse rights and credit require independent clearance. Existing MGU library components, canonical address, shared stylesheet and footer conventions are retained. Structure and internal links are validated locally; production deployment and external site assets have not been tested.
