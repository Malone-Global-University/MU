1. Historical Overview

Gregory Scarpa Sr. (1928–1994) was a Brooklyn-based member and crew leader of the Profaci–Colombo crime family whose career combined organized criminal activity with confidential reporting to the Federal Bureau of Investigation. His case is distinctive because the surviving judicial record does not merely describe an informant inside a Mafia organization: it details a senior criminal participant who remained active during a shooting conflict while passing information to an FBI supervisor. The relationship became an issue in multiple federal prosecutions and later in the supervision of confidential sources. [1] [2] [4] [5]

Two overlapping narratives therefore demand separate treatment. In the criminal narrative, Scarpa took part in extortion, fraud, loansharking and lethal factional violence; he eventually pleaded guilty to murder counts and received a ten-year prison sentence. In the institutional narrative, the FBI used him as a high-level source, tolerated a single-handler arrangement and failed to identify or properly escalate serious violent activity. Courts subsequently examined what the government knew, what it disclosed, and whether identified failures required new trials. The answer depended on the evidence and legal issues in each case, not merely on the disturbing nature of the relationship. [1] [2] [4] [5]

2. Name, Dates, Nicknames, and Identification

The subject of this article is Gregory Scarpa Sr., not his son Gregory Scarpa Jr. Public biographical directories commonly give May 8, 1928 as the elder Scarpa’s birthday and June 4, 1994 as his death date. The September 2005 Department of Justice inspector-general report and the 1997 Orena opinion independently corroborate death in June 1994, but the exact civil dates have not been checked here against birth and death certificates. For this reason, the article differentiates the widely published exact dates from the firmer judicially documented year and month. [2] [8] [15]

“The Grim Reaper” and “the Mad Hatter” occur in criminal-history accounts, and the 1997 judicial discussion also notes the nickname “General Schwarzkopf” in relation to Scarpa’s violent reputation. Such labels are evidence of how associates and later narrators represented him; they are not stand-alone proof of any particular alleged killing. The name Gregory Scarpa Jr. belongs to a different person with his own prosecution history, a distinction vital to understanding the 1987 investigation and subsequent disclosure disputes. [2] [8] [15]

3. Brooklyn Origins and the Early-Years Evidence Gap

Biographical narratives situate Scarpa in the New York metropolitan world that supplied recruits to Brooklyn’s Italian-American criminal groups. Yet the public decisions central to this article spend comparatively little time on his schooling, household finances, employment as a youth, or the precise year of his first criminal association. The evidentiary record is disproportionately rich in the final fifteen years of his life because that is when informant records, criminal trials, and witness debriefings became accessible to the courts. [2] [8] [13]

A rigorous biography should not fill those early gaps by extending late-life descriptions backward. Scarpa’s later violence is established or extensively documented in particular proceedings, but it does not authenticate every childhood anecdote or early “first murder” story reproduced in popular works. The more defensible starting point is the criminal network in which he emerged and the documentary trail that becomes clear in the 1970s and 1980s. [2] [8] [13]

4. The Profaci Organization and Colombo-Family Continuity

In the historical sequence of the New York Mafia, the organization later known as the Colombo family was led by Joseph Profaci, followed by the contested Magliocco transition and the 1960s prominence of Joseph Colombo. This institutional background matters because biographies sometimes attribute to Scarpa an unbroken command position across several decades. Such an interpretation exceeds what the principal federal records show. They identify him as an established member and subsequently an acting captain or capo, not as the formally recognized head of the entire crime family. [2] [4] [5]

By the early 1980s he operated a Brooklyn crew within the Colombo organization. Courts treated the organization’s hierarchy as relevant to the way information flowed, to the identification of potential targets and to the loyalty demanded during its internal war. His personal authority depended on crew membership, criminal revenue, reputation for violence and access to outside information, rather than on a documented election or designation as permanent family boss. [2] [4] [5]

5. Soldier, Capo, and Acting Captain: Reading the Titles

Federal opinions describe Scarpa as a made member of the Colombo family by the early 1980s and as an acting captain aligned with the Persico side during the 1991–1992 conflict. In organized-crime terminology a captain commands or supervises a crew; an acting captain carries out those responsibilities without necessarily holding a permanent formal rank. The same decisions sometimes use capo, captain and high-ranking member as practical descriptions rather than as a single, fixed appointment record. [1] [2] [5]

That distinction limits a frequent retrospective exaggeration. Scarpa was dangerous and influential, but he was neither Joseph Colombo’s successor nor Carmine Persico’s formal replacement. His battlefield role during the civil war should not be conflated with legitimate governance over the broader family. The sources support a strong operational role, while leaving some questions of internal title, duration and recognition unresolved. [1] [2] [5]

6. Violent Reputation and the Limits of a Body Count

Court records and major reporting describe Scarpa as an active participant in killings and murder conspiracies, and some sources attribute a much larger total to him than the convictions expressly established. A common weakness in Mafia biographies is to convert an estimated body count, an associate’s recollection and a proven conviction into a single numerical fact. These are different categories of evidence. Neither the federal decisions nor the DOJ inspector-general discussion provides a complete court-verified enumeration of every death ascribed to him. [2] [5] [8]

The article therefore examines named incidents where the courts provide factual accounts and separately notes the presence of broader allegations. The “Grim Reaper” reputation helps explain why his activities created intense pressure on criminal rivals and law-enforcement supervisors; the nickname does not replace an individualized evidentiary assessment for homicide victims or their families. [2] [5] [8]

7. The Criminal Portfolio: Fraud, Extortion, Gambling, and Loans

The Second Circuit described Scarpa’s criminal activity as including credit-card fraud, extortion, gambling, narcotics trafficking and loansharking. These were not simply unrelated sidelines: access to cash, threats of enforcement and the collection of illicit debts enabled a crew leader to maintain associates and exert control over local business activity. A 1997 district-court opinion likewise identified the Wimpy Boys Social Club as a base for an operation involving gambling, narcotics and extortionate lending. [2] [4] [5]

For a historical business analysis, revenue from such conduct must not be treated as a verified financial statement. The visible court record supplies categories of illegal activity, selected prosecutions and witness descriptions, not a complete audited ledger. It demonstrates diversification of criminal income, but it does not establish a precise annual income, personal net worth or lifetime revenue total for Scarpa. [2] [4] [5]

8. The 1986 Credit-Card Prosecution and Sentencing Controversy

The Orena decision recounts a 1986 federal case concerning possession and trafficking in fraudulent credit cards. It states that Scarpa had executed a plea arrangement carrying the possibility of a substantial prison term, yet the sentence imposed was probation and a fine. The court later considered claims that his FBI relationship and the flow of confidential information may have affected how he was treated. This case is a useful early example of the difficulty of assessing an informant who continued ordinary criminal activity. [1] [2] [8]

The existence of lenient sentencing is documented. The proposition that a particular FBI employee unlawfully caused that result is a different and more demanding proposition. Later opinions discuss apparent efforts by DeVecchio to protect Scarpa, while the details of every communication with the sentencing court were not conclusively adjudicated as a separate criminal offense. The distinction matters when tracing institutional responsibility. [1] [2] [8]

9. The Wimpy Boys Social Club and Crew Administration

The Wimpy Boys Social Club became an important setting in evidence surrounding Scarpa’s Brooklyn operations and the younger Gregory Scarpa’s associates. The club’s role in surveillance and criminal investigations shows how nominally ordinary social premises could provide repeated contacts among members of a crew. The organization’s activities did not become lawful because it was also an informal gathering place; nor does attendance alone establish culpability for every person who entered. [1] [2] [16]

Court discussions of the club are particularly valuable because they connect crew life to the government’s evidentiary methods: wiretaps, anticipated arrests, cooperating defendants and investigations of alleged narcotics and fraud activity. Informant leaks, if they occurred, could undermine all of these methods by alerting participants before evidence could be secured. [1] [2] [16]

10. First FBI Contact: Why the Start Date Is Qualified

The most secure continuous informant chronology begins in 1980. A federal district court reviewing the case in 1997 stated that Scarpa had been in contact with FBI agents in the 1970s and possibly the 1960s, but that the relationship later lapsed. The court then described renewal with R. Lindley DeVecchio in December 1980; the 2007 DeVecchio-related federal record cites reapproval in July 1980. These descriptions concern different administrative milestones or rely on different reconstructions, and should not be flattened into one indisputable “first recruitment” date. [1] [2] [6] [13]

In this edition, 1980 is treated as the documented beginning of the DeVecchio-era relationship, while earlier decades are identified as reported contacts. The FBI’s archival collection offers a promising route for finer reconstruction, but relevant releases would have to be inspected document by document before determining all original contact and termination dates. [1] [2] [6] [13]

11. 1964 Mississippi Civil-Rights Investigation: Claims and Cautions

The murders of James Chaney, Andrew Goodman and Michael Schwerner in Mississippi during June 1964 and the federal investigation that followed are firmly documented. Their remains were found in August after an extensive investigation. Separate stories published much later claim Scarpa traveled to Mississippi to obtain information from Ku Klux Klan members and that he coerced a suspect using violence. The 1997 Orena court expressly described parts of this dramatic narrative as unconfirmed rumor. [2] [10] [11]

This distinction is ethically significant. The civil-rights victims and the historical prosecution should not be turned into an unverified episode of Mafia heroism. The FBI’s official case history does not itself establish the alleged Scarpa interrogation scenario. A comprehensive inquiry would require contemporaneous case files and corroborated witness testimony. This biography records the existence of the allegation, explains its evidentiary standing and avoids treating torture claims as demonstrated fact. [2] [10] [11]

12. R. Lindley DeVecchio and the Sole-Handler Relationship

R. Lindley DeVecchio was a New York FBI supervisory special agent involved in organized-crime investigations and the principal contact with Scarpa during the 1980s and early 1990s. The court record and Justice Department review establish that he served as Scarpa’s sole handler for an extended period, notwithstanding a protocol requiring two agents for such a sensitive informant. The arrangement concentrated knowledge of meetings, payments and source reporting in one professional relationship. [1] [2] [5] [6]

This did not automatically make every piece of information from Scarpa false. It did, however, create weak checks on source motivation and opportunities for selective reporting. The inspector general describes failures of local supervision and reporting mechanisms, making the case an illustration of an organizational-control problem rather than a controversy confined to personalities alone. [1] [2] [5] [6]

13. The Top-Echelon Informant and FBI Reporting

FBI records identified Scarpa as a top-echelon source. Such informants were recruited because they could describe criminal leadership, meetings, personnel changes and planned offenses that outside investigators could not easily observe. In Scarpa’s case, reporting about the Colombo family included its hierarchy, rivalries and suspected violent plans. Court review of FBI source summaries—often described as Form 209 reports—later became central to questions about what the government had learned and what it withheld from defendants. [1] [2] [4]

The value of internal access was accompanied by a structural risk: Scarpa could supply accurate information about rivals while omitting or disguising his own participation. The Second Circuit later discussed examples in which Scarpa’s reporting attributed criminal acts to other people or failed to acknowledge his responsibility. A source’s knowledge and a source’s candor are separate qualities and must be assessed independently. [1] [2] [4]

14. Payments, Expectations, and Possible Protection

The 2013 Second Circuit opinion states that the FBI paid Scarpa for information and that he expected leniency if he were convicted. Those incentives matter because a source who supplies useful intelligence while continuing criminal operations may see a relationship with law enforcement as a shield. Contemporary public reporting and judicial review also addressed whether DeVecchio supplied advance warnings and exerted improper influence in Scarpa’s favor. [1] [2] [5]

There is no basis to presume that any informant payment was itself improper. The documented problem lies in retaining an actively violent source, failing to transmit relevant information and supplying confidential investigative intelligence in ways that could benefit the source. The historical judgment should rest on the documented control failures, not on an unsourced theory of a permanent immunity agreement or formal permission to kill. [1] [2] [5]

15. Gregory Scarpa Jr. and the 1987 Arrest-Warning Allegations

The younger Gregory Scarpa was implicated in the Wimpy Boys investigation in the late 1980s. Prosecutors and cooperating witnesses later recounted that the son or his associates received advance information about intended arrests. The 1997 district court discussed Carmine Sessa’s account that names of expected arrestees had circulated before law enforcement acted; Fredric Dannen’s reporting recounts that the younger Scarpa initially avoided apprehension while associates were arrested. [1] [2] [8] [16]

These facts are relevant to the elder Scarpa because of claims that the leaked material came through DeVecchio. The existence of the investigation and subsequent sworn accounts is established; responsibility for every purported leak was contested. A careful reconstruction distinguishes a witness’s assertion about the origin of the list from direct observation of an agent transmitting it. [1] [2] [8] [16]

16. Wiretap Intelligence and the Alleged Flow of Secrets

The inspector-general account describes suspicion that Scarpa learned about electronic surveillance at the Wimpy Boys Social Club and about intended law-enforcement operations. The practical significance is straightforward: if targets know an investigation’s methods or timetable, they can evade arrest, silence witnesses or alter their behavior. In a violent Mafia environment, disclosure of prospective cooperating witnesses can also create immediate physical danger. [1] [2] [4] [16]

Federal decisions refer to evidence supporting some unauthorized disclosures while treating other alleged leaks as matters of inference or witness testimony. The biography therefore does not attribute all compromised operations to one conclusively proved transfer. Instead it charts what the courts and the inspector general found, which claims were corroborated by multiple debriefings, and where the records leave uncertainty. [1] [2] [4] [16]

17. Carmine Persico, Victor Orena, and the Leadership Dispute

The Colombo family’s internal conflict developed while boss Carmine Persico was imprisoned and Victor Orena was functioning as acting boss. Rival factions contested authority; disputes involved not only personal loyalty but access to criminal revenue and control of crews. By late 1991 a shooting war had begun, and Scarpa played an aggressive role on the faction aligned with Persico. Federal cases identify the violence as a central feature of the criminal enterprise, not an unrelated series of street crimes. [1] [2] [4] [5]

Scarpa’s position as an informant made this conflict unusual. Information submitted to the FBI could alert investigators to attacks, yet information provided back to Scarpa could potentially shift the balance among the factions. Courts later assessed whether this reciprocal relationship helped generate false accusations or improperly influenced criminal trials. The existence of a genuine factional war was upheld in the relevant appellate litigation even while investigators criticized the handling of Scarpa. [1] [2] [4] [5]

18. Persico-Faction Alignment and Operational Power

Scarpa’s alignment with the Persico side is documented in district and appellate decisions. The record depicts him not as a neutral observer but as a participant who helped organize violence while reporting to the FBI. For understanding organized-crime governance, this places him within a nested command structure: his crew, the broader Persico-aligned network, and the competing Orena organization. A powerful crew leader could shape events without holding the family’s top title. [2] [4] [5]

Later defenses argued that the conflict was more accurately understood as a personalized Scarpa–Sessa confrontation than a true Persico–Orena war. The courts discussed that contention and, in the cases examined here, did not simply replace the charged family conspiracy with the defense account. This is an important limitation on treating one partisan witness’s narrative as the entire history of the war. [2] [4] [5]

19. Late 1991: The War Becomes an Armed Conflict

As rival factions prepared and attempted attacks, Scarpa’s crew participated in the escalating violence. The 1997 Orena opinion recounts plans, surveillance of rivals and attempted ambushes and describes a rise in Scarpa’s informant reporting after fighting began. Such materials support the existence of coordinated lethal activity, although particular acts require attention to the testimony and findings associated with each prosecution. [2] [5] [8]

The violence extended beyond the armed participants. Several public accounts record bystanders killed or wounded during the conflict, a reminder that descriptions such as “mob war” can obscure the impact on residents who were neither family members nor criminal rivals. This article does not glamorize the fighting as tactical prowess; it treats it as a series of crimes with identifiable legal and human consequences. [2] [5] [8]

20. December 1991: The Vincent Fusaro Killing

The district court’s 1997 reconstruction identified Scarpa as a participant in the early-December 1991 killing of Vincent Fusaro, an Orena-side associate. Later in the same opinion, the court described a guilty plea that included a count involving Fusaro’s murder. This is a much stronger evidentiary position than a nickname-based allegation: a named incident appears both in a judicial factual narrative and in a discussion of Scarpa’s subsequent criminal disposition. [1] [2] [4]

An account of the killing should nonetheless avoid assigning each operational detail—who fired which shot, who issued the order and how every participant arrived—unless supported by the underlying evidence. The published opinion establishes Scarpa’s responsibility at the level described; it does not supply a complete, uncontested reconstruction of every moment. [1] [2] [4]

21. January 1992: The Nicky Grancio Killing

The same 1997 decision describes Scarpa helping murder Nicky “Black” Grancio in early January 1992 as the factional war continued. This event illustrates the transition from threats and retaliatory planning to repeated killings carried out within a narrow period. The court’s treatment is also important because the retrospective record can be more precise about participants in some events than about the motives that each person claimed at the time. [2] [8]

It would be misleading to equate the court’s statement about participation with proof of a particular gunman’s precise position or a comprehensive list of everyone who ordered the killing. The event is included as a judicially described incident, while the narrower issue of what Scarpa was convicted of is handled separately in the legal-history sections. [2] [8]

22. May 1992: The Larry Lampesi Murder

The Orena opinion also connects Scarpa to the killing of Larry Lampesi in late May 1992. The sequence helps explain why FBI personnel suspected that a top-echelon source was deeply implicated in the very violence the Bureau was tasked to investigate. The Justice Department’s retrospective review treated this conflict between criminal activity and source retention as a failure of effective control. [1] [2] [4]

Federal judges repeatedly examined the difference between knowing that an informant had access to planned violence and knowing that he was himself participating in it. The former might make the source useful; the latter raises the question whether the source should be terminated and investigated as a suspect. In Scarpa’s case, the criminal conduct eventually resulted in arrest and prosecution. [1] [2] [4]

23. Other Victims and the Limits of a War-Wide Death Total

Several accounts of the 1991–1992 war identify killings, serious injuries and attacks on people with no role in the criminal organization. The 1996 New Yorker reconstruction offers a count for the initial period of intense fighting, while court decisions focus on the narrower incidents relevant to charges and motions. Their differing purposes make it inappropriate to assert an exhaustive war-wide total on the basis of a single retrospective article. [2] [8]

It is likewise important to distinguish Scarpa’s crimes from the entire Colombo conflict. He was a major participant, but the record describes many actors and rival groups. A study of all victims requires separate incident-by-incident documentation, including death certificates, police homicide files, indictments and judgments. Those records have not been comprehensively collated for this single-person profile. [2] [8]

24. March 3, 1992: Informant Closure

On March 3, 1992, senior FBI personnel closed Scarpa as a confidential informant after credible allegations emerged that he was involved in planning violent criminal activity. This date and the reason for the closure are set out in the DOJ inspector-general report and the Orena decision. DeVecchio was described as defending Scarpa against the suspicions; the handling of these warnings became a central example of the failure to escalate serious misconduct. [1] [2]

Termination from source status should not be mistaken for arrest, immunity withdrawal or a legal determination of guilt. It was an internal administrative action, and it did not immediately eliminate the underlying criminal danger. The event demonstrates that the Bureau possessed institutional mechanisms for suspending sources but did not consistently apply its controls to this particularly valuable one. [1] [2]

25. April 8–22, 1992: Reopening and Suitability Review

The DOJ inspector-general report records that DeVecchio initiated reopening Scarpa’s informant status in early April 1992. Reauthorization was granted on April 8, subject to a suitability inquiry, and on April 22 DeVecchio advised FBI headquarters that Scarpa had been deemed suitable. These administrative dates are important because they occurred during a period in which multiple violent incidents had already been attributed to Scarpa. [1] [2]

Reopening was not an exoneration by a criminal court. It was a source-management decision whose adequacy was later criticized. The record does not support the argument that the FBI had formally approved Scarpa’s murders; rather it demonstrates a pattern of deficient vetting, reporting and supervisory review that could enable continued criminal activity. [1] [2]

26. Special Agents Christopher Favo and Internal Alarm

During 1992, FBI Special Agent Christopher Favo and other personnel developed increasing concerns about DeVecchio’s relationship with Scarpa. The DOJ account states that Favo began withholding information from DeVecchio because he feared the investigation could be compromised. Other agents suspected Scarpa’s participation in killings but did not promptly submit the reports required by the informant guidelines. The result was a dangerous gap between field-level suspicion and institutional accountability. [1] [2] [16]

In January 1994, Favo and colleagues brought their suspicions to Assistant Special Agent in Charge Donald North, prompting a referral to the FBI Office of Professional Responsibility. These events unfolded after Scarpa’s criminal activity and after the trials of several Colombo defendants. Their timing matters for assessing prosecutorial disclosure obligations and for understanding how long an internal problem could remain unresolved. [1] [2] [16]

27. August 31, 1992: Firearms Arrest

The 1997 district-court account places Scarpa’s New York City police arrest on a firearms charge on August 31, 1992. Although the precise relationship between this local arrest and later federal charging instruments is sometimes compressed in summaries, the record plainly shows that law enforcement ultimately pursued him as a defendant rather than retaining him indefinitely as a protected source. [1] [2]

The event illustrates a change in operational posture. Once violence, weapons possession and crew activity became the focus of criminal prosecution, Scarpa’s role as a top-echelon source ceased to explain or excuse the conduct under investigation. The arrest by itself was not a conviction, and the later dispositions must be examined by their own dates and counts. [1] [2]

28. December 29, 1992: Shooting During Home Confinement

In a contemporaneous federal opinion, Judge Jack B. Weinstein described Scarpa leaving his residence between approximately 12:01 and 12:11 a.m. on December 29, 1992, contrary to his home-confinement restrictions. An exchange of gunfire followed, and Scarpa was wounded badly enough to lose an eye. The opinion explicitly stated that the details of the exchange were not known on the evidence then before the court. [2] [3]

Because those details were unsettled, it is inaccurate to turn the episode into a definitive action-movie reconstruction. The breach of release conditions and subsequent medical consequences were legally material. They contributed to the dispute about whether an extremely ill defendant could be detained safely and humanely while awaiting trial on violent federal charges. [2] [3]

29. February–March 1993: Judge Weinstein’s Hospital-Release Order

The federal district court in United States v. Scarpa, 815 F. Supp. 88, documented severe AIDS-related illness and a dispute over treatment available in detention. Judge Weinstein ordered release to a specified hospital under tightly controlled conditions, including a United States Marshals guard and financial obligations imposed on the defendant. The court distinguished punishment after conviction from the treatment owed to a person still awaiting trial. [3]

The order should not be represented as a finding that Scarpa was innocent, nor as a pardon for crimes already proved. It addressed detention and medical care under a specific procedural posture. The court acknowledged the grave allegations but emphasized that the legal system retained obligations even when dealing with a defendant accused of extreme violence. [3]

30. 1993 Federal Murder and Racketeering Charges

A federal charging instrument in early 1993 accused Scarpa of racketeering, murder-related conduct and weapons crimes connected to the Colombo conflict. The 2013 Second Circuit opinion identifies a February 4, 1993 indictment involving three murders. The 1993 hospital-release ruling confirms that he was awaiting trial on serious charges when his medical condition became central to the court’s detention decision. [1] [3] [5]

An indictment is an allegation, not a final factual finding. This article therefore avoids listing every indictment count as if it had resulted in a conviction. The ultimate disposition was a plea agreement and sentencing, which narrows the set of criminal acts that can be presented as legally adjudicated while leaving room to discuss other conduct described by witnesses and judicial findings. [1] [3] [5]

31. 1993 Guilty Plea and Ten-Year Prison Sentence

Court records establish that Scarpa pleaded guilty in 1993 and received a ten-year term. The March 1997 Orena ruling and September 2005 DOJ report describe a December 1993 sentence following pleas to two murder counts, one involving Vincent Fusaro. A 2013 appellate summary compresses the history by referring to a May 1993 plea and sentence. The full plea and sentencing minutes were not inspected for this edition, so the chronology follows the clearer separation between the plea during 1993 and the reported December sentencing. [1] [2] [5]

Accounts that assign Scarpa a life sentence or claim he was convicted of every murder with which he was associated conflict with the cited federal summaries. The ten-year sentence was a separate judgment from the pretrial medical order, and its limited duration should not be mistaken for evidence that the underlying violence was insignificant. [1] [2] [5]

32. HIV, AIDS, and the Reported Blood-Transfusion History

The medical fact firmly established in the 1993 district-court order is that Scarpa had advanced AIDS and was gravely ill. Contemporary reporting from 1992 and later accounts attribute his HIV infection to a transfusion associated with a 1986 surgical procedure, with a lawsuit naming a hospital and surgeon. That causal account was reported in the context of litigation; the underlying clinical files and final malpractice judgment were not consulted here. [3] [8] [9]

Consequently, this biography describes the transfusion explanation as a reported allegation rather than a verified laboratory finding. The circumstances are historically relevant because his illness altered detention, bail and sentencing issues and because later descriptions of his role in the gang war often stress the contrast between visible illness and continued violence. None of this invites speculation about personal medical facts beyond what the public court decision and reporting establish. [3] [8] [9]

33. June 1994: Death in Federal Custody

The DOJ inspector-general report records that Scarpa died in federal prison in June 1994. The Orena decision specifies that his death was associated with AIDS and occurred in federal custody in Minnesota. Public biographical references commonly give June 4, 1994 as the exact date; that exact day and the birth-date entry are secondary metadata here rather than a direct transcription of a civil death certificate. [1] [2] [15]

His death did not settle the allegations surrounding his informant relationship. On the contrary, the subsequent release of information, cooperating-witness debriefings and collateral criminal appeals made his conduct a central posthumous issue. Thus, 1994 marks the end of Scarpa’s life, not the end of the historical record relevant to the FBI’s supervision of him. [1] [2] [15]

34. January 1994 Internal Investigation and 1996 Closeout

In January 1994, reports concerning DeVecchio’s handling of Scarpa reached FBI supervisory personnel and the Office of Professional Responsibility. The inspector general describes subsequent consideration of possible wrongdoing and a review by the Justice Department’s Public Integrity Section. In September 1996 that section decided not to prosecute DeVecchio; the internal investigation closed, and he retired in October 1996. [1] [2]

This official outcome must be reported alongside the serious documented criticism. An agency finding that a relationship was mishandled is not identical to a criminal conviction for intentional misconduct. Likewise, a decision not to prosecute does not automatically refute concerns that the source relationship was dangerous or that FBI guidelines were inadequately enforced. [1] [2]

35. Carmine Sessa, Lawrence Mazza, and the Debriefing Record

The later reconstruction of Scarpa’s relationship with the FBI depended heavily on criminal associates who became cooperating witnesses. Federal opinions discuss information supplied by Carmine Sessa, William Meli, Lawrence Mazza and others about suspicious warnings, law-enforcement contacts, planned arrests and shootings. These narratives did not carry identical evidentiary status: some were corroborated by documents or other witnesses, while others rested on reported conversations with the now-deceased Scarpa. [1] [2] [4] [16]

The historical method used here is to identify which statements are witness reports, which are judicial findings, and which became contested appellate issues. That is particularly important when an informant may have intentionally misrepresented his own role. Witnesses may have had incentives to minimize or redirect their culpability as well, so corroboration and careful attribution are necessary in both directions. [1] [2] [4] [16]

36. 1997 Orena and Amato Post-Conviction Rulings

In Orena v. United States, 956 F. Supp. 1071, Judge Weinstein reviewed motions arising from the Colombo war prosecutions. The petitioners argued, among other things, that they should have been told about Scarpa’s informant relationship and DeVecchio’s conduct. The opinion devoted substantial attention to the war, the FBI’s reporting practices, the timing of informant closures and the defendants’ proposed alternatives to the government’s narrative. [1] [2]

The court acknowledged troubling aspects of the relationship yet did not grant wholesale relief on the theories presented in that proceeding. This is a pivotal example of the distinction between detecting government misconduct and satisfying legal tests for a new trial. Materiality, the relevance of undisclosed facts to a verdict and the strength of independent evidence remained decisive. [1] [2]

37. 2001 Monteleone Opinion: Informant and Co-Conspirator

The Second Circuit’s 2001 Monteleone decision supplied an important legal framework for understanding Scarpa’s dual activity. The court held that a person can be a government informant and still participate in a criminal conspiracy for his own purposes. Informant status, in other words, does not automatically erase criminal membership or convert all out-of-court statements into government-directed conduct. [4]

The same decision addressed how the withheld FBI Form 209 reports, information about Scarpa’s role in killings and accusations concerning DeVecchio could have affected trials of other Colombo defendants. Legal questions about co-conspirator statements, impeachment and disclosure led to fact-specific rulings. The opinion is therefore a source both for Scarpa’s conduct and for the limits of using that conduct to invalidate another defendant’s conviction. [4]

38. 2005 Department of Justice Inspector-General Assessment

The September 2005 Justice Department inspector-general report placed Scarpa within a broader review of FBI confidential-informant practices. It described failures of local supervision, inadequately reported violent conduct, conflicts over the decision to retain a source, and the risks created when the source handler monopolized sensitive information. The Scarpa section is particularly valuable because it integrates court records into an institutional analysis of informant governance. [1]

The review’s relevance extends beyond this biography. It shows how organizational incentives can favor intelligence gathering even when the subject is a major participant in the criminal activity under investigation. A high-value source requires stronger, rather than weaker, risk controls, independent assessments and documented escalation procedures when evidence of violence emerges. [1]

39. 2006 State Murder Indictment Against DeVecchio

A New York State grand jury indicted former FBI agent DeVecchio in 2006 on murder-related charges alleging that he provided information to Scarpa which facilitated killings. The federal case New York v. DeVecchio provides procedural background, while contemporary reporting explains the government’s allegations. These charges arose long after Scarpa’s death and followed extensive litigation concerning the source relationship. [5] [6] [7]

The allegations were not equivalent to a guilty verdict. At this stage the historian should describe precisely what the prosecution claimed, acknowledge conflicting evidence and await the disposition. It would be incorrect to cite the indictment as definitive proof that DeVecchio personally ordered killings or entered a criminal agreement with Scarpa. [5] [6] [7]

40. 2007 DeVecchio Trial and Dismissal

The 2007 New York prosecution collapsed during trial after older interview recordings contradicted significant portions of principal witness Linda Schiro’s testimony. Contemporary coverage reported that the judge dismissed the murder counts because the remaining evidence did not support the charged acts once that testimony was discredited. DeVecchio was not convicted in that case. [1] [6] [7] [14]

This outcome does not require erasing the inspector general’s separate findings or the federal courts’ criticism of the informant relationship. Criminal proof beyond a reasonable doubt, credibility of a key witness, administrative compliance and appellate disclosure analysis are different questions. Presenting the dismissal and the documented supervisory failings together is the only defensible summary of the mixed record. [1] [6] [7] [14]

41. 2013 Second Circuit Review of Scarpa and DeVecchio Evidence

In United States v. Orena (Sessa), decided March 29, 2013, the Second Circuit again considered claims stemming from the Scarpa–DeVecchio relationship. Its opinion described the FBI’s payments to Scarpa, the single-agent handling arrangement and concerns that DeVecchio supplied confidential investigative information. It also addressed whether information about those dealings required a new trial in the particular defendant’s prosecution. [5]

The court affirmed denial of relief under the legal standards it applied. Its account recognized serious informant-handling issues without endorsing every allegation advanced by the defense. This illustrates why conclusions about one prosecution should not be generalized to all Colombo war defendants, and why “FBI misconduct occurred” is not synonymous with “every related conviction was overturned.” [5]

42. FBI Vault, Public Records, and the FOIA Litigation

The FBI has placed a released Gregory Scarpa Sr. document collection in its online Vault. Separately, researcher Angela Clemente litigated requests for agency records, and the D.C. Circuit’s 2017 opinion addressed the adequacy of the government’s search and its use of exemptions. These resources show that posthumous research has continued through administrative disclosure as well as conventional criminal-case records. [12] [13]

However, the mere existence of an FBI release does not demonstrate that every page is complete, legible, unredacted or sufficient to establish a contested claim. This edition uses the Vault as a documentary gateway while relying principally on published judicial decisions and the inspector general for its verified narrative. A later archival edition should identify particular Vault files, page numbers, redactions and any conflicts among the original records. [12] [13]

43. Government Accountability and Informant Governance

The Scarpa case illustrates a failure of countervailing controls: access to unique intelligence created incentives to preserve an informant relationship even as the source continued criminal violence. A single handler could influence what supervisors saw, and incomplete reporting allowed concerns to remain dispersed among investigators. The official inspector-general review expressly examined the gap between written informant policies and their implementation in the field. [1] [2] [5]

The policy lesson is not that investigators can never use sources with criminal histories. It is that continuing crimes, particularly murder plots and assaults, require transparent escalation, supervision and independently verified accounts. Payments and operational benefits also create incentives that must be addressed explicitly. Failure to do so can endanger people and contaminate the fairness or perceived fairness of prosecutions. [1] [2] [5]

44. Conflicting Narratives and the Problem of Retrospective Certainty

Accounts of Scarpa often present one of two simplified portraits: an indispensable source whose information helped dismantle the Mafia, or a government-enabled assassin operating with blanket authority. The public judicial record is less simple. It demonstrates serious acts of violence and an unusually problematic handler relationship, but the legal findings differ by case and do not prove that every reported act was directed by a federal agent. [1] [2] [4] [7] [8]

Historical accuracy requires maintaining those distinctions even when they are inconvenient. Criminal testimony can reveal concealed operations but may also be motivated by plea negotiations. Journalism can capture people and events inaccessible to trial transcripts but sometimes relies on untested sources. Official reports identify institutional deficiencies while remaining limited by the records they reviewed. No single genre should be treated as omniscient. [1] [2] [4] [7] [8]

45. Family, Associates, and Names That Require Separation

The elder Scarpa’s biography intersects with that of Gregory Scarpa Jr., whose separate criminal history and 1987 arrest-warning claims appear repeatedly in the FBI litigation. It also intersects with accounts from longtime companion Linda Schiro and associates including Lawrence Mazza and Carmine Sessa. Each relationship has different evidentiary relevance: family ties are not criminal convictions, cooperating-witness testimony is not automatically reliable, and a companion’s recollection may be contradicted by earlier recordings. [2] [4] [7] [8]

The page therefore minimizes speculation about private life and concentrates on the relationships that materially affect the documented criminal and institutional history. Where later accounts attach sweeping allegations to relatives or acquaintances, those allegations require independent support before inclusion as facts. This avoids treating an entire circle of people as interchangeable with the acts of one convicted offender. [2] [4] [7] [8]

46. Victims, Bystanders, and the Editorial Balance

The most important corrective to the mythology surrounding Scarpa is the inclusion of victims. The court record names Vincent Fusaro, Nicky Grancio and Larry Lampesi among people killed during the war, and other prosecutions involved additional killings and attempted killings. Public accounts also describe injuries to bystanders. These harms are not secondary to the story of an informant’s relationship with a federal agent; they are why the source-management failures mattered. [1] [2] [4] [8]

An encyclopedia biography is not an indictment, but it should avoid entertainment-oriented language that elevates violent reputation over documented consequences. It should also refrain from assigning responsibility for unsolved or disputed killings simply because the suspect was notorious. The appropriate standard is incident-specific evidence with clearly identified legal and factual limitations. [1] [2] [4] [8]

47. Research Method: Primary Findings, Allegations, and Secondary Reports

This chapter prioritizes published federal judicial opinions and the DOJ inspector-general report for events directly addressed in those records. Second-tier materials include contemporary reporting and substantial later investigation; these are attributed when they add context that the courts did not decide. The presence of an allegation in a judicial opinion does not by itself convert the allegation into a judicial finding. Similarly, an appellate court’s quotation of a trial narrative must be interpreted in light of the issue actually resolved. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [9] [12] [13]

The most consequential open questions are the exact beginning of pre-1980 FBI contacts, the full number of homicides attributable to Scarpa, the complete circumstances of his blood-transfusion-related claim, and the scope of specific leaks that were not independently adjudicated. More systematic examination would require the original FBI file series, complete plea and sentencing transcripts, particular homicide case files and contemporaneous medical or civil records where publicly available. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [9] [12] [13]

48. Historical Conclusion

Scarpa’s significance lies at the collision of two systems of authority: the violent governance of a criminal crew and the state’s efforts to obtain intelligence from within it. His criminal activity was not negated by his informant work. Nor did the intelligence he provided relieve public institutions of their duty to investigate and restrain ongoing violence. The record supports his role in the Colombo war, the 1993 guilty plea and sentence, and a long period of troubling FBI handling. [1] [2] [4] [5] [12]

The lasting institutional issue is the ability of a criminal source to trade information for access, influence and apparent protection while the agencies receiving that information struggle to control its costs. Later prosecutions, appeals, investigative reports and FOIA disputes show that even after Scarpa died, questions about disclosure and accountability continued to shape the historical record. The most defensible biography preserves both the seriousness of his crimes and the exact boundaries of what the evidence proves. [1] [2] [4] [5] [12]

Research and Publication Limitations

Archival scope. The FBI Vault collection was identified as an original-record gateway, but the scanned series was not fully inspected page by page. No claim of exhaustive FBI file collation, full redaction reversal, or original Form 209 transcription is made.

Vital statistics. Public biographical sources commonly give May 8, 1928 and June 4, 1994; original vital records were not examined. June 1994 death in federal custody is supported by the DOJ inspector general and a published federal court opinion.

Legal disposition. The federal judicial summaries agree on the guilty plea and ten-year term but differ in compression of the plea and sentencing dates. Full judgment, plea transcript and December 1993 sentencing minutes would be needed for count-by-count verification.

Violence and homicide totals. The named Fusaro, Grancio and Lampesi events are addressed in the 1997 judicial record. Other alleged killings and estimated totals are not converted into convictions or treated as a verified comprehensive victim register.

Mississippi narrative. The 1964 civil-rights murders and federal investigation are documented. Scarpa’s alleged coercive role in extracting information from Klansmen is not independently substantiated by the cited official case summaries; the 1997 court describes a dramatic version as unconfirmed rumor.

Medical evidence. The 1993 court records severe AIDS-related illness. The allegation of HIV acquisition via a 1986 transfusion is based on contemporary reporting and associated litigation rather than collated medical records or an adjudicated clinical causation finding.

DeVecchio proceedings. Federal judicial criticism and the DOJ inspector general’s account of FBI handling remain important, but the 2007 New York State murder prosecution was dismissed. This page does not imply that DeVecchio was convicted, nor that every allegation made by witnesses was proved.
