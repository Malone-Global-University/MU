1. Historical Overview

Thomas Salvatore DiBella Sr. (1905–1988) was an older-generation figure in the Colombo crime family whose period of prominence followed the June 1971 shooting of Joseph Colombo. His public profile is unusually revealing because contemporary federal officials named him as a boss in 1974, an FBI report still called him the current boss in 1976, and a 1983 Senate investigative exhibit listed him instead as acting consigliere. Those descriptions cannot all represent one timeless title. They reflect a family in which formal appointment, visible representation, and actual operational influence were contested. [3][4][5]

DiBella appears most securely in the historical record through court proceedings rather than a fully documented personal memoir. In 1974 and 1975 the Second Circuit reviewed separate civil-contempt orders stemming from his repeated refusal to answer an immunized federal grand jury's questions. A decade later federal prosecutors included him in a major racketeering indictment, but a 1985 federal judge severed his prosecution because serious medical problems prevented trial at that point. He was not convicted in that 1985 ruling. [1][2][6][7]

His career challenges a common simplified narrative in which Carmine Persico instantly became the undisputed boss in 1971 and DiBella merely held a decorative title. The available record supports neither the conclusion that DiBella controlled everything nor that he counted for nothing: he was publicly and privately identified as a boss, yet some law-enforcement sources viewed him as constrained by Persico and others. His biography is therefore an inquiry into how Mafia leadership was recognized and exercised during the 1970s. [3][4][5][10][15]

2. Identity, Name Variants, and Dates

The name appears as Thomas Salvatore DiBella, with spacing and spelling variants Di Bella, Dibella, and, in the fiscal 1974 Attorney General's report, DiBello. The FBI's later deceased-subject index explicitly records Thomas Salvatore DiBella Sr. as a Colombo-family figure. Accounts commonly call him “Tom,” “Tommy,” or “The Old Man”; the last appears in the 1985 federal case caption. [3][6][7][12]

A 1983 Senate hearing exhibit lists November 29, 1905 as his birth date, and specialist biographical summaries give Brooklyn as the birthplace. The same calendar day appears in independent genealogical-style indexes. The birthplace and parentage have not been confirmed here against an original New York City birth certificate; the date itself is better supported by the governmental investigative record than by unsourced modern profile tables. [4][13][14]

His death occurred in 1988, at age 82, but the precise date is unresolved across sources. A specialist historical chronology and a genealogy index give June 10, 1988; a popular online biography gives June 6; the FBI's 2011 dead-list release oddly prints June 13 and July 13, 1988. Without a certified death record or contemporaneous obituary independently matched to this subject, this page does not silently choose a definitive day. [12][13][14][19]

3. Family Background and Claims about His Father

A specialist Mafia chronology names DiBella as the son of Salvatore DiBella, sometimes described in underworld histories as an early leader of the organization that preceded Joseph Profaci's long tenure. Such reconstructions are relevant but have not been checked against original birth, census, or marriage records in this edition. The identification should therefore be treated as a secondary historical claim, not a newly established genealogical conclusion. [13][18]

Even if that relationship is correct, it would not establish that Thomas automatically inherited underworld authority. The family's later emergence as one of the Five Families occurred across multiple organizational transitions. In histories of clandestine hierarchies, kinship, neighborhood connections, seniority, nomination, and recognition are different pathways to influence and should not be collapsed into one story of hereditary succession. [13][17]

4. Brooklyn Origins and Working Life

Contemporary reporting described DiBella in 1974 as a retired longshoreman. Later narratives place much of his working life in waterfront employment and associate him with Brooklyn and Staten Island. The employment description carries more evidentiary weight than dramatized claims that every dock position he held concealed an organized-crime function. The payroll history, union assignments, and individual work records were not recovered for this article. [11][13]

The waterfront nevertheless mattered to New York racketeering investigations because shipping, trucking, unions, stevedoring, and adjacent service industries created overlapping legitimate and illegal markets. The documented fact that DiBella worked on the docks belongs to his biography; generalized accounts of mob control of waterfront activity belong to the organizational context. Neither alone proves that DiBella committed a particular act of labor racketeering. [8][11]

5. Early Criminal History: An Unverified Retrospective

Some specialist compilations attribute Prohibition-era bootlegging and old criminal charges to DiBella, including a conviction or incarceration in the 1920s and another encounter with law enforcement in the early 1930s. The reports have not been matched here to docket numbers, police registers, or sentencing orders. These details cannot responsibly be formatted as a certain arrest-to-release timeline. [18]

The firmer source-based record begins much later, when the 1974 grand jury identified DiBella as a witness in an investigation of gambling, loan-sharking, and racketeering. The courts adjudicated his refusal to testify, not every underlying criminal accusation made by investigators. Distinguishing those propositions is crucial, because a biographical crime list can otherwise blend allegations, eyewitness claims, arrests, and convictions into an erroneous composite. [1][2]

6. The Profaci Organization and DiBella's Seniority

Joseph Profaci led the predecessor organization for decades, until his death in June 1962. Joseph Magliocco then held de facto command before his own removal and the elevation of Joseph Colombo in the 1963–1964 transition. DiBella's historical setting was therefore a family with established older members, succession disputes, and competing factions by the time he received heightened attention in the 1970s. [11][13][17]

What rank DiBella actually held at each point before 1971 remains less securely documented than his 1970s role. A modern narrative may say he had been a soldier, a captain, and later an adviser, but such ladder-like sequences are not supported by a single continuous institutional personnel register. His seniority and reputation among family figures are plausible; individual promotion dates should not be invented to bridge archival silence. [11][13]

7. Joseph Colombo's Shooting and the Leadership Vacuum

On June 28, 1971 Joseph Colombo was shot at a public Italian-American Unity Day event in Manhattan. He survived but was severely incapacitated until his death in 1978. The practical consequence for the crime family was that the man whose surname identified the organization could no longer routinely exercise ordinary leadership. Any subsequent chronology must separate the symbolic incumbent from the people performing operational or representative duties. [4][17]

Federal descriptions later summarized this troubled period in different ways. An early-1980s Senate hearing exhibit said DiBella acted as boss briefly immediately after Colombo was shot, while other chronologies place a substantial acting-boss tenure beginning in 1973. Those two statements need not be artificially forced into a single seamless term. The evidence is sufficient to establish that DiBella was treated as a leader during the decade, but not a universally agreed starting date or uninterrupted mandate. [4][17]

8. Interim Figures: Yacovelli, Aloi, and Brancato

Leadership after the 1971 shooting was not a simple passage from Colombo directly to DiBella. Accounts of the period identify Joseph Yacovelli, Vincenzo “Vincent” Aloi, and Joseph Brancato as figures who exercised authority or were treated as provisional leadership possibilities. Yacovelli's position was affected by the continuing Gallo conflict, and Aloi became involved in legal troubles. The succession involved overlapping authority, not merely vacancy followed by permanent appointment. [15][17]

Later lists often assign precise one-year or multi-month terms to each of these men. Such tables are useful navigational summaries but obscure simultaneous factional influence and uncertainty over Commission recognition. This edition uses a guarded periodization: post-1971 disorder, DiBella's clearly attested mid-1970s boss designation, and his subsequent reclassification as an adviser. [4][5][15][17]

9. The 1973 Appointment and Its Evidentiary Limits

Later accounts of Persico's post-conviction litigation reproduce passages said to come from 1973 FBI informant reports. Those passages assert that Carlo Gambino backed or designated DiBella as the new Colombo boss in June 1973 and that subsequent informants confirmed his position. These are important leads for assessing whether he was more than an acting stand-in, but the quotations are filtered through adversarial legal submissions subsequently discussed by a journalist. The full underlying FBI reports and pleadings have not been independently compared for this page. [15]

Consequently, “DiBella was formally installed in 1973 with Gambino's backing” should be presented as a reported investigative and legal interpretation, not as an unqualified Commission minute. The substantive point does not depend entirely on that lead: contemporary government annual reporting in fiscal 1974 independently identified him as a Colombo-family boss, and a 1976 FBI teletype called him the current boss. [3][5][15]

10. Public Recognition as Boss in 1974

In the Attorney General's fiscal 1974 report, a section on federal actions against organized-crime leaders describes DiBella as the boss of the New York Colombo syndicate and identifies his jailing for contempt following a refusal to testify. The report spells the surname “DiBello”; the associated circumstances and judicial record make clear the subject was Thomas Di Bella. It is contemporaneous federal evidence that the boss title was used, not a later label assigned solely by encyclopedists. [1][3]

A July 21, 1974 New York Times article, preserved in a digitized clipping collection, likewise described him as a retired longshoreman whom federal authorities believed was heading the organization. The report also reproduced competing evaluations of his influence: some investigators suggested senior figures wanted a more discreet representative, while others believed he was weak enough to be controlled by stronger bosses. These were informant and investigator views, not a unanimous institutional verdict. [11]

11. Carlo Gambino and Interfamily Influence

Some later source discussions put Carlo Gambino at the center of DiBella's rise. Their account is that Gambino preferred an acceptable, relatively discreet figure at a moment when the Colombo group had attracted police attention and internal disorder. Such an explanation is consistent with the political logic of a Commission system in which neighboring family leaders could influence a damaged organization's choice of boss. [11][15]

There is a substantial distinction between influence and ownership. The notion that Gambino simply “owned” the Colombo family is too strong for the surviving documentation used here. Federal investigations were tracking competing claims, personal loyalties, and shifting influence. The relationship can be analyzed as probable interfamily leverage without presenting every reported meeting, threat, or endorsement as documented fact. [5][11][15]

12. Persico's Imprisonment and Dual Sources of Authority

Carmine Persico's prison time in the 1970s was central to the arrangement that permitted other men to act publicly and conduct day-to-day business. A later Senate investigative exhibit described Persico as functioning as an interim leader after Colombo's shooting and as becoming formal boss upon Colombo's death in 1978, even as the same exhibit recorded DiBella's separate time as boss. That account is evidence of law-enforcement understanding by the early 1980s, not a self-consistent minute book of internal assignments. [4]

The historical issue is whether prison allowed Persico to retain strategic control while another man held a title, or whether DiBella exercised more independent authority than later Persico-centered histories concede. The documentary record supports competing accounts of this balance. This entry does not assume all boss decisions originated in prison, and it does not ignore evidence that Persico had influential supporters. [4][5][15]

13. The 1976 FBI Teletype and the Meaning of Boss

An FBI teletype dated October 15, 1976, describes DiBella as the current Colombo family boss while relaying sources who considered Persico the person exercising effective control through trusted intermediaries. The report is especially valuable because it places two potentially contrasting claims in a single contemporary document. Officials were not merely deciding whether DiBella had a title; they were assessing whose directives carried weight. [5]

The document also used a disparaging characterization of DiBella's independence, and the underlying informants' names are redacted. This is a record of what FBI sources asserted, not a transparent observation of every leadership conference. For research purposes, it is evidence that DiBella had recognized title status and that investigators questioned his autonomy—not conclusive proof of either absolute control or complete powerlessness. [5]

14. The 1974 Grand Jury: Investigative Context

A federal special grand jury sitting in the Eastern District of New York investigated organized-crime racketeering, particularly illegal gambling and loan-sharking. The government sought DiBella's testimony and secured a statutory immunity order under 18 U.S.C. §§ 6002–6003. Those procedures matter: the government could compel responsive testimony by removing specified risks of prosecutorial use, but the immunity order did not mean the grand jury's underlying suspicion had been proven. [1]

DiBella refused to answer questions after the court ordered him to testify. Chief Judge Jacob Mishler entered a civil-contempt order carrying a conditional term: six months, the duration of the grand jury, or earlier termination if DiBella complied. The question before the court of appeals concerned the validity of the government's immunity-application process and the contempt order—not whether DiBella was guilty of racketeering. [1]

15. First Contempt Appeal: In re Di Bella (1974)

The Second Circuit decided In re Di Bella, 499 F.2d 1175, on July 3, 1974. DiBella argued that the Strike Force attorney's role in obtaining Department of Justice approval rendered the immunity procedure invalid because the local U.S. Attorney had not participated in every step. The appeals court rejected the statutory challenge and affirmed the contempt judgment. It nevertheless acknowledged concerns about coordination between the local prosecutor and a separate Organized Crime Strike Force. [1]

The procedural nature of this judgment is important. “Held in contempt” is accurate, while “convicted of loan-sharking in the 1974 case” is not supported by the opinion. Civil contempt was meant to induce testimony. The court's published reasoning documents his choice not to answer, the government's method of procuring immunity, and judicial limits on the dispute; it does not establish every alleged family enterprise described by investigators. [1]

16. First Six-Month Confinement and Renewed Subpoena

The 1975 appellate opinion supplies a precise sequel: DiBella began serving the first contempt confinement on July 12, 1974 and remained unwilling to testify, so he served the full six months. In December 1974 he was again subpoenaed to the same grand jury. Lawyers contested the authority of the Strike Force prosecutor and the continuing effect of the immunity order. DiBella again refused to answer. [2]

This is not one continuous, casually described single contempt event. The record documents the original 1974 order, a completed six-month confinement, a new subpoena, and another contempt proceeding. Conflating them understates both the government's persistence and the degree to which litigation about the power of federal investigators became central to DiBella's documented public life. [1][2]

17. Second Contempt Appeal: In re Di Bella (1975)

In In re Di Bella, 518 F.2d 955, decided July 8, 1975, the Second Circuit affirmed the subsequent contempt order. DiBella's appeal challenged, among other matters, whether the Strike Force lawyer was properly authorized to conduct the grand jury examination and whether the procedure used to review grand jury transcripts deprived him of required representation. The court addressed these issues as procedural and constitutional questions within an active investigation. [2]

The appellate decision does not itself demonstrate the eventual disposition of every subpoena, nor does it yield a complete picture of grand jury evidence. It documents judicial rejection of his appellate objections in that proceeding. A source-disciplined biography should not assume that all questions posed to a compelled witness were factually proven by the witness's silence. [2]

18. Why Civil Contempt Was Historically Significant

The DiBella contempt litigation illustrates a federal investigative strategy distinct from the prosecution of a single assault, bet, loan, or narcotics transaction. Investigators aimed to uncover the organization and management of a criminal enterprise. A witness positioned within a suspected hierarchy could be important even when prosecutors had not yet brought a substantive racketeering count against him. Immunity and compelled testimony therefore became institutional weapons in the evolution of organized-crime enforcement. [1][2][3]

For DiBella, the public court record confirms a sustained decision to resist testimony, but it cannot tell readers his complete private rationale. Explanations invoking a Mafia code of silence, fear of retaliation, or loyalty to other leaders are plausible frameworks, not motives established in either reported opinion. The judicial documents are strong on conduct and procedure and limited on subjective intent. [1][2]

19. The Family's 1970s Administration and Induction Authority

A particularly consequential retrospective source comes from Michael Franzese, a former Colombo member who testified to a United States Senate subcommittee in May 1996. Franzese stated that DiBella presided as family boss over his formal induction in 1975. As a claim about the identity of the person conducting that ceremony, this is a first-person participant account attached to a public hearing. It also aligns with the official 1974 boss label and the 1976 FBI reference to DiBella as current boss. [3][5][10]

Franzese was testifying more than twenty years after the event and had his own life history, reputation, and reasons to narrate it in a particular way. The testimony does not prove that DiBella approved every subsequent criminal transaction or that Persico exercised no influence. It establishes that a named participant publicly identified him in a central authority ritual during the mid-1970s, which is stronger evidence than an undated internet boss list alone. [10]

20. Longshore, Unions, and the Distinction Between Context and Conduct

The government's later racketeering case alleged that the Colombo organization used illicit pressure and corruption in restaurant and construction labor structures, alongside gambling, extortion, and other offenses. DiBella's working-class waterfront background gives that setting biographical relevance, but labor-union allegations at the family level must not be pasted onto his personal résumé as individual convictions. [6][8][11]

A historian should ask three separate questions: where DiBella worked, what organizational offices he held, and what crimes a prosecutor actually proved against him. The sources here are strongest on his reported dock occupation, contempt proceedings, and the fact of his later indictment. They are weaker on a complete audited ledger of his personal income, concrete rackets, and actual supervision of every enterprise alleged in the family indictment. [1][2][6][7][11]

21. The Reported May 1981 Encounter

An investigative exhibit presented to the Senate in 1983 states that authorities arrested DiBella in May 1981 while he was meeting with DeCavalcante-family members in connection with reported narcotics interests in Florida. The same exhibit associates him with loansharking and narcotics. These are law-enforcement characterizations of an alleged event and a suspected business domain. The specific indictment or disposition, if any, arising from that encounter was not independently located for this edition. [4]

It would therefore be inappropriate to describe a proven 1981 Florida narcotics conviction or narrate a complete trafficking network as established by the Senate exhibit. The value of the passage is narrower: it reveals what officials thought DiBella was doing and why the Colombo organization's New York base did not limit the geographical scope of its investigations. [4]

22. The 1983 Senate Hierarchy: Acting Consigliere

The 1983 hearing record prints an organizational chart with Carmine Persico as boss, Gennaro Langella as acting boss, Alphonse Persico as consigliere, and Thomas DiBella as acting consigliere. Its biographical description again states that DiBella briefly acted as boss following Joseph Colombo's shooting. On its own terms, the exhibit shows that the investigators by then regarded him as serving an advisory function rather than holding the top current title. [4]

This snapshot does not retrospectively erase the 1974 Attorney General report or the 1976 FBI teletype. A clandestine organizational chart is an intelligence assessment taken at a moment, often reflecting both real changes and the reporting conventions of an agency. The responsible reconstruction uses dates and institutional provenance for each title instead of stitching a single “official boss 1973–1979” label onto every source. [3][4][5]

23. The Change of Leadership and Uncertain Retirement Year

Secondary leadership lists disagree on when DiBella ceased acting as boss: common dates include 1977, 1979, or roughly 1980–1981. One likely source of the confusion is that Joseph Colombo remained alive but incapacitated until 1978, Carmine Persico was in and out of prison, and the titles boss, acting boss, street boss, and consigliere were used in different ways. A precise retirement ceremony or Commission decree has not been independently verified. [4][5][13][15][17]

The safest supported formulation is that DiBella functioned in a leading capacity through at least the mid-1970s and was described as an acting adviser in a 1983 government exhibit. The record also supports that his health became a serious obstacle by 1984–1985. To assert an exact day on which he voluntarily stepped down would go beyond the evidence reviewed here. [3][4][5][7]

24. Gennaro Langella and the Rise of Operational Street Leadership

By the early 1980s, Gennaro “Jerry Lang” Langella was an important operational leader. The 1983 Senate chart identifies him as acting boss under Carmine Persico. A contemporary 1984 federal indictment grouped Langella and DiBella with Persico among the people prosecutors believed had controlled the organization. These accounts chart the emergence of a leadership structure that was not centered on DiBella alone. [4][8]

A street or acting boss could relay orders and coordinate captains while another figure claimed higher strategic authority. The distribution varied with incarceration, arrest risk, personal influence, and factional relations. Rather than attach an invented rank to DiBella for every year from 1971 to 1984, the relevant investigative snapshots show him moving from a reported senior operational figure to an elder counselor amid Langella's and Persico's prominence. [4][5][8]

25. Operation Star Quest and the 1984 Indictment

Federal investigators carried out a multi-year investigation known as Operation Star Quest. In October 1984, prosecutors announced a sweeping Colombo-family indictment involving labor racketeering, extortion, gambling, loansharking, bribery, narcotics allegations, and stolen goods. Contemporary news accounts named DiBella, Carmine Persico, Gennaro Langella, and others. The prosecution accused the enterprise as a whole; the indictment did not itself establish the truth of every count against every defendant. [6][8][9]

DiBella was then about 78 years old. Reports stated that he was hospitalized around the time the indictment became public. The presence of an elderly, medically frail former leader in a modern racketeering indictment demonstrates the prosecution's focus on long-lived institutional relationships. It does not erase the need to determine separately whether that defendant was medically capable of participating in trial and what disposition followed in his case. [7][8][9]

26. RICO Allegations and Individual Liability

The 1985 published opinions describe an alleged Colombo enterprise encompassing the coordinated use of racketeering activity and the exploitation of commercial and labor structures. The federal RICO statute permitted prosecutors to allege a pattern of related acts carried out through an enterprise, rather than proving each episode in isolation. Thus the family-wide description provides a context for DiBella's indictment but is not, by itself, a judicial declaration that he personally committed each act listed. [6][7]

In the reported case United States v. Persico, 620 F. Supp. 836, DiBella appears among the defendants named in the caption. That particular opinion addressed pretrial challenges by other defendants. It should be cited as part of the case record, not mislabeled a conviction of DiBella. The October 1985 opinion supplies his more decisive personal procedural result. [6][7]

27. Health-Related Severance in October 1985

In United States v. Persico, 621 F. Supp. 842 (October 2, 1985), federal district judge John F. Keenan considered DiBella's request to separate his trial from that of the other defendants. His lawyers supplied medical letters and records of hospitalization at Richmond Memorial Hospital on Staten Island. A court-appointed physician, selected by agreement of the defense and prosecution, also assessed his condition. [7]

The court concluded that DiBella's health precluded trial at that time and granted severance. The order expressly contemplated possible trial if his health later permitted, and it excluded the period of incapacity from Speedy Trial calculations under the applicable law. Severance is neither a finding of guilt nor an acquittal. In the records reviewed for this page, a later final merits judgment for DiBella in that indictment has not been established. [7]

28. Differentiating DiBella from Other Defendants' Convictions

General histories of the 1980s Colombo prosecutions often proceed directly from a sweeping 1984 indictment to the criminal convictions of other family figures. For DiBella this shorthand is misleading: he was not among those whose trial moved forward on the same timetable after the 1985 medical order. Claims that “the Colombo leaders were convicted” must be disaggregated by name, charge, and docket outcome. [7][8]

The earlier 1974 and 1975 contempt rulings remain documented adverse judicial results against DiBella himself. Those orders concern refusal to testify, not a RICO merits conviction. A complete account of his legal record must keep the two contempt episodes, the 1984 indictment, and the 1985 health severance distinct. [1][2][6][7]

29. Late Life and Death: A Source Conflict

The final years of DiBella's life are sparsely documented in the material directly collated for this entry. A court order demonstrates serious medical limitations as of October 1985. He died in 1988. Precise descriptions of diagnosis, last hospitalization, terminal events, and funeral arrangements should not be supplied without matched medical or contemporary obituary records. [7][12][13]

There is also a real date conflict. June 10, 1988 appears in a specialist Mafia-history chronology and in a genealogical index for a man of the same birth date and Staten Island residence; other modern biographies give June 6. An FBI deceased-person list prints June 13 and July 13, 1988, apparently reflecting inconsistent input data. This article reports 1988 as secure while identifying the competing dates rather than declaring any one fully verified. [12][13][14][19]

30. Historical Significance of His Low-Profile Leadership

DiBella has received far less popular attention than Joseph Colombo, Carmine Persico, Joseph Gallo, or the defendants whose court trials became media events. Yet his case is valuable precisely because his public reputation was quieter. Contemporary authorities called him a boss, an FBI teletype assessed his possible dependence on stronger allies, and a later government chart recorded him as an acting consigliere. His experience demonstrates that visibility and organizational standing were not identical. [3][4][5][11]

He also supplies one of the clearer documentary examples of a person described as an organized-crime leader refusing immunized grand jury testimony through repeated civil-contempt litigation. Combined with the 1985 medical severance, the sequence connects two generations of federal enforcement methods: testimony-compulsion investigations and broader enterprise racketeering prosecutions. This institutional history is more firmly documented than embellished anecdotes about his private personality or secret wealth. [1][2][6][7]

31. What Can Be Established and What Remains Open

With high confidence, the sourced record establishes DiBella's identity and approximate life span, his publicly identified senior Colombo standing in the mid-1970s, the 1974 and 1975 civil-contempt litigation, his presence in the 1984 indictment, and the 1985 order barring his immediate trial because of medical incapacity. His description as acting consigliere in 1983 is likewise directly attested in an official hearing exhibit, although that description rests on investigative intelligence rather than a public appointment certificate. [1][2][3][4][5][6][7]

Questions requiring further original-document review include the alleged early criminal convictions; exact family relationships; the 1973 selection process; whether and when DiBella ceded supreme authority to Persico; any 1981 arrest disposition; the final docket history after severance; and the exact June or July 1988 death date. A published encyclopedia entry should make these gaps visible as research problems instead of resolving them with false precision. [4][7][12][13][14][15]

32. Methodology: Intelligence, Testimony, Journalism, and Courts

The historical record uses several document types that do different evidentiary work. Judicial opinions reliably establish what a court ordered or decided, but their background recitals may summarize allegations. FBI communications preserve contemporaneous law-enforcement assessments, sometimes based on redacted informants. Public testimony by a former participant is valuable about matters personally experienced but can be shaped by memory and incentive. Newspapers provide dated reporting with varying access to primary files. [1][2][5][7][10][11]

These sources are cited according to their capacity to support a proposition. The 1983 hearing chart may accurately reproduce a law-enforcement belief without proving how a secret organization internally recognized rank. The 1996 Franzese testimony is firsthand evidence of his remembered induction, not a roster of every person DiBella controlled. The 1985 severance order is a legal fact, not a generic claim of exoneration. This separation is an editorial requirement for reliable institutional history. [4][7][10]

33. The Colombo Family's Succession in Historical Perspective

The Colombo succession from the 1960s to the early 1980s spanned more than one political crisis. Profaci's death led to Magliocco's contested command and Joseph Colombo's elevation. Colombo's incapacitation produced an extended period of provisional and overlapping figures, including DiBella, as imprisoned or active leaders contended for recognition. Later charts centered on Persico and Langella, while DiBella's surviving role was associated with counsel and continuity. [4][5][15][17]

The broad trend is clear, even where the dates are not: an elder figure apparently acceptable to multiple factions became an important representative during institutional instability, and his authority was subsequently eclipsed or redefined as another cohort took over. The documentary evidence warrants this limited synthesis without inventing an uninterrupted, uncontested succession list. [3][4][5][10][11]

Research and Publication Limitations

Documentary scope. Two published Second Circuit appellate rulings, two 1985 reported federal decisions, the 1974 Attorney General report, a 1976 FBI file, and the 1983 Senate investigative exhibit form the core evidence. An original 1973 FBI boss-designation sequence, personal birth and death certificates, alleged 1920s convictions, the 1981 arrest file, and the final 1984 racketeering docket have not been fully collated.

Contradictory leadership accounts. The 1974 government report and 1976 FBI teletype refer to DiBella as boss, but the 1983 Senate chart calls him acting consigliere and attributes executive authority to Carmine Persico and Gennaro Langella. The exact 1973 installation, degree of independence, and 1977–1981 changeover are not settled here.

Death-date discrepancy. Published secondary records conflict among June 6 and June 10, 1988; the FBI deceased-subject index contains June 13 and July 13. This first edition adopts 1988 only as secure. The primary death certificate or a matched newspaper obituary is needed before identifying a definitive day.

Legal characterization. The 1974 and 1975 contempt appeals upheld civil contempt for refusal to testify after immunity. The 1984 indictment alleged a RICO enterprise; the 1985 court separated DiBella's case because of medical incapacity. This decision was not an acquittal or conviction of the charged racketeering offenses. No unverified outcome is supplied.
