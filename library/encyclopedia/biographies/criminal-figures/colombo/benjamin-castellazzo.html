1. Historical Overview

Benjamin “Benji” Castellazzo became a publicly identified senior figure of New York’s Colombo crime family in the twenty-first century. The federal government identified him as acting underboss in January 2011 and as underboss in September 2021. Those two snapshots—separated by a federal racketeering conviction, imprisonment and return to public attention—are the best-documented coordinates of his organizational career. They are not a continuous, independently verified term of office extending through every intervening year. [1] [3] [8]

Castellazzo is especially significant as a case study in the endurance of an organized-crime hierarchy despite repeated prosecutions. In the first major case he admitted a racketeering conspiracy involving two extortion conspiracies. In the second he pleaded guilty to a different offense, money-laundering conspiracy, arising from a larger labor-union and employee-benefit investigation. Treating those prosecutions as interchangeable would obscure what the records actually establish. [3] [9] [10]

This biography proceeds from source-defined claims rather than popular rank lists. An indictment describes allegations; a guilty plea establishes a specific adjudicated offense; a press statement supplies an official characterization; journalism may report court proceedings without replacing the judgment. These distinctions matter most for leadership titles, alleged threats and the limits of his later plea. [2] [9] [10]

2. Name, Aliases, and Age

The name consistently given in federal sources is Benjamin Castellazzo. The 2011 indictment records “Benji,” “the Claw,” and “the Fang”; later government releases preferentially use “Benji.” The existence of an alias in a charging document documents law-enforcement identification, not the frequency of the nickname in private conversations. [2] [8]

Federal prosecutors listed him as 73 in January 2011, 83 in September 2021, and 85 in July 2023; contemporary reporting described him as 86 in January 2024. These figures indicate a birth year around 1937–1938 but do not independently establish an exact date of birth. This edition therefore does not invent a birthday. [4] [8] [9] [11]

3. Early Life and the Limits of the Record

The public sources used here focus on decades of alleged criminal enterprise activity, organizational rank, guilty pleas and sentencing. They are comparatively silent about childhood, schooling, early home life and initial entry into the organization. Those absences must not be filled with a standard Mafia-origin story merely because similar accounts exist for other people. [1] [2] [3]

Later sentencing coverage discusses a wife, children and grandchildren, and describes his residence in New Jersey. These are reported matters from mitigation filings and courtroom accounts, not a full independently reconstructed family genealogy. The article does not supply names or dates for private relatives without historical necessity. [5] [11] [12]

4. How the Colombo Family Was Organized

The Colombo family operated within the American La Cosa Nostra system, using an administration of boss, underboss and consigliere, with captains overseeing crews of soldiers and associates. The January 2011 federal indictment outlines that structure and acknowledges the use of acting officials or ruling panels when senior figures were jailed, ill, or seeking insulation from scrutiny. [2]

Rank is therefore a dated institutional claim, not a permanent personal attribute. The fact that an individual has served as a captain does not prove he was simultaneously underboss, and the term “acting underboss” does not imply that he was boss. Prosecution language about authority over others is relevant but does not identify every private succession vote or appointment. [2] [4] [8]

5. Reported Earlier Captain-Level Position

The 2011 indictment states that Castellazzo had occupied, at various relevant times, the statuses of associate, soldier, captain and underboss. Its phrasing is retrospective and deliberately broad: the document does not supply a reliable exact appointment date or uninterrupted tenure for each rank. [2]

Later secondary collections associate him with a Brooklyn crew, but precise crew boundaries and handover dates were not established by the primary sources reviewed for this edition. For that reason the profile describes an earlier captain-level role without asserting that he controlled a specified block or that every reported associate was his subordinate. [2] [7]

6. Colombo Family Conflict and Institutional Continuity

The Colombo family survived an exceptionally violent succession struggle in 1991–1993 between factions aligned with Carmine Persico and Victor Orena. The later 2011 racketeering indictment used an alleged conspiracy window beginning in June 1991, overlapping that period of conflict. A broad charged timeframe does not establish that Castellazzo personally participated in every violent act within that period. [2] [1]

To date, the sources reviewed for this biography do not provide a court finding tying Castellazzo personally to a specific wartime homicide. He must not be conflated with defendants who pleaded guilty to, or were convicted of, separate killings. His historical importance in the published record is clearer in later economic offenses and administration-level prosecutions. [2] [3]

7. The Underboss Position Before 2011

A June 2008 federal announcement identified John “Sonny” Franzese as the Colombo family underboss while Thomas Gioeli was described as acting boss. By January 2011, prosecutors instead identified Castellazzo as acting underboss and Andrew Russo as street boss. These are reliable snapshots, but they leave the precise process of succession between 2008 and 2011 unresolved. [14] [4]

A source-compliant institutional timeline should therefore avoid presenting 2009, 2010 or 2011 as an unqualified exact accession date absent a specific appointment record. A frequently circulated chronology is not the same as a documented formal transfer of authority. [4] [14]

8. January 2011: A Coordinated Mafia Prosecution

On January 20, 2011, the Justice Department announced a large multi-district organized-crime enforcement operation. In the Eastern District of New York, a sweeping indictment charged numerous alleged Colombo members and associates. Andrew Russo was publicly characterized as street boss, Castellazzo as acting underboss, and Richard Fusco as consigliere. [1] [4]

The size of the operation explains the widespread press attention, but the number of people charged across the national sweep must not be confused with the number of defendants in Castellazzo’s specific indictment. At this procedural stage the charges were allegations that each named defendant was entitled to contest. [1] [2]

9. The January 12, 2011 Indictment

The original indictment in United States v. Russo, No. 11-CR-30, was filed in the Eastern District of New York on January 12, 2011 and later publicly unsealed. It identifies Castellazzo with three nicknames and alleges that he was a participant in a criminal enterprise with a hierarchy and defined economic goals. [2]

Crucially, the indictment describes his statuses—associate, soldier, captain and underboss—as roles held at different relevant times. It also alleges racketeering conduct over an extended historical window. A charged conspiracy window is a prosecutorial allegation; the subsequent guilty plea must be consulted to determine what he legally admitted. [2] [3]

10. Andrew Russo, Castellazzo, and Richard Fusco

The three-person administration described at the 2011 arrests consisted of street boss Andrew Russo, acting underboss Castellazzo and consigliere Richard Fusco. The division is analytically useful: Russo was attributed the lead administrative position, Castellazzo the deputy role, and Fusco the advisory office. No credible source examined here establishes Castellazzo as overall boss at that moment. [1] [4]

Government reference to “the entire leadership” being charged reflected its investigative understanding of the then-active administration. It did not mean every Colombo member or every historical senior official was a defendant, nor did it mean that imprisonment instantly dissolved the organization. [1] [3]

11. Extortion and the Organizational Economy

Extortion is the extraction of payments or concessions through unlawful coercion. In the racketeering context it may be a revenue mechanism for the individuals conducting the scheme and for supervisors who receive a share. The 2011 indictment described extortion alongside gambling, fraud and other suspected money-generating activities. [2] [4]

Prosecution documents and later plea summaries are especially valuable because they connect broad organizational claims to narrower adjudicated conduct. They do not, by themselves, establish that every person named in a common indictment committed each category of offense alleged against the enterprise as a whole. [2] [3]

12. The 2011–2012 Racketeering-Conspiracy Guilty Plea

By November 9, 2012, federal prosecutors publicly confirmed that Castellazzo had pleaded guilty to racketeering conspiracy and that the plea included predicate conduct involving two separate extortion conspiracies. The announcement places his admission among the resolutions reached by members of the Colombo administration. [3]

A plea to racketeering conspiracy is not a jury verdict on every act described in the initial indictment. Nor should the date of the November 2012 announcement be recast as the precise day of his plea; the official statement calls it an earlier proceeding but does not specify its calendar date. [3]

13. The Construction Firm and Pizzeria Extortion Accounts

Contemporary January 2013 accounts identify Brooklyn’s La Quila construction company and a Staten Island restaurant called The Square as victims in the reported extortion matter. These names provide concrete local context to the racketeering plea, but the underlying plea allocution and each commercial record were not independently collated in full. [5] [6]

The targeting of a small business or contractor is economically distinct from attempts to capture a union’s benefits program a decade later. The comparison shows different points of vulnerability—direct coerced payment versus influence over collective purchasing decisions—without presuming the same individuals, dollar amounts or conduct in both cases. [5] [8] [9]

14. The 2013 Poverty and Family Mitigation Claims

As sentencing approached in January 2013, Castellazzo’s submissions reportedly described a family living in a New Jersey trailer park and relying on public benefits. The UPI account conveyed his remorse claims and his request for a reduced sentence. Such statements are the defendant’s mitigation position, not independently audited financial findings. [5]

Prosecutors, defense counsel and the court can disagree about the relevance of financial hardship when deciding a criminal sentence. A defendant’s statement that an organization brought him little financial reward does not legally negate an extortion-conspiracy conviction; it may nonetheless be considered as personal context at sentencing. [5] [6]

15. The Reported 63-Month Sentence in 2013

Contemporary secondary reporting records a 63-month federal sentence in early 2013, commonly dated January 30. The Justice Department’s 2012 announcement corroborates the plea but not the exact sentencing date or term. This article therefore labels the 63-month sentence as contemporaneously reported rather than claiming an independently inspected certified judgment. [3] [6] [13]

The case should not be misdescribed as a 63-month sentence for the 2021–2023 labor investigation: those charges did not yet exist. The earlier sentence arose from the separate 2011 racketeering-conspiracy prosecution. [3] [6] [8]

16. The Reported Return from Federal Custody

A historical overview published by The Mob Museum states that Castellazzo was released from prison in the summer of 2015. This is an approximate timeframe from retrospective reporting; it should not be converted into a verified prison-release date without Bureau of Prisons or court records. [7]

Release from custody is not equivalent to termination of all supervision, and public evidence of later activity does not establish that he continuously held a specific office during every year after his release. The subsequent charging record is clearer than any informal rank chronology for this interval. [7] [8]

17. An Expanded Labor-Union Pressure Campaign

The 2021 federal announcement alleges that a salary extortion scheme against a senior Queens labor-union official existed for years, and that late in 2019 the organization’s administration broadened the effort toward additional union and health-fund decisions. The government attributed leadership involvement to Andrew Russo, Castellazzo, Ralph DiMatteo and others. [8]

The announcement gives a framework for the later prosecution, not an individual verdict on each separate act. Not every factual assertion in the government’s collective narrative was admitted by every defendant when pleading guilty to particular counts. Castellazzo’s eventual money-laundering plea must be separated from those wider allegations. [8] [9]

18. September 14, 2021: New Federal Indictment

On September 14, 2021, federal prosecutors announced a 19-count indictment charging fourteen defendants associated with multiple criminal schemes. They identified Andrew Russo as boss, Benjamin Castellazzo as underboss, and Ralph DiMatteo as consigliere. The hierarchy was presented as the government’s assessment at the point of arrest. [8]

A second generation of captains and associates was named in the case, including Theodore Persico Jr., Richard Ferrara and Vincent Ricciardo. Some charged matters involved loansharking and fraudulent safety certifications rather than the precise offense to which Castellazzo later pleaded guilty. Shared indictment status does not transfer another person’s convictions to him. [8] [9]

19. The Queens-Based Labor Union

The prosecution described an attempt to exert unlawful influence over a Queens-based labor union by threatening a senior official and pressuring him to surrender part of his salary. The alleged operation exposed not only the threatened official but also union members whose representation could be distorted by coercion. [8]

Federal releases deliberately referred to the institution as the “Labor Union” and the threatened individual as “John Doe #1.” This article preserves that public-source convention instead of guessing the official’s identity or treating allegations of intimidation as authority to expose private persons. [8] [9]

20. The Health-Fund Vendor Scheme

The affiliated employee health fund was described as a source of contracts for medical and related benefits. Prosecutors alleged that members of the Colombo administration attempted to steer decisions toward vendors linked to the enterprise, creating a channel by which money serving members’ health needs could be diverted to criminal figures. [8] [9]

The government’s 2021 announcement described a plan to extract more than $10,000 per month from fund assets. That figure represented the alleged objective or contemplated stream; it should not be presented as money proven to have been collected by Castellazzo personally. The later plea and sentencing provide the adjudicated outcome applicable to him. [8] [9] [10]

21. Money Laundering as a Separate Legal Offense

Money-laundering conspiracy concerns agreements to conduct financial transactions involving proceeds or concealment-related purposes proscribed by federal law. It is analytically different from a substantive racketeering count, extortion of an official, or a count charging false workplace certifications. A plea to one crime does not automatically resolve the facts of all others. [9] [10]

In the July 2023 Justice Department announcement, Castellazzo was expressly distinguished from colleagues who pleaded guilty to racketeering offenses. That distinction is central to an accurate account of his case rather than a minor technicality. [9]

22. July 2023: Guilty Plea in the Later Case

The Justice Department reported on July 14, 2023 that Castellazzo had pleaded guilty to money-laundering conspiracy. Contemporary accounts of his later sentencing identify July 7 as his plea date. The government statement confirms that the plea had occurred by July 14; the specific earlier day relies on courtroom journalism unless a plea transcript is inspected. [9] [11]

There is no basis in those announcements to label his later disposition a new conviction of murder, drug trafficking, or substantive racketeering on all charged predicates. The resolved federal count identified for him was money-laundering conspiracy. [9] [10]

23. Andrew Russo’s Death During the Later Prosecution

Boss Andrew Russo, a central defendant in the 2021 proceedings, died while the case was pending. In its July 2023 and February 2024 summaries the Justice Department explicitly noted the death, preventing any interpretation of a later conviction or sentence against him from the same resolved cohort. [9] [10]

The loss of a leader during an ongoing federal investigation can complicate reconstructions of internal succession. The record examined here does not establish when or whether Castellazzo became the boss after Russo’s death. It continues to identify him as underboss in the 2023–2024 official descriptions. [9] [10]

24. January 22, 2024: Fifteen-Month Sentence

Contemporary New York Daily News courtroom reporting dates Castellazzo’s sentencing to Monday, January 22, 2024, before U.S. District Judge Hector Gonzalez. The judge imposed fifteen months of federal imprisonment on the money-laundering-conspiracy plea, a term subsequently confirmed in the Justice Department’s February 28, 2024 summary. [10] [11]

The fifteen-month term is not a modification of the earlier 63-month sentence: it belongs to the later case. Comparisons to other defendants’ penalties must account for differences in the offense of conviction, individual history, plea agreement, sentencing submissions and judicial findings. [6] [9] [10]

25. Age, Health, and the 2024 Sentence

At the January 2024 sentencing, defense counsel described age-related medical risks and asked the court to avoid a term that could endanger Castellazzo’s health. Reporting describes cardiac problems, prior cancer treatment and concerns over medical care in detention. Those are reported defense submissions rather than independent medical examinations carried out for this encyclopedia. [11] [12]

The court nevertheless imposed a custodial sentence while reducing the term below the prosecution’s request. The sentence reflects a judicial outcome, not a finding that Castellazzo was unfit for any incarceration. Accounts of his precise diagnoses or medication regimen should not be embellished beyond what was publicly reported. [11]

26. Housing and Financial Representations in 2024

His defense team also told the court that imprisonment could jeopardize subsidized senior housing, and filings reportedly claimed a very limited net worth. Such assertions were offered as mitigation in an adversarial proceeding; no independent asset audit or comprehensive balance sheet is included in the reviewed material. [12]

This episode echoed the earlier 2013 poverty arguments. The repetition is relevant to the history of his legal advocacy, but it does not establish the financial condition of the Colombo organization or the proceeds of any particular extortion scheme. It would be an analytical error to infer organization-wide poverty from one defendant’s reported personal circumstances. [5] [12]

27. The February 2024 Justice Department Retrospective

On February 28, 2024, prosecutors summarized the sentences in the wider case while announcing Vincent Ricciardo’s punishment. The release stated that underboss Castellazzo had pleaded guilty to money-laundering conspiracy and received fifteen months. It separately reported the sixty-month racketeering sentence for Theodore Persico Jr. and the thirty-six-month term for Ralph DiMatteo. [10]

This official comparison provides an important attribution safeguard. The restitution amounts reported for some other defendants are not automatically Castellazzo’s restitution obligations; neither are their offenses interchangeable with his. The register in this article preserves individual dispositions case by case. [10]

28. Scheduled Surrender and Unverified Release Status

Contemporary January 2024 reporting said the court directed Castellazzo to surrender in March of that year. A scheduled surrender is not proof of the exact day he entered custody, whether he received credit for pretrial detention, or the final date of release. Those details require an official custody or docket record. [11]

This edition establishes the January 2024 sentence but does not present a live Bureau of Prisons record or a verified November 2026 institutional status. It does not infer that he is currently incarcerated, released, active in leadership, or deceased from the arithmetic of the nominal fifteen-month term alone. [10] [11]

29. Limits of Present-Day Leadership Claims

The government described Castellazzo as an acting underboss in 2011 and an underboss in 2021–2024. These dated descriptions establish what prosecutors said at those moments. The reviewed material does not authenticate a new formal appointment to boss, a retirement date, or a definitive successor after his 2024 sentencing. [4] [8] [10]

Organized-crime leadership lists circulate online and may supply precise years with limited or recycled sourcing. Without corroborating evidence, a historian should not transform one such list into an official personnel record. This article accordingly uses “government-described underboss” rather than an unqualified current office. [8] [10]

30. “Benji,” “The Claw,” and “The Fang”

The nickname “Benji” appears in both the 2011 indictment and 2023 federal press material; “the Claw” and “the Fang” appear in the 2011 charging instrument. Journalism frequently uses “The Claw,” and a reported post-sentencing exchange linked that nickname to his work as a carpenter, but this was an anecdote rather than an independently documented etymology. [2] [9] [11]

Nicknames can humanize or sensationalize organized-crime biography. Their presence in a source is worth recording, but it does not make the bearer a cinematic character or establish the conduct supposedly implied by a nickname. [2] [11]

31. The Press Narrative and Its Limits

Across more than a decade of reporting, Castellazzo appeared both as a high-ranking alleged organized-crime administrator and as an elderly defendant arguing economic and medical hardship. Both descriptions arose from particular evidence and advocacy contexts. Neither is a complete account of the individual or his criminal history. [5] [11] [12]

The editorial method adopted here privileges charging documents for the fact of charges, plea summaries for admissions, and sentencing reports for the sentence. Headlines using terms such as “mob boss” cannot replace a document’s more precise “acting underboss” or “underboss.” [2] [3] [8]

32. Economic Harms and Union Members

Attempts to coerce a union official and direct employee-benefit vendor selections can harm workers even when no dramatic violent incident occurs. The loss is not limited to a particular cash demand: it can include distorted procurement, compromised labor representation, reduced trust in benefit governance, and risks transferred to members relying on the fund. [8] [9]

The government alleged a design to extract money through official influence. The biography distinguishes those alleged mechanisms from proved monetary losses and individual restitution amounts. A clear account must identify the affected labor institution rather than treating extortion as merely an internal Mafia dispute. [8] [10]

33. Avoiding Attribution from Co-Defendants

The 2021–2023 investigation included different defendants facing different combinations of racketeering, money laundering, loansharking, fraud and other allegations. Vincent Ricciardo’s threats, John Ragano’s workplace certification scheme, or the specific punishment of Michael Uvino cannot simply be assigned to Castellazzo because the men appeared in the same investigation. [8] [9] [10]

Collective descriptions of an organized-crime administration establish a prosecution theory of coordination. Individual criminal liability is narrower and must be derived from each defendant’s indictment, plea, verdict and judgment. This file’s evidence matrix accordingly includes explicit “not established” categories. [9] [10]

34. Two Federal Cases, Two Different Guilty Pleas

The first case grew out of the 2011 organized-crime sweep. By November 2012 Castellazzo had admitted racketeering conspiracy, with the Justice Department identifying two extortion-conspiracy predicates. A 63-month sentence in 2013 is supported by contemporary accounts. The second case emerged from the 2021 Queens union and health-fund investigation, leading to a 2023 money-laundering-conspiracy plea and fifteen months in January 2024. [3] [5] [6] [8] [9] [10] [11]

A composite biography that describes the 2024 conviction simply as “racketeering” would erase a material distinction. It would also blur separate prosecutors, offense periods, evidentiary narratives and sentencing considerations. The two proceedings must be treated as distinct transactions in the life history of the subject. [3] [9] [10]

35. What the Records Reveal About Criminal Administration

The prosecutions present the family as a system that collected income, allocated roles, and sought leverage over legitimate businesses and labor institutions. In 2011 prosecutors emphasized the traditional administration of street boss, acting underboss and consigliere. In 2021 they attributed renewed hierarchical coordination to the leadership around Russo, Castellazzo and DiMatteo. [1] [8]

This repetition does not mean that the organization was unchanged between the two cases. Different senior figures, different sources of revenue, different co-defendants and different prosecution strategies appeared in each. The structural continuity is the use of an administration and its purported share of illicit proceeds, not necessarily identical people holding every job. [1] [8] [9]

36. Legal Terminology: Indictment, Plea, and Sentence

An indictment is a formal accusation brought by a grand jury. It is not proof beyond reasonable doubt. A guilty plea is a defendant’s admission to a specified crime accepted by a court; it does not necessarily adopt the entire narrative in the original indictment. A sentence is the court’s punishment for the conviction, shaped by law and case-specific facts. [2] [3] [9] [10]

The distinction is indispensable in an organized-crime case where lengthy charging papers summarize the alleged affairs of many people. Historical synthesis must therefore avoid converting each paragraph in an indictment into a separate personal conviction. [2] [8]

37. Three Phases of the Publicly Documented Career

The first phase is the earlier criminal career culminating in the 2011 indictment and 2012 racketeering-conspiracy plea. The second phase is the sentence, reported return from custody and renewed appearance as a senior figure in the 2021 indictment. The third is the 2023 money-laundering plea, 2024 sentencing and the unresolved question of later status. [2] [3] [6] [7] [8] [9] [10]

This periodization is anchored to observable records rather than an assertion of lifelong continuous control. It leaves room for future archival additions such as certified judgments, supervised-release records and corroborated appointment details. [3] [8] [10]

38. Geography of the Two Prosecutions

Brooklyn and Staten Island appear in contemporary accounts of the earlier construction-business and pizzeria extortion matter. The later charging narrative centers on a Queens-based labor union and a health fund whose decisions could affect workers and vendors across the region. Castellazzo himself was listed as a resident of Manahawkin, New Jersey in the 2021 and 2023 federal identification. [5] [8] [9]

These locations should not be mistaken for a verified, exhaustive map of his personal operations. A defendant’s reported residence, a victim’s business location and the district in which a case was prosecuted answer different historical questions. [5] [8]

39. Castellazzo Among Other Colombo Leaders

John Franzese was identified as underboss in 2008; Andrew Russo was identified as street boss in 2011 and boss in 2021; Richard Fusco was the 2011 consigliere; Ralph DiMatteo occupied the consigliere role in the 2021 case. Their dated records make it possible to construct a partial leadership timeline without assuming all offices remained constant between public reports. [14] [4] [8]

Castellazzo’s underboss role is thus best understood through institutional relationships rather than a fixed nickname or an assumed succession. Other biographies in the MGU collection document the Persico–Orena conflict, the Russo administration and the family’s different generations. [4] [8] [10]

40. Unresolved Biographical Questions

The reviewed records do not independently settle his exact date and place of birth, early initiation, precise captain appointment, continuous officeholding during imprisonment, any later formal accession to boss, or his exact custody location and leadership status as of the editorial cutoff. [2] [8] [10]

Some older case details, including the exact original 2013 sentencing judgment and his reported 2015 release date, are supported by contemporary or scholarly secondary summaries rather than complete certified dockets. Those boundaries are made explicit so future revisions can target the right evidence rather than overwrite established distinctions. [6] [7]

41. Historical Interpretation

Castellazzo’s career illuminates how an organized-crime administration can be dismantled in a major publicized prosecution yet appear again in later investigative records. That observation does not demonstrate that particular actors exercised uninterrupted operational command; it shows that the institutional categories and economic incentives remained salient to prosecutors more than a decade later. [1] [3] [8] [9]

The most telling contrast is between the local-business extortion predicates admitted in the earlier racketeering case and the later attempt, alleged by prosecutors, to influence a union benefits system through vendor arrangements. Both illustrate the vulnerability of lawful commerce to coercion, but only the specific later money-laundering plea is attributable as Castellazzo’s individual 2023 conviction. [3] [8] [9] [10]

42. Historical Conclusion

Benjamin Castellazzo’s documented public record is that of a Colombo figure described by federal prosecutors as acting underboss in 2011 and underboss in 2021–2024. His established federal dispositions include an earlier racketeering-conspiracy plea, with a reported 63-month sentence, and a later money-laundering-conspiracy plea followed by a confirmed fifteen-month sentence. [3] [6] [8] [9] [10]

The record is historically substantial but bounded. It does not justify assigning him every crime in a multi-defendant indictment or declaring a verified current rank, birth date or custody status. The evidence-centered account presented here preserves both the significance of the prosecutions and the limits of what the sources prove. [2] [9] [10]

Research and Publication Limitations

Birth and personal data. The approximate birth-year range is calculated from reported ages in dated sources, not a certified birth record. Childhood and formal educational history are not independently documented.

Leadership appointments. Acting-underboss and underboss labels represent dated U.S. government identifications. Formal induction, promotion, resignation, replacement, retirement and present-day leadership status remain incompletely documented.

Original 2011 indictment. The indictment was inspected for named identity and roles and the relevant page reviewed visually. Its allegations are not treated as automatically admitted facts; the 2012 Justice Department plea announcement is used to establish admitted conduct.

Early sentence and release. The 63-month term and summer 2015 release are supported by contemporary or retrospective secondary reporting, not a personally inspected certified federal judgment or BOP release register. Exact plea date in that case remains unverified.

2021 group indictment. Government allegations involving extortion, union control, benefits vendor steering and other defendants are not presented as personal convictions of Castellazzo. The case resolved against him by a money-laundering-conspiracy plea.

2024 sentence. The fifteen-month sentence is confirmed by DOJ; the January 22 court date comes from contemporary reporting. The complete sentencing transcript, credits for custody, and exact present correctional status were not independently collated.

Financial and medical claims. Poverty, subsidized-housing and health descriptions derive from courtroom journalism about mitigation advocacy and are attributed accordingly, not certified by this encyclopedia.

Research currency. Public verification was conducted through November 11, 2026. No claim of current prison location, death, discharge, or active criminal-organization command is made without a verifiable source.

Publication environment. The page follows the MGU root-relative stylesheet and shared component paths. No unlicensed portrait image is embedded. Browser rendering under the deployed production domain and full external link persistence have not been independently tested.
