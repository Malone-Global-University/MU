1. Historical Overview

Joseph Magliocco (1898–1963) was Joseph Profaci’s longtime deputy and the short-lived successor to the Brooklyn-based organization later known as the Colombo crime family. His career bridged two moments of institutional formation: the late-1920s meetings of Sicilian-American underworld figures and the family’s succession crisis during the Gallo rebellion of the early 1960s. His significance rests not simply on holding the title of boss, but on his inability to secure recognition of that position from the wider Mafia Commission. [1][6][7]

The surviving record is unusually useful for separating these levels of authority. State investigative documents establish commercial interests and earlier legal encounters; a 1960 appellate ruling describes his conduct at Apalachin; Attorney General Robert F. Kennedy publicly addressed his unconfirmed leadership in September 1963; and a later congressional consultant report reconstructs his resignation. Together, those sources reveal a leadership succession that was provisional, negotiated, and vulnerable to intervention from rival bosses. [1][5][6][7]

2. Name, Dates, and Identification

The subject is generally identified as Giuseppe Joseph Magliocco, frequently called “Joe Magliocco” and sometimes styled “Joe Malayak” or “Evil Eye” in later Mafia histories. Both a Federal Bureau of Narcotics identification card reproduced in a 2025 academic working paper and a 1958 New York State investigation report give his birthday as June 29, 1898. His death on December 28, 1963, is supported by contemporary reporting retained in an FBI investigative file. [1][2][11]

Place-of-birth accounts differ. The state investigative report prints Villabate in the Palermo area, while the Federal Bureau of Narcotics card gives Portella di Mare, a nearby locality in the Palermo region. These cannot simply be silently reconciled into a single exact village label. The defensible short-form birthplace is “Palermo area, Sicily”; original municipal birth registration remains the decisive missing record. [1][2]

3. Immigration and the Conflicting Arrival Years

The 1958 New York State appendix assigns Magliocco an arrival year of 1924. A 2025 migration study, however, reproduces a passenger manifest for the Taormina sailing from Palermo in April 1914 and identifies a teenage Joe Magliocco traveling with his family. Its reconstruction places the family in Brooklyn before the First World War, rather than first arriving in the mid-1920s. [1][2]

Because the later scholarly study rests on a stated passenger-list and census comparison, it provides a stronger evidence trail for an earlier arrival than the state report’s unexplained summary date. The biography retains 1914 as the research-supported arrival but marks the discrepancy pending direct, independent comparison of the manifest, name variants, and subsequent naturalization file. It would be unsound to portray his migration as a direct consequence of the fascist anti-Mafia campaign of the mid-1920s if the earlier record is correct. [2]

4. Family Background and the Profaci Connection

Magliocco’s sister Ninfa married Giuseppe (Joseph) Profaci. This gives a clear documentary basis for identifying the two men as brothers-in-law, rather than treating the relationship as merely an expression of criminal closeness. The 1958 state report also calls Profaci and Magliocco second cousins, although that more distant kinship claim requires independent genealogical checking. [1][2]

The kinship network had practical consequences. A leader able to depend on a relative as deputy could centralize sensitive negotiations, introductions, and financial relationships. Yet family trust was not identical to political legitimacy inside the organization: Magliocco’s eventual successor would benefit precisely from repudiating a plan associated with this inherited inner circle. [7][10]

5. Early Brooklyn Residence and Employment

The 2025 academic study identifies Magliocco in the 1920 census living at 646 Union Street in a Brooklyn neighborhood with a sizable Sicilian immigrant population. It characterizes his recorded work then as laboring and later traces his developing commercial identity. This evidence is valuable because it places a traceable civilian life before the period in which police records identified him as an underworld figure. [2]

Early residence and occupation should not be retroactively turned into proof of an organized-crime initiation. The path from labor to commerce and then to alleged criminal leadership has to be reconstructed through differentiated evidence: manifests and census records for residence, public licenses for businesses, court records for offenses, and government intelligence for organizational attribution. [1][2][14]

6. Olive Oil and the Sunland Beverage Company

A 1958 New York State investigative appendix states that Magliocco worked in the olive-oil business before 1933, and began operating Sunland Beverage Company as a wholesale beer distributor that year. According to the report, the firm had been licensed since June 14, 1933, and he was its sole stockholder. A Federal Bureau of Narcotics identification card independently lists ownership of the same concern, making Sunland one of the most concretely documented elements of his economic life. [1][2]

The existence of a licensed beverage business does not in itself establish that its receipts were criminal proceeds. At the same time, distribution routes, credit relationships, warehouses, and business contacts could give well-connected figures legitimate commercial standing and information networks. Any specific allegation that Sunland functioned as a criminal front requires documentary support beyond its owner’s reported Mafia affiliation. [1][14]

7. The 1928 Cleveland Meeting

Police interrupted a meeting of prominent Italian-American figures at Cleveland’s Hotel Statler in December 1928. FBI historical material and contemporary research identify both Profaci and Magliocco among its attendees. The gathering has been interpreted as an effort to negotiate disputes between criminal groups, but later descriptions of its exact agenda are derived in part from retrospective intelligence rather than complete, contemporaneous minutes. [3][4]

The date requires care: the 1958 New York State report includes a reference to a “1926” Cleveland meeting in Magliocco’s appendix, while the documented Statler raid took place in 1928. The article follows the corroborated 1928 date. It does not claim that the meeting itself formally installed Magliocco in an office, because the available contemporary raid evidence does not certify such an appointment. [1][3][4]

8. The Cleveland Gun and Alcohol Arrests

New York State’s 1958 investigative report records that Magliocco was arrested in 1928 on accusations involving a gun and six ounces of alcohol. It also records that he produced a gun permit and that both charges were dismissed. These details belong in the same sentence as the arrest whenever the early case is summarized: an arrest record without its documented disposition would imply a conviction not supported by this source. [1]

Later popular narratives often flatten the Cleveland episode into a dramatic roundup of Mafia chiefs. The historically stronger statement is narrower: a raid occurred, Magliocco was identified among the participants, and the specific charges listed in the state biographical appendix did not result in a conviction. The broader significance is the visibility of intercity relationships, not proof of an adjudicated conspiracy at that hotel. [1][3][4]

9. The 1930 Wine-Transportation Case

The New York State report lists a November 20, 1930 conviction in federal court in Brooklyn for transporting wine and reports a fine; the exact amount is not confidently legible in the digitized reproduction. This is a particularized legal outcome, not an inference from association or a later criminal-intelligence assessment. It also places Magliocco within the regulatory and enforcement environment of the last years of national Prohibition. [1]

The conviction should not be inflated into a broader adjudication of bootlegging conspiracy, violence, or control of an alcohol empire. The summary report provides the offense description and disposition, but not the original charging instrument or judgment; those records should be obtained before adopting any more expansive legal characterization. [1]

10. Profaci’s Underboss and Senior Deputy

Investigative and congressional accounts identify Magliocco as Profaci’s underboss, the trusted deputy who remained near the top of the organization until Profaci died. His role was reinforced by marriage ties and decades of shared association. Historians sometimes attribute his appointment to the 1928 Cleveland meeting, but the strongest immediately accessible records do not document a formal installation date; the length of his tenure is more secure than its exact starting day. [1][7][10]

Within a Mafia family, an underboss could convey orders, resolve disputes, help supervise crews, and stand in for a boss. Those are general organizational responsibilities, not evidence that Magliocco personally authorized every act later attributed to the Profaci group. A useful biography distinguishes the documented office from conjecture about participation in particular rackets or killings. [7][14]

11. The Early-1930s Consolidation of the New York Families

The power struggles of 1930–1931 led to a more stable five-family arrangement and a coordinating Mafia Commission. Profaci’s Brooklyn organization survived this reordering, while Magliocco remained associated with its senior leadership. Retrospective accounts describe the pair as attempting to avoid the direct destruction that befell some rival factions during the Castellammarese War. [3][10][14]

It is tempting to portray the early Commission as a perfectly documented corporate board, but membership rosters and decisions are reconstructed from later cooperating witnesses, law-enforcement accounts, and independent historical research. Magliocco’s subsequent conflict illustrates why this distinction matters: assuming local control did not necessarily establish the right to occupy a Commission seat. [6][7]

12. The Family’s Criminal Economy and Magliocco’s Attribution

The Profaci organization operated in a Brooklyn environment associated in public investigations with illegal gambling, loansharking, extortion, and the manipulation of business relationships. Robert F. Kennedy’s 1963 Senate statement described a racketeering-related intrusion into a Brooklyn furniture business involving a high-ranking member of the Profaci–Magliocco family. It did not name Magliocco as the actor in that example, and the article does not assign that conduct to him personally. [6][14]

That distinction is an essential control on evidentiary overreach. General proof that an organization engaged in criminal activity cannot substitute for evidence that a named official planned, financed, directed, or participated in each offense. Magliocco’s known beverage enterprise and the family’s alleged rackets therefore appear as separate categories unless a specific source connects them. [1][6]

13. The 1957 Apalachin Gathering

On November 14, 1957, state police encountered prominent figures at or near the estate of Joseph Barbara in Apalachin, New York. The 1960 appellate opinion states that Magliocco was among the drivers stopped at a roadblock as men departed the area; it also lists Profaci among the attendees and describes police attempts to identify participants and determine the gathering’s purpose. [5]

New York’s 1958 report further records that Magliocco was registered at the Hotel Sterling in Wilkes-Barre, Pennsylvania, on November 14–15. Participation made him a visible subject of interstate law-enforcement scrutiny. It did not, by itself, establish that a federal or state crime had occurred at the gathering—a point the appellate judges stressed when assessing the prosecution that followed. [1][5]

14. The Federal Apalachin Conspiracy Prosecution

The government prosecuted Magliocco and other defendants on a theory that participants in the Apalachin gathering conspired to obstruct justice and commit perjury by coordinating false accounts of the meeting. A jury returned convictions, and Magliocco was among those who appealed. The case is reported as United States v. Bufalino, 285 F.2d 408 (2d Cir. 1960). [5]

On November 28, 1960, the Second Circuit reversed the conspiracy convictions and ordered that conspiracy count dismissed. It found insufficient evidence both of an agreement to lie under oath and of the necessary anticipation of sworn proceedings. The opinion discussed separate perjury counts for some defendants, but its disposition of the conspiracy charge must not be carelessly described as a ruling adjudicating every possible count or every earlier legal case. [5]

15. What the Apalachin Appellate Ruling Did—and Did Not—Decide

The appellate opinion did not certify that those assembled were law-abiding acquaintances or that New York’s Mafia families did not exist. The judges evaluated the federal indictment and the admissible proof offered to support it. The meeting’s perceived secrecy and the defendants’ similar explanations were not enough to establish the charged conspiratorial agreement. [5]

Likewise, a later Federal Bureau of Narcotics card still summarized Magliocco as having received a five-year term and $10,000 fine in 1960. That administrative snapshot must be read against the reported appellate reversal, not substituted for the final judicial disposition. For MGU, the judicial opinion controls the description of the conspiracy conviction’s legal status. [2][5]

16. Sources of the Gallo–Profaci Revolt

By the end of the 1950s, dissatisfaction within the Profaci organization had accumulated around tribute demands, unequal distribution of opportunities, and disputes involving the Gallo brothers and their Brooklyn associates. The murder of gambling operator Frank Abbatemarco in 1959 appears prominently in reconstructions of the dispute, although the allocation of particular roles and motives varies by source. [7][10][16]

The conflict was not merely a personal disagreement between Profaci and a few dissatisfied soldiers. It brought into question the distribution of illicit revenue and the legitimacy of leadership based on long-standing friendships and family alliances. As Profaci’s chief deputy and relative, Magliocco was one of the leadership figures most directly exposed to that challenge. [7]

17. The February 1961 Kidnapping

In February 1961, Gallo-aligned rebels kidnapped senior Profaci associates; multiple historical accounts identify Magliocco among the captives. The action was designed to force concessions from the organization’s leadership while Profaci himself remained beyond the immediate reach of the insurgents. Versions of the episode differ as to the precise number, identities, and duration of captivity. [7][16]

The kidnapping demonstrates that Magliocco was not a detached administrator observing an abstract family dispute. He was physically vulnerable as a senior representative of the leadership faction. Later negotiations secured the captives’ release, but the underlying argument over power and criminal profits continued, turning into a protracted series of shootings, threatened reprisals, and shifting allegiances. [7][10]

18. Escalation, Retaliation, and the Limits of Attribution

Violence escalated during 1961 as the Gallo and Profaci factions attempted to weaken one another. Later chronologies record a series of killings, disappearances, failed attacks, and defections; participants’ loyalties also changed, complicating simple stories of two fixed armies. In September 1963, the U.S. attorney general stated publicly that the Gallo–Profaci conflict had yielded five deaths and numerous serious injuries since summer 1961 as then understood by federal investigators. [6][7]

A historical account can explain how this violence constrained Magliocco without attributing every shooting to his personal command. Allegations of particular murder orders are distinct from documented leadership office. Where the article draws on law-enforcement chronologies or participant recollections rather than criminal verdicts, it identifies the material as reconstruction. [6][7]

19. Profaci’s Death in June 1962

Joseph Profaci died in June 1962, ending a long tenure as head of the organization. Magliocco was the most obvious internal successor because he had spent years as underboss and was Profaci’s brother-in-law. Both the 1958 state report and later official histories document the relationship; subsequent federal records unequivocally identify Magliocco as having taken over the Brooklyn family. [1][6][10]

A source-critical complication is that Ralph Salerno’s congressional consultant report prints Profaci’s death incorrectly as March 1962 in one passage and speaks of late 1962 in another. These are textual errors, not alternate valid dates. The corrected June 1962 chronology must govern any derivative timeline built from that report. [7]

20. Taking Control: 1962–1963

Magliocco assumed de facto leadership after Profaci died. His title was not fictional: Attorney General Robert F. Kennedy explicitly described him in September 1963 as the man who had taken over in Brooklyn. Yet the same federal statement made clear that the national Mafia Commission had not confirmed him. The distinction between acting as boss and securing Commission recognition is the pivotal organizational fact of his biography. [6]

Family continuity could secure support from longstanding Profaci loyalists but could not settle the opposition of the Gallo faction or other families’ concern that a prolonged war might provoke additional law-enforcement attention. Magliocco inherited both a hierarchy and a legitimacy dispute. That tension would shape each major decision of his brief tenure. [7][10]

21. The Reported War Fund and Gaming Assessments

A chronology reproduced in Salerno’s congressional consultant report says that after taking command Magliocco sought an additional $100 per month from each gambling operation to finance the conflict against the Gallo faction. The passage provides concrete evidence of how investigators understood the financial pressures associated with the succession. It is not itself a bank ledger, recorded demand, or sworn judicial finding. [7]

If accurate, the assessment would have reinforced the same pattern of concentrated tribute collection that had contributed to resistance against Profaci. The practical dilemma was self-reinforcing: raising revenue to fight an internal war risked alienating the people from whom the revenue was extracted. The report’s number should therefore be labeled as a historical source claim rather than a verified total for Magliocco’s personal receipts. [7]

22. Commission Recognition and Interfamily Authority

In his September 25, 1963 testimony, Robert F. Kennedy said Magliocco had not been confirmed by the Commission and probably would not be. Kennedy also stated that he had sought support from Commission members Angelo Bruno and Stefano Magaddino. This contemporaneous assessment is stronger evidence of the disputed leadership status than later succession lists that simply assign a seamless 1962–1963 term. [6]

An FBI field-office memorandum from January 1963 adds a view from another evidentiary angle: a confidential source reported that associates intended to bring concerns about the “Joe Magliocco Family” to the Commission and discuss how much of it they controlled. That is an intelligence allegation concerning a proposed meeting, not proof that the meeting occurred exactly as predicted. Still, it establishes that discontent with Magliocco’s authority was being reported well before his exit. [9]

23. The Continued War in 1963

The year following Profaci’s death did not produce peace. Salerno’s retrospective chronology identifies several June 1963 shootings and deaths and describes attempts by Commission-linked figures to negotiate a settlement. Those episodes help explain why senior Mafia figures treated the Brooklyn situation as a governance problem extending beyond one local family. [7]

In a secretive criminal organization, even a dispute confined geographically to Brooklyn could draw in other families through business partners, protection arrangements, and overlapping territories. The Commission’s interest in ending the Gallo conflict is therefore consistent with its perceived role as a mechanism for minimizing interfamily escalation. The record does not require us to assume a perfectly unified Commission or a single uncontested account of each mediation attempt. [6][7]

24. Joseph Bonanno and the Inherited Alliance

Joseph Bonanno had been a close associate of Profaci, and that relationship offered Magliocco a potential source of support at a time when other leaders withheld recognition. Bonanno’s son Salvatore (“Bill”) was married into the Profaci family; these ties intertwined family networks with the increasingly difficult question of Commission appointments. [7][10][15]

Later investigators and historical narratives depict Bonanno as an ally in an attempt to reshape the Commission. Bonanno’s own memoir rejects significant parts of the plot account. An evidence-based biography must identify these competing versions, especially because several stories of what Bonanno personally ordered are reconstructed from other participants, informants, and retrospective official reports rather than from an uncontested court verdict against him for the alleged Commission conspiracy. [7][8][15]

25. The Alleged 1963 Plot Against Commission Leaders

Official retrospective investigations describe a plan in 1963 to assassinate Carlo Gambino and Thomas Lucchese, prominent New York family leaders. The House Select Committee on Assassinations identified Bonanno as a key figure in the plan, while the Salerno consultant report attributes Magliocco a role in arranging its execution. Some later narratives add other intended victims, but the two New York targets are the most consistently grounded in the cited congressional passages. [7][8]

This proposed violence must be treated as a historical allegation made in government findings, not described as a completed assassination. Neither Gambino nor Lucchese was killed in the supposed attempt. The relevant historical outcome was political: discovery of the allegation strengthened the opposition to Magliocco and Bonanno and changed the balance of authority within the family structure. [7][8]

26. Joseph Colombo’s Disclosure

The standard account holds that Magliocco passed the murder assignment to Joseph Colombo, a younger member of his organization, who disclosed the plan to Gambino rather than carry it out. Salerno’s report and the later House Committee findings both repeat the essential outline, although they differ in emphasis about who originated the scheme. [7][8]

Colombo’s alleged disclosure turned him from a subordinate of Magliocco into a figure capable of obtaining support from more powerful family leaders. Historiographically, this is the hinge between the Profaci organization and the later Colombo family. It would be misleading to characterize the episode as an ordinary promotion by Magliocco; it was a challenge to his leadership that helped end it. [7][10][12]

27. The Reported Atlantic City Commission Meeting

Salerno’s consultant report describes a summer 1963 meeting in Atlantic City attended by Stefano Magaddino, Gambino, Lucchese, Magliocco, Bonanno, and Angelo Bruno. Its account portrays Magaddino rejecting Magliocco’s claim to Commission standing and instructing him to cease acting as boss. The precise conversational details derive from retrospective testimony and intelligence summaries and are not presented here as a verbatim transcript. [7]

The meeting narrative also captures two simultaneous disputes: whether Magliocco could govern a fractured Brooklyn family and whether Bonanno could seek a broader rearrangement of power. The episode belongs in both the Colombo and Bonanno histories, but its direct significance for Magliocco is the demonstrated fragility of the authority he inherited in 1962. [7][8]

28. September 1963: Removal and Retirement

One chronology in Salerno’s congressional consultant report gives September 3, 1963 as the date Magliocco stepped down and told assembled captains that the decision came from the Commission. It describes a cease-fire and a temporary assumption of supervisory authority by the Commission. This provides a more precise proposed date than vague formulations such as “sometime before his death.” [7]

There is, however, a documentary tension: Robert F. Kennedy’s public statement on September 25 still described Magliocco as having taken over while lacking confirmation. The statement can be read as a broad description of his disputed status rather than a day-by-day leadership update. The biography therefore presents September 3 as the retrospective report’s date, not as a simultaneously authenticated resignation document. [6][7]

29. Was the Commission Assessment $43,000 or $50,000?

Many popular accounts say the Commission imposed a $50,000 penalty before allowing Magliocco to retire. Salerno’s report, by contrast, twice identifies the assessment as $43,000 and states that it was paid in four installments. The report associates it with expenses incurred in investigating or settling the dispute. [7][12]

Neither figure should be represented as a payment established through a surviving audited receipt. For MGU’s first edition, the more specific government consultant figure of $43,000 is cited as that report’s finding, while the widely repeated $50,000 amount is labeled as a conflicting secondary figure. A facsimile of the underlying informant report or payment record would be needed to resolve the discrepancy definitively. [7][12]

30. Colombo’s Succession and the Family’s Name

After Magliocco’s displacement, Joseph Colombo emerged as the successor. Many reference timelines begin Colombo’s tenure in 1963; Salerno’s retrospective chronology instead places formal appointment in early 1964. The dates may reflect a distinction between growing de facto influence and Commission-sanctioned assumption of leadership, but the article does not assert that hypothesis as a documented procedural rule. [7][10][12]

The organization was previously identified with Profaci, not with Colombo, and its later customary name memorializes the successor rather than the short-lived Magliocco administration. This evolution can make Magliocco appear as a minor footnote. In fact, the failure of his succession was central to why the family came to carry someone else’s name. [10][14]

31. Death on December 28, 1963

Magliocco died on December 28, 1963 at Good Samaritan Hospital in West Islip, Long Island. A contemporary newspaper clipping preserved in an FBI file reports that a Suffolk County deputy medical examiner certified a heart attack. Official retrospective accounts likewise refer to his death from natural causes, occurring only months after his fall from effective leadership. [7][10][11]

His death date closes the individual chronology but does not automatically resolve the date when Colombo first exercised power. A family might have operated under contested or interim arrangements before a new leader’s public identification by investigators. It is therefore preferable to maintain separately the dates of Magliocco’s death, his reported resignation, and Colombo’s disputed recognition. [6][7]

32. The Later Exhumation and Poisoning Rumor

An archival newspaper clipping in the FBI’s Carlo Gambino files reports that investigators later exhumed Magliocco’s body after agents recorded a conversation in which two Mafia figures voiced a suspicion that Bonanno had poisoned him. The clipping says a chemical analysis was pending. This is evidence of an investigation prompted by hearsay, not evidence that poisoning occurred. [11]

The available clipping itself does not supply a completed toxicology finding contradicting the original medical examiner’s certification of heart attack. Unless a dispositive autopsy, toxicology report, or comparable official finding is produced, MGU should not narrate Magliocco’s death as murder. The allegation is included because it shaped later suspicion, with its evidentiary limitation plainly stated. [11]

33. Using Federal Intelligence Without Mistaking It for a Verdict

Some of the strongest biographical details come from intelligence files: the 1958 state profile, the FBI’s account of the 1928 Cleveland meeting, and memoranda discussing Commission politics. These sources often preserve names, dates, observations, and commercial connections that are absent from popular biographies. They also preserve suspicions, informant interpretations, clerical errors, and events that were never tested in open court. [1][3][9]

The required editorial method is to label the source kind at the point of use. Court decisions establish their specific dispositions; executive statements establish what officials said on a given date; records of organizational conversations establish reported beliefs, not the truth of every belief; and a late memoir establishes an author’s retrospective position. The result is a biography of a historical figure rather than an uncritical reproduction of Mafia folklore. [5][6][7][15]

34. Why His Brief Leadership Mattered

Magliocco’s period as boss was short, but the crisis it revealed was structural. A trusted deputy could inherit a family through proximity to the predecessor, yet lose office because rival family leaders regarded the transfer as unacceptable. His case thus documents the limits of hereditary and friendship-based succession within an organization that also claimed collective adjudicative authority through the Commission. [6][7][10]

The episode had consequences beyond his personal career. Colombo’s promotion reshaped the family’s public identity; the Gallo conflict remained an enduring feature of its history; and Bonanno’s own struggle with the Commission developed into further factional turmoil. Magliocco is best understood as the connecting figure between Profaci’s long regime and a more volatile period of organized-crime governance. [7][8][10]

35. Historiography and Lasting Significance

Most general Mafia histories emphasize the failed Gambino–Lucchese plot, a purported $50,000 penalty, and the rapid arrival of Joseph Colombo. Those elements are not necessarily invented, but the shorthand obscures important distinctions. A contemporaneous attorney general explicitly stated Magliocco was unconfirmed; a congressional consultant gave a different financial figure and a later Colombo appointment; and the original Apalachin appellate record prevents historians from casually misreporting an overturned conspiracy conviction as a final one. [5][6][7][12]

Newly accessible immigration research adds another dimension. The family migration and Brooklyn census evidence help place Magliocco in a longer history of Sicilian-American neighborhood life, business formation, and institutional association. It also reveals why a first-edition biography should retain contested arrival and birthplace details as research questions rather than inventing certainty. [1][2]

36. Historical Conclusion

Joseph Magliocco’s career shows a powerful underboss becoming an unstable successor at the moment his organization most needed uncontested leadership. His businesses and relationships made him a consequential figure well before 1962, and his presence at the Cleveland and Apalachin gatherings places him in two generations of organized-crime history. Yet the record of his brief command is defined above all by the distinction between wielding local power and obtaining interfamily acceptance. [1][3][5][6]

The historical verdict is not dependent on dramatic speculation about secret poisonings or on treating every reported plot as a court-proved conspiracy. It rests on the documented succession crisis, the official recognition dispute, the emergence of Joseph Colombo, and the inability of the Profaci family’s old leadership network to end its civil conflict on its own terms. [6][7][10][11]

Sources and Further Reading

The register prioritizes original judicial and executive records, contemporary government investigations, and identifiable secondary scholarship. Source citations identify the supporting text or its limits; they do not imply that every sentence in a linked document was independently authenticated.

Open source. Editorial use: Contemporaneous government report; biographical assertions, businesses, arrests, and kinship. Its immigrant-arrival date and an incidental Cleveland-meeting year conflict with independently documented sources.

Working paper, not a fully collated family biography.

  3. Federal Bureau of Investigation, Mafia Monograph, Part IV, “Cleveland Meeting, 1928.” Open source. Editorial use: Retrospective investigative synthesis about the 1928 Cleveland arrests and reported meeting purposes; intelligence claims are not judicial findings.

  4. Scott M. Deitche, “Hoodlums at the Statler,” The Mob Museum, December 18, 2018. Open source. Editorial use: Historical examination of the Cleveland meeting and its date; interpretation of the meeting remains partly speculative.

Research and Publication Limitations

This first-edition article is a research-led historical synthesis, not a certified archival edition. Original Sicilian birth registration, passenger-list identity cross-checks, the 1930 federal criminal judgment, the alleged Commission assessment’s underlying records, a signed September 1963 resignation record, and any completed toxicology determination following the later exhumation have not been independently collated as original documents for this publication. The published congressional consultant report includes incorrect dating of Joseph Profaci’s death; its precise historical claims are attributed and cross-checked where possible.

Law-enforcement characterizations of criminal affiliation and individual conduct are not treated as court convictions. The Apalachin conspiracy judgment’s appellate reversal is reflected explicitly, and the alleged assassination plan is attributed to government retrospective findings rather than to a conviction of Magliocco. Biographical uncertainties are retained for future documented correction.
