1. Historical Overview

Lawrence “Larry” Gallo (generally recorded as born November 3, 1927, and deceased in May 1968) was a Brooklyn organized-crime figure identified with the Joseph Profaci family, subsequently known as the Colombo crime family. He was the elder brother of Joseph “Crazy Joe” Gallo and Albert “Kid Blast” Gallo, and was widely described as the more deliberate negotiator and organizer of the dissident faction that confronted Profaci in 1961. His historical importance rests on a violent challenge to internal Mafia authority, the attempted strangling he survived, and his later efforts to hold the faction together while his brother Joseph was imprisoned. [3] [4] [5] [10] [11]

Larry never became the recognized boss of the entire New York crime family. Leadership of an insurrectionary street faction must not be confused with Commission-approved control over the parent organization. He became a public figure in his own right through reporting on the Gallo–Profaci conflict and, unexpectedly, through his participation in New York City’s effort to reduce racial violence in East New York in 1966. Those events reveal the entanglement of organized crime, neighborhood status, public authority and the press. [6] [9] [14]

Primary court papers and federal intelligence support several key events, although they do not prove every named attack or reported private conversation. The strongest direct documentary anchors are the New York Court of Appeals’ acknowledgment of the August 20, 1961 Sahara Lounge investigation and an original June 18, 1963 FBI message documenting intelligence about Larry Gallo’s attempted outside mediation. [1] [2] [3]

2. Identification and Name Variants

The subject appears as Lawrence Gallo and Larry Gallo. An FBI deceased-person index lists both “Lawrence Gallo” and “Lawrence Albert Gallo,” each associated with November 3, 1927; the latter form may reflect a middle name or indexing alias, rather than establishing an independent person. A reference biography should therefore use Lawrence (“Larry”) Gallo as its unambiguous headword and identify the date’s source. [5]

Lawrence was not Joseph Gregory Gallo, whose 1972 killing later dominated popular accounts, nor Joseph N. Gallo of the Gambino family. He must also be separated from a different Lawrence “Larry” Gallo indicted by federal prosecutors in 2011: a person alleged to have spoken to the FBI in 2010 plainly cannot be the man documented as dead in 1968. Same-name database hits are not sufficient to attribute criminal cases or biographical events. [7] [16]

3. Birth Date, Age and Uncertainties

The FBI’s index supports November 3, 1927, as a reported birth date. An individual born that day would have been 40—not 41—when dying on May 17, 1968. Nevertheless, both the New York Times and Newsday obituary headlines reportedly called him 41. Rather than silently choosing between these, the profile preserves the exact date with attribution and records the discrepancy. [5] [7] [15]

The original birth registration, complete Social Security or military file, and death certificate were not reviewed. Biographical accounts offer Brooklyn or New York City as birthplace, but this edition does not assign an unverified hospital or street address. Specific demographic details should remain open to correction when the underlying vital record is acquired. [5] [17]

4. Family and Brooklyn Environment

Larry belonged to the same Brooklyn household and network as brothers Joseph and Albert, whose shared surname and close association sometimes obscure the different functions they performed. The later press tended to portray Joey as the theatrical insurgent, Albert as the feared younger fighter, and Larry as the group’s head or sober manager. These are helpful descriptions of reputation, not fixed job titles established in a payroll or organizational constitution. [6] [10] [11]

The brothers developed their influence in the waterfront and working-class districts of South Brooklyn, including Red Hook and nearby streets. Neighborhood familiarity mattered to the criminal enterprise because local bars, gambling operators, lenders and social clubs could become both revenue centers and informal intelligence networks. No inference about ordinary Italian-American residents’ criminality follows from these associations. [9] [10] [11]

5. Profaci Family Affiliation

The Gallo faction emerged from the organization of Joseph Profaci, a longstanding New York Mafia boss whose sphere of activity included much of Brooklyn. Secondary histories place the brothers among Profaci-aligned enforcers and collectors before the rebellion, operating in a hierarchy in which senior figures distributed opportunities and expected payments from lower-ranking crews. Their existence as a crew does not establish an independent sixth Mafia family. [4] [10] [11]

The organization was later known by the name of Joseph Colombo. That later label is useful for navigation, but using “Colombo crime family” without qualification for 1961 can obscure the fact that Profaci, not Colombo, was then in control. The biography distinguishes institutional continuity from the change in leadership and public name. [3] [4]

6. Revenue, Racketeering and Factional Economics

Historians explain the rebellion partly through disputes over proceeds from gambling, loans and protection activities, and through the perception that Profaci favored his older associates and relatives. The killing of numbers operator Frank Abbatemarco and subsequent disagreements over lucrative operations appear repeatedly in these narratives. The precise bargain purportedly promised to the Gallos has not been corroborated here by financial records or signed testimony. [10] [11]

These disputes illustrate why internal Mafia arrangements were unstable. Participants could not bring disputes over criminal proceeds before a lawful contract tribunal. Threats and allegiance, rather than transparent accounting, became mechanisms for enforcing decisions. This explanation is about criminal-enterprise governance, not justification for the Gallo faction’s violence. [10] [11]

7. The Brothers as Distinct Actors

Larry’s significance can disappear when a narrative treats all actions taken by “the Gallos” as decisions by Joey. Joe’s later imprisonment removes any basis for assuming that he personally organized street-level negotiations throughout the 1960s. Larry is explicitly named in the FBI’s 1963 message as an individual seeking assistance with the continuing dispute; this is evidence that the elder brother retained agency in the feud. [3]

Conversely, individual incidents such as the attempted 1961 strangling concern Larry, not Joey. The family relationship matters, but historical responsibility must be assigned by incident, place and source. The same distinction applies when secondary accounts credit the brothers with rescue work, civic negotiations or violent acts without independently identifying each person’s role. [1] [6] [9]

8. The February 1961 Kidnappings

In late February 1961, insurgents associated with the Gallo brothers seized several senior Profaci personnel, including underboss Joseph Magliocco, in an effort to compel concessions. A later House report reproduces a chronology naming Nicholas Forlano, Carmine Persico Jr. and the brothers among the insurgents, and describing a hurried agreement followed by renewed conflict. The source reflects an FBI-linked account rather than a complete court docket of the kidnappings. [4]

The event was exceptional because it challenged the immediate authority of a recognized family boss while leaving the broader structure of the criminal organization intact. Figures commonly given for the number of captives or proportion of family supporters vary; numerical precision should not be manufactured from reconstructed testimony. Profaci remained determined to eliminate the rebellion despite reported concessions. [4] [10] [11]

9. Profaci Countermeasures and Renewed Hostility

Secondary accounts describe the leadership offering concessions and then drawing faction members back to its side. The resulting realignments placed men who had previously associated with the Gallos in opposition to Larry’s crew. The war was neither a simple personal vendetta between brothers and a boss nor an uninterrupted firefight: it involved shifting loyalties, regional mediation and the distribution of illicit income. [4] [10] [11]

Carmine Persico is especially central to the betrayal narrative, but accounts of the terms of his defection are typically retrospective. This page avoids transforming either a reported informant recollection or a repeated underworld nickname into proof of a precise conspiracy unless independent judicial documentation exists. [1] [4] [11]

10. August 20, 1961: The Sahara Lounge

The attempted strangling of Larry Gallo is among the most securely dated incidents in the conflict. In People ex rel. Cirillo v. Warden, the New York Court of Appeals states that a Kings County grand jury was investigating an attempted strangulation of Lawrence Gallo on August 20, 1961, at a Brooklyn restaurant called the Sahara Lounge. The opinion is a contemporaneous judicial record, not a retelling written decades later. [1]

Historical and photographic sources place the establishment on Utica Avenue and describe an ambush during a proposed negotiation. Several accounts identify men associated with Profaci as the suspected assailants. This information is pertinent, but it must not be made to imply that the Court of Appeals found any named person guilty of attempted murder: the case before it concerned a witness’s contempt proceedings. [1] [11] [12]

11. The Garrote and Police Intervention

Historical accounts describe a rope tightened around Larry’s neck while police officers entered or approached the lounge; the attackers fled and a patrolman was wounded by gunfire. The 1979 congressional report provides a concise timeline confirming the strangling attempt and that an officer was shot as attackers left. This supports the broad sequence without resolving every disputed detail in later dramatic reconstructions. [4] [10]

Some narratives supply the exact words spoken, the movements of bystanders and Larry’s physical condition at the moment police found him. Such details may derive from interviews, police accounts or later storytelling. They should be attributed rather than repeated as verbatim dialogue absent the contemporaneous incident report or authenticated witness statement. The police intervention was consequential because Larry survived and the faction did not lose its key negotiator. [1] [10] [11]

12. Carmine Persico and Evidentiary Limits

Carmine Persico is named in histories as a suspected participant in the Sahara Lounge attack. The 1962 court opinion reports that the grand-jury witness Aurelius Cirillo was asked whether he had seen a Scimone and a Persico on the day of the incident. That confirms the relevance of those names to the investigation; it is not equivalent to a trial verdict finding Persico personally committed the attack. [1] [11]

The distinction is especially important because the episode shaped Persico’s reputation and later criminal histories frequently frame it as proof of a dramatic double-cross. The Gallo biography records the allegation and the real investigative context while refraining from an unqualified claim that a particular individual was convicted for the strangling. [1] [2] [11]

13. The Wounded Officer and Public Consequences

The shooting of an officer during the attackers’ escape took the feud beyond clandestine disputes between criminals. Contemporary photo archives date the aftermath to August 20, 1961, and government accounts acknowledge the police wounding. Officer Melvin Blei is named in later histories, while personnel and hospital records remain outside the documents individually collated for this edition. [4] [12] [17]

Public danger cannot be reduced to background color. A confrontation at a neighborhood business placed workers, customers and responding officers at risk. The historical narrative therefore treats the attempted killing as criminal violence and analyzes the investigative aftermath, rather than portraying the scene as an ingenious maneuver in an underworld contest. [1] [4]

14. Aurelius Cirillo and the Grand Jury

The attempted strangling produced a documented court record because Aurelius Cirillo, called before a Brooklyn grand jury, resisted inquiries about his whereabouts on the afternoon of August 20. The New York Court of Appeals described his testimony, the grant of immunity and the prosecution’s accusation that repeated failures to remember were deliberate refusals to answer. A criminal-contempt judgment against Cirillo was the issue resolved on appeal. [1]

A follow-up 1963 appellate opinion addressed renewed questioning in the same inquiry. These cases show the investigation’s persistence and its procedural complexities. They do not establish that Cirillo personally attempted to strangle Gallo, nor do they adjudicate the guilt of all possible participants. Conflating an investigation of a killing attempt with a finding on that attempt would misstate the record. [1] [2]

15. President Street and the Gallo Headquarters

Histories place the Gallo faction’s headquarters at a President Street building in Brooklyn, often described as a guarded social and operational center. Scholarly accounts sometimes specify 49–51 President Street while an FBI contemporary report refers to associates meeting at 74 President Street. The specific site should therefore be presented with a source label rather than collapsing distinct addresses into one documented headquarters. [10] [11]

This space was not merely an eccentric meeting place. It anchored alliances among criminal associates and shaped the surrounding community’s experience of surveillance, raids and fear. The familiar image of a crew living under siege can obscure nearby residents who had no role in the conflict and faced the risk of retaliatory violence. [10] [11]

16. Prosecution, Detentions and Legal Exposure

News and photo archives show that Larry came repeatedly into contact with federal and local criminal justice authorities. A dated 1963 courthouse photograph associates him with an allegation involving a $10,000 Veterans Administration mortgage. This establishes that the archive records a federal-court appearance and contemporaneous sentencing context; without the original case docket, this biography does not assign a precise disposition or jail term. [13]

Separate legal questions about the 1961 strangling must not be mapped onto Larry as an offender. In the Cirillo decisions Larry was the reported victim of the attempted attack; the appellant was a different individual. Likewise, a 2011 federal indictment against a younger namesake cannot be grafted onto a man who died more than four decades earlier. [1] [2] [16]

17. Joseph Gallo’s Imprisonment and Leadership Continuity

Joe Gallo was convicted in an unrelated extortion prosecution in 1961 and remained imprisoned through much of the decade. That changed the operational balance within the faction. Larry and Albert were not simply waiting for their brother to return; they dealt with security, affiliation and political negotiations in a difficult environment. [3] [10] [11]

The distinction between Joe’s notoriety and Larry’s work is one of the main reasons a separate biography is justified. Media treatment of “Crazy Joe” has often served as the master narrative of the family rebellion. The 1963 intelligence record restores an independently named actor whose mediation efforts persisted during his brother’s confinement. [3] [6]

18. Profaci’s Death and the Magliocco Transition

Joseph Profaci died in June 1962. Joseph Magliocco, his underboss, took de facto leadership without secure Commission confirmation and inherited the unresolved conflict. A retrospective congressional staff report places the Gallo dispute in the Commission’s deliberations, although the report contains an incorrect month for Profaci’s death; the June date is retained here on the stronger historical record. [4] [11]

Larry’s faction had reason to view leadership succession as a possible opportunity for compromise, but changes at the top did not automatically settle control of criminal income or the status of dissidents. The record instead points to continuing talks and outside interventions into 1963. [3] [4]

19. The June 18, 1963 FBI Teletype

An original FBI teletype of June 18, 1963 reported intelligence that Larry contacted Nicholas Bianco near Easter 1963 and asked him to approach New England underworld leader Raymond Patriarca for assistance. The message also referred to Charles Lo Cicero as a former acting consigliere who had tried to negotiate between the Magliocco and Gallo factions for roughly two years. This source is a rare contemporaneous document naming Larry personally in a diplomatic role. [3]

The document used confidential police and FBI sources. Its contents are evidence that the government recorded these claims, not proof that the meetings occurred exactly as described or that Patriarca agreed to intervene. A responsible account avoids inventing the result of the request or portraying this one teletype as a complete diplomatic transcript. [3]

20. Reported Numbers-Operation Disputes

The same teletype says an FBI source reported that Larry wanted Frank Barranca, also called Frank Martin, killed in connection with a numbers-operation dispute. This serious allegation must retain its status as a confidential-source report. No criminal judgment establishing Larry ordered or carried out such a killing was located for this edition. [3]

Intelligence reporting can be historically valuable because it captures what investigators believed at the time. Yet the intelligence may contain informant error, exaggeration or unresolved identity confusion. The best usage is transparent attribution and a call for corroboration, not unqualified conclusions about culpability. [3]

21. Negotiation and the Colombo Succession

Magliocco’s tenuous leadership ended in 1963, and Joseph Colombo emerged as recognized head in the transition generally dated 1963–1964. The prospect of negotiations under new leadership did not erase grievances on the streets, where the Gallo faction maintained its distinct identity. Later histories describe a truce in 1964, but the exact demands and who spoke for each side remain unevenly documented. [3] [4] [11]

Larry’s importance lies in his role in navigating such disputed arrangements. While some accounts frame a clean declaration of peace, criminal organizations could agree to a truce without resolving all questions of income, personal loyalty or retaliation. The persistence of the faction in 1971–1972 reflects the limits of any mid-1960s settlement. [11] [18]

22. The Meaning of Boss, Captain and Crew Leader

The term “boss” has been applied to Larry in newspaper headlines and popular accounts. In this article, it means head of an internal Gallo faction unless a source explicitly documents a broader official office. He was not the recognized boss of all five New York families and is not known to have replaced Profaci or Colombo in the family leadership sequence. [4] [6] [11]

The distinction prevents an error in encyclopedia classification: a criminal organization can contain dissident crews with substantial local autonomy while still having a separately recognized hierarchical head. A mafia biography should give the precise organization, the applicable period and the source’s authority for a title rather than treating a colorful headline as a formal appointment. [4] [11]

23. The 1966 East New York Crisis

In 1966 East New York experienced violent confrontations amid racial tension, neighborhood change and local disputes involving young people. Mayor John V. Lindsay’s administration and the City Youth Board turned to Larry and Albert Gallo as intermediaries to discourage further street violence among some white ethnic youths. This episode is independently discussed in a historical study by Maria C. Lizzi and in contemporaneous journalism. [6] [9] [14]

The decision reflected a serious dilemma in urban governance: officials sought practical access to young men whom conventional agencies struggled to influence, but lending public visibility to organized-crime figures carried an obvious legitimacy cost. The Gallos were not thereby deputized as police officers or given legitimate power to use violence. [9] [14]

24. Claimed Peacekeeping and the Limits of Civic Credit

Contemporary reports portrayed Larry and Albert as helping to quiet confrontations, sometimes drawing on their ethnic and street connections. Lizzi’s analysis emphasizes that accounts also described intimidation and physical force. The episode cannot be simplified into a benevolent rescue mission or evidence that organized crime was an effective substitute for lawful public authority. [9]

Brooklyn District Attorney Aaron Koota and police representatives criticized the reliance on men connected to criminal organizations. The federal Congressional Record later reproduced the episode to argue that government engagement with crime figures could inadvertently make them models of neighborhood leadership. The public controversy is part of the historical event, not an editorial judgment imposed after the fact. [6] [14]

25. Media Construction of the Gallo Persona

Fred J. Cook’s 1966 New York Times Magazine feature contrasted public claims about the brothers’ good deeds with their underworld reputations. Its title framed the press’s central dilemma: whether a violent faction could also perform isolated acts perceived as helpful to neighbors. An archival catalog verifies the original article’s publication and identifies associated photographs, though a complete facsimile has not been collated here. [6]

This portrayal also created a durable myth of the “Robin Hood gangster.” Acts of charity or rescue do not cancel the effects of racketeering, and allegations of crime do not automatically invalidate every independently documented helpful action. The biography assesses individual events and resists adopting either a heroic or wholly theatrical caricature. [6] [9] [10]

26. Rescue Anecdotes and Attribution

Accounts of the Gallos rescuing neighborhood residents from a fire became part of the brothers’ favorable press image. The event is discussed in retrospective histories and contemporary media references, but exact participants, date and number of people rescued require the original fire-service or police report for full verification. This edition records the anecdote as historically reported rather than assigning Larry personally every action credited to the group. [6] [10] [17]

For a reference collection concerned with organized crime, the episode matters chiefly as an example of how criminal reputation is negotiated within a local community. A dramatic act can attract gratitude and press attention, while leaving unresolved the coercive structures from which the individual derives influence. [9] [10]

27. Personal Life and Residence

Secondary biographical accounts say that Larry lived on Long Island in his final years, with a wife named Gloria and a young son. A modern compilation gives a Merrick address, but this page does not publish detailed household location data as it is unnecessary for understanding his history and is not corroborated here by original residential or vital records. [7] [17]

Contemporary newspaper reporting on the brothers mixed family life with criminal celebrity, making the boundary between documented domestic facts and anecdote especially important. Where a household detail matters, an obituary, civil record or dated interview should be preferred to later retellings. [6] [7]

28. Illness and Declining Health

Accounts state that Larry lived with cancer and died during treatment in May 1968. Because the original hospital and death records were not directly obtained, this edition treats the medical cause as a widely reported contemporary conclusion rather than attributing a precise diagnosis, progression or treatment schedule. [7] [15] [17]

Illness changed the factional balance. His death came while Joe remained in prison, and before the public conflicts of the early 1970s that would bring the family back into the headlines. It is therefore inappropriate to describe Larry as a participant in the June 1971 Colombo shooting or the April 1972 killing of Joe Gallo. [7] [11]

29. Death Date, Hospital and Conflicting Age

The date most often given is May 17, 1968, with Nassau Hospital in Mineola, Long Island, identified in later accounts that cite the contemporary obituaries. A separate secondary page gives May 16, and different publications reported age 41 even though the FBI’s November 1927 birth date implies age 40. These conflicts are set out explicitly rather than removed by editorial preference. [5] [7] [15] [17]

The strongest available formulation is that Lawrence Gallo died in mid-May 1968, with May 17 the date used by the principal cited obituary tradition. Confirmation from a New York death certificate or hospital register would resolve the remaining discrepancy. A report of death from cancer is not evidence of homicide or poisoning. [7] [15] [17]

30. Funeral and Unresolved Burial Attribution

A New York Times funeral report dated May 22, 1968 described a highly visible procession, and its existence is supported by the Uwe Johnson archival catalogue. Secondary retellings recount mourners, vehicles and the public symbolism of the burial. These descriptions are useful for the history of organized-crime funerals and the press. [8] [17]

The grave location is less secure in easily accessible derivatives: some accounts say Green-Wood Cemetery, while cemetery-related listings associate Lawrence Gallo with St. Charles Cemetery. A verified interment record, not an unaudited Wikipedia derivative or a colorful funeral account, is needed before MGU assigns an exact cemetery in the profile. [8] [17]

31. Consequences for Albert and Joseph Gallo

Larry’s death left Albert and the imprisoned Joseph as the most prominent surviving brothers. The precise distribution of leadership duties afterward is better treated as a process than as a formal succession announced by a criminal board. When Joe emerged from prison in 1971, unresolved claims to money, loyalty and status again became sources of tension with Colombo leadership. [11] [18]

Larry should not be retrospectively inserted into these later developments. The first Gallo–Profaci War and its peace maneuvers form his direct career; the second round of Colombo-related violence belongs to the subsequent histories of Joe, Albert and the family. Clear chronological boundaries are essential to a cross-linked encyclopedia. [4] [11] [18]

32. The Historical Reputation of a Negotiator

The notion that Larry was the brother who preferred negotiation appears in reporting and organized-crime history. It is reinforced by the specific 1963 FBI record of a requested approach through Nicholas Bianco and the reported activity of Charles Lo Cicero. Yet the same report also contains a violent allegation about a numbers-business rival. Neither peacekeeper nor ruthless enforcer exhausts the documented complexity of the person. [3] [6] [11]

He operated within an illicit organization whose economic relationships depended in part on coercion. Diplomatic language in this context refers to conflict management among criminal networks, not legitimate interstate or municipal diplomacy. The historical task is to explain the mechanisms without romanticizing their consequences. [3] [9]

33. Evidence Hierarchy and Documentary Problems

The clearest sources for Larry are the 1962 and 1963 New York appellate decisions and the dated FBI teletype. These fix the Sahara Lounge inquiry and establish contemporaneous reporting of attempted mediation. Congressional narratives and later books provide broader context, but should be separated from the original judicial issues and confidential intelligence that they summarize. [1] [2] [3] [4]

Two identifiable errors must not propagate into reference writing. A scholarly study of 1966 East New York mistakenly labels the Gallo brothers as belonging to the Gambino family and treats a debated Anastasia assassination allegation as fact. A federal retrospective report misdates Profaci’s death. The useful analysis in both sources can be retained while rejecting these specific unsupported statements. [4] [9] [11]

34. Historical Assessment and Research Priorities

Larry Gallo was more than a supporting character in the story of “Crazy Joe.” The attempted attack on him generated appellate litigation, and federal intelligence named him in 1963 efforts to find outside assistance amid an unresolved intrafamily feud. His 1966 encounters with city officials also expose the risks of borrowing informal authority from criminal networks to manage community conflict. [1] [2] [3] [9] [14]

Further archival work should prioritize the 1961 original police complaint, records of Officer Melvin Blei’s shooting, the 1963 Veterans Administration mortgage prosecution docket, full 1966 City Youth Board and Kings County grand-jury documentation, both May 1968 obituaries, a death certificate and an authenticated cemetery interment ledger. Until those records are compared, this article labels missing details rather than manufacturing finality. [1] [6] [7] [8] [13]

Research and Publication Limitations

Primary court record. The 1962 and 1963 New York Court of Appeals decisions were reviewed as evidence that the August 20, 1961 Sahara Lounge strangling was being investigated. Their adjudicated issue concerns grand-jury witness Aurelius Cirillo’s contempt. These decisions cannot establish who committed the attempted murder.

Intelligence reporting. The June 18, 1963 FBI teletype was read as a dated agency record, including its underlying page image. Its statements about Nicholas Bianco, Raymond Patriarca, Frank Barranca and Charles Lo Cicero rely on confidential-source intelligence; statements are not certified testimony and do not constitute convictions.

Death and burial. An FBI index gives November 3, 1927 as a birth date, while contemporary obituary headlines reportedly described Gallo as 41 at his death in May 1968. Complete originals of both obituaries, death certificate and cemetery interment ledger were not compared. Exact burial site and reported age remain open questions. The May 17 date is provisionally accepted and attributed.

Historical-source conflicts. A 1979 congressional retrospective uses an erroneous month for Profaci’s 1962 death, and a modern scholarly article incorrectly labels the Gallo brothers as Gambino family members and treats contested historical allegations as established. The specific errors have not been adopted. The congressional and academic PDF passages were used for context but complete facsimile page-by-page collation was not conducted.

Contemporary press and personal records. Period newspaper headlines and publication details were checked in reliable bibliographic or archival catalogues where direct newspaper images were not accessible. The complete original August 1963 mortgage prosecution docket, 1966 City Youth Board records, full 1961 police file, civil birth certificate and full death record were not obtained.

Historical analysis. Reports of violence, intimidation and organized crime are treated as research subjects. No image is embedded because available historical photographs require rights clearance.
