1. Historical Overview

Joseph (Giuseppe) Profaci (1897–1962) was the founding-era leader of the Brooklyn-based Mafia organization that later became the Colombo crime family. He occupied a durable position among New York’s senior organized-crime figures through the consolidation of the Five Families, Prohibition, the creation of the national Mafia Commission, and the postwar federal investigations. His career combined commercial enterprises, enduring kinship alliances, government scrutiny, and an increasingly bitter dispute with younger members of his own organization. [4][7][10]

Two features make Profaci more than an entry in a list of bosses. First, his olive-oil companies and other businesses generated a substantial public identity as a merchant, even while officials connected his organization to illicit commerce. Second, his authority depended on men who ultimately challenged the tribute system, personal favoritism, and succession practices associated with his administration. The Gallo revolt that began in earnest in 1961 exposed those tensions before Profaci’s death and continued into his successors’ era. [4][6][9]

2. Name, Birthplace, and Identification

The historical subject was born Giuseppe Profaci on October 2, 1897, in Villabate, near Palermo in Sicily. Genealogical work traces his parents as Salvatore Proface and Rosalia Schillaci, demonstrating why the surname sometimes appears as ‘Proface’ in Italian civic records and ‘Profaci’ in American reporting. The court’s 1960 opinion identifies him as Joe, Joseph, and Giuseppe Profaci, making these variants refer to the same man rather than to separate individuals. [1][5]

Villabate mattered as an origin point for more than genealogy. Families, acquaintances, and commercial contacts carried across the Atlantic could provide housing, introductions, credit, and reputational standing in immigrant neighborhoods. Such networks did not make every relative an organized-crime participant. A rigorous biography distinguishes documented family relationships from later police characterizations of criminal affiliation and avoids treating a shared surname as proof of membership. [5][10]

3. Sicilian Family Networks and Early Social Position

Research into local registries describes Profaci’s father as a merchant with regional connections, rather than assuming that his son came to America without social resources. Those relationships complicate a familiar cinematic narrative in which every future boss arrives impoverished and rises only through street violence. Profaci moved through a world where family introductions could bridge villages, commercial enterprises, and eventually New York and Chicago. [5]

Much of the evidence for this early period comes from civil records, passenger manifests, and retrospective genealogy, not the direct testimony of Profaci in a comprehensive autobiography. The documented contacts are significant because they help explain access and trust. They cannot on their own date his entry into a specific Sicilian criminal organization, prove a ceremonial initiation, or assign every later business relationship an illicit purpose. [5]

4. The 1920 Italian Forgery Conviction

A particularly important early fact is established by federal judicial proceedings rather than Mafia folklore. The Second Circuit’s 1960 decision recounted a 1920 Italian conviction for forgery, arising from an arrest in Palermo and followed by a one-year prison sentence. The case mattered decades later because U.S. authorities argued that Profaci had concealed the arrest during his naturalization examination. The appellate court did not overturn the historical Italian conviction; it reviewed the American naturalization dispute. [1]

That distinction is essential. An overseas conviction, its relevance to immigration law, and later allegations about American organized crime are three different propositions. Treating Profaci’s legal victory in the U.S. appeal as an erasure of the Italian conviction would be inaccurate. Conversely, a forgery record in Sicily does not independently prove the later operational details often attributed to him in New York. [1]

5. Migration, Chicago, and Brooklyn

The 1960 federal appellate court stated that Profaci immigrated from Italy in 1921. The American National Biography entry instead gives 1922, while a genealogical reconstruction identifies a 1921 trip and a later 1925 visit and movement through Chicago. The court’s 1921 statement and the passenger-record analysis support using 1921 as the primary date, while retaining the disagreement in the research notes rather than obscuring it. [1][4][5]

He spent an early American period in Chicago before developing his longer career in Brooklyn. Commercial introductions and an existing Sicilian network appear to have mattered in that movement. Because accounts of his grocery and early financial success are often summarized from later histories, exact profits, the date of every relocation, and individual criminal assignments in the early 1920s should not be invented to give the story artificial precision. [4][5][10]

6. Naturalization and Marriage: 1927–1928

Profaci became a naturalized United States citizen in September 1927. In 1928 he married Ninfa Magliocco, a member of a family that would become central to his organizational administration. Genealogical research identifies the wedding at Our Lady of Peace Church in Brooklyn and connects marriage witnesses with a network of Sicily-born and Italian-American business acquaintances. The marriage produced six children according to the American National Biography. [1][4][5]

His new brother-in-law Joseph Magliocco ultimately served as underboss and later successor. The connection illustrates how Profaci’s organization overlapped with household relationships and trusted commercial partnerships. It should not be read to suggest that all family members participated in racketeering; the historical significance lies in the particular individuals whose organizational roles are independently documented. [5][7]

7. Building the Olive-Oil Business

Profaci’s public reputation rested heavily on olive-oil imports and related food businesses. The Mamma Mia Importing Company became associated with a prominent distribution enterprise, while federal filings also named Santuzza Oil Company and other corporate interests. The nickname ‘Olive Oil King’ captures this visible commercial identity but is not a measurement of his personal wealth or a finding that every company transaction served a criminal purpose. [2][3][4]

The commercial dimension is important because a large legitimate enterprise can supply social standing, employees, transport relationships, accounting infrastructure, and business contacts without every one of those functions necessarily being illicit. Federal authorities suspected that Profaci’s businesses intersected with organized criminal interests; historians must still distinguish a verified corporate officer, a tax lien, a food-law conviction, and an unproved allegation of laundering. [2][3][4]

8. The 1928 Cleveland Hotel Statler Meeting

Profaci was among the prominent figures detained when Cleveland police disrupted a gathering at the Hotel Statler in December 1928. The FBI’s historical monograph identifies him and Magliocco as participants and characterizes the meeting as an early occasion for intercity criminal coordination. A contemporary newspaper account establishes the police sweep, but competing stories about exactly why every attendee had assembled come from investigators and later reconstruction rather than a surviving official set of conference minutes. [11][12]

The episode is useful as evidence of Profaci’s participation in a network beyond Brooklyn before the definitive 1931 restructuring of New York’s Mafia leadership. It does not alone reveal his vote, rank, or influence at the meeting. The historical account should avoid inferring that the assembled group created the modern Five Families at that hotel, a chronology inconsistent with the later Castellammarese War and commission settlement. [10][11][12]

9. When Was the Profaci Family Founded?

Short historical timelines often date the Profaci crime family to 1928, and some specialist histories place his emergence as a boss even earlier. Other scholarship presents the 1931 settlement after the Castellammarese War as the crucial moment when his organization obtained its place within the Five Families structure. These accounts need not mean that Profaci had no followers before 1931: they may be referring to different milestones—formation of a crew, recognition as a boss, and incorporation into the later citywide balance. [4][10][15]

For MGU chronology, ‘founding-era boss from the late 1920s, consolidated or recognized by 1931’ is more defensible than specifying a single universally accepted inauguration date. The original leadership network also involved figures such as Salvatore DiBella and other Brooklyn Sicilian leaders, whose relationships to Profaci remain a subject of specialist reconstruction. [10]

10. The Castellammarese War and the 1931 Settlement

The 1930–1931 Castellammarese War changed the relationships among New York’s senior Mafia groups. Joe Masseria and Salvatore Maranzano became the best-known rivals; the deaths of Masseria in April 1931 and Maranzano in September reopened the structure of authority. Accounts differ over the timing and degree of Profaci’s support for Maranzano. The American National Biography places him within Maranzano’s orbit, while specialist chronology emphasizes periods of apparent neutrality and his bonds with Joseph Bonanno. [4][10]

After Maranzano’s murder, the bosses operated in a framework that later histories call the Mafia Commission system. This did not create a uniform, publicly documented constitution or eliminate rivalries. It did give men such as Profaci a long-term position in a governing network where disputes among families were meant to be managed without the most destructive forms of open warfare. His later internal war demonstrated the limits of that aspiration. [4][7][10]

11. The Mafia Commission and External Alliances

Profaci was recognized in organized-crime histories as one of the original New York bosses associated with the Commission system. His most durable political relationship was with Joseph Bonanno, another Brooklyn-based boss who shared Sicilian roots and overlapping personal and business networks. Their alliance mattered especially when younger men questioned Profaci’s rule and when other family leaders sought to contain the resulting conflict. [5][7][9]

The Commission was not a modern corporation with a transparent board calendar. Its actual meetings, voting rules, and informal understandings have been reconstructed through informants, witnesses, surveillance, and later prosecutions. Therefore broad descriptions of Profaci’s influence can be reasonable, but precise assertions about his vote on each murder proposal or his knowledge of every other boss’s plan require specific evidence. [6][7][13]

12. An Organization Built Around Trusted Captains

Within the Profaci group, a boss relied on an underboss and subordinates who coordinated crews operating in different markets and neighborhoods. Magliocco’s rise to second-in-command gave family ties a direct organizational function. Later figures associated with Profaci’s administration included factions led by the Gallo brothers and younger members such as Carmine Persico, although the timing of individual changes in rank is sometimes more clearly documented than the dates of formal induction. [6][7][9]

An index of members should distinguish bosses, acting leaders, captains, soldiers, associates, and outsiders who did business with members. Some later histories use ‘family’ to mean a criminal organization, not an exclusively biological household, and the two meanings should not be confused. The size of Profaci’s organization fluctuated and is especially uncertain during the 1961 rebellion when factional loyalties were changing. [6][10]

13. Tribute, Favoritism, and the Sources of Dissatisfaction

Historical assessments of Profaci’s rule emphasize tribute payments extracted from the income of subordinates. The practice was not peculiar to him, but the severity of his demands and his preference for relatives and long-standing allies became central complaints in accounts of the Gallo uprising. The precise rates and distribution formulas vary by source and by activity; published summaries should not convert rough memories of payments into an audited financial statement. [6][9]

The dispute illustrates a recurring organizational dilemma: a boss who rewards loyalty through family networks can maintain continuity while simultaneously creating resentment among ambitious operators who believe they produce the earnings. Profaci’s administration combined these elements for decades before factions concluded that negotiations and then violence offered a path to a different allocation of power. That is an interpretation of documented events, not an excuse for the crimes committed by either side. [6][9]

14. Rackets and the Limits of Attribution

Federal investigators and scholarly accounts connected the Profaci organization with illegal gambling, numbers operations, loansharking, narcotics, labor racketeering, and other extortionate businesses. These were allegations or intelligence assessments at the organizational level, not a set of personal convictions against Profaci for each enumerated offense. A defensible biography attributes the categories to their sources and separates them from the court-documented business offenses discussed below. [4][9][13]

The historical significance of the rackets lies partly in how proceeds and authority were distributed across crews. Illegal lottery operations generated collections and territorial disputes; loan debts could be enforced through threats; labor influence could create leverage over otherwise ordinary commerce. Those structural patterns are well established in the organized-crime literature, but assigning a particular violent act or specific drug shipment personally to Profaci requires more than a generic description of the family’s portfolio. [6][9][13]

15. The Apalachin Meeting of November 1957

The Apalachin gathering at Joseph Barbara’s New York State property on November 14, 1957, became one of the most publicized moments in American organized-crime history. Profaci and Magliocco were identified among the attendees in the FBI historical monograph and later accounts. Police disruption and subsequent public reporting challenged the notion that organized crime consisted only of disconnected local racketeers. [5][12]

The gathering was not a criminal conviction of everyone present for the offenses popularly associated with the Mafia. Its relevance here is organizational and evidentiary: it showed contact among suspected leaders and helped redirect investigators toward a national network. Profaci’s continued senior status in the late 1950s is clearer than any claim that he personally chaired the meeting or issued binding orders to all participants. [12][13]

16. Federal Tax-Lien Litigation in the 1950s

A 1955 federal district-court opinion in United States v. Profaci named Joseph and Ninfa Profaci, Mamma Mia Importing Company, Mamiapro Realty Corporation, and other parties in a civil action arising from large tax liens. This is an unusually useful record because it identifies actual corporate relationships rather than relying on generalized claims that Profaci ‘owned many businesses.’ It demonstrates the scale and complexity of a federal financial dispute. [2]

A tax lien and civil collection suit are not themselves a criminal tax-evasion conviction. Some popular accounts compress Profaci’s tax troubles into a story of prosecutors ‘getting’ the boss for tax crimes, but the particular opinion cited here does not establish such a verdict. MGU’s chronology consequently describes the proceeding as civil litigation and reserves any claim about additional criminal tax matters until verified by a separate judgment. [2]

17. Documented Food-Labeling Guilty Pleas and Fines

Federal Food and Drug Administration notices supply a more concrete criminal business record than many general biographies mention. Case no. 23174 identifies Joseph Profaci as president of Santuzza Oil Company and records a guilty plea concerning adulteration and misleading labeling of blended cooking oil; the January 26, 1956 disposition imposed a $3,000 fine on each defendant. The companion notice, no. 23175, names Profaci and Mamma Mia Importing Company in a case concerning olive oil represented as pure but containing peanut oil. Following October 1955 guilty pleas, each defendant was fined $1,000 on January 26, 1956. [3]

These were two separately recorded federal food-law matters, with different corporate defendants and fine amounts. They are not proof of every allegation ever made about Profaci’s underworld income, nor should they be dismissed as mere hearsay. They show adjudicated conduct in the legal food business and provide an empirical check on portrayals that depict his commercial reputation as either entirely spotless or entirely fictitious. [3]

18. The 1960 Citizenship Appeal

The government separately attempted to revoke Profaci’s naturalization on the theory that he had concealed his Italian arrest and forgery conviction. A lower court set aside his citizenship, but on January 12, 1960 the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit reversed. The appellate judges held that the government had not met the demanding evidentiary standard for proving that the arrest question was understood to include foreign arrests and that Profaci knowingly answered it falsely. [1]

The decision did not declare him innocent of the underlying Italian conviction. It turned on the scope and proof of a naturalization question asked many years earlier. The legal distinction matters because a biography that says only ‘deportation charges were dropped’ or ‘he won his case’ conceals both what federal authorities attempted and why the appellate court rejected the denaturalization judgment. The opinion remains one of the strongest surviving primary records for his early life. [1]

19. Federal and State Attention to the Organization

The late 1950s and early 1960s saw expanding federal and state efforts to understand coordinated Mafia groups. The public impact of Apalachin, the growing use of intelligence files, and congressional scrutiny of organized crime all placed the Profaci organization within a broader investigative framework. Later Senate hearings, particularly the 1963 Valachi hearings, were conducted after Profaci had died and therefore cannot be treated as testimony he personally answered. [12][13]

An intelligence report may correctly describe a network while still containing mistaken dates, garbled names, or unresolved informant assertions. For example, the later House consultant report on the Gallo War incorrectly dates Profaci’s death to March 1962 in one passage. That error does not invalidate all of the report’s organizational material, but it demonstrates why independent civil and death records must govern the most exact biographical claims. [5][6][8]

20. The Gallo Brothers and the Disaffected Faction

Larry, Albert, and Joseph ‘Crazy Joe’ Gallo became the most visible figures in the faction opposing Profaci. They operated within the world of the Brooklyn organization but resisted a leadership arrangement they believed deprived them of earnings and status. Other men who initially associated with the dissent included Carmine Persico and Nicholas Forlano; later realignments show that the rebellion did not remain a permanent two-sided coalition. [6][9]

The conflict is often marketed as a simple generational rebellion against an elderly dictator. It was also a struggle over concrete business opportunities, captains’ authority, and the ability to determine who controlled profitable gambling territory. Men on both sides used intimidation and violence. Historical analysis should not romanticize the rebels merely because they opposed Profaci, and it should not portray the entire family as unanimously faithful to him. [6][9]

21. Frank Abbatemarco and the 1959 Breaking Point

The murder of Brooklyn gambling operator Frank Abbatemarco in November 1959 became a widely cited precursor to the Gallo conflict. The accounts most often connect it with unpaid tribute and the transfer of his numbers operations. Some narratives identify Gallo-associated gunmen as participants in the killing; others dispute the precise role of the brothers and the motives assigned to particular participants. The reliable historical point is that the murder and subsequent redistribution of gambling opportunities sharpened an already unstable internal relationship. [9][14][15]

It would be unsafe to present one retrospective account of who personally pulled the trigger as an adjudicated finding without a specific charging or trial record. Likewise, the killing should not be reduced to a morality tale about ‘greed’ without describing the underlying protection payments and organizational conflict. The dispute over Abbatemarco’s territory gives the rebellion an economic history, even where the detailed assignment of responsibility remains contested. [6][9]

22. February 27, 1961: Kidnapping the Leadership

On February 27, 1961, insurgents seized members of the Profaci administration, including underboss Joseph Magliocco. The later House report describes five hostages; other accounts enumerate four principal named captives. That difference is preserved rather than forcing the sources into an invented consensus. Profaci was not taken and negotiated from outside the rebels’ control while the faction sought concessions over family leadership and earnings. [6][9]

The kidnapping was a direct challenge to the boss’s credibility: it suggested that a faction could isolate key officials and force bargaining without first removing Profaci himself. Congressional retrospective material says negotiations continued into the following day. Later narrative reconstructions sometimes describe more elaborate or extended bargaining, and they must be read alongside the dated record rather than substituted for it. [6]

23. Negotiation, Hostage Release, and Failed Accommodation

The initial hostage crisis ended without the permanent transfer of family power. Profaci’s side agreed to negotiations, and the hostages were released; descriptions of who mediated and precisely what was promised differ across later accounts. The continuing dispute showed that a settlement extracted under coercion did not necessarily establish a workable new distribution of authority. [6][9]

The Commission had an interest in stopping a family-level conflict that threatened neighboring organizations and produced repeated publicity. Its involvement, however, did not immediately end the struggle. Evidence of outside mediation is therefore more persuasive than any simplified claim that a single meeting fully reconciled Profaci and the Gallos. The escalating violence later in 1961 makes clear that the underlying grievances were unresolved. [6][7]

24. August 1961: Violence Escalates

The confrontation intensified in August 1961. Federal intelligence summarized in the House report described a plot against Larry Gallo at the Sahara Lounge in Brooklyn on August 20. He survived an attempted strangulation; a police officer was wounded during the perpetrators’ flight. The same retrospective chronology discussed the disappearance and killing of Joseph ‘Joe Jelly’ Gioielli, another figure associated with the Gallo faction. [6]

These incidents made the rebellion a sustained criminal conflict rather than a dispute limited to kidnapping and bargaining. Reports of ambushes, retaliatory assaults, disappearances, and armed protection followed. Particular roles assigned to named individuals were often based on intelligence reports and subsequent testimony, so a biography should identify the source of those attributions rather than representing every reported operational detail as a court finding. [6][8]

25. Carmine Persico, Nicholas Forlano, and Shifting Loyalties

The House consultant chronology states that Carmine Persico and Nicholas Forlano had participated with the insurgents but later returned to Profaci’s side. This shift altered the balance of manpower and knowledge: men familiar with the rebels’ operations could help their former adversaries identify targets and vulnerabilities. It also demonstrates why treating the factional rosters as fixed from 1959 through 1962 distorts the event. [6]

Persico’s later prominence in the Colombo family makes his conduct during this first war a major biographical cross-reference. But the later fact that he became a boss should not be projected backward into an assumption that he held undisputed command of Profaci’s organization in 1961. He was one participant in a wider contest whose senior leadership remained centered on Profaci and Magliocco until Profaci’s death. [6][7]

26. Effects on Brooklyn Neighborhoods and Businesses

The Gallo conflict affected more than the men whose names became famous. Violence in bars, restaurants, streets, and commercial premises imposed risks on employees, patrons, relatives, police officers, and residents who had no role in assigning rackets. The reports of a wounded officer in August 1961 and of subsequent public shootings illuminate the broader costs of armed private power in densely populated neighborhoods. [6][8]

A serious institutional history should track this community context without confusing the violence with an ordinary political dispute. The struggle unfolded within illegal enterprises, and its casualties were consequences of criminal methods of enforcing income, loyalty, and hierarchy. Explanations of why the factions fought are analytically useful, but they neither justify the crimes nor establish that all persons sharing social spaces with the groups were participants. [6][9]

27. Profaci’s Illness and Final Months

By the spring of 1962 Profaci was seriously ill with cancer. Historian Thomas Hunt’s reconstruction, drawing on contemporary obituaries, places him at Good Samaritan Hospital in March and then at the residence of his brother-in-law Magliocco in East Islip, before a final transfer to a Bay Shore hospital in June. That sequence illustrates how both illness and security concerns narrowed his ability to administer an organization already in armed conflict. [8]

The circumstances of those final months should not be overdramatized into a fictional deathbed agreement or a secret settlement with the Gallos. What can be established is the coexistence of advancing illness, open factional violence, and succession anxiety. Profaci’s prolonged leadership had kept the same senior figure at the top for decades; his impending death removed the personal authority around which the existing arrangement had been organized. [6][8]

28. Death Date: June 6 versus June 7, 1962

The strongest date-specific genealogical research identifies Profaci’s death as June 6, 1962, in Bay Shore, New York, at age sixty-four. Hunt’s examination of contemporary newspapers and a New York State death-index citation reaches the same date. The American National Biography entry instead prints June 7, and some popular histories follow that date. This page uses June 6 while recording the conflicting scholarly reference rather than pretending there is no discrepancy. [4][5][8]

A later House consultant report also contains an apparent March 1962 error while discussing the post-Profaci succession. The detailed contemporary obituary-based reconstruction and state-index reference take precedence over that internally inconsistent report. Profaci was buried at St. John Cemetery in Queens following funeral services in Brooklyn; contemporary descriptions of attendance and religious rites should be treated as reporting, not as evidence of ecclesiastical approval of any criminal conduct. [5][6][8]

29. Joseph Magliocco and the Immediate Succession

Joseph Magliocco, Profaci’s longtime underboss and brother-in-law, moved into the leadership position after Profaci died. The succession did not instantly resolve the Gallo rebellion. The Commission had concerns about the stability of the family and the acceptance of Magliocco by rival factions. The familial link explained his claim to continuity, but it could not by itself create a durable political settlement. [6][7]

The history of Magliocco’s brief administration should be told as a separate biography rather than compressed into Profaci’s final years. His involvement in a later plot reported against other bosses and the Commission’s subsequent intervention belonged primarily to 1963, after Profaci’s death. Assigning that scheme to Profaci would be chronologically wrong without specific evidence of an earlier role. [6][7]

30. Joseph Colombo and the Family’s Later Name

The organization now called the Colombo crime family was not known by that name during Profaci’s career. Joseph Colombo rose to its leadership after the short Magliocco period and the Commission’s intervention in the 1963–1964 crisis. The New Jersey State Commission of Investigation traces the succession from Profaci to Magliocco and then Colombo, while the House consultant report places Colombo’s appointment in early 1964. [6][7]

That sequence matters for directory architecture. ‘Colombo’ is a useful contemporary label for the family lineage, but an article set in 1930 or 1957 should normally say ‘Profaci organization’ rather than imply Joseph Colombo was already its namesake. Cross-referencing the modern family name helps readers navigate the encyclopedia without erasing the changing identities through which the organization was historically understood. [7][10]

31. The Bonanno Connection and Interfamily Consequences

Profaci’s relationship with Joseph Bonanno was unusually consequential. Their friendship and overlapping networks connected the two Brooklyn organizations in an era when the Commission sought to arbitrate conflict among autonomous family leaders. The Gallo rebellion tested whether another boss’s support could protect Profaci from dissatisfaction inside his own organization; it could not eliminate the underlying fractures. [5][7][9]

The later Bonanno–Magliocco confrontation with Carlo Gambino and Thomas Lucchese belongs to the succession period. Readers can follow that history through Bonanno’s biography and, when published, Magliocco’s and Colombo’s entries. The association should not be retroactively translated into proof that Profaci personally designed the later alleged assassination plot, for which this biography does not offer such an evidentiary basis. [6][7]

32. Private Family, Public Faith, and the Limits of Inference

Profaci maintained a public family life and a reputation for religious observance alongside the criminal associations attributed to his organization. Marriage records, corporate filings, and funeral coverage give historians more reliable access to some elements of that public identity than to private beliefs or conversations. It is legitimate to describe the contrast, but not to assert what Profaci privately believed based only on a church wedding, attendance, or photograph. [4][5][8]

The same caution protects relatives and descendants. A son, spouse, or cousin appearing in a civil corporate filing is not thereby established as an inducted Mafia member. A relative’s later role in the lawful olive-oil trade likewise cannot be treated as evidence of continuing racketeering. Biographical cross-references should be based on each person’s demonstrated acts and documented relationships, not inherited culpability. [2][5][16]

33. Public Reputation and the “Olive Oil King” Label

Profaci’s nickname epitomizes how a visible commercial occupation can shape the public memory of an organized-crime boss. Contemporary observers encountered a merchant with food brands and property interests, while federal officials investigated a network of alleged gambling, narcotics, and labor rackets. Both dimensions belong in a biography, and neither should be used to cancel the documentary evidence for the other. [2][3][4]

The actual court record complicates the neat binary of ‘legitimate cover’ and ‘criminal empire’: companies could conduct lawful business yet also be parties to civil claims or convicted of specific food-labeling offenses. The 1955–1956 food-law dispositions are especially valuable because they demonstrate a concrete breach in an otherwise ordinary commercial sector. At the same time, they do not prove that every shipment or employee was connected to Mafia business. [2][3]

34. How the Record Changes the Traditional Story

Several popular accounts portray Profaci as a static archetype: a Sicilian autocrat, an olive-oil magnate, and the enemy of the rebellious Gallos. The available evidence supports parts of that portrait but asks historians to refine it. Genealogical research shows active cross-border family networks, court opinions reveal immigration and company-law details, and congressional intelligence provides a dated—but not infallible—account of factional violence. [1][3][5][6]

A high-quality biography makes its evidence hierarchy visible. A certified conviction or appellate holding carries different weight from a government investigator’s suspicion; a contemporary obituary may fix a death date better than a later retrospective; and a firsthand participant’s memoir may illuminate personal perception without resolving all external facts. This approach also guards against treating cinematic scenes or criminal nicknames as historical documents. [1][4][6][8]

35. Historical Significance and Institutional Legacy

Profaci’s long leadership gave the present-day Colombo family one of its earliest and most persistent organizational lineages. He linked the late-1920s world of competing Brooklyn bosses to the post-1931 Commission framework and remained influential until the first great internal rebellion made the limits of personal authority visible. The succession from Profaci to Magliocco and then Colombo demonstrates that continuity of the organization did not mean continuity of its governing coalition. [7][10]

The larger lesson is institutional rather than celebratory. Profaci’s career shows how commercial legitimacy, family patronage, criminal markets, and coercive hierarchy could coexist inside a single historical network—and how those same arrangements could precipitate destructive violence. His biography is therefore a foundation for later profiles of Magliocco, Joseph Colombo, the Gallo brothers, Carmine Persico, and the organization’s later federal prosecutions. [6][7][9]

36. Historical Conclusion

Joseph Profaci died in 1962 before the conflict he had helped shape was conclusively settled. His more than three-decade leadership is a major part of New York’s organized-crime history, but the life cannot be reduced to an uninterrupted line of violent episodes. Immigration, citizenship, marriage, commercial disputes, corporate filings, alliances, and a destructive internal revolt all provide evidence for understanding his influence and its limitations. [1][2][3][4][6]

The first Colombo-family biography accordingly establishes two principles for the collection: persons should be studied within the history of institutions, and every consequential legal or criminal assertion should retain its evidentiary category. Profaci’s organization survived him and changed its name; it is the historical record, not retrospective legend, that should determine how his personal responsibility and institutional legacy are presented. [6][7][10]

The primary-source foundation consists of published court decisions, federal food-law notices, and historical government investigations; scholarly and specialist analyses supply biographical context. The sources may contain errors or rely on intelligence rather than adjudicated evidence.

  2. United States v. Profaci, 137 F. Supp. 795 (E.D.N.Y. 1955). U.S. District Court, Eastern District of New York; November 7, 1955. Open source. Research use: Civil proceeding concerning federal tax liens and named business defendants; no criminal tax conviction established by this source.

  4. Profaci, Joseph (1897–1962). Mary M. Stolberg, American National Biography, Oxford University Press, February 2000. Open source. Research use: Scholarly reference entry; useful for economic history and leadership, but lists June 7 as death date and 1922 as arrival.

Research and Publication Limitations

Archival scope: The cited judicial opinions and food-law notices were compared to their available published records. The birth registration and state death-index citations are mediated by a documented genealogical study; this edition has not independently authenticated the original archival images. Complete FBI case files, all trial transcripts, 1920 Italian court papers, and the full unpublished tax record have not been collated.

Historical uncertainty: The biography intentionally preserves disagreements about the first year of Profaci’s recognized leadership, the 1921/1922 arrival chronology, the June 6/7 death date, and participants in particular Gallo War killings. The 1979 House report’s “March 1962” statement is treated as a source error, not repeated as a competing credible date.

Media and presentation: No unlicensed historical photograph is embedded. External MGU shared components and full live-site behavior must be tested in production; this standalone document is designed to use the existing shared stylesheet and scripts.
