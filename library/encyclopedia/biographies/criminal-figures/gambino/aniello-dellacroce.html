1. Historical Overview

Aniello Dellacroce (1914–1985), often called “Neil,” “Mr. Neil,” or “Mr. O’Neill,” was a senior figure in the Gambino crime family and its underboss for much of the last two decades of his life. His significance lies in his role as the organization’s operational intermediary: formally subordinate to Carlo Gambino and later Paul Castellano, but influential among captains and crews whose support was not automatically available to the nominal boss. By November 1985, a police detective was describing Castellano as boss, Dellacroce as underboss, Joseph N. Gallo as consigliere and John Gotti as a captain—an unusually clear contemporaneous judicial snapshot.

Dellacroce belonged to the generation that bridged the Mangano–Anastasia organization, Carlo Gambino’s long leadership, the Castellano succession and the rise of John Gotti. His death on December 2, 1985 preceded the murders of Castellano and Thomas Bilotti by two weeks. That sequence matters, but chronological succession alone cannot establish that a single death caused the attack or that Dellacroce had approved any later murder plan. Court evidence, tax litigation, police intelligence and contemporary news reports reveal a record more complicated than the familiar image of a ruthless yet loyal traditionalist.

His criminal history includes an affirmed federal tax conviction involving compensation disguised as corporate stock. Equally important, he received a judgment of acquittal in a separate Florida racketeering case after a court found that prison records defeated a key prosecution allegation. Later 1985 indictments were left unresolved by his death. This article separates these outcomes and highlights the distinction between judicial findings, intelligence claims and posthumous legend. [1] [3] [7] [8] [10] [18]

2. Name, Birth, and Conflicting Descriptors

Aniello Dellacroce appears in court materials as Aniello Dellacroce and Aniello Della Croce, reflecting variation in surname spacing and capitalization. Nicknames include Neil, Mr. Neil, O’Neill and Mr. O’Neill; “Father O’Neill” belongs chiefly to the colorful retrospective literature about an alleged priest disguise. Some sources render a middle name John or Joseph. Without original civil-registration records, no middle name is assigned as definitively established here.

March 15, 1914 is the widely reported birth date. Contemporary December 1985 obituaries agreed that Dellacroce was 71, consistent with a 1914 birth. Specific borough, parents’ names, schooling, and the precise circumstances of his entry into organized crime are chiefly supplied by later biographies rather than records audited for this edition. An encyclopedia can present the commonly accepted date while making clear the evidentiary difference between age reporting and a birth certificate.

The surname and alias should be indexed as variants, not merged with unrelated “Della Croce” court defendants. In particular, court references to Armond Dellacroce in 1985 identify a different defendant, reported as Aniello’s son. A person-based directory must preserve those distinctions rather than treat every similar-looking name as the same subject. [10] [12] [19]

3. Family Background and Early Years in New York

Published biographies place Dellacroce’s childhood and early adulthood in New York’s Italian-American neighborhoods. His rise is often described against the commercial environment of small storefronts, neighborhood clubs, port activity and informal patronage networks. Those environmental descriptions are context rather than proof that he held a specific job or joined a criminal crew in a particular year.

The reliable judicial record becomes substantially clearer decades later, when tax prosecutions describe labor-related transactions and when grand-jury proceedings document the scrutiny surrounding his public associations. Claims of youthful violence, early prison terms and precisely dated street-level command should not be repeated as facts without identifiable dockets. The published 1980 Florida opinion states that he was incarcerated from 1973 through 1976; that is direct evidence of a later period, not a complete early-life criminal chronology. [1] [3] [16]

4. From the Mangano Organization to the Anastasia Era

The present Gambino organization developed through several leadership periods before the name Gambino became standard. Under Vincent Mangano and then Albert Anastasia, its personnel, rackets, informal alliances and internal leadership changed. Dellacroce’s career is routinely placed within this continuity, but the exact date he was formally inducted or first became a captain is not established by any direct membership roster examined for this edition.

Contemporary reporting in 1985 remembered his connection with Anastasia’s faction, including alleged pre-Castro Cuba gambling interests. These claims are relevant because they show how officials reconstructed his criminal network, but they do not independently prove he committed every killing later attributed to the legendary “Murder, Inc.” label. The more reliable historical formulation is that he emerged as a senior Gambino-family figure with ties to the Anastasia period. [10] [11] [16]

5. Albert Anastasia and the Question of Murder, Inc.

Dellacroce is frequently portrayed as a ruthless veteran of Anastasia’s circle. The Murder, Inc. label itself covers a disputed mix of prosecutions, syndicate networks and retrospective popular narratives. Describing Dellacroce as a named killer in every famous Anastasia-related operation would collapse historical allegations into proven personal responsibility. No complete catalog of individual homicide judgments against Dellacroce under that label has been authenticated for this profile.

Anastasia’s October 1957 murder and Carlo Gambino’s subsequent rise created the organizational conditions in which Dellacroce later became underboss. Contemporary authorities continued to connect him to Anastasia long after that leadership change. Those connections explain reputation and factional legitimacy, but they must not serve as substitute evidence for criminal acts or a precise promotion order. [10] [16] [18]

6. Carlo Gambino and the 1957 Leadership Transition

Carlo Gambino succeeded the murdered Anastasia in the fall of 1957. Later accounts interpret Dellacroce’s survival and advancement as evidence that he could work across rival internal constituencies. In a criminal organization, continuity of personnel often mattered more than the publicity surrounding an individual boss’s succession: captains and trusted intermediaries could retain influence across changes at the top.

The generally reported elevation of Dellacroce to underboss occurred around the mid-1960s after Joseph Biondo fell from favor. The exact internal appointment record has not been collated, so the date is treated as approximate. By April 1971 an FBI memorandum directly identified Dellacroce as the Gambino underboss and Carlo Gambino as the head of the family, providing a firm documentary anchor for the office. [9] [18]

7. Underboss: Formal Rank and Practical Authority

The underboss ranked below the boss and above ordinary captains in the Gambino organizational structure described by authorities. His responsibilities could include representing the boss, mediating crew disputes, supervising designated captains and enforcing organizational decisions. These functions are reconstructed from case evidence and law-enforcement testimony, not from a public corporate charter or by-laws.

A 1986 federal opinion reporting a detective’s November 1985 testimony identifies Dellacroce as underboss while placing Castellano above him and Joseph N. Gallo in the consigliere role. A different 1986 opinion describes an enterprise consisting of Dellacroce and two specific groups, including the Gotti crew. Together these documents support rank and identified supervisory connections. They do not establish that he personally controlled every Gambino crew or held the boss’s title. [7] [8]

8. The Ravenite Social Club as a Power Center

The Ravenite Social Club on Mulberry Street became a prominent meeting place associated with Dellacroce and later John Gotti. Such clubs functioned as social settings and potential nodes of criminal contact; their significance in investigations came from patterns of meetings, surveillance and testimony, not from the location itself being inherently unlawful. UPI reported that Dellacroce was arrested at the Ravenite in October 1984 amid a gathering of alleged senior family members.

Posthumous court testimony described Dellacroce’s earlier activity at the Ravenite when investigators assessed a Christmas Eve 1985 gathering. The site became part of the historical geography of the Gotti succession, but the identity of a visitor or presence near the club does not alone prove membership or criminal participation. Physical locations must be interpreted through specific acts and reliable source context. [7] [13]

9. Labor Peace as an Organized-Crime Commodity

One of the strongest pieces of evidence for Dellacroce’s economic role is not a generalized underworld biography, but a tax case about alleged services in obtaining “labor peace.” The court record traced compensation connected with a corporate merger and the avoidance of industrial disruption. This shows how the threat or promise of labor stability could be converted into compensation outside normal employment and contract channels.

The 1974 appellate decision described 22,500 shares of Yankee Plastics stock received through nominees as payment linked to services assisting a merger and assuring labor peace for Mr. Hanger, Inc. Crucially, the opinion did not require historians to guess at the value of a hypothetical criminal empire; it identifies an actual asset, a date, a tax return and a legal theory adopted by the jury. The later Tax Court addressed valuation and a separate proposed 1965 payoff on distinct evidence. [1] [2]

10. Yankee Plastics and the 1968 Stock Arrangement

In the 1968 transaction at issue in United States v. Catalano, stock certificates were held in another name rather than openly entered as Dellacroce’s taxable income. The government pursued Dellacroce, Michael Catalano and Martin Goldman on conspiracy and tax theories. The appellate court discussed testimony and financial transfers used to infer Dellacroce’s beneficial control notwithstanding the absence of his name on the certificates.

This was not a prosecution for simply knowing a business executive or meeting a union official. The legal dispute concerned ownership and concealment of a quantifiable asset. The appellate court affirmed the convictions in January 1974. A later tax case accepted that receipt of 22,500 shares and failure to report them had already been established, while allowing separate argument about fair market value. Neither source supports extrapolating this one stock transaction into an exact lifetime dollar figure for all Dellacroce operations. [1] [2]

11. The 1973 Tax Conviction and Its Affirmance

Dellacroce was convicted following a federal jury trial in 1973 of conspiring to evade tax, evading income tax and filing a false return concerning 1968 compensation; the co-defendants’ convictions involved the conspiracy count. Contemporary and later accounts describe a five-year prison sentence and a $15,000 fine. The Second Circuit affirmed in United States v. Catalano on January 21, 1974, after considering evidentiary and trial objections.

The distinction between charge, verdict, sentence and appeal is not optional: unlike the Florida racketeering allegation later rejected, the tax case resulted in convictions sustained on appeal. Dellacroce’s imprisonment from 1973 to 1976 also became decisive evidence in that later Florida case. The legal history therefore cannot be summarized coherently as an undifferentiated string of arrests or as a claim that he always avoided conviction. [1] [2] [3] [16]

12. Grand Juries, Contempt, and Testimonial Immunity

Dellacroce was repeatedly summoned in proceedings that tested the interaction between grand-jury testimony, immunity and the privilege against self-incrimination. A New York appellate decision in 1972 concerned a criminal-contempt indictment, and a later 1977 state court opinion addressed a subpoena served while he was on parole. These matters must be distinguished from the separate federal tax sentence and should not all be labeled a single “contempt conviction.”

The 1977 decision examined whether testimonial immunity could adequately protect a witness exposed to parole sanctions for associating with known criminals. That question illustrates how law-enforcement efforts to compel testimony intersected with continuing supervision and organized-crime investigations. The opinion records claims and legal reasoning; it does not establish that every alleged association was proved criminal conduct. [4] [5]

13. Imprisonment, Parole, and Documentary Alibis

The strongest dated incarceration evidence relevant to Dellacroce’s biography appears in the 1980 federal Florida opinion, which reviewed official prison records placing him in custody during the period in which prosecutors said he attended a pivotal 1974 meeting. That record prevented prosecutors from treating retrospective eyewitness identification as sufficient when it could not be reconciled with established confinement.

Later narratives sometimes suggest Dellacroce returned to active organizational work after release in late 1976. Contemporary period journalism depicts his renewed influence, but that account should be read as an intelligence-based claim rather than proof that the boss’s office transferred to him. The broader lesson is that imprisonment can restrict physical presence yet not necessarily terminate a reputed senior figure’s influence; the precise operation of that influence requires independently documented messages or decisions. [3] [5] [16]

14. Carlo Gambino’s Death and the 1976 Succession

Carlo Gambino died October 15, 1976. Paul Castellano became his successor, notwithstanding Dellacroce’s longstanding reputation and senior position. Later accounts often describe resentment, accommodation and an informal division between Castellano’s business-oriented administration and Dellacroce’s traditionally violent faction. Those descriptions capture a common interpretation of the family but are not the same as a recorded contract dividing all revenues and territories.

A responsible biography describes the governing arrangement in narrower terms: Castellano remained boss, Dellacroce retained the underboss position, and prominent captains, including John Gotti later in the period, maintained access to Dellacroce. The 1983 Senate material summarized in related MGU profiles and the November 1985 court-described hierarchy independently establish that the dual leadership relationship survived nearly a decade. The exact date of a private confirmation meeting is less secure than those public documentary anchors. [7] [8] [18]

15. Dellacroce and Castellano: Authority without Formal Equality

Castellano and Dellacroce are often presented as opposites: one focused on construction and legitimate-facing commercial influence, the other on street crews and coercion. The contrast is useful only if treated as an analytical model. Both were leaders in an organization alleged to coordinate multiple illicit markets; neither supervised a legally separated division with independently published accounts. The federal record specifically describes Castellano as boss and Dellacroce as underboss.

June 1985 surveillance illustrates an important limit on the caricature: a disagreement over appointment of an acting captain involved an individual who had reportedly consulted Castellano rather than Dellacroce. This was a dispute over authority and communication channels, not a clean split between “business” and “street crime.” The court record helps explain how a single organization could simultaneously have a formal hierarchy and disputed chains of practical obedience. [7] [8]

16. John Gotti and the Question of Patronage

John Gotti’s standing in the Dellacroce network is documented by later federal court proceedings. A district-court opinion describes the Charles Fatico/John Gotti crew as one of two groups overseen by Dellacroce, while 1985 law-enforcement testimony ranked Gotti as a captain. The June home recordings also bring Angelo Ruggiero and Dellacroce into direct discussion about internal organizational decisions.

Those connections support a mentorship or patronage relationship in the practical sense that Gotti’s crew operated within Dellacroce’s authority. Popular accounts of personal vows, promises of succession or exact private conversations before December 1985 require more cautious treatment. Gotti did not succeed Dellacroce as Gambino boss on December 2: Castellano was still boss until his murder on December 16. Conflating the death of an underboss with the instant transfer of the boss’s office distorts the succession chronology. [7] [8] [17] [18]

17. Angelo Ruggiero and the June 1985 Conversations

The October 1986 United States v. Gotti opinion is especially valuable because it describes intercepted conversations inside Dellacroce’s home, rather than merely reconstructing his character from anonymous criminal-world anecdotes. Several recordings from early June 1985 involved Angelo Ruggiero. They concerned the standing of Michael Caiazza, consultation with Castellano, an attempted acting-captain appointment and the authority of the underboss.

The tapes were litigated as potential co-conspirator statements under the federal evidence rules. The court’s admissibility discussion should not be recast as a finding that each person named in a conversation committed a separately charged offense. Equally, the existence of recorded disciplinary discussions makes it difficult to dismiss Dellacroce as a purely ceremonial figure. His office had administrative consequences within the structure investigators were describing. [8]

18. Michael Caiazza and the Limits of Chain of Command

According to the federal opinion on the June 1985 recordings, Michael Caiazza had selected Joseph “Buddy” LaForte Jr. as acting captain while hospitalized, without first seeking Dellacroce’s approval. Caiazza reportedly consulted directly with Castellano. Dellacroce and Ruggiero reproached him for bypassing the underboss’s role, and a recording was described as showing Dellacroce declaring Caiazza stripped of authority.

This episode offers unusually concrete organizational evidence. It identifies participants, a contested administrative appointment, a decision and efforts to communicate that decision to others. It should not be romanticized as formal legal discipline or used to imply that the court approved the organization’s practices. It is instead a window into the tensions created by overlapping personal loyalties and a hierarchical structure whose rules were enforced privately. [8]

19. Joseph N. Gallo and the Three-Person Administration

Joseph N. Gallo held the consigliere position in the November 1985 Gambino hierarchy recounted by a federal district judge. Along with Castellano as boss and Dellacroce as underboss, his office formed the leadership arrangement described by investigators immediately before the two December deaths. This Gallo is not the Colombo-family figure Joseph “Crazy Joe” Gallo, killed in 1972.

A consigliere was usually portrayed as an adviser and dispute mediator, not as an automatic replacement for boss or underboss. The court record establishes the title at a specific time, not the minute-to-minute distribution of authority. Connecting the three positions is nevertheless essential for understanding why Dellacroce’s death did not simply leave a binary struggle between Castellano and Gotti with no remaining senior intermediaries. [7] [8]

20. Regional Reach, National Contacts, and Evidentiary Boundaries

Investigators and journalists associated Dellacroce with contacts beyond New York, including Florida and alleged inter-family mediation. The 1979 Florida prosecution named him and Anthony Plate in an alleged Gambino-associated enterprise operating across jurisdictions. That indictment shows the government’s proposed regional theory, but the eventual judgment of acquittal means the central claimed 1974 directive cannot be reported as a proven personal act.

Likewise, law-enforcement speculation about wider Mafia murders should be labeled investigative attribution. Cross-family influence might be inferred from office, association and intelligence reporting; it cannot be elevated to a precise record of approved killings merely because Dellacroce was underboss. Geographic reach is best studied as a set of specific cases, named contacts and law-enforcement observations, each with its own evidentiary standard. [3] [14] [20]

21. The May 1979 Florida Racketeering Indictment

In May 1979 federal authorities charged Dellacroce and Anthony Plate in a racketeering case rooted partly in alleged Florida activity and the 1974 murder of bookmaker Charles Calise. Contemporary reporting described claims of extortion and violent acts; the government alleged that Dellacroce had told Plate to kill Calise at a restaurant meeting in Manhattan. An indictment, however, is an accusation prepared for trial, not evidence of an adjudicated order to kill.

The case soon encountered serious complications. Plate disappeared in August 1979, and a judge later reviewed the evidence against Dellacroce separately. Although prosecutors had witnesses who described an incriminating meeting, official correctional records showed Dellacroce imprisoned at the relevant time. Those records became the legal fulcrum of the prosecution’s collapse against him. [3] [14]

22. Anthony Plate’s Disappearance

Anthony Plate, described by prosecutors as a Florida-connected Gambino associate, vanished in 1979 after the federal indictment. The November 1980 court opinion noted that he and an associate left on an errand by car and were never located in the circumstances reviewed by the judge. The disappearance produced trial delays and a practical problem for prosecutors seeking to litigate the alleged conspiracy.

Plate’s disappearance has generated retrospective claims about who was responsible and why. The judicial record establishes disappearance and procedural impact; it does not furnish a criminal conviction of Dellacroce for causing it. The biography therefore treats the event as a distinct unresolved episode rather than presuming that the vanished co-defendant was murdered on Dellacroce’s orders. [3]

23. November 1980: The Court Grants Acquittal

After the jury deadlocked, the Florida district judge addressed Dellacroce’s reserved motion for judgment of acquittal. In United States v. Della Croce, 502 F. Supp. 1080, the judge found that official incarceration records placed him in custody during the alleged 1974 meeting. The prosecution offered no relevant evidence showing a departure or attendance that could reconcile the allegation with those records. The judge granted the motion on November 7, 1980.

This is not a technical dismissal that leaves the original allegation untouched as if proved. It is an acquittal on the evidence submitted, and the court specifically explained the deficiency in the government’s theory. The ruling does not prove every event surrounding Calise and Plate, nor does it establish the innocence of all other persons. It does establish that Dellacroce was not convicted in this prosecution and that an encyclopedia must not list the alleged Calise order as a judicial finding. [3]

24. The 1984 Tax Court: The Alleged 1965 Payoff

The United States Tax Court separately addressed an IRS theory that Dellacroce had received a $100,000 labor-related payment in 1965. The government’s evidence rested on an informant’s account of what someone else had said, without adequate admissible testimony supporting the proposed tax-generating transaction. The Tax Court concluded that the deficiency determination on this issue was arbitrary and eliminated the proposed 1965 income item.

This determination matters because later summaries sometimes combine the 1965 allegation with the established 1968 stock-income evasion, presenting both as proven. They are not equivalent. The Tax Court treated the claimed earlier payoff as unsupported under the applicable evidentiary standard even as the criminal conviction relating to the 1968 shares remained established. A careful profile must report both results, rather than only the ruling that confirms criminal misconduct. [2]

25. The 1984 Tax Court: Valuing the 1968 Shares

The same civil tax decision considered the fair market value of the 22,500 Yankee Plastics shares linked to Dellacroce’s 1968 income. His criminal conviction had fixed the existence of the concealed income and associated fraud, but a civil dispute remained over its valuation. The parties’ experts offered substantially different figures and methods, including market quotations and book-value measures.

This distinction illustrates why criminal verdicts do not resolve every associated financial calculation. One can correctly state that Dellacroce’s 1968 federal tax convictions were affirmed without treating the government’s original approximate stock valuation as an indisputable independent finding for every purpose. The stock, the dates, the method of holding title and the court’s analysis are more useful than an inflated estimate of his entire wealth. [1] [2]

26. October–November 1984: A New Tax Indictment

On October 31, 1984 federal tax agents arrested Dellacroce at the Ravenite Social Club; reports on November 1 described an indictment alleging evasion of tax on income reported for 1980. UPI specified a reported taxable-income figure a little above $26,500 and said prosecutors alleged the true taxable sum was higher. The report also noted chest-pain complaints following the arrest.

The 1984 charge must be placed in a separate legal column from his affirmed 1973 conviction concerning 1968 stock compensation. The later case was still described as pending when he died in December 1985. Nothing in the retrieved source record permits turning this 1984 indictment into an additional conviction or calculating the precise unreported 1980 income as an established finding. [10] [11] [13]

27. February 1985: The Mafia Commission Indictment

In February 1985 federal prosecutors indicted several alleged senior figures from the New York Mafia families in a case built around the claimed existence and activities of a national or citywide Commission. Dellacroce and Paul Castellano were both named. Contemporary obituaries recorded that the allegations included racketeering and conspiracy and that Dellacroce was expected to face trial in Manhattan.

The Commission case is distinct from the Brooklyn indictment later unsealed against particular Gambino crews and from the earlier Florida prosecution. Charges that a leader participated in a Commission are not proof that every alleged inter-family killing was personally directed by that person. Dellacroce died before the Commission trial, so the later verdicts against surviving defendants cannot be assigned to him. [10] [11] [20]

28. March 1985: The Gambino Two-Crew RICO Case

A separate federal indictment filed March 25, 1985 charged an alleged Gambino enterprise involving the Fatico/Gotti crew and the DiMaria/Corozzo crew, with Dellacroce described as the supervisor. The allegations included theft, illegal gambling, extortion, robbery, contraband cigarettes and violent racketeering predicates. The case was brought in the Eastern District of New York, not the Commission proceeding in Manhattan.

Subsequent judicial opinions preserve this hierarchy and distinguish the supervisor from crew captains. One 1985 decision also named Dellacroce’s son Armond as another defendant. The case’s legal afterlife is important: when the district court issued pretrial and evidence rulings in 1986, Dellacroce was already dead and no conviction could be entered against him. An indictment and admissible evidence against co-defendants are not posthumous proof of his guilt. [6] [8] [19]

29. June 1985 Intercepts at Dellacroce’s Home

Federal surveillance captured conversations at Dellacroce’s home in early June 1985, later litigated in United States v. Gotti, 644 F. Supp. 370. The published opinion identifies significant participants and reconstructs a dispute over a captain’s authority. This is unusually strong evidence that organizational meetings and disciplinary issues were being handled through Dellacroce’s office near the end of his life.

A judicial opinion ruling on admissibility should not be mistaken for a complete, authenticated public transcript of every intercept. The court selected portions relevant to the evidentiary motion and characterized the government’s theory. This edition does not reproduce the underlying tapes or claim to possess all surveillance authorizations, chain-of-custody records or full audio context. It uses the published judicial discussion for exactly what that discussion establishes. [8]

30. Armond Dellacroce: A Separate Defendant

The 1985 Brooklyn prosecution named both Aniello and Armond Dellacroce. Published court material identifies them separately, and contemporary reporting identified Armond as the elder Dellacroce’s son. The difference between a family relationship and shared criminal responsibility is legally significant: one defendant’s plea, flight, charges or later death cannot automatically be attributed to another defendant with the same surname.

For the present biography, Armond’s inclusion helps identify the case and the personal networks prosecutors were examining. It does not supply independent proof of how much income or command authority Aniello obtained from Armond’s activities. Future biographical treatment of Armond requires its own case chronology and legal disposition, with primary records rather than inference from kinship. [6] [19]

31. Violent Reputation and Unadjudicated Homicide Claims

Police and press accounts depicted Dellacroce as a feared underworld figure, sometimes associating him with the murders of Carmine Galante, Angelo Bruno, Charles Calise and Anthony Plate. Some of those claims were contemporaneous intelligence hypotheses; others entered popular accounts through later repetition. None can be written as a personal murder conviction without a matching judgment.

The Calise allegation illustrates why the distinction matters: a federal court actually granted Dellacroce judgment of acquittal in the prosecution most directly concerned with his alleged directive. Reports about Galante and Bruno relied on authorities’ suspicions, and the courts did not, in the sources reviewed here, adjudicate Dellacroce’s responsibility for those killings. His documented office and tough reputation are relevant context but never a shortcut around the evidentiary record. [3] [10] [14] [20]

32. The 1985 TIME Informant Allegation

A December 1985 TIME article asserted, after Dellacroce’s death, that he had for many years provided information to the FBI. The claim complicated the customary underworld image of unwavering secrecy and supplied material for later “double life” narratives. But this edition has not independently collated his FBI source file, source-payment records, handling memoranda or sworn testimony verifying the nature, frequency and duration of any cooperation.

The correct source label is therefore “reported by TIME,” not “independently verified FBI informant.” A person could provide tips, maintain investigative contact or be cited by sources without necessarily holding the same formal confidential-source designation throughout a claimed period. The article does not settle those categories, and its more dramatic claims about killings and costumes are not silently promoted to corroborated history. [15] [16]

33. The Priest Disguise and the Construction of a Myth

The image of Dellacroce traveling in priestly clothing under a version of the name O’Neill appeared in period reporting and later Mafia literature. It became a shorthand for a leader who could move through social settings while evading attention. The story is best treated as a contemporary journalistic allegation unless supported by independent photographs, surveillance reports or court testimony about specific episodes.

By contrast, his use of Timothy O’Neill when admitted to a Queens hospital in November 1985 was reported at the time of his death and appears in multiple contemporary accounts. These two facts are not interchangeable: an attested hospital alias does not verify every story about disguises, and the presence of a nickname does not by itself reveal why it was chosen. [11] [12] [15] [16]

34. Illness and Diminishing Capacity in 1985

Contemporary reports said Dellacroce had cancer and chronic heart disease during his final months. His attorney Barry Slotnick publicly described his medical difficulties, and news services reported that he entered Mary Immaculate Hospital in Queens approximately two weeks before death. Illness limited his ability to participate in the prosecutions pending against him, but the exact clinical course cannot be reconstructed from newspaper reporting.

The common retrospective assertion that he was diagnosed with a particular primary cancer or neurological condition is not presented here as a medical conclusion. His death’s organizational importance should be analyzed independently of speculative medical detail. The court still described Dellacroce as underboss in late 1985; the administrative question was not simply whether he could attend every meeting, but how his continued presence affected other figures’ calculations. [10] [11] [12]

35. December 2, 1985: Death in Queens

Aniello Dellacroce died on Monday, December 2, 1985, aged 71. UPI and the Los Angeles Times identified Mary Immaculate Hospital in Queens as the setting and reported that he had registered under the name Timothy O’Neill. Contemporary accounts attributed his medical problems to cancer and heart disease; no independent death certificate or hospital case file was reviewed for this edition.

At his death he remained under indictment in separate federal matters. These prosecutions are part of his historical legal exposure but were not tried to a verdict against him after death. Later court opinions explicitly described him as a deceased defendant when resolving motions affecting the surviving accused. His death date therefore marks the end of personal legal liability in those proceedings, not proof that the charges had been substantively sustained. [6] [7] [10] [11] [12]

36. Funeral, Public Memory, and the Meaning of Attendance

Contemporary obituaries reported that family arrangements would be private. Subsequent accounts have treated the absence or presence of specific Mafia leaders at memorial events as evidence of factional relations. Such interpretations can be useful in organized-crime history, but they require exact contemporary attendance records or credible participant accounts before one can assign motives.

Neither a funeral appearance nor a missing attendee proves assent to a later murder plot. Dellacroce’s funeral occurred during a tense period in which multiple senior figures faced federal prosecutions and internal authority was unsettled. This edition therefore concentrates on the documented dates and hierarchy rather than attempting to derive a conclusive assassination motive from funeral protocol alone. [10] [11] [12] [18]

37. December 16, 1985: Castellano and Bilotti Killed

Two weeks after Dellacroce died, Paul Castellano and Thomas Bilotti were shot outside Sparks Steak House in Manhattan. The killings ended Castellano’s leadership and helped clear the way for John Gotti’s succession. Federal criminal proceedings later supported convictions relating to the murder conspiracy. Dellacroce had died on December 2; he was not a surviving participant available for the events of December 16 or subsequent prosecutions.

Popular narratives argue that his presence had restrained Gotti’s faction and that his death removed a critical counterweight. This is plausible as a reading of leadership politics, and consistent with the organizational friction shown in 1985 recordings, but a claim about a hypothetical world in which Dellacroce lived longer cannot be established by a verdict. The strongest statement is temporal and structural: his death immediately preceded the leadership rupture, and the Gambino hierarchy changed decisively afterward. [7] [8] [17] [18]

38. Gotti’s Rise and the End of the Castellano Arrangement

The court-described November 1985 hierarchy listed Gotti as a captain and Dellacroce as underboss. By the end of December, Castellano and Bilotti were dead and Gotti’s faction had consolidated control. This change should not be narrated as if Gotti inherited an open underboss position from Dellacroce and was thereby automatically promoted to boss. The succession involved a separate leadership struggle and the murder of the incumbent.

The 1992 Gotti conviction and 1993 appellate affirmance provide later criminal adjudication of his role in the Castellano-related murder conspiracy. That finding belongs in a Gotti biography and the posthumous context for Dellacroce, not among Dellacroce’s own convictions. Dellacroce’s earlier influence on Gotti is better established by supervising the crew and interacting through Ruggiero than by unsupported accounts of a formal succession blessing. [7] [8] [17]

39. “Mr. O’Neill,” “Neil,” and the Problem of Aliases

Aliases are both documentary retrieval tools and common sources of confusion. Federal records, contemporary news reports and oral histories use the spaced surname “Della Croce,” the continuous form “Dellacroce,” and the diminutives Neil or O’Neill. For the canonical MGU title, the most familiar spelling “Aniello Dellacroce” is used while variants are retained in the biography and search metadata.

The hospital alias Timothy O’Neill is documented in contemporary reporting. The reported priest disguise “Father O’Neill” remains a journalistic characterization. Neither should be confused with another person whose actual surname is O’Neill. Likewise, the 1985 case’s co-defendant Armond Dellacroce is identified as a separate person. These clarifications support record linkage without creating false mergers. [11] [12] [16] [19]

40. Hierarchy, Intelligence, and Trial Evidence

A sound historical method distinguishes four source levels. A criminal appellate opinion can establish conviction, reversal or acquittal; a civil tax decision establishes findings under civil standards; an investigative memo identifies law-enforcement understanding at a time; and a newspaper story records what reporters, lawyers and officials were saying. These types of evidence cannot be treated as interchangeable.

Dellacroce provides useful examples of all four. The 1974 appeal affirms tax convictions, the 1980 Florida judgment acquits him, the 1971 FBI memorandum calls him underboss, and the 1985 obituary reports severe illness and multiple pending charges. A later federal decision recounts his June conversations but decides evidence admissibility for other defendants. Scholarly presentation should preserve which conclusion belongs to which source and legal standard. [1] [3] [7] [8] [9] [10]

41. Income, Assets, and the Limits of Financial Estimates

Claims about Dellacroce’s wealth often extrapolate from his status in the Gambino family, notorious crimes alleged against subordinates and the value of citywide rackets. Such estimates are weak substitutes for audited property records, tax schedules, judgments and bank statements. The documented 1968 stock transaction provides a concrete financial case study, but not a credible total net-worth figure.

The 1984 Tax Court case demonstrates why numerical precision matters: it dealt with a specific parcel of 22,500 shares and rejected the separate government claim of an unproved $100,000 receipt in 1965. Criminal enterprises may produce revenues across multiple participants, but an aggregate family estimate is not the same as Dellacroce’s personal income. This page therefore avoids unsourced global profit figures. [1] [2] [13]

42. Retrospective Portrayals and the “Old School” Image

Books, documentaries and crime dramas often depict Dellacroce as the intimidating traditionalist who mentored Gotti while respecting Castellano’s formal power. That characterization may summarize a set of relationships, but it also turns a complex administrative role into a familiar character type. Film dialogue, reconstructed meetings and stylized habits are not primary evidence of actual conversations or decisions.

The published 1986 court opinions are more informative in several respects than a dramatic portrait: they identify who was considered boss and underboss, describe a particular captain’s removal, and specify what an intercepted conversation appeared to show. Those bounded facts make it possible to evaluate later portrayals while withholding judgment on invented or unverifiable private exchanges. [7] [8] [15] [16]

43. Historical Significance and Institutional Lessons

Dellacroce’s career illustrates the difference between a formal underboss title and operational authority within a decentralized illicit organization. Court-described relationships connected him to specific crews, and surveillance showed him directly involved in enforcing internal discipline. These facts explain why observers regarded him as powerful even when someone else occupied the office of boss.

His biography also illustrates how a subject’s legal record can contain both established guilt and an important acquittal. A single confirmed tax conviction does not validate every murder allegation, just as an acquittal in a particular racketeering case does not erase unrelated convictions. The strongest historical treatment is neither celebratory nor sensational. It examines a senior criminal figure through cases, organizational evidence and the limits of each source, and places his December 1985 death inside a carefully dated succession crisis. [1] [3] [7] [8] [17]

44. Historical Conclusion

Aniello Dellacroce died as Gambino underboss, not as the family’s formally recognized boss. His long influence was rooted in personal authority among crews, continuity from earlier leadership eras and the position he held under both Carlo Gambino and Paul Castellano. The surviving contemporary evidence establishes the underboss title, a criminal tax conviction, an important Florida acquittal, multiple pending 1985 prosecutions and his death just before the Castellano assassination.

Three editorial safeguards govern his profile. First, state legal dispositions precisely: 1974 affirmance, 1980 acquittal, 1984 civil tax distinction, and 1985 charges unresolved by death. Second, attribute violent-conduct and informant allegations to their actual sources. Third, keep the 1985 succession’s sequence exact—Dellacroce died December 2, Castellano was killed December 16, and Gotti’s boss position followed that murder. Those distinctions make the record more historically useful than a compressed legend of an all-powerful underboss. [1] [2] [3] [7] [8] [10] [17]

Documentary Research Windows

Editorial source-study method. The five passages below are original editorial syntheses of published records, not verbatim primary-source transcriptions or original audiovisual collation.

Documentary Window 1: Federal Tax Appeal, 1974

The Second Circuit’s United States v. Catalano fixes the essential elements of Dellacroce’s affirmed tax case: a concrete stock arrangement, 1968 unreported income, separate roles for co-defendants, and appellate review. Researchers should track the indictment, jury counts, the government’s constructive-possession theory and the appellate disposition before adding financial conclusions. The court opinion is an adjudicated legal text; this research window is an editorial paraphrase of its significance, not a quotation or reproduction of courtroom transcripts. [1]

Documentary Window 2: Florida Acquittal, 1980

Judge Roettger’s published ruling records a striking conflict between eyewitness evidence and correctional records. The opinion grants Dellacroce a judgment of acquittal because the government could not place him at the claimed 1974 restaurant meeting while the official record placed him in custody. Readers should distinguish the judge’s order from the indictment’s allegations and the unresolved fate of Anthony Plate. This summary is a case-study paraphrase; it does not replace the complete opinion or all trial exhibits. [3]

Documentary Window 3: Tax Court’s Dual Findings, 1984

The civil tax decision deals with two analytically distinct questions: whether an alleged 1965 payoff was taxable income, and how to value the shares found to have been received in 1968. The court rejected the 1965 theory for lack of sufficient admissible evidentiary support, while the 1968 tax case remained grounded in a prior affirmed conviction. For teaching, this contrast is a concrete example of why matching name and year alone does not make two financial allegations equivalent. This is an editorial methodology window. [2]

Documentary Window 4: 1985 Crew Hierarchy and Home Recordings

The Eastern District of New York decisions describe Dellacroce as underboss, identify the alleged Gotti and DiMaria/Corozzo crews, and examine June home recordings concerning a contested captain appointment. Researchers should identify who said what, what the court attributed to the indictment, and which part of the case concerned admissibility. Internal discipline is strong evidence of organizational authority; it does not mean every named person was convicted of the alleged enterprise. No raw audio or full transcript was independently collated here. [6] [7] [8] [19]

Documentary Window 5: December 1985 Death and Two Succession Events

Contemporary obituaries fix Dellacroce’s December 2 death and underboss role. Federal cases then record the December 16 murder of Castellano and Bilotti, followed by Gotti’s ascendancy and later conviction. A rigorous chronology first separates these dates and people, then evaluates the claim that Dellacroce had served as a stabilizing intermediary. The chronological facts are supportable; a hypothetical assertion that he would have prevented the murders is not adjudicated historical proof. [7] [10] [11] [12] [17] [18]

Research and Publication Limitations

Legal dispositions. A 1973 tax conviction upheld in 1974, a 1980 Florida acquittal, a 1984 civil Tax Court record with separate 1965 and 1968 rulings, and unresolved 1985 indictments are four distinct legal outcomes. The underlying trial transcripts are not comprehensively collated.

Birth and genealogy. Common birth date and family identifications are based on secondary records and contemporary age reports; original civil certificates and full family genealogy were not reproduced.

Underboss promotion. The mid-1960s appointment date is a specialist reconstruction. The 1971 FBI identification and 1985 judicially recounted hierarchy provide stronger dated documentation.

1965 labor-payoff allegation. The 1984 Tax Court rejected the 1965 proposed deficiency because the record did not supply adequate admissible support. Do not cite the claim as established income.

1968 tax case. The 1974 affirmance is a criminal judgment. The later valuation dispute should not be confused with whether 22,500 shares were constructively received.

Calise–Plate case. The Florida court’s acquittal must be reported. The later fate of Anthony Plate and the murder of Charles Calise are not resolved by imputing guilt to Dellacroce.

1985 surveillance. The 1986 opinions describe selected intercepted conversations and admissibility questions; full tapes, laboratory authentication, warrants and all unquoted recordings were not reviewed.

FBI informant story. TIME’s 1985 allegation is presented as reporting, not as an FBI-confirmed confidential-source designation. No complete source file or sealed record was reviewed.

Homicide allegations. Claims concerning Galante, Bruno or other killings are attributed to investigators and reporters; a verdict against another person cannot be reassigned to Dellacroce.

Medical conditions. The contemporaneous descriptions of cancer and heart disease come from his attorney and reporters. No hospital chart or certified death record was inspected.

Posthumous succession. The death and assassination dates are established; a claim that Dellacroce would certainly have prevented Castellano’s murder is not susceptible to judicial proof.
