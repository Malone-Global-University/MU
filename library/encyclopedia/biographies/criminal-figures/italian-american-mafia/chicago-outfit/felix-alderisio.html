1. Historical Overview

Felix “Milwaukee Phil” Alderisio was a prominent associate of the Chicago Outfit whose reputation centered on enforcement, gambling, collection of disputed debts, and access to businessmen and organized-crime networks beyond Illinois. By the 1960s investigators identified him among the important figures around Sam Giancana, and later histories describe a brief period of senior operational authority. The record supports substantial influence but does not settle the precise formal title or duration of his authority.

His most consequential documented prosecution arose from an investment dispute reaching from Las Vegas to Denver. A federal jury convicted him in 1965 of conspiracy to transmit murderous threats through interstate communications, and imposed four and one-half years of imprisonment and a $7,500 fine. His challenge to government electronic surveillance brought his case into a landmark Supreme Court ruling before he died in federal custody in September 1971. The criminal conviction, the surveillance litigation, and accounts of the Outfit should be treated as related but distinct forms of evidence. [1] [2] [3] [4]

2. Documented Names and Identification

Published judicial opinions identify the defendant as Felix Antonio Alderisio. An FBI report also uses Felix Anthony Alderisio and records the claimed Italian-form name Felice Antonio Alderizio alongside a long list of aliases. Contemporary and retrospective writing most often calls him Felix “Milwaukee Phil” Alderisio. Such variants matter when searching records because catalogues, legal captions, and agents’ indexes may not agree.

The different given-name forms do not independently establish a certified birth name. FBI alias compilations combine investigative intelligence with observed self-identifications and therefore should not be mistaken for original birth-registration documentation. This edition anchors legal outcomes to the exact name in the judicial caption and retains the public nickname for historical cross-reference. [2] [4] [5]

3. Birth, Family, and Date Verification

The Chicago Crime Commission gives 1912 as Alderisio’s birth year; common reference accounts supply April 26, 1912, and Yonkers, New York. This biography treats the precise day and place as conventional reports rather than independently verified vital-registration facts. An original birth certificate, baptismal record, or contemporaneous family registration has not been collated for this edition.

The distinction is not pedantic. Organized-crime biographies often repeat dates from secondary biographical lists, while law-enforcement documents can include deliberate aliases, inconsistent spellings, and approximate ages. An archival editor should preserve the difference between a documented court appearance and a birth detail supplied by retrospective narrative. [1] [5]

4. The “Milwaukee Phil” Nickname

The nickname connects Alderisio’s public image to Milwaukee, but it should not be read as an official jurisdictional title. The Chicago Crime Commission credits him with involvement in gambling and prostitution markets there, and an FBI file incorporated into a Marshall Caifano investigation relays a 1958 source statement that he managed Milwaukee-area gambling. Those are intelligence descriptions, not a formal corporate hierarchy.

Chicago’s criminal networks could reach other cities through trusted intermediaries, money collectors, travel, or gambling business contacts. The nickname may reflect that geography, but the documentary material reviewed here does not establish the precise date of its adoption or every venture attributed to it. [1] [7]

5. Chicago in the Capone Era

Alderisio’s youth coincided with the final years of Prohibition, when Chicago organizations competed over alcohol distribution, gambling, political protection, and violence. The Chicago Crime Commission repeats a story that the young Alderisio waited near Al Capone’s Lexington Hotel headquarters hoping to secure work. It is a useful account of his later public legend, not an episode authenticated by employment records.

The social setting matters more than an attractive origin anecdote. The Outfit’s power drew upon a mixture of illegal enterprises, legal businesses, intermediaries, and the ability to intimidate. Later entrants did not need direct employment by Capone to inherit the organization’s operating relationships. [1]

6. Early Criminal Associations

The Chicago Crime Commission describes an introduction through a cousin and early connections with Sam Battaglia and John Marshall Caifano. That retrospective account supplies a plausible network context, but it does not offer a complete contemporary record of Alderisio’s first criminal acts or a verifiable date of formal induction.

Association is not the same thing as proved participation in every offense later attributed to another person. A historical profile should follow demonstrable communications, financial records, witness testimony, and adjudications where available. Early years are the least fully documented portion of this edition, and the available material is disproportionately drawn from later police and institutional recollection. [1] [7]

7. From Runner to Enforcer: What Can Be Shown?

Institutional accounts use the terms enforcer, bagman, and burglar to summarize the public role Alderisio acquired. The 1965 federal criminal trial establishes a narrower but powerful example: a threatening personal visit to a Denver businessman, in the context of interstate demands for repayment. This adjudicated incident supplies a firm evidentiary center for discussing his ability to project force beyond Chicago.

A title such as enforcer describes an alleged organizational function rather than a legal classification. It cannot establish that a person personally committed each rumored killing. The biography therefore identifies testimonial accounts, government characterizations, and the one adjudicated threats-conspiracy case separately. [1] [2]

8. Jake Guzik and the Protection Economy

Later Chicago Crime Commission writing places Alderisio within financial and payoff activities associated with Jake “Greasy Thumb” Guzik, a longtime Capone-era financial intermediary. Stories of payoffs to police and judges form part of the traditional picture of Chicago corruption. However, the material reviewed here does not include authenticated ledgers establishing individual transfers by Alderisio.

Such stories illustrate an important structural distinction: violence alone could not sustain a criminal market. Operators also depended on money movement, credit, commercial cover, and compromised public officers. Where payment details are not independently proven, the account should explain the institutional mechanism without inventing dates, recipients, or dollar amounts. [1]

9. Sam Giancana and Senior Network Position

A 1966 congressional discussion identifies Alderisio as one of Sam Giancana’s close Chicago underworld associates, while the Crime Commission describes him as an underboss in the 1960s. These assessments indicate proximity to leadership, but neither provides an appointment instrument that fixes a uniquely defined rank throughout the decade.

Giancana’s influence and eventual loss of control were not equivalent to a modern company leadership transition. Authority could be distributed between senior advisers, street bosses, and operators controlling particular sources of revenue. The correct historical question is what activities Alderisio could direct and whom he could influence at each documented moment, not simply what title a retrospective chart assigns. [1] [10]

10. Sam Battaglia and Marshall Caifano

Both Battaglia and Caifano recur in the Chicago history around Alderisio. The FBI’s Caifano records report information from 1958 concerning areas of influence in Milwaukee and the Chicago West Side. The Crime Commission associates Alderisio with the same broad enforcement milieu. These are valuable descriptions of how investigators imagined the network’s geography.

They should not be treated as proof that every named person worked under one fixed chain of command. An informant’s account can reflect partial knowledge, a temporary arrangement, or strategic exaggeration. Cross-referencing the names helps establish a research pathway while preserving the limits of each source. [1] [7]

11. Charles Nicoletti and the Automobile Accounts

The Crime Commission describes Alderisio and Charles Nicoletti being associated with a specially outfitted automobile containing compartments for firearms and controls for vehicle lights. A 1969 Senate statement likewise describes a 1962 police encounter with a concealed-weapons automobile. The convergence strengthens the proposition that such an incident circulated through official and institutional records.

It does not prove that the automobile was used in any named murder. Retrospective descriptions of a “hit car” often slide from the physical vehicle to a conclusion about particular homicides. This edition treats the automobile as a reported enforcement asset and separately assesses allegations of specific killings. [1] [9]

12. Gangland Homicide Allegations

The Crime Commission associates Alderisio with several violent incidents, including the notorious Billy McCarthy and Jimmy Miraglia case. Those accounts remain part of Chicago criminal-history literature, but this edition has not authenticated a murder conviction against Alderisio for those deaths, or established the events through original homicide files.

The difference between accusation and adjudication is especially important when cases involve multiple suspects and later retellings of torture. No number of attributed “hits” should be converted into a historical finding merely because a secondary list repeats it. Research should seek death certificates, police case files, sworn witnesses, and courtroom determinations before attaching particular homicides to one individual. [1]

13. Gambling and Cash Collection

Gambling is a recurring feature of institutional accounts of Alderisio’s influence. The 1958 FBI reporting about Milwaukee and the 1969 Senate description of gambling and loan-sharking activities are suggestive, although both chiefly reflect intelligence or political characterization. They do not furnish complete business books identifying every game, participant, or share of profits.

The broader historical context is still intelligible: an organization that controlled locations, collectors, debt demands, and protection could exercise economic power without owning a large conventional corporation. Distinguishing reported network influence from specific proven financial transactions protects the biography from a false precision that the evidence cannot bear. [1] [7] [9]

14. Loansharking and Violent Collection

The interaction between debt collection and intimidation is central to Alderisio’s best-documented prosecution. An investment dispute involving a Nebraska oil lease eventually prompted a 1961 trip to a Denver lawyer’s office. At trial, the government treated threats and interstate telephone communications as a conspiracy, rather than charging a conventional completed homicide.

It would be inaccurate to infer that the whole transaction was a stereotypical loan-sharking contract. The record describes an actual oil-investment relationship, subsequent nonpayment, an alleged effort to recoup money, and intimidation. A commercial disagreement can become criminal through the method of enforcement; the legal result turns on evidence of conspiracy and threats, not the investment’s mere failure. [2]

15. Business Ventures and Commercial Cover

Crime Commission accounts assign Alderisio interests in restaurants, clubs, hospitality operations, and other businesses. An FBI record from the later surveillance dispute identifies Gaylur Mercantile Company and First National Mortgage Company as locations relevant to electronic monitoring. The presence of such names in litigation demonstrates investigative contact with business premises, not necessarily ownership of the businesses by Alderisio.

Corporate documents, property transfers, tax records, and actual shareholder registers would be required to reconstruct a reliable portfolio. Rather than reproduce a comprehensive commercial empire as fact, this biography distinguishes retrospective business claims from judicially established places of surveillance. [1] [4]

16. Las Vegas Connections

The Denver case demonstrates a concrete connection between Chicago and Las Vegas. Ruby Kolod was an officer and stockholder of the Desert Inn, and the Tenth Circuit recorded that Alderisio had known Kolod and Willie Israel Alderman for approximately ten years. Alderisio had been a guest at the hotel shortly before the crucial 1961 Denver visit.

The opinion also identifies a $16,600 check to Alderisio after Kolod supplied the same amount of cash to the hotel teller. Those details show business and personal contact across jurisdictions; they do not establish that Alderisio owned or managed the Desert Inn or directed its wider casino activities. The documentary strength lies in the specific transaction and relationships, not an inferred corporate position. [2]

17. The Nebraska Oil-Lease Investment

In June 1959 Ruby Kolod paid $78,150 for an interest connected to Nebraska oil leases. According to the appellate opinion, $10,000 went to a geologist, William H. Young, and $68,150 was placed with Denver lawyer Robert Sunshine pending title and production assurances. Kolod’s interest was subsequently divided with Alderman.

Payments proved disappointing and escrow funds were used for other obligations. By October 1960 the escrow account was exhausted, producing a dispute over recovery. These financial details matter because they explain why a Chicago intermediary was eventually asked to visit Denver. The criminal prosecution grew out of a particular business breakdown rather than an otherwise unexplained act of violence. [2]

18. Failure of Payments and Escalation

The appellate court described a series of calls between Las Vegas and Denver during spring 1961. Kolod and Alderman allegedly demanded repayment after the oil interest failed to produce expected funds. Sunshine testified that callers invoked an impending visit by a Chicago “lawyer” who would not be coming to file suit. A witness who heard one call supported part of the government’s account.

That testimony was disputed, as is normal in an adversarial trial. The court emphasized the jury’s role in assessing credibility. The important historical distinction is between a debt demand, which may have civil remedies, and the use of communications conveying threats of serious harm. The latter formed the premise of the federal conspiracy prosecution. [2]

19. June 1961 Communications

The court traced a June 22, 1961 instruction that money be delivered to Kolod’s nephew, followed by a June 28 encounter at Denver’s airport. Sunshine reported that funds were unavailable. That evening, according to the prosecution evidence summarized by the judges, Kolod indicated that he and Alderman had decided to send a person to address the failure of repayment.

These dates provide an unusually detailed chain of events for an organized-crime biography. The sequence connects the alleged conspiracy’s financial object to an identifiable interstate visit. They also permit careful separation of what particular witnesses said from what the jury ultimately found proven. [2]

20. July 6, 1961: The Denver Visit

On July 6, 1961 Alderisio traveled to Denver with Americo Di Pietto and went to Sunshine’s office. The appellate opinion records that both visitors had registered at the Brown Palace Hotel, with Alderisio using an assumed name and address. Sunshine testified that Alderisio conveyed an explicit threat of death and explained that only Kolod could resolve the dispute.

The court treated the visit as evidence supporting the conspiracy to transmit threats through interstate communications; it did not establish the separate extortion-transmission charge on which the jury later acquitted. The exact witness account is preserved in the published opinion. This biography paraphrases rather than reproduces its most sensational language. [2]

21. The Phone Call from Sunshine’s Office

After the meeting began, Alderisio telephoned the Concord Hotel near Monticello, New York, where Kolod and Alderman were staying. Sunshine then spoke with Kolod about a possible resolution. The court described the call as linking the Denver visit directly to the Las Vegas investors, rather than as an isolated encounter.

The combination of travel, cross-country calls, and the alleged coercive purpose put the case within the reach of federal communications statutes. The legal significance lay in the agreement and interstate transmission, not in proving the existence of a conventional racketeering organization through a later-enacted RICO framework. [2]

22. August 1961 and Subsequent Settlement Pressure

The opinion records that Sunshine traveled to Las Vegas in August 1961 and agreed to repay the initial investment plus a one-third additional return, subject to deductions for earlier payments. Monthly installments of about $2,000 were contemplated. Some later payments failed, and calls continued into 1962.

This history complicates a simplistic account in which a single meeting concluded the dispute. The alleged threats occurred within an evolving commercial relationship. The prosecution’s argument depended on the purpose and effect of intimidation across those stages, not on the mere existence of an unsettled financial obligation. [2]

23. The Government’s Charges

Federal prosecutors charged a conspiracy involving the transmission of threats in interstate communications under 18 U.S.C. §§ 371 and 875(c). They also brought a separate count under § 875(b) alleging an interstate transmission of threats with intent to extort a sum described in the indictment. The difference between these counts is central to an accurate legal summary.

The trial jury convicted Alderisio, Kolod, and Alderman on the conspiracy count. It acquitted Alderisio and the other relevant defendants on the separate substantive extortion-transmission count; Di Pietto was acquitted of the conspiracy count as well. The profile must never reduce that mixed verdict to a conviction on every charge. [2]

24. The 1965 Federal Jury Verdict

The District of Colorado jury found Alderisio guilty of conspiracy to transmit murderous threats by interstate communications. He received four and one-half years in prison and a $7,500 fine. Kolod and Alderman received different sentences. The judgment provided a firm legal basis for describing Alderisio as a convicted participant in a coercive interstate scheme.

It does not turn all rumors about other offenses into established facts. Even where a jury accepts the prosecution’s account in a particular case, historical care requires limiting the finding to the charged offense and the evidence essential to that verdict. This is the principal individual criminal judgment discussed in the first half of the biography. [2] [4]

25. The Separate Extortion Count: Acquittal

Alderisio’s role is frequently summarized as an “extortion conviction.” That is understandable shorthand for the threatening-debt context, but it can be legally misleading. The 1967 published opinion explicitly states that the jury acquitted the charged defendants of the separate count alleging transmission of threats with intent to extort under § 875(b).

His conviction instead concerned the conspiracy count tied to § 875(c). A faithful encyclopedia entry therefore uses the longer description when discussing the formal verdict and identifies looser contemporary descriptions as summaries of the underlying conduct. The distinction also warns researchers against accepting parliamentary speeches or newspaper shorthand over the actual appellate record. [2]

26. The 1967 Tenth Circuit Appeal

In February 1967 the Tenth Circuit upheld the convictions of Alderisio, Kolod, and Alderman, with rehearing denied in March. Issues included the handling of electronic monitoring evidence, trial severance, the conspiracy instruction, and the sufficiency of the evidence against Alderisio. The court initially concluded that the challenged rulings did not warrant reversal.

The opinion is the strongest accessible source for the events from 1959 through 1962 and the content of the 1965 jury judgment. Yet the litigation did not end there. Information about separate FBI electronic surveillance in Chicago prompted the Supreme Court to reconsider the case. For that reason the 1967 result must be read together with later decisions. [2]

27. Las Vegas Monitoring Dispute

At trial the defense attempted to use the absence of corroboration from FBI monitoring at the Desert Inn. Kolod asserted that a conference-room device could have captured conversations central to the prosecution. On further examination, the Tenth Circuit recounted that logs and witnesses did not corroborate his claimed use of that telephone for the relevant calls.

This was one evidentiary controversy, separate from the later discovery of surveillance at Chicago business locations. Combining both investigations under a generic “wiretap” label obscures differences in place, timing, personal privacy interests, and legal remedies. A source-critical history keeps the Las Vegas monitoring argument and the Chicago monitoring remand distinct. [2] [3]

28. Discovery of Chicago Electronic Surveillance

After the 1967 affirmance, the Supreme Court learned of government electronic eavesdropping associated with premises connected to Alderisio in Chicago. The United States argued that the surveillance had not supplied evidence relevant to the Colorado criminal trial. The Supreme Court rejected reliance on the government’s one-sided judgment of relevance.

The central issue was not whether a person linked to organized crime was entitled to special protection. It was whether constitutional safeguards against unlawful surveillance applied when defendants claimed evidence in a criminal prosecution had been contaminated. The case consequently reached well beyond the history of Chicago crime. [3] [4]

29. The Supreme Court’s 1968 Remand

In a 1968 order reported at 390 U.S. 136, the Supreme Court required further proceedings rather than accepting federal officials’ assurance that the intercepted material was irrelevant. That remand shifted attention from the original debt-dispute facts to how courts should investigate and disclose possible electronic-surveillance evidence.

The order is not equivalent to an acquittal. It vacated the earlier appellate disposition for necessary inquiry. Some contemporary commentary described the conviction as “reversed,” but the subsequent history is more complicated: a new district-court judgment and later appellate ruling followed. Readers should not infer final exoneration from the 1968 procedural step. [3] [4]

30. Alderman v. United States (1969)

The Supreme Court decided Alderman v. United States, 394 U.S. 165, on March 10, 1969. Alderisio was one of the parties in the underlying criminal matter. The Court considered whose Fourth Amendment interests were invaded by electronic surveillance and what records had to be disclosed so defendants could test whether criminal evidence derived from unlawful monitoring.

The Court limited claims to defendants whose own rights were violated, while rejecting a system in which prosecutors or judges alone decided what illegally intercepted information might matter to an accused person. The decision remains an important example of procedural protection being defined through litigation initiated by a defendant notorious for unrelated alleged criminal activity. [3]

31. Fourth Amendment Standing

The Court explained that a person cannot ordinarily exclude evidence only because an unrelated person’s privacy was violated. Protection turned on whether the government unlawfully overheard that defendant or monitored conversations on premises in which the defendant had the relevant privacy interest. Thus being a co-conspirator did not automatically create standing to challenge every interception.

The historical significance is institutional rather than biographical sensationalism. A difficult prosecution forced the courts to decide whose rights matter and how to assess government conduct. The rule was not a categorical permission for organized-crime figures to evade prosecution, nor a blanket bar on evidence obtained from conversations involving others. [3]

32. Disclosure and Adversary Review

Alderman held that defendants with appropriate standing needed access to the records of intercepted conversations necessary to challenge possible evidentiary taint, subject to protective safeguards. The Court rejected allowing the government or trial judge to make all relevance decisions secretly before the defense could examine material it was entitled to see.

These principles reflected the concern that illegally gathered intelligence could influence a prosecution indirectly. The issue is more demanding than checking whether a trial exhibit is itself a tape recording. Investigative leads, witness identification, and the preparation of evidence can also be affected; determining whether that happened requires an adequate record and meaningful defense participation. [3]

33. The 1969 District-Court Hearing

The 1970 Tenth Circuit opinion describes a substantial hearing in the District of Colorado on June 18, 19, and 26, 1969. Defense lawyers received logs of monitored conversations; supplementary FBI memoranda, called airtels, also came under scrutiny. The trial court concluded that the disclosed surveillance did not taint the 1965 conviction and entered a new judgment imposing the same sentence.

The historical record here is unusually detailed about investigative procedure. It also reveals a limitation: the original recordings had not been preserved. The absence of tapes forced the courts to compare surviving logs, memoranda, testimony, and the flow of information between FBI offices rather than examine an intact original audio archive. [4]

34. Logs, Airtels, and Missing Recordings

The court distinguished logs, which summarized or partly transcribed conversations, from airtels, which could combine snippets, investigative summaries, source identifications, and hearsay. The latter might refer to material not present in the surviving logs. The original tapes were unavailable, adding uncertainty to the completeness of disclosure.

Airtels are not equivalent to sworn trial testimony or complete verbatim transcripts. Later historians who mine these files for dialogue must identify the record’s type and provenance. The dispute also anticipated broader questions about whether investigative files generated for intelligence purposes can adequately support the evidentiary needs of a criminal trial. [4]

35. The March 1970 Tenth Circuit Decision

On March 31, 1970 the Tenth Circuit issued United States v. Alderisio, 424 F.2d 20. It ordered a limited additional remand to check that omitted conversations in airtels were addressed and to permit cross-examination of an appropriate FBI official. The court otherwise affirmed the judgment. It specifically did not authorize unlimited exploration of every government file.

This careful outcome should replace sweeping claims that the Supreme Court had “thrown out” the case permanently or that every surveillance complaint was frivolous. The appellate court recognized a meaningful disclosure problem while retaining a narrow, evidence-based remedy. The ruling simultaneously illustrates legal accountability for investigators and the continuing force of the jury’s original conspiracy finding. [4]

36. Four Monitored Chicago Locations

The 1970 opinion notes that monitoring had occurred at four locations. Two—Celano’s Tailor Shop and the Armory Lounge—were not part of the specified hearing by agreement. The disputed locations were Gaylur Mercantile Company and First National Mortgage Company. Their surveillance histories overlapped the period leading up to Alderisio’s 1965 trial.

These names are archival identifiers, not proof of Alderisio’s ownership of each establishment. Correct use of the case requires distinguishing where devices were installed from whose conversations were heard, who occupied or controlled the premises, and whether information traveled into the Colorado investigation. [4]

37. Limits on an Attorney–Client Claim

The 1970 appellate court also examined whether conversations involving Michael Brodkin were protected by an attorney–client relationship. The court reviewed Brodkin’s involvement in removal proceedings and contacts with the defense but found no reversible error in the trial court’s treatment of the claimed privilege.

The opinion illustrates that an asserted relationship must be established with attention to the date, participants, and function of the communication. A journalist’s reference to a defendant having counsel is not enough to decide whether a particular intercepted conversation was privileged. The court’s ruling resolved the issue before it, not every possible confidentiality question in unrelated files. [4]

38. Parallel Miami and Florida Investigations

FBI documents released through the National Archives identify a separate investigation connected to Pan American Bank of Miami and individuals including Charles William Bray and Santo Trafficante Jr. The title sheets associate Alderisio with investigative categories that included interstate travel, mail fraud, and extortion. They are strong evidence that federal authorities were examining those matters, not that every suspected offense resulted in a conviction.

The 1964 FBI communication records a Miami grand-jury indictment concerning a separate alleged threat. Without a complete original docket and final disposition, this edition does not state an unverified conviction for that allegation. It must not be confused with the Colorado threats-conspiracy judgment or the later bank-fraud disposition described in Congress. [5] [6]

39. Investigative Rumor and Uncharged Conduct

Some FBI communications include confidential allegations of planned violence against witnesses or bankers. Such material may explain why investigators intensified surveillance, but it is not equivalent to proof that the proposed act happened, or that a court found it happened. Even a document bearing an official federal seal may contain an untested informant statement.

The correct encyclopedia practice is to describe the document as evidence of an investigation or allegation, then look for a corroborated charge, sworn testimony, or disposition. This is especially important in archival collections released for the Kennedy assassination records, where a record’s presence in a disclosure corpus does not imply that its subject participated in that assassination. [5] [6]

40. The 1969 Bank-Fraud Indictment

A period industry report described the federal government’s 1969 prosecution concerning alleged misuse of a Chicago-area bank through home-improvement loans. The report associated an Alderisio indictment with a larger campaign against the use of ostensibly legitimate financial institutions by organized-crime enterprises. Its reported allegations concerned improper loan documentation and insufficient collateral.

The indictment’s allegations should not be represented as findings on all 21 charges. The more useful later source is a 1970 Congressional Record account, which describes a negotiated resolution involving a single bank-fraud count and dismissals of other counts. Journalistic descriptions of an indictment and records of a final disposition are different evidentiary categories. [8] [11]

41. Firearms Charge and 1970 Plea Arrangement

A June 1970 statement in the Congressional Record says that Alderisio agreed in January 1970 to plead guilty to possessing 33 firearms as a convicted felon and to offer no defense to one of 21 bank-fraud counts. It reports concurrent terms of two years for the firearms offense and five years for fraud, together with dismissal of the remaining fraud counts.

That account is a legislative argument made in support of a proposed procedural reform, not a substitute for a certified plea agreement or judgment. It provides concrete leads and a summary of the federal government’s position. The biography records the statement as congressional reporting and avoids treating its polemical judgments about defense strategy as independently proven facts. [8]

42. The 1970 Proceedings: A Chronology Caution

The House account describes a January 1970 agreement to withdraw an appeal, while the Tenth Circuit published a March 31 opinion in the surveillance litigation ordering limited further proceedings. Those sources should not be collapsed into a single clean event without the underlying dockets. It is possible that reporting dates, procedural stages, or related filings differ, but this edition will not invent the reconciliation.

The reliable minimum is that the 1965 jury conviction generated years of litigation; a 1969 district-court judgment reimposed the original sentence; a published appellate opinion issued in March 1970; and a contemporaneous congressional speaker described additional 1970 plea arrangements. The full docket history remains an archival task. [4] [8]

43. Why the Cases Were Nationally Significant

The Alderisio litigation gave Congress an example for debates about criminal procedure and disclosure of government surveillance records. Senators and representatives portrayed the case differently according to their policy purposes. A criminal prosecution can be simultaneously an important effort to address violence and a test of rules protecting defendants from unlawful state monitoring.

Historical interpretation should separate the legal propositions resolved by courts from the political arguments that accompanied them. A member of Congress might characterize litigation as delay; a court might regard the same litigation as a necessary constitutional inquiry. Neither description by itself establishes the individual’s guilt beyond the charges actually adjudicated. [3] [4] [8] [9]

44. Leadership during the Late 1960s

The Crime Commission describes Alderisio as a short-term boss from about 1967 until imprisonment, though other retrospective succession lists and contemporaneous institutional descriptions use varying labels. A 1969 Senate speech called him a high-level Chicago leader and enforcer. These accounts support influence but do not provide a definitive appointment ceremony, formal vote, or uncontested succession date.

Readers should understand “boss” as a reported historical role that may denote a visible or operational leader under continuing senior influence. It should not erase the roles of Paul Ricca, Tony Accardo, Sam Battaglia, Jackie Cerone, and others. Treating the Outfit as a simple, single-office corporation would misrepresent the uncertainty built into its clandestine structure. [1] [9]

45. Relations with Paul Ricca and Tony Accardo

In retrospective analyses of Chicago’s leadership, Paul Ricca and Tony Accardo often appear as senior authorities whose influence survived changes in the public-facing or street-level leadership. Alderisio’s alleged temporary command should be interpreted against that wider pattern. This edition has not found a primary record stating that Ricca or Accardo transferred an exclusive office to him on a precise date.

Understanding this matters for interpreting responsibility. The ability to direct some gambling or collection activity is not necessarily authority over all extortion, labor, or homicide operations in the region. Institutional references to “boss” are useful starting points, but the analyst should reconstruct who actually gave orders in each well-documented case. [1] [9]

46. Political Protection and Its Evidentiary Limits

Stories about payments to judges, police, and officials help explain why public commentators viewed the Chicago Outfit as a durable institution. The Chicago Crime Commission associates Alderisio with bagman functions, but the materials examined for this edition do not establish a complete roster of paid officials or precise transfers.

Corruption is not a claim to be proved by organizational reputation alone. Records of bribery prosecutions, sworn testimony, financial ledgers, and administrative findings would support a stronger account. Absent such records, the page describes the historical model of criminal protection but avoids naming supposed recipients as corrupt actors. [1]

47. Milwaukee, Chicago, Denver, and Las Vegas as a Network

Alderisio’s case links multiple geographies with unusually strong evidence. Chicago was his base in the federal opinions; Las Vegas supplied personal relationships and the contested investment; Denver was the site of the alleged threats and trial; and a New York hotel phone call connected actors across the country. Milwaukee belongs to the nickname and intelligence-led account of regional gambling.

These locations show how criminal influence could depend on mobility rather than permanent control of one neighborhood. Travel, telephone access, financing, and reputation allowed an intermediary to carry pressure from one business community into another. That structural finding is better supported than claims assigning Alderisio unverified ownership of every relevant business. [1] [2] [7]

48. Life in Federal Custody

The Chicago Crime Commission reports that Alderisio entered prison after the late-1960s prosecutions and died while incarcerated. The exact custody sequence, transfers, and institutional medical history would require Bureau of Prisons and prison-administration records not collated for this edition.

It is important not to infer that a prisoner served every nominal year from each concurrent sentence consecutively. Different convictions, concurrent terms, credit for time, and appeal timing complicate simple calculations. The congressional report concerning the 1970 disposition specifically describes overlapping sentences. This biography records the known custody endpoint without inventing a complete incarceration ledger. [1] [8]

49. Death: September 25, 1971

The Chicago Crime Commission places Alderisio’s death on September 25, 1971, while he was imprisoned, and describes the cause as natural. Secondary accounts identify the federal penitentiary at Marion, Illinois. A 1975 Congressional Record item confusingly refers to a death in 1972, illustrating that even apparently official retrospective commentary can contain date errors.

This edition uses September 25, 1971, as the supported date, but has not examined an original death certificate or Bureau of Prisons medical record. Readers requiring cause-of-death certification should consult those materials. No evidence reviewed here supports portraying his death as a proven gangland homicide. [1] [12]

50. Funeral, Burial, and Memorialization

Some biographical directories name Queen of Heaven Cemetery in Hillside, Illinois, as Alderisio’s burial place. That association is not independently verified here through a cemetery register, funeral-home document, or burial card. It is therefore not presented as a confirmed archival finding.

Burial details can be relevant to family history but should never be the evidentiary foundation for claims about an individual’s criminal rank. This biography prioritizes published judgments and historical institutional accounts and explicitly identifies the missing vital and cemetery documents. [1]

51. The Crime Commission Retrospective

The Chicago Crime Commission’s October 2015 archival note is the main consolidated institutional sketch of Alderisio’s career used here. It supplies biographical dates, organizational descriptions, violent reputation, and stories of commercial enterprises. Its tone and some uncorroborated assertions reflect the tradition of crime-commission dossiers.

Its value is real: it preserves a coherent picture of what Chicago anti-crime institutions believed about Alderisio. Yet historical synthesis must compare it with the narrower federal court findings. When the Commission attributes a murder or an official position, the attribution is recorded as the Commission’s description unless independently supported by original documents. [1]

52. The FBI Intelligence Record

FBI documents are indispensable for mapping investigative interest, aliases, suspected associations, and surveillance chronology. They are also heterogeneous: a sworn affidavit, routine report, source claim, administrative memo, and court-entered exhibit do not have the same evidentiary status. Several files here are accessible through records released or indexed in the JFK records collection.

Alderisio appears among figures investigated for financial and interstate criminal activity, but the presence of his name within such an archive does not connect him to President Kennedy’s assassination. This edition intentionally avoids repurposing an archival disclosure category into proof of an unrelated allegation. [5] [6] [7]

53. Contemporary Press and the Construction of Fear

The public image of “Milwaukee Phil” was shaped by reporting on armed enforcement, dramatic police encounters, and violent debt collection. Such stories can capture how threats functioned socially: the reputation of an individual might intensify fear before any weapon appeared. The Denver opinion supplies one case where the government attempted to prove that mechanism through witnesses and calls.

Yet press and gangland reminiscence also favor memorable scenes and simplified hierarchies. Details such as numbered murders, exact speeches, or heroic police chases should not be recycled without original provenance. The task of an encyclopedia is to preserve evidence, not amplify the legend. [1] [2]

54. The Problem of Retrospective Leadership Charts

Many Mafia histories present leadership as a continuous, tidy succession from one named boss to another. Chicago’s historical organization was more complex: imprisoned leaders, hidden advisers, street-level coordinators, and geographical captains could all retain varying authority. Alderisio’s place between Giancana, Battaglia, and other senior figures illustrates the problem.

The Crime Commission’s “short-term boss” description is best rendered as reported leadership rather than an unqualified claim that he held a single formal office acknowledged by all factions. Future research might compare contemporaneous Senate hearings, police charts, and participant testimony to refine the position. [1] [9] [10]

55. What the Jury Actually Found

The foundational jury finding was an agreement to transmit threats in interstate communications. In the supporting evidentiary story, threats surrounding repayment of an oil investment were communicated over state lines, and Alderisio participated in the Denver visit. The 1967 Tenth Circuit opinion is explicit both about the conviction and about the separate acquittal.

This distinction protects against two opposite distortions: inventing convictions for alleged murders and claiming a Supreme Court hearing somehow erased the historical trial. A published legal record supports a bounded, specific conclusion. Anything beyond it should be marked as an allegation, inference, or later institutional interpretation. [2] [3] [4]

56. What the Supreme Court Did Not Decide

The Supreme Court did not declare Alderisio innocent of the underlying interstate-conspiracy offense. Its ruling concerned privacy rights, access to records of illegal monitoring, and the procedure needed to examine whether the conviction rested on tainted evidence. The Court’s intervention changed the litigation’s procedural posture.

A researcher should therefore avoid saying simply that Alderisio “won at the Supreme Court” or “had his case thrown out.” The judgment was vacated for further proceedings; the district court imposed a new judgment; and the Tenth Circuit later ordered a limited additional examination while otherwise affirming. Precision about these stages is essential. [3] [4]

57. The Case in Constitutional History

Alderman v. United States is cited in the law of Fourth Amendment standing and suppression of evidence derived from unlawful surveillance. The questions it posed affected defendants far beyond organized crime. The opinion defined when one person may assert a privacy violation and emphasized the need for meaningful examination of contested government recordings.

The irony of a feared criminal figure appearing in an important rights case should not distract from the constitutional stakes. Legal institutions must apply general safeguards to unpopular defendants because an exception based solely on public reputation would corrode the system’s independence. [3]

58. Methodological Assessment: Levels of Proof

This biography uses a hierarchy of claims. Court opinions are strongest for what a tribunal adjudicated and for its description of the record, though even courts can summarize disputed testimony. Government intelligence reports are strong for what officials recorded, but not automatically for the truth of each informant assertion. An institutional retrospective provides context and hypotheses to verify.

Statements about a murder, bribe, promotion, or financial stake must be rated by this framework. The absence of a proven conviction does not itself disprove an allegation, but neither does a dossier entry establish guilt. The goal is not artificial certainty; it is a reproducible account a reader can audit. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]

59. Historical Significance and Legacy

Alderisio’s legacy has two dimensions. In Chicago crime history, he stands as an example of a network operative whose influence was identified with intimidation, gambling, and cross-city relationships. In American legal history, his litigation helped define the procedure for evaluating the impact of unlawfully gathered electronic surveillance on criminal convictions.

An accurate biography therefore resists both romanticization and careless condemnation. It acknowledges the specific threats-conspiracy conviction, the narrower acquittal, the serious allegations that remain unadjudicated in this research, and the complexity of his appeals. His life illustrates how a criminal network and the institutions trying to investigate it could shape one another’s histories. [1] [2] [3] [4]

Documentary Research Windows

Each window is an editorial source-study guide, not a facsimile, a direct quotation, or an invented manuscript excerpt. Readers seeking original wording should open the referenced judicial opinion or archive item.

1. The 1967 Published Criminal Opinion

Read the opening factual and charge discussion in 371 F.2d 983. Compare the conspiracy count, the acquittal on the distinct extortion count, and the sentencing entries. Do not extract a criminal label from later shorthand in political commentary. [2]

2. The Oil-Lease Accounting Record

Use the court’s stated $78,150 investment, $10,000 payment to the geologist, and balance placed in escrow to reconstruct the economic basis of the dispute. These are case facts summarized in a judicial opinion, not original audited account books. [2]

3. The Denver Visit and Communications

Trace June calls, July 6 travel, the Concord Hotel telephone connection, and the August repayment plan. Separate the testimony of a named witness from the jury’s ultimate conspiracy verdict. [2]

4. The Supreme Court’s 1969 Opinion

Read 394 U.S. 165 for Fourth Amendment standing and disclosure; distinguish grounds for suppression from the existence of illegal monitoring. The opinion concerns procedural rights, not a final declaration of innocence. [3]

5. The Tenth Circuit’s 1970 Remand

Read 424 F.2d 20 and footnotes for the four Chicago monitoring sites, the destroyed tapes, and the need to compare logs with airtels. Note the decision’s limited remedy and its “otherwise affirmed” language. [4]

6. The FBI Alias and Florida Investigative Files

Review released 1963–1964 FBI subject sheets. Record names, investigating office, case categories and source status; do not equate investigative allegations with convictions or a proven link to the JFK assassination. [5] [6]

7. 1958 FBI Intelligence about Milwaukee

Locate the passage in the Marshall Caifano file referring to Alderisio and Milwaukee gambling. Identify it as a claim conveyed through investigative sources and test it against independently dated licensing, arrest, or financial documents. [7]

8. The 1970 Congressional Plea Account

Consult the House Congressional Record account of the firearms and bank-fraud dispositions. It was offered in a legislative debate and contains forceful advocacy; independently verify the precise sentencing and withdrawal dates in certified dockets. [8]

9. The Crime Commission Retrospective

Compare the Commission’s 2015 claims about leadership, alleged murders, and death to contemporaneous court decisions. Note how institutional histories summarize reputational claims that no individual judicial case settled. [1]

10. Death Year Source Conflict

Compare the Crime Commission’s September 25, 1971 date with a 1975 congressional passage referring to 1972. A death certificate or Bureau of Prisons file would resolve the contradiction; do not treat both as equally strong. [1] [12]

Research Limitations and Editorial Notes

This first-edition historical text establishes the detailed 1961–1970 legal chronology primarily from federal appellate opinions and the Supreme Court decision. It does not claim to reproduce original trial transcripts, all government surveillance recordings (the appeals court expressly says tapes were not preserved), complete FBI field files, a certified 1970 plea agreement, or all Bureau of Prisons records. It does not establish every alleged organized-crime killing, exact internal rank, financial ownership claim, or early-life family story.

The 1970 Congressional Record is a contemporary legislative source summarizing a plea arrangement but includes argumentative descriptions and should not replace signed judgments. The Chicago Crime Commission supplies the September 25, 1971 date, but full vital and medical-file verification remains outstanding. A 1975 legislative excerpt gives a contradictory death year. Some source material is digitized as scanned PDF; bibliographic verification is targeted rather than exhaustive. External images are omitted because reuse rights were not established. Live-site rendering with MGU components has not been tested.

No connection to the assassination of President John F. Kennedy is inferred from a federal archive’s release label. This is a historical study of the Chicago Outfit and a documented constitutional-law case, not an allegation of involvement in unrelated conspiracies.
