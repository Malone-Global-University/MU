1. Historical Overview

Frank DeCicco occupied one of the most consequential short-term leadership positions in the modern Gambino crime family. He emerged from the Brooklyn organization during Paul Castellano’s administration, helped link the faction that removed Castellano to established figures within the family, and became underboss after John Gotti assumed control in late 1985. The period between the December 16, 1985 assassination of Castellano and Thomas Bilotti and DeCicco’s own death on April 13, 1986 was less than four months. Nevertheless, it offers a revealing case study of succession, factional legitimacy, surveillance, and the limits of violent authority within the American Mafia. [1][2][8][11]

The sequence also explains why DeCicco must be treated as a historical subject in his own right rather than merely a side character in biographies of Gotti or Castellano. Underboss authority, the acquisition of legitimacy and the effects of retaliatory violence were central institutional processes of this period. [1][2][8][11]

2. Name, Nicknames, and Identification

The subject appears in official records as Frank DeCicco and Frank De Cicco. Commonly printed nicknames include ‘Frankie Cheech’ and ‘Frankie D,’ but the documentary strength behind each nickname differs; the 1986 FBI teletype and federal opinion establish the core identity and organizational designation without relying on a nickname. The name should not be confused with other people named Frank DeCicco or with the several DeCicco relatives and associates appearing in later cases. Exact birth and genealogical details are less secure than the documented death and underboss position. [1][2][15]

3. Birth Date and Contradictory Age Reports

Many modern secondary profiles list November 5, 1935 as DeCicco’s birthday. This would make him fifty on April 13, 1986. Yet reporting published immediately after the bombing described him variously as fifty, fifty-two, or fifty-eight. The conflicts are real, not a reason to average the numbers or invent another date. No original birth certificate, Social Security application, or contemporaneous sworn identity form has been collated for this first edition. The biography therefore identifies 1935 as a commonly reported birth year, not a conclusively authenticated civil-record fact. [3][4][5][7][15]

The conflict between press ages is a useful source-criticism exercise. Reporters on the same day can accurately document an explosion while printing inconsistent personal details; accuracy in one field does not establish accuracy in every field. [3][4][5][7][15]

4. Early Life and the Limits of the Record

DeCicco is generally associated with a Brooklyn upbringing and later Staten Island residence, but standard accounts of his childhood often recycle unsourced descriptions. Unlike the April 1986 investigation, childhood schooling, apprenticeship, and the exact course of his entry into organized crime are not established here by a contemporaneous file. The presence of DeCicco relatives in Gambino circles helps explain why later writers treat the name as embedded in the organization; it does not by itself establish when he joined, who sponsored him, or what authority he held in each decade. [2][3][4][14]

5. Family Connections and the DeCicco Name

Contemporary judicial reporting names a Joseph ‘Joe’ DeCicco in connection with the Brooklyn social club and identifies him as Frank’s uncle. Other accounts refer to a father named Vincent and to wider DeCicco family participation in the Gambino organization. These names must not be merged into one individual merely because surnames recur. A person identified in the record as an uncle is not the same as a brother, and later prosecutions involving DeCicco family members do not constitute evidence that Frank himself committed those later offenses. The familial record merits a separate genealogical audit. [2][12][14]

6. Brooklyn and Staten Island Networks

The setting of DeCicco’s established career is more securely documented than his private history. Federal investigators and reporters placed his activities within a Brooklyn-centered network of clubs, restaurants, and criminal associations, with Staten Island repeatedly identified as his place of residence. These sites mattered because they facilitated meetings and surveillance, not because every person seen nearby was necessarily participating in racketeering. The court’s 1986 discussion of the Bath Avenue club is particularly valuable because it records specific police observations and searches rather than a generalized description of an underworld headquarters. [2][3][4]

7. Arrests, Allegations, and Criminal Dispositions

An April 1986 news report asserted that DeCicco had been arrested numerous times between 1958 and 1981 on charges said to have ranged from gambling to kidnapping. It did not supply a complete docket, case numbers, or dispositions for each arrest. An arrest history is not interchangeable with a conviction history. No inventory of verified criminal convictions against DeCicco is presented in this first edition because the available materials do not support one. His placement in FBI intelligence files and his documented underboss status are distinct from the resolution of any particular criminal charge. [2][4]

8. The Gambino Hierarchy Before 1985

From 1976 until December 1985, Paul Castellano led the Gambino organization, while Aniello Dellacroce represented another major center of authority as underboss. Joseph N. Gallo held the consigliere role. The important context for DeCicco was the tension between formal office and practical alliances: the existence of a recognized boss did not ensure the loyalty of all captains or the compliance of those who expected to inherit Dellacroce’s influence. Later court testimony and investigative reconstructions place DeCicco among figures navigating both the Castellano administration and the emerging Gotti coalition. [2][8][11][16]

9. Reported Captain-Level Standing

A later FBI administrative identification list classifies DeCicco as both a capodecina and underboss. His older captain-level position is also a premise of accounts of the 1985 coalition against Castellano. It is nevertheless best to say that law enforcement and subsequent histories identified him as a captain before his 1985 promotion; the date of induction, succession to a particular crew, and exact membership roster cannot be reconstructed from the cited court opinion alone. This distinction matters for an encyclopedia series that assigns formal organizational titles only where evidence warrants them. [1][2][11][18]

10. Relationship with Paul Castellano

DeCicco is repeatedly described as having access to Castellano and the confidence needed to arrange or attend a dinner meeting with the Gambino boss. This access gave him an unusual role in the December 1985 leadership coup: he occupied a position close enough to the existing administration to know of a proposed gathering while participating, according to later testimony, in the circle that facilitated Castellano’s removal. The record does not require choosing between simplistic labels of loyalist and traitor before examining the specific meetings and testimony. [3][8][9][11]

11. The Dellacroce–Gotti Faction

Aniello Dellacroce’s long service as underboss provided an established institutional base for John Gotti and other Brooklyn figures. His death on December 2, 1985 removed an important intermediary in the strained relationship with Castellano. The direct documentary evidence for DeCicco’s role begins to strengthen as the succession crisis escalates. Later narratives present a coalition of dissatisfied senior members, not merely a personal contest between Gotti and Castellano. DeCicco’s involvement should be evaluated in relation to that coalition rather than reduced to an isolated friendship with either faction. [8][10][11][16]

12. The 1985 Succession Crisis

By late 1985, Castellano’s legal jeopardy, internal disagreements, and disputes over authority intensified an already unstable situation. The assassination of Dellacroce’s successor as underboss, Thomas Bilotti, at the same time as Castellano underscored that the plot was aimed at the top of the chain of command. DeCicco’s subsequent elevation suggests that those directing the change needed recognizable senior leadership to consolidate their position. This is a structural inference from the sequence of offices, not proof that every participant shared identical motives or plans. [2][8][11][16]

13. The Sparks Steak House Appointment

Contemporary later trial reporting states that DeCicco was expected to dine with Castellano at Sparks Steak House in Manhattan on December 16, 1985. Gravano’s 1992 testimony and subsequent historical reconstructions link information about that appointment to Gotti’s assassination plan. Accounts differ on which captains were expected to attend and on the precise steps by which the appointment was arranged; a definitive guest list cannot be extracted from one press summary. The important supported point is that the dinner appointment created the opportunity at which Castellano and Bilotti were shot. [8][9][11][14]

Whether a witness personally attended a planning session, learned the intended guest list afterward, or supplied an inference can materially change the level of historical confidence. This edition does not invent dialogue between Castellano and DeCicco. [8][9][11][14]

14. The December 16, 1985 Double Murder

Paul Castellano and Thomas Bilotti were shot outside Sparks Steak House on December 16, 1985. The murders were later established as part of the racketeering case against John Gotti; Gravano testified that Gotti watched the operation from a nearby vehicle. DeCicco’s place in the plot is supported principally by accomplice testimony and retrospective investigation, not by a personal conviction for the murders. He died before the 1992 Gotti trial. Accordingly, descriptions of what he intended, said, or arranged must be attributed to witnesses or later reconstructions rather than recast as findings in a trial of DeCicco himself. [8][9][10][16]

The chronological order matters: DeCicco’s post-murder advancement is documented, but promotion following a murder does not, on its own, identify all murder conspirators. The evidence for participation rests on later witness-based accounts. [8][9][10][16]

15. John Gotti’s Accession

After the killings, John Gotti emerged as Gambino boss. The May 1986 federal opinion recounted an investigator’s information that Gotti held that position and that DeCicco was his underboss. The opinion also quoted surveillance observations that Gotti and DeCicco appeared together in December 1985. Leadership in a clandestine organization is not certified by a public oath; independent FBI reporting, a federal judicial account of testimony, and contemporary news converging on the same relationship make the office assignment substantially better supported than speculation about private succession promises. [1][2][3]

16. Underboss: Scope of Authority

DeCicco’s role as underboss is not merely an invention of later popular Mafia histories. It is explicit in an April 15, 1986 FBI New York teletype, in law-enforcement evidence summarized in United States v. Gotti, and in contemporary reporting that called him the Gambino family’s second-ranking figure. Such records demonstrate the position assigned to him in the organization as investigators understood it. They do not establish his personal supervision of every crew, his share of illicit revenues, or any separate criminal liability for conduct undertaken by others. [1][2][3][5]

The underboss role denotes the No. 2 office in a criminal hierarchy; it does not signify an elected corporate position, a public legal office, or authority independent of the new boss’s support. [1][2][3][5]

17. A Tenure of Less Than Four Months

The period from the December 16, 1985 succession killings to the April 13, 1986 bombing was approximately 118 days. That brevity is analytically important. DeCicco’s administration was an early part of the Gotti regime, before its position within the broader Commission could settle and while investigators were pursuing criminal proceedings against Gotti. No claim is made that DeCicco introduced a new long-term economic program or reorganized the whole family; the archival record more clearly shows transitional meetings, visible alliances, and continuing factional tensions. [1][2][3][13]

18. The Ravenite and Public Visibility

NYPD and federal testimony described DeCicco and John Gotti together around the Ravenite Social Club in Manhattan during the weeks following the killings. One investigator observed them walking together away from the club on December 24, 1985, and another saw DeCicco present during surveillance in January 1986. The observations are evidence of repeated association and high-level contact. They are not recordings of the full content of conversations and should not be exaggerated into proof of particular orders, payments, or plans that the investigators could not hear. [2]

19. The Veterans and Friends Social Club

The Veterans and Friends Social Club on Brooklyn’s 86th Street was central to the 1986 bombing. The FBI described DeCicco’s vehicle as parked across the street from the club, while contemporary reporting located it outside a nearby Italian restaurant. Both descriptions may refer to the same small area. A later edited transcript of Michael DiLeonardo’s 2006 testimony describes the club’s continued operation after Castellano’s death and his own recollection of that Sunday. The differing source dates mean these statements should not be merged into one composite eyewitness narrative. [1][4][12]

20. The Bath Avenue Club and Police Searches

The May 1986 federal opinion described a separate social club at 1628 Bath Avenue. According to the police testimony recounted by the judge, detectives investigating DeCicco’s killing later entered the location, found concealed firearms, and arrested Robert Fapiano. This was testimony in litigation concerning the release conditions of Gotti and others, not a posthumous case against DeCicco. The opinion also described Joseph DeCicco at the doorway several days after the assassination. These facts show why identifying distinct clubs and distinct family members is necessary for an accurate physical chronology. [2]

21. Federal Surveillance of the Gotti Administration

The available FBI file reveals how quickly the government responded to the leadership change: the office sought electronic-surveillance information on Gotti and associates, and after DeCicco’s death it began surveillance of another suspected meeting place. The April 15 teletype identifies a gathering in an apartment above the Altadona restaurant and states that investigators were preparing a Title III affidavit. This record is operational intelligence written at the time, rather than a retrospective statement of who was later convicted. Its value lies in dates, investigative priorities, and observed locations. [1]

22. April 13, 1986: The Fatal Explosion

On Sunday, April 13, 1986, an explosive device detonated at a vehicle on 86th Street in the Bensonhurst area of Brooklyn as DeCicco and another man were nearby or entering it. Federal investigators identified DeCicco as the Gambino underboss and reported that he died after being fatally injured; reporters described extensive destruction to the vehicle and adjacent area. Accounts differ on the exact sequence in which the men approached or entered the car. The date, street, explosive attack, death, and injury to a second man are supported across independent contemporary accounts. [1][3][4][5]

The federal teletype and the police news briefing are complementary, not identical: they use different conventions for identifying the location, describe different stages of emergency response, and should be read together with their original dates. [1][3][4][5]

23. Scene and Vehicle Evidence

The April 14 police briefing described the damaged gray sedan, a high-order explosive, damage in the surrounding street, and severe injury to the man who survived. Some later narratives refer to the vehicle simply as ‘DeCicco’s Buick,’ but a contemporary report attributed the car’s registration or ownership to another Staten Island individual, Angelo Giammarino. Ownership, use, and the location of an explosive are separate evidentiary questions. This edition does not reconstruct the bomb’s physical components or placement beyond the publicly documented fact that an explosive device destroyed the vehicle. [1][4]

24. Frank Bellino and Other Injured Persons

Contemporary reports identified Frank Bellino, described as associated with the Lucchese family, as the seriously wounded man with DeCicco. The released FBI teletype refers to an injured Lucchese soldier but redacts his name; the surviving contemporary reporting supplies Bellino’s identification. Newspaper accounts also mentioned two people with minor injuries and collateral damage near the blast. Any historical reconstruction that mentions only the targeted underboss and ignores the other wounded and endangered people is incomplete. Bellino’s organizational status in these sources is a law-enforcement description, not a separate adjudication. [1][3][4][7]

Bellino was a serious casualty of the attack even though most later retellings focus on DeCicco. Injury to additional people and the public setting are essential to understanding the nature of the violent episode. [1][3][4][7]

25. Death Timing and Medical Accounts

The April 15 FBI teletype says DeCicco was fatally injured and died around two hours later in a hospital, whereas some press summaries used language suggesting that he died at the scene or immediately from the blast. The same event can produce different early reports about time of death; the original death certificate and complete hospital record were not reviewed. The reliable conclusion is that he died on April 13, 1986 as a direct result of the car bombing. More precise medical details require documentary confirmation and should not be asserted as a settled narrative here. [1][3][4]

This limitation is deliberate: calling a victim ‘killed instantly’ when a dated official teletype says death followed hospital treatment would erase a material documentary disagreement. [1][3][4]

26. Immediate Law-Enforcement Response

Police investigators canvassed the scene and federal agents monitored suspected organized-crime meeting sites after the bombing. The FBI teletype dated April 15 described a Gambino-source contact and surveillance above the Altadona restaurant. These follow-up actions demonstrate the perceived threat of additional violence and the investigative value placed on identifying who was meeting whom immediately after the death. They do not establish that anyone observed in those meetings planned the explosion. Separating surveillance from proof of participation is a central evidentiary obligation in this case. [1][2][4]

27. Contemporary Theories of Responsibility

In April 1986 the FBI spokesman quoted by UPI framed retaliation for Castellano’s murder as a theory, not a solved case. Other contemporaneous reports entertained possibilities involving rivalry within Gotti’s own organization. These early alternatives deserve inclusion because they show investigators did not then claim a proven perpetrator. Later accounts associated the attack with an interfamily retaliation plot, but the development of that narrative over time should not erase what was—and was not—known to police on April 14. This biography therefore separates initial investigative suspicion from later testimony and published reconstructions. [3][4][5]

28. Commission Rules and Unauthorized Boss Killing

The Castellano assassination implicated norms of the New York Mafia Commission regarding the killing of a sitting boss. In the later Gigante case, the district court found evidence that senior Genovese and other organized-crime figures planned the murder of the Gotti brothers as punishment for the unauthorized Castellano killing. That later judicial finding gives the retaliation theory a stronger historical context, but the opinion discusses the Gotti murder conspiracies; it is not a criminal verdict identifying the person who placed the April 1986 device under DeCicco’s car. Different conspiracies and criminal episodes must remain distinct. [13][16]

Later findings about Commission retaliation show a causal political context. They should not be transformed into a single chain of inferred orders naming a specific bomber without separate evidence about that homicide. [13][16]

29. Lucchese and Genovese Attribution in Later Accounts

Specialist and journalistic accounts have implicated Anthony Casso, Victor Amuso, and Genovese-linked figures in the planning or execution of DeCicco’s bombing. Some assign the device to Herbert Pate and describe a plan that also targeted Gotti. These details are primarily sourced to Raab’s retrospective reporting and claimed underworld admissions; the cited 1986 police records did not make those identifications. The presence of later named suspects does not itself demonstrate a judicial conviction specifically for this homicide. The article records the interpretation while clearly distinguishing it from contemporary, publicly established findings. [3][4][13][17]

The distinction between a historical hypothesis supported by cooperating-witness narratives and a specific court judgment is particularly important when naming alleged participants who are not the defendants in the cited case. [3][4][13][17]

30. Was John Gotti Also a Target?

The widespread assertion that the 1986 blast was intended to kill both Gotti and DeCicco rests heavily on later accounts of the retaliation conspiracy. The 1997 Gigante judgment independently establishes a separate conspiracy to murder John and Gene Gotti, including plans that did not proceed, thereby confirming the broader danger facing the new leadership. It does not prove that Gotti was scheduled to enter DeCicco’s vehicle on April 13 or that a particular bystander was mistaken for him. Those more specific claims remain attributed to later witness-based narratives rather than recited as established forensic facts. [13][17]

31. Interfamily and Intrafamily Violence

The spring 1986 crisis unfolded across two potentially overlapping dimensions: internal disputes between supporters of the former Castellano administration and the new Gotti leadership, and the response of leaders in other Mafia families. Both dimensions appeared in contemporary explanations of DeCicco’s death. It would be inaccurate to infer from the timing alone that the murder definitively resulted from either one. The evidence of leadership conflict is strong, while the specific allocation of responsibility for this device requires a different standard—one not supplied by newspaper speculation about motive. [2][3][4][13]

32. The Separate 1986 John Gotti Racketeering Trial

John Gotti and several codefendants were facing a federal racketeering proceeding when the bomb exploded. Defense counsel asked for a mistrial or relief in light of publicity that could affect impartial jurors. The May 1986 opinion addressed detention and bail issues while recounting investigators’ testimony about Gotti’s activities and DeCicco’s status. DeCicco was not on trial in that proceeding and his killing did not constitute a conviction of any defendant then before the court. This distinction prevents a major error: taking evidence introduced at a bail hearing as a trial verdict about the bombing. [2][5]

The publicity dispute is independently significant: defense lawyers argued that a notorious bombing could make an unbiased jury harder to obtain. That procedural claim was separate from determining the murderer. [2][5]

33. Reading United States v. Gotti Carefully

The federal opinion is among the best contemporary sources for the Gotti–DeCicco leadership relationship because its author summarized testimony from several law-enforcement witnesses. It also contains evident date errors: one passage gives DeCicco’s assassination as April 13, 1980, and another mentions April 13, 1985 in a context plainly about the 1986 death. The correct year is established by the same opinion’s surrounding chronology, FBI’s April 1986 teletype, and multiple independent news reports. Scholarly use of a judicial source requires identifying typographical mistakes rather than silently reproducing them. [1][2][3]

A court document can be authoritative on its own proceedings and still contain an editorial error. The text’s errors were retained here as analytical warnings rather than concealed by emending a quotation. [1][2][3]

34. Funeral and Public Response

A UPI report of April 17, 1986 described private funeral services in Brooklyn, surveillance of the mourners, and a plan for burial in Moravian Cemetery on Staten Island. The report also recorded church officials’ refusal at that moment to authorize a funeral Mass, while leaving open the possibility of later Catholic services. Funeral reporting is evidence of how the killing reverberated through family and neighborhood networks, but neither attendance nor absence conclusively establishes guilt in the bombing. DeCicco’s death affected relatives, witnesses, other victims of the explosion, and nearby residents—not solely a leadership succession chart. [2][6]

35. Gotti at DeCicco’s Wake

The federal opinion recorded investigators’ observations at the wake: John Gotti appeared with associates and was accorded conspicuous deference. Those observations help date the consolidation of his personal status within the Gambino organization. They do not show the private substance of discussions among mourners, and they do not determine who planted the device. This source is most appropriately used as evidence of visible organizational conduct after a fatal event, especially when compared with investigators’ observations before DeCicco’s death. [2]

36. Succession to Joseph Armone

After DeCicco’s murder, the Gambino underboss post passed to Joseph ‘Piney’ Armone, according to later testimony and historical reconstructions. Michael DiLeonardo’s 2006 testimony identifies Armone as the successor, while contemporary legal records independently place Armone among important Gambino participants in 1986. The exact appointment ceremony or effective date has not been independently established by an original organizational document. Describing the transfer as an eventual 1986 succession is more defensible than asserting a precise date or presenting Armone as the perpetrator or beneficiary of the bombing without evidence. [2][9][12]

37. Salvatore Gravano and Retrospective Testimony

The most widely repeated narrative of the Castellano conspiracy relies in important part on testimony by cooperating witness Salvatore Gravano at Gotti’s 1992 trial. His testimony has the advantage of sworn examination and cross-examination, but also reflects a participant who had criminal exposure and incentives to cooperate. It is especially important to preserve the difference between his direct statements, a reporter’s summary, and later embellishments by other writers. Gravano’s descriptions of the 1985 plan help explain DeCicco’s position in the transition; they do not automatically authenticate every subsequent claim about the 1986 bombing. [8][9][10]

38. Michael DiLeonardo’s Later Eyewitness Account

In testimony dated February 22, 2006, later republished in edited form, Michael DiLeonardo described being present at Veterans and Friends on the day of the bombing. He recalled observing a suspiciously positioned man and hearing an explosion shortly after DeCicco left the club with a Lucchese associate. The testimony is valuable for reconstructing what one witness said he saw, but it was delivered approximately twenty years after the event and appears in a later edited presentation. It should be labeled as recollection, not treated as a contemporaneous police deposition or a conclusive identification of the bomber. [12]

39. Newspaper Reporting and Age Discrepancies

Journalism published on April 14, 1986 is indispensable to the immediate chronology but was written amid uncertainty and urgency. Different contemporaneous reports printed three inconsistent ages for DeCicco, supplied alternative hypotheses about his murder, and varied in their descriptions of exactly where he stood when the bomb detonated. A sound article does not resolve those disagreements by copying the most convenient version. It uses the news reports together with the FBI teletype and court records, labels uncertain details, and identifies where later sources inherit errors from earlier coverage. [1][3][4][5][7]

40. Popular Myth and the Motive Problem

Descriptions of DeCicco as a ‘traitor,’ ‘kingmaker,’ or inevitable victim of a Commission death sentence are narrative interpretations, not criminal charges tried against him. They may summarize the character of the succession struggle, but they compress uncertainty about intention, participation, and causation. The fact that he was scheduled for a dinner where Castellano was murdered, then became Gotti’s underboss, provides a strong chronological connection. It does not give historians access to every private commitment between the participants or establish the identity of the person who later triggered the bomb. [3][8][9][11][13]

41. Comparison with Castellano and Dellacroce

Castellano, Dellacroce, and DeCicco illuminate three different ways Mafia authority operated: Castellano as official boss with substantial commercial and political relationships; Dellacroce as a long-serving underboss associated with a powerful Brooklyn constituency; and DeCicco as a short-lived underboss elevated during a violent succession. Comparing them requires keeping their formal ranks and time periods separate. Dellacroce died of illness before the coup, Castellano was murdered in December 1985, and DeCicco was killed the following April. Conflating the dates makes the causes and effects of the succession unintelligible. [1][2][8][11][16]

42. Victims and the Civilian Cost of Violence

The public scene of a car bombing on a residential and commercial Brooklyn street created risks beyond the intended target. Contemporary police and hospital reports described serious injury to Frank Bellino, other minor injuries, damaged property, and frightened bystanders. An encyclopedia devoted to organized crime should preserve those consequences rather than treat the bombing simply as a dramatic succession event. That analytical choice also helps explain why law-enforcement leaders spoke publicly in April 1986 about the broader instability caused by homicides, arrests, and competition for vacant criminal positions. [3][4][7]

43. No Personal Conviction for the 1985 Murder Plot

DeCicco’s reputed participation in the Castellano killing is historically significant, but he died before later prosecution and testimony clarified much of the 1985 conspiracy. His presence in journalistic descriptions and in accounts of a planned Sparks dinner is not equivalent to a conviction for murder. Equally, no criminal verdict against DeCicco can be inferred from the 1992 conviction of John Gotti or the 1997 judicial findings against Vincent Gigante. Each proceeding had different defendants, charges, witnesses, and rules of proof. A biographical profile must preserve those legal boundaries. [8][9][10][13][16]

44. Investigative Methods and Institutional Evidence

The DeCicco case sits at the intersection of street-level homicide investigation, federal intelligence-gathering, physical surveillance, informant reporting, and court-authorized electronic monitoring. The contemporaneous FBI document shows how swiftly an organized-crime investigation can pivot following a leadership killing, while the May 1986 court opinion exposes the testimony offered to justify bail-related government action. Neither investigative intelligence nor sworn testimony becomes a finding of a particular person’s guilt merely by being included in a published case. This edition identifies the legal status of each type of record. [1][2][16]

45. What the Gigante Judgment Actually Proves

The 1997 judgment in United States v. Gigante found that Gigante participated in a conspiracy against John and Gene Gotti after Castellano’s unauthorized killing. It described planned attacks, including schemes interrupted by FBI warnings or arrests. This judicial finding makes broader retaliation by rival leadership more than an unsupported legend. It does not, by itself, determine who carried out DeCicco’s April 13, 1986 bombing; the fatal incident should not be silently equated with separate unexecuted Gotti plots discussed in the judgment. The distinction is vital to both chronology and legal accuracy. [13]

The Gigante judgment is valuable as corroboration of an organized response to the succession, but the specific fatal bombing remains a distinct event requiring its own individual evidentiary support. [13]

46. Historical Significance and Legacy

DeCicco’s lasting significance lies in his transitional position. He had enough standing within the existing Gambino structure to participate in the reconfiguration of leadership, but his underboss tenure ended before that transition stabilized. His murder illuminated the danger facing the new regime and became a focal point of contemporary reporting, federal surveillance, and later witness testimony. The documentary record is strongest on his office, the timing and location of the explosion, and the surrounding leadership changes; it is weaker on childhood details, motives privately expressed, and the exact identity of all bomb conspirators. [1][2][3][12][13]

47. Research Method and Evidentiary Weight

This profile prioritizes dated FBI communications and published judicial records for the facts they actually establish. It uses same-week news reports for the immediate public chronology, court-reporting of later testimony for the 1985 plot, and retrospective scholarship to frame disputed interpretations. Each source genre has different limits: informants can be mistaken, reporters can publish erroneous ages, courts can reproduce typographical errors, and cooperating witnesses may offer self-interested accounts. Editorial certainty is calibrated accordingly. Original death, birth, full docket, and forensic files are not claimed to have been completely collated. [1][2][3][4][9][12][13]

48. Historical Conclusion

Frank DeCicco’s brief career as underboss stands at the hinge between the Castellano administration and John Gotti’s rule. His identification as Gotti’s second-ranking officer is supported by the FBI, contemporaneous press, and federal judicial summaries. His death on April 13, 1986 in a Brooklyn car bombing is firmly established. The more difficult questions concern the precise early biography, his private role in planning the Castellano murders, and responsibility for the bombing itself. The most defensible first-edition conclusion recognizes the significance of his position while refusing to turn contested accounts into settled facts. [1][2][3][9][13]

Future editions would benefit most from original police scene files, hospital and death records, complete interviews, bomb-forensics material, and complete proceedings concerning the later alleged perpetrators. [1][2][3][9][13]

Documentary Research Windows

These five windows are attributed editorial studies of the identified materials—not original-page reproductions, verbatim trial transcriptions, or authenticated forensic exhibits.

Window 1. The FBI’s April 15, 1986 Teletype

Read the memo as a contemporaneous operational communication. It calls DeCicco underboss, dates his death April 13 and locates the vehicle across from Veterans and Friends. It also records the injury of another person, followed by surveillance of a Gotti meeting site. The memo is useful for these specific facts but does not name the bombers or establish guilt. Its wording on the interval between the explosion and death differs from some news reports. [1]

Window 2. The May 1986 District-Court Opinion

United States v. Gotti was a bail-related proceeding in another case. Its summary of testimony identifies the Gotti–DeCicco leadership arrangement, visible Ravenite associations, and investigative responses. The printed text contains apparent year mistakes—1980 and 1985 in contexts that describe the April 13, 1986 bombing. Editorial verification against the FBI telegram and contemporary newspapers is required before transcribing those dates. [2][1]

Window 3. The April 14–17, 1986 News Cycle

Compare UPI’s early coverage with the Los Angeles Times account: ages differ, proposed motives were theories, and technical descriptions of the explosion changed as information developed. The correct method is not to give every early report equal weight on every field, but to differentiate direct official quotes, unattributed speculation, reporter synthesis and later confirmation. Reports on Bellino’s injuries and funeral arrangements serve different evidentiary functions. [3][4][5][6][7]

Window 4. Gravano’s 1992 Trial Testimony

News reports summarize sworn witness statements made at the Gotti trial about the December 1985 conspiracy. Those reports help explain why DeCicco’s scheduled meeting with Castellano mattered. They are not transcripts of every witness question and answer; a reconstruction should distinguish what Gravano said from how reporters or later books packaged the testimony. Separate the 1985 plot from DeCicco’s later bombing. [8][9][10][11]

Window 5. The 1997 Gigante Judgment and 2006 DiLeonardo Testimony

Gigante’s judgment establishes specific conspiracies to murder the Gotti brothers but does not identify the killer in DeCicco’s case. DiLeonardo offers later recollection of the 1986 scene and organizational succession. Used together, these sources strengthen understanding of the climate and the club, while demonstrating how judicial findings, retrospective eyewitness memory and unadjudicated allegations must remain analytically distinct. [12][13]

Research and Publication Limitations

Identity and birth. Exact birth date, genealogical relations, pre-1980 residence and childhood narrative have not been independently validated from original certificates, census, and immigration records.

Early criminal record. Claims about arrests cannot substitute for docket-based conviction assessments; no complete lifetime disposition table is asserted.

1985 conspiracy. The role in the Castellano plot is reconstructed from later witness testimony and reportage; DeCicco was never personally tried in the later Gotti proceedings.

Bomb scene. The FBI teletype and contemporary press reports were examined, but original NYPD forensic photographs, laboratory materials and emergency records have not been completely collated.

Time of death. FBI and newspaper sources conflict on immediate versus later in-hospital death; the certificate and medical chart were not independently reviewed.

Attribution. Later claims naming Amuso, Casso, Pate or Genovese leadership do not amount to a court judgment specifically convicting them for DeCicco’s murder in the sources analyzed.

Judicial typographical errors. The May 1986 Gotti opinion includes isolated erroneous 1980 and 1985 references to the 1986 bombing; correct chronology is independently corroborated.

Documentary windows. Five research windows summarize attributed sources but are not archival-facsimile collations or complete transcript reprints.

Photography. No historic photograph is embedded because a publication-cleared image license was not established.
