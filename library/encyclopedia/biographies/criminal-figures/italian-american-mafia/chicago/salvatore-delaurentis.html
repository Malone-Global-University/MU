1. Historical Overview

Salvatore V. DeLaurentis, known in public reporting as “Solly D,” is a Chicago-area racketeering figure whose documented criminal case concerned the Ferriola Street Crew, a component of the Chicago Outfit. His significance lies both in the northern-suburban protection network prosecuted during the early 1990s and in later accounts identifying him as an influential senior figure after older Outfit leaders died or were imprisoned. The former is established by federal judicial decisions; the latter rests primarily on investigator and journalist assessments rather than a court finding of present-day command.

The case that made DeLaurentis a national organized-crime defendant arose from efforts to tax independent bookmakers and other businesses in suburban Chicago. The evidence assembled against the crew described unlawful gambling, extortion, high-interest loans, bribery, and violence. He was convicted in 1992 along with numerous co-defendants. Yet one especially serious allegation—the separate count charging conspiracy to murder bookmaker Hal Smith—was vacated by the trial judge after an inquiry revealed that the recorded jury verdict could not reliably be treated as the jury’s actual decision. Other convictions remained in force. [3] [4] [5]

Following his reported release in 2006, DeLaurentis attracted renewed attention as investigators assessed the Outfit’s surviving leadership. In 2018, Chicago television reporting identified him as a possible consigliere; reporting in 2022 and later historical commentary described him as a reputed leader. Those statements are time-specific assessments, not proof of a continuously verified formal office. [7] [8] [9]

2. Name, Identity, and Public Record

The federal court record uses the name Salvatore V. DeLaurentis. Press reports often shorten his name to Sal DeLaurentis, while “Solly D” serves as a durable journalistic and underworld nickname. Searchable court captions frequently list Rocco Infelise first, because the multi-defendant prosecution is usually published as United States v. Infelise; a later appellate opinion is captioned United States v. DiDomenico. Both include substantial information about DeLaurentis. [3] [4] [5]

This distinction matters for an encyclopedia because searches limited to the subject’s surname will miss rulings crucial to his conviction, sentencing, and forfeiture history. The profile’s sources therefore identify court citations, docket number 90 CR 87, and named investigative reports, rather than relying only on popular biographies.

3. Birth Date and Unresolved Civil Documentation

The Chicago Crime Commission’s seventieth-anniversary publication, produced around 1989–1990, identifies DeLaurentis with a date of birth of August 20, 1938. That is a contemporary institutional listing reproduced in a federally archived publication; it is not itself a certified birth certificate. Later secondary profiles and condensed online listings sometimes give the year 1939, while a 1991 neighborhood-era profile calls him 53, broadly consistent with a 1938 birth year. [1] [2]

As no original vital-record certificate was collated for this profile, the Commission’s entry is presented as the best specifically documented date, with the competing birth-year convention acknowledged. The place of birth and childhood details are not confidently reconstructed from the sources reviewed. Speculative family-origin narratives are not substituted for missing records.

4. Geographical Setting: Chicago and the Collar Counties

DeLaurentis’s public story is unusually suburban for a subject commonly placed in Chicago Mafia succession tables. Contemporary reporting associated him with Inverness, Island Lake, Wauconda, Lake Zurich, and the expanding residential and commercial corridors north and northwest of Chicago. Those localities mattered because independent bookmaking, small entertainment establishments, and retail operations could generate recurring cash outside the city’s best-known organized-crime districts. [1] [2] [4]

Lake and McHenry counties were not administratively independent criminal empires. The later prosecution described their illicit markets as operating within a street-crew system subordinated to the Chicago Outfit. The overlap between municipal boundaries, informal collection areas, and criminal associations helps explain why historical accounts sometimes call DeLaurentis a “Lake County boss” while courts and journalists describe him in broader organizational terms.

5. Suburban Expansion and Economic Opportunity

The northern suburbs experienced substantial growth in housing, retail businesses, and regional travel in the late twentieth century. Such development created opportunities for ordinary commerce and for racketeers seeking a percentage of illicit gambling profits. The federal appellate court explained the crew’s activities in terms of collecting protection payments from bookmakers and other illegal enterprises. This was a financial extraction system, not a formal legal tax. [4]

An analytical separation is essential: the appearance of crime in a suburb does not make the community or its businesses complicit. The record concerns specific actors and specific offenses. Nor does proximity to a reputed criminal figure establish participation by an individual resident, employee, or relative.

6. Family Businesses and Local Reputation

A Spring 1991 investigative profile portrayed DeLaurentis as a recognizable figure around Island Lake and noted family interests in a bowling alley, liquor store, and formerly a pizza establishment. The account emphasized how ordinary commercial activity and public sociability could coexist with law-enforcement suspicions. The documentary status of each enterprise differs: historical newspaper testimony of a business relationship is not the same thing as proving the business was itself a criminal instrument. [2]

Federal forfeiture records later addressed property interests and the rights of relatives, illustrating another important distinction. An enterprise might be associated with a person convicted of racketeering without every asset, employee, family member, or transaction being proven criminal. Asset ownership and third-party claims required separate judicial analysis. [5]

7. The Public Persona of a Tradesman

In interviews recalled by ABC7 Chicago, DeLaurentis described himself as a bricklayer and part-time gambler and denied knowledge of a formal Mafia organization. A 2018 interview also reported his assertion that he worked in carpet cleaning. Such remarks form part of the evidence of how he presented himself to the public; they do not erase the record of his federal conviction. [7]

The difference between a self-description and a judicial finding should be explicit. A defendant may accurately describe a trade and still be convicted of other activities; equally, a journalist’s suspicion cannot transform every described commercial activity into racketeering.

8. The Chicago Outfit after the Capone Era

The organization associated with Al Capone did not disappear after Prohibition. By the 1970s and 1980s federal cases described a network of street crews managing territorial and revenue relationships. The Seventh Circuit characterized these crews as subdivisions of the Outfit and identified the Ferriola Street Crew as one such unit. [4]

That institutional framing is more useful than imposing a simplified single-boss chronology. Leadership authority could be distributed by crew, geography, commercial specialty, and seniority. Publicly available succession charts sometimes make cleaner claims than the underlying evidence permits.

9. Joseph Ferriola and the Crew Name

Federal appellate judges identified Joseph Ferriola as the leader of the crew bearing his name from 1979 until his death in 1989. The crew’s organization and activities extended beyond Ferriola’s lifetime; its name remained a shorthand for a charged criminal enterprise. Rocco Infelise then succeeded him in the crew leadership as described in the appellate record. [4]

DeLaurentis’s connection to that network was not merely an inference from press photographs. He was a named defendant in the federal racketeering prosecution and his conviction was addressed in subsequent published judgments. The stronger judicial link should take precedence over retrospective assertions about initiation ceremonies or exact mafia rank, which are less securely documented.

10. Rocco Infelise and Operational Authority

Rocco E. Infelise is a central figure in the court record. The Ferriola crew’s collectors, gambling arrangements, and extortion practices were described with reference to his direction. The federal case filed as 90 CR 87 placed DeLaurentis alongside Infelise and others charged with participation in the enterprise. [3] [4]

Co-defendant status does not alone establish equal rank. The trial and appeals specify organizational relationships and adjudicated offenses, but they do not establish the precise day-to-day instructions behind every reported transaction. Claims that DeLaurentis was simultaneously a county boss, a crew captain, and the supreme Outfit leader should be dated and source-qualified rather than fused into one timeless title.

11. Contemporary Crime-Commission Assessment

A Chicago Crime Commission report published in conjunction with its seventieth anniversary identified DeLaurentis as a long-time associate of Ferriola, Sam Carlisi, Infelise, Louis Marino, Wayne Bock, and Joe Amato, and assigned him senior organized-crime authority in Lake and McHenry counties. Its description predates the principal federal conviction and is valuable evidence of contemporary surveillance-era assessment. [1]

Nevertheless, this was an investigative organization’s published assessment, not a criminal verdict or an authenticated internal Outfit promotion record. The Commission’s document was later digitized and hosted by the U.S. Department of Justice; the federal hosting platform does not convert the Commission’s opinions into an independent Department of Justice finding.

12. The Role of Regional Businesses

The criminal enterprise targeted revenue generated by bookmakers and other illegal operators and sometimes used socially ordinary venues as contact points. Federal judges detailed recurring payments and pressure on those who resisted collection demands. For a historical reader, the distinction between the place where a meeting occurred and ownership or participation in a racketeering enterprise is indispensable. [4]

Building a complete property and business map would require deeds, licenses, corporate filings, tax returns, and trial exhibits beyond those directly reviewed. This account therefore does not claim that every locality named in contemporary reporting was a confirmed site of extortion or a venue controlled by DeLaurentis.

13. Illegal Bookmaking and Street Tax

The crew’s core revenue practice was the extraction of a recurring percentage or fixed payment from bookmakers who operated outside the Outfit’s immediate control. The Seventh Circuit used the familiar phrase “street tax” for these demands and explained why refusal could expose operators to intimidation. [4]

Unlike a government tax or legitimate commercial royalty, these payments were enforced by an unlawful threat environment. The economic model helps explain why federal investigators could build RICO cases from repeated conduct across multiple independent businesses rather than from a single robbery or gambling offense.

14. Extortion as a Continuing Enterprise

Extortion in the Ferriola case was not described only as a dramatic physical confrontation. Its more important organizational feature was predictability: a feared name, territorial expectation, recurring collections, and implied consequences for nonpayment. The crime could be sustained through repetition and reputation. [4]

A rigorous biography need not reproduce operational instructions. The historically significant fact is that an imposed share of private profits became a mechanism of control over independent actors, producing financial gain and discouraging competition.

15. Loansharking and Unlawful Debts

The appellate opinion also describes usury and efforts to collect unlawful debts. Such activity could interact with gambling, since borrowers involved in cash-based businesses were vulnerable to high-pressure collection. RICO’s definition of enterprise activity enabled prosecutors to present these matters within a wider pattern instead of treating each loan as an isolated offense. [4]

The existence of a crew-wide loan-sharking practice does not establish that DeLaurentis personally originated every loan listed in trial evidence. When the record describes the enterprise generally, the profile identifies it as enterprise conduct rather than falsely attributing each act to him.

16. Bribery and Corrupt Protection

The Seventh Circuit described bribery of officials among the crew’s criminal activities. Protection by corrupted public employees can weaken ordinary law enforcement and create the appearance that extortion is inevitable. The case also involved evidence of official relationships and jury or witness-related manipulation by some associates. [4] [10]

Such evidence must be attributed carefully. A crew-wide finding concerning bribery cannot be transferred automatically to every named defendant or every police officer who served in its territory. Individual guilt, official misconduct, and employer responsibility remain separate questions.

17. Violence and the Credibility of Threats

Threats of violence gave the protection racket its leverage. The Hal Smith murder, which became a central feature of investigative narratives, served as a stark example in public accounts of the era. Prosecutors used the atmosphere of intimidation to explain why bookmakers paid, cooperated, fled, or feared retaliation. [4] [9]

The law requires precision, however. An individual may be convicted of racketeering that relies on intimidation without being convicted of every killing alleged during the prosecution. The disputed verdict concerning DeLaurentis and Hal Smith must therefore be reported separately.

18. The Hal Smith Case: Historical Setting

Hal Smith was a suburban bookmaker whose refusal to meet organized-crime demands became part of the federal investigation. The violence against him in 1985 and the discovery of his body were major elements of the story later recounted by ABC7 Chicago. That journalism provides important contextual reporting but does not supersede the adjudicated result as to each defendant. [9]

Federal appellate findings place the killing within the broader criminal environment of the crew, yet the trial record does not support reducing every allegation about its planning to an established personal conviction of DeLaurentis. This distinction is central rather than technical.

19. Threats Described in Later Reporting

In a 2020 retrospective on the Smith case, ABC7 reported claims that DeLaurentis demanded a recurring payment and warned Smith of the consequences of refusing. Those descriptions were attributed to federal authorities and the historical investigation. [9]

The biography presents this as reported prosecution-era evidence and investigative narrative, not as proof that the subject was convicted of the separate Smith murder conspiracy. Contemporary accusation, testimony, jury verdict, and a later judgment are distinct evidentiary stages.

20. The Role of Cooperating Witnesses

One of the people central to the prosecution’s evidence was cooperating associate B. J. Jahoda. The appellate decision discusses testimony and recordings that provided jurors with an unusual view of the crew’s conversations and conduct. The 2020 ABC7 retrospective also describes his cooperation as essential to the successful prosecution. [4] [9]

Cooperator evidence is not automatically either true or false. Its strength depends upon corroboration, recording integrity, credibility assessment, and the opportunity for adversarial cross-examination. The appellate court addressed multiple claims about the management of evidence and trial procedure.

21. Surveillance and Recorded Conversations

Federal investigators assembled a case using conversations, witnesses, financial traces, and other evidence of enterprise behavior. In later reporting, hundreds of conversations were said to have been recorded during the investigation. Such recordings can preserve direct speech but still require identification, contextual interpretation, and admissibility decisions. [9]

An encyclopedia should avoid treating a short quotation or nickname as a complete confession. The court record, rather than selective excerpts, determines what was admitted, contested, proved, and ultimately sustained.

22. The February 1990 Indictments

Court records identify the federal case as No. 90 CR 87, and related reporting describes a large indictment of Ferriola-crew associates in February 1990. That date is important: some online summaries incorrectly shift the major indictment into 1993, confusing the prosecution with sentencing and post-verdict decisions. [3] [9]

The indictment combined the enterprise theory with individual charges, requiring a sprawling trial and detailed verdict forms. Allegation at indictment is only the prosecution’s accusation; the later jury decisions and judicial modifications supply the appropriate final status of each count.

23. RICO and the Enterprise Theory

The Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act allowed prosecutors to charge a pattern of related criminal activity undertaken through an enterprise. The Ferriola Street Crew was treated as an Outfit component rather than a fictitious business used merely as a label. The Seventh Circuit’s explanation remains among the clearest published descriptions of that structure. [4]

A racketeering conviction can encompass multiple kinds of conduct and does not require an identical personal act by every participant. Yet courts still must determine each defendant’s liability and evaluate the precise verdict on each independent charge.

24. Trial Duration and Complexity

Jury selection began on October 23, 1991, according to Judge Ann Claire Williams’s published decision. Closing arguments finished March 2, 1992, after a lengthy multi-defendant trial, and deliberations followed. The complexity of the case became legally significant when one verdict entry later proved unreliable. [3]

This was not a simple single-defendant trial in which an apparent inconsistency could be resolved by glancing at one form. It involved numerous counts, multiple defendants, and complex findings about distinct racketeering acts. That structure shaped the post-verdict litigation.

25. March 10, 1992: The Principal Verdict

On March 10, 1992, jurors returned verdicts finding DeLaurentis guilty on multiple charges, including racketeering-related counts and tax-related allegations. The published district court opinion lists the counts and confirms that he was found not guilty on Count 3. The court also recorded the inconsistent treatment of the allegation involving Hal Smith. [3]

The accurate summary is neither that he was acquitted of the entire indictment nor that he was convicted of every charge. The criminal judgment rested on numerous sustained findings while the separate Smith conspiracy count required correction.

26. Racketeering Act 17(a): The Jury’s Finding

Within Count One the jury was asked whether DeLaurentis had agreed to a conspiracy to murder Hal Smith, identified as Racketeering Act 17(a). The answer entered on that predicate was that the alleged agreement had not been proved against him. [3]

This finding cannot be overlooked simply because other parts of the racketeering verdict were adverse to him. It records the jury’s express rejection of one particular alleged racketeering act, under the structure of the verdict form.

27. Count Eight: The Apparent Contradiction

The same alleged conspiracy appeared separately as Count Eight. Although the verdict form initially marked DeLaurentis guilty on that count, it conflicted with the finding on Racketeering Act 17(a), which depended on substantially the same underlying facts. The trial judge described this as a serious problem requiring inquiry into whether the form accurately expressed the jury’s verdict. [3]

An inconsistency in itself does not always invalidate a criminal verdict. Here the judge confronted an additional issue: specific evidence that jurors might have marked the form in error. The eventual ruling turned on confidence in the recorded verdict rather than an abstract rule requiring perfectly consistent verdicts.

28. The Juror Inquiry

Following the trial, the court interviewed jurors under controlled conditions. The January 1993 opinion reports that three jurors said a guilty finding on Count Eight was correct, three recalled a not-guilty verdict, one remembered a nonunanimous vote, and five recalled no vote or could not remember. These responses convinced the court that the checked guilty finding did not reliably represent a unanimous decision. [3]

The inquiry was legally sensitive because Federal Rule of Evidence 606(b) limits probing the jury’s deliberative process. Judge Williams sought to verify the existence and accuracy of the verdict without conducting an unrestricted inquiry into private deliberations.

29. January 14, 1993: Count Eight Vacated

Judge Williams granted DeLaurentis’s motion to vacate the guilty verdict on Count Eight and declared a mistrial on that count. She did not set aside the rest of the verdict against him. That is the controlling disposition of the separate Hal Smith conspiracy charge in this reviewed district-court record. [3]

The ruling is not properly paraphrased as “he was convicted of murdering Hal Smith,” nor as “the entire prosecution was overturned.” It removed one discrete count while leaving an extensive racketeering conviction intact.

30. The Difference Between Murder and Racketeering Allegations

The Smith case shows why classifications matter. A murder, a conspiracy to murder, and participation in an enterprise containing violent members are different legal propositions. Police theories, indictment language, cooperating-witness accounts, jury findings, and final judicial dispositions must each be attributed to their own source. [3] [4]

For MGU’s biographical standards, the independent conspiracy verdict cannot be counted as a final conviction after the January 1993 order. At the same time, the order provides no basis to erase the separate convictions or the appellate description of the enterprise.

31. Conviction on Tax-Related Counts

The appellate decision describes tax counts among the charges arising out of the crew prosecution, as well as a subsequent dispute over instructions concerning materiality. In 1996 the Seventh Circuit did not grant defendants a new trial on those counts, explaining the issue had not been preserved and did not amount to reversible plain error under the circumstances. [4]

Popular summaries that list “tax fraud” alongside RICO and extortion are directionally consistent with the press account, but the preferred legal formulation should remain tied to the precise charged counts and appellate holding rather than an invented standalone conviction date.

32. Initial Sentencing and Its Scale

The Seventh Circuit recorded a sentence of 222 months for DeLaurentis—18 years and six months—following the 1992 verdict. It also noted the district court’s calculation of a base offense level and the upward departure based on the number of criminal acts attributed to his role in the enterprise. [4]

This figure describes the sentence reviewed on appeal. It should not be presented as the unaltered final term after appellate remand, because the same opinion ordered resentencing. The final post-remand sentencing docket was not independently collated for this edition.

33. The 1996 Appeal: United States v. DiDomenico

On March 1, 1996, the Seventh Circuit decided the major consolidated appeal, later recording denial of rehearing requests on May 23. The published opinion, United States v. DiDomenico, 78 F.3d 294, broadly affirmed the judgments while identifying a sentencing error affecting DeLaurentis. [4]

An appeal’s caption can obscure the individual subject because the lead appellant was William DiDomenico. Researchers must not interpret the absence of DeLaurentis’s name from the opinion title as proof he was not a party. The opinion expressly discusses him and his sentence.

34. Why Resentencing Was Required

The court considered whether the sentencing judge properly used a guidelines grouping provision to justify an upward adjustment for additional crimes. The problem was that the calculation counted unconvicted, unstipulated offenses in a mechanism reserved for convicted or stipulated crimes. This did not invalidate the underlying racketeering conviction; it required the district judge to revisit the sentence. [4]

The appellate opinion also recognized that other possible legal grounds for departure might exist. For an accurate summary, “sentence reversed and remanded” is narrower than “conviction reversed,” and “resentencing ordered” does not automatically establish a particular new term.

35. The Jail Meeting-Room Recording Dispute

The appeal included a serious claim that a confidential attorney-client meeting room in Chicago’s federal jail had been secretly recorded. The FBI inquiry was inconclusive about the tape’s origin. The defendants argued that illicit access to defense discussions might have undermined fairness; the appellate court examined the issue at length. [4]

This procedural controversy helps explain the breadth of the appeal. It does not establish that prosecutors themselves planted the recorder, or that every conversation between defendants and lawyers was intercepted. The historical account must retain the investigation’s uncertainty.

36. The Limits of the Appellate Result

The 1996 appellate opinion concludes that DeLaurentis was entitled to resentencing and that, apart from that exception, the judgments were affirmed. It therefore supplies strong evidence for the surviving conviction even while offering a concrete correction to the length-of-sentence narrative. [4]

Later accounts of his years in federal custody should be read against that chronology. A reported release date can describe his actual confinement without proving the precise post-remand sentence when the final docket or Bureau of Prisons records have not been collated.

37. The Criminal Forfeiture Proceeding

The federal racketeering case also produced criminal forfeiture litigation. Judge Williams’s August 23, 1996 opinion, 938 F. Supp. 1352, addressed property claimed by DeLaurentis, Infelise, and third-party family members. The government sought to enforce the forfeiture authorized by RICO. [5]

The distinction between forfeiture and conventional criminal sentencing is important. Forfeiture addresses interests in property or proceeds associated with criminal enterprise conduct; it does not turn every item in a family’s possession into proven contraband or all of its members into criminal defendants.

38. The Three-Million-Dollar Joint Liability

As described in the forfeiture decision, the jury found DeLaurentis and Infelise jointly and severally liable for $3 million in proceeds from racketeering activity. This was a legal amount attached to the case; it is not a verified statement that DeLaurentis personally held three million dollars in liquid wealth. [5] [11]

The court considered substitute assets when proceeds could not be located. The mechanics demonstrate the importance of distinguishing jointly imposed financial liability, personally owned property, and disputed third-party property interests.

39. Real Estate and Third-Party Rights

The 1996 forfeiture decision examined interests in real property located in suburban Illinois as well as banking assets. Relatives asserted interests arising from arrangements and trusts, and the court considered the applicable statutory and state-law questions. [5]

The fact that relatives appeared in forfeiture litigation does not by itself establish that they participated in criminal activities. Judicial decisions about who legally owned an asset must be reported apart from any implication of family guilt. The page omits historical street addresses that do not help identify the legal holdings.

40. Banking and Substitute Assets

The forfeiture litigation discussed funds in bank accounts and proceeds obtained through asset disposition. The government sought to satisfy the criminal forfeiture judgment from substitute assets when directly traceable proceeds were unavailable. These orders followed legal rules governing ownership, priority, and the effect of racketeering convictions. [5]

A historical account should not convert accounting exhibits into a coherent personal balance sheet unless all parties, liens, ownership interests, and later transactions have been reconciled. The record documents financial remedies, not a comprehensive audit of the defendant’s lifetime assets.

41. A Later Appellate Forfeiture Ruling

The Seventh Circuit returned to related forfeiture questions in United States v. Infelise, 159 F.3d 300 (1998), addressing contested substitute assets associated with Infelise and claims by his wife. The court recounted DeLaurentis’s joint liability for the $3 million forfeiture but the appeal’s particular contested interests concerned Infelise-related property. [11]

The 1998 opinion is corroborative of the overall forfeiture framework, not a new independent criminal conviction of DeLaurentis. Its proper place in the chronology is as later judicial interpretation of the proceeds and property orders.

42. Media Attention after Sentencing

The subject became a familiar name in Chicago organized-crime reporting during the 1990s. His manner in interviews—presenting himself as a tradesman or gambler rather than an organized-crime executive—created a vivid contrast with the federal verdict. ABC7 preserved examples of that public self-description years later. [7]

This type of media narrative can tempt historians to equate an engaging public persona with an exculpatory story or with a theatrical criminal caricature. Neither shortcut is adequate. The strongest points remain adjudicated offenses and attributable statements.

43. Reported Release in 2006

ABC7 Chicago reported that DeLaurentis was released from federal prison in 2006 following a long sentence. The reported release does not independently establish the details of the sentence imposed on remand after the 1996 appellate decision, which is not reconstructed here from a full prison or sentencing file. [7]

That release nevertheless represents a clear historical transition: subsequent coverage concerns suspected influence, public contacts, and leadership speculation, not incarceration under the early-1990s Ferriola-crew judgment.

44. A Changed Chicago Outfit

By the 2000s and 2010s the Chicago Outfit operated in a markedly different investigative environment from its Prohibition-era counterpart. Extended federal racketeering prosecutions and sustained imprisonment of senior figures altered its public profile. Historians caution against simply transferring titles or organizational forms from Capone’s Chicago to later decades. [8] [10]

The Outfit’s continued existence in law-enforcement assessments does not establish that all alleged senior officers held unchanged authority year to year. Personnel, health, territorial relationships, and secrecy make such claims difficult to verify from outside the organization.

45. Connections to Samuel Carlisi

The Chicago Crime Commission grouped DeLaurentis with Samuel Carlisi in a late-1980s assessment. Later federal rulings involving the Carlisi Street Crew show the larger organization could contain multiple overlapping networks without a single street-crew case encompassing every alleged Outfit operation. [1] [12]

This connection is therefore best described as a contemporaneous association recorded by investigators, not a complete documentary chain of command from Carlisi to DeLaurentis. The Carlisi and Ferriola prosecutions are distinct and should remain separate in the encyclopedia.

46. Connections to Joseph Ferriola

Ferriola appears in both the Commission’s associated-figure listing and the judicial description of the street crew’s history. Unlike an unverified nickname association, these sources supply complementary kinds of evidence: a contemporary identification and a legal account of a criminal organization. [1] [4]

Yet even agreement between two sources on association does not establish the exact date of induction, rank, financial arrangement, or degree of independent territorial authority. Those questions would require other records.

47. Connections to Rocco Infelise and Louis Marino

Infelise and Louis Marino were principal names in the same federal prosecution, and the Commission report separately identified them among DeLaurentis’s associates. The overlap provides a strong basis for describing an organized network. [1] [4]

It does not mean every co-defendant committed every predicate act. The opinion differentiates individual verdicts, and this profile maintains that differentiation when discussing the Smith count and other violent conduct.

48. The 2018 Succession Question

John DiFronzo’s death in 2018 prompted Chicago investigative reporters to revisit who might lead the surviving Outfit. ABC7 identified DeLaurentis as a prominent unincarcerated figure and described him as considered a consigliere. The same report said succession was difficult to establish because the organization did not issue public announcements. [7]

For publication purposes, “reported consigliere in 2018” is defensible; “officially elected boss in 2018” is not independently established by that report. Apparent succession timelines are retrospective models and should always disclose their source and confidence level.

49. Public Denial of Present-Day Criminal Activity

In 2018 DeLaurentis rejected suggestions that he was the boss or currently engaged in criminal rackets, and described ordinary commercial work. His denial is part of the documented public record and should be presented fairly. [7]

The denial does not undermine court-established conduct from decades earlier, just as those older convictions do not prove any particular new offense after his release. The relevant question for each period is what the available evidence actually demonstrates.

50. Law-Enforcement View of the Outfit’s Resilience

The 2018 ABC7 report quoted the Chicago FBI office’s view that reduced public violence did not prove organized crime had ceased to pose a threat. The analysis focused on the possibility of financial crime and adaptive organizational behavior rather than a constant cycle of killings. [7]

This is an institutional perspective about the broader Outfit. It should not be converted into an assertion that the FBI publicly announced a new specific criminal charge or appointed DeLaurentis to a formal rank.

51. The 2020 Retrospective on Hal Smith

ABC7’s 2020 report revisited the Smith murder and the years-long federal investigation, describing DeLaurentis as a former north-suburban gambling authority and summarizing federal allegations about threats made to Smith. It connected the prosecution to later efforts against the Chicago Outfit. [9]

The reader must distinguish this retrospective account from the 1993 district court’s direct legal disposition: a disputed Smith murder-conspiracy verdict against DeLaurentis was vacated. The two sources describe different evidentiary questions and neither should be silenced.

52. The 2022 Assessment of Senior Leadership

An ABC7 investigation marking the anniversary of Al Capone’s death described an elderly DeLaurentis as considered the current leader of the organization by investigators. Its wider discussion noted the lack of modern public organizational charts and the difficulty of maintaining precise intelligence. [8]

This is evidence of contemporary attribution, not proof of an uncontested, formally documented office. The description is properly dated to 2022 rather than presented as an independently verified situation for every succeeding year.

53. The Mob Museum’s 2023 Commentary

A 2023 Mob Museum interview described DeLaurentis as said to be the boss and noted reports of declining health, while suggesting that other figures might handle more of the daily activity. The commentary itself signaled uncertainty through qualifying language. [6]

An interpretive museum article and a federal appellate holding have different strengths. The museum is valuable for contextualizing organized-crime historiography and competing reported leadership roles; it is not a court adjudication or first-hand certified health record.

54. Recent Claims Through 2026

An online leadership timeline reviewed in October 2026 names DeLaurentis as reported boss following DiFronzo. Such compilations may serve as guides to modern popular understanding, but the underlying leadership arrangement remains difficult to independently establish. [13]

This profile does not state as fact that he personally directs every present Outfit crew, that he remains in full health, or that he has been charged with any new offense. No authenticated November 2026 leadership appointment or medical record has been reviewed.

55. Consigliere, Boss, and Street Boss: Competing Labels

Post-2018 coverage places different emphasis on adviser status, overall leadership, and operational delegation. Those titles are not interchangeable, especially when attributed by journalists rather than by an authenticated organizational document. [6] [7] [8]

The biography records titles as “reported” and identifies the source year. The same person can be described differently by reporters using confidential sources at different times, and organizational influence can exist without a publicly demonstrable office.

56. Reported Delegation and Other Senior Figures

In 2023 commentary, Albert Vena was named as someone observers believed may have handled day-to-day activities while DeLaurentis was described as an aging reputed leader. This is an outside assessment and should not be recast as a validated chain of command. [6]

Publishing a hierarchy that reads like a government organizational chart would overstate what can be known about a covert enterprise. The encyclopedia privileges specific court-established relationships while presenting recent reported roles as contestable.

57. Organizational Influence versus Criminal Conviction

A person’s reputation for criminal leadership may persist after release from prison, but a historical conviction does not establish the elements of a new crime committed decades later. DeLaurentis’s post-2006 profile must be read with that distinction constantly in view. [7] [8]

The 1992 conviction is a final adjudicated event subject to the described sentencing remand. The claim that he occupied a senior post in 2018 or 2022 is an attributed assessment. Neither type of evidence can substitute for the other.

58. The Language of Organized-Crime Reporting

Nicknames and evocative expressions can convey subcultural history while encouraging exaggeration. In DeLaurentis’s coverage, a chilling phrase associated with bodies found in vehicle trunks became a recurring journalistic motif. [7] [9]

For an academic biography, dramatic phrasing must not become a substitute for facts about the Smith case, nor should an interview fragment stand in for the final legal record. The meaningful story concerns how threats, fear, and illegal taxation were alleged and proved within a criminal enterprise.

59. Local Memory and Conflicting Reputations

Contemporary local reporting described DeLaurentis as affable and industrious in ordinary public settings, while federal prosecution portrayed him as connected to a coercive criminal network. Such accounts can coexist because social reputation and criminal adjudication address different aspects of a person’s life. [2] [4]

Historical method requires both voices to be framed correctly. Respectable conduct in one transaction does not disprove wrongdoing elsewhere, and documented wrongdoing does not justify accusing every associate or local business of participation.

60. Business Records and the Forfeiture Problem

The 1996 property ruling provides an unusual window into the difficulty of following ownership through families and commercial properties. The judge addressed competing claims and the interpretation of property-law rules rather than treating a criminal defendant’s name on a document as the complete answer. [5]

The research opportunity is substantial: combining deeds, probate materials, trial exhibits, and the court’s stipulations could yield a more granular economic biography. Such reconstruction lies beyond this initial public-source edition.

61. The Historical Significance of the Infelise Prosecution

The prosecution helped establish a detailed judicial record of the Outfit’s north-suburban street-crew model. The case shows how federal law enforcement shifted from investigating isolated violent incidents to demonstrating an enterprise sustained by multiple revenue schemes and threats. [4]

It also became a substantial appellate case about jury integrity, lawyer confidentiality, tax jury instructions, sentencing methodology, and the permissible scope of post-trial inquiry. These procedural issues are part of the history, not legal trivia to be discarded.

62. Legal Importance of the Count-Eight Correction

The correction to Count Eight illustrates how a criminal system can produce a verdict-form error even in a lengthy, closely managed trial. Judge Williams examined whether jurors had truly agreed, rather than simply assuming that an apparent check mark conclusively represented the required unanimity. [3]

For writers, the consequence is straightforward: this particular count was vacated. A responsible biography makes that outcome immediately accessible to readers who may otherwise encounter a list of initial jury findings stripped of their procedural aftermath.

63. Legal Importance of the Sentencing Remand

The 1996 appeal demonstrates that an upheld conviction may coexist with a partially successful appeal. DeLaurentis prevailed on the technical method used to adjust his sentence, without obtaining reversal of the main judgment. [4]

The distinction supports a disciplined crime-encyclopedia method: identify trial conviction, appellate disposition, any remand, and any later resentencing with separate citations. Do not collapse the entire process into “appeal denied” or “case overturned.”

64. Financial Responsibility and Collective Proceeds

The court-approved forfeiture structure included joint-and-several liability in a multimillion-dollar amount, illustrating the financial reach of a racketeering case. It also demonstrated the government’s ability to pursue substitute assets and the need to adjudicate third-party property claims. [5] [11]

For historical interpretation, these remedies reveal how enterprise crime could be translated into recoverable monetary judgments. They do not provide a clean ledger of personal net worth or annual income.

65. Sources of Evidence: Courts and Media

This biography deliberately favors published court opinions for convictions and legal outcomes, contemporaneous crime-commission records for what investigators publicly believed at the time, and dated journalism for interviews and later leadership speculation. Each source type answers a different question. [1] [3] [4] [7]

A printed report reproduced by a government digital archive is still authored by the originating commission. Likewise, a modern blog republishing an interview is not an independent witness. Recognizing such provenance prevents evidentiary double counting.

66. Namesakes and False Matches

The subject must not be conflated with other people whose surnames resemble DeLaurentis, including unrelated defendants in different federal circuits. In this biography the primary identifier is Salvatore V. DeLaurentis, the defendant in Northern District of Illinois case 90 CR 87. [3] [5]

Court case identifiers, nicknames, and place context provide better disambiguation than a surname-only internet search. Even within the same family, the forfeiture opinion explicitly differentiates Salvatore V. from another relative with a similar name.

67. Limits of Personal Biography

The accessible records are rich in legal history but comparatively thin on early schooling, family immigration history, private education, religious life, and daily routines. A fuller biography would require authenticated civil certificates and interviews, not just recopied popular profiles. [1] [2]

The page therefore does not manufacture early-life scenes or psychological explanations to fill gaps. The absence of evidence is disclosed rather than disguised behind conventional Mafia storytelling.

68. Limits of Current Status Information

No independently authenticated November 2026 notice of death or definitive current organizational chart was located in the source set used for this edition. Consequently, this page avoids presenting a precise living-status determination or certain current office where the record supports only earlier reported assessments. [6] [8] [13]

The question of who commands a clandestine group is intrinsically difficult, but that difficulty should not license categorical guessing. Later corrections should cite dated sources of comparable authority and should distinguish changes in evidence from changes in the underlying organization.

69. Historical Assessment

Salvatore DeLaurentis is an instructive figure in the post-Capone history of the Chicago Outfit: a north-suburban racketeering defendant whose documented career intersects with evolving illegal gambling markets, the Ferriola crew, a large federal conspiracy prosecution, significant forfeiture litigation, and decades of disputed leadership reporting. [3] [4] [5]

His case is particularly valuable because the record resists a simplified narrative. The convicted racketeering participant was also a successful appellant on the method of sentencing and obtained vacatur of a separate, serious murder-conspiracy count; he later publicly denied organized-crime leadership even as investigators continued to attribute influence to him. A serious encyclopedia retains all of those facts without treating contradiction as an editorial inconvenience.

Documentary Research Windows

These are attributed editorial source-study exercises, not original witness transcripts or facsimile-collated quotations. Consult the linked primary texts for exact language and courtroom exhibits.

Documentary Window 01: Chicago Crime Commission, c. 1989–1990

Read the “Organized Crime Spotlight” in its original document context. Separate investigator identification and reported county authority from a judicial finding. Record the Commission’s stated 1938 birth date and note that the archive host is a reproducing agency. [1]

Documentary Window 02: IPSN Spring 1991 local profile

Compare descriptions of businesses, work habits, and community familiarity against the crimes charged later. Classify each as witnessed observation, attributed report, or interpretive judgment; never infer that all businesses were criminal. [2]

Documentary Window 03: United States v. Infelise, 813 F. Supp. 599

Build a three-column worksheet showing the jury’s Count One predicate, its marked Count Eight response, and the court’s January 1993 disposition. The critical final entry is “vacated and mistrial declared,” not “convicted of conspiracy to murder.” [3]

Documentary Window 04: Juror recollections and Rule 606(b)

Trace the judge’s limited inquiry into whether the verdict form represented an actual unanimous finding. Distinguish validating the verdict from probing the substance of jury deliberations. [3]

Documentary Window 05: United States v. DiDomenico, 78 F.3d 294

Extract the crew’s territorial business model, the surviving convictions, and the disposition of each appellate issue. Record the date of decision separately from rehearing denial. [4]

Documentary Window 06: The 222-month sentence and guidelines dispute

Read the appellate sentencing discussion. Note that the court invalidated a counting methodology and ordered remand, without reversing guilt. Do not guess the final adjusted term. [4]

Documentary Window 07: Attorney-client meeting-room controversy

Identify what the court knew about the illicit recording, what the investigation did not determine, and why that uncertainty mattered. Avoid attributing the recording to an actor without evidence. [4]

Documentary Window 08: United States v. Infelise, 938 F. Supp. 1352

Map forfeiture of proceeds and substitute assets while protecting the distinction between a defendant’s interest and claims by family members. A joint judgment is not a personal net-worth calculation. [5]

Documentary Window 09: United States v. Infelise, 159 F.3d 300

Compare the later appeal with the 1996 property ruling, paying attention to which party owned which contested item and why the opinion mentions DeLaurentis. [11]

Documentary Window 10: ABC7 June 2018 television investigation

Compare the network’s reported consigliere attribution with DeLaurentis’s own denial. Both are historically relevant but neither settles an official organizational appointment. [7]

Documentary Window 11: ABC7 2022 and Mob Museum 2023 assessments

Mark the year of each account and whether a statement is attributed to investigators, an unnamed source, or a commentator. The distinction is crucial for contemporary leadership claims. [8]

Documentary Window 12: 2026 leadership timelines and future updates

Treat a secondary succession chart as a hypothesis requiring corroboration. Before revising the profile’s present tense, seek dated official findings, reliable investigative reporting, or independently authenticated vital notices. [13]

Research Limitations

The principal court opinions were reviewed as published reproductions of judicial text. The Chicago Crime Commission’s original page was also visually inspected in its scanned form. The entire trial transcript, original jury forms, the complete 1996 sentencing remand docket, full Bureau of Prisons inmate record, all family-property title exhibits, certified birth record, and a current law-enforcement organizational assessment have not been independently collated.

Accordingly, the biography does not claim an authenticated 2026 internal rank, a verified specific post-remand prison term, an independent determination of current health, or a proved role in the separate Hal Smith murder-conspiracy count after the 1993 vacatur. Later media accounts of criminal leadership are attributed and dated, not transmuted into fresh convictions.
