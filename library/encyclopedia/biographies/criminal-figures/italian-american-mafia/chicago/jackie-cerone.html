1. Historical Overview

John “Jackie” Cerone was a senior figure in the Chicago Outfit whose career connected postwar Chicago bookmaking to the intercity casino-skimming schemes exposed by federal prosecutors in the 1980s. His name appears in two unusually detailed sets of federal appellate opinions: the early-1970s prosecution of an interstate gambling conspiracy and the later Las Vegas casino case. Those opinions establish not only the legal outcomes but also the networks of intermediaries, business arrangements and government witnesses through which the organization operated.

The prevailing shorthand that Cerone was simply “the boss” conceals shifting authority. At the 1983 Senate hearing a former FBI official depicted him as a major operational decision-maker; the 1987 casino appeal described Joseph Aiuppa as Chicago boss and Cerone as underboss. Those descriptions are evidence of roles at particular moments, not a single formally certified succession chart. A research biography must connect leadership claims to dated testimony and separate proven convictions from attributed allegations. [2] [3] [9]

2. Name Variants and Documentary Identification

Cerone is identified by the public name Jackie, the nickname “Jackie the Lackey,” and multiple versions of his given names. The 1971 Seventh Circuit caption prints John Philip Cerone, Sr.; the 1987 Eighth Circuit caption gives John Peter Cerone; and the 1988 caption again uses John Philip Cerone. These are archival spellings of the defendant as presented in separate published proceedings, not a license to assume that every similarly named relative was a defendant in those cases.

Some biographical listings present John Philip Cerone Jr. and a July 7, 1914 birth. An indexed National Archives document also records that date, but it is not a certified civil birth record. The encyclopedia gives 1914 as a sourced working year and flags the name discrepancies rather than manufacturing certainty where the primary records disagree in their captions. [2] [9] [10] [15]

3. Family Background and Chicago Childhood

Cerone is conventionally described as born in Chicago to an Italian immigrant family and as the younger brother of Frank Cerone. Family-oriented narratives supply additional personal details, including his marriage to Clara and his children, but original county birth, marriage and probate certificates were not collated for this edition. Those details are presented as reported biography, not independent proof of organizational roles.

His trajectory belongs to a Chicago environment in which family businesses, taverns, transport networks and neighborhood gambling overlapped. This does not mean that every relative or acquaintance participated in illegal activity. The distinction is particularly important because later reporting about his son’s legal career and union-related relationships concerns another person with a similar name. [12] [15] [16]

4. The Chicago Outfit after Prohibition

The Chicago organization associated with Johnny Torrio and Al Capone survived the repeal of Prohibition by reallocating attention toward gambling, illegal wagering, labor influence and other revenue streams. Senior members could be publicly notorious while the practical management of a particular bookmaking district remained dispersed among local operators. Cerone’s later rise developed within this layered system rather than from a simple takeover of one undivided “mob company.”

Federal authorities used terms such as organized crime group, hierarchy, boss and lieutenant to describe relationships that were not registered in public corporate filings. The terms are analytically useful when tied to investigators’ evidence, but they should not obscure the difference between formal legal ownership, nominal business employment and illicit control. [3] [4] [13]

5. Accardo and the Senior Leadership Circle

Biographical accounts associate Cerone with longtime Outfit leader Tony Accardo and describe service as a chauffeur or close aide. This is consistent with the high-level working relationship attributed to the pair in the 1983 Senate hearing. The historical significance of this association lies not in a picturesque driver story but in repeated access to leadership discussions, an opportunity to establish trust and a channel for delegated instructions.

The Senate transcript’s narrative distinguishes Accardo as a veteran strategic authority, Aiuppa as a senior leader and Cerone as a manager of the day-to-day business. These judgments were given by named witnesses and were not findings in a trial against Accardo. They nevertheless illuminate the relationship between personal confidence and operational influence in clandestine organizations. [3] [4] [17]

6. The Problem with a Simple Succession List

Popular mafia reference charts sometimes give Cerone a brief separate term as boss in the late 1960s and depict Aiuppa as succeeding him. Former FBI agent William Roemer told senators that Cerone headed operations for several years and then was removed from active command by imprisonment. Such a narrative is plausible as an account of changing operational power, but the exact dates and boundaries of formal title remain interpretive.

The 1987 appellate judgment, by contrast, introduced the Las Vegas prosecution by saying that Aiuppa was boss and Cerone underboss. The two accounts need not be contradictory: they describe different periods and different dimensions of authority. This page therefore gives period-specific characterizations rather than an unqualified “boss, 1966–1986” designation. [3] [9]

7. Bookmaking as Organized Business

Illegal sports wagering required more than collecting bets. The 1971 federal opinion records betting sheets, odds lines, telephones, record books, intermediaries, payment arrangements and changes of location. Such details expose an enterprise with internal division of labor, even though the underlying transactions were unlawful. The network also faced routine risks of police surveillance, telephone disconnection and internal mistrust.

The historical lesson is institutional. Cerone’s prosecution did not arise only from a reputation for violence; it stemmed from evidence linking otherwise dispersed bookmakers to coordinated interstate wagering activity. The court’s narrative is particularly valuable because it presents operational detail that can be checked against the legal questions the judges actually decided. [1] [2]

8. Louis Bombacino and Federal Informant Evidence

Louis Bombacino served as a paid FBI informant and became the government’s principal witness in the first prosecution. The appellate record recounts his involvement in bookmaking work beginning in 1965, shifts among premises and his meetings with multiple defendants. The defense attacked his credibility, including his personal conduct and disputed details of reported conversations. The court did not treat these challenges as automatic grounds for throwing out the case.

An informant’s evidence requires two simultaneous cautions. A witness who directly observed transactions can supply probative testimony, but motives, compensation and inconsistencies require examination. The Seventh Circuit’s affirmance is a judgment about the admissibility and sufficiency of the assembled evidence; it does not transform every remembered statement in the witness’s account into an independently verified event. [2]

9. The 1965–1967 Wagering Network

The judicial narrative begins with local bookmaking locations and describes changes in control and reporting as bettors, operators and recordkeepers interacted. It recounts daily sports wagering in Illinois, out-of-town betting contacts and persons whose instructions carried authority beyond individual premises. These transactions supplied context for a conspiracy theory reaching across state lines.

The period is important because it shows the gradual legal construction of the enterprise. Prosecutors had to prove an agreement and its unlawful interstate purpose rather than rely on membership labels. When the defendants challenged the existence of a single conspiracy, the appellate court looked to the way business was directed, information was shared and proceeds were handled. [2]

10. December 1966 Meetings Described at Trial

The Seventh Circuit opinion recounts Bombacino’s testimony about a late-December 1966 meeting with Cerone and Paul DeLucia, followed by a meeting at Armand’s Pizzeria. According to that witness account, Cerone arranged an introduction to James Cerone and suggested that Bombacino handle additional customers. Another account in the same opinion connected meetings with Joseph Ferriola and daily betting records.

These episodes matter not because the locations are dramatic but because they supplied the jury with evidence of Cerone’s alleged supervisory role. They remain courtroom testimony described in an appellate opinion. Without the original transcript, this biography does not claim to reproduce exact uninterrupted dialogue or assess witness demeanor. [2]

11. Meetings, Delegation and Trust

The record describes an organization that relied on personal meetings at restaurants and bars as well as telephone instructions. A betting business can be economically decentralized while still following centrally recognized rules about customers, pricing and settlement. The relationship between Cerone and James Cerone is one example of delegated responsibility reported by the government’s witness.

The appellate court also recorded defense witnesses who contradicted portions of Bombacino’s narrative. Its finding that the evidence supported conviction should not erase the adversarial process. A sound historical reconstruction explains what was alleged, contested and resolved rather than pretending that an appellate summary was a diary. [2]

12. Interstate Commerce and the Travel Act

The prosecution used federal conspiracy law and the Travel Act to reach activity associated with organized wagering. This framework matters because federal jurisdiction did not require federal prosecutors to show that every single bet physically crossed a state line. The alleged joint operation and use of interstate facilities were central to the charges, and individual defendants disputed whether the proof established the charged agreement.

The 1971 decision addressed conspiracy sufficiency, informer statements, telephone-related evidence and other procedural issues. It upheld the convictions. The legal outcome is specific: it is not an all-purpose verdict on every crime later attributed to Cerone, nor is it proof of murders described in retrospective accounts. [2]

13. May 1970 Verdict and June Sentencing

The Department of Justice’s annual report records convictions in May 1970 of John P. Cerone and four others for conducting a major interstate wagering operation. It further records that members of the Cerone group received five-year prison sentences on June 19, 1970. The report places these convictions within a wider federal antigambling campaign rather than presenting the case as an isolated local arrest.

This official report is especially useful for correcting compressed chronologies: conviction and sentencing were separate events. It is also evidence of what the department publicly said at the time. Details concerning how long Cerone actually remained in federal custody require a separate prison ledger and cannot be derived simply by subtracting dates from the nominal five-year sentence. [1] [2]

14. The First Cerone Appeal

In United States v. Cerone, 452 F.2d 274, the Seventh Circuit rejected challenges to the evidence and legal treatment of the gambling conspiracy and affirmed the convictions. The decision was rendered in September 1971, with rehearing and certiorari proceedings extending into 1972. Some database headers emphasize February 1972 because they refer to denial of Supreme Court review rather than the initial appellate decision.

Where secondary summaries loosely say Cerone was “acquitted” or that the case evaporated, the published opinion supplies the controlling correction. A conviction affirmed on appeal remains a conviction even when the defendants raised substantial issues of witness credibility or evidentiary law. This page gives the governing citation and procedural chronology rather than a generic “gambling charge.” [2]

15. Imprisonment and Return

Cerone served time after the 1970 convictions. Retrospective reporting and a later government indexing file describe his reappearance in Chicago circles in 1973–1974, but a complete Bureau of Prisons movement record was not inspected. A period of absence helps explain why subsequent leadership narratives distinguish the man who held operational influence before prison from the man who resumed a senior position afterward.

It would overstate the evidence to infer the precise date of every organizational meeting during his confinement. What the later testimony supports is that senior law-enforcement observers again regarded him as a significant decision-maker by the early 1980s. [1] [3] [15]

16. The Killing of Louis Bombacino: Attribution Limits

Later organized-crime histories connect the 1975 killing of informant Louis Bombacino to fears of retaliation from the Chicago organization. The first prosecution explains why Bombacino was a conspicuous witness, but it does not adjudicate a later homicide. Allegations that Cerone personally arranged the killing require original homicide evidence or a criminal finding that has not been produced for this profile.

This distinction is mandatory for reference work. A witness’s subsequent death can be historically relevant to the risks of cooperation without becoming proof that each defendant in an earlier case directed it. Here the murder is treated as a serious but separately unresolved accusation rather than attached to Cerone as a proven personal crime. [2] [12]

17. Chicago Leadership in the 1970s

The years after the first prison sentence brought renewed attention to the relationship between Accardo, Aiuppa, Cerone and other senior associates. The Senate hearing account portrayed Aiuppa as a comparatively insulated leader and Cerone as the person handling more immediate decisions. This model depended on a combination of interpersonal authority, delegation to local capos and the availability of trusted representatives across several neighborhoods.

Unlike a public corporation, the organization left no reliable annual list of officers. Law-enforcement diagrams represent intelligence judgments at a particular time and can change as new witnesses, surveillance and prosecutions emerge. Cerone’s role is strongest when described in the specific terms given by named observers. [3] [4]

18. The 1978 Return to Senior Responsibility

In his prepared 1983 Senate statement, former FBI agent William Roemer described Cerone as assuming an underboss role around 1978 following years of relative caution after his earlier imprisonment. The account explained that Accardo and Aiuppa retained influence over important questions while Cerone dealt with practical issues and discussed recommendations with them.

This is testimony by an experienced investigator, not the minutes of an Outfit appointment. It is nevertheless unusually detailed evidence about how contemporary specialists understood the division of power. It also explains why later reports sometimes call Cerone both an “underboss” and an effective day-to-day leader. [3] [17]

19. The Five Street Bosses Model

The 1983 hearing described distinct local power centers and identified figures such as Joseph Ferriola, Joseph Lombardo, Angelo LaPietra, Vincent Solano and Albert Tocco. These men were not interchangeable. Their interests and territories included gambling, labor, automobile theft and other businesses, though individual affiliations could shift and some claims remained under investigation.

Cerone’s stated role was to exercise oversight across such divisions. That does not mean the available evidence proves he personally authorized each offense committed by any person linked to those neighborhoods. Distinguishing leadership assessment from proof of an individual act is essential to evaluating the institutional testimony. [3] [4]

20. Gus Alex and Political Intermediaries

The same Senate narrative gave Gus Alex a specialized role associated with corrupt relationships and contacts. Cerone, in contrast, was depicted primarily as an operational leader. This difference illustrates the Outfit’s division of functions among people who possessed different networks of influence. A senior figure could exercise authority by resolving disputes without personally collecting protection money or approaching officials.

For Cerone, reported proximity to Alex is evidence of membership in the same senior milieu, not documentary proof that Cerone bribed every official named in hearings or books. The legal record supporting his own convictions concerns particular conspiracies and specified Travel Act acts. [3] [9]

21. Labor Unions and Pension-Fund Vulnerability

The Chicago Outfit’s ties to the Teamsters pension fund became central to prosecutions because the fund could furnish financing for casino acquisitions. The 1987 appeal describes how Allen Dorfman and Roy Williams allegedly helped defendants maintain influence over pension-fund decisions. The scheme was not simply a direct transfer of casino winnings to Chicago; it involved financing, managerial placement and the maintenance of hidden business interests.

The government’s theory included organizational pressure on union structures. The appellate court ultimately upheld criminal convictions in the resulting Travel Act conspiracy. The distinction between a lawfully constituted pension fund and illegal influence over particular decisions should remain clear: ordinary members and beneficiaries were not the criminals simply because their fund became a target. [5] [9]

22. Intercity Coordination beyond Chicago

The Las Vegas case illustrates an illicit arrangement involving figures associated with Chicago, Kansas City, Milwaukee and Cleveland. The court described a shifting distribution of proceeds and credited evidence that Chicago representatives became participants after an early dispute among other interests. This was a network of separate groups coordinating an illicit financial enterprise, not a merger into one permanent national crime family.

Cerone’s place in the case came partly from the government’s account of Chicago leadership and partly from evidence considered at trial. Treating the network’s entire historical activity as his personal work would exceed the conviction, which addressed the charged conspiracy and substantive Travel Act counts. [9]

23. Casino Ownership and the Argent Corporation

Allen Glick’s Argent Corporation owned the Stardust and Fremont casino properties discussed prominently in the appellate opinion. Its acquisition financing came from the Teamsters Central States pension fund. The court described how personnel placed in management positions helped conspirators retain hidden influence despite the nominal legal ownership. This gap between registered ownership and actual coercive control was fundamental to the government’s case.

An encyclopedia should not confuse a defendant’s hidden financial interest with his name on a public deed or casino license. The theory relied on proof that defendants could direct personnel, receive money and take part in decisions inconsistent with Nevada gaming requirements. The judicial record identifies the relevant enterprise and the acts proved at trial. [5] [9]

24. Frank Rosenthal and Casino Management

Frank Rosenthal was installed in a managerial role and was described by the appellate court as supervising skimming from the casino business. He later faced regulatory problems in Nevada that threatened the conspirators’ desired continuity. The trial record also addressed Carl Thomas and competing management choices. These arrangements show why illegal control depended on managers who could move through ordinary corporate systems.

Cerone’s historical role is best understood in relation to those management mechanisms, not through the fictional personalities created by Hollywood. He was a Chicago-side participant in a criminal conspiracy that extended to Nevada, rather than the operator of the casino floor in his own name. [5] [9]

25. The Flow of Skimmed Cash

The Eighth Circuit outlined a route by which cash taken from Las Vegas went to Chicago and was then apportioned among participants in different cities. It described the role of Angelo LaPietra, Anthony Chiavola Sr. and Kansas City contact Carl DeLuna, whose notes also became important evidence. Multiple steps in distribution were relevant because they helped tie the hidden beneficiaries to the casino operation.

Even though Cerone was convicted, the opinion did not reconstruct the individual provenance of every banknote or identify a verified personal balance sheet for him. The convicted conspiracy is a legally bounded finding about coordinated conduct, not a reliable basis for inventing his total lifetime wealth. [9]

26. DeLuna Notes as Financial Evidence

Investigators found documents in Carl DeLuna’s possession, including notes recording meetings, contacts and disbursements. The appellate court explained their role in connecting participants and evaluating the prosecution’s case. These papers are valuable because a clandestine enterprise may maintain working records even while trying to avoid an ordinary regulatory paper trail.

The documents were admitted and analyzed through evidentiary rules, including rules concerning statements of co-conspirators. Readers should not assume every notation is self-explanatory without the courtroom testimony identifying its author, context and meaning. The opinion’s discussion supplies the accessible legal interpretation in this edition. [9]

27. Electronic Surveillance and the Investigation

Law-enforcement agencies conducted court-authorized electronic surveillance and searches in several states during 1978–1980. The appeal summarizes interceptions in Kansas City, Leavenworth, Las Vegas, Chicago and Milwaukee and explains that the work eventually supported a broad federal indictment. This multijurisdictional effort reflected the operation’s dispersed geography.

Electronic surveillance was evidence, not by itself proof of every defendant’s guilt. The trial required witnesses, recordings and documents to be admitted and evaluated. The appellate judges considered procedural objections, including the treatment of confidential informants, before affirming the verdicts. [9]

28. Teamsters and Casino Sale Pressure

The opinion described pressure on Allen Glick over management decisions and his eventual sale of Argent. It also recorded efforts by conspirators to influence the pension fund’s internal governance after independent investment managers limited their access. Such activity linked the casino operation to an attempt to maintain favorable financial decision-makers.

In historical interpretation these are mechanisms of institutional capture. They should not be collapsed into vague claims that the Outfit owned the entire Teamsters union. The record describes specific persons, negotiations and periods, and the scope of any conclusion must remain proportionate to those facts. [5] [9]

29. The September 1983 Indictment

On September 30, 1983 a federal grand jury in Kansas City returned an eight-count indictment against fifteen defendants. The charged offenses involved conspiracy and use of interstate travel or facilities to advance unlawful gaming interests. This event followed years of surveillance and investigation. Some defendants later pleaded guilty, some faced severed proceedings and some were acquitted.

The size of the indictment alone did not establish each named person’s guilt. Cerone’s status became legally settled only after the eventual jury verdict and appellate review. The case’s multi-defendant procedural history helps explain why articles about one “skimming trial” can unintentionally merge different defendants’ cases and dates. [5] [6] [9]

30. The Distinct Tropicana Prosecution

Federal cases concerning the Tropicana casino preceded the final Argent-Stardust-Fremont convictions and generated double-jeopardy disputes. The Eighth Circuit distinguished the agreements and transactions alleged in the separate cases. That distinction mattered because a criminal defendant cannot simply be tried twice for the same offense, but related actors can participate in distinct criminal agreements.

Cerone’s encyclopedia profile centers on the Argent case in which he was actually convicted. It does not attribute every Tropicana proceeding or every prior defendant’s plea to him. A precise subject biography preserves the borders among legal dockets despite their common Las Vegas setting. [5] [14]

31. Pretrial Release Restrictions and Litigation

Before trial, federal prosecutors sought to restrict certain defendants’ associations while their case was pending. The Eighth Circuit’s 1986 opinion in United States v. Spilotro discusses the contested pretrial conditions. It dismissed Cerone and Aiuppa’s appeals as moot after the posture changed and separately addressed Spilotro’s position.

A procedural dismissal as moot neither proves nor disproves the underlying organized-crime allegations. It means that the court no longer had a live controversy requiring a merits resolution for those appellants. This distinction should remain visible because case databases sometimes display the names of defendants without clarifying the narrow issue decided. [6]

32. The Four-Month Kansas City Trial

The merits appeal describes a trial lasting roughly four months and testimony from casino owner Allen Glick, Cleveland figure Angelo Lonardo, Teamsters official Roy Williams and casino insider Carl Thomas. Recorded communications, DeLuna notes and agents’ observations supplemented the witnesses. The defense challenged aspects of the theory, the evidence and several rulings on admissibility.

The trial was not a single confession or a bare assertion of rank. The jury assessed several types of corroborating information across multiple defendants. The later court reviewed that assembled record under the standards applicable to criminal appeals, not as a historian independently interviewing all participants decades later. [7] [9]

33. The January 1986 Verdict

The January 1986 jury returned guilty verdicts against Cerone, Aiuppa, Lombardo, LaPietra and Milton Rockman on the eight counts remaining for them. A contemporary Washington Post account reported the outcome and characterized the prosecution as a major challenge to the midwestern organized-crime networks involved in Las Vegas.

For Cerone, the legally accurate description is a conspiracy count and seven substantive Travel Act offenses. It is imprecise to substitute “convicted of owning a casino” or “convicted of being a Mafia boss.” The charged statutory elements and the transactions proved at trial define the conviction. [7] [9]

34. The March 25, 1986 Original Sentence

Contemporary United Press International reporting says that a federal judge imposed an aggregate sentence of twenty-eight years in prison, with fines exceeding $143,000 when multiple assessments were counted. The 1988 appellate decision, which reviewed the later modification, explains the original consecutive terms as four years for conspiracy plus seven terms of three and one-half years—an aggregate of twenty-eight and one-half years.

The half-year discrepancy between the news summary and the court arithmetic is not resolved here by inventing a missing order. A reader should distinguish an initial sentence, fines on statutory counts, restitution and court costs. The 1988 opinion is the stronger authority for how the original counts were structured. [8] [10] [11]

35. The 1987 Merits Appeal and Its Sentence Recital

The 1987 Eighth Circuit opinion affirmed the convictions after addressing nineteen asserted errors. Its factual narrative identifies Aiuppa and Cerone as the Chicago leadership of the skimming conspiracy. The court also reported, somewhat unexpectedly, twenty-and-one-half-year prison totals for those two defendants in its account of the sentencing result.

That numerical recital conflicts with the 1988 panel’s later and more detailed description of the original sentence and its subsequent reduction. Because the published record contains both figures, this biography states them separately and refrains from presenting the 1987 number as the definitive final term. [9] [10]

36. The 1988 Sentence-Modification Appeal

In 1988, the Eighth Circuit reviewed a district court order responding to Rule 35 sentence-modification requests. The district judge made the conspiracy sentence concurrent with the Travel Act sentences but retained consecutive substantive terms. The appellate court said this reduced Cerone’s term to twenty-five years and upheld the modified sentence.

The distinction has direct editorial consequences. A summary saying “Cerone got 28 years” reports the initial sentencing stage; “25 years” describes the later modified term; and “20½ years” occurs in the 1987 merits opinion’s account. They should never be silently presented as the same figure. A final custody calculation would additionally require later records of credits, parole and medical release. [9] [10]

37. Pinkerton Liability and Individual Conduct

The merits appeal considered jury instructions permitting liability for substantive offenses committed in furtherance of a conspiracy. The government relied in part on the Pinkerton doctrine, under which a conspirator may in certain circumstances be held legally responsible for foreseeable acts of co-conspirators. The defense contended that the instructions and charging approach were improper.

The appellate court did not accept those objections as a basis for reversal. The 1988 sentencing opinion went on to acknowledge the concern that some substantive convictions rested on acts of others while maintaining that the district judge’s consecutive terms fell within lawful sentencing discretion. This legal nuance matters because conviction on a count does not necessarily mean the defendant personally carried out every physical act in the statute. [9] [10]

38. Credibility of Government Witnesses

Government testimony from business participants, union figures and defendants from related organizations formed a significant part of the skimming proof. The 1987 court described concerns about informant identity and the use of statements that originated within the conspiracy. The defense raised procedural fairness objections, including whether some information should have been disclosed sooner.

The panel ultimately concluded that the challenged rulings did not require a new trial. That is a legal disposition, not a statement that any particular witness was free from self-interest. An encyclopedia should acknowledge plea arrangements, witness background and the possibility of disputed recollection while reporting the resulting convictions accurately. [9]

39. Financial Evidence Is Not a Personal Net-Worth Statement

Published accounts often place a single dollar amount on the amount skimmed by the participants. The prosecution, however, involved a business arrangement extending across properties and years, with shared proceeds routed among groups. The amount attached to the larger scheme cannot simply be relabeled Cerone’s personal profit or his estate at death.

The strongest records show his participation in the charged unlawful scheme and the courts’ conclusions about the defendants’ collective control and benefits. Individual percentages, private asset inventories and hidden investments require additional financial documentation that was not available for this edition. [7] [9]

40. The Role of Angelo LaPietra and Joseph Lombardo

The court described LaPietra as an operational contact with Kansas City and Lombardo as important in later union and pension-fund discussions. They were among the Chicago participants convicted in the same case, but their functions were not identical to Cerone’s. One defendant could coordinate money transfer while another helped exert pressure on a financial intermediary.

The presence of multiple senior Chicago defendants demonstrates why a simple org chart is inadequate. Leadership and criminal liability were distributed through different links, and the court tested the evidence as to each defendant while also applying conspiracy doctrines to their common scheme. [9] [10]

41. Tony Spilotro and Las Vegas

Anthony Spilotro was another Chicago-associated figure with activities in Las Vegas. He was included in the original indictment but severed from the main trial; the Eighth Circuit’s merits history records that he died after the trial. His case should not be treated as a conviction within Cerone’s jury verdict.

In later cultural memory, Spilotro often overshadows the financial organization underlying the casinos. Cerone’s significance was different: the federal case associated him with high-level Chicago participation in the hidden-interest and cash-distribution arrangements rather than with day-to-day enforcement on the casino floor. [6] [9]

42. Interpreting Frank Balistrieri and Carl DeLuna

Frank Balistrieri of Milwaukee and Carl DeLuna of Kansas City appear in the trial history and accounts of the intercity arrangement. Both took different procedural paths from Cerone and the four co-defendants who received the 1986 jury verdicts. The appellate opinion describes guilty pleas during trial and related earlier proceedings.

This procedural disaggregation is central to reliable biography writing. The participation of a person in the same wider narrative does not justify giving that person Cerone’s convictions, dates or sentence. It likewise does not convert a plea in another case into a verdict against Cerone. [5] [9]

43. The Long Reach of Organized-Crime Investigations

Cerone’s cases reflect a federal shift from prosecution of isolated gambling operations to analysis of larger networks and their institutional advantages. In 1970 the government assembled a case around wagering operators and communications. By the 1980s, investigators connected casino management, pension finance and intercity distributions under a common federal conspiracy theory.

The shift did not mean that gambling vanished as a revenue source. It meant the investigative unit of analysis became broader. For historians, the resulting courts’ opinions offer more verifiable detail about organizational systems than uncorroborated claims about who personally ordered every crime. [1] [2] [3] [9]

44. Prison and Leadership After 1986

The casino convictions removed Cerone and Aiuppa from day-to-day operations as their custodial terms began. A 1990 United Press International report described them as imprisoned while analysts disagreed about whether Sam Carlisi or John DiFronzo exercised the next phase of operational command. The article illustrates contemporary uncertainty over succession even after the legal removal of prominent leaders.

It is not sound to assign Cerone new crimes occurring during incarceration without evidence connecting him to those acts. At the same time, imprisonment alone does not resolve every possible influence that a senior convict could retain. This edition limits its claims to the period record rather than hypothesizing from general Mafia practice. [10] [13]

45. Illness, Parole and Death in July 1996

Contemporary obituary reporting, as catalogued and repeatedly cited by later biographical sources, places Cerone’s death on July 26, 1996, at age eighty-two, after a late release from federal custody for serious illness. The reported setting was the Barrington, Illinois area. The source also describes death occurring approximately six days after release. A full hospital chart and final Bureau of Prisons order were not examined.

Any detailed medical assertion, including an exact pathological cause, should therefore be identified as reported rather than independently verified. His death date and post-custody circumstances are more securely reported than elaborate stories about how or why his illness developed. This page does not adopt speculative claims that other mobsters caused his medical condition. [12] [16]

46. The Family’s Public Memory

In posthumous reporting, Cerone’s family described him in terms of gambling, bookmaking, business and personal relationships. Later stories about his son’s union-law work reproduce some of those remarks. Family recollections are important evidence of how a person was remembered privately but cannot displace official verdicts about his crimes.

The reverse is also true: a parent’s criminal record does not itself establish a child’s criminal conduct. The biography therefore mentions relatives only where the distinction improves historical identification, avoiding guilt by association and unwarranted claims about their legal or professional work. [12] [16]

47. Nickname and Public Persona

The nickname “Jackie the Lackey” evokes a figure who worked closely with powerful bosses, especially in stories emphasizing his early service to Accardo. It can mislead modern readers into picturing him as merely a subordinate driver. Judicial accounts and Senate testimony instead place him among recognized senior Chicago participants in later decades.

Nicknames in organized-crime reporting often combine sarcasm, journalism and criminal self-presentation. They are useful search terms, not reliable descriptions of a person’s permanent rank. Cerone’s career shows the possibility of movement from trusted aide to senior operational authority. [3] [9] [12]

48. Reported Violence and the Standard of Proof

Accounts of Chicago Outfit history have attributed involvement in assaults and killings to Cerone, sometimes naming William “Action” Jackson or other victims. Those attributions are not established by the appellate judgments used here and should not be converted into personal murder convictions. Investigative reports can also record a discussion about violence without proving that a later killing took place at the named suspect’s direction.

This article therefore limits definite criminal findings to the adjudicated gambling and casino cases, while treating other alleged violence as unproven unless separately supported. The standard protects the integrity of the historical narrative: serious accusations require more, not less, documentation than ordinary biographical anecdotes. [2] [3] [15]

49. Why “Chicago Outfit Boss” Can Be Misleading

An enduring interpretive problem is the distinction between visible day-to-day command and ultimate authority over high-level policy. In the Senate account, Cerone managed routine operational matters while Accardo and Aiuppa retained influence over major decisions. In the 1987 court description, Aiuppa was boss and Cerone underboss. Both are descriptions of a dynamic hierarchy.

A well-designed encyclopedia can show this without falsifying clarity. The biographical table uses “senior leader; at times operational leader and underboss” rather than a rigid continuous reign. The section-level text supplies the dates and sources needed for anyone studying the organization’s succession. [3] [9]

50. Primary Records versus Retrospective Crime Writing

Published appellate opinions record what judges found legally sufficient on a defined record. Congressional testimony records what witnesses told lawmakers at a particular historical moment. Contemporary newspapers preserve chronology, public reactions and sometimes information absent from judgments. Retrospective books can synthesize decades but may recycle unverified legends.

Cerone’s biography benefits from all four kinds of source, but they cannot be assigned identical weight. The source register distinguishes each document’s provenance, and the documentary windows give readers a method for testing the strongest points against their original setting. This is necessary for a subject whose public record contains repeated aliases, official dossiers and narrative exaggerations. [1] [2] [3] [7] [9] [10] [12]

51. Historical Significance

Cerone’s significance to the history of organized crime rests less on a spectacular single event than on the continuity between local Chicago gambling administration and national criminal finance. The 1970 prosecution demonstrated a multioperator wagering system. The 1986 case demonstrated that senior Chicago figures could participate in hidden casino interests and intercity cash distributions through institutional intermediaries.

His biography also illustrates a limit of retrospective certainty: formal rank, private motives, alleged violence and the final accounting of proceeds are not fully resolved by even detailed appellate decisions. An accurate reference profile therefore combines strong legal findings with explicitly bounded historical interpretation rather than replacing incomplete evidence with confident legend. [1] [2] [3] [9] [10] [13]

52. The Federal Conspiracy Standard

Conspiracy liability centers on agreement and participation in an unlawful objective. The first appeal examined whether conduct across multiple bookmakers belonged to the charged criminal agreement. A defendant could dispute whether the government had shown one continuous operation rather than a collection of loosely related local gambling activities.

The Seventh Circuit did not accept the defense’s sufficiency challenge. Its analysis is the primary reason the 1970 case should be presented as an adjudicated enterprise, not merely as a nickname or a rumor about bookmaking. The legal standard also prevents researchers from inferring that every person who placed a bet belonged to the conspiracy. [2]

53. Conspirator Statements and Hearsay

One contentious aspect of the first prosecution involved Bombacino repeating conversations he heard within the gambling operation. Defense counsel argued that, as a government informer, he could not become a genuine conspirator and that his testimony should therefore be excluded. The court distinguished the informant’s direct recounting of what he witnessed from assertions by unrelated persons offered without the necessary foundation.

This is valuable instruction for archival interpretation. In a court opinion, “the witness testified that…” and “the court found that…” are not equivalent phrases. The former identifies evidence put before a jury; the latter identifies a judicial determination, usually for a limited procedural purpose. [2]

54. Communication Technology and Gambling

The 1960s operation relied heavily on telephone calls, copied wagering information and betting “lines.” Its use of communications technology produced both efficiencies for operators and evidentiary opportunities for investigators. Such systems also made federal interstate-commerce laws a practical tool against operations that were physically concentrated around Chicago.

The later casino investigation similarly used recordings and interstate communication evidence, but at a higher organizational level. The contrast shows continuity in law-enforcement methods across a changing criminal economy. Technology did not by itself create or dissolve the underlying unlawful agreements. [1] [2] [9]

55. The 1983 Hearing as Institutional Testimony

The Senate hearing was held on March 4, 1983, by the Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations. Witnesses described the structure of Chicago organized crime, alleged influence in unions and government and possible enforcement approaches. Roemer’s account is highly relevant to Cerone, but the purpose of the event was legislative investigation, not the adjudication of particular charges against him.

Scholars should distinguish testimony from verdict. A witness with decades of experience may offer a useful expert synthesis while still making judgments from intelligence, impressions and sources that cannot all be independently replicated. The hearing is accordingly cited as evidence of contemporary law-enforcement assessment of Cerone’s position. [3] [4] [17]

56. The Scope of Nevada Gaming Regulation

The casino-skimming case hinged partly on hidden influence that violated Nevada gaming law. The corporate owner held visible legal interests while the conspirators maintained undisclosed control and benefited from unreported proceeds. The federal Travel Act provided a statutory route to prosecute interstate promotion of that illegal activity.

It is therefore insufficient to say that the defendants only “took money from casinos.” The government described a system for creating, maintaining and benefiting from managerial and ownership influence. That broader financial architecture helps explain both the difficulty of detection and the scale of the eventual investigation. [5] [9]

57. The Jury and the Appellate Standard

When a federal appellate court reviews whether evidence supports a guilty verdict, it generally considers the record in the light most favorable to the jury’s decision. The 1987 opinion repeatedly used that framework as it rejected challenges to the sufficiency of proof and argued legal errors. The decision is thus a controlling legal account, not an independent neutral chronicle of every fact that occurred.

For this biography, findings that a verdict was supportable are accurately described as affirmed convictions. Assertions outside the trial record—especially retrospective stories about homicides, private wealth or supposed plots—do not acquire similar standing merely because they appear in the same narrative tradition. [9]

58. Documenting the Sentencing Conflict

Three numbers are common in the record: the 1986 news report’s twenty-eight years; the 1987 opinion’s twenty-and-one-half years; and the 1988 sentencing opinion’s account of an initial twenty-eight-and-one-half years followed by a twenty-five-year modification. The conflict cannot be solved by treating a news article, an appellate summary and a later order as if they were all descriptions of one unchanged sentence.

The publication adopts the following rule: report each number with its documentary source and identify the procedural stage. The 1988 court’s account of the modified sentence is the strongest available record for that stage. Original judgment, amended judgment, credits and release order would be required for a complete custodial timeline. [8] [9] [10] [11]

59. Interpreting a Twenty-Five-Year Sentence

The later modified term did not mean Cerone spent twenty-five calendar years in prison. He died in 1996, roughly a decade after conviction, after accounts of serious illness and release. Sentencing law, credit for time served, medical parole and prison administration can produce a custodial duration different from the nominal term.

Without an individualized Bureau of Prisons ledger or parole file, the biography does not calculate credits or imply an unverified early-release formula. Historical precision sometimes requires stating that a final order is missing rather than making apparently exact arithmetic from incomplete public records. [10] [12]

60. Popular Culture and the Casino Story

Film treatments of Las Vegas organized crime frequently compress multiple Chicago and Kansas City figures into a handful of dramatic personalities. Such works may reflect broad themes of casino skimming while changing chronology, motives and the relationship between corporate management and criminal beneficiaries.

Cerone’s evidentiary record belongs primarily in federal cases, not screenplay reconstructions. The United States v. Cerone opinions provide the names, procedural sequence, transactions and adjudicated offenses from which a serious narrative can distinguish fiction, analogy and actual historical findings. [5] [9] [10]

61. Archival Gaps and Future Research

The edition does not claim inspection of a certified birth record, family correspondence, all complete trial transcripts, FBI source files, the final prison release file or the original death certificate. A full study of Cerone’s alleged role in violent crimes would require separate homicide dockets, witnesses and source analysis beyond the casino and gambling appeals.

Future archival work should prioritize the original June 1970 judgment, complete appellate appendices, the March 1986 sentencing transcript, the Rule 35 modification order and the Bureau of Prisons medical and parole chronology. These are concrete research targets, not a reason to replace well-established court outcomes with speculation. [1] [2] [8] [9] [10] [12] [15]
