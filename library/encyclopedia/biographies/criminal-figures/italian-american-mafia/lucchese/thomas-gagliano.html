1. Historical Overview

Thomas “Tommy” Gagliano, also documented as Tommaso or Gaetano Gagliano, emerged from the Bronx-based Mafia circle of Gaetano “Tom” Reina and became leader of the organization now described as a predecessor of the Lucchese crime family. His ascent occurred during the bloody leadership struggle of 1930–31. As Reina’s old organization resisted an outside successor favored by Giuseppe Masseria, Gagliano and his allies gained the support that allowed the group to survive and eventually become one of New York’s five enduring Mafia organizations. [1][3][4]

Gagliano is unusually difficult to profile because most publicly accessible materials emphasize the transition of 1930–31, his 1932 income-tax punishment, and the more visible career of his underboss Gaetano “Tommy” Lucchese. Scholarly evidence supports a death on February 16, 1951; later claims of 1953 stem in part from an approximate recollection repeated by the FBI in 1962. This profile keeps the three source layers separate: contemporary public records, decades-later informant testimony, and modern scholarly synthesis. [1][2][5][10][11]

2. Names and Historical Identity

The man commonly called “Thomas Gagliano” appears as “Tom,” “Tommaso,” “Tommy,” and “Gaetano” in modern works. The presence of related but nonidentical given names creates a serious research problem, especially when records from another city list someone named Thomas Gagliano. This profile uses Thomas Gagliano as its catalog heading, preserving historical variants in searches and source notes. [1][2][5]

Neither a name match nor an underworld association is enough to prove that two separate records describe the same person. Researchers must compare residence, age, spouse, occupation, family relationship and registration number. The 1962 FBI file itself reports another Thomas Gagliano associated with a New Orleans jewelry store; without individual linkage, that business should not be assigned automatically to the Bronx boss. [5]

3. Birthplace and Early Dates

Later reference compilations commonly give May 29, 1883, in Corleone, Sicily, as Gagliano’s birth date. A secondary genealogical citation points to a May 30, 1883, baptism in the Corleone parish registers, but the baptismal image and civil birth act have not been independently collated for this publication. The birth year is a useful working identification rather than an excuse to fabricate childhood detail. [7]

The date does not agree neatly with every later reported age. Hunt’s death summary says he was sixty-six in February 1951, while a May 1883 birth would make him sixty-seven; such differences can arise through inaccurate reporting, registration or variant dates. The page therefore attributes the 1883 date to genealogy and reports the 1951 death on stronger identified index and newspaper leads. [1][10][11]

4. Sicilian Origins and Migration

Biographical summaries place Gagliano’s origins in western Sicily and connect his later life to the Corleone migrant networks present in New York. Immigration is usually dated around 1905 in popular biographies, but the specific passenger manifest and naturalization record were not available for direct inspection in this project. The arrival year must not be treated as certified simply because it appears repeatedly online. [1][7]

The broader significance of migration is social rather than deterministic. Family and hometown ties sometimes facilitated trust and business relationships in New York; countless migrants from the same towns had no connection to criminal enterprise. No inference about early Mafia membership follows automatically from birthplace, surname, occupation or an immigrant neighborhood address. [6][7]

5. Marriage, Relatives and Privacy

Secondary accounts identify Gagliano’s wife as Giuseppina or “Josephine” Pomilla and connect her relatives to his trade businesses. The partnership of relatives within a construction-industry enterprise would be consistent with the structure of immigrant family businesses, but direct marriage and commercial records are needed to specify every partner’s legal interest. [2][7]

The historical record is much richer about Gagliano’s alleged criminal and business connections than about his family members. Relatives must not be presumed to have participated in the Mafia. This encyclopedia uses family links to clarify documentary identity and relevant business relationships, not to imply guilt by kinship or to reconstruct private lives on the basis of speculation. [1][7]

6. The Bronx Economic Setting

By the 1920s the Bronx was a rapidly expanding borough where new apartment houses and commercial buildings required plastering, lathing, hoisting and coordinated contractor labor. Control of essential subcontracting, union access or job scheduling could generate both legitimate earnings and coercive power. These conditions explain why investigators later focused on Gagliano’s construction contacts rather than on flamboyant bootlegging stories. [1][2][6]

A historical crime narrative can easily confuse a firm’s lawful service with illegal pressure to join an association, pay dues or avoid competition. The two were often entangled, but each allegation requires its own evidentiary basis. The modern term “labor racket” summarizes suspected coercion and market manipulation; it should not erase the existence of ordinary construction work and union organization. [2][8][9]

7. Position in the Early Mafia Network

Gagliano operated within a preexisting Mafia milieu that crossed East Harlem and the Bronx. The early organizations were networks of personal loyalty and territory rather than subsidiaries of a corporation with published bylaws. Later writers place him among Reina’s principal lieutenants, alongside Tommy Lucchese and Stefano Rannelli or Rondelli. Precise formal rank in every year is harder to document. [1][2][13]

The 1963 Senate staff described Gagliano as Reina’s underboss, a valuable government record of how investigators understood the hierarchy. That account was produced more than thirty years after the relevant events and partly depended on Valachi. The title is well supported for historical interpretation, but should not be mistaken for a contemporaneous written appointment. [3][4]

8. Service Under Gaetano Reina

Gaetano Reina ran the Bronx-centered organization that became a central combatant in the 1930–31 conflict. It was not yet called the Lucchese family, a name attached retrospectively to a later administration. Gagliano was positioned to inherit substantial loyalty because he belonged to Reina’s long-standing inner circle and apparently had his own economic connections and followers. [1][4][13]

It is difficult to separate Gagliano’s independent economic standing from his access to Reina’s authority. The important question is not whether every later captain answered directly to him in 1929, but how a core within Reina’s membership remained cohesive after the boss was killed. Subsequent testimony identifies Gagliano as the focus of that alternative leadership. [3][4]

9. Reina’s Ice Distribution and Organizational Conflict

Reina’s commercial influence included the distribution of ice, a significant service before household electric refrigeration became commonplace. Later informant accounts explain his death partly in terms of a threatened encroachment into the Bronx ice trade by Joe Masseria. That interpretation is plausible within the contemporary economy, but a motive is not established merely because it explains later factional alignment. [1][2][17]

Gagliano’s role in the ice business should not be exaggerated. The most specific economic reports about him concern construction subcontracting and building trades. The political connection between Reina’s ice contacts and Gagliano’s building industry networks was the continuity of local economic power, not proof that Gagliano personally owned every Bronx ice route. [1][6]

10. Plasterers’ Information Bureau

Allan May, drawing on work by Thom L. Jones, places Gagliano and Antonio Monforte in the formation of a Bronx Plasterers’ Information Bureau in 1928. The account says contractors who declined membership were subjected to intimidation. The Bureau may be understood as an alleged mechanism for coordinating firms, dues and employment access. This is important economic history, although the underlying incorporation and court records still require direct archival collation. [2][15]

Because the founding year varies slightly between later accounts, the editorially sound formulation is “in the later 1920s.” Identifying an information bureau does not establish that every member acted criminally. The strongest criminal conclusions require evidence of extortion, threats or fraud against identifiable contractors, the type of proof later tax and racket investigations might preserve. [2][6][9]

11. United Lathing Company

The United Lathing Company is the clearest repeatedly named commercial concern associated with Gagliano. May identifies a partnership active by 1929, and the academic work of David Critchley connects the firm to Gagliano’s May 1932 income-tax punishment. A named company is a more productive archival anchor than an unsupported general statement that he controlled “all construction” in the Bronx. [2][6][8]

Company filings, shareholders, contracts, payrolls, subpoenaed books and tax schedules would show how the business operated and which revenues investigators considered unlawful. In their absence, an encyclopedia should explain that the enterprise was involved in racketeering allegations while distinguishing the tax offense punished by a court from every other alleged practice. [6][9]

12. Antonio Monforte and Other Business Links

Secondary sources identify Antonio Monforte as Gagliano’s partner in the plastering and lathing sector. Spelling variants such as Monteforte are common in reconstructions. These names matter because a credible explanation of organized-crime influence requires identifying concrete organizations and people rather than presenting labor corruption as an impersonal force. [2][15]

That specificity also creates a duty of caution. Business association is not proof of identical intent, guilt or organizational rank. The personnel of a building-trade partnership and the membership of the Mafia family overlapped in the literature but are not interchangeable populations. The profile does not create a formal gangster hierarchy from a commercial registration. [6][7]

13. Construction Labor and Michael McCloskey

Accounts of the late-1920s construction enterprises mention Michael McCloskey and the Lathers’ Union. The proposed value of such a relationship was access: who could work, when a job could proceed, and under what conditions a contractor would obtain the labor or materials needed to complete a building. This gave potential leverage over an entire supply chain. [2][15]

Yet an assertion that a union figure was involved does not establish that the union’s members supported racketeering. A source-driven analysis must distinguish labor organizations, their elected or appointed officials, independent contractors, and alleged criminal enforcers. The coercive conduct alleged in the literature should be assessed through prosecutorial exhibits and hearing testimony rather than by treating organized labor as inherently illicit. [6][8][9]

14. Morello Connections and Investing

An academic account links Giuseppe Morello to investment in Gagliano’s United Lathing Company. If confirmed by incorporation and financial records, that relationship would demonstrate the intersection of older East Harlem Mafia leadership with the Bronx construction sector. It is also consistent with the shifting allegiances that made the 1930–31 war more complicated than a simple contest between two ethnic hometown factions. [6][7]

Investment does not automatically imply a permanent alliance. Morello was killed in August 1930 amid the wider conflict, by which point older links could have become strategically obsolete or actively dangerous. For that reason, organizational affiliation must be tracked through time instead of copied from a single financial association onto every subsequent event. [1][3][17]

15. Extortion Allegations and Revenue Estimates

May recounts allegations of a Treasury investigation that found approximately half a million dollars had been extracted from the construction sector. The figure is large and has often been repeated, but a precise fiscal conclusion requires access to the relevant tax-file calculations and the defined time interval. It is safer to label the amount a reported investigative estimate than a judicial finding of Gagliano’s personal profits. [2][15]

This distinction prevents an important category error. A trade-wide estimate of pressure payments could include multiple people and firms; it cannot be divided or assigned to a single individual without records. Nor is every revenue entry necessarily illegal simply because a criminal investigation covered the firm. [6][8][9]

16. The 1932 Federal Tax Case

Contemporaneous newspaper bibliographies report that a federal tax proceeding resulted in a fifteen-month Atlanta penitentiary sentence for “Tomasso” Gagliano in May 1932. The New York Times identified multiple Bronx defendants and described a racketeering context. Critchley also independently cites the May 1932 tax outcome. This is a concrete criminal conviction, unlike many of the earlier underworld accusations. [6][8][9]

The original judgment, indictment and prison commitment are not incorporated in this edition. Accordingly, the precise tax years, amounts and each legal count should not be manufactured. It is accurate to record a reported 1932 tax-evasion conviction and fifteen-month sentence while marking the details awaiting original docket and Bureau of Prisons verification. [2][6][8]

17. What the Tax Conviction Does and Does Not Prove

A conviction for income-tax evasion is a legal determination about tax conduct, not a general court verdict confirming every organized-crime activity alleged in newspaper coverage. The 1932 sentence is important precisely because it is one of the few prosecutorial outcomes concretely tied to Gagliano by named contemporary reporting. Its evidentiary value should not be diluted through exaggeration. [6][8]

The court may have considered the wider business background, but a complete criminal-liability history would require the charge, adjudication and disposition of each alleged offense. This page therefore avoids saying he was convicted of every extortion claimed in secondary sources, and it does not assert that fifteen months was the only incarceration of his life without a definitive criminal-history search. [8][9]

18. The Late-1920s Balance of Power

By 1928–29, multiple New York leaders were competing over revenue, territory and internal loyalty. The October 1928 killing of Salvatore D’Aquila and the rise of Joe Masseria altered the balance between older Mafia groups. Gagliano’s connection to Reina placed him in an organization exposed to pressures arising both from its Bronx economic position and from citywide underworld rivalries. [1][6][13]

Researchers should resist reading all events as preparations for a precisely planned “Five Families” blueprint. David Critchley’s work emphasizes that later retellings sometimes impose unity and inevitability on a conflict that had multiple local causes. The organizations that survived 1931 were built from networks already in existence, not invented wholesale at a single banquet. [6][7]

19. February 26, 1930: Reina’s Murder

Gaetano Reina was killed with a shotgun in the Bronx on February 26, 1930. The date and the physical circumstances of his death are much more secure than claims about who ordered the crime. Later Senate testimony cast the shooting as a decisive turning point and described Gagliano as the subordinate who wanted to avenge him. [1][3][4]

For Gagliano, the homicide created a succession crisis. A leader with personal standing among Reina’s men suddenly had to operate beneath a replacement favored by an outside power. It is this crisis, more than a single unproved motive for the killing, that explains why the group’s internal politics became so important to the Castellammarese War. [1][2][17]

20. Masseria’s Sponsorship of Pinzolo

Following Reina’s death, Masseria supported Bonaventura “Joe” Pinzolo as head of the organization. Specialist historical reconstructions describe Gagliano and Lucchese as resistant to the appointment because it limited their group’s autonomy. A succession engineered by a powerful outsider could give the newly installed boss a title but not necessarily deep loyalty. [1][2][13]

The exact chain of approval for the appointment and the language used to describe Pinzolo’s rank come largely from later witness accounts. A biography should therefore distinguish his actual presence in the boss position from speculative scenes in which Masseria supposedly issued detailed orders. The political consequence—an alienated faction around Gagliano—is better attested than private conversation. [1][3][4]

21. A Quiet Opposition Inside the Organization

Accounts of 1930 describe Gagliano and Lucchese forming a splinter faction opposed to Pinzolo while concealing their hostility. This combination of outward deference and covert preparation is important because it shows that Mafia leadership depended on information management: an openly mutinous group might be defeated before it could build allies. [1][2][13]

Valachi’s later testimony portrays recruitment of new men who could operate without being recognized by rival factions. He entered the story only after Reina’s death, which limits his firsthand knowledge of the former boss’s final decisions. Nevertheless, his testimony offers evidence for how the anti-Pinzolo faction understood secrecy and recruitment. [3][4]

22. The September 1930 Killing of Pinzolo

Pinzolo was shot to death in an office in the Brokaw Building on Broadway during September 1930. The date is commonly given as September 9 in the retrospective Senate account; Thomas Hunt cites contemporaneous newspapers indicating the body was found on Friday, September 5. This discrepancy illustrates why police records recited decades later and immediate news reporting cannot simply be merged without comment. [1][3][17]

His death ended the short Masseria-backed period of command and opened the way for Gagliano’s ascendancy. But the outcome is not proof that Gagliano personally fired the shots or even that a court established his responsibility for planning the murder. Competing later accounts name different gunmen and the available material does not contain a homicide judgment convicting Gagliano. [1][2][4]

23. The Question of Responsibility for Pinzolo

Valachi attributed Pinzolo’s killing to Girolamo “Bobby Doyle” Santuccio in later testimony, while a separate Luciano-derived narrative identified Dominick Petrilli. Lucchese was questioned and arrested in connection with the homicide; the case was not pursued to a successful conviction in the documentation cited by Hunt. These incompatible accounts should be treated as competing recollections, not assembled into a fictitious joint murder team. [1][2][3]

Gagliano’s benefit from Pinzolo’s removal is a matter of political history. A motive or advantage does not by itself identify a shooter or prove an order. This page describes his faction’s role in the succession dispute and avoids treating contested perpetrator attributions as adjudicated fact. [1][6]

24. Gagliano Emerges as the Group’s Leader

Once Pinzolo was gone, Gagliano became the principal leader of the reorganized Bronx faction. The Senate staff in 1963 described him as the man who took over Reina’s organization, and Valachi recalled operating under his leadership in the conflict. These accounts establish the broad lineage even though precise dates and internal election procedures cannot be reconstructed from a minute book. [3][4]

Some historians distinguish de facto command in late 1930 from formal recognition during the Maranzano arrangements of 1931. This is a useful distinction: control within a group and recognition by peer groups were related but not identical. It also explains why biographies sometimes assign 1930 and sometimes 1931 as the starting date of Gagliano’s boss tenure. [1][6]

25. Joseph Valachi’s Entry into the Faction

Joseph Valachi is one of the principal informants through whom later readers learned of the Gagliano organization. His October 1963 Senate testimony described recruitment into a group headed by Gagliano after Reina’s killing. The hearing is an unusually clear source for Gagliano’s public historical footprint, while simultaneously introducing the problems of memory and self-justification after a thirty-year interval. [3][4]

Valachi was an underworld participant, not an independent detective reconstructing every step in real time. His descriptions of what he did and whom he met deserve careful attention; his accounts of orders passed between leaders he did not witness require attribution. The encyclopedia marks the difference rather than treating the whole testimony as either wholly false or wholly conclusive. [3][16]

26. The Alleged $140,000 War Contribution

On October 1, 1963, Valachi stated that Gagliano had spent about $140,000 personally on the 1930–31 struggle. This is a striking claim of financial capacity and commitment that can be traced to a specific printed hearing passage. The number, however, is a witness estimate, not an audited bank transfer or documentary proof of a precise cash expenditure. [3][4]

Historians have questioned the scale and plausibility of figures quoted from the war. Critchley warns that retrospective financial assertions may be inflated or derived from information Valachi only heard while embedded in the faction. The prudent use of the $140,000 figure is to illustrate how supporters described Gagliano’s contribution, not to record it in an audited balance sheet. [6][7]

27. Relations with the Maranzano Coalition

Gagliano’s group eventually aligned with forces opposing Joe Masseria, including the faction centered on Salvatore Maranzano. This was a coalition of convenience as much as a single ethnic or hometown block. Valachi specifically testified that Gagliano was not himself one of the Castellammarese, even while cooperating with them during the conflict. [3][4][17]

The distinction is crucial. The term “Castellammarese War” should not cause a writer to label every anti-Masseria figure as born in Castellammare del Golfo. Gagliano was linked to the Bronx organization that had emerged under Reina, and his reasons for coalition could include revenge, autonomy and local business security. Those interests overlapped with but did not duplicate Maranzano’s. [6][7]

28. November 1930 and the War’s Escalation

The murders of Alfredo Mineo and Stefano Ferrigno in the Bronx in November 1930 formed part of the broader fighting among New York’s Mafia factions. In later testimony Valachi connected his own induction with the anti-Masseria side after such killings. The deaths demonstrate that the contest extended beyond the single Reina–Pinzolo succession into multiple organizational fronts. [1][2][3]

A comprehensive account of the killings would require each police file and contemporary press record, including exact dates and named suspects. For Gagliano’s biography, the central historical fact is the widening of the alliance his group had joined. Responsibility for particular murders should be assigned only to the level supported by independently identified records. [6][17]

29. April 1931: Masseria’s Assassination

On April 15, 1931, Masseria was murdered in New York. His death removed the central opponent of Maranzano’s coalition and transformed the bargaining conditions for groups including Gagliano’s. The conflict did not end simply through one leader’s decisive battlefield victory; defections, competing interests and assassinations all shaped the outcome. [1][2][6]

The later “founding of the Five Families” story often compresses the subsequent months into a single decision. Historians now distinguish the survival of preexisting organizations from the formal or symbolic allocation of their leadership. Gagliano’s consolidation is best understood as an outcome of that political process, not as the creation of an organization that had no prior membership or territory. [6][7]

30. Maranzano’s Leadership Announcement

Valachi told the Senate in October 1963 that a large postwar meeting in the Bronx announced “Tom Gagliano” as boss with “Tommy Brown,” meaning Lucchese, as underboss. The remembered announcement supplies a coherent organizational succession and is one of the rare explicit statements of their respective positions. [3][4]

The meeting’s size, exact order of announcements and the precise administrative system allegedly adopted are debated. The event is relevant as testimony about recognition among peers, not as a contemporaneous signed charter. This distinction is particularly important when presenting 1931 as a watershed in American organized crime history. [6][7]

31. September 1931: Maranzano Is Killed

Salvatore Maranzano was murdered on September 10, 1931, months after Masseria’s death. In the source reconstruction by Hunt, Gagliano and Lucchese were among the figures who visited the office that day. Some versions imply they understood the plot; others emphasize how later storytellers connected names and meetings after the fact. [1][2]

No evidence reviewed for this entry supports writing that Gagliano personally carried out the assassination. What is historically significant is that the new political order survived the death of the man who had temporarily claimed authority over the other bosses. Gagliano remained associated with one of the New York organizations during the restructuring that followed. [1][6]

32. The Commission and Family Autonomy

After Maranzano’s fall, the New York Mafia moved toward a form of shared inter-family dispute resolution generally called the Commission. Later organizational histories include Gagliano among the leading New York bosses. His place on that body is an inference supported by histories of the postwar hierarchy, not a directly inspected 1931 set of meeting minutes. [1][2][6]

The larger lesson is the difference between one supreme boss and a collection of bosses with power over their own organizations. The Commission should not be described as a lawful corporation; it was an underworld governance mechanism for criminal groups. Its scope, attendance and decisions changed over time, making rigid year-by-year membership charts vulnerable to overstatement. [6][12]

33. How Gagliano’s Leadership Worked

The Gagliano organization retained a base in the Bronx while also possessing connections in East Harlem and elsewhere. In accounts of its internal administration, Gagliano was the superior authority and Lucchese was the deputy who had increasing day-to-day visibility. This division of roles is central to understanding why the later Lucchese name displaced Gagliano’s in public memory. [1][2]

But the image of a silent strategist directing every move should not be treated as a complete operational record. Few documented orders survive, and the allocation of responsibilities was not stable in a modern corporate sense. The strongest statements concern the leadership pairing and eventual succession, not a daily schedule of meetings and instructions. [1][5]

34. Tommy Lucchese as Underboss

Gaetano “Tommy” Lucchese was more visible in police, court and newspaper materials than his superior. His connections in New York business, political circles and illicit enterprises allowed him to become the principal public point of reference for the organization. As underboss, he could manage relationships and daily matters while Gagliano retained the ultimate title. [1][2]

It is sometimes said that Lucchese effectively ran the family long before 1951. Hunt describes that as a plausible interpretation given Gagliano’s withdrawal from publicity after the early-1930s construction case. Yet there is a difference between taking the lead in practical affairs and possessing formal boss rank. The profile preserves that distinction until the well-documented 1951 succession. [1][10][11]

35. Valachi’s Wedding in September 1932

Allan May identifies Gagliano’s attendance at Joseph Valachi’s wedding on September 18, 1932 as one of the few personal appearances he could trace after the 1931 conflict. Valachi’s marriage to Mildred Reina also linked the former Reina household to a man who later gave extensive federal testimony. The story is valuable chiefly because it locates Gagliano in the social world of the successor organization. [2][14]

A wedding guest list, however, is not a criminal organization chart. Attending a family event does not automatically disclose the status of every attendee or confirm involvement in particular crimes. The cited date remains a retrospective biographical detail until contemporary civil and photographic sources are independently assembled. [2]

36. After Prohibition

When national Prohibition ended in December 1933, underworld businesses shifted but did not suddenly cease to exist. Gambling, loansharking, labor influence, illicit distribution and commercial infiltration offered varied opportunities. Secondary literature suggests the Gagliano organization participated in several such sectors, but Gagliano himself appears comparatively rarely in individual public investigations. [1][2]

The scarcity of appearances cannot prove he was uninvolved in every case, nor that he gave instructions for every racket attributed to the family. In historical writing the organization and the individual must remain distinct subjects. This biography therefore identifies the family’s economic setting without manufacturing a detailed personal portfolio for an elusive boss. [1][6]

37. Wartime Black-Market Claims

Allan May cites Ralph Salerno and John S. Tompkins for a claim that the Gagliano family engaged in World War II black-market schemes involving sugar, gasoline ration stamps and meat. Such activities were common targets of wartime criminal investigation because price and ration controls created opportunities for illegal diversion. The cited statement concerns the organization, not a documented personal purchase or sale by Gagliano. [2]

No underlying indictment, rationing file or verdict from the named wartime activities is incorporated here. As a result, the claim is presented as a secondary historical allegation about family-level behavior. It cannot responsibly be converted into an assertion that Gagliano was convicted of particular ration-stamp violations. [2][12]

38. Why the Public Record Is Sparse

Much of Gagliano’s reputation rests on the unusual scarcity of news coverage once the 1930–32 events faded. May describes an almost invisible boss and admits difficulty locating even basic biographical records. That observation must now be read alongside the stronger death-index and funeral-notice references identified by later research. Scarcity is a research condition, not conclusive evidence of supernatural discretion. [1][2][10][11]

Absence of a press story may reflect deliberate avoidance of attention, poor indexing, the use of alternate personal names, or incomplete digitization. It also might reflect Lucchese’s more prominent role in public contact. A scholarly biography should acknowledge those possibilities rather than convert lack of coverage into a psychological diagnosis. [1][2]

39. Claims About Major Mafia Conferences

Retrospective profiles often list celebrated meetings—Cleveland in 1928, Atlantic City in 1929, Havana in 1946—and emphasize that Gagliano was not publicly recorded among certain attendees. A person’s absence from a reported roster does not necessarily prove he knew nothing of the political decisions discussed or deliberately avoided them. [2]

The methodological point matters because underworld conferences themselves were reconstructed from incomplete arrest reports, witness accounts and later memoir. This page reports May’s observations as historical arguments, not as a verified exhaustive attendance database for all years. [2][6]

40. From the Gagliano Family to the Lucchese Family

Names such as “Reina family,” “Pinzolo family,” “Gagliano family,” and “Lucchese family” can refer to substantially continuous membership under successive leaders. The contemporary organization did not change its legal corporate name in a public filing. Historians use each label to clarify the dominant leader during a given period. [1][3][7]

For cross-referencing the modern MGU Crime Encyclopedia, Thomas Gagliano belongs under the Lucchese category even though he was boss before the family was generally known by that name. This taxonomy helps readers navigate while avoiding a false claim that Gagliano belonged to an organization named “Lucchese” in 1930. [1][12]

41. Bronx Residence and Yonkers

Hunt’s source notes place the family at 638 East 227th Street in the Wakefield section of the Bronx before a move to 168 Seminary Avenue in Yonkers. The 1962 FBI memo likewise repeated the Yonkers address in a statement attributed to Valachi. Two independent kinds of record therefore point to the same later locality, although the exact period of occupancy requires property and census verification. [1][5][18]

Residential evidence helps identify the right person when multiple New York records bear similar names. It should not be repurposed as proof that criminal meetings occurred inside the home or that household members participated in crime. Address data is included solely where it is relevant to historical record matching and sources have already published it. [1][5]

42. February 16, 1951: Death and Funeral

The best identified death record for Gagliano is a New York City death-index entry for Tommaso Gagliano, certificate no. 3796, dated February 16, 1951. Hunt also cites a February 18 New York Times notice and reports a February 20 funeral Mass at Our Lady of Grace Church in the Bronx. Together these substantially strengthen the 1951 chronology. [1][10][11][18]

Popular works long repeated both 1951 and 1953. The 1953 figure should not displace an identified 1951 index and contemporaneous funeral notice merely because it appears in a government memorandum. The original certified death certificate and full newspaper facsimile still merit inspection, but the evidence reviewed here favors February 16, 1951. [1][2][5]

43. Why the FBI Repeated 1953

A December 31, 1962 FBI memorandum recorded Valachi’s statement that Thomas Gagliano had died of natural causes “about 1953” and was followed by Thomas Lucchese. That is a genuine primary government document, and its words should not be ignored. But what it establishes beyond doubt is that the agency recorded the recollection, not that the death actually occurred in 1953. [5]

The same memorandum said investigators were still checking whether Gagliano or Vincent Alo directly preceded Lucchese. Such open uncertainty is valuable historical evidence about what the Bureau knew in 1962. It also demonstrates why later historians should not treat every FBI intelligence lead as a verified vital record. [1][5][11]

44. A New Orleans Namesake in the FBI File

The 1962 FBI memorandum also recited 1949 intelligence about people named Joseph Gagliano and Thomas Gagliano operating a custom jewelry store in New Orleans, with speculation about criminal contacts. It did not establish within the excerpt that this Thomas Gagliano was the same person as the Bronx family boss. The namesake problem is aggravated by the fact that different records use both Tommaso and Gaetano. [5]

This profile therefore does not treat the store as the boss’s proved legitimate business, does not attribute the informant’s suspicions as convictions, and does not fold the New Orleans individuals into his New York family tree. Documented identity confirmation would require matching individual fingerprints, birth dates and other identifiers to the subject of this biography. [5]

45. The 1951 Transfer to Lucchese

With Gagliano’s death in 1951, Tommy Lucchese formally emerged as boss of the organization that later bore his name. Hunt’s reconstruction explains that Lucchese had already exercised extensive public-facing authority. A death-index and funeral-traceable succession fits that trajectory more securely than an undocumented theory that Gagliano lived until 1953 after a secret retirement. [1][10][11]

The transition underscores how a durable organization can outlive a low-profile leader. Lucchese inherited a network built through the Reina, Pinzolo and Gagliano administrations, then developed it under his own personal alliances. The history of the family is therefore one of continuity through leadership change, not successive unrelated criminal startups. [1][7]

46. Evidence Hierarchy and Historiography

Four bodies of evidence shape the narrative: contemporary death and tax newspaper reporting; the 1930–31 policing history recited in the 1963 Senate hearings; Valachi’s and other insiders’ recollections; and modern research that triangulates these materials. Each source answers a different question and has a different vulnerability. A witness can describe inside practices but misremember dates; a newspaper may report a sentence accurately without knowing a boss’s entire network. [1][3][5][8][10]

The most reliable biography is not the one with the greatest number of confident stories. It is the one that can show a reader which statements are established by contemporary records, which depend on a witness’s firsthand account, and which rest on later interpretations. Gagliano is a particularly good demonstration because early Mafia literature treated his later life as almost unknowable while archival citations have since clarified key facts. [1][2][6]

47. Historical Significance

Gagliano’s leadership is the bridge between the violent displacement of Gaetano Reina and the relatively durable midcentury organization headed by Tommy Lucchese. The faction he organized resisted a Masseria-sponsored successor, joined a wider coalition and survived the 1931 reordering. Its continuity helps explain how New York’s Mafia families acquired recognizable identities over time. [1][3][4][7]

His commercial history also illustrates an important shift in organized-crime analysis: protection payments, building-trade companies, union access and taxes can be as historically revealing as spectacular killings. At the same time, the surviving criminal conviction should be kept distinct from allegations about murders and the entire family’s later activity. [2][6][8]

48. Research Priorities for a Future Edition

Priority archives include the Corleone civil birth act and church baptism register; immigration and naturalization records; Gagliano’s marriage and residence documentation; Plasterers’ Information Bureau and United Lathing Company filings; the March and May 1932 newspaper pages; federal tax judgment and commitment; and 1951 death certificate no. 3796. Completing these checks could close the remaining gaps around identity, business and criminal record. [1][6][8][9][11]

Researchers should also compare the 1963 Senate hearing images to the original 1930 police entries and search for any official records of early-1930s leadership changes. These tasks are documentary rather than speculative: an unexplained date difference or absent photograph should prompt a targeted archival search, not a new crime legend. Until the records are collected, the profile remains an explicitly qualified scholarly reference. [3][5][17]

Documentary Research Windows

Eight source-study windows follow. These are original editorial paraphrases, not newly authenticated verbatim transcriptions, court exhibits, or newly reproduced facsimiles. They teach source classification and identify what remains to be collated.

Research Window A — 1963 Senate Hearing: Identifying an Underboss

The Senate staff named Tom Gagliano as Reina’s underboss and described his organizational reaction to the 1930 killing. This is documented congressional testimony and staff interpretation, not a contemporaneous Mafia appointment paper. The page references preserve which part of the statement belongs to staff and which belongs to Valachi. [3][4]

Research Window B — Valachi’s $140,000 Expenditure Claim

Valachi testified in 1963 that Gagliano personally committed about $140,000 to the war. It is a useful account of perceived financial capacity but not an audited ledger. Critchley’s skeptical analysis shows why historians should not convert a recollected round figure into a verified transaction. [3][4][6]

Research Window C — 1932 Tax-Sentence News Report

A New York Times May 22, 1932 headline names Tomasso Gagliano and a fifteen-month Atlanta sentence. The specific newspaper page is cited in scholarly bibliographies but not reproduced here; the window therefore studies a bibliographic record rather than pretending to supply a certified court judgment. [6][8]

Research Window D — Pinzolo’s Date Conflict

The 1963 police-summary chronology gives September 9 for the Pinzolo killing, while Hunt cites contemporary September reporting that places discovery of the body September 5. The case reminds readers that a police note recited three decades later can conflict with a near-contemporary account. [1][3][17]

Research Window E — Maranzano’s Announcement of the New Bosses

Valachi recalled a postwar meeting in which Gagliano was named boss and Tommy Brown (Lucchese) underboss. The testimony is central evidence for the succession but cannot supply an exact documentary constitution of the Five Families. [3][4][6]

Research Window F — 1951 Death Index and Funeral Notice

Hunt traces Tommaso Gagliano to death certificate no. 3796 and a February 18, 1951, New York Times death notice, with a February 20 funeral. These identified documents are stronger evidence for the date than later approximate oral recollections. The original index image and notice facsimile remain outstanding for a subsequent audit. [1][10][11][18]

Research Window G — The FBI’s “About 1953” Intelligence Lead

The FBI memorandum of December 31, 1962 faithfully records Valachi’s memory that Gagliano died “about 1953,” but explicitly continues its own investigation into Lucchese succession. Government provenance proves the statement was recorded, not that the memory was correct. This edition reviewed the final-page scan. [5]

Research Window H — New Orleans Namesake and Identity Resolution

A separate 1949 informant report embedded in the same FBI memo discusses a Thomas Gagliano and jewelry store in New Orleans. The document does not securely connect that man to the Bronx crime family boss. It is a warning against merging unrelated biographies based solely on a shared name. [5]

Research and Publication Limitations

The strongest dated evidence includes the 1932 newspaper-reported tax sentence, the public 1963 Senate hearing record, the inspected final page of the December 31, 1962 FBI report, and named 1951 New York City death-index and funeral-notice references in later documentary scholarship. The underlying federal trial docket, original death certificate, full 1951 funeral newspaper facsimile, Italian birth/baptism register, property records and original 1930 murder files were not independently assembled.

Where the FBI repeated Valachi’s approximate “about 1953” death recollection, this page retains that record as evidence of contemporary intelligence uncertainty, not proof of the actual death year. The stronger identified death-index and 1951 funeral record support February 16, 1951. Another Thomas Gagliano mentioned in the FBI memorandum in relation to a New Orleans business is not presumed identical with this subject.

The profile does not claim a firsthand verified 1931 Commission minute book; does not turn war-spending estimates into audited facts; does not assign a murder conviction based on hearsay; and does not embed an image without independently verified historical identity and rights.
