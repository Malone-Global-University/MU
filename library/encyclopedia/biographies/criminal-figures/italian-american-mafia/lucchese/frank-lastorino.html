Research Abstract

Frank “Big Frank” Lastorino was a Lucchese crime-family captain identified in a 1994 federal opinion as a member of the organization’s temporary ruling panel while Vittorio Amuso and Anthony Casso faced federal prosecution. Wiretap records placed him in communication with fugitive Casso about proceeds, legal fees, extortion and organizational management. A separate 1992 state ruling affirmed dismissal of a weapons prosecution following suppression of evidence. Retrospective Mafia histories also attribute several homicides to Lastorino, but these allegations are distinguished here from independently verified judicial dispositions.

This first-edition MGU biography emphasizes the documentary difference between reported ranks, testified events, intercepted calls, charged offenses, guilty pleas and criminal convictions. The original Lastorino sentencing judgment and original vital records were not available for direct comparison.

Research Methodology

This biography prioritizes reported judicial decisions for legal outcomes, dated official reports for investigative and institutional context, contemporary reporting for chronology, and carefully attributed secondary reconstructions for crew membership and episodes not fully resolved in public law reports. Statements from a cooperating witness are not independently verified merely because later websites repeat them.

Key distinction: The February 1994 suppression opinion describes allegations and probable-cause determinations; the July 1992 state ruling records dismissal after evidence suppression. Neither is a blanket conviction or acquittal of every claim made in public Mafia histories. All eight documentary windows below are editorial analyses, not newly transcribed archival exhibits. [1][2][3]

§01. Scope: a captain in the Amuso–Casso organization

Frank “Big Frank” Lastorino occupies a more verifiable place in the late twentieth-century Lucchese record than the sensational stories frequently attached to his name. A federal district judge explicitly described him as an alleged captain in the organization and evaluated surveillance of his telephone communications with fugitive underboss Anthony Casso. These records make the operation of a criminal hierarchy, rather than folklore about individual violence, the strongest starting point for his biography. [1]

Lastorino’s career is particularly useful for understanding the transition from an overt territorial crew system to a structure managed through intermediaries, cash accounting, electronic communication and small leadership panels. Allegations about specific murders are addressed below, but they are never presented as automatically proved by his appearance in a multi-defendant indictment. [1][3]

Back to contents

§02. Identity, dates, and nickname

Lastorino is routinely identified as Frank Lastorino, called “Big Frank” in organized-crime reporting. Secondary indexes give April 9, 1939 as his birth date. The dated funeral listing and independent entertainment biographical index both record his death on November 5, 2022, though an original civil birth record and certified death certificate were not inspected for this edition. [7][8]

The nickname should not be mistaken for a formal office or an evidentiary descriptor. News coverage variously calls him a captain and a consigliere; courts, in their 1994 account of the relevant period, more precisely identify him among the captains and panel participants. [1][9]

Back to contents

§03. Brooklyn and the geographic setting

A detailed October 2026 historical reconstruction places Lastorino’s upbringing on East 87th Street in Canarsie, Brooklyn, and connects his later residence to Howard Beach in Queens. Those neighborhood particulars come from secondary research and have not been independently matched here to birth, school or property files. [3]

Canarsie, Flatlands, Bensonhurst and Howard Beach form the immediate geographic frame for the crews and incidents later attributed to him. In reading crime histories, a street location may establish where a victim or meeting was reported, but it does not by itself establish a person’s participation. [3][11]

Back to contents

§04. Family and immigrant background: limited verification

The 2026 research reconstruction associates Lastorino’s family roots with Sicilian and Calabrian localities, specifically Santa Lucia del Mela and Rossano. This is useful as a lead for genealogical study but remains an attributed secondary claim in the absence of collected passenger lists, vital records or the family’s own documents. [3]

This biography does not invent parents, schooling, occupations or childhood episodes. Those absences matter: Mafia biographies often extrapolate a complete early life from a well-documented late criminal prosecution, making the undocumented decades appear deceptively precise.

Back to contents

§05. The Lucchese family before Lastorino’s prominence

The organization had emerged from the Reina–Gagliano line and was headed for decades by Thomas Lucchese, then Carmine Tramunti and Anthony Corallo. By the mid-1980s federal prosecutions had weakened the older leadership cohort and opened the way to Vittorio Amuso and Anthony Casso. A New Jersey investigative report describes that institutional transition. [4]

Lastorino’s rising profile belongs to the period after that succession. It would be anachronistic to transfer the documented offices of the 1990s back to his unverified 1970s activities, or to presume he was part of the top administration merely because he appeared in photographs with its leaders. [1][4]

Back to contents

§06. Associations before formal induction

The October 2026 specialist reconstruction places him near the milieu of Brooklyn and Flatlands organized-crime associates in the early 1980s, including people connected to the former DeMeo crew. It also mentions suspicion surrounding the 1983 death of Roy DeMeo, but no Lastorino conviction for that killing is identified in the source examined. [3]

An acquaintance, a surveillance sighting, an investigative suspicion and a criminal conviction answer different questions. For an encyclopedia, the first three belong in an attribution framework; none justifies a categorical statement that Lastorino perpetrated the DeMeo murder.

Back to contents

§07. Becoming a made member

Secondary reconstructions place Lastorino’s formal induction into the Lucchese family in or around 1987 and initially associate him with the crew of Robert “Bobby” Amuso, brother of boss Vittorio Amuso. The exact ceremony details are not independently established by a contemporaneous indictment or sworn transcript reviewed for this page. [3][13]

A formally inducted member and a crew captain possessed different kinds of authority. Induction established reported organizational membership; promotion to captain signaled delegated oversight of a group of other members and associates. Those distinctions explain the importance of the promotion described for 1989. [1][3]

Back to contents

§08. Robert Amuso and the initial crew setting

Robert Amuso features prominently in the later wiretap evidence because he apparently had been a conduit for money moving through the family. The January 1993 court opinion recounts Casso instructing Lastorino that proceeds from particular operations should be routed through Lastorino rather than Robert. [1]

This is a narrower and more reliable statement than an assertion that Lastorino commanded all of Robert Amuso’s former personnel. Reported crew reorganizations and leadership transfers require corroboration at the level of dated testimony or surveillance, particularly where participants later disagreed. [3]

Back to contents

§09. Promotion to captain in 1989

Research reproducing Alphonse D’Arco’s testimony places Lastorino’s promotion to captain at a Brooklyn meeting in March 1989 attended by senior figures including Vittorio Amuso, Anthony Casso and Peter Chiodo. The account describes members of the New Jersey faction and their internal disputes as part of the meeting’s broader agenda. [3]

The 1994 judicial opinion provides independent support for the later status, calling Lastorino an alleged captain and identifying D’Arco and Chiodo as witnesses who described the Lucchese hierarchy. It does not independently certify the day of the 1989 promotion. [1]

Back to contents

§10. Captaincy as an administrative office

A capo was not merely a street enforcer. Under the hierarchy described in the federal opinion, captains functioned as collection points, distributors and managers of criminal proceeds. They could convey messages, account for money and supervise local operations while the most senior figures were fugitives or incarcerated. [1]

Lastorino’s recorded conversations with Casso are therefore significant precisely because they concern routine administrative matters: cash receipts, lawyers’ fees and transfers. The normality of those conversations, rather than dramatic violence alone, reveals how the organization continued functioning despite prosecution. [1]

Back to contents

§11. Territory and composition of the crew

D’Arco testimony excerpted in the specialist biography locates Lastorino’s regime around Canarsie and elsewhere describes it in connection with Utica Avenue. Published accounts name George Conte, Richard Pagliarulo and other associates whose assignments changed as captains were promoted, imprisoned or reassigned. [3]

These names should not be converted into a fixed annual roster without a dated reference. Informal criminal organizations reassign people in ways not captured by a publicly filed chart; some witnesses distinguish between a crew’s continuity and the changing captain responsible for it. [3]

Back to contents

§12. Christopher Furnari and the gasoline business

A secondary historical summary connects Lastorino, Christopher Furnari and the gasoline-tax fraud schemes that attracted both Italian-American and Russian organized-crime actors in New York. Anthony Casso’s 1996 Senate testimony independently discussed the family’s involvement with Russian figures in gasoline distribution, tax evasion and protection. [6][13]

The Senate testimony provides valuable context, but it is not an adjudication of a specific Lastorino gasoline-fraud count. The precise span, profits and personnel of any operation assigned to him remain attributed claims rather than proven individual sentencing facts in the sources reviewed. [6]

Back to contents

§13. Relations with Anthony Casso

Lastorino’s direct contacts with Anthony Casso are among the best-documented elements of his career. The 1994 opinion describes two Brooklyn cellular telephones used by Lastorino and a later cellular link used by Casso while he was a fugitive, allowing federal agents to connect organizational conversations to the search for him. [1]

The contacts were not merely incidental: the judge summarized recorded discussions of continuing racketeering cash flows and the transmission of operating instructions. They also provide independent context for Lastorino’s place within the small leadership network of the early 1990s. [1]

Back to contents

§14. Vittorio Amuso’s 1990 flight and the family crisis

The 1990 Windows-related investigation and later prosecution of Vittorio Amuso altered the conditions in which Lastorino operated. Amuso fled, was captured and was convicted in June 1992 on 54 counts, according to the February 1994 opinion. Casso remained at large until January 1993. [1][14]

When senior figures were absent, authority flowed to trusted intermediaries. The court’s description of a panel including Lastorino should be read within that context: it is evidence of operational delegation, not proof that he displaced the imprisoned or fugitive leaders as sole boss. [1]

Back to contents

§15. The 1990 murder of James Bishop

James Bishop, a figure in the New York painters’ union context, was murdered on May 17, 1990, during an episode of organized-crime violence and labor racketeering. A federal Labor Department inspector-general report names Amuso and Casso in the account of the order and describes the broader prosecution of the painting industry. [5]

Some later narratives assign Lastorino a role as a lookout or blocker. In this edition that is identified only as a secondary allegation; the agency summary alone does not convict Lastorino of Bishop’s homicide or describe a finding against him on that count. [3][5]

Back to contents

§16. Bruno Facciolo: the August 1990 killing

Published Mafia histories attribute the August 24, 1990 killing of Lucchese member Bruno Facciolo (also spelled Facciola) to a team involving Lastorino, Richard Pagliarulo and Louis Daidone. They describe a Brooklyn garage and the use of a dead canary as part of the crime-scene staging. [3][13]

This account should be framed as later witness-based reporting. The 1994 Casso suppression decision established neither Lastorino’s guilt of that killing nor the exact role of every participant. The spelling variants in the sources have been retained so researchers do not mistake them for separate victims. [1][3]

Back to contents

§17. Informant suspicions and internal violence

The violence of the Amuso–Casso period frequently hinged on allegations that an insider had begun cooperating. In several cases the organization’s suspicion outran reliable proof. Assigning that suspicion to a victim must therefore be distinguished from an independently established informant relationship. [3]

An encyclopedia should also avoid interpreting brutality as proof of a formal promotion bargain. The reported causal link between particular murders and a later administrative appointment is a hypothesis arising from witness narratives, not a self-evident rule of Mafia governance.

Back to contents

§18. Bartholomew Boriello and April 1991

Accounts identify the murder of Gambino figure Bartholomew “Bobby” Boriello on April 13, 1991 and accuse Lastorino of carrying out the attack. The allegations also appear in broader narratives about police corruption and the Amuso–Casso organization, but the sources inspected for this entry do not include a certified Lastorino trial judgment separately convicting him of the Boriello murder. [3][13]

The biography therefore does not reduce the event to a declarative claim of personal guilt. The killing and the charge or attribution of participation are distinct propositions requiring separate evidence.

Back to contents

§19. Peter Chiodo’s attempted murder and cooperation

Peter Chiodo, once a captain, survived a 1991 attempt on his life and became a government cooperator. The 1994 court later cited Chiodo and Alphonse D’Arco as sources for family organization, cash operations and leadership assignments. [1][11]

Chiodo’s defection had two historical consequences: it supplied detailed insider evidence, and it disrupted relationships that had previously enabled leaders to operate without conventional business records. It also means that much of the later reconstruction is based on testimony given after serious internal violence. [1]

Back to contents

§20. Anthony Fava: reported death and uncertain responsibility

A contemporary Newsday report, preserved among National Archives records, described the death of Anthony Fava, an associate linked to Chiodo, with detailed police observations. Subsequent accounts suggested Lastorino’s possible involvement, but those suggestions do not constitute a judicially determined finding about the perpetrator. [11][3]

Where contemporary reporting can verify the victim, date and discovery, a later witness account may inform motive or suspected responsibility. The evidentiary weight should not be silently merged into one “established fact.”

Back to contents

§21. The 1991 ruling panel

The February 1994 opinion recounted witness descriptions of a four-person panel consisting of Alphonse D’Arco, Salvatore Avellino, Frank Lastorino and Anthony Baratta. It was created while Amuso and Casso were fugitives to oversee family business. [1]

Being named to that panel is more solidly documented than many dramatic later claims about lasting appointment as consigliere. A panel was a working arrangement under conditions of leader absence; it did not necessarily replace the hierarchy that recognized Amuso as boss and Casso as underboss. [1]

Back to contents

§22. D’Arco’s rupture with the organization

D’Arco began cooperating with federal authorities in September 1991. Later reporting describes an alleged plot against him at a Manhattan hotel involving Lastorino and other figures. That account helps explain the internal crisis but remains an attributed allegation here unless tied to a specific verdict or admitted conduct. [3][13]

In the following years D’Arco’s testimony became a source of financial records and hierarchical evidence. The 1994 judge specifically wrote about copies of records he retained and turned over to the FBI on deciding to cooperate. [1]

Back to contents

§23. The October 1991 induction accounts

Secondary accounts place Lastorino among participants in an October 1991 induction ceremony for new Lucchese members in Howard Beach. Related witness testimony also describes changes in crew control during this period. [3][13]

Neither attending a ceremony nor placing a member under a given captain is equivalent to a formal judicial finding of who was family consigliere. Because retrospective rosters are fluid, this chapter treats exact lineup, participant order and office as claims requiring contemporaneous support.

Back to contents

§24. Crew transfers and Richard Pagliarulo

Later testimony reproduced by a specialist researcher describes Pagliarulo taking over leadership of a crew after Lastorino’s changing assignments. The witness distinguished a continuing crew from a new captain, a detail that guards against assuming every reported personnel change represented a new organization. [3]

The resulting chains of command complicate prosecutions: a subordinate might have answered to more than one figure over time, or carried out instructions transmitted through a captain. The 1994 opinion is particularly valuable because it describes a moment when Casso bypassed another intermediary to direct funds through Lastorino. [1]

Back to contents

§25. Loansharking and internal cash flows

Criminal credit was one of the revenue streams associated with Lucchese crews. Secondary testimony attributes to Lastorino administrative influence over loansharking proceeds and related crew assignments. Such claims should be kept distinct from the cash collections the 1994 court specifically described from intercepted calls and records. [1][3]

The distinction matters in prosecution history: a document may establish an intermediary’s possession of accounts without resolving every question about who advanced an individual loan, who threatened a borrower or what statutory count attached to a particular transaction.

Back to contents

§26. Garment center and Sidney Lieberman

The federal opinion describes a January 7, 1993 telephone conversation in which Casso referred to “Sidney,” whom investigators believed to be Sidney Lieberman, a person connected to the family’s garment center interests. D’Arco and Chiodo had testified about the wider mechanism of payments, with Avellino involved in collecting money. [1]

Lastorino’s role in these conversations was as an interlocutor and channel for instructions. The opinion’s identification of “Sidney” reflects an investigator’s belief; it should not be transformed into a claim that the recorded speakers themselves supplied a full legal name. [1]

Back to contents

§27. Joseph Martinelli and Northberry Concrete

A second January 1993 conversation concerned money from Joseph Martinelli of Northberry Concrete. The judge described Lastorino’s assigned collection role and observed that Martinelli’s extortion was charged in the pending case and had figured in Amuso’s conviction. [1]

This provides one of the clearest examples of how a family captain could connect leadership to a commercial victim. It also provides a methodological check: the court describes charges, testimony and recorded discussion in different sentences, which should remain separate in the historical narrative. [1]

Back to contents

§28. Legal fees and the costs of prosecution

The January 16, 1993 call covered payment of legal expenses for co-defendants in ongoing federal proceedings. Criminal organizations must fund defense efforts while trying to maintain revenue and manage risks posed by witnesses and seizures. The 1994 opinion treated those calls as evidence relevant to the enterprise’s continuity. [1]

A payment for a lawyer is not itself an unlawful act. Its significance in this context arises from the alleged criminal source and distribution of funds, the ongoing racketeering investigation and the persons coordinating the transfer. [1]

Back to contents

§29. The George Zappola fugitive issue

An affidavit sworn December 7, 1992 supported a proposed interception of two Brooklyn cellular telephones associated with Lastorino. According to the judge, investigators believed the lines were used to contact fugitive George Zappola and hinder attempts to find him. [1]

The court found probable cause for the wiretap on the record before it. Probable cause to authorize surveillance is not the same thing as a later conviction of hindering prosecution, and the ruling should not be summarized as if it were a jury verdict on that allegation. [1]

Back to contents

§30. Confidential informants and corroboration

The wiretap affidavit cited informants, physical surveillance, pen registers and telephone records. Lastorino and Casso challenged the evidentiary sufficiency of the affidavit, including a reference to earlier testimony by Dominick Costa. Judge Eugene Nickerson held that the remaining material supported issuance of the warrant. [1]

That discussion illustrates why judicial opinions are especially useful: they preserve both the defense challenge and the court’s narrower reason for rejecting it. Selecting only the government’s summary would conceal contested credibility issues. [1]

Back to contents

§31. The December 1992 wiretap and January 1993 intercepts

The intercept chronology in the federal opinion spans a December 7, 1992 affidavit, January 1993 recorded conversations and a subsequent attempt to locate Casso using the cellular connections. The defense also raised a sealing-timeliness argument; the court found the sealing procedure acceptable under then-controlling precedent. [1]

These procedural dates are independently stated in a reported decision and provide firmer chronological anchors than retrospective recollections of meetings. They also show that electronic evidence must satisfy statutory requirements before it can be used in a prosecution. [1]

Back to contents

§32. Locating Casso through cellular evidence

Federal investigators traced a cellular call to a region of northern New Jersey where they suspected Casso had been hiding. In January 1993 Casso was captured at a Budd Lake residence. The judicial opinion outlines the investigative chain and rejects the claim that the interception was unnecessary simply because agents had already suspected the address. [1]

Lastorino’s recorded contact with Casso is therefore meaningful as evidence of a functioning communication channel during the fugitive period. It does not require inventing conversations or meetings for which the published text contains no details. [1]

Back to contents

§33. The searched residences and financial paperwork

The government sought warrants for the residences of Avellino and Lastorino. The court summarized records found at Casso’s hiding place, including alleged criminal proceeds, proposed-membership lists, crew information and material linked to accounts prepared by D’Arco. [1]

The opinion’s structure is important: papers recovered from Casso’s location must not be casually reassigned to Lastorino’s home. The published discussion distinguishes the different properties, warrants and affidavits. [1]

Back to contents

§34. The 1992 New York gun-search ruling

In People v. Lastorino, the New York Appellate Division affirmed a lower court ruling suppressing a firearm and statements obtained after a traffic stop. The lower court found the police account of the supposed plain-view gun implausible; the appellate court found no reason to disturb that credibility assessment. [2]

The outcome was dismissal of the state indictment following suppression. It was not a finding that Lastorino was convicted of unlawful gun possession. It also should not be conflated with the separate federal Casso wiretap litigation decided in 1994. [2][1]

Back to contents

§35. Why the two suppression proceedings must not be conflated

The state gun case concerned an officer’s conduct during a road stop and the credibility of an account used to justify a vehicle search. The federal case concerned intercepted communications, sufficiency of wiretap applications, tape sealing and searches linked to organized-crime investigations. [1][2]

Their results differed because their facts and legal contexts differed. A reader cannot infer from suppression in 1992 that all subsequent federal surveillance was unlawful; in 1994 the judge rejected significant suppression challenges. [1][2]

Back to contents

§36. 1993 indictment and pretrial posture

By February 1994, Lastorino, Casso, Avellino and other defendants were named in a seventy-two-count federal indictment that encompassed numerous racketeering, homicide-related, extortion and labor offenses. The court addressed motions involving Lastorino’s wiretaps and residential search. [1]

A multi-defendant count total is the number of counts in the indictment as described by the court, not proof that Lastorino personally pleaded guilty to seventy-two offenses. The precise count allocation and ultimate disposition require his own plea agreement and judgment. [1]

Back to contents

§37. The 1994 suppression decision

On February 8, 1994 Judge Eugene H. Nickerson ruled on pretrial challenges in United States v. Casso. He sustained the core wiretap authorization and rejected challenges to warrants while reserving an evidentiary hearing concerning the older Jaguar recordings connected to Avellino. [1]

It is a pretrial evidentiary opinion. Its references to “defendants,” “alleged” leadership positions and government affidavits must be read in context: it is not a finding that every accusation in the indictment had been proved beyond a reasonable doubt. [1]

§38. Cooperating witnesses and disputed recollections

The government relied heavily on former Lucchese members, including D’Arco and Chiodo, in reconstructing the hierarchy. Those witnesses had extraordinary firsthand access but also incentives and the potential for selective memory, especially when discussing incidents after violent internal conflicts. [1]

Court-recorded cross-examination, corroborating papers and intercepts give the historical account more discipline than a single memoir. This entry therefore marks witness-attributed events separately and gives the judicial record precedence where it speaks directly to the issue. [1][3]

Back to contents

§39. The reported guilty plea and prison term

Available secondary records agree broadly that Lastorino entered a federal racketeering-related guilty plea and received an eighteen-year prison term. They disagree about date: an October 2026 archival reconstruction gives sentencing on September 16, 1994, whereas a widely repeated tertiary summary places the plea and sentence in June 1995. [3][13]

No certified Lastorino judgment and plea transcript were retrieved to resolve that conflict. Accordingly this entry reports the eighteenth-year term as secondary-source information and explicitly withholds a verified exact sentence date; it does not claim to have audited individual admitted racketeering acts. [3][13]

Back to contents

§40. Alleged plot against John A. Gotti and others

Some retrospective narratives identify plots involving John A. Gotti, Nicholas Corozzo or rival Lucchese personnel and place Lastorino in a proposed campaign of violence after 1992. They draw on cooperator-based material and crime reporting, but a dedicated adjudicated Lastorino verdict on each proposed target is not among the sources reviewed. [3][13]

This is not evidence that no such plan existed. It is a statement of the boundary between allegations and independently verified court outcomes. The biography avoids assigning a specific murder or conspiracy conviction without the relevant count and judgment. [1]

Back to contents

§41. Was Lastorino formally consigliere?

Numerous later profiles call Lastorino a former consigliere. However, the 1994 federal opinion—nearer the events under discussion—describes him as a captain and a member of the operational panel. Research based on witness statements suggests a possible acting-consigliere appointment but notes uncertainty about transitions in crew command. [1][3]

The supported formulation is therefore “captain and ruling-panel member; later described in some accounts as consigliere.” A definitive term of service as formal consigliere would require better dated organizational testimony or law-enforcement documentation. [1][3]

§42. Rival power centers within the family

The Amuso–Casso hierarchy developed tensions between Brooklyn-focused violent crews and other family factions. Reports of demotions, shelvings and changes in control followed Amuso’s imprisonment and Casso’s arrest and cooperation. The specialist reconstruction says Lastorino and Casso were later sidelined by Amuso. [3]

Because this is primarily a witness-based and secondary reconstruction, the precise date and underlying cause of any such disciplinary decision should not be treated as a judicial finding. The category of “shelved” itself is an internal Mafia term rather than a legal status. [3]

Back to contents

§43. Prison and the end of his operational role

Lastorino’s incarceration disrupted whatever command or collection functions he had performed in the early 1990s. Secondary histories place the sentence at eighteen years and describe a release in December 2008. The original Bureau of Prisons inmate-location entry was referenced by earlier researchers but has not been independently obtained for this edition. [3][13]

Statements that he remained an active decision-maker throughout the prison term, or resumed control afterward, require separate proof. This page does not presume continuous organizational power simply because older histories continue listing him among former leadership figures.

Back to contents

§44. December 2008 release: reported date

The commonly repeated release date is December 23, 2008. That date appears in tertiary summaries, with an underlying citation to the Federal Bureau of Prisons inmate locator. The source list here identifies the indirect provenance rather than representing a current, person-specific BOP record as freshly accessed. [13]

Release from federal custody is not the same as exoneration, and it does not itself establish renewed criminal activity. The surviving legal materials address the earlier decades; a responsible biography does not fill later years with unverified allegations.

Back to contents

§45. Carl Lastorino’s 2011 shooting: separate responsibility

In June 2011, contemporary reporting documented Carl Lastorino’s shooting of Peter Argentina at a tire shop followed by Carl’s fatal encounter with police. The reports identify Carl as Frank Lastorino’s son and separately discuss the father’s past Lucchese affiliation. [9][10]

Crucially, the contemporaneous accounts did not establish that Frank directed the attack; one report stated investigators did not believe it was a sanctioned mob hit. The event belongs in the family’s later public chronology but must not be recast as a criminal act by Frank Lastorino. [9][10]

Back to contents

§46. Later years and reports of inactivity

A specialist retrospective quoting a later cooperating member suggests that Lastorino remained out of favor with the Lucchese hierarchy after leaving custody. The underlying statement was not compared to an original deposition or hearing transcript for this page. [3]

No independently verified post-release managerial appointment or renewed criminal conviction was found in the materials used here. Absence of identified records is a publication limit, not proof of absolute inactivity.

Back to contents

§47. Death and memorial record, November 2022

An obituary aggregation identifies Lastorino’s death on November 5, 2022, with funeral arrangements held in Brooklyn and Howard Beach later that month. A separate biographical index gives the same date and lists his age as eighty-three. [7][8]

The cause of death is not stated as fact here. No original death certificate, clinical file or independently authenticated medical report was reviewed. The death date is more secure than claims about the circumstances of death. [7]

§48. The place of Frank Lastorino in Lucchese history

Lastorino is historically distinctive because a published court opinion places him at a practical junction between the fugitive Casso, cash collection, family leadership and telephone surveillance in the winter of 1992–1993. This is a rare window into the mechanics of a Mafia family while its senior hierarchy was under sustained federal pressure. [1]

A measured biography can incorporate grave murder allegations without erasing the legal difference between a source’s accusation, a defendant’s charged count, a guilty plea and a judicial finding. The strongest conclusion is about the documented organizational function, not an unverified total of victims. [1][3]

§49. Historiography: memoir, cooperator, court, and obituary

Four source types shape the modern Lastorino biography. First are published federal and state opinions. Second are testimony and investigative summaries, including statements by former Mafia members. Third are retrospective organized-crime histories, which often merge those accounts. Fourth are the memorial sources relevant to basic life dates. [1][2][3][7]

These categories cannot simply be counted as four independent confirmations. A specialist blog may reproduce a trial witness; a tertiary encyclopedia may copy that blog; a later summary may cite the same witness again. Independent corroboration requires separate evidentiary origins.

§50. Methods for evaluating murder allegations

For each named victim, the relevant questions are: was the death independently established, was Lastorino charged, what act was attributed to him, did he admit conduct, and was that act proved through a verdict or judgment? The answers may differ sharply across the Bishop, Facciolo, Boriello and Fava accounts. [3][5][11]

This page does not assign a single aggregate homicide count as if it were a court-certified number. The narrative distinguishes the documented murders from secondary attributions of participation and invites future verification against case-specific indictments and judgments.

§51. Legal methods: allegation versus result

A published ruling on a suppression motion states the court’s view of probable cause, admissibility or search authority. It does not ordinarily decide all elements of every charged offense. Similarly, a civil investigative report can identify patterns while not substituting for a criminal jury verdict. [1][2][4]

The Lastorino case is a useful teaching example because a state weapons indictment was dismissed following suppression, while a distinct federal court later approved significant electronic-surveillance evidence. The different outcomes should not be collapsed into a single claim about whether “the government case” was legal. [1][2]

§52. Questions for further archival work

The outstanding priorities are Lastorino’s original birth documentation; certified 1993–1995 plea and judgment records; full wiretap exhibits and returns; any certified homicide-count dispositions; and the original BOP release data. These are identifiable tasks, not gaps to fill with confident narrative invention.

A second research line would compare the quoted D’Arco and DeFede testimony against court stenographic transcripts, identify which meetings can be independently dated, and check whether Lastorino ever held a formal rather than acting consigliere title. [3]

§53. Interpretive conclusion

Frank Lastorino was a senior Lucchese figure whose captaincy and ruling-panel role are supported by a 1994 federal court opinion. He was central enough to the enterprise for investigators to intercept his communications with Casso and to cite their exchanges about extortion collections, legal expenses and organization finances. [1]

The documented record also demands restraint. The 1992 state case was dismissed, his exact federal sentencing date remains disputed in readily available secondary sources, several murder narratives derive from witnesses or retrospective accounts rather than separately verified judgments, and the 2011 actions of his son were not legally his own. [2][3][9]

Documentary Research Windows

These eight windows describe how to read and evaluate source material. They are not verbatim quotations, manuscript collations or images of archival documents.

Window 1: 1992 state appellate decision

Read the July 13, 1992 People v. Lastorino opinion for the police testimony, suppression ruling and affirmed dismissal. It is a procedural judgment in a state gun case, not a federal organized-crime acquittal.

Source links: [2]

Window 2: December 1992 wiretap affidavit

The 1994 opinion describes the affidavit’s sources: informants, telephone records, surveillance and connections to fugitive George Zappola. The court rejected the principal probable-cause challenge. This window is analysis, not a facsimile of the affidavit.

Source links: [1]

Window 3: January 7, 1993 monitored call

The court recounts an exchange involving garment-center money and “Sidney,” identified by the investigating agent as Sidney Lieberman. Attribution of the name should remain the government’s inference, not an invented full conversation.

Source links: [1]

Window 4: January 16, 1993 monitored call

The published opinion recounts directions about cash transfers, legal fees, Martinelli and Robert Amuso. It shows operational coordination without equating every discussed item to a separate conviction.

Source links: [1]

Window 5: 1994 residential search warrants

Court-described recovered papers at Casso’s hiding place must not be falsely relocated to Lastorino’s house. Separate the address, warrant, search and disposition in any later primary-source collation.

Source links: [1]

Window 6: James Bishop and labor oversight

The U.S. Labor Department inspector-general report names the painting-industry context and homicide. It documents agency conclusions while remaining distinct from an individual Lastorino homicide judgment.

Source links: [5]

Window 7: Competing sentencing chronologies

The 2026 specialist account dates eighteen-year sentencing to September 16, 1994; the common tertiary list gives June 1995. Compare a certified EDNY docket, plea minutes, judgment and BOP number before locking a date.

Source links: [3][13]

Window 8: Obituary and 2011 family-event records

The 2022 obituary supplies a dated memorial. The 2011 reports concern Carl Lastorino, not Frank as actor. These sources offer end-of-life and family context, not evidence of a renewed organized-crime offense by Frank.

Source links: [7][9][10]

Research and Publication Limitations

No original birth certificate, certified death certificate, Lastorino-specific federal plea colloquy, signed sentencing judgment, full trial transcripts, original BOP locator return or underlying homicide-forensic files were independently collated for this edition. The conflict between September 1994 and June 1995 sentence accounts remains unresolved. Reported 1987 induction details, formal consigliere title, crew roster changes and specific murder roles are attributed to secondary sources where necessary.

Contemporary official documents are prioritized for what they directly establish. A state suppression dismissal is not a federal acquittal; a 72-count multi-defendant indictment is not a Lastorino guilty plea to 72 crimes; a witness attribution is not a murder conviction; and Carl Lastorino’s 2011 conduct is not treated as Frank Lastorino’s conduct. The document contains no unlicensed embedded photographs, no fabricated verbatim quotations and no claim of live deployment.
