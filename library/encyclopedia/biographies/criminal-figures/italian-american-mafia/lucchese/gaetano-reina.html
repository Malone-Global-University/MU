1. Historical Overview

Gaetano “Tommy” Reina (1889–1930) led the Bronx- and East Harlem-based Mafia group that historians identify as the earliest recognizable predecessor of today’s Lucchese crime family. He died in a shotgun attack on February 26, 1930, when struggles over territory, revenue, influence and allegiance were destabilizing New York’s Italian-American underworld. His organization survived him, but the contest to control it became one of the central narratives of the 1930–31 Castellammarese War. [1][2][3]

Reina’s biography is not a straightforward account of one man founding a modern institution with a fixed name. It is an account of an immigrant underworld network becoming an autonomous family before its future identity was established. The contemporary shooting and the subsequent appointment of Bonaventura “Joe” Pinzolo are well attested; specific claims about an assassin, an advance alliance with Salvatore Maranzano and a formal founding date are substantially less secure. [1][2][7]

2. Name, Birth and Identification

The name most commonly given in later Mafia histories is Gaetano Reina, with “Tommy” or “Tom” used as the familiar English-language name. A birth date of September 27, 1889, in Corleone, Sicily, appears in biographical research and published genealogical summaries. Historians should distinguish this repeated date from the certified civil-registration record: a directly inspected Italian birth act is not part of the present source packet. [1][3]

Reina was about forty at death, consistent with the age given by the February 27, 1930, newspaper coverage. Earlier household and occupational records sometimes render the personal name differently. The existence of name variants is commonplace in immigrant records and not evidence for several different bosses. [3][8]

3. Corleone and Migration

Reina came from Corleone, a western Sicilian town that produced several figures prominent in the early New York Mafia. The migration story matters because family, neighborhood and hometown connections helped determine whom people trusted, sponsored and financed. It would be an error, however, to assign an organized-crime career to Reina merely because of his birthplace. [1][3][11]

Petepiece places Reina’s American arrival in 1906, aboard the Sicilian Prince; Hunt describes immigration as a minor in the early 1900s. Both portray an early transition into the East Harlem world around 107th Street. The precise passenger manifest, ship roster and naturalization trail warrant direct archival inspection before making 1906 an unqualified official fact. [1][3]

4. The East Harlem Setting

East Harlem contained dense immigrant residential blocks, laundries, markets, contractors and taverns as well as networks organized around mutual aid and sometimes coercion. Reina’s early life is associated with the 107th Street neighborhood. The presence of criminal leaders there did not mean that Italian or Sicilian residents generally participated in their enterprises. [1][3][11]

The neighborhood’s spatial importance is easier to document than a precise criminal apprenticeship. It connected people and businesses across 106th–116th Streets and outward into the Bronx. Personal acquaintances could become commercial partners, political brokers or criminal associates; each role requires separate evidence, not merely a shared address. [1][11]

5. Census and Early Employment

Petepiece’s reconstructed chronology identifies Reina in the 1910 federal census near 107th Street with “laborer” as an occupation and places him at 227 East 107th Street around 1913. The same account links him to a laundry at 339 East 107th Street around 1914. These are testable genealogical leads, but the underlying enumeration images and employment records were not directly reproduced for this edition. [3]

An early laborer designation and later wealth from ice distribution are compatible: businessmen in the urban service economy could begin with manual work. The census does not establish membership in a secret criminal association, and an occupation appearing on a census should not be retrospectively relabeled as a front without corroboration. [3]

6. The Morello–Terranova Milieu

Many reconstructions situate the early Reina organization within or near the older Morello network. Giuseppe Morello’s imprisonment in 1910 weakened that network’s centralized command and opened opportunities for rivals and lieutenants. Reina’s exact chain of command before independence remains harder to document than the fact that he subsequently headed a separate group. [1][3][11]

The Morello connection is a genealogy of criminal organization, not proof of a modern corporate-style division. Later historians often impose neat family labels on relationships that were fluid and contentious. Where an account says Reina “split from the Morellos,” it should be read as a plausible organizational reconstruction, not as a dated merger or separation instrument. [3][11]

7. When Did the Reina Family Begin?

Popular succession lists variously start Reina’s independent leadership around 1920 or 1922. A more conservative formulation, based on Hunt’s synthesis, is that Reina appears to have established his own Mafia organization by the middle of the 1920s. Petepiece explicitly acknowledges the lack of primary evidence establishing the founding moment. [1][3]

The difference matters for reference works: naming a single creation year suggests documentary precision that is not available. The page therefore uses “by the mid-1920s” and treats early-1920s dates as estimates. The social connections were older than the fully differentiated New York family later associated with Lucchese. [1][3]

8. Bronx and Harlem Organizational Base

At the end of the 1920s, Reina’s network operated across the Bronx and parts of Manhattan’s East Harlem. His status as a Mafia leader is supported by both later insider testimony and the events following his death: another boss was promptly installed, powerful subordinates rebelled, and the organization continued as a distinct unit. [1][2][7]

What survives is not a reliable full membership list or a modern personnel chart for 1929. Labels such as “captain” and “underboss” may describe actual relationships, but exact assignments are not always fixed across sources. It is better to identify Gagliano and Lucchese as central lieutenants than to insist upon every disputed formal title. [1][3][5]

9. Tommaso Gagliano

Tommaso “Tommy” Gagliano was one of Reina’s closest and most consequential subordinates. Through the early years of the Bronx organization he had important business and criminal connections, and he became the focal point for men who opposed Masseria’s effort to shape the group’s succession. Later histories often identify him as Reina’s underboss, though titles reconstructed from informants should be handled with care. [1][3][5]

Gagliano is historically important because his rise shows that Reina’s group did not evaporate with the assassination. The successor organization under Gagliano was the bridge between the prewar Reina network and the later family named after Lucchese. It is misleading to treat Gagliano’s later tenure as evidence that Reina planned or approved the succession. [1][2]

10. Gaetano “Tommy” Lucchese

Gaetano Lucchese, an East Harlem street criminal who became a skilled organizer, joined Reina and Gagliano’s circle amid the changing alliances of the late 1920s. He would later become the dominant public name associated with the family, but in Reina’s lifetime he was one influential member of an emerging leadership group, not the family’s namesake. [1][5]

Contemporaneous evidence for Lucchese’s early rackets, arrests and associations is more extensive than for many comparable figures. Those details belong partly in his own biography. For Reina, the important point is the network connection: Lucchese’s opposition to Pinzolo in 1930 helped keep Reina’s original group coherent even as its leadership changed. [1][14]

11. Other Associates and the Limits of a Roster

The early organization’s associates appear in later accounts of the war, including Stefano Rannelli and men linked to Gagliano and Lucchese. Joseph Valachi, whose 1963 testimony helped popularize the later sequence, was brought into the group after Reina’s death, during Pinzolo’s period. Valachi was not therefore an eyewitness to every decision in Reina’s career. [1][2][7]

This distinction prevents retrospective inflation of Reina’s known membership. Being associated with the successor family, knowing a boss, or appearing in a later Senate hearing does not automatically establish a documented rank in Reina’s organization before February 1930. [2][3]

12. Legitimate Commerce and Illicit Influence

Contemporary reporting called Reina a prosperous wholesale ice dealer. His commercial work was real enough to be described in the press: routes, vehicles, customers and the intense seasonal need for refrigeration created considerable earning potential. These activities coexisted with his leadership of a Mafia group rather than proving that every transaction was criminal. [1][3][8]

A rigorous account separates the legal supply of an essential commodity from allegations of coercive market control. The ability of a business leader to influence competing vendors, suppliers or drivers can create leverage without a visible criminal transaction in every exchange. The term “ice racket” should therefore refer to an alleged coercive business system, not an assumption that selling ice was itself illegal. [1][3]

13. Why Ice Distribution Mattered

Before household electric refrigeration became widespread, homes, restaurants and stores depended on regular delivery of ice. Delivery territories, plant capacity, storage, customer lists and reliable daily routes could give established dealers an advantage. In a crowded metropolitan market, route disputes could become financially and physically significant. [3]

Petepiece cites a 1925 study of the city’s huge ice demand and contemporaneous complaints about dealer combinations. Those quantitative statements deserve verification against the original study and regulator records, but the underlying structural point is clear: control over ice deliveries could confer an enduring local economic position. [3]

14. Wealth, Trucks and Newspaper Estimates

Press accounts after Reina’s death portrayed him as wealthy and linked him to several ice-delivery trucks, multiple automobiles and a large family residence. Petepiece repeats a period newspaper claim that his fortune may have reached one million dollars. Such figures are historically interesting as reporting, but they are not audited valuations of assets or criminal proceeds. [3][8]

In this biography, claims about wealth are attributed to contemporary newspapers rather than translated into a modern net-worth ranking. They show how Reina appeared to journalists and investigators in 1930; they do not independently establish ownership percentages, the financing of his businesses, or the precise share of income obtained by coercion. [1][3]

15. Marriage, Children and Household

Secondary reconstructions identify Reina’s wife as Angelina, often given in genealogies as Angela or Angelina Oliveri, and report a household with nine children. Hunt locates their family home at 3183 Rochambeau Avenue in the Bronx. The volume of press coverage after Reina’s murder brought his family into a public story they did not control. [1][3]

Relatives should not be conflated with members of a criminal organization. Reina’s children and spouse were civilians whose relationships became visible because he was killed. Statements about individual family members’ later lives should be based on independent records and not inferred from the father’s role. [1][3]

16. Mildred Reina and Joseph Valachi

A daughter, commonly called Carmela “Mildred” Reina, later married Joseph Valachi. Their relationship became historically salient because Valachi eventually supplied extensive testimony about the Mafia and the events surrounding the 1930–31 war. The marriage occurred after Reina’s death and cannot be treated as evidence that Reina personally sponsored Valachi’s membership. [2][3]

The connection illuminates how family and underworld histories can intersect across generations. Yet Valachi’s later testimony is not a substitute for documents from 1930, and family relationship alone does not turn his retrospective account into eyewitness evidence of a confidential order to kill Reina. [2][7]

17. The Barnet Baff Murder Allegations

The 1914 murder of poultry dealer Barnet Baff generated multiple accounts of an underworld contract and recurring references to Reina. Petepiece reconstructs statements implicating Reina and describes how witnesses later recanted or substantially revised their testimony. In one account Frank Ferrara said officials supplied Reina’s name. The evidentiary posture changed repeatedly. [3][10]

Reina was not established as Baff’s murderer by any conviction found in the reviewed record. Historical accounts should explain the contradictory witness statements and the fact of the investigation without turning accusation into proven participation. Baff’s murder also resurfaced as one theory in the reporting on Reina’s own 1930 death. [1][3][10]

18. Why the Baff File Cannot Establish Guilt

The Baff investigation is a case study in the difference between accusation, sworn testimony, recantation and adjudication. Petepiece reports that witnesses attributed the killing to different combinations of participants at different moments; the prosecution of some men did not prove the involvement of everyone named in those statements. [3]

For this reason the Baff affair is recorded as an allegation with conflicting statements. It supplies evidence of Reina’s exposure to violent commercial disputes in the 1910s, but not a reliable factual basis for declaring that he committed or ordered that homicide. [3][10]

19. Prohibition and the Wider Underworld

National Prohibition in 1920 expanded illicit alcohol opportunities in New York, and competition over supply, enforcement and territory reshaped relationships among criminal groups. Reina’s organization emerged within this environment, though surviving direct evidence about his personal bootlegging accounts is thinner than evidence of the ice trade and his leadership. [1][4]

The underworld’s commercial networks overlapped: an individual could be involved in lawful distribution, protection, gambling and illegal alcohol without every activity belonging to one integrated “cartel.” Avoid assigning to Reina the specific earnings or operations of other Bronx figures simply because they fought over the same neighborhoods. [1][3]

20. Giuseppe Masseria and the Balance of Power

Giuseppe “Joe the Boss” Masseria exercised wide influence in New York’s Italian-American Mafia by the late 1920s. Reina appears to have accommodated this power for a time while preserving his own organization. Bonanno’s autobiography later portrayed him as trying not to offend Masseria. Because this description is retrospective, it should be treated as the memoirist’s assessment rather than a transcript of Reina’s private thinking. [1][6]

Masseria’s influence matters for understanding what followed the assassination. He backed the replacement boss, Pinzolo, and thereby intervened in the autonomy of the group. That subsequent intervention is a major reason historians suspect Masseria’s role in the killing; it does not by itself identify the shooter or prove the exact prior motive. [1][2][4]

21. The 1928 Assassination of Salvatore D’Aquila

The killing of Salvatore D’Aquila in October 1928 disrupted established balances among prominent Mafia leaders and increased Masseria’s perceived reach. Reina’s already important local position therefore existed within a changing regional order. The murder did not automatically create an immediate, unified two-camp war stretching to Reina’s last night. [4][11]

The period between late 1928 and early 1930 involved shifting gangs, business conflicts and personal antagonisms. A narrative that treats everything after D’Aquila as part of a single, preplanned “Castellammarese War” risks flattening a chronology that contemporaries did not necessarily see in the same terms. [1][2]

22. Schultz, the Coll Gang and Bronx Conflicts

Hunt’s reconstruction places Reina’s associates in an environment where Dutch Schultz and the Terranova circle confronted the emerging opposition around Peter and Vincent Coll. Some associates aligned with competing sides of the Bronx and East Harlem underworld, and there were violent incidents before Reina was killed. The extent of Reina’s personal direction of particular attacks remains uncertain. [1]

This local conflict provides an alternative or complementary explanation for his vulnerability. Reina’s political position was not a private duel with one other Mafia boss; he operated amid multiple networks whose alliances cut across ethnicity, criminal trade and borough borders. Speculation should not become proof of direct responsibility for unsolved robberies or shootings. [1][11]

23. The Roman Gardens Robbery as a Contextual Clue

One notorious December 1929 incident involved robbers attacking political and underworld guests at a Bronx banquet. Hunt suggests the emerging anti-Schultz coalition may have had connections to the event, but explicitly notes that the robbers were not definitively identified. The incident helps illustrate the instability of the neighborhood without making Reina a convicted or established organizer. [1]

It is tempting to convert the chronology of violence into a simple sequence of orders from Reina. The source record does not permit that simplification. This page treats the robbery as part of the surrounding conflict and leaves the identification of its perpetrators open. [1]

24. Salvatore Maranzano: An Alliance in Dispute

Many later tellings say that Reina had aligned himself with Salvatore Maranzano before his murder. Bonanno presented a version in which Reina’s private preference for Maranzano reached Masseria. Hunt points out a serious chronological problem: the major Maranzano-led coalition is commonly associated with developments after Gaspare Milazzo’s May 1930 murder, months after Reina died. [1][6]

Reina could have had private reservations about Masseria without possessing a formally established alliance with Maranzano. The distinction is material. This profile does not state a confirmed Reina–Maranzano defection before February 26, 1930, and it presents the familiar “he switched sides and was immediately killed” story as an interpretation, not an incontrovertible timeline. [1][2]

25. The Morello Informant Story

Another story attributed to Bonanno holds that private criticism of Masseria was conveyed to Giuseppe Morello, who then passed it along. The chain is plausible given old Corleone contacts and Morello’s proximity to Masseria. But the story rests on retrospective insider recollection, not a surviving letter, recorded conversation or contemporaneous judicial finding. [1][6][11]

Its historical value lies in illustrating the suspected mechanism of political vulnerability: an apparently independent boss might have private views exposed through mutual acquaintances. Whether this was the decisive cause of Reina’s death cannot be established from the reviewed sources. [1][6]

26. The Ice-Distribution Motive

Valachi’s later account and several histories attribute Reina’s killing to Masseria’s efforts to take control of profitable Bronx ice-distribution interests. Economic competition is a strong interpretive candidate because Reina’s commercial position was well publicized and Pinzolo’s appointment gave Masseria leverage over the group. [2][3][7]

A motive reconstructed decades after the fact is not the same as a proven conspiracy. Other contemporaneous theories involved rival dealers or old poultry-market grievances, while newer documentary historians emphasize the broader Bronx gang war. The responsible conclusion is that ice distribution was a significant possible issue, not the only conclusively demonstrated trigger. [1][3][8]

27. Rochambeau Avenue and Sheridan Avenue

Reina’s family home was at 3183 Rochambeau Avenue, in the Bronx, according to Hunt. In late 1929 he and Maria Ennis reportedly occupied an apartment in the 1511–1521 Sheridan Avenue complex under the name “Mr. James Ennis.” Distinguishing home from the location of the fatal attack is essential because some later retellings confuse the two. [1][3]

The 1930 New York Times account, as cited by subsequent researchers, locates the murder at the Sheridan Avenue apartment building. The New York police record later read in the Senate hearing says 1522 Sheridan Avenue; Hunt gives 1521. Petepiece briefly gives a Rochambeau address for the shooting before elsewhere citing Sheridan. These are documented source discrepancies, not grounds to invent a single exact street number. [1][2][3][8]

28. Maria Ennis and Contemporary Reporting

Maria Ennis, reported in newspapers as the woman accompanying Reina on his final evening, appears repeatedly in accounts of the murder. Reporting about her private relationship with Reina was sensational and often framed through moralizing language. Such reporting is evidence of public reaction and newspaper practice, not a reliable complete account of her identity or motives. [1][3][9]

Names and aliases in the newspaper accounts can be confusing: “James Ennis” reportedly was the name under which Reina presented himself at the apartment. A scholar should avoid confusing this alias, the name of the woman with him, and Reina’s own family residence. The key homicide facts do not depend on that tabloid framing. [1][8]

29. February 26, 1930: The Shooting

On Wednesday evening, February 26, 1930, Reina was attacked outside the apartment building associated with Maria Ennis in the Bronx. The 1963 Senate hearing’s NYPD summary fixes the event at approximately 8:10 p.m. and describes an unknown shooter firing both barrels of a sawed-off shotgun. Contemporary newspaper reporting places the scene on Sheridan Avenue and records that Reina died after being taken to Morrisania Hospital. [1][2][7][8]

The murder is a firm event; the identity of the murderer is not. The police record as read into the hearing did not name a shooter, and subsequent historians have disagreed about the exact address and the men allegedly responsible. This page therefore attributes assassination theories and avoids representing any particular suspect as legally established. [1][2]

30. Weapons, Cash and the Police Investigation

Later historical accounts, drawing on contemporary coverage, report that police found a revolver and cash on Reina and an additional rifle or ammunition in his vehicle. These are descriptions of material said to have been seized or discovered; the complete police inventory and chain of custody were not independently examined here. [3]

The presence of a firearm might be consistent with fear of attack, ordinary weapons possession, or other circumstances; it does not identify the assailant. A serious homicide biography should avoid using dramatic inventory details as shortcuts to a speculative conclusion about either the motive or the personal guilt of other people. [3][8]

31. Who Fired the Shotgun?

Some secondary accounts identify Vito Genovese as the shooter and describe an order coming from Masseria, sometimes through Luciano. Other reconstructions speak more cautiously of a member of the Masseria faction. The October 1963 police-record testimony preserved in the Senate hearings explicitly used “unknown” for the gunman. [1][2][5][7]

The distinction is especially important in a public encyclopedia. A person named by a later historical writer has not thereby been convicted, conclusively identified by contemporary police, or shown through surviving physical evidence to have fired the weapon. Vito Genovese may be discussed as a reported assassin, never as a proven legal finding in this case. [2][5][7]

32. Who Ordered Reina’s Death?

Masseria is widely suspected of ordering Reina’s killing because he subsequently supported the appointment of Pinzolo, a figure viewed by some Reina loyalists as imposed from outside. Valachi’s later testimony repeated the belief that Masseria was behind the attack. The link is historically substantial but principally retrospective or inferential. [1][2][7]

Hunt’s research emphasizes that the local Schultz–Terranova conflict may also explain the targeting, and he questions overly tidy claims about Reina secretly joining Maranzano before his death. A fair assessment is that Masseria’s responsibility is a serious and influential interpretation, not a proven homicide conviction or a unanimously resolved documentary fact. [1][11]

33. What Newspapers Believed in 1930

Contemporaneous reporting looked for explanations in rivalry among ice dealers and older violence in the poultry trade, including the Barnet Baff case. Reporters knew Reina as a wealthy ice wholesaler and covered details of his private life. They did not possess the later organized-crime framework popularized by the Valachi testimony. [1][8][9][10]

These early reports should neither be dismissed as ignorant nor accepted wholesale. They provide strong dating, location and public-description evidence while leaving the full criminal conspiracy unknown. Later stories about Mafia politics may clarify some connections, but they can also impose retrospective coherence on an initially unsolved homicide. [1][2]

34. Immediate Organizational Consequences

The killing created a leadership vacancy in a group that operated in a commercially valuable and contested territory. Masseria’s backing of Bonaventura Pinzolo was taken by Gagliano and Lucchese as interference with the family’s internal authority. Later accounts agree broadly that Reina’s death was followed by a contested succession. [1][2][3]

This is the hinge that makes Reina important well beyond his relatively short career. The struggle to determine who would control his former organization helped reorganize alliances in New York’s Mafia. It was not simply the loss of an individual businessman or an isolated criminal shooting. [1][4]

35. Bonaventura “Joe” Pinzolo as Successor

Bonaventura “Joe” Pinzolo, whose surname is also spelled Pinzola, became boss of Reina’s former organization with Masseria’s support. Historians differ on how formally or openly his appointment was imposed. The key political fact is that significant members of the organization resented his elevation, especially Gagliano and Lucchese. [1][2][3]

There was no universally accepted, publicly documented constitutional procedure for choosing a 1930 Mafia boss. A claim that Masseria personally “appointed” him summarizes the power relationship, not a transparent modern election record. It should be understood as an account reconstructed from insiders and the sequence of events. [1][3]

36. The Gagliano–Lucchese Rebellion

Gagliano and Lucchese became leaders of a faction opposing Pinzolo and the perceived extension of Masseria’s authority. The rebellion was initially discreet. Later Valachi-related accounts present the conspirators as outwardly obedient while coordinating against Pinzolo and ultimately with the growing anti-Masseria camp. [1][2][7]

Precisely how many men supported each faction is contested. Petepiece cautions that some accounts overstate the size of the opposition by projecting the victors’ later prestige backward. What can be said with confidence is that the leadership struggle persisted until Pinzolo’s death and helped define the group’s subsequent independence. [1][3]

37. August 1930: Giuseppe Morello Killed

Giuseppe Morello was shot dead at an East Harlem office on August 15, 1930. This occurred nearly six months after Reina’s killing and before Pinzolo’s death. The attack further weakened Masseria’s political position and intensified the sense of an escalating conflict among entrenched New York Mafia networks. [1][2][4]

Reina was already dead and could not have personally directed that event. The chronology is important: narratives that place every war action under a single commander overlook changing allegiances and the role of subordinates. Reina’s significance at this stage lies in the disputed succession to his group, not in direct participation. [1][2]

38. September 1930: Pinzolo Killed

Pinzolo was killed in the Brokaw Building in Manhattan, where business premises were associated with Lucchese. Contemporary newspaper-based research by Hunt dates discovery of the body to Friday, September 5, 1930. In contrast, the older NYPD summary read to the 1963 Senate committee gives September 9. The conflicting dates should be preserved rather than silently harmonized. [1][2]

Police questioned Lucchese in connection with the death; the grand jury did not indict him. Valachi later attributed the actual shooting to a man known as “Bobby Doyle.” None of this establishes that Reina, who had died months earlier, participated in the killing. These events belong to the succession narrative, not to Reina’s personal conduct. [1][2][7]

39. Reina’s Place in the Castellammarese War

Many standard narratives treat the murder of Reina as the first shot of the Castellammarese War. This is useful periodization, but it can be too neat. The anti-Masseria alliance consolidated over time, and violence involving Bronx and East Harlem gangs had already been underway. The name of the war comes from the Castellammarese network around Maranzano, not from Reina’s Sicilian hometown. [1][2]

Reina’s death should be understood as a catalytic event: it made one leadership question acute and helped move Gagliano and Lucchese toward the opposition. It did not by itself fix every later coalition, nor does it establish that Reina had consciously joined the war under Maranzano’s command. [1][2][6]

40. Gagliano Takes Control

After the internal conflict and the defeat of Masseria’s position, Gagliano emerged as leader of the organization formerly headed by Reina and Pinzolo. Lucchese became an increasingly influential lieutenant. The group’s persistence through these transitions is central to the historical claim that the modern Lucchese family descends from the Reina organization. [1][2][3]

Exact succession dates in reference lists vary because wartime control, recognition by other bosses and formal public categorization did not always coincide. Rather than presenting an unbroken, perfectly documented sequence of ceremonial appointments, this biography describes the transition as one reconstructed from testimony, public violence and later history. [1][3]

41. Why Historians Call It the Lucchese Family

Reina never led an organization under the modern name “Lucchese crime family.” That name commemorates the later long-serving leader Gaetano “Tommy” Lucchese, and it became standard in law-enforcement and public history well after Reina died. The historical organizational lineage is Reina → Pinzolo → Gagliano → Lucchese, with disputes about certain transition details. [1][3][13]

For encyclopedia indexing, the Lucchese classification is necessary to help readers locate the biography. In the narrative, “Reina family” or “Reina organization” is preferable for the 1920s. The distinction preserves historical context while maintaining modern collection-level navigation. [1][3]

42. Joseph Valachi as a Retrospective Source

Joseph Valachi’s testimony to the U.S. Senate in 1963 was pivotal in disseminating inside descriptions of Mafia structure and early power struggles. He entered the network after Reina’s death and repeated what fellow members later told him about the reasons for the killing and the resistance to Pinzolo. [2][7]

His account contains the strengths and weaknesses of participant testimony: proximity to actors and institutions, but also decades of elapsed time, hearsay and incentives to frame a compelling narrative. The related NYPD murder-record summary supplies a different type of evidence, establishing the registered event while naming no killer. These sources should be read side by side, not collapsed into a single unambiguous “Valachi proves” statement. [2][7]

43. Conflicting Addresses and Dates: An Audit

The strongest supported shooting location is Sheridan Avenue in the Bronx. Hunt and the cited 1930 Times article identify 1521 Sheridan Avenue, whereas the later NYPD hearing summary says 1522. Petepiece includes an inconsistent Rochambeau Avenue address in one sentence, although he elsewhere discusses the Sheridan apartment. Reina’s family residence was a different address on Rochambeau Avenue. [1][2][3][8]

For the September Pinzolo murder, contemporary newspaper research supports September 5, while the 1963 police-summary presentation says September 9. Neither discrepancy undermines Reina’s own established date of death, February 26, 1930. The correct editorial procedure is to name the sources and their different readings and refrain from inventing certainty where the record diverges. [1][2]

44. Allegations, Convictions and the Absence of a Murder Judgment

Reina is historically identified as a Mafia boss, and law-enforcement or press investigations linked him to criminal disputes. But the materials reviewed do not support a comprehensive verified criminal conviction chronology. The Baff murder allegations cannot be converted into a judicial finding of Reina’s guilt; nor was the murder of Reina himself solved by a court conviction in the evidence assessed here. [2][3]

A public reference work should not report a “conviction” based merely on arrest, gossip or an informant’s account. This page explicitly differentiates the fact of his leadership, the fact of his killing and the unresolved accusations surrounding other events. [2][3][7]

45. Interpreting “Monopoly” and “Racket”

Accounts frequently say Reina “controlled” or “monopolized” Bronx ice distribution. Those words may express regional influence, intimidation, dominance of certain routes or a literal share of the commercial market. The surviving source packet does not contain a quantitative route register or a court decree establishing a total borough-wide monopoly. [1][3]

For analytical precision the biography describes his ice business as influential and his alleged coercive position as a prominent theme in the historical literature. It does not assume that every ice dealer in the Bronx answered to him or that an estimated fortune can be cleanly divided into legitimate and illicit revenue streams. [1][3]

46. Historical Significance

Reina’s significance is institutional rather than simply sensational. He led a group with a recognizable base and continuity, was killed at a moment of acute competition, and left behind a succession struggle that helped reshape New York Mafia alliances. His business position also demonstrates the value that organized-crime groups placed on mundane essential services such as ice delivery. [1][2][3]

The historical challenge is to preserve that significance without relying on a murder legend whose certainty exceeds the evidence. Reina’s assassination remains central to understanding the prehistory of the Lucchese family, yet questions of exact founding date, the assassin’s identity and the motive remain open to archival reassessment. [1][2]

47. How the Story Has Been Retold

The first major layer is 1930 newspaper reporting: ice merchant, apartment, shotgun attack and immediately proposed explanations. The second is the 1963 Valachi hearings, which supplied a structured Mafia-centered story while retaining a police record naming an unknown gunman. The third is the later memoir and scholarly literature, in which Masseria, Maranzano, Luciano and Genovese receive increasingly prominent narrative roles. [1][2][6][8]

Each layer answers different questions. A newspaper may know where the shooting occurred but little about secret hierarchy. An insider may know organizational practices yet repeat hearsay about a pre-membership murder. A modern author can reconcile contradictions but may still depend on partial archives. This profile makes those evidentiary layers explicit rather than fusing them into a single omniscient story. [1][2][3]

48. Research Priorities for a Future Edition

The most useful next evidence would be certified Corleone birth records, the 1906 passenger manifest, original census images, Bronx death certificate no. 1800, marriage and property records, the full February 27, 1930 newspaper pages, ice-industry trade proceedings and NYPD homicide case files. Collectively they could correct dates, addresses and commercial claims. [3][8][12]

Another priority would be the full 1963 hearing exhibits and the underlying police files rather than later paraphrases alone. Such documents may narrow uncertainties but should not be presumed to identify a killer in advance. Until that verification occurs, the historically honest treatment is one that remains open about what is known and unknown. [2][7]

Documentary Research Windows

The following eight windows are editorial source studies and paraphrases; they are not newly authenticated verbatim transcriptions or facsimile reproductions. Use the cited material to distinguish contemporary observation from later interpretation.

Research Window A — February 27, 1930 Newspaper Record

The contemporary New York Times report identified Reina as an ice dealer and placed the fatal attack outside a Sheridan Avenue apartment. This is evidence of how police and journalists described the scene the following day. It is not a finding about the person who ordered the killing. The available source trail includes published excerpts and bibliographic references, but the full archived page was not independently facsimile-collated in this build. [8] [1]

Research Window B — 1963 NYPD Police-Record Presentation

Sergeant Ralph Salerno read an account of a February 26, 1930, shooting by an unknown man using a sawed-off shotgun. This source has institutional weight because the witness presented a departmental entry, yet it was read decades afterward and does not resolve the 1521/1522 Sheridan discrepancy. A reader should distinguish the official entry from the Mafia witnesses’ theories about responsibility. [2] [7]

Research Window C — Valachi’s Narrative of Organizational Conflict

Valachi joined the network after Reina’s death and repeated later explanations of Masseria’s commercial interference and the opposition to Pinzolo. His testimony supplies a valuable insider picture of political reactions, not direct observation of an assassination order. The window demonstrates how to extract an organizational account from testimony without turning hearsay into physical proof. [2] [7]

Research Window D — Joseph Bonanno’s Memoir as Retrospective Interpretation

Bonanno’s memoir helps explain why Reina might have been politically vulnerable but places some 1930 milestones in a disputed order. The reader should cross-check the date of Reina’s February death with the later May murder of Milazzo and the consolidation of the anti-Masseria front. Memoir evidence is best cited with explicit attribution and a chronological check. [1] [6]

Research Window E — The Baff Investigation and Witness Recantations

Petepiece outlines successive police statements, withdrawals and changed identifications tied to the earlier killing of poultry dealer Barnet Baff. The window is a lesson in procedural precision: a named suspect is not necessarily an indicted defendant, a recanted accusation is not a conviction, and later newspaper speculation is not independent forensic evidence. No Reina murder conviction emerged from the packet studied. [3] [10]

Research Window F — Ice Trade and Market Structure

Secondary research cites the 1925 scale of New York’s ice business and later inquiries into price and competition. The case teaches how a mundane commercial service could create control over local delivery routes. Because the original industry statistics and trade-hearing minutes were not directly assembled, those figures remain research leads rather than audited economic findings. [3]

Research Window G — The Pinzolo Date Conflict

Hunt’s contemporaneous-newspaper reading dates Pinzolo’s death to September 5, 1930; the police account presented in 1963 gives September 9. This source window preserves both statements and explains why the contradiction should be flagged instead of silently choosing the later figure. The distinction has no effect on Reina’s own February date. [1] [2]

Research Window H — Organizational Naming Across Time

Modern federal materials call the organization the Lucchese crime family and describe its hierarchy, while historians call the 1920s predecessor the Reina family. The modern legal framework is valuable for identifying the current category, not for imposing a modern job chart on 1922. A sound chronology uses contemporary terms where possible and explanatory retrospective labels where necessary. [1] [3] [13]

Research and Publication Limitations

The documentary foundation is predominantly the contemporary 1930 newspaper reports as cited by specialist historians, the published 1963 Senate hearing summary of NYPD homicide entries, and carefully distinguished later scholarly and memoir reconstructions. The primary 1930 newspaper facsimiles, original death certificate, Italian birth record, passenger manifest, early census sheets, ice-industry hearing files, NYPD casebook and ballistic reports were not independently collated in this production. The linked NCJRS historical PDF was indexed but its page image was inaccessible during review; it supplies no independent factual claim here.

Some accounts assert a specific 1922 formation, an all-Bronx ice monopoly, a Vito Genovese firing squad, a completed pre-death Reina–Maranzano alliance, or a definitive murder order from Masseria. Such statements are not established to the same standard as Reina’s death and the succession dispute. The profile documents those differences and does not claim any person was convicted for Reina’s murder. Published descriptions of Pinzolo’s death also conflict over September 5 versus September 9, 1930.
