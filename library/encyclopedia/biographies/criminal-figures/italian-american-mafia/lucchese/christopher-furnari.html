1. Historical Overview

Christopher “Christie Tick” Furnari (April 30, 1924–May 28, 2018) served as a senior figure in the New York organization known as the Lucchese crime family. Official material presented to Congress in 1983, the February 1985 federal Commission indictment, and the 1989 appellate opinion identify him as consigliere—the adviser immediately below the family’s most senior leadership. His historical significance lies in the combination of internal counsel, interfamily coordination, and his prosecution in one of the most consequential organized-crime trials of the twentieth century. [1][2][3][4][5][11]

The strongest evidence for this biography is documentary rather than anecdotal: a government organizational chart, an official charging announcement, two published federal appellate decisions about parole, the Commission appeal itself, and contemporary death notices. Assertions about his early criminal activity and homicide responsibility are not placed on the same evidentiary footing as a federal conviction. The central verified criminal judgment concerns racketeering, extortion and labor bribery related to the concrete-construction industry, not a stand-alone murder conviction. [1][2][3][4][5][11]

2. Names, Nickname and Identification

Court filings consistently use Christopher Furnari. Government intelligence records and subsequent Mafia reporting employ spellings such as “Christy Tick,” “Christie Tick” and, in the 1983 New Jersey State Police material, “Cristy Tick.” The short form “Tick” and “Skipper” are also listed in the government entry. Search systems should index these spellings as aliases for one person, not as independent biographies. [1][2][3]

The relevant organizational term is consigliere, sometimes spelled consiglieri in English-language journalism. It identifies a senior advisory office rather than a criminal charge. A reader should not infer a precisely documented appointment ceremony or a continuous term spanning later prison decades simply because later leadership lists place the nickname against a range of years. [1][2][3]

3. Birth, Death and Vital Records

A Senate-published government profile identifies Furnari’s date of birth as April 30, 1924. The Staten Island funeral-home record supplies the same birth date and reports May 28, 2018 as the date of death. A notice placed in the Staten Island Advance states that he died at home at age 94. The concordance of records makes these the best-supported life dates available in this edition. [1][11][12]

The funeral notices also identify the New York funeral and burial arrangements, but neither should be stretched into a detailed account of his final medical circumstances. An original birth certificate and death certificate have not been examined for this biography. The biographical file therefore reproduces the documented dates while keeping the provenance of private-life details clear. [1][11][12]

4. Early Life and the Limits of the Record

The verified birth date places Furnari’s childhood amid the changing Italian-American neighborhoods of interwar New York. Contemporary court and congressional sources used here, however, do not supply a sufficiently detailed, independently checked childhood narrative. Specific claims about schooling, youthful occupations, family migration routes or his first organized-crime contacts require sources beyond later recollection. [1][7][11]

Rather than invent a seamless path from childhood to Mafia office, this biography begins with the adult networks that are traceable in government records. It treats the absence of early evidence as a research limitation, not as proof that particular events did or did not occur. The distinction is important when reconstructing an organization in which retrospective testimony may have been shaped by plea bargains and personal disputes. [1][7][11]

5. Brooklyn and Staten Island Connections

Furnari is strongly associated in later reporting with Brooklyn’s organized-crime milieu, including the Bensonhurst area, and with Staten Island in official records and his final years. The 1983 Senate-published intelligence page lists a Staten Island address at that time; publishing an old personal address is unnecessary to understanding his criminal-history significance. The public death notices confirm continuing Staten Island ties. [1][7][10][11]

A frequently repeated account presents the 19th Hole as an informal neighborhood meeting site for Furnari and other figures. That narrative appears prominently in retrospective Mafia coverage, not in the appellate findings that establish his convictions. It is a useful lead for subsequent research, but not a proven substitute for surveillance logs, business records or first-hand contemporaneous testimony. [1][7][10][11]

6. Lucchese Family after Thomas Lucchese

The family that Furnari later helped administer had passed through the leadership of Thomas Gagliano and Thomas Lucchese before the 1967 succession to Carmine Tramunti and the subsequent rise of Anthony Corallo. By the early 1980s prosecutors and New Jersey police intelligence described a defined hierarchy with a boss, underboss, consigliere, captains, members and associates. Furnari’s position must be understood in this changing institutional context. [1][2][3][15]

Historical ranks are partly reconstructions of how a secretive organization was believed to operate. The Senate chart is a contemporary law-enforcement representation; the Commission case proves certain enterprise activities beyond a reasonable doubt. Those are related but distinct levels of evidence. It would be a mistake to treat an intelligence diagram as a judicial finding about every listed person or activity. [1][2][3][15]

7. Relationship with Anthony Corallo

Anthony “Tony Ducks” Corallo was identified in both the 1983 and 1985 government records as head of the Lucchese family. The same materials listed Furnari as consigliere and Salvatore “Tom Mix” Santoro as underboss. This three-part arrangement gives one of the most secure portraits of the senior team during the period immediately preceding the Commission prosecution. [1][2][3]

In the sentencing discussion preserved by the Second Circuit, the trial judge described Furnari’s advisory office as practically equivalent to an underboss in operational importance. That was the judge’s evaluation of his influence and culpability, not a formal judicial declaration that Furnari had become the family’s underboss or boss. The exact division of authority in private meetings remains outside the court’s full documentary view. [1][2][3]

Back to contents

8. Salvatore Santoro and Divided Responsibilities

Santoro and Furnari held different senior titles under Corallo. Government organizational records list Santoro as underboss and Furnari as consigliere; the Commission jury convicted both in connection with the broader racketeering enterprise. Their shared appearance in a trial should not erase the separate institutional functions their offices were said to perform. [1][2][3]

The evidence indicates that the New York Mafia was not governed solely by a single-person command system. Senior figures could serve as negotiators, trusted advisers, messengers, and participants in interfamily arrangements. The surviving court opinion addresses activities legally tied to the enterprise, rather than delivering a complete manual of informal day-to-day duties. [1][2][3]

Back to contents

9. 1983 Senate Organizational Chart

A particularly valuable snapshot appears in the 1983 Senate Judiciary Committee hearings on organized crime. The New Jersey State Police submission shows Anthony Corallo as boss, Christopher Furnari as consigliere, and Salvatore Santoro as underboss. Its accompanying page gives Furnari’s birth date, aliases and a brief intelligence-based description of alleged activities and associates. [1]

This is a contemporaneous government source, but its categories require discipline: intelligence assessments can reflect reports not yet tested in court. The entry lists arrests and suspected involvement in narcotics finance, gambling, hijacking, and loansharking. An arrest is not a conviction, and an intelligence description is not a verdict. The most securely documented fact from the page is the position the authorities attributed to him in 1983. [1]

Back to contents

10. The Reported Succession Expectation

The Senate-published intelligence entry asserted that Furnari was being groomed to succeed Corallo. This is historically revealing because it captures what investigators believed while Corallo was still active. It is not itself proof that a completed succession plan existed, that all family factions agreed, or that Furnari ever became boss. [1][3][7]

Subsequent events went in another direction. Commission prosecutions removed senior figures from daily control, while later testimony associated with Anthony Casso described the rise of Vittorio “Vic” Amuso. The proper historical claim is therefore narrow: investigators recorded Furnari as a prospective successor in 1983; the eventual leadership outcome cannot be read backward into that intelligence prediction. [1][3][7]

Back to contents

11. Louis Foceri, Paul Vario and Other Reported Associates

The 1983 government entry listed Louis Foceri, Paul Vario Sr., Charles Alaimo and Frank Caggiano among Furnari’s associates. Such a list indicates the intelligence community’s assessment of relationships at a particular moment. It does not by itself establish how often these individuals met, what business they shared, or whether each had participated in a specific offense alongside Furnari. [1]

The list matters because it places him within an older Lucchese network as well as within the Corallo-era leadership. Additional court files, surveillance reports and contemporaneous corporate records would be needed to turn an associates list into a fully mapped history of joint enterprises. The distinction between contact and common criminal purpose is maintained throughout this file. [1]

12. The 19th Hole Story

Mafia histories and later online reporting repeatedly describe Furnari as associated with a Brooklyn social club called the 19th Hole and depict it as an important space for meetings and introductions. The image of the club helps explain the role informal physical spaces could play as social infrastructure for criminal organizations. [9][10]

Yet the exact ownership, membership, chronology and operations of the location have not been independently established from title documents or sworn testimony in the sources gathered here. It is therefore presented as a reported social setting rather than a legally established command headquarters. Researchers should consult archival property records and the original investigative files before attaching individual crimes to the premises. [9][10]

Back to contents

13. Vittorio Amuso and the Younger Leadership Network

Later insider accounts connected Furnari’s circle with Vittorio “Vic” Amuso and Anthony “Gaspipe” Casso. Casso told a Senate subcommittee in 1996 that he had become associated with Furnari at age twenty-one, and later described Amuso’s advancement and the late-1986 succession discussions. This testimony is useful for understanding how participants retrospectively portrayed the transition. [7][15]

Casso was a cooperating criminal witness with incentives and a deeply contested record. His comments supply evidence that he made these claims, not independent proof of every causal assertion. The public judicial findings in Furnari’s Commission case establish an influential consiglierial role; they do not identify Furnari as the final decision-maker who selected or installed Amuso. [7][15]

Back to contents

14. Anthony Casso: Source, Associate and Reliability Problem

Casso appears in two very different bodies of evidence: as a later congressional witness describing Lucchese succession and as a source of information used by the federal Parole Commission. The 2000 and 2008 Third Circuit opinions explain why Furnari challenged the credibility of information attributed to Casso and others. This overlapping role makes Casso historically significant but also methodologically hazardous. [4][5][7]

In 2000 an appellate court required a fuller explanation when the Parole Commission failed to address significant credibility-related material, including an affidavit undermining one witness. That procedural ruling did not establish that every accusation was false. Nor did it transform the homicide allegations discussed at parole into charges of which Furnari had been found guilty by a criminal jury. [4][5][7]

15. The Mafia Commission as an Enterprise

The February 1985 indictment described the Mafia Commission as a coordinated body overseeing relationships and joint ventures among New York crime families. Its allegations included control of concrete contractors, labor-related extortion and decisions about disputes and leadership. The formal charging theory therefore reached beyond a single crew or a collection of unrelated street crimes. [2][3][8]

The 1989 appellate opinion explains how the government proved a racketeering enterprise at trial. Furnari’s inclusion is best understood in that interfamily context: the prosecutors alleged that his high-ranking participation facilitated an operation spanning family lines. A high title alone did not replace the need for proof of legally relevant activity. [2][3][8]

Back to contents

16. New York Concrete Industry and “the Club”

One core prosecution theory concerned major New York City concrete-pouring projects. The government described a system in which firms seeking high-value contracts faced a coordinated organization and extortionate payments, with labor influence helping reinforce the arrangement. The later appellate record describes a two-percent extraction mechanism involving qualifying contracts above two million dollars. [2][3][5]

The point of studying this episode is organizational rather than sensational. It demonstrates how a criminal group can seek to extract rents from otherwise legitimate commercial processes by influencing bid opportunities, labor relations and contractor expectations. The case did not criminalize construction work itself; it targeted racketeering conduct that used business institutions as pressure points. [2][3][5]

Back to contents

17. Labor Influence and Extortion

The Commission case involved both Hobbs Act extortion and prohibited labor-related payments. Labor peace was presented as an economic resource criminals could threaten to withhold. In the appellate description, cooperating or coerced construction firms faced disruption and other consequences unless they complied with the extortion scheme. [2][3][5]

An accurate biography does not assume that every labor official or union member was compromised. The proven offenses concerned identified illegal arrangements and participants; the legitimate purpose of collective bargaining remains analytically distinct from corrupt exploitation of labor institutions. This distinction also helps avoid turning the trial into an indiscriminate allegation against an entire industry. [2][3][5]

Back to contents

18. Interfamily Negotiations and Governance

The Commission theory emphasized procedures for settling disputes and coordinating ventures across the Five Families. Recorded conversations and cooperating-witness evidence were used to establish that the enterprise’s activity could not be understood as isolated acts by one neighborhood crew. Furnari’s position as senior Lucchese adviser gave prosecutors a route to explain his presence in meetings where sensitive interfamily business was discussed. [2][3]

The Second Circuit later accepted the trial court’s assessment that participation in these recorded discussions reflected access to the Commission’s violent business. The appellate opinion should not be paraphrased as finding that Furnari personally committed every predicate act attributed to the organization. Collective enterprise liability and individual historical responsibility are overlapping, but not identical, questions. [2][3]

Back to contents

19. February 26, 1985 Federal Indictment

On February 26, 1985 federal officials announced indictments naming senior figures from New York Mafia families, including Furnari. The Reagan Presidential Library preserves the Department of Justice/FBI materials from the announcement. In the hierarchy stated by the government, Corallo was the Lucchese boss, Santoro the underboss, and Furnari the consigliere. [2][8]

The charging instrument alleged violations under racketeering and extortion statutes; by itself it proves only that prosecutors brought charges. Later verdicts and appellate rulings, rather than the initial press announcement, determine the offenses that can properly be described as convictions. This separation also explains why some names present in the indictment did not appear in exactly the same posture in the ultimate appellate caption. [2][8]

20. Contemporary Press and Public Framing

The February 27, 1985 Washington Post account described a federal effort to prosecute Mafia leadership as a governing organization rather than piecemeal. It called Furnari a reputed Lucchese adviser and connected the indictment to the alleged control of New York construction work. Such reporting is useful evidence of what the public was told as the litigation began. [8]

A reporter’s phrase “reputed” should not be silently transformed into a proven finding, and contemporaneous newspapers could not know the future jury verdict. The press report is read alongside the later judicial opinion: the first describes the accusation at its inception, while the second records what was established in court and survived appeal. [8]

21. The Eleven-Week Commission Trial

The Commission case was tried in the Southern District of New York before Judge Richard Owen. The appellate court describes an eleven-week jury trial that ended with guilty verdicts on November 19, 1986. The defendants were charged under RICO, the Hobbs Act, and related labor-bribery provisions, reflecting the breadth of the government’s enterprise theory. [3][9]

For Furnari, the jury’s verdicts concerned racketeering conspiracy, substantive racketeering, extortion and related violations as enumerated in the appellate opinion. The outcome is not accurately reduced to “guilty of being in the Mafia.” Membership and high rank were relevant to the evidence, but the judgment rested on statutory elements the prosecution was required to prove. [3][9]

Back to contents

22. RICO Conspiracy and Substantive Racketeering

The Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act allows prosecution of specified patterns of racketeering conduct involving an enterprise. The Commission jury found the charged RICO offenses proven. The appellate decision distinguishes a conspiracy count from substantive participation in racketeering activity; they are legally separate bases for conviction. [3][5]

Understanding those categories is essential to reading the sentence. A RICO count is not a free-floating judgment that everything said about the defendant is true. Courts examine the charged enterprise, statutory predicates, evidence and the defendant’s specific participation. Researchers must cite the operative verdict and appellate disposition rather than substitute general descriptions of organized crime. [3][5]

Back to contents

23. Hobbs Act Extortion Counts

The Commission verdict also included extortion and attempted-extortion counts under the Hobbs Act. These involved obtaining property through wrongful use of threats and related coercive conduct affecting commerce. In the case’s account of the construction industry, extortion payments were related to the operation of “the Club.” [3]

Furnari’s sentence later consisted of consecutive twenty-year terms attached to five specified counts: RICO conspiracy, substantive RICO, and three extortion counts. Other verdicts and counts remain part of the trial history, but the consecutive-count structure is what made the aggregate 100-year sentence. This precision matters more than an undifferentiated label of “racketeering conviction.” [3]

Back to contents

24. Labor-Bribery Counts and Aiding Liability

The Second Circuit describes labor-bribery counts under federal labor statutes as part of the verdict. Furnari, like most defendants on those counts, was convicted as an aider and abettor rather than in the precise principal posture described for Ralph Scopo. The distinction shows that criminal participation can take forms beyond receiving the underlying payment directly. [3]

A historical explanation should identify that these were criminal findings in a specific federal trial; it should not imply that every meeting with a union official establishes aiding-and-abetting liability. The evidence and statutory elements are what distinguish ordinary professional contact from participation in an unlawful payment scheme. [3]

Back to contents

25. Recorded Conversations and Evidentiary Inference

The Commission investigation used electronic intercepts, surveillance and witnesses to connect people in different families to common decision-making processes. The appellate opinion discusses how recorded conversations were admitted and interpreted in the context of an alleged joint enterprise. Furnari’s presence in sensitive meetings figured in the sentencing court’s assessment of his level of responsibility. [2][3]

Tape recordings are not self-interpreting. Speakers may use indirection or jargon, and evidentiary rules govern which statements can be used against whom. The legal system considers them alongside independent corroboration and instructions to the jury. For a historical biography, their value lies in the adjudicated context rather than in sensationalized snippets without surrounding testimony. [2][3]

26. January 1987: One-Hundred-Year Sentence

After the November 1986 verdict, Judge Owen imposed a 100-year sentence on Furnari. The appellate opinion specifies five consecutive twenty-year terms, involving two RICO counts and three extortion counts. Other terms could be concurrent, but the five-count sequence determined the headline aggregate punishment. [3][5][9]

Because the offenses preceded the federal sentencing reforms that removed ordinary parole for later offenses, a nominal 100-year term was not necessarily imprisonment without any legal possibility of release. That historical feature became central to decades of parole review. The length of the sentence should therefore be described as a judicial sentence, not as proof that Furnari remained in custody until death. [3][5][9]

Back to contents

27. Furnari’s Sentence Challenge

Furnari challenged the proportionality of his punishment on direct appeal, emphasizing that he was not the boss or underboss of a crime family. The Second Circuit rejected that Eighth Amendment challenge. It relied on the trial court’s appraisal of the consigliere’s seniority and access to Commission discussions, as well as the sentencing judge’s individualized treatment. [3]

The judges did not say that every defendant occupied an identical office or committed the same acts. Their holding was that the sentencing choices lay within the permissible constitutional and statutory range in this case. A useful biographical synthesis therefore reports the challenge, the reasons given, and the fact of affirmance without treating the judicial discussion as a full biography of every activity the government alleged. [3]

Back to contents

28. January 31, 1989 Second Circuit Decision

The Second Circuit decided the major Commission appeal on January 31, 1989. As relevant to Furnari, the court affirmed the conviction and the 100-year sentence. The reported opinion reviews evidentiary objections and challenges to the RICO enterprise and sentencing, making it the most authoritative accessible summary of the criminal judgment. [3]

The opinion also records a partial reversal relating to Anthony Indelicato’s substantive RICO conviction as time-barred. That reversal was not Furnari’s. A careful chronology must avoid claiming that because one codefendant won limited relief, Furnari’s own judgment was overturned. The court’s final disposition specifically affirmed the remainder of the appealed counts. [3]

Back to contents

29. Judge Bright’s Partial Dissent

Senior Judge Myron Bright concurred in much of the Commission appeal but dissented on discrete evidentiary issues, particularly concerning other defendants’ loansharking and the relationship between the Galante killings and the RICO conspiracy. His writing cautioned against substituting Mafia membership for proof of charged acts. [3]

That dissent is an important reminder that a common enterprise prosecution raises contested questions about inference, proof and individual culpability. It was not the controlling judgment and did not vacate Furnari’s convictions. Including the disagreement gives students a fuller account of the appellate process without promoting dissenting analysis into binding law. [3]

30. Custody, Transfer and the Federal Prison System

Later court records place Furnari at the federal correctional institution in Allenwood, Pennsylvania while he pursued habeas challenges to parole decisions. These proceedings occurred years after the original New York racketeering trial and were litigated in the Third Circuit, reflecting the federal court system’s jurisdiction over the place of custody. [4][5]

Prison assignment and supervision can change over a long sentence. The court records establish Allenwood during the relevant litigation; they do not independently prove every place or date of incarceration over the nearly three decades between indictment and release. Facility histories should not be filled out from guesswork. [4][5]

Back to contents

31. Why Parole Remained Legally Available

The Commission convictions concerned conduct preceding the effective date of the federal abolition of ordinary parole for later federal offenses. The 2000 and 2008 Third Circuit decisions therefore discuss the older statutory parole regime and the continuing authority of the United States Parole Commission. That legal framework is essential to understanding why a man serving a 100-year federal sentence received parole hearings. [4][5]

Parole eligibility was not a guarantee of release; nor was the number printed on the sentencing judgment the only consideration determining the actual custody term. Under the old system the commission assessed guidelines, offense severity, claimed aggravating conduct, and mitigation, subject to limited judicial review. Furnari’s subsequent litigation focused heavily on how these administrative decisions were made. [4][5]

32. December 1996 Initial Parole Hearing

At Furnari’s first parole hearing in December 1996, the commission classified his offense behavior in Category Eight, its most severe category. The government submitted information alleging his participation in murders and violence beyond the conduct of the Commission case conviction. The category could support a lengthy postponement of further parole consideration. [4][5]

The crucial evidentiary distinction is that these were administrative allegations evaluated for parole under different procedural rules from a criminal jury trial. A statement that the commission attributed homicide involvement to Furnari does not establish a conviction for homicide. His challenges to the rating and to informant credibility occupied the next decade of appellate litigation. [4][5]

33. Uncharged Homicide Allegations

The parole opinions describe information from Anthony Casso, Thomas Carew and Alphonse D’Arco about suspected past murders. Furnari disputed those allegations, attacked source credibility and sought underlying interview materials. The Third Circuit’s discussions concern whether an agency had sufficient grounds and followed its own process in relying on contested material. [4][5]

These opinions are not judgments from a separate murder prosecution against Furnari. They identify allegations introduced in an administrative setting; they do not present proof beyond a reasonable doubt or a jury verdict on individual killings. This biography neither erases the allegations nor publishes them as settled biographical facts. [4][5]

34. Evidence Undermining an Informant

In the proceedings reviewed in 2000, Furnari brought forward information challenging the reliability of a witness the Parole Commission had relied on. The appellate court concluded that the commission needed to explain whether it still credited that witness or relied instead on other evidence after receiving the new material. The defect involved the adequacy of the agency’s stated reasoning. [4]

A demand for reasons is not the same as a judicial acquittal of all uncharged conduct. The Third Circuit remanded for a new explanation rather than ordering his immediate release. This narrow disposition is a central source-critical point: a victory on an administrative procedure can coexist with an unchanged underlying criminal sentence. [4]

Back to contents

35. 2000 Third Circuit Remand

In Furnari v. Warden, reported at 218 F.3d 250, the Third Circuit vacated the district court’s denial and conditionally directed further action because the parole authorities had not adequately explained their response to significant new credibility evidence. The panel emphasized the obligation to give reasons when denying relief after relevant new information is presented. [4][13]

The 2000 ruling did not erase the 1986 RICO and extortion verdicts. Nor did it direct the commission to grant parole. Its enduring importance lies in the principle that even a prisoner convicted in a major organized-crime case is entitled to the procedures imposed by the parole framework and the applicable regulations. [4][13]

Back to contents

36. Subsequent Parole Reviews and Appeals

The 2008 opinion recounts additional administrative actions after the 2000 remand, including reviews in which the Parole Commission adhered to a severe offense rating and declined release. Furnari continued to dispute the use of informant information, severity calculations and the weight given to mitigating considerations such as age and prison behavior. [5]

The successive reviews matter because a chronology made only of the 1986 verdict and the 2014 release would obscure how release decisions were contested and repeatedly reconsidered. At the same time, an adverse parole decision is an administrative determination, not a new criminal judgment. [5]

Back to contents

37. February 15, 2006 Habeas Petition

On February 15, 2006 Furnari filed another petition under 28 U.S.C. § 2241 challenging the denial of parole. His claims included objections to the severity category and allegations that important exculpatory material had not been properly considered. He also advanced arguments about the length of punishment, parole legislation and the repeated reconsideration process. [5][6]

The case was heard in the Middle District of Pennsylvania, which denied the petition in June 2007. This placed a different issue before the appellate court than the original Commission verdict: whether the parole administration and district court had complied with the applicable standards for reviewing custody and release decisions. [5][6]

Back to contents

38. July 9, 2008 Third Circuit Affirmance

The Third Circuit affirmed the district court’s denial of Furnari’s 2006 habeas petition in a precedential opinion dated July 9, 2008, reported as 531 F.3d 241. It considered the timing of the Parole Commission’s duties, attempts to relitigate severity classification and arguments concerning exculpatory material and mitigation. [5][6][14]

The court ruled against Furnari on the claims presented. In particular, it treated renewed challenges to the Category Eight rating as an improper repetition of previously litigated grounds. The outcome was continued denial of habeas relief, not a fresh determination by a criminal jury that the disputed homicide allegations were true. [5][6][14]

39. Offense Severity and Judicial Review

The parole opinions explain that the commission could consider substantial relevant information, including uncharged conduct, subject to its regulations and limited judicial oversight. Courts ordinarily reviewed whether the decision had a rational basis and complied with required procedures; they did not simply redo the parole hearing. The system created a difficult evidentiary boundary between allegation, administrative finding and criminal conviction. [4][5]

A Category Eight determination should be described by its administrative function rather than converted into an extra criminal count. In Furnari’s case, the classifications were deeply contested and central to his applications, while the 1986 Commission judgment remained intact. Readers should maintain separate timelines for the sentencing court and the parole authority. [4][5]

Back to contents

40. Age, Behavior and Compassionate Arguments

By the mid-2000s Furnari was in his eighties. His submissions invoked age, behavior in custody and the disproportionality he perceived between his role and the amount of time he was required to serve. The 2008 court acknowledged these arguments but upheld the parole decision under the standards governing commission discretion. [5]

Age and favorable prison conduct can matter to release authorities, but their presence in a legal brief does not compel the same decision in every case. Nor does judicial refusal to overturn a commission’s balance mean that the defendant’s age was contested. The legal conclusion concerned the breadth of the agency’s decision-making power. [5]

41. The Scheduled 2011 Reconsideration

The courts described a lengthy schedule under which the parole authorities anticipated a reconsideration hearing in 2011. Furnari argued that the statutory end date or extension of the Parole Commission affected whether a release date needed to be set. The 2008 opinion rejected the specific timing challenge then before it. [4][5]

A scheduled hearing date must not be confused with a scheduled release date. The historical record collected here does not include complete original transcripts or outcomes of every hearing anticipated after 2008. Accordingly, the biography records the published judicial discussion and the later release, without inventing intermediate administrative steps. [4][5]

42. September 19, 2014 Reported Release

A 2014 organized-crime news report reproduced a then-displayed Bureau of Prisons inmate-locator entry showing September 19, 2014 as Furnari’s release date. The Mob Museum’s later historical retrospective also reports that he was paroled in 2014. Taken together, these sources provide a reasonably supported endpoint to his imprisonment. [9][10]

The original historical Bureau of Prisons page capture was not independently obtained for this edition, and the exact administrative release instrument has not been collated. Consequently the preferred formulation is “reported released on September 19, 2014” rather than a claim to have inspected the original prison discharge order. The release did not void the original convictions. [9][10]

43. Duration of Imprisonment and Arithmetic

From the 1986 Commission conviction to the reported September 2014 release spans roughly twenty-eight years, although the exact start of counted custody and pretrial credits cannot be reconstructed from the accessible source set alone. The phrase “served twenty-eight years” is therefore a rounded historical description, not a certified sentence-computation report. [3][9][10]

The contrast between a century-long imposed sentence and a term ending during Furnari’s lifetime is an important teaching example of pre-Guidelines federal sentencing and parole. It also illustrates why criminal-history entries must distinguish a sentence pronounced by a judge from a projected release date and from the actual date a person left custody. [3][9][10]

44. Life after Federal Custody

Published material concerning Furnari’s years after prison is limited. The next well-supported milestone is his death in 2018 and the funeral notice naming Staten Island as the setting of his final arrangements. Nothing in the gathered court and obituary records warrants a precise account of his private activities during the intervening years. [10][11][12]

A first-edition reference article should resist speculation about continuing criminal command, succession claims or household circumstances after release. Such assertions would require new dated government records, direct testimony or contemporary reporting. The legal case is overrepresented in surviving documents partly because the private years are not comparably documented. [10][11][12]

45. May 28, 2018 Death

A published family death notice states that Furnari died peacefully at home on May 28, 2018 at age ninety-four. The associated funeral-home memorial confirms the dates April 30, 1924–May 28, 2018. Those two forms of record reinforce the date and distinguish death information from earlier hearsay about imprisonment or projected release. [11][12]

The obituary records identify family survivors and funeral observances, but privacy-sensitive family details are not required for an encyclopedia entry about organized-crime history. An obituary is a valuable contemporaneous death notice, though a civil vital-record certificate would provide a stronger documentary endpoint if available for scholarly collation. [11][12]

46. Funeral Notices and Memorial Records

The funeral-home memorial lists viewings at a Staten Island funeral home, a June 1, 2018 service at St. Patrick’s Church, and burial at St. John Cemetery in Middle Village. These dates help corroborate the death notice and anchor the close of the biographical timeline. [11][12]

The entry does not reproduce private condolences, family photographs or memorial page material. Such information generally adds little to the historical interpretation and may be subject to copyright or privacy expectations. For public catalog purposes, the burial and memorial records are cited only to establish the end-of-life chronology. [11][12]

47. Why Furnari Did Not Become the Confirmed Successor

The 1983 intelligence assertion that Furnari was being prepared to succeed Corallo must be read against the upheaval caused by the Commission prosecution. By late 1986 Furnari was already under sentence from that prosecution. Casso’s later testimony instead described Amuso becoming boss during the leadership transition. [1][7][15]

This sequence demonstrates why a forecast is not an outcome. The sources support Furnari’s high status and the government’s belief about a possible succession, but they do not establish that he served as recognized boss. Retrospective charts often compress disputes and acting arrangements. Any definitive date-by-date succession history would need multiple contemporary witnesses and archival verification. [1][7][15]

Back to contents

48. Historical Significance

Furnari’s career illuminates the advisory tier of the Lucchese organization and the construction-industry racketeering enterprise that federal prosecutors used to show coordinated power among Mafia families. His 1986 conviction and 1989 affirmance anchor the legal history of the Commission prosecution; his parole decisions extend the story into the evolution of federal sentencing administration. [1][2][3][4][5]

The most distinctive lesson is evidentiary. The same man can appear in an intelligence chart, a criminal indictment, a jury verdict, a sentencing judgment, a parole allegation and a family obituary. Those are different kinds of documents, created for different purposes, with different standards of proof. Careful scholarship preserves the distinctions instead of flattening them into a single legend. [1][2][3][4][5]

Back to contents

49. Evidence Standards and Attribution

This biography assigns greatest weight to reported appellate dispositions for legal outcomes, and to dated public records for identification and death. Government intelligence is described as such; the 1985 indictment is treated as a charging allegation until later verdict records are consulted. Cooperating witness testimony is quoted only as attributed historical evidence and never silently upgraded to an established fact. [1][2][3][4][5]

For disputes about violence, the benchmark is especially strict. A parole commission’s evaluation of uncharged conduct, even if permitted by regulation and left undisturbed on judicial review, is not equivalent to proof beyond a reasonable doubt in a separate criminal case. This file also avoids conflating the partial appellate reversal for a codefendant with a reversal for Furnari. [1][2][3][4][5]

50. Priority Archival Research Questions

Further work should identify Furnari’s complete pre-1985 criminal docket, contemporaneous documentation of the 19th Hole location, original transcripts of the Commission trial, sentencing minutes, fuller surveillance exhibits and the exact sequence of parole decisions following 2008. A historical Bureau of Prisons release record would strengthen the reported September 2014 date. [1][2][3][4][5][10]

Researchers should also seek original birth and death certificates, specific appointment evidence for the consigliere office, and independent corroboration for claims involving violent acts. This publication makes its own limits visible rather than treating an extensive source list as proof that every claim has been facsimile-verified. [1][2][3][4][5][10]

Documentary Research Windows

The following are original editorial research notes and paraphrases, not documentary transcriptions or quotations from facsimile pages. They identify how to read the supplied primary and secondary sources critically.

Source Study 1: 1983 Senate-published intelligence profile

Read the table and individual entry as a snapshot of law-enforcement assessment. It establishes that investigators represented a boss–underboss–consigliere hierarchy, and that they expected Furnari might succeed Corallo. It does not convert the listed arrests, suspicions or forecasts into proven criminal findings. The entry is reproduced in a Senate hearing volume, not individually sworn testimony by Furnari. [1]

Source Study 2: February 1985 DOJ charging announcement

The Reagan Library copy records what prosecutors and investigators announced on indictment day, including Furnari’s title and an enterprise theory spanning five crime families. Indictment summaries are one side’s formal allegations. The appropriate comparison is the 1989 appellate decision, which states what the jury ultimately convicted and what the court affirmed. [2][3]

Source Study 3: Commission trial and 1989 appellate opinion

Read the judgment as a record of both verdict and limits. The opinion enumerates RICO and extortion counts, distinguishes bribery liability, and describes the construction enterprise. Its sentencing section specifies five consecutive twenty-year terms for Furnari. The partial reversal for a different defendant should not be generalized to Furnari. [3]

Source Study 4: Judge Bright’s partial appellate dissent

A dissenting opinion offers insight into judicial disagreement over proof and inference, especially concerning codefendants and particular predicate acts. It is not the controlling outcome. Here it underscores why scholars should avoid reasoning from Mafia affiliation alone to guilt of every alleged offense. [3]

Source Study 5: Casso’s 1996 congressional testimony

The witness reported an association with Furnari and described later Lucchese succession. As a participant and cooperator, Casso provided potentially valuable insider details, but his assertions need corroboration. Later parole litigation itself documents significant controversy about Casso-linked allegations. Students should compare witness incentives and dates of recollection. [7][4]

Source Study 6: 2000 Third Circuit parole remand

The appellate panel identified a failure to provide adequate reasons in the face of materially changed credibility information. It remanded for explanation rather than declaring the uncharged violence disproven or ordering parole. This window illustrates a procedural holding whose historical meaning is frequently overstated in abbreviated biographies. [4]

Source Study 7: 2008 Third Circuit habeas decision

The appellate court reviewed continued challenges to the severity rating, late parole-schedule arguments and mitigation. It affirmed denial of relief. Its recitation of suspected murders belongs to the parole-litigation record, not the set of crimes for which the Commission jury convicted Furnari. [5][6]

Source Study 8: 2014 release report and 2018 death notices

The 2014 report reproduces a release date attributed to the Bureau of Prisons, while a later museum retrospective confirms parole in that year. An obituary and funeral-home memorial separately document death in May 2018. A fully audited final edition would add the underlying BOP release instrument and original vital-record certificates. [9][10][11][12]

Research and Publication Limitations

Judicial findings are grounded in the publicly accessible appellate records cited above, not in an exhaustive page-by-page collation of trial transcripts, exhibits, indictments and sentencing minutes. The 1983 Senate hearing material represents law-enforcement intelligence, not sworn admissions by Furnari. The 1985 DOJ release documents accusations and public statements on indictment day; the later jury verdict and the Second Circuit opinion establish the convictions.

Parole allegations about uncharged homicides were evaluated administratively and disputed by Furnari. Nothing in these materials warrants labeling him separately convicted of murder. The 2000 remand concerned agency explanation; the 2008 judgment affirmed a habeas denial. The September 19, 2014 release date is supported by secondary publication of what was represented as a contemporary Bureau of Prisons locator entry and a later museum retrospective, not by a collated original release order. Dates of birth and death are supported by official intelligence and funeral records; vital certificates were not obtained. Original licensed portrait rights, exact MGU stylesheet parity, and live-site deployment were not independently verified.
