Historical biography · Section 01

1. Historical Overview

James “Jimmy the Gent” Burke (1931–1996) was an Irish-American criminal associate of New York’s Lucchese crime family. He was never an inducted Italian-American Mafia member, yet he occupied a consequential position in a network connecting Paul Vario’s Brooklyn and Queens associates, cargo theft around John F. Kennedy International Airport, illegal gambling, and intimidation. A close connection to an organization is not the same thing as holding a formal rank within it.

Burke is widely identified as a principal organizer of the December 1978 Lufthansa cargo-terminal robbery. That claim is supported by later federal prosecutorial allegations, witness accounts, and historical reporting; it did not produce a Lufthansa robbery conviction against Burke. By contrast, his federal conviction in the Boston College point-shaving scandal and New York conviction for murdering Richard Eaton were formally adjudicated and affirmed on appeal. The biography treats those different evidentiary statuses separately.

The 1990 film Goodfellas made the fictionalized Jimmy Conway a popular shorthand for Burke. Historical assessment must begin with dated judicial opinions and contemporary proceedings, then measure recollections, government allegations, and dramatized scenes against them.[1][2][3][8][9][11][12]

Historical biography · Section 02

2. Identity, Birth, Death and Sources

Near-contemporaneous obituary reporting gives July 5, 1931 as Burke’s birth date and April 13, 1996 as the date of death, at age sixty-four, in Buffalo, New York. Later biographical pages generally repeat these dates. No certified birth record or state death certificate has been collated for this edition.

Burke was commonly nicknamed “Jimmy the Gent,” a label associated in reminiscences with conspicuous generosity and calculated social charm. Those anecdotes should not obscure the criminal convictions and the experiences of victims. Some popular texts repeat “James Conway” as an early name or confuse his identity with Goodfellas character Jimmy Conway; a direct civil identity chain has not been independently authenticated here.[11][16][18]

Historical biography · Section 03

3. Early Life and Foster-Care Accounts

Posthumous biographical reporting describes instability during Burke’s childhood, including movement among foster households, abuse, early offending, and juvenile detention. These details primarily derive from later narrative accounts rather than contemporaneous welfare or court files. A responsible biography records that provenance rather than transforming each remembered episode into verified documentary history.

Foster-care trauma does not explain or excuse later violence, nor does the available record justify diagnosing Burke retrospectively. The useful historical question is how early contact with punitive institutions and later prison networks became elements in his reported career. Without original juvenile files, the precise chronology remains a research question.[11][16]

Historical biography · Section 04

4. Early Detention and Criminal Reputation

Obituaries and organized-crime narratives describe extensive juvenile and adult custody before Burke reached his mid-twenties. One oft-repeated figure says that he spent almost all of his late adolescence in custody. Because the relevant intake and discharge records have not been obtained, the number should be read as an attributed retrospective claim.

His reputation among other offenders was founded partly on an alleged willingness to remain silent under questioning and to mediate criminal business. Such features appear repeatedly in witness-driven gangster biographies, making independent records especially important. This profile does not affirm sensational unprosecuted murders from that early period as established facts.[11][16]

Historical biography · Section 05

5. Irish-American Associate and Mafia Membership

Burke’s Irish ancestry matters institutionally because the traditional New York Mafia restricted induction into its made-member hierarchy. It did not prevent non-Italian associates from handling profitable criminal business on behalf of captains and crews. Burke therefore operated near formal power but did not occupy the office of caporegime, underboss, or boss.

The Department of Justice’s January 2014 Lufthansa release expressly describes Burke as a Lucchese crime family associate. This official terminology is more precise than popular descriptions of him as a “Lucchese boss.” A powerful associate could supervise associates, coordinate robbery crews, and channel proceeds while remaining outside the family’s formal membership roll.[8][9][16]

Historical biography · Section 06

6. Paul Vario’s Organizational Environment

Paul Vario was a Lucchese captain associated with a Brooklyn crew and access to airport freight activity. Burke’s relationships with people in that orbit gave him criminal opportunities without making every enterprise a centrally directed family operation. Crews are better understood as overlapping circles of trust, profit sharing, intimidation, and service to higher-ranking members.

Vario’s position and Burke’s relationship are not interchangeable. A robbery might involve Burke’s associates, cooperation from workers, and perceived family protection, but this alone cannot establish that Vario personally designed, ordered or profited from a particular crime. The Vario biography and Burke biography must be cross-read without importing one defendant’s criminal convictions onto the other.[8][11][16]

Historical biography · Section 07

7. Henry Hill: Participant, Witness and Narrator

Henry Hill was an important associate of Burke and Vario and later became the principal narrator of widely circulated accounts of their network. His cooperation provided investigators with details about business relationships, crimes, and the Boston College gambling scheme. The federal appellate court identifies Hill as a significant prosecution witness in the 1981 trial.

Hill’s testimony was consequential, but his status as a participant and compensated cooperator required careful credibility testing. In the Eaton murder prosecution, the New York appeals court discussed attacks on his financial arrangements and the admission of the cooperation agreement to contextualize them. A historical writer should distinguish what Hill testified from what jurors accepted in a particular proceeding.[1][2][3][16]

Historical biography · Section 08

8. Jimmy Burke’s Operating Circle

Burke’s reported associates included Hill, Thomas DeSimone, and others involved in stolen freight, gambling, loan collections and airport-related criminal opportunities. The boundaries of a “crew” were porous. Some individuals held full Mafia membership, others had no formal membership, and some appeared only in specific criminal ventures.

Because memoirs collapse years into memorable episodes, a roster should be dated to the relevant investigation. An individual who associated with Burke in 1978 should not automatically be assigned to every earlier theft or subsequent killing. This page uses the term “reported member of the Burke circle” where a formal organizational relationship is unproven.[8][12][16][17]

Historical biography · Section 09

9. Roberts Lounge and Social Infrastructure

Biographical reporting connects Burke to Roberts Lounge in Queens, a location described as a meeting place within his criminal milieu. Neighborhood businesses could serve legitimate customers while becoming settings for gambling conversations, favors, intimidation, and coordination. Physical proximity is not itself evidence that every patron participated in crime.

The historical importance of these sites is infrastructural: informal meetings could connect drivers, bettors, fences, and men with established Mafia access. Mapping those connections requires corroborating specific dates, business ownership, surveillance reports, or witness evidence. The existence of a social hub does not create a separate criminal conviction.[11][16]

Historical biography · Section 10

10. John F. Kennedy Airport as a Criminal Opportunity

JFK’s high-volume cargo operations concentrated portable, valuable goods, cash shipments, temporary storage, employees and distribution routes in one complex. For organized thieves, the combination of insider access, timing information, transport vehicles and fencing networks could convert legitimate logistics into illicit profit.

The historical record surrounding Burke centers partly on this airport environment. Yet airport access and a reputation for hijacking are broader than proof of responsibility for an individual robbery. The 1978 Lufthansa case must be evaluated using the specific indictment history, witness statements, and later government filings rather than a blanket assertion that the Burke–Vario network “controlled” all cargo.[8][9][12]

Historical biography · Section 11

11. Cargo Theft and Hijacking Methods

In accounts of the Burke network, cargo theft typically required intelligence about a shipment, access to its route or storage, a transport and concealment plan, and a means of disposing of stolen goods. These broad criminal mechanisms explain why an airport or warehouse worker could be indispensable to a theft.

Descriptions of the methods in books are not themselves certified records of Burke’s individual involvement. The distinction is crucial where later authors link disparate Air France, Lufthansa and trucking episodes into a single uninterrupted career. The earliest reliable documents should define each alleged incident.[11][12][16][17]

Historical biography · Section 12

12. Air France Robbery and Prior Airport Cases

The late-1960s Air France cargo robbery is often portrayed as a formative theft in the Vario–Hill–Burke milieu. Published retellings vary in the specific division of labor, date and proceeds, and this edition has not obtained the full police case jacket or certified criminal dispositions on that robbery.

Its presence in organized-crime history is useful as context for the development of insider-assisted airport theft. It should not be used to claim an Air France conviction for Burke. When cross-referencing Hill or Vario, an editorial attribution such as “associated in retrospective accounts” is more accurate than asserting a judicial finding that does not appear in the identified record.[11][12][16]

Historical biography · Section 13

13. The Early-1970s Extortion Case

Contemporaneous 1981–1982 newspaper coverage identifies Burke as having an extortion conviction dated 1974 and states that he was imprisoned or on parole supervision from that matter when the Boston College case proceeded. Later popular summaries sometimes place the underlying event or conviction in 1972, suggesting possible confusion between conduct, proceedings and sentence dates.

The certified trial judgment, charging document and supervision ledger for this early case have not been collated. Accordingly, this edition states the conviction as reported by contemporary coverage, but does not assign an unverified exact conviction day, victim name or original sentence. The 1982 point-shaving sentence is an independent judgment.[4][5][6][7]

Historical biography · Section 14

14. Operation Gold Bug and Informant Reporting

Accounts of the early-1970s police investigation known as Operation Gold Bug describe electronic surveillance of a Canarsie junkyard location linked to the Vario network. Such operations help explain how prosecutors began assembling a larger portrait of local corruption, theft and association.

Gold Bug was a discrete investigation, not a conviction of Burke for every conversation recorded there. Historians should obtain contemporaneous surveillance affidavits, original tapes and chain-of-custody logs before quoting specific speakers. In this biography, the investigation is contextual and does not substitute for primary evidence on later Lufthansa or point-shaving conduct.[11][16]

Historical biography · Section 15

15. The 1978 Lufthansa Robbery: Event and Losses

In December 1978 armed robbers entered the Lufthansa cargo terminal at JFK and took millions of dollars in currency and valuable jewelry. Official federal materials published in 2014 describe more than five million dollars in United States currency and approximately one million dollars in jewelry; older newspaper estimates and popular histories give somewhat different aggregate totals.

Different numbers may reflect preliminary police estimates, currency and jewelry accounting, and subsequent reconstructions. It is inaccurate to present a single precise total as beyond dispute without the airline’s inventory, insurance documents and related financial records. The robbery itself is well established; the individual attribution of planning and subsequent proceeds is a separate question.[8][9][12]

Historical biography · Section 16

16. Burke’s Alleged Place in Lufthansa Planning

The 2014 federal prosecution memorandum in the later Vincent Asaro case states that cooperating witnesses identified planning meetings attended by Burke, Asaro and others. It describes a proposed multimillion-dollar “score” and Burke’s close criminal relationship with Asaro. This is detailed evidence of what federal prosecutors alleged decades after the robbery.

Burke was not charged and convicted of the Lufthansa robbery. The memorandum is a pretrial advocacy filing, and the press release announced accusations against other people. A scholar can accurately report that prosecutors described him as a central participant without turning that claim into a personal conviction or claiming a definitive court finding about his precise role.[8][9][10]

Historical biography · Section 17

17. Cross-Family Cooperation with Vincent Asaro

The Lufthansa record shows how associates could collaborate across New York Mafia family boundaries. Prosecutors alleged that Vincent Asaro, a Bonanno figure, and Burke, a Lucchese associate, joined the planning and expected substantial shares of the stolen proceeds. This helps explain the practical authority of networks over rigid family diagrams.

In November 2015 a jury acquitted Asaro on the charges prosecuted in that case. His acquittal does not prove that Burke was uninvolved, but it requires any retrospective biography to distinguish accusation from conviction and to avoid treating 2014 government charging narratives as settled adjudications.[8][9][10]

Historical biography · Section 18

18. Insider Access and the Robbery’s Mechanics

Historical accounts describe the Lufthansa robbery as dependent on an insider’s knowledge of the terminal’s operations. An armed group moved employees under coercion and removed stored valuables from the site. The details of timing, stolen containers and getaway arrangements have been reconstructed through multiple forms of evidence, but some widely circulated versions rely on later participants’ accounts.

For institutional use, the case illustrates the dependence of major cargo robbery on workplace access and insider knowledge. It should not be turned into a tactical how-to guide. The pedagogical focus is labor vulnerability, investigative provenance, and the effect of violent theft on workers and public confidence.[8][9][12]

Historical biography · Section 19

19. Counting the Stolen Money

Published Lufthansa totals are frequently rounded to “five million,” “5.8 million,” or “six million,” sometimes including jewelry and sometimes separating it. The Department of Justice’s 2014 public release distinguishes more than five million dollars in cash from jewelry valued around one million. That formulation is clearly attributed to the later case.

No verified accounting establishes exactly how much money Burke personally retained. Claims about his personal share are especially dependent on witness recollections about promised divisions, actual payments, and losses after the robbery. A later allegation that each participant expected roughly $750,000 is not the same as proof that the expected payment was ever received.[8][9][12]

Historical biography · Section 20

20. Violence After Lufthansa: Attribution Boundaries

A series of killings and disappearances among people associated with the robbery became a central feature of memoirs and investigative retrospectives. Some sources attribute decisions to Burke. The record available for this edition does not substantiate converting every such allegation into a separate established murder by him.

For each death, historians need the victim’s identity, disappearance date, forensic findings, criminal charge and disposition. In the absence of these, phrases such as “suspected by investigators” or “alleged by witnesses” are necessary. His proven conviction for the murder of Richard Eaton remains analytically distinct from allegations about other heist participants.[2][3][11][12][17]

Historical biography · Section 21

21. The 1969 Paul Katz Disappearance

Paul Katz disappeared in 1969. A federal prosecution memorandum filed in 2014 alleged that Burke and Vincent Asaro strangled him because they suspected cooperation with law enforcement and concealed the body at a Queens property. This is a specific later governmental accusation, not an adjudication against Burke, who had died in 1996.

The allegation should not be conflated with the Richard Eaton murder for which Burke was convicted. The two cases have different victims, years, evidentiary tracks, and procedural histories. Treating them as interchangeable would erase the distinct public record for each victim.[8][9]

Historical biography · Section 22

22. 2013 Excavation and the Katz Identification

Investigators searched a former Burke-associated Queens property in June 2013. Contemporary reporting said that human remains were recovered. The government’s 2014 filing subsequently reported DNA identification of those remains as Paul Katz and referenced forensic examination by the New York City medical examiner.

Forensic identification materially supports the historical claim that Katz’s remains were present, but it does not independently establish every assertion concerning the killers, motive, or person who moved the body. That inference depends on additional evidence and testimony. The distinction between identification and attribution is a central lesson of the case.[8][9][14][20]

Historical biography · Section 23

23. The Boston College Point-Shaving Scheme

The point-shaving conspiracy concerned games in Boston College’s 1978–1979 basketball season. According to the Second Circuit, the Perla brothers and Richard Kuhn were central to an attempt to manipulate final scoring margins so gamblers could profit from point-spread bets. Hill and Paul Mazzei connected this operation to Burke.

The scheme was not a simple agreement to lose games. Its design involved preserving a favorable betting margin even when a team could win. The appellate decision traces meetings, recruiting efforts, payments, and unsuccessful as well as successful attempts. It is one of the most detailed judicially documented episodes in Burke’s career.[1][6][13][19]

Historical biography · Section 24

24. The Perla Brothers, Mazzei and Kuhn

Rocco and Anthony Perla emerged in the federal court narrative as the initial gambling organizers. Their contact with Boston College player Rick Kuhn created the prospect of deliberate manipulation. Paul Mazzei and Henry Hill then provided a connection to New York gambling and to Burke, whom the organizers believed might provide influence or protection.

The 1983 appellate court specifies these distinct roles. Misidentifying Burke as the sole architect neglects the defendants who initiated the operation and the recruited college player whose decisions affected the games. The companion Mazzei appeal is useful for distinguishing each participant’s sentence and appeal.[1][15]

Historical biography · Section 25

25. What Point Shaving Meant for Players

The betting operation exposed college athletes to coercion, intimidation, and conflicts of loyalty. Contemporary testimony from James Sweeney described approaches and fear of those soliciting cooperation. Whether a particular player accepted cash or changed play is an individualized legal and factual question, not something to infer from inclusion in a team roster.

The case demonstrates that organized gambling can exploit an athletic institution even where individual attempted fixes fail. Students should differentiate a point-spread calculation, evidence of corrupt agreement, actions on the court, and the completed federal conspiracy offenses established by the jury.[1][13][19]

Historical biography · Section 26

26. Henry Hill’s Cooperation and the Investigation

After legal exposure tied to narcotics, Hill began cooperating with federal investigators in 1980. His disclosures about point shaving enabled prosecutors to move from a partially concealed gambling arrangement to a detailed case against identified participants. The appellate opinion records how witness accounts were supplemented by telephone, hotel and money-transfer evidence.

Cooperation supplied leads but also created questions of credibility, bargaining and payment. The judicial process required testimony to be heard alongside corroboration and defense challenges. In the historian’s reconstruction, Hill is neither automatically reliable nor automatically false because he later became famous.[1][2][3]

Historical biography · Section 27

27. 1981 Indictment and Public Charges

In July 1981 a federal grand jury in Brooklyn indicted Burke, Kuhn, Mazzei, and Anthony and Rocco Perla. Contemporary coverage describes racketeering conspiracy, sports bribery conspiracy and travel connected to unlawful activity. Newspaper coverage separately mentioned federal suspicion concerning the Lufthansa robbery; the point-shaving indictment was not a Lufthansa indictment.

Dates matter. An indictment is an allegation at filing. The later November 1981 jury verdict and January 1982 sentencing are separate legal events, and neither should be described as occurring on the July charging date. The original indictment and docket remain desirable primary documents for a future archival edition.[1][6][7]

Historical biography · Section 28

28. The November 1981 Federal Jury Verdict

After a federal trial in the Eastern District of New York, the defendants were convicted in November 1981. The Second Circuit’s 1983 opinion lists offenses under RICO conspiracy, conspiracy to commit sports bribery and interstate travel with intent to facilitate bribery. Contemporary newspaper reports identify the result as a five-defendant verdict.

Crucially, the judgment demonstrated a prosecutable betting enterprise, not a federal conviction for every robbery and homicide in Burke’s public reputation. The appellate discussion concentrated on evidentiary and procedural questions concerning the basketball case.[1][7]

Historical biography · Section 29

29. January 22, 1982: Federal Sentencing

Contemporaneous UPI reporting dated January 22, 1982 states that Judge Henry Bramwell sentenced Burke to a 20-year federal term and fined him $30,000. A February 6 Washington Post account independently repeats that term. The published Second Circuit opinion likewise records a 20-year prison sentence.

Many later biographical summaries substitute a 12-year sentence, perhaps confusing a reported custodial term, an offense-specific count, a later account or simple repetition. The three near-contemporaneous and judicial sources make 20 years the properly documented imposed federal term. This biography explicitly flags the contradictory 12-year retellings rather than incorporating them as equal findings.[1][4][5][11]

Historical biography · Section 30

30. The 1983 Second Circuit Decision

In United States v. Burke, 700 F.2d 70, the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit affirmed the federal point-shaving convictions on January 28, 1983. The opinion recounts how the conspiracy arose, identifies testimony from Hill, Sweeney, Beaulieu and Reed, and addresses evidentiary challenges.

Among the appellate issues was Burke’s attempt to obtain material relating to a Sports Illustrated account prepared with Hill. This was a dispute about evidence disclosure and the reporter’s privilege, not a new prosecution for the 1978 robbery. The federal appeal should be read for the specific counts and judgment it actually reviewed.[1][15]

Historical biography · Section 31

31. Evidence Beyond Hill in the Basketball Case

According to the appellate decision, prosecutors used witness testimony, confessions attributed to other participants, telephone evidence, Western Union records and hotel records. These materials strengthened the case by linking people and meetings in the same time frame.

Such corroboration does not eliminate every factual uncertainty, but it matters when contrasting this conviction with episodes known mainly from memoirs. A reconstruction that treats all witness accounts as equivalent ignores the difference between evidence tested through an adversarial criminal trial and descriptions introduced in books decades later.[1][6][19]

Historical biography · Section 32

32. Richard Eaton: Discovery of the Body

New York’s highest court recorded that Richard Eaton’s body was discovered on February 18, 1979, bound and gagged inside an abandoned trailer. Burke was indicted for Eaton’s murder more than four years afterward. The resulting prosecution developed on a different track from the Boston College federal conspiracy.

Eaton should not be reduced to a plot device in the story of a Mafia associate. The date, the physical evidence, and the eventual criminal judgment establish a particular homicide case. The public judicial opinions available here do not answer every question about the full forensic investigation.[2][3]

Historical biography · Section 33

33. The State Murder Indictment and Trial

Burke was prosecuted in Kings County, New York, for Eaton’s murder. The state case relied heavily on Henry Hill’s testimony, which included an account of an admission Burke allegedly made to him. Defense counsel challenged Hill’s motivations, including the financial benefits and protections connected with his cooperation.

The prosecution did not rest on a generic claim that Burke had a violent character; it had to prove a specified homicide charge. The appellate courts later examined the legal propriety of admitting and discussing Hill’s cooperation agreement. The trial transcript and full jury charge have not been collated in this edition.[2][3]

Historical biography · Section 34

34. February 25, 1985: Second-Degree Murder Conviction

The published intermediate appellate decision identifies a Kings County judgment rendered February 25, 1985, convicting Burke of second-degree murder on a jury verdict. This is the central established homicide conviction in his biography. Contemporary and retrospective sentencing reports give a state term of twenty years to life.

The sentence must be distinguished from his earlier federal point-shaving term. One case was a state murder prosecution involving Richard Eaton; the other was a federal sports-bribery and RICO conspiracy. Popular summaries sometimes compress these cases into a single prison story, obscuring separate victims, jurisdictions and legal standards.[2][3][11]

Historical biography · Section 35

35. The 1987 Appellate Division Affirmance

On March 2, 1987 the New York Appellate Division, Second Department affirmed Burke’s second-degree murder judgment and denied relief on the associated motion to vacate. The opinion specifically discusses Hill’s cooperation agreement and defense questioning about payments, relocation and travel expenses.

The appellate court held that the agreement’s introduction was permissible in the circumstances presented. This does not mean that every narrative Hill later told was certified as fact. It shows why that portion of his testimony and the prosecution’s response did not require reversal of the Eaton conviction.[2]

Historical biography · Section 36

36. The 1988 New York Court of Appeals Ruling

On June 9, 1988 the New York Court of Appeals affirmed the Appellate Division’s order. The decision summarizes the evidence relevant to the admissibility dispute, including Hill’s claimed account of Burke’s motive in the Eaton killing and Hill’s 1980 cooperation relationships.

An affirmance establishes the durability of the legal judgment following the presented appeal. It does not adjudicate the Lufthansa robbery or the Katz disappearance. Keeping the appellate scope clear prevents the criminal record from being exaggerated through association.[3]

Historical biography · Section 37

37. Evaluating the Eaton Motive

Hill testified that Burke said Eaton had failed to pay for a cocaine transaction and that the murder was intended to intimidate others who owed money. The New York Court of Appeals discussed this testimony as part of the case record. It is proper to say the prosecution’s primary witness gave this account.

The precise inner motive, beyond what the jury concluded about murder, remains an interpretive problem. The same evidence that supports guilt for an intentional homicide may not independently establish every claimed drug-debt amount or the full scope of other dealings. Attribution should remain visible in the prose.[3]

Historical biography · Section 38

38. Witness Cooperation, Money and Credibility

The Eaton appeal offers a rare judicial lens on the economics of cooperation. Defense counsel explored Hill’s compensation and support under witness-protection arrangements; the prosecution introduced the agreement to explain those payments. The appellate court evaluated whether these events prejudiced the trial.

For historians, the lesson is methodological. A cooperating witness may be uniquely informed and also have powerful incentives. Corroboration, contemporaneous records, cross-examination and the exact question a court decided must shape the assessment. The category “informant” alone is insufficient to establish either truth or fabrication.[2][3]

Historical biography · Section 39

39. Why Two Convictions Must Not Be Merged

Burke’s 1981 federal verdict and 1985 state verdict were separate proceedings. The first concerned sports betting and an interstate racketeering conspiracy, while the second addressed the killing of Richard Eaton. Federal appeal was resolved in 1983; the murder appeals were decided in 1987 and 1988.

The distinction also clarifies sentence reporting: the contemporary point-shaving disposition was twenty years, whereas a twenty-years-to-life state homicide term is separately reported. A condensed chronology must not add these sentences mechanically or imply any particular release eligibility without consulting the custody record.[1][2][3][4][5]

Historical biography · Section 40

40. Criminal Status During the 1980s

Burke’s custody and convictions curtailed the ability to conduct operations openly during the 1980s. Contemporary reporting nevertheless shows that his reputation continued to shape witness accounts, law-enforcement investigations and popular interpretations of the earlier robberies.

It would be speculative to assign him ongoing command over a Lucchese crew while imprisoned without dated prison communications and independent evidence. His documented institutional status remained a nonmember associate with serious convictions, not an officially appointed acting boss.[1][2][8][11]

Historical biography · Section 41

41. The 1987 Killing of Frank Burke

Published accounts describe the 1987 killing of Burke’s son Frank James Burke. The event appears in some reconstructions of the losses endured by people connected to the broader Mafia milieu, but a full homicide investigative record has not been collated for this biography.

Family members should not automatically be treated as participants in every enterprise associated with Burke. The historical issue is the specific victim, evidence and outcome in that case. An editorial treatment should avoid turning an individual death into a generalized claim about a parent’s guilt or motives.[11][16][17]

Historical biography · Section 42

42. Prison, Illness and 1996 Death

Burke remained in New York State custody during his later years. His April 13, 1996 death in Buffalo, New York is supported by contemporaneous obituary reporting and subsequent corrections-image records. Retrospective sources describe a cancer diagnosis, often specified as lung cancer.

The precise terminal medical details should be attributed to the obituary and corrections-related secondary reporting, not to an unseen hospital file. Burke died before any hypothetical later parole date could be adjudicated. His death closed the possibility of further criminal prosecution against him, although investigators continued to study older events.[11][18]

Historical biography · Section 43

43. New York’s 2013 Cold-Case Search

Nearly seventeen years after Burke’s death, federal investigators excavated a Queens property previously associated with him. The search drew renewed attention to old disappearance cases and to the fact that buildings and land can preserve physical evidence even after suspects and witnesses have died.

Contemporary reporting described the discovery as suspected human remains and soon confirmed that the material was human. The 2014 prosecutorial memorandum added a reported DNA identification as Paul Katz. The separate dates of search, forensic confirmation and public charging document should not be compressed into a single event.[8][9][14][20]

Historical biography · Section 44

44. What the Katz Forensic Record Can Establish

The government’s later memorandum describes multiple recovered bones and DNA confirmation of Katz’s identity. This is stronger physical support than decades-old rumor that a body might have been buried on the property. It also demonstrates a chain of evidence that can be studied through forensic and procedural records.

But physical identification does not convict a deceased person of killing the identified victim. The 2014 filing’s separate murder narrative still rested on witness evidence and prosecution theory; the 2015 Asaro acquittal confirms the need to preserve that difference.[8][9][10][20]

Historical biography · Section 45

45. The 2015 Asaro Acquittal

On November 12, 2015 Vincent Asaro was acquitted in a Brooklyn federal trial involving the Lufthansa robbery and racketeering allegations. Reporting on the verdict identified the government’s reconstruction of the robbery, which had included Burke as an alleged conspirator.

An acquittal concerns the defendant charged in that case and the offenses tried. It neither legally exonerates Burke nor establishes his guilt. The value for Burke’s biography is the visible limit of the prosecution narrative: a named allegation can be extensive, supported by witnesses, and still fail to result in conviction for the person actually on trial.[8][9][10]

Historical biography · Section 46

46. Wiseguy as a Source

Nicholas Pileggi’s Wiseguy, developed from Henry Hill’s recollections, profoundly shaped the public image of the Burke–Vario milieu. Its narrative supplies names, relationships, settings and participant perspectives that can guide historical inquiry, but its interview-based storytelling is not equivalent to certified judicial findings.

When citing Pileggi, researchers should distinguish Hill’s own knowledge from stories he heard secondhand, and check dates against the 1981–1988 appellate record. Details that cannot be corroborated should remain attributed rather than recited as neutral fact. Literary vividness is not a substitute for documentation.[1][2][3][16]

Historical biography · Section 47

47. Goodfellas and the Fictional Jimmy Conway

Martin Scorsese’s 1990 film Goodfellas, adapted from Pileggi’s book, features Robert De Niro as Jimmy Conway, a fictionalized character based on Burke. The character borrows the name, reputation and broad trajectory of Burke but exists within a scripted dramatic work.

Film viewers often remember compressed dialogue, dramatic deaths and composed scenes more readily than court citations. An encyclopedia must reverse that relationship: the criminal judgments, dates and source limitations should determine the biography, while the film belongs in a separate section on cultural reception.[11][12][16]

Historical biography · Section 48

48. The Problem of Sensational Biography

Accounts of Burke often emphasize grim anecdotes about dismemberment, threats, generous tipping and a supposed trail of dozens of murders. These stories vary in proximity to witnesses and records. Some are from participant narratives, some are reported as investigator suspicions, and others circulate without a retrievable primary source.

Repeating all of them as proven crimes undermines historical accuracy and blurs the boundaries between Eaton’s judicially affirmed murder conviction and uncharged allegations involving other people. This edition records significant attributed stories only when they illuminate source reliability or organizational mechanisms.[2][3][11][12][16]

Historical biography · Section 49

49. Victim and Community Impacts

The network around Burke operated through predatory gambling, illicit cargo acquisition, coercion and violence. The documented Boston College conspiracy harmed the integrity of collegiate sport and exploited the vulnerability of students; the Eaton homicide ended a person’s life and produced a lasting record of family and community loss.

Airport robberies also placed civilian employees under threat and imposed losses on enterprises and insurers. An historical biography that dwells exclusively on criminal ingenuity risks excluding the lives and economic interests affected. Institutional scholarship should retain those consequences at the center of analysis.[1][2][3][8][13]

Historical biography · Section 50

50. Business and Organizational Interpretation

The significance of Burke’s career lies partly in a hybrid operating model: close relationships with an inducted Mafia captain could supply protection and market access, while a nonmember associate handled direct coordination with outsiders, drivers or gamblers. This arrangement could be profitable because the organizational boundary was flexible.

That flexibility also complicates responsibility. A criminal enterprise may be highly interconnected without every act reflecting a standing order from higher leadership. For each charge or alleged event, identify the relevant participant, legal authority, and record before attributing an organizational policy.[1][8][9][16]

Historical biography · Section 51

51. Federal and State Jurisdiction Compared

The Boston College case was a federal prosecution invoking interstate activity, sports bribery conspiracy and RICO. The Eaton case proceeded under New York’s homicide law, with appellate review in the state judicial system. These were different sovereign authorities, even when witnesses or participants overlapped.

The 2014 Asaro case was again federal but postdated Burke’s death. A chronology that places all three under the label “FBI conviction” would be inaccurate. The distinctions between indictment, guilty verdict, sentence, appeal, prosecution memorandum and acquittal are central to interpreting the record.[1][2][3][8][9][10]

Historical biography · Section 52

52. The Sentence Discrepancy Explained

Published short biographies repeatedly describe a 12-year sentence for the point-shaving case. This does not accord with the Second Circuit’s 20-year statement or the January 1982 UPI and February 1982 Washington Post reports of a 20-year federal term. No verified judgment in the accessible record establishes the later 12-year number as the sentence originally imposed on Burke.

For first-edition publication, the editorial standard is to prefer the appellate and contemporary sentencing evidence, clearly disclosing the alternative. A later shortened custody period or parole computation must not be inferred as an explanation without a sentencing judgment or corrections ledger.[1][4][5][11]

Historical biography · Section 53

53. Source Hierarchy and Evidentiary Weight

Published appellate opinions are strongest for proving the counts convicted, the procedural history and the legal result. They may summarize contested testimony without certifying every detail as historical truth. A prosecution press release establishes what officials charged and alleged, not what a jury later accepted.

Contemporaneous journalism can preserve sentencing facts and eyewitness descriptions but must be checked for errors. Memoirs and historical syntheses are useful for perspective and context. Fiction is valuable for reception history, not proof of events. The reference register below identifies each source type so readers can evaluate claims without guesswork.[1][2][3][4][8][9][10][11]

54. Chronological Research Gaps

A full scholarly edition would collate Burke’s birth and marriage records; juvenile custody documents; the early extortion indictment and sentence; the original Lufthansa case investigation and cargo-loss report; the complete 1981 federal trial transcript; the 1985 Eaton trial record; and the 1996 death certificate.

Historical biography · Section 55

55. Comparative Place in the Lucchese Network

Burke is best studied alongside Paul Vario, Henry Hill, Thomas DeSimone and the larger history of the Lucchese organization. He differs from formal bosses such as Anthony Corallo because his criminal influence was exercised as a nonmember associate, not through the official hierarchy. His notoriety does not change that institutional status.

His career also overlaps in time with the transition from Thomas Lucchese to Carmine Tramunti and then Anthony Corallo, but that overlapping chronology alone cannot prove personal access to each boss. The cross-references in this series should reinforce affiliation and chronology while maintaining independent standards of attribution.[8][11][16]

Historical biography · Section 56

56. Historical Assessment and Legacy

James Burke’s enduring significance is not simply his alleged place in a record-setting robbery or the fictional portrayal of “Jimmy Conway.” His record provides a documented example of the relationship between nonmember criminal associates, organized gambling, extortion, witness cooperation, and violent enforcement in late-twentieth-century New York.

The 1983 federal appellate judgment and 1987–1988 New York appellate decisions establish the essential criminal convictions; the 2014–2015 Lufthansa and Katz records reveal the limits of later accusation and forensic reconstruction. Read together, they produce a more rigorous biography than either heroic gangster legend or undifferentiated law-enforcement allegation.[1][2][3][8][9][10][11]

Documentary Research Windows

The following eight windows are analytical guides and editorial paraphrases. None claims to be an original, page-collated primary-source excerpt or a recreated quotation.

Documentary research window 1

The federal point-shaving appellate record

Read United States v. Burke, 700 F.2d 70, as a judicial decision with findings about charges, evidence and appellate disposition. Chart the Perlas, Mazzei, Kuhn, Hill and Burke without falsely reducing the scheme to one architect. The source says twenty years, despite later twelve-year summaries.[1][4][5]

The original 1982 sentencing report

Compare UPI’s January 22, 1982 account with the February Washington Post report and the appellate judgment. Record dates, imposed term, fine and court. Identify whether any later twelve-year figure refers to a different legal metric; do not invent an explanation.[1][4][5]

Documentary research window 3

The Eaton state murder appeals

Examine the 1987 Appellate Division ruling and the 1988 Court of Appeals decision as two separate procedural acts. Their discussion of Henry Hill’s cooperation relates to admissibility and fairness in Eaton’s murder case, not to an omnibus determination about Burke’s life.[2][3]

The 2014 Lufthansa prosecution narrative

Research and Publication Limitations

The following primary records were not collated for this edition: original birth and death certificates; juvenile case files; the certified 1974 extortion judgment; FBI Lufthansa source exhibits and original loss inventory; Burke’s complete 1981 federal trial transcript and sentencing judgment; full 1985 Eaton trial transcript and sentencing minutes; original state prison custody ledger; the complete 2015 Asaro verdict form; and Katz forensic laboratory records. Accordingly, early-career incidents, specific Lufthansa proceeds, some rank claims and exact custody calculations remain qualified.

The 2014 Asaro memorandum is a government submission stating allegations and descriptions of cooperators. The later Asaro acquittal is separately reported. The Eaton conviction was affirmed, but that does not establish guilt in the Lufthansa or Katz allegations. One popular twelve-year term for the basketball case conflicts with independent contemporary and appellate evidence establishing a twenty-year imposed federal term. The eight documentary research windows are editorial paraphrases, not original-source transcriptions.
