1. Historical Overview

Anthony “Tony Ducks” Corallo occupied one of the most consequential positions in the postwar New York Mafia: the leadership of the Lucchese organization during the period when federal prosecutors shifted from individual crimes to the governing structures of organized crime. Contemporary law-enforcement descriptions identified him as Lucchese boss; the 1989 Second Circuit judgment in the Commission prosecution records his role in a racketeering enterprise that coordinated activities across criminal families. [6][13]

His significance rests on two linked historical developments. First, labor organizations and their relationships with contractors became channels through which criminal actors extracted payments and influenced business decisions. Second, court-authorized recordings in a chauffeur’s Jaguar exposed conversations far beyond the reach of conventional witness interviews. The 1986 guilty verdict and 1987 century-long sentence ended Corallo’s active reign and reshaped family succession. [8][9][12][13]

2. Names, Spellings and the “Tony Ducks” Nickname

Federal court captions identify the defendant as Antonio Corallo, while newspapers and reference works often use Anthony Corallo. The nickname “Tony Ducks” became the identifying shorthand in congressional hearings, police profiles and Commission-trial reporting. Name variants must be connected to the same person through record context rather than assumed from a matching surname. [1][4][6]

Later accounts explain “Ducks” through his ability to evade prosecutions, questioning or unwanted attention. That etymology is plausible and widely repeated but is not a judicial finding in the cases examined. It should be read as a retrospective explanation of a nickname, not as a literal account of every arrest or charge. [15][22]

3. Birth, Death and Identity Evidence

A 1983 U.S. Senate organized-crime profile identifies Antonio Corallo as born February 12, 1913. Published obituaries establish his death on August 23, 2000, aged 87, in a federal prison hospital in Springfield, Missouri. These records support the essential biographical dates, although this edition does not reproduce an original civil birth certificate or Bureau of Prisons death file. [6][15]

Death in federal custody followed his 100-year Commission sentence; that term should not be recast as a separate formal life-imprisonment sentence simply because death occurred in prison. Obituary writers often describe the practical result as a life term. The operative sentencing record, however, is the federal judgment analyzed by the Second Circuit. [12][13][15]

4. East Harlem and the Urban Setting

Corallo grew up in the East Harlem world that also produced major figures in the Reina–Gagliano–Lucchese succession. The neighborhood’s streets linked immigrant-owned businesses, transportation, social clubs, tenements and a complex local economy. Organized crime operated within that wider environment but did not define the lives of the community’s residents. [15]

Later biographical sketches place Corallo in a teenage street network and describe an early tile-setting occupation. Independent school, trade and juvenile-court documents have not been collated here, so exact dates for his earliest criminal associations remain provisional. The documentary foundation becomes much firmer once his mid-century union activities and federal cases enter the public record. [15][7]

5. Early Criminal Career and Arrest Narratives

Secondary accounts describe a series of arrests and an early narcotics-possession conviction that helped shape Corallo’s public reputation. An official mid-1970s retrospective also mentions a short 1941 drug sentence. Such assertions deserve further testing against police ledgers and certified judgments because contemporary media regularly compressed arrests, charges and convictions into one career summary. [22]

The later 1962, 1968 and 1986 convictions are supported by accessible judicial opinions. This page therefore treats those outcomes as the core criminal-history register while identifying the 1920s–1940s arrest narratives as archival leads. Neither the absence of a conviction in one particular case nor the presence of an arrest proves every other allegation true. [1][4][13]

6. Reina, Gagliano and Lucchese: Organizational Lineage

The organization eventually known as the Lucchese family passed through the leadership of Gaetano Reina, Thomas Gagliano and Thomas Lucchese before the Tramunti–Corallo succession. This historical sequence matters because calling the group the Lucchese family in 1930 is a retrospective naming convention. Contemporary observers used different labels and had incomplete knowledge of its internal offices. [15][17]

Corallo’s rise unfolded within that continuity. He was not an independent family founder, and the 1980s evidence of him as boss is much stronger than detailed claims about his precise standing in the 1930s. The distinction guards against reading the well-documented end of his career backward into unverifiable early promotion stories. [6][13]

7. Johnny Dio and Labor-Racketeering Networks

Accounts of Corallo’s mid-century advancement repeatedly place him alongside John “Johnny Dio” Dioguardi in labor-related rackets. Investigators linked criminal intermediaries, union officers, employers and legal business entities through arrangements that gave connected actors leverage over workers and management. No single union local or transaction can explain the entire network. [7][22]

The historically important question is institutional: who held formal office, who directed policy, how contracts were negotiated, and which enterprises paid for protection or favorable treatment. A criminal association and a union title are different evidentiary propositions. The 1988 Teamsters litigation and earlier congressional studies should be used to document them separately. [16][7]

8. Teamsters Local 239 and Official Positions

A 1988 account of the federal civil action against organized-crime influence in the Teamsters identified Corallo as a former vice president of Local 239 in East Meadow. An official title supplied access to employers, labor discussions and workers’ institutions, but it does not by itself establish when a particular payment was unlawful. [16]

The presidency, vice presidency, business agency and trusteeship of each local should be checked against union charters and Department of Labor records before assigning exact terms or dollar figures. Official reports describe a broader problem of coercive influence over Teamsters units, and Corallo appears repeatedly in that discussion. The biography avoids attributing every abusive contract to a personal directive by him. [7][16]

9. Consequences for Workers and Employers

Labor racketeering is not merely a meeting of crime figures or a colorful subplot of Mafia history. It can weaken workers’ bargaining power, divert dues, frustrate democratic elections, penalize independent employers and impose artificial costs on customers. Congressional investigators described conditions in which workers faced intimidation and management secured agreements through covert inducements. [7][22]

A historical assessment must distinguish legal collective bargaining from criminal exploitation of a union’s authority. The cases and hearings cited here concern alleged or proved coercive uses of institutional power. They cannot justify a conclusion that entire unions, industries or ethnic communities were criminal organizations. [7][16]

10. McClellan Committee and Public Exposure

The Senate Select Committee on Improper Activities in Labor and Management brought widespread attention to criminal influence over union affairs in the late 1950s. Federal retrospectives treated Corallo as an important subject of that inquiry and connected his role to contentious Teamsters organizing and business practices. [7][22]

The often repeated stories about Corallo’s appearance before senators, his demeanor and the number of occasions when he invoked constitutional protections should not replace the original hearing transcript. Invocation of the Fifth Amendment is a lawful procedure, not proof of a particular crime. The underlying hearing volumes would permit exact reconstruction of each exchange and each witness’s claims. [7][22]

11. 1962 Conspiracy to Obstruct Justice

The clearest early federal prosecution concerned an effort to influence the administration of justice in a bankruptcy-fraud case. Corallo, former prosecutor Elliott Kahaner and New York Supreme Court Justice James Vincent Keogh were convicted in 1962 of a conspiracy to obstruct justice. The Second Circuit opinion describes evidence about attempted intervention in a sentencing process and disputed payments. [1][2]

The case is sometimes condensed to a “judge-bribery conviction.” A more exact formulation is the charge and verdict in the published opinion: conspiracy to corruptly influence, obstruct or impede the administration of justice under federal law. That distinction matters when compiling a criminal-history index and when separating the underlying proposed acts from the offenses actually adjudicated. [1]

12. 1963 Appellate Affirmance and Witness Evidence

In United States v. Kahaner, the Second Circuit affirmed the jury judgment after examining challenges to the prosecution’s witnesses, evidence and instructions. The appellate court acknowledged that principal government witnesses had incentives to cooperate; it nevertheless upheld the verdict as legally sustainable. [1]

The case illustrates how an appellate opinion can give historians more than a simple guilty/not-guilty label. Its factual narrative is written to resolve legal disputes, not to furnish an exhaustive biography. Assertions about money, meetings and motive should therefore be attributed to the government’s case, to the jury’s finding or to the appellate holding as appropriate. [1]

13. After the First Federal Sentence

Congressional sources list a two-year sentence for the 1962 conspiracy case, and later summaries describe Corallo’s return to underworld and labor-related influence. The exact dates of custody, parole and work with individual locals warrant review of Bureau of Prisons and union records, which have not been examined directly here. [5][7]

Continuity of professional contacts after incarceration does not mean legal proceedings lacked effect. Rather, it demonstrates the difficulty of dismantling institutional rackets solely through prosecution of one participant. By the late 1960s, Corallo was under renewed federal scrutiny, this time connected to a New York City infrastructure contract and a city commissioner. [3][4]

14. Thomas Lucchese’s Death and the Transition

Thomas Lucchese died in July 1967. Carmine Tramunti was widely identified as the succeeding boss or as an interim figure, while many later histories suggest Corallo was the organization’s intended or operational successor. The chronology becomes especially complicated because Corallo faced another federal corruption case and served a prison term. [15][17]

A precise day on which Corallo acquired all functions of boss cannot be established from the judicial cases alone. The edition therefore uses a conventional early-1970s transition, with full public identification as boss firmly documented by the 1983 congressional record. It does not treat acting authority, Commission recognition and later historical labels as interchangeable. [6][17]

15. The December 1967 James Marcus Indictment

A separate federal indictment filed on December 18, 1967 charged Corallo and other defendants with a conspiracy involving interstate communications to promote violations of state bribery law. The dispute centered on dealings with James L. Marcus, New York City’s former water commissioner, and contracting interests associated with municipal public works. [3][4]

The record is not simply an illustration of generic “mob corruption.” It contains identifiable defendants, corporate entities, telephone communications, alleged inducements and a public official. Reconstructing the case requires following both the pretrial opinion and the 1969 appellate decision, rather than importing factual details from the earlier Keogh proceedings. [3][4]

16. Municipal Infrastructure and Contractor Access

The Marcus case connected public administration, debt, contractors and the attempted conversion of government decision-making into a private revenue stream. The appellate opinion describes Marcus’s contact with intermediaries and the alleged efforts to influence a reservoir construction project and other city matters. [4]

Municipal procurement offered high-value leverage because public agencies controlled contracts, approvals and project timing. Yet the formal offense was a federal conspiracy involving interstate facilities and state bribery laws, not a blanket conviction for every suspected loan, contract or construction-company arrangement mentioned in the narrative. [4]

17. 1968 Trial and the Importance of Procedural Records

The district court reported extensive pretrial publicity and litigation over whether the case should be moved or postponed. The subsequent trial resulted in convictions for Corallo, Henry Fried, Daniel Motto and S. T. Grand, Inc.; another accused participant was acquitted, and James Marcus had pleaded guilty before trial. [3][4]

These distinctions make the appellate narrative useful for teaching how conspiracy prosecutions unfold: one indictment may yield different outcomes for defendants. Neither a person’s presence in the caption nor evidence admitted against another defendant automatically establishes the first person’s guilt on every issue. [4]

18. 1969 Appellate Decision and the Bribery Conspiracy

On July 8, 1969, the Second Circuit affirmed the convictions in United States v. Corallo, 413 F.2d 1306. Its treatment of telephone contacts, witness credibility, the role of intermediaries and admissibility of coconspirator evidence offers a detailed judicial basis for the outcome. [4]

Biographical timelines often mention only a “three-year prison sentence” and an early 1970s release. This edition emphasizes what the consulted appellate opinion firmly establishes—the charged scheme, conviction and affirmance—and marks precise incarceration chronology for further certified-record research. [4]

19. Tramunti and Corallo: The Disputed Boss Years

During the years after Lucchese’s death, Carmine Tramunti remained visible in law-enforcement and court records while Corallo gained influence. Some biographies label Tramunti an acting boss and Corallo an eventual substantive leader; others give Tramunti the formal title until about 1973. Those positions should be presented as historical interpretations rather than opposing court judgments. [6][15][17]

The strongest fixed point is the Senate’s 1983 organized-crime profile, which identifies Corallo as boss, together with the 1986–1989 federal verdict and appeal. The actual mechanics of transition are better tested with contemporary police assessments and independent insider evidence than with a single retrospective boss chart. [6][13]

20. Structure of Leadership in the 1970s

By the 1970s Corallo had consolidated a leadership position in a family with operations in New York City, Long Island and northern New Jersey. As boss, he could influence members and select managers, but organizational authority was distributed through underbosses, consigliere, captains, crews and associated businesses. [6][17]

Official intelligence profiles are useful for reconstructing hierarchy but not definitive for each relationship. A formal Lucchese title may be correctly identified while an individual racketeering charge remains unproven. The Commission case later supplied court-tested evidence of interfamily authority, some of which confirmed the earlier intelligence model. [13][17]

21. Salvatore “Tom Mix” Santoro

Salvatore Santoro was described as the Lucchese underboss in a 1983 Senate profile and was a codefendant of Corallo in the Commission case. The 1989 appellate opinion places him in meetings and communications relevant to the Commission and to the loansharking-conspiracy count. [6][13]

Treating Santoro as a distinct decision-maker helps avoid a “one boss controlled everything” interpretation. The court assessed conduct by participants and their relationships with the enterprise. A later judicial dissent challenged the evidence for the loansharking conviction shared by Corallo and Santoro, even though the majority affirmed it. [13]

22. Christopher “Christie Tick” Furnari

Christopher Furnari served as a major senior figure and was identified by the sentencing judge as Lucchese consigliere, effectively near the underboss in importance. His Commission-trial conviction means Corallo’s fall was not isolated: several layers of the family’s administration were incapacitated together. [13]

Later accounts link Furnari to the post-1986 choice of Vittorio Amuso and Anthony Casso. Those accounts should be assigned to their individual sources, particularly Casso’s retrospective statements. A reported succession conversation is different from a judicial finding in the Commission case. [18][17]

23. Salvatore Avellino and the Chauffeur Relationship

Salvatore Avellino owned the black 1982 Jaguar used to drive Corallo to appointments and meetings. The car’s apparently private interior became a source of decisive evidence when investigators installed an electronic device under judicial authorization. [8][9]

Avellino was more than an incidental driver in the state’s account: officials connected him to the private sanitation industry and criminal relationships. The historians’ interest, however, is not that car travel was illicit in itself. The recordings mattered because investigators and prosecutors connected conversations to specific activities, participants and alleged enterprise functions. [8][21]

24. Long Island Carting and Waste-Hauling Networks

Private garbage collection occupied a strategically valuable position in the regional economy. Firms depended on access to customers, routes, dumps, workers and trade associations. The President’s Commission studied how those components could be manipulated through threats, coordinated arrangements and union influence. [21]

Corallo-era reporting ties Lucchese participants to carting-industry control, but specific conduct should be traced to separate cases and administrative findings. The federal Commission trial emphasized a broader interfamily construction-extortion enterprise; it should not be treated as one universal conviction for every Long Island carting allegation. [9][13][21]

25. Concrete, Construction and the “Club” Arrangement

The 1985 indictment alleged a system in which selected contractors gained access to major Manhattan concrete-pouring jobs while rivals were pressured to refrain from competitive bids. Prosecution theory described payoffs and the power to manipulate labor relations and concrete supply as the instruments of enforcement. [9]

The appellate decision later sustained RICO and related extortion and labor-bribery convictions arising from the Commission arrangement. It describes the “Club,” high-value contracts and an interfamily enterprise rather than merely a Lucchese-only scheme. The participants’ roles must be read count by count. [13]

26. The Commission as an Interfamily Institution

The Mafia Commission, as the court described it, regulated relations between families, mediated disputes, recognized leadership and coordinated criminal ventures. It functioned at a level above individual crews. In the federal trial, prosecutors alleged a continuing association that could be prosecuted as a racketeering enterprise. [13]

Corallo’s role as a Lucchese boss mattered because the case concerned institutional authority, not solely personal participation in a single incident. The appellate opinion sets out how conversations about rules, induction, family disputes and business coordination could illuminate the enterprise’s purpose within the evidentiary framework. [13]

27. Why Early 1980s Investigators Prioritized Leadership

Federal and state investigators increasingly combined conspiracy analysis with enterprise statutes and electronic surveillance. The FBI’s institutional history presents the Commission prosecution as part of a larger turn toward coordination among agencies and prosecution of organized-crime structures. [20]

That shift did not eliminate ordinary constitutional requirements. Authorities required court authorization for relevant interception methods and prosecutors had to prove charged elements before a jury. The important change was that they could connect numerous acts and conversations to a continuing enterprise rather than rely on a single informant’s account. [13][14]

28. March 18, 1983: Installing the Jaguar Device

On March 18, 1983, investigators from the New York State Organized Crime Task Force installed a listening device behind the dashboard of Avellino’s Jaguar while it was parked at a Long Island event venue. Contemporary reporting says the team had practiced on a similar car and took care not to attract attention while working under a court order. [8][9]

The technical operation is often repeated as a dramatic legend. Its historical importance lies in the provenance and continuity of the recorded conversations. Newspaper descriptions of minute-by-minute tactics should be treated as contemporary attributed reporting; the admissibility, interpretation and relevance of the tapes are addressed in later court proceedings. [8][13]

29. Five Months of Recorded Conversations

Investigators monitored the Jaguar in the spring and summer of 1983, recovering conversations among Corallo, Avellino and other figures. The tapes provided evidence of relationships, industry arrangements, family rules and attempts to deal with perceived surveillance. [8][21]

A recorded statement can be exceptionally valuable, but it is not self-explanatory: speaker identification, context, transcript accuracy and the charged issue all matter. The 1989 appellate decision reviewed challenges to recordings and coconspirator statements, which makes it stronger evidence of their legal use than post hoc anecdotes about one memorable remark. [13]

30. State and Federal Intelligence Cooperation

The Jaguar evidence moved from New York state investigators into a much larger federal enterprise investigation. Contemporary reports credited cooperation among prosecutors, police and federal agents with identifying interfamily structures otherwise difficult to document. [8][9][20]

A story focused only on a single listening device oversimplifies the prosecution. The National Archives describes prolonged court-authorized surveillance, and the appellate opinion discusses a range of witnesses and recordings. It was the combination of investigative strands, rather than a one-off trick, that ultimately supplied the government’s trial theory. [13][14]

31. February 1985 Commission Indictment

On February 26, 1985, prosecutors announced indictments accusing major New York Mafia figures of taking part in an organized-crime governing commission. Corallo was named alongside leaders and senior members of other families. The indictment charged a pattern including extortion and labor-related conduct around the concrete industry. [8][9]

A criminal indictment is the prosecution’s accusation, not proof. The 1985 press coverage is invaluable for reconstructing what officials publicly alleged at the time, but conclusions about guilt should be drawn from the November 1986 verdict and the 1989 appeal, not from the original press release or arrest photograph. [9][10][13]

32. 1986 Commission Trial: Enterprise Rather Than Folklore

The federal Commission trial began in September 1986 and ran for about eleven weeks. Testimony and recordings were offered to establish a coordinated racketeering enterprise, with the defendants linked through management positions and conduct. Its legal framework differed from the mythic notion that simply possessing a “Mafia boss” title automatically established criminal liability. [11][13]

The jury had to decide charged counts against identified defendants. The record includes discussions of construction contracts, labor payoffs, organizational rules, investigations and other conduct. Defense attorneys challenged the prosecution’s interpretation and admissibility of evidence; those objections were part of the adversarial process, not inconvenient details to omit. [13][19]

33. Defense Arguments and the Need for Precision

Corallo’s defense attorney contested the government’s approach to construction payments during closing arguments. The appellate record preserves challenges to evidentiary rulings, recordings and the scope of the enterprise theory. Historical writing should acknowledge those disputes even where the court ultimately rejected them. [13][19]

The defense was not obliged to prove Corallo’s innocence. Prosecutors bore the burden of proof on each charged offense. The final verdict and appeal resolve the legal result for the counts affirmed, but do not transform every allegation advanced in a prosecutor’s speech into a separate adjudicated fact. [13]

34. November 19, 1986: Guilty Verdict

On November 19, 1986, the Commission jury convicted Corallo and seven codefendants on racketeering charges. Contemporaneous accounts characterized the ruling as a major government victory against the organizational leadership of the New York Mafia. [10][11]

For Corallo, the appellate opinion identifies RICO conspiracy and substantive RICO convictions together with relevant extortion, labor-bribery and loansharking-conspiracy counts. The conviction register should use this charge-specific account rather than the misleading shorthand that the entire jury simply convicted him of “being on the Commission.” [13]

35. January 13, 1987: The One-Hundred-Year Sentence

U.S. District Judge Richard Owen sentenced Corallo to a total of 100 years in federal prison on January 13, 1987. The 1989 appellate opinion specifies consecutive twenty-year terms on selected counts, a $250,000 fine and costs of prosecution. Several fellow defendants received comparably long aggregate terms. [12][13]

The severity was intended to reflect a sustained racketeering enterprise and the defendants’ roles. The appellate panel found no abuse of sentencing discretion in the century-long aggregate terms. Describing the result as an effective life sentence is fair in ordinary language, but the exact judgment was a 100-year term. [13]

36. January 31, 1989: Appellate Affirmance

The Second Circuit affirmed Corallo’s convictions in United States v. Salerno, 868 F.2d 524, decided January 31, 1989. The panel addressed hearsay and coconspirator statements, relevance, severance issues and the severity of consecutive sentences. [13]

One part of the decision reversed a substantive RICO conviction of codefendant Anthony Indelicato because of limitations issues. That modification is sometimes flattened into “the Commission case was partly overturned.” For Corallo, the operative result was affirmance. The exception concerned another defendant and must not be carried into Corallo’s personal conviction line. [13]

37. The Loansharking Count and Judicial Dissent

The majority sustained a conspiracy-to-make-extortionate-extensions-of-credit conviction against Corallo and Santoro. Senior Circuit Judge Myron Bright dissented in part, questioning whether the complete surveillance context proved their agreement on that count. [13]

Reporting the dissent is not equivalent to treating the conviction as vacated. It is evidence that the proof was contested and that judges could disagree about inferences from recorded speech. The legal disposition remained affirmance of Corallo’s judgment, while the dissent provides an important reading companion for students examining the proof. [13]

38. Carmine Galante and the Boundaries of Attributed Responsibility

The Commission trial encompassed evidence concerning the 1979 killings of Carmine Galante and two associates, including allegations about Commission authorization and charged acts involving a codefendant. The appellate opinion distinguishes individual defendants’ predicates and addresses the Galante evidence in the course of resolving appeals. [13]

This biography does not assign a personal murder conviction to Corallo based on the prosecution’s broader account of Commission authority. Legal liability for a racketeering enterprise, evidence of a governing body and responsibility for a specific homicide are related but analytically distinct questions. [13]

39. Labor Bribery, Extortion and Separate Legal Elements

The appellate case lists convictions under RICO, the Hobbs Act and labor-bribery statutes. The prosecution alleged that the Commission used leverage over contractors and unions to extract payments. These statutes address different conduct and require different elements, despite forming part of an overall pattern. [13]

A careful record should not combine “labor bribery,” “extortion,” “loan sharking” and “racketeering” into a single undifferentiated offense. Corallo’s individual convictions are specified in the appellate opinion. The page maintains that granularity in its timeline and evidence register. [13]

40. Economic Effects of Industry Racketeering

The construction and carting records illustrate the material consequences of criminally distorted markets: fewer independent bids, increased project costs, unequal access for legitimate firms, coercion of labor, and risk for workers and consumers. Those effects are structural, not merely the colorful personalities of an underworld story. [7][9][21]

Historical evidence varies in granularity. The indictment alleged dollar figures and designated construction contracts; court findings sustain particular offenses; policy commissions analyzed wider market failures. Where the sources describe different populations and periods, their totals must not be merged into an invented estimate of Corallo’s personal income. [9][13]

41. Leadership Vacuum and the Amuso Succession

Corallo’s imprisonment and the simultaneous conviction of other senior Lucchese figures disrupted the existing chain of command. In a 1996 Senate statement, former underboss Anthony Casso asserted that Corallo named Vittorio “Vic” Amuso as successor near the end of 1986. New Jersey organized-crime reporting later described the Amuso–Casso regime as having emerged from the previous hierarchy’s convictions. [17][18]

Casso’s testimony is firsthand but self-interested and retrospective. It is substantial evidence for the conventional succession account, not a certified minute of a leadership election. This biography dates the effective transition to late 1986–1987 and distinguishes it from the final 1989 appellate disposition. [13][18]

42. Anthony Casso and the Limits of Insider Retrospectives

Casso later claimed a direct role in choosing Amuso and described his own subsequent promotions. His published Senate statement is unusually explicit about Corallo’s leadership and the transfer of authority, which makes it an important research source. [18]

Its limits are equally important. Casso was a cooperating former organized-crime leader with incentives and a record of serious misconduct. An insider’s account can illuminate events inaccessible to official observers while also serving the narrator’s interests. Where a detail is not independently corroborated, the page attributes it to Casso rather than stating it as an undisputed fact. [18]

43. Final Years in Federal Custody

Following the Commission sentence, Corallo remained incarcerated for the rest of his life. His age, health and lack of public activity made the conventional narrative of ongoing command increasingly doubtful; the documented organizational change had already occurred by the late 1980s. [13][15][17]

A prison record can establish place of custody and legal status, while official news coverage may capture health and death information. Claims about secret commands issued from prison or detailed family influence during the 1990s should not be accepted without source-specific evidence and a clear distinction between reputation and operational authority. [15]

44. August 23, 2000: Death in Springfield

Corallo died August 23, 2000, in the federal prison hospital at Springfield, Missouri, according to contemporaneous obituary reporting. At 87, he was decades older than most defendants sentenced under the Commission case and had served much of his post-1987 life incarcerated. [15]

His death ended a life that bridged Depression-era New York crime networks, mid-century labor investigations and the modern RICO era. Obituaries rightly summarize his prominence but are not a substitute for court records when listing convicted offenses, dates or the disputed start of his leadership. [1][4][13][15]

45. The Cardigan, Opera and “Old-School Boss” Image

Contemporary journalists sometimes emphasized Corallo’s reserved public manner and appearance during the Commission trial. Later accounts added stories about his tastes, restaurants and apparent preference for avoiding attention. Such details explain how a media image emerged, not the substantive basis for legal findings. [15]

The historical narrative must resist the temptation to romanticize organized crime as a form of gentlemanly management. The Commission record concerned extortion, coercive control of economic sectors and interference with legitimate businesses, while legislative investigators described harm to workers and communities. [7][13]

46. Common Source Errors and Biographical Myths

Popular biographies can inadvertently merge Corallo’s 1962 corruption-of-justice case with his unrelated 1968 municipal-bribery case; treat an early-1970s leadership estimate as a precisely dated election; or imply the Jaguar recordings alone account for every charge. Each shortcut obscures the evidentiary sequence. [1][4][8][13]

Other frequent problems include adding Galante-related murder convictions that do not appear against Corallo personally, confusing the codefendant Indelicato’s limited appellate relief with Corallo’s appeal, and treating all reported union influence as adjudicated individual acts. The source register permits readers to test each proposition. [13][17]

47. Secrecy, Delegation and Recorded Evidence

Corallo’s reputation for keeping a low profile mattered operationally because he relied on associates and trusted venues to conduct business. The Jaguar episode exposed the vulnerability of informal communication when investigators obtained reliable court-ordered interception. Secrecy was a tactic, not proof of innocence or wrongdoing in itself. [8][13]

The court treated recorded exchanges in conjunction with other evidence. Some recordings showed participants discussing investigations and leadership concerns, while others illuminated commercial cooperation. Their legal meaning depended on the particular defendant, date and charged enterprise purpose. [13]

48. RICO, the Hobbs Act and Federal Enterprise Law

RICO enabled prosecutors to charge management of a continuing criminal enterprise as well as the predicate pattern of crimes. The Hobbs Act addressed extortion affecting commerce; labor-bribery statutes addressed unlawful employer–union transactions. Together the charges allowed a larger view of the alleged construction scheme. [13]

The statutes still required count-specific proof. Readers should distinguish a statutory element, a jury finding, a co-conspirator statement and a broader historical inference. Merely naming an enterprise in an indictment does not satisfy every element of a RICO charge. [13]

49. Public Officials and Contracting Vulnerabilities

The 1968 Marcus proceedings demonstrated corruption risk when a public administrator’s decisions intersected with contracting interests, debt and intermediaries. The appellate opinion used testimony and telephone records to reconstruct the government’s case and rejected the defendants’ principal appeals. [3][4]

A public official’s participation in one case cannot justify assuming that all municipal agencies or projects were compromised. The proper scope is the identified prosecution. Broader structural lessons about oversight and procurement remain analytical conclusions grounded in that specific record. [4]

50. Courtroom Procedure and the Limits of Public Narrative

Corallo’s prosecution history contains disputed witness incentives, publicity motions, electronic evidence and lengthy appellate review. These issues matter because prosecutors may construct a persuasive narrative while defense counsel test admissibility, reliability and the fit between evidence and statute. [1][3][13]

The historical obligation is to preserve both the result and the procedural route to it. It would be misleading to present every allegation as fact; equally, it would be misleading to suggest that affirmed convictions disappear because counsel contested them. [1][4][13]

51. Northern New Jersey and Interregional Scope

Later official state reporting mapped the Lucchese network across New York and northern New Jersey, documenting that the organization was not confined to Manhattan or one social club. The report places the Corallo hierarchy in a cross-jurisdictional structure whose members and associates pursued different activities. [17]

Those organizational diagrams are retrospective snapshots. They should not be projected unchanged across the 1960s, 1970s and late 1980s. The page thus uses regions to describe the family’s operational environment rather than asserting that Corallo personally supervised each particular transaction in each locality. [17]

52. Historical Memory and the “Boss” Label

Popular timelines typically present Anthony Corallo as Lucchese boss from about 1973 until 1986. This is a practical index entry, but it compresses legal and organizational uncertainties created by Thomas Lucchese’s death, Carmine Tramunti’s public role, Corallo’s incarceration and later attribution of de facto authority. [6][15][17]

A more rigorous formulation distinguishes formally recognized leadership, day-to-day management and outside law-enforcement identification. By 1983 the official designation is clear. The exact first day is not, and a careful encyclopedia should preserve that asymmetry rather than manufacture an inauguration date. [6]

53. Legitimate Enterprise and the Problem of Capture

Construction contractors, carting firms, unions and public agencies were overwhelmingly legitimate institutions whose activity made the city function. Racketeering became profitable when coercive actors inserted themselves between the services and the people who depended on them. This allowed criminal revenue without necessarily replacing a legitimate organization outright. [7][9][21]

The distinction helps locate victims and affected stakeholders: independent firms lost competition, workers lost accountable representation, public budgets paid inflated or distorted costs, and ordinary customers faced the consequences. Analytical attention to these mechanisms yields more historical value than repeating folklore about criminal personalities. [7][21]

54. From Local Syndicates to Enterprise Prosecutions

Corallo’s lifetime covered a fundamental shift in American organized-crime enforcement. Earlier cases often targeted identifiable schemes such as obstruction of justice or misuse of interstate facilities to facilitate bribery. The Commission case coordinated those individual acts within a continuing interfamily structure. [1][4][13]

This institutional development explains why the 1986 prosecution occupied such a prominent place in public memory. Its legal innovation lay in combining enterprise proof, financial patterns and communications evidence; the effect was to incapacitate a leadership network rather than one crew at a time. [13][20]

55. Using Court Decisions Alongside Official Intelligence

Published appellate opinions provide charge specificity, procedural history and a record of the issues actually litigated. Law-enforcement intelligence profiles can preserve otherwise inaccessible organizational details but may contain untested assumptions. Newspapers establish what was publicly reported on a particular date but can compress legal distinctions. [6][9][13]

A useful hierarchy for this page is: judgment for convictions and sentences; contemporaneous reporting for public dates and reactions; official inquiry for investigative assessments; and attributed insider testimony for disputed organizational succession. These categories are complementary, not interchangeable. [6][13][15][18]

56. Historical Significance of the Corallo Period

Corallo’s career illustrates the use of labor and contracting institutions as organized-crime revenue channels and the effect of long-term surveillance on leadership prosecutions. The Commission trial became a milestone because the government proved participation in an interfamily enterprise rather than simply exposing isolated crimes. [7][13][20]

His fall also marks a change in Lucchese governance. A leadership group accustomed to secrecy and delegated authority was weakened by recordings, coordinated prosecution and long sentences, while younger successors emerged from a destabilized organization. The full consequences belong to subsequent Amuso–Casso biographies, not to mythology about a single leader’s personal control. [17][18]

57. Archival Questions for a Future Edition

Priority research includes obtaining certified records for early arrests and the alleged 1941 sentence; union-office registers for Local 239 and related entities; complete transcripts from the McClellan hearings; original trial exhibits and audio logs for the Jaguar interception; and the sentencing judgment in the Commission case. [5][7][13]

A second set concerns the exact 1967–1974 leadership transition, the administrative date of Amuso’s recognition, prison records, and Corallo’s underlying civil vital documents. The present edition supplies an auditable framework without fabricating answers to questions that the consulted record does not settle. [6][15][18]

Documentary Research Windows

Eight editorial source studies follow. They paraphrase published records for historical instruction; they are not original facsimile transcriptions, newly authenticated exhibits or verbatim archival quotations.

Research Window A — The 1962 Justice-Obstruction Judgment

Read 317 F.2d 459 as a legal account of a conspiracy to obstruct justice, not as a generic narrative about corruption. The opinion addresses the participants, the underlying bankruptcy case, contested testimony and the final affirmance. It teaches that witness incentives and a sustained conviction can coexist. [1][5]

Cross-check the 1967 congressional conviction table for the two-year sentence and note that alleged means of bribery are not interchangeable with the formal charge. [1][5]

Research Window B — 1968–69 Marcus Municipal-Bribery Proceedings

Use the 1968 pretrial decision to identify the indictment and motions, then the 1969 appellate case to determine who was convicted, who pleaded guilty, and who was acquitted. This sequence is a model for separating stages within one prosecution. [3][4]

The crime involved an interstate-facilities conspiracy to facilitate state bribery in municipal contracting; it is separate from the 1962 Keogh case. [3][4]

Research Window C — 1983 Congressional Organized-Crime Profile

The U.S. Senate profile identifies Antonio Corallo, birth date, alias and Lucchese boss designation. It is strong evidence of what law enforcement reported in 1983, and useful in fixing the latest possible date for his recognized leadership. [6]

Its allegations concerning gambling, extortion, loansharking and other activity are not automatically individualized judicial convictions. Original profile images were not independently facsimile-collated in this edition. [6]

Research Window D — March 1983 Jaguar Installation and Monitoring

Contemporary reporters describe investigators opening Avellino’s parked Jaguar under court authority and installing equipment behind the dashboard. Hearing material discusses subsequent conversations and the Long Island carting context. [8][13][21]

A surveillance narrative becomes probative only once source, date, speaker identity and legal admissibility are tested. The published appellate opinion provides the controlling judicial assessment. [8][13][21]

Research Window E — February 1985 Commission Indictment

The 1985 indictment put a previously secretive governing commission at the center of a federal case. Newspaper coverage explains the theory that high-value concrete contracts were steered through threats and illicit payments. [9][13]

An indictment alleges offenses. The subsequent verdict and court review are separate records; historic dollar figures from charging documents should never be restated as independently audited Corallo income. [9][13]

Research Window F — November 1986 Verdict and January 1987 Sentence

Contemporary verdict and sentencing reporting aligns with the Second Circuit opinion: Corallo was convicted and received an aggregate 100-year term with a $250,000 fine. The appellate opinion specifies the consecutive count structure. [10][12][13]

This window distinguishes a 100-year sentence from the colloquial “life sentence,” and demonstrates why multiple counts in one indictment must be identified before summarizing a criminal record. [10][12][13]

Research Window G — 1989 Appeal and the Loansharking Dissent

The appellate court affirmed Corallo’s convictions, including the controversial loansharking-conspiracy count. Judge Bright’s partial dissent disagreed over evidence on that count; the majority’s affirmance remained binding. [13]

A narrow reversal in the same judgment involved co-defendant Anthony Indelicato. It would be a substantial attribution error to report that Corallo’s RICO conviction was vacated. [13]

Research Window H — Late-1986 Succession and the Limits of Casso’s Account

Anthony Casso’s 1996 Senate statement says Corallo selected Vittorio Amuso as family boss near the end of 1986. An official New Jersey retrospective depicts the later Amuso–Casso hierarchy, providing contextual convergence. [17][18]

Casso’s account is retrospective interested testimony, not a contemporaneous recorded vote. Documenting the transition requires preserving those source limitations alongside the best-supported chronology. [17][18]

Research and Publication Limitations

This edition directly reviewed publicly accessible appellate and district-court text for the 1962, 1968 and 1986 cases, together with dated contemporary reporting. It did not inspect all original trial exhibits, the full Jaguar audio archive, the original verdict forms, trial transcripts, prison records, birth certificates, every Senate hearing page or union-office ledgers. The large official government PDF collections and 1996 Senate testimony were located and used as official research leads; original page images were not comprehensively verified. [1][3][4][13][18]

The customary start of Corallo’s Lucchese leadership is approximately 1973–1974, but claims of authority during Carmine Tramunti’s tenure vary. Casso’s later succession testimony is attributed, not treated as an independent court finding. Nothing in the Commission appellate opinion supports assigning a personal Galante murder conviction to Corallo; a separate codefendant received the limited 1989 appellate reversal. Corallo’s conviction and 100-year sentence stood. [6][13][17][18]
