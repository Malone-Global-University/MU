1. Historical Overview

Anthony Casso, widely known as “Gaspipe,” was a senior administrator and underboss of the New York Lucchese crime family during the violent leadership years associated with Vittorio Amuso. His importance lies not simply in his rank but in the intersection of industrial racketeering, internal killings, compromised police intelligence and a failed federal cooperation agreement. His own guilty plea and the trials of others furnish separate, overlapping records of those activities. [1][2][6][10]

The most authoritative procedural benchmark is March 1, 1994, when Casso admitted guilt to seventy federal offenses including racketeering, murder-related crimes and extortion. Later proceedings did not erase that plea. His attempt to secure a reduced sentence failed; the Bureau of Prisons recorded his death in federal custody on December 15, 2020.

2. Identity, Names and Documentary Precision

Federal opinions identify him as Anthony Casso; the form “Anthony Salvatore Casso” appears in later biographical accounts but has not been verified here against an original birth certificate. “Gaspipe” was his familiar criminal nickname. Congressional testimony uses “Lucchese,” while several federal court captions and press releases use “Luchese,” a spelling variation not denoting another organization. [1][3][14]

There was also a younger Anthony Casso, a fact made unmistakable by a 2005 New Jersey casino investigation that briefly mistook the son for the imprisoned father. An encyclopedia must therefore use dates, case numbers and context when connecting records; a shared name does not establish that a criminal or regulatory event involved the same person.

Return to contents

3. Birth-Year Conflict: 1940 or 1942

The New Jersey Division of Gaming Enforcement’s official exclusion page records May 21, 1940. Popular biographical accounts instead give May 21, 1942, a year consistent with the Bureau of Prisons describing Casso as seventy-eight at his death in December 2020. Both have identifiable provenance, and the divergence cannot be settled merely by counting how many websites repeat either date. [10][11][12]

For this edition the day and month are reported with a disputed year; no birth certificate has been examined. This unusually clear documentary disagreement is included in the profile because a verified death date does not, by itself, authenticate an underlying date-of-birth entry.

Return to contents

4. Brooklyn and the Waterfront Setting

In his 1996 Senate appearance Casso described beginning his organized-crime activities as a young person working on Brooklyn docks. Waterfront commerce, hauling routes and related businesses made the borough an environment in which legitimate trade and organized coercion could intersect. The Senate transcript records his own account rather than an independent reconstruction of each early job. [3][11]

Geographical context should not be mistaken for individual culpability: the majority of employees and businesses in those trades were not connected to racketeering. Casso’s later authority has much stronger documentary support than the precise chronology of his childhood or first encounters with particular associates.

Return to contents

5. The “Gaspipe” Nickname

Casso acknowledged in prepared Senate testimony that he acquired the nickname “Gaspipe” early in life. Later retellings supply conflicting stories about its origins and are prone to sensational detail. The contemporary official record securely establishes use of the alias, but does not resolve which specific incident, if any, gave rise to it. [3][5][6]

The nickname is retained for indexing because it appears in indictments, testimony and news accounts. It is not used as an explanation for alleged violence or as evidence that any particular assault occurred.

Return to contents

6. Early Convictions: 1962 and 1974

New Jersey’s public gaming exclusion record lists a 1962 conviction for bookmaking and a 1974 conviction for attempted bribery. In his 1996 testimony, Casso also referred to a short detention after bookmaking activity in the waterfront district, presenting much of his earlier history as his own recollection. [3][11]

An agency summary is useful corroboration of the existence and dates of convictions, but it does not replace underlying sentencing papers. This edition does not convert his other self-described arrests, many of which he said ended without conviction, into proven crimes.

Return to contents

7. Connection to Christopher Furnari

Casso testified to meeting Christopher “Christie Tick” Furnari when he was twenty-one and becoming associated with Furnari’s Lucchese circle. The relationship supplied a route into the organization and was important to later accounts of its administration. Furnari subsequently appeared in the major Mafia Commission prosecution as a senior family officer. [3][4]

The chronology comes principally from Casso’s sworn statement; it is not an original membership register. The biography distinguishes proximity to Furnari from formal membership and later appointments, because organized-crime biographies often collapse years of association into one unsupported induction story.

Return to contents

8. Reported 1974 Induction into the Lucchese Family

In the May 1996 hearing Casso said he was formally “made” in 1974. He also placed Amuso’s induction around 1977. These details are important for understanding their different paths into the administration, yet they are first-person statements made after Casso had entered a cooperation agreement. [3]

No ceremony record is presented here. The correct evidentiary label is “reported by Casso in Senate testimony,” rather than “independently documented induction.” Readers should treat the year as a credible but explicitly attributed reconstruction of an organization whose rituals were not publicly recorded.

Return to contents

9. The Relationship with Vittorio Amuso

Casso told senators he met Vittorio “Vic” Amuso in the early 1970s. By the later 1980s, the two had become the central leadership partnership in the Lucchese organization. The 1994 Amuso appeal independently established the prosecution context in which the family exploited labor access and public-contract bidding while directing violence. [3][4][1]

Amuso and Casso must not be treated as interchangeable defendants. Amuso was convicted by a federal jury on fifty-four counts in 1992; Casso was prosecuted separately, fled and entered a guilty plea in 1994. Each outcome supports a different legal claim even when the alleged enterprise overlaps.

Return to contents

10. The Corallo Succession

Anthony “Tony Ducks” Corallo’s prosecution and imprisonment destabilized the Lucchese hierarchy in the mid-1980s. In testimony Casso recalled that Corallo selected Amuso as boss at the end of 1986 after internal discussions. The resulting command structure reflected the family’s capacity to transfer authority while senior figures were incarcerated. [3][4]

The exact institutional path and timing are reconstructed from insider testimony and later judicial descriptions. They should not be presented as a formal corporate appointment or an uncontested universal hierarchy. The distinction is especially relevant to claims that Casso, as later underboss, was effectively the sole leader.

Return to contents

11. Capo, Consigliere and Underboss

Casso’s own chronology places his promotion to captain in 1986, appointment as consigliere at the end of 1987, and appointment as underboss in 1989. These are specific, sourceable statements made in 1996. The 1998 district court independently called him a former underboss and consigliere, confirming that the senior positions themselves were not invented by later biographers. [3][1][6]

Other government summaries loosely describe him as underboss by the time of the September 1986 shooting, creating a conflict with his stated sequence. The entry therefore distinguishes the documented senior rank from a single supposedly exact date on which he assumed each post.

Return to contents

12. Relationship to Other Senior Lucchese Figures

Corallo’s administration included leaders such as Salvatore Santoro and consigliere Christopher Furnari; Salvatore Avellino and other influential members operated across labor and commercial fronts. As this older tier came under pressure, Casso and Amuso exercised expanding authority amid attempts to preserve criminal revenue streams and police intelligence. [3][4][6]

This organizational overview does not assert that each named official participated in the acts for which Casso pleaded guilty. Separate biographies and convictions supply the proper records for those individuals, and an affiliation or shared decade cannot substitute for evidence of a particular agreement.

Return to contents

13. The Window-Replacement Racketeering Enterprise

Federal prosecutors described collusion to control New York window-replacement work through union influence, threatened disruption, extortion and manipulation of public housing contracts. The 1994 Second Circuit opinion in the Amuso case recounts the 1990 Windows indictment, the use of Local 580 and the subsequent expansion of murder allegations. [4][1]

The organized-crime business model combined ostensibly lawful enterprises with illegal control over access and bidding. It is wrong to equate all window contractors or union members with conspirators; the point of the prosecution was that identifiable criminal actors corrupted structures that served legitimate economic functions.

Return to contents

14. Labor Pressure, Contracts and Corruption

Control of workers, transportation, job-site access and the allocation of bids could allow an organized-crime group to extract money without openly owning a public contract. In the Windows case prosecutors alleged that coerced labor peace and intimidation distorted competition. Related investigative material identifies other industries in which union access was used as leverage. [4][1][13]

For attribution the 1994 Amuso appellate opinion supplies a judicially reviewed account of his conviction; Casso’s own later racketeering plea establishes his admitted criminal conduct. The two are related but should not be merged into a single jury verdict against Casso.

Return to contents

15. Painting Trades and the James Bishop Case

A Department of Labor inspector-general report described a 1990 campaign to influence District Council 9 of the painters’ union and the killing of official James Bishop. The report stated that investigators attributed the murder order to Amuso and Casso after Bishop began cooperating with authorities. [13][1]

That administrative source is not an appellate judgment specifically adjudicating every detail of the Bishop murder against Casso. It provides a research lead and the government’s investigative assessment, which should be compared against the relevant charging documents, plea transcripts and trial records before assigning an individual conviction.

Return to contents

16. Gasoline Excise-Tax Fraud and Russian-Speaking Networks

In 1996 Casso testified to collaborations with Russian-speaking organized criminals in Brooklyn gasoline distribution. He described a network that used corporate fronts and evasion of gasoline taxes, and named Marat Balagula as an important counterpart. His statement illustrates interaction between established Mafia power and newer criminal entrepreneurs. [3]

The hearing is direct evidence of what Casso said under oath; it is not independent proof of every claimed transaction or of a person’s guilt based solely on their nationality or association. The editorial account separates the criminal scheme from ordinary Russian-American businesses and immigrants, most of whom were not participants.

Return to contents

17. Marat Balagula and the Protection Relationship

Casso presented his family’s relationship with Marat Balagula as a bargain over protection, commercial control and tax-avoidance proceeds. His evidence helped the Senate show how cross-group alliances could form around practical profits rather than ethnicity or a permanent unified leadership structure. [3]

One witness’s explanation requires corroboration before particular payments, ownership percentages or claimed interventions are reported as settled fact. This entry therefore limits the narrative to the scope and stated source of the account, avoiding speculative figures for revenues and the frequency of particular encounters.

Return to contents

18. September 1986 Shooting of Casso

The Justice Department’s 2006 Eppolito–Caracappa case account says that Gambino-family associates shot and wounded Casso in September 1986. It presents the attack as the precipitating event behind a cycle of retaliatory violence and the procurement of insider police intelligence. [6][5]

The attack is described in a prosecution account later presented to a jury in the police-corruption case; individual attackers and their motivations should not be embellished with untested memoir details. The distinction between being attacked and later ordering unlawful revenge is fundamental to the historical sequence.

Return to contents

19. The Corrupt-Police Channel

Two former NYPD detectives, Louis Eppolito and Stephen Caracappa, became a conduit for confidential law-enforcement information. The government’s evidence showed the involvement of intermediary Burton Kaplan. Prosecutors described monthly payments and the use of police files to identify suspected witnesses or locate intended victims. [6][7][8][9]

This was not ordinary police investigative access by an outside criminal: it was a breach of public duty that affected the ability of victims and witnesses to seek state protection. The officers’ trial, appeals and sentences are separate proceedings and furnish independent evidence of institutional corruption.

Return to contents

20. Burton Kaplan as Intermediary

At the detectives’ trial, Burton Kaplan testified about his relationship with Casso and his dealings with the former police officers. The 2008 appellate court reviewed the testimony and other evidence while analyzing the duration of the racketeering conspiracy. His testimony is a key bridge between the internal Mafia hierarchy and corrupt police assistance. [7][6]

Kaplan’s cooperation created both firsthand knowledge and a potential incentive to satisfy prosecutors; judges and jurors considered the evidentiary context. His statements should be cited as testimony and, where appropriate, as supported by the verdict, not transformed into universal proof of every claim attributed to him.

Return to contents

21. James Hydell: Kidnapping and Killing

The 2006 Justice Department summary reported that Eppolito and Caracappa kidnapped Gambino associate James Hydell in October 1986 and delivered him to Casso, who sought information about the attempt on his life and then killed him. The release states that Hydell’s body had not been located. [6][5][1]

The account arose from evidence presented in the detectives’ prosecution, a distinct judicial case. Casso’s 1994 plea included murder-related offenses, but a historian should not assume that every named narrative in a later case maps one-to-one onto a particular count without consulting the actual plea specification.

Return to contents

22. Nicholas Guido: A Fatal Identification Error

The prosecution record describes the Christmas Day 1986 murder of Nicholas Guido, an innocent man who shared his name with a different person believed to be connected to Casso’s attempted killing. According to the government’s trial summary, corrupted police information contributed to targeting the wrong individual. [6][7]

The case exposes a particularly devastating consequence of criminal use of official intelligence. The victim’s identity and non-involvement must be stated plainly rather than buried in a narrative of inter-family revenge. The government’s evidence was directed at the accused former detectives, not a separate jury trial of Casso.

Return to contents

23. Edward Lino and a Conflicting Date in DOJ Accounts

The April 2006 Justice Department verdict announcement dates Edward “Eddie” Lino’s fatal Belt Parkway stop to November 6, 1990. An earlier 2005 indictment announcement instead gave November 6, 1992. Since the later release discusses the evidence at trial, this entry uses the 1990 date while preserving the existence of the conflict. [6][5][7]

Both documents are prosecutorial summaries, not original death certification. Casso was described as a person who commissioned the killing; the judicial convictions at issue in 2006 were those of Eppolito and Caracappa. The source discrepancy is a reminder that even government press materials require comparison.

Return to contents

24. Witness Identification and the Risk of Retaliation

The 2006 prosecutors described several instances in which the detectives provided information concerning suspected cooperators and investigative targets. The account connects those leaks to the deaths of or attempts on people including Bruno Facciola and Anthony DiLapi. The trial record portrayed information as a weapon used to undermine law enforcement and control internal dissent. [6][7]

An individual listed as suspected of cooperating was not necessarily a proven informant. The entry avoids repeating unverified labels as factual identities and specifies whether a claim derives from a prosecutor’s presentation, a witness statement or a court judgment.

Return to contents

25. Anthony DiLapi and Internal Discipline

The Department of Justice described Lucchese member Anthony DiLapi as refusing a summons from Casso; the corrupt police channel reportedly helped locate him. The government dated his killing February 4, 1990 and used the episode to explain how the scheme serviced the underboss’s internal power. [6]

Whether a killing is described as discipline, a dispute over orders or an attempt to silence a witness, the legal issue remains homicide and organized intimidation. The treatment here excludes romantic language suggesting that criminal seniority created legitimate authority to punish a subordinate.

Return to contents

26. Bruno Facciola and Disclosure of Confidential Intelligence

The prosecutors’ 2006 account says the detectives disclosed information about Lucchese soldier Bruno Facciola, who was murdered in August 1990. The case demonstrates how a compromised investigative system can intensify a group’s suspicion of its own members. [6][7]

The summary does not itself furnish a certified transcript of every conversation preceding the killing. It is cited as evidence of what the government presented and what the later jury credited against the officer defendants, not as permission to invent dialogue or a precise narrative of each participant’s state of mind.

Return to contents

27. The 1990 Indictment and Flight

Amuso and Casso were tied to the window-replacement investigation of 1990. The later corrupt-police prosecution stated that sensitive advance warning helped them avoid capture; Amuso was arrested in Pennsylvania in July 1991 while Casso remained a fugitive. The Amuso opinion separately provides the earlier prosecution chronology. [4][6][16]

The fact of fugitive status is not a substitute for proof of any specific substantive charge, but flight materially affected the investigation and later public understanding. Reporting should distinguish original indictment, arrest dates and the eventual convictions in their separate prosecutions.

Return to contents

28. Amuso’s Arrest and Casso’s Continued Flight

Following Amuso’s arrest in 1991, the family’s leadership and the federal investigation took a different form. Casso told the Senate that while he remained a fugitive he exercised family authority as underboss. The claim is consistent with his documented senior role, though the precise scope of practical control remains based primarily on retrospective testimony. [3][1][4]

The leadership vacuum did not make Casso the judicially established boss of the family. He was prosecuted as a different defendant, and the attribution “acting administrator” should not be used as if it were a corporate title with a recorded date of appointment.

Return to contents

29. January 19, 1993 Arrest

Contemporaneous UPI reporting dates Casso’s arrest to January 19, 1993 following a prolonged period as a fugitive. The 1996 Senate transcript also describes him as in custody since 1993. The arrest ended his physical freedom but preceded the formal guilty plea and the long legal dispute over cooperation. [16][3][1]

An arrest on an indictment is a procedural event; the disposition of charges comes later. The entry therefore separates the public narrative of a notorious capture from the specific criminal admissions subsequently recorded by the district court.

Return to contents

30. March 1, 1994 Plea to Seventy Crimes

Judge Frederic Block’s published 1998 opinion states that on March 1, 1994 Casso pleaded guilty before Judge Eugene Nickerson to seventy crimes. They included racketeering, racketeering conspiracy, substantive murder, attempted murder, conspiracy to commit murder in aid of racketeering, and extortion. The court further reported that he admitted conspiracy with other members or associates to murder fifteen people. [1][2]

The statutory counts and his fifteen-person conspiracy admission must not be summed as if they were independent totals of killed victims. This was a guilty plea, not a jury verdict after a contested trial; its legal force comes from the plea allocution and resulting judgment.

Return to contents

31. Why the Senate Said Seventy-Two Counts

In 1996 Senate testimony Casso described having pleaded guilty in relation to a seventy-two-count indictment. The district court subsequently identified seventy offenses to which he formally pleaded guilty on March 1, 1994. A charging instrument can contain counts not ultimately encompassed in a plea, and self-described summaries are not definitive judgments. [1][3]

Accordingly, this biography uses “seventy crimes” when identifying the legal plea while noting his “seventy-two-count indictment” wording as testimony. Without collating each iteration of the superseding indictment, the exact mechanics of the difference should not be invented.

Return to contents

32. The Fifteen-Murder Conspiracy Admission

The 1998 district court wrote that Casso admitted conspiring to murder fifteen people. That figure does not establish that he personally shot fifteen people, nor does it reconcile every victim named in later memoirs or news stories. Murder, attempt, solicitation and conspiracy carry distinct legal meanings. [1][2]

More expansive claims about thirty-six homicides circulate in secondary sources, but their enumerations are not independently established by the court opinion cited here. The entry treats the court-recorded admission as firm and treats higher tallies as additional claims requiring separately identified records.

Return to contents

33. The 1994 Cooperation Agreement

With his plea Casso agreed to provide documents, truthful interviews and requested testimony, keep his cooperation confidential and commit no further crimes. The government agreed to consider a substantial-assistance sentencing motion if satisfied with his performance. The agreement also permitted a recommendation for protected custody under the Witness Security Program. [1][2]

Such an arrangement is conditional; it is not a general promise of release or blanket immunity. A defendant can plead guilty, furnish useful information and still receive a severe sentence if the government declines to certify substantial assistance under the agreement’s terms.

Return to contents

34. May 15, 1996 Senate Testimony

Casso appeared before the Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations during hearings on Russian organized crime in the United States. He described Lucchese leadership, the gasoline business and interactions with Russian-speaking criminal figures. The published hearing provides direct access to his sworn testimony and prepared statement rather than a newspaper reconstruction. [3]

His first-person account is a primary source for what he said and believed; it is not equivalent to independent verification of every episode. The Senate proceeding was a policy hearing, not a trial finding him guilty or acquitting another named person.

Return to contents

35. Evaluating a Cooperating Witness’s Account

Casso’s testimony contained admissions, explanations and claims about other individuals while he was awaiting sentence. He had potential incentives to demonstrate utility to prosecutors, minimize some responsibility and emphasize parts of his experience. These incentives are not proof that every statement is false; they are reasons to check the statement against other records. [3][1][2]

The same principle applies to narratives from rival mobsters, officials and documentary filmmakers. This biography attributes claims, identifies when testimony is later contradicted and prefers court rulings when the specific question is the content of the criminal judgment.

Return to contents

36. August 1997 Termination of Cooperation Benefits

On August 8, 1997, prosecutors advised that they considered Casso in breach of the cooperation agreement. In September they explained their grounds: assault of another inmate, bribes and prison contraband, and alleged fabrication concerning cooperating witnesses Alphonse D’Arco and Salvatore Gravano. The published ruling records that Casso admitted the assault and bribes while disputing aspects of the government’s other accusations. [1]

The court rejected his attempt to compel a sentencing-benefit motion. Its nuanced analysis did not require finding that every allegation was an independently charged or convicted offense; it emphasized the prosecutor’s bona fide dissatisfaction and the conditional terms of the agreement.

Return to contents

37. June 29, 1998 District Court Ruling

Judge Block’s decision in United States v. Casso, 9 F. Supp. 2d 199, considered whether the prosecutor had unlawfully withheld the substantial-assistance motion. The opinion held that the government was not compelled to file it and rejected Casso’s bid for specific performance. It separately addressed his complaint about removal from protected custody. [1]

The distinction matters: enforcement of a plea bargain, adjudication of additional prison misconduct, and eligibility to remain in a protective program pose different legal questions. A summary that Casso simply “lost witness protection for lying” oversimplifies the actual decision.

Return to contents

38. Witness Security Program and the 1997 Removal

Casso was admitted to the federal Witness Security Program during the cooperation period; authorities removed him on October 9, 1997. Placement in that program while incarcerated should not be confused with an ordinary civilian witness-relocation story or with a guarantee of favorable sentencing. [1][2]

Under the relevant federal statute the Attorney General could terminate participants who substantially breached program conditions. The appeal later addressed the courts’ jurisdiction over that decision, not whether Casso’s earlier murder-related plea remained valid.

Return to contents

39. The 1999 Second Circuit Disposition

The Second Circuit affirmed the district court’s rejection of the substantial-assistance claim in an unpublished summary order. In the published opinion, 187 F.3d 261, it held that the district court lacked jurisdiction to review the Attorney General’s termination from witness protection and remanded solely so the claim could be dismissed. [2]

Thus “vacated and remanded in part” did not mean that the guilty plea, severe sentence or government’s refusal to file the sentencing motion had been reversed. It referred to a narrow procedural correction on the witness-protection issue.

Return to contents

40. Life Imprisonment and the 455-Year Figure

The Bureau of Prisons’ 2020 notice describes Casso as serving a federal life sentence for racketeering, murder, conspiracy and related offenses. Secondary reporting frequently translates the late-1990s sentence into an aggregate 455 years; a judicial interview recalls the sentencing as occurring in 1999, while many biographical summaries assign 1998. [10][1][2][15]

Because the original sentencing minutes and judgment have not been collated for this edition, the profile emphasizes the independently confirmed life sentence and qualifies the precise aggregate and calendar year. The 1998 district-court decision and 1999 appeal establish that sentencing litigation was underway during that period.

Return to contents

41. The 2005 Eppolito–Caracappa Prosecution

Federal prosecutors charged retired NYPD detectives Louis Eppolito and Stephen Caracappa with a conspiracy in which they provided organized criminals with confidential intelligence and participated in killings. Casso figured prominently in the government’s case as the alleged recipient of information and as an organizer of violence. Burton Kaplan’s cooperation helped make the prosecutions possible. [5][6][7]

Indictment announcements are charging documents rather than verdicts. The case gained more evidentiary weight with the April 2006 trial verdict and later appellate decisions, which are separately recorded here.

Return to contents

42. April 2006 Jury Verdict Against Former Detectives

On April 6, 2006, a federal jury convicted Eppolito and Caracappa after hearing evidence of eight murders, attempted murders, obstruction, drug distribution and the disclosure of sensitive police information. The prosecution described a long-term relationship with Casso and crimes committed at his behest. [6][7]

The defendants convicted by that jury were the former detectives. Casso was already imprisoned under his own judgment. Naming him in the evidence did not itself constitute a new jury verdict against him in the officers’ prosecution.

Return to contents

43. The 2006–2008 Statute-of-Limitations Dispute

After the 2006 verdict, the district court set aside the racketeering convictions based on its interpretation of the applicable limitations period. On September 17, 2008, the Second Circuit reversed, holding the jury could find the conspiracy continued into the permitted charging period. The case was remanded for reinstatement of the verdicts and sentencing. [7][6]

This sequence explains why brief news reports can appear to contradict one another, with a 2006 story describing a discarded conviction and later records describing the convictions as restored. Neither event should be misreported as a reversal of Casso’s own 1994 plea.

Return to contents

44. 2009 Sentences and 2010 Appeal

On March 6, 2009, the two former detectives received life sentences for the racketeering-murder conspiracy. The Second Circuit’s July 2010 decision affirmed the convictions. These later decisions helped establish a lasting legal account of the misuse of police power that figured in the Amuso–Casso years. [8][9]

The defendants’ sentences are distinct from Casso’s own life term. Their judicial history is included because it clarifies the evidentiary status of episodes often told in sensationalized form as a single “Mafia Cops” story.

Return to contents

45. The 2005 Casino Namesake Error

New Jersey officials rescinded a monetary penalty against a casino after discovering that the guest identified as prohibited gambler Anthony Casso was actually his son, who bore the same name. The elder Casso was in federal prison at the time and could not have been the patron. [14][11]

The story is valuable as documentary methodology rather than as a major criminal event: administrative records can be wrong when identifiers are merged, and the mistake was corrected. Namesake confusion is particularly consequential when a person has a highly publicized criminal identity.

Return to contents

46. Prison Years and Limits of Available Records

Casso remained incarcerated through the 2000s and 2010s. Public accounts variously describe periods of high-security confinement and restrictive housing, but institutional placements are dynamic; the only current institutional placement expressly verified by the BOP notice is USP Tucson beginning March 25, 2020. [10][15][1]

Claims about private conversations from confinement, purported threats, or attempts to direct family decisions require specific sourcing. This entry does not infer continuing operational command merely from a senior title formerly held in the organization.

Return to contents

47. Arrival at USP Tucson in March 2020

The Bureau of Prisons reported that Casso entered USP Tucson custody on March 25, 2020. That recorded transfer anchors the final period of his life without depending on speculative prison-chronology compilations. The facility was a high-security federal penitentiary, and the notice was issued only after his death. [10]

The documented transfer date does not establish why he moved, whether a prior request for transfer was granted, or his complete medical history. The scope of the official record is narrow but sufficient to place his final custody in Arizona.

48. COVID-19 Illness and Hospitalization

The Bureau of Prisons stated that Casso tested positive for COVID-19 on November 5, 2020, was transported to a local hospital with respiratory distress on November 9, and was placed on a ventilator after his condition worsened on November 17. The agency also referenced pre-existing medical risk factors. [10][18]

This is an institutional account, not a complete hospital chart. It is adequate to establish the progression reported by federal authorities, but it does not support speculative claims about individual treatment decisions or the precise medical contribution of each chronic condition.

49. Death on December 15, 2020

The Federal Bureau of Prisons confirmed that hospital personnel pronounced Anthony Casso dead on December 15, 2020, following his COVID-19 illness. The notice gave his age as seventy-eight and stated that he had been serving a life sentence. This direct government record establishes the date far more securely than later retrospective biographies. [10][11]

The death also closes the question of any claim that Casso exercised present-day Lucchese leadership. His role is historical. The reported age reinforces the common 1942 birth year but does not eliminate the conflicting 1940 New Jersey date record.

50. Why Published Homicide Counts Differ

Memoirs and popular biographies often report dramatically larger numbers of killings linked to Casso than the specific fifteen-person conspiracy admission described in the 1998 decision. Some estimates mix claimed direct involvement, orders, attempts, associated deaths and murders attributed to his wider network. [1][6][7]

For scholarly purposes, each category must be counted separately and anchored to a plea count, a court finding, corroborated testimony or a carefully attributed claim. A single grand total without definitions risks both exaggerating and obscuring the documented deaths of identifiable victims.

Return to contents

51. Victims and Public Harm

The documented history includes the deaths of victims targeted through criminal intelligence, including innocent Nicholas Guido, as well as threatened workers and businesses in corrupt contracting schemes. Behind the notoriety of senior Mafia figures were the families of murdered people, employees who feared coercion, taxpayers and communities affected by compromised law enforcement. [6][7][4]

No murder becomes less serious because it occurred inside a criminal organization, and mistakes in identification were not abstract technical defects. The encyclopedia’s purpose is to describe systems of violence and accountability without glorifying authority exercised through threat and corruption.

Return to contents

52. How to Reconcile Conflicting Official Sources

Three conflicts in this case demand transparent source comparison: the New Jersey 1940 versus common 1942 birth year; the seventy-two-count Senate description versus the seventy-offense judicial plea; and the November 1992 versus November 1990 Lino date found in two Justice Department releases. All concern seemingly straightforward facts. [1][3][5][6][10][11]

The source hierarchy is proposition-specific. A judgment is strongest for what was pleaded and decided; a government death notice for a custodial death; an official index for what that index records; and contemporaneous testimony for what the witness said. No one source automatically controls every question.

Return to contents

53. Historical Assessment

Casso’s career illustrates the capacity of a criminal network to convert commercial access, secrecy and official corruption into continuing coercive power. His senior role in the Lucchese organization, partnership with Amuso and admitted federal crimes were not merely the product of later popular storytelling. They are supported by judicial and congressional records. [1][2][3][6][7][10]

The record also demonstrates why a cooperation deal is not the equivalent of exoneration: his guilty plea remained operative after prosecutors concluded that he had violated conditions for reduced sentencing. The police-corruption convictions and the Bureau of Prisons death notice complete a documentary history that must be kept distinct from folklore.

Documentary Research Windows

These are editorial research notes and prompts keyed to identified records. They are not original-document facsimiles or verbatim transcript reproductions.

Documentary window 1

March 1994 plea and murder-conspiracy admission

Record: United States v. Casso, 9 F. Supp. 2d 199 [1]

Read the district court’s summary of the pleaded crimes and fifteen-person conspiracy admission. This editorial window is an analysis of the opinion, not a certified transcript of Judge Nickerson’s plea hearing.

Research question: How should a historian distinguish an offense count from an individual homicide victim?

Documentary window 2

May 1996 Senate hearing

Record: S. Hrg. 104-604; testimony and prepared statement [3]

Compare Casso’s first-person succession account, nickname and gasoline-tax descriptions with the questions senators asked. The hearing’s official text is provided through GPO.

Research question: Which details are sworn statements, and which are independent factual determinations?

Documentary window 3

Birth-year and namesake checks

Record: New Jersey state exclusion entry; BOP 2020 notice [11][10][14]

Compare the state record’s 1940 birth year with the prison agency’s age at death and the verified 2005 namesake error. No vital record is reproduced.

Research question: What identifying fields would resolve the discrepancy conclusively?

Documentary window 4

The Amuso–Casso Windows enterprise

Record: United States v. Amuso, 21 F.3d 1251 [4][1]

Follow the role of union access and public housing contracting. The appellate court decided Amuso’s case, so a Casso-specific conclusion still requires his separate plea records.

Research question: What is the difference between a co-conspirator’s conviction and a defendant’s own judgment?

Documentary window 5

1997 breach and 1998 performance dispute

Record: United States v. Casso, 9 F. Supp. 2d 199 [1]

Identify admitted prison misconduct, contested assertions and the conditional sentencing motion. Avoid claiming the court entered an independent conviction for every allegation described.

Research question: How can the government refuse a sentencing benefit while the guilty plea remains binding?

Documentary window 6

1999 witness-protection appeal

Record: United States v. Gigante, 187 F.3d 261 [2]

Read the narrow appellate holding: the court lacked jurisdiction to review termination from the program, while the cooperation-motion ruling was not overturned.

Research question: What exactly was vacated, and what remained operative?

Documentary window 7

The “Mafia Cops” evidence and chronology

Record: DOJ releases of 2005–2009; 2008 and 2010 appellate opinions [5][6][7][8][9]

Track the indictment, jury verdict, limitations ruling, reinstatement and sentencing. Note the conflicting published dates for Eddie Lino and the distinction among defendants.

Research question: Which statements are allegations, which are jury findings, and why did a 2006 reversal not survive?

Documentary window 8

Death and COVID-19 care

Record: BOP December 16, 2020 notice [10][18]

Review the dated federal statement for the infection, hospital transfer, ventilator and pronouncement. No clinical file or death certificate is reproduced in this edition.

Research question: Which facts does the institution establish, and what medical details remain beyond the record?

Research and Publication Limitations

This entry uses published federal opinions, the official congressional hearing text, Department of Justice statements, the New Jersey gaming exclusion database, the Department of Labor inspector-general report, and the Bureau of Prisons death release. It does not claim to have collated original birth certification, the complete seventy-count plea allocation, certified 1990–1994 indictment iterations, the full sentencing judgment, original witness interviews or underlying medical records. The 1940/1942 birth-year conflict, 70/72 offense-description distinction, 1990/1992 Lino date conflict, and aggregate-sentence dating remain expressly identified.

First-person Senate testimony and later prosecutorial accounts are source-attributed rather than treated as direct personal-conviction findings. Trial verdicts against Eppolito and Caracappa are not reclassified as a later Casso trial. The eight documentary windows are research explanations, not purported newly transcribed source passages.
