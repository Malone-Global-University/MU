Editorial note. Albert “Kid Blast” Gallo, Jr. is distinct from brothers Lawrence and Joseph. Contemporary U.S. Senate and Newsday documents classify him as a Colombo-family soldier in the 1960s, while later accounts describe factional leadership and a transfer to the Genovese family. A precise death date or verified present-day status is not established; the 1972 restaurant killings are not presented as an adjudicated Albert Gallo conspiracy. [1] [2] [10] [17]

1. Historical Overview

Albert “Kid Blast” Gallo is remembered as the surviving younger brother of Lawrence “Larry” Gallo and Joseph “Crazy Joe” Gallo and as an important participant in the Brooklyn faction that challenged the Profaci–Colombo crime family. He appears by name and nickname in a 1965 U.S. Senate organized-crime roster, which placed him among the soldiers rather than the bosses, and in a 1969 Newsday investigation that again called him a family soldier. Those contemporary distinctions matter because later descriptions of him as a “boss” generally concern his crew after his brothers’ deaths, not formal control of the entire Colombo organization. [1] [2] [8]

After Joseph Gallo was murdered in April 1972, Albert became the most recognizable surviving brother, and contemporary news profiles attached to him a role of factional continuity. A further series of murders and shootings followed, culminating in an apparent truce in 1974 and later accounts of some Gallo associates moving into the Genovese family. The detailed private bargaining behind that transfer is harder to document than the events publicly reported at the time. [8] [9] [13] [14] [17]

2. Name, Identity, and the ‘Kid Blast’ Nickname

The 1965 Senate roster gives “Albert Gallo, Jr.” and the alias “Kid Blast,” placing both within a contemporaneous law-enforcement identification table. He should not be confused with his father, also named Albert, or with Joseph Gallo and Lawrence Gallo, whose distinct entries appear directly below his on the same roster. That printed list is stronger identity evidence than a subsequently compiled internet biography. [1]

A birth date of June 6, 1930 is widely repeated in modern biographical references. The 1969 Newsday report describes him as 38, consistent with a 1930 birth year, but the newspaper does not confirm the exact June date. This article uses the date as reported in secondary biographies while clearly distinguishing it from an authenticated birth certificate, which has not been examined. [2] [17]

3. The Gallo Household and Sibling Relationships

Later biographies locate Albert’s upbringing in Brooklyn and identify the three brothers as Lawrence, Joseph and Albert, with Lawrence usually the organizer or negotiator and Joseph the most publicly flamboyant. Their kinship is corroborated by period press coverage that discussed all three together, and by congressional identification lists that placed them within the same suspected criminal network. [1] [5] [9]

Accounts of their father’s role in Prohibition-era bootlegging have become a recurrent origin story. The historical evidence used here does not establish a particular household income or a direct parental order to join the Mafia. A responsible account can situate the brothers in a Brooklyn environment where illicit markets were visible without treating family legend as proof of each boy’s personal conduct. [5] [17]

4. Brooklyn Neighborhoods and the President Street Network

The Gallo faction is closely associated with President Street and adjoining parts of south Brooklyn. The neighborhood social-club image matters because Mafia authority operated through repeated face-to-face contact, gambling clients, street relationships and the capacity to marshal men, rather than through publicly registered leadership offices. Period reporting in the early 1970s still described the Gallo headquarters as an important meeting point. [8] [9] [17]

Neighborhood labels such as Red Hook, Carroll Gardens and South Brooklyn can describe overlapping spheres in historical journalism; they are not by themselves proof of the exact boundaries of one criminal enterprise. Nor does a residence, a club or a street address establish ownership of all surrounding illicit business. For this edition, geographic claims are kept at the level supported by the cited press and congressional material. [1] [2] [17]

5. Entry into the Profaci Criminal Organization

By the early 1960s contemporary authorities treated Albert as a member of the Joseph Profaci organization, subsequently reorganized and renamed for Joseph Colombo. The Senate roster is particularly useful for fixing his claimed position: it did not elevate him to a caporegime or family boss in the 1963–1965 investigative period. The associated activity codes point to investigators’ suspicions, not adjudicated offenses for every listed item. [1]

Popular accounts tie the brothers to Harry Fontana’s group and describe a later autonomous faction. The precise date of Albert’s induction, the ritual, who sponsored it and his formal rank at every intermediate stage have not been verified by contemporary membership records. The safest formulation is membership or affiliation as asserted in the period investigative record, followed by a more prominent role after 1968 and 1972. [1] [17] [18]

6. The Profaci Family and Grievances of 1959–1960

Joseph Profaci led a Brooklyn-centered Mafia family for decades. Conflict between his administration and the Gallos is commonly traced to disagreements over sharing racketeering income, internal discipline and the aftermath of the 1959 death of Frank Abbatemarco. Histories differ in how directly each brother participated in the decisions that produced the rebellion. The record permits description of a developing factional split without assigning Albert responsibility for every grievance. [1] [9] [17]

The structural issue was more than personal disrespect. Profaci’s position depended on recognition by fellow leaders, while a group of local operatives could withdraw cooperation and control meaningful street resources. That mismatch made violent insubordination possible but did not transform the rebels into another officially recognized New York crime family. [1] [17]

7. The 1961 Revolt and Kidnapping Episodes

The Gallo–Profaci rebellion reached a public crisis in 1961 when personnel loyal to the dissident group were reported to have kidnapped or detained people associated with Profaci’s leadership. Retrospective accounts describe attempts to force concessions over money and operating autonomy; the exact participation of Albert in every abduction has not been demonstrated by the sources reviewed here. [9] [17]

The core historical distinction is between a crew’s collective campaign and an individual criminal act. Even where authorities identified all three Gallo brothers together, such identification cannot support assigning to Albert every threat, abduction or shooting perpetrated by members or alleged allies. This article therefore attributes collective conduct to the faction unless evidence specifies his own involvement. [1] [9]

8. The Sahara Lounge Attack on Lawrence Gallo

On August 20, 1961, Lawrence Gallo survived an attempted strangling during the violent conflict, an incident that generated a grand-jury investigation and later litigation involving a witness, Aurelius Cirillo. A New York appellate decision documents the date and investigative context; it does not conclude that Albert committed or directed the attack. The event influenced the continuing balance among the brothers and their opponents. [17]

For Albert, the attack’s significance lies in its effect on family cohesion and the credibility of proposed peace meetings. Accounts that supply names of assailants can be useful investigative leads, but the guilt of any named person must not be inferred solely from an inquiry into a witness’s failure to answer questions. The attempt is treated here as a well-supported historical event, not a completed murder. [17]

9. Joseph Gallo’s Imprisonment and the Remaining Brothers

Joseph “Crazy Joe” Gallo received a lengthy sentence in the early 1960s and remained imprisoned through much of the decade. The imprisonment did not end the faction’s history: Lawrence and Albert remained present while the larger Profaci organization underwent its own changes in leadership. Contemporary press reporting periodically revived public attention to both men and to their close associates. [5] [8] [17]

This period is essential to correcting the misconception that Albert became prominent only after April 1972. A 1965 congressional list and public stories from 1966–1969 show his name independently in contemporary records. That visibility should not be exaggerated into proof that he already possessed sole command of the faction while Lawrence was alive. [1] [2] [5]

10. Profaci’s Death and the Colombo Succession

Profaci died in June 1962. His deputy Joseph Magliocco assumed de facto control for a short period, before Joseph Colombo emerged as family leader during the 1963–1964 transition. The Gallos’ insurgency helped expose weaknesses in the older leadership, but no contemporary evidence reviewed in this study shows Albert as a claimant accepted by the Commission to replace Profaci or Magliocco. [1] [17]

The shift in family leadership created the possibility of truces but did not necessarily erase individual injuries or competing revenue claims. Albert’s later conflict with Colombo figures is better understood as a continuation and transformation of earlier factional disputes than as proof of a stable decade-long war with an unchanging set of participants. [13] [14] [17]

11. The 1962 Fire-Rescue Story and Public Reputation

A February 1962 New York Times report, frequently cited in historical biographies, described the Gallo group’s rescue of children during a Brooklyn fire. The episode became part of the family’s public image, particularly in later contrasts between reputed criminal violence and isolated acts of neighborhood assistance. Exactly which brothers performed every reported rescue action has not been established for this edition through fire-service records. [5] [17]

Such stories are historically meaningful without reversing the legal or moral character of organized crime. They show that public reputation depended on neighborhood relationships, dramatic incidents and reporting choices. The article does not treat a rescue as evidence that the group protected residents generally, nor does it assume that every person present at the scene was part of criminal leadership. [3] [5]

12. Albert Gallo in the 1965 Senate Roster

A congressional report dated March 4, 1965 placed Albert Gallo, Jr., alias Kid Blast, among named soldiers of the Colombo organization. It listed codes “2” and “6” against his name: the report’s legend describes them as gambling and extortion/strong-arm/murder activity classifications. Those are the investigative panel’s categories, not individual convictions on every stated offense. [1]

The same roster includes Joseph and Lawrence immediately beneath Albert, strengthening the identity match and showing why a biographer must not collapse the brothers’ records. Its historical value is exceptional because it was created close to the events under investigation and is available as a readable original printed image rather than just as a modern retelling. Its limits are equally important: intelligence lists can be inaccurate, overinclusive or lag events. [1]

13. East New York and the Crisis of 1966

During the summer of 1966, racial and neighborhood tensions in East New York produced serious violence, local fear and an urgent political response. Scholarly accounts document that city officials were criticized for relying on Albert and Lawrence Gallo as informal intermediaries who might influence some Italian American youths. John Lindsay’s administration defended its approach in the face of controversy. [3] [4] [6] [19]

The episode must be placed within the history of the neighborhood’s racial transformation and the experience of Black residents, who faced violence and instability; it cannot be narrated as a heroic story about two reputed gangsters without those circumstances. The Gallos were neither legitimate representatives of all Italian Americans nor police officers authorized to coerce others. The scholarly sources also caution that claims of successful peacemaking often depend on partisan period accounts. [3] [4]

14. Informal Mediation and Public Accountability

Whether the Gallos could persuade particular individuals to desist from violence is different from whether government should have relied upon them. City officials and critics weighed immediate crowd-control objectives against the danger of appearing to sanction a criminal network. The later Congressional Record reproduced criticism of official contact with Mafia-linked figures. [3] [6] [19]

Albert’s presence in this controversy illustrates the practical influence a person may have outside the formal Mafia hierarchy. It does not show an elected civic position, a contract to maintain order, a lawful delegation of police authority or a right to intimidate young people. This page treats the public-government controversy as the central historical fact while leaving any claimed direct mediation outcome carefully attributed. [3] [4] [19]

15. 1966 Press Coverage and the Robin Hood Narrative

Fred J. Cook’s October 1966 New York Times Magazine feature on Larry, Joe and “Kid Blast” framed the issue as one of public contradiction: figures known for criminal associations had also appeared in positive neighborhood stories. The article survives in an archival finding aid; the complete page images were not reviewed in this edition. [5]

The media frame has persisted in Mafia popular history. Albert’s biography must not simply recycle the “Robin Hood” persona as factual proof of charitable character, nor should it dismiss every reported incident because of criminal associations. Evidence should be event-specific and separated from labels that journalists, law enforcement and later entertainment used to simplify a complicated social history. [3] [4] [5]

16. The 1967 Roosevelt Raceway Coercion Case

The Uwe Johnson archive identifies a New York Times clipping published October 24, 1967 under the title “A Gallo Arrested in Coercion Case,” with a subheading connecting the case to a ticket-cashing scheme at Roosevelt. That establishes the existence and subject of the contemporary report, not the eventual verdict. [7]

The more detailed 1969 Newsday retrospective said that an extortion charge connected to an unlawful twin-double ticket-cashing scheme was awaiting trial and that Albert had already been convicted of contempt of court, with a reported thirty-day sentence. The two legal categories must not be conflated. An arrest or pending charge is not a conviction; a contempt conviction is not a judgment of guilt on a separate extortion count. [2] [7]

17. The February 1969 Newsday Investigation

In a two-page series on organized crime on Long Island, reporter Tom Renner identified Albert as a thirty-eight-year-old Colombo family soldier. The paper associated him with gambling, loansharking and extortion, but those reported activities were presented in the language of a police-informed journalistic directory, not as itemized criminal judgments. The article also described an unsuccessful attack by gunmen against him. [2]

Because the contemporary page images can be inspected, it is possible to distinguish the newspaper’s explicit conviction line from its allegations and pending matters. This biography intentionally omits his precise 1969 household street address, which does not materially improve the historical explanation. The value of the source is its contemporaneous organizational identification, recorded legal status and indication that danger continued beyond the formal ending of the first Gallo conflict. [2]

18. Lawrence Gallo’s Death and Succession Questions

Lawrence Gallo died in May 1968 after an illness generally identified as cancer. His loss left the surviving brothers with changed responsibilities, while Joseph remained imprisoned. The Gallo faction did not have a public constitution or succession register that establishes an instantaneous transfer of all Larry’s functions to Albert on a specific date. [5] [8] [17]

It is therefore more precise to speak of an evolving leadership role than to assign Albert an officially dated “promotion” in 1968. His 1969 description as a soldier and his 1972 portrayal as a central surviving spokesman reveal changing perceptions over time. They do not conflict once the difference between formal whole-family rank, dissident crew standing and newspaper characterization is maintained. [1] [2] [8]

19. Joseph Gallo’s Release in 1971

Joseph Gallo left prison in 1971, renewing attention to a faction whose earlier conflict with Profaci had never entirely disappeared from Mafia memory. Some contemporary and later accounts associated Joseph with demands for financial compensation and a return to broader influence. The scale and terms of any private demands are not independently recoverable from a criminal organization’s books. [8] [9] [17]

Albert’s public identity remained linked to both surviving family continuity and the practical day-to-day relationships that had persisted during his brother’s confinement. There is no sound basis for assigning Albert personal responsibility for every confrontation associated with Joe’s return. That distinction is especially necessary when discussing the separate shooting of Joseph Colombo in June 1971. [8] [9]

20. The June 1971 Shooting of Joseph Colombo

Joseph Colombo was shot at an Italian-American Civil Rights League rally in Columbus Circle in June 1971 and survived with catastrophic injuries until his death in 1978. The event destabilized the organization and provoked claims of conspiracy or retaliation. Some Mafia actors suspected the Gallo faction, but suspicion does not establish that Albert planned, financed or carried out the shooting. [9] [17]

The biography avoids retroactively treating that suspicion as proven merely because violence subsequently occurred between Colombo loyalists and Gallo associates. A historical chain of motive, opportunity and retaliation is not identical to a documentary chain of evidence. No court judgment proving Albert’s responsibility for the Columbus Circle attack was located for this edition. [9] [17]

21. Joe Gallo’s Murder on April 7, 1972

Joseph “Crazy Joe” Gallo was shot to death at Umberto’s Clam House in Manhattan’s Little Italy on April 7, 1972. Contemporary coverage places his killing within the atmosphere of the renewed Colombo–Gallo conflict. The death removed the most famous brother, following Lawrence’s earlier death in 1968, and left Albert as the most prominent surviving member of the immediate sibling trio. [8] [9]

The killing became a major cultural and journalistic episode, but the specific shooter, instigator and chain of command have been described differently across sources and decades. The record should not be flattened into a single cinematic version. Albert’s ensuing role in the faction is central to this page; it does not make him a proven participant in the restaurant homicide itself. [8] [9] [17]

22. Albert as Surviving Faction Leader

An Eric Pace profile published in August 1972 described Albert as the younger brother who had taken up the family’s mantle of honor. The language of personal vengeance was part of period reporting about the Gallo crew’s reputation. In organizational terms, his prominence meant that associates and adversaries increasingly treated him as a significant figure in the faction after the loss of both older brothers. [8]

Many popular biographies call him “boss” from 1972 onward. This is not equivalent to being Colombo family boss, a role associated with a separate administrative succession. Nor does the term automatically prove that every faction member obeyed his directions. Local faction leadership and full-family command should remain distinct classification fields within the MGU encyclopedia. [1] [2] [8] [17]

23. The Neapolitan Noodle Shooting of August 1972

On August 11, 1972 a gunman fired on four innocent businessmen at the Neapolitan Noodle restaurant on Manhattan’s East 79th Street, killing two and wounding two. Contemporary and later accounts describe mistaken identification: the victims were at a bar previously occupied by men associated with the Colombo organization, including relatives or associates of Carmine Persico. This was a severe case of violence harming uninvolved civilians. [10] [11] [20]

Journalists associated the attempted attack with retaliation by the Gallo faction. Some later summaries explicitly allege that Albert ordered or arranged it, but the cited contemporary accounts and accessible records do not supply a criminal conviction finding him responsible for that order. The shooting, its date and victim count are well supported; the claim of Albert’s personal command requires attribution and is not asserted as an adjudicated fact. [10] [11] [17]

24. Victims, Mistaken Identity, and Historical Accountability

The men killed in the Neapolitan Noodle attack were businesspeople, not the alleged targets of the factional revenge plan. A detailed retrospective by Steve Dougherty states that four meat wholesalers were hit while meeting to mark a family engagement; it identifies the intended targets as Colombo-connected figures. The shooting illustrates the costs of targeting on the basis of appearance, location and unverified intelligence. [11]

Some later bibliographies contain headlines about another shooting or give an inconsistent number of deaths. The two-fatality, two-wounded account of the Neapolitan Noodle attack is corroborated by both period TIME coverage and a 1992 retrospective. A headline about three people killed in an East Side restaurant must not be uncritically merged into this separate account without checking the original story. [10] [11] [12] [20]

25. The 1972–1974 Fragmented Truce

After Joe’s murder and the August 1972 restaurant attack, the relationship between the Gallo faction and Colombo administration moved between caution, negotiation and renewed violence. Later newspaper histories sometimes label the period the second Colombo war, though the violence consisted of distinct incidents and changing combinations of associates rather than continuously mobilized armies. [13] [14] [17]

A dated truce story from November 1974 is a useful public marker, but it is not evidence that every remaining grievance was settled. An agreement among leaders could decrease attacks while leaving unresolved the practical placement of men, share of gambling proceeds, status of defectors and personal fears of retaliation. The long chronology of subsequent killings demonstrates that announcing peace and enforcing it were different matters. [13] [15]

26. The 1974 Defections and Internal Rivalries

Retrospective accounts identify John “Mooney” Cutrone and Gerry Basciano among people who broke from Albert’s side or challenged its control. Contemporary 1974 reporting documented a succession of shootings involving people described as Gallo followers and rival associates. Claims about who secretly authorized particular attacks require case-by-case evidence rather than the assumption that every casualty belonged to one leader’s plan. [13] [14] [17]

The internal split is historically important because it complicates the idea of a single unified “Gallo gang” after 1972. Faction members could negotiate separately, shift associations and pursue their own sources of income. The scale of Albert’s control after those defections is best assessed through named contemporaneous actions rather than idealized organizational charts that presume command was stable. [13] [17]

27. Frank Illiano and the Remaining Gallo Associates

Frank “Punchy” Illiano appears repeatedly in later histories as an associate of the Gallo brothers and, subsequently, of the Genovese family. Accounts of a September 1974 shooting in which he was wounded have become a standard episode in the literature on the faction’s fragmentation. The episode is treated as reported violence; the name of any person who ordered it is not established here by a verdict. [13] [17] [18]

The relationship between Albert and Illiano is significant for later claims of organizational transfer. If a group of former Colombo associates moved under Genovese protection, their continued interactions did not by themselves prove that the men all held equal rank or that they jointly served as bosses. Different reports describe Illiano or Albert as a captain, acting captain or senior crew figure at different times. [17] [18]

28. The November 1974 Truce

A November 10, 1974 New York Times report by Mary Breasted appeared under the headline “Gallo Factions Declare a Truce After a Series of Shootings.” Its title is solid bibliographic evidence that a negotiated pause was reported publicly. Later secondary narratives attribute broader involvement to leaders of multiple New York Mafia families. [13] [17]

Without transcripts of the private meetings, however, precise terms cannot be confirmed: accounts differ about whether Albert and his followers changed affiliation immediately in late 1974 or during 1975, who formally approved the process and how the remaining Colombo-associated faction was assigned. This article records the commonly reported mid-1970s shift and clearly labels the exact date and membership conditions unresolved. [13] [14] [17] [18]

29. The Reported Transfer to the Genovese Family

Later mafia histories generally place Albert and key surviving associates with the Genovese crime family by the mid-1970s, often connecting the move to the intervention of higher-ranking leaders and the influence of Vincent “Chin” Gigante. No contemporaneous public membership certificate is available, and narratives vary in the precise calendar year. The phrase “reported transfer” best matches the evidence. [17] [18]

This transition also changes the proper indexing of Albert’s biography. He belongs in a Colombo collection because of the Gallo–Profaci and Colombo conflicts, while his later alleged affiliation requires cross-reference to the Genovese family. That dual classification must not imply that the two families were a single organization or that Albert was simultaneously formal boss of both. [1] [17] [18]

30. Cutrone, Basciano, and the Violence of 1976

Contemporary reporting in 1976 described continued lethal violence among people associated with the old conflict. Later accounts report that Gerry Basciano was killed in June and John Cutrone in October. The October 6 New York Times article about Cutrone confirms that his death was significant enough for period coverage, but not who directed it. [15] [16] [17]

A report that a meeting was proposed, a participant failed to attend or members of rival families disapproved does not establish the identity of those responsible for subsequent homicides. Even apparent benefits to Albert’s side are not proof that he personally issued a murder order. This distinction is a baseline evidentiary requirement for an MGU biographical reference entry. [15] [16]

31. Alleged Later Leadership and the Limits of Rank Claims

Secondary descriptions of Albert’s Genovese years sometimes call him a capo, acting capo, senior soldier or member of a temporary leadership arrangement. Those labels can reflect different dates, sources or uncertainty about the hierarchy of a clandestine group. The materials reviewed for this edition do not establish a single uninterrupted official command covering all of those periods. [17] [18]

Albert’s reported later standing should therefore be described as a claim found in organized-crime histories, not a permanently certified office. The earlier 1965 and 1969 classifications as a Colombo soldier remain the best directly viewed contemporary organizational anchors. They do not deny that he later accumulated influence, but they should not be retroactively overwritten. [1] [2] [17]

32. Business Interests and Alleged Criminal Revenue

A variety of gambling, loansharking and extortion activities appear in official and period accounts of the Gallo faction. The 1965 congressional roster uses activity codes rather than case-by-case criminal judgments; the 1969 newspaper account alleges specific schemes at a raceway while documenting a separate contempt conviction. These are distinct types of historical evidence. [1] [2]

Some later accounts say that Albert operated a furniture business. Without business registration, licensing or contemporaneous interview text, this remains a reported commercial interest rather than a demonstrated mechanism for laundering proceeds. It would be especially unsound to infer a company’s financial scale, ownership structure, partners or tax behavior from the existence of a generic secondary sentence. [8] [17]

33. Criminal Charges, Convictions, and Presumption of Innocence

It is possible to document that Albert was named in a congressional organized-crime roster, arrested in a coercion-related matter, reported by Newsday to have a contempt conviction, and linked by journalists to allegations of extortion and violent factional activity. It is not sound to list all allegations as his convictions. The 1969 report specifies that an extortion case was then pending and identifies the reported contempt sentence separately. [1] [2] [7]

A complete modern rap sheet, certified docket and disposition history have not been collated. The present entry therefore declines to supply a grand total of arrests, years imprisoned, “murder convictions” or other definitive criminal-history metrics beyond the explicitly cited contemporary record. Readers should distinguish a police allegation, indictment, trial, guilty verdict and sentencing order. [2] [7]

34. Media Legends, Pop-Cultural Retellings and ‘The Godfather’ Era

The Gallo brothers’ story was repeatedly narrated in magazines, newspapers and books as a drama of rebellion and revenge. That appeal was intensified by the public fascination with Mafia films of the early 1970s. The Neapolitan Noodle mistaken-identity deaths became an example of real violence that contradicted glamourized fictional representations of precision and “honor.” [9] [10] [11]

A historian’s objective is neither to preserve the legend nor to suppress every interesting story. Newspapers can offer accurate dated evidence about public events while still using theatrical labels such as “Crazy Joe” and “Kid Blast.” This article includes such nicknames as historical identifiers, but does not use them as psychological diagnoses, proof of violent conduct or an explanation of complex organizational choices. [5] [8] [17]

35. Present Status, Birth Date, and Records Still Needed

Widely circulated reference entries give June 6, 1930 as Albert’s birth date. The 1969 Newsday age supports approximately 1930. The investigation for this edition did not establish a verified death certificate or other authoritative document declaring his death, nor an independently verified recent record demonstrating that he is alive in November 2026. The article therefore does not invent either a death date or a present-day status. [2] [17]

This is especially important because automatically calculated ages in online entries can keep increasing long after a person’s death if the record is never updated. A reliable biography distinguishes the last dated source that names the person from claims that it knows his current location, health, business or position. An exact civil birth record and definitive vital-status record remain outstanding. [2] [17]

36. Legacy and the Importance of Accurate Classification

Albert Gallo’s biography captures the movement from a neighborhood faction within one Mafia family toward a reported later place in another family. It also illustrates the distinction between different forms of power: soldiers on official investigative lists, factional spokesmen in newspapers, negotiating parties at alleged sit-downs and leaders identified retrospectively by specialist writers. [1] [2] [8] [13] [17]

The historical significance extends beyond Mafia leadership charts. His story intersects with 1966 urban racial conflict, a government controversy over informal mediation, the 1972 killing of civilians in mistaken identity and long-running disputes over factual attribution. A comprehensive crime encyclopedia should preserve those wider effects rather than measuring the subject only by underworld reputation. [3] [4] [10] [11]

37. Historical Assessment and Research Priorities

Contemporary government and newspaper documentation securely identify Albert Gallo and establish his early place in the Colombo organization. Academic history offers a critical account of the unusual 1966 civic episode, while 1972–1976 reporting records the lethal consequences of the continuing factional conflict. The strongest late-career claims, particularly exact Genovese ranks and the terms of a family-to-family transfer, are predominantly retrospective. [1] [2] [3] [4] [13] [17] [18]

The next archival priorities are a certified birth record, a systematic search for any death record, the complete 1967 coercion case disposition and 1969 extortion docket, the August 1972 homicide investigation and original 1974 negotiation reporting, plus validated contemporaneous law-enforcement records on the alleged mid-1970s Genovese transfer. Nothing in this first edition should be construed as a court finding of responsibility for the 1972 restaurant shooting. [2] [7] [10] [11] [13] [17]

Research and Publication Limitations

Government primary documentation. The original image of S. Rep. 89-72 at the printed 1965 roster page was reviewed in a 1980 Italian parliamentary reproduction. Classification as a soldier and the printed gambling/extortion-related activity codes are evidence of contemporary government identification, not a separate court conviction for each coded crime.

Contemporary newspaper facsimile. Both pages of Tom Renner’s February 28, 1969 Newsday feature were inspected. It reports an extortion prosecution then pending, a separate contempt conviction and allegations of criminal business. It does not establish the final outcome of the pending charge. Historical residential addresses are omitted as editorially unnecessary.

Press-access boundaries. A selection of 1966–1976 New York Times reports is identified by dated bibliographic entries and, for the 1967 coercion story, archival catalog verification. Full original pages were not inspected for each story. They are not represented as fully facsimile-collated sources, nor are invented direct article URLs supplied.

Later affiliation. Reports of a mid-1970s movement to the Genovese crime family and alleged later captain-level authority rest principally on later organized-crime reporting and narrative history. The exact date of transfer, formal induction and offices cannot be verified from the directly reviewed Senate and Newsday documents.

Violence and personal culpability. The August 11, 1972 Neapolitan Noodle attack killed two uninvolved civilians and wounded two others, as reported by period TIME coverage and the 2012 retrospective. The article attributes suspected factional responsibility to reporting; it does not present Albert Gallo as having been convicted of directing this shooting or of later 1976 murders.

Vital records and present status. Secondary publications commonly give a June 6, 1930 birth date. Direct birth-certificate verification and a definitive vital-status record through the November 11, 2026 research cutoff were not obtained. A website’s automatic age counter does not verify survival. This page does not assert a current address, medical condition or unconfirmed death.

Historical geography and media. The article is text-only and does not reproduce rights-managed photographs. It retains the existing MGU shared stylesheet, dynamic header, breadcrumb and footer. Internal anchors are audited locally; website deployment and stylesheet-byte parity with other biographies have not been tested.
