1. Historical Overview

Vincent Aloi—also identified as Vincenzo Aloi and commonly called “Vinnie”—was a senior member of the New York organization known as the Colombo crime family. His documentary profile is unusually substantive for a man whose underworld rank is often reduced to a brief entry on succession charts. A published federal appellate decision establishes his involvement in an At-Your-Service Leasing stock-fraud conspiracy; separate New York state litigation records a perjury conviction tied to the grand-jury investigation of Joseph Gallo’s 1972 murder; and New Jersey investigators document his extensive role in the garment-trucking economy in the late 1980s. These are different bodies of evidence and must not be merged into a general allegation of “racketeering.” [1][2][4][6] In the wake of Joseph Colombo’s incapacitation in June 1971, Aloi was among the figures described as having temporarily exercised leadership. A New Jersey official report published in 1990 treated Aloi and Joseph Brancato as prominent caporegimes during the family’s period of weakened direction, while its 1991 garment investigation described him as a former acting boss. The wording documents the state commission’s assessment of his status; it does not settle when every incumbent recognized him or whether he ever possessed unrestricted authority. [2][3][9] His life illustrates three overlapping histories: the instability of a Mafia family after a leader’s removal, the use of fraudulent securities promotions to extract money from outside investors, and the disputed relationships between organized-crime figures and legitimate apparel transportation. The last two are unusually well attested by actual court rulings and state testimony, rather than by repetition of an underworld anecdote. [1][2][3]

2. Identity, Birth Date, and the “Vincent Alo” Confusion

The person profiled here appears in court opinions as Vincent Aloi and in some biographical compilations as Vincenzo Aloi. His commonly cited birth date is September 22, 1933. This precise date appears on the FBI deceased-subject list as well as in modern reference compilations; an original civil birth record has not been inspected for this edition. The FBI entry also gives a contradictory death year, examined separately below. [12][13] Aloi must not be confused with Vincent “Jimmy Blue Eyes” Alo, the different Genovese-family figure with a similar name and a different birth era. An image search or automated criminal-figure directory can easily substitute a portrait of Vincent Alo for Vincent Aloi. This biography deliberately includes no unverified portrait and uses family affiliations, documentary records and birth dates to maintain identification. [12][13] The label “Vincent Aloi Sr.” occurs in the 1991 New Jersey report because the investigators also describe his son Vincent Jr. The other repeatedly recorded first-name forms are not evidence of two separate Colombo captains. [2]

3. Sebastian “Buster” Aloi and the Family Network

Aloi was the son of Sebastian (“Buster”) Aloi, whose place in Brooklyn gambling circles is described in contemporary reporting and whose relationship to the younger Aloi appears in the federal appellate court’s reconstruction of the leasing-company case. In that opinion the elder Aloi was in Florida when business associates consulted him concerning the proposed securities offering; the account records family contacts but does not itself adjudicate every aspect of Sebastian’s alleged criminal career. [1][9] Aloi’s brother Benedetto (“Benny”) Aloi is identified in later accounts as another prominent Colombo member. Neither a father’s standing nor a brother’s reputation proves that Vincent held a particular administrative title at a specified date. Kinship nevertheless mattered to the organization’s continuity: trusted personal links could connect a local crew, other New York organizations and Florida contacts across decades. [1][3][13] Claims that Vincent was Carlo Gambino’s godson or that family connections alone dictated promotions recur in secondary narratives. In the absence of a matched baptismal record or an independently documented promotion decision, such claims should remain attribution rather than unqualified fact. [9][13]

4. Gambling, Loan-Sharking, and the Early Criminal Economy

Contemporary press reconstruction linked the Aloi family to gambling and lending activities in Williamsburg, Brooklyn, and reported that Sebastian passed interests to Vincent around 1963 because of declining health. These are press-reported accounts of alleged illicit enterprise; they should not be confused with the specific securities-fraud charges later tested in federal court. The extent of the son’s control over individual gambling locations is not established here through original ledgers, indictments or property files. [9] A declassified March 29, 1972 FBI memorandum alleged that Aloi and associates were using rooms at the Doral Country Club and a Miami Beach hotel in connection with an extortionate-credit investigation. The document sought authority to apply for surveillance and described information received from investigators and informants. It did not announce a resulting conviction, and this page does not infer one. [8] The geographic movement from New York to Florida is significant because it shows the reach of law-enforcement interest, not proof that every person linked to a venue was a member of a single nationwide conspiracy. [8][9]

5. Caporegime Status and the Mid-1960s

A 1972 newspaper feature described Aloi as a Colombo caporegime from the mid-1960s. This is a contemporaneous description, albeit one dependent on reporting sources rather than a public personnel register. The New Jersey State Commission of Investigation’s reports two decades later independently identified Aloi as a caporegime, strengthening the conclusion that he held that organizational grade at multiple points in his adult career. [2][3][9] In Mafia terminology a caporegime generally supervised a crew of members and associates, but the title did not imply an open-ended mandate to represent the entire family. Any assertion that a captain “controlled” a business requires additional evidence specifying whether he owned shares, managed day-to-day operations, exerted intimidation or merely appeared in an investigator’s organizational chart. [1][2]

6. Early Garment Business: City Carriers and Related Work

Aloi’s business profile extended beyond criminal case captions. The New Jersey garment-industry investigation recounts his participation in City Carriers, a company transporting finished clothing from manufacturers to retail sellers. A former partner, Louis Bettelheim, testified under immunity that Aloi previously worked in coat manufacturing and joined the trucking firm without bringing new trucking customers at the outset. Bettelheim described himself as the operator of its transport work and Aloi as the outward-facing commercial representative. [2] According to that testimony, Aloi approached retailers for business, discussed customer complaints and tried to negotiate higher rates. These activities have an ordinary commercial form even where investigators believed the industry was vulnerable to organized-crime pressure. The historical question is not whether a trucking company was inherently illegal but how particular customer relationships, payments and labor practices developed under Aloi’s ownership. [2]

7. Colombo’s Shooting and the Leadership Vacuum in 1971

On June 28, 1971, Joseph Colombo was shot at a public Italian-American Civil Rights League gathering in Manhattan and survived in a severely disabled condition. The resulting inability of the nominal boss to perform day-to-day leadership created a contested period in the organization. Individual accounts name Joseph Yacovelli, Aloi, Joseph Brancato, Thomas DiBella and Carmine Persico in differing roles and sequences. [3][9][13] It is misleading to render that entire period as a straightforward succession in which each name automatically held complete control. The 1990 New Jersey state report described Aloi and Brancato as captains associated with poor leadership during Colombo’s incapacity, and it referred to DiBella’s temporary acting status. These were official investigators’ conclusions, not an authenticated internal succession instrument. [3] Aloi’s personal criminal cases in 1973–1974 further complicate a continuous-tenure story: incarceration and legal appeals materially constrained his availability even if he retained influence or loyal associates. [1][6]

8. Contemporary Reports of Acting-Boss Authority

Fred J. Cook’s June 4, 1972 feature is an early published account describing Aloi’s prominence in the family and reporting a rapid rise to acting leadership after an acquittal in a separate securities case. The contemporaneity of the claim matters: it is not simply a succession date invented decades later. But a newspaper characterization, like a police chart, can reflect informant beliefs or a temporary accommodation rather than an uncontested formal appointment. [9][14] The 1991 state commission’s later description of him as a former acting boss provides an official retrospective corroboration that some such role was attributed to him. Neither source proves a month-by-month appointment term. It is safer to write that Aloi was reported to have acted as boss during the 1971–1973 transition than to print an exact term as settled. [2][3][9]

9. Joseph Yacovelli, Joseph Brancato, and Thomas DiBella

The overlapping names in Colombo histories reflect the difference between street supervision, a title recognized by other bosses and the person most influential inside particular crews. Joe Yacovelli is often identified as one of the early interim leaders following Colombo’s shooting. New Jersey’s 1990 investigative account specifically connected Aloi and Joseph Brancato to ineffective leadership while describing Thomas DiBella as temporary boss. [3][13] Aloi’s role should therefore be examined relationally. That he had standing as a captain is well supported. That he was sometimes called acting boss is supported by contemporary reporting and later official description. That he replaced every other figure decisively in a single documented ceremony is not established by the materials reviewed here. [2][3][9]

10. The April 1972 Murder of Joseph Gallo

Joseph (“Crazy Joe”) Gallo was killed in Manhattan’s Little Italy in April 1972, an event situated within the long and violent conflicts of the Profaci–Colombo organization. Aloi’s name entered the ensuing investigative history because prosecutors questioned him about a location in Nyack, New York, said to have been associated with plotting and concealment of the murder. That connection is documented in his later perjury litigation. [4][5] The homicide and perjury cases must remain legally distinct. Being questioned about a place allegedly used by conspirators, or being convicted of false testimony about having visited it, is not equivalent to a homicide conviction. This distinction is especially important because later summaries sometimes compress the 1972 murder, 1973 testimony and changes in family leadership into one unqualified narrative. [4][6][10]

11. The Informant Allegation Concerning the Gallo Killing

Contemporary press reported that witness Joseph Luparelli accused Aloi of directing Gallo’s killing. That allegation is an attributed statement by a criminal participant or informant, not a judicial finding that Aloi ordered a murder. The 1978 federal habeas opinion shows that the grand-jury case was concerned with a Nyack apartment allegedly linked to preparations or concealment, and it records that trial witnesses disputed Aloi’s denial of visiting the apartment. [4][10] The documented conviction was for perjury, not for ordering Gallo’s death. This page does not turn an informer’s accusation into an established homicide role; a responsible treatment of the episode distinguishes testimony, prosecutorial theories and verdicts. The murder itself is important to Aloi’s biography because it precipitated extensive investigative pressure that produced the perjury case. [4][6][10]

12. November 1972 Grand-Jury Testimony

Aloi appeared before a New York County grand jury on November 28, 1972. Under New York’s then-applicable rules he received transactional immunity when he testified; that protection did not extend to intentionally false statements. He told the jury that he had never visited a specified apartment in Nyack. The grand jury was examining the Gallo homicide and related events. [4][5] The subsequent perjury dispute focused not only on whether that particular denial was false but also on what portions of the remainder of Aloi’s compelled testimony prosecutors could introduce against him at trial. A 92-page record containing inquiries into his employment and alleged underworld status was later admitted in substantial part. The admissibility of that broader testimony became the issue in his federal habeas litigation. [4][5]

13. June 1973 Perjury Verdict and August Sentence

A state jury convicted Aloi of first-degree perjury after trial in June 1973. New York appellate records specify that on August 7, 1973, he received an indeterminate state sentence of two and one-third to seven years. He initially entered custody; the judgment was stayed and he obtained bail on October 3, 1973 while pursuing an appeal. [4][6] Those dates are particularly important because later accounts sometimes describe a seven-year sentence without its minimum and maximum components, or confuse the state sentence with the separate nine-year federal securities sentence. There were two convictions, separate sovereign prosecutions and successive rounds of legal review. [1][6]

14. 1978 Federal Habeas Judgment

In October 1978, U.S. District Judge Gerard Goettel granted federal habeas relief in Shargel v. Fenton, initially setting aside the perjury conviction on the theory that the trial court had permitted excessive use of Aloi’s immunized grand-jury testimony. The decision analyzed the distinction between the alleged false answer and extensive truthful or unrelated compelled statements appearing in the broader transcript. [4] That was a significant but nonfinal victory. The state appealed, and later Supreme Court developments required reconsideration. Any biography that ends the legal story with the 1978 order incorrectly presents a temporary district-court result as the lasting disposition of the criminal judgment. [5][6]

15. The 1979 Second Circuit Decision

The Second Circuit, in Shargel v. Lefkowitz, 596 F.2d 42 (March 13, 1979), affirmed the federal district court’s grant of relief. Its reasoning placed particular emphasis on the potentially improper evidentiary use of testimony compelled under a grant of immunity beyond what was necessary to try the charged perjury. [5] The opinion is authoritative for what the appellate panel decided in March 1979, but the subsequent remand means its holding cannot be reported as the last word. Chronological precision is essential: a later court can reverse, vacate or otherwise alter a once-valid judicial decision. [5][6][16]

16. Reconsideration and the Final Perjury Litigation Trajectory

Following Supreme Court reconsideration of related immunity doctrine, the Second Circuit reversed the earlier habeas relief in 1980. The New York Appellate Division’s 1984 published decision records explicitly that the writ was ultimately vacated in June 1980 and that further review was denied. Thus the state perjury judgment was not permanently eliminated by the successful 1978 and 1979 rulings. [6][16][17] On September 29, 1980, Aloi surrendered to New York authorities to continue serving his state sentence. New York thereafter litigated how to treat periods of federal confinement as jail-time credit; the 1984 Appellate Division decision rejected the credit theory in the proceeding before it. The state and federal custodial periods must not be counted as if all days were one undifferentiated nine-year sentence. [6]

17. The Separate 1970–1971 Securities Prosecution

Sources identify an earlier stock-fraud indictment reported in November 1970 and an acquittal in December 1971. That acquittal is not the same legal proceeding as the later At-Your-Service Leasing fraud conviction. The 1972 press account discussing Aloi’s reported promotion referred to an acquittal in this earlier period. [9][14] The archival newspaper materials cited in secondary accounts have not been fully page-collated for this edition. Consequently, the precise indictment counts, precise verdict wording and all co-defendants in the earlier matter are not reconstructed here from unsourced web summaries. The securely documented securities-fraud conviction is instead the later case reported at 511 F.2d 585. [1][14]

18. At-Your-Service Leasing: Corporate and Financial Context

The federal case concerned At-Your-Service Leasing Corporation, often abbreviated AYSL, a company seeking to sell securities despite financial weakness and difficulty attracting investors. The published appellate opinion recounts an offering circular describing substantial investment risks, failed early sales, and a subsequent group effort to place shares by questionable means. [1] Businessman Sidney Graifer and intermediaries connected to Sebastian Aloi, Ralph Lombardo, securities promoter Michael Hellerman and John Dioguardi entered into arrangements concerning the sale and distribution of shares. The government alleged and the appellate court upheld jury findings that the scheme involved false or misleading representations and the movement of proceeds through participants. [1][7] A stock promotion is not fraudulent merely because the people involved are reputed Mafia associates. The relevant findings concerned deceptive securities practices and the concrete evidence of their implementation. [1]

19. The Structure of the Stock-Fraud Arrangement

In its detailed reconstruction, the Second Circuit explained the arrangement for 50,000 AYSL shares and a proposed $45,000 payment connected to the promoters. Negotiations included an initial $22,500 transfer and later revisions in which participants discussed what would be paid to intermediaries. A telephone conversation from Sebastian in Florida to Vincent in Suffern, New York figured in the appellate court’s account of the evidence linking the latter to the operation. [1] The court assessed Vincent’s conduct separately, finding that the jury had sufficient evidence that he helped keep the transaction moving and participated in distributing proceeds. This was not a conviction by association with his father or with Dioguardi; it rested on evidence of his acts within the alleged securities conspiracy. [1]

20. Investors and the Financial Harm of the Scheme

The appellate court devoted particular attention to purchasers whose money entered the offering. It described testimony from five people who acquired AYSL stock after broker solicitation and referred to $127,500 from sales to purchasers whom the court characterized as victims of the fraudulent transaction. [1] This dimension matters historically because organized-crime stock fraud is sometimes narrated exclusively as competition among underworld financiers and brokers. The court’s record shows an external public affected through a market offering. The money figure in the opinion should not be casually enlarged into a total loss figure for all investors or conflated with the proposed promoter kickbacks. [1]

21. December 1973 Convictions and the Federal Sentence

Aloi was convicted in the AYSL proceeding of conspiracy to violate the securities laws, use of a false and misleading offering circular, and wire fraud. The court’s reported judgment specifies a five-year sentence plus a $10,000 fine on conspiracy, a consecutive four-year sentence plus a $5,000 fine on the offering-circular count, and a suspended five-year term plus a $1,000 fine on the wire-fraud count. The aggregate custodial term was nine years and the fines totaled $16,000. [1] The often repeated shorthand “nine years for stock fraud” is therefore broadly correct but loses the charge structure and conditional aspect of the suspended term. Contemporary news accounts dated February 1974 are consistent with the period of sentencing, but the actual appellate opinion is the controlling source here for the disposition. [1][15]

22. The 1975 Federal Appeal

In United States v. Aloi, 511 F.2d 585, the Second Circuit analyzed the evidence, joint-trial challenges and sentencing complaints. The decision was issued January 31, 1975, and rehearing was denied July 7. Some electronic case listings display the rehearing date as the decision date without explaining the two events; the published report specifies both. [1] The appellate court upheld the convictions and refused to disturb Aloi’s sentence based on the arguments presented. The defense had challenged the use of hearsay in government sentencing submissions and argued that the joint trial created unfair prejudice. The panel found that defense counsel had an opportunity to examine and respond to the sentencing information and that the trial judge took meaningful precautions about jury prejudice. [1]

23. Federal Imprisonment, 1979 Reduction, and State Custody

The 1984 state appellate decision gives a reliable framework for the overlapping sentences. Aloi was in federal custody following the securities conviction while state authorities maintained an interest in his previously imposed perjury sentence. The decision states that the federal sentence was reduced to time served on June 11, 1979. That reduction did not itself erase the pending state sentence. [6] After interim litigation, Aloi returned to state custody in September 1980. The 1991 New Jersey commission reports that his imprisonment ended with release in February 1985. The state commission summarized nine years of incarceration overall, which is not identical to saying he served a continuous nine years of the federal sentence. [2][6]

24. The 1989 New Jersey Organized-Crime Assessment

A New Jersey State Commission of Investigation annual report submitted in September 1990 discussed the Colombo organization in the late 1980s. It identified Aloi as a caporegime living in Suffern, New York and described his work with two sons in a Hudson County garment-trucking firm. The report also evaluated the damage it believed the family had experienced after Colombo was shot in 1971. [3] Its discussion of Aloi and Joseph Brancato as poor leaders is an opinion attributed to investigators or law-enforcement sources; it should not become an objective measure of every business result or violent incident during that period. The report is especially useful because it demonstrates that, years after prison, investigators still considered Aloi an active figure worth discussing in the family’s organizational assessment. [3]

25. The 1991 Garment Inquiry and Evidentiary Character

The New Jersey State Commission of Investigation devoted a major portion of its April 1991 report to garment trucking and the role of organized-crime figures in commercial networks. It named Aloi of Suffern as a Colombo captain and former acting boss. It also heard testimony concerning owners, drivers, customers and relationships among competing apparel carriers. [2] The commission could investigate and expose patterns, but it was not conducting a criminal trial in which the report’s descriptions of misconduct automatically equaled convictions. It used testimony from business participants, documents and refusals to testify; Aloi declined to provide answers to the commission. For that reason allegations about motive and customer switching should be attributed to witnesses or findings rather than stated as proved criminal offenses. [2]

26. TSI-City Carriers and Commercial Reorganization

According to the commission’s summary of Bettelheim’s testimony, Aloi and Bettelheim merged City Carriers with TSI around 1979. The resulting Jersey City business, TSI-City Carriers, included 25 percent interests for each of the two men. The commission describes other partners and their separate industry interests; an equity share did not by itself establish exclusive operational control. [2] Witness testimony portrayed a continuing division of responsibilities between trucking management and commercial outreach. The arrangement also highlights why even seemingly straightforward underworld biographies require corporate evidence: the businesses had multiple shareholders, actual vehicles, transport customers and payroll expenses. A family member’s reputation cannot replace those concrete records. [2]

27. Formation of Via Motor Services in December 1985

After leaving prison, Aloi became dissatisfied with the existing arrangement, Bettelheim told the commission, because he wanted his sons to acquire an ownership role. In December 1985, Aloi took his allocated share of accounts and trucks, characterized by the report as worth about $1 million, and formed Via Motor Services (VMS) in South Kearny, New Jersey. [2] The report described his wife Elizabeth as a partner alongside David Gross and identified sons Vincent Jr. and Sebastian as partners; his daughter also worked for the business. These names are relevant family-business facts from an official investigation, not evidence that all relatives committed crimes. The customers, assets and partners need to be distinguished from allegations about influence in the transport industry. [2]

28. Reported Salaries and Client Transfers, 1986–1989

The 1991 commission calculated that between 1986 and 1989, Aloi, his wife and two sons received a combined $1,212,343 in salary from VMS. This is a collective payroll total for the identified individuals over the period, not an estimate of Aloi’s personal net worth, company profit or criminal proceeds. The figure should not be casually recast into one man’s annual income. [2] The report also recounted testimony that certain TSI-City customers were told their trucking would be transferred to VMS without first being asked if they wished to move. It described gifts, bonuses and a disputed customer relationship involving a clothing company. Those observations support a history of contentious business practices; establishing a particular extortion offense would require a separately verified criminal disposition. [2]

29. Garment Labor and the Limits of Guilt by Association

The state report linked VMS drivers to Local 102 of the International Ladies’ Garment Workers’ Union. It also described broader problems in the garment industry involving contractors, brokers, union relationships, trucking costs and alleged organized-crime influence. Neither employment by a trucking firm nor membership in a garment workers’ union proves individual complicity in crime. [2] The useful distinction for research is between ordinary labor institutions and situations where particular witnesses alleged coercion, improper payments or pressure on customer selection. An allegation against a company’s senior figure must not be applied to every employee or family member. This page records the commission’s account while preserving those boundaries. [2]

30. Aloi, the Gambinos, and the Other Joseph Gallo

The commission’s garment inquiry discussed business interests of other New York organized-crime figures, including members of the Gambino organization and Joseph N. Gallo, described as a former Gambino consigliere. This Joseph N. Gallo is not Joseph “Crazy Joe” Gallo, the Colombo-family rebel killed in April 1972. The shared name has sometimes caused confusion across accounts of trucking and homicide. [2][4] The report considered whether figures associated with different families operated in overlapping commercial and geographic spaces. Such proximity is meaningful for understanding why garment suppliers and trucking contractors could face pressure in a concentrated market, but shared business sectors alone do not establish an interfamily pact or joint culpability for specific crimes. [2]

31. The Third Colombo Conflict, 1991–1993

The early 1990s saw another major Colombo-family conflict between supporters of the imprisoned Carmine Persico and the faction associated with Victor Orena. Later Mafia histories sometimes place Aloi, or his brother Benedetto, among men with interests on one side of that divide. The accessible 1991 investigative reports predate much of the subsequent open warfare and therefore cannot independently establish a personal operational role in every incident. [2][3][13] Aloi’s lifetime overlapped with successive disputes over family legitimacy; that is historically clear. It is less certain from the primary records assembled here which meetings he attended, what orders he issued, or whether he personally sponsored any violent act during the 1991–1993 struggle. Assertions of his allegiance should be marked as later reporting, not elevated to a proved battlefield command. [3][13]

32. Later Claims to the Consigliere Title

Secondary succession lists assign Aloi a period as consigliere during the 1990s and sometimes an acting advisory role in the 2000s. These compilations are useful finding aids but are not authenticated internal appointment records; some lists disagree about chronology and about whether a post was formal or acting. The New Jersey commission’s 1991 evidence more firmly confirms the earlier status of captain and former acting boss. [2][13][18] A sound organizational biography therefore distinguishes the reported late-career advisory appointments from the well-grounded 1970s criminal judgments and 1980s business records. A future edition could establish precise later ranks with dated FBI organizational exhibits, surveillance records or firsthand testimony properly attributed to its source. [2][3][18]

33. Departure from Public View and Reported Death in 2025

A November 9, 2025 article by journalist Scott Burnstein stated in its publicly visible headline that Aloi had died in the summer of that year. Later reference compilations give July 14, 2025, but a contemporaneous death certificate, funeral notice or accessible full obituary independently confirming that particular calendar day has not been matched in this review. The article also uses “at 92,” which would be mathematically inconsistent with a September 22, 1933 birth date if death occurred in July 2025; he would have been 91 then. [11][13] An FBI deceased-person index released in 2009, surprisingly, labels “VINCENZO ALOI” with a September 22, 1933 birth and a March 15, 1999 death date. This conflicts with accounts of later activity and the 2025 report, and is likely a record error or misidentification—but it cannot simply be edited away. This first edition records the 2025 report with an explicit warning that the exact death day and the anomalous earlier index require original vital-record verification. [11][12][13]

34. Personal Wealth, Reputation, and Unsupported Lore

Mafia biographies often substitute colorful claims for documented financial history. Here the federal court supplies concrete amounts relating to the fraud and sentencing, while New Jersey investigators supply ownership percentages, a business-asset valuation and collective salary figures. None of these figures is a validated comprehensive net-worth statement, and none supports an invented estimate of cash reserves, property portfolios or total lifetime illicit revenue. [1][2] Accounts describing Aloi as uniquely powerful, trusted by every boss or able to direct all Colombo crews are also stronger than the evidence permits. The historically defensible picture is of a longstanding captain, occasionally identified as acting boss, who was imprisoned after serious convictions and resumed substantial participation in an investigated commercial sector. [1][2][3][9]

35. Evidence Hierarchy: Courts, Investigators, and Testimony

The different sources in this entry perform different work. United States v. Aloi fixes the federal verdict and sentencing framework. People ex rel. Aloi v. LeFevre fixes custodial dates and the outcome of a state post-conviction dispute. The New Jersey garment report demonstrates what investigators found, what a named partner said under immunity, and what questions were not answered. A newspaper story can date a public claim but may not independently prove a secret Mafia appointment. [1][2][6][9] This separation is particularly necessary when discussing the Gallo killing: the allegation that Aloi ordered it and the judicial finding that he lied about a Nyack apartment are categorically different. Likewise, the 2025 death headline and the anomalous FBI index cannot jointly verify an exact death day. The reader should be able to trace every material assertion to the correct evidentiary class. [4][6][10][11][12]

36. Historical Significance and Conclusion

Aloi’s career demonstrates how organizational influence, lawful commercial ownership and provable personal criminal liability could coexist across different phases of life. The public record does not support a simple caricature of a perpetually dominant acting boss. Instead, it records a captain drawn into the post-1971 leadership crisis, serious court-tested securities wrongdoing, a protracted state perjury sentence, and later garment-trucking ventures scrutinized by the state of New Jersey. [1][2][3][6] The long span of the record also cautions against compressing a criminal biography into a promotion chart. Names of businesses, transaction structures, purchasers’ losses, grand-jury immunity doctrines and postconviction appeals explain the mechanisms by which authorities encountered him. These particulars are better grounded than elaborate claims about unrecorded orders or secret deals. Future editions should prioritize original death documentation, definitive 1972–1973 appointment materials and corroborated post-1991 leadership evidence. [1][2][6][11][12]

Research and Publication Limitations

Primary verification. Court decisions were directly examined for the 1973 perjury conviction, federal fraud judgment and 1978–1984 post-conviction sequence. New Jersey’s 1990 and 1991 reports were checked against their published texts and selected page images. No proprietary court docket, original FBI full subject file, corporate shares register or civil death certificate was obtained.

Acting leadership. Contemporary journalism and the 1991 commission confirm that Aloi was described as an acting boss, but exactly when the family’s executive authority passed among Yacovelli, Aloi, DiBella and Persico is not resolved by those references. The published hierarchy is not an authenticated internal Mafia record.

Homicide attribution. A witness accusation that Aloi directed Joseph Gallo’s killing is only a reported allegation. The court-tested perjury conviction concerned denial of a visit to a Nyack apartment. This edition does not state he was adjudicated responsible for Gallo’s murder.

Business misconduct. The New Jersey commission quoted a former partner testifying under immunity and identified customers and payments. Those findings do not constitute a blanket racketeering conviction of every employee, shareholder or relative in the apparel-transport businesses.

Death records. The publicly accessible 2025 death report, the exact July 14 date in tertiary compilations, and the FBI’s anomalous 1999 dead-list entry do not form one consistent original-document chain. Date of death remains editorially flagged pending a civil or contemporary funeral record. Reported ages also require arithmetic reconciliation.
