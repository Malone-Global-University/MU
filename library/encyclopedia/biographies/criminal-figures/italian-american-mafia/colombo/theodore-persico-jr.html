1. Historical Overview

Theodore N. “Teddy” Persico Jr., also called “Skinny Teddy,” is a New York organized-crime figure repeatedly identified in court and government records with the Colombo crime family. His public record is unusually extensive: a state narcotics conviction from the late 1980s, a federal extortion-related racketeering plea in 2005, a later admitted conspiracy tied to the 1993 killing of Joseph Scopo, and the 2021–2023 federal prosecution over a labor-union extortion enterprise. The most important documents are the Department of Justice announcements, 2017 judicial discussion of the Scopo evidence, and the 2023 guilty-plea announcement. [1] [4] [6] [10]

An accurate account cannot turn each prosecution into a generic statement that he controlled the family. Prosecutors called him a soldier in 2005, an administration member in 2010, a captain and possible future boss in 2023, and—according to reporting on court proceedings—called him the boss in 2026. These descriptions concern different times and different evidentiary postures. His specific position in any one year requires a dated source, not an inherited title from the Persico surname. [1] [4] [10] [12]

2. Name, Date of Birth, and Identification

The September 29, 2005 federal prosecutor’s release lists the subject as Theodore Persico, Jr., alias “Teddy,” and gives his date of birth as September 9, 1963. A published court opinion in a related family prosecution uses the form Theodore N. Persico, Jr. His nickname “Skinny Teddy” is particularly common in later investigative reporting. No independent birth certificate or baptismal record has been examined, but the government identification provides a reliable documentary anchor. [1] [5] [12]

He must be distinguished from his father Theodore Persico, sometimes called Teddy, whose 1990s federal trial and 1999 appellate litigation are a different criminal case. The former’s son and nephew relationships link him to Carmine Persico’s generation, but they do not merge three distinct legal biographies. The full identifying distinction, including middle initial where available, is used to prevent erroneous assignment of convictions. [1] [5] [14]

3. Two Theodore Persicos: The Older Man and His Son

The older Theodore Persico was Carmine Persico’s brother and a Colombo family figure. His criminal history included a 1997 federal sentence stemming from the early-1990s internal war and an appeal published as United States v. Persico, 164 F.3d 796. That litigation can be mistaken for the son’s because published decisions sometimes shorten defendant names to “Theodore Persico.” The elder man was not born in 1963 and was not the person first released from a narcotics sentence in April 2004. [1] [14]

The junior Persico is the man named in the 2005 racketeering admission, March 2010 indictment, 2012 Scopo-conspiracy plea, and 2021–2023 labor-union case. Some later proceedings contain both the elder and younger generations as historical background. Each case citation in this article is consequently tied to the specific defendant and proceeding rather than treated as a pooled family indictment. [1] [4] [5] [6] [10]

4. Carmine Persico, Family Kinship, and Inherited Influence

Carmine “The Snake” Persico was Theodore Jr.’s paternal uncle, not his father. Federal prosecutors explicitly identified that relationship in 2005. Carmine’s long tenure, much of it spent in prison, created recurring debates about who could speak for the family. For a younger relative, a surname and access to older figures could create standing, but family connection did not itself constitute a judicially documented promotion. [1] [2] [10]

Other Persico relatives appear in several investigations, including Michael J. Persico, a cousin named in 2010 and 2012 prosecutions, and Carmine L. Persico, a distinct younger relative named in the 2005 case. Treating all of these as one defendant obscures different guilty pleas and judgments. The family map is historically significant principally because investigators encountered criminal actors across several generations. [2] [4] [5]

5. Childhood, Education, and Early Personal Life

The reviewed primary judicial and prosecutorial materials do not supply a full childhood biography, school transcript, or independently checked neighborhood upbringing. His September 1963 birth is more securely supported than colorful retrospective stories about his youth. Although public accounts associate the family with Brooklyn, it would be inappropriate to assign a specific lifelong address, educational institution, or earliest criminal contact without corroboration. [1] [4]

A later New York regulatory proceeding recounted testimony and recollections regarding long friendship with Edward Garofalo Jr. and Persico’s reintegration after imprisonment. Such statements are evidence of how associates represented their relationships in the regulatory record, not a substitute for school, employment, or family archives. This entry therefore prioritizes the well-documented adult legal chronology. [3] [4]

6. The Colombo Family and the Meaning of Its Ranks

The Colombo organization, one of New York’s five Mafia families, has been described in federal records as consisting of a boss, underboss, consigliere, captains, soldiers and non-inducted associates. A captain manages a crew; the administration supervises the organization. Statements that a defendant was an “administration member” or “captain” need not signify the same position, and being designated as a successor does not itself establish that the succession occurred. [4] [8] [10]

A Mafia leadership claim can be supported by intercepted communications, informants, undercover recordings, meetings or court testimony; none is a public corporate appointment register. In 2023 federal prosecutors described Persico Jr. as a captain slated to become boss. A 2026 prosecutor’s characterization reported in the press went further. The biography retains the attribution and date of each description instead of silently merging them into continuous formal boss status. [10] [11] [12]

7. The 1988 Narcotics Conviction

The Justice Department stated that Persico Jr. had been convicted in 1988 on New York state narcotics-trafficking charges. His later federal prosecution began soon after he completed the resulting custodial term. The state judgment itself and the entire appellate history were not independently retrieved for this edition; the 1988 conviction and broad custodial period are drawn from the prosecutor’s verified later summary. [1] [2]

This first major conviction matters for chronology because he spent substantial time imprisoned during the later stages of the Colombo family’s internal conflict. A significant physical absence did not necessarily prevent a figure from exercising influence through visits and messages, but each claim about specific war-period actions must rely on testimony or his later criminal admission rather than speculation about how much he “must have known.” [1] [6] [7]

8. The 1991–1993 Colombo Conflict

The internal struggle between backers of imprisoned Carmine Persico and acting boss Victor Orena escalated into the violent 1991–1993 Colombo family war. Rival crews tried to control personnel, territory and the organization’s revenue; the violence included the murder of Joseph Scopo in Queens in October 1993. Later prosecutions linked Theodore Jr. to an agreement to kill Scopo, and the judicial record supports treating his role as more than an unproved journalistic allegation. [6] [7]

Historical accounts often compress the entire war into a Persico-versus-Orena narrative. The factional alignment matters here, but the actual criminal judgment concerning Theodore Jr. arose from a specific murder conspiracy. He was not, through that plea alone, adjudged responsible for every killing in the war, nor does his guilt automatically establish the exact decisions of every relative. [5] [6]

9. Joseph Scopo and the October 1993 Killing

Joseph Scopo was a Colombo figure associated with the Orena side of the dispute. He was murdered in October 1993. A 2017 Eastern District of New York opinion in cousin Michael Persico’s case recounted evidence from cooperating witness Anthony Russo and referred specifically to Theodore Jr.’s separate guilty plea to conspiracy to murder Scopo in aid of racketeering. This is a strong judicial anchor for his role in the conspiracy, not merely a later underworld legend. [5] [6]

The court summarized evidence that, during a temporary escorted visit from custody for his grandmother’s funeral services in August 1993, Theodore Jr. urged other figures to pursue Scopo. The court’s discussion concerned sentencing findings against Michael Persico, so the distinctions remain important: the younger Theodore’s own plea is one outcome; trial testimony about meetings and the shooter is another; the cousin’s sentencing findings are a third. [6] [7]

10. August 1993 Funeral-Home Meeting

The 2017 judicial opinion identified prison transport records connected with the funeral of Eleanor Avena and described an August 18, 1993 escorted visit to Brooklyn. At the funeral home, according to testimony the court found credible for that proceeding, Persico Jr. pressed Anthony Russo and other participants to target Scopo. The unusual setting has been repeated widely in news reports, but its documentary significance lies in the contemporaneous transport materials and later sworn testimony. [6] [7]

The testimony does not justify transforming every detail of conversational exchanges into verbatim historical dialogue. The accused’s admission of conspiracy and the judicially summarized circumstances support a narrower conclusion: the plot involved communication across prison boundaries during the war. The identity of each person who personally executed the killing is a distinct legal and evidentiary question. [6] [7]

11. April 28, 2004: Release after the State Sentence

Federal prosecutors recorded April 28, 2004 as the date Persico Jr. was released from Green Haven Correctional Facility in New York after approximately sixteen years served on the state narcotics conviction. This date is unusually precise for a biographical transition and serves as a useful check against later accounts that wrongly treat him as continuously imprisoned through 2005. [1] [2]

The same prosecutors said he promptly resumed Colombo activity. That assertion is supported in part by wiretap-based charges and his subsequent guilty plea, but it should not be taken to imply that every allegation raised after his release was admitted or convicted. The 2004 release is a custody fact; the legal outcome of the related 2004 conduct came in the 2005–2006 proceedings. [1] [3]

12. 2004 Surveillance and Extortion Threats

The Justice Department reported that electronic surveillance captured Persico Jr. discussing coercive debt collection with associates in 2004. The September 2005 release described a November 23 conversation in which he threatened violence against a debtor’s son as a way to compel payment. In court, the defendant admitted using threats intended to create fear, forming part of the racketeering plea. [1] [2]

The distinction between prosecutors’ quotation of surveillance and the defendant’s admitted offense matters. Some government descriptions of additional criminal conversations were investigative assertions, while the extortionate threat conduct was specifically admitted during the guilty plea. This article avoids assigning responsibility for an uncharged physical assault or treating threatening language as evidence that it was actually carried out. [1] [3]

13. May 2005 Federal Racketeering Charges

A May 5, 2005 federal announcement charged Theodore Jr. with racketeering including extortion-related conduct, and named Carmine L. Persico and Andre D’Apice as associates charged with extortion. The record also concerned an alleged separate effort to interfere with a grand-jury inquiry. It was a charging announcement, not a conviction; its value is the identification of participants and the allegations prompting arrest. [2] [1]

Several individuals shared the Persico surname in this proceeding, but the defendant identified by the September 9, 1963 birth date was Theodore Jr. His cousin or other relative’s allegations must not be merged into his guilt. The later plea provides the legally narrower resolution of the core racketeering charge attributed to him. [1] [2]

14. September 2005 Federal Guilty Plea

On September 28, 2005, before District Judge Carol B. Amon, Persico Jr. pleaded guilty to a racketeering charge; the Justice Department publicly announced that plea on September 29. He admitted involvement in the Colombo organization and coercive conduct intended to obtain a debt payment, including a threat directed toward a victim’s son. [1] [2]

The plea should be differentiated from the original multi-count indictment. The announcement noted that the offense carried a statutory maximum of twenty years, which was not the sentence imposed. A subsequent New York City regulatory decision cited a March 14, 2006 judgment of forty-two months in custody. The correct profile lists that 42-month disposition rather than confusing maximum exposure with punishment. [1] [3]

15. March 2006: Forty-Two-Month Sentence

The Business Integrity Commission later recorded a March 14, 2006 federal sentencing to forty-two months imprisonment in the racketeering matter. It characterized the defendant’s 2005 allocution as an admission that the predicate conduct furthered his association with the Colombo family. Although the commission was adjudicating a licensing matter rather than criminal guilt, its citation to the judgment is useful as a secondary government confirmation of the penalty. [3] [1]

The actual credited time and precise release date following this sentence were not reconstructed from prison administrative records. Consequently, it is inaccurate to infer a date of freedom by simply adding forty-two months to the sentencing date. Overlapping custody, pre-sentence detention and supervision conditions require record-specific accounting. [1] [3]

16. Business Relationships and Regulatory Scrutiny

Public regulatory records describe friendships and business connections involving Edward Garofalo Jr. and hauling-related businesses. These materials are significant because organized-crime investigations often intersected with construction contracting, waste handling and union benefit contributions—areas where a criminal association might influence licensing decisions even without a separate conviction in the regulatory case. [3] [4]

A denial of a trade-waste license is an administrative outcome with its own burden and purpose. It should not be recast as a criminal conviction of Persico Jr. for every transaction examined by the commission. The page instead uses the regulatory record to illuminate his public identification and business surroundings, with the criminal dispositions anchored elsewhere. [3] [4]

17. March 2010 Multi-Defendant Indictment

On March 9, 2010, federal prosecutors announced an indictment charging Theodore Jr., his cousin Michael Persico and several associates with offenses connected to a racketeering enterprise. The announcement classified him as an administration member at the time of the investigation, a distinct characterization from the soldier label of 2005. It described alleged wire fraud, extortion and benefits-related schemes connected with construction work. [4] [5]

The 2010 announcement is a reliable source for the existence and scope of the charges but not by itself for guilt on every underlying act. Subsequent prosecution and plea records, particularly the later murder-conspiracy plea, must be analyzed separately. Details about other defendants’ pleas are not silently assigned to Theodore Jr. [4] [5] [6]

18. Construction Debris Removal and World Trade Center Work

The 2010 case alleged that Colombo-connected participants used All Around Trucking in connection with debris removal subcontracting for Testa Corporation. The alleged arrangement involved kickbacks to a contractor’s foreman and threats aimed at obtaining payment. Investigators said the scheme touched projects including the World Trade Center construction site and the Newtown Creek wastewater treatment plant. [4] [5]

The association with a high-profile construction site is historically important as evidence of the economic sectors prosecutors believed the organization sought to penetrate. It should not be described as proof that the defendant controlled the site, all trucking at the project, or every related contract. The alleged acts, the business relationships and the ultimate pleas require distinct evidentiary labels. [4] [5]

19. Local 282 and Employee-Benefit Allegations

The March 2010 announcement also described alleged double-breasting and employee-benefit evasion involving companies and people associated with the Teamsters Local 282 environment. Such arrangements, if established, deprived workers’ benefit plans of required contributions and changed the competitive conditions under which legitimate contractors operated. [4] [3]

Because the charging release names multiple defendants and does not state that Theodore Jr. personally committed every benefits offense, this biography refers to the scheme as part of the charged enterprise rather than listing every allegation as his personal conviction. The 2021–2023 prosecution later involved a different labor union and health fund; those should not be conflated with Local 282. [4] [8] [10]

20. 2012 Plea Concerning the Scopo Conspiracy

In the wider 2010 federal case, the junior Persico entered a guilty plea to conspiracy to murder Joseph Scopo in aid of racketeering, a plea specifically acknowledged by the federal court in its 2017 opinion. That conviction materially differs from an unproved murder allegation: the defendant admitted criminal involvement in an agreement to kill, while whether he personally fired a weapon is not established by that plea. [6] [7]

Court discussions of related cases can be misleading because the defendant Michael J. Persico also pleaded guilty in June 2012, but to a separate conspiracy involving an extortionate credit extension. Michael’s plea is not Theodore’s plea. The two proceedings overlap in evidence and the court’s narrative, not in the precise offense each defendant admitted. [5] [6]

21. May 29, 2014: Twelve-Year Sentence

Contemporary courthouse coverage reports that federal Judge Sandra Townes sentenced Theodore Jr. to twelve years of imprisonment on May 29, 2014, following the admitted murder conspiracy associated with Scopo. The reported sentence is twelve years, not an undifferentiated “life sentence” or a punishment for every homicide alleged in the earlier multi-defendant indictment. [7] [6]

Reports of a ten-year negotiated expectation should not be mistaken for the judicially reported sentence of twelve years. Nominal term, custody already served, and supervised-release start are different concepts. A July 2023 account states that he returned to supervised release in May 2020, but this article does not calculate time served without a complete prison movement record. [7] [15]

22. 2020 Supervised Release and Reentry

Reporting at the time of the 2023 racketeering plea placed Persico Jr.’s release to supervision in May 2020. The fact is important because government prosecutors later argued that, after release, he was considered for succession to the organization’s top position. A specific prison-to-community transition does not by itself prove subsequent leadership in every period. [15] [10]

Conditions of release often restrict association with known organized-crime figures, but their details depend on the individual judgment and subsequent court orders. The 2026 violation case returned to that distinction: particular contacts could breach a court order even if no new substantive racketeering conviction was entered. [9] [12] [13]

23. Andrew Russo and the Alleged Succession Plan

By 2021 the Justice Department identified Andrew “Mush” Russo as the family boss and Theodore Jr. as one of its captains. Its July 2023 guilty-plea announcement said Persico Jr. was slated to become the next boss. Media accounts linked the succession discussion to meetings around 2020, but those accounts describe an investigative theory of future authority, not a formal publicly documented installation date. [8] [10] [15]

Russo died in April 2022 while his prosecution was pending. His death created an obvious leadership question but did not retrospectively convert a captain into boss on the date of the indictment. Where federal authorities later spoke of Persico as the boss, this page records that as their later assertion rather than inventing a succession ceremony or an uninterrupted tenure. [8] [10] [12]

24. September 2021 Federal Indictment

The Justice Department announced a sweeping Colombo-family indictment in September 2021. It named the organization’s reported boss, underboss, consigliere and several captains, including Theodore Jr. The case charged a series of fraud, extortion, money-laundering, loansharking and drug-related offenses around a Queens labor union and other ventures. [8] [10]

The charging instrument and related government press release are accurate descriptions of prosecutorial allegations as of 2021. Guilty pleas in later years resolved the criminal responsibility of individual defendants to particular offenses. The subject’s eventual plea to racketeering is confirmed by a separate 2023 announcement and should not be replaced with a blanket assumption of conviction on every original count. [8] [10]

25. The Queens Labor-Union Extortion Scheme

The 2021–2023 prosecution centered partly on extortion directed at a senior official of a Queens-based labor union. Prosecutors said organized-crime figures demanded part of the official’s salary and attempted to control decisions affecting the union and its associated employee health-benefit fund. They sought financial benefit for members of the Colombo administration. [8] [10] [11]

This is distinct from the construction-debris and Local 282 allegations of the 2010 case. Both illustrate vulnerabilities where organized-crime influence could affect payroll, procurement, health benefits and bargaining institutions, but the cases involved different time periods and alleged schemes. The 2023 guilty plea supplies a more secure basis for his personal criminal liability in the later case. [4] [10] [11]

26. Health-Fund Vendor Steering and Financial Consequences

According to the July 2023 Justice Department account, defendants sought to compel trustees of an associated health fund to select vendors aligned with or selected by the criminal enterprise. Prosecutors alleged a goal of diverting more than $10,000 monthly from fund assets. The allegations involved prospective vendor selection and financial pressure as well as direct threats to a union official. [10] [11]

The amount described as the scheme’s objective is not interchangeable with money actually withdrawn from the fund, a particular defendant’s personal profit, or the amount ordered in restitution. The February 2024 prosecutor’s summary reported $280,890 restitution as part of Persico Jr.’s sentence; that judicially imposed obligation has a different evidentiary meaning from a projected monthly diversion. [10] [11]

27. 2022–2023 Detention and Bond Requests

A March 17, 2023 judicial opinion records the September 2021 indictment, subsequent superseding charges and repeated bail applications. The court rejected release proposals, citing the danger it concluded Persico posed in light of the alleged enterprise and the available evidence. The record identifies proposals involving substantial financial security and extensive supervision. [9]

A pretrial detention order is not a determination that every charged crime has been proved beyond a reasonable doubt. The judge considered risk and conditions of release at that stage. This biography therefore keeps the detention finding in its procedural place and cites the later guilty plea for the adjudicated criminal disposition. [9] [10]

28. 2023 Racketeering Guilty Plea

In July 2023 the Eastern District of New York announced that Persico Jr., identified as a captain and designated possible successor, had pleaded guilty to racketeering. The same case also produced pleas by other family administrators, captains and associates, including Benjamin Castellazzo and Ralph DiMatteo. Those other defendants’ penalties and precise plea counts remain their own. [10] [15]

The 2023 plea converted an important subset of the prosecution’s allegations into an admitted federal crime. It does not logically establish the truth of every statement in the broader charging announcement. In particular, the succession designation was a prosecutor’s investigative assessment; it was not an additional offense to which the defendant pleaded guilty. [8] [10] [11]

29. Sixty Months and Restitution

A February 28, 2024 Justice Department sentencing release states that Persico Jr. had already been sentenced to sixty months of federal imprisonment and ordered to pay $280,890 in restitution. This is the best directly confirmed term in the later racketeering case. It supersedes reporting that described a projected sentence or attorney estimate at the time of the 2023 plea. [11] [15]

The government summary did not give the exact day of Persico’s own sentencing in the text reviewed here. Accordingly, the chronology dates it “by February 28, 2024,” rather than inventing a specific day. The imposed sixty-month term should not be converted into an expected release date by arithmetic because jail credits, supervision and later penalties affect the custodial sequence. [11]

30. What the 2023 Case Revealed about Administration

The 2023 release named a hierarchy under Andrew Russo: underboss Benjamin Castellazzo, consigliere Ralph DiMatteo, and captains including Theodore Jr., Richard Ferrara and Vincent Ricciardo. The government’s description is more detailed than generic claims that the “entire Mafia” was led by any one defendant. It shows a network whose operational authority could persist despite arrests and vacancies. [10] [11]

The fact that multiple leaders pleaded guilty in a coordinated investigation did not by itself eliminate the organization or fix its next internal leadership structure. Prosecutorial claims that the family’s upper ranks had been struck were statements about enforcement impact, not proof that all related criminal activity ceased. This helps explain continued official scrutiny of Persico in 2025–2026. [10] [11] [12]

31. Reported 2025 Release from Custody

Later 2026 reporting describes Persico Jr. as having returned to community supervision during 2025 after the federal racketeering sentence. The precise Bureau of Prisons release record has not been independently collated for this edition. The reported return is significant principally because it supplied the setting for the subsequent violation-of-supervised-release proceedings. [12] [13]

A reader should not infer that sixty nominal months after a 2023 sentencing must mean he remained incarcerated throughout 2028; defendants can receive credit for pretrial detention and other adjustments. Nor should the report of release be treated as a finding that supervision had ended. The documented 2026 matter arose from restrictions still in force. [9] [11] [13]

32. August 2025 Reported Hospital Encounter

A later published review of the 2026 proceedings reports that surveillance at NYU Langone Hospital on August 29, 2025 showed Persico interacting with a person law enforcement considered associated with the Colombo group. He reportedly offered an innocuous explanation of the visit. The cited materials available for this edition are secondary summaries of the hearing, not the original surveillance footage or certified hearing transcript. [13]

The allegation and eventual admission of prohibited contact should not be enlarged into proof that any crime was planned during the encounter. The legal relevance was whether court-imposed association limits had been violated. Specific dates, individuals and alleged motives should remain as the reported court record presents them rather than supplied from inference. [12] [13]

33. December 2025 Bay Ridge Restaurant Contact

Contemporary reporting of federal proceedings identified a December 1, 2025 encounter with several Colombo members at Ponte Vecchio, a restaurant in Bay Ridge, Brooklyn. It described greetings at a holiday gathering and prosecutors’ contention that the contact violated supervision restrictions. Media coverage called the event a Christmas-season meeting; that description is not proof a criminal enterprise transaction took place there. [12] [13]

Persico’s account reportedly emphasized the accidental or social nature of the contact, while prosecutors cited the interaction as evidence of continued ties. The distinction matters: a supervised-release violation requires application of a court order, not proof of a new racketeering offense. Any inference about the visitors’ deference to him belongs in the attributed section on alleged organizational rank. [12] [13]

34. January 2026 Admission of Supervision Violations

Reporting dated January 22–23, 2026 says Persico acknowledged violating his supervised-release restrictions through prohibited contacts. Federal prosecutors argued that the contacts demonstrated his continuing standing within the Colombo group. The legal admission of prohibited association is distinguishable from the prosecutors’ separate conclusion that he had become the head of the family. [12] [13]

Contemporaneous reporting described defense arguments about the social context and a dispute over the leadership inference. Because no complete signed revocation judgment was obtained in this research pass, the precise wording of every admitted violation is presented as reported, not recited as though transcribed from a certified courtroom record. [12] [13]

35. 2026 Alleged Boss Designation

In January 2026, the New York Daily News reported that federal prosecutors characterized Persico Jr. as the Colombo family’s boss rather than merely its designated successor. This updated allegation is materially newer than the 2023 Justice Department label of captain and prospective boss. It must therefore appear in a biography current through November 2026. [10] [12]

Nonetheless, prosecutors’ characterization of clandestine leadership does not supply a complete succession ledger, corroborated induction vote, or externally verifiable start and end dates. Some reporting around the February hearing described him as a former boss. The record supports describing him as “captain and alleged subsequent boss,” with the timing and duration of any formal tenure explicitly left unresolved. [12] [13]

36. February 11, 2026: Nine-Month Sentence

A June 2026 secondary legal synthesis, citing contemporary courtroom reporting, identifies February 11, 2026 as the date Judge Hector Gonzalez imposed nine months imprisonment for the admitted supervised-release violations. This term is separate from, and subsequent to, the five-year racketeering sentence recorded by the Justice Department in February 2024. [11] [13]

The source hierarchy is explicit: the federal prosecutor’s releases establish the 2005, 2023 and 2024 legal outcomes, while the 2026 nine-month sentence is presently documented here by secondary reports rather than a certified revocation judgment or transcript. This article does not assert an exact current cell, remaining time, or release date as of the research cutoff. [11] [12] [13]

37. Three Different Types of Custody and Sentence

Persico Jr.’s chronology spans state imprisonment for narcotics, federal imprisonment for his 2005 racketeering plea, a later conspiracy-to-murder-related federal term, a 2023 racketeering sentence, and a 2026 penalty for breaking release conditions. Each is a separate legal episode. Adding nominal sentence lengths would not accurately reconstruct his total days in custody. [1] [3] [7] [11] [13]

A further historical danger is a false extra term created by attributing the father’s late-1990s federal racketeering sentence to the son. The 1999 Second Circuit case concerns the older Theodore. Even if accounts share relatives, aliases and family organization, the legal judgments must remain attached to the correct individual defendant. [14] [1]

38. What the Scopo Plea Proves—and Does Not Prove

The homicide-related plea permits a strong factual statement that Persico Jr. admitted participating in a conspiracy to kill Scopo. It does not by itself establish that he personally fired the fatal shots. The 2017 opinion discusses testimony about the operational crew and the murder in the context of sentencing proceedings against Michael Persico, illustrating how adjacent but distinct findings enter the historical record. [5] [6]

Historians should use the narrowest well-supported formulation: Persico Jr. pleaded guilty to the murder conspiracy; later accounts identify others in carrying out the attack; the cousin’s separate sentencing hearing addressed the weight of testimony on related acts. This method preserves the difference between a plea, testimony and an adjudicated role for each defendant. [6] [7]

39. Court Decisions, Prosecutorial Claims, and Witness Evidence

Different document genres have different strengths. A published appellate or district court decision can identify a legal disposition and summarize credited testimony, but may also recount accusations for background. A prosecutor’s announcement states the government’s theory and sometimes a defendant’s plea; a regulatory decision applies administrative standards; a news article reports a hearing with the journalist’s own editorial choices. [1] [3] [5] [6] [9] [12]

The source register uses direct legal materials wherever available and labels reliance on secondary contemporary reporting for otherwise unverified details. A future archival edition could add certified judgments, the supervised-release revocation transcript, state narcotics-case records, relevant FBI files and the complete sentencing docket. This first edition avoids false precision where those sources are missing. [9] [11] [13]

40. Victims, Union Members, and the Economics of Extortion

The conduct reflected in the federal records involved people pressured to pay debts, construction businesses exposed to coercion, and union officials and health-plan participants whose funds and decisions were targeted by criminal actors. The story is not only one of Mafia succession; the allegations and admissions concern extraction of money and potential deterioration of employment protections. [1] [4] [10] [11]

The economic mechanisms were varied. One scheme used threats against a debtor’s family, another allegedly sought favored subcontracting and kickbacks, and the later admitted racket involved extorting a union official and steering health-benefit procurement. These mechanisms should be analyzed separately, rather than as a single continuous company or one unchanging criminal business. [1] [4] [10] [11]

41. Media Coverage and the Problem of the Mafia Persona

The nickname “Skinny Teddy,” courtroom appearances, and repeated coverage of succession gave Persico Jr. a public persona. Such reporting can be useful for dates and contested courtroom assertions but often simplifies a complex record into a single arc of inevitable leadership. The recurring expression “boss-to-be,” for example, was an investigative claim in 2023, not an adjudicated office-holding fact. [10] [12] [15]

The 2026 reports demonstrate a second simplification risk: statements that he “is boss” and descriptions calling him a “former boss” are not wholly equivalent. The correct scholarly approach is to distinguish official investigative assessments from independent proof of exact hierarchy and to identify the source date for each formulation. [12] [13]

42. Related Defendants and Non-Transferable Convictions

Cousin Michael Persico was separately indicted and pleaded guilty to an extortionate-credit conspiracy in 2012. Other figures in the 2021 case, including Castellazzo, DiMatteo, Ferrara and Ricciardo, received different plea agreements and sentences. Their adjudications illuminate the larger enterprise but should never be pooled into Theodore Jr.’s criminal record. [4] [5] [10] [11]

Even the related 2017 judicial discussion of Scopo reached sentencing findings about Michael Persico, not a new trial verdict for Theodore. These identities are particularly prone to confusion because of repeated given names and family relationships. Correct cross-references are a safeguard against creating phantom convictions or overstating participation in other offenses. [5] [6] [14]

43. Patterns of Imprisonment and Remote Influence

The record illustrates how leadership and influence could be alleged or exercised despite repeated imprisonment. The 1993 conspiracy arose in part from communication during an escorted funeral visit, the 2004 extortion followed a long state prison term, and the 2020–2026 litigation focused on alleged reentry into criminal networks following federal custody. [1] [6] [7] [12]

Such patterns do not establish that every imprisoned member could direct the family at will. They show why investigators emphasized contacts, financial schemes, court-authorized recordings and the distinction between nominal title and practical influence. The historical interest lies in institutional continuity under legal pressure, not celebration of the individuals involved. [6] [9] [10]

44. Historical Interpretation and Legacy

Persico Jr.’s documented life links three decades of Colombo history: violence during the final phase of the 1991–1993 family war, racketeering and construction-related investigations in the 2000s and 2010s, and labor-union extortion litigation in the 2020s. It also demonstrates the continuity of a family network across generations, tempered by repeated state and federal intervention. [1] [4] [6] [10] [11]

For the MGU encyclopedia, the essential finding is not a claim that he held every possible Mafia rank. It is that a man with multiple adjudicated convictions was still identified by federal prosecutors as a captain and prospective boss in 2023, and was the subject of new alleged-boss claims and supervised-release sanctions in 2026. Organizational influence, charged conduct and legal guilt should remain distinct analytical categories. [10] [11] [12] [13]

45. Historical Conclusion

Theodore N. Persico Jr. emerges from the accessible record as an important Colombo family figure with verified dates of birth, conviction and plea milestones, and a substantial later leadership controversy. His most securely anchored events include the 2005 racketeering plea, the admission of a conspiracy connected with Scopo’s 1993 murder, and the 2023 racketeering plea followed by a five-year federal sentence. [1] [6] [10] [11]

Later government allegations and reporting described him as a successor and then a boss, while the February 2026 court outcome reflected prohibited associations during supervised release. The research record supports a carefully attributed account of those developments, not a fabricated internal succession date or a precise current prison location. This profile remains open to correction if fuller court docket or archival material becomes available. [12] [13]
