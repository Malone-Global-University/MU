Daniel “Danny” Greene (d. 1977)

Cleveland longshoremen’s union leader and Irish-American racketeer whose conflict with the local Mafia ended in a 1977 car-bomb assassination and consequential federal prosecutions.

1. Historical Overview

Daniel “Danny” Greene came to symbolize the violent realignment of Cleveland organized crime during the 1970s. A waterfront union official turned racketeer, Greene became an independent power in local gambling and business disputes. He was closely associated with Irish heritage in his public image, but he operated within a citywide network of shifting alliances involving men of different backgrounds. His ultimate conflict was not a contest between two uniform ethnic blocs: it involved competing figures, revenue streams, relationships, and succession claims within Cleveland’s criminal underworld. [1] [2]

His career is unusually well suited to documentary reconstruction because two federal appellate decisions provide firm legal boundaries. The Sixth Circuit reversed his conviction in a 1968 union-funds case because the prosecutor’s irrelevant questioning was exceptionally prejudicial. Another Sixth Circuit decision, issued in 1984, sustained racketeering convictions against six men associated with the conspiracy to murder him. These cases establish judicial outcomes, though neither alone supplies a complete account of his daily criminal activity or personal motivations. [3] [4]

Greene was killed on 6 October 1977 after leaving a dental appointment in Lyndhurst, a Cleveland suburb. The killing eventually helped expose extensive organized-crime operations and led to significant convictions. Popular accounts sometimes depict him as a lone rebel whose death automatically destroyed an entire Mafia organization. The more accurate history is that his killing was a major episode in a broader prosecutorial campaign, not proof that crime in Cleveland ended with one assassination. [5] [6]

2. Identity, Dates, and Historical Classification

Local historical institutions agree on Greene’s identity and date of death but differ on his precise birth date. The Encyclopedia of Cleveland History, published by Case Western Reserve University, gives 14 November 1929; Cleveland Historical’s biography gives 9 November 1929. A popular biographical reference even prints a different birth year. This edition favors the two Cleveland institutions’ shared birth year, while marking the day as unsettled pending direct verification against birth or baptism records. A discrepancy in secondary sources should not be concealed behind a falsely exact entry. [1] [2]

The label “Irish-American crime syndicate” describes the editorial collection in which Greene is placed. It does not mean that his followers constituted an ethnically exclusive organization or a formal branch of a national Irish Mafia. Likewise, while stories refer to a Celtic or Irish organization under his influence, the legal record identifies a rival criminal enterprise without supplying a permanent corporate-style charter, roster, or unchanging territorial border. [4]

The distinction also applies to rival organizations. Greene had dealings with Cleveland figures connected to Italian-American Mafia leadership, especially as disputes intensified after John Scalish’s death. That contact did not make Greene an inducted member of the Cleveland Mafia family. The record is better understood as a struggle involving competing authorities in a city where criminal markets overlapped legitimate labor, transportation, and waste businesses. [7]

3. Collinwood Childhood and Family Background

Greene grew up in Cleveland’s Collinwood area. Case Western Reserve identifies his parents as John Greene and Irene Fallon Greene, and Cleveland Historical reports that his mother died shortly after his birth. The latter account describes a period in institutional care and eventual upbringing by his paternal grandfather. Those details come through local historical synthesis; individual admission, guardianship, and census records were not independently collated for this edition. [1] [2]

Collinwood belonged to an industrial city connected by rail, lake shipping, manufacturing, and labor organizations. Work opportunities were shaped by the Great Lakes economy, while immigrant and working-class families maintained ties through neighborhoods and local institutions. Those conditions provide context for Greene’s later employment but cannot be presented as an inevitable route from hardship to criminal conduct. Many Cleveland residents lived under the same economic pressures without entering racketeering. [1]

Later profiles emphasize a combative adolescence and his withdrawal from high school. His distinctive public self-presentation in adulthood—an outspoken, confrontational Irish-American identity—became inseparable from the stories told about his youth. Historians should treat these recollections as aids to interpretation, not as direct proof of every schoolyard confrontation or an uninterrupted criminal career beginning in childhood. [9]

4. Cleveland Waterfront Work and Longshoremen’s Local 1317

Greene began work as a stevedore in 1957 and rapidly became president of International Longshoremen’s Association Local 1317. This was an important foothold: cargo-handling jobs, access to dock assignments, and control over union administrative machinery could confer substantial local influence. A waterfront leader exercised authority affecting ordinary workers as well as shippers, and that authority created incentives for both lawful representation and potential abuse. [1]

His early standing should not be explained only as a criminal takeover. Organized labor served real workplace functions in a hazardous sector, and rank-and-file members could have practical reasons to support a forceful official. At the same time, the existence of a legitimate union did not immunize its officers from federal financial-reporting laws. The specific criminal case brought against Greene concerned funds and records of Local 1317 and was adjudicated under the Labor-Management Reporting and Disclosure Act. [3]

In later memory, the waterfront period became the origin story for all subsequent Greene enterprises. That narrative can obscure the institutional distinction between a member-based labor union and the independently organized criminal network that later formed around Greene. Neither the union nor its general membership should be classified as a criminal conspiracy merely because a former president became a well-known racketeer. [3]

5. The 1964 Indictment and 1966 Jury Trial

A federal grand jury indicted Greene in 1964 in a case involving alleged diversion of union money and false financial reporting. At a 1966 trial he was convicted on five counts of a seven-count indictment. The Sixth Circuit’s published opinion identifies three fund-embezzlement counts—$296.46, $5,000, and $6,000—and two false-reporting counts. These are the specific charges in the appellate case, not a general judicial finding about every financial practice or entertainment expense attributed to Greene. [3]

The central appellate issue became the prosecution’s questioning about expenditures for travel, restaurants, entertainment, and other matters not charged in the indictment. The trial court eventually told jurors to disregard this evidence, but the questioning had consumed substantial time and had portrayed Greene’s personal habits in a highly unfavorable light. The appeals court concluded that the resulting prejudice was too severe to be cured by the instruction. [3]

The trial’s co-defendant, Leon Ponikvar, was acquitted, and this separate result must not be merged with Greene’s conviction. The proceedings expose how an accused public official’s reputation can dominate a jury trial: even where financial charges are properly brought, the verdict must rest on relevant admissible evidence rather than unrelated lifestyle attacks. The court addressed procedural fairness, not a universal factual judgment about Greene’s character. [3]

6. The 1968 Reversal and the 1970 Guilty Plea

On 8 August 1968 the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit reversed Greene’s conviction and remanded the case for a new trial. The appellate judges did not announce that the funds had unquestionably been used properly. Rather, they found that the prosecutor’s extended cross-examination about uncharged expenditures was irrelevant and unacceptably prejudicial. Because this error required reversal, the court declined to resolve the remaining appellate issues. [3]

A reversal for trial error is distinct from an acquittal after retrial, and it is also distinct from an appellate determination that the charged conduct never happened. The Encyclopedia of Cleveland History reports a later 1970 guilty plea involving union laws, a $10,000 fine, and the loss of his union position. The exact amended count, plea transcript, and sentencing docket were not independently collated here; the article therefore attributes those particulars to the local institutional history. [1]

These outcomes should be kept separate from later homicide proceedings. A reader examining Greene’s legal history needs a chronology of which case produced which result: jury verdict in 1966, appellate reversal in 1968, and the separately reported 1970 plea. Casual summaries that call him either simply “convicted in 1966” or “completely exonerated” erase material distinctions. [3]

7. The Cleveland Trade Solid Waste Guild

After his waterfront career suffered legal and institutional disruption, Greene entered another commercially sensitive sector: private refuse collection. The Cleveland Trade Solid Waste Guild was chartered in June 1969 and offered a common body through which private waste haulers might coordinate their interests. A trade association can serve legitimate functions, including collective advocacy, but coordinated pressure on nonmembers can also turn into coercion or exclusion. [2]

Cleveland Historical recounts accounts from collectors who felt compelled to join and records Greene’s aggressive statements about competing for customers of businesses outside the guild. The precise quotation is preserved in that local history; the important point for this article is the structure of pressure reported by the affected businesses. The historical record supports examining the guild as an instrument of market leverage without assuming that every member joined through criminal intimidation. [2]

Waste hauling involved recurring customer routes, contracts, equipment, and access to disposal facilities. An actor able to influence routes or deter competitors could obtain bargaining power extending beyond ordinary business negotiation. Greene’s transition illustrates how a person removed from one institutional office might leverage contacts, organizational skills, and reputation to operate in another sector. The move also brought him into rivalry with other ambitious operators. [1]

8. Michael Frato and the Escalation of a Business Dispute

One rival in the waste-hauling disputes was Michael Frato, described in local histories as a hauler who had parted company with Greene’s guild. The rivalry escalated from commercial competition to threats and violence. In the record preserved by Cleveland Historical and Case Western Reserve, the conflict cannot be reduced to one simple quarrel over a contract; it occurred amid fears of explosives, retaliation, and shifting loyalties. [8]

The confrontation culminated on 26 November 1971, when Frato encountered Greene during a walk or jog. Local histories describe Frato as producing or firing a weapon and Greene returning fire, killing him. Accounts vary in small details of the encounter; the essential legal outcome is much more certain: Greene stood trial and was acquitted after advancing self-defense. The acquittal should be reported accurately rather than rewritten as a homicide conviction. [1] [6]

Self-defense in a particular confrontation is a narrower legal question than the legitimacy of an entire criminal career. The verdict did not decide whether Greene’s commercial practices had been coercive or whether other unidentified violence could be attributed to him. By separating the shooting itself, Greene’s account, and the adjudicated outcome, the biography avoids turning a disputed underworld confrontation into either an unqualified act of murder or a blanket declaration of innocence. [6]

9. Arthur Sneperger and the Limits of Attribution

The death of Arthur Sneperger during an explosives incident was another flashpoint in the waste-hauling rivalry. Cleveland Historical locates the event on Halloween night in 1971 at a Coventry-area service station and describes a blast that killed Sneperger. Local accounts have linked him to Greene, and investigators reportedly suspected Greene’s involvement or possible betrayal. Those suspicions are not equivalent to a criminal conviction establishing that Greene directed Sneperger’s death. [8]

The event acquired a durable place in stories about Greene because it combined the secrecy of improvised explosive devices with the prospect of a conspirator dying in the course of an intended attack. Precisely because the narrative is dramatic, it requires care: which parts arise from police suspicions, which from firsthand witnesses, and which from reconstruction long after the episode? The accessible source record supports saying that Sneperger died in an explosion and that Greene was a person discussed in interpretations of the incident. [8]

The Sneperger episode also illustrates an analytical problem. A surviving associate’s silence or public bravado is often treated as tacit admission in popular gangster writing. Neither silence nor personality proves direction of an explosive attack. The evidence standard must remain the same whether the individual is already notorious or not. [9]

10. Shondor Birns and Cleveland’s Numbers Economy

Alex “Shondor” Birns was an influential Cleveland racketeer associated with illegal gambling, particularly the numbers business. He was a significant figure in the environment Greene entered after leaving his union presidency. Local accounts describe a period in which Greene exercised influence over numbers operations during Birns’s imprisonment, followed by a breakdown in their relationship over money, authority, and independent criminal activity. [10]

The numbers economy offered regular small-stakes income and could coexist with loan-sharking and informal enforcement. Control did not necessarily require one all-powerful gang president; it depended on collectors, financiers, customers, and enforceable understandings with rivals. Greene’s dispute with Birns was therefore a commercial as well as personal confrontation. Local histories differ in the details of a supposed debt, making any exact sum or definitive origin story less secure than the overall existence of the conflict. [8]

Birns was killed by a car bomb on 29 March 1975. Police and subsequent historians suspected Greene or his allies, but the Cleveland Police Museum explicitly notes that Greene was not arrested or charged for the murder. This publication preserves that boundary: suspicion and motive can be discussed, while the killing must not be presented as Greene’s legally proven act. [10]

11. Bomb Attacks and the Violence of 1975

Greene experienced repeated attacks on his own life. A 1975 explosion badly damaged his apartment and office on Waterloo Road in Collinwood. Cleveland Historical identifies the date as 12 May 1975 and says Greene survived with minor injuries. A short encyclopedia entry gives June for the same episode, a discrepancy most responsibly resolved by citing the more specific contemporary-photo-based chronology while noting the alternative secondary date. [2] [1]

The bombing demonstrated how organized-crime disputes could endanger neighbors, businesses, and public responders, not only their intended targets. Blast damage to a residence was an attack on a place in an ordinary neighborhood. In writing about bombings, attention often gravitates to the intended target’s survival or taunting reactions. The broader civic harm deserves equal weight, because the tactic exposed bystanders to indiscriminate physical danger. [11]

Accounts of Greene responding defiantly became a recurring element of his media persona. That portrayal has some basis in interviews and later local historical descriptions, but the rhetoric of fearlessness should not be confused with a reliable catalog of every attempted shooting or bombing. The article treats major documented incidents separately and does not accept an exact total without a defined police-case inventory. [2]

12. The Cleveland Mafia and John Scalish’s Death

Cleveland’s Italian-American Mafia network had its own longer institutional history, connected to the Mayfield Road organization and interests in gambling, labor, loans, and business. John Scalish led the family for decades. When Scalish died in May 1976, successor relationships and commercial authority became contested. A power struggle developed among men with different claims to leadership and to proceeds from Cleveland’s illegal markets. [7]

Greene was not simply a foreign invader attacking a cohesive organization from outside. He had already established regional influence, and his relationships overlapped with those of Italian-American associates, including John Nardi. The evolving conflict included local Cleveland actors, participants elsewhere in Ohio, and men who carried out work across organizational boundaries. It is analytically misleading to treat every Cleveland-Italian figure as a single uniform bloc or to infer that ethnicity determined every alliance. [4]

The death of a long-serving leader could destabilize understandings about territory and payments without bringing an immediate formal declaration of war. The 1976 succession crisis provided a setting in which Greene and his associates became perceived by rival leaders as a threat to their interests. The 1984 appellate opinion describes Greene as leader of a rival criminal organization, not as an elected or accepted boss of the Italian-American Mafia itself. [4]

13. John Nardi and the West Side Alignment

John Nardi, a labor-connected Cleveland underworld figure with ties to the local Mafia world, became an important ally of Greene. The alliance complicated public storytelling that framed the 1970s struggle as nothing more than “Irish versus Italian.” Nardi’s presence reflected a wider dispute involving specific individuals, their business relationships, and rival leadership expectations following Scalish’s death. [7]

Nardi was killed in a car bombing on 17 May 1977. A contemporary-era investigative reconstruction in Cleveland Magazine describes the blast at a parking area near his union activities. The exact attribution of every participant in Nardi’s killing is not supplied by the Greene murder appellate opinion, which chiefly adjudicates Greene’s homicide and the related enterprise. This biography does not transfer findings from one victim’s case to the other without the appropriate record. [12]

The loss of Nardi reduced Greene’s available support at a crucial point. It also demonstrated that explosives had become a tool of assassination in the broader criminal war. The temporal proximity of the two deaths has led subsequent accounts to treat them as a pair; historically, they are related events that nonetheless require separate victim-specific case records, evidence, and verdicts. [6]

14. The Conspiracy to Kill Greene

The strongest source for the specific conspiracy against Greene is the opinion in United States v. Licavoli, decided in January 1984. Summarizing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, the Sixth Circuit described James Licavoli as seeking Greene’s death by spring 1976. It named Raymond Ferritto as a man recruited for the killing and identified planning contacts with figures inside the rival enterprise. [4]

According to the appellate account, previous plans failed or were abandoned. Testimony concerned attempts to locate Greene and possible attacks at locations where other people might be present. These details are important as evidence of sustained intent and planning, but the judicial text is not a neutral eyewitness record of every private meeting: it summarizes prosecution evidence after a conviction. The proper formulation is that the jury accepted the prosecution’s case and the appellate court found the record sufficient to affirm. [4]

The court also described a separate effort to obtain inside information about Greene’s movements. Its factual summary included unlawfully intercepted communications, reports on his appointments, and personnel charged with preparing the attack. These activities demonstrate a broader conspiracy involving organizers, surveillance, logistics, and alleged killers, rather than a purely spontaneous confrontation between two adversaries. [4]

15. The 6 October 1977 Lyndhurst Assassination

On Thursday, 6 October 1977, Greene visited a dentist at Brainard Place in Lyndhurst. A vehicle had been arranged to park beside his car and conceal an explosive device. The account later accepted in federal court described Ferritto positioning the device and Ronald Carabbia using a remote control to detonate it as Greene came out of the office. Greene died in the explosion. The site is confirmed independently in Cleveland State University’s local history collections. [4] [6]

The legal narrative identifies additional participants in surveillance and planning, including Pasquale Cisternino and others in the rival organization. It distinguishes the planning of the homicide, the actual placement and detonation of the bomb, and the wider racketeering conspiracy. The distinction matters because shared involvement in a criminal enterprise does not make every defendant the person who physically carried out the detonation. [4]

Historical photographs of the aftermath survive in the Cleveland Memory Project and are reproduced or described by local historical institutions. No third-party photograph is embedded in this page because identification of an image source is not, by itself, proof that republication rights have been cleared. The contemporary scene is nevertheless an essential part of the evidentiary trail for the death date, location, and public danger of the attack. [6]

16. Investigation, Cooperating Witnesses, and the Criminal Network

The murder investigation implicated a network rather than a solitary assassin. Raymond Ferritto later cooperated, and prosecutors developed evidence connecting people involved in execution of the attack to higher levels of Cleveland organized crime. The record includes testimony about meetings, planned payments, surveillance, equipment, and the roles of accused participants. These records make Greene’s assassination one of the more legally reconstructible criminal episodes in the city’s late twentieth-century history. [4]

Cooperating-witness testimony requires evaluation, not reflexive acceptance or rejection. A participant may have first-hand knowledge and simultaneously possess a motive to minimize personal responsibility or obtain favorable treatment. The strongest historical account reads such testimony together with judicial findings and available corroboration. The Sixth Circuit decision confirms a set of convictions, but a scholarly biography should still identify which details are drawn from prosecution evidence rather than independently verified physical exhibits. [4]

The prosecution illuminated relationships beyond Greene’s murder, including efforts to obtain confidential FBI information. The appellate opinion describes a separate bribery matter connected with documents from the Cleveland FBI office. Those acts were relevant to the organization’s broader operation and the resulting RICO case; they should not be misdescribed as actions Greene himself carried out or approved after his death. [4]

17. State Murder Convictions and Acquittals

The Ohio homicide proceedings produced different outcomes for different defendants. The Sixth Circuit later recorded that Pasquale Cisternino, Ronald Carabbia, and Kenneth Ciarcia were convicted in state court for Greene’s murder. A Cleveland State University historical overview separately describes acquittals in the state proceedings, including for James Licavoli and Angelo Lonardo. This does not erase the state convictions obtained against the others. [4] [6]

The distinction between individual liability and association is critical. A person could be investigated or charged and then acquitted on one allegation while later facing a different federal charge under a distinct statute and evidentiary theory. Likewise, a state murder verdict and a federal RICO verdict should not be combined into a fictitious single judgment. [4]

A careful biography therefore identifies the court, legal theory, defendant, and disposition whenever it refers to Greene’s murder. The shorthand that “the Mafia was convicted of murder” collapses individual adjudications into an ethnic or institutional abstraction and makes it impossible for readers to determine exactly what a court decided. [6]

18. The 1982 RICO Trial and 1984 Federal Appeal

In 1982, a federal jury convicted six defendants—James Licavoli, Anthony Liberatore, John Calandra, Pasquale Cisternino, Ronald Carabbia, and Kenneth Ciarcia—on RICO charges. The government’s theory involved participation in an enterprise through a pattern of racketeering activity, including acts surrounding the plot against Greene and, for certain defendants, bribery. The law examines a criminal enterprise and connected acts rather than a single isolated murder alone. [4]

On 9 January 1984 the Sixth Circuit affirmed the federal convictions. The appellate court’s opinion explains why conspiracy to murder could qualify as a racketeering predicate under the statute and considers other objections raised on appeal. This was a significant judicial outcome: its importance lay both in establishing individual federal responsibility and in demonstrating how coordinated violence could be prosecuted through an enterprise-based statutory framework. [4]

The cases did not prove that the death of one adversary suddenly dissolved every organized-crime operation in Cleveland. The convictions substantially weakened figures in the local Mafia network and helped change law-enforcement understanding of its activities. The longer institutional history, however, included later investigations, informant cooperation, and continued illegal markets. [7]

19. Organization, Revenue, and Methods

The available record characterizes Greene as a leader of an independent criminal enterprise with connections in labor, waste collection, gambling, and coercive enforcement. These spheres should not be mistaken for a single perfectly integrated corporation. Some activity occurred through lawful organizations, some through personal dealings, and some through illicit markets involving multiple associates. Court findings and local histories permit broad classification while leaving the exact membership roll and day-to-day chains of command incomplete. [1] [4]

Violence was an instrument of competition but not the whole economy. Influence over workers, routes, credit, and revenue opportunities could be as important as the ability to intimidate a rival. Understanding these financial and organizational mechanisms is more useful than reducing Greene to a string of dramatic bombings. Those bombings were outcomes of contests over money and power; they were not a substitute for a coherent criminal-business strategy. [7]

The term “Celtic Club” appears in popular accounts of Greene’s associates, but it does not establish a formally chartered national crime family. It is safer to treat it as a reported label or association within Cleveland’s underworld, not as the legal equivalent of the Cleveland Cosa Nostra. No membership status should be attributed to a named person solely because that person was a neighbor, labor colleague, or fellow Irish-American. [9]

20. Irish Identity, Public Image, and Community Memory

Greene actively cultivated an Irish-American image, and later retellings made that image central to his identity. His nickname “The Irishman” was a personal and media designation. The historical fact of cultural self-presentation is distinct from the false idea that all Irish-Americans in Cleveland constituted his constituency, or that community heritage provides a defense for his criminal activities. [9]

His reputation mixed defiance, generosity anecdotes, violent conduct, and public notoriety. Some residents remembered him favorably while rivals and investigators saw an aggressive racketeer. Neither perspective supplies a full moral accounting. Good historical interpretation includes the intimidation of businesses, the cost of bombings to the wider public, and the specific workers or residents affected by legal and illegal enterprises. [2]

The use of an ethnic hero narrative can obscure the plural membership of criminal networks and glamorize organized violence as a matter of pride. This article treats Greene as a documented Cleveland actor whose identity mattered to his symbolism, not as a representative of a whole population. In the encyclopedia’s cross-references, “Irish-American” functions as a historically bounded classification of leadership association rather than a blanket claim of ethnic criminality. [1]

21. Books, Film, and the Construction of a Gangster Legend

Rick Porrello’s To Kill the Irishman: The War That Crippled the Mafia, first published in 1998 and issued in later editions, helped establish the contemporary narrative of Greene’s rise, defiance, and assassination. It remains a major book-length popular history, useful for identifying episodes and interpretations for further source checking. The present article cites the publisher and catalog records rather than claiming a complete page-by-page comparison with the book’s archival notes. [13]

The 2011 feature film Kill the Irishman, directed by Jonathan Hensleigh and starring Ray Stevenson as Greene, brought the story to a wider audience. The American Film Institute catalog identifies the picture and its literary source. A dramatization can preserve recognizable names and event sequences while inventing dialogue, combining characters, or compressing time. The movie is evidence of later cultural memory, not an independent primary source for 1970s criminal acts. [14]

These works also suggest why Greene’s death is often narrated as a kind of paradoxical victory. The investigation following his murder damaged those who ordered or facilitated it. Yet it would be analytically unsound to say Greene planned his own death as a law-enforcement strategy or guaranteed the collapse of a powerful criminal family. Retrospective irony should not be mistaken for intent or historical inevitability. [7]

22. Historical Interpretation and Evidentiary Limits

Greene’s career highlights the overlap between urban work institutions, commercial competition, and racketeering in a postwar industrial city. It also shows how violence can arise when informal criminal agreements break down after a leadership transition. The 1976 death of John Scalish, Greene’s alliance with John Nardi, and the continuing role of gambling revenue help explain the pressures behind the conflict without excusing them. [7]

The documented outcomes are strong in several areas: Greene was a longshoremen’s union president; the 1968 federal reversal is reported in a published judicial opinion; he was acquitted in the Frato shooting; he died on 6 October 1977 in a bombing; and federal racketeering convictions related to his killing were affirmed in 1984. More uncertain elements include the precise birth date, the details of some alleged bomb plots, assertions that he personally arranged Birns’s death, and the membership and operational scope of every organization attributed to him. [3] [4]

The resulting biography should neither romanticize a “warrior” nor present every rumor as established fact. Its purpose is to situate Greene within institutions and conflicts that can be studied through court documents, local historical collections, and later scholarly or journalistic reconstructions. Additional archival work—especially vital records, full Ohio trial transcripts, union files, and contemporary police case materials—could sharpen the narrative further. [6]

Sources and Further Reading

Source method: Published federal appellate decisions carry high authority for procedural outcomes and the scope of convictions; their factual summaries reflect evidence accepted under particular standards. Cleveland State University and Case Western Reserve provide local institutional history and archival provenance. Popular histories and film catalogs are sources for historiography and cultural reception, not substitutes for court records.

  1. Case Western Reserve University, Encyclopedia of Cleveland History, “Greene, Daniel J.” Local institutional biographical summary of family, union career, union legal outcomes, Frato shooting, and death; birth date differs from Cleveland Historical.
  2. Morgan Choffin, “Danny Greene: ‘The Irishman,’” Cleveland Historical (2012, revised). Local history using Cleveland State University Memory Project photographs and reporting, including waterfront, waste guild, and 1975 bombing.
  3. United States v. Greene, 400 F.2d 847 (6th Cir. 1968). Published appellate decision reversing 1966 union-fund and reporting convictions for prejudicial cross-examination; primary judicial opinion.
  4. United States v. Licavoli, 725 F.2d 1040 (6th Cir. 1984). Published appellate decision affirming six RICO convictions; summarizes prosecution evidence and separates Ohio state murder convictions from federal outcomes.
  5. Case Western Reserve University, Encyclopedia of Cleveland History, “Mayfield Rd. Mob.” Institutional history of Cleveland Mafia background, Scalish succession, Greene and Nardi murders, Licavoli, and later prosecution.
  6. Cleveland State University, Cleveland Memory Project, “Crime Scene, Cleveland” — Danny Greene section. University special-collections historical summary for the Lyndhurst murder, archival photographs, state verdict distinctions, and later RICO trial.
  7. Thomas M. Burnett, “Cleveland’s Reluctant ‘Godfather,’” United Press International (8 July 1982). Contemporary reporting on the 1976 Cleveland Mafia succession after Scalish and the context of Licavoli’s prosecution; investigative reporting is distinguished from court findings.
  8. Michael Rotman, “Danny Greene Bombs Coventry?,” Cleveland Historical (2012, revised). Local historical reconstruction of the Frato/Sneperger episode and Greene’s rivalry with Shondor Birns; allegations distinguished from adjudications.
  9. Rick Porrello, To Kill the Irishman (1998; later editions), bibliographic record. Major book-length popular history; publishing record verified, but this edition has not been page-collated against its primary-source notes.
  10. Cleveland Police Museum, “Shondor Birns: A Look Into the Mobster’s Life and Gruesome Death.” Local historical article recording Birns’s death and specifically noting Greene was never charged for it; source is historical synthesis, not trial verdict.
  11. Henry Palattella, “1975: An Early Morning Bomb Nearly Takes Danny Greene’s Life,” Cleveland Magazine (2021). Retrospective historical account locating the Waterloo Road bombing on 12 May 1975 and discussing its aftermath.
  12. “The Life and Hard Times of Cleveland’s Mafia,” Cleveland Magazine (1978). Extended contemporary-era investigative reporting on mob politics and John Nardi’s 17 May 1977 killing; distinguishes reported narrative from legal findings.
  13. Rick Porrello, Kill the Irishman (Pocket Books, 2011), publisher record. Publisher confirmation for later edition and author; promotional content is not primary evidence of specific crimes.
  14. American Film Institute, Kill the Irishman (2011), AFI Catalog entry. Confirms film adaptation, cast, director, and Porrello’s source book; film is a dramatization.
