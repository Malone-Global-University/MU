Hogan Gang Biographies

Edward “Jelly Roll” Hogan, St. Louis's Prohibition underworld, political influence, and the violent rivalry with Egan's Rats.

Collection Overview

The Hogan Gang was a St. Louis criminal organization associated with Edward J. “Jelly Roll” Hogan Jr., a saloon operator, Democratic politician, and figure in the city's Prohibition-era illicit liquor economy. Accounts of the organization describe an Irish-American-led but multiethnic network operating in a city where neighborhood gangs, saloons, bootlegging enterprises, and electoral politics could intersect. The gang should be understood as a distinct St. Louis organization—not as another name for its rival, Egan's Rats.

The Hogan organization is especially remembered for its feud with Egan's Rats during the early 1920s. The conflict escalated after the October 31, 1921 killing of William “Willie” Egan. Later accounts implicated individuals associated with the Hogan faction, but specific allegations about who ordered or carried out the killing must be attributed rather than presented as adjudicated fact. Retaliatory shootings, the involvement of bystanders, and efforts to negotiate a truce made the conflict a significant episode in St. Louis criminal and civic history.

This MGU directory presently contains one selected canonical biography: Edward Hogan. Other people named below supply historical context and research leads; their mention does not mean that MGU has completed a separate biography for them or that every person connected with the gang was a formal member.

Research Themes

  • The development of the St. Louis Hogan organization and its relationship to neighborhood saloons and patronage networks.
  • Edward Hogan's simultaneous historical roles as a political officeholder, liquor-industry figure, and leader associated with a criminal organization.
  • The Prohibition economy, competition for illicit distribution, and the hazards of attributing unproved incidents to specific individuals.
  • The Egan–Hogan conflict of 1921–1923 and the effects of shootings on St. Louis residents and civic institutions.
  • Attempts at mediation and the limits of press accounts and retrospective narratives as evidence of a durable truce.
  • Hogan's later electoral career, which is documented independently in the Missouri Secretary of State's historical legislator register.
  • The difference between the St. Louis Hogan Gang and the separate Danny Hogan Organization of St. Paul, Minnesota.

Principal Biography

  1. Edward J. “Jelly Roll” Hogan Jr.

    Historical classification: Namesake leader of the St. Louis Hogan organization; Missouri legislator. Born in 1885, Hogan became a prominent political and underworld figure during the period surrounding national Prohibition. Historical studies place his organization's activities within the city's illegal liquor market and its rivalry with Egan's Rats. His political career is independently documented: Missouri's official historical register lists service in the state House in 1916 and 1934–1940 and the state Senate in 1944–1956 (as tabulated by the register). The officeholding record does not by itself establish liability for any alleged criminal offense.

    Hogan's biography should address his saloon and liquor connections, his relations with competing St. Louis networks, and the distinction between contemporary allegations, later historical reconstructions, and official legislative records. His nickname is commonly rendered “Jelly Roll” or “Jellyroll”; both forms refer to the same individual.

Historical Context and the Egan–Hogan Feud

St. Louis organized crime: The early twentieth-century city contained overlapping neighborhood, saloon, political, and criminal networks. The Hogan organization competed with Egan's Rats for influence and illicit markets. These organizations were historically associated with Irish-American leadership, but individual membership cannot be inferred from ethnicity or neighborhood residence.

Prohibition and political influence: Regional historical reporting describes Hogan's saloon operations, a state beverage-inspection post, and the growth of illicit liquor activity under Prohibition. The precise scope of official protection or corruption must be assessed case by case; holding public office or a regulatory role does not, on its own, prove a particular criminal act.

Escalation after Willie Egan's death: Willie Egan was killed on October 31, 1921. Sources describe accusations against men connected to the Hogan side and retaliatory violence between factions. William “Dinty” Colbeck emerged as an important Egan's Rats figure during the conflict. The historic feud included attacks on residences and public streets and threatened uninvolved residents.

Renewed violence in 1923: The killing of lawyer Jacob Mackler in February 1923 and additional shootings formed part of the feud's escalation as described in regional historical reconstructions. Mackler should be identified as an attorney associated with the Hogan side, not automatically classified as a gangster. Responsibility for individual incidents requires contemporaneous and legal-source verification.

Mediation and cessation: Accounts credit Monsignor Timothy Dempsey, police representatives, and local journalism with efforts toward a truce. The historical record also contains competing descriptions of how effective mediation was. The end of open warfare should not be equated with the end of illicit business or a complete organizational merger.

Later political career: Hogan reentered the legislature after the gang-war period and later served in the Missouri Senate. The Missouri Secretary of State's historical legislator register is the appropriate institutional starting point for his legislative service and should be distinguished from sources addressing allegations of criminal conduct.

Associated and Rival Figures

People appearing in historical accounts of the conflict include James Hogan, Luke Kennedy, John Doyle, Max Greenberg, and Jacob Mackler. Sources differ in the precision of their affiliations and in their accounts of specific incidents. Greenberg is often described as changing sides during the dispute; Mackler was a lawyer associated with the Hogan faction. Their inclusion here is not a validated membership list and does not create additional biography links.

For the opposing organization, see Egan's Rats biographies, including Thomas “Snake” Kinney, Willie Egan, and William “Dinty” Colbeck. These figures are rivals or opposing leaders in this directory, not Hogan Gang members.

Editorial Standards

Collection scope: One selected linked biography in the MGU filing plan. This index is not an exhaustive organizational roster.

  • Identity: Edward J. “Jelly Roll” Hogan Jr. of St. Louis must not be conflated with Daniel “Danny” Hogan of St. Paul, Minnesota.
  • Membership: Distinguish leader, member, associate, lawyer, political ally, and rival; do not equate a person's appearance in a narrative with confirmed gang membership.
  • Allegations and outcomes: Attribute accusations concerning the killing of Willie Egan and later shootings; do not turn allegations into convictions or judicial findings.
  • Political record: Use the official Missouri legislator register to establish officeholding; use separate documentary evidence for criminal allegations or conduct.
  • Historical chronology: Differentiate the organization's origins, 1921–1923 violence, attempted truces, and Hogan's later career.
  • Source hierarchy: Prefer contemporaneous newspapers, court and legislative records, and documented historical research; clearly label secondary reconstructions.
  • Ethnic precision: “Irish-American” describes an organizational context rather than the ancestry of every participant.
  • Canonical filing: Retain one biography per person; connect rival and cross-city subjects through links rather than duplicated articles.
  • Historical purpose: Present the consequences of organized crime and corruption without romanticizing gang warfare.

Research Starting Points

The following sources support the directory's historical outline or provide research pathways. Each full biography should maintain its own source register and verify disputed incident-level claims against original records.

  • Missouri Secretary of State — Historical Missouri State Legislators: H — official listing for Edward J. Hogan Jr.'s legislative offices and recorded years of service.
  • St. Louis County Arts & History — “Jelly Roll Hogan: Eureka's Most Notorious Gangster” (2017) — regional historical essay on Hogan, his political connections, the Egan conflict, and efforts at mediation; individual allegations require corroboration.
  • St. Louis Magazine — “Of Cabbages and Kings” — context for the city's Irish-American neighborhoods and earlier street-gang history; not a direct membership roster of the Prohibition-era Hogan organization.
  • Timothy Reynolds — “Gangs of St. Louis: The Hogan Gang” (2023) — secondary overview of the organization and its rivalry with Egan's Rats; compare claims with archival records.
  • Daniel Waugh, Egan's Rats: The Untold Story of the Prohibition-Era Gang That Ruled St. Louis (2007) — specialist account of the rival organization and St. Louis gang politics; consult the full text and its citations for incident-level research.
