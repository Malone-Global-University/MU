Earl “Hymie” Weiss (c. 1898–1926)

Polish-American leader of Chicago’s North Side Gang, associate of Dean O’Banion, rival of the Torrio–Capone organization, and victim of the October 1926 State Street attack.

1. Historical Overview

Earl J. “Hymie” Weiss was one of the central leaders of Chicago’s North Side Gang during the violent middle years of Prohibition. He rose beside Dean O’Banion and, after O’Banion was murdered in November 1924, became a principal organizer of the group’s response to Johnny Torrio and Al Capone. Weiss’s short period of prominence ended on October 11, 1926, when gunfire struck him and Patrick Murray near Schofield’s Flower Shop, across from Holy Name Cathedral. His career exposes the economic and political consequences of an illegal alcohol market in which control of distribution was protected by personal alliances, intimidation and increasingly public violence. [1] [4] [5]

The term “Irish-American crime syndicates,” under which this university indexes Weiss, identifies the history of the North Side organization and the neighborhood networks from which it developed. It is not a declaration that Weiss himself was Irish. Accounts consistently describe him as Polish-American and Catholic. Nor was the North Side Gang a formal branch of the Italian-American Mafia; it was an independent, multiethnic rival that made occasional arrangements with some of the same actors it fought at other times. [5] [7] [8]

A source-critical biography must do more than repeat that Weiss was “the man Capone feared.” The phrase is memorable, but it compresses several years of criminal economics into a personality contest. The strongest evidence concerns recorded deaths, contemporary places and people, and broad organizational relationships. Details about the precise circumstances of Weiss’s birth, his involvement in individual murders, the authorship of attempted assassinations, and the identity of his own killers require additional degrees of caution. [1] [6] [8]

2. Names, Birthplace, and Biographical Identification

The name most firmly attached to contemporary police reporting is Earl J. Weiss, often accompanied by the nickname “Hymie.” Northwestern University’s transcription of the Chicago Police homicide ledger records “Weiss, Earl J.” Michigan State University’s archive of a later Chicago Tribune image uses the same public name. Other biographical histories give a Polish family surname, Wojciechowski, and variants such as Henry Earl J. Wojciechowski or Earl Wojciechowski. These versions should be indexed together as naming traditions rather than transformed into independently certified birth-register facts. [1] [8] [9] [10]

Most modern reference works assign 1898 as his birth year, commonly January 25. They disagree about where he was born: an EBSCO research biography identifies Chicago, while some other accounts place his birth in Poland. The police-ledger entry for October 1926 calls him thirty-five years old, incompatible with the conventional age of twenty-eight. A homicide ledger is a valuable contemporary document, but a clerk’s age field is not automatically superior to a verified birth or baptism record, which has not been examined for this article. The page therefore uses circa 1898 with a disclosure of the conflict rather than pretending that it has been settled. [1] [8] [9]

The nickname “Hymie” and the family surname “Weiss” have led some modern readers to assume Jewish ancestry. The institutional biographies available for this article instead describe a Catholic Polish-American household. The distinction is not peripheral: ethnic misclassification alters the meaning of the collection, the history of Chicago’s immigrant neighborhoods, and the way criminal affiliation was reported. These biographies document an underworld network; they do not equate a person’s ethnicity or religious practice with criminality. [8] [9]

3. North Side Chicago and the Neighborhood Setting

Weiss came of age amid Chicago’s rapidly changing neighborhoods, where immigration, industrial employment, the press, saloons, local ward organization and street violence overlapped. Dean O’Banion’s early associations are linked by the Mob Museum to the largely Irish-American district called Kilgubbin, near Goose Island. Weiss belonged to the same broader social and criminal world without sharing every participant’s ancestry. The neighborhood served as a point of recruitment and mutual acquaintance, not the permanent border of a later bootlegging enterprise. [5]

The North Side’s criminal economy cannot be understood solely through rival gangsters. Commercial establishments needed liquor deliveries despite federal restrictions; trucks needed routes; customers and bar owners required suppliers; stolen loads could be resold; policemen and local politicians could obstruct enforcement or, in some cases, be corrupted. The legal ban created a high-premium market in which operating an illegal business depended on both logistics and violent protection. Different crews bargained, competed, and occasionally collaborated to gain access to those margins. [7]

North Side leadership operated within a citywide framework that was never neatly divided into an Irish North Side and an Italian South Side. O’Banion’s group included men of different backgrounds, while Torrio’s organization also worked through varied intermediaries and counterparties. Territory labels are useful reference points; they should not be mistaken for exclusive ownership of whole communities, and they provide no justification for assigning criminal activity to the ethnic populations among whom a gang happened to recruit. [5] [7]

4. Early Criminal Career and the Problems of Folklore

Secondary accounts place Weiss among the young associates who performed thefts and other criminal acts before Prohibition expanded the market for illicit alcohol. The popular “Perfume Burglar” anecdote, in which a botched theft reportedly spilled perfume or fragrance, appears in later narratives. It is best treated as an underworld nickname story until a matching dated arrest report or court record is established; its appearance in multiple secondary works does not turn it into independently corroborated evidence. [8] [9]

One of the most frequently repeated stories concerns Stephen Wisniewski, alleged to have hijacked a North Side liquor shipment and then disappeared after a ride with Weiss. Later writers use the case to explain the phrase “one-way ride” and sometimes call it the first example of that criminal method. Without a contemporaneous homicide file, victim identification, or adjudication tying Weiss to the disappearance, the alleged incident should not be presented as a proven murder or a verified invention of criminal vocabulary. [8]

These tales are revealing because they attribute to Weiss two qualities that later commentators repeatedly emphasize: discipline in illicit commerce and a willingness to employ severe violence. They also illustrate the danger of source circularity. An assertion repeated in a succession of gangster books can appear widely attested even when each account ultimately descends from one unnamed informant or sensational newspaper story. This article retains the contextual significance of the accounts while separating them from court-established outcomes.

5. Dean O’Banion, Vincent Drucci, and Bugs Moran

The circle from which the North Side Gang emerged is repeatedly associated with Dean O’Banion, Weiss, Vincent “The Schemer” Drucci, and George “Bugs” Moran. Its leadership was not a succession of isolated men who could simply inherit a corporate office. Those figures were partners and competitors in an illicit market; their importance depended on access to customers, employees, transport, violence and political relationships. O’Banion’s experience and local notoriety gave the group a recognizable public face before Weiss became its principal figure. [5] [8]

O’Banion’s place in Chicago press history and the North Side’s early gang culture helped link relatively small-scale street offending to a much larger liquor business after 1920. Weiss operated in the same expanding environment, but surviving public sources do not permit an exact day-by-day description of his assignments as an apprentice, lieutenant or equal partner. Popular biographies often speak as if gang positions were registered offices. In practice, an organization’s internal rank must be reconstructed from fragmentary, biased testimony and the changing distribution of economic influence. [5] [7]

The ethnic composition of this circle underscores the editorial classification question. O’Banion and Moran are commonly associated with Irish-American Chicago; Drucci and Weiss did not share that identical background. Their collective enterprise was defined by common criminal interests, not a uniform ancestry. Later analysis of the group is distorted when it is described as a national “Irish Mafia” whose members owed obedience to one shared ethnic hierarchy. [5] [7]

6. Prohibition, Bootlegging, and Revenue

National Prohibition began in January 1920, with implementation under the Volstead Act. Its restrictions made illicit alcohol distribution an exceptionally attractive enterprise to groups able to move supplies and maintain steady customers. Some producers and wholesalers operated through organized networks; others exploited stolen shipments, corrupt protection or temporary political arrangements. Weiss’s rise belongs to this structural transformation, not merely to a sequence of personal quarrels. Chicago’s breweries, transport systems, taverns and wealthy districts all shaped the division of criminal opportunities. [7]

The Mob Museum describes the North Side group as a rival to the Torrio organization while also noting the earlier understandings through which O’Banion retained North Side and Gold Coast interests. Such arrangements were unstable because contracts among competing criminal organizations lacked lawful mechanisms of enforcement. If a territory produced substantial revenue, an opposing crew had an incentive to intrude, hijack trucks, cultivate customers or enlist politicians. Each response risked launching another round of killings. [5] [7]

The North Siders’ prosperity has sometimes been translated into exact figures for Weiss’s personal wealth without adequate accounting evidence. Revenue of a large illegal enterprise was not identical to one person’s owned assets; cash circulated through partners, debts, enforcement costs, nominal businesses and informal claims. Even a probate estate could omit concealed funds. Claims of multimillion-dollar personal fortunes or extraordinarily precise racket proceeds therefore require a documented accounting method and should not be adopted merely because they are familiar in popular histories. [8]

7. Schofield’s Flower Shop and the Geography of Power

Schofield’s Flower Shop on North State Street became one of the most recognizable locations in Chicago gangland history. O’Banion held an interest in the business, and his death occurred inside it in November 1924. The shop was more than a convenient dramatic backdrop: its position in a busy commercial district reflected the way individuals associated with illegal enterprises could also take part in legitimate retail trade. Contemporary police and later local histories placed Weiss’s group near the same address when he was killed in 1926. [3] [4] [5] [11]

Modern recollections sometimes describe the shop itself as a front for a fully documented headquarters, while other accounts distinguish the storefront from rooms used to meet, plan and store materials. These are not necessarily contradictory, but they should not be treated as equivalent evidence. A shop address does not establish exactly what transactions occurred within every room. More precise claims must be supported by contemporary police reports, title documents, lease records or testimony, not merely by the site’s fame. [11]

The location’s proximity to Holy Name Cathedral gave it unusual symbolic force. The 1926 shooting damaged the area around the cathedral cornerstone, leading later writers to present a vivid contrast between sacred space and organized-crime violence. For the historian, the more significant relationship is geographic: the killing took place in a public urban intersection, with a line of fire crossing streets used by ordinary residents. The danger was not confined to gang participants. [1] [3] [4]

8. Failed Arrangements with the Torrio Organization

During the first years of Prohibition, rival Chicago operators attempted to allocate market shares and settle business disagreements without constant warfare. Johnny Torrio was particularly associated with such negotiations. O’Banion’s North Side interests initially benefited from arrangements with the South Side leadership, but disputes over gambling, breweries, deliveries and factional relations weakened them. Historians differ about which dispute supplied the immediate trigger for O’Banion’s killing; most reconstruct a process of cumulative mistrust rather than one simple cause. [5] [7]

An important setting was the relationship among O’Banion, Torrio, Capone and the Genna brothers. The Gennas were an additional West Side force whose liquor and political interests could clash with O’Banion’s suppliers and markets. These relationships demonstrate that Chicago’s underworld did not consist of just two organizations. The eventual feud was embedded in a more complicated field of competitors, suppliers and temporary allies. [5]

Weiss’s particular role in the negotiations before November 1924 is harder to isolate than O’Banion’s. The later depiction of Weiss as a man who would never compromise may have been shaped by the spectacular violence after O’Banion’s death. Accounts of personality are useful clues, but a coherent causal explanation requires examining when an agreement was made, what each faction expected to gain, and what event made it unenforceable. [5] [8]

9. 10 November 1924: O’Banion’s Murder

On November 10, 1924, Dean O’Banion was shot to death at Schofield’s Flower Shop. The killing marked a decisive change in the North Side group’s position. The Mob Museum recounts a meeting in which the New York gangster Frankie Yale and two armed men approached O’Banion, and the account situates the murder within rivalry among the O’Banion, Torrio and Genna interests. The general event and location are widely established; specific criminal assignments were not all proved in a court proceeding. [4] [5]

Weiss subsequently emerged as a dominant North Side figure. This did not mean that all decisions or criminal earnings instantly passed to him alone. Drucci, Moran and other experienced associates remained significant, and leadership worked through a combination of mutual dependence and coercive reputation. A person might be called the “boss” by newspapers even as important choices were made through discussions among several senior operators. [6] [8]

The murder also changed the group’s incentives. As long as different gangs could divide profits, some compromise remained possible. After a prominent leader was killed, retaliation became simultaneously a personal obligation, an assertion of organizational capacity and a warning to rivals. That logic helped transform negotiable commercial disputes into a cycle of shootings. It did not produce a simple or automatic chain from O’Banion to every later casualty. [5] [7]

10. January 1925: The Attack on Johnny Torrio

The struggle escalated in January 1925. The Mob Museum’s chronology places a North Side attack on Capone’s automobile on January 12 and an attempt on Johnny Torrio’s life on January 24. Weiss, Drucci and Moran are identified in later historical accounts as men involved in the second attack. Torrio survived serious injuries and subsequently withdrew from active command, allowing Capone to consolidate control of the organization known as the Outfit. [6]

The events are important for the contrast they reveal between intended and actual results. The assassination attempt did not eliminate Torrio, but his retirement changed the opposing organization’s leadership and could reasonably encourage North Side participants to believe that violence altered the balance of power. It also increased the political and economic role of Capone, who was already capable of mobilizing substantial resources. The outcome was therefore not an undisputed North Side victory, even though Torrio was no longer personally directing the old arrangements. [6] [7]

The involvement of Weiss in planning or authorizing specific shots should be described as the historical reconstruction of the feud, rather than the equivalent of a judicially established individual conviction. The distinction matters because gangster histories frequently collapse group responsibility, identification of gunmen and motive into a single statement. The record supports a violent North Side–Torrio conflict; it does not, by itself, prove which person discharged every weapon or issued each order. [6]

11. 1925: A Fragile North Side Command

After the attempted killing of Torrio, the North Side organization continued to compete for Chicago’s alcohol markets. This was not a period of unbroken territorial conquest. The Genna faction suffered lethal internal and external pressure; Capone’s expanding enterprise competed across neighborhood lines; other gang leaders managed independent operations. The ability of Weiss’s associates to survive depended on customers, alliances and access to weapons or skilled personnel, not on the possession of an official citywide charter. [5] [7]

Weiss’s public reputation for determined resistance distinguished him from leaders who sought compromise. Yet the premise that his decisions can be explained entirely by an unforgiving temperament should be treated cautiously. Escalating competition affected the price of cooperation: a leader who was seen as weak after the killing of a close associate could invite further attacks and defections. Personal loyalty and reputation thus interacted with an economic need to deter rivals. [8]

Records of gang warfare are inherently uneven. A shooting known from a newspaper account may be documented without a proven association to one gang; an alleged witness may have wished to discredit someone; a later memoir may project the reputation of a famous boss backward onto events in which his personal role was minor. This section therefore avoids attaching every unsolved murder of the period to Weiss, even when that attribution is traditional in popular summaries.

12. The Wider Network of North Side Alliances

The North Siders did not face Capone as an isolated, perfectly unified force. Other independent Chicago groups had their own rivalries with the Outfit and distinct interests in alcohol and gambling. Reports of possible understandings with Joe Saltis and additional operators reflect this environment. A common enemy could produce cooperation in one dispute without creating durable subordination or a formally integrated coalition. [9]

For criminal enterprises, alliances served several purposes. They could reduce the cost of obtaining liquor, permit use of safe delivery routes, pool knowledge of enforcement threats, or create bargaining strength against a powerful competitor. However, these benefits had to be weighed against the risk that associates would inform, defect or act without authorization. When personal protection was uncertain, even a temporary ally could become a source of danger. [7]

A careful account should distinguish an alleged meeting or rumored agreement from an attested organizational merger. The fact that Weiss attended a court proceeding involving another criminal figure, for example, may indicate an association; it does not prove a specific conspiracy to influence the trial. These limits are especially important in the last weeks of his life, when a number of accounts rely heavily on police speculation and retrospective assertions. [1] [9]

13. 20 September 1926: The Hawthorne Hotel Attack

On September 20, 1926, gunfire struck the vicinity of Capone’s Hawthorne Hotel headquarters in Cicero. Historical timelines attribute the attack to North Side assailants and associate the initiative with Weiss, Drucci and Moran. Capone survived. The event became a centerpiece of accounts portraying the feud as an escalating contest in which automatic weapons threatened not only adversaries but everyone near the target. [6] [9]

Several later narratives offer precise counts of cars, guns and rounds fired. Such figures should be attributed to the individual reporting tradition rather than stated without qualification. The central historically useful fact is that the attack demonstrated the North Side’s capacity to strike a conspicuous location associated with Capone while also intensifying the pressure for retaliation or negotiation. The attempt did not remove Capone or resolve the underlying fight for the alcohol market. [6]

The term “Hawthorne Hotel attack” refers to the event at Capone’s Cicero base, not to the killing of Weiss a few weeks later on North State Street in Chicago. Keeping the incidents geographically and chronologically separate helps avoid errors frequently introduced by condensed gangland summaries. It also clarifies how quickly the situation moved from attempted assassination to public proposals for an accommodation and then to another major murder. [6] [9]

14. The October 1926 Peace-Conference Accounts

A Chicago Crime Commission retrospective describes a peace meeting on October 4, 1926, at which Weiss allegedly demanded the deaths of men associated with O’Banion’s murder. According to that account, Capone rejected the demand and the negotiations failed. This is a specific later interpretation of a proposed truce; the details of the conversation should not be presented as a verbatim transcript independently verified by this article. [9]

Even if the meeting took place substantially as described, it would not establish that the participants were engaged in a straightforward effort to end all organized crime. Gangland “peace” generally meant a potential division of profitable activities, a cessation of retaliatory attacks or an agreement over who might operate in contested territory. Such arrangements might spare lives, but they did not necessarily remove coercion, extortion or corruption from the affected neighborhoods. [7] [9]

The failed-negotiation story also illustrates the limitations of personality-driven explanation. A demand impossible for an opponent to accept could be an ultimatum, a genuine expression of anger or a bargaining tactic. Unless a reliable contemporary witness recorded what was said and why, historians cannot know which interpretation Weiss intended. It remains significant as part of the remembered breakdown of relations immediately before his death. [9]

15. 11 October 1926: The Final Afternoon

Weiss died on Monday, October 11, 1926, at approximately four o’clock in the afternoon, in the State and Superior Street area. The Chicago Police homicide ledger, preserved in the Northwestern University database, records that he and Patrick Murray were walking with attorney W. W. O’Brien when unknown persons fired from an upstairs window. The same case record identifies a location in front of 742 North State Street; an FBI-reproduced photograph labels a firing window at 740 North State and marks the nearby Schofield shop. [1] [2] [3]

Contemporary reconstructions name several other people in or near the group. The homicide entry identifies O’Brien as wounded and also records the wounding and later coroner scrutiny of Samuel Peller and Benjamin Jacobs. The presence of a lawyer and associates appears in the contemporary case record, but the precise position of each person at the instant of fire, and which account of the first seconds should be preferred, cannot be settled from a brief ledger transcription alone. [1] [2]

Weiss and Murray were killed; the other three named individuals survived their injuries. Public histories emphasize the spray of gunfire near Holy Name Cathedral and the damage to its stonework. The records support the broad geography and the deadly, public character of the attack. Specific descriptions of who ordered which weapon fired, the precise number of shots, and whether Weiss was pronounced dead at the scene or while being transported should be evaluated against the original hospital, coroner and newspaper reports before being treated as final. [1] [3] [4]

16. Patrick Murray and the Other Victims

Patrick Murray was a separate homicide victim, not a background character in Weiss’s story. Northwestern University preserves an individual Chicago Police entry for Murray, describing him as a bootlegger, age forty, killed at the same address and time. The database identifies his ethnicity as Irish, making him a particularly relevant person in the history of the Irish-associated North Side organization while emphasizing that Weiss himself had Polish family origins. [2]

Attorney William W. O’Brien was wounded in the same attack. The police-ledger narratives also mention injuries to Samuel Peller and Benjamin Jacobs. The fact that the victims were involved in or near the orbit of criminal activity does not make their deaths merely an internal organizational accounting event. Public gunfire risked employees, residents, worshippers and passersby who had no role in the conflict. The killing’s setting beside the cathedral amplified its public visibility, but the underlying safety threat was present throughout the city’s gang wars. [1] [2] [4]

Several secondary retellings mistakenly turn one of the wounded companions into a dead man or reduce the attack to a duel between Weiss and Capone. The two North Side fatalities were Weiss and Murray. Preserving the distinct names and outcomes is a basic evidentiary obligation and helps keep the historical focus on actual people harmed by the organized-crime conflict. [1] [2] [3]

17. The Homicide Investigation and Coroner Proceedings

The contemporary police ledger describes the shooters as unknown persons. That formulation is an important baseline: it records the state of the original investigation rather than a later author’s preferred explanation. The October 21, 1926 coroner proceedings included scrutiny of Samuel Peller and Ben Jacobs, whom the narrative says were present and armed and were believed to have had foreknowledge of the attack. The database also notes a coroner-only disposition rather than a completed criminal judgment against an identified killer. [1] [2]

The existence of an arrest, detention or decision to hold a witness for further inquiry does not prove that the person planned or committed murder. Chicago’s underworld investigations frequently unfolded amid conflicting witness accounts, fear of retaliation and the publicity surrounding prominent names. A verdict of guilt requires a different evidentiary standard from a newspaper headline, a police hypothesis or a coroner’s preliminary conclusion. [1]

Later histories frequently name Capone as the organizer, and some identify Jack McGurn, Frank Nitti or others as operatives. The Mob Museum provides an example of that retrospective identification. Such reconstructions may reflect knowledgeable historical traditions and plausible motives, but the Northwestern homicide entries do not identify those men as convicted perpetrators in the Weiss–Murray shooting. The article consequently distinguishes a widely attributed Capone-associated attack from legal proof of a specific command chain. [1] [12]

18. Holy Name Cathedral, Religion, and the Funeral

The location of Weiss’s death led to some of the most enduring images of the Prohibition gang wars: a Catholic church struck by nearby gunfire, an illicit liquor leader lying mortally wounded outside a public landmark, and clergy faced with a grave scene. Holy Name Cathedral’s institutional chronology records the shooting, its proximity to the cathedral and a clerical response. It also recounts restrictions on burial services at the cathedral. [4]

The same source associates Weiss with Catholic religious identity, a point compatible with the Chicago Crime Commission’s account of his Polish-American background. Nevertheless, a gangster’s possession of devotional objects or interaction with a priest cannot be converted into a complete psychological explanation. Private belief, public reputation and criminal participation were separate dimensions of his life and should be handled with the same care applied to ethnicity or family background. [4] [9]

The religious setting has encouraged legends and sensational retellings. For scholarly purposes, it is enough to describe what can be dated: the 1924 murder at Schofield’s, the 1926 attack across the street, and the cathedral’s later preservation of those events within its local chronology. The public violence imposed social costs on the institution and the neighborhood; it should not be romanticized because it occurred beside a celebrated building. [4] [11]

19. Succession: Vincent Drucci and Bugs Moran

Weiss’s death did not immediately dissolve the North Side Gang. The Mob Museum’s chronology identifies Vincent Drucci as the leader after October 1926. Drucci died in April 1927 after an encounter with a Chicago police officer, and George “Bugs” Moran subsequently became the best-known surviving North Side leader. This sequence provides a broad framework; it should not conceal the organization’s changing membership, sources of revenue and fractured authority. [6]

The notorious St. Valentine’s Day Massacre on February 14, 1929, took place more than two years after Weiss’s death. It belongs to the longer history of the same feud and North Side organizational lineage, not to Weiss’s personal career. Treating the massacre as something Weiss planned or witnessed is a chronological error. It is relevant here only as evidence that the struggle outlived the people who had shaped its early and middle phases. [6]

Successor histories can also exaggerate institutional continuity. Being called the next “boss” does not necessarily mean inheriting identical territory, customers, methods or influence. Weiss’s death removed a prominent organizer and rival, but the structural incentives created by Prohibition persisted. Subsequent leaders inherited a violent contest and adapted it under conditions different from those faced in late 1924. [6] [7]

20. Wealth, Private Life, and Unsettled Personal Claims

Stories of Weiss’s income, household arrangements and relationships frequently arrive through later press retrospectives. Some biographies report large personal assets and make definite claims about a wife or partner. These subjects deserve particular caution because an underworld reputation for wealth can be inconsistent with what a probate court actually recorded, and a romantic relationship is not automatically proof of a legal marriage. [8]

Josephine Simard appears in later accounts of disputed estate claims. A precise legal resolution, property valuation and marital status should be established through probate documents or carefully cited contemporary reporting before this encyclopedia treats them as certified facts. A secondary work’s statement that Weiss left a multimillion-dollar estate to his wife is not a substitute for an inventory, marriage certificate or decree. No such documents were independently collated for this edition. [8]

The temptation to fill gaps with dramatic personal details is strong because Weiss died young and left no comprehensive, reliable autobiography. A responsible historical account need not turn every uncertainty into speculation. It is enough to report that he gained substantial prominence in a profitable illegal market while the exact distribution of personally owned wealth and the documentary status of some familial claims remain unsettled.

21. Historical Interpretation and Evidentiary Limits

Weiss is significant because his career links Chicago’s neighborhood criminal networks with the larger institutional problems of Prohibition. The federal ban on alcohol created opportunities in production, transport and wholesale distribution; rival groups developed violent ways to police what they treated as commercial territory. Weiss became a leading participant in that environment, and his conflict with Capone exemplifies how personal loyalty and organizational rivalry magnified the costs of an illegal but highly demanded commodity. [5] [7]

His death also illustrates why organized-crime history requires multiple classes of sources. A police homicide ledger is strong evidence that named people were killed at a specified place and date but may preserve errors in age or terminology. An archived photograph locates a scene but does not alone identify the gunman. A museum’s narrative can reconstruct the larger chronology but may draw on later testimony and legend. A church history may accurately preserve institutional memory while making confident retrospective attributions. [1] [3] [4] [6]

This biography therefore avoids three shortcuts: it does not identify Weiss as ethnically Irish simply because he led an Irish-associated group; it does not count every crime attributed to the North Siders as a personally proven act by Weiss; and it does not transform the widely accepted Capone-linked explanation of the October 1926 attack into an assertion that a particular killer was convicted. Such distinctions are necessary for an educational crime encyclopedia rather than a retelling of gangster folklore. [1] [7] [8] [12]

Sources and Further Reading

Source method: This educational synthesis prioritizes police-ledger transcripts for the recorded murders, an archived FBI-file photograph for crime-scene geography, museum chronology for organizational interpretation, and explicitly labeled later biographies for disputed personal details. Links are supplied for independent review. No original birth certificate, full probate case file, homicide prosecution file, or all-page newspaper collation was obtained for this edition.

  1. Northwestern University, Homicide in Chicago 1870–1930, Case 9302, “Weiss, Earl J. ‘Hymie’,” transcribed Chicago Police Department homicide ledger, October 11 and 21, 1926. View source. Source note: Contemporary police-ledger transcription; central for date, witnesses, crime-scene geography and coroner proceedings; the listed age conflicts with later biographies.
  2. Northwestern University, Homicide in Chicago 1870–1930, Case 8606, “Murray, Patrick,” Chicago Police Department homicide ledger, October 11, 1926. View source. Source note: Separate victim record confirms Murray as the other fatality; contains its own categorization and coroner fields.
  3. Federal Bureau of Investigation, Al Capone file, Part 35 of 36, including a reproduced Chicago Tribune annotated photograph of the October 11, 1926 crime scene. View source. Source note: Historic photograph reproduced in an FBI archive; the published image caption is not itself FBI eyewitness testimony or a judicial ruling on the killers.
  4. Holy Name Cathedral Parish, “History of the Cathedral: 1904–1939,” entries for November 10, 1924, and October 11, 1926. View source. Source note: Institutional local history; useful for the setting, parish response and commemorative geography; some perpetrator attributions are retrospective.
  5. The Mob Museum, “Dean O’Banion,” biographical profile. View source. Source note: Secondary museum account of the North Side group, its Irish-associated neighborhood origins, bootlegging arrangements and O’Banion’s death.
  6. The Mob Museum, “The St. Valentine’s Day Massacre: What Happened Before That Caused This Infamous Crime?” View source. Source note: Interpretive chronology of North Side–Outfit violence and succession, including January 1925, September 1926 and October 1926 events.
  7. The Mob Museum, “Prohibition Profits Transformed the Mob,” Prohibition: An Interactive History. View source. Source note: Historical overview of revenue, rivalry and the multinational, multiethnic character of bootlegging in Chicago.
  8. Paul M. Klenowski, “Hymie Weiss,” EBSCO Research Starters: Biography (2024). View source. Source note: Modern biographical synthesis; differs from other works about birth location and makes individual crime and estate claims requiring careful qualification.
  9. Chicago Crime Commission, “From the Archives – May 2014: Henry Earl J. Wojciechowski, aka ‘Hymie Weiss’.” View source. Source note: Professional historical summary; useful for name and upbringing traditions, but some details require examination of contemporaneous source material.
  10. Michigan State University Libraries, Janet A. Ginsburg Chicago Tribune Collection, “Earl ‘Hymie’ Weiss,” photograph identification record, Chicago Sunday Tribune, June 17, 1934. View source. Source note: Archived photograph metadata, corroborating the public name and 1898–1926 dates; not evidence of all reported life events.
  11. Adam Harrington, “Chicago Hauntings: Gangland Vengeance and Ghosts near Holy Name Cathedral,” CBS Chicago, November 2, 2022. View source. Source note: Retrospective local history for Schofield’s storefront and the State/Superior streetscape; ghost-story framing is not adopted as historical evidence.
  12. The Mob Museum, “‘Machine Gun’ Jack McGurn Leads List of Top 5 Most Notorious Mob Hitmen,” May 2019. View source. Source note: Later reconstruction naming alleged participants in the 1926 shooting; not a documented conviction or definitive judicial identification.

Publication qualification: Historical responsibility attributed to a gang or prominent individual in a secondary account is not equivalent to conviction of a named person. The homicide-ledger age conflict, variant full names and birthplace accounts, and disputed particulars of the attack are preserved rather than concealed.
