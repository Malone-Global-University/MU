Danny Greene's Celtic Club Biographies

Cleveland's 1970s Irish-American-led underworld: Danny Greene, rival criminal networks, labor and gambling disputes, and the violence of the city's gang conflict.

Collection Overview

Danny Greene's Celtic Club is the conventional directory label for the network of associates and criminal activities organized around Daniel John Patrick “Danny” Greene in the Cleveland area during the 1970s. Greene cultivated a public Irish identity and used the Celtic Club name during his disputes with rival criminal figures. His network arose in a setting shaped by labor racketeering, illegal gambling, waste-hauling conflicts, and competing Cleveland organized-crime factions. It was not the same organization as the Italian-American Cleveland Mafia family.

The name “Celtic Club” requires historical precision. Contemporary coverage documented a sign announcing a planned Celtic Club on a trailer at Greene's bomb-damaged property. That public name and symbolism do not establish the existence of a continuously incorporated club, a fixed membership roll, or a single formal hierarchy for everyone who worked with Greene. In its 1984 decision in United States v. Licavoli, the Sixth Circuit described Greene as the leader of a rival criminal organization; the opinion is a stronger source for the criminal coalition and its conflict than for any supposed formal club membership.

This MGU directory provides one selected canonical biography, Danny Greene. Associated people and adversaries appear below for historical context, with their relationships classified rather than treated as proof of membership. The directory complements the Crime Encyclopedia's organization, case, event, and place records.

Research Themes

  • Greene's career in Cleveland's waterfront labor movement and the evidentiary limits of his union-related court cases.
  • The Cleveland Trade Solid Waste Guild, waste-hauling competition, and the reported use of coercion in commercial disputes.
  • Underworld relationships among Greene, numbers operator Shondor Birns, and various Cleveland racketeering networks.
  • Irish symbols, the Celtic Club name, and the distinction between public self-presentation and a documented organizational structure.
  • The alliance with John Nardi and the conflict with the Cleveland crime family under James Licavoli.
  • The frequency and consequences of bombings in Cleveland during the 1970s, including risks to bystanders.
  • The 1977 killings of John Nardi and Danny Greene and the later federal racketeering case against participants in the murder conspiracy.
  • How court records, local archives, contemporary journalism, and later popular portrayals differ in evidentiary value.

Principal Biography

  1. Daniel John Patrick “Danny” Greene

    Historical classification: Irish-American criminal-network leader; central figure associated with the Celtic Club name. November 9, 1929 – October 6, 1977. Greene became a longshoremen's union leader in Cleveland before entering a series of commercial and criminal disputes. Cleveland Historical documents his prosecution in a union-funds case and the 1968 reversal of his original conviction; that reversal must not be omitted when discussing the case. Later investigations concerned his activities in the waste-hauling industry and his relationships with local racketeering figures.

    During the 1970s Greene developed his own group amid conflicts with older Cleveland organized-crime interests. His Irish imagery and the “Celtic Club” name became prominent in reporting after bombing attacks against his property. Greene and Cleveland underworld figure John Nardi became allies against the faction headed by James Licavoli, but an alliance should not automatically be recorded as membership in the same gang. The Sixth Circuit's 1984 appellate opinion describes the prosecution evidence concerning the conspiracy to murder Greene.

    Greene was killed on October 6, 1977 by a remotely detonated car bomb after a dental appointment in Lyndhurst, Ohio. The bombing and subsequent prosecutions are important evidence of the scope and consequences of organized-crime violence in the Cleveland region. Accounts of his career must distinguish adjudicated facts, investigative assertions, and the romanticized image developed in later books and film.

Historical Context and Organizational Development

Labor leadership: Greene worked as a longshoreman and served as president of International Longshoremen's Association Local 1317 in Cleveland. Federal criminal proceedings concerning union funds produced an initial conviction that was later reversed on appeal. The full procedural history should be reconstructed from docket and appellate materials before assigning any final criminal disposition.

Waste hauling and early criminal relationships: The Cleveland Trade Solid Waste Guild was chartered in June 1969. Historical coverage describes pressure on independent rubbish haulers and investigations of violence in that industry. The record also places Greene in relationships with older racketeering figures, notably Alex “Shondor” Birns, whose numbers operations became a source of conflict. A business relationship or earlier role as an enforcer does not imply that Birns belonged to Greene's later network.

The Celtic Club name: In the mid-1970s Greene deliberately displayed Irish flags and imagery. Contemporary reporting cited by Cleveland Magazine describes his trailer with a sign announcing a future Celtic Club. This is evidence of the name and identity Greene promoted, but not of a comprehensive membership register or corporate continuity. MGU uses “Danny Greene's Celtic Club” as a recognizable index title rather than a claim that all his activities were formally carried out by a registered club.

Conflict with Birns: Greene's relationship with Shondor Birns collapsed amid disputed financial dealings and violent reprisals. Birns died in a car bombing on March 29, 1975. Greene has often been suspected in retrospective accounts, but the Cleveland Police Museum notes that he was not charged with Birns's murder; responsibility should not be presented as proven in the absence of an adjudication.

Alliance with John Nardi: In the mid-1970s Greene's associates challenged Cleveland's established Mafia interests. John Nardi, a Teamsters-connected criminal figure with ties to the city's Italian-American underworld, allied with Greene against rival leaders. Nardi's affiliation must be recorded as ally or partner rather than presumed Celtic Club membership. Nardi was killed in a car bombing on May 17, 1977.

The Licavoli conflict: The Sixth Circuit in United States v. Licavoli, 725 F.2d 1040 (6th Cir. 1984), described the evidence that James Licavoli sought Greene's killing and that conspirators repeatedly attempted to locate and attack him. The case traces relationships among the defendants, hired killer Raymond Ferritto, and the eventual car bombing. The legal decision is authoritative for the appellate disposition and the evidence considered, not a universal finding about every rumored Cleveland bombing.

October 1977 assassination and aftermath: On October 6, a remotely detonated explosive device concealed in a nearby car killed Greene as he approached his vehicle after an appointment in Lyndhurst. Investigations and testimony from cooperating participants contributed to federal convictions arising from the murder conspiracy. The violence harmed public safety, destabilized criminal networks, and generated extensive prosecutorial and journalistic records.

Long-term historical significance: Greene's career illustrates a locally rooted criminal network shaped by labor, illicit markets, personal alliances, ethnic identity, and competition with established organizations. His murder and the subsequent federal proceedings provide a case study in racketeering investigations and the difficulty of reconstructing gang history from a combination of court findings, police assessments, press narratives, and later mythology.

Associated Figures, Allies, and Rivals

  • John Nardi — ally: Cleveland criminal and labor figure who joined Greene in opposition to the Licavoli faction. Do not classify Nardi as a Celtic Club member without case-specific evidence.
  • Keith Ritson — Greene loyalist / associate: Later federal organized-crime reporting identifies Ritson as a Greene loyalist. More precise role and chronology require individual source review.
  • Alex “Shondor” Birns — earlier associate, later rival: Numbers operator and racketeer tied to Greene's earlier career. Birns's 1975 death predates Greene's 1977 assassination.
  • James “Jack White” Licavoli — rival organization leader: Principal defendant in the later federal racketeering prosecution concerning the conspiracy to kill Greene.
  • Raymond Ferritto — perpetrator in the murder conspiracy, not member: His role in the 1977 bombing is documented by testimony described in the federal appellate opinion.

Editorial Standards

Collection scope: One selected linked biography from the current MGU production plan. This page is not a complete membership register.

  • Organizational naming: Treat “Celtic Club” as a historically used label associated with Greene and an identity he projected; distinguish it from evidence of a formal club structure.
  • Membership standards: Keep leaders, direct associates, occasional partners, allies, informants, antagonists, and convicted killers in separate categories.
  • Separate organizations: Greene's network and the Cleveland Mafia family must not be merged into one affiliation. John Nardi's alliance does not automatically make him a Celtic Club member.
  • Legal accuracy: Distinguish a charge, an original conviction, appellate reversal, new proceedings, and final outcome; do not present allegations or investigative descriptions as convictions.
  • Bombing attribution: Avoid assigning every 1970s Cleveland bombing to Greene, Licavoli, or an associate without incident-specific evidence.
  • Chronology: Separate the union period, waste-hauling disputes, emergence of the Celtic Club name, factional warfare, 1977 deaths, and subsequent federal trials.
  • Source hierarchy: Prioritize court opinions and case records, official archives, contemporaneous reporting, and well-documented historical scholarship. Identify retrospective reconstructions as such.
  • Ethnic precision: Irish-American identity and symbolism do not establish the ethnicity or formal membership of each individual in Greene's network.
  • Canonical filing: Maintain one biography for Greene and use cross-links for other people and organization records rather than duplicate biographies.
  • Historical purpose: Describe coercion, criminal activity, victims, institutional consequences, and the law-enforcement response without treating organized-crime violence as heroism.

Research Starting Points

These sources provide independent historical context and documentary evidence for the major themes. Not every source verifies every person or allegation; detailed biographies should maintain claim-specific references and legal dispositions.

  • United States v. Licavoli, 725 F.2d 1040 (6th Cir. 1984) — published federal appellate opinion concerning the Greene murder conspiracy, the relationship between criminal factions, testimony of Raymond Ferritto, and related racketeering convictions.
  • Cleveland Historical — “Danny Greene: ‘The Irishman’” — Cleveland State University-connected public-history article covering Greene's longshoremen career, union-funds litigation, waste-hauling disputes, and October 1977 death; includes archival images.
  • Cleveland Magazine — “The Irishman Chronicles” — retrospective feature incorporating contemporary 1977 reporting on Greene's Irish-themed self-presentation, the Celtic Club sign, and his reported FBI information-sharing.
  • Cleveland Police Museum — “Shondor Birns: A Look Into the Mobster's Life and Gruesome Death” — historical account of Birns's numbers operations, Greene's earlier relationship with him, their falling-out, and the distinction between suspicion and criminal charges concerning Birns's death.
