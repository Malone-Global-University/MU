William P. “Dinty” Colbeck (d. 1943)

St. Louis Egan’s Rats leader and Prohibition-era political figure whose 1924 federal mail-robbery conviction ended his first period of criminal power; killed in an unsolved 1943 shooting.

1. Historical Overview

William P. “Dinty” Colbeck was a St. Louis political operative and organized-crime figure who led the group called Egan’s Rats during a particularly violent phase of its Prohibition-era history. The strongest surviving record of his activities is not a memoir or a gangster anecdote, but a federal mail-robbery prosecution. Colbeck was tried alongside eight other defendants in 1924; all nine received twenty-five-year penitentiary sentences, and the United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit affirmed those judgments in December 1925. Contemporary newspapers identified him as the leader of the gang, while a National Archives case-file description likewise characterizes him as its head.

The story does not end with a 1924 conviction. Colbeck lived long enough to leave prison and return to a city whose criminal economy had changed markedly. On 17 February 1943 he was killed by gunfire while in an automobile in St. Louis. A subsequent Senate committee staff exhibit placed the shooting at Ninth and Destrehan Streets and listed it among unsolved gangland killings. The basic chronology is secure, but major gaps remain: precise birth information is conflicting, early alleged acts are incompletely documented, and there is no surviving court verdict assigning a perpetrator to his death.

Colbeck’s career offers a particularly revealing case study of the intersection of neighborhood gangs, electoral politics, Prohibition, interstate postal crime, cooperating witnesses and federal criminal procedure. The chronology below is structured to distinguish facts proved in court from statements made by journalists, archival catalogers, witnesses and later historians. It is equally important to remember the postal employees, targets of violence, witnesses and communities affected by the gang’s operations rather than treat its members simply as folk heroes. 2. Identity, Name, and the Birth-Date Problem

Federal case documents identify the defendant as William P. Colbeck; his nickname appears as “Dinty,” “Dint,” and sometimes “Dinty Colbeck.” There is no ambiguity about the man’s identity in the Staunton case because the appellate opinion, National Archives file, contemporary verdict coverage and Bureau of Prisons photograph converge on the same name and proceeding. The middle initial is documented; a fully expanded middle name has not been established in the records inspected here.

His date of birth is considerably less secure. Familiar biographical sites repeat 17 November 1890, while a compiled St. Louis genealogical index lists William P. “Dinty” Colbeck as born 17 March 1889. Neither statement has been verified against a civil birth certificate, baptism register, original draft card or contemporaneous government form. Accordingly this profile identifies his birth period as the late 1880s or circa 1890, declines to calculate a definitive age at death, and records both competing dates in the Evidence Assessment.

The names of criminals in early twentieth-century press reports often vary through nicknames, misspellings and syndication. These variants are useful search terms, but should not be mistaken for separate people or used to infer lineage. The spelling Egan’s Rats also appears in older newspapers as “Eagan’s Rats”; it refers to the same St. Louis gang rather than to a distinct rival organization. [1] [3] [9] [13]

3. St. Louis, Neighborhood Organization, and Political Protection

Colbeck’s career unfolded within a large, heterogeneous St. Louis metropolitan area divided by municipal boundaries and by the Mississippi River. North St. Louis saloons, ward clubs, streets and nearby Illinois communities formed overlapping commercial and political spaces. Legal retail businesses, labor, immigrant social networks and neighborhood politics coexisted with extortion, illicit gambling and armed robbery. A biographical account must distinguish the people living in these districts from the smaller criminal networks that exploited them.

Before national Prohibition, gangs in St. Louis could already be used for coercive political work: rallying supporters, intimidating opponents, delivering favors and imposing consequences on rivals. Historians describe Egan’s Rats as having roots in Irish-American street politics. These arrangements were not identical to formal city government; even when individual gang members obtained positions or party contacts, they did not have lawful authority to commit assaults or steal.

Political descriptions in 1924 called Colbeck a prominent river-ward figure. Later histories characterize him as involved with the Democratic City Committee. A political label does not establish how often he held an office, precisely what powers it conferred, or which elected officials knew of particular crimes. The necessary distinction is between documented reputation or position and an allegation that an entire political institution was itself a criminal gang. [3] [6] [8] [11]

4. Thomas Kinney, Tom Egan, and the Origins of the Rats

The organization predates Colbeck’s emergence. Thomas “Snake” Kinney and Tom Egan are repeatedly identified in St. Louis histories as founders or principal early patrons of the group. Their overlapping political and saloon interests helped create a reservoir of men available for coercive work. As the group expanded, its activities were no longer confined to election-day pressure and ordinary street violence. The connection of such a group to ward-level politics was a source of protection and a potential source of exposure whenever violence attracted wider scrutiny.

The death of Kinney in 1913 and Tom Egan in 1919 preceded the later Prohibition-era leadership struggle. Histories usually place Tom Egan’s younger brother Willie Egan in command after 1919. These succession dates supply orientation, not a formal corporate chart: the Rats were a shifting coalition of associates, not a corporation with recorded directors or an inducted Mafia family with fixed membership rules.

The term “Irish-American crime syndicate” is an MGU classification for the organizational history, not an assertion that every member of the Rats was of identical ancestry. Criminal associations commonly extended through convenience, kinship, business and rivalries across ethnic lines. Colbeck’s position is therefore best understood as part of a St. Louis criminal-political network rather than an ethnically closed institution. [6] [8] [9]

5. Plumbing, World War I Service, and the Surviving Record

Later accounts describe Colbeck as a plumber or plumbing-shop proprietor before and during his rise in the gang. A historian using the St. Louis Post-Dispatch report on Willie Egan’s death places Colbeck among the men who attempted to carry the wounded leader to medical assistance in 1921 and describes his plumbing business. This occupational detail matters: men active in illegal enterprises could also maintain trades, storefronts and political roles, and a trade label did not indicate the absence of criminal activity.

The National Archives description of the Staunton prosecution calls him a World War I veteran. Some popular biographies add unit assignments, service in France and combat details, but an original service file, Army roster or discharge record has not been independently examined for this edition. “World War I veteran” is thus used here with explicit attribution to the federal archivist’s summary; narrower claims about combat experience and military awards are not presented as verified.

The distinction between a cited catalog description and original personnel documentation is essential. A cataloger’s identification can be strongly suggestive and help readers find records, yet it is not itself a contemporaneous service certificate. Similarly, a later description of Colbeck as a master plumber should not be projected backward into every year of his early adult life. [2] [6] [9]

6. Prohibition and the Expansion of Criminal Markets

The Eighteenth Amendment and the Volstead Act provided a powerful new environment for illegal liquor distribution beginning in 1920. Demand for alcoholic beverages did not vanish, while the federal ban increased the rewards of avoiding regulation and enforcement. Established local gangs could use contacts with saloons, warehouses, transport providers and political intermediaries to participate in bootlegging. The Rats took part in this broader shift, with historians placing the group among St. Louis organizations that exploited the new economy.

Descriptions of the gang’s income also mention gambling, protection and robberies. Yet a numerical estimate for the value of all bootlegging or the number of gang members requires careful sourcing: the figures often repeated in popular overviews are not annual audited accounts. This profile does not treat citywide “control” as a quantifiable monopoly. Instead, it recognizes multiple competing organizations and distinguishes geographic influence, intimidation and actual ownership of businesses.

Prohibition increased opportunities for violence because a transaction in an illegal market had limited lawful contract remedies. Theft, intimidation and killing could become means of enforcing agreements, while witnesses and traders feared retaliation. These dynamics help explain why Colbeck’s leadership years saw both fierce neighborhood conflict and ambitious robberies targeting federal mail shipments. [8] [12]

7. 31 October 1921: The Killing of Willie Egan

On 31 October 1921 Willie Egan was shot outside his Franklin Avenue establishment in St. Louis. A later local-history reconstruction, drawing on the St. Louis Post-Dispatch of 1 November, reports that Colbeck was among those attempting to move Egan to a hospital. The narrative places the killing within an extended dispute involving Max Greenberg and other criminal associates. Crucially, the same reconstruction reports that Egan did not identify his attackers to police and that his murder was not solved.

Some familiar retellings claim that the dying Egan privately named three members of the rival Hogan group and that Colbeck immediately relayed a deathbed accusation. That detail is not compatible with the cited report that Egan refused to name his assailants. An accusation made by a gang participant also would not by itself prove responsibility. Rather than repeat an unverified last-words scene, this profile records the killing, its setting, the contemporary uncertainty and the succession that followed.

Egan’s death was an important leadership break. It deprived the Rats of the person who had inherited Tom Egan’s position after 1919 and exposed competing ambitions within the organization. Colbeck’s proximity to the dying leader and his preexisting standing made succession plausible, but the degree of support he commanded at each moment is reconstructed principally from newspaper and historical accounts, not minutes or membership records. [6] [8] [9]

8. Colbeck’s Succession and the Limits of the “Boss” Label

After Willie Egan’s death, Colbeck emerged as the gang’s leader. This conclusion rests on mutually reinforcing evidence: a documented historical reconstruction says he took over; contemporary press coverage called him the gang head at the time of his conviction; a National Archives case-file description describes the men as members of Egan’s Rats led by Colbeck; and the later Senate staff exhibit calls him a former Egan leader.

These sources should not be treated as identical kinds of proof. The appellate court affirmed a mail-robbery verdict without making a formal finding about who governed Egan’s Rats. The National Archives description is a later archival summary rather than sworn trial testimony. The journalist’s term “head of the gang” is a period characterization. Together they support the historical description of leadership, while leaving uncertain the exact internal distribution of authority.

The hierarchy was also contested by the basic nature of the group. Lieutenants had their own associates and access to criminal opportunities; some may have acted outside Colbeck’s immediate supervision. Describing him as “boss” does not automatically mean he personally approved every robbery, assault, shooting or intimidation attributed collectively to the Rats. [2] [3] [6] [7] [9]

9. The Hogan Gang and the North St. Louis Feud

The chief named rival of the Egan organization in the early 1920s was a faction associated with Edward “Jelly Roll” Hogan. Accounts describe competition over gambling, illicit liquor, neighborhood influence and political connections. Violence on both sides damaged public safety and alarmed residents whose streets and businesses had little to do with the gangs’ disputes. A later Senate staff overview described the Egan–Hogan rivalry as a major confrontation for north St. Louis rackets.

Many gang histories provide long lists of retaliatory shootings and assign motives with confidence. Such lists often depend on journalists’ speculation, the self-justifying testimony of criminals or later retellings rather than judicial proof. A shooting that occurred during the feud cannot automatically be attributed to Colbeck by proximity in time. Nor does presence at a meeting necessarily make a participant criminally responsible for a subsequent attack.

The safest interpretation is that Colbeck took command amid escalating organized-crime conflict and that the Egan and Hogan groups became widely associated with interlocking cycles of violence. The precise command relationship behind each killing must be established incident by incident. The evidence assessment below lists these limitations rather than compiling untested murder counts. [7] [8] [9]

10. Political Functions, Public Image, and Coercive Power

Newspapers in 1924 did not describe Colbeck solely as a bootlegger or accused robber. They also identified him as a St. Louis politician, revealing how his public presence crossed the boundary between ward politics and a criminal subculture. Retrospectives have described him as a Democratic committee sergeant-at-arms, a post whose title could suggest an organizing or order-keeping function rather than an elected legislative role.

Political influence mattered to criminal organizations because police priorities, licensing, elections and public appointments shaped opportunities for protection. However, a connection to a political committee is not proof that any specific public official accepted a bribe. This biography therefore does not construct a detailed roster of corrupt beneficiaries without records identifying particular transactions or adjudications.

The effect on civic life was wider than the fortunes of individual gang figures. Coercive political practices could suppress independent voting, intimidate neighborhood rivals and erode confidence that legal businesses and residents would receive equal enforcement. The social history of Egan’s Rats belongs alongside the case law, not as a glamorous supplement but as an explanation of why organized crime posed a governance problem. [3] [8] [11]

11. From Local Racketeering to Interstate Mail Crime

Egan’s Rats became associated with large robberies that reached beyond saloon territories and local disputes. Mail shipments were an attractive target because money, securities and other valuable items could move through postal custody. Attacking a postal custodian also created a federal offense capable of moving a case beyond local policing and into district court, where prosecutors could bring charges affecting several participants in a joint plan.

The federal record most directly tied to Colbeck concerns the May 1923 robbery at Staunton, Illinois. The existence of another large mail theft in April 1923 appears in an official Postal Inspection Service history of witness Ray Renard. The two descriptions should not be collapsed into one incident or assigned the same loss amount. Claims that one robbery yielded more than two million dollars and claims that Staunton involved roughly fifty-four thousand dollars refer to different report contexts and require independent document-by-document reconstruction.

A further complication is that contemporary press reports sometimes valued mail pouches using face amounts, negotiable securities or cash differently. The historian must ask whether a figure measures stolen currency, securities at par, recovered property or an allegation in an indictment. This biography concentrates on the adjudicated Staunton case and preserves the distinction between that case and other alleged gang robberies. [1] [3] [4] [9]

12. 26 May 1923: The Staunton Mail Robbery

On 26 May 1923, a mail pouch in the custody of postal personnel was robbed at Staunton, in Macoupin County, Illinois. The federal indictment subsequently named Colbeck and others in connection with that theft. An archival description identifies the custodians as George A. Roberts and Harry Kuehnan and specifies registered packages with listed amounts of $45,000, $5,000 and $4,134. A newspaper report of the verdict rounded or otherwise reported the total as $54,130. The few-dollar discrepancy is not material to guilt, but it matters for documentary precision.

The federal court’s 1925 opinion describes the legal charges rather than narrating every minute of the robbery. Count one alleged robbery of persons lawfully entrusted with the mails. Count two added the allegation that deadly weapons put their lives in jeopardy. Counts three and four involved receiving, concealing or possessing stolen mail matter with knowledge that it had been stolen. The original indictment and any witness statements are distinct source types and should be read alongside the opinion for a full incident reconstruction.

The location is important: Staunton was in Illinois, not in downtown St. Louis. Federal jurisdiction followed the interstate postal system and the charged interference with mail custodians. The episode illustrates how a St. Louis-based group could be prosecuted for activity across a state border, expanding the evidentiary reach of federal law. [1] [2] [3] [9]

13. Ray Renard, Cooperation, and Witness Reliability

Raymond “Ray” Renard was a central witness in the federal prosecution and later became a subject of Postal Inspection Service historical writing. By his own account he had accumulated an extensive juvenile and young-adult record and moved into increasingly serious thefts. His proximity to the gang made him a potentially valuable source of information about planning and participants, while his criminal record created substantial reasons for defense counsel and jurors to scrutinize credibility.

The published appellate opinion expressly identifies Renard as an admitted convict who testified regarding his own participation in the robbery. Defense witnesses said his general reputation for truth and honesty was poor. The appellate court evaluated the restrictions placed on their testimony, holding that the defense had already placed damaging credibility information before the jury and that the challenged ruling caused no substantial injury.

Neither extreme is sound historical method. Renard’s criminal history did not make him automatically incompetent to testify under federal law, and the existence of his testimony did not make every thing he said independently corroborated. The verdict established guilt under the jury instructions and available evidence. A researcher examining a particular uncharged allegation would still need evidence beyond the broad fact that Renard cooperated. [1] [4] [5]

14. 3 September 1924: Indictment and the Nine Defendants

The four-count indictment was returned on 3 September 1924. The Seventh Circuit later described the trial as involving nine plaintiffs in error, including Colbeck. The opinion records that the group was charged under provisions of the federal Criminal Code concerning the robbery of mail custodians and knowing possession of stolen mail. A larger indictment could name additional people not ultimately covered by the same verdict; the number initially accused must be distinguished from the number convicted and appealing.

The surviving federal sources and associated prison records identify several co-defendants, including Oliver Daugherty, David Robinson, Charles Smith, Stephen Ryan, Charles Lanham, Frank Hackethal, Frank Eppelsheimer and Gustav B. Dietmeyer. Spelling variations appear across the transcripts and prison materials; those variants do not by themselves justify inventing extra defendants. Their individual convictions and the extent of the evidence against each require attention to the actual opinion rather than a generalized statement that all were interchangeable lieutenants.

The proceedings also show that defendants could pursue procedural objections without admitting the underlying crime. Motions attacking an indictment, and later appellate challenges concerning the instructions or testimony, were attempts to contest the legality of the prosecution. They were not admissions, and the failure of those motions did not establish unrelated gangland accusations. [1] [2] [9]

15. November 1924: Federal Trial at Quincy

The case came on for trial on 10 November 1924 in Quincy, Illinois, before United States District Judge Louis FitzHenry. The court’s opinion records defense efforts to challenge the indictment, including a motion presented just as trial was beginning. Different defendants had filed or sought to file objections on different dates. The trial court declined to permit the last-minute withdrawal of pleas for a new motion to quash, and that ruling later became one issue on appeal.

Contemporary newspaper reporting dated 15 November announced the jury’s guilty verdict against Colbeck and eight others. The same report identified the charges as connected with the 1923 Staunton mail robbery and described Colbeck as a St. Louis political and gang leader. It initially said sentencing would occur later; separate contemporary reporting and records place the twenty-five-year terms in the same November sentencing period.

The distinction matters because the journalist on the day of the verdict did not necessarily possess the later sentencing record, and the 1925 opinion speaks from the vantage point of completed proceedings. Combining these documents permits a precise account: jury trial in November 1924, nine convictions, and sentences of twenty-five years, later affirmed on appeal. [1] [3] [5] [9]

16. The Verdicts and Twenty-Five-Year Sentences

The appellate opinion reports that eight of the convicted defendants, including Colbeck, were found guilty on all four counts. Gustav Dietmeyer was convicted on counts one and two but not counts three and four. Every one of the nine men received a sentence of twenty-five years in a federal penitentiary. It would therefore be inaccurate to say the defendants all received exactly the same four-count verdict or to conflate a sentence of twenty-five years with an actual custodial term of equal duration.

These convictions concern the federal mail case. They do not constitute a jury finding that Colbeck personally committed each killing attributed in later books to Egan’s Rats, nor do they prove every allegation of bootlegging or political corruption. The court punished federal offenses within the indictment, while later historians may describe a broader criminal career based on other records.

The verdict was an institutional turning point for the Rats because imprisoning multiple leading associates curtailed the group’s operating capacity. A historian must nevertheless avoid presenting the end of a specific leadership regime as the instantaneous disappearance of every associate, business link or successor enterprise. Crime markets changed and fragmented rather than vanishing in one courtroom. [1] [3] [5] [8]

17. The Appellate Challenge to the Indictment

The defendants pursued appellate review after conviction. In Colbeck v. United States, the Seventh Circuit analyzed challenges based on the evidence presented to the grand jury, the timing of the objections and the claimed incompetence of a witness with a criminal record. Some defendants argued that an indictment allegedly resting on a convict’s information should be quashed; the court rejected the premise that convicted persons were legally incompetent federal witnesses.

The opinion stressed that unsupported assertions about the grand-jury evidence did not require a district court to reconstruct the secret proceedings. The court also observed that prosecutors had described eyewitness evidence presented to the grand jury. Colbeck and several others sought leave to withdraw their pleas for a motion to quash on the very morning trial began, and the court treated the decision to deny the request as discretionary.

These details matter because a courtroom challenge to procedure is easily transformed in popular retellings into a story of technical escape or prosecution trickery. The printed opinion instead explains the governing legal standards and its conclusion that the challenged rulings did not supply grounds for reversal. Readers should examine the reasoning without importing modern procedural rules backward unchanged. [1] [9]

18. Alibi Instructions, Impeachment, and Reasonable Doubt

The appeal also addressed the judge’s instructions on alibi and reasonable doubt, along with limits on questioning witnesses presented to impeach Renard. Those defense witnesses could describe his poor reputation for truthfulness; the specific additional question about whether they would personally believe him under oath was disallowed. The appellate court said that even if the ruling had been erroneous, it had not substantially damaged the defense because substantial impeachment evidence already stood in the record.

The court reviewed the general instructions, finding no reversible error. Its analysis also addressed the distinction between a person actually present during a robbery and one participating in preparations for a joint plan. A conviction for participation did not always require proof that the individual physically stood at the scene. In Dietmeyer’s particular appeal, the court discussed evidence of preparatory conduct and the difference between the contemplated earlier attempt and the robbery carried out in May.

The judicial opinion supplies a rich example of how a collective criminal prosecution can turn on both eyewitness credibility and law concerning joint participation. Its findings should not be simplified into “all nine men were arrested red-handed” or “the gang boss personally carried the mail away”; neither formulation follows from the published judgment. [1] [9]

19. 8 December 1925: Convictions Affirmed

On 8 December 1925 the Seventh Circuit affirmed the judgments. The published report is Colbeck v. United States, 10 F.2d 401 (7th Cir. 1925). A rehearing was denied on 13 January 1926. This is a clear outcome: the federal convictions and the twenty-five-year sentences survived the appellate arguments presented in the consolidated proceeding.

An appellate affirmance does not mean that every witness statement is now an independent judicial fact. The court reviews identified legal errors under standards that differ from those governing a fresh trial. It did not write a complete history of St. Louis organized crime or rule on each alleged murder in the Egan–Hogan feud. Its value for this biography is precision: it identifies the indictment, verdict allocations, sentences, relevant testimony and appellate outcome.

This distinction guides the broader narrative. Where the question is “Was Colbeck convicted in 1924 and was the conviction affirmed?” the appellate opinion is primary controlling evidence. Where the question is “Who ordered a particular unsolved shooting?” the same opinion may provide little or no evidence, regardless of how strongly later writers associate the event with the gang. [1] [9]

20. Leavenworth Records and Federal Imprisonment

Bureau of Prisons material identifies Colbeck as prisoner number 22066 at Leavenworth. A National Archives catalog record for his identification photographs includes a front and profile view. The image is valuable documentary evidence linking a named individual to federal custody, but the catalog does not date the photograph or supply a birth certificate. This profile accordingly does not use the portrait to settle his age, nor reproduce an unverified copy as if it were a contemporary public-domain portrait without attribution.

Incarceration was a practical disruption for a criminal group dependent on its leader’s availability, payments and interpersonal enforcement. The gang’s networks did not necessarily disappear, but federal custody sharply restricted Colbeck’s capacity to manage local disputes directly. Former associates could retire, change markets, join other circles or compete for territory during his absence.

Popular accounts sometimes describe colorful relationships with other prisoners, including figures famous outside St. Louis. Without a verified prison register, correspondence or contemporaneous meeting record, such anecdotes do not belong in the established biography. The prison number and appellate sentence are far stronger evidence than stories about private conversations among celebrated gangsters. [1] [9] [10]

21. What the Egan Organization Lost After 1924

The federal conviction of Colbeck and eight associates is often treated as the decisive collapse of Egan’s Rats as a commanding St. Louis organization. The observation is persuasive in a limited sense: the men convicted in the Staunton case could no longer openly administer day-to-day operations in the city while confined. The publisher’s account of Daniel Waugh’s research likewise identifies internal testimony and federal imprisonment as major forces ending the organization’s earlier dominance.

But organizational endings rarely have a single date. Surviving associates and commercial contacts could remain active, while other groups filled opportunities left by prosecution. An institutional history must specify whether “collapse” means the imprisonment of named leaders, the end of a particular bootlegging network, or the eventual disappearance of a label. These are related but different questions.

The local geography also changed with enforcement and market conditions. St. Louis and the Illinois side of the river continued to support gambling and other criminal enterprises, and organized crime did not end with the finality of the 1924 verdict. The trial marks a leadership break within a longer regional history rather than an end to illicit commerce. [1] [5] [6] [8]

22. Attributing Gang Shootings and Unproved Crimes

Retrospective gangster histories sometimes attribute specific deaths, assassination plots and internal executions directly to Colbeck. Particular versions identify named rivals or associates as targets and set out supposed orders issued from gang headquarters. Such accounts may be worth investigating, but this edition has not located court findings, original witness depositions or contemporary police conclusions establishing Colbeck’s responsibility for each incident.

A statement that an individual belonged to a violent organization does not make every action by the organization a personal act of that individual. Conversely, the absence of a surviving conviction should not be misrepresented as proof that an event never occurred. A careful account should identify what contemporaries alleged, what authorities investigated, what courts determined, and what remains unknown. In a number of the attributed shootings, this chain is currently incomplete.

The historical temptation is to write a streamlined sequence in which a new boss takes power and directly orders every subsequent attack. That is a compelling narrative device but not a substitute for evidence. This biography therefore treats the Egan–Hogan feud as documented context, refrains from assigning unsolved murders to Colbeck without incident-specific support, and directs further research toward original newspapers, coroner files and police records. [1] [6] [7] [8] [9]

23. Consequences for St. Louis Civic Institutions

The Rats’ history illuminates more than one criminal biography. Underlying the violence were organized relationships with businesses, political intermediaries and public institutions. Ward politics could supply legitimate representation and social assistance, yet coercive gang operations could exploit the same places and relationships to influence elections, silence complainants or build profitable illegal markets.

The case demonstrates a different institutional response: the federal government could prosecute a case involving mail custodians and a group of suspected perpetrators from across the region. That reach was not necessarily evidence that local officials were uniformly unwilling to enforce the law; it reflects federal jurisdiction and a body of proof capable of sustaining convictions. The comparison between local political influence and federal appellate review is central to the biography’s significance.

It is misleading to treat Irish-American ethnicity itself as a causal explanation for crime. The original gang emerged in a particular political economy and used specific coercive methods. Contemporary victims and ordinary people in the same communities had varied ancestries and livelihoods. The distinctive subject is a criminal institution and the protections and opportunities it exploited, not an entire population. [1] [3] [8] [12]

24. Release and the Changed St. Louis Underworld

Colbeck was released after serving a substantial but not full twenty-five-year period of imprisonment. Many accounts place his return around 1940, while this edition has not independently verified a parole certificate or precise release date. A 2013 retrospective describes him as trying to regain position in St. Louis organized crime after coming out of prison; that interpretation is later reporting rather than a finding in the 1925 appellate case.

The city to which he returned differed from the St. Louis of 1921. National Prohibition had ended in 1933, the gang’s old leadership structure had been disrupted, and new actors held influence in gambling, liquor-related commerce and other illegal markets. Colbeck could retain name recognition and associates without recovering his former operational leverage. The historical question is not simply whether he “wanted a comeback,” but what activities he demonstrably undertook and how rivals interpreted his return.

Retrospectives occasionally say he planned to resume lawful plumbing work, while others emphasize a renewed attempt to enter criminal rackets. These portraits need not be mutually exclusive, but neither should be treated as proved without parole interviews, business records or contemporaneous surveillance. The securely established endpoints are his earlier conviction, subsequent release before February 1943, and violent death. [1] [7] [11]

25. 17 February 1943: Colbeck’s Killing

On 17 February 1943 Colbeck was shot to death in St. Louis. The later Senate Kefauver Committee staff exhibit described a machine-gun attack while he sat in his automobile at Ninth and Destrehan Streets. The event and date are corroborated by retrospective news accounts, although this edition has not collated the original death certificate, medical examiner’s report or first-day newspaper account. He did not die in prison; he had already returned to the community.

The method and public setting of the killing immediately invited interpretation as gang retaliation or the elimination of a potential competitor. Those hypotheses are plausible in the context of his criminal history but remain hypotheses. The Senate exhibit listed the killing as unsolved and did not identify a convicted offender. Later versions that confidently name a mastermind should therefore be assessed by their evidence, not simply by how frequently a theory has been repeated.

The location also deserves precision. Ninth and Destrehan is a St. Louis intersection, not an Illinois robbery site or an assumed location of a gang headquarters. The event in 1943 is separate from the 1923 Staunton offense for which Colbeck had been imprisoned. Keeping the places and dates distinct prevents the biography from collapsing two decades of changing criminal activity into a single dramatic narrative. [7] [9] [11]

26. Unsolved Homicide and Limits of Attribution

The word “unsolved” should be used carefully. It means that the sources reviewed here do not identify a judicially established perpetrator and that a federal committee’s retrospective list categorized the death among unsolved gangland murders. It does not establish that investigators conducted no inquiry, nor that every rumor was false. Police may have considered suspects or motives without producing a court-tested result.

An unsolved killing is particularly vulnerable to retrospective storytelling. One interpretation portrays Colbeck as the victim of newer criminal bosses who did not want him to regain influence; another evokes a personal grievance left from the Egan–Hogan years. Both may be discussed as later speculative explanations, but neither is a verified finding. A responsible edition cannot convert motive speculation into a named murder conviction.

Original city homicide records, surviving coroner files and St. Louis press coverage for 18 February 1943 would be priorities for subsequent verification. Until those materials are collated, this biography gives the reported location, date and means of death with attribution and preserves the unresolved status of responsibility. [7] [11] [14]

27. The Kefauver Committee and Retrospective Evidence

In 1950 the United States Senate established a special committee to investigate organized crime in interstate commerce. Commonly called the Kefauver Committee, it gathered testimony and documentary exhibits in several major cities. Its staff compiled historical lists of gangland violence, including St. Louis and East St. Louis killings. One of these exhibits referred to Colbeck’s 1943 death and characterized earlier Egan–Hogan competition.

The evidentiary status of such a document is important. A Senate staff exhibit is a primary source for what the committee collected and published in 1950; it is not automatically a contemporaneous 1943 police finding. It may rest on records or witness recollections that need tracing before they can settle details. The passage identifying the date and intersection is therefore cited as a retrospective governmental statement rather than as the result of a criminal judgment.

The hearings also help show how public understanding of gang history was created. Officials selected events, witnesses and descriptions that made regional criminal networks intelligible to a national audience. The committee had investigative significance and historical value, but interpretation still requires asking who supplied each assertion and how it was checked. [7] [12]

28. Personal Life, Burial, and Archival Gaps

Colbeck’s public record is far more complete for prosecution than for family life. The documents directly checked for this edition do not establish an exact birthplace, complete parentage, marriage history, children or full occupational chronology. Repeated biographical assertions about these topics should not be reproduced merely to make the profile look complete. Avoiding invention is particularly important when a public figure shares names or initials with unrelated people in census and city-directory records.

Some secondary accounts place his burial in Calvary Cemetery in St. Louis. That identification is plausible and widely circulated, but the original cemetery register or death certificate was not independently collated for this edition. It is therefore identified as a reported burial rather than an independently authenticated grave record. The date of death, unlike burial arrangements, has stronger corroboration from the Senate exhibit and later reporting.

The most promising future archival leads include the National Archives prisoner case file, original municipal birth and death records, newspapers around October 1921, court testimony from 1924 and police records concerning the 1943 murder. Such research could refine dates and perhaps resolve contradictory retellings, but it should not be represented as already complete. [9] [10] [11] [13] [14]

29. Comparison with Other Irish-American Syndicates

MGU’s Irish-American Crime Syndicates collection covers organizations operating in distinct local settings. Egan’s Rats belonged to the St. Louis criminal and political history of the early twentieth century. The group was neither the Chicago Outfit nor a Boston Winter Hill Gang predecessor in a straightforward organizational chain. Similar business models—gambling, bootlegging, extortion and the use of violence—do not prove that one gang was an incorporated branch of another.

Prohibition created a national environment in which many groups developed parallel illicit markets and sometimes intercity contacts. Federal prosecutions could target interstate transactions while local conflicts remained deeply tied to particular neighborhoods and political offices. Colbeck’s case is a St. Louis–Illinois federal mail prosecution rather than a chapter of the Chicago Capone trials, a New York Five Families leadership dispute or the Boston Bulger litigation.

Comparison is valuable when it highlights documented distinctions: different courts, leaders, protection systems, witness dynamics and historical phases. It becomes misleading when famous names are inserted into one another’s biographies solely because they shared a general criminal milieu or served sentences during overlapping decades. [1] [8] [12]

30. Reading the Case: Record, Charge, Verdict, and Narrative

Four different kinds of statement organize this biography. An indictment alleges wrongdoing and specifies the charged offenses. A jury verdict establishes guilt on particular counts as adjudicated at trial. An appellate opinion addresses designated legal errors and explains whether the verdict and sentence will stand. A historical narrative can then add context from newspapers and secondary scholarship, but it must not represent its inferences as findings from the case.

The Colbeck materials offer a practical illustration. The 1924 indictment specified offenses against the postal system; the jury found nine men guilty, but with a difference in the count allocation for Dietmeyer; the Seventh Circuit affirmed all nine twenty-five-year sentences; later histories described the defendants as members of Egan’s Rats and situated the case in the collapse of their political-criminal network. These statements connect without being identical.

The archival record also contains summaries generated after the original proceedings. The National Archives case-file description is an authoritative finding aid, but its language about gang leadership does not appear as a formal legal determination in the appellate text. This edition cites both rather than silently replacing one with the other. [1] [2] [9]

31. Historical Interpretation and Legacy

Colbeck is historically significant not because his life can be reduced to a sensational nickname, but because his documented career joins three realms frequently studied in isolation: urban political organization, commercialized criminal violence and federal law enforcement. The earlier generation of Rats operated in the world of saloons and ward politics; Prohibition broadened illicit revenue opportunities; armed mail theft prompted a prosecution the gang could not neutralize through local influence alone.

The 1924 judgment and 1925 affirmance have a firmer evidentiary footing than many tales of gangland retaliation. His eventual murder illustrates the hazards of reentering the environment from which the earlier organization drew power, but its perpetrator remains unproved in the checked record. Whether he sought a full restoration of leadership or only a measure of economic security in his final years cannot be answered conclusively from the accessible sources.

MGU’s editorial approach is to preserve these distinctions. It recognizes the real harms produced by coercion and robbery, gives adequate weight to defendants’ procedural rights, and requires different levels of proof for legal outcomes, organizational labels and unsolved homicides. That method produces a more durable account of William P. “Dinty” Colbeck than either a celebratory outlaw biography or an uncritical repetition of criminal folklore. [1] [2] [7] [8] [9]

Sources and Further Reading

This bibliography prioritizes the published federal appellate opinion, federal archival finding aids, contemporaneous reports and official agency history. The principal source distinctions are explained within the biography. A link to an archive catalog is not a claim that the complete physical case file was reviewed.

  2. National Archives and Records Administration. United States v. William P. “Dinty” Colbeck et al., Case File No. 25, Southern District of Illinois, archival case file, 1924–1952.
