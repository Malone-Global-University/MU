Daniel “Dapper Dan” Hogan (c. 1880–1928)

St. Paul underworld broker, gambling and stolen-property racketeer, and central intermediary in the O’Connor layover system; killed by a car bomb in 1928.

1. Historical Overview

Daniel “Dapper Dan” Hogan (circa 1880–4 December 1928) was a central intermediary between St. Paul’s criminal underworld and parts of the city’s police and political establishment. His influence came not simply from managing illegal businesses but from administering the informal arrangement under which visiting offenders could remain in St. Paul provided they registered, paid for protection, and avoided major local crimes. The arrangement, conventionally called the O’Connor layover agreement, preceded Hogan and outlived him. He was its most recognizable criminal-side administrator during much of the 1910s and 1920s. [1] [2]

Hogan operated in a regional economy of gambling, unlawful liquor, stolen securities, and protection. Sources connect his Wabasha Street business with visiting criminals and transactions that were difficult to conduct through ordinary banks. Some writers call him St. Paul’s “Irish mob boss,” but this is a retrospective classification: the documented network was a fluid protection and racketeering system, not an American Cosa Nostra family with a formal membership roll. [2] [3] [4]

On 4 December 1928 an explosive device detonated as Hogan tried to start his car at his West Seventh Street home. He died that evening after emergency surgery. The killing attracted intense press attention and remains unsolved. Later researchers suspect his associate Harry Sawyer, also known as Harry Salter, but no documented conviction resolves responsibility for the bombing. The biography therefore separates events strongly established by the period record from subsequent explanations and legend. [4] [9]

2. Names, Birth Year, and the Limits of the Civil Record

“Danny” and “Dapper Dan” are the names most consistently associated with Hogan in newspaper-based histories and institutional accounts. Many later profiles identify him as Daniel P. Hogan and place his birth around 1880. The approximate year is consistent with the age of forty-eight reported in a 2018 reconstruction of his death, but this edition has not inspected a birth certificate to confirm his birthday, middle initial, or parentage. [4] [8]

Published material also conflicts over birthplace. A St. Paul tourism profile describes California, while the chronology of a Minnesota Then biography gives St. Paul even though its surrounding narrative does not establish that fact from a civil source. A 2026 historical-preservation nomination unexpectedly calls him “David Hogan.” Because the larger body of material uses Daniel or Danny, the page title follows that usage but treats the divergence as a source-control problem, not a reason to rewrite his name. [7] [8] [13]

The title’s “c. 1880” avoids invented precision. Birthplaces, genealogical claims, exact middle names, and family anecdotes should be revised only if supported by direct vital-record evidence. Nothing about the documented police–criminal arrangement depends upon deciding those unresolved personal particulars.

3. Early Career and Arrival in St. Paul

The most credible public histories place Hogan’s entry into the St. Paul underworld in the first decade of the twentieth century. A regional historical report identifies his first St. Paul arrest in 1909, while an interpretative local history places his arrival from California around 1906. Those dates are compatible as broad waypoints, but they are not a complete chronological record of his movements. [2] [14]

Accounts of earlier California incarceration and a progression from petty offenses to large-scale crime circulate in underworld histories. This biography does not supply a prison number, exact sentence, or lengthy early arrest narrative because the underlying files have not been collated. The provable historical importance of Hogan begins when he becomes part of the system accommodating fugitives and professional thieves in St. Paul. [5] [14]

St. Paul’s role as a railroad, financial, commercial, and political center mattered. Transportation links let offenders travel between cities, while hotels, cash businesses, and intermediaries made temporary residence possible. These were features of an ordinary growing metropolitan economy; organized criminals exploited them rather than defining the entire city.

4. The Twin Cities and the Geography of Criminal Refuge

St. Paul and Minneapolis formed a connected metropolitan region but maintained distinct police jurisdictions. For criminals whose principal offenses occurred elsewhere, the separation created opportunities: an offender could plan or finance a robbery outside St. Paul and then return to a city in which certain officials were prepared to overlook that person’s presence. The practical effect was to export violence and property crime while preserving an appearance of local order. [1] [2]

This was not genuine public security in the broad sense. The system rewarded people who could pay, made cooperation with law enforcement selective, and shifted risk onto victims in neighboring communities. It also gave criminal brokers leverage over arrivals: someone who failed to respect the local understanding could jeopardize the benefits shared by other offenders. [1]

The geography of Hogan’s career is concentrated in central St. Paul, especially Wabasha Street and his home on West Seventh Street. Later gangland narratives often aggregate every Midwestern underworld figure into one continuous organization; the more accurate picture is a collection of overlapping visitors, local operators, and corrupt patrons, not a single unified gang.

5. John O’Connor and the Layover Arrangement

St. Paul police chief John O’Connor took office on 11 June 1900. MNopedia describes the ensuing layover policy as permitting visiting criminals to remain in the city if they notified appropriate intermediaries, paid bribes, and refrained from major crimes within city limits. The method combined intelligence collection with protection for known offenders, a contradictory arrangement in which official power and illicit commerce reinforced each other. [1]

Calling the system an “agreement” should not suggest a publicly adopted ordinance or lawful immunity contract. It was an informal practice described by historians, journalists, and investigation records. For an arriving offender, a reliable local intermediary could provide access, reassure suspicious hosts, direct payments, and warn of police attention. For cooperating officials, it supplied cash and a degree of apparent predictability. [1] [14]

O’Connor’s retirement in 1920 and death in 1924 did not immediately end the scheme. Its dependence on networks and habits, rather than on a single signed instrument, explains both its longevity and its gradual loss of discipline. Hogan’s own death in 1928 was another important break, but the system persisted into the 1930s. [1]

6. William “Reddy” Griffin and the 1913 Succession

Before Hogan became the central intermediary, William H. “Reddy” Griffin performed the role for the O’Connor system. According to the Minnesota Historical Society’s MNopedia, visiting criminals checked in with Griffin at the downtown Hotel Savoy. He acted as both reception point and distributor of payments to police officials. [1]

Griffin died in 1913. Hogan then assumed the criminal-side intermediary’s position. The succession matters because it shows that Hogan neither created the layover system nor personally exercised all the authority within it. Instead he inherited a recognized office within a corrupt relationship between police power and criminal networks. [1] [14]

Later histories sometimes describe this transition as a “takeover” by a gang leader. That shorthand is incomplete. It involved acquiring the confidence of offenders and the toleration of officials, relationships whose precise mechanisms cannot be reconstructed from public retellings alone. Hogan’s authority lay in making both sides believe he could keep the arrangement operating.

7. A Criminal-Side Broker of Police Protection

Hogan’s work depended on information. Visiting offenders needed to know whom to approach; local authorities needed an informal way to learn who had arrived; and criminal businesses needed protection from raids and competing thieves. Hogan’s reported role was to translate these overlapping interests into practical arrangements. A 1926 federal investigative memorandum, summarized in a public-agency historical report, identified him as a principal contact for out-of-town criminals. [2]

That account indicates how federal authorities understood his position, but the memorandum has not been independently examined in its original file context for this edition. It should be cited as a reported bureau assessment, not as sworn testimony or a finding that every traveler personally dealt with Hogan. [2]

The network’s underlying contradiction was obvious: officials claimed to protect a city by permitting known offenders to establish a protected base. In practice, the distinction between “no crimes here” and “crimes elsewhere” made policing depend upon where victims were harmed rather than whether crimes took place.

8. The Wabasha Street Establishment and the Green Lantern Name

Hogan’s headquarters was associated with 545 Wabasha Street in downtown St. Paul. Local historical accounts commonly call the venue the Green Lantern Saloon and describe it as a social center, gambling setting, and meeting place for criminals. The building was important not as a mythic underground chamber but as a functioning point of contact among customers, protection intermediaries, and financial transactions. [2] [4] [7]

There is a subtle naming conflict in the source record. A federal investigative summary about Hogan’s later associate Harry Salter describes the venue in Hogan’s lifetime as Dapper Danny’s Restaurant, then says that Salter continued operating it and changed the name to Green Lantern after Hogan’s death. Later narratives, however, use “Green Lantern” for the Hogan-era establishment as well. This page uses the familiar descriptive name but does not claim the original sign or licensing history has been proven. [3] [7]

A complete establishment chronology would compare city directories, licenses, advertisements, photographs, and business registrations. The name question does not undermine the well-documented Wabasha Street setting, but it cautions against treating retrospective place names as fixed facts for every year.

9. Bootlegging, Gambling, and Illicit Financial Services

Hogan’s influence was connected to multiple rackets rather than a single commodity. During national Prohibition, illegal liquor moved through private supply channels while gambling and entertainment venues generated cash. A person who controlled a trusted downtown meeting place could earn money from hospitality, introduce buyers and sellers, and profit from transactions outside the reach of lawful credit markets. [2] [4] [9]

Historically reported activities also include dealing in stolen property and securities. Investigative and regional histories describe Hogan’s capacity to move illicit valuables and help owners of dubious assets convert them into usable funds. The distinction between fencing and laundering matters: the former involves handling property known or alleged to be stolen, while the latter conceals the source or movement of unlawful proceeds. [2]

This combination made Hogan useful to people who did not necessarily belong to the same gang. A visiting bank robber, liquor operator, or gambling organizer might require different services. The network was valuable because it assembled those services in one environment, not because all participants reported through one formal chain of command.

10. Stolen Bonds, Transactions, and Documentary Limits

Stolen bonds and negotiable securities appear repeatedly in descriptions of Hogan’s business. In the period’s financial system, paper instruments, cash proceeds, and informal brokers offered criminal operators methods of turning theft into spendable wealth. The fact that an establishment was discussed as a place to handle such property, however, does not prove the details of each alleged transaction. [2] [7]

Some accounts link Hogan to robberies and to the circulation of government bonds. Those broader associations should not be converted into lists of specific convictions without court citations. A 2026 historic-preservation text also connects him with the Denver Mint robbery of 1922; this edition reports that as a later allegation rather than an adjudicated role in the case. [13]

An archival reconstruction would require federal files, local indictments, testimony, and business ledgers to distinguish what the police suspected, what witnesses said, and what a jury established. The record presently supports describing Hogan as a reputed high-level fence and criminal broker, not assigning him every offense attributed to his circle.

11. Payments, Corruption, and the Appearance of Order

The layover system rested on bribery as well as informal behavioral restrictions. MNopedia describes direct payments to corrupt officials; participants benefited by avoiding interference so long as outside crimes did not draw destructive attention to St. Paul. Corruption was therefore not a side effect of organized crime but part of the mechanism that made the local criminal market sustainable. [1]

Hogan’s reputation as a peacemaker should be interpreted against that structure. He had a financial incentive to limit public bloodshed in the jurisdiction that protected him. The purported preference for negotiation over violence did not make his activity benign: it could preserve and strengthen a system in which others suffered the results of exported robbery and coercion. [4] [7]

The arrangement also eroded equal enforcement. Residents without criminal relationships could not bargain for selective policing on comparable terms. The capacity to buy tolerance exposed weaknesses in the governance of police, courts, and political oversight that would later become a central part of St. Paul’s reform narrative. [1] [2]

12. Associates, Affiliations, and the “Irish Mob” Label

Hogan is commonly classified as an Irish-American underworld leader, and that classification explains his placement in this MGU biography collection. It does not imply that every St. Paul criminal intermediary was Irish or that a unified Irish ethnic syndicate exercised an exclusive franchise in the city. The more useful unit of analysis is a brokerage network whose participants crossed ethnic and geographic boundaries. [3] [8]

One especially important associate was Harry Sawyer, described in federal records as Harry Salter, formerly Harry Sandlovitch. The records place him beside Hogan during the 1920s and identify him as operator of the former Hogan business after the 1928 killing. The relationship confirms that criminal succession could cross the ethnic boundaries suggested by convenient gang labels. [3] [4]

The term “boss” is justified by Hogan’s supervisory role and public reputation, but hierarchy should not be overstated. Some visitors were independent criminals using a hospitable jurisdiction, while others ran local operations. No trustworthy source examined for this edition supplies a formal roster of Hogan’s organization or initiation rules comparable to those attributed to Mafia families.

13. Out-of-Town Gangsters and the National Reputation of St. Paul

St. Paul’s criminal hospitality drew offenders from beyond Minnesota. National histories mention many prominent gangsters as visitors during the broader layover period, particularly in the 1930s. It would be anachronistic, however, to portray each visitor as Hogan’s client: Hogan died in 1928, before several of the city’s most famous later refuge episodes. [1] [10]

The nationwide visibility of the system grew from both police corruption and interstate offending. Railway lines, automobiles, and rapid information exchange enabled criminals to move between jurisdictions while fugitives required lodging and introductions. St. Paul became a nodal point in networks of crime rather than the singular home base of every person associated with it. [2]

Distinguishing eras is essential. John Dillinger’s later prominence and the Barker–Karpis kidnappings form part of the history of the layover system, but not Hogan’s personal operations after his death. Their inclusion here concerns institutional continuity and the failures that ultimately exposed the system.

14. Police Chief O’Connor’s Retirement and the 1920s

John O’Connor retired from police service on 29 May 1920 and died in 1924. Those events removed the official with whom the layover arrangement was most closely associated, but they did not erase criminal habits or patronage networks built over preceding decades. Hogan remained prominent during the transition into the middle of Prohibition. [1]

Power in St. Paul was not concentrated in a single personality. Police officers, elected officials, business proprietors, and criminal brokers held different kinds of leverage. Later descriptions of Hogan as a shadow ruler illuminate his influence but risk replacing a complex institutional problem with the biography of one gangster. [1] [14]

The eventual exposure of the system depended upon a combination of high-profile offenses, federal pressure, and local anti-corruption work. Hogan’s career thus provides an important early chapter in a broader political history, not a complete explanation of the city’s later crisis.

15. Violence, Reputation, and the “Peacemaker” Paradox

The “smiling peacemaker” label comes from later summaries of how Hogan reportedly resolved disputes and discouraged public feuds. His incentives were practical: gunfire, abductions, and robberies in the protected city could provoke federal intervention and destabilize the local arrangement. Mediation was therefore a tool of criminal governance. [7] [8]

Public narratives also contain allegations of violent enforcement and funding of assaults. Those accounts deserve attention but differ in evidentiary strength. It is improper to attribute a particular murder or order to Hogan unless the accusation can be connected to a named witness, a dated investigative document, or a judicial outcome. [13]

The paradox of such a figure is that reducing conflict among offenders could prolong the capacity of criminal organizations to exploit victims. A restrained style of leadership should not be conflated with lawfulness, and the public generosity ascribed to Hogan should not obscure the costs of the system he administered.

16. Respectability, Charity, and Local Memory

Period accounts remembered Hogan as unusually approachable and generous to people in need. The 1928 newspaper coverage, as summarized by later reporters, described people from both legitimate businesses and criminal circles volunteering blood during his final hours. That public response shows the breadth of his social reputation, not a verdict on the legality of his work. [4] [11]

The “Robin Hood” comparison appears in newspaper-derived retrospectives. It is useful evidence of a contemporary public image, but it should not be treated as a demonstrated accounting of gifts, thefts, and their recipients. Such labels often humanize local criminal celebrities while minimizing the harms of their underlying business model. [4]

For a scholarly biography, the coexistence of benevolence and coercion is analytically significant. A criminal leader can be personally helpful to neighbors and simultaneously profit from systemic corruption. One observation neither cancels nor establishes the other.

17. Federal Scrutiny, Arrests, and the Legal Record

Public historical reports identify a St. Paul arrest in 1909 and a federal memorandum from 1926 describing Hogan’s intermediary role. The documentary labels are valuable but do not amount to a reviewed, sequential criminal-history file. Dates of arrest, formal charges, indictment outcomes, and sentences would have to be checked against police and court records. [2]

In 1927 Hogan was reportedly indicted in connection with a railroad-station robbery and held on bail, with Harry Sawyer alleged to have helped provide bond. Secondary narratives preserve this story, but an official docket and final disposition have not been inspected here. This distinction matters: an indictment alleges participation and is not proof of guilt. [7]

The Minnesota Historical Society’s Paul Maccabee research collection includes substantial FBI, St. Paul Police, Bureau of Criminal Apprehension, and court material, as well as three subject folders for Hogan. Those archival holdings offer a route toward stricter legal reconstruction; their presence in a finding aid is not itself evidence that each reputed crime was prosecuted or proved. [5]

18. Harry Sawyer: Lieutenant, Successor, and Suspect

Harry Sawyer—also called Harry Salter in federal records and formerly Harry Sandlovitch—was closely associated with Hogan’s downtown business. The FBI’s later Bremer-kidnapping file describes him as Hogan’s lieutenant and states that he continued to manage the operation after 4 December 1928. This provides stronger documentation for a business and leadership succession than for any proposed motive in the killing. [3]

Researchers have advanced the view that Sawyer profited from Hogan’s removal. Later newspaper accounts refer to disputed money, access to safes, and other alleged grievances. Those descriptions are hypotheses built from interviews and historical reconstruction; they are not findings of guilt by a jury or court. [4] [7]

Sawyer’s subsequent role in the 1934 Edward Bremer kidnapping is documented separately. Evidence of later serious criminal conduct may help explain why historians examine his activities, but it is logically insufficient to establish that he commissioned the earlier bombing. The homicide remains unsolved. [3] [4]

19. Household, the West Seventh Street Home, and Privacy

The home at 1607 West Seventh Street is a well-attested setting of Hogan’s death. It appears in local architectural history, later federal investigative records, and retrospective newspaper accounts. The address is important because it locates the assassination in a domestic setting rather than at his downtown business. [10] [12]

Published narratives identify his widow as Leila Hogan and mention family members present around the final day. The surviving record consulted here does not justify expanding that into an unverified family tree or discussing descendants as though they participated in his activities. [7]

Some later reconstructions describe warnings, men seen near the garage, or conversations about anticipated danger. These details may help explain investigative theories, but they come through secondary reconstructions and should not be written as if this edition had examined the original witness statements.

20. 4 December 1928: The Garage Explosion

On Tuesday, 4 December 1928, Hogan entered his Paige automobile in a garage at his West Seventh Street residence. An explosive device detonated as he engaged the starter. Contemporary press-based reporting identifies the device as placed beneath the vehicle’s floorboards and describes severe injuries, especially to his right leg. [4] [9] [10]

Investigators considered the blast deliberate. Later narratives refer to nitroglycerin and a starter-triggered mechanism. For historical interpretation, the significant facts are targeted placement, the private garage, and the timing of detonation; this profile does not reconstruct device assembly or operational details. [4] [9]

The attack is sometimes called the first automobile-bomb assassination in American history. The evidence supports describing it more cautiously as an early, widely reported example. “First” claims depend on definitions and systematic comparisons with earlier vehicle and explosive attacks that no single Hogan biography can establish. [9]

21. The Hospital, Emergency Treatment, and Death

The explosion did not kill Hogan immediately. He was taken for emergency care; his badly injured leg was amputated, and doctors attempted to stabilize extensive blood loss. Regional reporters describe a gathering of associates and citizens, including people offering blood for transfusion. His condition worsened, and he died that evening. [4] [11]

The Star Tribune’s reconstruction gives the time of death as 8:55 p.m.; broader narrative accounts round the interval to approximately nine hours after the morning blast. His death date is consistently reported as 4 December 1928, while the major newspaper coverage appeared on 5 December. [4] [7]

There is no reliable basis here for attributing a dying declaration identifying a killer. Accounts describing Hogan’s denial of knowing who attacked him must be distinguished from police-authenticated verbatim interviews. That distinction is especially important when interpreting alleged motives and suspects. [4]

22. The Bomb Investigation and Competing Motives

Police confronted several possible motives: disagreements over illicit financial proceeds, resentments against local protection rules, and retaliation by criminals whose interests Hogan had obstructed. Contemporary reporting described underworld anger, but public anger and rumor are not the same as an evidentiary case against a named suspect. [4] [7]

Later researchers, notably Paul Maccabee as interviewed by Curt Brown in 2018, identified Sawyer as the most plausible organizer. The proposed case is circumstantial—benefit from succession, alleged financial disputes, and claimed access—rather than a prosecution proved beyond reasonable doubt. The distinction must remain clear whenever Sawyer is named. [4]

As of the sources reviewed for this edition, the homicide is recorded as unsolved. There is no verified conviction or court finding establishing the person who planted the bomb or ordered its placement. Historical scholarship may evaluate the strongest theory while leaving the legal outcome unresolved.

23. Funeral, Burial, and Public Response

Hogan received an elaborate funeral attended by an unusually broad cross-section of St. Paul society. Later reconstructions describe extensive floral tributes, numerous cars, and mourners from both legitimate and illicit circles. Estimates of attendance and cost differ in retellings and should be treated as reported figures rather than independently counted totals. [7]

Local history and historic-preservation materials place his burial at Calvary Cemetery in St. Paul. The burial setting is relevant both as an endpoint in the individual biography and as part of the way Prohibition-era criminal figures became embedded in civic memory. [8] [13]

The prominence of the funeral should be read alongside contemporary newspaper rhetoric. Public displays of grief measured local relationships and reputational influence, not proof that the broader population supported the activities of Hogan’s organization.

24. Succession After 1928 and the Limits of Continuity

Federal investigative materials and regional histories identify Harry Sawyer as the successor operator of Hogan’s Wabasha Street establishment and an important intermediary in the subsequent underworld. This suggests continuation of some networks, though it does not mean the organizational arrangements were unchanged or that one person simply inherited every business relationship. [2] [3]

The post-Hogan years saw increasingly conspicuous violent crime, including kidnappings that challenged the city’s prior understanding that major offenses should not occur in St. Paul itself. The 1933 kidnapping of brewer William Hamm and the 1934 kidnapping of banker Edward Bremer heightened federal and civic attention. [1] [2]

It would be a chronological error to count these kidnappings among Hogan’s personal crimes: he had been dead for years. They belong in his biography because they illuminate the fragility of the institutional structure in which his influence developed.

25. 1933–1935: Exposure and Collapse of the Layover System

The end of Prohibition in 1933 altered the economics of illicit liquor while the Barker–Karpis gang’s kidnapping campaigns drew national attention. Federal interventions and expanded investigative authority changed the cost of protecting visiting criminals. At the same time, local journalists and reformers intensified scrutiny of police misconduct. [1]

MNopedia attributes a major role to journalist Howard Kahn and investigations involving Chicago detective Jamie Wallace. The resulting wiretaps and 1935 reporting exposed contacts between officials and organized criminals. The historical sequence makes clear that the system’s collapse arose from institutional investigation and public pressure, not merely from Hogan’s 1928 death. [1]

By 1935 a wave of resignations and prosecutions had dismantled the core arrangement. In retrospect, Hogan represents a mature phase of the layover system; the later collapse demonstrated the vulnerabilities of local protection networks when exposed to outside law enforcement and independent reporting. [1] [2]

26. Historical Interpretation: The Broker, the Legend, and the Archive

Popular histories tend to present Hogan through a binary of kindly neighborhood fixer and ruthless Irish underworld boss. Both images capture elements of the later narrative; neither substitutes for a record-based analysis of his position in police corruption and stolen-property markets. The central question is how an intermediary could operate so effectively between people whose public roles were supposed to be adversarial. [1] [2] [4]

Sources are uneven. The institutional MNopedia account offers a useful chronology of policy and reform. The Ramsey County historic-context study synthesizes scholarship and some bureau references. The FBI file supports facts about Harry Salter/Sawyer, not a judicial conclusion about homicide. The 1928 newspapers are contemporary evidence filtered through reporting conventions, while modern reconstructions preserve research leads but sometimes repeat unresolved details as settled biography. [1] [2] [3] [4]

The Minnesota Historical Society preserves Maccabee’s research folders, a practical starting point for further manuscript and case-file work. A future critical edition would collate civil records, contemporaneous 1928 newspapers, the 1926 federal memorandum, 1927 court files, business directories, and the original bombing investigation. Until then, the page keeps documented outcomes, reported allegations, and interpretative claims visibly distinct. [5]

Sources and Further Reading

Source numbers in the text point to the items below. This page uses institutional public history, agency investigative summaries, contemporary journalism cited through later reporting, and bibliographic archival guides. Online access to a description of an archive or book does not mean the underlying files or every printed page were inspected. No external photographs are copied into this file.

  1. Matt Reicher, “O’Connor Layover Agreement,” MNopedia (Minnesota Historical Society), first published 2014, revised 2025. Source or catalog link. Public-history institutional account of the protection arrangement and Griffin-to-Hogan succession; chronology of its 1935 collapse.
  2. Garneth O. Peterson et al., Historic Context of the Riverview Modern Streetcar Corridor in Saint Paul, 1850–1980 (Ramsey County Regional Railroad Authority, 2020), 132–133. Source or catalog link. Public-agency historical synthesis discussing 545 Wabasha Street, the 1926 bureau memo, 1909 arrest, and aftermath. PDF search text consulted; photographic pages not independently collated.
  3. U.S. Department of Justice, FBI Vault, Barker–Karpis Gang / Bremer Kidnapping, Part 193, biographical memorandum on Harry Salter (Sawyer). Source or catalog link. Federal investigative-file account identifying Harry Salter/Sawyer as Hogan’s lieutenant and later operator of the establishment; an investigative summary, not a finding about the Hogan murder.
  4. Curt Brown, “1928 car bombing that killed St. Paul mob boss ‘Dapper Dan’ Hogan still unsolved,” Minnesota Star Tribune, 28 April 2018. Source or catalog link. Retrospective based on the 1928 press and interviews with researcher Paul Maccabee; distinguishes murder suspicion from charges.
  5. Minnesota Historical Society, “Paul Maccabee: An Inventory of His St. Paul Gangster History Research Collection,” finding aid. Source or catalog link. Archival repository guide identifying three Hogan subject folders and police, FBI, newspaper, interview, and judicial-record holdings. The underlying folders were not examined for this edition.
  6. Paul Maccabee, John Dillinger Slept Here: A Crooks’ Tour of Crime and Corruption in St. Paul, 1920–1936 (Minnesota Historical Society Press, 1995). Source or catalog link. Major book-length research work identified in institutional finding aid and the Ramsey County report; specific print pages were not independently consulted here.
  7. Matt Reicher, “Life and Death of Daniel ‘Dapper Dan’ Hogan (1880–1928),” Minnesota Then, published 19 December 2025, updated 19 June 2026. Source or catalog link. Later public-history reconstruction with 1928 newspaper references; contains a contradictory birthplace in its own chronology and should not be treated as a civil register.
  8. Visit Saint Paul, “Gangster Past,” visitor-history profile of “Dapper” Danny Hogan. Source or catalog link. Municipal tourism history with approximate birth year, California birthplace, death date, and identification of the unsolved case.
  9. HISTORY.com Editors, “‘Irish Godfather’ killed by car bomb in St. Paul, Minnesota,” originally 2010; last updated 2025. Source or catalog link. Secondary narrative of the attack and the sources for “first car bomb” claims; its superlatives require caution.
  10. Historic Twin Cities, “Twin Cities Gangster Locations and Hideouts” and “Dapper Dan” Hogan home at 1607 West Seventh Street. Source or catalog link. Local architectural and place-history listing; links to the preserved location account and 1928 newspaper imagery.
  11. Minneapolis Morning Tribune, “Danny Hogan Slain; Victim of Auto Bomb,” 5 December 1928, p. 1; contemporary press account identified in public historical literature. Source or catalog link. Contemporary reporting accessed through later quotation/description, not independently page-by-page collated against the original newspaper facsimile.
  12. FBI Vault, Kansas City Massacre Part 61, September 1934 investigative memorandum identifying 1607 West Seventh Street as Hogan’s former address. Source or catalog link. Federal retrospective address corroboration; does not solve his murder.
  13. Minnesota State Historic Preservation Office, Calvary Cemetery National Register nomination (2026), discussion of John O’Connor and “Dapper Dan” Hogan. Source or catalog link. Historic-preservation synthesis; names him “David Hogan” in an apparent conflict with multiple Daniel/Danny sources, illustrating need for civil-register verification.
  14. Robert O’Connor, “The Big Fellow and the Cardinal,” 3:AM Magazine, 2011. Source or catalog link. Local historical essay about the O’Connor system and the transition from William Griffin to Hogan; interpretative secondary account.

Research qualification: A first-edition historical synthesis. Civil birth records, property and business-license histories, the 1926 federal memorandum in original file context, the complete 1927 indictment and disposition, original 1928 autopsy and police investigation, Maccabee’s three Hogan subject folders, and full contemporary newspaper facsimiles have not been exhaustively collated. The 2026 cemetery nomination’s “David” name and the conflicting Green Lantern chronology are presented as unresolved discrepancies, not as a new discovery of a second person.
