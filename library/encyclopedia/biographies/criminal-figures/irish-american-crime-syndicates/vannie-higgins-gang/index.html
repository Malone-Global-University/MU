Vannie Higgins Gang Biographies

Charles “Vannie” Higgins, Brooklyn rum-running, changing criminal alliances, and the 1932 assassination.

Collection Overview

The Vannie Higgins Gang directory covers the criminal network associated with Charles Van Wyck “Vannie” Higgins (1897–1932), a prominent Prohibition-era rum-runner based in Brooklyn, New York. During the 1920s and early 1930s, Higgins became associated with illicit liquor shipments and distribution, transport arrangements, and shifting connections among New York-area criminal organizations.

Organizational terminology: “Vannie Higgins Gang” is an MGU biographical-directory designation. Contemporary journalists described Higgins as a gang leader and referred to his associates as a gang, but that does not establish a formally named or continuously organized syndicate with an authenticated membership roll. The directory therefore distinguishes direct leadership from commercial alliances, competitors, and purported associates.

Higgins had reported connections with Jack “Legs” Diamond, Owney Madden, and Frank Costello. Rivalry over liquor routes and beer markets involved other prominent racketeers, and contemporary reports also raised questions about the protection of illegal imports by corrupt officials. Higgins was fatally shot in Brooklyn in June 1932. The identity of his attackers and the motive were not conclusively established. The directory presently includes one assigned canonical biography.

Research Themes

  • Brooklyn criminal enterprises before and during national Prohibition.
  • Maritime rum-running, vessels, landing points, and illegal liquor distribution.
  • Fluid alliances among Brooklyn, Long Island, and Manhattan criminal networks.
  • Higgins's relationship with Jack “Legs” Diamond, Owney Madden, and Frank Costello.
  • Competition involving Dutch Schultz and Anthony “Little Augie” Carfano.
  • The 1929 Owl's Head Tavern shooting, competing accounts, and its investigation.
  • Higgins's 1931 stabbing and contemporaneous accounts of underworld disputes.
  • The 1932 federal liquor-conspiracy proceeding and documented acquittals.
  • Alleged police protection for rum-running and the distinction between accusations and findings.
  • The June 1932 shooting of Higgins and the unresolved responsibility for his death.

Principal Biography

  1. Charles Van Wyck “Vannie” Higgins

    Historical classification: Brooklyn bootlegging leader and organizer of an associated criminal network. 1897–June 19, 1932. Higgins was born in Bay Ridge, Brooklyn, and gained notoriety during Prohibition through reported liquor-smuggling and distribution operations. Period newspaper coverage associated his circle with rum-running vessels, transportation interests, and competition for the illegal alcohol market. Descriptions of his power and wealth should be compared with specific criminal proceedings rather than accepted from newspaper epithets alone.

    Higgins was associated at different times with Jack “Legs” Diamond, and later scholarship also describes connections with Owney Madden, Frank Costello, and other liquor-market operators. These connections did not create one consolidated gang. The Gopher Gang, Big Bill Dwyer's syndicate, the criminal network around Diamond, and Higgins's own group have distinguishable histories.

    Higgins survived a serious stabbing in 1931. Research drawing on contemporary reports documents a January 1932 federal trial concerning an alleged liquor conspiracy and police protection, which ended in acquittals for Higgins and other remaining defendants. During the night of June 18–19, 1932, he was shot after attending a children's event with his family in Brooklyn; his young daughter was also injured. He died of his wounds on June 19. No individual or organization should be described as conclusively responsible for the killing on the basis of competing press theories.

Historical Context and Organizational Development

Brooklyn origins: Higgins's early history was rooted in Brooklyn, including Bay Ridge. Accounts describe his involvement in street crime prior to the expansion of Prohibition markets. The geography of Higgins's later liquor operations should not be merged with Owney Madden's earlier Hell's Kitchen-based Gopher Gang or the different waterfront organization of the White Hand Gang.

Prohibition and the waterfront: The criminalization of alcohol distribution created incentives for maritime importing, storage, trucking, and corrupt protection arrangements. Historical reporting about Higgins includes rum-running boats and transport infrastructure, but exact claims about the number of vessels, aircraft, or total revenue require verification against period records.

Beer-market competition: Disputes involving Higgins, Jack “Legs” Diamond, and criminal figures in the competing New York liquor market extended across borough boundaries. Contemporary accounts connected Diamond and Higgins with violence attributed to rivals aligned with Anthony “Little Augie” Carfano, while other sources discussed competition with Dutch Schultz. Gang alliances and rivalries shifted; a contemporary report attributing an attack to a group is not itself a judicial determination.

Owl's Head Tavern, March 1929: A violent episode in Brooklyn resulted in the death of a police officer amid contradictory accounts involving alleged participants and investigators. Higgins was associated with the ensuing inquiry in newspaper coverage. A complete incident history should separate evidence of the event, individual allegations, arrests, and legal outcomes rather than treating every rumor as confirmed.

1931 violence: Higgins suffered knife wounds during a reported dispute in 1931 and survived. The incident attracted contemporary attention, but specific motives and responsibility should remain attributed where the record is uncertain.

Federal liquor case, January 1932: Research based on contemporary newspapers describes a federal conspiracy prosecution implicating Higgins and members of a Long Island police department in alleged protection of illegal alcohol shipments. Accounts report acquittals following trial. Those outcomes must be distinguished from the underlying allegations and independently checked against original court files where available.

Assassination, June 1932: Higgins was attacked in Brooklyn after a family event and died on June 19; his daughter sustained an injury in the gunfire. The attack was widely covered by newspapers, which circulated competing theories about its motive and authorship. Responsibility for the murder remains disputed and should not be assigned to any faction without corroboration.

Institutional significance: The record illustrates the organization of illicit commerce, the costs of violence and corruption, and the difficulty of reconstructing criminal groups when surviving evidence consists partly of adversarial testimony, police allegations, and sensational reporting.

Associated Figures and Rival Networks

  • Jack “Legs” Diamond — reported ally: Contemporary accounts associated Diamond and Higgins with Brooklyn liquor distribution. Their relationship should be evaluated for the specific year and incident, not treated as permanent membership in one group.
  • Owney Madden — connected bootlegging operator: Reported contact with Higgins and a separate career that included leadership of the Gopher Gang. See Madden's canonical biography under that directory.
  • Frank Costello — connected liquor-market figure: Linked to Higgins in later historical accounts; his biographical classification belongs in the Italian-American Mafia collection, not as an assumed member of Higgins's crew.
  • Leo Steinberg — reported commercial associate: A Long Island liquor-market figure whose particular connections should be established through source-specific contemporary records.
  • Anthony “Little Augie” Carfano — rival-market figure: Appears in reports of Brooklyn liquor conflicts. His presence in an account does not establish membership in Higgins's organization.
  • Dutch Schultz — competing racketeer: Important to the wider Manhattan beer wars; not an authenticated member of Higgins's organization or conclusively identified as Higgins's killer.

These are relationship classifications rather than a verified membership roster. Only Higgins receives a linked biography in this directory.

Editorial Standards

Collection scope: One selected biography from the MGU Irish-American criminal-figures filing plan. This folder is an additional directory outside the original 29-organization index; it may be added to that parent index when deployed.

  • Terminology: Use “Vannie Higgins Gang” as a descriptive MGU category, without claiming that a formal gang charter or reliable complete roster survives.
  • Affiliations: Distinguish followers, commercial associates, partners, competitors, and enemies. Do not infer a person's gang membership from mere contact.
  • Legal precision: Differentiate arrests, allegations, acquittals, convictions, firsthand accounts, and disputed retrospective claims.
  • Unsolved homicide: Do not assign responsibility for the June 1932 shooting to a named rival as established fact.
  • Geography: Distinguish Brooklyn operations from related but separate Manhattan and Long Island organizations.
  • Sources: Prefer digitized court records and newspaper originals, supported by carefully sourced historical scholarship.
  • Public impact: Analyze the consequences of corruption, intimidation, and violence without romanticizing organized crime.
  • Canonical filing: Maintain one vannie-higgins.html biography and link from related collections rather than duplicating the entry.

Biography linking: vannie-higgins.html is the assigned filename from the MGU filing plan. Deploy the biography file beside this index.html to activate its relative link. Its live publication has not been independently verified.

Research Starting Points

These sources provide historical context and pathways toward archival verification, not a complete roster. Review the cited contemporary newspaper records before adopting a detailed allegation in the complete biography.

  • Rebecca Rector, New York Almanack — “Vannie Higgins: ‘Brooklyn's Last Irish Boss’” (April 14, 2023) — researched overview of Higgins's Brooklyn origins, rum-running interests, and death.
  • Tom Hunt, MobHistory — “Brooklyn's Last Irish Gang Boss Is Eliminated, 1932” (December 5, 2025) — source-noted analysis of Higgins's final months, the 1932 federal proceeding, and assassination reports.
  • Tom Hunt, MobHistory — “‘Vannie’ Reaches the Rum-Running Big Leagues, 1929–1930” — footnoted treatment of illicit liquor operations and the Owl's Head Tavern investigation.
  • The Camden Chronicle — “Storm of Bullets Kill Racketeer” (June 24, 1932) — a digitized contemporaneous report preserved by the University of South Carolina historical newspaper program.
  • TIME — “Crime: Rumors of War” (September 8, 1930) — contemporary reporting on liquor-market territorial disputes; separate journalistic allegations from established facts.
