What were Irish-American crime syndicates?

Irish-American criminal organizations were not one national “Irish Mafia.” The term Irish-American Crime Syndicates is an editorial category for distinct American gangs, bootlegging operations, gambling enterprises, waterfront networks, and later racketeering groups associated with Irish-American leadership or historical communities. Individual organizations had different origins, ethnic compositions, leadership practices, criminal markets, and lifespans.

Early urban gangs were often shaped by poverty, political patronage, neighborhood conflict, and nineteenth-century policing. Later criminal enterprises developed more durable revenue structures through illegal alcohol sales, gambling, cargo theft, labor corruption, loansharking, and narcotics. Some Irish-led groups were multiethnic, while others worked with, competed against, or paid particular shares to Italian-American Mafia organizations. Such relationships did not make them formal La Cosa Nostra families.[1][4][5]

Scope of this collection. Entries document criminal organizations and historical institutional relationships; they do not characterize Irish Americans generally. Ancestry is not a substitute for evidence of membership, and the inclusion of nineteenth-century street gangs provides historical context rather than implying that every gang qualified as a modern organized-crime syndicate.

New York City

From the Five Points gangs and waterfront syndicates to the Westies. The organizations below were not subsidiaries of one national Irish command structure.

Forty Thieves

Principal period: c. 1820s–1850s · Classification: Five Points street gang

An early Irish-associated Five Points gang whose reported theft quotas and internal rules illustrate organized neighborhood offending before the later bootlegging era. It was not a twentieth-century Mafia family.[1]

Dedicated article: forty-thieves.html (planned filename; not yet verified published)

Dead Rabbits and Roach Guards

Principal period: c. 1840s–1850s · Classification: Interrelated Five Points street-gang identities

The Dead Rabbits were associated with Irish immigrant street fighting, theft, and the 1857 gang riots. Accounts disagree whether the Dead Rabbits began as a separate Roach Guards offshoot or were sometimes an alternative designation for the same network; their relationship requires careful treatment.[1]

Dedicated article: dead-rabbits.html (planned filename; not yet verified published)

Whyos

Principal period: c. 1860s–1890s · Classification: Urban criminal gang

A successor generation of Five Points offenders associated with robbery, gambling, coercion, and violence for hire. Their criminal activity and commercialized rackets mark a transition between street-gang activity and more enduring urban criminal enterprises.[1]

Dedicated article: whyos.html (planned filename; not yet verified published)

Gopher Gang

Principal period: c. 1890s–1910s · Classification: Hell’s Kitchen street gang

A West Side Manhattan gang associated with Owney Madden and early twentieth-century neighborhood violence. It preceded, and should not be equated with, the later Spillane organization or the Westies.[2]

Dedicated article: gopher-gang.html (planned filename; not yet verified published)

Hudson Dusters

Principal period: c. 1890s–1910s · Classification: Multiethnic urban gang

A Greenwich Village–area gang sometimes grouped with Irish-American gangs but more accurately described as multiethnic. Included for its place in the historical urban-gang landscape; the composition of its membership should not be reduced to one ancestry.[2]

Dedicated article: hudson-dusters.html (planned filename; not yet verified published)

White Hand Gang

Principal period: c. 1900s–1925 · Classification: Waterfront criminal syndicate

A Brooklyn waterfront criminal network associated with leaders including Dinny Meehan, Bill Lovett, and Richard Lonergan. The name is frequently used for overlapping or successive waterfront factions, not necessarily one stable hierarchy throughout the whole period.[3]

Dedicated article: white-hand-gang.html (planned filename; not yet verified published)

Big Bill Dwyer Syndicate

Principal period: 1920s · Classification: Prohibition bootlegging enterprise

William “Big Bill” Dwyer helped organize major illegal liquor operations during Prohibition. This is best cataloged as a bootlegging enterprise and associated network rather than an assumed formal ethnic Mafia family.[2]

Dedicated article: big-bill-dwyer-syndicate.html (planned filename; not yet verified published)

Vannie Higgins Gang

Principal period: late 1920s–early 1930s · Classification: Brooklyn bootlegging network

A Prohibition-era Brooklyn organization identified with Charles “Vannie” Higgins. Its independent commercial operations warrant a separate entry, subject to additional case-by-case source work.[14]

Dedicated article: vannie-higgins-gang.html (planned filename; not yet verified published)

Mickey Spillane Organization

Principal period: c. 1950s–1970s · Classification: Hell’s Kitchen racketeering organization

Mickey Spillane was a prominent earlier Hell’s Kitchen underworld leader. The organization associated with him should not be collapsed into Jimmy Coonan’s later Westies: federal appellate findings describe the Westies as challenging and displacing Spillane’s control.[4]

Dedicated article: mickey-spillane-gang.html (planned filename; not yet verified published)

The Westies

Principal period: mid-1960s–late 1980s · Classification: Hell’s Kitchen racketeering enterprise

Associated especially with James Coonan and Francis “Mickey” Featherstone, the Westies were a distinct racketeering enterprise operating in labor-related extortion, loansharking, narcotics, and violence. Their dealings with Italian-American Mafia figures did not make them an inducted Cosa Nostra family.[4]

Dedicated article: westies.html (planned filename; not yet verified published)

Chicago

Irish-led Prohibition syndicates, political gangs, and Capone-era rivals. The organizations below were not subsidiaries of one national Irish command structure.

Dukies

Principal period: late nineteenth century · Classification: Irish neighborhood street gang

An early stockyards-area gang documented in Chicago’s urban history. Its prominence belongs to the pre-Prohibition world of neighborhood violence and local political patronage, rather than the later bootlegging syndicate model.[5]

Dedicated article: dukies.html (planned filename; not yet verified published)

Shielders

Principal period: late nineteenth century · Classification: Irish neighborhood street gang

Another stockyards-area Irish gang described in the Encyclopedia of Chicago. The Shielders were distinct from the Dukies even though both belonged to the same broader urban-gang environment.[5]

Dedicated article: shielders.html (planned filename; not yet verified published)

Ragen’s Colts

Principal period: early twentieth century–1930s · Classification: Athletic-club and political gang

An Irish-associated Chicago group tied to ward politics and anti-Black violence, notably during the 1919 Chicago race riot. Its political and racist violence should be treated directly, not romanticized as Prohibition entrepreneurship.[5]

Dedicated article: ragens-colts.html (planned filename; not yet verified published)

North Side Gang

Principal period: c. 1920s–1930s · Classification: Irish-led, multiethnic bootlegging syndicate

Led successively by figures including Dean O’Banion, Hymie Weiss, Vincent Drucci, and Bugs Moran. This was an independent rival of Torrio and Capone, but its leadership was ethnically mixed; its classification is based on origins and historical association, not exclusively Irish membership.[6]

Dedicated article: north-side-gang.html (planned filename; not yet verified published)

Valley Gang

Principal period: late nineteenth century–1930s · Classification: Irish-American gang turned bootlegging syndicate

A street-gang lineage that expanded into substantial Prohibition liquor operations under Terry Druggan and Frankie Lake. It often cooperated with Torrio–Capone interests and must not be mistaken for the North Side Gang.[7]

Dedicated article: valley-gang.html (planned filename; not yet verified published)

Touhy Gang

Principal period: 1920s–1930s · Classification: Independent criminal syndicate

Roger Touhy’s Chicago-area group became associated with bootlegging, kidnapping allegations, and violent rivalry involving Capone interests. Contemporary FBI records establish the group’s existence and later federal pursuit, while disputed criminal accusations require separate evidentiary assessment.[8]

Dedicated article: touhy-gang.html (planned filename; not yet verified published)

West Side O’Donnell Gang

Principal period: 1920s · Classification: Chicago bootlegging gang

An independent O’Donnell-associated criminal operation on Chicago’s West Side. Its leadership, territory, and disputes must be distinguished from the separately named South Side O’Donnell group.[7]

Dedicated article: west-side-odonnell.html (planned filename; not yet verified published)

South Side O’Donnell Gang

Principal period: 1920s · Classification: Chicago bootlegging gang

A separate Irish-associated Prohibition faction active in Chicago’s South Side gang conflicts. Similar surnames do not establish a common command structure with the West Side O’Donnells.[7]

Dedicated article: south-side-odonnell.html (planned filename; not yet verified published)

Saltis–McErlane Gang

Principal period: 1920s · Classification: Multiethnic Prohibition criminal syndicate

An organization associated with Joseph Saltis and Frank McErlane. Included because of Irish-American leadership participation, but it should be described as mixed rather than presented as exclusively Irish.[7]

Killeen Gang

Winter Hill Gang

The enterprise linked Howard Winter’s Somerville group with associates from South Boston and later became associated with Whitey Bulger and Stephen Flemmi. Its structure and personnel changed over time; the Bulger period should not be projected onto its origins.[9]

Rooted in St. Louis’s Kerry Patch neighborhood and associated with Tom Egan and Thomas “Snake” Kinney, Egan’s Rats combined robbery, coercion, and political intimidation before the Prohibition era. Their conflicts with the Hogan faction are central to the city’s history.[11]

Historical Development

Nineteenth-century urban gangs

Some of the earliest well-documented Irish-associated groups operated in New York’s Five Points and Chicago’s working-class districts. Street fighting, robbery, political intimidation, and neighborhood rivalry occurred amid immigration, economic insecurity, nativist hostility, and local electoral machines. The organizational continuity from these gangs to later twentieth-century syndicates should not be presumed.[1][5]

Prohibition and organized rackets, 1920–1933

The national prohibition of alcoholic beverages created a new illegal wholesale and retail market. Some criminal networks accumulated capital, broadened distribution, and engaged in coordinated violence and police corruption. New York, Chicago, St. Louis, and St. Paul each developed separate criminal organizations, rather than branches directed by a single Irish national authority.[6][8][11]

Post-Prohibition regional syndicates

Other enterprises developed around waterfront labor, burglary, illegal gambling, extortion, and eventually narcotics. By the later twentieth century, the Westies in New York, Winter Hill in Greater Boston, and the K&A Gang in Philadelphia represented distinct organizational histories, with different relationships to Italian-American Mafia families and law enforcement.[4][9][10]

Organizational Structure and Relationships

Unlike American La Cosa Nostra’s system of formally recognized families and made members, Irish-American criminal groups did not share one universal induction ceremony, Commission, national hierarchy, or membership rule. Some groups relied on neighborhood ties or individual leadership; others formed networks through criminal opportunities, alliances, and division of proceeds.

  • Independent organization: a group with its own leadership and operations, such as the North Side Gang in Chicago.
  • Successor or rival: a later group that displaced an earlier leader in the same territory; Coonan’s Westies did not simply continue Mickey Spillane’s organization unchanged.[4]
  • Alliance or merger: previously separate groups joining or cooperating; federal prosecutors described a merger of Boston-area factions in the formation of the enterprise associated with Winter Hill.[9]
  • Business relationship: exchange of services, protection, payments, or joint criminal proceeds with an Italian-American Mafia group; this was not equivalent to induction into Cosa Nostra.
  • Ethnic identification: a historical origin or leadership association, not proof that every participant had the same ancestry.

The category also distinguishes street gangs from crime syndicates: some early organizations principally fought for neighborhood power, while later syndicates maintained sustained revenue-producing rackets. The boundary was sometimes gradual and should be determined for each group rather than from its name alone.

Principal Criminal Enterprises

Activities varied by city and time; no one roster describes the conduct of every organization listed here.

  • Bootlegging and liquor distribution: illegal production, transport, wholesale distribution, and retail sales during Prohibition.
  • Gambling and betting: unlawful games, sports betting, bookmaking, and protection demands.
  • Robbery, burglary, and theft: street theft, commercial burglary, warehouse losses, and stolen-goods resale.
  • Waterfront and labor racketeering: theft, coercion, access to jobs or unions, and threats affecting legitimate commerce.
  • Extortion and loansharking: threats or violence to obtain payments or enforce unlawful debts.
  • Narcotics trafficking: documented in some later networks, including the Westies, Winter Hill enterprise, and K&A Gang.[4][9][10]
  • Political corruption and intimidation: historical connections between gangs, ward politics, and coercive election activity, particularly in nineteenth- and early twentieth-century cities.[5][11]

Selected Historical Chronology

  1. 1820s–1850s: Five Points groups such as the Forty Thieves and Dead Rabbits become prominent in New York’s gang history. [1]
  2. Late 1800s: Chicago’s Dukies and Shielders operate in the stockyards environment; St. Louis’s later Egan’s Rats develop from Irish neighborhood gang culture. [5,11]
  3. 1900s–1910s: New York’s Gopher Gang and White Hand waterfront organizations become part of the city’s changing criminal landscape.
  4. 1919: Chicago’s race riot exposes the role of white ethnic gangs, including Ragen’s Colts, in racist violence. [5]
  5. 1920–1933: Prohibition enlarges bootlegging opportunities for Irish-led and multiethnic syndicates in several cities.
  6. 1924–1929: Dean O’Banion’s murder, successive North Side leadership changes, and the 1929 St. Valentine’s Day Massacre mark escalation in Chicago’s gang conflicts. [6]
  7. 1928: St. Paul underworld figure Danny Hogan is killed; his network is distinct from the Hogan Gang in St. Louis. [13]
  8. 1960s–1970s: Boston-area Charlestown, Killeen, and Mullen/Mullins conflicts precede the Winter Hill network’s transformation. [9]
  9. Mid-1960s–1980s: Coonan’s Westies displace Spillane’s dominance in parts of Hell’s Kitchen; federal prosecutions later document their racketeering enterprise. [4]
  10. 1977: Danny Greene is killed by a car bomb in the Cleveland area. [12]
  11. 1987–1991: Federal proceedings against the Westies lead to significant racketeering convictions and appellate opinions. [4]
  12. 2013: A federal jury convicts Whitey Bulger in a major racketeering and murder prosecution, following his 2011 arrest. [9]

Chronology is selective. Approximate dates describe eras of activity and should not be mistaken for exact formation or dissolution dates.

Investigations, Prosecutions, and Historical Evidence

Modern federal criminal cases provide strong documentary evidence for some later organizations. The Second Circuit’s 1991 Westies opinion describes the Coonan organization, its rivalry with Spillane, and proven racketeering activity; the underlying defendants’ individual convictions and appellate findings should be kept distinct from additional popular allegations.[4]

For Greater Boston, a federal superseding indictment describes the formation of a group involving Somerville and South Boston participants. Because indictments are charging documents, claims made by prosecutors must not be presented as verdicts. The Department of Justice’s 2013 account of Whitey Bulger’s conviction separately records adjudicated racketeering and murder offenses.[9]

Nineteenth-century and Prohibition-era subjects require different evidence: contemporary newspapers, local archives, court documents, scholarly histories, and police records may disagree. Criminal reputations, sensational nicknames, and popular books are not equivalent to corroborated organizational membership or specific adjudicated acts.

Definitions and Important Distinctions

Irish-American Crime Syndicates
An MGU editorial collection of distinct criminal organizations associated with Irish-American leadership or historical organized-crime traditions; not one national organization.
Irish Mob
A common shorthand for various regional Irish-associated criminal enterprises; not evidence of one shared chain of command.
Street Gang
A localized group associated with neighborhood activities, conflict, and sometimes crime; not necessarily a continuing racketeering enterprise.
Criminal Syndicate
A relatively enduring enterprise or network that coordinates criminal activities and revenue-generating rackets.
American La Cosa Nostra
A distinct Italian-American Mafia family system. Relationships with Irish-American groups varied by time and city; economic cooperation did not establish formal LCN membership.
Predecessor, Successor, and Alias
A predecessor may give rise to a later organization, a successor may replace one, and an alias may be another name for the same group. They should not automatically produce separate encyclopedia profiles.
