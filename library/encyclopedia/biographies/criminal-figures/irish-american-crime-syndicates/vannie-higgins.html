Vannie Higgins (c. 1897/1898–1932)

Brooklyn bootlegging entrepreneur and suspected racketeer: Navy veteran, Bay Ridge liquor operator, subject of major Prohibition investigations, and victim of an unresolved 1932 killing.

1. Historical Overview

Charles “Vannie” Higgins occupied a conspicuous but often misunderstood position in New York’s Prohibition underworld. The Brooklyn racketeer was associated with coastal liquor importation, motor-vehicle distribution and networks of corrupt protection, and his name appeared repeatedly in press accounts of disputed shootings. His reputation as “Brooklyn’s last Irish boss” belongs to the language of the period and its later retellings; it does not establish that he commanded every Irish-American gang in the borough. [1] [2] [6]

For a historical biography, the strongest narrative is not a romantic account of an invulnerable gang chief. It is a study of a Brooklyn-born entrepreneur who combined legitimate-seeming transport businesses with an illicit liquor market, achieved considerable notoriety, repeatedly faced criminal investigation, and died from gunshot wounds after a family event on 19 June 1932. An extant federal court opinion identifies Higgins by name in a liquor-conspiracy indictment, while contemporary press accounts document a separate 1929 shooting and a 1932 jury acquittal. [3] [5] [8] [9]

Higgins also illustrates a fundamental editorial distinction: the existence of police charges, association with a suspected killer or a newspaper nickname is not the same as proof that a particular crime was committed. This biography identifies confirmed dates and legal dispositions separately from disputed alliances, press interpretations, and unresolved murder attributions. [2] [3] [4]

2. Full Name, Nickname, and Birth-Year Disagreement

The principal name found in the criminal and press record is Charles Higgins, popularly called “Vannie” Higgins. A regional-history account gives the fuller name Charles Van Wyck Higgins, but other documentary discussions use Charles without the middle names; that longer form is recorded as a reported identification rather than used to infer an additional legal identity. The nickname “Vannie” appears in the 1931 federal judicial record as an alias, confirming its currency during his lifetime. [5] [6]

His birth year presents a genuine archival conflict. The 1900 federal census, as reviewed by historian Tom Hunt, places his birth in November 1897. Naval records cited in the same study give 4 November 1898, while the death certificate's reported age of 33 points to an approximately 1898–1899 birth. Some contemporary press notices supplied still other ages. No authenticated birth certificate has been produced in the consulted research; this article therefore displays c. 1897–1898 rather than declaring one disputed date certain. [1] [6] [8]

The death date is far more secure. Higgins was wounded after midnight on Sunday, 19 June 1932, and died later that day. Writing “June 18” as the death date confuses the evening of the dance performance with the fatal attack and subsequent hospital death. [4] [10]

3. Parents and Childhood in Brooklyn

Higgins came from a Brooklyn family headed by Daniel Higgins and Ellen or Nellie McCarthy Higgins. Census and sibling vital records assembled in the modern archival study place the family at addresses in or near Gowanus during Charles’s childhood. Daniel worked for the municipal Street Cleaning Department and was described as a foreman. These details qualify popular accounts that simply present Higgins as born and raised in Bay Ridge, with no intervening family history. [1] [6]

The Gowanus environment was shaped by the canal, waterside industry, commercial traffic, overcrowding and working-class housing. It offered legitimate pathways through transport and dock work as well as opportunities for petty crime. The existence of urban poverty or a crowded neighborhood is not evidence of gang membership for every resident. Higgins’s own first traceable encounters with the criminal courts belong to his adolescence rather than to an identifiable childhood gang command structure. [1] [12]

Higgins later lived in Bay Ridge, which became the geographical center of his public identity. Keeping Gowanus and Bay Ridge distinct helps explain the family’s mobility and his subsequent ability to exploit access to Brooklyn docks and New York Harbor without rewriting his entire early life as a continuous criminal apprenticeship. [1]

4. Move to Bay Ridge and Local Reputation

By the middle 1910s the Higgins family had moved toward Bay Ridge, where residential streets lay close to the Narrows and to piers and roadways useful in coastal commerce. Hunt identifies the household at a Seventy-second Street residence and subsequently nearer Shore Road. These changes occurred before the organization’s major Prohibition prominence. [1]

Bay Ridge was not an isolated criminal enclave. It was a neighborhood of households, workers, businesses, religious and civic institutions, and active shoreline commerce. Higgins’s operations were part of a specialized illegal market rather than a description of the borough’s community as a whole. He could maintain a public family life and local acquaintances while being investigated as a suspected rum-runner. [1] [6] [7]

Contemporary press stories repeatedly linked him to Bay Ridge taxicabs, motorboats and well-dressed public appearances. Those stories document his notoriety, not the existence of a precise territorial border recognized by every competing bootlegging firm. Likewise, the claim that the entire Brooklyn waterfront answered to Higgins is a journalistic generalization requiring much stronger operational records than are available here. [2] [6] [7]

5. Early Arrests and Legal Outcomes

The source-annotated account identifies two assault convictions between approximately 1915 and 1917; both resulted in probation. These are distinct from far more dramatic allegations of murder during the late 1920s and early 1930s. A documented early conviction should not be diluted by vague statements that Higgins was “never convicted,” but later accusations must also not be recast as convictions. [1]

In May 1926 he was arrested after appearing at a Brooklyn hospital with injuries and was accused in a robbery-and-assault matter involving another man. Historical reporting supplies competing accounts of the confrontation, while later review records that the criminal case did not produce the straightforward serious conviction imagined in some gang chronicles. His frequent police exposure became part of his public identity. [1] [7]

In July 1928 another arrest concerned possession of a revolver and allegations of police harassment. The sequence is useful principally as evidence of friction with law enforcement. It does not, by itself, prove a criminal conspiracy encompassing everyone arrested near Higgins, and a police complaint against an officer is not proof that official protection existed. [1] [7]

6. First World War Naval Service

Higgins’s biography included military service that is often omitted from simplified bootlegging profiles. According to a reconstruction drawing on naval records, he enlisted in the United States Navy in April 1917 and served aboard several vessels or naval facilities. He advanced to quartermaster third class during the service period and was discharged in June 1919. [1]

The quartermaster rating involved watch duties and navigation-related tasks, a credible basis for identifying familiarity with maritime operations. That does not establish that he later performed every smuggling voyage personally or that the Navy trained him to evade Coast Guard patrols. The distinction between professional skill and a specific criminal technique matters when interpreting a career that crossed legitimate and illicit maritime commerce. [1] [12]

After the war, Higgins reportedly worked as a dock cargo checker. This detail offers a plausible bridge from naval service to the waterfront world in which he became prominent after 1920. The evidence supports a sequence of occupations; it does not supply a complete payroll record, business ledger or authoritative list of vessels he managed. [1]

7. Marriage, Children, and Private Life

Archival research indicates that Higgins married Catherine Kelly around 1921; she died in May 1922. He subsequently married Grace De Mayo, and the couple had a daughter reported as Jeanne, also rendered Jean in contemporary stories. Later accounts commonly describe the daughter as seven at the time of the 1932 shooting, while the reconstructed birth chronology appears less consistent with that age. Both the spelling and the age warrant careful attribution rather than a fabricated precise family register. [1] [4] [6]

Higgins’s home life did not remain private. Newspapers, police and eventually gunmen knew of his public appearances, automobiles and family outings. The attack that killed him occurred after a children’s dance event, demonstrating how visible domestic routines could become entangled with the risks of a public criminal reputation. This does not make his wife, daughter or other relatives participants in his suspected enterprises. [4] [10]

Some retrospective portrayals emphasize his devotion to family as if it disproved his participation in criminal rackets; others use his public notoriety to obscure the injury suffered by the child during the final attack. An evidence-based account can recognize both family life and the public record without converting relatives into characters in a gang organization chart. [4] [6]

8. National Prohibition and the Liquor Market

The Eighteenth Amendment and the Volstead Act created extraordinary demand for liquor that remained lawful to possess in some circumstances but illegal to manufacture, transport and sell for beverage purposes. New York City had a large consumer market and an extensive port system. The National Archives’ historical analysis explains how international suppliers, Rum Row vessels and fast contact craft moved liquor toward Long Island, New Jersey and the city. [12]

A successful bootlegging concern needed more than armed men. Supply required payment, scheduling, landings, warehouses, trucks, retail buyers and often arrangements that insulated operators from enforcement. Higgins’s Bay Ridge connections and transport businesses fit into this wider economy. Still, assigning him ownership of every ship or dock in a newspaper account would overstate the available evidence. [1] [2] [5] [12]

The geography of the trade also complicates a purely ethnic label. Irish-American, Italian-American, Jewish and other operators could compete, broker shipments or temporarily cooperate. The relevant historical unit is a specific commercial and coercive network around Higgins, not a single unified “Irish Mafia” governing New York’s liquor economy. [2] [6] [12]

9. Boats, Coastal Landings, and Rum Row

Accounts of Higgins’s coastal interests describe fast motorboats, notably one called the Cigarette, alongside contacts with larger liquor carriers. Some later stories call that boat the fastest rum-runner in New York waters. The superlative is a reputation claim, not a verified comparative trial against all contemporary craft. [1] [6] [7]

Rum Row functioned as an offshore transfer zone rather than a secure private estate of one Brooklyn operator. The National Archives documents how vessels from abroad met smaller craft that brought cargo ashore, while the Coast Guard collected intelligence, intercepted communications and occasionally seized boats. Higgins was one name in this larger trade; an individual boat’s presence near him cannot establish legal ownership or a completed illicit delivery without specific records. [5] [12]

The 1931 indictment discussed a proposed Long Beach operation in which liquor would be transferred from vessels into smaller boats, concealed on trucks and distributed. Those details are allegations in the charging instrument, not findings of a completed offense by Higgins. They nevertheless provide valuable evidence of what federal investigators believed the network’s logistics to be. [5]

10. Taxicabs, Trucks, and the Distribution System

Press accounts repeatedly linked Higgins to a taxi business and motor vehicles used in or suspected of supporting liquor distribution. The combination of a legitimate transport company with illegal commerce was neither unique to him nor necessarily proof that every driver or passenger was involved in bootlegging. Transportation capacity mattered because imported alcohol became profitable only after reaching urban wholesalers and customers. [1] [6]

The distinction between a legal business asset and its illegal use is especially important. A van, taxi or warehouse could have many lawful functions. The surviving record does not establish a complete fleet list, audited ownership register or revenue statement for the Higgins operation. Nor does every large dollar estimate in the press identify a personally owned fortune. [1] [5] [7]

Understanding the fleet as infrastructure also clarifies the organization’s vulnerability: police surveillance at piers, seizures at sea, rival hijackers and compromised drivers could threaten supply even when the owner was not present. Such vulnerabilities help explain why alliances and rumors of protection became central to the public picture of New York bootlegging. [2] [12]

11. The Big Bill Dwyer Relationship

William Vincent “Big Bill” Dwyer was among New York’s best-known Prohibition liquor entrepreneurs. Retrospective accounts place Higgins at times alongside Dwyer’s operation, whether as an associate, recipient of opportunities or independent Brooklyn rival. The language varies by source and period. It is safer to describe commercial association and potential overlap than to assert that Dwyer permanently controlled Higgins or that Higgins inherited the entire Dwyer business in 1927. [1] [6] [7]

Higgins’s rise coincided with shifts in the business after federal prosecutions pressured large importers. That context makes evolving supply connections plausible, but operational succession requires evidence beyond the convenience of a linear underworld story. Dwyer’s organization and Higgins’s Bay Ridge network have separate MGU organizational profiles for this reason. [5] [12]

Other figures—particularly Frank Costello—could bridge multiple suppliers and markets. Their mention in a biography should mark a relationship of the kind that is documented, not silently attach Higgins to an Italian-American Mafia family hierarchy or treat association as proof of a common command structure. [2] [6]

12. Owney Madden and Other New York Operators

Owney Madden, a former Hell’s Kitchen street-gang figure, participated in the New York Prohibition economy and later gained prominence in nightlife enterprises. Higgins and Madden are repeatedly discussed in the same rum-running and aviation accounts. Some reports in June 1932 placed them at Floyd Bennett Field for flying instruction. The contemporaneous comparison demonstrates familiarity or shared interest; it does not prove that the two men jointly owned every vessel attributed to either. [4] [6]

The New York underworld of the late 1920s was neither one immutable alliance nor an organized network with a universal “boss.” Relationships changed as market access, arrests and violence altered bargaining power. Higgins could be described as cooperating with a figure in one episode and competing with another faction the next. [2] [3] [7]

For MGU classification, the relevant related biographies include Madden and Dwyer and the related organizational profile is the Vannie Higgins Gang. These categories are navigation tools, not evidence that the subjects belonged to one ethnic national criminal federation. [1] [12]

13. Aviation and the Flying-Rumrunner Reputation

Higgins’s interest in aircraft was unusual enough to attract sustained press attention. Articles described seaplanes and an interest in piloting; later writers embellished those reports into the claim that every Higgins aircraft was engaged solely in smuggling. Aircraft were part of the broader technological development of Rum Row, but individual flights and illicit cargoes must be established separately. [1] [4] [6] [12]

The 2025 source-annotated research identifies reports of Higgins and Madden taking flying lessons at Floyd Bennett Field in June 1932. That documented interest is compatible with earlier rumors that he used aircraft, but the lessons do not establish the date he qualified for a license or the exact status of a reported seaplane fleet. [4]

The story also reveals how celebrity, technology and crime intersected. Journalists enjoyed describing a stylish operator who could move by taxi, motorboat or plane; for historians, those details are useful only when the underlying reporting is separated from promotional exaggeration and speculation about flight from prosecution. [4] [6]

14. The Comstock Prison Visit and Official Relationships

A frequently repeated episode describes Higgins landing an aircraft near the state prison at Comstock, New York, to visit Warden Joseph H. Wilson, whom he reportedly knew from childhood. The visit drew criticism from then Governor Franklin D. Roosevelt. The story appears in both regional biographical research and contemporary-source reconstructions. [1] [6] [7]

The historical significance lies in the proximity between a publicly notorious suspected liquor trafficker and a correctional official. A social visit and preferential accommodation warrant scrutiny, but they do not, without further evidence, prove that the warden directed Higgins’s criminal business, arranged prisoner releases or shared smuggling income. [1] [6]

The event also cautions against treating every personal connection to a politician, official or police officer as proven corruption. Some relationships reflected ordinary familiarity, some improper favors and some actual criminal conduct. Each has a different evidentiary threshold; a biography should not compress them into a single unexplained assertion of blanket “protection.” [3] [5]

15. What Did Higgins Actually Command?

The phrase Vannie Higgins Gang identifies a network associated with Higgins in press and retrospective underworld histories, not a surviving organizational constitution. The evidence supports a recognizable leader, commercial contacts, violent associates and suspected liquor operations; it does not supply an exhaustive membership roster, permanent executive committee or reliably fixed territories. [1] [2] [7]

Contemporaries named men such as William “Tough Bill” Bailey, while other figures appear as customers, partners, rivals, police officers or suspects. These are different kinds of relationships. Even if two men were arrested together, that does not automatically establish a lifelong partnership, formal rank or shared participation in each alleged killing. [1] [3] [5]

The organization should be classified as a Brooklyn-based Prohibition liquor and racketeering network, not as a La Cosa Nostra family. Its historical importance lies in coastal logistics and personal rivalries during the 1920s, and its dissolution cannot be precisely timed to the moment of one leader’s death without records of surviving branches. [2] [4] [12]

16. Competition, Alliances, and Ethnic Boundaries

The identities of Higgins’s competitors changed with the market. Later accounts place him in varying relationships with Jack “Legs” Diamond, Vincent “Mad Dog” Coll, Anthony Carfano and Dutch Schultz. These people did not constitute a stable formal alliance with Higgins for every year of Prohibition. Conflicts over delivery routes, access to customers, beer supplies and protection could turn temporary partners into adversaries. [2] [3] [6] [7]

Higgins’s Irish-American classification relates to his family and historic associations, not to a claim that his entire workforce or every trading partner had Irish ancestry. The underworld was interethnic and commercial; revenue and violence could override group identity. A biography should therefore avoid suggesting that an “Irish–Italian war” fully explains every episode. [2] [6] [12]

A useful analytical distinction is between an allegation that Higgins was present at a meeting, testimony that he traded with another operator, and a documented prosecution establishing a joint enterprise. The available sources more often provide the first two than the third. [3] [5]

17. Manhattan Beer Disputes, 1928–1930

By the late 1920s rival bootlegging groups competed over the delivery of beer to Manhattan establishments. Retrospective accounts describe Higgins operating with or near factions identified with Diamond and Carfano against Dutch Schultz. Source-annotated reporting demonstrates a tangled pattern of commercial competition and publicity, but it does not validate a simple roster of permanent “teams.” [2] [6] [7]

The proximity of shootings and attacks to a business disagreement is circumstantial evidence. Rival gangs had motives to intimidate, and individual gunmen might act without a single leader’s direct order. Unless the prosecution or another robust record identifies the shooter and authorizes attribution, the biography should record the allegation as disputed. [2] [3]

These conflicts were also shaped by Prohibition enforcement and public corruption investigations. A liquor route might become vulnerable because of a raid or an arrest, not merely because one faction defeated another in violence. That reality helps explain why Higgins was simultaneously a subject of public fascination and a target of repeated official attention. [2] [12]

18. The Owl’s Head Tavern Shooting: March 1929

On the night of 18–19 March 1929, shots were fired near the Owl’s Head Tavern at Third Avenue and Sixty-ninth Street in Bay Ridge. Higgins, Thomas Connell and Paul Eckert appeared in the ensuing investigation, while a police officer died. This incident is sometimes dated to 1928 in later popular biographies; period newspapers establish the correct date as March 1929. [2] [8] [9] [13]

The circumstances were disputed from the beginning. Officers and witnesses offered different explanations for who fired, why the parties were at the tavern and whether a bootlegging rivalry precipitated the confrontation. Contemporary reports placed Higgins near the scene but did not independently demonstrate that he fired the fatal round. [2] [8] [9]

A later hearing found insufficient evidence to maintain the assault charges against Higgins and certain other defendants. Those dispositions are central to the biography: it is not accurate to report the event as a murder committed by Higgins simply because he was notorious or had been arrested. [2] [13]

19. Daniel Maloney and the Fatal Police Gunfire

The officer who died at Owl’s Head was Daniel Maloney, an off-duty New York policeman. Contemporary reports indicate that he was shot by another officer, Sergeant John J. McGarty, who arrived amid gunfire and believed he was confronting an armed assailant. The case raised questions about the off-duty officer’s weapons and his apparent relationship to suspected bootleggers. [2] [8] [9] [13]

Maloney’s death is a genuine fatal event with a documented institutional context. It cannot, however, be used as evidence that Higgins shot a policeman. The distinction between presence, arrest and proven responsibility is basic to accurate criminal biography. [2] [9]

The confrontation also prompted scrutiny of police personnel who spent time with underworld figures. Such scrutiny illustrates the vulnerability of enforcement institutions to off-duty conflicts, but the evidence should not be enlarged into proof that the entire police department acted as a single protective apparatus for Higgins. [2] [13]

20. Investigation, Witnesses, and Dismissed Charges

Higgins, Connell and Eckert were held in connection with the Owl’s Head shooting. Contemporary reporting described firearms recovered at or near the scene, injuries to other men and an unusually confused sequence of shots. The initial narrative produced by police was not identical to the account established at the hearing. [2] [8] [9]

At the April 1929 hearing, according to a reconstruction citing Brooklyn newspapers, witnesses did not identify the defendants as having fired the relevant shots. The assault charges against Higgins and the other men were dismissed for insufficient evidence. It would be wrong to substitute the unproven allegations in the arrest stories for the judicial disposition. [2] [13]

For historical purposes the affair has two separate consequences: it intensified Higgins’s notoriety and exposed troubled contacts between off-duty policing and illicit markets. The dismissal established only the insufficiency of the evidence in that proceeding; it did not prove that the broader bootlegging activity attributed to Higgins was legitimate. [2] [13]

21. Growing Publicity and the 1930 Conflict Narratives

Accounts of the 1930 underworld often place Higgins in the shifting struggle around Diamond, Schultz and their associates. Newspapers reported threats, disputes and suspected partnerships. Later accounts supplied additional dramatic motives for attacks on Diamond and for the rise of competing distributors. Much of that narrative depends on retrospective testimony and should be read as reconstruction rather than settled litigation. [2] [4] [7]

The interest of the press itself was an important factor in Higgins’s public power. Photographs and sensational nicknames made him recognizable beyond Bay Ridge. Reporters could convert one verified arrest into an account of an apparently unified citywide racketeering organization, even where the documents concerned only a single occurrence. [6] [7]

Higgins was not a figure with a clear documentary trail for each claimed alliance. A cautious chronology follows the press attention while acknowledging that it was intermittent and often shaped by investigators’ hopes of assigning responsibility for unsolved crimes. [2] [3]

22. The Vivian Gordon Inquiry and Public Rumor

In early 1931, the killing of Vivian Gordon became part of a wider New York scandal involving alleged police and judicial corruption. Higgins was questioned in connection with that inquiry, according to newspapers assembled in later source-critical research. The fact of questioning is not evidence that he participated in Gordon’s murder. [3]

The relationship between Gordon, other underworld acquaintances and Higgins was reported in competing newspaper narratives, some of which rested on rumor. A historical biography should name her as a distinct individual whose death had its own investigative history rather than use her killing merely as a dramatic episode in Higgins’s criminal legend. [3]

This section illustrates a recurring methodological problem: public association with someone whose death was notorious may invite police questioning and extensive journalism even when no criminal charge against the person being profiled is established. The available record supports that limited conclusion, not a murder attribution. [3]

23. The May 1931 Stabbing

On 21 May 1931, Higgins was seriously injured in a stabbing after a confrontation at a Manhattan dining or entertainment establishment commonly identified as the Blossom Heath. Press accounts place him at hospital care and describe transfusions and attempts to locate the assailant. [3] [6]

The location and details vary across retellings, including conflicting street addresses. The safest account is that Higgins survived knife wounds sustained during an episode connected by reporters to a dispute over liquor supply; claims about the exact instigator and any prearranged plot remain unverified. [3] [6] [7]

Survival contributed to the later popular image of an unusually resilient racketeer. That impression should not be confused with evidence that Higgins ordered a specific retaliation or that the stabbing proved one particular gang alliance. In contemporary New York such incidents were investigated amid multiple overlapping disputes, and witnesses could be reluctant or inconsistent. [3]

24. Violence Among Associates During 1931

The 1931 source-annotated chronology documents several violent episodes involving men described by investigators as associates or former associates of Higgins. Among the reported deaths was that of Robert “Whitey” Benson, a gunman publicly connected with his circle. The reports are important evidence of a violent environment but cannot be assumed to show that every attack was ordered from a single headquarters. [3]

Accounts also name other victims and suspected rivals in the region. Where prosecutors did not secure convictions connecting Higgins to a specific murder, the incident should appear as an unresolved contemporaneous allegation. A gang biography and a personal biography must avoid different standards for the same event. [3] [7]

The network appears to have faced increasing strain from law enforcement, competition and personal disputes. That is a historical inference from the pattern of attention and violence, not proof that one organized command structure collapsed on a single known date. [3] [4]

25. The Robert “Whitey” Benson Case

Robert “Whitey” Benson’s death in October 1931 led investigators to question Higgins and William “Tough Bill” Bailey. Contemporary accounts alleged that Benson had become suspect within the bootlegging network. Higgins denied involvement, and the sources reviewed do not establish a murder conviction against him in the Benson case. [3] [6] [7]

Names and nicknames in these reports are prone to confusion; it is particularly important not to merge Benson with unrelated people merely because of a shared nickname. The biography records him as a reported associate and homicide victim, not as proof that Higgins maintained an internal tribunal or had authority to execute suspected informants. [3]

The outcome also demonstrates the gap between suspicion and adjudication. The murder became part of Higgins’s notorious reputation even without an established judicial finding assigning responsibility to him or a particular subordinate. [3] [7]

26. The December 1931 Killing of Legs Diamond

Jack “Legs” Diamond was killed in Albany on 18 December 1931. Higgins was among the underworld figures questioned or discussed in connection with the killing. Contemporary police interest and later biographers’ suspect lists reflect a wide range of theories about motive, including rivals, financial disputes and different criminal factions. [4]

No source consulted establishes that Higgins was convicted of or conclusively responsible for Diamond’s murder. Several later writers have proposed competing perpetrators, and the evidence is not strong enough to promote one theory into a canonical MGU finding. [4]

The case remains relevant because Diamond’s death changed the political and commercial context in which Higgins operated. It is a contextual turning point, not a documented personal crime of Higgins. Retrospective claims of direct responsibility are treated as hypotheses, not undisputed narrative facts. [4]

27. United States v. Grossman: The Long Beach Indictment

A 1931 federal district-court opinion, United States v. Grossman, 55 F.2d 408, preserves part of the indictment naming Charles Higgins, alias “Vannie” Higgins, among people alleged to have planned liquor imports near Long Beach, Long Island. The prosecution also named police officials and alleged that protection or noninterference helped unlawful cargo movements. [5]

The published opinion addressed a procedural request for a more detailed statement of the accusations against Morris Grossman, who had become Long Beach police chief only after the conspiracy’s alleged start. The judge ordered a bill of particulars specifying the claimed official interference and approximate dates. That interlocutory ruling did not convict Higgins, Grossman or anyone else of the offenses alleged. [5]

Reading the indictment and ruling together is essential. The indictment tells researchers how prosecutors characterized the network; the court’s decision demonstrates a procedural limit on broad or ambiguous accusations. Neither should be cited as a final finding that Higgins bribed every named police officer. [5]

28. January 1932 Federal Trial and Acquittal

In January 1932, Higgins and other defendants stood trial in Brooklyn federal court on liquor-conspiracy charges relating to Long Beach. The government presented claims about shoreline landings and official facilitation, while the defense challenged the sufficiency of proof. Newspaper reports reviewed by Hunt describe several defendants being discharged during the proceedings. [4]

On 20 January 1932, the jury acquitted the remaining defendants, including Higgins. The ruling is not a broad historical declaration that there was no bootlegging in the district; it is a concrete legal outcome in that prosecution. Higgins’s acquittal must be preserved even when evaluating the wider history of illicit liquor imports. [4]

Some later stories describe Higgins boasting about his control of a police department. Such quotations are retrospective and potentially self-serving. They must not override the acquittal or be treated as an authenticated confession of bribery without a reliable contemporaneous record and adjudicative context. [4] [5]

29. The WhichOne Incident, May 1932

In early May 1932, Coast Guard vessels intercepted the rum-running ship WhichOne near the New Jersey coast. A smaller boat containing Higgins and companions was also stopped. Contemporary newspaper accounts, summarized in the research chronology, described liquor found aboard the larger vessel while Higgins denied that the smaller boat had carried contraband. [4]

The distinction between presence near an interdicted vessel and possession of its cargo is central. Higgins was released after officials failed to sustain a charge against him over the smaller craft. The seizure and his encounter with Coast Guard personnel are historical events; a completed importation conspiracy personally proved against him is not. [4]

The episode occurred only weeks before his murder. It illustrates that Higgins remained active in the coastal maritime world despite the earlier federal acquittal, while also showing why careful source reading is necessary when newspaper headlines join a famous suspect to seized liquor aboard a different vessel. [4] [12]

30. The Last Weeks and Family Dance Event

In June 1932 Higgins’s public interests included aviation, family activity and continuing police attention. He planned to attend a dance performance in which his young daughter Jeanne took part. The event was held at a Knights of Columbus clubhouse near Prospect Park and Grand Army Plaza in Brooklyn on Saturday evening, 18 June. [4] [6] [10]

As the family departed after midnight, the calendar date had become Sunday, 19 June 1932. Reports consistently place the shooting outside or near the clubhouse after the performance. Some accounts describe one vehicle, others two, and details of the gunmen’s movements differ. It is more accurate to describe the core event than harmonize incompatible eyewitness reports into a single cinematic sequence. [4] [10]

Higgins was accompanied by relatives including his wife and daughter. His daughter sustained a minor gunshot injury according to contemporary coverage; the attempt on Higgins therefore endangered people who had not been accused of participation in the liquor trade. [4] [10]

31. The June 19, 1932 Assassination

Armed attackers opened fire on Higgins as he and his family approached their car after the dance event. He suffered multiple gunshot wounds, was taken to a hospital and died on 19 June 1932. The child’s injury and the public setting made the attack especially notorious. [4] [10]

Reporters described Higgins attempting to move away from the immediate vicinity of his daughter. The exact positions of the participants, the number of gunmen and the alleged final words vary across reports. Where the stories conflict, this biography reports the common evidentiary core and avoids invented dialogue. [4] [6] [7] [10]

The shooting was a homicide. It is not adequate to transform that finding into a confident declaration that a named rival, a particular Mafia family or a government agency ordered it. Contemporary police theories and later narratives identified several possible motives, but the sources reviewed do not establish a murder conviction resolving the case. [4]

32. Suspects, Motives, and the Unresolved Homicide

Potential motives advanced after Higgins’s death included resentment over liquor territories, payback for other underworld killings, financial interests and longstanding hostility among rival groups. Later accounts variously implicated associates of Dutch Schultz, Jack Diamond and other networks. These claims are historically significant as theories circulating after the murder, not as an adjudicated verdict. [4] [7]

The most responsible summary is that the identity of the person or persons who commissioned and carried out Higgins’s killing remains unresolved in the record consulted. Statements that named assassins were certainly acting for one leader or another should not appear in an encyclopedia as settled fact unless substantiated by stronger evidence. [4]

The uncertainty matters for connected profiles. An Italian-American Mafia organization’s prominence after Higgins’s death is not proof that it directed his murder. Nor does a rivalry documented in 1930 prove responsibility for an attack in 1932 without linking evidence. [2] [4]

33. Burial, Family, and Public Reaction

Higgins’s death received prominent newspaper coverage, with reports contrasting his criminal notoriety against the spectacle of an attack that injured his daughter. Regional historical work identifies his burial at St. John’s Cemetery, Queens. The funeral belongs to the history of a family and community as much as to the public theater of organized-crime reporting. [6] [10]

Published obituaries and later narratives sometimes described Higgins as the “last Irish boss” or the most powerful survivor of older Brooklyn racketeering conflicts. Such phrases do not mean that Irish-American criminal associations disappeared after June 1932. Instead they describe the end of a prominent individual’s career and a change in the public identity of New York’s underworld. [6] [7]

The archive also preserves a fuller family context, including relatives’ occupations, addresses, and different dates in their vital records. Those materials permit cautious reconstruction without making every named relative a criminal associate or assuming a uniquely settled birth date for Higgins. [1] [6]

34. What Happened to the Higgins Network?

Higgins’s death undoubtedly deprived his contacts of an identifiable public leader. But a criminal network can disperse, change patrons, be absorbed piecemeal into new arrangements or persist informally. The materials reviewed do not furnish a signed dissolution, a comprehensive asset transfer or a verified list of successors. [4] [7]

Later accounts depict other racketeers expanding in Brooklyn and Manhattan as the liquor economy approached the end of national Prohibition. This wider transition is plausible and well grounded in the decade’s history, but it is not proof that all of Higgins’s boats, drivers and clients passed to one named successor in June 1932. [4] [12]

For the MGU directory, Higgins’s biography connects to the Vannie Higgins Gang and to associated figures such as Big Bill Dwyer and Owney Madden. It should not be used to infer an uninterrupted institutional line from his personal circle to a modern Mafia family. [1] [6]

35. Irish-American Identity and Mixed Networks

The historical label “Irish-American racketeer” is appropriate to Higgins’s family identity and the way newspapers situated his organization within Brooklyn’s immigrant underworld. It does not convert every police officer, boat captain, transporter or business associate into an Irish-American gang member. [1] [6]

Prohibition’s transport and financing systems crossed neighborhood and ethnic lines. Higgins was placed in narratives involving Italian-American and Jewish racketeers as well as other Irish-American figures. These contacts were shaped by commercial opportunity, protection arrangements and violent competition, not by a clean, permanent ethnic division of labor. [2] [3] [12]

The value of a distinct Irish-American biography collection is editorial discoverability. It permits research into people commonly grouped under that historical label while maintaining precise, person-by-person documentation and links to other organizations when the evidence justifies them. [1] [6]

36. Common Biographical Errors and Exaggerations

Several errors recur in compressed accounts. First, some place the Owl’s Head shooting in 1928, although dated newspapers place it in March 1929. Second, descriptions of that shooting sometimes imply Higgins personally killed Officer Maloney, whose fatal wounds were inflicted by another officer. Third, the 1931 indictment is sometimes cited without mention of the January 1932 acquittal. [2] [4] [5] [8] [9]

Additional errors involve unsupported lifetime body counts, the unqualified assertion that Higgins inherited all Dwyer operations, confident lists of the killers of Legs Diamond and Higgins, and exaggerated aircraft fleets. Claims of his precise birth year also vary despite the existence of incompatible census, military and death-record data. [1] [4] [6] [7]

Finally, some narratives confuse his daughter’s reported age and spelling, the night of the family event and the day he died, or the changing alliances among Diamond, Coll, Schultz, Carfano and other groups. Each is a reason to place chronology and source qualification ahead of vivid but unverified anecdotes. [1] [2] [4]

37. Historical Significance

Higgins’s career illuminates a transitional period in American criminal history. His reputation was built in the maritime and trucking economy of Prohibition, rather than through a documented initiation into a traditional Mafia family. His public persona combined local family life, technological interest, illicit commerce and frequent police attention. [1] [6] [12]

The 1931–1932 court and newspaper trail is especially useful because it permits a legal distinction between indictments, procedural rulings, dismissed charges and acquittals. Together with contemporary reports of his murder, it establishes major events while leaving some of the most sensational allegations unresolved. [4] [5] [8] [9] [10]

As an MGU biographical entry, Higgins is best understood not as the embodiment of Brooklyn’s entire underworld but as a documented participant in several overlapping networks whose personal history requires close attention to source genre, timing and the difference between notoriety and proof. [1] [2] [4]

38. Historical Method and Evidentiary Boundaries

This article uses the 1931 federal opinion and period newspaper notices for dated events, source-annotated archival research for family records and trial chronology, and broader scholarship for the Prohibition economy. Published transcripts or original images of every cited contemporary newspaper have not all been separately collated for this edition. The distinction between an original court opinion and a modern historian’s summary of a period article is retained throughout. [1] [2] [4] [5] [12]

Press descriptions of “gang leaders” are treated as evidence of contemporary reputation, not an audited roster. Arrests are recorded without implying conviction, judicial allegations are labeled as such, and no murder is attributed to Higgins or to his killer without a legally or historically sufficient foundation. [2] [3] [4] [5]

Further archival work should prioritize the Brooklyn birth certificate, full Navy service file, official 1929 magistrate record, complete 1932 trial transcript and verdict, and 1932 homicide-investigation files. Until those records are examined, the biography’s uncertainties are substantive research findings rather than gaps to fill with invented detail. [1] [4] [5]
