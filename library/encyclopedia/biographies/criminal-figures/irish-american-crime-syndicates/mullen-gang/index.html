Mullen Gang Biographies

Patrick Nee, Paul McGonagle, the South Boston gang conflict, and the organizational transition that preceded the rise of the Winter Hill network.

Collection Overview

The Mullen Gang (also rendered Mullens or Mullins in historical and federal sources) was a South Boston criminal group whose members became involved in theft, robbery, and violent conflict with the locally established Killeen organization during the late 1960s and early 1970s. The name refers to the group's local identity, not to a formal Mafia family or a person named Mullen. Accounts place its neighborhood associations around John Joseph Mullen Square; the early membership and exact origins should be checked against contemporary records.

Its conflict with the Killeens was a contest between separate organizations, not an internal succession dispute. Federal prosecutors later described an approximately 1972 accommodation in which surviving members of the rival South Boston groups came together and became associated with the Somerville-based organization led by Howard Winter. That later network was known as the Winter Hill Gang. The federal account is an indictment's description of an alleged enterprise, not a finding that every former Mullen joined or accepted a particular leadership arrangement.

This directory features two selected MGU biographies: Patrick “Pat” Nee and Paul “Paulie” McGonagle. They are important subjects for studying the gang's leadership, its feud with the Killeens, and competing later recollections. The page is a biographical index, not a complete membership roll or a substitute for independent legal and historical documentation.

Research Themes

  • The Mullen Gang's neighborhood origins and the limits of reconstructing an informal criminal group's membership
  • South Boston theft, robbery, and competition over illicit revenue before and during the Killeen–Mullen conflict
  • Patrick Nee and Paul McGonagle as distinct leadership figures, including differences between contemporary records and retrospective accounts
  • Escalation of the Killeen–Mullen feud in the late 1960s and the deaths and injuries associated with that violence
  • Donald McGonagle's killing and the importance of distinguishing non-participants and victims from criminal-group members
  • The 1972 intervention associated with Howard Winter and the evolving relationships among former rivals
  • Paul McGonagle's 1974 disappearance, subsequent recovery of his remains, and federal proceedings involving James “Whitey” Bulger
  • Post-conflict links to the Winter Hill Gang without presuming that every Mullen figure belonged to the later enterprise

Principal Biographies

  1. Patrick “Pat” Nee

    Historical classification: Mullen Gang member and leadership figure; later participant in other criminal activities. Nee was an Irish-born South Boston resident and U.S. Marine veteran whose own retrospective account places him within the Mullen organization and its confrontation with the Killeen group. His involvement is significant for the history of the conflict, subsequent negotiations, and the overlapping identities of former enemies and later associates.

    Nee is a coauthor of the memoir A Criminal and an Irishman, originally published in 2006. The book provides a participant's perspective but is not an independent adjudication of disputed shootings, individual culpability, or the precise terms of the gang settlement. His biography should distinguish his firsthand claims from records that independently corroborate them, and should examine later allegations or convictions on their own evidentiary basis.

    Main biography page: patrick-nee.html

  2. Paul “Paulie” McGonagle

    Historical classification: Prominent Mullen Gang leader and participant in the South Boston gang conflict. McGonagle is identified in historical reporting as a principal figure in the group. His rivalry with the Killeens, and later hostility toward Bulger, made him central to the violence and factional struggle of the period.

    McGonagle disappeared in November 1974. A May 23, 2001 federal superseding indictment alleged that Bulger and others murdered him and concealed his remains near Tenean Beach in Dorchester. Later federal court material also addressed the recovery of his remains. An indictment alleges criminal conduct; it should not be treated interchangeably with the jury's findings, a final judgment, or the evidentiary record of a particular charge. His biography should additionally make clear that Donald McGonagle, Paul's brother, must not be catalogued as a Mullen member merely because he was killed during the feud.

    Main biography page: paul-mcgonagle.html

Other associated people for future research: Accounts identify Thomas “Tommy” King, Francis “Buddy” Leonard, Dennis “Buddy” Roache, and Mickey Dwyer in connection with the Mullen organization. Their inclusion here does not imply that MGU has completed biography pages for them. Donald Killeen and James “Whitey” Bulger belong to the opposing Killeen faction during the principal feud; their roles should be cross-referenced, not misfiled as original Mullen members.

Historical Record and Organizational Context

Neighborhood rather than formal-family organization: The Mullen Gang operated as a South Boston street-level criminal network rather than a chartered criminal association with clearly documented offices, ranks, and initiation procedures. Published accounts connect it to burglaries, thefts, and armed robbery. The identity of its early members, the number of participants at any given date, and the scope of particular operations are contested or incompletely documented.

Conflict with the Killeens: In the late 1960s the Mullens' activities collided with those of the Killeen organization. Retrospective accounts identify a confrontation involving member Mickey Dwyer in 1969 as an important escalation. The conflict involved repeated shootings and retaliatory threats. Individual killings need separate case-level treatment; attributing an act to a faction is not the same as establishing which person committed it.

Victims and mistaken identity: Reporting on the conflict describes the killing of Donald McGonagle, Paul's brother, in an apparent mistaken-identity attack. He is generally described as not being part of the criminal organization. MGU should identify him as a victim in relevant accounts, not turn a family connection into an affiliation finding. Allegations about who fired the shot and when should be paired with the corresponding testimony, court record, or clearly attributed later recollection.

1972 organizational realignment: The federal government's 2001 description of the Bulger enterprise traces its formation to a merger or consolidation in or about 1972 of rival South Boston groups, followed by association with the organization headed by Howard Winter. This explains why some people linked to the old gang subsequently appear in histories of the Winter Hill network. It does not eliminate the need for separate Mullen, Killeen, and Winter Hill organizational records or establish a uniform later affiliation for every individual.

McGonagle's death and historical reconstruction: McGonagle's 1974 disappearance and the eventual discovery of his remains later became part of the federal investigation and litigation surrounding Bulger. Prosecutorial allegations, court findings, witness accounts, and later memoirs can be compared to establish a chronology while preserving their different legal and evidentiary weight. Retrospective descriptions of the conflict sometimes compress several distinct events into a single uncomplicated story; this index deliberately avoids that simplification.

Longer-term significance: The Killeen–Mullen feud illustrates how neighborhood criminal competition, violent coercion, mediated settlements, and shifting alliances contributed to the later geography of Boston organized crime. It also demonstrates the importance of accounting for people harmed by gang violence, rather than presenting the episode only as an exchange among notorious criminal figures.

Editorial Standards

Collection scope: This directory organizes two biographies assigned to the Mullen Gang in MGU's current filing plan. The selections do not constitute a comprehensive roster.

  • Names and spelling: Preserve Mullen Gang as the MGU directory title, while noting the Mullens and Mullins forms found in sources. Do not infer these are necessarily different organizations.
  • Affiliations: Distinguish a documented group member, leader, later associate, rival, family member, witness, and uninvolved victim.
  • Chronology: Separate the pre-1972 feud, the 1972 settlement, the later Winter Hill network, and McGonagle's disappearance in 1974.
  • Legal standards: Identify whether a statement comes from an indictment, testimony, a judgment, a police theory, a memoir, or reported history; do not equate an allegation with a conviction.
  • Memoir assessment: Treat participant recollections as valuable but interested evidence; seek contemporaneous corroboration before adopting contested accounts as established fact.
  • Victim representation: Do not classify Donald McGonagle or other nonparticipants as gang members solely because of family ties or victimization.
  • Canonical biographies: Use one principal biography per person and provide cross-links from other relevant collection pages rather than duplicate the HTML article.
  • Historical purpose: Examine organized crime as part of social, legal, and institutional history without romanticizing coercion, retaliation, or violence.

Linking status: The biography links use the assigned MGU filenames, patrick-nee.html and paul-mcgonagle.html. Both files should be placed in the same directory as this index.html. Their current publication status has not been verified on the live website.

Research Starting Points

These sources are starting points for verifying organizational history, biography affiliations, and legal chronology. A federal indictment records the government's allegations, while a participant memoir supplies one account of events; neither alone establishes every contested claim.

  • United States v. James J. Bulger et al. — Third Superseding Indictment (U.S. District Court, District of Massachusetts, May 23, 2001) — pages 3 and 10 set out the government's history of the South Boston groups and its allegation concerning Paul McGonagle's death. Read with the procedural outcomes of the case.
  • WCVB — Paul McGonagle profile (archival local reporting) — identifies McGonagle's leadership and recounts disputed events from the Killeen–Mullen conflict; access may be restricted.
  • WCVB — Donald McGonagle profile (archival local reporting) — emphasizes the distinction between Paul's criminal affiliation and his brother's death as a nonparticipant; access may be restricted.
  • Patrick Nee, Richard Farrell, and Michael Blythe, A Criminal and an Irishman (Steerforth Press, 2006) — firsthand memoir and biographical research lead; independently verify disputed descriptions of violence and organizational negotiations.
  • U.S. Attorney's Office, District of Massachusetts — Statement on Bulger's victims (August 18, 2022) — names Paul McGonagle among the victims recognized by the office.
  • Trekking Toward the Truth — Historical reconstruction of the Killeen–Mullen feud (December 30, 2012) — provides names, neighborhood context, and a proposed chronology; treat as secondary interpretation requiring corroboration.
