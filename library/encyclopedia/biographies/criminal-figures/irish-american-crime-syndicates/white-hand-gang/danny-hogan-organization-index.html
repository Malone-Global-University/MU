Danny Hogan Organization Biographies

Daniel “Dapper Dan” Hogan, St. Paul's Prohibition-era underworld, the O'Connor layover system, and the 1928 assassination.

Collection Overview

The Danny Hogan Organization is MGU's directory designation for the network of criminal associates, illicit business interests, and intermediaries connected to Daniel “Dapper Dan” Hogan in St. Paul, Minnesota, during the early twentieth century. Hogan was an influential underworld figure who helped arrange contact between criminals visiting St. Paul and corrupt segments of the city's law-enforcement and political establishment. This directory title is an editorial classification, not evidence that a continuously constituted gang with the formal name “Danny Hogan Organization” and an authenticated membership roll existed.

Hogan was closely associated with the O'Connor layover agreement, the arrangement under which visiting criminals could reportedly find refuge in St. Paul by checking in with intermediaries, paying bribes, and avoiding major crimes within the city. The Minnesota Historical Society identifies Hogan as the intermediary who succeeded William “Reddy” Griffin in 1913. His saloon operations, including the Green Lantern establishment on Wabasha Street, became prominent in accounts of the city's interconnected bootlegging, gambling, and stolen-property markets.

On December 4, 1928, Hogan was killed by an explosive device placed in his automobile. His murder remains unsolved. Later St. Paul criminal enterprises overlapped with people and places from Hogan's milieu, but those subsequent groups are not automatically parts of the same organization. This index contains one selected canonical biography: Hogan's own. Other figures are identified for historical context and future research, not as an invented gang roster.

Research Themes

  • The creation and operation of St. Paul's corrupt refuge system for visiting criminals.
  • The distinction between police-chief John O'Connor's tenure and the longer persistence of the O'Connor layover arrangement.
  • Hogan's position as an intermediary following William “Reddy” Griffin's death in 1913.
  • The use of saloons and hospitality businesses as meeting places for bootleggers, gamblers, and financial intermediaries.
  • Illicit alcohol, stolen property, money handling, and the commercial organization of the Twin Cities underworld.
  • Hogan's December 1928 assassination, its unresolved investigation, and the dangers of assigning responsibility without proof.
  • Harry “Dutch” Sawyer and later St. Paul criminal networks, with attention to historical succession without assuming a single continuous gang.
  • The difference between contemporary documentation, later scholarship, retrospective journalism, and popular legend.

Principal Biography

  1. Daniel “Dapper Dan” Hogan

    Historical classification: St. Paul criminal-network leader and underworld intermediary. Approximately 1880 – December 4, 1928. Hogan became a well-known broker within the city's criminal marketplace and was closely connected to the informal system permitting visiting criminals to stay in St. Paul. After William “Reddy” Griffin died in 1913, Hogan took over an intermediary role in the layover arrangement associated with John O'Connor.

    Hogan operated a business associated with the Green Lantern name on Wabasha Street. Accounts connect his activities to bootlegging, gambling, the handling of stolen property, and mediation of disputes among criminals. These descriptions should be assessed against contemporary reports and records of specific investigations; they do not by themselves establish his responsibility for every offense attributed to the wider underworld. His reputation for negotiating conflicts existed alongside the broader corruption and harm of the city's refuge system.

    Hogan suffered fatal injuries when a bomb detonated in his car at his West Seventh Street home on December 4, 1928. He died that evening. Historians and journalists have discussed suspected rivals, but the case remains unsolved. His biography should focus on the documented chronology of his career, the institutional significance of the layover system, and the unresolved nature of his death.

Historical Context and Organizational Development

St. Paul's refuge system: The O'Connor layover agreement emerged during the early 1900s. Historical accounts describe an informal bargain in which visiting criminals registered their presence, paid bribes, and refrained from conspicuous offenses inside St. Paul. The system did not eliminate criminal activity; rather, it helped protect illicit business relationships and displaced some offenses to other jurisdictions.

From Griffin to Hogan, 1913: The Minnesota Historical Society identifies William “Reddy” Griffin as an earlier intermediary and Hogan as his successor after Griffin's death in 1913. Hogan should therefore not be described as the original founder of the entire layover system. O'Connor retired as police chief in 1920, whereas the corrupt arrangement continued beyond his tenure.

Prohibition and the Green Lantern: By the 1920s, St. Paul had become a meeting point for criminal figures involved in bootlegging and other illicit markets. Hogan's Wabasha Street establishment was reported as one of the important settings for those relationships. The existence of a shared venue does not prove that all its patrons or visiting fugitives were members of a formally organized Hogan gang.

The 1928 automobile bombing: On December 4, 1928, an explosive device placed beneath or within Hogan's car detonated when he attempted to start it at his home. Contemporary accounts record severe injuries and death later the same day. The killing remains unsolved, and retrospective theories about suspects are not equivalent to criminal convictions or conclusive investigative findings. Nor should the attack be simplistically called the world's first car bombing.

After Hogan's death: Historical accounts identify Harry “Dutch” Sawyer as a significant underworld associate and successor in local operations. Subsequent criminal incidents, notably high-profile Barker–Karpis kidnappings in the 1930s, should be researched as separate organizations and cases, even where participants made use of St. Paul's earlier refuge arrangements. The layover system finally came under heavy public and federal pressure in the mid-1930s.

Broader impact: This history illuminates police corruption, the influence of illicit markets on civic administration, and the costs imposed on victims and neighboring jurisdictions. Hogan's reputation for social generosity or for settling disputes should not obscure the system's documented criminal context.

Associated Figures and Institutional Relationships

  • John O'Connor — police chief: Associated with the layover arrangement and its earlier operation, rather than treated as a formally enrolled member of Hogan's organization.
  • William “Reddy” Griffin — predecessor: Earlier liaison with visiting criminals, succeeded by Hogan after Griffin's death in 1913.
  • Harry “Dutch” Sawyer — associate and later successor: Connected with Hogan's criminal-business milieu and later underworld leadership in St. Paul. Allegations concerning any particular offense or Hogan's murder require separate verification.
  • Barker–Karpis figures — later, distinct network: Their activities illustrate the subsequent history of St. Paul's criminal refuge system, but do not by themselves establish membership in Hogan's earlier circle.

These are relationship classifications, not an authenticated membership list. This directory currently links only Daniel Hogan's biography.

Editorial Standards

Collection scope: One selected linked biography from MGU's current Irish-American organized-crime filing plan.

  • Identity: Daniel “Dapper Dan” Hogan of St. Paul, Minnesota, is not Edward J. “Jelly Roll” Hogan Jr. of the St. Louis, Missouri Hogan Gang.
  • Organizational terminology: “Danny Hogan Organization” is an MGU directory label, not proof of a formal charter, membership register, or stable leadership chart.
  • Membership: Differentiate close associates, business contacts, public officials, intermediaries, criminal visitors, and later operators.
  • Legal precision: Distinguish contemporaneous allegations, arrests, documented crimes, judicial findings, and unresolved investigations.
  • Unsolved case: Do not assign responsibility for the December 1928 bombing as established fact.
  • Chronology: Separate Griffin's intermediary role, Hogan's 1913–1928 activity, and subsequent 1930s criminal organizations.
  • Institutional accountability: Include the harms associated with police corruption and organized crime, not simply colorful folklore about gangsters.
  • Source standards: Prefer original court, police, and newspaper records alongside documented regional historical scholarship.
  • Canonical filing: Maintain a single Daniel Hogan biography and refer to other organizations through cross-links rather than duplicate pages.

Research Starting Points

These references provide context and research pathways. The full Hogan biography should independently corroborate disputed incident-level details with primary records.

  • Minnesota Historical Society, MNopedia — “O'Connor Layover Agreement” — authoritative regional background on the arrangement, Griffin's 1913 succession, and Hogan's death.
  • Minnesota Historical Society Library — “Gangsters in St. Paul” — research guide identifying collections of police, court, newspaper, and historical-source materials.
  • Curt Brown, Star Tribune — “1928 car bombing that killed St. Paul mob boss 'Dapper Dan' Hogan still unsolved” (2018) — historical reconstruction using contemporary coverage and archival materials.
  • Minnesota Public Radio Archive — “A Story of Crime, Criminals and Corruption” (1985) — radio documentary and retrospective accounts of regional criminal networks.
  • Visit Saint Paul — “Gangster Past” — accessible contextual overview; verify crime-specific claims independently.
  • Paul Maccabee, John Dillinger Slept Here: A Crooks' Tour of Crime and Corruption in St. Paul, 1920–1936 (Minnesota Historical Society Press, 1995) — specialist study listed in the Historical Society library guide; consult its original notes for detailed claims.
