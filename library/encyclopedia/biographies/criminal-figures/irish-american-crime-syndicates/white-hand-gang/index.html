White Hand Gang Biographies

Brooklyn's Irish-associated waterfront gangs, labor coercion, intergang rivalry, and the leadership periods of Dinny Meehan, Bill Lovett, and Richard Lonergan.

Collection Overview

The White Hand Gang was a name used for Irish-associated criminal networks operating in Brooklyn during the early twentieth century, especially around the Red Hook waterfront and neighboring dock districts. Historical records describe extortion, violence, robberies, and attempts to influence access to waterfront work. The label should not be read as proof that every local crew shared a permanent membership roster or an uninterrupted, centralized command structure.

The organization developed within a changing labor and immigrant landscape. Competition over jobs, hiring arrangements, street commerce, and illicit income brought some Irish-American and Italian-American criminal figures into conflict. The designation White Hand was framed in opposition to the Black Hand label associated with certain extortion practices and criminal groups. Neither expression should be used to portray an entire ethnic community as criminal, or to imply that every person described as a Black Hand offender belonged to one unified Italian organization.

This MGU directory presents three selected canonical biographies: Dennis L. “Dinny” Meehan, William “Wild Bill” Lovett, and Richard “Peg Leg” Lonergan. They represent prominent phases of the waterfront network from the 1910s through 1925. This page is a guide to individual biographies rather than a comprehensive membership roll, judicial finding on every offense, or definitive account of the organization's origins.

Research Themes

  • Red Hook, South Brooklyn, and the economic geography of docks, warehouses, streets, and hiring sites
  • Waterfront coercion, extortion allegations, theft, and the distinction between dockworkers and criminal organizations
  • Irish-American and Italian-American criminal rivalry without treating either immigrant community as a single faction
  • Gang identities and leadership labels in contemporary newspapers versus later historical narratives
  • Leadership changes after Meehan's killing in 1920 and Lovett's killing in 1923
  • The December 1925 Adonis Social Club shootings, police investigation, and limits of claims about responsibility
  • The difference between the decline of the named White Hand leadership and the later persistence of waterfront racketeering

Early Waterfront Leadership: Dinny Meehan

  1. Dennis L. “Dinny” Meehan

    Historical classification: Prominent early White Hand leader. Meehan was identified in period newspaper reporting as a Brooklyn gang leader, and contemporary accounts associated him with waterfront hiring disputes, robbery accusations, and violence. These are distinct evidentiary categories: reporting on charges, police suspicions, and claimed motives must not be restated as proven findings of guilt.

    On March 31, 1920, Meehan was shot and killed in his home in Brooklyn while his wife was also wounded. A New York Times report dated April 1 described various police theories about the killing. Those theories are evidence of the investigation's reported direction, not a conclusive identification of the killers.

Succession and Gang Conflict: Bill Lovett

  1. William “Wild Bill” Lovett

    Historical classification: White Hand leader following Meehan's death. Lovett was a prominent figure in the Brooklyn waterfront underworld during a period of internal rivalries and violent competition. Contemporary press accounts document allegations and arrests, but an arrest alone cannot establish responsibility for a particular killing.

    Lovett survived a serious shooting in January 1923. He was later found murdered on November 1, 1923, near a dockworkers' meeting place on Bridge Street; the New York Times reported the discovery the following day. The circumstances of the homicide have been retold in conflicting ways, so dramatic claims about the method or identity of the killers require corroboration rather than repetition as fact.

    Main biography page: william-lovett.html

Family and succession context: Lovett married Anna Lonergan, whose brother Richard Lonergan was associated with the same Brooklyn gang network. This relationship is relevant to the next leadership period but should not be used on its own to infer a formal appointment or an exact succession date.

Final Major Leadership Period: Richard Lonergan

  1. Richard “Peg Leg” Lonergan

    Historical classification: Later White Hand leader and a central figure in the final major period of the organization's 1920s history. Lonergan was connected to Lovett's Brooklyn network and became prominent after Lovett's murder. His biography should examine factional conflict, waterfront criminal activity, and the distinction between contemporary investigative evidence and retrospective stories about gang warfare.

    In the early hours of December 26, 1925, Lonergan and two companions were shot dead during a disturbance at the Adonis Social Club in Brooklyn. The New York City Municipal Archives preserves a police homicide photograph and cites the contemporary Brooklyn Daily Eagle reporting. The municipal archive also notes allegations involving Al Capone; it specifies that Capone and others were arrested but not charged in the killings. Their culpability must therefore not be presented as judicially established.

    Main biography page: richard-lonergan.html

Historical Record and Organizational Context

Waterfront economy: Dock hiring, cartage, warehouse traffic, and maritime labor offered both legitimate livelihoods and opportunities for coercive control. The gang's history should distinguish the actions attributed to particular racketeers from the larger population of immigrant laborers, trade union members, business owners, and residents.

Sources and categories: Newspapers of the period used terms such as gang, dock loaders' gang, and White Hand in overlapping ways. They also reported police beliefs, incomplete investigations, and pending charges. A biographical entry should identify who made each claim, when, and whether later evidence changed its status.

Historical chronology: The killings of Meehan in 1920, Lovett in 1923, and Lonergan in 1925 form useful organizational markers. Their deaths do not by themselves establish that every associated crew ceased to exist immediately, that each successor controlled precisely the same territory, or that the named gang had one continuously documented command structure.

Adonis Social Club: The municipal archive's homicide material establishes the date and documented scene of the 1925 triple killing. The reported involvement of particular well-known gangsters should remain explicitly attributed as an allegation, not as a conviction or settled historical fact.

Ethnic context: The history includes prejudicial rhetoric and conflict among criminal factions. MGU's treatment should analyze the institutional setting and evidence without amplifying historical slurs or projecting gang identities onto entire immigrant populations.

Editorial Standards

Collection scope: Three selected biography subjects from the MGU production roster. This directory is not a complete record of all White Hand-associated individuals or the rival criminal networks operating in Brooklyn.

  • Identity: Distinguish Dennis L. “Dinny” Meehan, William “Wild Bill” Lovett, and Richard “Peg Leg” Lonergan, including alternate names and contemporary spelling variants.
  • Affiliation: Separate established leadership, alleged membership, criminal association, rivalry, family relationships, and neighborhood proximity.
  • Evidence: Give priority to original newspaper editions, court files, arrest and trial records, surviving police records, municipal archives, and critically evaluated scholarship.
  • Legal precision: Distinguish suspect identification, arrest, charge, conviction, acquittal, disputed attribution, and unresolved homicide.
  • Chronology: Preserve the dates 1920, 1923, and 1925 and avoid projecting later organized-crime institutions backward onto earlier gangs.
  • Organization: Avoid treating the White Hand designation as a permanent corporation, a complete membership ledger, or proof that all associated crews were homogeneous.
  • Canonical filing: Keep one biography per subject; cross-reference related gang histories rather than duplicating individual profiles.
  • Historical purpose: Analyze exploitation and violence without glorifying criminal authority or the ethnic hostility surrounding these conflicts.

Research Starting Points

These sources offer contemporary documentation, archival context, and historical interpretation. Transcribed newspaper items and publisher descriptions are research starting points, not substitutes for collating original documents or establishing each full biography's source register.

  • NYC Department of Records and Information Services — “Prohibition” (2019) — municipal archival commentary on the December 26, 1925 Adonis Social Club shooting, surviving NYPD homicide photographs, and the distinction between suspected and charged participants.
  • Brooklyn Genealogy — White Hand Gang newspaper transcription collection — transcribed New York Times and Brooklyn Eagle reports, including April 1, 1920 (Meehan), November 2, 1923 (Lovett), and December 1925–January 1926 (Lonergan). Verify significant claims against original newspaper pages when available.
  • University of North Texas, Portal to Texas History — Houston Post-Dispatch, December 27, 1925 — digitized period newspaper containing contemporary wire reporting on the Lonergan killings; period reports may contain provisional or erroneous claims.
  • Paul Moses, An Unlikely Union: The Love-Hate Story of New York's Irish and Italians (NYU Press, 2015) — historical framework for interethnic competition and relations in New York; consult the full study rather than treating the publisher synopsis as case-specific evidence.
  • Patrick Downey, Gangster City: The History of the New York Underworld, 1900–1935 (Barricade Books, 2004) — secondary underworld history for comparison with contemporaneous and archival evidence.
