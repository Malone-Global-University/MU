Killeen Gang Biographies

Donald Killeen, South Boston's gambling and loansharking networks, the Killeen–Mullen feud, and the reorganization preceding the Winter Hill Gang.

Collection Overview

The Killeen Gang was a South Boston criminal organization associated with brothers Donald “Donnie” Killeen and Kenneth “Kenny” Killeen. During the middle decades of the twentieth century, the organization became prominent in local bookmaking, loansharking, and related illicit business. The Killeens' position was challenged by the rival Mullen Gang in a violent competition that intensified in the late 1960s and early 1970s.

James “Whitey” Bulger was aligned with the Killeen group during the feud. A 1999 federal court decision discusses Bulger's connection to Donald Killeen, while a 2001 federal indictment identifies the Killeen brothers and describes an approximately 1972 consolidation of the surviving South Boston factions, followed by their association with Howard Winter's Somerville-based group. That subsequent network became known as the Winter Hill Gang. An indictment is the government's allegation of events and must be distinguished from adjudicated findings and independently verified historical reporting.

This directory includes one MGU biography assigned to the Killeen Gang: Donald Killeen. Kenneth Killeen, Bulger, and others are discussed to establish organizational context, but they are not additional linked biographies in this folder. The collection is a selective biographical directory, not an exhaustive gang-membership register.

Principal Biography

  1. Donald “Donnie” Killeen

    Historical classification: South Boston criminal-organization leader; gambling and loansharking operator. Donald Killeen was a central figure in the group identified by his surname. Contemporary law-enforcement accounts, later federal proceedings, and historical reporting describe his role in South Boston's illegal betting and credit markets. His organization became involved in increasingly violent competition with the Mullens as its earlier local position was challenged.

    Killeen was shot and killed in Framingham, Massachusetts, on May 13, 1972. Newspaper accounts reconstruct the circumstances of his death and its effect on the ongoing feud, but interpretations of responsibility differ. His biography should distinguish documented facts of the murder, police suspicions, later memoir accounts, and criminal findings; it should not identify an individual killer as established fact without appropriate evidence.

    Main biography page: donald-killeen.html

Other associated people for historical context: Kenneth “Kenny” Killeen was identified with Donald as a leader in a federal indictment. James “Whitey” Bulger was aligned with the brothers before becoming central to the later Winter Hill network. William “Billy” O'Sullivan was a close colleague of Bulger whose killing was discussed in federal investigative records. These references do not establish that MGU has published separate biography pages for Kenneth Killeen or O'Sullivan. Bulger's assigned canonical biography is linked from the Winter Hill Gang directory.

Historical Record and Organizational Context

Local criminal organization: The Killeen group developed within South Boston's illicit gambling and lending economy. Published reporting associates Donald Killeen's operations with the Transit Cafe on West Broadway. Its neighborhood-based structure should not be confused with the formal ranks or initiation system of a Mafia family; membership, leadership, and business partnerships may have shifted over time.

Rivalry with the Mullens: By the late 1960s, the Killeens' control of local criminal revenue was being contested by the Mullen Gang. The conflict became marked by shootings, threats, and retaliations. Retrospective accounts describe individual confrontations as catalysts, but the motives and participants in particular incidents require independent evidentiary review. The Mullen Gang remained a separate rival organization during this stage.

Bulger and O'Sullivan: The federal district court's opinion in United States v. Salemme (1999) recounts FBI records from 1971 describing Bulger as associated with Donald Killeen and noting that William O'Sullivan, a close colleague of Bulger, had been murdered. The opinion establishes that these investigative records existed and were examined in court; it does not by itself determine every contested attribution made in later accounts of the feud.

Donald Killeen's death: Donald Killeen was killed outside his Framingham home on May 13, 1972. His murder accelerated the breakdown of the Killeen group's earlier position. Reporting in The Boston Globe examined police suspicions and competing interpretations; the index does not assign responsibility to any specific individual without an established evidentiary basis.

Realignment in or about 1972: The May 23, 2001 third superseding indictment in the federal Bulger case described the surviving Killeen and Mullen factions as consolidating after Howard Winter intervened, with Bulger assuming leadership of their South Boston grouping before it associated with Winter's Somerville organization. This is a useful record of the government's theory of the enterprise, not proof that every person in either original gang joined the later Winter Hill network.

Distinct organizational identities: For reference and filing purposes, the Killeen Gang, Mullen Gang, and Winter Hill Gang require separate directories. Their participants and affiliations intersected, but the historical chronology and geographic bases were not identical. One canonical biography should be maintained for a person with multiple affiliations and linked from each relevant index.

Historical significance: The Killeen story connects local criminal markets, retaliatory gang violence, federal investigative practices, and the changing structure of Boston-area organized crime. Treatment of the feud should recognize victims and residents affected by the violence without presenting gang conflict as heroic or inevitable.

Editorial Standards

Collection scope: This index contains the single biography assigned to the Killeen Gang folder under MGU's current filing plan. Other figures are discussed as context or directed to their separate canonical collections.

  • Identity and leadership: Distinguish Donald Killeen from his brother Kenneth, whose leadership role appears in federal prosecutorial records.
  • Affiliation precision: Record separately whether an individual was a leader, member, associate, rival, victim, or later participant in another criminal group.
  • Chronology: Do not conflate the pre-1972 South Boston feud with the later Winter Hill organization's period of activity.
  • Legal standards: A grand-jury indictment is an allegation; distinguish it from a court's factual findings, contemporaneous police records, convictions, and subsequent reporting.
  • Unsolved violence: Do not elevate theories of who killed Donald Killeen into established facts without reliable corroboration or an adjudicated record.
  • Sources: Favor federal court opinions, court filings, contemporary newspapers, documented interviews, and well-sourced historical scholarship; document disagreements.
  • Canonical biographies: Store donald-killeen.html here and cross-link Bulger through the Winter Hill directory rather than creating duplicate person pages.
  • Historical treatment: Emphasize context, accuracy, and the social consequences of organized crime, without romanticizing the organizations involved.

Linking status: This index links to the planned local file donald-killeen.html. That biography should be placed beside this index.html; its existence on the published site has not been independently verified.

Research Starting Points

These sources document or describe different parts of the history; they do not independently verify every affiliation or contested allegation. Where an indictment and a judicial opinion are both cited, preserve the distinction between the prosecution's theory and the court's findings.

  • United States v. James J. Bulger et al. — Third Superseding Indictment (U.S. District Court, District of Massachusetts, May 23, 2001), especially page 3 — identifies Donald and Kenneth Killeen, their rivalry with the Mullens, and the federal government's account of the approximately 1972 organizational realignment.
  • United States v. Salemme, 91 F. Supp. 2d 141 (D. Mass. 1999) — court findings discussing FBI records, Bulger's association with Donald Killeen, the killing of William O'Sullivan, and federal investigative contacts.
  • The Boston Globe, “As brothers flex muscles, busing enters the picture” (September 19, 1988) — historical reporting on the Killeen gambling operation, Bulger's early role, and Donald Killeen's death; some reporting involves attributed police suspicions, not convictions.
  • WCVB — Thomas King profile (archival local reporting) — retrospective discussion of the Killeen–Mullen conflict and subsequent organizational changes; participant memoir claims should be independently corroborated.
