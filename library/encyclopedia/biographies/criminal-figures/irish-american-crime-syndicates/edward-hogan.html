Edward J. “Jelly Roll” Hogan Jr. (1885–1963)

St. Louis prohibition-era gang leader, principal adversary of Egan’s Rats, and long-serving Missouri Democratic legislator.

1. Historical Overview

Edward John “Jelly Roll” Hogan Jr. was an exceptional figure in the history of Prohibition-era organized crime because his public political career was neither a marginal sideline nor simply a postscript to gang leadership. He was already involved in Democratic politics before national Prohibition, commanded an armed bootlegging organization in St. Louis during its most violent years, and subsequently served for decades in Missouri’s General Assembly. His career exposes the porous boundary between ward-level electoral organization, commercial vice, protection, labor politics and formal legislative authority in an American industrial city. It also complicates the familiar narrative in which an organized-crime boss inevitably dies in a shooting or disappears into prison. [1] [2]

The Hogan Gang competed most fiercely with the organization known as Egan’s Rats. That struggle accelerated after the October 1921 murder of William “Willie” Egan and produced retaliatory shootings through 1923. Newspapers, police and rival participants supplied much of the evidentiary trail, but later histories often compress allegation, suspicion and proof into a single story. This profile treats Hogan’s leadership and legislative service as well established while distinguishing gang attribution from a judicial finding that he personally ordered or committed a particular killing. The purpose is to explain a criminal-political system, not to recreate a colorful legend as though every detail were verified. [4] [8]

2. Identity, Family, and the Birth-Date Conflict

Hogan’s full name appears in political reference material as Edward John Hogan Jr. He was born in St. Louis and grew up in a family that had significant contact with law enforcement rather than existing wholly outside it. His father, Edward John Hogan Sr., is described in the gang histories as a St. Louis police officer or captain. Biographical references name his mother as Margaret N. Farrell. Published narratives also identify a younger brother, James, whose role in early-1920s gang events illustrates how kinship overlapped with the less formal loyalties of political and criminal crews. These connections are useful context, but they do not license assumptions about every relative’s activities. [2] [4]

A genuine dating discrepancy deserves notice. The Political Graveyard’s compiled political biography supplies 15 October 1885, while a St. Louis County local-history account states 1886 without a full birth-record citation. The precise 1885 date is preferred here because it comes with a detailed political and family profile, but this edition has not compared an original civil birth or baptism register. The nickname “Jelly Roll,” sometimes printed as “Jellyroll,” referred in contemporary and retrospective descriptions to his physique; writers commonly add that he resented it. His marriage to Jennie Durr on 15 February 1905 and her death in 1931 appear in political biographical sources. [2] [10]

3. St. Louis, Ward Politics, and the Criminal Marketplace

St. Louis had a distinctive urban political geography. Its dense wards, saloons, industrial employment, transportation routes and municipal patronage networks created places where one person could be useful to a party organization, a labor constituency and an illegal commercial venture at the same time. Beer and liquor distribution relied on taverns, delivery labor, storage facilities and trusted retailers. Political influence could protect a livelihood or grant access to jobs; coercive influence could intimidate commercial rivals or people expected to testify. Those functions need not be identical to qualify as mutually reinforcing sources of power.

The city also housed competing Irish-American-led criminal networks and Italian and Jewish operators who collaborated or fought according to circumstances. “Irish gang” is therefore a label for leadership and historical milieu, not an assertion that each participant belonged to one ethnicity. This biography places Hogan in the St. Louis context rather than assuming that the hierarchical arrangements of New York’s later Mafia families existed in the same form in Missouri during the early 1920s. St. Louis gang alliances were frequently opportunistic and local: the historian should ask which business, street, official and personal conflict was at stake before assigning a permanent organization chart. [4] [8]

4. Early Democratic Organizing and the First House Term

The official Missouri legislator index records Hogan as a Democrat elected to the state House from St. Louis City’s Fourth District in 1916. That electoral year corresponds to legislative service during 1917–1918, not to continuous representation throughout the 1920s. The distinction matters because some popular biographies loosely describe him as leaving the House “around 1920”; the state roster indicates that his initial elected term had already ended. He operated within an era when ward figures could mobilize voters through personal relationships, clubs and direct assistance as well as, in some cases, intimidation. [1] [2]

The electoral history is more reliably attested than lurid descriptions of individual campaigns. Local gang accounts accuse Hogan’s circle of election pressure and machine-style strong-arm behavior, but the sources available for this edition do not provide a complete case-by-case record of such charges. It is more accurate to say that his political standing was a significant feature of the criminal organization’s operating environment. A legislator could know ward captains and law-enforcement figures, and a saloon proprietor could convert acquaintances into support. Neither fact proves that every public decision was criminal or that every supporter was a gang member.

5. Saloon Keeping, Labor Connections, and Economic Base

Hogan’s association with saloon keeping appears across histories of the St. Louis underworld. Accounts locate a principal base around the intersection of Cass and Jefferson Avenues. Such establishments offered more than a point of retail sale: they provided meeting space, a place to recruit carriers or guards, and a clearinghouse of neighborhood intelligence. Before and after Prohibition, tavern trade was tied to breweries and delivery workers. A saloon also made a political figure visible to regular patrons in ways an office in Jefferson City could not. [4]

Biographical registers separately list Hogan as an electrician and as a business representative of Local 303 of the Brewery and Soft Drink Bottlers Union. These are recorded occupational and labor affiliations; they should not automatically be equated with a racketeering conviction or with ownership of the union. Labor relations could provide an entirely legitimate channel of influence while sitting close to the same production and delivery economy that made illegal liquor distribution attractive. The combination of public office, commercial experience and union connections is part of Hogan’s institutional significance, irrespective of later accounts of his gang’s violence. [2]

6. National Prohibition and the Expansion of Bootlegging

Federal Prohibition took effect in January 1920, increasing the value of illegal alcohol supply without erasing demand. Organized sellers who already knew tavern operators, bottlers, delivery routes and the location of warehouses possessed an economic advantage. Hogan’s group is described as moving into large-scale beer and liquor trafficking and competing for distributors and retail customers. St. Louis’s river and rail connections helped make the broader metropolitan region a commercial hub, although specific routes and profits attributed to Hogan should be verified against local records rather than inferred from the city’s transport geography alone. [4]

This change did not make the Hogan Gang an instant, fully centralized corporation. The available narrative evidence suggests a cluster of associates whose shared ventures could include alcohol distribution, enforcement and other offenses. Because it is often difficult to separate the acts of a named member from those directed by Hogan, this article uses “Hogan-associated” for events where responsibility rests chiefly on later attribution. His reputation as a gang boss is supported by multiple accounts, but an institutional analysis of Prohibition benefits from preserving differences among leadership, criminal participation, access to illicit revenue and legal responsibility for a specific act.

7. The Hogan Gang and Its Multiethnic Membership

Hogan’s organization is usually described as predominantly Irish-American rather than exclusively Irish. Local histories list James Hogan, Humbert Costello, Charles Mercurio, Leo Casey, Abe Goldfeder, John “Kink” Connell and Patrick Scanlon among men linked to the operation. Later gang histories also identify Luke Kennedy as a prominent armed associate. The presence of Italian and Jewish members or allies is historically important because it undermines an overly rigid scheme of ethnic gangs that never crossed social boundaries. In practice, capital, experience, personal trust and shared opposition to a rival could matter more than the category applied by a newspaper. [4] [8]

Membership rosters compiled retrospectively require caution. They may combine people active at different times, partners in a single venture, and close associates who were not under a stable chain of command. The list above therefore demonstrates the network’s composition rather than providing a certified membership roll. The gang’s political dimension further complicates the problem: a voter, union member or saloon customer who supported Hogan was not necessarily part of his armed enterprise. The distinction matters for an encyclopedia that seeks to identify individuals without treating an entire community as a criminal organization.

8. The State Beverage Department Appointment

A particularly revealing episode was Hogan’s reported appointment in 1920 as a deputy inspector in Missouri’s state beverage administration. The St. Louis County historical account places this role alongside his leadership of illegal alcohol activity, producing an evident conflict between a state-regulated supply chain and an illicit commercial interest. The claim is repeated in specialized gang histories, but this first-edition biography has not reproduced an original appointment certificate, personnel file, payroll record or disciplinary order. It should therefore be read as well-developed secondary reporting, rather than as independently authenticated appointment documentation. [4]

The practical stakes are apparent without speculating about precise sums paid in bribes. An inspection position could provide information about bottling activity, inspections and enforcement priorities. The possibility of abuse is historically relevant; the existence of a position alone does not prove that every inspection was manipulated or that a particular producer bought favorable treatment. Where a local-history author employs humorous hypotheticals about delayed inspections, this biography avoids converting those hypotheticals into named crimes. The larger issue is the blurred line between public regulatory authority and the market that Prohibition drove underground.

9. Bootlegging, Robbery Allegations, and the Limits of Attribution

Liquor distribution provided the central economic context for the Hogan Gang’s rise, but its members were also linked in histories to armed robberies and violent enforcement. Accounts describe mail-robbery episodes in Missouri in 1921 and the killing of a potential witness, bank messenger Erris Pillow. It is essential to distinguish a gang member’s alleged involvement, an arrest or trial, and a proven order by Hogan personally. A narrative that merges these categories might tell an exciting story while weakening the historian’s confidence in it. The robbery and witness-killing material is treated here as part of the group’s reported violent milieu, not as a court-established personal conviction of Hogan. [8]

Protection and intimidation served economic purposes in Prohibition markets: suppliers feared diversion of cargo, witnesses feared retaliation, and businesses wanted reliable deliveries without interference. Yet not every shooting or robbery around the Cass Avenue neighborhood was a centrally planned action. The evidence is often drawn from rival testimony, police attribution and later retelling. Some members may have been pursuing independent operations, or alliances may have shifted over time. For these reasons the article does not assign a comprehensive body count, invent ledger totals or claim that every named offense was part of a formal Hogan chain of command.

10. Egan’s Rats and the Struggle for St. Louis

Hogan’s most consequential adversary was Egan’s Rats, an Irish-American-led St. Louis organization associated with William “Willie” Egan and, after his death, William “Dint” Colbeck. The rivals operated within overlapping commercial and political territory. Their conflict therefore involved more than two groups of men seeking notoriety: it concerned distribution, personal loyalty, control of valuable business relationships and the ability of rival leaders to appear untouchable. Names attached to these groups sometimes persist in later narratives even when actual membership and alliances changed. [4] [8]

Contemporary factional politics were part of the antagonism. Egan and Hogan had competing political relationships as well as competing underworld interests. Analysts should avoid treating that rivalry as a struggle between two homogeneous ethnic groups: the opposing organizations shared an Irish-American milieu and drew on broader networks. The central distinction was organizational and personal, not ethnic. Moreover, the existence of a celebrated gang “war” should not erase the impact on nonparticipants—workers, neighbors, pedestrians and the families whose homes lay in the path of gunfire.

11. Max Greenberg, Defection, and the Murder of Willie Egan

The crisis escalated in 1921 amid allegations about an Egan associate, Max Greenberg, and a disputed or misappropriated liquor shipment. Later accounts hold that Greenberg broke with Egan’s circle, aligned with Hogan’s men and financed a killing. On 31 October 1921, Willie Egan was shot outside his saloon near Fourteenth and Franklin in St. Louis. The shooting transformed commercial antagonism into an extended feud. Historical descriptions frequently name James Hogan, Luke Kennedy and John Doyle as suspected attackers, drawing on identifications and subsequent retellings, but those allegations must not be presented as a judicial finding against every individual named. [4]

Egan’s death also altered the rival organization’s leadership. Dint Colbeck emerged as a central figure in the campaign against the Hogan network. A criminal-political conflict that had been negotiated by living rivals became more difficult to contain once a leader was killed: personal revenge, credibility and the need to reassure followers were added to the economic dispute. Greenberg’s apparent change of allegiance shows how defection and protection arrangements could destabilize several enterprises at once. This reconstruction follows the best-developed local historical account, while acknowledging that no complete original murder-file collation was performed for this edition.

12. Retaliation and the December 1921 Shooting

Retaliatory attacks in the weeks after Egan’s murder help explain why St. Louis residents perceived the feud as a public emergency. On 30 December 1921, a group linked to Egan’s Rats fired on Hogan-associated men as they left a downtown police station. Historical accounts describe Luke Kennedy as seriously wounded and attorney Jacob Mackler as narrowly escaping injury. The incident is often cited because violence at or near an official building exposed the limits of ordinary police deterrence in a conflict involving recognizable local figures. [4]

The same episode also cautions against assuming that proximity to police or an attorney conferred immunity. The men involved could be detained, questioned or publicly identified and still remain targets. In the early months of the war, gunmen selected visible, symbolic locations as well as private homes. Precise motives for each attack can be inferred only cautiously from the sequence; later accounts written with knowledge of the war’s end sometimes interpret every event as part of one coherent plan. The surviving summary record is strongest on the fact of repeated attacks and much thinner on definitively establishing each principal’s instructions.

13. The Death of Luke Kennedy and the Cycle of Violence

The violence continued into 1922. Following earlier injuries, Luke Kennedy was caught and killed in April in an attack usually attributed to men from the Egan faction. He appears in subsequent underworld histories as a particularly prominent Hogan-associated gunman. His killing illustrates the escalation of retaliatory violence from commercial enforcement to an increasingly personal conflict in which association with a rival leader could make a person a target. The specific words reportedly spoken by attackers appear in later storytelling and are not reproduced here as independently verified dialogue. [4]

In this environment, crime did not confine itself to a bounded underground. Gunfire affected adjoining streets and could endanger anyone near an intended target. The label “gang war” should be understood as a retrospective shorthand for multiple attacks, not a declaration of legal belligerency or a guarantee that each victim was a participant. Hogan’s prominence made him a symbolic target as well as an organizational leader; it did not require him to be physically present at each violent incident. This distinction is important when evaluating later claims about orders, counterattacks and responsibility.

14. Cass Avenue, the Plumbing-Shop Attack, and Civilian Danger

Among the most vivid incidents in the surviving accounts were attacks on places tied to the two factions. Writers describe Hogan-associated men firing on Colbeck’s plumbing business and retaliatory shots at the Hogan family residence at 3035 Cass Avenue. Those attacks brought the conflict into a neighborhood setting where relatives and passersby might be endangered regardless of whether they participated in illegal trade. More than one assault on the Hogan property is described across 1922–1923; individual accounts differ in how incidents are grouped. [4] [8]

A residence targeted for its owner’s notoriety becomes a form of coercion against an entire household. Similarly, a legitimate commercial frontage could be used as a message to a rival even when workers or customers had no role in the dispute. Such conduct is one reason the history should not be reduced to a series of charismatic names. The economics of the feud must be assessed together with its public consequences: fear, damaged businesses, police deployment and the pressure placed on local mediators to halt the shootings.

15. Monsignor Timothy Dempsey and the 1922 Truce

With public concern rising, Monsignor Timothy Dempsey became associated with efforts to persuade the factions to stop shooting. Secondary accounts describe private meetings with representatives of the rival groups and a ceasefire in mid-1922. The intervention is best understood as community mediation intended to prevent immediate bloodshed. It was not an amnesty, a court judgment or a public finding that claims against either organization were unfounded. The practical incentive for the factions was straightforward: continued warfare was costly, dangerous and increasingly likely to invite aggressive policing. [4]

Accounts say Greenberg was escorted out of St. Louis after this tentative arrangement and that the resulting peace left Egan’s Rats with a stronger immediate position. Such descriptions are reconstructions drawn from later histories rather than a fully authenticated written treaty. A ceasefire does not eliminate the political or economic causes of a conflict, and the subsequent course of the war shows how fragile the accommodation was. This biography labels the mid-1922 understanding as a first truce to distinguish it from the later effort in 1923 to end the violence publicly.

16. The September 1922 Ambush and the Failure of Peace

Despite a nominal lull, violence did not disappear. Local histories report that on 2 September 1922 Colbeck-associated gunmen pursued Abe Goldfeder and Max Gordon and shot at them in the vicinity of Locust Street. Gordon is reported to have lost an eye. These details belong to a sufficiently consistent secondary chronology, but the available material does not include every original arrest report, witness statement or hospital record. The event illustrates the central weakness of an informal ceasefire: factions could retain their weapons, personnel and incentives while members pursued revenge or demonstrated resolve. [4]

Hogan’s position in that interval is difficult to reconstruct in individual detail. As the organization’s public face, he was blamed for or associated with a variety of actions, but leadership did not mean each shooter acted pursuant to a documented order. Rival crews could exploit uncertainty by blaming opponents for new incidents. Public officials, meanwhile, confronted the practical problem of suppressing armed groups with strong neighborhood ties and knowledge of enforcement routines. The history of the truce is therefore not simply the history of a promise broken; it is a case study in the limits of personal agreements without credible institutional enforcement.

17. The Killing of Jacob Mackler and the 1923 Escalation

The murder of attorney Jacob Mackler on 21 February 1923 marked a renewed high point in the confrontation. Mackler had been associated with Hogan’s side and had survived the December 1921 shooting. Accounts attribute his death to Egan-aligned attackers under Colbeck’s influence, but one must distinguish commonly accepted historical attribution from the outcome of any particular homicide prosecution. The killing of a lawyer with ties to the organization reinforced the public impression that professional relationships offered little protection during the feud. [4]

During March 1923, accounts describe another attack on Hogan’s Cass Avenue home and a roadside exchange involving Hogan and Humbert Costello with men associated with the rival faction. A car connected with the attackers reportedly struck and injured a schoolboy. That detail deserves special attention: criminal violence was not confined to its declared principals, and the most enduring consequence of an attack could fall on someone outside both networks. Dates, injuries and identities warrant checking against the original 1923 newspaper and police archive; this edition does not overstate secondary summaries as complete incident files.

18. Public Letters and the End of the Hogan–Egan War

By the latter part of 1923, public pressure to stop the violence had mounted. Histories credit Monsignor Dempsey, police officials and a newspaper intermediary with helping bring the conflict to a second and more durable accommodation. Hogan and Colbeck were reported to have issued public statements that they would end hostilities, with the letters printed in the St. Louis Star. The public aspect of the arrangement mattered: the leaders were making a claim before residents who had seen both sides disregard normal restraints. [4] [8]

The date 1923 is used here as the effective end point of the principal Egan–Hogan gang war, although isolated violence and later rivalries should not be ruled out simply because a newspaper announced a settlement. The settlement did not resolve every robbery, assault or murder case, nor did it convert bootlegging into lawful business. Rather, it reduced the immediate incentives for reciprocal street warfare. This distinction between a negotiated stop to shootings and the actual disappearance of criminal enterprise is central to understanding Hogan’s later career.

19. Egan’s Rats Decline and the Reconfiguration of Local Crime

In the years following the ceasefire, Egan’s Rats lost influence through internal fractures, violent incidents and major criminal cases involving robbery and other offenses. The weakening of the rival organization did not necessarily represent a strategic military triumph for Hogan. It was also the consequence of policing, prosecution and unstable partnerships within the underworld. Later summaries often present the Hogan Gang as ultimately benefiting from this collapse, with a continuing market in illegal liquor during the remainder of Prohibition. [8]

The larger St. Louis criminal economy also changed over time. Networks that survived the early 1920s could find new partners and commercial arrangements. There is no reason to imagine the Hogan Gang existed in an unchanged form from 1917 through 1933. Its name is most useful for the period when Hogan was a conspicuous leader and Egan’s Rats a powerful enemy. The end of Prohibition in December 1933 changed the legal structure of alcohol distribution and helps explain why direct continuity claims should be examined carefully instead of assuming the gang simply continued the same work under a different sign.

20. Return to the Missouri House, 1935–1942

Hogan’s second period in the Missouri House is a firmly documented element of his career. The secretary of state’s election index records victories in 1934, 1936, 1938 and 1940; political biographical references translate those elections into House service from 1935 through 1942. Combined with the earlier 1917–1918 term, the record accounts for five House terms. Dates commonly printed as 1934–1940 refer to election years, not to the complete years of service, a distinction that has caused confusion in popular accounts. [1] [2]

His reentry into office demonstrates political durability, but historians should resist the assumption that the electorate endorsed every allegation about him. Voting decisions were formed within party organizations, local services, labor relationships and a very different public-information environment. His criminal reputation was widely discussed in later histories; the legislative record demonstrates officeholding, not the content of each voter’s reasons. A scholarly biography should therefore examine the coexistence of political legitimacy and criminal notoriety without substituting a single moralistic explanation for a complex local political culture.

21. The 1943–1944 Missouri Constitutional Convention

Hogan’s public life extended beyond routine legislative sessions. The political-biographical record identifies him as a delegate to the Missouri constitutional convention from the Thirty-Third District during 1943–1944. The convention formed part of a broader state-level process of constitutional revision. The identification is significant because it locates Hogan not merely in ward elections but in a formal institution responsible for the framework of Missouri government. [2]

The documentation reviewed for this edition establishes his participation and dates, but it does not support a detailed narrative of his speeches, committee assignments or decisive influence over particular constitutional provisions. Those questions require a session-by-session reading of the convention debates, journals and roll calls. His role should also not be made a proxy for the positions of every other delegate. What can safely be said is that the same individual who had been publicly associated with St. Louis’s 1920s underworld took part in a significant statewide political institution two decades later.

22. Four Senate Terms and the Official Record, 1945–1960

The Missouri Secretary of State records Hogan’s Senate election years as 1944, 1948, 1952 and 1956. These four election victories correspond to service from 1945 through 1960. Political reference material distinguishes his Thirty-Third District tenure in 1945–1948 from his Seventh District tenure from 1949–1960. A reader encountering a state index that ends with the election year 1956 should not misread that shorthand to mean Hogan ceased to serve in 1956. The term produced by the 1956 election ran into 1960. [1] [2]

These years unfolded long after the peak of the Egan–Hogan war and extended into the period of postwar urban change. Hogan’s political and labor associations provide a basis for further research into legislative priorities, patronage and city–state relations, but this biography does not invent bills he sponsored or committee votes he cast. The State Historical Society of Missouri’s oral-history project records other political observers remembering Hogan as part of the old St. Louis world. One such interview explicitly reports secondhand knowledge and uncertain details, a useful reminder that memory is not a substitute for official legislative journals. [6]

23. The 1960 Election and Theodore D. McNeal

Hogan’s Senate service ended after the 1960 election, when Theodore D. McNeal won the seat. Missouri’s state historical timeline identifies McNeal as the state’s first Black state senator, elected on 8 November 1960. That transition carries significance beyond one veteran senator’s departure: it marks an important change in Missouri political representation and the ability of Black voters and candidates to gain access to the state legislature. [7]

Some biographies say Hogan “retired” in 1960, but political accounts describe McNeal defeating him. Because language about the precise primary-versus-general-election contest varies among retrospective accounts, this edition states the secure outcome rather than asserting an unverified stage of the contest. The political transition should not be treated as a simplistic duel between a lone criminal figure and a reformer: the result emerged within the wider dynamics of St. Louis political representation, neighborhood organization and civil-rights-era change. The key fact for Hogan’s chronology is that the Senate tenure ending in 1960 was followed by McNeal’s service.

24. Later Life, Property, and Death in 1963

The St. Louis County local-history account describes Hogan’s later residence on a sizable rural property near Eureka, with a substantial house constructed in the 1930s. Local architectural history provides a different perspective on the figure remembered in underworld narratives: a landowner with a durable presence outside the sites of his earlier saloons and shootings. The article’s statements about the property, acreage and building date are helpful leads, but primary deeds, construction documents and ownership chains were not independently collated here. [4]

Hogan died in St. Louis on 11 August 1963, aged 77. Political biographical records cite heart disease and diabetes and list burial at Calvary Cemetery. He therefore outlived the period that made the Hogan Gang infamous and most of the politicians and criminals who figured in the early-1920s feud. His death appears to have been from natural causes; there is no factual basis in the sources consulted for presenting it as a late gangland assassination. The contrast with associates who died violently should be observed without making survival into a moral endorsement of his conduct. [2]

25. Historical Assessment: Gang Leadership and Political Power

Hogan’s biography is best understood as a study of how several kinds of authority could converge in one city. His documented legislative career belonged to formal democratic institutions; his reported leadership of bootleggers and violent associates belonged to the illicit economy. The overlap created incentives for influence and raised questions about enforcement and public trust, but the researcher must still distinguish public office from a criminal conviction and a gang’s conduct from an individual’s legally proven act. Neither a political record nor a gangster memoir can substitute for the other. [1] [4]

The Hogan–Egan war also illustrates the costs imposed by violent market competition on communities not party to the conflict. The deaths of gang associates and the wounding or endangerment of nonparticipants were not mere episodes in a contest for reputation. They were consequences of businesses operated through coercion when Prohibition produced valuable illicit distribution channels. The periodic truces show how informal mediation could lower immediate violence while leaving the underlying economic activity intact.

Finally, Hogan’s career resists an easy story of criminal “retirement” followed by respectable civic service. He entered politics before the peak of his gang’s notoriety, left and returned to legislative office, and remained a major officeholder into 1960. His historical importance lies not in an unverified body count or mythical underworld title but in the documented coexistence of party politics, labor affiliation, neighborhood commerce and organized crime in a changing St. Louis. Further archival work should focus on contemporaneous police files, legislative debates, court records and party records rather than copying unqualified details from later compilations.

Police and politicsHis criminal reputation and electoral roles coexisted. This establishes no particular quid pro quo or political-corruption conviction.

Sources and Further Reading

Source policy: State archival indexes and political records take precedence for election and office dates. For violent events, the narrative attributes details to specialist histories and local-history reporting, with explicit limitations where original police and newspaper records were not collated. Sources 10–12 are principally research pathways and are not evidence that every referenced archival volume was inspected.

  1. Source 1. Missouri Secretary of State, Missouri State Archives. “Historical Missouri State Legislators: H.” Official retrospective election listing for Hogan’s House and Senate service.
  2. Source 2. The Political Graveyard. “Hogan, Edward John, Jr.” Detailed political-biographical register, giving birth and death dates, parental names, marriage, occupations, state constitutional convention and service terms. Secondary compilation, not an original vital-record image.
  3. Source 3. Missouri State Capitol Commission. “Centennial Timeline,” entry of 14 September 1920. Public institutional account of the Capitol confrontation, citing Daniel Waugh; the page contains an evident gang-name slip in its parenthetical description.
  4. Source 4. St. Louis County Arts & History. “Jelly Roll Hogan: Eureka’s Most Notorious Gangster” (1 November 2017). Detailed local-history narrative of the Hogan Gang, gang war and later property. Useful context, but colorful interpretation and birth-year error require comparison with official records.
  5. Source 5. St. Louis Public Library. “St. Louis/Missouri Portraits Collection,” SC 18:84. Archival finding aid expressly includes an Edward J. “Jelly Roll” Hogan portrait; this biography does not reproduce the image or claim reproduction permission.
  6. Source 6. State Historical Society of Missouri, Politics in Missouri Oral History Project. Robert R. Welborn, interview by Will Sarvis, 24 March 1997, collection C3929, transcript page 49. An explicitly secondhand recollection of Hogan and Michael Kinney; offered as evidence about historical memory, not eyewitness proof of 1920s offenses.
  7. Source 7. Missouri Secretary of State. “Timeline of Historic Missouri: 1950–Present.” Lists Theodore D. McNeal’s election on 8 November 1960 as the state’s first Black state senator.
  8. Source 8. Daniel Waugh. Egan’s Rats: The Untold Story of the Gang That Ruled Prohibition-Era St. Louis (Cumberland House, 2007); Gangs of St. Louis: Men of Respect (History Press, 2010). Specialist book-length histories cited by local accounts; not exhaustively checked against every printed page in preparing this edition.
  9. Source 9. T. J. English. Paddy Whacked: The Untold Story of the Irish American Gangster (HarperCollins, 2005). Broad comparative treatment of Irish-American organized crime, offered for further reading rather than individual event-level authentication.
  10. Source 10. Missouri Digital Heritage, Missouri State Archives. “Official Manual of the State of Missouri—The Blue Book.” Institutional entry point to digitized contemporary state manuals. A proposed primary-verification pathway; not represented as a fully collated 1917–1960 set.
  11. Source 11. Missouri Historical Society, Library and Research Center. “Newspapers.” Describes access to historical St. Louis Post-Dispatch, Globe-Democrat and other city newspaper runs for future event-level authentication. Research access guide, not contemporaneous newspaper evidence by itself.
  12. Source 12. State Historical Society of Missouri. “Politics in Missouri Oral History Project,” detailed index, collection C3929. Provides additional leads concerning Hogan’s place in Missouri political memory.
  13. Source 13. St. Louis County Arts & History and Daniel Waugh. Cross-reference methodological note: 1921–1923 events in local synthesis are best verified next against contemporaneous police records, newspapers and Waugh’s full citations. The edition distinguishes historical attribution from a criminal judgment.
