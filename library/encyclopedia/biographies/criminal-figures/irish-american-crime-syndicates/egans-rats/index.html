Egan's Rats Biographies

St. Louis's Kerry Patch, political patronage, Prohibition-era racketeering, and the leadership succession from Thomas “Snake” Kinney to Willie Egan and William “Dinty” Colbeck.

Collection Overview

Egan's Rats was a St. Louis criminal organization whose origins are generally traced to the Ashley Street Gang and the city's historically Irish-American Kerry Patch neighborhood. Historical accounts connect its early development to Thomas “Snake” Kinney and Thomas “Tom” Egan. The organization operated at the intersection of street-level crime, neighborhood political machines, saloon networks, and later Prohibition-era racketeering. Those connections require historical analysis, not an assumption that all local political activity or neighborhood residents were involved in crime.

After the deaths of its early leaders, the organization underwent important changes. William “Willie” Egan, Thomas Egan's younger brother, became a principal leader before he was killed in 1921. William P. “Dinty” Colbeck subsequently directed the gang during a period marked by the Egan–Hogan conflict and federal robbery prosecutions. The 1925 federal appellate decision Colbeck v. United States provides an especially valuable judicial record for the later period; it should not be mistaken for a complete judicial history of the gang's membership or earlier activity.

This directory identifies three selected canonical biographies from MGU's current filing list: Kinney, Willie Egan, and Colbeck. Thomas Egan, the organization's namesake and an important earlier leader, belongs in the historical overview but does not have a confirmed biography filename in this three-profile set. The biography directory is therefore selective, not a comprehensive leadership roster.

Research Themes

  • Irish-American migration, the Kerry Patch neighborhood, and the Ashley Street Gang's development into Egan's Rats
  • Thomas “Snake” Kinney's political career and the use of intimidation and patronage by neighborhood criminal networks
  • Thomas Egan's influence and the distinction between the gang's namesake and his younger brother Willie Egan
  • Saloon headquarters, organized theft, political protection, and the shift into the Prohibition economy
  • Leadership succession after 1919 and 1921, including conflict with the rival Hogan Gang
  • The 1923 Staunton, Illinois mail robbery, 1924 federal convictions, and 1925 appellate decision concerning Colbeck and others
  • Distinguishing judicial findings, federal archival descriptions, contemporaneous journalism, and retrospective crime histories

Origins and Political Influence

  1. Thomas “Snake” Kinney

    Historical classification: Early founder and political figure. Kinney was associated with the Ashley Street Gang and the formative St. Louis network that became Egan's Rats. He entered electoral politics and served in the Missouri State Senate. His biography should examine the relationship between neighborhood political mobilization and criminal intimidation without equating all political supporters with gang members. Historical studies identify Thomas Egan as Kinney's early associate and an increasingly influential leader of the criminal organization.

    Main biography page: thomas-kinney.html

Foundational figure without a linked biography: Thomas “Tom” Egan helped build the organization and became its namesake. He is discussed here for historical completeness, but no thomas-egan.html entry is asserted to exist in MGU's present selected set.

The Egan Succession

  1. William “Willie” Egan

    Historical classification: Gang leader and successor to Thomas Egan. Willie Egan was the younger brother of the organization's namesake and a central figure in its transition to the Prohibition era. Historical accounts describe his leadership following Thomas Egan's death in 1919 and his killing outside a St. Louis saloon on October 31, 1921. The killing intensified already hostile relations with the Hogan faction, but claims identifying specific perpetrators must be separated from legal findings and contemporary police reporting.

    Main biography page: willie-egan.html

Identity distinction: Thomas Egan and William “Willie” Egan were different people, and their leadership periods must not be conflated. Similarly, the St. Louis Hogan Gang was a rival organization, not an alternative name for Egan's Rats.

The Colbeck Era

  1. William P. “Dinty” Colbeck

    Historical classification: Later leader of Egan's Rats; federal robbery defendant and convicted offender. Colbeck is identified as a leader in federal archival descriptions and later congressional material. He was a principal defendant in federal prosecutions arising from the May 26, 1923 robbery of mail custodians at Staunton, Illinois. In Colbeck v. United States, 10 F.2d 401 (7th Cir. 1925), the appellate court affirmed convictions and sentences. The opinion is judicial evidence of the federal criminal case; it does not itself establish every gang affiliation or rank described in later histories.

    Main biography page: william-colbeck.html

Source distinction: The National Archives' description of the Colbeck case file characterizes the defendants as members of Egan's Rats led by Colbeck. That catalog description is a useful archival finding aid, while the appellate opinion directly addresses the adjudicated charges and convictions. Both should be attributed according to their distinct evidentiary roles.

Historical Record and Organizational Context

Neighborhood origins: The Kerry Patch district and Ashley Street Gang form the setting for the organization's emergence. Daniel Waugh's Egan's Rats and regional historical journalism describe how Kinney and Thomas Egan combined local criminal networks with electoral influence. The scale of membership given in retrospective narratives is not treated here as an audited count.

Leadership chronology: The three selected biography subjects mark different periods: Kinney's early influence, Willie Egan's succession after Thomas Egan, and Colbeck's later leadership. The group is not presented as a single uninterrupted command structure with a fully documented roster.

Prohibition and gang rivalry: St. Louis experienced conflict among criminal networks competing over illicit markets and local influence. The Egan–Hogan feud belongs in the history of both organizations, but being a rival or an alleged participant in a violent episode does not by itself prove individual gang membership.

Federal prosecution: The 1923 Staunton mail robbery and ensuing convictions provide a firm documentary anchor for Colbeck's era. The 1925 decision upheld the relevant judgments; the legal record should be consulted rather than relying on popular retellings to determine the charges, procedural history, and outcome.

Editorial Standards

Collection scope: Three selected biography subjects from the MGU filing plan. This is neither a complete Egan's Rats membership roll nor a claim that every historically significant leader already has a completed biography.

  • Identity: Differentiate founder Thomas “Snake” Kinney, namesake Thomas Egan, brother William “Willie” Egan, and successor William P. “Dinty” Colbeck.
  • Affiliation: Distinguish leaders and documented members from associates, political allies, alleged accomplices, and Hogan Gang rivals.
  • Evidence: Prefer court opinions, original case files, contemporaneous St. Louis newspapers, legislative material, and critically evaluated historical scholarship.
  • Legal precision: Separate an indictment or police allegation from a conviction, appellate ruling, or unresolved homicide.
  • Chronology: Preserve the differences among the formative, Egan-family, and Colbeck leadership periods.
  • Ethnic precision: “Irish-American” describes the historical organizational context; it is not proof of every participant's identity or ancestry.
  • Canonical filing: Maintain one biography per subject and use cross-references for activity involving multiple gangs or related organizations.
  • Historical purpose: Examine the costs of coercion, political corruption, and violence without glamorizing organized crime.

Linking status: Biography hyperlinks use the filenames identified in MGU's prior filing list. Deploy those HTML files beside this index.html to make the relative links operational; their presence at the final live location has not been independently verified.

Research Starting Points

The sources below support the directory's outline and provide different types of historical evidence. They are not substitutes for a source register in each full biography.

  • St. Louis Magazine — “Raising Kane” (2009) — regional historical reporting on Kerry Patch, Thomas Egan, Kinney, political influence, and the Rats' early violence.
  • Daniel Waugh, Egan's Rats: The Untold Story of the Prohibition-Era Gang That Ruled St. Louis (Cumberland House, 2007) — specialist monograph providing a framework for the gang's origins and development; use alongside primary records.
  • Colbeck v. United States, 10 F.2d 401 (7th Cir. 1925) — appellate opinion addressing federal mail-robbery convictions arising from the 1923 Staunton incident.
  • Mob Files — William P. “Dinty” Colbeck — record-based finding aid identifying National Archives case records, the federal appeal, and a later Senate committee exhibit, with explicit distinctions between their evidentiary weight.
  • Ken Zimmerman Jr. — “Death of the Head Rat” — secondary reconstruction of Willie Egan's killing with references to contemporary St. Louis Post-Dispatch coverage; verify details in the cited newspapers before drawing definitive conclusions.
