James Coonan (born 1946)

Hell’s Kitchen organized-crime leader whose Westies enterprise was dismantled through federal racketeering prosecutions and whose 75-year sentence remained in force after a 2025 appeal.

1. Historical Overview

James Michael “Jimmy” Coonan was a principal leader of the Westies, a criminal organization based in Manhattan’s Hell’s Kitchen that challenged Mickey Spillane’s established network in the 1960s and developed into an association engaged in illegal lending, extortion, narcotics, gambling, and violent enforcement. The record of Coonan’s career is unusually well documented for a neighborhood organized-crime figure because it appears in federal trial histories, appellate opinions, and later sentence-reduction proceedings. At the same time, many colorful stories about his early years come primarily from retrospective reporting rather than documents contemporaneous with the alleged incidents. [1] [2]

His leadership was not merely a sequence of personal confrontations. Federal evidence described an enduring criminal enterprise with an identifiable core, overlapping revenue streams, labor-union contacts, and mechanisms for distributing proceeds. Coonan’s relationship with Francis “Mickey” Featherstone was critical to the organization’s development; its later relationship with the Gambino crime family changed its access to financing, protection, and outside influence. The sources distinguish Coonan’s group from the earlier Spillane-led operation, even though both operated in the same urban territory. [2]

A February 1988 jury verdict ended in convictions on ten counts against Coonan, followed by a combined federal sentence of 75 years on 11 May 1988. In July 2025, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit rejected his request to use the modern sentence-reduction statute, holding that the relevant provision does not apply to his pre-November 1987 conduct. The court recorded a Bureau of Prisons projected mandatory-release date of 1 June 2030, which is a projection, not a guarantee of future release or independently verified current custody information. [1] [3]

2. Early Life and the Limits of the Biographical Record

Secondary biographical descriptions commonly report that Coonan was born in Manhattan on 21 December 1946 and use the full name James Michael Coonan. This profile retains those details as reported biographical information, not as a certified vital-record finding: no original birth certificate was examined for this edition. His upbringing was connected to Hell’s Kitchen, but a precise reconstruction of his schooling, employment, family finances, and earliest criminal contacts requires records beyond the appellate decisions. [7] [8]

A widely repeated origin story claims that an encounter between Coonan’s father and Mickey Spillane motivated the younger Coonan to mount a gun attack on Spillane or his associates. The Crime Museum recounts a version of this story, but its popular biographical page is not a transcript of contemporary police testimony or a certified case record. A disciplined encyclopedia should neither suppress the existence of the narrative nor present its dramatic details as independently proven. [7]

The better-attested turning point is organizational rather than psychological. The Second Circuit summarized trial evidence that Coonan and associates had begun contesting Spillane’s predominance by the mid-1960s. The motive may have included family grievance, ambition, money, or multiple factors; the available judicial record does not establish one anecdote as a complete causal explanation. [2]

3. Hell’s Kitchen: Territory, Labor, and Criminal Opportunity

In the period of Coonan’s rise, Hell’s Kitchen was a densely inhabited West Side neighborhood of residents, workers, neighborhood businesses, taverns, industrial employers, and transportation connections. The district’s social networks and proximity to the waterfront and major employment channels created opportunities for a small coercive group to influence illegal credit, gambling, or access to work. This did not make crime representative of the neighborhood or of its Irish-American residents, most of whom were not participants in the enterprise. [2] [6]

The Westies’ activities crossed neighborhood boundaries. The 1991 appellate opinion describes links to the West Side piers and International Longshoremen’s Association Local 1909, the Theatrical Teamsters’ Local 817, and later cocaine operations extending into Manhattan’s Upper East Side. The organization therefore cannot be interpreted only as a gang protecting a few blocks of streets: it was also an illicit network exploiting commercial and labor-market interdependencies. [2]

In historical analysis, the term territory should be understood as an asserted power to control criminal income and intimidate competing participants, not as recognized ownership of streets or public institutions. A frightened business owner or indebted borrower could comply with demands without consenting to any legitimate authority.

4. The Challenge to Mickey Spillane

Mickey Spillane led an older Hell’s Kitchen organized-crime network associated with gambling, loansharking, and neighborhood influence. According to the Second Circuit’s later review of Westies trial evidence, Coonan and his confederates sought to take control from Spillane in the mid-1960s and eventually succeeded. This judicial reconstruction supports the existence of a prolonged struggle but does not establish every shooting, conversation, and supposed ultimatum in the later popular literature. [2]

The struggle was shaped by the economics of competition. Criminal authority could yield a share of gambling revenue, interest payments, influence over who collected debts, and opportunities to extort access to work. Removing rival organizers or frightening their intermediaries had financial implications. Historical accounts place the conflict’s violent peak in the mid-1970s; one specialist synthesis records the killing of Spillane associate Eddie Cummiskey in August 1976. [4]

Spillane was murdered in Queens in May 1977, after his control had eroded. Some published reconstructions attribute the killing to Gambino-linked killer Roy DeMeo acting to assist Coonan. That attribution should be labeled a historical allegation rather than presented as if Coonan had been tried and convicted of ordering Spillane’s murder. The federal RICO case history can substantiate Coonan’s eventual control of the West Side without independently settling the identity or legal responsibility of every participant in Spillane’s homicide. [2] [4]

5. The Partnership with Mickey Featherstone

Francis “Mickey” Featherstone became Coonan’s principal lieutenant and, in the language of the 1991 opinion, second-in-command by the mid-1970s. Their partnership combined Coonan’s senior leadership with a close associate whose role in intimidation, enforcement, and later criminal activity figured prominently in testimony. The pair were important to the Westies’ reputation, but the group also depended on numerous other associates, intermediaries, borrowers, and labor-linked contacts. [2]

Calling the Westies a formal hierarchy can be misleading. Trial witnesses described an informal inner crew that controlled many major transactions and operations. Membership and task assignment changed over time, and the criminal enterprise’s coherence lay in coordinated action rather than a charter or uniform organizational structure. Courts can recognize an association-in-fact enterprise without identifying a corporation-like chain of command. [2]

The Coonan–Featherstone relationship later deteriorated. Featherstone’s wrongful conviction in the Michael Holly killing became a decisive source of alienation; Featherstone and his wife Marcelle eventually aided investigators, and his conviction was vacated. The evidentiary record supports that outcome more strongly than some familiar descriptions of the partners’ private quarrels. [2] [5]

6. The Westies as an Organized-Crime Enterprise

“The Westies” was one of several names used for the Coonan organization. The 1991 appellate decision also records “Coonan’s Crew,” “Westside Guys,” and “Westside Irish Mob.” The important institutional identity is the specific group assembled around Coonan and his confederates, not the prior Spillane organization. The label Westies should not be applied retrospectively to every Irish-American criminal in Hell’s Kitchen. [2]

Prosecutors used the Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act (RICO) to address the combination of repeated offenses and organizational continuity. An association-in-fact could consist of a flexible criminal group acting together over time. The legal issues in the 1991 appellate opinion concerned Kevin Kelly, a later associate, but the court’s historical summary included evidence about Coonan, Featherstone, the criminal businesses, and the larger enterprise. [2]

The Westies’ structure enabled participants with different specialties to collect illegal debts, arrange jobs through coercive influence, sell narcotics, or commit violent acts while sharing organizational backing. This did not mean every member was personally responsible for every offense. A sound biography separates leadership findings, acts attributed to Coonan personally in the judicial record, and acts committed by other members within the enterprise. [1] [2]

7. Loansharking, Gambling, and Everyday Coercion

The appellate record describes a loansharking business that Coonan established by the mid-1970s. Coonan supplied money to Westies members at high rates, and members then made unlawful high-interest loans to people in and around Hell’s Kitchen. Such an arrangement placed leadership closer to wholesale financing while collectors and enforcers handled individual borrowers. It also spread both profits and risks through the organization. [2]

Collections were backed by intimidation and violence. The court recounted trial evidence that individual associates physically abused borrowers who fell behind and used the Westies’ broader reputation to enforce payment. Loansharking thus functioned as more than lending at a prohibited rate: customers could face coercive threats, and repayment disputes could produce assault. [2]

Gambling and counterfeiting appear among the enterprise’s activities in later judicial descriptions, as do fraud and other offenses. Distinguishing the full group’s repertoire from proven criminal counts against Coonan remains necessary. There is no well-supported single number for the organization’s annual revenue, nor a verifiable accounting that converts alleged proceeds into a precise personal net worth for Coonan. Published claims of millions in profits should be read skeptically absent audited seized records or a traceable court exhibit. [1] [3]

8. Waterfront and Labor-Union Extortion

The West Side waterfront was a lucrative setting for criminal interference because access to jobs, loading activity, transport, and union-linked positions could be used to pressure workers and employers. The court’s narrative describes Westies activity affecting International Longshoremen’s Association Local 1909, with payments flowing to Westies associates and eventually to Coonan. It is important to distinguish corrupt individuals and criminally extorted payments from the many union members who did not participate in wrongdoing. [2]

Another important target was the Theatrical Teamsters’ Local 817, associated with film and television transportation work. Trial evidence described Westies demands for union cards and employment opportunities, backed in some instances by threats or assaults against officials. For the organization, control of job placement or collection streams was a recurring economic resource rather than a one-time theft. [2]

The waterfront evidence also shows how orders could be conveyed across physical distance. When Coonan was incarcerated during parts of the 1980s, the appellate account describes messages and demands transmitted through associates, including Edna Coonan, and proceeds shared among participants. Incarceration therefore did not necessarily dissolve the group’s command relationships, although the precise degree of Coonan’s active oversight varied across periods. [2]

9. Alliance with the Gambino Crime Family

The Westies’ late-1970s association with the Gambino family was a strategic shift. According to the trial-evidence summary in United States v. Coonan (1991), Gambino members provided financing for Westies loansharking, allowed use of the Gambino name to bolster intimidation, and received a ten-percent share of proceeds from various illegal activities. That figure is reported as the court’s description of the specific arrangement; it is not a reliable measure of every revenue stream or of the organization’s gross income. [2]

Published investigations often personalize the alliance through Gambino figures such as Paul Castellano and Roy DeMeo. Those connections help explain the Westies’ place in the broader New York underworld, but the court’s substantiated organizational terms are a sounder foundation than assuming Coonan was a formally initiated member of an Italian-American Mafia family. The Westies remained a separate Irish-American criminal enterprise working with a Mafia organization, not a Gambino family branch in the formal Mafia sense. [2] [6]

For Coonan, an outside alliance offered access to capital, reputation, and arbitration power beyond Hell’s Kitchen. It also exposed the group to pressures that could deepen internal disagreements. Retrospective accounts describe tensions among Westies members regarding the relationship; the precise deliberations of individual men require more than the existence of the alliance itself to establish. [6]

10. The Stein and Tassiello Killings

The 1991 appellate opinion described the killing of Charles “Ruby” Stein in May 1977 and the killing of Richard Tassiello in January 1978 as especially significant examples of Westies violence. The court summarized evidence associating Coonan with both killings and with the dismemberment of bodies. These descriptions derive from the later federal trial record; they should not be confused with a complete independent forensic reconstruction for every detail of the two crimes. [2]

Stein was described as a financier of loansharking who was owed substantial money by Coonan. The killing thus illustrates an overlap between debt, internal financing, and lethal coercion. Tassiello was described as a loansharking debtor who had fallen behind. The two cases reveal different positions inside the criminal lending economy—financier and borrower—yet both became targets within a system sustained by violence. [2]

Popular retellings often concentrate on gruesome particulars of the aftermath. The relevant historical questions are narrower and more substantial: who was proven to participate, what relationship existed to the enterprise, how witnesses obtained their knowledge, and what the crimes reveal about the organization’s methods. A factual account need not embellish the physical details to explain their significance.

11. The 1980s: Prison, Delegation, and Narcotics

Coonan spent part of the early 1980s in prison on earlier matters, yet the government’s case described a continuing chain of direction and collections. In the 1991 appellate history, Coonan directed or approved violence from custody, communicated through intermediaries, and participated in the allocation of proceeds after his release in late 1984. The distinction between his physical absence and continuing influence was central to explaining the enterprise’s persistence. [2]

During 1984–1986 the Westies became involved in interconnected cocaine distribution operations. The appellate account identifies Kevin Kelly and others in distribution, financing, and collection roles, with operations reaching the Upper East Side. The judicial evidence described the group’s cocaine business as overseen by Coonan. This is evidence of an organizationally supported narcotics enterprise, not grounds to assign every street-level transaction personally to Coonan. [2]

Changes in the area’s economy, labor markets, and illicit revenue streams made the 1980s enterprise different from the older image of an Irish neighborhood gambling crew. The Westies’ combination of union access, credit, violence, and cocaine trafficking created opportunities for expansion but also produced new witnesses, financial trails, and conflicts. These features made a federal enterprise prosecution more feasible. [1]

12. The Michael Holly Killing and Featherstone’s Exoneration

The murder of Michael Holly on 25 April 1985 was pivotal in the investigation of the Westies. In the 1991 court summary, members of the gang targeted Holly in retaliation for the earlier death of John Bokun, who had been killed in a confrontation with an off-duty police officer. The opinion states that Coonan directed members to kill Holly; William “Billie” Bokun shot him while disguised, and Kenneth Shannon drove the getaway car. These details are presented as the appellate court’s account of trial evidence. [2]

Francis Featherstone was arrested and convicted in a separate state proceeding, but he maintained his innocence. He and his wife Marcelle (“Sissy”) Featherstone later cooperated with investigators. That work revealed that Featherstone had not participated in the Holly killing, and his conviction was set aside. The National Registry of Exonerations independently summarizes the wrongful-conviction and exoneration history. [2] [5]

The episode is indispensable to the story for two reasons. First, it fractured the cooperation between Coonan and his former deputy. Second, it illustrates why witness identification, disguises, and underworld accusations require careful treatment. The wrongful conviction is a documented procedural outcome, whereas the claim that one rival deliberately framed another should be connected to specific testimony and judicial evidence rather than used as unqualified shorthand. [5] [6]

13. Investigation and the Federal Indictment of 1987

The defection of former participants and the cooperation of the Featherstones substantially strengthened federal investigators’ ability to document the Westies as a continuing enterprise. Rather than prove isolated violent incidents without context, prosecutors could present testimony about relationships, financing, threats, union demands, and the distribution of proceeds. Such testimony often came from implicated witnesses with reasons to cooperate, making corroboration and cross-examination important. [2] [6]

On 17 September 1987, federal prosecutors indicted Coonan and nine co-defendants on charges involving participation in or conspiracy to participate in a racketeering enterprise and related offenses. The case was heard in the Southern District of New York before Judge Whitman Knapp. The combination of RICO and associated violent-crime, financial, and narcotics allegations allowed prosecutors to address the group’s repeated conduct rather than treat each act as unrelated. [1] [2]

It is important to distinguish what an indictment alleges from what a jury ultimately finds. Contemporary press reports of a criminal charge or a purported crime count may use broad language that does not accurately communicate the verdict on each specific count. For Coonan, the later district-court order supplies a definitive summary of conviction and sentence. [3] [9]

14. The 1988 Conviction and 75-Year Sentence

In February 1988, a federal jury convicted James Coonan on ten counts and acquitted him on one, according to the Second Circuit’s 2025 opinion. The 2024 district-court order identifies the convictions as including racketeering, violent offenses in aid of racketeering, extortion, and tax fraud. Coonan was not merely indicted or portrayed as a criminal in press accounts; he was convicted after a federal trial. [1] [3]

Judge Whitman Knapp imposed an aggregate 75-year federal term on 11 May 1988, together with a $1 million fine, according to the district court’s later examination of the original judgment. Some derivative websites give a 60-year sentence, but that figure conflicts with the judgment summarized in the judicial record and must not be repeated as Coonan’s controlling sentence. [3]

A contemporary United Press International report of 24 February 1988 described the jury’s verdict and the evidence aired during the trial. Such reporting is useful for the atmosphere and timing of the proceedings, while the sentencing order and appellate rulings carry greater evidentiary weight for the exact disposition. Not everyone named in the enterprise was convicted of the same offenses; one defendant in the first group was acquitted, and Kevin Kelly was later tried separately. [9] [2]

15. The 1991 Opinion and Its Proper Scope

United States v. Coonan, 938 F.2d 1553 (2d Cir. 1991), is often cited as the canonical published account of the Westies. Its caption includes Coonan’s name, but the actual appellant was Kevin Kelly, who had been tried separately while the principal defendants were prosecuted earlier. The court affirmed Kelly’s convictions and summarized earlier testimony about the enterprise to explain his participation. The distinction matters: the 1991 opinion is not a direct appeal in which the court newly adjudicated every allegation against Coonan. [2]

The opinion is nevertheless important evidence for the organization’s existence and methods. It identifies the leadership roles of Coonan and Featherstone, describes the inner crew, reports the Gambino alliance, outlines loansharking and cocaine activity, and addresses violence and union extortion. Its legal reasoning on association-in-fact enterprise helped explain why a flexible gang could be prosecuted under RICO. [2]

Because this decision recounts trial evidence, an editor should distinguish an appellate summary of proved or credited facts from a source such as a death certificate or a separately adjudicated murder case. Keeping those levels of proof separate improves accuracy without minimizing the gravity of the crimes established in federal proceedings.

16. Imprisonment, Parole, and the 2025 Appeal

After his 1988 sentencing, Coonan remained subject to the older federal sentencing framework applicable to conduct predating 1 November 1987. The Second Circuit reported that he appeared before the U.S. Parole Commission in 2012, 2021, and 2023 and was denied parole on those occasions. It also recorded that the Bureau of Prisons had refused a separate 2020 request to seek a sentence reduction on his behalf. [1]

In August 2023 Coonan filed a motion under 18 U.S.C. § 3582(c)(1)(A), asking the district court for a reduction. His submissions cited considerations including advanced age, health, his long term in prison, institutional conduct, and the effects of pandemic-era confinement. The district court denied relief on statutory eligibility grounds, and Coonan appealed. These are his asserted reasons, not findings that the court granted relief based on them. [1] [3]

On 9 July 2025, in a separate case also styled United States v. Coonan, No. 24-2119, the Second Circuit affirmed the denial. It held that the 2018 First Step Act did not extend § 3582(c)(1) to sentences for offenses committed before 1 November 1987. That legal conclusion concerns the applicability of one sentence-reduction procedure, not a new verdict on Coonan’s 1980s racketeering crimes. The court acknowledged the older parole framework; its opinion does not state that parole is categorically impossible. [1] [10]

In its 2025 opinion, the court described the Bureau of Prisons’ projected mandatory release as 1 June 2030. That date reflects information reported to the court, not a promise or a real-time Bureau of Prisons status search. As of this edition, no independently verified subsequent release or new court order has been established; this account therefore does not infer that a future release date has already occurred or that the 2025 decision resolved every possible route for relief. [1]

17. Historical Interpretation and Legacy

Coonan’s career exemplifies the change from locally concentrated illegal activity to a more complex enterprise drawing on lending, labor-related extraction, narcotics, and relationships with a larger Mafia organization. The Westies were not unique because they were Irish-American; they were historically consequential because a relatively small group exercised disproportionate coercive influence and became the subject of a major federal case that documented how its activities fit together. [2]

The case also illustrates the risks of sensational criminal biography. A dramatic anecdote about the feud with Spillane may become repeated more often than the court-established enterprise facts. Conversely, popular fascination with spectacular murders can eclipse the less visible victims of extortion, predatory credit, and threats around employment. A balanced historical assessment considers both forms of harm and avoids granting the organization cultural glamour. [6]

Finally, the 2025 litigation shows that Coonan’s case is not confined to the organized-crime history of the 1980s. Long-running federal sentences may remain subject to legal procedures created under older statutory schemes, and eligibility for newer remedies can turn on offense dates rather than the passage of decades. Thus Coonan’s entry connects the history of the Westies with the continuing history of American federal sentencing law. [1]
