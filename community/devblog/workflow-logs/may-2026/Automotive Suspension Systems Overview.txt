WORKFLOW NOTE

Title
Automotive Suspension Systems Overview

Date
2026-05-21

Area
MGU automotive / mechanical systems reference content

Problem
The automotive suspension topic needed a clear classification of the major suspension system types. The information needed to distinguish between basic structural categories, common modern vehicle layouts, truck/heavy-duty systems, and advanced electronically controlled systems.

Intended Goal
Create a usable overview that explains the main types of automotive suspension systems in a way that can support future MGU lesson, encyclopedia, or “How Things Work” content.

Root Cause
Automotive suspension can be confusing because systems are often classified in several overlapping ways: by wheel relationship, by spring type, by axle layout, and by control technology. Without grouping these categories clearly, the topic becomes a long list of names without structure.

Steps Taken

1. Identified the three main suspension architecture categories: dependent, independent, and semi-independent suspension.
2. Listed common dependent systems, including solid/live axle, leaf spring, trailing arm solid axle, four-link/five-link solid axle, Panhard bar, and Watts linkage arrangements.
3. Listed common independent systems, including MacPherson strut, double wishbone, multi-link, trailing arm, semi-trailing arm, swing axle, and twin I-beam suspension.
4. Listed semi-independent systems, including torsion beam/twist beam and De Dion suspension.
5. Added a second classification layer by spring or support type: coil springs, leaf springs, torsion bars, air suspension, hydropneumatic suspension, magnetic/adaptive suspension, and active suspension.
6. Reorganized the information into practical teaching groups: common modern car systems, truck/heavy-duty systems, and advanced systems.

Key Change
The suspension topic was converted from a loose list of parts into a structured taxonomy based on how the wheels are connected, how the vehicle is supported, and how the suspension is controlled.

Outcome
The session produced a clear reference explanation of automotive suspension systems that can be reused for MGU automotive lessons, mechanical systems pages, encyclopedia entries, or future infographic planning.

Validation
The overview was checked for coverage across passenger cars, trucks, older designs, modern independent systems, and advanced suspension technologies. It also separated full suspension architectures from supporting axle-location components such as Panhard bars and Watts linkages.

Rules Going Forward
Keep automotive system explanations organized by function and architecture first, then list examples under each category. Avoid mixing spring types, axle layouts, and electronic control systems as if they are the same classification level. When creating lessons, begin with dependent, independent, and semi-independent suspension before moving into specific named designs.

Notes
This topic could support a future “How Automotive Suspension Works” page, a suspension comparison chart, or individual pages for MacPherson strut, double wishbone, multi-link, torsion beam, solid axle, air suspension, and adaptive suspension systems.
