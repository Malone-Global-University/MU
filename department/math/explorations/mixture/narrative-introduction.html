Narrative Introduction

Before writing mixture equations, learn to see what is actually being mixed, what is being conserved, and why concentration alone never tells the whole story.

Two Glasses on a Table

Imagine two glasses sitting beside one another. One contains \(10\) liters of a weak salt solution. The other contains \(5\) liters of a much stronger salt solution. You pour both into a larger container and stir until the liquid is uniform.

A simple question immediately appears:

How concentrated is the new mixture?

At first, it may seem reasonable to look only at the two percentages. If one solution is \(20\%\) salt and the other is \(50\%\) salt, perhaps the final concentration should simply be halfway between them:

\[ \frac{20\%+50\%}{2}=35\%. \]

But that conclusion ignores something important. There were not equal amounts of the two solutions. There were \(10\) liters of the weaker solution and only \(5\) liters of the stronger one.

The larger quantity has more influence on the final mixture.

This is the central difficulty of mixture problems: a concentration tells us a proportion, but a proportion by itself does not tell us how much substance is actually present.

A Percentage Is Not an Amount

Suppose a container holds \(10\) liters of a \(20\%\) salt solution. The statement \(20\%\) does not mean that the container contains \(20\) liters of salt. It means that \(20\%\) of the total \(10\) liters is salt.

The actual amount of salt is

\[ 0.20(10)=2. \]

So the mixture contains \(2\) liters of salt and \(8\) liters of everything else.

Now consider \(5\) liters of a \(50\%\) salt solution. Its salt content is

\[ 0.50(5)=2.5. \]

Even though the second solution is much more concentrated, its smaller total volume means that it contributes only slightly more salt than the first.

This distinction between concentration and component amount is the key to understanding mixture problems.

Every Mixture Has a Part and a Whole

Mixture problems are built around a familiar mathematical idea: a part compared with a whole.

If \(3\) liters of a \(12\)-liter mixture are alcohol, then the concentration of alcohol is

\[ \frac{3}{12}=\frac14=0.25=25\%. \]

The same relationship can be read in reverse. If a \(12\)-liter mixture is \(25\%\) alcohol, then the amount of alcohol is

\[ 0.25(12)=3. \]

So three quantities continually interact:

  • Total quantity: the entire mixture.
  • Concentration: the fraction or percentage belonging to the component being studied.
  • Component amount: the actual amount of that component.

Once these three quantities are distinguished clearly, many mixture problems become much easier to organize.

What Survives the Mixing?

When two mixtures are poured together, their labels disappear. There is no longer a separate \(20\%\) solution and \(50\%\) solution. There is only one new mixture.

Yet something important has not disappeared.

The actual amount of the substance being tracked is still present.

If the first solution contributed \(2\) liters of salt and the second contributed \(2.5\) liters, then after they are mixed there must still be

\[ 2+2.5=4.5 \]

liters of salt.

The container may change. The concentration may change. The total volume may change. But ordinary mixing does not magically create or destroy the component being measured.

That simple observation is the foundation of the algebra that follows.

Mixture Problems Are Conservation Problems

At their core, mixture problems are examples of a broader mathematical idea called conservation.

A conserved quantity is something whose total amount remains accounted for while a system changes.

When two salt solutions are mixed, the total amount of salt after mixing must equal the amount of salt contributed before mixing:

\[ \text{salt before} = \text{salt after}. \]

If two alloys are melted together, the amount of copper in the final alloy comes from the copper already present in the original alloys.

If two grades of coffee are blended, the total monetary value of the blend comes from the values contributed by the individual coffees.

The surface details change, but the mathematical structure remains recognizable:

\[ \boxed{\text{contributions from the parts}=\text{amount in the whole}}. \]

Recognizing what is being conserved is usually more important than memorizing a special mixture formula.

Returning to the Two Glasses

Now return to the original example: \(10\) liters of a \(20\%\) solution are mixed with \(5\) liters of a \(50\%\) solution.

The first solution contributes

\[ 0.20(10)=2 \]

liters of the measured substance.

The second contributes

\[ 0.50(5)=2.5. \]

Together they contain

\[ 2+2.5=4.5 \]

liters of the substance.

The total amount of mixture is

\[ 10+5=15 \]

liters.

So the final concentration is

\[ \frac{4.5}{15}=0.30. \]

Therefore, the new mixture is

\[ \boxed{30\%}. \]

Notice that the answer is not \(35\%\). The weaker solution had twice the volume of the stronger solution, so it exerted more influence on the result.

Why Simple Averaging Can Fail

A common mistake in mixture problems is to average two concentrations without considering how much of each mixture is present.

Suppose \(99\) liters of a \(10\%\) solution are mixed with only \(1\) liter of a \(90\%\) solution. Averaging the two percentages would give

\[ \frac{10\%+90\%}{2}=50\%. \]

But it would be unreasonable for a single liter of strong solution to transform \(99\) liters of weak solution into a mixture that is half concentrated.

The actual component amount is

\[ 0.10(99)+0.90(1) \] \[ =9.9+0.9 \] \[ =10.8. \]

The final mixture contains \(100\) liters, so its concentration is

\[ \frac{10.8}{100}=10.8\%. \]

That answer makes much more sense. Almost the entire final mixture came from the original \(10\%\) solution.

Percentages must therefore be weighted by the quantities to which they apply.

Mixtures Are Not Just Liquids

The word mixture often suggests two liquids being poured together, but the mathematics applies much more broadly.

A jeweler may combine two alloys containing different percentages of gold. A grocer may blend coffee beans costing different amounts per pound. A farmer may combine feeds containing different percentages of protein. A manufacturer may combine materials with different compositions. A laboratory may dilute a chemical solution with water.

In each case, some measurable property is carried by each quantity being combined.

For a chemical solution, that property may be concentration.

For an alloy, it may be the percentage of a particular metal.

For a commercial blend, it may be value per unit.

The language changes, but the reasoning is remarkably similar.

What Happens When Something Pure Is Added?

Suppose pure salt is added to a salt solution. Pure salt can be interpreted as \(100\%\) salt, so its concentration is

\[ 1.00. \]

If \(x\) units of pure salt are added, then the amount of salt contributed is simply

\[ 1.00x=x. \]

This may look like a special case, but mathematically it fits the same framework as every other mixture.

The concentration has simply reached its maximum possible value.

What Happens When Water Is Added?

Now suppose water is added to a salt solution. If the component being tracked is salt, pure water contains no salt at all.

Its salt concentration is therefore

\[ 0\%. \]

Adding water increases the total amount of mixture without increasing the amount of salt.

The salt amount stays fixed while the denominator in the concentration ratio becomes larger. As a result, the concentration decreases.

This process is called dilution.

Again, the mathematics follows naturally from understanding what is and is not being contributed to the mixture.

Removing a Mixture Is Different from Removing a Pure Component

Mixture problems become more subtle when some of the mixture is removed before something else is added.

Suppose a tank contains a well-mixed \(30\%\) salt solution. If \(2\) liters of the mixture are removed, those \(2\) liters are also \(30\%\) salt.

The removed amount therefore contains

\[ 0.30(2)=0.6 \]

liters of salt.

This is important because removing mixture removes both the measured component and the other material in the same proportion as the current mixture.

Replacement problems therefore require careful attention to what is leaving before calculating what is added.

From a Mixture Story to an Equation

Eventually, mixture problems introduce an unknown quantity.

Suppose \(10\) liters of a \(30\%\) solution are mixed with \(x\) liters of a \(60\%\) solution to produce a \(40\%\) solution.

The first mixture contributes

\[ 0.30(10) \]

units of the measured substance.

The second contributes

\[ 0.60x. \]

The final mixture contains

\[ 10+x \]

liters at a concentration of \(40\%\), so its component amount is

\[ 0.40(10+x). \]

Because the component is conserved, the situation can be modeled by

\[ 0.30(10)+0.60x=0.40(10+x). \]

The algebra is important, but the equation itself comes from understanding the story.

Each term represents an actual contribution to the final mixture.

Organizing the Information

Mixture problems are often easier to understand when the three central quantities are organized together:

Mixture Concentration Total Quantity Component Amount
First mixture \(C_1\) \(T_1\) \(C_1T_1\)
Second mixture \(C_2\) \(T_2\) \(C_2T_2\)
Final mixture \(C_f\) \(T_f\) \(C_fT_f\)

The table is not the mathematics itself. It is simply a way of keeping the quantities organized so that the conserved component can be tracked accurately.

The Answer Should Fit the Story

Mixture problems provide several natural ways to check whether an answer is reasonable.

If two positive quantities are mixed and no pure substance is added, the final concentration should normally lie between the two starting concentrations.

For example, mixing a \(20\%\) solution with a \(50\%\) solution should produce something between

\[ 20\% \]

and

\[ 50\%. \]

An answer of \(70\%\) would immediately suggest that something went wrong.

Likewise, if much more of the weaker mixture is present, the final concentration should usually be closer to the weaker concentration than to the stronger one.

Mathematical modeling is strongest when algebra and common sense agree.

A Broader Mathematical Idea

Mixture problems are valuable because they reveal a pattern that appears throughout mathematics: a whole can often be understood by tracking how its parts contribute to it.

The same style of reasoning appears in weighted averages, probability, center of mass, economics, statistics, chemistry, and many conservation models in science and engineering.

A mixture problem therefore teaches more than how to combine percentages. It develops the habit of asking:

  • What quantities are present?
  • What does each quantity contribute?
  • What changes?
  • What remains conserved?
  • How can those relationships be represented algebraically?

Those questions are useful far beyond this particular family of problems.

The Three Quantities to Remember

Throughout this exploration, return to these three ideas:

\[ \boxed{\text{Concentration}} \] \[ \boxed{\text{Total Quantity}} \] \[ \boxed{\text{Component Amount}} \]

They are connected by a simple relationship:

\[ \text{Component Amount} = \text{Concentration} \times \text{Total Quantity}. \]

Once this relationship is understood, the various forms of mixture problems become variations on a common theme rather than unrelated tricks.
