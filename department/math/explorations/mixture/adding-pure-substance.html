Adding a Pure Substance

Explore mixture problems in which a pure component is added to an existing mixture and learn why \(100\%\) concentration fits naturally into the Mixture Principle.

Overview

Some mixture problems involve adding a substance that consists entirely of the component being tracked. Examples include adding pure salt to a salt solution, pure alcohol to an alcohol mixture, or pure metal to an alloy.

These problems may appear different from ordinary concentration problems, but mathematically they follow the same Mixture Principle:

\[ A=CT. \]

The only new feature is that a pure substance has concentration

\[ 100\%=1. \]

That means the component amount contributed by \(x\) units of pure substance is simply

\[ 1(x)=x. \]

No new formula is required.

Pure Means One Hundred Percent

Suppose \(x\) kilograms of pure copper are added to an alloy.

Because the material is pure copper, its copper concentration is

\[ 100\%=1. \]

Using the Mixture Principle,

\[ A=CT, \]

the amount of copper contributed is

\[ A=1(x). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{A=x}. \]

Every unit added is also one full unit of the component being tracked.

The General Model

Suppose an existing mixture has concentration \(C\) and total quantity \(T\). Then its component amount is

\[ CT. \]

Now suppose \(x\) units of pure component are added.

The pure substance contributes

\[ x \]

units of the component.

The final mixture contains

\[ T+x \]

units in total.

If the desired final concentration is \(C_f\), conservation gives

\[ \boxed{CT+x=C_f(T+x)}. \]

This equation is the standard model for adding a pure substance to an existing mixture.

Example 1: Adding Pure Salt

A tank contains \(10\) kilograms of a mixture that is \(20\%\) salt. How much pure salt must be added to make the mixture \(40\%\) salt?

Let \(x\) represent the kilograms of pure salt added.

The original mixture contains

\[ 0.20(10)=2 \]

kilograms of salt.

The pure salt contributes

\[ x \]

kilograms of salt.

The final mixture has total mass

\[ 10+x. \]

At \(40\%\) concentration, the final amount of salt is

\[ 0.40(10+x). \]

Apply conservation:

\[ 2+x=0.40(10+x). \]

Expand:

\[ 2+x=4+0.40x. \]

Subtract \(0.40x\):

\[ 2+0.60x=4. \]

Subtract \(2\):

\[ 0.60x=2. \]

Therefore,

\[ x=\frac{2}{0.60}. \] \[ x=\frac{10}{3}. \]

So approximately

\[ \boxed{3.33\text{ kilograms}} \]

of pure salt must be added.

Checking the Result

The original mixture contained \(2\) kilograms of salt. Adding approximately \(3.33\) kilograms of pure salt gives

\[ 2+3.33\approx5.33 \]

kilograms of salt.

The total mixture becomes

\[ 10+3.33\approx13.33 \]

kilograms.

Then

\[ \frac{5.33}{13.33}\approx0.40. \]

So the final concentration is approximately

\[ \boxed{40\%}. \]

The result is consistent with the conditions of the problem.

Organizing the Problem in a Table

Material Concentration Total Quantity Component Amount
Original mixture \(0.20\) \(10\) \(2\)
Pure salt \(1.00\) \(x\) \(x\)
Final mixture \(0.40\) \(10+x\) \(0.40(10+x)\)

Reading across the component-amount column gives

\[ 2+x=0.40(10+x). \]

The table helps show that a pure substance is not a special exception. It is simply a mixture row with concentration \(1.00\).

Why the Concentration Increases

When pure component is added, two things happen at the same time:

  • The amount of the tracked component increases.
  • The total amount of mixture also increases.

However, the added material is entirely made of the tracked component. Because its concentration is higher than the concentration of the original mixture, the final concentration rises.

This leads to a useful qualitative rule:

\[ \boxed{\text{Adding a substance above the current concentration raises the concentration.}} \]

A pure substance is simply the most extreme case because its concentration is \(100\%\).

Example 2: Adding Pure Alcohol

A container holds \(15\) liters of a \(30\%\) alcohol solution. How many liters of pure alcohol must be added to produce a \(50\%\) solution?

Let \(x\) be the amount of pure alcohol added.

The original amount of alcohol is

\[ 0.30(15)=4.5. \]

Pure alcohol contributes

\[ x. \]

The final quantity is

\[ 15+x. \]

The final alcohol amount is therefore

\[ 0.50(15+x). \]

Conservation gives

\[ 4.5+x=0.50(15+x). \]

Expand:

\[ 4.5+x=7.5+0.50x. \]

Subtract \(0.50x\):

\[ 4.5+0.50x=7.5. \]

Subtract \(4.5\):

\[ 0.50x=3. \]

Therefore,

\[ x=6. \]

So

\[ \boxed{6\text{ liters of pure alcohol}} \]

must be added.

Solving the General Pure-Addition Equation

Begin with

\[ CT+x=C_f(T+x). \]

Expand the right side:

\[ CT+x=C_fT+C_fx. \]

Move the terms containing \(x\) to one side:

\[ x-C_fx=C_fT-CT. \]

Factor:

\[ x(1-C_f)=T(C_f-C). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ x= \frac{T(C_f-C)}{1-C_f} }. \]

This formula gives the amount of pure component required to raise an existing mixture from concentration \(C\) to concentration \(C_f\).

The ordinary conservation equation is usually easier to remember, but this derived form reveals how the required amount depends on the starting concentration, target concentration, and original quantity.

What the Formula Tells Us

Consider

\[ x= \frac{T(C_f-C)}{1-C_f}. \]

Several relationships become visible.

If the starting amount \(T\) doubles while the concentrations stay the same, then \(x\) also doubles.

If the target concentration \(C_f\) moves farther above the starting concentration \(C\), more pure component is required.

As the target concentration approaches \(100\%\),

\[ C_f\rightarrow1, \]

the denominator

\[ 1-C_f \]

approaches zero.

This means that obtaining a concentration arbitrarily close to \(100\%\) requires an increasingly large amount of pure substance.

Can an Impure Mixture Reach Exactly 100%?

Suppose a mixture initially contains some material other than the tracked component. Can adding a finite amount of pure component make the final concentration exactly \(100\%\)?

No.

The original non-component material remains in the mixture. Adding more pure component can make its proportion smaller and smaller, but it does not remove it.

The general equation helps show why. If

\[ C_f=1, \]

then

\[ x= \frac{T(1-C)}{1-1}, \]

which requires division by zero.

There is no finite value of \(x\) that makes an initially impure mixture exactly pure merely by adding more pure material.

Approaching Purity

Although exactly \(100\%\) cannot be reached by finite addition, concentrations can approach \(100\%\).

Suppose \(10\) liters of a \(20\%\) solution are strengthened with pure component.

To reach \(80\%\):

\[ x= \frac{10(0.80-0.20)}{1-0.80} \] \[ = \frac{6}{0.20} \] \[ =30. \]

To reach \(90\%\):

\[ x= \frac{10(0.90-0.20)}{1-0.90} \] \[ = \frac{7}{0.10} \] \[ =70. \]

To reach \(99\%\):

\[ x= \frac{10(0.99-0.20)}{1-0.99} \] \[ = \frac{7.9}{0.01} \] \[ =790. \]

As the target moves closer to \(100\%\), the required amount grows dramatically.

Example 3: Strengthening an Alloy

A \(24\)-kilogram alloy is \(25\%\) copper. How much pure copper should be added to make the resulting alloy \(40\%\) copper?

Let \(x\) be the kilograms of pure copper added.

The original amount of copper is

\[ 0.25(24)=6. \]

After adding pure copper, the amount of copper is

\[ 6+x. \]

The final mass is

\[ 24+x. \]

So

\[ 6+x=0.40(24+x). \]

Expand:

\[ 6+x=9.6+0.40x. \]

Subtract \(0.40x\):

\[ 6+0.60x=9.6. \]

Subtract \(6\):

\[ 0.60x=3.6. \]

Therefore,

\[ x=6. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{6\text{ kilograms of pure copper}} \]

must be added.

Reverse Problems: Finding the Final Concentration

Sometimes the amount of pure substance added is known and the final concentration must be determined.

Suppose \(12\) kilograms of a \(25\%\) mixture receive \(3\) kilograms of pure component.

The original component amount is

\[ 0.25(12)=3. \]

The pure substance adds another

\[ 3. \]

So the final component amount is

\[ 3+3=6. \]

The final total quantity is

\[ 12+3=15. \]

Therefore,

\[ C_f=\frac{6}{15}=0.40. \]

The resulting mixture is

\[ \boxed{40\%}. \]

Example 4: Finding the Original Concentration

A \(10\)-liter solution has an unknown concentration. After \(5\) liters of pure component are added, the resulting \(15\)-liter mixture is \(60\%\). Find the original concentration.

Let \(C\) represent the original concentration.

The original component amount is

\[ 10C. \]

The pure component contributes

\[ 5. \]

The final mixture contains

\[ 0.60(15)=9 \]

liters of the component.

Therefore,

\[ 10C+5=9. \]

Subtract \(5\):

\[ 10C=4. \]

Divide by \(10\):

\[ C=0.40. \]

So the original concentration was

\[ \boxed{40\%}. \]

Pure Substance as a Limit Case

In ordinary concentration problems, one source might have concentration \(C_2\). The conservation equation is

\[ C_1T_1+C_2T_2=C_f(T_1+T_2). \]

For a pure substance,

\[ C_2=1. \]

So the equation becomes

\[ C_1T_1+1(T_2)=C_f(T_1+T_2). \]

or simply

\[ C_1T_1+T_2=C_f(T_1+T_2). \]

This demonstrates that pure-substance problems are not a separate mathematical species. They are ordinary concentration problems in which one concentration happens to equal \(1\).

Pure Addition Cannot Lower the Concentration

Suppose the existing concentration is \(30\%\). Adding material that is \(100\%\) component cannot produce a final concentration of \(20\%\).

The added substance is more concentrated than the original mixture, so the final concentration must rise.

In general, if

\[ C_{\text{added}}>C_{\text{original}}, \]

then the final concentration moves upward.

If a problem asks for a lower target concentration, a different process such as dilution is required.

Method for Adding a Pure Substance

  1. Identify the component being tracked. For example, salt, alcohol, copper, or another component.
  2. Find the original component amount. Use \[ A=CT. \]
  3. Represent the pure addition. If \(x\) units are added, the component amount added is also \[ x. \]
  4. Determine the new total quantity. If \(x\) units are added to \(T\), the final total is \[ T+x. \]
  5. Write the final component amount. Use \[ C_f(T+x). \]
  6. Apply conservation. Write \[ CT+x=C_f(T+x). \]
  7. Solve and check. Make sure the answer is positive and produces a final concentration between the starting concentration and \(100\%\).

Common Errors

  • Treating pure substance as having concentration \(100\). Use \(1.00\), not \(100\), in the equation.
  • Forgetting that the pure substance increases the total quantity. The final quantity is \(T+x\), not \(T\).
  • Adding the concentration instead of the component amount. The pure contribution is \(x\), not \(1+x\) or \(100+x\).
  • Assuming the final concentration can reach \(100\%\) with a finite addition. If the original mixture contains another component, that material remains present.
  • Accepting a target below the starting concentration. Adding pure component raises concentration; it cannot dilute the mixture.
  • Confusing component amount with total mixture amount. Keep the two quantities separate throughout the problem.

Practice Problems

  1. A \(12\)-liter solution is \(25\%\) salt. How many liters of pure salt equivalent must be added to make the mixture \(40\%\) salt?
  2. A \(20\)-kilogram alloy is \(30\%\) copper. How many kilograms of pure copper must be added to produce a \(50\%\) copper alloy?
  3. A \(10\)-liter mixture is \(20\%\) alcohol. If \(5\) liters of pure alcohol are added, what is the final concentration?
  4. A \(16\)-kilogram alloy is \(25\%\) gold. Pure gold is added until the alloy is \(40\%\) gold. How much pure gold is added?
  5. A \(15\)-liter solution has an unknown concentration. After \(5\) liters of pure component are added, the final \(20\)-liter mixture is \(50\%\). Find the original concentration.
  6. How much pure component must be added to \(8\) liters of a \(35\%\) mixture to obtain a \(65\%\) mixture?
  7. Can pure component be added to a \(60\%\) mixture to produce a \(45\%\) mixture? Explain.
  8. A \(30\)-kilogram mixture is \(40\%\) component. How much pure component must be added to make the final mixture \(70\%\)?

Practice Answers

  1. \[ 0.25(12)+x=0.40(12+x) \] \[ 3+x=4.8+0.40x \] \[ 0.60x=1.8 \] \[ \boxed{x=3}. \]
  2. \[ 0.30(20)+x=0.50(20+x) \] \[ 6+x=10+0.50x \] \[ 0.50x=4 \] \[ \boxed{x=8\text{ kg}}. \]
  3. Original component: \[ 0.20(10)=2. \] After adding \(5\): \[ 2+5=7. \] Final quantity: \[ 10+5=15. \] Thus, \[ C_f=\frac{7}{15}\approx0.4667. \] So \[ \boxed{46.67\%}. \]
  4. \[ 0.25(16)+x=0.40(16+x) \] \[ 4+x=6.4+0.40x \] \[ 0.60x=2.4 \] \[ \boxed{x=4\text{ kg}}. \]
  5. Let \(C\) be the original concentration: \[ 15C+5=0.50(20). \] \[ 15C+5=10. \] \[ 15C=5. \] \[ C=\frac13. \] Therefore, \[ \boxed{33\tfrac13\%}. \]
  6. \[ 0.35(8)+x=0.65(8+x) \] \[ 2.8+x=5.2+0.65x \] \[ 0.35x=2.4 \] \[ x=\frac{48}{7}\approx6.86. \] Therefore, \[ \boxed{6.86\text{ units approximately}}. \]
  7. \[ \boxed{\text{No}}. \] Adding a \(100\%\) component to a \(60\%\) mixture can only increase the concentration above \(60\%\).
  8. \[ 0.40(30)+x=0.70(30+x) \] \[ 12+x=21+0.70x \] \[ 0.30x=9 \] \[ \boxed{x=30\text{ kg}}. \]

Summary

A pure substance has concentration

\[ \boxed{100\%=1}. \]

Therefore, if \(x\) units of pure component are added, the amount of component contributed is

\[ \boxed{x}. \]

For an original mixture with concentration \(C\) and quantity \(T\), adding \(x\) units of pure component to reach final concentration \(C_f\) gives

\[ \boxed{CT+x=C_f(T+x)}. \]

Solving this equation for \(x\) gives

\[ \boxed{ x= \frac{T(C_f-C)}{1-C_f} }. \]

The deeper principle remains conservation. Pure-substance problems are simply ordinary mixture problems in which one source has concentration \(1\).
