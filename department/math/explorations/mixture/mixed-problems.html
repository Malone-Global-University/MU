Mixed Mixture Problems

Bring together the major mixture models and practice deciding what is conserved, what changes, and which algebraic structure best represents each problem.

Overview

The earlier lessons separated mixture problems into recognizable categories: concentration, pure additions, dilution, replacement, price and value, alloys and composition, and unknown concentrations.

Real problems do not always announce which category they belong to.

The purpose of mixed mixture problems is therefore not merely to perform algebra. The more important skill is deciding what mathematical model describes the situation.

Before writing an equation, ask:

  • What quantity is being tracked?
  • What is being added?
  • Is anything being removed?
  • Does the total quantity change?
  • What is conserved?
  • Which quantity is unknown?

Once these questions are answered, the algebra usually becomes much clearer.

The Common Structure Behind Mixture Problems

Most mixture problems can be understood through the general idea

\[ \boxed{ \text{final amount} = \text{initial amount} + \text{amount added} - \text{amount removed} }. \]

For a component described by concentration,

\[ A=CT. \]

So a very general mixture equation may look like

\[ \boxed{ C_iT_i + \sum C_{\text{added}}T_{\text{added}} - \sum C_{\text{removed}}T_{\text{removed}} = C_fT_f }. \]

Not every problem needs every term. The equation should reflect only what actually happens in the story.

Model Selection Comes First

A mixture problem may resemble another type while requiring a different equation.

Compare the following situations.

Adding water:

\[ CT=C_f(T+x). \]

Removing mixture and replacing it with water:

\[ CT-Cx=C_fT. \]

Adding pure component:

\[ CT+x=C_f(T+x). \]

Combining two ordinary mixtures:

\[ C_1T_1+C_2T_2=C_f(T_1+T_2). \]

The equations are similar, but they represent different physical processes.

Identify What Is Being Conserved

The most important first question is often:

What quantity should remain accounted for?

For a salt solution, the conserved quantity may be salt.

For an alloy, it may be copper.

For a coffee blend, it may be total value.

For a dilution problem, the amount of solute may remain unchanged while the total volume increases.

The labels change, but the structure remains:

\[ \boxed{\text{contributions from the parts}=\text{amount accounted for in the whole}}. \]

Example 1: Which Model Applies?

A tank contains \(20\) liters of a \(30\%\) solution. How many liters of a \(60\%\) solution must be added to produce a \(40\%\) solution?

Nothing is removed, and the added material has concentration \(60\%\).

Let \(x\) be the amount added.

The model is

\[ 0.30(20)+0.60x=0.40(20+x). \]

Simplify:

\[ 6+0.60x=8+0.40x. \] \[ 0.20x=2. \] \[ x=10. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{10\text{ liters}} \]

of the \(60\%\) solution must be added.

Example 2: Pure Addition or Ordinary Mixing?

A \(15\)-kilogram alloy is \(40\%\) copper. How much pure copper must be added to make the alloy \(55\%\) copper?

Because pure copper has concentration

\[ 1, \]

let \(x\) represent the mass of pure copper added.

The model is

\[ 0.40(15)+x=0.55(15+x). \]

Simplify:

\[ 6+x=8.25+0.55x. \] \[ 0.45x=2.25. \] \[ x=5. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{5\text{ kilograms}} \]

of pure copper must be added.

Example 3: Dilution or Replacement?

A \(24\)-liter tank contains a \(50\%\) solution. Six liters are removed and replaced with water. Find the final concentration.

This is not simple dilution because some of the original component leaves the tank.

The initial component amount is

\[ 0.50(24)=12. \]

The \(6\) liters removed contain

\[ 0.50(6)=3 \]

liters of component.

So

\[ 12-3=9 \]

liters remain.

Water contributes none of the component, and the final volume returns to \(24\) liters.

Therefore,

\[ C_f=\frac{9}{24}. \] \[ C_f=0.375. \]

The final concentration is

\[ \boxed{37.5\%}. \]

Example 4: Unknown Concentration

Eight liters of an unknown solution are mixed with \(12\) liters of a \(50\%\) solution. The final mixture is \(38\%\). Find the unknown concentration.

Let \(x\) be the unknown concentration.

Then

\[ 8x+0.50(12)=0.38(20). \]

Simplify:

\[ 8x+6=7.6. \] \[ 8x=1.6. \] \[ x=0.20. \]

The unknown solution was

\[ \boxed{20\%}. \]

Example 5: Price Mixture

A merchant wants \(30\) pounds of a coffee blend worth \(\$9\) per pound using coffee worth \(\$6\) and \(\$11\) per pound. How much of each should be used?

Let \(x\) be the pounds of the \(\$6\) coffee.

Then

\[ 30-x \]

pounds of the \(\$11\) coffee are needed.

Conservation of value gives

\[ 6x+11(30-x)=9(30). \]

Expand:

\[ 6x+330-11x=270. \] \[ 330-5x=270. \] \[ -5x=-60. \] \[ x=12. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{12\text{ lb at }\$6\text{ and }18\text{ lb at }\$11}. \]

The Same Algebra Can Appear in Different Contexts

Consider these two equations:

\[ 0.20x+0.50(30-x)=0.35(30) \]

and

\[ 6x+10(30-x)=8(30). \]

The first might describe concentrations. The second might describe prices.

Structurally, both have the form

\[ \boxed{ \text{rate}_1(\text{quantity}_1) + \text{rate}_2(\text{quantity}_2) = \text{target rate}(\text{total quantity}) }. \]

This is one of the major insights of mixture problems: the same algebraic model can describe very different situations.

Example 6: Combining Addition and Replacement

A \(20\)-liter tank contains a \(40\%\) solution. Five liters are removed and replaced with a \(60\%\) solution. Find the final concentration.

The initial component amount is

\[ 0.40(20)=8. \]

The removed mixture contains

\[ 0.40(5)=2 \]

liters of component.

So

\[ 8-2=6 \]

liters remain.

The replacement contributes

\[ 0.60(5)=3. \]

The final component amount is

\[ 6+3=9. \]

The total volume remains \(20\) liters.

Therefore,

\[ C_f=\frac{9}{20}=0.45. \]

The final concentration is

\[ \boxed{45\%}. \]

Example 7: Several Sources

Five liters of a \(20\%\) solution, \(10\) liters of a \(30\%\) solution, and \(5\) liters of an unknown solution are combined to produce \(20\) liters of a \(40\%\) solution. Find the unknown concentration.

Let \(x\) represent the unknown concentration.

The equation is

\[ 0.20(5)+0.30(10)+5x=0.40(20). \]

Simplify:

\[ 1+3+5x=8. \] \[ 5x=4. \] \[ x=0.80. \]

Therefore, the unknown solution is

\[ \boxed{80\%}. \]

Check Extreme Results Carefully

The \(80\%\) answer in the previous example may initially seem high, but it is reasonable.

The first two sources contribute only

\[ 1+3=4 \]

units of component.

The final mixture requires

\[ 0.40(20)=8. \]

So the unknown \(5\)-liter source must supply

\[ 8-4=4 \]

units.

Its concentration must therefore be

\[ \frac{4}{5}=0.80. \]

A surprising answer is not automatically a wrong answer. It should be checked against the structure of the problem.

Example 8: An Impossible Mixture

A merchant wants to combine products worth \(\$5\) and \(\$8\) per pound to create a blend worth \(\$10\) per pound.

This is impossible using positive quantities of only those two products.

The final value of a mixture must lie between the source values:

\[ 5\leq P_f\leq8. \]

A target of

\[ 10 \]

lies outside that range.

The correct conclusion is therefore

\[ \boxed{\text{No physically meaningful mixture exists}}. \]

Algebra Can Reveal an Impossible Story

Suppose we ignore the warning and try to solve a \(20\)-pound version of the previous problem.

Let \(x\) pounds cost \(\$5\), leaving

\[ 20-x \]

pounds worth \(\$8\).

Then

\[ 5x+8(20-x)=10(20). \]

Expand:

\[ 5x+160-8x=200. \] \[ -3x=40. \] \[ x=-\frac{40}{3}. \]

The negative quantity is algebraically valid but physically meaningless.

It tells us that the requested blend cannot be made from the available products.

Example 9: Repeated Replacement

A \(30\)-liter tank contains a \(60\%\) solution. Six liters are removed and replaced with water. The process is repeated twice more, with the mixture stirred thoroughly after each replacement. Find the final concentration.

Each cycle leaves the fraction

\[ \frac{30-6}{30} = \frac{24}{30} = 0.8 \]

of the existing component.

After three cycles,

\[ C_3=0.60(0.8)^3. \]

So

\[ C_3=0.60(0.512). \] \[ C_3=0.3072. \]

Therefore, the final concentration is

\[ \boxed{30.72\%}. \]

Example 10: Related Unknown Concentrations

Ten liters of one solution are mixed with \(20\) liters of another solution that is \(15\) percentage points stronger. The final mixture is \(40\%\). Find both original concentrations.

Let the weaker concentration be

\[ x. \]

Then the stronger concentration is

\[ x+0.15. \]

The mixture equation is

\[ 10x+20(x+0.15)=0.40(30). \]

Expand:

\[ 10x+20x+3=12. \] \[ 30x=9. \] \[ x=0.30. \]

So the weaker solution is

\[ 30\%, \]

and the stronger solution is

\[ 45\%. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{30\%\text{ and }45\%}. \]

Example 11: Composition with a Pure Addition

A \(40\)-kilogram alloy is \(25\%\) nickel. Pure nickel is added until the final alloy is \(40\%\) nickel. How much pure nickel is needed?

The original nickel amount is

\[ 0.25(40)=10. \]

Let \(x\) be the amount of pure nickel added.

Then

\[ 10+x=0.40(40+x). \]

Expand:

\[ 10+x=16+0.40x. \] \[ 0.60x=6. \] \[ x=10. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{10\text{ kilograms of pure nickel}} \]

must be added.

Example 12: Dilution with an Unknown Original Concentration

A \(12\)-liter solution of unknown concentration is diluted by adding \(8\) liters of water. The final concentration is \(30\%\). Find the original concentration.

Let \(x\) be the original concentration.

The original component amount is

\[ 12x. \]

Water contributes no component.

The final mixture contains

\[ 20 \]

liters and must contain

\[ 0.30(20)=6 \]

liters of component.

Therefore,

\[ 12x=6. \] \[ x=0.50. \]

The original concentration was

\[ \boxed{50\%}. \]

A Decision Framework for Mixture Problems

When reading a new mixture problem, the following sequence can help identify the correct model.

  1. What quantity is being tracked? Salt, alcohol, copper, value, purity, or another measurable property?
  2. What is the starting amount? Calculate the component amount using \[ A=CT. \]
  3. Is anything added? If so, determine its concentration or rate per unit.
  4. Is anything removed? If mixture is removed, use the concentration of the mixture at the time of removal.
  5. Does the total quantity change? Addition usually increases the total; equal removal and replacement usually leave it unchanged.
  6. What must be true at the end? Translate the final concentration, value, or composition into a final amount.
  7. What is unknown? Quantity, concentration, value, or composition?

Common Mixture Models

Situation Typical Model
Two mixtures combined \(C_1T_1+C_2T_2=C_f(T_1+T_2)\)
Pure component added \(CT+x=C_f(T+x)\)
Zero-concentration diluent added \(CT=C_f(T+x)\)
Remove and replace \(CT-Cx+Rx=C_fT\)
Price or value blend \(P_1Q_1+P_2Q_2=P_f(Q_1+Q_2)\)
Unknown concentration \(xT_1+C_2T_2=C_fT_f\)
Repeated replacement with water \(C_n=C_0\left(\frac{T-x}{T}\right)^n\)

One Equation or Several Stages?

Some problems are easiest to model with one equation.

For example:

\[ 0.30(20)+0.60x=0.40(20+x). \]

Other problems are clearer when broken into stages:

  1. Find the initial component amount.
  2. Subtract what is removed.
  3. Add what enters.
  4. Determine the final concentration.

Both approaches are valid.

Use the representation that makes the conservation of the tracked quantity easiest to see.

Mixture Problems Are Modeling Problems

The equation is not the starting point.

The situation comes first.

A correct equation should emerge from answering questions about the quantities in the story.

This makes mixture problems valuable algebra practice because they require two distinct skills:

  • Modeling: deciding what equation represents the situation.
  • Solving: manipulating that equation correctly.

A student may be excellent at solving equations but still struggle with mixture problems if the modeling step is skipped.

Weighted Averages Reappear Throughout the Exploration

When ordinary mixtures are simply combined, the final concentration is

\[ C_f= \frac{C_1T_1+C_2T_2}{T_1+T_2}. \]

For prices,

\[ P_f= \frac{P_1Q_1+P_2Q_2}{Q_1+Q_2}. \]

These are both weighted averages.

The larger a source quantity becomes, the more strongly its rate, concentration, or value influences the final result.

This connection places mixture problems within a much broader family of mathematical ideas involving averages, expected values, centers of mass, statistics, and probability.

Mixture Problems and Functions

Many mixture relationships can be written as functions.

If \(A\) units of component are held fixed while \(x\) units of water are added, the concentration is

\[ C(x)=\frac{A}{T+x}. \]

If pure component is added,

\[ C(x)=\frac{A+x}{T+x}. \]

If an amount \(x\) is removed and replaced with a material of concentration \(R\),

\[ C(x)=\frac{CT-Cx+Rx}{T}. \]

The mixture framework therefore connects naturally to linear, rational, and repeated-process functions.

From Exact Targets to Inequalities

The problems in this exploration usually ask for an exact target:

\[ C_f=0.40. \]

But real applications often involve limits instead.

For example:

How much of a stronger solution may be added while keeping the final concentration below \(40\%\)?

The model might become

\[ 0.30(20)+0.60x<0.40(20+x). \]

The same mixture reasoning now leads directly into inequalities.

This is one reason mixture problems form a useful bridge between functions, equations, and inequality modeling.

General Strategy

  1. Read the entire problem before calculating. Determine the physical or economic process first.
  2. Choose one quantity to track. Do not switch between salt, water, copper, or other components unless the problem requires it.
  3. Convert percentages to decimals. For example, \[ 45\%=0.45. \]
  4. Use the Mixture Principle. Write component amounts as \[ A=CT. \]
  5. Account for additions and removals. Use positive terms for contributions and negative terms for quantities removed.
  6. Determine the final total quantity. Do not assume it stays constant unless the process actually preserves it.
  7. Write the conservation equation. Make sure each term represents an actual quantity in the story.
  8. Solve carefully. Then interpret the algebraic result in context.
  9. Check whether the answer is possible. Reject negative quantities, impossible percentages, or target values outside the feasible range.

Common Errors

  • Choosing a formula before understanding the story. Different mixture processes may look similar but require different models.
  • Adding concentrations instead of component amounts. Track \(CT\), not concentration alone.
  • Forgetting what is removed. Removing mixture removes some of every component present in that mixture.
  • Forgetting that an addition changes the total. If \(x\) units are added, the final amount is usually \(T+x\).
  • Changing the total during equal replacement. Removing \(x\) and adding \(x\) leaves the total unchanged.
  • Using the original concentration during repeated replacement. The concentration changes after each cycle.
  • Accepting a mathematically valid but physically impossible answer. Negative quantities and concentrations outside \(0\%\) to \(100\%\) require interpretation.
  • Ignoring units. Volumes, masses, and prices must use compatible units.

Practice Problems

  1. A \(20\)-liter solution is \(25\%\) component. How many liters of a \(55\%\) solution must be added to obtain a \(35\%\) mixture?
  2. A \(15\)-liter solution is \(40\%\) component. How much water must be added to reduce the concentration to \(30\%\)?
  3. A \(20\)-kilogram alloy is \(30\%\) copper. How much pure copper must be added to make the alloy \(50\%\) copper?
  4. A \(25\)-liter tank contains a \(40\%\) solution. Five liters are removed and replaced with a \(20\%\) solution. Find the final concentration.
  5. A merchant wants \(30\) pounds of a blend worth \(\$8\) per pound using products worth \(\$5\) and \(\$10\) per pound. How many pounds of each are needed?
  6. Ten liters of an unknown solution are mixed with \(20\) liters of a \(50\%\) solution. The final mixture is \(40\%\). Find the unknown concentration.
  7. A \(24\)-liter tank contains a \(60\%\) solution. Six liters are removed and replaced with water twice, stirring after each replacement. Find the final concentration.
  8. A \(30\)-kilogram alloy is \(20\%\) nickel. Pure nickel is added until the final alloy is \(35\%\) nickel. How much pure nickel is required?
  9. Eight liters of one solution are mixed with \(12\) liters of another solution that is \(25\) percentage points stronger. The final concentration is \(45\%\). Find both starting concentrations.
  10. Can a \(30\%\) solution and a \(50\%\) solution be mixed in positive quantities to produce a \(60\%\) solution? Explain.

Practice Answers

  1. Let \(x\) be the amount of \(55\%\) solution: \[ 0.25(20)+0.55x=0.35(20+x). \] \[ 5+0.55x=7+0.35x. \] \[ 0.20x=2. \] \[ \boxed{x=10\text{ liters}}. \]
  2. \[ 0.40(15)=0.30(15+x). \] \[ 6=4.5+0.30x. \] \[ 1.5=0.30x. \] \[ \boxed{x=5\text{ liters}}. \]
  3. \[ 0.30(20)+x=0.50(20+x). \] \[ 6+x=10+0.50x. \] \[ 0.50x=4. \] \[ \boxed{x=8\text{ kg}}. \]
  4. Initial component: \[ 0.40(25)=10. \] Removed: \[ 0.40(5)=2. \] Replacement added: \[ 0.20(5)=1. \] Final component: \[ 10-2+1=9. \] Thus, \[ C_f=\frac{9}{25}=0.36. \] \[ \boxed{36\%}. \]
  5. Let \(x\) be the amount of the \(\$5\) product: \[ 5x+10(30-x)=8(30). \] \[ 5x+300-10x=240. \] \[ -5x=-60. \] \[ x=12. \] Therefore, \[ \boxed{12\text{ lb at }\$5\text{ and }18\text{ lb at }\$10}. \]
  6. \[ 10x+0.50(20)=0.40(30). \] \[ 10x+10=12. \] \[ 10x=2. \] \[ x=0.20. \] \[ \boxed{20\%}. \]
  7. Each replacement leaves \[ \frac{24-6}{24} = \frac34 \] of the existing component. After two cycles, \[ C_2=0.60\left(\frac34\right)^2. \] \[ C_2=0.60\left(\frac{9}{16}\right). \] \[ C_2=0.3375. \] \[ \boxed{33.75\%}. \]
  8. \[ 0.20(30)+x=0.35(30+x). \] \[ 6+x=10.5+0.35x. \] \[ 0.65x=4.5. \] \[ x=\frac{90}{13}\approx6.92. \] \[ \boxed{6.92\text{ kg approximately}}. \]
  9. Let the weaker concentration be \(x\). Then the stronger concentration is \[ x+0.25. \] So \[ 8x+12(x+0.25)=0.45(20). \] \[ 8x+12x+3=9. \] \[ 20x=6. \] \[ x=0.30. \] Therefore, \[ \boxed{30\%\text{ and }55\%}. \]
  10. \[ \boxed{\text{No}}. \] A positive mixture of \(30\%\) and \(50\%\) solutions must have a final concentration between \(30\%\) and \(50\%\).

Final Synthesis

Mixture problems may involve liquids, metals, prices, purity, dilution, replacement, or several sources, but the same conceptual structure appears repeatedly.

First identify a measurable quantity:

\[ \boxed{\text{component, value, or other property}}. \]

Then determine how much each source contributes:

\[ \boxed{\text{rate or proportion}\times\text{quantity}}. \]

Finally, account for everything that enters and leaves:

\[ \boxed{ \text{initial} + \text{added} - \text{removed} = \text{final} }. \]

The algebra changes from problem to problem, but the deeper reasoning remains conservation.

Exploration Complete

You have now examined the major structures of mixture problems:

  • basic concentration problems,
  • adding pure substances,
  • dilution,
  • replacement,
  • price and value mixtures,
  • alloys and composition,
  • unknown concentrations, and
  • mixed applications.

The central relationship throughout the exploration has been

\[ \boxed{A=CT}, \]

supported by the broader conservation idea

\[ \boxed{ \text{what enters} - \text{what leaves} = \text{what remains or results} }. \]

The goal is not to memorize a separate trick for every mixture problem. It is to recognize the quantities involved, determine what must be conserved, and build an equation that tells the same story mathematically.
