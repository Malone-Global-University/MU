Understanding Proportions

Discover what it really means for two ratios to be equivalent and why the cross-products of a proportion must be equal.

The Same Relationship in Different Forms

A proportion is more than an equation containing fractions. It expresses the idea that two comparisons describe the same relationship.

Consider the ratios

\[ \frac{2}{3} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{6}{9}. \]

The numbers are different, but the second ratio is created by multiplying both parts of the first ratio by 3:

\[ \frac{2\times3}{3\times3} = \frac{6}{9}. \]

Because the relationship has not changed, the ratios are equivalent:

\[ \frac{2}{3}=\frac{6}{9}. \]

That equation is a proportion.

Equivalent Ratios

Two ratios are equivalent when they represent the same relative comparison.

For example,

\[ \frac{3}{5} = \frac{6}{10} = \frac{9}{15} = \frac{12}{20}. \]

Each ratio has different numbers, but every ratio expresses the same relationship: 3 units of one quantity for every 5 units of another.

Equivalent ratios work much like equivalent fractions. Multiplying or dividing both parts of a ratio by the same nonzero number changes its appearance without changing its value.

\[ \frac{3}{5} = \frac{3\times4}{5\times4} = \frac{12}{20}. \]

Scale Factors Preserve the Relationship

One useful way to recognize equivalent ratios is to look for a common scale factor.

Suppose we compare

\[ \frac{4}{7} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{20}{35}. \]

The numerator 4 became 20 by multiplying by 5:

\[ 4\times5=20. \]

The denominator 7 changed by the same factor:

\[ 7\times5=35. \]

Because both quantities were scaled by the same amount, the original relationship was preserved:

\[ \frac{4}{7}=\frac{20}{35}. \]

This idea of preserving a relationship while quantities scale upward or downward is at the heart of proportional reasoning.

What Makes an Equation a Proportion?

A proportion has the general form

\[ \frac{a}{b}=\frac{c}{d}, \]

where \(b\neq0\) and \(d\neq0\).

The denominators cannot be zero because division by zero is undefined.

The equation states that the ratio \(a:b\) has the same value as the ratio \(c:d\).

For example,

\[ \frac{5}{8}=\frac{15}{24} \]

is a proportion because both ratios simplify to the same value.

But

\[ \frac{5}{8}\neq\frac{15}{20} \]

is not a proportion because the two ratios are not equivalent.

Checking a Proportion by Simplifying

One way to determine whether two ratios form a proportion is to simplify them.

Consider

\[ \frac{8}{12} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{14}{21}. \]

Simplify the first ratio:

\[ \frac{8}{12}=\frac{2}{3}. \]

Simplify the second:

\[ \frac{14}{21}=\frac{2}{3}. \]

Since both ratios simplify to the same value,

\[ \frac{8}{12}=\frac{14}{21}. \]

They form a proportion.

Cross-Products

There is another powerful way to test whether two ratios are equivalent. Consider a proportion written as

\[ \frac{a}{b}=\frac{c}{d}. \]

The products formed diagonally across the equality are called cross-products.

\[ a\cdot d \qquad\text{and}\qquad b\cdot c. \]

If the two ratios form a proportion, these products are equal:

\[ ad=bc. \]

This relationship is commonly called the fundamental property of proportions.

Why Do Cross-Products Work?

Cross multiplication is not a mathematical trick. It follows directly from ordinary multiplication.

Begin with

\[ \frac{a}{b}=\frac{c}{d}. \]

Multiply both sides by \(bd\):

\[ bd\left(\frac{a}{b}\right) = bd\left(\frac{c}{d}\right). \]

On the left, the factor \(b\) cancels:

\[ ad. \]

On the right, the factor \(d\) cancels:

\[ bc. \]

Therefore,

\[ ad=bc. \]

The cross-product property is simply a convenient consequence of the original equality of the two ratios.

Checking with Cross-Products

Consider

\[ \frac{4}{7}=\frac{12}{21}. \]

Form the cross-products:

\[ 4(21)=84 \]

and

\[ 7(12)=84. \]

Since the cross-products are equal,

\[ 4(21)=7(12), \]

the ratios form a proportion.

Now compare

\[ \frac{4}{7} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{10}{20}. \]

Their cross-products are

\[ 4(20)=80 \]

and

\[ 7(10)=70. \]

Since

\[ 80\neq70, \]

the ratios are not equivalent and therefore do not form a proportion.

Three Ways to Recognize Equivalent Ratios

At this stage, you have several ways to decide whether two ratios form a proportion.

  1. Look for a common scale factor. If both parts of one ratio were multiplied or divided by the same nonzero number, the ratios are equivalent.
  2. Simplify both ratios. If they reduce to the same ratio, they are equivalent.
  3. Compare the cross-products. If the cross-products are equal, the ratios form a proportion.

These are not three unrelated techniques. They are three different ways of examining the same underlying fact: the ratios represent the same relationship.

Keep Corresponding Quantities in the Same Order

A proportion only makes sense when corresponding quantities are compared consistently.

Suppose 3 notebooks cost $12 and 5 notebooks cost $20. One correct proportion is

\[ \frac{3\text{ notebooks}}{\$12} = \frac{5\text{ notebooks}}{\$20}. \]

Both ratios use \(\text{notebooks}/\text{dollars}\).

We could also reverse both ratios:

\[ \frac{\$12}{3\text{ notebooks}} = \frac{\$20}{5\text{ notebooks}}. \]

Now both ratios use \(\text{dollars}/\text{notebooks}\).

What we should not do is reverse only one side:

\[ \frac{3\text{ notebooks}}{\$12} \neq \frac{\$20}{5\text{ notebooks}}. \]

Writing units carefully is one of the best ways to keep a proportion organized correctly.

Proportions with Units

Proportions often compare measurements rather than plain numbers.

Suppose a vehicle travels 90 miles in 3 gallons of fuel and 150 miles in 5 gallons.

We can compare distance with fuel:

\[ \frac{90\text{ miles}}{3\text{ gallons}} = \frac{150\text{ miles}}{5\text{ gallons}}. \]

Both sides have the same unit rate:

\[ 30\text{ miles per gallon}. \]

The equality of the ratios tells us that the same distance-to-fuel relationship is being maintained.

Proportional or Not?

It is important to distinguish between quantities that merely change together and quantities that maintain a constant ratio.

Suppose 2 tickets cost $10 and 6 tickets cost $30.

\[ \frac{\$10}{2\text{ tickets}} = \$5\text{ per ticket} \]

and

\[ \frac{\$30}{6\text{ tickets}} = \$5\text{ per ticket}. \]

The unit rate remains the same, so the relationship is proportional.

Now suppose a service charges a $10 starting fee plus $5 for each hour. One hour costs $15, while two hours cost $20.

\[ \frac{\$15}{1\text{ hour}} \neq \frac{\$20}{2\text{ hours}}. \]

The ratios are not equal. The quantities change together, but they do not form a proportional relationship.

Cross Multiplication Is Not the Definition

It is easy to begin thinking that a proportion is simply an equation where we cross multiply. That reverses the real logic.

A proportion exists because two ratios are equal.

Cross-products are useful because equal ratios have the property

\[ ad=bc. \]

In other words, cross multiplication helps us analyze proportions; it is not what makes a relationship proportional in the first place.

  4. What must be true of the cross-products if \[ \frac{a}{b}=\frac{c}{d}? \]
  5. Why is \[ \frac{4\text{ books}}{\$20} = \frac{\$35}{7\text{ books}} \] not written with corresponding quantities in consistent positions?

  1. Yes. \[ 4(15)=60 \] and \[ 5(12)=60. \]
  2. No. \[ 7(30)=210 \] while \[ 9(21)=189. \]
  3. The scale factor is \(4\): \[ 5(4)=20,\qquad 8(4)=32. \]

Key Ideas

  • A proportion is an equation stating that two ratios are equal.
  • Equivalent ratios represent the same relative relationship.
  • Scaling both parts of a ratio by the same nonzero factor preserves the ratio.
  • Equivalent ratios can often be recognized by simplifying them.
  • If \(\frac{a}{b}=\frac{c}{d}\), then \(ad=bc\).
  • The equality \(ad=bc\) is the fundamental property of proportions.
  • Cross multiplication follows from the equality of ratios; it is not the definition of proportion.
  • Corresponding quantities and units should remain in consistent positions.
