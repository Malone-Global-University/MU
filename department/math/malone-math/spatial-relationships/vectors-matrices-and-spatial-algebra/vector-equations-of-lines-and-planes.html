Vector Equations of Lines and Planes

Vectors give lines and planes compact algebraic descriptions. A line can be generated by starting at one known point and moving in one fixed direction. A plane can be generated from one known point and two independent directions, or described by a normal vector perpendicular to the entire plane. These forms connect geometry, coordinates, and algebra directly.

The Central Idea

A geometric object can often be described by a starting position together with allowed directions of motion.

For a line:

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}_0+t\mathbf{v} } \]

where:

  • \(\mathbf{r}_0\) is the position vector of a known point;
  • \(\mathbf{v}\) is a nonzero direction vector;
  • \(t\) is a scalar parameter.

Why One Direction Generates a Line

Start from a fixed point with position vector

\[ \mathbf{r}_0. \]

Multiplying a direction vector \(\mathbf{v}\) by every real value of \(t\) produces all scalar multiples of \(\mathbf{v}\).

Adding those displacements to \(\mathbf{r}_0\) moves through every point on the line.

The Parameter \(t\)

The parameter

\[ t \]

controls how far and in which direction we move from the known point.

  • \(t=0\) gives the starting point.
  • \(t>0\) moves in the direction of \(\mathbf{v}\).
  • \(t<0\) moves in the opposite direction.

Example: A Line in the Plane

Suppose a line passes through

\[ P=(2,1) \]

and has direction vector

\[ \mathbf{v}=\langle 3,4\rangle. \]

The position vector of \(P\) is

\[ \mathbf{r}_0=\langle 2,1\rangle. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{r} = \langle 2,1\rangle + t\langle 3,4\rangle }. \]

Expanding the Vector Equation

From

\[ \mathbf{r} = \langle 2,1\rangle + t\langle 3,4\rangle, \]

scalar multiplication gives

\[ t\langle 3,4\rangle = \langle 3t,4t\rangle. \]

Therefore,

\[ \mathbf{r} = \langle 2+3t,\;1+4t\rangle. \]

Parametric Equations of a Line

If

\[ \mathbf{r} = \langle x,y\rangle, \]

then

\[ \langle x,y\rangle = \langle 2+3t,\;1+4t\rangle. \]

Matching components gives

\[ \boxed{ x=2+3t } \]

and

\[ \boxed{ y=1+4t }. \]

These are the parametric equations of the line.

Vector Form and Parametric Form Say the Same Thing

The vector equation

\[ \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}_0+t\mathbf{v} \]

packages all coordinate equations into one statement.

The parametric form separates that statement into one scalar equation for each coordinate.

General Line Form in Two Dimensions

If a line passes through

\[ P=(x_0,y_0) \]

with direction vector

\[ \mathbf{v}=\langle a,b\rangle, \]

then

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{r} = \langle x_0,y_0\rangle + t\langle a,b\rangle }. \]

Its parametric equations are

\[ \boxed{ x=x_0+at,\qquad y=y_0+bt }. \]

General Line Form in Three Dimensions

If a line passes through

\[ P=(x_0,y_0,z_0) \]

with nonzero direction vector

\[ \mathbf{v}=\langle a,b,c\rangle, \]

then

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{r} = \langle x_0,y_0,z_0\rangle + t\langle a,b,c\rangle }. \]

Three-Dimensional Parametric Equations

The vector equation

\[ \mathbf{r} = \langle x_0,y_0,z_0\rangle + t\langle a,b,c\rangle \]

is equivalent to

\[ \boxed{ x=x_0+at } \] \[ \boxed{ y=y_0+bt } \] \[ \boxed{ z=z_0+ct }. \]

Example: A Line in Three Dimensions

A line passes through

\[ P=(1,-2,4) \]

with direction vector

\[ \mathbf{v}=\langle 2,3,-1\rangle. \]

Its vector equation is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{r} = \langle 1,-2,4\rangle + t\langle 2,3,-1\rangle }. \]

Parametric Form of the Example

Expanding component-wise gives

\[ \boxed{ x=1+2t } \] \[ \boxed{ y=-2+3t } \] \[ \boxed{ z=4-t }. \]

Finding a Direction Vector from Two Points

If a line passes through

\[ A=(x_1,y_1,z_1) \]

and

\[ B=(x_2,y_2,z_2), \]

then a direction vector is

\[ \boxed{ \overrightarrow{AB} = \langle x_2-x_1,\; y_2-y_1,\; z_2-z_1 \rangle }. \]

Example: Line through Two Points

Let

\[ A=(1,2,3) \]

and

\[ B=(5,0,4). \]

Then

\[ \overrightarrow{AB} = \langle 4,-2,1\rangle. \]

A vector equation is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{r} = \langle 1,2,3\rangle + t\langle 4,-2,1\rangle }. \]

A Direction Vector Is Not Unique

If

\[ \mathbf{v} \]

is a direction vector for a line, then any nonzero scalar multiple

\[ k\mathbf{v} \]

is also a direction vector for the same line.

For example,

\[ \langle 4,-2,1\rangle \]

and

\[ \langle 8,-4,2\rangle \]

determine the same line direction.

A Point on the Line Is Not Unique Either

Any point already lying on the line may be used as

\[ \mathbf{r}_0. \]

Different point-direction pairs can therefore produce different-looking vector equations for the same geometric line.

Checking Whether a Point Lies on a Line

Suppose

\[ \mathbf{r} = \langle 1,2,3\rangle + t\langle 4,-2,1\rangle. \]

To test a point, substitute its coordinates into the parametric equations and determine whether one common value of \(t\) satisfies all coordinates.

Example: Check a Point on a Line

Test

\[ Q=(9,-2,5) \]

on

\[ x=1+4t,\qquad y=2-2t,\qquad z=3+t. \]

From the first coordinate,

\[ 9=1+4t \]

gives

\[ t=2. \]

Complete the Point Check

Using \(t=2\):

\[ y=2-2(2)=-2 \]

and

\[ z=3+2=5. \]

Both coordinates match.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ Q=(9,-2,5) \text{ lies on the line} }. \]

Symmetric Form of a Line

If

\[ x=x_0+at,\qquad y=y_0+bt,\qquad z=z_0+ct \]

and \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\) are all nonzero, solve each equation for \(t\):

\[ \frac{x-x_0}{a} = \frac{y-y_0}{b} = \frac{z-z_0}{c}. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \frac{x-x_0}{a} = \frac{y-y_0}{b} = \frac{z-z_0}{c} }. \]

Example: Convert to Symmetric Form

Suppose

\[ x=1+2t, \qquad y=-2+3t, \qquad z=4-t. \]

Then

\[ t=\frac{x-1}{2}, \] \[ t=\frac{y+2}{3}, \] \[ t=4-z. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \frac{x-1}{2} = \frac{y+2}{3} = 4-z }. \]

Zero Components in a Direction Vector

Symmetric form must be handled carefully when a direction component is zero.

If

\[ \mathbf{v}=\langle 2,0,-3\rangle, \]

then the line has

\[ y=y_0 \]

as a constant coordinate.

We do not divide by the zero direction component.

Parallel Lines

Two lines are parallel when their direction vectors are scalar multiples.

If

\[ \mathbf{v}_2=k\mathbf{v}_1 \]

for some nonzero scalar \(k\), then the line directions are parallel.

The lines may be distinct or may coincide.

Coincident Lines

Parallel direction vectors are not enough to prove that two lines are the same line.

To be coincident, one known point from one line must also lie on the other line.

Perpendicular Line Directions

Direction vectors

\[ \mathbf{u} \]

and

\[ \mathbf{v} \]

are perpendicular when

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=0 }. \]

The dot product therefore provides a direct test for perpendicular line directions.

From Lines to Planes

One direction is enough to generate a line.

A plane requires two independent directions.

Starting from a fixed point, we may move by any scalar multiple of one direction and any scalar multiple of another.

Vector Equation of a Plane

Let

\[ \mathbf{r}_0 \]

be the position vector of a point on a plane.

Let

\[ \mathbf{u} \]

and

\[ \mathbf{v} \]

be two nonparallel direction vectors lying in the plane.

Then

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}_0 + s\mathbf{u} + t\mathbf{v} }. \]

Why a Plane Needs Two Parameters

The parameter \(s\) controls motion in the direction of \(\mathbf{u}\).

The parameter \(t\) controls motion in the direction of \(\mathbf{v}\).

Because the two directions are not parallel, varying both parameters fills a two-dimensional plane.

Example: Vector Equation of a Plane

Suppose a plane passes through

\[ P=(1,2,3) \]

and contains the directions

\[ \mathbf{u}=\langle 1,0,2\rangle \]

and

\[ \mathbf{v}=\langle 0,3,-1\rangle. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{r} = \langle 1,2,3\rangle + s\langle 1,0,2\rangle + t\langle 0,3,-1\rangle }. \]

Parametric Equations of the Plane

Expand:

\[ \mathbf{r} = \langle 1+s,\; 2+3t,\; 3+2s-t \rangle. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{x=1+s} \] \[ \boxed{y=2+3t} \] \[ \boxed{z=3+2s-t}. \]

The Two Plane Directions Must Be Nonparallel

If

\[ \mathbf{v}=k\mathbf{u}, \]

then

\[ \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}_0+s\mathbf{u}+t\mathbf{v} \]

reduces to motion along only one direction.

The result is a line, not a plane.

A Plane through Three Points

Suppose a plane passes through three noncollinear points

\[ A,\qquad B,\qquad C. \]

Two direction vectors in the plane are

\[ \overrightarrow{AB} \]

and

\[ \overrightarrow{AC}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{a} + s\overrightarrow{AB} + t\overrightarrow{AC} }. \]

Example: Plane through Three Points

Let

\[ A=(1,0,2), \qquad B=(3,1,2), \qquad C=(1,2,5). \]

Then

\[ \overrightarrow{AB} = \langle 2,1,0\rangle \]

and

\[ \overrightarrow{AC} = \langle 0,2,3\rangle. \]

Complete the Three-Point Plane Equation

Using \(A\) as the base point,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{r} = \langle 1,0,2\rangle + s\langle 2,1,0\rangle + t\langle 0,2,3\rangle }. \]

Because the two direction vectors are not scalar multiples, they span a plane.

Normal Vectors

A normal vector to a plane is a nonzero vector perpendicular to every direction lying in the plane.

We often write the normal vector as

\[ \mathbf{n}. \]

If

\[ \mathbf{n}=\langle A,B,C\rangle, \]

then \(\mathbf{n}\) determines the plane's orientation.

Point-Normal Form of a Plane

Suppose a plane passes through a point with position vector

\[ \mathbf{r}_0 \]

and has normal vector

\[ \mathbf{n}. \]

For any point \(\mathbf{r}\) on the plane, the displacement

\[ \mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_0 \]

lies in the plane and is therefore perpendicular to \(\mathbf{n}\).

Hence,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{n}\cdot(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_0)=0 }. \]

Coordinate Form of the Point-Normal Equation

Let

\[ \mathbf{n}=\langle A,B,C\rangle, \] \[ \mathbf{r}=\langle x,y,z\rangle, \]

and

\[ \mathbf{r}_0=\langle x_0,y_0,z_0\rangle. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{ A(x-x_0) + B(y-y_0) + C(z-z_0) = 0 }. \]

Standard Scalar Equation of a Plane

Expanding the point-normal form gives

\[ Ax+By+Cz=D \]

for some constant \(D\).

Thus the standard scalar equation is

\[ \boxed{ Ax+By+Cz=D }. \]

The vector

\[ \langle A,B,C\rangle \]

is normal to the plane.

Example: Plane from a Point and Normal

Suppose a plane passes through

\[ P=(1,2,-1) \]

with normal vector

\[ \mathbf{n}=\langle 2,-3,4\rangle. \]

Point-normal form is

\[ 2(x-1)-3(y-2)+4(z+1)=0. \]

Convert the Plane to Standard Form

Expand:

\[ 2x-2-3y+6+4z+4=0. \]

Combine constants:

\[ 2x-3y+4z+8=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ 2x-3y+4z=-8 }. \]

Reading a Normal Vector from a Plane Equation

For

\[ 2x-3y+4z=-8, \]

a normal vector is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{n} = \langle 2,-3,4\rangle }. \]

Any nonzero scalar multiple of this normal is also a valid normal vector.

Finding a Normal from Two Plane Directions

If a plane is given by two nonparallel direction vectors

\[ \mathbf{u} \]

and

\[ \mathbf{v}, \]

then a vector perpendicular to both can serve as a normal vector.

In three dimensions, a cross product

\[ \mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v} \]

provides such a vector when the directions are not parallel.

Using a Known Normal without Computing a Cross Product

Sometimes a normal vector is already given or can be recognized directly.

If both plane directions satisfy

\[ \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{u}=0 \]

and

\[ \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{v}=0, \]

then \(\mathbf{n}\) is perpendicular to both directions and is therefore normal to the plane they span.

Checking Whether a Point Lies on a Plane

If a plane is written

\[ Ax+By+Cz=D, \]

substitute the coordinates of the point.

If the equation is satisfied, the point lies on the plane.

Example: Check a Point on a Plane

Test

\[ Q=(2,0,-3) \]

on

\[ 2x-3y+4z=-8. \]

Substitute:

\[ 2(2)-3(0)+4(-3) = 4-12 = -8. \]

Therefore, \(Q\) lies on the plane.

Parallel Planes

Two planes are parallel when their normal vectors are scalar multiples.

For example,

\[ 2x-y+3z=4 \]

and

\[ 4x-2y+6z=7 \]

have parallel normal vectors:

\[ \langle 2,-1,3\rangle \]

and

\[ \langle 4,-2,6\rangle. \]

Coincident Planes

Parallel normals do not automatically mean that the planes are distinct.

If one scalar equation is a nonzero scalar multiple of the other, then the equations describe the same plane.

Perpendicular Planes

Two planes are perpendicular when their normal vectors are perpendicular.

Thus, if

\[ \mathbf{n}_1\cdot\mathbf{n}_2=0, \]

the planes meet at a right dihedral angle.

A Line Parallel to a Plane

Let a line have direction vector

\[ \mathbf{v} \]

and let the plane have normal vector

\[ \mathbf{n}. \]

If

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0 }, \]

then the line direction is parallel to the plane.

The line may lie in the plane or may be a distinct parallel line.

A Line Perpendicular to a Plane

A line is perpendicular to a plane when its direction vector is parallel to the plane's normal vector.

If

\[ \mathbf{v}=k\mathbf{n} \]

for some nonzero scalar \(k\), the line meets the plane orthogonally.

Intersection of a Line and a Plane

A line and a plane may:

  • intersect at one point;
  • have no intersection because the line is parallel to the plane;
  • have infinitely many common points because the line lies in the plane.

Substitution provides an algebraic way to determine which case occurs.

Example: Intersect a Line with a Plane

Consider the line

\[ \mathbf{r} = \langle 1,0,2\rangle + t\langle 1,2,-1\rangle \]

and the plane

\[ x+y+z=6. \]

The line has parametric equations

\[ x=1+t,\qquad y=2t,\qquad z=2-t. \]

Complete the Line-Plane Intersection

Substitute into the plane:

\[ (1+t)+2t+(2-t)=6. \]

Simplify:

\[ 3+2t=6. \]

Therefore,

\[ t=\frac32. \]

Substitute back:

\[ \boxed{ \left( \frac52,\; 3,\; \frac12 \right) }. \]

Intersection of Two Planes

Two distinct nonparallel planes intersect in a line.

Their normal vectors determine the orientations of the planes.

A direction vector for their intersection line must be perpendicular to both normals.

In three dimensions, such a direction may be obtained from a cross product of the two normals.

Why Plane Equations Use Normals

A single direction vector cannot determine a plane because infinitely many planes contain that direction.

A normal vector behaves differently: once a point and a normal are fixed, the plane's orientation is determined.

Vector Form versus Normal Form

A plane can be described in two complementary ways.

Form Main Data Geometric Idea
Vector form Point + two in-plane directions Generate the plane by moving within it
Point-normal form Point + normal vector Require every in-plane displacement to be perpendicular to the normal

Common Error: Using a Point as a Direction Vector

A point identifies a location.

A direction vector identifies a directed change.

Although their coordinates may look similar, their mathematical roles are different.

Common Error: Using the Zero Vector as a Line Direction

A direction vector must be nonzero.

If

\[ \mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}, \]

then

\[ \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}_0+t\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{r}_0 \]

describes only one point, not a line.

Common Error: Using Two Parallel Vectors to Define a Plane

A plane requires two independent in-plane directions.

If

\[ \mathbf{v}=k\mathbf{u}, \]

then the two vectors provide only one direction.

Their linear combinations generate a line rather than a plane.

Common Error: Forgetting One Common Parameter for a Line

In

\[ x=x_0+at,\qquad y=y_0+bt,\qquad z=z_0+ct, \]

the same value of \(t\) must satisfy all coordinate equations for a given point.

Using unrelated parameter values would describe a larger set than one line.

Common Error: Dividing by Zero in Symmetric Form

If a direction component is zero, do not write it in a denominator.

Instead, the corresponding coordinate remains constant.

For example, if

\[ y=y_0, \]

retain that equation separately.

Common Error: Confusing Plane Directions with the Normal

Direction vectors in the plane lie along the plane.

The normal vector points perpendicular to the plane.

Therefore, a normal vector is not generally one of the plane's spanning directions.

Common Error: Reading the Wrong Normal from a Plane Equation

For

\[ Ax+By+Cz=D, \]

the normal vector is

\[ \boxed{ \langle A,B,C\rangle }. \]

The constant \(D\) is not a component of the normal vector.

Common Error: Assuming Parallel Normals Mean Distinct Planes

Parallel normals mean the planes are parallel in orientation.

The planes may still coincide.

Additional comparison of the equations or a point-membership test is needed to distinguish coincident from distinct parallel planes.

A Reliable Procedure for Writing a Line Equation

  1. Identify one point on the line.
  2. Find or identify a nonzero direction vector.
  3. Write the point as a position vector \(\mathbf{r}_0\).
  4. Use \(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}_0+t\mathbf{v}\).
  5. Expand into parametric equations if needed.
  6. Verify that known points satisfy one common parameter value.

A Reliable Procedure for a Line through Two Points

  1. Choose one point as the base point.
  2. Subtract coordinates to form the direction vector between the points.
  3. Confirm that the direction vector is nonzero.
  4. Insert the point and direction into vector form.
  5. Check the second point using a parameter value.

A Reliable Procedure for Writing a Plane from a Point and Two Directions

  1. Identify one point on the plane.
  2. Identify two nonparallel direction vectors in the plane.
  3. Write the point as \(\mathbf{r}_0\).
  4. Use \(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}_0+s\mathbf{u}+t\mathbf{v}\).
  5. Expand component-wise if parametric equations are required.
  6. Check that the two directions are not scalar multiples.

A Reliable Procedure for Writing a Plane from a Point and Normal

  1. Identify the point \((x_0,y_0,z_0)\).
  2. Identify the nonzero normal vector \(\langle A,B,C\rangle\).
  3. Write \(A(x-x_0)+B(y-y_0)+C(z-z_0)=0\).
  4. Expand if standard scalar form is requested.
  5. Substitute the known point to verify the equation.

Example: Full Line Workflow

Find a vector equation for the line through

\[ A=(-1,2,0) \]

and

\[ B=(3,5,-2). \]

First find the direction:

\[ \overrightarrow{AB} = \langle 3-(-1),\; 5-2,\; -2-0 \rangle. \]

Therefore,

\[ \overrightarrow{AB} = \langle 4,3,-2\rangle. \]

Complete the Full Line Workflow

Use \(A\) as the base point:

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{r} = \langle -1,2,0\rangle + t\langle 4,3,-2\rangle }. \]

The parametric equations are

\[ \boxed{ x=-1+4t,\qquad y=2+3t,\qquad z=-2t }. \]

Example: Full Plane Workflow

Find a plane through

\[ P=(2,-1,3) \]

with normal vector

\[ \mathbf{n}=\langle 1,2,-4\rangle. \]

Point-normal form is

\[ (x-2)+2(y+1)-4(z-3)=0. \]

Complete the Full Plane Workflow

Expand:

\[ x-2+2y+2-4z+12=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ x+2y-4z+12=0. \]

Equivalently,

\[ \boxed{ x+2y-4z=-12 }. \]

What to Remember

  • A line can be written \(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}_0+t\mathbf{v}\).
  • A line needs one point and one nonzero direction vector.
  • Parametric equations come from matching vector components.
  • A direction vector can be found by subtracting coordinates of two points.
  • A plane can be written \(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}_0+s\mathbf{u}+t\mathbf{v}\).
  • A plane requires two nonparallel in-plane directions.
  • A normal vector is perpendicular to every direction in the plane.
  • Point-normal form is \(\mathbf{n}\cdot(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_0)=0\).
  • In \(Ax+By+Cz=D\), \(\langle A,B,C\rangle\) is a normal vector.
  • Direction vectors test line relationships; normal vectors test plane relationships.
  • Substitution can test membership and find line-plane intersections.
