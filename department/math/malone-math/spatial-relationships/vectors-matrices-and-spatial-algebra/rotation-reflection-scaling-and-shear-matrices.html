Rotation, Reflection, Scaling, and Shear Matrices

Matrices can act as transformation operators. A single matrix can rotate a vector, reflect it across a line, enlarge or compress selected directions, or shear a figure so that one coordinate shifts in proportion to another. These transformations turn geometric motions into systematic algebra.

The Central Idea

A matrix can transform a vector by multiplication.

If

\[ A= \begin{bmatrix} a & b\\ c & d \end{bmatrix} \]

and

\[ \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} x\\ y \end{bmatrix}, \]

then

\[ \boxed{ A\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} ax+by\\ cx+dy \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Different matrices produce different geometric effects.

Matrices as Rules for Space

A transformation matrix does not merely store numbers.

It defines a rule that sends each input vector to an output vector.

Symbolically,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{v} \longmapsto A\mathbf{v} }. \]

Applying the matrix to every point or position vector of a figure transforms the entire figure.

The Identity Matrix

The simplest transformation matrix is

\[ I= \begin{bmatrix} 1&0\\ 0&1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

For every vector

\[ \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} x\\ y \end{bmatrix}, \]

we have

\[ \boxed{ I\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} }. \]

The identity transformation changes nothing.

Why the Columns Matter

The columns of a transformation matrix show where the standard basis vectors go.

Recall

\[ \mathbf{i} = \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{j} = \begin{bmatrix} 0\\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

If

\[ A= \begin{bmatrix} a&b\\ c&d \end{bmatrix}, \]

then

\[ A\mathbf{i} = \begin{bmatrix} a\\ c \end{bmatrix}, \qquad A\mathbf{j} = \begin{bmatrix} b\\ d \end{bmatrix}. \]

The Matrix Is Determined by the Basis Directions

Every plane vector can be written

\[ \mathbf{v} = x\mathbf{i}+y\mathbf{j}. \]

A matrix transformation acts linearly:

\[ A\mathbf{v} = xA\mathbf{i}+yA\mathbf{j}. \]

Therefore, once we know where \(\mathbf{i}\) and \(\mathbf{j}\) go, we know how the matrix acts on every vector in the plane.

Rotation about the Origin

A counterclockwise rotation through angle \(\theta\) about the origin is represented by

\[ \boxed{ R_\theta = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta \end{bmatrix} }. \]

This transformation changes direction while preserving distance from the origin.

Why the Rotation Matrix Has This Form

The standard unit vector

\[ \mathbf{i} = \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \]

rotates to

\[ \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta\\ \sin\theta \end{bmatrix}. \]

Likewise,

\[ \mathbf{j} = \begin{bmatrix} 0\\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

rotates to

\[ \begin{bmatrix} -\sin\theta\\ \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}. \]

These two images become the columns of \(R_\theta\).

Rotation of a General Vector

Applying

\[ R_\theta = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta \end{bmatrix} \]

to

\[ \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} x\\ y \end{bmatrix} \]

gives

\[ \boxed{ R_\theta\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} x\cos\theta-y\sin\theta\\ x\sin\theta+y\cos\theta \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Rotation by \(90^\circ\)

For

\[ \theta=90^\circ, \]

we have

\[ \cos90^\circ=0, \qquad \sin90^\circ=1. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ R_{90^\circ} = \begin{bmatrix} 0&-1\\ 1&0 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

It sends

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(-y,x). \]

Example: Rotate by \(90^\circ\)

Rotate

\[ \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 3\\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \]

counterclockwise by \(90^\circ\).

\[ R_{90^\circ}\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 0&-1\\ 1&0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 3\\ 2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ R_{90^\circ}\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} -2\\ 3 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Rotation by \(180^\circ\)

Since

\[ \cos180^\circ=-1, \qquad \sin180^\circ=0, \]

we get

\[ \boxed{ R_{180^\circ} = \begin{bmatrix} -1&0\\ 0&-1 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Therefore,

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(-x,-y). \]

This is also reflection through the origin.

Rotation by \(270^\circ\)

A counterclockwise rotation by \(270^\circ\) is equivalent to a clockwise rotation by \(90^\circ\).

\[ \boxed{ R_{270^\circ} = \begin{bmatrix} 0&1\\ -1&0 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

It sends

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(y,-x). \]

Rotation Preserves Magnitude

Rotating a vector changes its direction but not its length.

If

\[ \mathbf{w} = R_\theta\mathbf{v}, \]

then

\[ \boxed{ \|\mathbf{w}\| = \|\mathbf{v}\| }. \]

Rotations are therefore distance-preserving transformations.

Rotation Preserves Angles

If two vectors are rotated by the same rotation matrix, the angle between them remains unchanged.

A rigid rotation therefore preserves:

  • lengths;
  • angles;
  • parallelism;
  • perpendicularity;
  • shape.

Reflection across the \(x\)-Axis

Reflection across the \(x\)-axis leaves the \(x\)-coordinate unchanged and reverses the sign of the \(y\)-coordinate.

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(x,-y). \]

The matrix is

\[ \boxed{ F_x = \begin{bmatrix} 1&0\\ 0&-1 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Example: Reflect across the \(x\)-Axis

Let

\[ \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} -3\\ 5 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Then

\[ F_x\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1&0\\ 0&-1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} -3\\ 5 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ F_x\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} -3\\ -5 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Reflection across the \(y\)-Axis

Reflection across the \(y\)-axis sends

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(-x,y). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ F_y = \begin{bmatrix} -1&0\\ 0&1 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Reflection across the Line \(y=x\)

Reflection across

\[ y=x \]

exchanges the coordinates:

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(y,x). \]

The matrix is

\[ \boxed{ F_{y=x} = \begin{bmatrix} 0&1\\ 1&0 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Reflection across the Line \(y=-x\)

Reflection across

\[ y=-x \]

sends

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(-y,-x). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ F_{y=-x} = \begin{bmatrix} 0&-1\\ -1&0 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Reflection through the Origin

Reflection through the origin sends

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(-x,-y). \]

Its matrix is

\[ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} -1&0\\ 0&-1 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

This is the same matrix as rotation by \(180^\circ\).

Reflections Preserve Length

Like rotations, ordinary reflections preserve distances.

If

\[ \mathbf{w} = F\mathbf{v}, \]

where \(F\) is a reflection matrix, then

\[ \boxed{ \|\mathbf{w}\| = \|\mathbf{v}\| }. \]

Reflections preserve shape but reverse orientation.

What Orientation Reversal Means

Imagine three noncollinear points listed in counterclockwise order.

After a reflection, their images appear in clockwise order.

The figure retains its lengths and angles, but its handedness is reversed.

Uniform Scaling

A uniform scaling multiplies every coordinate by the same scalar \(k\).

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(kx,ky). \]

The matrix is

\[ \boxed{ S_k = \begin{bmatrix} k&0\\ 0&k \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Example: Enlarge by a Factor of \(3\)

Let

\[ \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 2\\ -1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Use

\[ S_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 3&0\\ 0&3 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{ S_3\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 6\\ -3 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Uniform Contraction

If

\[ 0 then \(S_k\) contracts vectors toward the origin.

For example,

\[ S_{1/2} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac12&0\\ 0&\frac12 \end{bmatrix} \]

halves every distance from the origin.

Negative Uniform Scaling

If \(k<0\), uniform scaling changes magnitude by \(|k|\) and reverses every direction through the origin.

For example,

\[ S_{-2} = \begin{bmatrix} -2&0\\ 0&-2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

This combines a \(180^\circ\) rotation with enlargement by a factor of \(2\).

Nonuniform Scaling

Horizontal and vertical directions may be scaled by different factors.

The general axis-aligned scaling matrix is

\[ \boxed{ S = \begin{bmatrix} a&0\\ 0&b \end{bmatrix} }. \]

It sends

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(ax,by). \]

Example: Different Horizontal and Vertical Scales

Let

\[ S= \begin{bmatrix} 2&0\\ 0&\frac12 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Then

\[ (x,y)\mapsto \left( 2x,\frac12y \right). \]

The transformation stretches horizontally and compresses vertically.

Nonuniform Scaling Can Change Angles

Uniform scaling preserves angles because all directions are scaled equally.

Nonuniform scaling generally does not preserve angles.

A square, for example, can become a rectangle when its horizontal and vertical directions are scaled differently.

Scaling and Area

If horizontal lengths are multiplied by \(a\) and vertical lengths by \(b\), areas are multiplied by

\[ \boxed{|ab|}. \]

For a uniform scaling by \(k\), area scales by

\[ \boxed{k^2}. \]

What Is a Shear?

A shear shifts one coordinate by an amount proportional to another coordinate.

Unlike a rotation or reflection, a shear usually changes angles and lengths.

Parallel lines remain parallel, but shapes become slanted.

Horizontal Shear

A horizontal shear with factor \(k\) sends

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(x+ky,y). \]

Its matrix is

\[ \boxed{ H_k = \begin{bmatrix} 1&k\\ 0&1 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

The \(y\)-coordinate controls how much horizontal shift occurs.

Example: Horizontal Shear

Let

\[ H_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1&2\\ 0&1 \end{bmatrix} \]

and

\[ \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 3\\ 4 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Then

\[ H_2\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 3+2(4)\\ 4 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ H_2\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 11\\ 4 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Vertical Shear

A vertical shear with factor \(k\) sends

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(x,y+kx). \]

Its matrix is

\[ \boxed{ V_k = \begin{bmatrix} 1&0\\ k&1 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

The \(x\)-coordinate controls how much vertical shift occurs.

Example: Vertical Shear

Let

\[ V_{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1&0\\ -1&1 \end{bmatrix} \]

and

\[ \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 2\\ 5 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Then

\[ V_{-1}\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 2\\ -2+5 \end{bmatrix} = \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} 2\\ 3 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

What a Shear Does to the Basis

For the horizontal shear

\[ H_k = \begin{bmatrix} 1&k\\ 0&1 \end{bmatrix}, \]

we have

\[ H_k\mathbf{i} = \mathbf{i} \]

and

\[ H_k\mathbf{j} = \begin{bmatrix} k\\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The horizontal basis direction stays fixed while the vertical basis direction tilts.

Shears Preserve Parallelism

A shear sends parallel lines to parallel lines.

This happens because linear combinations and scalar multiples are preserved by matrix transformations.

However, perpendicular lines need not remain perpendicular.

Shears Preserve Area in the Standard Case

The standard horizontal and vertical shear matrices

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1&k\\ 0&1 \end{bmatrix} \]

and

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1&0\\ k&1 \end{bmatrix} \]

preserve area.

A rectangle may become a parallelogram, but its area remains the same.

Transforming an Entire Figure

To transform a polygon or other coordinate figure, apply the same matrix to every vertex.

If a triangle has position vectors

\[ \mathbf{a},\qquad \mathbf{b},\qquad \mathbf{c}, \]

then its transformed vertices are

\[ A\mathbf{a},\qquad A\mathbf{b},\qquad A\mathbf{c}. \]

Example: Rotate a Triangle by \(90^\circ\)

Suppose the vertices are

\[ A=(1,0),\qquad B=(3,0),\qquad C=(1,2). \]

Under

\[ R_{90^\circ} = \begin{bmatrix} 0&-1\\ 1&0 \end{bmatrix}, \]

the coordinate rule is

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(-y,x). \]

Complete the Triangle Rotation

Therefore,

\[ A=(1,0)\mapsto A'=(0,1), \] \[ B=(3,0)\mapsto B'=(0,3), \] \[ C=(1,2)\mapsto C'=(-2,1). \]

Every vertex is transformed by the same operator.

Linear Transformations Fix the Origin

For any matrix \(A\),

\[ A\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}. \]

Therefore, pure matrix transformations of the form

\[ \mathbf{v}\mapsto A\mathbf{v} \]

always leave the origin fixed.

Why Ordinary Translations Need Special Treatment

A translation such as

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(x+a,y+b) \]

generally moves the origin.

Therefore, it cannot be represented by an ordinary \(2\times2\) matrix acting alone on \(\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}\).

Translations are affine transformations rather than purely linear ones.

Combining Transformations

Several transformations can be applied in sequence.

For example, a vector may first be reflected and then rotated.

If

\[ \mathbf{v} \]

is first transformed by \(A\) and then by \(B\), the result is

\[ \boxed{ B(A\mathbf{v}) }. \]

Order Matters

In general,

\[ B(A\mathbf{v}) \neq A(B\mathbf{v}). \]

Rotating and then reflecting can produce a different result from reflecting and then rotating.

Transformation composition is therefore order-sensitive.

Example: Reflect Then Rotate

Let

\[ \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 2\\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

First reflect across the \(x\)-axis:

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 2\\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \mapsto \begin{bmatrix} 2\\ -1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Then rotate \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise:

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 2\\ -1 \end{bmatrix} \mapsto \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 2 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Example: Rotate Then Reflect

Start again with

\[ \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 2\\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Rotate first:

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 2\\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \mapsto \begin{bmatrix} -1\\ 2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Then reflect across the \(x\)-axis:

\[ \begin{bmatrix} -1\\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \mapsto \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} -1\\ -2 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

The result differs from the previous order.

Transformation Invariants

An invariant is a property that remains unchanged under a transformation.

Different matrix families preserve different features.

Transformation Typical Preserved Features
Rotation Lengths, angles, area, orientation
Reflection Lengths, angles, area
Uniform scaling Angles, shape, ratios of lengths
Nonuniform scaling Parallelism, collinearity
Shear Parallelism, collinearity, standard shear area

Fixed Vectors and Fixed Directions

Some transformations leave certain vectors or directions unchanged.

Reflection across the \(x\)-axis fixes every vector lying on the \(x\)-axis.

A horizontal shear fixes the horizontal direction.

These ideas lead naturally toward eigenvectors in a later lesson.

Recognizing a Transformation from Its Matrix

Certain matrix patterns reveal familiar transformations.

For example,

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 0&-1\\ 1&0 \end{bmatrix} \]

is a \(90^\circ\) rotation, while

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1&0\\ 0&-1 \end{bmatrix} \]

is reflection across the \(x\)-axis.

Reading a Transformation from Basis Images

Suppose

\[ A\mathbf{i} = \begin{bmatrix} 2\\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \]

and

\[ A\mathbf{j} = \begin{bmatrix} 0\\ 3 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Then

\[ A= \begin{bmatrix} 2&0\\ 0&3 \end{bmatrix}. \]

This scales horizontally by \(2\) and vertically by \(3\).

Constructing a Matrix from Coordinate Rules

Suppose a transformation satisfies

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(2x+y,\;x-3y). \]

Then

\[ \begin{bmatrix} x\\ y \end{bmatrix} \mapsto \begin{bmatrix} 2x+y\\ x-3y \end{bmatrix}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ A= \begin{bmatrix} 2&1\\ 1&-3 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Why Matrix Transformations Preserve the Origin and Lines through It

If a line through the origin consists of vectors

\[ t\mathbf{v}, \]

then applying \(A\) gives

\[ A(t\mathbf{v}) = tA\mathbf{v}. \]

The images still lie along a line through the origin, unless every vector collapses to the zero vector.

Common Error: Multiplying in the Wrong Pattern

For

\[ A= \begin{bmatrix} a&b\\ c&d \end{bmatrix} \]

and

\[ \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} x\\ y \end{bmatrix}, \]

the correct product is

\[ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} ax+by\\ cx+dy \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Each matrix row combines with the entire vector column.

Common Error: Using the Clockwise Rotation Matrix for Counterclockwise Rotation

The standard counterclockwise rotation matrix is

\[ \boxed{ \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Changing the signs in the wrong places reverses the direction of rotation.

Common Error: Reflecting the Wrong Coordinate

Reflection across the \(x\)-axis changes the sign of \(y\):

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(x,-y). \]

Reflection across the \(y\)-axis changes the sign of \(x\):

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(-x,y). \]

The coordinate perpendicular to the mirror line changes sign.

Common Error: Treating Nonuniform Scaling as Uniform

The matrix

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 2&0\\ 0&3 \end{bmatrix} \]

does not enlarge every length by one common factor.

Horizontal and vertical directions scale differently, so angles and shapes may change.

Common Error: Confusing Horizontal and Vertical Shears

A horizontal shear changes the \(x\)-coordinate:

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(x+ky,y). \]

A vertical shear changes the \(y\)-coordinate:

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(x,y+kx). \]

The unchanged coordinate identifies the shear's primary direction.

Common Error: Assuming Composition Order Does Not Matter

Matrix transformations generally do not commute.

Applying \(A\) and then \(B\) can differ from applying \(B\) and then \(A\).

Always preserve the stated sequence of transformations.

Common Error: Trying to Represent Translation with a \(2\times2\) Matrix Alone

An ordinary \(2\times2\) linear transformation fixes the origin.

A nonzero translation moves the origin.

Therefore, translation requires an affine description or an expanded coordinate system such as homogeneous coordinates.

A Reliable Procedure for Applying a Transformation Matrix

  1. Write the vector as a column vector.
  2. Confirm that matrix and vector dimensions are compatible.
  3. Multiply each matrix row by the vector column.
  4. Simplify the resulting coordinates.
  5. Interpret the output geometrically.
  6. Check whether the result matches the expected direction, size, or symmetry.

A Reliable Procedure for Identifying a Standard Transformation

  1. Inspect the matrix entries.
  2. Determine where \(\mathbf{i}\) and \(\mathbf{j}\) are sent.
  3. Check whether lengths or directions appear preserved.
  4. Compare the pattern with standard rotation, reflection, scaling, or shear matrices.
  5. Test one simple vector if needed.

A Reliable Procedure for Transforming a Figure

  1. List the coordinates of every relevant vertex.
  2. Convert each vertex to a position vector.
  3. Apply the same matrix to every vector.
  4. Record the transformed coordinates.
  5. Reconnect corresponding image vertices.
  6. Compare preserved and changed geometric features.

A Reliable Procedure for Sequential Transformations

  1. Read the transformation order carefully.
  2. Apply the first transformation to the original vector.
  3. Use that output as the input to the second transformation.
  4. Continue in sequence.
  5. Do not reverse the order unless the problem explicitly asks you to compare orders.
  6. Check the final geometry against the expected combined effect.

Example: Full Rotation Workflow

Rotate

\[ \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 4\\ -1 \end{bmatrix} \]

counterclockwise by \(90^\circ\).

Use

\[ R_{90^\circ} = \begin{bmatrix} 0&-1\\ 1&0 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Then

\[ R_{90^\circ}\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 0(4)+(-1)(-1)\\ 1(4)+0(-1) \end{bmatrix}. \]

Complete the Rotation Workflow

Simplify:

\[ \boxed{ R_{90^\circ}\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 4 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Check the magnitude:

\[ \sqrt{4^2+(-1)^2} = \sqrt{17}, \]

while

\[ \sqrt{1^2+4^2} = \sqrt{17}. \]

The rotation preserved length as expected.

Example: Full Shear Workflow

Apply the horizontal shear

\[ H= \begin{bmatrix} 1&3\\ 0&1 \end{bmatrix} \]

to

\[ \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} -2\\ 4 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Multiply:

\[ H\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} -2+3(4)\\ 4 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ H\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 10\\ 4 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

What to Remember

  • Matrices can act as spatial transformation operators.
  • The columns of a transformation matrix show the images of the standard basis vectors.
  • Rotation through \(\theta\) uses the matrix \(\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}\).
  • Rotations preserve lengths and angles.
  • Reflections preserve lengths and angles but reverse orientation.
  • Uniform scaling changes every direction by one common scale factor.
  • Nonuniform scaling can distort angles and shapes.
  • Horizontal and vertical shears shift one coordinate in proportion to the other.
  • Standard shears preserve area and parallelism.
  • Ordinary linear matrix transformations fix the origin.
  • Transformation order matters when several matrices are applied in sequence.
