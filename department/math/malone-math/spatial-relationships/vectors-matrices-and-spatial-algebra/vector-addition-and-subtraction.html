Vector Addition and Subtraction

Vectors describe directed changes. When several directed changes occur in sequence, we need a way to combine them. Vector addition joins successive movements into one resultant vector, while vector subtraction compares one directed quantity with another by adding its opposite. The geometric and algebraic viewpoints tell the same story.

The Central Idea

If one vector represents one directed change and a second vector represents another directed change, their sum represents the combined change.

If

\[ \mathbf{u} \]

occurs first and

\[ \mathbf{v} \]

occurs second, then the total change is

\[ \boxed{\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}}. \]

Geometrically, the vectors can be placed head to tail.

Head-to-Tail Addition

To add two vectors geometrically:

  1. Draw the first vector.
  2. Move the second vector without rotating or resizing it.
  3. Place the initial point of the second at the terminal point of the first.
  4. Draw the resultant from the initial point of the first to the terminal point of the second.

That resultant represents

\[ \boxed{\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}}. \]

Why Vectors May Be Moved for Addition

In elementary vector geometry, vectors are usually treated as free vectors.

A free vector may be translated to another location without changing the vector, provided its magnitude and direction remain unchanged.

This is what allows us to arrange vectors head to tail.

Example: Two Successive Displacements

Suppose a person moves \(4\) units east and then \(3\) units north.

Let

\[ \mathbf{u}=\langle 4,0\rangle \]

and

\[ \mathbf{v}=\langle 0,3\rangle. \]

The combined displacement is

\[ \mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} = \langle 4,3\rangle. \]

The two-step trip is therefore represented by one resultant vector.

Component-Wise Vector Addition

If

\[ \mathbf{u}=\langle a,b\rangle \]

and

\[ \mathbf{v}=\langle c,d\rangle, \]

then add corresponding components:

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} = \langle a+c,\;b+d\rangle }. \]

Horizontal changes combine with horizontal changes, and vertical changes combine with vertical changes.

Example: Add Two Vectors

Let

\[ \mathbf{u}=\langle 2,5\rangle \]

and

\[ \mathbf{v}=\langle 4,-1\rangle. \]

Then

\[ \mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} = \langle 2+4,\;5+(-1)\rangle. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} = \langle 6,4\rangle }. \]

Why Component Addition Works

Suppose \(\mathbf{u}\) contributes \(a\) horizontal units and \(b\) vertical units.

Suppose \(\mathbf{v}\) then contributes \(c\) horizontal units and \(d\) vertical units.

The total horizontal change is

\[ a+c, \]

while the total vertical change is

\[ b+d. \]

This produces

\[ \langle a+c,b+d\rangle. \]

The Resultant Vector

A vector formed by combining two or more vectors is often called the resultant.

If

\[ \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}, \]

then \(\mathbf{R}\) summarizes the net directed effect of \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\).

Resultant Does Not Mean Total Distance

Vector addition gives net directed change, not total path length.

If you travel \(5\) units east and then \(5\) units west, your total distance is

\[ 10, \]

but your resultant displacement is

\[ \langle 5,0\rangle+\langle -5,0\rangle = \boxed{\langle 0,0\rangle}. \]

The Parallelogram Rule

Two vectors can also be added by placing their initial points together.

Complete a parallelogram using the two vectors as adjacent sides.

The diagonal beginning at the common initial point represents

\[ \boxed{\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}}. \]

This is called the parallelogram rule.

Head-to-Tail and Parallelogram Addition Agree

The head-to-tail rule and the parallelogram rule are not competing methods.

They are two geometric constructions of the same vector sum.

Component addition gives the same result algebraically.

Vector Addition Is Commutative

For vectors of the same dimension,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u} }. \]

In component form,

\[ \langle a+c,b+d\rangle = \langle c+a,d+b\rangle \]

because ordinary scalar addition is commutative.

Geometric Meaning of Commutativity

If two vectors are arranged head to tail in one order, their resultant is the same as when the order is reversed.

The intermediate path changes, but the overall displacement does not.

This distinction between path and resultant is important.

Vector Addition Is Associative

For compatible vectors,

\[ \boxed{ (\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v})+\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u}+(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w}) }. \]

Thus several vector additions may be grouped in different ways without changing the final resultant.

Adding Several Vectors

Suppose

\[ \mathbf{u}=\langle 2,1\rangle, \qquad \mathbf{v}=\langle -1,4\rangle, \qquad \mathbf{w}=\langle 3,-2\rangle. \]

Then

\[ \mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w} = \langle 2-1+3,\;1+4-2\rangle. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w} = \langle 4,3\rangle }. \]

The Zero Vector Is the Additive Identity

For every vector \(\mathbf{v}\),

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{v}+\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{v} }. \]

In two dimensions,

\[ \langle a,b\rangle+\langle 0,0\rangle = \langle a,b\rangle. \]

Opposite Vectors Are Additive Inverses

The opposite of

\[ \mathbf{v}=\langle a,b\rangle \]

is

\[ -\mathbf{v} = \langle -a,-b\rangle. \]

Their sum is

\[ \mathbf{v}+(-\mathbf{v}) = \langle 0,0\rangle. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{v}+(-\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{0} }. \]

Vector Subtraction

Vector subtraction is defined using the additive inverse.

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u}+(-\mathbf{v}) }. \]

So subtracting a vector means adding the same magnitude in the opposite direction.

Component-Wise Vector Subtraction

If

\[ \mathbf{u}=\langle a,b\rangle \]

and

\[ \mathbf{v}=\langle c,d\rangle, \]

then

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v} = \langle a-c,\;b-d\rangle }. \]

Example: Subtract Two Vectors

Let

\[ \mathbf{u}=\langle 7,2\rangle \]

and

\[ \mathbf{v}=\langle 3,5\rangle. \]

Then

\[ \mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v} = \langle 7-3,\;2-5\rangle. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v} = \langle 4,-3\rangle }. \]

Subtraction as Addition of an Opposite

Using the same vectors,

\[ \mathbf{u}=\langle 7,2\rangle, \qquad \mathbf{v}=\langle 3,5\rangle, \]

first find

\[ -\mathbf{v} = \langle -3,-5\rangle. \]

Then

\[ \mathbf{u}+(-\mathbf{v}) = \langle 7-3,\;2-5\rangle = \langle 4,-3\rangle. \]

This confirms the subtraction rule.

Geometric Meaning of \(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\)

Suppose \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) are drawn from the same initial point.

Then

\[ \mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v} \]

can be represented by a vector pointing from the terminal point of \(\mathbf{v}\) to the terminal point of \(\mathbf{u}\).

Why the Difference Points from \(\mathbf{v}\) to \(\mathbf{u}\)

Rearrange

\[ \mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{d}. \]

Adding \(\mathbf{v}\) to both sides gives

\[ \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{v}+\mathbf{d}. \]

Therefore, \(\mathbf{d}\) is the vector that must be added to \(\mathbf{v}\) to reach \(\mathbf{u}\).

Position Vectors and Displacement

Suppose two points have position vectors

\[ \mathbf{p} \]

and

\[ \mathbf{q}. \]

The displacement from the point represented by \(\mathbf{p}\) to the point represented by \(\mathbf{q}\) is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p} }. \]

This is another form of terminal minus initial.

Example: Displacement from Position Vectors

Let

\[ \mathbf{p}=\langle 2,1\rangle \]

and

\[ \mathbf{q}=\langle 8,5\rangle. \]

The displacement from \(\mathbf{p}\) to \(\mathbf{q}\) is

\[ \mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p} = \langle 8-2,\;5-1\rangle. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\langle 6,4\rangle}. \]

Connecting Point Notation and Vector Subtraction

If

\[ A=(x_1,y_1) \]

and

\[ B=(x_2,y_2), \]

their position vectors are

\[ \mathbf{a}=\langle x_1,y_1\rangle, \qquad \mathbf{b}=\langle x_2,y_2\rangle. \]

Then

\[ \overrightarrow{AB} = \mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a} = \langle x_2-x_1,\;y_2-y_1\rangle. \]

A Closed Vector Path

If several successive vectors bring us back to the starting point, their sum is the zero vector.

For example, if

\[ \mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{0}, \]

the three vectors form a closed head-to-tail path.

Example: Closed Triangle

Suppose

\[ \overrightarrow{AB} + \overrightarrow{BC} + \overrightarrow{CA}. \]

Traveling from \(A\) to \(B\), then \(B\) to \(C\), then \(C\) back to \(A\) produces no net displacement.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \overrightarrow{AB} + \overrightarrow{BC} + \overrightarrow{CA} = \mathbf{0} }. \]

The Triangle Relation

If you travel from \(A\) to \(B\) and then from \(B\) to \(C\), the total displacement is from \(A\) directly to \(C\).

\[ \boxed{ \overrightarrow{AB} + \overrightarrow{BC} = \overrightarrow{AC} }. \]

This is the head-to-tail rule written using point notation.

Verifying the Triangle Relation Algebraically

Let

\[ A=(x_1,y_1),\quad B=(x_2,y_2),\quad C=(x_3,y_3). \]

Then

\[ \overrightarrow{AB} = \langle x_2-x_1,\;y_2-y_1\rangle \]

and

\[ \overrightarrow{BC} = \langle x_3-x_2,\;y_3-y_2\rangle. \]

Adding gives

\[ \langle x_3-x_1,\;y_3-y_1\rangle = \overrightarrow{AC}. \]

Adding Vectors in Three Dimensions

If

\[ \mathbf{u} = \langle a,b,c\rangle \]

and

\[ \mathbf{v} = \langle d,e,f\rangle, \]

then

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} = \langle a+d,\;b+e,\;c+f\rangle }. \]

Subtracting Vectors in Three Dimensions

Using the same notation,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v} = \langle a-d,\;b-e,\;c-f\rangle }. \]

The rule is unchanged: operate on corresponding components.

Example: Three-Dimensional Addition

Let

\[ \mathbf{u}=\langle 2,-1,4\rangle \]

and

\[ \mathbf{v}=\langle 3,5,-2\rangle. \]

Then

\[ \mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} = \langle 2+3,\;-1+5,\;4-2\rangle. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} = \langle 5,4,2\rangle }. \]

Example: Three-Dimensional Subtraction

With

\[ \mathbf{u}=\langle 2,-1,4\rangle, \qquad \mathbf{v}=\langle 3,5,-2\rangle, \]

we have

\[ \mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v} = \langle 2-3,\;-1-5,\;4-(-2)\rangle. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v} = \langle -1,-6,6\rangle }. \]

Vector Addition in \(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}\) Form

Suppose

\[ \mathbf{u}=2\mathbf{i}+3\mathbf{j} \]

and

\[ \mathbf{v}=5\mathbf{i}-\mathbf{j}. \]

Combine like unit-vector terms:

\[ \mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} = (2+5)\mathbf{i} +(3-1)\mathbf{j}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} = 7\mathbf{i}+2\mathbf{j} }. \]

Vector Subtraction in \(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}\) Form

If

\[ \mathbf{u}=4\mathbf{i}+7\mathbf{j} \]

and

\[ \mathbf{v}=\mathbf{i}+3\mathbf{j}, \]

then

\[ \mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v} = (4-1)\mathbf{i} +(7-3)\mathbf{j}. \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v} = 3\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j} }. \]

Net Change over Several Stages

Vectors are particularly useful when motion or change occurs in several stages.

If the stage vectors are

\[ \mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2,\ldots,\mathbf{v}_n, \]

then the net change is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{v}_1+\mathbf{v}_2+\cdots+\mathbf{v}_n }. \]

Example: Net Motion

Suppose a robot moves according to

\[ \langle 3,2\rangle, \qquad \langle -1,4\rangle, \qquad \langle 5,-3\rangle. \]

The net displacement is

\[ \langle 3-1+5,\;2+4-3\rangle. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\langle 7,3\rangle}. \]

Magnitude of a Resultant

Once vectors are added, the magnitude of the resultant can be found using the ordinary magnitude formula.

If

\[ \mathbf{R}=\langle a,b\rangle, \]

then

\[ \boxed{ \|\mathbf{R}\| = \sqrt{a^2+b^2} }. \]

Example: Magnitude of a Resultant

Suppose

\[ \mathbf{u}=\langle 4,0\rangle \]

and

\[ \mathbf{v}=\langle 0,3\rangle. \]

Their resultant is

\[ \mathbf{R} = \langle 4,3\rangle. \]

Its magnitude is

\[ \|\mathbf{R}\| = \sqrt{4^2+3^2} = \boxed{5}. \]

The Magnitudes Usually Do Not Simply Add

In general,

\[ \|\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\| \neq \|\mathbf{u}\|+\|\mathbf{v}\|. \]

Direction matters.

For perpendicular vectors of magnitudes \(4\) and \(3\), the resultant magnitude is \(5\), not \(7\).

When Magnitudes Do Add

If two nonzero vectors point in exactly the same direction, then the magnitude of their sum equals the sum of their magnitudes.

For example,

\[ \langle 2,0\rangle+\langle 5,0\rangle = \langle 7,0\rangle. \]

Thus,

\[ 2+5=7. \]

Partial Cancellation

Opposing component directions can reduce a resultant.

For example,

\[ \langle 8,3\rangle+\langle -5,2\rangle = \langle 3,5\rangle. \]

The horizontal components partially cancel because they point in opposite directions.

Complete Cancellation

A vector and its opposite cancel completely:

\[ \mathbf{v}+(-\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{0}. \]

For example,

\[ \langle 6,-2\rangle + \langle -6,2\rangle = \boxed{\langle 0,0\rangle}. \]

Vector Subtraction Is Not Commutative

In general,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u} }. \]

In fact,

\[ \mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u} = -(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}). \]

Example: Order Matters in Subtraction

Let

\[ \mathbf{u}=\langle 5,2\rangle, \qquad \mathbf{v}=\langle 1,4\rangle. \]

Then

\[ \mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v} = \langle 4,-2\rangle, \]

while

\[ \mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u} = \langle -4,2\rangle. \]

These are opposite vectors.

Common Error: Adding All Components Together

If

\[ \mathbf{u}=\langle 2,3\rangle \]

and

\[ \mathbf{v}=\langle 4,5\rangle, \]

do not write

\[ 2+3+4+5=14. \]

Vectors are added component by component:

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} = \langle 6,8\rangle }. \]

Common Error: Mixing Component Positions

The first component must combine with the first component.

The second must combine with the second.

For

\[ \langle a,b\rangle+\langle c,d\rangle, \]

the correct result is

\[ \boxed{\langle a+c,b+d\rangle}, \]

not \(\langle a+d,b+c\rangle\).

Common Error: Forgetting to Negate Every Component

If

\[ \mathbf{v}=\langle 3,-4\rangle, \]

then

\[ -\mathbf{v} = \boxed{\langle -3,4\rangle}. \]

Every component changes sign.

Common Error: Treating Subtraction as Reordering

Vector subtraction does not mean placing the smaller-looking vector first.

The order is determined by the expression:

\[ \mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u}+(-\mathbf{v}). \]

Reversing the order produces the opposite result.

Common Error: Adding Magnitudes Instead of Vectors

Knowing

\[ \|\mathbf{u}\|=4 \]

and

\[ \|\mathbf{v}\|=3 \]

is not enough to conclude that

\[ \|\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\|=7. \]

The directions of the vectors must also be considered.

Common Error: Confusing Path Length with Resultant Magnitude

Several movements may accumulate a large total distance while producing a small net displacement.

Vector addition tracks net directed change.

Distance traveled and magnitude of displacement are different quantities.

A Reliable Procedure for Vector Addition

  1. Verify that the vectors have the same dimension.
  2. Line up corresponding components.
  3. Add first components.
  4. Add second components.
  5. Continue component by component in higher dimensions.
  6. Interpret the resulting vector geometrically when useful.
  7. Find its magnitude or direction only if the problem asks for them.

A Reliable Procedure for Vector Subtraction

  1. Preserve the order of the vectors.
  2. Replace subtraction with addition of the opposite if helpful.
  3. Change the sign of every component of the vector being subtracted.
  4. Add corresponding components.
  5. Check that reversing the subtraction would reverse the result.

A Reliable Procedure for Net Displacement

  1. Represent each stage of motion as a vector.
  2. Choose one consistent coordinate orientation.
  3. Add all horizontal components.
  4. Add all vertical components.
  5. Include additional components in higher dimensions.
  6. Write the resultant vector.
  7. Compute magnitude or direction if required.

Example: Full Addition Workflow

Let

\[ \mathbf{u}=\langle -2,6\rangle, \qquad \mathbf{v}=\langle 5,-1\rangle. \]

Add corresponding components:

\[ \mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} = \langle -2+5,\;6-1\rangle. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} = \langle 3,5\rangle }. \]

The resultant points right and upward.

Example: Full Subtraction Workflow

Let

\[ \mathbf{u}=\langle 8,-3\rangle, \qquad \mathbf{v}=\langle 2,4\rangle. \]

First find the opposite:

\[ -\mathbf{v} = \langle -2,-4\rangle. \]

Then add:

\[ \mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v} = \langle 8,-3\rangle + \langle -2,-4\rangle. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v} = \langle 6,-7\rangle }. \]

What to Remember

  • Vector addition combines directed changes.
  • Geometrically, vectors may be added head to tail.
  • The parallelogram rule gives the same sum.
  • Components are added component by component.
  • Vector addition is commutative and associative.
  • The zero vector is the additive identity.
  • The opposite vector is the additive inverse.
  • Vector subtraction means adding the opposite.
  • Subtraction is not commutative.
  • Resultant displacement is not generally the same as total distance traveled.
  • \(\overrightarrow{AB}+\overrightarrow{BC}=\overrightarrow{AC}\).
