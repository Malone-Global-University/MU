Topological Invariants

Topology compares spaces by asking which properties survive continuous deformation and homeomorphism. These preserved properties are called topological invariants. Some invariants are simple yes-or-no properties such as connectedness or compactness. Others are numerical, such as Euler characteristic or genus. Still others are algebraic objects, such as fundamental groups or knot polynomials. Invariants turn geometric comparison into a structured method for proving that spaces are, or are not, topologically equivalent.

The Central Idea

A topological invariant is a property, number, or algebraic object that is preserved under homeomorphism.

If

\[ X\cong Y, \]

then every topological invariant must agree for \(X\) and \(Y\).

Symbolically,

\[ \boxed{ X\cong Y \Longrightarrow I(X)=I(Y) }. \]

Invariants as Tests for Non-Homeomorphism

The most important practical use of invariants is often negative.

If one invariant differs, then the spaces cannot be homeomorphic:

\[ \boxed{ I(X)\ne I(Y) \Longrightarrow X\not\cong Y }. \]

A single difference can settle the comparison.

Matching Invariants Do Not Always Prove Homeomorphism

The converse is usually false.

Two spaces may share several invariants and still fail to be homeomorphic.

\[ I(X)=I(Y) \centernot\Longrightarrow X\cong Y. \]

Invariants are often incomplete descriptions of topology.

Qualitative vs. Numerical Invariants

Some invariants are qualitative.

Examples:

  • connected or disconnected;
  • compact or noncompact;
  • orientable or nonorientable;
  • path connected or not path connected.

Others assign numerical values, such as:

  • number of connected components;
  • genus;
  • Euler characteristic;
  • crossing number of a knot.

Algebraic Invariants

More advanced topology assigns algebraic structures to spaces.

Examples include:

  • fundamental groups;
  • homology groups;
  • cohomology groups;
  • knot groups;
  • polynomial invariants.

These invariants can detect distinctions that simpler properties miss.

Connectedness

Connectedness is a topological invariant.

If

\[ X\cong Y, \]

then

\[ X\text{ connected} \iff Y\text{ connected}. \]

A homeomorphism cannot tear one connected piece into two.

Example: Connectedness Distinguishes Spaces

Compare

\[ X=(0,1) \]

and

\[ Y=(0,1)\cup(2,3). \]

The first is connected.

The second is disconnected.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{X\not\cong Y}. \]

Number of Connected Components

The number of connected components is also a topological invariant.

If one space has \(3\) components and another has \(5\), then they cannot be homeomorphic.

\[ \boxed{ \#\operatorname{Comp}(X) \ne \#\operatorname{Comp}(Y) \Longrightarrow X\not\cong Y }. \]

Path Connectedness

Path connectedness is preserved under homeomorphism.

If every pair of points in \(X\) can be joined by a continuous path, then the corresponding points in \(Y\) can be joined by the image of that path.

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ X\cong Y \Longrightarrow \bigl( X\text{ path connected} \iff Y\text{ path connected} \bigr) }. \]

Compactness

Compactness is a topological invariant.

If \(X\) is compact and

\[ f:X\to Y \]

is a homeomorphism, then \(Y=f(X)\) is compact because continuous images of compact spaces are compact.

Applying the inverse gives the converse.

Example: Compactness Distinguishes Intervals

Compare

\[ [0,1] \]

and

\[ (0,1). \]

The closed interval is compact.

The open interval is not compact.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{[0,1]\not\cong(0,1)}. \]

Boundedness Is Not a Topological Invariant

Boundedness depends on a metric or geometric embedding.

The interval

\[ (0,1) \]

is bounded, while

\[ \mathbb{R} \]

is unbounded.

Yet

\[ (0,1)\cong\mathbb{R}. \]

Therefore boundedness is not topological.

Length Is Not a Topological Invariant

The intervals

\[ [0,1] \]

and

\[ [0,1000] \]

have very different lengths.

But a linear map gives a homeomorphism between them.

Therefore length is not a topological invariant.

Angle Is Not a Topological Invariant

Homeomorphisms may bend and distort a space.

They do not need to preserve angles.

A square boundary is homeomorphic to a circle even though one has corners and the other does not.

Area and Volume Are Not Topological Invariants

A homeomorphism may stretch regions and change their area or volume.

Therefore ordinary Euclidean measure is not preserved in general.

Topology ignores these metric quantities unless additional structure is imposed.

Local Dimension

Dimension is a topological invariant for manifolds and Euclidean spaces.

In particular,

\[ \mathbb{R}^m\not\cong\mathbb{R}^n \]

whenever

\[ m\ne n. \]

This fact is supported by deep results such as invariance of domain and invariance of dimension.

Example: The Line and Plane

The spaces

\[ \mathbb{R} \]

and

\[ \mathbb{R}^2 \]

are not homeomorphic.

One elementary clue comes from deleting a point:

\[ \mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\} \]

is disconnected, while

\[ \mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{(0,0)\} \]

is path connected.

Cut-Point Behavior

A cut point is a point whose removal disconnects a connected space.

Cut-point behavior is preserved under homeomorphism.

If \(f:X\to Y\) is a homeomorphism and \(x\in X\), then

\[ X\setminus\{x\} \cong Y\setminus\{f(x)\}. \]

Thus a cut point must correspond to a cut point.

Example: Circle vs. Closed Interval

Both

\[ S^1 \]

and

\[ [0,1] \]

are compact and connected.

But interior points of \([0,1]\) are cut points, while no point of \(S^1\) is a cut point.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{S^1\not\cong[0,1]}. \]

Orientability

Orientability is a topological invariant of surfaces.

A homeomorphism cannot convert an orientable surface into a nonorientable one.

Therefore orientability is a powerful classification tool.

Example: Torus vs. Klein Bottle

The torus and Klein bottle both have Euler characteristic

\[ 0. \]

But the torus is orientable and the Klein bottle is nonorientable.

Hence,

\[ \boxed{T^2\not\cong K}. \]

Genus

For connected closed orientable surfaces, genus is a topological invariant.

It records the number of handles.

Thus:

\[ g(S^2)=0, \] \[ g(T^2)=1, \] \[ g(\text{double torus})=2. \]

Genus Distinguishes Closed Orientable Surfaces

For connected closed orientable surfaces,

\[ g_1\ne g_2 \]

implies

\[ \boxed{S_{g_1}\not\cong S_{g_2}}. \]

In fact, genus completely classifies connected closed orientable surfaces up to homeomorphism.

Euler Characteristic

The Euler characteristic is a numerical topological invariant.

For a finite cell decomposition of a surface,

\[ \boxed{\chi=V-E+F}. \]

Different subdivisions may change \(V\), \(E\), and \(F\), but not their alternating combination.

Euler Characteristic of Common Surfaces

For the sphere,

\[ \chi(S^2)=2. \]

For the torus,

\[ \chi(T^2)=0. \]

For a double torus,

\[ \chi=-2. \]

Different Euler characteristics prove non-homeomorphism.

Euler Characteristic and Genus

For a connected closed orientable surface,

\[ \boxed{\chi=2-2g}. \]

Therefore Euler characteristic and genus encode the same classification information within this restricted family.

Boundary Components

The number of boundary components of a compact surface is a topological invariant.

A disk has

\[ b=1, \]

while an annulus has

\[ b=2. \]

Therefore they cannot be homeomorphic.

Example: Disk vs. Annulus

A disk and annulus are both compact, connected, and orientable.

But their boundary-component counts differ:

\[ b(D^2)=1, \qquad b(A)=2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{D^2\not\cong A}. \]

Boundary Type Is Intrinsic for Manifolds with Boundary

For manifolds with boundary, homeomorphisms send boundary points to boundary points.

Interior points have neighborhoods modeled on

\[ \mathbb{R}^n, \]

while boundary points have neighborhoods modeled on a half-space.

This local distinction is topological.

Compact Surface Classification Data

For compact connected surfaces, the main classification data are:

  • orientability;
  • genus or nonorientable genus;
  • number of boundary components.

Euler characteristic is closely related to these quantities.

Classification as a Complete Invariant System

Within the class of compact connected surfaces, the combination of orientability, genus type, and boundary count forms a complete classification system.

In that setting, matching full classification data is enough to conclude homeomorphism.

The Fundamental Group

The fundamental group

\[ \pi_1(X) \]

is an algebraic invariant that records how loops in \(X\) can be continuously deformed.

It becomes especially useful when simpler invariants fail to distinguish spaces.

Simply Connected Spaces

A path connected space is simply connected if every loop can be continuously contracted to a point.

In terms of the fundamental group,

\[ \boxed{ X\text{ simply connected} \Longrightarrow \pi_1(X)\text{ is trivial} }. \]

Example: Circle vs. Disk

Both the circle and disk are path connected.

But:

\[ \pi_1(D^2) \]

is trivial, while

\[ \pi_1(S^1)\cong\mathbb{Z}. \]

Therefore the circle and disk are not homeomorphic.

Homology Groups

Homology assigns a sequence of algebraic groups

\[ H_0(X),H_1(X),H_2(X),\ldots \]

to a topological space.

Very roughly:

  • \(H_0\) detects connected components;
  • \(H_1\) detects independent loop-like holes;
  • \(H_2\) detects enclosed surface-type voids;
  • higher groups detect higher-dimensional structure.

Betti Numbers

The ranks of homology groups are called Betti numbers.

They are written

\[ b_0,b_1,b_2,\ldots \]

and give numerical summaries of topological features in different dimensions.

Euler Characteristic from Betti Numbers

For suitable spaces,

\[ \boxed{ \chi = b_0-b_1+b_2-b_3+\cdots }. \]

This connects elementary counting with algebraic topology.

Example: Sphere Betti Numbers

For the two-sphere,

\[ b_0=1, \qquad b_1=0, \qquad b_2=1. \]

Therefore,

\[ \chi=1-0+1 \] \[ \boxed{\chi=2}. \]

Example: Torus Betti Numbers

For the torus,

\[ b_0=1, \qquad b_1=2, \qquad b_2=1. \]

Therefore,

\[ \chi=1-2+1 \] \[ \boxed{\chi=0}. \]

Knot Invariants

Knot theory studies embeddings of circles in three-dimensional space.

Useful invariants include:

  • minimal crossing number;
  • unknotting number;
  • knot genus;
  • Alexander polynomial;
  • Jones polynomial;
  • knot group.

Crossing Number

The crossing number

\[ c(K) \]

is the smallest number of crossings in any diagram of a knot.

For the unknot,

\[ c=0. \]

For the trefoil,

\[ c=3. \]

Therefore the two knot types are distinct.

Linking Number

For an oriented two-component link, linking number is a topological invariant.

The standard Hopf link has

\[ |\operatorname{lk}|=1, \]

while the two-component unlink has

\[ \operatorname{lk}=0. \]

Therefore they are distinct links.

Invariants Can Be Coarse or Fine

A coarse invariant distinguishes only broad classes of spaces.

A finer invariant distinguishes more cases.

Connectedness is coarse.

Fundamental groups and homology groups are often finer.

No single invariant is automatically best for every problem.

A Complete Invariant

A complete invariant for a class of objects is an invariant that agrees exactly when the objects are equivalent.

In other words,

\[ I(X)=I(Y) \iff X\cong Y \]

within the specified class.

Complete invariants are rare and usually depend on restricting the class of spaces being studied.

Example of a Complete Classification System

For compact connected surfaces, the combination of:

  • orientability;
  • genus type;
  • boundary-component count;

gives complete classification up to homeomorphism.

Thus the invariant system is complete within that family.

Invariants and Deformation

A valid invariant must survive every homeomorphism.

Stretching, bending, twisting, or smoothly reshaping a space cannot change its topological invariants.

Cutting, gluing, or changing identifications may change them.

Example: Sphere to Ellipsoid

Stretching a sphere into an ellipsoid changes lengths and curvature.

But:

\[ \text{connectedness}, \quad \text{compactness}, \quad g=0, \quad \chi=2 \]

remain unchanged.

The sphere and ellipsoid are homeomorphic.

Example: Sphere to Torus

Creating a handle changes:

\[ g:0\to1 \]

and

\[ \chi:2\to0. \]

Therefore a sphere cannot be turned into a torus by homeomorphism alone.

Invariants and Quotient Spaces

Quotient constructions can change topology by identifying points.

Such identifications may alter:

  • connectedness;
  • fundamental group;
  • Euler characteristic;
  • orientability;
  • boundary structure.

Comparing invariants before and after a quotient helps reveal what changed.

Invariants and Local Structure

Some invariants describe global structure.

Others capture local behavior.

Examples of local topological information include:

  • local dimension;
  • whether a point is a manifold boundary point;
  • local connectedness;
  • local compactness.

Homeomorphisms preserve both local and global topological structure.

Choosing the Right Invariant

When comparing spaces, start with the simplest invariants.

A practical order might be:

  1. number of components;
  2. connectedness;
  3. compactness;
  4. path connectedness;
  5. boundary behavior;
  6. orientability;
  7. Euler characteristic or genus;
  8. fundamental group or homology when needed.

Why Start with Simple Invariants?

If compactness already distinguishes two spaces, there is no need to compute a fundamental group.

Good topological reasoning uses the least complicated invariant that solves the problem.

Common Error: Treating a Geometric Quantity as Topological

Length, angle, area, and curvature are generally not topological invariants.

A homeomorphism may change all of them.

Always ask whether the quantity is preserved under arbitrary homeomorphism.

Common Error: Thinking Equal Invariants Prove Homeomorphism

Equal values of one invariant usually provide only necessary evidence.

For example, the torus and Klein bottle both have

\[ \chi=0, \]

yet they are not homeomorphic.

Common Error: Thinking Every Useful Property Is an Invariant

A property is topological only if every homeomorphism preserves it.

Visual appearance alone is not enough.

One must test the property against the definition of homeomorphism.

Common Error: Confusing Intrinsic and Extrinsic Properties

Intrinsic properties belong to the space itself.

Extrinsic properties depend on how the space sits inside another space.

Knotting, for example, concerns an embedding of \(S^1\) in three-dimensional space rather than the intrinsic topology of the circle alone.

Common Error: Assuming Euler Characteristic Is Universal

Euler characteristic is extremely useful but does not distinguish every topological space.

It is one invariant among many.

Stronger invariants may be needed when Euler characteristics agree.

Common Error: Assuming Fundamental Group Is Complete

Even fundamental groups do not classify all topological spaces.

Distinct spaces can have isomorphic fundamental groups.

Topology often requires several layers of invariants.

A Reliable Procedure for Comparing Spaces

  1. Identify the category of spaces being compared.
  2. List easy invariants first.
  3. Compare connectedness and component structure.
  4. Compare compactness.
  5. Compare local or boundary behavior.
  6. For surfaces, compare orientability, genus, boundary count, and Euler characteristic.
  7. Use algebraic invariants if simpler tests agree.
  8. If one invariant differs, conclude non-homeomorphism.

A Reliable Procedure for Evaluating a Proposed Invariant

  1. State the property or quantity clearly.
  2. Ask whether it is preserved by every homeomorphism.
  3. Try simple homeomorphic examples with different geometry.
  4. If the property changes under a homeomorphism, it is not topological.
  5. If it is known to be preserved, it can be used as an invariant.

Example: Is Boundedness an Invariant?

Compare

\[ (0,1) \]

and

\[ \mathbb{R}. \]

They are homeomorphic, but one is bounded and the other is not.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\text{boundedness is not a topological invariant}}. \]

Example: Is Compactness an Invariant?

Yes.

Homeomorphisms are continuous in both directions, and continuous images of compact spaces are compact.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\text{compactness is a topological invariant}}. \]

Example: Is Genus an Invariant?

For connected closed orientable surfaces, yes.

A sphere and torus have different genus:

\[ 0\ne1. \]

Therefore they are not homeomorphic.

Example: Is Crossing Count of One Diagram an Invariant?

No.

Reidemeister moves can add or remove crossings in a knot diagram without changing knot type.

The invariant is the minimal crossing number, not the crossing count of an arbitrary diagram.

What to Remember

  • Topological invariants are preserved under homeomorphism.
  • Different invariant values prove that spaces are not homeomorphic.
  • Matching one or several invariants may still fail to prove homeomorphism.
  • Connectedness, compactness, path connectedness, and component count are topological invariants.
  • Length, angle, area, volume, and boundedness are generally not topological invariants.
  • Orientability, genus, boundary count, and Euler characteristic are important invariants for surfaces.
  • Fundamental groups and homology provide stronger algebraic invariants.
  • Knot theory uses invariants such as minimal crossing number, knot group, and knot polynomials.
  • A complete invariant classifies objects completely within a specified category.
  • The best strategy is usually to begin with the simplest invariant that can settle the question.

The core logic is:

\[ \boxed{ X\cong Y \Longrightarrow \text{all topological invariants agree} }. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \text{one differing invariant} \Longrightarrow X\not\cong Y }. \]

Where We Go Next

Topological invariants unify many of the ideas developed throughout topology. Connectedness, compactness, genus, orientability, Euler characteristic, boundary data, and knot invariants all serve the same purpose: they preserve structural information while ignoring irrelevant geometric distortion.

From here, the natural next steps include fundamental groups, homotopy, homology, cohomology, covering spaces, manifolds, and algebraic topology. These subjects develop increasingly powerful invariants for distinguishing spaces that look topologically similar at first glance.
