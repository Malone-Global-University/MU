Fields, Flows, and Physical Systems

Many physical systems are not described by a single number or direction. Instead, a quantity is assigned to every point in a region. Temperature, pressure, gravitational potential, electric force, wind velocity, and fluid motion can all be modeled as fields. Spatial mathematics helps us describe how these fields vary, where they point, how they flow, and how much passes through a boundary.

The Central Idea

A field assigns information to points in space.

The assigned information might be a scalar:

\[ \boxed{f(x,y,z)} \]

or a vector:

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = \langle P(x,y,z),Q(x,y,z),R(x,y,z)\rangle }. \]

Fields turn geometry into a distributed spatial system.

Scalar Fields

A scalar field assigns one numerical value to each point in a region.

Examples include:

  • temperature;
  • pressure;
  • density;
  • height;
  • concentration;
  • potential energy per unit quantity.

In two dimensions, a scalar field may be written

\[ \boxed{f(x,y)}. \]

Example: Temperature Field

Suppose temperature in a room is modeled by

\[ T(x,y)=20+2x-y. \]

At point

\[ (3,4), \]

the temperature is

\[ T(3,4)=20+2(3)-4=22. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{T(3,4)=22}. \]

Vector Fields

A vector field assigns a vector to each point in space.

In two dimensions,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{F}(x,y) = \langle P(x,y),Q(x,y)\rangle }. \]

The vector can represent both magnitude and direction.

Examples of Vector Fields

Vector fields appear in many physical systems:

  • wind velocity;
  • water flow;
  • electric fields;
  • gravitational fields;
  • magnetic fields;
  • force distributions.

Reading a Vector Field

At every point, the vector field gives a local direction and magnitude.

If

\[ \mathbf{F}(x,y) = \langle y,-x\rangle, \]

then at

\[ (2,1), \]

the field vector is

\[ \mathbf{F}(2,1) = \langle1,-2\rangle. \]

The local field therefore points rightward and downward from that point.

Field Magnitude

The magnitude of a vector field at a point is

\[ \boxed{ \|\mathbf{F}\| = \sqrt{P^2+Q^2+R^2} }. \]

In two dimensions,

\[ \boxed{ \|\mathbf{F}\| = \sqrt{P^2+Q^2} }. \]

Example: Field Magnitude

Suppose

\[ \mathbf{F}(x,y)=\langle3x,4y\rangle. \]

At

\[ (1,2), \]

we have

\[ \mathbf{F}(1,2)=\langle3,8\rangle. \]

Its magnitude is

\[ \sqrt{3^2+8^2} = \sqrt{73}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\|\mathbf{F}(1,2)\|=\sqrt{73}}. \]

Level Curves and Level Surfaces

A scalar field can be visualized by connecting points with equal values.

In two dimensions, a level curve satisfies

\[ \boxed{f(x,y)=c}. \]

In three dimensions, a level surface satisfies

\[ \boxed{f(x,y,z)=c}. \]

Example: Circular Level Curves

Let

\[ f(x,y)=x^2+y^2. \]

The level curve

\[ f(x,y)=9 \]

becomes

\[ x^2+y^2=9. \]

Therefore, the level curve is a circle of radius

\[ \boxed{3}. \]

The Gradient

The gradient of a scalar field is the vector

\[ \boxed{ \nabla f = \left\langle \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial z} \right\rangle }. \]

In two dimensions,

\[ \boxed{ \nabla f = \left\langle f_x,f_y \right\rangle }. \]

What the Gradient Means

The gradient points in the direction of greatest local increase of a scalar field.

Its magnitude gives the rate of that maximum increase.

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \nabla f = \text{direction and strength of steepest increase} }. \]

Gradient and Level Curves

The gradient is perpendicular to a level curve at regular points.

If

\[ f(x,y)=c, \]

then

\[ \boxed{ \nabla f \perp \text{level curve} }. \]

This provides a direct geometric connection between scalar fields and direction.

Example: Gradient

Let

\[ f(x,y)=x^2+2y^2. \]

Then

\[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=2x, \] \[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=4y. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \nabla f = \langle2x,4y\rangle }. \]

Gradient at a Point

For

\[ f(x,y)=x^2+2y^2, \]

at

\[ (1,-1), \]

the gradient is

\[ \nabla f(1,-1) = \langle2,-4\rangle. \]

Therefore, the direction of greatest local increase is

\[ \boxed{\langle2,-4\rangle}. \]

Directional Derivative

The rate of change of scalar field \(f\) in unit direction

\[ \hat{\mathbf{u}} \]

is

\[ \boxed{ D_{\hat{\mathbf{u}}}f = \nabla f\cdot\hat{\mathbf{u}} }. \]

The dot product extracts the component of the gradient in the chosen direction.

Flow Fields

A fluid velocity field assigns a velocity vector to every point:

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{v}(x,y,z,t) }. \]

If the field changes with time, the flow is time-dependent.

If it does not change with time, it is called steady in the simplified sense.

Flow Lines

A flow line or streamline is a curve whose tangent direction matches the local velocity field.

If

\[ \mathbf{r}(s) \]

traces the curve, then its tangent direction is parallel to

\[ \mathbf{v}(\mathbf{r}(s)). \]

Two-Dimensional Streamline Equation

For a steady two-dimensional field

\[ \mathbf{v}(x,y)=\langle P(x,y),Q(x,y)\rangle, \]

streamlines satisfy

\[ \boxed{ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{Q(x,y)}{P(x,y)} } \]

where \(P\ne0\).

Example: Horizontal Flow

Suppose

\[ \mathbf{v}(x,y)=\langle5,0\rangle. \]

Then

\[ \frac{dy}{dx}=0. \]

The streamlines are horizontal:

\[ \boxed{y=c}. \]

Radial Fields

A radial field points directly away from or toward a central point.

In three dimensions, a simple outward radial field may have the form

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}) = g(r)\hat{\mathbf{r}} }. \]

The direction depends only on the line connecting the point to the center.

Inverse-Square Fields

Some idealized central fields have magnitude proportional to

\[ \frac1{r^2}. \]

A generic radial inverse-square field can be written

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{F} = \frac{k}{r^2}\hat{\mathbf{r}} }. \]

The sign of \(k\) determines whether the direction is outward or inward.

Why Inverse-Square Behavior Appears

Spherical area grows as

\[ 4\pi r^2. \]

If a fixed amount of influence spreads uniformly across spheres centered on a source, the amount per unit area naturally scales like

\[ \frac1{r^2}. \]

Potential Fields

Some vector fields can be obtained from a scalar potential.

A common relationship is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{F} = -\nabla V }. \]

The field points in the direction of most rapid decrease of the potential \(V\).

Conservative Fields

A field derived from a scalar potential is called conservative under suitable conditions.

In such fields, work between two points depends only on the endpoints, not on the particular path taken.

Line Integrals

A line integral can accumulate a field's contribution along a path.

For a vector field \(\mathbf{F}\) along curve \(C\),

\[ \boxed{ \int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} }. \]

In physical applications, this can represent work done by a force field along a path.

Flux

Flux measures how much of a vector field passes through a curve or surface.

For a surface \(S\),

\[ \boxed{ \iint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS }. \]

The dot product measures the component of the field perpendicular to the surface.

Field Direction Relative to a Surface

If the field is tangent to a surface, then

\[ \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0, \]

so there is no normal flux through that surface at that point.

If the field is parallel to the normal, the normal component is maximized in magnitude.

Example: Constant Field Through a Flat Surface

Suppose a constant field has magnitude \(F\), and a flat surface has area \(A\).

If the angle between the field and the surface normal is \(\theta\), then the flux is

\[ \boxed{ \Phi=FA\cos\theta }. \]

Example: Perpendicular Flux

A constant field of magnitude

\[ 6 \]

passes perpendicularly through a flat surface of area

\[ 4. \]

Since

\[ \theta=0^\circ, \]

we have

\[ \Phi=6(4)\cos0^\circ=24. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\Phi=24}. \]

Divergence

The divergence of a vector field measures local net outflow.

For

\[ \mathbf{F} = \langle P,Q,R\rangle, \]

divergence is

\[ \boxed{ \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial P}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial Q}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial R}{\partial z} }. \]

Interpreting Divergence

Positive divergence suggests local source-like behavior.

Negative divergence suggests local sink-like behavior.

Zero divergence suggests no net local expansion or compression in the simplified geometric sense.

Example: Divergence

Let

\[ \mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = \langle x,y,z\rangle. \]

Then

\[ \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} = 1+1+1. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}=3 }. \]

Curl

The curl measures local rotational tendency in a vector field.

In three dimensions,

\[ \boxed{ \nabla\times\mathbf{F} }. \]

For

\[ \mathbf{F} = \langle P,Q,R\rangle, \]

the curl is

\[ \boxed{ \nabla\times\mathbf{F} = \left\langle R_y-Q_z,\; P_z-R_x,\; Q_x-P_y \right\rangle }. \]

Interpreting Curl

Curl indicates the axis and strength of local rotational behavior.

A paddle wheel placed in a flowing field is a useful mental model: if the local flow tends to rotate the wheel, the field has rotational character at that point.

Example: Rotational Field

Consider

\[ \mathbf{F}(x,y) = \langle-y,x\rangle. \]

The field circulates around the origin.

In three-dimensional form,

\[ \mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = \langle-y,x,0\rangle. \]

Its curl is

\[ \boxed{ \nabla\times\mathbf{F} = \langle0,0,2\rangle }. \]

Divergence Is Not Curl

Divergence and curl measure different spatial behaviors.

Divergence asks:

\[ \text{Is there net local outflow?} \]

Curl asks:

\[ \text{Is there local rotational tendency?} \]

A field can have one, both, or neither.

Incompressible Flow

A common idealized condition for incompressible flow is

\[ \boxed{ \nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}=0 }. \]

This expresses zero local volumetric expansion in the velocity field under the model.

Conservation

Many physical systems obey conservation laws.

A local conservation model often compares:

  • change inside a region;
  • flow through its boundary;
  • sources or sinks within the region.

Geometry is essential because conservation depends on both interior volume and boundary flux.

Control Volumes

A control volume is a chosen region used to analyze what enters, leaves, or accumulates inside.

The boundary of the region is called the control surface.

Flux through that surface helps track transport across the boundary.

Mass Flow Rate

In a simple uniform-flow model, mass flow rate through a cross-sectional area can be approximated by

\[ \boxed{ \dot{m}=\rho vA }. \]

Here:

  • \(\rho\) is density;
  • \(v\) is normal flow speed;
  • \(A\) is cross-sectional area.

Example: Uniform Pipe Flow

A fluid has density

\[ \rho=1000\text{ kg/m}^3, \]

speed

\[ v=2\text{ m/s}, \]

and cross-sectional area

\[ A=0.01\text{ m}^2. \]

Then

\[ \dot{m} = 1000(2)(0.01) = 20. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \dot{m}=20\text{ kg/s} }. \]

Continuity in a Simple Pipe Model

For steady incompressible flow through two cross sections,

\[ \boxed{ A_1v_1=A_2v_2 }. \]

If the pipe narrows, the flow speed must increase under this simplified model.

Example: Narrowing Pipe

Suppose

\[ A_1=4, \qquad v_1=3, \]

and

\[ A_2=2. \]

Then

\[ 4(3)=2v_2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{v_2=6}. \]

Pressure Fields

Pressure can be modeled as a scalar field

\[ p(x,y,z). \]

Spatial pressure differences can drive fluid motion.

The gradient

\[ \nabla p \]

identifies how pressure changes most rapidly through space.

Temperature Fields

Temperature is also a scalar field.

Heat tends to flow from higher temperature toward lower temperature under common physical models.

A simplified heat-flux model is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{q} = -k\nabla T }, \]

where \(k\) is a material-dependent coefficient.

Electric Fields

An electric field assigns a force-per-unit-charge direction and magnitude throughout space.

In a simplified electrostatic setting,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{E} = -\nabla V }, \]

where \(V\) is electric potential.

Gravitational Fields

A gravitational field similarly assigns acceleration-like influence to points in space.

In a simplified central model around a spherical mass,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{g} = -\frac{GM}{r^2}\hat{\mathbf{r}} }. \]

The negative sign indicates attraction toward the center.

Field Lines

Field lines are curves drawn tangent to a vector field.

They help visualize:

  • direction;
  • source-like behavior;
  • sink-like behavior;
  • circulation;
  • spatial variation.

Field lines are visual representations, not physical threads.

Field-Line Density

Diagrams often draw more field lines where field magnitude is stronger.

This is a visual convention rather than a literal count of physical lines.

The underlying field is continuous whether or not a diagram displays many arrows or curves.

Sampling a Field

Real fields are often measured only at discrete points.

Data may be collected at:

  • weather stations;
  • sensors;
  • grid nodes;
  • satellite samples;
  • simulation cells.

Interpolation can then estimate values between measured locations.

Discrete Vector-Field Representation

A computational model may store vectors on a grid:

\[ \mathbf{F}_{i,j,k}. \]

Numerical methods then approximate derivatives such as gradients, divergence, and curl from nearby samples.

Finite Differences

A partial derivative can be approximated numerically.

For example,

\[ \boxed{ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \approx \frac{ f(x+\Delta x,y)-f(x,y) }{ \Delta x } }. \]

Smaller spacing can improve local resolution, though numerical error and computational cost must still be considered.

Field Visualization

Common field visualizations include:

  • contour maps;
  • color maps;
  • arrow plots;
  • streamlines;
  • surface plots;
  • isosurfaces;
  • particle traces.

Different visualizations emphasize different spatial properties.

Steady Versus Time-Dependent Fields

A steady field does not explicitly change with time:

\[ \mathbf{F}=\mathbf{F}(x,y,z). \]

A time-dependent field does:

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{F}(x,y,z,t) }. \]

Particle Paths in a Flow

A moving particle in a velocity field satisfies

\[ \boxed{ \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} = \mathbf{v}(\mathbf{r}(t),t) }. \]

This differential equation connects the local velocity field to the trajectory of an individual particle.

Streamlines and Particle Paths Can Differ

In a steady flow, streamlines and particle paths align naturally.

In time-dependent flow, a streamline describes the instantaneous field direction, while a particle path records the history of one moving particle.

These are different concepts and need not coincide.

Source and Sink Models

A source emits or produces outward flow in a simplified model.

A sink draws flow inward.

Source-like and sink-like behavior is associated with positive or negative divergence.

Vortices

A vortex is a region of rotational flow.

Curl helps describe local rotational tendency, though real vortex dynamics can be much more complex than a single curl value.

Flux Through a Closed Surface

A closed surface encloses a volume.

Net outward flux is

\[ \boxed{ \iint_{\partial V} \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS }. \]

This quantity measures the net field flow through the entire boundary.

Divergence Theorem

The divergence theorem connects net outward flux through a closed surface to divergence inside the enclosed volume:

\[ \boxed{ \iint_{\partial V} \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = \iiint_V \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}\,dV }. \]

It links local source behavior to global boundary flow.

Circulation

Circulation measures how strongly a vector field follows a closed path:

\[ \boxed{ \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} }. \]

Positive or negative values depend on the chosen orientation and field direction.

Stokes' Theorem

Stokes' theorem connects circulation around a boundary curve to curl across the spanning surface:

\[ \boxed{ \oint_{\partial S} \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_S (\nabla\times\mathbf{F}) \cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS }. \]

This links local rotational tendency to accumulated circulation.

Local and Global Field Behavior

Differential operators such as gradient, divergence, and curl describe local field behavior.

Integrals such as work, flux, and circulation accumulate behavior across paths, surfaces, or volumes.

Together they connect local structure with global physical behavior.

Common Error: Confusing Scalar and Vector Fields

Temperature assigns one number to each point.

Wind velocity assigns both magnitude and direction.

The first is a scalar field; the second is a vector field.

Common Error: Treating the Gradient as a Scalar

The gradient is a vector.

It contains one partial derivative for each spatial coordinate and points in the direction of steepest increase.

Common Error: Confusing Field Lines with Particle Paths

A field line shows local field direction.

A particle path records the trajectory of a moving object.

In time-dependent fields, these may be different.

Common Error: Confusing Divergence and Curl

Divergence measures net local outflow.

Curl measures local rotational tendency.

One should not be interpreted as the other.

Common Error: Ignoring Surface Orientation in Flux

Flux depends on the chosen normal direction.

Reversing the normal reverses the sign of

\[ \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}. \]

Common Error: Using Total Field Magnitude Instead of Normal Component

Flux depends on the component of the field perpendicular to the surface.

For a constant field and flat surface,

\[ \Phi=FA\cos\theta, \]

not simply \(FA\) unless the field is parallel to the normal.

Common Error: Treating an Ideal Field Model as Exact Physics

Radial inverse-square fields, steady incompressible flow, uniform flux, and other simplified models are useful approximations.

Real physical systems may include boundaries, nonlinear behavior, turbulence, anisotropy, time dependence, and material effects.

A Reliable Procedure for Scalar-Field Problems

  1. Identify the scalar quantity and coordinate system.
  2. Write the field function.
  3. Evaluate it at the requested point if needed.
  4. Use level sets to study equal-value geometry.
  5. Compute the gradient when direction of change is needed.
  6. Use a directional derivative for change along a specified direction.
  7. Check units and physical interpretation.

A Reliable Procedure for Vector-Field Problems

  1. Identify the vector field and its components.
  2. Evaluate the field at the required point.
  3. Compute magnitude or direction if needed.
  4. Use streamlines or trajectories for motion questions.
  5. Use divergence for local source or sink behavior.
  6. Use curl for local rotational behavior.
  7. Use dot products with normals for flux.
  8. Use line integrals for accumulated tangential effects such as work or circulation.

Example: Full Field Workflow

Consider

\[ \mathbf{F}(x,y) = \langle x,-y\rangle. \]

At point

\[ (2,3), \]

the field is

\[ \mathbf{F}(2,3) = \langle2,-3\rangle. \]

Its magnitude is

\[ \sqrt{2^2+(-3)^2} = \sqrt{13}. \]

Its two-dimensional divergence is

\[ \frac{\partial x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial(-y)}{\partial y} = 1-1=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{F}(2,3)=\langle2,-3\rangle, \qquad \|\mathbf{F}(2,3)\|=\sqrt{13}, \qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}=0 }. \]

What to Remember

  • A scalar field assigns one number to each point, while a vector field assigns a magnitude and direction.
  • Level curves and level surfaces connect points with equal scalar-field values.
  • The gradient points in the direction of steepest local increase.
  • Directional derivatives use the dot product of the gradient with a chosen unit direction.
  • Streamlines follow the local direction of a steady velocity field.
  • Potential fields can generate conservative vector fields through a gradient relationship.
  • Line integrals accumulate field effects along paths.
  • Flux measures the normal component of a field passing through a curve or surface.
  • Divergence measures local net outflow or source-like behavior.
  • Curl measures local rotational tendency.
  • Conservation laws connect changes inside a region with flow through its boundary.
  • Continuity relates cross-sectional area and speed in simplified incompressible flow.
  • Temperature, pressure, electric, gravitational, and velocity fields use the same underlying spatial language.
  • Field data can be sampled numerically and approximated on grids.
  • Divergence and Stokes-type theorems connect local differential behavior with global integral behavior.

The central relationship is:

\[ \boxed{ \text{field values} + \text{spatial change} + \text{boundaries} \longrightarrow \text{physical system behavior} }. \]
