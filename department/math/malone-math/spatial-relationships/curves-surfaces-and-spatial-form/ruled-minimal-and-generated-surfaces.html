Ruled, Minimal, and Generated Surfaces

Many surfaces are easiest to understand by how they are made. Straight lines can sweep through space to form ruled surfaces, curves can rotate or translate to generate larger forms, and certain surfaces arise as equilibrium shapes with zero mean curvature. These ideas connect parametrization, motion, curvature, structure, and geometric design.

The Central Idea

A surface can often be classified by a construction rule rather than only by an equation.

  • Ruled surfaces are swept out by straight lines.
  • Minimal surfaces have zero mean curvature at regular points.
  • Generated surfaces arise by moving curves or profiles through space.

Ruled Surfaces

A ruled surface can often be parametrized as

\[ \boxed{\mathbf r(u,v)=\mathbf c(u)+v\,\mathbf d(u)}. \]

For fixed \(u\), varying \(v\) traces a straight line. The curve \(\mathbf c(u)\) acts as a directrix, while \(\mathbf d(u)\) gives the direction of the ruling.

The Plane

A plane can be written

\[ \mathbf r(u,v)=\mathbf p+u\mathbf a+v\mathbf b. \]

Holding either parameter fixed gives a straight line. A plane is therefore ruled in infinitely many directions.

The Cylinder as a Ruled Surface

A circular cylinder has parametrization

\[ \boxed{\mathbf r(u,v)=\langle R\cos u,R\sin u,v\rangle}. \]

For fixed \(u\), varying \(v\) produces a vertical straight line. These parallel generators rule the cylinder.

Generalized Cylinders

If \(\mathbf c(u)\) is any curve and \(\mathbf d\) is a fixed nonzero vector, then

\[ \boxed{\mathbf r(u,v)=\mathbf c(u)+v\mathbf d} \]

creates a generalized cylindrical surface. Every ruling is parallel to \(\mathbf d\).

The Cone as a Ruled Surface

A cone can be generated by lines passing through a common vertex. If \(\mathbf c(u)\) is a curve not passing through the origin, then

\[ \boxed{\mathbf r(u,v)=v\,\mathbf c(u)} \]

traces the lines from the origin through the points of \(\mathbf c\).

Example: Circular Cone

Let

\[ \mathbf c(u)=\langle\cos u,\sin u,1\rangle. \]

Then

\[ \mathbf r(u,v)=\langle v\cos u,v\sin u,v\rangle. \]

Since \(x^2+y^2=v^2=z^2\),

\[ \boxed{x^2+y^2=z^2}. \]

The Hyperbolic Paraboloid Is Ruled

Consider

\[ \boxed{z=xy}. \]

Fix \(y=a\). Then

\[ z=ax, \]

which is a straight line in the plane \(y=a\). As \(a\) varies, these lines sweep the entire surface.

Doubly Ruled Surfaces

Some surfaces contain two distinct families of straight-line rulings. These are called doubly ruled.

Standard examples include planes, hyperbolic paraboloids, and one-sheet hyperboloids.

One-Sheet Hyperboloid

The surface

\[ \frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}-\frac{z^2}{c^2}=1 \]

is curved yet contains straight lines. It is a classical doubly ruled surface.

Ruled Does Not Mean Flat

A ruled surface may be strongly curved. The defining condition concerns the existence of straight generators, not global flatness.

Cylinders, cones, helicoids, and hyperbolic paraboloids are all ruled but geometrically very different.

Developable Ruled Surfaces

A ruled surface is developable if it can be flattened locally onto a plane without stretching.

Standard examples include planes, cylinders, and cones.

Not every ruled surface is developable.

Why a Cylinder Can Be Unrolled

Cutting a cylinder along one generator allows it to flatten into a rectangle. This idealized unfolding preserves distances along the surface.

Why a Cone Can Be Unfolded

A cone can be cut along a generator and flattened into a circular sector. Its surface is curved in space but developable away from the vertex.

Generated Surfaces

A generated surface is produced by moving a curve or profile through space according to a rule.

Common generation methods include translation, rotation, sweep, scaling, extrusion, lofting, and ruled interpolation.

Translational Surfaces

If two curves are \(\mathbf a(u)\) and \(\mathbf b(v)\), then

\[ \boxed{\mathbf r(u,v)=\mathbf a(u)+\mathbf b(v)} \]

defines a translational surface.

Example: Paraboloid by Translation

Let

\[ \mathbf a(u)=\langle u,0,u^2\rangle, \qquad \mathbf b(v)=\langle0,v,v^2\rangle. \]

Then

\[ \mathbf r(u,v)=\langle u,v,u^2+v^2\rangle, \]

so

\[ \boxed{z=x^2+y^2}. \]

Surfaces of Revolution as Generated Surfaces

Rotating a profile \(y=f(u)\) around the \(x\)-axis gives

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf r(u,v)= \langle u,f(u)\cos v,f(u)\sin v\rangle }. \]

Revolution is therefore one special case of surface generation.

Sweep Surfaces

A sweep surface is formed by moving a profile along a path. During the motion, the profile can remain fixed, rotate, scale, or deform according to a rule.

Simple Sweep Formula

If \(\mathbf c(u)\) is a path and \(\mathbf p(v)\) is a profile, a simple translational sweep is

\[ \boxed{\mathbf r(u,v)=\mathbf c(u)+\mathbf p(v)}. \]

Lofted Surfaces

A lofted surface interpolates among a sequence of profile curves. Unlike a fixed sweep, its cross-sectional shape may change from one position to another.

Lofting is widely used in industrial design, architecture, ship design, aircraft geometry, and computer graphics.

Minimal Surfaces

A classical minimal surface has zero mean curvature at every regular point:

\[ \boxed{H=0}. \]

Minimal Does Not Mean Absolutely Smallest in Every Situation

The word minimal refers to a precise variational and local geometric condition. Questions about global area minimization depend on boundary conditions and the class of competing surfaces.

Soap Films

An ideal soap film spanning a wire frame is a standard physical model for minimal-surface behavior. Surface tension drives the film toward an equilibrium shape that is locally area-stationary under suitable assumptions.

Principal Curvatures

At a regular point on a smooth surface, the normal curvature varies with direction. Two special extremal values are called the principal curvatures:

\[ k_1,\qquad k_2. \]

Mean Curvature

Mean curvature is

\[ \boxed{H=\frac{k_1+k_2}{2}}. \]

A minimal surface satisfies

\[ k_1+k_2=0. \]

Gaussian Curvature

Gaussian curvature is

\[ \boxed{K=k_1k_2}. \]

Mean curvature and Gaussian curvature are different quantities.

The Plane Is Minimal

A plane has

\[ k_1=k_2=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{H=0}. \]

The Catenoid

A catenoid is obtained by rotating a catenary around an axis. One standard parametrization is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf r(u,v)= \langle a\cosh(v/a)\cos u,\, a\cosh(v/a)\sin u,\, v \rangle }. \]

The catenoid is a classical nonplanar minimal surface of revolution.

The Helicoid

A helicoid is generated by a line that rotates around an axis while translating along that axis:

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf r(u,v)= \langle v\cos u,\, v\sin u,\, au \rangle }. \]

The Helicoid Is Ruled

Hold \(u\) fixed:

\[ \mathbf r(u,v) = \langle0,0,au\rangle + v\langle\cos u,\sin u,0\rangle. \]

This is a straight line. Therefore, the helicoid is ruled.

The Helicoid Is Also Minimal

The helicoid satisfies

\[ \boxed{H=0} \]

at every regular point. It is both a ruled surface and a minimal surface.

Catenoid and Helicoid

The catenoid and helicoid are visually very different but both are classical minimal surfaces. One is generated by revolution, while the other comes from screw motion.

The Minimal-Surface Equation for a Graph

If a surface is written as

\[ z=f(x,y), \]

then \(H=0\) leads to

\[ \boxed{ (1+f_y^2)f_{xx} -2f_xf_yf_{xy} +(1+f_x^2)f_{yy} =0 }. \]

This equation is introduced here as a structural connection, not as a required computational tool.

Why Minimal Surfaces Often Look Saddle-Like

On a nonflat minimal surface,

\[ k_1+k_2=0, \]

so typically the principal curvatures have opposite signs. The surface bends one way in one principal direction and the opposite way in the other.

Surface Tangent Vectors

For a parametrized surface

\[ \mathbf r(u,v), \]

the vectors

\[ \mathbf r_u,\qquad \mathbf r_v \]

give two tangent directions.

Surface Normal

When

\[ \mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\neq\mathbf0, \]

the cross product

\[ \boxed{\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v} \]

is normal to the surface.

Example: Normal to a Cylinder

For

\[ \mathbf r(u,v)=\langle R\cos u,R\sin u,v\rangle, \]

we have

\[ \mathbf r_u=\langle-R\sin u,R\cos u,0\rangle, \qquad \mathbf r_v=\langle0,0,1\rangle. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v = \langle R\cos u,R\sin u,0\rangle }. \]

Screw Motion

A screw motion combines rotation around an axis with translation along the same axis. The helicoid is the standard surface generated by continuous screw motion.

Extrusion

If a profile \(\mathbf c(u)\) is translated along a fixed vector \(\mathbf d\), then

\[ \boxed{\mathbf r(u,v)=\mathbf c(u)+v\mathbf d}. \]

An extrusion is therefore a generalized cylindrical ruled surface.

Offset Surfaces

If a surface has unit normal \(\mathbf N(u,v)\), an idealized offset at distance \(d\) is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf r_d(u,v) = \mathbf r(u,v)+d\mathbf N(u,v) }. \]

Offset surfaces are useful in geometric modeling and manufacturing but may develop self-intersections or singularities.

Self-Intersection

A parametric surface self-intersects when

\[ (u_1,v_1)\neq(u_2,v_2) \]

but

\[ \mathbf r(u_1,v_1)=\mathbf r(u_2,v_2). \]

Singular Parametrizations

A parametrization is singular where

\[ \boxed{\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v=\mathbf0}. \]

At such a point, the two parameter directions fail to span a regular tangent plane.

Example: Cone Vertex

For

\[ \mathbf r(u,v)= \langle v\cos u,v\sin u,v\rangle, \]

every value of \(u\) maps to the origin when \(v=0\). The angular parameter direction collapses, so the vertex is singular in this parametrization.

Generated Surfaces in Design

Common geometric-modeling constructions include:

  • extrusions;
  • revolutions;
  • sweeps;
  • lofts;
  • ruled blends;
  • offsets.

Ruled Surfaces and Structural Form

Straight generators can be useful in construction because globally curved forms can sometimes be assembled from straight members. Hyperbolic paraboloids and one-sheet hyperboloids are classic examples.

Minimal Surfaces and Structural Form

Minimal-surface geometry can suggest efficient membrane-like forms, but real structures must also satisfy material, loading, support, and stability constraints.

Common Error: Ruled Means Flat

False. A ruled surface merely contains straight generators. A helicoid and hyperbolic paraboloid are both ruled and nonplanar.

Common Error: Every Ruled Surface Is Developable

False. Cylinders and cones are developable, while the hyperbolic paraboloid is ruled but not developable.

Common Error: Minimal Means Globally Smallest Without Qualification

The condition \(H=0\) is local and differential. Global area-minimization statements require specified boundaries and admissible competitors.

Common Error: Confusing Mean and Gaussian Curvature

Mean curvature is

\[ H=\frac{k_1+k_2}{2}, \]

while Gaussian curvature is

\[ K=k_1k_2. \]

A minimal surface has \(H=0\), not necessarily \(K=0\).

Common Error: Assuming One Surface Has Only One Natural Construction

A cylinder can be viewed as a ruled surface, an extrusion of a circle, or a surface of revolution of a line. Different constructions reveal different properties.

Common Error: Ignoring Parameter Domains

A parametrization requires both formulas and a domain. Different parameter ranges can describe a patch, the entire surface, duplicate coverage, or repeated sweeps.

A Reliable Procedure for Analyzing a Generated Surface

  1. Identify the generating object.
  2. Identify the motion or construction rule.
  3. Choose parameters tied to that rule.
  4. Write the parametrization.
  5. Determine the parameter domain.
  6. Hold one parameter fixed and inspect the resulting curves.
  7. Compute \(\mathbf r_u\) and \(\mathbf r_v\) if needed.
  8. Check regularity using \(\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\).
  9. Look for rulings, symmetry, self-intersections, and singularities.
  10. Determine whether special properties such as developability or minimality apply.

Example: Full Ruled-Surface Analysis

Consider

\[ \mathbf r(u,v)=\langle u,v,uv\rangle. \]

Since \(x=u\), \(y=v\), and \(z=uv\),

\[ \boxed{z=xy}. \]

For fixed \(u=a\),

\[ \mathbf r(a,v)=\langle a,v,av\rangle, \]

which is a straight line. Therefore the surface is ruled.

Example: Full Helicoid Analysis

Consider

\[ \mathbf r(u,v)= \langle v\cos u,v\sin u,2u\rangle. \]

Holding \(u\) fixed gives a straight radial line. As \(u\) increases, that line rotates and rises. The surface is a

\[ \boxed{\text{helicoid}}, \]

which is both ruled and minimal.

What to Remember

  • Ruled surface: \[ \mathbf r(u,v)=\mathbf c(u)+v\mathbf d(u). \]
  • Cylinders, cones, helicoids, hyperbolic paraboloids, and one-sheet hyperboloids provide important ruled examples.
  • Some ruled surfaces are developable, but not all ruled surfaces are.
  • Minimal surface: \[ H=0. \]
  • Mean curvature: \[ H=\frac{k_1+k_2}{2}. \]
  • Gaussian curvature: \[ K=k_1k_2. \]
  • The plane, catenoid, and helicoid are classical minimal surfaces.
  • Generated surfaces include revolutions, extrusions, sweeps, translations, lofts, offsets, and ruled constructions.

The central habit is: identify what is moving, how it moves, and what geometric properties that generating rule forces onto the surface.
