Circular Coordinates

An angle can do more than measure a turn. Once a circle is placed on a coordinate plane, an angle can identify a specific point on that circle. Circular coordinates connect rotation, geometry, algebra, and trigonometry: the angle tells us where to look, while the coordinates tell us where the point is.

The Central Idea

Place a circle with center at the origin. Begin with a ray pointing along the positive horizontal axis and rotate it through an angle \(\theta\). The ray meets the circle at a point \(P\).

That single point can be described in two complementary ways:

  • by its angular position \(\theta\), and
  • by its Cartesian coordinates \((x,y)\).

For a circle of radius \(r\), the connection is

\[ \boxed{x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta}. \]

On the unit circle, where \(r=1\), this becomes the especially important relationship

\[ \boxed{P=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)}. \]

Putting a Circle on the Coordinate Plane

A circle centered at the origin with radius \(r\) consists of all points whose distance from the origin is \(r\).

By the distance formula,

\[ \sqrt{x^2+y^2}=r. \]

Squaring both sides gives the familiar coordinate equation

\[ \boxed{x^2+y^2=r^2}. \]

Every point produced by a circular coordinate must satisfy this equation. Conversely, every point satisfying the equation lies on the circle.

Standard Position

To use angles consistently as coordinates, we need a common starting direction. An angle is in standard position when

  • its vertex is at the origin, and
  • its initial side lies on the positive \(x\)-axis.

Counterclockwise rotation is positive and clockwise rotation is negative. The terminal side of the angle indicates the circular direction of the point.

This convention allows angles measured by different people or in different problems to refer to the same geometric locations.

Angle and Point on a Circle

The radius from the origin to \(P\) forms a right triangle with horizontal leg \(x\), vertical leg \(y\), and hypotenuse \(r\).

This triangle is the bridge between angular and Cartesian descriptions.

From Right Triangles to Circular Coordinates

For an acute angle in a right triangle,

\[ \cos\theta=\frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}} \]

and

\[ \sin\theta=\frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}}. \]

In the coordinate picture, the adjacent leg is \(x\), the opposite leg is \(y\), and the hypotenuse is \(r\). Therefore,

\[ \cos\theta=\frac{x}{r},\qquad \sin\theta=\frac{y}{r}. \]

Solving for the coordinates gives

\[ \boxed{x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta}. \]

The circle extends these relationships beyond acute angles and allows sine and cosine to describe every angular direction.

The Unit Circle

The unit circle is the circle centered at the origin with radius \(1\).

\[ \boxed{x^2+y^2=1}. \]

Because \(r=1\), the coordinate formulas simplify to

\[ \boxed{x=\cos\theta,\qquad y=\sin\theta}. \]

Therefore, the terminal point determined by \(\theta\) is

\[ \boxed{(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)}. \]

This interpretation is one of the most important ideas in elementary trigonometry: cosine is the horizontal coordinate on the unit circle, and sine is the vertical coordinate.

The Four Axis Points

The easiest unit-circle coordinates occur on the coordinate axes.

Angle Radians Unit-circle point
\(0^\circ\) \(0\) \((1,0)\)
\(90^\circ\) \(\frac{\pi}{2}\) \((0,1)\)
\(180^\circ\) \(\pi\) \((-1,0)\)
\(270^\circ\) \(\frac{3\pi}{2}\) \((0,-1)\)
\(360^\circ\) \(2\pi\) \((1,0)\)

The first and last rows describe the same point because one complete revolution returns to the starting direction.

Benchmark Coordinates in the First Quadrant

Three first-quadrant angles appear repeatedly:

Angle Radians \(\cos\theta\) \(\sin\theta\) Point
\(30^\circ\) \(\frac{\pi}{6}\) \(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\) \(\frac12\) \(\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2},\frac12\right)\)
\(45^\circ\) \(\frac{\pi}{4}\) \(\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\) \(\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\) \(\left(\frac{\sqrt2}{2},\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)\)
\(60^\circ\) \(\frac{\pi}{3}\) \(\frac12\) \(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\) \(\left(\frac12,\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)\)

These values come from the special \(30^\circ\)-\(60^\circ\)-\(90^\circ\) and \(45^\circ\)-\(45^\circ\)-\(90^\circ\) triangles.

Coordinates and Quadrants

The signs of \(x\) and \(y\) depend on the quadrant containing the terminal point.

Quadrant Sign of \(x\) Sign of \(y\) Sign of cosine Sign of sine
I Positive Positive Positive Positive
II Negative Positive Negative Positive
III Negative Negative Negative Negative
IV Positive Negative Positive Negative

Since cosine is the \(x\)-coordinate and sine is the \(y\)-coordinate, these sign patterns follow directly from ordinary coordinate geometry.

Reference Angles

A reference angle is the positive acute angle between the terminal side of an angle and the \(x\)-axis.

Reference angles allow us to reuse first-quadrant coordinate magnitudes in every quadrant. The magnitudes remain the same; only the signs change.

For example, \(150^\circ\) has reference angle

\[ 180^\circ-150^\circ=30^\circ. \]

The \(30^\circ\) unit-circle coordinates have magnitudes

\[ \left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2},\frac12\right). \]

Because \(150^\circ\) lies in Quadrant II, \(x\) is negative and \(y\) is positive. Therefore,

\[ \boxed{P=\left(-\frac{\sqrt3}{2},\frac12\right)}. \]

Example: A Point at \(225^\circ\)

Find the point on the unit circle corresponding to \(225^\circ\).

The angle lies in Quadrant III. Its reference angle is

\[ 225^\circ-180^\circ=45^\circ. \]

At \(45^\circ\), both coordinate magnitudes are \(\sqrt2/2\). In Quadrant III, both coordinates are negative.

\[ \boxed{P=\left(-\frac{\sqrt2}{2},-\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)}. \]

Example: A Point at \(\frac{11\pi}{6}\)

The angle

\[ \frac{11\pi}{6} \]

is equivalent to \(330^\circ\). It lies in Quadrant IV and has reference angle \(30^\circ\).

Therefore,

\[ \cos\left(\frac{11\pi}{6}\right)=\frac{\sqrt3}{2} \]

and

\[ \sin\left(\frac{11\pi}{6}\right)=-\frac12. \]

The circular coordinate is

\[ \boxed{\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2},-\frac12\right)}. \]

Coterminal Angles Locate the Same Point

Angles that differ by complete revolutions are coterminal. They have the same terminal side and therefore identify the same point on a circle.

In degrees,

\[ \theta,\quad \theta+360^\circ k \]

are coterminal for every integer \(k\).

In radians,

\[ \theta,\quad \theta+2\pi k \]

are coterminal.

Thus \(30^\circ\), \(390^\circ\), and \(-330^\circ\) all locate the same unit-circle point:

\[ \boxed{\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2},\frac12\right)}. \]

Why Sine and Cosine Repeat

One full revolution returns to the same point. Consequently, adding \(2\pi\) radians does not change the unit-circle coordinates.

\[ \boxed{\cos(\theta+2\pi)=\cos\theta} \]

and

\[ \boxed{\sin(\theta+2\pi)=\sin\theta}. \]

This is the geometric source of the periodicity of sine and cosine. A later lesson will develop cyclic and periodic relationships more fully.

Symmetry Across the Axes

Circular coordinates also reveal familiar coordinate symmetries.

Reflecting \((x,y)\) across the \(x\)-axis gives

\[ (x,-y). \]

Reflecting across the \(y\)-axis gives

\[ (-x,y). \]

Rotating halfway around the circle gives the opposite point

\[ (-x,-y). \]

These symmetries explain many relationships among sine and cosine values without requiring separate memorization.

Opposite Points on a Circle

If \(P=(x,y)\) lies on a circle centered at the origin, the point directly opposite \(P\) across the center is

\[ \boxed{(-x,-y)}. \]

The two points differ by a half-turn:

\[ \theta\longrightarrow\theta+\pi. \]

Therefore,

\[ \cos(\theta+\pi)=-\cos\theta, \qquad \sin(\theta+\pi)=-\sin\theta. \]

Coordinates on a Circle of Radius \(r\)

The unit circle is convenient, but the same angular description works for any radius.

For a circle centered at the origin with radius \(r\),

\[ \boxed{P=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)}. \]

The angle determines the direction, while the radius determines how far the point lies from the center.

If \(r\) doubles while \(\theta\) stays fixed, both coordinates double. The point moves outward along the same ray.

Example: Radius Other Than One

A point lies on a circle of radius \(8\) at angle \(60^\circ\). Find its coordinates.

Use

\[ x=8\cos60^\circ, \qquad y=8\sin60^\circ. \]

Since

\[ \cos60^\circ=\frac12, \qquad \sin60^\circ=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}, \]

we obtain

\[ x=4, \qquad y=4\sqrt3. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{P=(4,4\sqrt3)}. \]

Checking the Radius

Circular coordinates should satisfy the equation of the circle.

For the point

\[ (4,4\sqrt3) \]

on a circle of radius \(8\), check:

\[ 4^2+(4\sqrt3)^2=16+48=64=8^2. \]

The coordinate pair is consistent with the radius.

A Circle with a Different Center

Suppose the circle has center \((h,k)\) and radius \(r\). The angular displacement from the center is still

\[ (r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta). \]

Add the center coordinates to translate the circle into position:

\[ \boxed{x=h+r\cos\theta,\qquad y=k+r\sin\theta}. \]

Equivalently,

\[ \boxed{P=(h+r\cos\theta,\ k+r\sin\theta)}. \]

This formula combines rotation around a center with translation of that center through the plane.

Example: Shifted Circle

A circle has center \((3,-2)\), radius \(5\), and angular coordinate \(180^\circ\).

Since

\[ \cos180^\circ=-1, \qquad \sin180^\circ=0, \]

the point is

\[ (3+5(-1),-2+5(0)). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{P=(-2,-2)}. \]

Recovering Circular Information from Coordinates

If the point \((x,y)\) and the radius \(r\) are known, then

\[ \boxed{\cos\theta=\frac{x}{r},\qquad \sin\theta=\frac{y}{r}}. \]

These ratios tell us the horizontal and vertical components of the angular direction.

The signs of \(x\) and \(y\) identify the quadrant. Known benchmark ratios may then reveal the angle directly.

Example: Recovering an Angle from a Point

The point

\[ P=(-3,3\sqrt3) \]

lies on a circle centered at the origin. Find its radius and a standard positive angle locating it.

First find the radius:

\[ r=\sqrt{(-3)^2+(3\sqrt3)^2} =\sqrt{9+27} =6. \]

Then

\[ \cos\theta=\frac{-3}{6}=-\frac12, \qquad \sin\theta=\frac{3\sqrt3}{6}=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}. \]

The point lies in Quadrant II and has reference angle \(60^\circ\). Thus,

\[ \boxed{\theta=120^\circ=\frac{2\pi}{3}}. \]

Angular Coordinate and Arc Distance

Radians provide a direct connection between angular position and distance traveled along the circle.

If \(\theta\) is measured in radians, then an arc from the starting point to the terminal point has signed length

\[ \boxed{s=r\theta}. \]

Rearranging gives

\[ \boxed{\theta=\frac{s}{r}}. \]

Thus an angular coordinate may also be interpreted as arc distance measured in units of the radius.

Example: A Point on a Rotating Wheel

A wheel has radius \(10\) cm. A marked point begins at \((10,0)\) relative to the center and rotates counterclockwise through \(\frac{2\pi}{3}\) radians.

Its new coordinates are

\[ \left(10\cos\frac{2\pi}{3}, 10\sin\frac{2\pi}{3}\right). \]

Since

\[ \cos\frac{2\pi}{3}=-\frac12, \qquad \sin\frac{2\pi}{3}=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}, \]

the point becomes

\[ \boxed{(-5,5\sqrt3)}. \]

Its arc distance traveled is

\[ s=10\left(\frac{2\pi}{3}\right) =\boxed{\frac{20\pi}{3}\text{ cm}}. \]

Angular Coordinates Are Not Unique

Cartesian coordinates identify a point in the plane uniquely. Angular coordinates on a circle behave differently because rotations can repeat.

The point \((1,0)\) on the unit circle can be represented by

\[ 0,\quad 2\pi,\quad -2\pi,\quad 4\pi,\quad \ldots \]

More generally, if one angular coordinate is \(\theta\), then all angular coordinates for the same point are

\[ \boxed{\theta+2\pi k,\qquad k\in\mathbb Z}. \]

Cartesian and Circular Descriptions Serve Different Purposes

Cartesian coordinates emphasize horizontal and vertical displacement. Circular coordinates emphasize distance from a center and direction around that center.

For motion around a circle, angular description is often more natural. For intersections, graphing, and ordinary planar measurement, Cartesian coordinates may be more convenient.

Mathematics frequently becomes easier when a problem is represented in the coordinate system that matches its geometry.

Connection to Polar Coordinates

The pair

\[ (r,\theta) \]

describes a point by its distance \(r\) from the origin and its angular direction \(\theta\). This is the basic idea of polar coordinates.

Circular coordinates are the fixed-radius case: if \(r\) is constant, then changing \(\theta\) moves a point around one circle.

Allowing both \(r\) and \(\theta\) to vary produces a coordinate system for the entire plane rather than for a single circle.

Common Error: Reversing Sine and Cosine

On a circle centered at the origin,

\[ \boxed{x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta}. \]

Cosine is the horizontal coordinate; sine is the vertical coordinate.

A useful check is \(\theta=0\). The point must lie on the positive \(x\)-axis:

\[ (r\cos0,r\sin0)=(r,0). \]

Common Error: Ignoring the Quadrant

A reference angle determines coordinate magnitudes, not signs.

For example, \(150^\circ\) and \(30^\circ\) share the same reference angle, but they do not have the same point:

\[ 30^\circ:\quad \left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2},\frac12\right), \] \[ 150^\circ:\quad \left(-\frac{\sqrt3}{2},\frac12\right). \]

Common Error: Forgetting the Radius

The pair \((\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\) applies directly only to the unit circle.

On a circle of radius \(r\), multiply both coordinates by \(r\):

\[ \boxed{(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)}. \]

On a circle of radius \(7\), the point at \(90^\circ\) is \((0,7)\), not \((0,1)\).

Common Error: Forgetting the Center

The formula

\[ (r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta) \]

assumes the circle is centered at the origin.

If the center is \((h,k)\), use

\[ \boxed{(h+r\cos\theta,k+r\sin\theta)}. \]

Common Error: Treating Coterminal Angles as Different Points

Angles such as \(45^\circ\), \(405^\circ\), and \(-315^\circ\) describe different amounts or directions of rotation, but their terminal sides are identical.

Therefore, they locate the same point on any circle centered at the vertex.

Common Error: Mixing Degrees and Radians

The formulas for circular coordinates work with either degrees or radians provided the calculator or computational system interprets the angle in the intended unit.

For example,

\[ 60^\circ=\frac{\pi}{3}\text{ radians}. \]

Entering \(60\) while a calculator is in radian mode does not represent a \(60^\circ\) angle.

A Reliable Procedure for Finding Circular Coordinates

  1. Identify the center and radius of the circle.
  2. Place or interpret the angle in standard position.
  3. Determine the terminal quadrant or axis.
  4. Find a reference angle when useful.
  5. Determine the corresponding cosine and sine values.
  6. Apply the correct signs from the quadrant.
  7. Multiply by the radius.
  8. Translate by the center coordinates if the center is not the origin.
  9. Check that the point satisfies the equation of the circle.

A Reliable Procedure for Recovering an Angle

  1. Determine the circle's center.
  2. Translate the point relative to that center if necessary.
  3. Find the radius using the distance formula.
  4. Compute \(x/r\) and \(y/r\).
  5. Use the signs to identify the quadrant.
  6. Recognize a benchmark reference angle when possible.
  7. State the requested angular coordinate.
  8. Add \(360^\circ k\) or \(2\pi k\) if all coterminal coordinates are requested.

Example: Full Circular-Coordinate Workflow

A circle has center \((-2,4)\), radius \(6\), and angular coordinate \(\frac{7\pi}{6}\). Find the point.

The angle \(\frac{7\pi}{6}=210^\circ\) lies in Quadrant III with reference angle \(30^\circ\). Therefore,

\[ \cos\frac{7\pi}{6}=-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}, \qquad \sin\frac{7\pi}{6}=-\frac12. \]

Multiply by the radius:

\[ 6\cos\frac{7\pi}{6}=-3\sqrt3, \qquad 6\sin\frac{7\pi}{6}=-3. \]

Translate by the center:

\[ x=-2-3\sqrt3, \qquad y=4-3=1. \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{P=(-2-3\sqrt3,1)}. \]

\[ \boxed{\left(-\frac12,\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)}. \]

\[ 420^\circ-360^\circ=60^\circ. \]

while

What to Remember

Circular coordinates connect angular position to ordinary coordinates.

On the unit circle,

\[ \boxed{P=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)}. \]

On a circle of radius \(r\) centered at the origin,

\[ \boxed{P=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)}. \]

On a circle centered at \((h,k)\),

\[ \boxed{P=(h+r\cos\theta,k+r\sin\theta)}. \]
  • cosine gives the horizontal component;
  • sine gives the vertical component;
  • quadrants determine signs;
  • reference angles determine familiar coordinate magnitudes;
  • coterminal angles locate the same point;
  • a full turn creates the repeating behavior of circular coordinates.

The central geometric habit is to move freely between the angle describing a direction and the coordinate pair describing the corresponding point.
