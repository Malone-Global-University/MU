Rotations and Angular Displacement

A rotation describes turning around a fixed center. Angular displacement tells us not merely how far an object turns, but also in which direction it turns. By treating angles as directed quantities, we can describe clockwise and counterclockwise motion, repeated revolutions, final orientation, and the geometry of objects moving around circles.

The Central Idea

A circle gives us a natural way to measure turning. Choose a center, choose a starting direction, and rotate a ray or object around that center. The amount and direction of the turn can be represented by an angle.

Counterclockwise rotation is usually assigned a positive angle, while clockwise rotation is assigned a negative angle. This sign convention turns ordinary angle measure into a language for directed change.

The central relationship is simple:

\[ \boxed{\text{angular displacement}=\text{final angular position}-\text{initial angular position}} \]

When repeated turns are involved, however, the path matters. Two motions can end in the same orientation while having very different angular displacements.

What Is a Rotation?

A rotation is a transformation that turns a figure around a fixed point called the center of rotation.

If a point is rotated around a center, its distance from that center does not change. A point that begins at distance \(r\) from the center remains at distance \(r\) throughout the rotation.

Because the distance remains fixed, the path of the point lies on a circle centered at the center of rotation.

A rotation is a rigid motion: it preserves lengths and angle measures. The figure changes orientation or position, but it does not change size or shape.

Initial and Terminal Positions

A rotation begins in an initial position and ends in a terminal position.

For a rotating ray, the initial ray is called the initial side of the angle, and the final ray is called the terminal side.

The angle between them is not enough by itself to describe the motion. We must also specify the direction of rotation and, when necessary, the number of complete turns made along the way.

Positive and Negative Rotation

The usual sign convention is

\[ \boxed{\text{counterclockwise}=+} \]

and

\[ \boxed{\text{clockwise}=-}. \]

Thus a counterclockwise quarter-turn is \(+90^\circ\), while a clockwise quarter-turn is \(-90^\circ\).

A Full Revolution

One complete revolution returns a rotating ray to the same direction from which it started.

\[ \boxed{1\text{ revolution}=360^\circ=2\pi\text{ radians}}. \]

A half-turn is

\[ 180^\circ=\pi\text{ radians}, \]

and a quarter-turn is

\[ 90^\circ=\frac{\pi}{2}\text{ radians}. \]

These relationships let us move freely among revolutions, degrees, and radians.

Angular Position

An angular position describes the orientation of a ray or rotating object relative to a chosen reference direction.

A common reference direction is a horizontal ray pointing to the right. If that ray is assigned angular position \(0\), then a counterclockwise quarter-turn has position \(90^\circ\), a half-turn has position \(180^\circ\), and so on.

Angular position tells us where the object is oriented. It does not by itself tell us how the object arrived there.

Angular Displacement

Angular displacement is the directed change in angular position.

If the initial angular position is \(\theta_i\) and the final angular position is \(\theta_f\), then for a specified motion without hidden extra revolutions,

\[ \boxed{\Delta\theta=\theta_f-\theta_i}. \]

The symbol \(\Delta\) means "change in," so \(\Delta\theta\) is read as "change in theta."

A positive value indicates net counterclockwise displacement. A negative value indicates net clockwise displacement.

Example: Finding Angular Displacement

A pointer begins at \(35^\circ\) and rotates counterclockwise until its angular position is \(125^\circ\). Find its angular displacement.

\[ \Delta\theta=\theta_f-\theta_i. \]

Substitute:

\[ \Delta\theta=125^\circ-35^\circ. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\Delta\theta=90^\circ}. \]

The positive sign agrees with the stated counterclockwise rotation.

Example: Clockwise Angular Displacement

A dial begins at \(140^\circ\) and rotates clockwise to \(50^\circ\), without making a complete revolution.

\[ \Delta\theta=50^\circ-140^\circ=-90^\circ. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\Delta\theta=-90^\circ}. \]

The negative sign records the clockwise direction.

Position and Displacement Are Different

Angular position describes orientation. Angular displacement describes a directed change in orientation.

Suppose a pointer begins at \(0^\circ\) and rotates counterclockwise through \(450^\circ\). Its angular displacement is

\[ \boxed{450^\circ}, \]

but its terminal side has the same orientation as \(90^\circ\), because

\[ 450^\circ=360^\circ+90^\circ. \]

The terminal orientation does not preserve the full history of the motion.

Coterminal Angles

Angles that have the same initial side and the same terminal side are called coterminal angles.

In degrees, adding or subtracting a complete revolution produces another coterminal angle:

\[ \boxed{\theta+360^\circ k},\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}. \]

In radians, the corresponding family is

\[ \boxed{\theta+2\pi k},\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}. \]

For example,

\[ 30^\circ,\quad390^\circ,\quad-330^\circ \]

all have the same terminal side.

Why Coterminal Angles Matter

Coterminal angles separate two ideas that are easy to confuse.

  • The terminal orientation repeats after each complete revolution.
  • The angular displacement can continue increasing or decreasing without bound.

A rotating wheel can make ten complete turns and end with the same visual orientation it had initially. Its orientation has repeated, but its angular displacement has not vanished.

Multiple Revolutions

Rotations are not limited to one trip around a circle. An angle greater than \(360^\circ\) or \(2\pi\) radians represents more than one counterclockwise revolution. An angle less than \(-360^\circ\) or \(-2\pi\) radians represents more than one clockwise revolution.

For example,

\[ 810^\circ=2(360^\circ)+90^\circ. \]

This represents two full counterclockwise revolutions followed by another quarter-turn.

Its terminal side is coterminal with \(90^\circ\).

Example: Decomposing a Large Rotation

Describe the rotation \(-840^\circ\) in terms of complete revolutions and a remaining rotation.

Two clockwise revolutions account for

\[ -2(360^\circ)=-720^\circ. \]

The remaining rotation is

\[ -840^\circ-(-720^\circ)=-120^\circ. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{-840^\circ=-720^\circ-120^\circ}. \]

The motion consists of two full clockwise turns followed by another \(120^\circ\) clockwise turn.

Finding a Coterminal Angle in One Revolution

It is often useful to replace a large positive or negative angle with a coterminal angle in the interval

\[ 0^\circ\leq\theta<360^\circ. \]

For \(810^\circ\), subtract complete revolutions:

\[ 810^\circ-720^\circ=90^\circ. \]

Thus \(810^\circ\) is coterminal with

\[ \boxed{90^\circ}. \]

For \(-100^\circ\), add one full revolution:

\[ -100^\circ+360^\circ=260^\circ. \]

Therefore, \(-100^\circ\) is coterminal with

\[ \boxed{260^\circ}. \]

Rotations in Radians

The same ideas work naturally in radians. A complete counterclockwise turn is \(+2\pi\), and a complete clockwise turn is \(-2\pi\).

For example,

\[ \frac{13\pi}{3} \]

can be separated into complete revolutions by writing

\[ \frac{13\pi}{3}=\frac{12\pi}{3}+\frac{\pi}{3}=4\pi+\frac{\pi}{3}. \]

Since \(4\pi\) is two complete revolutions, the terminal side is coterminal with

\[ \boxed{\frac{\pi}{3}}. \]

Revolutions, Degrees, and Radians

The three common ways to express rotation are connected by

\[ 1\text{ revolution}=360^\circ=2\pi\text{ radians}. \]

Therefore,

\[ n\text{ revolutions}=360n^\circ=2\pi n\text{ radians}. \]

For instance, \(2.5\) counterclockwise revolutions equal

\[ 2.5(360^\circ)=900^\circ \]

or

\[ 2.5(2\pi)=5\pi\text{ radians}. \]

Example: Converting Revolutions

Convert \(-1.75\) revolutions to degrees and radians.

In degrees,

\[ -1.75(360^\circ)=\boxed{-630^\circ}. \]

In radians,

\[ -1.75(2\pi)=-3.5\pi=\boxed{-\frac{7\pi}{2}}. \]

Both negative results indicate clockwise rotation.

Rotation Preserves Radius

If \(OP=r\), then after any rotation about \(O\),

\[ \boxed{OP'=r}. \]

This fixed radius is why circular motion can be described with one changing angular quantity while the radial distance remains constant.

The Arc Traced by a Rotation

When a point at radius \(r\) rotates through an angle \(\theta\), it traces an arc along its circular path.

If \(\theta\) is measured in radians, the magnitude of the corresponding arc length is

\[ \boxed{s=r|\theta|}. \]

The absolute value appears because ordinary arc length is a nonnegative distance. If we instead use a directed arc displacement, we may keep the sign and write

\[ \boxed{\Delta s=r\Delta\theta}. \]

Thus the sign can record the chosen direction while the magnitude records how far along the circle the point moves.

Example: Linear Distance from Angular Displacement

A point lies \(8\text{ cm}\) from the center of rotation and turns through \(3\pi/4\) radians. Find the distance traveled along its circular path.

\[ s=r|\theta|. \]

Substitute:

\[ s=8\left|\frac{3\pi}{4}\right|. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{s=6\pi\text{ cm}}. \]

Same Angle, Different Linear Distance

Two points on the same rotating object can have the same angular displacement while traveling different linear distances.

Suppose two points rotate through the same angle \(\theta\), but their radii are \(r_1\) and \(r_2\). Their arc lengths are

\[ s_1=r_1|\theta| \]

and

\[ s_2=r_2|\theta|. \]

The point farther from the center travels farther. Angular displacement is shared across the rigid rotation; linear displacement along the circular path depends on radius.

Composition of Rotations About the Same Center

Consecutive rotations about the same center combine by adding their directed angles.

If the first rotation is \(\alpha\) and the second is \(\beta\), then the combined rotation is

\[ \boxed{\alpha+\beta}. \]

For example, a \(+120^\circ\) rotation followed by a \(-45^\circ\) rotation produces the net rotation

\[ 120^\circ-45^\circ=\boxed{75^\circ}. \]

Because the center is the same, the rotations combine directly through angle addition.

Example: Several Successive Rotations

A pointer undergoes three rotations:

\[ +150^\circ,\qquad-80^\circ,\qquad+35^\circ. \]

Add the signed angles:

\[ 150^\circ-80^\circ+35^\circ=105^\circ. \]

The net angular displacement is

\[ \boxed{105^\circ}. \]

The positive result means the net rotation is counterclockwise.

Inverse Rotations

Every rotation can be undone by rotating through the opposite angle about the same center.

The inverse of a rotation through \(\theta\) is a rotation through

\[ \boxed{-\theta}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \theta+(-\theta)=0. \]

A \(+65^\circ\) rotation is undone by \(-65^\circ\). A clockwise rotation of \(-\pi/3\) is undone by a counterclockwise rotation of \(+\pi/3\).

Rotation by Zero and by a Full Turn

A rotation by \(0^\circ\) leaves every point unchanged.

A full \(360^\circ\) rotation also returns every point to its original location, even though a complete turn has occurred.

More generally, any rotation by an integer number of full turns,

\[ 360^\circ k \]

or

\[ 2\pi k, \]

returns a figure to the same orientation and position, where \(k\in\mathbb{Z}\).

This repeating behavior will later become part of the study of cyclic and periodic relationships.

Angular Distance Versus Angular Displacement

Directed displacement and total amount of turning are not always the same.

Suppose a pointer rotates \(+120^\circ\) and then \(-50^\circ\). Its net angular displacement is

\[ 120^\circ-50^\circ=70^\circ. \]

But the total amount of angular travel is

\[ 120^\circ+50^\circ=170^\circ. \]

The first quantity records the net change. The second records how much turning occurred along the entire route.

Example: Net Change and Total Turning

A control knob rotates \(-210^\circ\), then \(+90^\circ\), then \(-40^\circ\).

The net angular displacement is

\[ -210^\circ+90^\circ-40^\circ=\boxed{-160^\circ}. \]

The total angular distance traveled is

\[ 210^\circ+90^\circ+40^\circ=\boxed{340^\circ}. \]

Net displacement retains direction; total travel does not cancel opposite motions.

Rotation as a Geometric Transformation

A rotation acts on an entire figure, not merely on one point. Every point of the figure turns through the same directed angle about the same center.

If triangle \(ABC\) is rotated to triangle \(A'B'C'\), then corresponding lengths and angles are preserved:

\[ AB=A'B',\qquad BC=B'C',\qquad CA=C'A' \]

and corresponding angle measures are equal.

For this reason, the original figure and its rotated image are congruent.

The Center Is Fixed

The center of rotation is the one point that does not move under the rotation. If \(O\) is the center, then

\[ O\mapsto O. \]

Every other point moves along a circle centered at \(O\), unless the rotation is a whole number of complete turns and returns the point to its starting place.

Identifying the center is therefore fundamental to describing a rotation precisely.

Angular Displacement Does Not Require a Particular Radius

Angular displacement measures turning, not linear distance. A rotation of \(60^\circ\) is still a \(60^\circ\) rotation whether the moving point is \(1\text{ cm}\) or \(100\text{ cm}\) from the center.

Radius becomes necessary only when we want to convert that angular change into a linear distance along the circular path.

This distinction is one reason angular measure is so powerful: one angle can describe the common rotational change of an entire rigid object.

Common Error: Ignoring Direction

A clockwise rotation of \(70^\circ\) is not written \(+70^\circ\) under the standard convention. It is

\[ \boxed{-70^\circ}. \]

The sign carries geometric information. Dropping it can reverse the intended motion.

Common Error: Confusing a Terminal Angle with the Actual Rotation

A rotation of \(450^\circ\) ends in the same orientation as \(90^\circ\), but the two rotations are not the same motion.

\[ 450^\circ=360^\circ+90^\circ. \]

The extra revolution matters when the problem asks for angular displacement, number of turns, or distance traveled.

Common Error: Subtracting Positions Without Considering the Described Path

Suppose a pointer begins at \(20^\circ\) and the problem states that it makes one full counterclockwise revolution and then stops at \(80^\circ\).

Simply calculating

\[ 80^\circ-20^\circ=60^\circ \]

loses the complete revolution. The actual angular displacement is

\[ 360^\circ+60^\circ=\boxed{420^\circ}. \]

Endpoint subtraction works only when the stated angular positions already encode the intended continuous motion or when no additional turns occur.

Common Error: Using Degrees in the Arc-Length Radian Formula

The compact relationship

\[ s=r\theta \]

requires \(\theta\) to be measured in radians.

If a radius of \(5\text{ cm}\) rotates through \(60^\circ\), first convert:

\[ 60^\circ=\frac{\pi}{3}. \]

Then

\[ s=5\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right)=\boxed{\frac{5\pi}{3}\text{ cm}}. \]

Common Error: Treating Arc Length as Signed Distance

If a point rotates clockwise through \(-2\) radians at radius \(6\text{ m}\), its directed arc displacement may be written

\[ \Delta s=6(-2)=-12\text{ m}. \]

But the distance traveled is

\[ \boxed{12\text{ m}}. \]

Direction can be represented with a sign; physical distance along the path is nonnegative.

A Reliable Procedure for Rotation Problems

  1. Identify the center of rotation.
  2. Identify the initial and terminal positions.
  3. Determine whether the rotation is clockwise or counterclockwise.
  4. Assign the correct sign to the angular change.
  5. Account for any complete revolutions explicitly stated.
  6. Use \(360^\circ\) or \(2\pi\) to simplify coterminal angles when useful.
  7. Add signed angles when several rotations about the same center are composed.
  8. If linear distance is needed, convert the angle to radians and use \(s=r|\theta|\).
  9. Check whether the question asks for final orientation, net displacement, or total turning.

Example: Full Rotation Workflow

A point begins at angular position \(30^\circ\). It rotates one full counterclockwise revolution, then another \(135^\circ\) counterclockwise. Find the angular displacement and a coterminal terminal position between \(0^\circ\) and \(360^\circ\).

The total angular displacement is

\[ \Delta\theta=360^\circ+135^\circ=\boxed{495^\circ}. \]

Add this to the starting position:

\[ 30^\circ+495^\circ=525^\circ. \]

Subtract one complete revolution:

\[ 525^\circ-360^\circ=165^\circ. \]

Therefore, the terminal orientation is coterminal with

\[ \boxed{165^\circ}. \]

The two answers describe different features of the motion: \(495^\circ\) is the angular displacement, while \(165^\circ\) identifies the terminal orientation within one revolution.

What to Remember

  • A rotation turns a figure around a fixed center.
  • Rotations preserve distances and angle measures.
  • Counterclockwise rotation is positive; clockwise rotation is negative.
  • One revolution is \(360^\circ=2\pi\) radians.
  • Angular position describes orientation; angular displacement describes directed change.
  • For a specified motion without extra revolutions, \(\Delta\theta=\theta_f-\theta_i\).
  • Coterminal angles differ by \(360^\circ k\) or \(2\pi k\).
  • Several rotations about the same center combine by adding their signed angles.
  • The inverse of a rotation through \(\theta\) is a rotation through \(-\theta\).
  • For radians, circular distance traveled is \(s=r|\theta|\).
  • Final orientation, net angular displacement, and total turning are different quantities.

The most important distinction is this:

\[ \boxed{\text{where you finish is not always enough to tell how you rotated there}.} \]
