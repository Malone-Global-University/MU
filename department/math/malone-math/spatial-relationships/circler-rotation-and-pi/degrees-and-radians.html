Degrees and Radians

Angles describe how much one direction turns toward another. Degrees divide a complete revolution into 360 equal parts. Radians measure the same turning in a way built directly from the geometry of a circle. Because radians connect angle measure with radius and arc length, they become the natural angular unit for trigonometry, calculus, physics, and higher mathematics.

The Central Idea

Degrees and radians are two systems for measuring the same geometric quantity: angular displacement.

One complete revolution can be written as

\[ \boxed{360^\circ=2\pi\text{ radians}}. \]

Half a revolution can therefore be written as

\[ \boxed{180^\circ=\pi\text{ radians}}. \]

These equalities provide the bridge between the two systems.

What Does an Angle Measure?

An angle measures the amount of turn from one ray to another around a common endpoint called the vertex.

The physical lengths of the rays do not determine the angle. Extending either ray farther does not change the amount of turning between them.

This is why angular measure is fundamentally different from ordinary linear distance.

A Full Revolution

Imagine a ray rotating around its endpoint until it returns to its starting direction. That motion makes one full revolution, also called one full turn.

In degrees,

\[ \boxed{1\text{ full revolution}=360^\circ}. \]

In radians,

\[ \boxed{1\text{ full revolution}=2\pi\text{ radians}}. \]

Common Fractions of a Revolution

Familiar angles can be understood as fractions of a complete turn.

Full turn \(360^\circ\) \(2\pi\)
Half turn \(180^\circ\) \(\pi\)
Quarter turn \(90^\circ\) \(\pi/2\)
Eighth turn \(45^\circ\) \(\pi/4\)

Seeing One Complete Turn

What Is a Degree?

A degree is one of 360 equal parts of a complete revolution. Therefore,

\[ \boxed{1^\circ=\frac{1}{360}\text{ of a full turn}}. \]

The degree symbol \(^\circ\) must be written with the numerical measure. For example,

\[ 30^\circ,\qquad90^\circ,\qquad225^\circ. \]

Why 360 Degrees?

The division of a circle into 360 degrees is a historical convention rather than a geometric necessity. The number 360 is highly divisible, so it can be partitioned evenly into many useful fractions, including halves, thirds, fourths, fifths, sixths, eighths, ninths, tenths, and twelfths.

Mathematics could use a different number of equal parts for a full turn, but the 360-degree system became standard and remains widely used in geometry, navigation, surveying, and everyday measurement.

What Is a Radian?

A radian is defined using a circle's radius and an arc on that circle.

One radian is the measure of a central angle that intercepts an arc whose length is equal to the radius of the circle.

If an arc has length \(s\) in a circle of radius \(r\), then the central angle \(\theta\), measured in radians, satisfies

\[ \boxed{\theta=\frac{s}{r}}. \]

When \(s=r\),

\[ \theta=\frac{r}{r}=1. \]

The angle therefore measures exactly one radian.

Seeing One Radian

Radians Are Ratios

The radian measure

\[ \theta=\frac{s}{r} \]

compares one length with another length. If both are measured in the same unit, those units cancel.

For example, if

\[ s=12\text{ cm} \]

and

\[ r=8\text{ cm}, \]

then

\[ \theta=\frac{12\text{ cm}}{8\text{ cm}}=\frac{3}{2}. \]

The angle is \(3/2\) radians.

Why a Full Turn Is \(2\pi\) Radians

A complete revolution intercepts the entire circumference of the circle. The circumference is

\[ C=2\pi r. \]

Since radian measure is arc length divided by radius,

\[ \theta=\frac{C}{r}. \]

Substitute \(C=2\pi r\):

\[ \theta=\frac{2\pi r}{r}=2\pi. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{1\text{ full turn}=2\pi\text{ radians}}. \]

Why \(180^\circ=\pi\) Radians

A half turn is half of a complete revolution. Half of \(360^\circ\) is

\[ 180^\circ. \]

Half of \(2\pi\) radians is

\[ \pi\text{ radians}. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{180^\circ=\pi\text{ radians}}. \]

This equality is the fundamental conversion relationship between degrees and radians.

The Two Conversion Factors

From

\[ 180^\circ=\pi\text{ radians}, \]

divide both sides by \(180^\circ\):

\[ \boxed{1^\circ=\frac{\pi}{180}\text{ radians}}. \]

Or divide both sides by \(\pi\):

\[ \boxed{1\text{ radian}=\frac{180^\circ}{\pi}}. \]

These lead to the conversion factors

\[ \boxed{\frac{\pi\text{ rad}}{180^\circ}} \]

and

\[ \boxed{\frac{180^\circ}{\pi\text{ rad}}}. \]

Converting Degrees to Radians

To convert degrees to radians, multiply by

\[ \frac{\pi}{180}. \]

In formula form,

\[ \boxed{\theta_{\text{rad}}=\theta_{\text{deg}}\frac{\pi}{180}}. \]

The degree units cancel, leaving a radian measure.

Example: Convert \(60^\circ\) to Radians

Multiply by the degree-to-radian conversion factor:

\[ 60^\circ\left(\frac{\pi\text{ rad}}{180^\circ}\right). \]

Cancel the degree units and simplify:

\[ \frac{60\pi}{180}=\frac{\pi}{3}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{60^\circ=\frac{\pi}{3}\text{ radians}}. \]

Example: Convert \(225^\circ\) to Radians

\[ 225^\circ\left(\frac{\pi}{180^\circ}\right) =\frac{225\pi}{180}. \]

Divide numerator and denominator by \(45\):

\[ \frac{225\pi}{180}=\frac{5\pi}{4}. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{225^\circ=\frac{5\pi}{4}\text{ radians}}. \]

Converting Radians to Degrees

To convert radians to degrees, multiply by

\[ \frac{180}{\pi}. \]

In formula form,

\[ \boxed{\theta_{\text{deg}}=\theta_{\text{rad}}\frac{180}{\pi}}. \]

Example: Convert \(\frac{2\pi}{3}\) to Degrees

\[ \frac{2\pi}{3}\left(\frac{180^\circ}{\pi}\right). \]

Cancel \(\pi\):

\[ \frac{2(180^\circ)}{3}=120^\circ. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\frac{2\pi}{3}\text{ radians}=120^\circ}. \]

Example: Convert \(\frac{7\pi}{6}\) to Degrees

\[ \frac{7\pi}{6}\left(\frac{180^\circ}{\pi}\right) =\frac{7(180^\circ)}{6}. \]

Since \(180/6=30\),

\[ 7(30^\circ)=210^\circ. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{\frac{7\pi}{6}\text{ radians}=210^\circ}. \]

Recognizing the Pattern in the Denominators

The familiar first-quadrant angles can be connected directly to fractions of \(180^\circ\):

\[ 30^\circ=\frac{180^\circ}{6}, \] \[ 45^\circ=\frac{180^\circ}{4}, \] \[ 60^\circ=\frac{180^\circ}{3}, \] \[ 90^\circ=\frac{180^\circ}{2}. \]

Replace \(180^\circ\) by \(\pi\) radians and the corresponding radian measures appear immediately:

\[ \frac{\pi}{6},\qquad\frac{\pi}{4},\qquad\frac{\pi}{3},\qquad\frac{\pi}{2}. \]

Radians Usually Omit the Unit Label

Degree measures require the degree symbol. Radian measures are often written without the word “radians” or the abbreviation “rad.”

Thus,

\[ \frac{\pi}{3} \]

ordinarily means \(\pi/3\) radians when the context is angular measure.

By contrast,

\[ 60^\circ \]

must retain the degree symbol.

One Radian in Degrees

Since

\[ 1\text{ radian}=\frac{180^\circ}{\pi}, \]

and \(\pi\approx3.14159\),

\[ \frac{180^\circ}{\pi}\approx57.2958^\circ. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{1\text{ radian}\approx57.3^\circ}. \]

A radian is therefore noticeably larger than \(45^\circ\) but smaller than \(60^\circ\).

Exact Values Versus Decimal Approximations

When an angle has a simple exact radian measure involving \(\pi\), it is usually best to keep that form.

For example,

\[ 135^\circ=\frac{3\pi}{4} \]

is exact, while

\[ \frac{3\pi}{4}\approx2.35619 \]

is approximate.

Exact radian forms preserve the geometric relationship to \(\pi\) and are generally preferable in symbolic mathematics.

Decimal Radians Are Still Radians

Not every radian measure contains \(\pi\). An angle can be given directly as

\[ 1.2\text{ radians} \]

or simply

\[ 1.2. \]

To convert \(1.2\) radians to degrees,

\[ 1.2\left(\frac{180^\circ}{\pi}\right) \approx68.75^\circ. \]

Angles Can Exceed One Full Revolution

Angular measure is not restricted to values between \(0^\circ\) and \(360^\circ\), or between \(0\) and \(2\pi\).

For example,

\[ 450^\circ=360^\circ+90^\circ. \]

Converting to radians,

\[ 450^\circ\left(\frac{\pi}{180^\circ}\right) =\frac{5\pi}{2}. \]

The measure \(5\pi/2\) therefore represents one complete revolution plus an additional quarter turn.

Negative Angular Measures

Angles may also be assigned negative measures when a direction of rotation is specified. Under the standard convention, counterclockwise rotation is positive and clockwise rotation is negative.

For example,

\[ -90^\circ=-\frac{\pi}{2}. \]

The next lessons will use this idea more extensively when studying rotation and angular displacement.

Coterminal Angles

Two directed angles are coterminal when they end in the same direction after differing by one or more complete revolutions.

In degrees, coterminal angles differ by integer multiples of \(360^\circ\):

\[ \boxed{\theta+360^\circ k},\qquad k\in\mathbb Z. \]

In radians, they differ by integer multiples of \(2\pi\):

\[ \boxed{\theta+2\pi k},\qquad k\in\mathbb Z. \]

Example: Finding Coterminal Measures

The angle \(45^\circ\) is coterminal with

\[ 45^\circ+360^\circ=405^\circ \]

and

\[ 45^\circ-360^\circ=-315^\circ. \]

In radians,

\[ \frac{\pi}{4}+2\pi=\frac{9\pi}{4} \]

and

\[ \frac{\pi}{4}-2\pi=-\frac{7\pi}{4}. \]

All four measures describe rotations ending in the same direction.

Degrees and Radians Measure the Same Angle

Converting units does not change the geometric angle. It changes only the numerical language used to describe it.

The statements

\[ 90^\circ, \]

and

\[ \frac{\pi}{2}\text{ radians} \]

describe exactly the same quarter-turn angle.

This is analogous to describing the same length as \(1\) meter or \(100\) centimeters.

Why Radians Become the Natural Unit

Degrees are convenient for many geometric and practical purposes, but radians connect angle measure directly to the circle's own geometry.

The relationship

\[ \theta=\frac{s}{r} \]

contains no arbitrary division into 360 parts. It compares the intercepted arc directly with the radius.

This direct relationship is why formulas involving circular motion, trigonometric functions, derivatives, and periodic phenomena become especially simple when angles are measured in radians.

Scale Does Not Change Radian Measure

Suppose a central angle intercepts an arc of length \(s\) in a circle of radius \(r\). Its radian measure is

\[ \theta=\frac{s}{r}. \]

If the entire circle is scaled by a factor \(k\), then the arc length becomes \(ks\) and the radius becomes \(kr\). The new ratio is

\[ \frac{ks}{kr}=\frac{s}{r}. \]

Thus the angle measure remains unchanged. Radian measure depends on the shape of the turn, not the size of the circle used to represent it.

Degrees, Radians, and Dimensional Reasoning

Conversion factors can be handled like unit fractions. To convert degrees to radians, choose the factor that places degrees in the denominator:

\[ 150^\circ\left(\frac{\pi\text{ rad}}{180^\circ}\right). \]

The degree symbols cancel, leaving radians.

To convert radians to degrees, reverse the factor:

\[ \frac{5\pi}{6}\text{ rad} \left(\frac{180^\circ}{\pi\text{ rad}}\right). \]

Thinking in terms of unit cancellation makes it much harder to multiply by the wrong conversion factor.

A Reliable Degree-to-Radian Procedure

  1. Write the angle in degrees.
  2. Multiply by \(\pi/180\).
  3. Cancel the degree unit.
  4. Reduce the numerical fraction.
  5. Keep \(\pi\) symbolic unless a decimal approximation is requested.

For example,

\[ 300^\circ\left(\frac{\pi}{180^\circ}\right) =\frac{300\pi}{180} =\boxed{\frac{5\pi}{3}}. \]

A Reliable Radian-to-Degree Procedure

  1. Write the angle in radians.
  2. Multiply by \(180/\pi\).
  3. Cancel \(\pi\) when it appears as a factor.
  4. Simplify the numerical expression.
  5. Attach the degree symbol to the result.

For example,

\[ \frac{11\pi}{6}\left(\frac{180^\circ}{\pi}\right) =11(30^\circ) =\boxed{330^\circ}. \]

Common Error: Treating \(\pi\) Radians as \(360^\circ\)

A full revolution is

\[ 2\pi\text{ radians}, \]

not \(\pi\) radians.

The correct equivalences are

\[ \boxed{\pi\text{ radians}=180^\circ} \]

and

\[ \boxed{2\pi\text{ radians}=360^\circ}. \]

Common Error: Multiplying by the Wrong Conversion Factor

Suppose we want to convert \(60^\circ\) to radians. Multiplying by \(180/\pi\) would make the degree unit fail to cancel properly and would produce an unreasonable result.

For degrees to radians, use

\[ \boxed{\frac{\pi}{180}}. \]

For radians to degrees, use

\[ \boxed{\frac{180}{\pi}}. \]

Common Error: Replacing \(\pi\) Too Early

Converting \(120^\circ\) to radians is most efficiently done exactly:

\[ 120^\circ\left(\frac{\pi}{180^\circ}\right) =\boxed{\frac{2\pi}{3}}. \]

Replacing \(\pi\) with \(3.14\) at the beginning introduces rounding and hides the exact relationship.

Common Error: Forgetting the Degree Symbol

The expressions

\[ 60 \]

and

\[ 60^\circ \]

are not automatically the same angular notation. In contexts where unlabeled angle measures are interpreted as radians, writing \(60\) can mean 60 radians, an enormous angle compared with \(60^\circ\).

Always retain \(^\circ\) when an angle is measured in degrees.

Common Error: Calling Radians a Different Kind of Angle

A degree angle and a radian angle are not different geometric objects. They are different numerical descriptions of the same amount of turning.

For example,

\[ 45^\circ=\frac{\pi}{4}\text{ radians}. \]

Nothing about the physical angle changes during conversion.

Common Error: Assuming Radian Measures Must Contain \(\pi\)

Measures such as

\[ 1,\qquad1.7,\qquad\frac{5}{2} \]

can all be radian measures. The symbol \(\pi\) appears frequently because many important angles are rational fractions of a half or full revolution, but \(\pi\) is not required in every radian measure.

Example: A Nonstandard Degree Measure

Convert \(72^\circ\) to radians.

\[ 72^\circ\left(\frac{\pi}{180^\circ}\right) =\frac{72\pi}{180}. \]

Divide numerator and denominator by \(36\):

\[ \boxed{72^\circ=\frac{2\pi}{5}}. \]

Example: A Decimal Radian Measure

Convert \(2.4\) radians to degrees.

\[ 2.4\left(\frac{180^\circ}{\pi}\right) =\frac{432^\circ}{\pi}. \]

Numerically,

\[ \frac{432}{\pi}\approx137.51. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{2.4\text{ radians}\approx137.51^\circ}. \]

Example: Reasoning from a Fraction of a Turn

Suppose an angle represents three eighths of a full revolution.

In degrees,

\[ \frac{3}{8}(360^\circ)=135^\circ. \]

In radians,

\[ \frac{3}{8}(2\pi)=\frac{3\pi}{4}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\frac{3}{8}\text{ turn}=135^\circ=\frac{3\pi}{4}\text{ radians}}. \]

Estimating Without a Calculator

Because one radian is about \(57.3^\circ\), rough mental comparisons are often possible.

  • \(0.5\) radian is about \(29^\circ\).
  • \(1\) radian is about \(57^\circ\).
  • \(1.5\) radians is about \(86^\circ\).
  • \(3\) radians is about \(172^\circ\).

Such estimates provide a useful reasonableness check on exact or calculator conversions.

What to Remember

  • A full revolution is \(360^\circ=2\pi\) radians.
  • A half revolution is \(180^\circ=\pi\) radians.
  • A degree is \(1/360\) of a complete turn.
  • One radian is the central angle that intercepts an arc equal in length to the radius.
  • Radian measure is given by \(\theta=s/r\).
  • To convert degrees to radians, multiply by \(\pi/180\).
  • To convert radians to degrees, multiply by \(180/\pi\).
  • Keep exact radian measures in terms of \(\pi\) when possible.
  • One radian is approximately \(57.3^\circ\).
  • Coterminal angles differ by multiples of \(360^\circ\) or \(2\pi\).

The main conversion relationship is compact enough to memorize:

\[ \boxed{180^\circ=\pi\text{ radians}}. \]
