Centers, Radii, Diameters, Chords, Secants, and Tangents

A circle is one of the simplest geometric objects to define, but a surprisingly rich collection of relationships grows from that definition. Once the center and radius are fixed, we can distinguish segments that lie inside the circle, lines that cut through it, and lines that meet it at exactly one point. These objects form the basic language needed for nearly every later result about circles.

The Central Idea

A circle is the set of all points in a plane that are the same fixed distance from one fixed point.

The fixed point is the center, and the fixed distance is the radius.

If the center is called \(O\) and the radius is \(r\), then every point \(P\) on the circle satisfies

\[ \boxed{OP=r}. \]

This single distance condition controls all of the geometry developed in this lesson.

Circle Versus Disk

In precise geometry, a circle contains only the points whose distance from the center is exactly \(r\).

\[ OP=r. \]

The points whose distance from the center is less than or equal to \(r\) form the corresponding disk:

\[ OP\le r. \]

Everyday language often uses “circle” for both the boundary and the filled-in region. In geometric reasoning, keeping the distinction clear prevents later confusion.

The Center

The center is the unique point from which every point on the circle is the same distance.

It does not lie on the circle unless the radius is zero. In ordinary elementary circle geometry, we assume

\[ r>0. \]

The center is often labeled \(O\), partly because it is visually easy to recognize and partly because it frequently serves as an origin when coordinates are introduced.

Radius: Segment and Length

A radius can mean either a segment from the center to a point on the circle or the length of such a segment.

If \(A\) lies on a circle centered at \(O\), then \(\overline{OA}\) is a radius segment and

\[ OA=r. \]

Every radius of the same circle has equal length. Thus, if \(A\), \(B\), and \(C\) lie on the circle, then

\[ OA=OB=OC=r. \]

Diameter

A diameter is a chord that passes through the center of the circle.

If \(A\) and \(B\) are opposite points on a circle centered at \(O\), then \(\overline{AB}\) is a diameter and

\[ AB=AO+OB=r+r=2r. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{d=2r} \]

and equivalently

\[ \boxed{r=\frac d2}. \]

Every Diameter Is a Chord

A chord is a line segment whose two endpoints lie on the circle.

Because a diameter has two endpoints on the circle, every diameter is a chord. But most chords do not pass through the center, so not every chord is a diameter.

This is a useful classification pattern:

\[ \boxed{\text{diameters are a special class of chords}}. \]

A Radius Is Usually Not a Chord

A radius has one endpoint at the center and one endpoint on the circle. A chord requires both endpoints to lie on the circle.

Therefore, for an ordinary circle with \(r>0\), a radius is not a chord.

This distinction is easy to overlook because radii, diameters, and chords are all segments associated with the same circle.

Anatomy of a Circle

The Diameter Is the Longest Chord

Among all chords of a circle, a diameter has the greatest possible length.

The intuitive reason is that a diameter passes directly through the center, reaching from one side of the circle to the opposite side along a straight line. Moving a chord away from the center shortens it.

Since every diameter has length \(2r\), every chord \(AB\) satisfies

\[ \boxed{AB\le 2r}. \]

Equality occurs exactly when the chord is a diameter.

Distance from the Center to a Chord

Drop a perpendicular from the center \(O\) to a chord \(AB\), meeting the chord at \(M\).

A fundamental circle theorem says that this perpendicular bisects the chord:

\[ AM=MB. \]

So the line from the center that meets a chord at a right angle also cuts that chord into two equal pieces.

Why a Perpendicular from the Center Bisects a Chord

Suppose \(OM\perp AB\). Because \(A\) and \(B\) lie on the same circle,

\[ OA=OB. \]

The right triangles \(\triangle OMA\) and \(\triangle OMB\) share \(OM\), and their hypotenuses are equal radii. By the hypotenuse-leg congruence criterion, the triangles are congruent.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{AM=MB}. \]

The Converse Chord Relationship

The relationship also works in reverse. If a line from the center of a circle bisects a chord, then that line is perpendicular to the chord.

Thus, for a chord \(AB\) with midpoint \(M\),

\[ AM=MB \quad\Longrightarrow\quad OM\perp AB. \]

The center, the midpoint of a chord, and perpendicularity are tightly linked.

Chord Length from Its Distance to the Center

Let a circle have radius \(r\), and let chord \(AB\) lie a perpendicular distance \(p\) from the center. If \(M\) is the midpoint of the chord, then

\[ OM=p \]

and

\[ OA=r. \]

The Pythagorean relationship in right triangle \(OMA\) gives

\[ AM=\sqrt{r^2-p^2}. \]

Since \(M\) bisects the chord,

\[ \boxed{AB=2\sqrt{r^2-p^2}}. \]

What the Chord Formula Tells Us

In

\[ AB=2\sqrt{r^2-p^2}, \]

the chord becomes shorter as its distance \(p\) from the center increases.

When \(p=0\), the chord passes through the center:

\[ AB=2\sqrt{r^2}=2r. \]

That is a diameter. When \(p\) approaches \(r\), the chord length approaches zero.

Equal Chords and Distance from the Center

In the same circle, chords of equal length are equally distant from the center. The converse is also true: chords equally distant from the center have equal length.

If chords \(AB\) and \(CD\) lie in the same circle, then

\[ AB=CD \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \operatorname{dist}(O,AB)=\operatorname{dist}(O,CD). \]

This gives another way to compare chords without measuring them directly.

Secant Lines

A secant line intersects a circle at two distinct points.

If a line intersects the circle at \(A\) and \(B\), then the entire line is a secant, while the segment \(\overline{AB}\) inside the circle is a chord.

This distinction separates the infinite line from the finite segment determined by its two intersection points.

Tangent Lines

A tangent line to a circle is a line that intersects the circle at exactly one point.

That unique point is called the point of tangency or point of contact.

If line \(\ell\) is tangent to the circle at \(T\), then \(T\) is the only point shared by \(\ell\) and the circle.

Secant, Tangent, or External?

A line in the plane can meet a circle in three basic ways.

  • A secant meets the circle at two points.
  • A tangent meets the circle at one point.
  • An external line meets the circle at no points.

These three cases can be classified by measuring the perpendicular distance from the center to the line.

The Distance Test for a Line

Let \(q\) be the perpendicular distance from the center of a circle of radius \(r\) to a line.

  • If \(q<r\), the line is a secant.
  • If \(q=r\), the line is tangent.
  • If \(q>r\), the line is external to the circle.

Symbolically,

\[ \boxed{ q<r\Rightarrow\text{secant},\qquad q=r\Rightarrow\text{tangent},\qquad q>r\Rightarrow\text{external} }. \]

The Radius-Tangent Theorem

If a line is tangent to a circle, then the radius drawn to the point of tangency is perpendicular to the tangent line.

If line \(\ell\) is tangent at \(T\) to a circle centered at \(O\), then

\[ \boxed{OT\perp \ell}. \]

This theorem is one of the most important structural facts about tangents.

Why the Radius Is Perpendicular to the Tangent

Let \(Q\) be the foot of the perpendicular from the center \(O\) to the tangent line. The perpendicular segment is the shortest distance from a point to a line.

If \(Q\neq T\), then \(OQ<OT=r\). That would place \(Q\) inside the circle. A line passing through an interior point of a circle meets the circle at two points, so the line would be a secant rather than a tangent.

Therefore \(Q=T\), and the radius to the point of tangency is perpendicular to the tangent line:

\[ \boxed{OT\perp \ell}. \]

The Converse Tangent Theorem

The tangent theorem also has a useful converse.

If a line passes through a point \(T\) on a circle and is perpendicular to the radius \(OT\), then that line is tangent to the circle at \(T\).

\[ T\text{ on the circle and }OT\perp\ell \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ell\text{ is tangent at }T. \]

This converse is often used to prove that a particular line is tangent.

Tangent Segments from the Same External Point

Suppose a point \(P\) lies outside a circle and two tangent segments from \(P\) touch the circle at \(A\) and \(B\).

Then the tangent segments have equal length:

\[ \boxed{PA=PB}. \]

This follows because \(OA\perp PA\), \(OB\perp PB\), the radii satisfy \(OA=OB\), and both right triangles share hypotenuse \(OP\).

How Many Tangents Can Pass Through a Point?

The number depends on where the point lies relative to the circle.

  • From a point inside the circle: no tangent lines.
  • From a point on the circle: exactly one tangent line.
  • From a point outside the circle: exactly two tangent lines.

This is another example of how position relative to the circle determines what geometric constructions are possible.

Example: Finding a Diameter

A circle has radius \(7.5\) centimeters. Find its diameter.

Use

\[ d=2r. \]

Therefore,

\[ d=2(7.5)=15. \]

The diameter is

\[ \boxed{15\text{ cm}}. \]

Example: Finding a Radius

A circle has diameter \(26\) inches. Find the radius.

\[ r=\frac d2=\frac{26}{2}=13. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{r=13\text{ in}}. \]

Example: Finding a Chord Length

A circle has radius \(10\), and a chord lies \(6\) units from the center. Find the chord length.

Use

\[ AB=2\sqrt{r^2-p^2}. \]

Substitute \(r=10\) and \(p=6\):

\[ AB=2\sqrt{10^2-6^2} =2\sqrt{100-36} =2\sqrt{64} =16. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{AB=16}. \]

Example: Classifying a Line by Distance

A circle has radius \(9\). A line is \(9\) units from the center. Is the line secant, tangent, or external?

Since the distance from the center to the line equals the radius,

\[ q=r=9. \]

Therefore, the line is

\[ \boxed{\text{tangent}}. \]

Example: Tangent Length from a Right Triangle

A point \(P\) lies outside a circle centered at \(O\). A tangent from \(P\) touches the circle at \(T\). Suppose

\[ OP=13 \qquad\text{and}\qquad OT=5. \]

Because a radius is perpendicular to a tangent at the point of tangency, \(\triangle OPT\) is a right triangle. Thus,

\[ PT^2=OP^2-OT^2=13^2-5^2=169-25=144. \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{PT=12}. \]

Example: Equal Tangents

From an external point \(P\), tangent segments \(PA\) and \(PB\) touch a circle. If

\[ PA=3x+2 \]

and

\[ PB=5x-8, \]

then equal tangent segments give

\[ 3x+2=5x-8. \]

Solving,

\[ 10=2x, \qquad x=5. \]

Each tangent segment has length

\[ 3(5)+2=17. \]

Circle Parts as a Classification System

Several terms in this lesson describe closely related objects, so it helps to organize them by type.

  • Center: a point.
  • Radius: a segment from the center to the circle, or its length.
  • Chord: a segment with both endpoints on the circle.
  • Diameter: a chord through the center.
  • Secant: a line meeting the circle at two points.
  • Tangent: a line meeting the circle at exactly one point.

Classifying the object first usually tells you which theorem or measurement relationship is available.

Common Error: Calling the Filled Region a Circle

A circle is the boundary determined by

\[ OP=r. \]

The filled region consisting of the circle together with its interior is the disk.

The distinction becomes important in formal definitions, area arguments, and later work with loci and coordinates.

Common Error: Treating Every Chord as a Diameter

Every diameter is a chord, but a chord becomes a diameter only when it passes through the center.

The statement

\[ \text{chord}\Rightarrow\text{diameter} \]

is false. The correct implication is

\[ \boxed{\text{diameter}\Rightarrow\text{chord}}. \]

Common Error: Confusing a Chord with a Secant

A chord is a finite segment whose endpoints lie on the circle. A secant is an entire line that continues beyond the circle in both directions.

A secant determines a chord between its two intersection points, but the line and the segment are not the same object.

Common Error: “A Tangent Never Crosses”

For a circle, it is tempting to define a tangent as a line that “touches but does not cross.” The safer geometric definition is that a tangent line meets the circle at exactly one point.

That definition gives a precise intersection count and connects directly to the perpendicular radius theorem.

Common Error: Forgetting the Radius-Tangent Right Angle

If \(T\) is a point of tangency, then the radius \(OT\) and the tangent line form a right angle.

\[ \angle OTP=90^\circ \]

whenever \(P\) is another point on the tangent line. This right angle often creates a Pythagorean relationship immediately.

Common Error: Using the Diameter as the Radius

The diameter is twice the radius:

\[ d=2r. \]

If a problem gives a diameter of \(18\), then the radius is \(9\), not \(18\). Marking the center and identifying whether a given segment spans the entire circle is a reliable safeguard.

A Reliable Procedure for Circle-Part Problems

  1. Identify the center and the radius if they are given or can be inferred.
  2. Classify each object as a point, segment, or line.
  3. Check how many endpoints or intersection points lie on the circle.
  4. For a chord, ask whether it passes through the center.
  5. For a line, compare its distance from the center with the radius.
  6. For a tangent, mark the right angle with the radius to the point of tangency.
  7. Use symmetry, congruence, or the Pythagorean relationship when appropriate.
  8. Check that the result is geometrically possible.

Practice Answers

Practice 1

\[ d=2r=2(11)=\boxed{22\text{ cm}}. \]

Practice 2

\[ r=\frac d2=\frac{34}{2}=\boxed{17\text{ m}}. \]

Practice 3

Because both endpoints lie on the circle, \(\overline{AB}\) is a chord. Because it also passes through the center, it is a diameter.

\[ \boxed{\text{chord and diameter}}. \]

Practice 4

The line is \(7\) units from the center while the radius is \(12\). Since \(7<12\), the line intersects the circle twice.

\[ \boxed{\text{secant}}. \]

Practice 5

Since the line is farther from the center than the radius,

\[ 10>8, \]

so the line does not meet the circle.

\[ \boxed{\text{external}}. \]

Practice 6

With \(r=13\) and \(p=5\),

\[ AB=2\sqrt{13^2-5^2} =2\sqrt{169-25} =2\sqrt{144} =\boxed{24}. \]

Practice 7

The radius to the tangent point is perpendicular to the tangent, so \(\triangle OPT\) is right.

\[ PT=\sqrt{17^2-8^2} =\sqrt{289-64} =\sqrt{225} =\boxed{15}. \]

Practice 8

Tangent segments from the same external point are equal:

\[ 4x-1=2x+9. \] \[ 2x=10, \qquad \boxed{x=5}. \]

Practice 9

A perpendicular from the center to a chord bisects the chord. Therefore,

\[ AM=MB=\frac{22}{2}=\boxed{11}. \]

Practice 10

A chord must have both endpoints on the circle. A segment from the center to a point on the circle has only one endpoint on the circle, so it is a radius, not a chord.

What to Remember

  • A circle consists of all points a fixed radius from its center.
  • A radius joins the center to the circle.
  • A chord joins two points on the circle.
  • A diameter is a chord through the center and has length \(2r\).
  • The diameter is the longest chord.
  • A perpendicular from the center to a chord bisects the chord.
  • A secant line intersects the circle at two points.
  • A tangent line intersects the circle at exactly one point.
  • The radius to a point of tangency is perpendicular to the tangent.
  • Tangent segments from the same external point have equal length.

The organizing principle is distance from the center. Radii define the circle, chord position is measured relative to the center, and secant-versus-tangent behavior is determined by how far a line lies from the center.
