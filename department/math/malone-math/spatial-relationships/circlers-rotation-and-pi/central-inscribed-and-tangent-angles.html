Central, Inscribed, and Tangent Angles

Angles around a circle behave differently depending on where their vertices lie. A central angle has its vertex at the center, an inscribed angle has its vertex on the circle, and a tangent angle can be formed when a tangent meets a chord at a point of tangency. These locations are not cosmetic: each determines a precise relationship between the angle and the arc it intercepts.

The Central Idea

Circle-angle problems become much easier once we ask two questions:

  1. Where is the angle's vertex?
  2. Which arc does the angle intercept?

Those two pieces of information determine the rule.

The three main relationships in this lesson are

\[ \boxed{m\angle AOB=m\widehat{AB}} \]

for a central angle,

\[ \boxed{m\angle ACB=\frac12m\widehat{AB}} \]

for an inscribed angle, and

\[ \boxed{m\angle TAB=\frac12m\widehat{AB}} \]

for an angle formed by a tangent and a chord at the point of tangency.

What Does It Mean to Intercept an Arc?

An angle associated with a circle determines an arc between the points where its sides meet the circle. That arc is called the angle's intercepted arc.

If the sides of an angle meet the circle at points \(A\) and \(B\), the angle intercepts an arc with endpoints \(A\) and \(B\).

For an inscribed angle, the intercepted arc is the arc lying inside the angle and not containing the angle's vertex.

Central Angles

A central angle is an angle whose vertex is the center of a circle and whose sides contain radii of the circle.

If \(O\) is the center and \(A\) and \(B\) lie on the circle, then

\[ \angle AOB \]

is a central angle.

Its intercepted arc has endpoints \(A\) and \(B\).

Central Angle Measure Equals Arc Measure

The degree measure of a central angle equals the degree measure of its intercepted arc:

\[ \boxed{m\angle AOB=m\widehat{AB}}. \]

Thus, if

\[ m\angle AOB=74^\circ, \]

then

\[ \boxed{m\widehat{AB}=74^\circ}. \]

This is the direct bridge between angles at the center and arc measure.

Central Angles Partition the Circle

Central angles around a point make a complete turn, so their measures sum to

\[ \boxed{360^\circ}. \]

Their intercepted arcs likewise partition the circumference into arc measures totaling \(360^\circ\).

If three central angles measure \(90^\circ\), \(125^\circ\), and \(x^\circ\), then

\[ 90+125+x=360, \]

so

\[ \boxed{x=145}. \]

Central Angles and Their Arcs

Minor and Reflex Central Angles

Two radii determine two turns from one radius to the other. The smaller turn gives the ordinary central angle when its measure is less than \(180^\circ\). The larger turn gives a reflex central angle measuring more than \(180^\circ\) and less than \(360^\circ\).

If the minor central angle is \(110^\circ\), then the reflex central angle is

\[ 360^\circ-110^\circ=\boxed{250^\circ}. \]

The corresponding minor and major arcs have the same respective measures.

Inscribed Angles

An inscribed angle is an angle whose vertex lies on the circle and whose sides are chords of the circle.

If \(A\), \(B\), and \(C\) lie on a circle, then

\[ \angle ACB \]

is an inscribed angle when \(CA\) and \(CB\) are chords.

The angle intercepts the arc \(\widehat{AB}\) that does not contain \(C\).

The Inscribed-Angle Theorem

The measure of an inscribed angle is one-half the measure of its intercepted arc:

\[ \boxed{m\angle ACB=\frac12m\widehat{AB}}. \]

Equivalently, the intercepted arc has twice the measure of the inscribed angle:

\[ \boxed{m\widehat{AB}=2m\angle ACB}. \]

This factor of \(\tfrac12\) is the defining quantitative difference between a central angle and an inscribed angle intercepting the same arc.

Central and Inscribed Angles Intercepting the Same Arc

Suppose central angle \(\angle AOB\) and inscribed angle \(\angle ACB\) intercept the same arc \(\widehat{AB}\). Then

\[ m\angle AOB=m\widehat{AB} \]

while

\[ m\angle ACB=\frac12m\widehat{AB}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{m\angle AOB=2m\angle ACB}. \]

The central angle is twice the inscribed angle when both intercept the same arc.

Central and Inscribed Angle Diagram

Example: Find an Inscribed Angle

Suppose an inscribed angle intercepts an arc measuring \(126^\circ\). Then

\[ m\angle=\frac12(126^\circ). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{m\angle=63^\circ}. \]

Example: Find the Intercepted Arc

Suppose an inscribed angle measures \(38^\circ\). Since the intercepted arc is twice the angle measure,

\[ m\widehat{AB}=2(38^\circ). \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{m\widehat{AB}=76^\circ}. \]

Inscribed Angles Intercepting the Same Arc

If two inscribed angles intercept the same arc, both equal one-half the measure of that arc. Therefore, the two angles are congruent.

If \(\angle ACB\) and \(\angle ADB\) both intercept \(\widehat{AB}\), then

\[ \boxed{\angle ACB\cong\angle ADB}. \]

The vertices may lie at different points on the same side of chord \(AB\), but the intercepted arc controls the angle measure.

Equal Inscribed Angles and Equal Arcs

In the same circle, or in congruent circles, equal inscribed angles intercept arcs of equal measure.

If

\[ m\angle ACB=m\angle DFE, \]

then their intercepted arcs satisfy

\[ \boxed{m\widehat{AB}=m\widehat{DE}}. \]

This follows because each intercepted arc is twice its corresponding inscribed angle.

Angles in a Semicircle

A diameter intercepts a semicircle measuring \(180^\circ\). An inscribed angle that intercepts that semicircle therefore measures

\[ \frac12(180^\circ)=90^\circ. \]

Hence:

\[ \boxed{\text{An inscribed angle subtending a diameter is a right angle.}} \]

This result is often called Thales' theorem.

Recognizing a Right Angle from a Diameter

Suppose \(AB\) is a diameter and \(C\) is any other point on the circle. Then \(\angle ACB\) intercepts semicircle \(\widehat{AB}\).

Therefore,

\[ m\angle ACB=\frac12(180^\circ)=\boxed{90^\circ}. \]

The location of \(C\) can change, but as long as \(C\) remains on the circle and \(AB\) remains a diameter, the angle stays right.

The Converse of the Semicircle Result

The relationship also works in reverse. If an inscribed angle is a right angle, then its intercepted arc measures \(180^\circ\). Its endpoints therefore form a diameter.

Thus, if \(A\), \(B\), and \(C\) lie on a circle and

\[ m\angle ACB=90^\circ, \]

then

\[ \boxed{AB\text{ is a diameter}.} \]

Why the Inscribed-Angle Theorem Works

The theorem can be proved by drawing radii from the center to the points forming the inscribed angle. Those radii create isosceles triangles because all radii of a circle are congruent.

Triangle angle sums then connect the inscribed angle to one or more central angles. Because each central angle equals the measure of its intercepted arc, the inscribed angle becomes exactly one-half the intercepted arc.

The diagram changes depending on whether the center lies on, inside, or outside the inscribed angle, but the same relationship survives every case.

Tangents and the Radius at the Point of Tangency

Recall that a tangent touches a circle at exactly one point. If line \(t\) is tangent to a circle at \(A\), then the radius \(OA\) drawn to the point of tangency is perpendicular to the tangent:

\[ \boxed{OA\perp t}. \]

Therefore, the angle formed by the radius and the tangent at \(A\) is

\[ \boxed{90^\circ}. \]

This right-angle relationship is different from the tangent-chord theorem that follows.

Angles Formed by a Tangent and a Chord

Suppose a tangent touches the circle at \(A\), and chord \(AB\) begins at the same point. The tangent and chord form an angle whose vertex lies on the circle.

This angle intercepts an arc with endpoints \(A\) and \(B\).

Its measure is one-half the measure of the intercepted arc:

\[ \boxed{m\angle TAB=\frac12m\widehat{AB}}. \]

Tangent-Chord Angle Diagram

Example: Tangent-Chord Angle from an Arc

Suppose a tangent-chord angle intercepts an arc measuring \(148^\circ\). Then

\[ m\angle=\frac12(148^\circ)=\boxed{74^\circ}. \]

Example: Arc from a Tangent-Chord Angle

Suppose the angle formed by a tangent and a chord measures \(57^\circ\). Its intercepted arc measures twice as much:

\[ m\widehat{AB}=2(57^\circ)=\boxed{114^\circ}. \]

Tangent-Chord and Inscribed Angles Intercepting the Same Arc

An inscribed angle and a tangent-chord angle both equal one-half the measure of their intercepted arc. Therefore, if they intercept the same arc, the two angles are congruent.

Symbolically, if both angles intercept \(\widehat{AB}\), then

\[ \boxed{m\angle TAB=m\angle ACB}. \]

This gives another way to transfer angle information around a circle.

Which Arc Does a Tangent-Chord Angle Intercept?

A tangent and a chord create two supplementary angles at the point of tangency. Each angle corresponds to the arc lying inside that angle.

If one intercepted arc measures \(120^\circ\), the other measures

\[ 360^\circ-120^\circ=240^\circ. \]

The associated tangent-chord angles measure

\[ \frac12(120^\circ)=60^\circ \]

and

\[ \frac12(240^\circ)=120^\circ. \]

As expected, the two angles are supplementary.

A Unified Comparison

The location of the vertex determines the multiplier relating an angle to an intercepted arc.

Angle type Vertex location Relationship to intercepted arc
Central At the center \(m\angle=m\widehat{\text{arc}}\)
Inscribed On the circle \(m\angle=\frac12m\widehat{\text{arc}}\)
Tangent-chord On the circle at the point of tangency \(m\angle=\frac12m\widehat{\text{arc}}\)

Do Not Classify by Appearance Alone

A diagram may make two angles look alike even when they obey different rules. Classification depends on geometric relationships, not visual appearance.

  • If the vertex is the center, use the central-angle relationship.
  • If the vertex is on the circle and both sides are chords, use the inscribed-angle theorem.
  • If the vertex is the point of tangency and one side is tangent while the other is a chord, use the tangent-chord theorem.

Always identify the vertex and the sides before calculating.

Example: Central Angle and Inscribed Angle Together

A central angle and an inscribed angle intercept the same arc. The inscribed angle measures \(41^\circ\).

The intercepted arc measures

\[ 2(41^\circ)=82^\circ. \]

Therefore, the central angle also measures

\[ \boxed{82^\circ}. \]

Example: Solve for a Variable

Suppose an inscribed angle measures \((3x+4)^\circ\) and its intercepted arc measures \((8x-16)^\circ\). Apply the inscribed-angle theorem:

\[ 3x+4=\frac12(8x-16). \]

Multiply by \(2\):

\[ 6x+8=8x-16. \]

Then

\[ 24=2x, \]

so

\[ \boxed{x=12}. \]

The angle measure is

\[ 3(12)+4=40^\circ, \]

and the arc measure is \(80^\circ\), confirming the one-half relationship.

Example: Use the Full Circle

An inscribed angle intercepts a major arc measuring \(250^\circ\). Then the angle measures

\[ \frac12(250^\circ)=\boxed{125^\circ}. \]

The remaining minor arc measures

\[ 360^\circ-250^\circ=110^\circ. \]

An inscribed angle intercepting that minor arc would measure \(55^\circ\).

Supplementary Angles from Complementary Arcs

Two arcs that together make the full circle have measures summing to \(360^\circ\). Half of those arc measures sum to

\[ \frac12(360^\circ)=180^\circ. \]

Thus two inscribed or tangent-chord angles that intercept the two complementary arcs determined by the same endpoints are supplementary.

This is why choosing the correct intercepted arc matters: the minor and major arcs lead to supplementary, not equal, angle measures.

From Diameter to Right Triangle

If one side of an inscribed triangle is a diameter, the angle opposite that diameter is a right angle. Therefore, a diameter can immediately identify an inscribed triangle as a right triangle.

Once that right angle is known, ordinary triangle relationships become available. For example, if the other acute angle measures \(34^\circ\), the remaining acute angle measures

\[ 90^\circ-34^\circ=\boxed{56^\circ}. \]

Tangent, Radius, and Chord in One Figure

At a point of tangency \(A\), a radius \(OA\), tangent line, and chord \(AB\) can all appear in the same diagram. Two different angle facts may then be used:

  • the radius and tangent form a \(90^\circ\) angle;
  • the tangent and chord form an angle equal to one-half its intercepted arc.

These are separate relationships. Do not assume every angle containing a tangent is \(90^\circ\); the right angle requires the radius drawn to the point of tangency.

Example: Tangent, Radius, and Chord

At point \(A\), radius \(OA\) is perpendicular to a tangent. Suppose chord \(AB\) forms a \(32^\circ\) angle with the radius inside the right angle.

The tangent-chord angle is therefore

\[ 90^\circ-32^\circ=\boxed{58^\circ}. \]

The intercepted arc then measures

\[ 2(58^\circ)=\boxed{116^\circ}. \]

A Reliable Procedure for Circle-Angle Problems

  1. Locate the angle's vertex.
  2. Identify the sides of the angle: radii, chords, or a tangent and chord.
  3. Identify the intercepted arc.
  4. Choose the correct angle-arc relationship.
  5. Write an equation before substituting or solving.
  6. Use \(360^\circ\) for a full circle or \(180^\circ\) for a semicircle when needed.
  7. Check that the resulting angle and arc sizes are geometrically reasonable.

Common Error: Forgetting the Factor of One-Half

If an inscribed angle intercepts a \(100^\circ\) arc, the angle does not measure \(100^\circ\).

The correct calculation is

\[ \frac12(100^\circ)=\boxed{50^\circ}. \]

Equality of angle and arc measure applies to central angles, not inscribed angles.

Common Error: Halving a Central Angle

If a central angle measures \(72^\circ\), its intercepted arc also measures

\[ \boxed{72^\circ}, \]

not \(36^\circ\). The one-half rule belongs to inscribed and tangent-chord angles.

Common Error: Using the Arc that Contains the Inscribed Vertex

For an inscribed angle \(\angle ACB\), the intercepted arc has endpoints \(A\) and \(B\) and does not contain \(C\).

Choosing the other arc changes the angle from an acute value to its supplementary obtuse value, or vice versa. Trace the interior of the angle to see which arc it opens toward.

Common Error: Assuming Every Tangent Angle Is Right

A tangent is perpendicular to the radius drawn to the point of tangency. That produces a right angle.

An angle between a tangent and a chord generally is not \(90^\circ\). Its measure is one-half the measure of its intercepted arc.

Common Error: Trusting the Drawing

Geometry diagrams are often not drawn to scale. An angle that appears to be \(90^\circ\), a chord that appears to be a diameter, or an arc that appears to be a semicircle must not be assumed to have that property unless it is given or proved.

Use marks, definitions, equations, and theorems rather than visual estimation.

Common Error: Confusing Arc Measure with Arc Length

An intercepted arc may measure \(80^\circ\), but that does not mean its length is \(80\) units. Arc measure is angular; arc length is linear and depends on the circle's radius.

A later lesson will convert angular measure into actual arc length.

What to Remember

  • A central angle has its vertex at the center of the circle.
  • A central angle equals the measure of its intercepted arc.
  • An inscribed angle has its vertex on the circle and sides that are chords.
  • An inscribed angle equals one-half the measure of its intercepted arc.
  • Inscribed angles intercepting the same arc are congruent.
  • An inscribed angle intercepting a diameter is a right angle.
  • A radius is perpendicular to a tangent at the point of tangency.
  • A tangent-chord angle equals one-half the measure of its intercepted arc.
  • A tangent-chord angle and an inscribed angle intercepting the same arc are congruent.
  • Always identify the vertex and intercepted arc before choosing a formula.

The central organizing principle is simple: where the vertex lies tells you how the angle is related to its intercepted arc.
