Cyclic and Periodic Relationships

Circular motion naturally repeats. After one complete turn, a rotating point returns to its original position; after two turns, it returns again. This repeated behavior creates cycles and periodic relationships. The same structure appears in clocks, wheels, waves, seasons, oscillations, trigonometric functions, and many physical systems.

The Central Idea

A relationship is periodic when its values repeat after a fixed interval.

If a quantity \(f\) satisfies

\[ f(x+T)=f(x) \]

for every relevant \(x\), then \(T\) is a period of the relationship.

On a circle, one full revolution provides the most basic period:

\[ \boxed{360^\circ=2\pi\text{ radians}}. \]

What Is a Cycle?

A cycle is one complete repetition of a process or pattern.

For a rotating point on a circle, one cycle is one complete revolution. Starting from an initial position, the point travels around the circle and returns to that same position.

In angular measure, one complete cycle corresponds to

\[ 360^\circ \]

or

\[ 2\pi. \]

Repeated Rotation

Suppose a point begins at an angle \(\theta\). After one full counterclockwise revolution, its angle is

\[ \theta+2\pi. \]

After two complete revolutions, its angle is

\[ \theta+4\pi. \]

After \(n\) complete revolutions,

\[ \boxed{\theta+2\pi n}, \]

where \(n\) is an integer.

Coterminal Angles Are a Cyclic Relationship

Angles that differ by an integer number of complete turns have the same terminal side.

In radians,

\[ \boxed{\theta+2\pi n,\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}} \]

describes all angles coterminal with \(\theta\).

In degrees,

\[ \boxed{\theta+360^\circ n,\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}}. \]

Coterminal angles are one of the simplest manifestations of periodicity.

Example: Finding Coterminal Angles

Start with

\[ \theta=45^\circ. \]

Adding one full revolution gives

\[ 45^\circ+360^\circ=405^\circ. \]

Subtracting one full revolution gives

\[ 45^\circ-360^\circ=-315^\circ. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{45^\circ,\ 405^\circ,\ -315^\circ} \]

all determine the same terminal direction.

Position Repeats Even When Angle Accumulates

A rotating object can continue accumulating angular displacement even though its physical orientation repeats.

For example,

\[ 30^\circ,\qquad390^\circ,\qquad750^\circ \]

represent different total rotations but the same terminal orientation.

This distinction separates accumulated rotation from cyclic position.

The Unit Circle Repeats Every \(2\pi\)

A point on the unit circle at angle \(\theta\) has coordinates

\[ (\cos\theta,\sin\theta). \]

After one complete revolution,

\[ (\cos(\theta+2\pi),\sin(\theta+2\pi)). \]

Because the point has returned to the same location,

\[ \boxed{\cos(\theta+2\pi)=\cos\theta} \]

and

\[ \boxed{\sin(\theta+2\pi)=\sin\theta}. \]

Periodic Functions

A function is periodic if its output repeats after some fixed change in its input.

Formally, if

\[ f(x+T)=f(x), \]

then \(T\) is a period.

For sine and cosine, \(2\pi\) is a period:

\[ \sin(x+2\pi)=\sin x, \] \[ \cos(x+2\pi)=\cos x. \]

Fundamental Period

A periodic relationship can have many periods.

If \(T\) is a period, then so are

\[ 2T,\qquad3T,\qquad4T,\ldots \]

when those shifts remain in the domain.

The smallest positive period, when it exists, is called the fundamental period.

For both sine and cosine, the fundamental period is

\[ \boxed{2\pi}. \]

Not Every Repetition Has Period \(2\pi\)

Circular geometry often produces \(2\pi\)-periodic behavior, but other periods are possible.

Consider

\[ f(\theta)=\cos(2\theta). \]

Increasing \(\theta\) by \(\pi\) gives

\[ f(\theta+\pi) = \cos(2\theta+2\pi) = \cos(2\theta). \]

Therefore, the fundamental period is

\[ \boxed{\pi}. \]

Angular Speed Changes the Period

Suppose a rotating quantity is modeled by

\[ f(t)=\cos(\omega t), \]

where \(\omega\) describes how rapidly the angle changes.

One complete cycle occurs when the angle increases by \(2\pi\):

\[ \omega T=2\pi. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{T=\frac{2\pi}{|\omega|}}. \]

Period and Frequency

Period measures how long one cycle takes.

Frequency measures how many cycles occur per unit of input, often per unit of time.

If \(T\) is the period and \(f\) is the frequency, then

\[ \boxed{f=\frac{1}{T}} \]

and

\[ \boxed{T=\frac{1}{f}}. \]

Example: Period and Frequency

Suppose a wheel completes one revolution every \(4\) seconds.

Its period is

\[ T=4\text{ s}. \]

Its frequency is

\[ f=\frac{1}{4}\text{ cycle/s}. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{f=0.25\text{ Hz}}. \]

Cycles per Revolution and Revolutions per Cycle

Circular processes can be described from either direction.

If a mechanism completes \(3\) repeating events during each revolution, then the event pattern repeats every

\[ \frac{1}{3} \]

revolution.

In degrees, that period is

\[ \frac{360^\circ}{3} = \boxed{120^\circ}. \]

Period from the Number of Repetitions

If a pattern repeats \(n\) equally spaced times during one full revolution, then its angular period is

\[ \boxed{\frac{2\pi}{n}} \]

radians, or

\[ \boxed{\frac{360^\circ}{n}}. \]

This relationship appears naturally in regular polygons, gears, rotating machinery, and rotational symmetry.

Rotational Symmetry Is Periodic Structure

A figure has rotational symmetry when some rotation smaller than a complete revolution maps the figure onto itself.

A regular hexagon matches itself after rotations of

\[ 60^\circ,\ 120^\circ,\ 180^\circ,\ldots \]

because

\[ \frac{360^\circ}{6}=60^\circ. \]

Its rotational structure therefore repeats six times per revolution.

Phase: Where in the Cycle?

Two periodic motions can have the same period but begin at different places in the cycle.

That relative position is called phase.

For example,

\[ y=\cos\theta \]

and

\[ y=\cos\left(\theta-\frac{\pi}{2}\right) \]

have the same period but are shifted relative to each other.

Phase Shift as Angular Translation

Consider

\[ y=\cos(\theta-\phi). \]

The value \(\phi\) shifts the cycle horizontally in angular input.

Geometrically, it changes the angle at which the repeating pattern begins.

Phase therefore connects periodic functions directly to rotation around a circle.

Example: Same Period, Different Phase

Compare

\[ y=\sin\theta \]

with

\[ y=\cos\theta. \]

Since

\[ \cos\theta = \sin\left(\theta+\frac{\pi}{2}\right), \]

the two functions have the same period but differ by a quarter-cycle phase shift.

Quarter, Half, and Full Cycles

For a circular cycle of \(2\pi\) radians:

  • a quarter-cycle is \(\frac{\pi}{2}\);
  • a half-cycle is \(\pi\);
  • three quarters of a cycle is \(\frac{3\pi}{2}\);
  • a full cycle is \(2\pi\).

These benchmark fractions make it easier to describe phase and repeated position.

Periodic Coordinates

A point moving around a circle of radius \(r\) centered at the origin can be written as

\[ x=r\cos\theta, \qquad y=r\sin\theta. \]

Both coordinates repeat after one full revolution:

\[ x(\theta+2\pi)=x(\theta), \] \[ y(\theta+2\pi)=y(\theta). \]

The circular path itself can therefore be viewed as two coordinated periodic motions.

Circular Motion Produces Oscillation in Each Coordinate

Although the point travels around a circle, its horizontal coordinate moves back and forth between

\[ -r \]

and

\[ r. \]

Its vertical coordinate does the same.

This is why circular motion is one of the fundamental geometric sources of sine-wave and cosine-wave behavior.

Example: Position After Many Revolutions

A point on the unit circle is at angle

\[ \theta=\frac{\pi}{3}+14\pi. \]

Since

\[ 14\pi=7(2\pi), \]

the angle differs from \(\frac{\pi}{3}\) by seven complete revolutions.

Therefore, the point has the same coordinates as at

\[ \frac{\pi}{3}, \]

namely

\[ \boxed{\left(\frac12,\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)}. \]

Reducing an Angle to One Cycle

To identify cyclic position, it is often useful to reduce an angle modulo one complete revolution.

In degrees, reduce modulo \(360^\circ\).

In radians, reduce modulo \(2\pi\).

For example,

\[ 765^\circ-2(360^\circ)=45^\circ. \]

Thus \(765^\circ\) and \(45^\circ\) determine the same cyclic position.

Modulo Language for Circular Position

The relationship between coterminal angles can be written compactly as

\[ \theta_1\equiv\theta_2\pmod{2\pi}. \]

This means that

\[ \theta_1-\theta_2 \]

is an integer multiple of \(2\pi\).

In degree measure, the corresponding statement is

\[ \theta_1\equiv\theta_2\pmod{360^\circ}. \]

Cycles in Clocks

A clock provides several overlapping periodic systems.

  • The second hand completes one cycle every \(60\) seconds.
  • The minute hand completes one cycle every \(60\) minutes.
  • The hour hand completes one cycle every \(12\) hours.

Their periods differ even though all three move around the same type of circular path.

Cycles in Wheels and Gears

A rotating wheel returns to the same orientation after one full revolution. A marked point on its rim therefore follows a periodic path.

Gears add another layer: the teeth create repeated subcycles. If a gear has \(20\) equally spaced teeth, neighboring teeth are separated by

\[ \frac{360^\circ}{20} = \boxed{18^\circ}. \]

The tooth pattern therefore repeats every \(18^\circ\).

Cycles Can Be Nested

A system can contain more than one relevant period.

For example, a wheel may complete one geometric revolution while a pattern painted on the wheel repeats four times during that revolution.

The wheel's orientation period is

\[ 2\pi, \]

while the painted pattern may have period

\[ \frac{2\pi}{4} = \frac{\pi}{2}. \]

Periodic Does Not Mean Constant

A periodic quantity repeats, but it need not stay unchanged.

For example,

\[ \sin\theta \]

rises, falls, becomes negative, and returns to earlier values.

A constant function is periodic in a broad sense, but most interesting periodic relationships change continuously or discretely within each cycle.

Periodic Does Not Mean Symmetric in Every Way

Repetition and symmetry are related but distinct ideas.

A pattern can repeat after a fixed interval without possessing reflection symmetry. Likewise, a figure can have reflection symmetry without describing a time-varying periodic process.

Periodicity concerns repetition under translation of the input.

Common Error: Confusing Period with Amplitude

Period describes the horizontal length of one repetition.

Amplitude describes vertical size in oscillatory models.

For

\[ y=5\sin\theta, \]

the amplitude is \(5\), but the period remains

\[ \boxed{2\pi}. \]

Common Error: Forgetting the Full-Revolution Unit

In degree measure, one revolution is

\[ 360^\circ. \]

In radian measure, one revolution is

\[ 2\pi. \]

Mixing these units can produce incorrect coterminal-angle or period calculations.

Common Error: Treating All Periods as \(2\pi\)

The basic sine and cosine functions have period \(2\pi\), but transformed periodic relationships may repeat more quickly or more slowly.

For

\[ y=\sin(3x), \]

the period is

\[ \boxed{\frac{2\pi}{3}}. \]

Common Error: Confusing Terminal Position with Total Rotation

The angles

\[ 20^\circ \]

and

\[ 740^\circ \]

have the same terminal position because they differ by

\[ 720^\circ=2(360^\circ). \]

But they do not represent the same accumulated angular displacement.

A Reliable Procedure for Cyclic Problems

  1. Identify what constitutes one complete cycle.
  2. Express that cycle in appropriate units.
  3. Determine whether the problem concerns position, total rotation, period, or frequency.
  4. Reduce angles modulo \(360^\circ\) or \(2\pi\) when only terminal position matters.
  5. Use \(T=2\pi/|\omega|\) when angular rate controls the cycle.
  6. Use \(f=1/T\) to convert between period and frequency.
  7. Check that the units match the interpretation.

What to Remember

Circular motion provides a natural model of periodicity because position repeats after complete revolutions.

  • One full circular cycle is \(360^\circ=2\pi\).
  • Coterminal angles differ by whole numbers of revolutions.
  • A period \(T\) satisfies \(f(x+T)=f(x)\).
  • The fundamental period is the smallest positive repeating interval.
  • For \(f(t)=\sin(\omega t)\) or \(\cos(\omega t)\), \(T=2\pi/|\omega|\).
  • Frequency and period satisfy \(f=1/T\).
  • Phase describes where a repeating process begins within its cycle.

The broad idea is:

\[ \boxed{\text{circular repetition turns geometry into periodic structure}}. \]
