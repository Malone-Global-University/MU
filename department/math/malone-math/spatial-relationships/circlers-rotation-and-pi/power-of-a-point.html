Power of a Point

A point and a circle can be related by more than distance alone. If a line through the point meets the circle, the lengths cut off along that line obey a remarkable invariant product. That product is called the power of the point. It unifies several familiar circle theorems involving chords, secants, and tangents.

The Central Idea

Fix a circle and a point \(P\). Draw a line through \(P\) that intersects the circle at points \(A\) and \(B\).

The product

\[ PA\cdot PB \]

has the same value for every secant line through \(P\).

This invariant product is the geometric foundation of the power of \(P\) with respect to the circle.

Power Is an Invariant

Suppose two different lines through \(P\) meet the circle at \(A,B\) and \(C,D\). Then

\[ \boxed{PA\cdot PB=PC\cdot PD}. \]

The individual lengths can change from one line to another, but the product remains constant.

That constancy is what makes the power of a point useful: many apparently different diagrams are governed by the same underlying quantity.

Three Possible Locations of the Point

A point \(P\) can lie:

  • inside the circle;
  • on the circle;
  • outside the circle.

The geometric form of the theorem changes slightly in each case, but all three cases belong to the same power-of-a-point framework.

Point Inside the Circle: Intersecting Chords

Suppose two chords \(AB\) and \(CD\) intersect at an interior point \(P\). Then

\[ \boxed{PA\cdot PB=PC\cdot PD}. \]

This is often called the intersecting chords theorem.

Each product multiplies the two pieces of one chord created by the intersection point.

Example: Intersecting Chords

Suppose two chords intersect at \(P\), with

\[ PA=4,\qquad PB=9,\qquad PC=6. \]

Let \(PD=x\). Then

\[ 4(9)=6x. \]

So

\[ 36=6x \]

and therefore

\[ \boxed{x=6}. \]

Why the Chord Products Match

The intersecting-chords relationship follows from similar triangles. Connecting appropriate endpoints produces two triangles whose angles match: one pair of angles is vertical, and another pair intercepts the same arc.

Similarity gives a proportion of corresponding sides. Cross multiplication then produces

\[ PA\cdot PB=PC\cdot PD. \]

The product formula is therefore a compressed form of a similarity relationship.

Point Outside the Circle: Two Secants

Now suppose \(P\) lies outside the circle. Two secants from \(P\) meet the circle at \(A,B\) and \(C,D\), where \(A\) and \(C\) are the nearer intersection points.

Then

\[ \boxed{PA\cdot PB=PC\cdot PD}. \]

In words:

\[ \boxed{\text{external part}\times\text{whole secant} = \text{external part}\times\text{whole secant}}. \]

External Part and Whole Secant

In an exterior secant problem, the two factors on the same secant are not usually the two visible pieces.

If \(P\), \(A\), and \(B\) occur in that order, then

\[ PB=PA+AB. \]

The power product is

\[ PA\cdot PB, \]

not

\[ PA\cdot AB. \]

This distinction is one of the most important practical details in using the theorem correctly.

Example: Two Secants from an Exterior Point

Suppose one secant has external length \(3\) and internal length \(9\). Its whole length is

\[ 3+9=12. \]

A second secant has external length \(4\) and whole length \(x\).

Apply the power relationship:

\[ 3(12)=4x. \]

Therefore,

\[ 36=4x \]

and

\[ \boxed{x=9}. \]

Tangent and Secant from the Same Exterior Point

Suppose \(PT\) is tangent to the circle at \(T\), while a secant from \(P\) meets the circle at \(A\) and \(B\).

Then

\[ \boxed{PT^2=PA\cdot PB}. \]

This is the tangent-secant theorem.

The tangent appears twice in the product because its two intersection points have merged into the single point of tangency.

Example: Tangent and Secant

Suppose

\[ PA=5,\qquad PB=20. \]

Let the tangent length be \(PT=t\). Then

\[ t^2=5(20)=100. \]

Because a geometric length is nonnegative,

\[ \boxed{t=10}. \]

Two Tangents from the Same Exterior Point

If two tangents \(PA\) and \(PB\) are drawn from the same exterior point \(P\), then

\[ \boxed{PA=PB}. \]

One way to interpret this is through power:

\[ PA^2=\operatorname{Pow}(P) \]

and

\[ PB^2=\operatorname{Pow}(P). \]

Hence

\[ PA^2=PB^2. \]

Since lengths are nonnegative, \(PA=PB\).

The Coordinate Formula for Power

Let a circle have center \(O\) and radius \(r\). If a point \(P\) is at distance \(d=OP\) from the center, then its power can be expressed as

\[ \boxed{\operatorname{Pow}(P)=d^2-r^2}. \]

This formula connects the secant product directly to the point's distance from the center.

Interpreting the Sign of the Power

From

\[ \operatorname{Pow}(P)=d^2-r^2, \]

three cases follow immediately.

  • If \(d>r\), then \(\operatorname{Pow}(P)>0\): \(P\) is outside the circle.
  • If \(d=r\), then \(\operatorname{Pow}(P)=0\): \(P\) lies on the circle.
  • If \(d<r\), then \(\operatorname{Pow}(P)<0\): \(P\) is inside the circle.

Thus the sign of the power records the point's position relative to the circle.

Why Interior Power Is Negative

In elementary chord problems, segment lengths are usually treated as positive magnitudes, so the intersecting-chords theorem appears as

\[ PA\cdot PB=PC\cdot PD. \]

In a directed-length formulation, however, the two intersections lie in opposite directions from an interior point. One directed factor is positive and the other negative, producing a negative power.

The elementary positive-length formula and the signed power formula are therefore compatible; they use different conventions.

Point on the Circle

If \(P\) lies on the circle, then

\[ OP=r. \]

Therefore,

\[ \operatorname{Pow}(P)=r^2-r^2=0. \]

This matches the secant interpretation: one intersection point can be \(P\) itself, giving a factor of zero.

Power and Tangent Length

If \(P\) lies outside the circle and \(PT\) is tangent, then

\[ \operatorname{Pow}(P)=PT^2. \]

Combining this with the center-distance formula gives

\[ PT^2=OP^2-r^2. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{PT=\sqrt{OP^2-r^2}}. \]

This is also a direct consequence of the right triangle formed by the center, tangent point, and exterior point.

Example: Tangent Length from Center Distance

A circle has radius \(6\), and an exterior point \(P\) is \(10\) units from the center.

Then

\[ PT=\sqrt{10^2-6^2}. \]

So

\[ PT=\sqrt{100-36} =\sqrt{64} =\boxed{8}. \]

A Unified View of the Three Product Theorems

The common classroom formulas can be organized as follows.

  • Chord-chord: \(\;PA\cdot PB=PC\cdot PD\).
  • Secant-secant: \(\;(\text{external})(\text{whole}) =(\text{external})(\text{whole})\).
  • Tangent-secant: \(\;(\text{tangent})^2 =(\text{external})(\text{whole})\).

These are not three unrelated tricks. Each computes the same power of the same point.

Choosing the Correct Formula

First identify where the point of intersection lies.

  1. If the intersection point is inside the circle, use chord pieces.
  2. If the point is outside and both lines are secants, use external times whole.
  3. If one line is tangent and one is secant, square the tangent length.
  4. If two tangents leave the same exterior point, their lengths are equal.

Classifying the diagram correctly usually matters more than the algebra that follows.

Example: Solving a Quadratic Power Equation

Suppose two chords intersect inside a circle and their segment lengths are

\[ x,\qquad x+2,\qquad 3,\qquad 8. \]

Then

\[ x(x+2)=3(8). \]

So

\[ x^2+2x-24=0. \]

Factor:

\[ (x+6)(x-4)=0. \]

The algebraic solutions are \(x=-6\) and \(x=4\). Since a segment length cannot be negative,

\[ \boxed{x=4}. \]

Example: Secant with an Unknown Internal Part

One secant has external length \(4\) and internal length \(x\). Its whole length is

\[ 4+x. \]

A second secant has external length \(6\) and whole length \(10\).

Therefore,

\[ 4(4+x)=6(10). \]

Expand:

\[ 16+4x=60. \]

Hence

\[ 4x=44 \]

and

\[ \boxed{x=11}. \]

Power Has Units of Length Squared

Power is formed from a product of two lengths:

\[ PA\cdot PB. \]

If each length is measured in centimeters, then the power is measured in

\[ \text{cm}^2. \]

The same dimensional result appears in

\[ OP^2-r^2. \]

Power is therefore a squared-length quantity, even though it is not generally an area.

Power Is Not Area

Because power has units of length squared, it can be tempting to interpret it as an area.

That interpretation is usually incorrect. Power is an invariant geometric quantity associated with a point and a circle. Its squared units arise because the quantity is built from products or differences of squared lengths.

Scaling Behavior

Suppose every length in a configuration is multiplied by a scale factor \(k\).

Then

\[ PA\cdot PB \]

becomes

\[ (kPA)(kPB)=k^2(PA\cdot PB). \]

Thus power scales by

\[ \boxed{k^2}. \]

This agrees with its squared-length dimension.

Common Error: Multiplying External by Internal

For an exterior secant, students sometimes multiply the external part by the internal part.

If the external part is \(4\) and the internal part is \(7\), the whole secant is

\[ 4+7=11. \]

The correct power contribution is

\[ \boxed{4(11)}, \]

not \(4(7)\).

Common Error: Squaring the Secant

In the tangent-secant theorem,

\[ PT^2=PA\cdot PB, \]

only the tangent length is squared.

The secant side uses two different lengths: the external part and the whole secant.

Common Error: Using the Whole Chord Inside the Circle

When two chords intersect inside a circle, use the two pieces created by the intersection point:

\[ PA\cdot PB. \]

Do not replace either factor with the entire chord length unless that entire length happens to equal the required segment.

Common Error: Keeping a Negative Length

Power equations can lead to quadratic equations with two algebraic roots. A negative root may be mathematically valid for the equation but invalid as an ordinary geometric length.

Always check the solution against the diagram and the meaning of the variable.

A Reliable Procedure

  1. Identify the point whose power is being used.
  2. Determine whether the point is inside, on, or outside the circle.
  3. Classify each line as a chord, secant, or tangent.
  4. Label external, internal, and whole secant lengths carefully.
  5. Write the appropriate power relationship before substituting numbers.
  6. Solve the resulting equation.
  7. Reject any value that is impossible as a geometric length.
  8. Check that both sides of the power equation agree.

Practice 5: Tangent-Secant

A tangent from \(P\) has length \(12\). A secant from \(P\) has external length \(8\) and whole length \(x\). Find \(x\).

Practice 6: Tangent Length

A secant has external length \(9\) and whole length \(16\). Find the length of a tangent drawn from the same exterior point.

Practice 7: Power from Center Distance

A circle has radius \(5\). A point is \(13\) units from the center. Find the power of the point.

Practice 8: Locate the Point from Its Power

A circle has radius \(7\), and a point has power \(-24\). Is the point inside, on, or outside the circle?

Practice 9: Quadratic Chord Equation

Two intersecting chords give

\[ x(x+3)=4(10). \]

Find the positive geometric solution.

Practice 10: Identify the Error

A secant has external length \(3\) and internal length \(8\). A student writes its power contribution as

\[ 3(8). \]

Explain the error and write the correct product.

What to Remember

The power of a point is the invariant quantity that unifies chord, secant, and tangent product relationships.

  • Inside the circle: multiply the two pieces of one chord.
  • Outside with two secants: multiply external part by whole secant.
  • Outside with a tangent: the tangent length is squared.
  • The center-distance form is \(\operatorname{Pow}(P)=OP^2-r^2\).
  • Positive power means outside, zero means on, and negative means inside.

The central structural idea is

\[ \boxed{\text{different lines through the same point produce the same power}}. \]
