Incidence, Intersection, Containment, and Separation

Spatial objects are understood not only by what they are, but also by how they relate. A point may lie on a line. Two curves may meet. One region may lie inside another. A line or surface may divide space into separate parts. Incidence, intersection, containment, and separation provide the language for describing these relationships precisely.

The Central Idea

Four basic questions organize many spatial relationships.

  • Incidence: Does one object lie on or pass through another?
  • Intersection: Which points do two or more objects share?
  • Containment: Is one object entirely inside another?
  • Separation: Do objects remain apart, or does an object divide space into distinct parts?

These ideas often work together. Incidence can produce an intersection. Containment can be described with subsets. Separation can be created by a boundary. The same diagram may involve all four.

Relationships Are About Shared Structure

Earlier lessons focused on objects such as points, lines, curves, surfaces, regions, and solids.

We now ask how those objects interact.

For example:

  • a point can belong to a line;
  • two lines can share a point;
  • a segment can lie entirely within a line;
  • a curve can form the boundary between an interior and an exterior;
  • two regions can overlap, touch, or remain disjoint.

Spatial relationships are therefore naturally connected to the language of sets.

Incidence

Incidence describes a relationship in which one geometric object lies on, passes through, or belongs to another.

If point \(P\) lies on line \(\ell\), we may say:

  • \(P\) lies on \(\ell\);
  • \(\ell\) passes through \(P\);
  • \(P\) is incident with \(\ell\).

These statements describe the same relationship from different viewpoints.

Incidence Can Be Written with Membership

If a geometric object is treated as a set of points, incidence can be expressed using set membership.

If point \(P\) lies on line \(\ell\), then

\[ \boxed{P\in\ell}. \]

If \(P\) does not lie on \(\ell\), then

\[ \boxed{P\notin\ell}. \]

This notation connects geometry directly to foundational set language.

Point-Line Incidence

A line contains infinitely many points.

If distinct points \(A\) and \(B\) lie on the same line, then both are incident with that line:

\[ A\in\overleftrightarrow{AB}, \qquad B\in\overleftrightarrow{AB}. \]

In Euclidean geometry, those two distinct points determine the unique line through them.

Point-Plane Incidence

A point can also lie in a plane.

If point \(P\) belongs to plane \(\Pi\), we may write

\[ P\in\Pi. \]

A plane contains infinitely many points and infinitely many lines.

Incidence does not require the smaller object to be visibly marked in a diagram; it is a mathematical relationship.

Line-Plane Incidence

A line may lie entirely in a plane.

If every point of line \(\ell\) belongs to plane \(\Pi\), then

\[ \boxed{\ell\subseteq\Pi}. \]

We can also say that \(\ell\) lies in \(\Pi\).

This is stronger than saying that the line merely meets the plane at one point.

Intersection

The intersection of two sets or geometric objects consists of all points they have in common.

If objects \(A\) and \(B\) are treated as point sets, their intersection is

\[ \boxed{A\cap B}. \]

The intersection may contain no points, one point, several points, a curve, a segment, an entire line, or some more complicated set.

Intersection Means Shared Points

The most important question is:

Which points belong to both objects?

If exactly one point \(P\) belongs to both objects \(A\) and \(B\), then

\[ A\cap B=\{P\}. \]

If they share no points, then

\[ A\cap B=\varnothing. \]

Two Lines Can Intersect at a Point

In a plane, two distinct lines may meet at exactly one point.

If lines \(\ell\) and \(m\) meet at point \(P\), then

\[ \boxed{\ell\cap m=\{P\}}. \]

The point \(P\) is incident with both lines.

More specialized relationships between lines will be developed in the next lesson.

Two Objects Can Share More Than One Point

Intersection is not limited to single points.

If two geometric objects overlap along an entire segment, their intersection is that segment.

If one segment is

\[ \overline{AB} \]

and another overlaps it from \(C\) to \(D\), then the intersection may be

\[ \overline{CD}. \]

The exact answer depends on the relative positions of the endpoints.

Coincident Objects Have Large Intersections

Two geometric objects may coincide, meaning that they occupy exactly the same point set.

If lines \(\ell\) and \(m\) are actually the same line, then

\[ \ell=m \]

and therefore

\[ \boxed{\ell\cap m=\ell=m}. \]

Their intersection is not one point; it is the entire common line.

A Line Can Intersect a Plane

In three-dimensional Euclidean space, a line and a plane can relate in several ways.

  • The line can meet the plane at exactly one point.
  • The entire line can lie in the plane.
  • The line can have no points in common with the plane.

The intersection therefore depends on the line's position and direction relative to the plane.

Two Planes Can Intersect in a Line

In ordinary three-dimensional Euclidean space, two distinct nonparallel planes intersect in a line.

If planes \(\Pi_1\) and \(\Pi_2\) meet along line \(\ell\), then

\[ \boxed{\Pi_1\cap\Pi_2=\ell}. \]

This is a useful reminder that intersections can have positive dimension.

Curves Can Intersect

Curves may meet at one point, several points, or infinitely many points.

For example, the line

\[ y=0 \]

intersects the circle

\[ x^2+y^2=4 \]

at

\[ (-2,0) \]

and

\[ (2,0). \]

Thus the intersection contains two points.

Intersection Can Be Found by Solving Equations

Coordinate geometry translates intersection into a system of equations.

Suppose

\[ y=x+1 \]

and

\[ y=-x+5. \]

At an intersection point, both equations must hold.

Set the right sides equal:

\[ x+1=-x+5. \]

Then

\[ 2x=4, \qquad x=2. \]

Substitution gives

\[ y=3. \]

Therefore, the intersection is

\[ \boxed{\{(2,3)\}}. \]

Containment

Containment means that every point of one object belongs to another object.

If every point of object \(A\) belongs to object \(B\), then

\[ \boxed{A\subseteq B}. \]

Geometrically, we may say that \(A\) lies in \(B\), is contained in \(B\), or is a subset of \(B\).

A Segment Is Contained in Its Line

If segment \(\overline{AB}\) lies on the line through \(A\) and \(B\), then

\[ \boxed{ \overline{AB}\subseteq\overleftrightarrow{AB} }. \]

Every point of the segment is a point of the line, but the line contains many additional points beyond the endpoints.

Therefore, the containment is proper:

\[ \overline{AB}\subsetneq\overleftrightarrow{AB}. \]

A Ray Is Contained in Its Line

Similarly,

\[ \boxed{ \overrightarrow{AB}\subseteq\overleftrightarrow{AB} }. \]

The ray contains all points beginning at \(A\) and extending through \(B\) in one direction.

The full line contains that ray plus points extending in the opposite direction.

A Boundary Can Be Contained in a Region

Consider the closed disk

\[ D=\{(x,y):x^2+y^2\le9\}. \]

Its circular boundary is

\[ C=\{(x,y):x^2+y^2=9\}. \]

Every point of \(C\) belongs to \(D\), so

\[ \boxed{C\subseteq D}. \]

Interior Containment

Sometimes an object lies completely inside another without touching its boundary.

For example, the disk

\[ x^2+y^2\le1 \]

lies entirely inside

\[ x^2+y^2<4. \]

Every point of the smaller closed disk belongs to the interior of the larger radius-\(2\) disk.

Containment Is Not the Same as Intersection

If \(A\subseteq B\), then

\[ A\cap B=A. \]

But two objects can intersect without either containing the other.

Two overlapping circles, for example, may share points even though neither circle is contained in the other.

Nested Objects

Containment can occur in layers.

If

\[ A\subseteq B \]

and

\[ B\subseteq C, \]

then

\[ \boxed{A\subseteq C}. \]

This transitive structure appears throughout spatial mathematics.

Separation

Separation describes the way objects remain apart or the way one object divides a larger space into distinct parts.

The word is used in several related senses:

  • two objects may share no points;
  • one object may lie between others;
  • a line may divide a plane into two sides;
  • a surface may divide three-dimensional space into regions;
  • a boundary may distinguish interior from exterior.

Disjoint Objects

Two sets are disjoint when they have no points in common.

If objects \(A\) and \(B\) are disjoint, then

\[ \boxed{A\cap B=\varnothing}. \]

Disjointness is one of the simplest forms of separation.

Separated Does Not Always Mean Far Apart

Two objects can be disjoint even when they are arbitrarily close.

For example, the open intervals

\[ (0,1) \]

and

\[ (1,2) \]

share no point because the common boundary value \(1\) is excluded from both.

Their intersection is empty even though points from the two intervals can be chosen as close to each other as desired.

A Line Separates the Plane

In Euclidean geometry, a line divides the plane into two half-planes.

Consider the line

\[ y=0. \]

It separates the plane into the regions

\[ y>0 \]

and

\[ y<0. \]

The line itself forms their common boundary.

A General Line Defines Two Sides

A line written as

\[ ax+by=c \]

separates the plane into two open half-planes:

\[ ax+by and

\[ ax+by>c. \]

Including the boundary produces the corresponding closed half-planes:

\[ ax+by\le c \]

and

\[ ax+by\ge c. \]

A Plane Separates Three-Dimensional Space

A plane in three-dimensional Euclidean space separates space into two half-spaces.

For a plane

\[ ax+by+cz=d, \]

the two sides can be described by

\[ ax+by+cz and

\[ ax+by+cz>d. \]

The plane itself is their common boundary.

A Simple Closed Curve Separates the Plane

A simple closed curve in the plane distinguishes an interior from an exterior.

A circle is the most familiar example.

The circle

\[ x^2+y^2=r^2 \]

separates the plane into

\[ x^2+y^2 and

\[ x^2+y^2>r^2. \]

The curve itself forms the boundary between the two.

Boundary Connects Containment and Separation

A boundary often plays two roles at once.

It can belong to a larger closed region, making it part of a containment relationship.

It can also separate interior points from exterior points.

This is why boundary language becomes central in geometry and topology.

Touching Is a Kind of Intersection

Two objects may touch at exactly one point.

For example, the circles

\[ x^2+y^2=1 \]

and

\[ (x-2)^2+y^2=1 \]

meet only at

\[ (1,0). \]

Their intersection is

\[ \boxed{\{(1,0)\}}. \]

Overlap Is More Than Touching

When regions overlap, their intersection contains an entire region rather than only a boundary point.

For example, two disks whose interiors partly cover the same area have a two-dimensional intersection.

The word overlap is therefore often used when the shared portion has substantial extent.

Crossing and Touching Are Different

Two curves can intersect by crossing through one another or by merely touching.

Both situations produce shared points, but their local behavior differs.

Later geometry and calculus make this difference precise using tangent directions, multiplicity, and local structure.

Distance Can Measure Separation

When two points do not coincide, their distance provides a numerical measure of separation.

For

\[ A=(x_1,y_1) \]

and

\[ B=(x_2,y_2), \]

the distance is

\[ \boxed{ AB= \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2} }. \]

If \(A=B\), the distance is \(0\).

Distance Between Sets

The idea of distance can be extended from points to sets.

Informally, the distance between two sets is the smallest distance that can occur between a point of one set and a point of the other.

In advanced notation, one often writes

\[ d(A,B) = \inf\{d(a,b):a\in A,\ b\in B\}. \]

This gently introduces a more general way to quantify separation.

Zero Set Distance Does Not Always Mean Intersection

In more advanced settings, two sets can have distance \(0\) without sharing a point.

For example,

\[ A=(0,1) \]

and

\[ B=(1,2) \]

are disjoint, but points in the two intervals can approach \(1\) from opposite sides.

This shows that geometric separation can depend on more than one concept.

Betweenness Is a Form of Separation

If \(B\) lies between \(A\) and \(C\) on a line, then \(B\) separates the segment \(\overline{AC}\) into two subsegments:

\[ \overline{AB} \]

and

\[ \overline{BC}. \]

Their lengths satisfy

\[ \boxed{AB+BC=AC}. \]

Betweenness gives a one-dimensional version of spatial separation.

Intersection Can Create New Objects

The intersection of larger-dimensional objects can produce a lower-dimensional object.

Examples include:

  • two lines meeting at a point;
  • two planes meeting in a line;
  • a plane cutting a solid in a cross section;
  • a surface meeting another surface along a curve.

Intersection is therefore a constructive operation as well as a relationship.

Containment Can Create Hierarchies

Spatial structures are often nested.

A point may lie on a segment, the segment may lie in a polygon, the polygon may lie in a plane, and the plane may lie in a larger coordinate space.

Symbolically, one might have

\[ P\in S, \qquad S\subseteq R, \qquad R\subseteq\Pi. \]

This nested structure helps organize complex geometric systems.

Equations and Inequalities Reveal the Relationship

Algebra often distinguishes boundaries from contained regions.

For the unit circle,

\[ x^2+y^2=1 \]

describes the boundary.

The inequality

\[ x^2+y^2<1 \]

describes the interior.

The inequality

\[ x^2+y^2>1 \]

describes the exterior.

Intersection of Regions Can Be Written as Simultaneous Conditions

Suppose

\[ A=\{(x,y):x\ge0\} \]

and

\[ B=\{(x,y):y\ge0\}. \]

A point belongs to \(A\cap B\) exactly when both conditions hold:

\[ \boxed{x\ge0,\qquad y\ge0}. \]

Thus \(A\cap B\) is the first quadrant together with its boundary axes.

Containment Can Be Checked with Conditions

Suppose

\[ A=\{(x,y):x^2+y^2\le1\} \]

and

\[ B=\{(x,y):x^2+y^2\le4\}. \]

If

\[ x^2+y^2\le1, \]

then automatically

\[ x^2+y^2\le4. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{A\subseteq B}. \]

Common Error: Confusing Incidence with Intersection

Incidence describes a relationship such as “point \(P\) lies on line \(\ell\).”

Intersection describes the shared point set of two objects.

If two lines meet at \(P\), then \(P\) is incident with each line, and the intersection of the two lines is

\[ \{P\}. \]

The concepts are connected but not identical.

Common Error: Assuming Every Intersection Is a Point

Intersections can have many forms.

Two coincident lines intersect in an entire line.

Two overlapping regions can intersect in a region.

Two planes can intersect in a line.

Always determine the full set of shared points.

Common Error: Confusing Containment with Membership

Membership and subset notation apply to different kinds of relationships.

If \(P\) is a point on line \(\ell\), write

\[ P\in\ell. \]

If segment \(S\) lies within line \(\ell\), write

\[ S\subseteq\ell. \]

A point is an element of the line's point set; a segment is itself a set of points contained in the line.

Common Error: Treating Disjoint as Distant

Disjoint means only that two objects share no points.

It does not require a large positive gap between them.

Objects can be disjoint and still approach each other arbitrarily closely.

Common Error: Assuming a Boundary Is Outside the Region

Whether a boundary belongs to a region depends on the definition of the region.

The disk

\[ x^2+y^2\le1 \]

includes its boundary.

The open disk

\[ x^2+y^2<1 \]

does not.

Common Error: Trusting the Diagram Alone

Two objects may look as though they intersect, touch, or lie inside one another because of the way a diagram is drawn.

Exact spatial relationships should come from stated conditions, coordinates, equations, markings, or valid reasoning.

A diagram is evidence only when its relationships are formally specified.

A Reliable Relationship Procedure

When analyzing two or more spatial objects, ask:

  1. What point set does each object contain?
  2. Does one object lie on or in another?
  3. Which points belong to both objects?
  4. Is one entire object contained in another?
  5. Are the objects disjoint, touching, crossing, or overlapping?
  6. Does one object divide the surrounding space into separate parts?
  7. Is a boundary included or excluded?
  8. Can equations or inequalities verify the relationship?

An intersection is the complete set of shared points. Two objects may share one point, several points, a segment, a curve, a region, or even coincide completely. The intersection is not required to be a single point.

What to Remember

  • Incidence describes when one object lies on or in another.
  • Intersection is the complete set of points shared by two or more objects.
  • Containment means every point of one object belongs to another.
  • Disjoint objects have empty intersection.
  • Intersections can be points, segments, lines, curves, regions, or larger sets.
  • Membership notation such as \(P\in\ell\) differs from subset notation such as \(S\subseteq\ell\).
  • A line separates a plane into two half-planes.
  • A plane separates three-dimensional space into two half-spaces.
  • A simple closed curve separates the plane into interior and exterior regions.
  • Equations and inequalities often make incidence, intersection, containment, and separation precise.
