Congruence, Similarity, Equivalence, and Symmetry

Geometry uses several different meanings of “the same.” Two figures may be identical in size and shape, identical only in shape, equivalent under a chosen rule, or unchanged by a transformation. Congruence, similarity, equivalence, and symmetry provide precise ways to distinguish these forms of sameness.

The Central Idea

Mathematical sameness depends on which properties we require to remain unchanged.

  • Congruence preserves size and shape.
  • Similarity preserves shape while allowing uniform scaling.
  • Equivalence means objects are treated as the same under a specified relation.
  • Symmetry means an object remains unchanged under a transformation.

These ideas overlap, but they answer different questions.

Sameness Depends on What We Preserve

Suppose two triangles have the same angles but different side lengths.

They have the same shape, but not the same size.

They may therefore be similar without being congruent.

If one triangle can be moved, rotated, or reflected exactly onto the other without resizing it, then the triangles are congruent.

Congruence

Two geometric figures are congruent when they have the same size and the same shape.

Congruence is commonly written

\[ \boxed{A\cong B}. \]

For polygons, congruence means that corresponding side lengths and corresponding angle measures agree.

Congruence Through Rigid Motions

A more structural definition says that two figures are congruent if one can be mapped onto the other by a sequence of rigid motions.

Rigid motions include:

  • translations;
  • rotations;
  • reflections;
  • compositions of these transformations.

Rigid motions preserve distances and angle measures.

Translation Preserves Congruence

A translation moves every point by the same displacement.

For example,

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(x+4,y-2) \]

shifts a figure without changing its side lengths, angles, area, or shape.

The original figure and its image are congruent.

Rotation Preserves Congruence

A rotation turns a figure around a fixed center.

For example, a \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise rotation about the origin sends

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(-y,x). \]

Distances and angles are preserved, so the image remains congruent to the original figure.

Reflection Preserves Congruence

A reflection flips a figure across a line.

Reflection across the \(y\)-axis sends

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(-x,y). \]

The figure may reverse orientation, but its size and shape remain unchanged.

Therefore, reflection also preserves congruence.

Congruent Figures Need Not Have the Same Orientation

Two congruent figures do not need to face the same direction.

One may be translated, rotated, or reflected relative to the other.

Congruence concerns intrinsic size and shape, not absolute position or orientation.

Corresponding Parts

When figures are congruent, their corresponding parts match.

If

\[ \triangle ABC\cong\triangle DEF, \]

the order of the letters indicates the correspondence

\[ A\leftrightarrow D, \qquad B\leftrightarrow E, \qquad C\leftrightarrow F. \]

Therefore,

\[ AB=DE,\qquad BC=EF,\qquad AC=DF, \]

and

\[ \angle A\cong\angle D,\qquad \angle B\cong\angle E,\qquad \angle C\cong\angle F. \]

Similarity

Two figures are similar when they have the same shape, although their sizes may differ.

Similarity is commonly written

\[ \boxed{A\sim B}. \]

Similar figures have equal corresponding angle measures and proportional corresponding lengths.

Scale Factor

Similar figures are related by a scale factor.

If corresponding lengths in one figure are multiplied by \(k>0\), then the new figure is similar to the original.

For example, if

\[ AB=4 \]

corresponds to

\[ DE=10, \]

then the scale factor from the first figure to the second is

\[ \boxed{k=\frac{10}{4}=\frac52}. \]

Similarity Preserves Angles

Uniform scaling changes lengths but does not change angle measures.

Therefore, corresponding angles of similar figures are congruent.

If

\[ \triangle ABC\sim\triangle DEF, \]

then

\[ \angle A\cong\angle D, \qquad \angle B\cong\angle E, \qquad \angle C\cong\angle F. \]

Similarity Preserves Ratios of Corresponding Lengths

For similar triangles,

\[ \triangle ABC\sim\triangle DEF, \]

corresponding side lengths satisfy a common ratio:

\[ \boxed{ \frac{DE}{AB} = \frac{EF}{BC} = \frac{DF}{AC} =k }. \]

The common ratio \(k\) is the scale factor.

Congruence Is a Special Case of Similarity

Congruent figures are automatically similar.

Their scale factor is

\[ \boxed{k=1}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \text{congruence} \Longrightarrow \text{similarity}. \]

The reverse implication is not generally true.

Dilations Produce Similar Figures

A dilation scales distances from a chosen center by a common factor.

A dilation centered at the origin with scale factor \(k\) sends

\[ (x,y)\mapsto(kx,ky). \]

If \(k\neq1\), the image is generally similar but not congruent to the original.

Similarity Transformations

A similarity transformation can be built from rigid motions together with a uniform dilation.

Thus, one figure is similar to another when it can be transformed into the other by:

  • translation;
  • rotation;
  • reflection;
  • uniform scaling;
  • combinations of these operations.

Length, Area, and Volume Under Scaling

If a figure is scaled by a linear factor \(k\), then different measurements scale by different powers of \(k\).

  • Lengths scale by \(k\).
  • Areas scale by \(k^2\).
  • Volumes scale by \(k^3\).

For example, doubling every length multiplies area by

\[ 2^2=4 \]

and volume by

\[ 2^3=8. \]

Example: Similar Rectangles

Consider a rectangle with side lengths

\[ 3\quad\text{and}\quad5. \]

A second rectangle has side lengths

\[ 6\quad\text{and}\quad10. \]

Both corresponding lengths have ratio

\[ \frac63=\frac{10}{5}=2. \]

Therefore, the rectangles are similar with scale factor \(2\).

Equivalent Is Broader Than Congruent or Similar

Equivalence is a broader mathematical idea.

Two objects are equivalent when a chosen relation treats them as belonging to the same class.

The exact meaning depends on the relation being used.

Congruence and similarity are themselves important examples of equivalence relations in suitable geometric settings.

Equivalence Must Be Defined

The statement

\[ A\sim B \]

can mean different things in different contexts.

In geometry, \(\sim\) often denotes similarity. In other branches of mathematics, the same symbol may represent another equivalence relation.

The governing definition must therefore be clear.

Properties of an Equivalence Relation

A relation \(\sim\) is an equivalence relation when it is:

  • reflexive: every object is equivalent to itself;
  • symmetric: if \(A\sim B\), then \(B\sim A\);
  • transitive: if \(A\sim B\) and \(B\sim C\), then \(A\sim C\).

These three properties allow objects to be grouped into equivalence classes.

Congruence Is an Equivalence Relation

Congruence satisfies the three equivalence properties.

Every figure is congruent to itself.

If \(A\cong B\), then \(B\cong A\).

If \(A\cong B\) and \(B\cong C\), then

\[ \boxed{A\cong C}. \]

Similarity Is an Equivalence Relation

Geometric similarity is also reflexive, symmetric, and transitive.

If one figure is similar to a second, and the second is similar to a third, then the first and third share the same shape structure.

Scale factors compose multiplicatively.

Equivalence Classes

An equivalence class collects all objects equivalent to a chosen object.

Under geometric similarity, for example, every square belongs to the same similarity class because all squares have the same angle structure and proportional side lengths.

Under congruence, squares of different side lengths belong to different classes.

Symmetry

A symmetry of an object is a transformation that leaves the object unchanged as a whole.

Individual points may move, but after the transformation the transformed object occupies exactly the same point set or structural configuration.

Symmetry is therefore a form of invariance.

Identity Symmetry

Every object has at least one symmetry: doing nothing.

The identity transformation sends every point to itself.

Symbolically,

\[ I(P)=P. \]

The identity is an important symmetry even though it makes no visible change.

Reflection Symmetry

A figure has reflection symmetry when reflection across a line maps the figure onto itself.

That line is called an axis of symmetry or line of symmetry.

A square has four lines of reflection symmetry.

Rotational Symmetry

A figure has rotational symmetry when a rotation through some angle smaller than a full turn maps the figure onto itself.

A square maps onto itself under rotations of

\[ 90^\circ,\qquad180^\circ,\qquad270^\circ, \]

in addition to the full-turn identity.

Order of Rotational Symmetry

The order of rotational symmetry counts the number of orientations during one full turn in which the object matches itself.

A square has rotational symmetry of order

\[ \boxed{4}. \]

The smallest positive rotation angle is therefore

\[ \frac{360^\circ}{4}=90^\circ. \]

Symmetry of a Regular Polygon

A regular \(n\)-gon has rotational symmetry of order

\[ \boxed{n}. \]

Its smallest positive rotational symmetry angle is

\[ \boxed{\frac{360^\circ}{n}}. \]

It also has \(n\) reflection symmetries.

Point Symmetry

A planar figure has point symmetry about a point when a \(180^\circ\) rotation about that point maps the figure onto itself.

A parallelogram has point symmetry about the intersection of its diagonals.

Point symmetry is therefore a special case of rotational symmetry.

Translation Symmetry

Infinite patterns can have translation symmetry.

A pattern has translation symmetry when shifting it by a certain nonzero vector leaves the overall pattern unchanged.

Repeating tilings, lattices, and periodic strip patterns provide common examples.

Glide Reflection Symmetry

A glide reflection combines a reflection with a translation along the reflection axis.

Some repeating patterns possess glide reflection symmetry even when neither the reflection nor the translation alone is a symmetry of the same pattern.

This is especially important in the classification of planar patterns.

Symmetry in Three Dimensions

Three-dimensional objects can have rotational, reflection, and other spatial symmetries.

A sphere has exceptionally high symmetry: any rotation about its center maps the sphere onto itself.

A cube has many rotational and reflection symmetries, but fewer than a sphere.

Symmetry Preserves the Object

A transformation is a symmetry only if the final object matches the original object exactly.

A transformation may preserve shape without being a symmetry.

For example, translating a finite triangle several units to the right produces a congruent triangle, but it does not leave the original triangle in the same location.

The translation is therefore a congruence transformation but not a symmetry of that isolated triangle.

Congruence and Symmetry Are Related but Different

Congruence compares two figures.

Symmetry usually compares an object with itself after a transformation.

Every symmetry of a Euclidean figure is a congruence transformation of the figure onto itself.

But not every congruence transformation between two locations is a symmetry of one fixed figure.

Similarity and Symmetry Are Related but Different

Similarity allows uniform resizing.

A finite figure usually does not map onto itself after a nontrivial dilation because its size changes.

However, some infinite structures can possess scaling symmetries or self-similarity.

Fractals provide important later examples.

Self-Similarity

An object is self-similar when parts of it resemble the whole after rescaling.

Exact self-similarity appears in mathematical fractals.

Approximate self-similarity also appears in natural forms and statistical models.

Self-similarity extends the basic idea of geometric similarity across multiple scales.

Invariants

An invariant is a property that remains unchanged under a chosen transformation or relation.

Under rigid motions, important invariants include:

  • distance;
  • angle measure;
  • collinearity;
  • parallelism;
  • perpendicularity;
  • area;
  • shape.

Under similarity transformations, angle measures and ratios of corresponding lengths remain invariant, while absolute lengths may change.

Different Relations Preserve Different Invariants

Congruence preserves more metric information than similarity.

Similarity preserves shape but not absolute scale.

A broader equivalence relation may ignore still more structure.

Thinking in terms of invariants helps explain why mathematics uses several distinct notions of sameness.

Symmetry as an Invariance Test

To test whether a transformation is a symmetry, apply the transformation and ask whether the object remains unchanged.

If

\[ T(S)=S, \]

then \(T\) is a symmetry of \(S\).

This compact equation captures the general principle.

Example: Symmetry of the Parabola

Consider

\[ y=x^2. \]

Replacing \(x\) with \(-x\) gives

\[ y=(-x)^2=x^2. \]

Therefore, reflection across the \(y\)-axis leaves the graph unchanged.

The \(y\)-axis is an axis of symmetry.

Example: Rotational Symmetry of the Origin-Centered Circle

The circle

\[ x^2+y^2=r^2 \]

is unchanged by any rotation about the origin.

Its symmetry is therefore much richer than the symmetry of a polygon with only finitely many rotational symmetries.

Example: Even Functions and Reflection Symmetry

A function satisfying

\[ f(-x)=f(x) \]

is called an even function.

Its graph has reflection symmetry across the \(y\)-axis.

This gives an algebraic test for a geometric symmetry.

Example: Odd Functions and Point Symmetry

A function satisfying

\[ f(-x)=-f(x) \]

is called an odd function.

Its graph has \(180^\circ\) rotational symmetry about the origin.

Algebra and geometry again describe the same structure from different viewpoints.

Symmetry Groups

The complete collection of symmetries of an object can itself be studied as an algebraic structure called a symmetry group.

Symmetries can be composed, reversed, and combined.

For a square, for example, rotations and reflections form a finite group.

This idea forms an important bridge between geometry and abstract algebra.

Congruence in Measurement

In pure geometry, congruence is exact.

In physical measurement, manufactured objects may be treated as congruent only within a tolerance because measurements are never perfectly exact.

This distinction between mathematical exactness and applied approximation is important in engineering and science.

Similarity in Modeling

Scale models rely on similarity.

A map, architectural model, or engineering drawing can preserve shape and proportional relationships while using a different physical scale.

The scale factor connects measurements in the model to measurements in the represented object.

Symmetry in Modeling and Design

Symmetry appears in architecture, engineering, biology, physics, art, and data visualization.

It can simplify calculation because repeated or mirrored structures often allow one part of a system to represent others.

Recognizing symmetry is therefore both a descriptive and computational tool.

Common Error: Assuming Same Shape Means Congruent

Two figures can have the same shape but different sizes.

Such figures may be similar without being congruent.

Congruence requires scale factor

\[ \boxed{1}. \]

Common Error: Ignoring Correspondence Order

In

\[ \triangle ABC\cong\triangle DEF, \]

the order communicates correspondence.

It tells us that \(A\) corresponds to \(D\), \(B\) to \(E\), and \(C\) to \(F\).

Rearranging names carelessly can produce incorrect side and angle matches.

Common Error: Using One Side Ratio to Prove Similarity

One matching ratio alone does not generally establish similarity.

The complete shape relationship must satisfy an appropriate similarity condition.

For triangles, later lessons will develop standard tests such as angle-angle and proportional-side criteria.

Common Error: Treating Equivalent as Automatically Congruent

Equivalence depends on the relation being used.

Two objects can be equivalent under a broad rule without having the same size, shape, or coordinates.

Always identify the specific equivalence relation.

Common Error: Assuming Every Rigid Motion Is a Symmetry

A rigid motion always produces a congruent image.

But it is a symmetry of a particular object only when the image coincides with the original object.

Moving a square to a new location creates a congruent square, but the translation is not a symmetry of the original isolated square.

Common Error: Counting the Full Turn Incorrectly

When determining rotational symmetry order, the identity orientation is part of the symmetry count.

A square matches itself at

\[ 0^\circ,\quad90^\circ,\quad180^\circ,\quad270^\circ. \]

Therefore, its rotational symmetry order is \(4\), not \(3\).

Common Error: Assuming Symmetry Requires Every Point to Stay Fixed

A symmetry may move individual points.

What matters is that the entire object maps onto itself.

During a \(90^\circ\) rotation of a square, most vertices move to different positions, yet the square as a whole remains unchanged.

A Reliable Procedure for Congruence

  1. Identify corresponding parts.
  2. Check whether corresponding lengths agree.
  3. Check whether corresponding angles agree.
  4. Look for a rigid motion mapping one figure onto the other.
  5. Do not treat position or orientation differences as failures of congruence.

A Reliable Procedure for Similarity

  1. Identify corresponding parts.
  2. Check corresponding angle measures.
  3. Compare corresponding length ratios.
  4. Determine whether one common scale factor applies.
  5. Remember that rigid motions may also be part of the mapping.

A Reliable Procedure for Symmetry

  1. Choose a candidate transformation.
  2. Apply it to the object.
  3. Check whether the transformed object occupies the same overall structure.
  4. For reflection symmetry, test likely axes.
  5. For rotational symmetry, test angles dividing \(360^\circ\).
  6. For repeating patterns, test translations or glide reflections.

What to Remember

  • Congruent figures have the same size and shape.
  • Rigid motions preserve congruence.
  • Similar figures have the same shape with proportional corresponding lengths.
  • Congruence is similarity with scale factor \(1\).
  • A dilation changes scale while preserving similarity.
  • Lengths scale by \(k\), areas by \(k^2\), and volumes by \(k^3\).
  • Equivalence is defined by a chosen relation and is broader than any one geometric notion of sameness.
  • An equivalence relation is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive.
  • A symmetry is a transformation that maps an object onto itself.
  • Reflection, rotation, translation, and glide reflection can produce symmetries in appropriate objects or patterns.
  • Symmetry is a form of invariance.
  • Different notions of sameness preserve different properties.
