Angles, Curves, Surfaces, Regions, and Solids

Spatial mathematics quickly expands beyond points and straight lines. Rays can form angles. Paths can bend into curves. Two-dimensional forms can create surfaces and regions. Three-dimensional forms can occupy volume as solids. These objects give geometry the vocabulary needed to describe turning, shape, enclosure, extent, and space.

The Central Idea

Geometry studies more than location and straight extension. It also studies how objects turn, bend, enclose space, form boundaries, and occupy dimensions.

Five broad object types are especially useful:

  • angles describe turning or separation between directions;
  • curves describe paths that may bend;
  • surfaces describe two-dimensional extent, often in three-dimensional space;
  • regions describe portions of space enclosed or selected by boundaries;
  • solids describe three-dimensional objects occupying volume.

These categories overlap in important ways, but each emphasizes a different spatial idea.

From Basic Objects to More Complex Forms

The previous lesson introduced points, lines, rays, segments, and planes. Those objects become building blocks for more complicated forms.

For example:

  • two rays with a common endpoint can form an angle;
  • a moving point can trace a curve;
  • a surface can contain curves and regions;
  • closed curves can bound planar regions;
  • closed surfaces can bound three-dimensional solids.

Geometry often grows by combining simpler spatial objects into richer ones.

Angles

An angle is formed by two rays with a common endpoint.

The common endpoint is called the vertex.

If rays

\[ \overrightarrow{BA} \]

and

\[ \overrightarrow{BC} \]

share endpoint \(B\), they form the angle

\[ \angle ABC. \]

The middle letter names the vertex.

The Vertex Must Be in the Middle

In three-letter angle notation, the vertex is written in the middle.

Thus,

\[ \angle ABC \]

has vertex \(B\).

The same angle may also be named

\[ \angle CBA. \]

Both names identify the same pair of rays.

An Angle Describes Turning or Separation

An angle can be understood as a measure of how much one direction differs from another.

If one ray is rotated about the common endpoint until it aligns with the other, the amount of rotation gives the angle measure.

Angle measure is commonly expressed in degrees or radians.

A full turn is

\[ 360^\circ \]

or

\[ 2\pi \]

radians.

Common Angle Types

Angles are often classified by their measure.

  • An acute angle is greater than \(0^\circ\) and less than \(90^\circ\).
  • A right angle measures \(90^\circ\).
  • An obtuse angle is greater than \(90^\circ\) and less than \(180^\circ\).
  • A straight angle measures \(180^\circ\).
  • A reflex angle is greater than \(180^\circ\) and less than \(360^\circ\).

These classifications become useful when analyzing figures, orientation, and perpendicular relationships.

Angles Are Not Physical Corners

A drawn corner can represent an angle, but an angle is not merely the small visible area near the vertex.

The angle is determined by the two rays and their directional relationship.

Extending the rays farther does not change the angle measure.

Curves

A curve is a path through space.

A curve may be straight in some places, bent in others, smooth, sharply turning, open, closed, finite, infinite, planar, or three-dimensional.

In elementary geometry, the word often refers informally to a path that bends. In more advanced mathematics, straight lines can also be treated as special kinds of curves.

A Curve Can Be Traced by a Moving Point

One useful way to imagine a curve is as the path traced by a point moving through space.

For example, in the coordinate plane, the graph

\[ y=x^2 \]

forms a curve called a parabola.

Every point

\[ (x,x^2) \]

lies on that curve.

Open and Closed Curves

A curve is often described as open or closed.

An open curve does not return to its starting point in a way that closes the path.

A closed curve loops back to form a complete enclosure.

A circle is a familiar example of a closed curve.

Simple and Self-Intersecting Curves

A simple curve does not cross itself, aside from the possible coincidence of its starting and ending points when the curve is closed.

A curve that crosses itself is self-intersecting.

This distinction matters because a simple closed curve gives a particularly clear separation between an inside and an outside in the plane.

Examples of Curves

Familiar curves include:

  • circles;
  • ellipses;
  • parabolas;
  • hyperbolas;
  • spirals;
  • arcs;
  • polynomial graphs;
  • trigonometric graphs.

Later lessons will classify many of these curves according to equations, transformations, and geometric properties.

Curves in Three Dimensions

Curves do not need to lie in a plane.

A helix, for example, winds through three-dimensional space.

A three-dimensional curve can be represented parametrically by coordinates depending on a parameter \(t\):

\[ \mathbf{r}(t)= \langle x(t),y(t),z(t)\rangle. \]

This notation is advanced, but the concept remains simple: changing \(t\) moves a point along a spatial path.

Surfaces

A surface is a two-dimensional spatial object.

A surface may be flat, like a plane, or curved, like the outer surface of a sphere.

The defining idea is that a surface has two-dimensional extent even when it sits inside three-dimensional space.

Flat and Curved Surfaces

A plane is the simplest example of a flat surface.

Curved surfaces include:

  • the surface of a sphere;
  • the lateral surface of a cylinder;
  • the lateral surface of a cone;
  • saddle-shaped surfaces;
  • wave-like surfaces.

A surface can therefore bend through three-dimensional space while still being two-dimensional in its own local geometry.

Why a Surface Is Two-Dimensional

On an ordinary surface, a point can move in two independent directions while remaining on the surface.

On a plane, those directions may be thought of as horizontal and vertical.

On a sphere, there is no single global horizontal and vertical direction, but movement still has two local degrees of freedom.

This is why surfaces are classified as two-dimensional objects.

Surfaces Can Have Boundaries

Some surfaces have edges or boundaries, while others do not.

A disk-like surface has a circular boundary.

The entire surface of a sphere has no edge.

The concepts of interior, exterior, and boundary will be developed more systematically later in this section.

Regions

A region is a specified portion of a space.

In elementary geometry, a region is often the part of a plane enclosed by a boundary.

For example, the inside of a circle together with or without its boundary can be treated as a region, depending on the definition being used.

A Curve and the Region It Encloses Are Different

A circle and a disk are related but distinct objects.

A circle consists of points at a fixed distance \(r\) from a center \((h,k)\):

\[ (x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=r^2. \]

The disk includes the interior points as well:

\[ (x-h)^2+(y-k)^2\le r^2. \]

The equation with equality describes the boundary curve. The inequality describes a region.

Regions Can Be Open or Closed

Whether a region includes its boundary can matter.

For example,

\[ x^2+y^2<1 \]

describes the interior of the unit disk without its circular boundary.

By contrast,

\[ x^2+y^2\le 1 \]

includes the boundary.

In more advanced mathematics, these distinctions lead to formal definitions of open sets, closed sets, interiors, boundaries, and neighborhoods.

Regions Can Be Defined by Inequalities

Algebraic inequalities often describe geometric regions.

For example,

\[ y\ge 2x-1 \]

describes the half-plane on or above the line

\[ y=2x-1. \]

The equation describes the boundary. The inequality selects a region relative to that boundary.

Bounded and Unbounded Regions

A bounded region fits inside some sufficiently large finite distance from a chosen reference point.

A disk is bounded.

A half-plane is unbounded because it continues indefinitely.

The distinction between bounded and unbounded spatial objects becomes increasingly important in geometry, topology, optimization, and analysis.

Solids

A solid is a three-dimensional geometric object occupying volume.

Familiar solids include:

  • cubes;
  • rectangular prisms;
  • pyramids;
  • cylinders;
  • cones;
  • spheres.

Solids extend in three independent spatial dimensions.

Solids Have Volume

The amount of three-dimensional space occupied by a solid is its volume.

For a rectangular prism with side lengths \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\),

\[ \boxed{V=abc}. \]

For a sphere of radius \(r\),

\[ \boxed{V=\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3}. \]

Volume is a measurement of the solid, just as length measures segments and area measures two-dimensional regions.

A Solid and Its Surface Are Different

The surface of a three-dimensional object is not the same as the entire solid.

For a sphere of radius \(r\):

  • the spherical surface consists of points exactly \(r\) units from the center;
  • the solid ball includes all points at distance at most \(r\) from the center.

Symbolically, if the center is the origin, the sphere satisfies

\[ x^2+y^2+z^2=r^2, \]

while the solid ball satisfies

\[ x^2+y^2+z^2\le r^2. \]

Faces, Edges, and Vertices

Many solids built from flat polygonal surfaces are polyhedra.

Their basic parts include:

  • faces, which are polygonal surfaces;
  • edges, where two faces meet;
  • vertices, where edges meet.

A cube, for example, has

\[ 6 \]

faces,

\[ 12 \]

edges, and

\[ 8 \]

vertices.

Curved Solids

Not every solid is a polyhedron.

Cylinders, cones, and spheres contain curved surfaces.

A cylinder combines flat circular bases with a curved lateral surface.

A cone combines a circular base with a curved surface that narrows to an apex.

A sphere has a fully curved surface and no edges or vertices.

Dimension Provides a Useful Hierarchy

The objects introduced so far can be organized by dimension.

  • Points are zero-dimensional.
  • Lines and curves are one-dimensional.
  • Planes, surfaces, and planar regions are two-dimensional.
  • Solids are three-dimensional.

This hierarchy is not merely a list. Higher-dimensional objects often contain lower-dimensional ones.

Boundaries Often Have Lower Dimension

A useful pattern appears repeatedly in geometry.

The boundary of a typical two-dimensional region is one-dimensional.

The boundary of a typical three-dimensional solid is two-dimensional.

For example:

  • the boundary of a disk is a circle;
  • the boundary of a rectangle is made from line segments;
  • the boundary of a solid ball is a sphere;
  • the boundary of a cube is made from six square faces.

This relationship between an object and its boundary is foundational in geometry and topology.

Interior and Boundary Are Different

A point may belong to the interior of a region, lie on its boundary, or lie outside it.

For the disk

\[ x^2+y^2\le 9, \]

the points satisfying

\[ x^2+y^2<9 \]

are interior points, while points satisfying

\[ x^2+y^2=9 \]

lie on the boundary.

A later lesson will develop interior, exterior, boundary, and neighborhood as a unified set of spatial ideas.

Geometry Distinguishes Object from Measurement

As with segments, geometric objects should be distinguished from numerical measurements attached to them.

  • An angle is an object; its measure is a number.
  • A curve is an object; its arc length may be measured.
  • A region is an object; its area may be measured.
  • A solid is an object; its volume may be measured.

Keeping object and measurement separate makes notation and reasoning more precise.

Equations Can Describe Spatial Objects

Many geometric objects can be represented algebraically.

A circle:

\[ x^2+y^2=25. \]

A disk:

\[ x^2+y^2\le 25. \]

A sphere:

\[ x^2+y^2+z^2=25. \]

A solid ball:

\[ x^2+y^2+z^2\le 25. \]

The change from equality to inequality often changes a boundary object into an entire region or solid.

Parameters Can Describe Curves

A curve can also be described by giving coordinates as functions of a parameter.

For example,

\[ x=\cos t,\qquad y=\sin t \]

traces the unit circle as \(t\) varies.

In vector form,

\[ \mathbf{r}(t)=\langle \cos t,\sin t\rangle. \]

This representation emphasizes motion along the curve rather than an equation relating \(x\) and \(y\).

Parameters Can Describe Surfaces

A surface generally requires two independent parameters.

For example, a plane can be represented in the form

\[ \mathbf{r}(u,v)=\mathbf{r}_0+u\mathbf{a}+v\mathbf{b}, \]

where \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) point in two independent directions within the plane.

The need for two parameters reflects the two-dimensional nature of the surface.

Cross Sections Connect Solids to Regions

When a plane cuts through a solid, the intersection can form a cross section.

For example:

  • a horizontal cut through a cylinder can produce a circular region;
  • a vertical cut through a cylinder can produce a rectangular region;
  • many plane cuts through a sphere produce circular regions.

Cross sections provide a powerful bridge between two-dimensional and three-dimensional geometry.

Projections Connect Higher and Lower Dimensions

A three-dimensional object can be represented on a two-dimensional page by projection.

This is why a cube can be drawn on paper even though the page itself is flat.

The drawing preserves enough spatial relationships to communicate the form, but it cannot reproduce the full three-dimensional object.

Diagrams and models will be examined more carefully later in this section.

Common Error: Confusing an Angle with Its Measure

The notation

\[ \angle ABC \]

names the geometric angle.

The notation

\[ m\angle ABC=60^\circ \]

states its measure.

The object and its numerical measurement are related but distinct.

Common Error: Treating a Curve as Its Enclosed Region

A closed curve may form the boundary of a region, but the curve and the region are not identical.

A circle is one-dimensional as a curve.

The disk enclosed by that circle is two-dimensional.

Confusing these objects leads to errors involving circumference, area, and equations versus inequalities.

Common Error: Confusing a Surface with a Solid

The spherical surface

\[ x^2+y^2+z^2=16 \]

is two-dimensional.

The solid ball

\[ x^2+y^2+z^2\le16 \]

is three-dimensional.

Equality identifies the boundary surface, while the inequality includes the interior.

Common Error: Assuming Every Surface Is Flat

A plane is a flat surface, but a surface need not be flat.

Spheres, cylinders, cones, and many graphs of functions have curved surfaces.

The word surface refers to dimensional structure, not necessarily flatness.

Common Error: Assuming Every Closed Curve Bounds One Simple Region

Simple closed curves behave differently from self-intersecting closed curves.

A simple closed curve in the plane separates an interior from an exterior in a direct way.

A self-intersecting curve can produce several loops or more complicated regions.

Later geometric and topological language makes these distinctions precise.

A Reliable Classification Procedure

When identifying a spatial object, ask:

  1. Is the object primarily describing turning between two directions? If so, think angle.
  2. Is it a one-dimensional path? If so, think line or curve.
  3. Does it have two-dimensional extent? If so, think plane, surface, or region.
  4. Is it a selected or enclosed portion of a larger space? If so, think region.
  5. Does it occupy three-dimensional volume? If so, think solid.
  6. Am I looking at the boundary only, or at the boundary together with the interior?

The last question is especially important because boundaries and the objects they enclose often have different dimensions.

What to Remember

  • An angle describes turning or separation between two rays with a common vertex.
  • A curve is a one-dimensional path through space.
  • A surface is a two-dimensional object and may be flat or curved.
  • A region is a selected or enclosed portion of space.
  • A solid occupies three-dimensional volume.
  • A boundary and the object it encloses are not necessarily the same object.
  • Equations often describe boundaries, while inequalities often describe regions or solids.
  • Object and measurement should be kept distinct.
  • Dimension helps organize spatial objects from points through solids.
  • Diagrams represent mathematical objects but do not reproduce them exactly.
