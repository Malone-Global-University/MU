Interior, Exterior, Boundary, and Neighborhood

Spatial objects divide nearby space in meaningful ways. A point may lie inside a region, outside it, directly on its edge, or simply near another point. Interior, exterior, boundary, and neighborhood provide the language needed to describe these local and global relationships precisely.

The Central Idea

Given a set or spatial object \(S\), points around it can often be classified by how their immediate surroundings relate to \(S\).

  • An interior point lies fully inside \(S\).
  • An exterior point lies fully outside \(S\).
  • A boundary point lies at the transition between \(S\) and its complement.
  • A neighborhood describes the points sufficiently close to a chosen point.

These ideas help us distinguish an object from its edge and understand what happens locally around individual points.

Why Local Spatial Language Matters

Two points can both belong to the same region but behave very differently.

One may lie deep inside the region, while another lies directly on its edge.

Likewise, two points outside the region may differ: one may be just beyond the boundary, while another may be very far away.

Neighborhood language lets us study what happens close to a point rather than only asking whether the point belongs to the set.

Interior

The interior of a set consists of the points that lie fully inside the set.

Informally, an interior point has some amount of room around it that remains entirely inside the set.

If \(p\) is an interior point of \(S\), then a sufficiently small neighborhood around \(p\) is contained in \(S\).

Interior of an Interval

Consider the closed interval

\[ [2,7]. \]

The points strictly between \(2\) and \(7\) are interior points.

Therefore, its interior is

\[ \boxed{(2,7)}. \]

The endpoints belong to the interval but are not interior points.

Interior of a Disk

Consider the closed disk

\[ D=\{(x,y):x^2+y^2\le 9\}. \]

Its interior consists of the points satisfying

\[ \boxed{x^2+y^2<9}. \]

Every such point has a sufficiently small disk around it that remains inside \(D\).

Interior Does Not Mean Center

An interior point does not have to be near the center of a figure.

A point very close to the boundary can still be interior as long as some sufficiently small neighborhood around it remains entirely inside the set.

Interior is a local property, not a measure of centrality.

Exterior

The exterior of a set consists of points that lie fully outside the set.

Informally, an exterior point has some small neighborhood containing no points of the set.

Thus, exterior points are not merely points that fail to belong to the set. Boundary points may also fail to belong to a set without being exterior.

Exterior of a Disk

For the closed disk

\[ D=\{(x,y):x^2+y^2\le9\}, \]

the exterior is

\[ \boxed{x^2+y^2>9}. \]

These points lie strictly beyond the circular boundary.

Exterior Depends on the Ambient Space

The meaning of exterior depends on the larger space in which the set is being considered.

A line segment viewed inside a line behaves differently from the same segment viewed inside the entire plane.

This broader surrounding space is sometimes called the ambient space.

Later mathematics makes this dependence explicit.

Boundary

The boundary of a set consists of points where the set and its outside meet.

A boundary point has the following intuitive property:

Every sufficiently small neighborhood around it contains points of the set and points outside the set.

Boundary of an Interval

For the interval

\[ [2,7], \]

the boundary consists of the two endpoints:

\[ \boxed{\{2,7\}}. \]

Near either endpoint, one can find points inside the interval and points outside it.

Boundary of a Disk

For

\[ D=\{(x,y):x^2+y^2\le9\}, \]

the boundary is the circle

\[ \boxed{x^2+y^2=9}. \]

Every small neighborhood around a point on this circle contains points inside the disk and points outside it.

A Boundary May Belong to the Set

Boundary points are not automatically outside a set.

In the closed disk

\[ x^2+y^2\le1, \]

the boundary

\[ x^2+y^2=1 \]

is included in the set.

A Boundary May Be Excluded from the Set

Consider the open disk

\[ x^2+y^2<1. \]

Its boundary is still

\[ x^2+y^2=1, \]

but none of those boundary points belong to the open disk.

Thus, whether a boundary belongs to a set and whether a point is a boundary point are separate questions.

The Same Boundary Can Belong to Different Sets

The open disk

\[ x^2+y^2<4 \]

and the closed disk

\[ x^2+y^2\le4 \]

have the same boundary:

\[ \boxed{x^2+y^2=4}. \]

The difference is whether the boundary is included in the set.

Interior Points and Neighborhoods

A point \(p\) is an interior point of \(S\) when there exists some radius \(r>0\) such that

\[ \boxed{B_r(p)\subseteq S}. \]

The radius does not need to be large.

It only needs to be positive.

Exterior Points and Neighborhoods

A point \(p\) is exterior to \(S\) when some neighborhood around \(p\) contains no points of \(S\).

Equivalently, for some \(r>0\),

\[ \boxed{B_r(p)\cap S=\varnothing}. \]

Boundary Points and Neighborhoods

A point \(p\) is a boundary point of \(S\) when every neighborhood around \(p\) meets both \(S\) and its complement.

Symbolically, every \(r>0\) satisfies

\[ B_r(p)\cap S\neq\varnothing \]

and

\[ B_r(p)\cap S^c\neq\varnothing. \]

This is the precise local meaning of being “on the edge.”

Interior, Boundary, and Exterior Partition Space

For many familiar subsets of Euclidean space, every point can be classified as interior, boundary, or exterior relative to the set.

These categories do not overlap.

Informally,

\[ \text{space} = \text{interior} \cup \text{boundary} \cup \text{exterior}. \]

This gives a useful organizational picture for spatial classification.

Open Sets

A set is open when every point of the set is an interior point.

For example,

\[ (2,7) \]

is open in the real number line.

Likewise,

\[ x^2+y^2<9 \]

describes an open disk in the Euclidean plane.

Closed Sets

A set is closed when it contains all of its boundary points.

For example,

\[ [2,7] \]

is closed in the real number line.

Similarly,

\[ x^2+y^2\le9 \]

is a closed disk in the Euclidean plane.

Open and Closed Are Not Opposites in Ordinary Language

In topology, open and closed are technical properties.

A set can be both open and closed in a given space.

For example, the entire real number line

\[ \mathbb{R} \]

is both open and closed as a subset of itself.

The empty set has the same property.

The Boundary of an Open Set Need Not Belong to It

The interval

\[ (0,1) \]

is open.

Its boundary is

\[ \{0,1\}. \]

Neither boundary point belongs to the interval.

The Boundary of a Closed Set Belongs to It

The interval

\[ [0,1] \]

is closed.

Its boundary is again

\[ \{0,1\}, \]

and both endpoints belong to the set.

A Set Can Have Empty Interior

A line in the plane has no two-dimensional interior.

No matter how small a disk is centered at a point on the line, that disk contains points not on the line.

Therefore, viewed as a subset of the plane, a line has empty interior.

Dimension Affects Interior

Whether an object has interior depends on the surrounding space.

A line segment has interior relative to the line containing it, but it has no interior when viewed as a subset of the entire plane.

This distinction is called relative interior in more advanced mathematics.

The Boundary Can Be the Entire Set

A lower-dimensional object embedded in a higher-dimensional space can consist entirely of boundary points.

For example, a line viewed as a subset of the plane has empty interior, and every point on the line is a boundary point.

Thus, its boundary in the plane is the entire line.

Interior and Exterior Are Open

In Euclidean space, the interior of a set is open.

Its exterior is also open.

This follows from the neighborhood definitions: interior and exterior points each have some small neighborhood that stays entirely within the same classification.

The Boundary Is Closed

The boundary of a set is closed.

Intuitively, taking limits of boundary points cannot suddenly produce a point that is safely inside or safely outside without encountering the boundary behavior nearby.

Formal proofs of this statement belong to topology, but the geometric idea is already useful here.

Closure

The closure of a set is obtained by including all of its boundary points.

It is commonly written

\[ \overline{S}. \]

For example,

\[ \overline{(2,7)}=[2,7]. \]

The closure combines the interior or existing set with its limiting edge points.

Closure of an Open Disk

If

\[ D=\{(x,y):x^2+y^2<4\}, \]

then its closure is

\[ \boxed{ \overline{D} = \{(x,y):x^2+y^2\le4\} }. \]

The circular boundary is added.

Boundary from Closure and Interior

A useful advanced identity is

\[ \boxed{ \partial S = \overline{S}\setminus\operatorname{int}(S) }. \]

Here,

\[ \partial S \]

denotes the boundary of \(S\).

The boundary is what remains after the interior is removed from the closure.

Neighborhoods Need Not Be Circular in Every Geometry

Circular disks and spherical balls arise from ordinary Euclidean distance.

If a different distance rule is used, neighborhoods can have different shapes.

For example, under the taxicab metric,

\[ d((x_1,y_1),(x_2,y_2)) = |x_2-x_1|+|y_2-y_1|, \]

neighborhoods appear diamond-shaped in Cartesian coordinates.

The concept of neighborhood is more general than any particular drawing.

Neighborhoods Express Local Behavior

Many later mathematical ideas ask what happens in every sufficiently small neighborhood of a point.

This language supports:

  • continuity;
  • limits;
  • local extrema;
  • connectedness;
  • topological equivalence;
  • local geometry.

The neighborhood concept is therefore one of the bridges between geometry and analysis.

Interior and Constraints

In optimization, a feasible region may contain interior points and boundary points.

For example,

\[ x\ge0,\qquad y\ge0,\qquad x+y\le10 \]

defines a closed triangular feasible region.

Points satisfying all three inequalities strictly,

\[ x>0,\qquad y>0,\qquad x+y<10, \]

lie in its interior.

Boundary and Equality Constraints

Boundaries often arise when an inequality becomes an equality.

For

\[ x+y\le10, \]

the line

\[ x+y=10 \]

forms part of the boundary of the corresponding half-plane.

This algebraic pattern appears repeatedly in geometry and optimization.

Interior of a Polygonal Region

The interior of a polygonal region consists of points lying inside all of its boundary edges.

The edges and vertices themselves form the boundary.

If the region includes its edges, it is a closed polygonal region.

If the edges are excluded, the interior alone forms an open region relative to the plane.

Interior of a Solid

For a solid ball

\[ x^2+y^2+z^2\le r^2, \]

the interior is

\[ \boxed{x^2+y^2+z^2 The spherical surface

\[ x^2+y^2+z^2=r^2 \]

is the boundary.

Exterior of a Solid

For the same solid ball, the exterior consists of points satisfying

\[ \boxed{x^2+y^2+z^2>r^2}. \]

Interior, boundary, and exterior therefore extend naturally from the plane into three-dimensional space.

Common Error: Assuming Every Point in a Set Is Interior

Boundary points can belong to a set without being interior points.

In

\[ [0,1], \]

the point \(0\) belongs to the interval but is not interior.

Every neighborhood of \(0\) contains real numbers less than \(0\), which lie outside the interval.

Common Error: Assuming Every Point Outside a Set Is Exterior

A boundary point may be excluded from a set without becoming an exterior point.

For the open interval

\[ (0,1), \]

the point \(0\) does not belong to the set.

But every neighborhood of \(0\) contains points of \((0,1)\), so \(0\) is a boundary point, not an exterior point.

Common Error: Assuming Boundary Means Included

Boundary describes local position relative to a set.

Inclusion is a separate membership question.

Closed sets contain their boundaries. Open sets generally do not.

Common Error: Treating Open and Closed as Exact Opposites

A set can be neither open nor closed.

For example,

\[ [0,1) \]

is neither open nor closed in \(\mathbb{R}\).

A set can also be both open and closed, as with \(\mathbb{R}\) considered inside itself.

Common Error: Forgetting the Ambient Space

The same object can have different interior and boundary behavior depending on the larger space in which it is considered.

A segment has nonempty relative interior along its supporting line but empty interior in the full plane.

Always identify the surrounding space when precision matters.

Common Error: Thinking Neighborhood Means Nearest Point

A neighborhood is not a single nearby point.

It is an entire collection of points surrounding a chosen point according to some distance or topological rule.

A Reliable Classification Procedure

To classify a point \(p\) relative to a set \(S\), ask:

  1. Does \(p\) belong to \(S\)?
  2. Can I place a sufficiently small neighborhood around \(p\) entirely inside \(S\)?
  3. If yes, \(p\) is an interior point.
  4. Can I place a sufficiently small neighborhood around \(p\) entirely outside \(S\)?
  5. If yes, \(p\) is an exterior point.
  6. If every neighborhood contains both points of \(S\) and points outside \(S\), then \(p\) is a boundary point.

A Reliable Procedure for Inequality-Defined Regions

  1. Identify the equality case; it often describes the boundary.
  2. Determine which side of the boundary satisfies the inequality.
  3. Use strict inequalities to identify interior portions when appropriate.
  4. Check whether equality is included.
  5. Classify points by substitution when possible.

What to Remember

  • Interior points have some neighborhood entirely inside the set.
  • Exterior points have some neighborhood entirely outside the set.
  • Boundary points have every neighborhood meeting both the set and its complement.
  • A boundary point may belong to the set or may be excluded from it.
  • Open sets contain only interior points.
  • Closed sets contain all of their boundary points.
  • Open and closed are technical properties, not simple opposites.
  • Neighborhoods make the everyday idea of “nearby” mathematically precise.
  • Interior and boundary depend on the ambient space.
  • Equations often describe boundaries, while strict and non-strict inequalities distinguish interiors and closed regions.
