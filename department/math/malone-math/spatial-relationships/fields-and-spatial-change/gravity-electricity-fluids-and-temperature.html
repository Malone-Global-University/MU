Gravity, Electricity, Fluids, and Temperature

Field mathematics becomes especially powerful when it is used to describe physical systems. Gravity assigns forces or accelerations throughout space. Electric phenomena involve potentials and vector fields. Fluids carry velocity, pressure, and density fields. Temperature varies from point to point and drives heat transfer. These applications differ physically, but they share a common mathematical language of scalar fields, vector fields, gradients, trajectories, potentials, and spatial change.

The Central Idea

Many physical systems can be modeled by assigning quantities to points in space.

A scalar field has the form

\[ f(\mathbf{r})=\text{one scalar value at each position}. \]

A vector field has the form

\[ \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}) = \text{one vector at each position}. \]

Gravity, electricity, fluids, and temperature provide four major families of examples.

Fields Let Us Describe Space without Tracking One Object

Ordinary motion problems often follow one object through time.

Field models instead describe what would be measured or experienced at every point in a region.

Once the field is known, many possible objects, particles, sensors, or locations can be analyzed from the same mathematical structure.

Scalar and Vector Quantities in Physical Systems

Common scalar fields include:

  • temperature \(T\);
  • pressure \(P\);
  • mass density \(\rho\);
  • electric potential \(V\);
  • gravitational potential \(\Phi\).

Common vector fields include:

  • velocity \(\mathbf{v}\);
  • force \(\mathbf{F}\);
  • acceleration \(\mathbf{a}\);
  • electric field \(\mathbf{E}\);
  • gravitational field \(\mathbf{g}\).

Gravity as a Field

A gravitational field assigns a gravitational acceleration or force direction to positions in space.

In a simplified model near Earth's surface,

\[ \mathbf{g} \approx \langle 0,0,-g\rangle. \]

This treats the field as approximately constant over a sufficiently small region.

Uniform Gravity Is a Local Approximation

Near Earth's surface, the direction toward Earth's center changes very little across an ordinary laboratory or classroom scale.

The field can therefore be approximated as uniform:

\[ \mathbf{g} \approx -g\mathbf{k}. \]

Globally, however, Earth's gravitational field is radial rather than parallel everywhere.

Radial Gravitational Structure

Outside an idealized spherically symmetric mass, the gravitational field points toward the center.

A simplified radial form is

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{r}) = -\frac{GM}{\|\mathbf{r}\|^3}\mathbf{r} }. \]

Here \(G\) is the gravitational constant, \(M\) is the central mass, and \(\mathbf{r}\) is the position vector measured from the center.

Why the Gravitational Field Is Radial

The vector

\[ \mathbf{r} \]

points outward from the center.

The negative sign in

\[ -\mathbf{r} \]

reverses that direction.

Therefore, the gravitational field points inward toward the attracting mass.

Gravitational Field Magnitude

The magnitude of

\[ \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{r}) = -\frac{GM}{\|\mathbf{r}\|^3}\mathbf{r} \]

is

\[ \|\mathbf{g}\| = \frac{GM}{\|\mathbf{r}\|^2}. \]

Thus the field weakens with the square of the distance from the center.

Gravitational Potential

Outside an idealized point mass or spherical mass, a gravitational potential can be written

\[ \Phi(\mathbf{r}) = -\frac{GM}{\|\mathbf{r}\|}. \]

The gravitational field is related by

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{g}=-\nabla\Phi }. \]

This is an important example of a vector field derived from a scalar potential.

Gravitational Equipotential Surfaces

If

\[ \Phi = -\frac{GM}{r}, \]

then a constant potential corresponds to a constant radius \(r\).

The equipotential surfaces are therefore spheres centered on the mass.

The gravitational field is perpendicular to those spheres.

Gravity Shows Local and Global Scale Differences

Locally near Earth's surface, gravity may be approximated by a constant downward vector.

Globally, the field is curved and radial.

This illustrates a central modeling principle: a local approximation can be excellent without being globally exact.

Electric Fields

An electric field assigns a vector to every point:

\[ \mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}). \]

The vector indicates the force per unit positive test charge that would be experienced at that location.

Electric fields therefore carry both magnitude and direction.

Electric Potential

Electrostatic electric fields can be related to a scalar electric potential \(V\):

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{E}=-\nabla V }. \]

The field points toward the direction of greatest local decrease in electric potential.

Electric Equipotential Surfaces

An equipotential surface satisfies

\[ V(\mathbf{r})=c. \]

Since

\[ \mathbf{E}=-\nabla V, \]

the electric field is perpendicular to equipotential surfaces wherever the gradient is nonzero.

Point-Charge Geometry

A simplified electric field from an isolated point charge has radial form:

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}) = k\frac{q}{\|\mathbf{r}\|^3}\mathbf{r} }. \]

The sign of \(q\) determines the orientation.

Positive \(q\) gives an outward field, while negative \(q\) gives an inward field.

Electric Field Magnitude

The magnitude of the idealized point-charge field is

\[ \|\mathbf{E}\| = k\frac{|q|}{r^2}. \]

Like the gravitational point-source field, it follows an inverse-square dependence on distance.

Similar Mathematics, Different Physics

Gravitational and electrostatic point-source fields have similar radial mathematical forms.

But their physical interpretations differ.

Gravity between ordinary masses is attractive, while electric interactions can be attractive or repulsive depending on charge.

Mathematical similarity does not imply physical identity.

Superposition of Electric Fields

If several sources contribute electric fields, the total field is obtained by vector addition:

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{E}_{\text{total}} = \mathbf{E}_1+\mathbf{E}_2+\cdots+\mathbf{E}_n }. \]

Each source contributes one vector at the same spatial point.

The resulting field can have a much more complicated global pattern than any individual contribution.

Superposition of Potentials

Electric potentials also add:

\[ V_{\text{total}} = V_1+V_2+\cdots+V_n. \]

Because potential is scalar, this addition can be simpler than adding vectors directly.

Taking the negative gradient of the total potential recovers the total electric field.

Field Lines in Electricity

Electric field lines are curves tangent to the electric field vectors.

At each point,

\[ \text{field-line tangent} \parallel \mathbf{E}. \]

Their geometry helps visualize the direction of the electric field through space.

Field Lines Are Not Physical Threads

Field lines are mathematical and graphical constructions.

They are not literal strings or tracks occupying space.

A diagram uses selected curves to summarize the direction structure of a continuous vector field.

Fluids Require Several Fields at Once

Fluid systems often involve multiple fields simultaneously.

Examples include:

\[ \mathbf{v}(x,y,z,t) \]

for velocity,

\[ P(x,y,z,t) \]

for pressure,

\[ \rho(x,y,z,t) \]

for density, and

\[ T(x,y,z,t) \]

for temperature.

Velocity as a Fluid Vector Field

A fluid velocity field assigns one velocity vector to each point:

\[ \mathbf{v}(x,y,z,t). \]

The vector gives the instantaneous direction and speed of the fluid at that location and time.

Streamlines in a Fluid

A streamline is tangent to the instantaneous velocity field.

In a steady flow,

\[ \mathbf{v}(x,y,z) \]

does not depend explicitly on time, so the streamline pattern remains fixed.

In unsteady flow, the streamline pattern can evolve.

Particle Trajectories in Fluids

A passive particle moving with a fluid follows

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{r}'(t) = \mathbf{v}(\mathbf{r}(t),t) }. \]

Its trajectory records the actual path of that particle through time.

In a time-dependent flow, particle paths and instantaneous streamlines need not coincide globally.

Pressure as a Fluid Scalar Field

Pressure assigns one scalar value to every point:

\[ P(x,y,z,t). \]

Equal-pressure sets satisfy

\[ P=c. \]

In atmospheric science, equal-pressure contours are called isobars.

Density as a Fluid Scalar Field

Density may vary across a fluid:

\[ \rho(x,y,z,t). \]

A constant-density model is simpler, but real fluids can have density variations caused by temperature, composition, compression, or other effects.

Fluid Flow Can Converge or Spread

A vector field may visually appear to converge toward a region or spread outward from one.

Divergence gives a local mathematical measure related to this behavior.

In fluid models, divergence is closely connected to local expansion, compression, and conservation statements.

Fluid Flow Can Rotate

A velocity field may also have local rotational tendency.

Curl provides a mathematical measure related to local rotation.

Vortices, swirling flows, and rotational patterns are therefore natural applications of vector-field analysis.

Flux in Fluid Flow

Flux measures how much of a vector field passes through a curve or surface.

In fluid flow, velocity or mass-flow fields can be integrated through a boundary to quantify transport across that boundary.

The dedicated flux lesson develops this idea in detail.

Temperature as a Scalar Field

Temperature is one of the most intuitive scalar fields:

\[ T(x,y,z,t). \]

At each position and time, the field assigns one temperature value.

The spatial pattern may contain warm regions, cool regions, gradients, and interfaces.

Isotherms and Isothermal Surfaces

Equal-temperature sets satisfy

\[ T=c. \]

In two-dimensional maps, these contours are called isotherms.

In three dimensions, the corresponding level sets are isothermal surfaces.

Temperature Gradient

The gradient

\[ \nabla T \]

points toward the direction of greatest local temperature increase.

The vector

\[ -\nabla T \]

points toward greatest local temperature decrease.

Heat Flow and Temperature Gradients

In many conductive models, heat flows from hotter regions toward colder regions.

A common constitutive model has the form

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{q} = -k\nabla T }. \]

Here \(\mathbf{q}\) is heat-flux density and \(k>0\) is thermal conductivity.

The negative sign indicates flow toward decreasing temperature.

Temperature Contours and Heat-Flow Direction

Because

\[ \nabla T \]

is perpendicular to temperature contours, conductive heat flow modeled by

\[ -k\nabla T \]

also crosses those contours approximately perpendicularly, toward lower temperature.

Close Isotherms Mean Rapid Temperature Change

If neighboring temperature contours differ by equal amounts, tightly spaced contours indicate a large temperature gradient magnitude.

Widely spaced isotherms indicate more gradual spatial temperature change.

Contour geometry therefore gives immediate visual information about thermal gradients.

Steady Temperature Fields

A steady temperature field has no explicit time dependence:

\[ T(x,y,z). \]

The temperature may vary from place to place while remaining unchanged in time.

Spatial variation and temporal variation are distinct ideas.

Time-Dependent Temperature Fields

A changing thermal system is represented by

\[ T(x,y,z,t). \]

At a fixed time, this gives a spatial snapshot.

At a fixed location, it gives a temperature history through time.

One Physical System Can Contain Both Scalar and Vector Fields

A fluid may simultaneously have:

  • scalar pressure;
  • scalar density;
  • scalar temperature;
  • vector velocity.

These fields interact mathematically even though they represent different physical quantities.

Scalar Fields Can Generate Vector Fields

Several important physical vector fields arise from scalar gradients.

Examples include

\[ \mathbf{E}=-\nabla V \]

and

\[ \mathbf{g}=-\nabla\Phi. \]

Heat conduction also relates a flux vector to

\[ -\nabla T. \]

The same gradient structure appears across multiple disciplines.

Potentials and Equipotentials

Gravity and electrostatics provide especially clear examples of scalar potentials.

Equal-potential sets organize space into contours or surfaces.

The corresponding vector field is perpendicular to those sets.

This geometry is the same one developed abstractly in the gradient and conservative-field lessons.

Inverse-Square Fields

Both idealized point gravity and point-charge electricity produce vector magnitudes proportional to

\[ \frac{1}{r^2}. \]

Their potentials vary like

\[ \frac{1}{r}. \]

Differentiating a \(1/r\)-type potential naturally produces a \(1/r^2\)-type field magnitude.

Singularities in Point-Source Models

Expressions such as

\[ \frac{1}{r} \]

and

\[ \frac{1}{r^2} \]

are undefined at

\[ r=0. \]

Idealized point-source models therefore have a mathematical singularity at the source location.

Idealization versus Physical Size

A point source is an approximation that treats an extended physical object as concentrated at one location.

Such models can be extremely useful outside the source region.

But behavior at the mathematical singularity should not automatically be interpreted as a literal description of a finite-sized physical object.

Local Models Can Be Simpler than Global Models

Near a small region, a curved field can often be approximated by a uniform one.

Examples include:

  • approximately uniform gravity near Earth's surface;
  • approximately uniform electric fields between idealized parallel plates;
  • approximately linear temperature variation across a thin region;
  • approximately constant fluid velocity over a very small neighborhood.

Boundary Conditions Matter

Physical fields are strongly affected by boundaries.

Examples include:

  • fixed temperatures on the walls of a solid;
  • fluid velocity constrained by a pipe wall;
  • electric potential specified on conducting surfaces;
  • gravitational models restricted to regions outside matter.

A field equation is not the whole model; domain and boundary conditions are also part of the problem.

Initial Conditions Matter in Time-Dependent Fields

If a field evolves with time, the initial state helps determine its later behavior.

A temperature-evolution model may need an initial temperature distribution:

\[ T(x,y,z,0)=T_0(x,y,z). \]

A moving particle in a fluid needs an initial position:

\[ \mathbf{r}(0)=\mathbf{r}_0. \]

Local Measurements Build Global Maps

Physical fields are often inferred from measurements at selected points.

Sensors may record:

  • temperature;
  • pressure;
  • wind velocity;
  • electric potential;
  • density.

Interpolation and numerical modeling then help reconstruct the field between measurements.

Field Maps Are Representations

A physical field can be represented by:

  • formulas;
  • tables;
  • contour maps;
  • arrow plots;
  • surface plots;
  • streamlines;
  • numerical grids.

Each representation highlights different information.

Contour Maps Favor Scalar Fields

Scalar quantities such as

\[ T,\quad P,\quad V,\quad \Phi \]

are naturally displayed using equal-value contours.

The contour geometry shows where the scalar value is constant and where it changes rapidly.

Arrow and Flow Plots Favor Vector Fields

Vector quantities such as

\[ \mathbf{v},\quad \mathbf{E},\quad \mathbf{g} \]

are naturally displayed with arrows or field lines.

Direction and magnitude can then be represented visually.

Magnitude Fields Bridge Scalar and Vector Views

Every vector field generates a scalar magnitude field:

\[ M(\mathbf{r}) = \|\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r})\|. \]

Therefore, a vector field can be studied with both vector diagrams and scalar contour maps.

For example, wind direction may be shown with arrows while wind speed is shown with color or contours.

Field Interaction Can Be Directional

Suppose a particle with velocity \(\mathbf{v}\) moves through a temperature field \(T\).

The rate at which the particle experiences temperature change is

\[ \frac{dT}{dt} = \nabla T\cdot\mathbf{v} \]

when \(T\) has no explicit time dependence.

The dot product measures how much the motion aligns with the temperature gradient.

Time Dependence Adds Another Source of Change

If

\[ T=T(x,y,z,t), \]

then a moving particle can experience change both because it moves through space and because the field itself changes with time.

The total rate becomes

\[ \boxed{ \frac{dT}{dt} = \frac{\partial T}{\partial t} + \nabla T\cdot\mathbf{v} }. \]

This formula illustrates how spatial and temporal change combine.

Interpreting the Two Terms

In

\[ \frac{dT}{dt} = \frac{\partial T}{\partial t} + \nabla T\cdot\mathbf{v}, \]

the term

\[ \frac{\partial T}{\partial t} \]

measures change at a fixed location.

The term

\[ \nabla T\cdot\mathbf{v} \]

measures change caused by moving through spatial variation.

Local Field Behavior Can Have Physical Meaning

A large gradient can signal rapid change.

A zero vector field may indicate an equilibrium.

Strong divergence may indicate local spreading or compression.

Strong curl may indicate local rotational tendency.

These mathematical descriptors become physically meaningful only when interpreted within the correct model.

Global Field Behavior Can Have Physical Meaning

Global questions include:

  • where field lines begin or end;
  • whether trajectories remain bounded;
  • whether total flux through a boundary is zero;
  • whether a potential is defined across the whole region;
  • how a temperature distribution evolves over time.

Such questions require the domain and boundary conditions, not only one local formula.

Common Mathematical Structures across the Four Applications

Gravity, electricity, fluids, and temperature repeatedly use the same structures:

  • scalar fields;
  • vector fields;
  • gradients;
  • level sets;
  • flow lines;
  • potentials;
  • divergence and curl;
  • flux;
  • local and global analysis.

But the Same Operator Can Mean Different Things

The gradient of temperature describes thermal change.

The gradient of electric potential relates to electric field.

The gradient of gravitational potential relates to gravitational field.

The operator is the same, but the physical quantity and units differ.

Units Keep the Models Honest

Field expressions must be interpreted with units.

Examples include:

  • temperature in kelvins or degrees Celsius;
  • velocity in meters per second;
  • pressure in pascals;
  • electric field in newtons per coulomb or volts per meter;
  • gravitational acceleration in meters per second squared.

Dimensional consistency is a basic validation tool.

Common Error: Treating Every Physical Field as a Vector Field

Pressure and temperature are scalar fields.

Velocity and electric field are vector fields.

The word field only means that a quantity is assigned throughout a region; it does not determine whether that quantity is scalar or vector.

Common Error: Treating Field Lines as Particle Paths in Every Context

A field line is tangent to a vector field.

A physical particle follows that line only if its velocity is actually governed by the field in the required way.

Electric field lines, for example, are not automatically the literal trajectories of arbitrary charged particles.

Common Error: Confusing Potential with Field Strength

A potential value such as

\[ V(\mathbf{r}) \]

is a scalar.

The associated electric field depends on the spatial gradient:

\[ \mathbf{E}=-\nabla V. \]

A large potential value does not automatically mean a large field magnitude.

Common Error: Confusing Close Equipotentials with High Potential

Closely spaced equipotential surfaces indicate rapid spatial change in potential when the potential interval is fixed.

They do not necessarily indicate that the potential values themselves are large.

Common Error: Treating Local Approximations as Global Laws

Uniform gravity is useful near Earth's surface.

It is not a globally accurate description of Earth's gravitational field.

Similar caution applies to locally uniform electric fields, temperature gradients, and fluid velocities.

Common Error: Ignoring Singularities

Point-source formulas involving

\[ \frac{1}{r} \quad\text{or}\quad \frac{1}{r^2} \]

fail at \(r=0\).

The singular point must be excluded from the mathematical domain unless a different physical model replaces the idealization there.

Common Error: Ignoring Time Dependence

A field such as

\[ \mathbf{v}(x,y,z,t) \]

can change even at a fixed spatial point.

Treating it as

\[ \mathbf{v}(x,y,z) \]

removes that temporal behavior and may change the model substantially.

A Reliable Procedure for Interpreting a Physical Field

  1. Identify the physical quantity.
  2. Determine whether it is scalar or vector-valued.
  3. Identify the spatial and temporal variables.
  4. Check the units.
  5. Determine the domain and any boundaries or singularities.
  6. Identify useful level sets, gradients, or flow lines.
  7. Separate local approximations from global behavior.
  8. Interpret mathematical operations in the context of the physical model.

Example: Gravitational Field at a Point

Suppose an idealized radial gravitational field is

\[ \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{r}) = -\frac{GM}{\|\mathbf{r}\|^3}\mathbf{r}. \]

At

\[ \mathbf{r}=\langle a,0,0\rangle, \qquad a>0, \]

we have

\[ \|\mathbf{r}\|=a. \]

Therefore,

\[ \mathbf{g} = -\frac{GM}{a^3}\langle a,0,0\rangle = \boxed{ \left\langle -\frac{GM}{a^2},0,0\right\rangle }. \]

Example: Electric Potential and Field Direction

Suppose

\[ V(x,y)=10-x^2-y^2. \]

Then

\[ \nabla V = \langle -2x,-2y\rangle. \]

Therefore,

\[ \mathbf{E} = -\nabla V = \boxed{\langle 2x,2y\rangle}. \]

The electric field points outward from the origin in this model.

Example: Fluid Speed from Velocity Components

Let

\[ \mathbf{v}(x,y) = \langle 3x,4y\rangle. \]

At \((1,1)\),

\[ \mathbf{v}(1,1)=\langle 3,4\rangle. \]

The speed is

\[ \boxed{ \|\mathbf{v}(1,1)\|=5 }. \]

Example: Temperature Gradient

Let

\[ T(x,y)=100-3x+4y. \]

Then

\[ \nabla T = \boxed{\langle -3,4\rangle}. \]

The direction of greatest temperature increase is the direction of \(\langle -3,4\rangle\).

The greatest local rate of increase is

\[ \sqrt{(-3)^2+4^2}=5. \]

Example: Heat-Flow Direction

If

\[ \mathbf{q}=-k\nabla T \]

with \(k>0\), and

\[ \nabla T=\langle -3,4\rangle, \]

then

\[ \mathbf{q} = k\langle 3,-4\rangle. \]

The heat-flow direction is therefore opposite the temperature gradient.

What to Remember

Gravity, electricity, fluids, and temperature are major applications of the same field language.

  • gravity and electric fields are vector fields often related to scalar potentials;
  • fluid systems commonly combine velocity, pressure, density, and temperature fields;
  • temperature is scalar, while conductive heat flux is vector-valued;
  • gradients point toward greatest scalar increase;
  • negative gradients often appear in force and transport laws;
  • equipotential and equal-temperature sets are level sets;
  • flow lines and trajectories organize vector-field motion;
  • inverse-square laws create important radial fields;
  • local approximations may differ from global field geometry;
  • units, domain, boundary conditions, and time dependence are part of the model.

The central modeling habit is: identify what quantity is assigned to space, determine whether it is scalar or vector-valued, and then use the corresponding field tools to interpret how it varies, points, flows, or accumulates.
