Symmetry Groups

The symmetries of a figure do more than form a list. They can be composed, inverted, and organized into an algebraic structure called a group. Symmetry groups connect geometry with abstract algebra by treating transformations as mathematical objects in their own right. Rotations, reflections, translations, and other symmetries become elements of a system governed by precise rules of composition.

The Central Idea

A symmetry group is the collection of all symmetries of a figure, together with composition as the operation.

If \(G\) is the set of symmetries of a figure \(F\), then every transformation \(g\in G\) satisfies

\[ \boxed{g(F)=F}. \]

The key idea is that these symmetries can themselves be combined algebraically.

From Geometry to Algebra

Earlier lessons asked:

Which transformations leave a figure unchanged?

Group theory asks an additional question:

How do those symmetries interact when they are composed?

This change of viewpoint turns geometric motion into algebraic structure.

The Four Group Properties

A set \(G\) with an operation forms a group when four conditions hold:

  1. closure;
  2. associativity;
  3. identity;
  4. inverses.

For symmetry groups, the operation is transformation composition.

Closure

If \(g\) and \(h\) are symmetries of a figure, then their composition must also be a symmetry.

\[ \boxed{ g,h\in G \Longrightarrow g\circ h\in G }. \]

This property is called closure.

Why Symmetry Composition Is Closed

Suppose

\[ h(F)=F \]

and

\[ g(F)=F. \]

Then

\[ (g\circ h)(F) = g(h(F)) = g(F) = F. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{g\circ h} \]

is also a symmetry.

Associativity

Transformation composition is associative:

\[ \boxed{ (f\circ g)\circ h = f\circ(g\circ h) }. \]

The grouping of transformations does not change the final result.

The order still matters, but the placement of parentheses does not.

The Identity Symmetry

Every symmetry group contains the identity transformation \(e\), which does nothing:

\[ \boxed{e(P)=P}. \]

It satisfies

\[ \boxed{ e\circ g=g\circ e=g } \]

for every symmetry \(g\in G\).

Inverse Symmetries

Every symmetry \(g\) has an inverse symmetry \(g^{-1}\) that undoes it.

\[ \boxed{ g^{-1}\circ g = g\circ g^{-1} = e }. \]

Since undoing a symmetry also maps the figure onto itself, the inverse is again in the symmetry group.

Symmetry Groups Need Not Be Commutative

A group is abelian when

\[ gh=hg \]

for all elements.

Many symmetry groups are not abelian.

In such groups,

\[ \boxed{gh\neq hg} \]

for at least some pairs.

Why Order Matters Geometrically

Reflecting and then rotating may produce a different result from rotating and then reflecting.

This noncommutativity is one of the first places where the algebraic structure of symmetry becomes visible.

Notation for Composition

Group theory often writes composition more compactly.

Instead of

\[ g\circ h, \]

we may write

\[ \boxed{gh}. \]

Unless another convention is stated, \(gh\) means apply \(h\) first and then \(g\).

The Symmetry Group of an Equilateral Triangle

An equilateral triangle has six rigid symmetries:

  • the identity;
  • two nontrivial rotations;
  • three reflections.

These six transformations form the dihedral group commonly denoted

\[ \boxed{D_3}. \]

Rotations of an Equilateral Triangle

Let \(r\) denote rotation by

\[ 120^\circ \]

counterclockwise.

Then

\[ r^2 \]

is rotation by

\[ 240^\circ, \]

and

\[ \boxed{r^3=e}. \]

Reflection of an Equilateral Triangle

Let \(s\) denote reflection across one symmetry axis.

Since reflection is self-inverse,

\[ \boxed{s^2=e}. \]

A Fundamental Dihedral Relation

In the symmetry group of a regular polygon, rotation and reflection satisfy the relation

\[ \boxed{srs=r^{-1}}. \]

Equivalently,

\[ \boxed{sr=r^{-1}s}. \]

This relation captures the noncommutative interaction between rotation and reflection.

Why Reflection Reverses Rotation Direction

Reflection reverses orientation.

Therefore, conjugating a rotation by a reflection changes a counterclockwise rotation into the corresponding clockwise rotation.

Algebraically,

\[ srs=r^{-1}. \]

Cyclic Groups

A group is cyclic if every element can be generated by repeated powers of one element.

If \(r\) generates the group, then

\[ \boxed{ G=\langle r\rangle. } \]

Rotational Symmetry as a Cyclic Group

The rotational symmetries of a regular \(n\)-gon form a cyclic group.

If \(r\) is rotation by

\[ \frac{360^\circ}{n}, \]

then

\[ \boxed{ C_n = \{e,r,r^2,\ldots,r^{n-1}\} }. \]

Order of a Group

The order of a finite group is the number of elements in the group.

It is written

\[ \boxed{|G|}. \]

Example: Order of \(C_4\)

The rotational symmetry group of a square is

\[ C_4=\{e,r,r^2,r^3\}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{|C_4|=4}. \]

Order of a Group Element

The order of an element \(g\) is the smallest positive integer \(n\) such that

\[ \boxed{g^n=e}. \]

Group order and element order are related ideas but are not the same.

Example: Order of a Quarter-Turn

Let \(r\) be a \(90^\circ\) rotation of a square.

Then

\[ r^4=e, \]

and no smaller positive power gives the identity.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\operatorname{ord}(r)=4}. \]

Example: Order of a Reflection

For any reflection \(s\),

\[ s^2=e. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\operatorname{ord}(s)=2}. \]

Dihedral Groups

The full rigid symmetry group of a regular \(n\)-gon is the dihedral group

\[ \boxed{D_n}. \]

It contains:

  • \(n\) rotations;
  • \(n\) reflections.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{|D_n|=2n}. \]

Generators of \(D_n\)

The dihedral group can be generated by:

  • a rotation \(r\) through \(360^\circ/n\);
  • one reflection \(s\).

Symbolically,

\[ \boxed{ D_n=\langle r,s\rangle. } \]

Presentation of the Dihedral Group

The defining relations may be written

\[ \boxed{ D_n = \langle r,s \mid r^n=e,\ s^2=e,\ srs=r^{-1} \rangle. } \]

This compact algebraic description encodes the geometry of a regular \(n\)-gon.

Symmetry Group of a Square

The full symmetry group of a square is

\[ \boxed{D_4}. \]

It contains eight elements:

\[ e,\ r,\ r^2,\ r^3, \]

together with four reflections.

The Four Rotations of a Square

Let \(r\) be a \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise rotation.

Then the rotational elements are:

\[ e=r^0, \] \[ r, \] \[ r^2, \] \[ r^3. \]

They correspond to rotations by

\[ 0^\circ,\quad90^\circ,\quad180^\circ,\quad270^\circ. \]

The Four Reflections of a Square

A square has four reflection axes:

  • the vertical midpoint line;
  • the horizontal midpoint line;
  • one diagonal;
  • the other diagonal.

Each reflection is an element of \(D_4\).

Composition Tables

A finite group's operation can be displayed in a composition table, also called a Cayley table.

The row and column labels are group elements, and each entry gives the composition of the corresponding pair.

Composition Table for \(C_3\)

Let

\[ C_3=\{e,r,r^2\} \]

with

\[ r^3=e. \]
\(\circ\) \(e\) \(r\) \(r^2\)
\(e\) \(e\) \(r\) \(r^2\)
\(r\) \(r\) \(r^2\) \(e\)
\(r^2\) \(r^2\) \(e\) \(r\)

What a Composition Table Reveals

A composition table can help identify:

  • closure;
  • the identity;
  • inverse pairs;
  • commutativity;
  • repeated powers of elements.

Rows and Columns in a Group Table

In a finite group table, each group element appears exactly once in every row and every column.

This follows from cancellation and invertibility.

The resulting pattern resembles a Latin square.

Subgroups

A subgroup is a subset of a group that is itself a group under the same operation.

The rotational symmetries of a regular polygon form a subgroup of its full dihedral symmetry group.

Example: \(C_4\) Inside \(D_4\)

The square's rotation group

\[ C_4=\{e,r,r^2,r^3\} \]

is contained in

\[ D_4. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{C_4\le D_4}. \]

Generated Subgroups

For an element \(g\), the subgroup generated by \(g\) is

\[ \boxed{ \langle g\rangle = \{\ldots,g^{-2},g^{-1},e,g,g^2,\ldots\}. } \]

In a finite group, the powers eventually repeat.

Example: Generated Half-Turn Subgroup

In \(D_4\), let \(r\) be a quarter-turn.

Then

\[ \langle r^2\rangle = \{e,r^2\}. \]

This is a subgroup of order \(2\).

Group Actions

A symmetry group acts on the points, vertices, edges, or other features of a figure.

A group action formalizes how each group element moves elements of a set.

If \(G\) acts on a set \(X\), we may write

\[ \boxed{g\cdot x} \]

for the image of \(x\) under \(g\).

Action Properties

A group action satisfies:

\[ \boxed{e\cdot x=x} \]

and

\[ \boxed{ (gh)\cdot x = g\cdot(h\cdot x). } \]

These conditions mirror identity and composition in the group.

Orbits

The orbit of a point \(x\) under a group \(G\) is the set of all positions reachable by the group's symmetries:

\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{Orb}(x) = \{g\cdot x:g\in G\}. } \]

Example: Orbit of a Square Vertex

Under the full symmetry group \(D_4\), any vertex of a square can be moved to any of the four vertices.

Therefore, the orbit of one vertex contains

\[ \boxed{4} \]

points.

Orbits Partition the Set

Under a group action, every element belongs to exactly one orbit.

Thus the set being acted upon is partitioned into classes of positions that are symmetry-equivalent.

Stabilizers

The stabilizer of \(x\) is the set of group elements that leave \(x\) fixed:

\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{Stab}(x) = \{g\in G:g\cdot x=x\}. } \]

A stabilizer is always a subgroup.

Example: Stabilizer of a Square Vertex

In \(D_4\), a chosen vertex is fixed by:

  • the identity;
  • the reflection across the diagonal through that vertex.

Therefore, its stabilizer has order

\[ \boxed{2}. \]

Orbit-Stabilizer Relationship

For a finite group action,

\[ \boxed{ |G| = |\operatorname{Orb}(x)| \cdot |\operatorname{Stab}(x)| }. \]

This is the orbit-stabilizer theorem.

Example: Orbit-Stabilizer for a Square Vertex

For the square,

\[ |D_4|=8. \]

A vertex orbit has size

\[ 4, \]

and its stabilizer has size

\[ 2. \]

Thus

\[ \boxed{8=4\cdot2}. \]

Symmetry-Equivalent Features

Two features of a figure are symmetry-equivalent when some group element maps one to the other.

They then belong to the same orbit.

This provides a precise way to describe equivalent vertices, edges, faces, or positions.

Vertex Orbits in a Rectangle

Under the full symmetry group of a non-square rectangle, every vertex can be mapped to every other vertex.

Therefore, all four vertices lie in one orbit.

Different Feature Types Need Not Share an Orbit

In a non-square rectangle, long sides cannot be mapped to short sides by a rigid symmetry because length is preserved.

Therefore, long edges and short edges lie in different edge orbits.

Faithful Actions

A group action is faithful when different group elements produce genuinely different transformations of the set.

The natural action of a geometric symmetry group on the entire figure is typically faithful.

Permutation Viewpoint

A symmetry permutes the vertices of a finite polygon.

Therefore, symmetry groups can be represented as permutation groups.

For example, a square rotation may act on vertices as

\[ A\mapsto B,\quad B\mapsto C,\quad C\mapsto D,\quad D\mapsto A. \]

Cycle Notation

The previous square rotation can be written compactly as

\[ \boxed{(A\,B\,C\,D)}. \]

A reflection might be written as a product of swaps, depending on the chosen axis.

Permutation Composition

Permutations compose just like geometric transformations.

This gives an algebraic model of how symmetries rearrange labeled features.

Matrix Representations

Many symmetry transformations can be represented by matrices.

For example, the quarter-turn rotation of the plane is

\[ \boxed{ R= \begin{bmatrix} 0&-1\\ 1&0 \end{bmatrix} }. \]

Matrix Multiplication Models Group Composition

If symmetries \(g\) and \(h\) are represented by matrices \(A\) and \(B\), then their composition is represented by

\[ \boxed{AB} \]

with the rightmost transformation acting first.

Orthogonal Matrices and Euclidean Symmetry

Linear rigid symmetries fixing the origin are represented by orthogonal matrices satisfying

\[ \boxed{A^TA=I}. \]

Their determinants are

\[ \boxed{\pm1}. \]

Determinant and Orientation

In a matrix representation:

  • \(\det A=1\) for orientation-preserving rotations;
  • \(\det A=-1\) for orientation-reversing reflections.

Thus determinant separates two major types of symmetry elements.

Translation Symmetry Groups

Infinite periodic patterns often have infinite symmetry groups.

If a one-dimensional pattern repeats by translation vector

\[ \mathbf{v}, \]

then its translation subgroup may be

\[ \boxed{ \{T_{n\mathbf{v}}:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}. } \]

Infinite Cyclic Translation Group

Repeated translation by one fundamental displacement generates

\[ \ldots,T_{-2\mathbf{v}},T_{-\mathbf{v}},I, T_{\mathbf{v}},T_{2\mathbf{v}},\ldots \]

This structure is an infinite cyclic group.

Two-Dimensional Lattice Translation Groups

A periodic plane pattern may have independent translations

\[ \mathbf{u} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \mathbf{v}. \]

Its translation subgroup can be written

\[ \boxed{ \{T_{m\mathbf{u}+n\mathbf{v}}:m,n\in\mathbb{Z}\}. } \]

Frieze and Wallpaper Groups

Repeating patterns have symmetry groups that may include:

  • translations;
  • rotations;
  • reflections;
  • glide reflections.

One-directional periodic patterns lead to frieze groups, while two-directional periodic patterns lead to wallpaper groups.

Symmetry Groups of Three-Dimensional Objects

Polyhedra and spatial structures also have symmetry groups.

These may include:

  • rotations about axes;
  • reflections across planes;
  • inversions or related spatial symmetries;
  • compositions of spatial motions.

Example: Cube Symmetry

A cube has many rotational symmetries about axes passing through:

  • centers of opposite faces;
  • opposite vertices;
  • midpoints of opposite edges.

Its full symmetry group is much larger than the symmetry group of a square.

Symmetry Groups and Conservation of Structure

Every symmetry group is defined relative to something being preserved.

Depending on context, a symmetry may preserve:

  • a polygon;
  • a tiling;
  • a graph;
  • an equation;
  • a physical law;
  • a combinatorial structure.

Symmetry of Equations

If an equation remains unchanged under a transformation, that transformation is a symmetry of its solution set.

For example,

\[ x^2+y^2=r^2 \]

is invariant under every rotation about the origin.

Continuous Symmetry Groups: A Gentle Preview

Some objects possess infinitely many continuously varying symmetries.

A circle is invariant under rotation through any angle

\[ \theta. \]

Such symmetries form a continuous group rather than a finite one.

The Circle Rotation Group

Rotations about a fixed center can be combined by adding angles modulo \(360^\circ\).

Symbolically,

\[ R_\alpha R_\beta = R_{\alpha+\beta}. \]

This forms a continuous rotational symmetry group.

Group Isomorphism

Two groups are isomorphic when they have the same algebraic structure even if their elements look different.

An isomorphism preserves the group operation.

Why Isomorphism Matters

A geometric symmetry group may have the same structure as a group built from:

  • integers modulo \(n\);
  • permutations;
  • matrices;
  • abstract symbols and relations.

Isomorphism lets us recognize these as different representations of one underlying structure.

Example: \(C_n\) and Integers Modulo \(n\)

The rotational symmetry group

\[ C_n=\{e,r,\ldots,r^{n-1}\} \]

behaves like addition of integers modulo \(n\).

The correspondence

\[ r^k\longleftrightarrow k\pmod n \]

preserves the operation.

Group Invariants

When comparing groups, useful structural features include:

  • group order;
  • element orders;
  • number and sizes of subgroups;
  • commutativity;
  • orbit structure under actions.

These help distinguish groups that may initially look similar.

Symmetry Breaking

Adding markings, colors, or labels to a figure may reduce its symmetry group.

A square has eight rigid symmetries, but labeling its vertices differently can leave only the identity symmetry.

Color-Preserving Symmetry

For a colored figure, a symmetry must preserve both:

  • the geometric arrangement;
  • the color pattern.

The colored symmetry group may therefore be a subgroup of the uncolored symmetry group.

Symmetry Groups in Crystallography

Crystal structures are classified partly through symmetry groups involving translations, rotations, reflections, and other spatial operations.

Group structure provides a precise language for repeated atomic arrangements.

Symmetry Groups in Physics

In physics, symmetries can represent transformations that leave physical laws unchanged.

This extends the geometric idea of invariance into broader mathematical models.

Symmetry Groups in Chemistry

Molecular symmetry groups help classify arrangements of atoms and predict structural behavior.

Rotations and reflections become algebraic symmetry operations of the molecule.

Symmetry Groups in Computer Science

Group actions and symmetry reduction can simplify:

  • search problems;
  • graph algorithms;
  • pattern recognition;
  • computer graphics;
  • configuration spaces.

Common Error: Thinking a Group Is Just a Collection

A group is not merely a set of transformations.

The operation and the four group properties are essential.

Common Error: Assuming Every Symmetry Group Is Abelian

Rotational symmetry groups \(C_n\) are abelian.

Full dihedral groups \(D_n\) are generally nonabelian for

\[ n\ge3. \]

Reflections and rotations often fail to commute.

Common Error: Confusing Group Order with Element Order

Group order counts all group elements:

\[ |G|. \]

Element order counts how many repetitions of one element are needed to reach the identity.

Common Error: Forgetting the Identity

The identity is always part of a symmetry group.

Even when discussing nontrivial symmetries, the group itself must contain the identity.

Common Error: Listing a Transformation Without Its Inverse

If a set is claimed to be a group, every element must have its inverse inside the same set.

A proposed symmetry set missing necessary inverses cannot be a group.

Common Error: Reversing Composition Order

If

\[ gh \]

means \(g\circ h\), then \(h\) acts first.

Composition order is especially important in nonabelian symmetry groups.

Common Error: Assuming Every Subset Is a Subgroup

A subset must itself satisfy the group conditions under the same operation.

Closure and inverses are common points of failure.

Common Error: Confusing Orbit with Stabilizer

The orbit tells where a point can go.

The stabilizer tells which group elements leave it where it is.

These are different but closely related concepts.

A Reliable Procedure for Finding a Symmetry Group

  1. List all transformations that map the figure onto itself.
  2. Include the identity.
  3. Check that inverses are present.
  4. Use composition to confirm closure.
  5. Identify natural generators.
  6. Determine whether the group is cyclic, dihedral, or another type.

A Reliable Procedure for Building a Composition Table

  1. List all group elements across the top and side.
  2. Choose a clear convention for composition order.
  3. Compute each row-column composition.
  4. Verify that every result remains in the group.
  5. Check that every element appears once in each row and column.

A Reliable Procedure for Finding Element Order

  1. Start with \(g\).
  2. Compute \(g^2,g^3,\ldots\).
  3. Stop at the first positive exponent \(n\) with \(g^n=e\).
  4. Conclude \(\operatorname{ord}(g)=n\).

A Reliable Procedure for Finding an Orbit

  1. Choose a point or feature \(x\).
  2. Apply every group element to \(x\).
  3. Collect the distinct images.
  4. The resulting set is \(\operatorname{Orb}(x)\).

A Reliable Procedure for Finding a Stabilizer

  1. Choose a point or feature \(x\).
  2. Test each group element \(g\).
  3. Keep exactly those satisfying \(g\cdot x=x\).
  4. Verify that the resulting set forms a subgroup.

Example: Full Analysis of Triangle Rotations

Let \(r\) be a \(120^\circ\) rotation of an equilateral triangle.

Then

\[ C_3=\{e,r,r^2\}. \]

Since

\[ r^3=e, \]

the group is cyclic of order

\[ \boxed{3}. \]

Each vertex has an orbit containing all three vertices.

Example: Full Analysis of Square Symmetry

The square has full symmetry group

\[ D_4. \]

Its order is

\[ \boxed{8}. \]

It is generated by:

\[ r=\text{quarter-turn}, \] \[ s=\text{one reflection}. \]

The defining relations include

\[ r^4=e, \] \[ s^2=e, \] \[ srs=r^{-1}. \]

What to Remember

The symmetries of a figure form a group under composition.

A group requires:

\[ \boxed{ \text{closure, associativity, identity, and inverses}. } \]

The essential facts are:

  • symmetry groups turn geometric transformations into algebraic objects;
  • composition is the group operation;
  • the identity symmetry belongs to every symmetry group;
  • every symmetry has an inverse symmetry;
  • rotational symmetries of a regular \(n\)-gon form \(C_n\);
  • all rigid symmetries of a regular \(n\)-gon form \(D_n\);
  • \(|D_n|=2n\);
  • dihedral groups are generated by one rotation and one reflection;
  • orbits describe where points can move;
  • stabilizers describe which symmetries fix a point;
  • matrix and permutation representations provide algebraic models of geometric symmetry.

The most important structural idea is: symmetry is not only about what transformations exist, but also about how those transformations combine.
